Amoebicidal effect of pyocyanin and its mechanism of action against Naegleria fowleri, the “brain-eating” amoeba
Authors: Weluga Bootsongkorn, Nongnat Tongkrajang, Siratcha Phanthong, Nawannaporn Saelim, Suteera Prachumsarn, Pichet Ruenchit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNaegleria fowleri is a free-living amoeba that causes primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) in healthy humans. Infection occurs through deep inhalation of contaminated water during water-related activities. Treatment of PAM is complicated due to the low efficacy and high toxicity of available drugs. Therefore, novel therapeutics and control agents are urgently needed. Since this amoeba shares an aquatic habitat with other microbes, it is logical to search for bioactive molecules produced by aquatic bacteria to find novel therapeutic and control agents to manage N. fowleri infection. Previously, pyocyanin (PCN), a secondary metabolite produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, was proven to have anti-amoebic activity against N. fowleri. However, its mechanism of action needs clarification. This study aimed to investigate the molecular mechanism of PCN against N. fowleri. Trophozoites of N. fowleri were treated with 300 μg/ml of PCN, and their survival was assessed at different time points. Morphological changes, DNA fragmentation, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, autophagy induction, and cytotoxicity were investigated. PCN was found to have an amoebicidal effect against N. fowleri in a time-dependent manner. Trophozoite survival decreased significantly to 34% and 26% at 12 and 24 h, respectively. Treatment of N. fowleri with PCN caused a morphological alteration. Trophozoites exposed to a high dose of PCN were fragmented. Mechanistically, PCN induced mitochondrial dysfunction similar to amphotericin B, a standard drug. Moreover, it also induced ROS accumulation but did not cause DNA fragmentation. Autophagy was also induced in PCN-treated trophozoites, as evidenced by the increased expression of autophagy-related genes. These findings indicate that PCN showed an amoebicidal effect against N. fowleri by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction-driven cell death with autophagic features. Reduction of toxicity by structural modification is necessary in the case of use as a therapeutic or control agent for the management of N. fowleri infection. Overall, this study highlights the potential of PCN as a candidate anti-N. fowleri agent, either as a standalone treatment or in combination with other drugs used for PAM management.Keywords: Amoebicidal effect, Autophagic cell death, Brain-eating amoeba, Mechanism of action, Naegleria fowleri, Programmed cell death, Pyocyanin

Introduction

Naegleria fowleri is a free-living pathogenic amoeba that causes primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) in humans (Hoffmann et al., 1978). It is also known as the “brain-eating” amoeba that belongs to the family Vahlkampfiidae of the order Schizopyrenida (Piñero et al., 2019). Naturally, this protozoan lives in aquatic (ponds, rivers, and sewage) and soil habitats within three forms, including trophozoite (amoeboid and flagellate) and cyst (Griffin, 1983). Trophozoites encyst to cyst form when they are confronted with unfavorable conditions such as high carbon dioxide concentration and high hydrostatic pressure (Chávez-Munguía et al., 2009). Humans are infected by deep inhaling contaminated water or dust into the nostril, from which the pathogen migrates to the brain via the olfactory nerve and cribriform plate and causes inflammation and necrosis (Lawande et al., 1979; Martinez, 1985). Infections have occurred most often through water-related activities such as swimming, diving, and skiing (DeNapoli et al., 1996; Jones et al., 2009; Phu et al., 2013). Ordinarily, N. fowleri infection is sporadic; however, an outbreak has also been reported. A recent comprehensive review reported that of the 416 global waterborne parasitic protozoan outbreaks that occurred between 2017 and 2022, N. fowleri was identified as the etiological agent in four outbreaks or represented 1%. However, the number of cases associated with these outbreaks was not reported (Bourli et al., 2023). The number of infections may be increasing due to climate change that supports the growth and spread of N. fowleri. Clinical symptoms of PAM are nasal congestion, sore throat, smell impairment, severe frontal headache, high fever, nausea, vomiting, stiff neck, confusion, seizures, and unconsciousness (Symmers, 1969). The progression of the disease from the onset of symptoms is generally rapid and leads to coma and death within a week in undiagnosed and untreated patients (Shariq et al., 2014).

PAM occurs most often in healthy children and young immunocompetent adults (Visvesvara and Stehr-Green, 1990). The fatality rate is more than 95% (Alisky, 2008). As of 2023, 488 cumulative cases of PAM had been reported worldwide since 1962 (Alanazi et al., 2025). This number may be underestimated, since misdiagnosis usually occurs based on symptom similarity to bacterial and viral meningitides and the lack of appropriately sensitive diagnostic tools. Among those reported cases, there were only seven confirmed cases that survived infections (Gharpure et al., 2021). Low efficacy and high toxicity of current drugs of choice (amphotericin B or AmpB) were the main reason for ineffective treatment (Leal Dos Santos et al., 2022). The lack of control measures is another key factor hindering effective infection control. Therefore, high-efficacy and low-toxicity agents acting as therapeutic drugs and control agents of this protozoan in the environment are urgently needed for saving human lives.

Naegleria fowleri is a thermophilic protist that naturally inhabits warm water such as hot springs, lakes, ponds, rivers, and sewage (Griffin, 1983; Jahangeer et al., 2020). It lives together with other organisms as a complex community. In this circumstance, it is well recognized that environmental microbes sharing the same niches generally gain more competitive advantages over others to access limited spaces and resources by secreting extracellular chemical substances detrimental to other microorganisms (Ruenchit et al., 2017). This concept persuaded us to find an anti-N. fowleri agent produced by bacteria isolated from the natural aquatic habitat that could have potential applications to control the pathogenic amoeba population. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an aquatic bacterium that produces various bioactive molecules, such as pyocin, which inhibits the growth of P. aeruginosa by forming pores in the bacterial membrane, and pyocyanin, which inhibits the growth of Candida albicans and Staphylococcus aureus by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and disrupting the electron transport chain, respectively (Méar et al., 2013; McCaughey et al., 2016; Biswas and Götz, 2022).

Recent studies have increasingly focused on elucidating the mechanisms underlying programmed cell death in N. fowleri. Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al. (2017a) demonstrated that amphotericin B exerts its amoebicidal activity by inducing programmed cell death in N. fowleri trophozoites. This process was characterized by several hallmarks of apoptosis-like cell death, including ultrastructural changes, increased ROS production, phosphatidylserine externalization, intracellular potassium depletion, DNA degradation, and expression of apoptosis-related genes (Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al., 2017b). Lê et al. (2023) reported that the anti-amoebic activity of kaempferol against N. fowleri involves both apoptosis-like and autophagy-associated cell death pathways. Kaempferol treatment induced morphological alterations, ROS accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion, accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, and the upregulation of autophagy-related genes (Lê et al., 2023). Our previous study found that P. aeruginosa-produced pyocyanin (PCN) showed a dose-dependent anti-N. fowleri effect (Ruenchit et al., 2021). However, the mechanism of action of this potential agent awaits clarification. To expand the therapeutic options for the treatment and control of PAM, this study aimed to extensively study the amoebicidal effect of PCN and to disclose its mechanism of action against the “brain-eating” amoeba N. fowleri. This will be beneficial for understanding the mode of action of targeted therapeutics or control agents aimed at treating and controlling N. fowleri infections, as well as the biology of this protozoan.

Materials and methods

Protozoan, cell lines, and culture conditions

The reference strain from the Center for Disease Control (CDC) VO 3081 N. fowleri was used to study the effects of pyocyanin (PCN) and the mechanism of action throughout the present study. The amoeba was grown axenically in Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone, GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences Austria GmbH, Pasching, Austria) at 37 °C in a T 25-cm2 flask (Corning, NY, USA) in the secure facility. When necessary, the maintained amoeba was subcultured in fresh medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin (Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, NY, USA), and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, NY, USA) until it reached the logarithmic phase. The desired trophozoite number was prepared for testing. Hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2, kidney African green monkey Vero, and neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell lines were used for the cytotoxicity test. They were grown in T 25-cm2 flasks in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) was used to detach cells from culture flasks when necessary.

Chemical agents

Pyocyanin (PCN) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Rockville, MD, USA). Amphotericin B (AmpB) was purchased from Bharat Serums and Vaccines (Mumbai, India). Stock solutions (5 mg/ml) of PCN and AmpB were prepared by dissolving the reagents in a mixture of sterile distilled water and 1% dimethylsulfoxide (1% DMSO), and sterilized by filtering through a 0.22-μm pore-size filter (PALL, Fribourg, Switzerland). Working solutions were prepared by diluting the stock solutions in Nelson’s medium to the desired concentrations.

Time-dependent inhibition assay

The time-dependent susceptibility of N. fowleri trophozoites to PCN was evaluated in vitro using the CellTiter 96® Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). A total of 1 × 104 logarithmic-phase trophozoites of N. fowleri were seeded in 200 μl of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin in individual wells of a 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After discarding the culture medium, 200 μl of fresh medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was added. Medium alone was used as a negative anti-amoeba control. The plate was kept at 37 °C. The number of living amoebae in each treatment was determined at various time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h) using the CellTiter 96® Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay. The percentage of trophozoite survival was calculated as: [(OD595 nm of test sample/OD595 nm of negative anti-amoeba sample)] × 100. Results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. Morphological changes in PCN-treated trophozoites and controls were observed microscopically (Olympus IX70 inverted tissue culture microscope, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 200× magnification and recorded with a WiFi Microscope Digital Camera (Model MC4KWG1, Microscope X, JiangSu, China).

Scanning electron microscopy

To observe the morphological alteration of PCN-treated N. fowleri, 1 × 105 trophozoites of N. fowleri were prepared on chamber slides in a 24-well culture plate containing 400 μl of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and treated with PCN at 37 °C for 6 h. Briefly, after discarding the culture medium, 400 μl of fresh medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was added to a 24-well plate. Medium containing 10 μg/ml of AmpB (positive control) and medium alone (negative control) were also added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. After incubation, treated trophozoites were washed thrice with phosphate-bufferred saline, fixed with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by fixing with 2% (w/v) osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 40 min in the dark. After the washing step, fixed trophozoites were dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol solutions (50%, 60%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and 100%). Samples were subjected to critical-point drying with CO2 as the transitional fluid, mounted on stubs, and coated with gold. The morphology of treated trophozoites was observed under a JSM-6610LV scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, and recorded.

DNA fragmentation test

A total of 5 × 105 trophozoites of N. fowleri in 3 ml of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin were seeded in an individual T 25-cm2 flask, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After discarding the culture medium, 3 ml of fresh medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was added. Medium alone was used as a negative anti-amoeba control, while medium containing 10 μg/ml of AmpB was used as a positive anti-amoeba control. The amoebae were cultivated at 37 °C for 6 h. The morphology of PCN-, medium-, and AmpB-treated trophozoites was observed under an Olympus IX70 inverted tissue culture microscope and recorded. After 6 h treatment, trophozoites were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). Briefly, treated trophozoites were harvested by freezing the culture at −20 °C for 5 min followed by centrifugation, washed once with Nelson’s medium, lysed in ATL buffer containing proteinase K at 56 °C for 1 h, and incubated with AL buffer at 70 °C for 10 min. Then 200 μl of absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was transferred to the QIAamp Mini spin column. DNA was collected by centrifugation, washed with AW1 and AW2 buffers, and eluted with AE buffer. Extracted DNA (150 ng) was separated by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis at 100 V for 50 min, stained with Green Stain Fluorescent Nucleic Acid Stain (Cyanagen, Bologna, Italy), and imaged under a Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Mitochondrial function disruption test

To investigate the change in mitochondrial inner membrane function of PCN-treated N. fowleri trophozoites, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production was measured after PCN treatment using the Celltiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega Biotech Ibérica, Madrid, Spain). To test this, 1 × 104 trophozoites per well in 200 μl of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin were seeded in a 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After discarding the culture medium, 100 μl of fresh medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was added to a 96-well plate. Furthermore, 10 μg/ml of AmpB was tested as a positive control, while medium alone and medium supplemented with 1% DMSO were tested as negative controls. The plate was kept at 37 °C for 6 h. The ATP level was measured using the Celltiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay and the multichannel detection microplate reader.

Determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation

To study the accumulation of ROS within PCN-treated N. fowleri, 1 × 105 cells on chamber slides in a 24-well culture plate containing 400 μl of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin were treated with PCN at 37 °C for 6 h. Briefly, after discarding the culture medium, 400 μl of fresh medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was added to a 24-well plate. To each well, 50 μM of tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide (tbHP; positive oxidant control), medium alone (negative control), and 1% DMSO (diluent control) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. The morphology of PCN-treated, tbHP-treated, medium-treated, and 1% DMSO-treated trophozoites was observed under the Olympus IX70 inverted tissue culture microscope and recorded. ROS generation in treated N. fowleri was determined using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA - Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). The treated cells were washed with 1× buffer, stained with 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA) solution (1× buffer containing 10 μM of DCFDA), and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min in darkness, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation, stained trophozoites were washed once with 1× buffer, fixed with cold methanol at room temperature for 20 min, and placed on a glass slide containing 10 μl of mounting medium. Cellular ROS production was observed under confocal microscopy (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA) at 400× magnification with a filter set appropriate for fluorescein (FITC).

Autophagy induction test

To test whether PCN-mediated cell death of N. fowleri is related to autophagy induction, 1 × 104 cells per well of N. fowleri were prepared in 200 μl of Nelson’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics as previously described in a 96-well black/clear bottom plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium containing 300 μg/ml of PCN was replaced on a 96-well black/clear bottom plate. Medium alone was used as a negative anti-amoeba control, while medium containing 1% DMSO was used as a diluent control. The plate was kept at 37 °C for 6 h. The morphology of all treatments was observed with the Olympus IX70 inverted tissue culture microscope and recorded. The treated trophozoites were tested for autophagy induction by detecting bright blue dot staining of autophagic vacuoles using the Autophagy Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Briefly, the medium was removed after treatment, 100 μl of the working solution of autophagosome detection reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in darkness. After washing with the wash buffer, autophagy induction of PCN-treated, medium-treated, and 1% DMSO-treated trophozoites was observed and immediately imaged under confocal microscopy at 200× magnification with a DAPI channel.

At the same time, the PCN-treated trophozoites (5 × 105 cells) were also extracted for RNA using the Blood/Cultured Cell Total RNA Mini Kit (Geneaid, New Taipei, Taiwan) and investigated for expression levels of autophagy-related genes by real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay. Briefly, the PCN-treated trophozoites were harvested by centrifugation, washed once with Nelson’s medium, extracted for RNA using the Blood/Cultured Cell Total RNA Mini Kit, and subjected to cDNA synthesis using the iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for qRT-PCR (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The expression levels of autophagy-related genes (nfatg5, nfatg8, and nfbeclin) were investigated using qRT-PCR assay. Primers used in this study are shown in Table 1. Gene amplification was performed using the Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, MA, USA) and 10 μM of each primer. qRT-PCR conditions were denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, 45 cycles of DNA denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s, and melt curve at 60–95 °C for 5 s. Transcription of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as internal controls for normalizing the level of gene expression. The threshold cycles of each transcript were compared using the 2-delta delta CT method. The level of gene expression is expressed as the relative expression among the samples. All samples were run in triplicate across three independent experiments.

In silico toxicity prediction

The toxicity of PCN was predicted in silico using ProTox 3.0, a freely available in silico toxicity prediction web server (Banerjee et al., 2024). The chemical structure of PCN was developed using the SMILES in the ProTox 3.0 web server for analysis. The prediction was performed based on machine learning algorithm-based models.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate PCN cytotoxicity in vitro, 5 × 103 cells per well of different cell lines (hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2, kidney African green monkey Vero, and neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell lines) in 100 μl of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin were seeded in individual wells of a 96-well microplate, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After discarding the culture medium, 100 μl of fresh medium containing different concentrations of PCN (0, 4.6875, 9.375, 18.75, 37.5, 75, 150, and 300 μg/ml) were added to a 96-well plate. These concentrations were selected based on our previous study (Ruenchit et al., 2021). In addition, AmpB with different concentrations (0, 4.6875, 9.375, 18.75, 37.5, 75, 150, and 300 μg/ml) were also tested. The plate was kept at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 24 h. The number of living cells was determined using the CellTiter 96® Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay. Percentage cell survival was calculated as: [(OD595 nm of test sample/OD595 nm of negative sample)] × 100. Results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. The cytotoxic concentration 50 (CC50) values of both PCN and AmpB were calculated using probit analysis with data on percentage cell survival of each cell line (Postelnicu, 2011). The selectivity index (SI) was also calculated as the ratio of a compound’s cytotoxic concentration 50 (CC50) to its inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50), with IC50 values obtained from previous studies (Ruenchit et al., 2021; Chao-Pellicer et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS Statistics. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was used to compare the mean difference between treatment groups. The results were considered significantly different at ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001. All experiments were performed in triplicate in three independent experiments.

Results and discussion

Time-dependent effect of pyocyanin against N. fowleri trophozoites

Since our previous study found a dose-dependent anti-N. fowleri effect of pyocyanin (PCN), the present study aimed to further investigate whether it displays a time-dependent activity. To explore this assumption, logarithmic-phase trophozoites of N. fowleri were treated with PCN at 300 μg/ml and an amoebicidal effect was observed at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h after treatment (Fig. 1A). The results revealed that PCN showed a time-dependent amoebicidal effect toward the trophozoite of N. fowleri. The survival rate of the trophozoite gradually reduced to less than 30% as exposure time increased (Fig. 1B). The change was observed since 6 h (77% trophozoite survival) and then decreased significantly to 34% and 26% at 12 and 24 h, respectively (Fig. 1B). This time-dependent activity of PCN against N. fowleri is consistent with previous reports describing the time-dependent activity of amphotericin B (AmpB) against Aspergillus spp. and N. fowleri (Al-Saigh et al., 2013; Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al., 2017b).

Observation under inverted tissue culture microscope, the morphological change of N. fowleri was found in a group of PCN-treated trophozoites (Fig. 1C). Trophozoite morphology gradually altered from intact to fragmented cells at 24 h, which is different from the negative anti-amoeba control group. This finding is similar to the results demonstrated previously. It was shown that the trophozoites exposed to PCN and AmpB were fragmented and only the remnants were seen as small black particles in the culture medium (Tiewcharoen et al., 2002; Ruenchit et al., 2021). This was confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, which showed that PCN induced morphological changes in N. fowleri trophozoites, similar to those caused by AmpB. The trophozoites became rounded and smaller in size (Fig. 2A), with some of them lysed (Fig. 2B) at 6 h after exposure to PCN. Similar morphological changes were observed in AmpB-treated trophozoites, which also became rounded and smaller in size (Fig. 2C), with some cells undergoing lysis (Fig. 2D) after 6 h of exposure. However, untreated trophozoites still displayed a typical morphology (amoeboid form) (Fig. 2E and F). It is widely known that AmpB disrupts the fungal cell membrane by interacting with lipids and phospholipids located on membranes, leading to cell death (Fernández-García et al., 2022). This implied that PCN might disturb N. fowleri cell membrane leading to cell fragmentation.

DNA fragmentation of treated N. fowleri

Amphotericin B has been proven to cause DNA fragmentation in Aspergillus fumigatus and N. fowleri (Mousavi and Robson, 2004; Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al., 2017b). To test whether PCN mediates cell death by causing DNA fragmentation, N. fowleri treated with 300 μg/ml of PCN (a concentration that produced clear and reproducible changes in N. fowleri trophozoite viability) were collected by centrifugation after 6 h treatment, DNA was extracted and separated by gel electrophoresis using equal quantities, and DNA degradation was observed by visualization as outlined in the schematic diagram of the DNA fragmentation test (Fig. 3A). It was observed that PCN produced by P. aeruginosa did not cause DNA fragmentation. DNA extracted from PCN-treated trophozoites did not present as a smear, a degraded pattern, similar to a medium control group (Fig. 3B). On the contrary, AmpB caused DNA fragmentation in N. fowleri (Fig. 3B). Trophozoites treated with AmpB at 10 μg/ml displayed degraded genomic DNA as shown in Fig. 3B. For morphological change, the trophozoites were fractured and the remnants were seen as small black particles in medium at 6 h (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that PCN induces programmed cell death without causing DNA fragmentation. A similar phenomenon has been reported in enucleated cells. Although DNA fragmentation is widely regarded as a hallmark of apoptosis, it is not essential for the initiation or execution of apoptotic cell death (Schulze-Osthoff et al., 1994). Schulze-Osthoff et al. (1994) demonstrated that cells enucleated with cytochalasin B still underwent apoptosis following treatment with menadione, an oxidant quinone compound involved in physiological cell death, despite the absence of nuclear DNA fragmentation (Schulze-Osthoff et al., 1994).

Mitochondrial dysfunction of treated N. fowleri

To evaluate whether PCN could alter mitochondrial function in N. fowleri trophozoites, 1 × 104 trophozoites were treated with 300 μg/ml of PCN for 6 h. ATP production was measured after PCN treatment using the Celltiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Fig. 4A). The present study showed that the ATP level generated by PCN-treated N. fowleri decreased significantly compared to that of the negative control groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B), suggesting mitochondrial membrane potential dysfunction. This result is similar to that of AmpB-treated trophozoites and also in agreement with the previous study that reported that kaempferol, which is the most well-known flavonol displaying various antimicrobial activities, also caused the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential in N. fowleri (Lê et al., 2023). Observation of morphological alteration, the treated trophozoites were smaller in size compared to the controls, while some of them were fractured (Fig. 4C).

Accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The production of ROS is a universal mode of action of AmpB against different pathogenic fungal species. It contributes to its fungicidal activity (Mesa-Arango et al., 2014). To investigate whether ROS accumulation contributes to amoebicidal activity caused by PCN, 1 × 105 cells of N. fowleri trophozoites were treated with PCN for 6 h. The accumulation of ROS in treated N. fowleri was determined using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA - Cellular ROS Assay Kit and observed under confocal microscopy. The results demonstrated that PCN induced ROS accumulation in trophozoites of N. fowleri after 6 h of treatment. Green fluorescence generated by dichlorodihydrofluorescein oxidation was observed in trophozoites, the same as that found in a positive oxidant control (tbHP-treated group) (Fig. 5C and D). On the contrary, medium alone and medium supplemented with 1% DMSO did not cause ROS generation within the trophozoites. The morphology of PCN-treated trophozoites was similar to the results described previously, while the other groups were not (Fig. 5A and B). These results are in agreement with the study by Escrig et al. (2020) describing the mechanism of the FDA-approved anti-inflammatory and anti-arthritic drug named “auranofin” that also induced the accumulation of intracellular ROS within N. fowleri and caused cell death (Escrig et al., 2020).

Autophagy induction

Autophagy is a selective degradation process in which proteins, organelles, and other cytoplasmic components are targets for the lysosomal pathway dependent degradation (Seglen and Bohley, 1992). It begins with formation of double-membrane phagophores, follows by engulfment of cytoplasmic organelles and proteins, fusion of the membranes to form an autophagic vacuole called autophagosome, fusion between the autophagosome and the lysosome or endosome to form an autolysosome, and degradation of their contents (Kang et al., 2011). This process is required for cell survival, cell development, intracellular component clearance, and cell death (Kaushik and Cuervo, 2006). In protozoans, autophagy is also involved in cell development, cell differentiation, the recycling of unnecessary organelles, and the mechanism of cell death (Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al., 2017a). To test whether PCN-mediated cell death of N. fowleri is related to autophagy induction, 1 × 104 cells of N. fowleri were treated with 300 μg/ml of PCN for 6 h, tested for autophagy induction using the autophagy assay kit, and imaged under confocal microscopy. This autophagy assay kit detects the autophagic vacuoles as bright blue dots. After treatment, morphological alteration was only observed in a group of N. fowleri treated with PCN, while those of the control groups were still intact (Fig. 6A and B). Unlike medium- and 1% DMSO-treated trophozoites, the bright blue dot representing the staining of autophagic vacuoles was slightly detected in a group of PCN-treated amoebae, while it was predominant in a group of medium- and 1% DMSO-treated trophozoites (Fig. 6C). The predominance of autophagic vacuoles in untreated trophozoites may reflect basal autophagy, which is required for normal cellular homeostasis and the continuous turnover of cytoplasmic components (Musiwaro et al., 2013). In contrast, the lower number of detectable autophagic vacuoles in PCN-treated trophozoites should be interpreted with caution. Because the autophagy detection assay measures the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles rather than autophagic flux, the reduced fluorescence observed after 6 h of PCN treatment does not necessarily indicate reduced autophagic activity. Instead, it may reflect the dynamic nature of autophagy during severe cellular stress. PCN-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress may initially activate autophagy as an adaptive response; however, as cellular damage progresses, autophagic vacuoles may be rapidly turned over or become difficult to detect because of disruption of the autophagic machinery, loss of cellular integrity, or progression to late-stage programmed cell death. Alternatively, severe oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction may impair the formation, maturation, or maintenance of autophagic vacuoles (Gouda et al., 2025). The evidence could be supported by the previous findings demonstrating that autophagy was induced during the encystation process of N. gruberi and also plays a critical role in proliferation and differentiation in Acanthamoeba spp. and Entamoeba invadens (Picazarri et al., 2008; Cárdenas-Zúñiga et al., 2017a). However, unlike these physiological processes, PCN treatment induces extensive oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, which may alter the normal progression of autophagy.

To further investigate the involvement of autophagy, expression of the autophagy-related genes was examined by qRT-PCR. It revealed that PCN-treated trophozoites displayed higher expression levels of nfatg5, nfatg8, and nfbeclin genes than untreated amoebae, with significant differences (Fig. 7A-C). This scenario was also observed in AmpB-treated trophozoites (Fig. 7A-C). Atg5 plays a central role in autophagy. Its functions include mitochondrial quality control after oxidative damage, negative regulation of the innate antiviral immune response, and apoptosis. Atg5 initiates the formation of the autophagic vacuoles or autophagosomes, and also plays a role in apoptosis (Ye et al., 2018). Atg8 is another core element of autophagic events. It binds to autophagic receptors and recruits cargo proteins for degradation in the growing autophagosomes (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2017). In addition, Atg8 is also involved in oxidative stress (Mikawa et al., 2010). Beclin 1 also has a critical role in autophagy. It was reported to increase during cell stress (Kang et al., 2011). The apparent discrepancy between the qualitative confocal observations and the quantitative gene expression analysis can be explained by the dynamic nature of autophagy. Whereas qRT-PCR demonstrated activation of the autophagy-related transcriptional program in response to PCN-induced stress, the confocal assay detects only the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles at a single time point. Consequently, transcriptional activation of autophagy-related genes does not necessarily correlate with the abundance of detectable autophagic vacuoles. The reduced number of autophagic vacuoles observed after 6 h of PCN treatment may therefore reflect rapid autophagic turnover, impaired autophagic vacuole formation or maintenance caused by severe oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, or progression of trophozoites to late-stage mitochondrial dysfunction-driven programmed cell death (Gouda et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest that PCN activates autophagy-related signaling in N. fowleri; however, further studies assessing autophagic flux and earlier treatment time points are required to define the precise role of autophagy in PCN-induced programmed cell death.

Cytotoxicity of pyocyanin

Considering the toxicity, a newly discovered bioactive molecule must be evaluated for cytotoxicity in addition to efficacy testing to evaluate the safety margins. To do this, PCN toxicity was evaluated in silico and in vitro. Based on in silico analysis, PCN shows inactive predictions for hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity and cardiotoxicity. However, it shows active predictions for respiratory toxicity and neurotoxicity (probability values of 0.72 and 0.79, respectively) (Fig. 8).

Radar plot for toxicity prediction of pyocyanin (PCN). PCN was predicted for toxicity using ProTox 3.0, a freely available in silico toxicity prediction web server.

For in vitro tests, different cell lines representing each key target organ for an investigational toxicology study included hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2, African green monkey Vero kidney, and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell lines. The cytotoxic concentration 50 (CC50) values and selectivity indices (SI) of PCN and AmpB were compared (Table 2). The CC50 value of PCN against the HepG2 cell line was comparable to that of AmpB (CC50 = 26.13 and 30.61 μg/ml for PCN and AmpB, respectively) (Fig. 9A, B). The SI values of both PCN and AmpB against the HepG2 and Vero cell lines were greater than 1.0, indicating a favorable safety profile in these cell types (SI = 1.14 and 255.08 for PCN and AmpB, respectively, in HepG2 cell line; SI = 2.50 and 925.92 for PCN and AmpB, respectively, in Vero cell line) (Table 2). However, PCN exhibited markedly higher cytotoxicity toward SH-SY5Y cell line than AmpB (Fig. 9C-F, Table 2). These in vitro findings are consistent with the in silico toxicity predictions, which classified PCN as inactive for hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity but active for neurotoxicity. PCN is a natural phenazine that has been used in antimicrobial therapy (Gonçalves and Vasconcelos, 2021). It was produced by P. aeruginosa exhibiting several advantages such as biodegradation, biomass production, antitumor treatment, and antibiotic treatment (Norman et al., 2004; Gharieb et al., 2013; Guttenberger et al., 2017; Vipin et al., 2017). Combination of PCN with antimicrobial blue light has been described to have potential as a therapeutic modality for the treatment of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (Leanse et al., 2021). Therefore, the findings reported here indicated that this typical form of PCN (without structural modification) could not be used as a therapeutic drug but could be used as a control agent of amoeba in order to limit the population of amoeba in the environment and reduce the risk of human exposure. If this activity is confirmed under environmental conditions, PCN or its derivatives could potentially be developed as amoebicidal agents to reduce the viability of N. fowleri in high-risk aquatic environments, thereby lowering the likelihood of human exposure. However, such an application would require extensive future studies to evaluate its efficacy, stability, environmental persistence, ecological safety, and potential toxicity toward non-target organisms before any practical implementation. However, if PCN will be applicable for therapeutic purposes, an antibody-drug conjugate may be an attractive solution in which a small amount of cytotoxic agent such as PCN is combined with a monoclonal antibody to reduce toxicity and increase target specificity. This concept is now increasingly recognized as a novel approach to targeted cancer therapy (Fu et al., 2022). In addition, because primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) affects the central nervous system, successful clinical translation of PCN will require careful evaluation of its pharmacokinetic properties, including blood-brain barrier penetration, tissue distribution, metabolic stability, and systemic safety. Future studies should therefore investigate the in vivo efficacy and toxicity of PCN in experimental PAM models and explore alternative drug delivery strategies, such as nanoparticle- or liposome-based formulations, to enhance brain-targeted delivery while reducing systemic exposure. These approaches may improve the therapeutic window of PCN and facilitate its potential clinical application. Furthermore, the CC50 values of PCN and AmpB against different cell lines reported herein together with their corresponding selectivity indices can be used as screening criteria for drug discovery and design. These parameters may serve as important benchmarks for identifying PCN derivatives or novel compounds with improved anti-N. fowleri activity and reduced cytotoxicity.

Conclusions

Primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) caused by N. fowleri is a neglected parasitic disease. Although it is rare, effective management is still highly necessary due to the infection’s high fatality rate. The search for novel therapeutic and control agents is urgently needed to provide alternative treatment and control options. This study revealed extensive findings on the amoebicidal activity of pyocyanin (PCN) and its molecular mechanism against N. fowleri. PCN exhibits in vitro amoebicidal activity against N. fowleri and is associated with morphological alterations, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and transcriptional changes related to autophagy. However, most mechanistic experiments were performed using a single concentration of pyocyanin (300 μg/ml) and evaluated after 6 h of treatment. Therefore, the observed cellular and molecular responses reflect the effects of pyocyanin under these specific experimental conditions and may not fully represent responses at lower concentrations or different exposure times. Overall, these findings suggest that, with structural modifications to reduce toxicity, PCN has potential as a therapeutic candidate, either as a standalone agent or in combination with amphotericin B or other drugs available for PAM treatment.
