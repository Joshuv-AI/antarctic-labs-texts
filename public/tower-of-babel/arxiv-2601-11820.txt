Large deviations and the matrix product ansatz
Davide Gabrielli, Federica Iacovissi

Abstract
. We consider probability measures on A N A^{N} , the set of sequences of symbols on a finite alphabet A A of length N N , that give a weight to each sequence in terms of a collection of matrices with non-negative entries and having rows and columns labeled by a finite or countable set B B . We prove for such kind of measures large deviations principles for several empirical measures. Our approach is based on a simultaneous combination of an enlargement of the state space to sequences on A × B A\times B and a spectral conjugation that produces a stochastic matrix, as discussed in [ 19 ] . As a result we describe the measures as hidden Markov measures and can deduce the large deviations results by contraction from the corresponding ones for the enlarged Markov chain. The measure on the enlarged state space is a Markov bridge. The invariant measures of several non equilibrium models of interacting particle systems can be represented by the so called Matrix Product Ansatz that corresponds to measures of the type that we consider and with matrices labeled by B B that is typically countable infinite. The large deviations behavior is different in the cases with B B finite or countable. In the finite case we give a variational formula for both the algebraic and the spatial empirical measures, that can be solved in special cases. For the infinite case, we illustrate the method through an example that is the invariant measure of the boundary driven TASEP model in a special regime. We recover in this way the celebrated results in [ 12 , 15 ] , and in particular we obtain a variational representation of the rate function similar to that in [ 3 ] . Our approach is general and can in principle be applied to any measure represented by the matrix product ansatz with matrices having positive entries.

1.
Introduction

We consider a class of probability measures that are called
Rational Models
in the area of theoretical informatics, in particular for the
analysis of pattern statistics (see, for example,
[
1
,
22
]
and references therein). A rational model is a probability measure on symbolic sequences defined by using a collection of matrices with non-negative elements and two vectors with non-negative coordinates. An informal definition of the measure is the following. Consider a finite alphabet and associate a matrix with each element. To a symbolic sequence we associate the matrix obtained by the row by column product of all the matrices associated with the symbols, in the order that they appear. Finally, one computes the bilinear form of this matrix between the two fixed vectors. The resulting positive number is the weight associated with the symbolic sequence. Normalizing to one we obtain a probability measure that we call a rational model.

We prove some large deviations principles for rational models. In particular, we will introduce several notions of
Empirical Measures
both algebraically, defined in terms of frequencies, and spatially defined by considering the sequence rescaled and embedded on a unit interval. This second definition is natural considering the symbolic sequence as a configuration of particles and having in mind a scaling limit. The phenomenology of this class of models can be very different depending on several features of the matrices and of the vectors. We will concentrate on the major distinction when the matrices are finite or infinite. For the finite case we will just consider the simpler case of irreducible and aperiodic matrices, while for the infinite case we will consider a remarkable example.

We will see that, while in the finite case (at least under the assumptions on the matrices) we have a general compact variational representation for the rate functional of both algebraic and spatial empirical measures, in the infinite case we may have different behaviors. This is the counterpart of the fact that irreducible finite Markov chains are recurrent while in the infinite case different behaviors may occur. The specific infinite example that we discuss has not a joint large deviations principle for the algebraic empirical measures while that for the spatial empirical measure has a non local structure.

In non-equilibrium statistical mechanics a very important role is played by the
Matrix Product Ansatz
(see
[
2
,
30
]
for some reviews) that allows to give combinatorial representations of stationary non-equilibrium states. The matrix product ansatz represents the invariant measure of some non reversible particle systems as a rational model, with the matrices and the vectors satisfying suitable algebraic relations. Typically, there are several representations of matrices satisfying such relations, which are infinite dimensional and often have positive entries, so that the invariant measure is exactly a rational model according to our definition. We will consider a special case of the boundary driven TASEP recovering with a different approach the large deviations results in
[
13
,
15
,
3
]
, but our approach can be applied in principle to any probability measure represented by the matrix product ansatz.

The strategy of our proof is a general construction showing that any rational model can be represented as an
Hidden Markov Model
on a suitable enlarged state space
[
19
]
. When the matrices are finite, the enlarged state space is finite while it is infinite when the matrices are infinite. The enlarged state space is obtained by considering the original sequence together with the labels of rows and columns of the matrices. On this enlarged state space, the measure is obtained by a product of elements of one single matrix with blocks given by the original collection of matrices. We can now transform this single matrix into a stochastic one by a conjugation operation using the Perron eigenvalues and eigenvectors. This operation is a generalized Doob transform and it is related to the Hammersley-Clifford theorem of equivalence between Gibbs and Markov measures. The enlarged probability measure is therefore naturally described in terms of a Markov bridge. We point out that the rate functionals obtained by this procedure will always have a variational representation as an infimum over rate functionals for enlarged Markov measures.

In
[
19
]
an additional structure for rational models is enlightened, namely that measures of this kind can be represented as mixtures of inhomogeneous product measures with the parameters of the product measures distributed according to a Markov bridge. This fact gives an additional structure to the rate functionals that can indeed be represented by variational problems over a double entropy functional. This different and simpler representation will be discussed in
[
20
]
. This relates the matrix product ansatz to the mixture representations of
some stationary non equilibrium states
[
5
,
6
,
4
,
21
,
27
]

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section 2 we introduce notation, define the empirical measures, give a formal definition of the rational models, explain the matrix product ansatz with an emphasis on the boundary driven TASEP and finally illustrate the enlarging construction that is at the core of our arguments; the basic construction is explained in
[
19
]
and we here discuss some details that were postponed there.

In Section 3 we prove a general large deviations Theorem for the finite case with a variational representation of the rate functional for both the algebraic and the spatial empirical measures.
We also illustrate some special cases when the variational formula can be made explicit. A different representation will be discussed in
[
20
]
.

In Section 4 we apply the ideas developed in the finite setting to the boundary driven TASEP reobtaining the results in
[
13
,
15
]
and in particular getting a representation of the rate functional as an infimum similar to that in
[
3
]
.

In Appendix we collect some useful classic results and some auxiliary Lemmas.

2.
The framework

2.1.
Notation

We consider
A,B
finite or countable sets and introduce notation using mainly
A
but the same notation holds also for
B
and other sets. We define
A^{*}=\cup_{N=1}^{+\infty}A^{N}
the set of non empty finite words in the alphabet
A
.
If
\eta\in A^{*}
is a word with symbols written in the alphabet
A
, with
\eta\in A^{N}
, we write
|\eta|=N
and use also the extended notation
\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N})
, so that
\eta_{i}\in A
is the letter at position
i
for the word
\eta
. For integers
n<m\leq|\eta|
we denote by
\eta_{n}^{m}:=(\eta_{n},\eta_{n+1},\dots\eta_{m})
the finite portion of the string
\eta
between the indices
n
and
m
. In some constructions, terms of the form
\eta_{i}
with
i>|\eta|
may appear; in this case we mean that the index of the position of the letter in the string is taken modulus
|\eta|
, i.e.
\eta_{i+N}=\eta_{i}
for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
when
|\eta|=N
. Given
\eta,\xi\in A^{*}
, we denote by
\eta\xi\in A^{*}
the concatenation of the two words.

Consider
M
a
B\times B
square matrix. We say that
M
is non-negative if
M_{b,b^{\prime}}\geq 0
for any
b,b^{\prime}\in B
.
We associate a directed graph
(B,E)
to the matrix
M
considering
(b,b^{\prime})\in E
if and only if
M_{b,b^{\prime}}>0
.
The matrix
M
is called irreducible if
(B,E)
is strongly connected. Given a state
b\in B
we call the period of
b
as the greatest common divisor of the set
\{k\in\mathbb{N}:M^{k}_{b,b}>0\}
. For irreducible matrices the period of each state is the same. We call
aperiodic
a matrix having the period of each state equal to 1.

We call respectively
\mathcal{M}^{1}(S)
and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(S)
the set of probability measures and positive measures on a given set
S
endowed with the topology induced by the weak convergence, i.e.
\mu_{n}\to\mu
when
\int fd\mu_{n}\to\int fd\mu
for any continuous and bounded
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
.

Given
\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(A^{k})
we say that the measure satisfies a finite stationary condition if

\sum_{a\in A}\nu(\eta a)=\sum_{a\in A}\nu(a\eta)\,,\qquad\forall\eta\in A^{k-1}\,,

(2.1)

and we call
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{st}(A^{k})
the set of such probability measures.

Consider the real interval
[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
We call a function
\pi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
absolutely continuous if there exists a function
\dot{\pi}\in L^{1}([0,1])

such that for almost all
x\in[0,1]
we have
\pi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\dot{\pi}(y)dy
. We call
\mathcal{AC}([0,1])
the set of absolutely continuous functions.

2.2.
Empirical measures

2.2.1.
Algebraic empirical measures

The set
A
will always be finite while
B
may be either finite or countably infinite.
We assume, for simplicity, that in the finite case
A:=\left\{0,1,\dots\ |A|-1\right\}
and
B:=\left\{0,1,\dots,|B|-1\right\}
while instead in the infinite countable case we have
B=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}=:\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Given
\eta\in A^{*}
we define its
algebraic empirical measure
of order one as

\widehat{\nu}^{1}=\widehat{\nu}^{1}[\eta]:=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta|}\delta_{\eta_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(A)\,,

(2.2)

where
\delta_{a}
is the delta measure at
a\in A
. When
\eta\in A^{N}
that is
|\eta|=N
we call
\widehat{\nu}^{1}_{N}
the empirical measure (
2.2
). The empirical measures are constructed starting from a sample, that is
\eta
in this case, and later on it will be a pair
(\eta,\zeta)
; we denote such dependence on the sample using squared parenthesis.

For any positive integer
k
we define the
algebraic empirical measure of order k
as

\widehat{\nu}^{k}=\widehat{\nu}^{k}[\eta]:=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta|}\delta_{\eta_{i}^{i+k-1}}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}_{st}(A^{k})\,,

(2.3)

that, by definition, satisfies the finite version of the stationary condition (
2.1
); again when
|\eta|=N
we use the notation
\widehat{\nu}^{k}_{N}
for (
2.3
).

For any fixed
\eta
, we have that
\left(\widehat{\nu}^{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of compatible probability measures, i.e.

\sum_{a\in A}\widehat{\nu}^{k+1}(\xi a)=\widehat{\nu}^{k}(\xi)\,,\qquad\forall\xi\in A^{k}\,,\ \forall k\geq 1\,,

(2.4)

and, by Kolmogorov theorem, are therefore the marginals of a shift invariant measure on
\mathcal{M}^{1}(A^{\mathbb{N}})
called the stationary process (see for example
[
8
]
for details and notice that you do not need to have
k<|\eta|
).

2.2.2.
Spatial empirical measures

In this section we introduce some natural empirical measures having a spatial structure. Consider the interval
[0,1]
as reference geometric space. Given
\eta\in A^{*}
, we define the corresponding
spatial empirical measure
as

\widehat{\pi}=\widehat{\pi}[\eta]:=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta|}\eta_{i}\delta_{i/|\eta|}\,.

(2.5)

We have that
\widehat{\pi}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}([0,1])
is a positive measure on the interval
[0,1]
. When
|\eta|=N

we call
\widehat{\pi}_{N}
the spatial empirical measure (
2.5
). Equivalently, we can define the empirical measure by its action on continuous functions
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

\int_{[0,1]}f\,d\widehat{\pi}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}f(i/N)\,.

(2.6)

We introduce also a
generalized spatial empirical measure
defined by

\widehat{\Pi}^{k}=\widehat{\Pi}^{k}[\eta]:=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta|}\delta_{\eta_{i}^{i+k-1}}\delta_{i/|\eta|}(dx)\,,\qquad k=1,2,\dots

(2.7)

where we added the symbol
dx
to distinguish
\delta_{y}(dx)
, the spatial delta measure at
y\in[0,1]
, from
\delta_{\eta}
, the element of
\mathcal{M}^{1}(A^{k})
concentrated on
\eta
such that
|\eta|=k
. We have that
\widehat{\Pi}^{k}\in\mathcal{M}([0,1];\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{k}))
, i.e. it is a measure valued measure on the interval
[0,1]
, that can be equivalently characterized by its action on continuous vector fields. Consider
\mathfrak{f}=(\mathfrak{f}_{\zeta})_{\zeta\in A^{k}}
where for any
\zeta
we have that
\mathfrak{f}_{\zeta}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function. We have then

\widehat{\Pi}^{k}[\eta](\mathfrak{f}):=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta|}\mathfrak{f}_{\eta_{i}^{i+k-1}}(i/|\eta|)\,.

Given a measurable
S\subset[0,1]
and
\eta\in A^{k}
we have that

\widehat{\Pi}^{k}(S)=\frac{1}{|\eta|}\sum_{\left\{i:i/|\eta|\in S\right\}}\delta_{\eta_{i}^{i+k-1}}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{k})

and
\widehat{\Pi}^{k}(S;\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the weight associated with
\zeta\in A^{k}
by
\widehat{\Pi}^{k}(S)\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{k})
. Some simple relations are
\widehat{\Pi}^{k}([0,1])=\widehat{\nu}^{k}
and
\widehat{\pi}(S)=\sum_{a\in A}\widehat{\Pi}^{1}(S;a)a
.

The topology considered for the spatial empirical measures is always that induced by the weak convergence. Consider a measure
\Pi^{k}\in\mathcal{M}([0,1];\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{k}))
; when
\Pi^{k}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure we use the same symbol for the measure and its density, i.e.
\Pi^{k}=\Pi^{k}(x)dx
, and we have
\Pi^{k}(S)=\int_{S}\Pi^{k}(x)dx
. For almost any
x\in[0,1]
, we have that the density
\Pi^{k}(x)\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{k})
.

2.3.
Rational models

We have for each
a\in A
a non-negative
|B|\times|B|
matrix
M^{(a)}
whose rows and columns are labeled by the elements of
B
. We recall also that the term non-negative here refers to the fact that all the elements
M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}
are non negative. We call
M:=\sum_{a\in A}M^{(a)}
.

To denote vectors we use the Bra-Ket formalism of quantum mechanics since this is the one traditionally used for the matrix product ansatz.
We denote by Ket vectors
\ket{x},\ket{y}
the column vectors

\ket{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x(1)\\
x(2)\\
\vdots\\
x(|B|)\,,\end{bmatrix},\qquad\ket{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y(1)\\
y(2)\\
\vdots\\
y(|B|)\ \\
\end{bmatrix}.

We will consider only vectors with positive coordinates and will also use the shorthand
\ket{x}=\left(x(b)\right)_{b\in B}
,
\ket{y}=\left(y(b)\right)_{b\in B}
. We call
\bra{x},\bra{y}
the corresponding row vectors obtained by transposition. We do not consider complex conjugation since we restrict to the cases of vectors with real and positive elements. In the case of infinite alphabets we will consider suitable summability conditions so that the model is well defined; we will discuss such cases afterwards.

We define a probability measure
\mu_{N}
on
A^{N}
by

\mu_{N}(\eta):=\frac{\bra{y}\prod_{i=1}^{N}M^{(\eta_{i})}\ket{x}}{Z_{N}}\,,\qquad\eta\in A^{N}\,,

(2.8)

where
Z_{N}
is the normalization factor

Z_{N}:=\bra{y}M^{N}\ket{x}\,.

(2.9)

Note that the measure and the normalization factor depend on
\ket{x},\ket{y}
and
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
. In cases where we need to underline the dependence on such factors, we will make the dependence explicit, for example by writing
\mu_{N}=\mu_{N}^{x,y}
.

2.4.
Enlarging the state space

Here we illustrate the construction in
[
19
]
in order to understand the statistics and the large deviations of the measures
\mu_{N}
; in the next section we will apply it to some specific cases. We define a coupling measure
\mathcal{C}_{N}
on
A^{N}\times{B}^{N+1}
(i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{N}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(A^{N}\times{B}^{N+1})
) defined as follows. Consider
\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N})\in A^{N}
and
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N+1})\in{B}^{N+1}
. We define

\mathcal{C}_{N}(\eta,\zeta):=\frac{y(\zeta_{1})\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}M^{(\eta_{i})}_{\zeta_{i},\zeta_{i+1}}\right)x(\zeta_{N+1})}{Z_{N}}\,.

(2.10)

Multiplying by
|A|
the normalization constant, the above measure can also be thought of as a probability measure on
A^{N+1}\times{B}^{N+1}
whose value does not depend on
\eta_{N+1}
.
By construction we have

\sum_{\zeta\in{B}^{N+1}}\mathcal{C}_{N}(\eta,\zeta)=\mu_{N}(\eta)\,.

(2.11)

It turns out that a very natural approach to compute large deviations rate functionals for the measures
(\mu_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is to prove large deviations principles for the measures
(\mathcal{C}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and then apply the contraction principle.

2.5.
The matrix product anstatz

The Matrix Product Ansatz (MPA) is a remarkable algebraic method to get a combinatorial representation of the invariant measure of a variety of non equilibrium stochastic particle systems; it has a long history starting from
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
28
]
, with a large number of applications to many different models (see for example
[
2
,
30
]
for some reviews and
[
26
]
for connections to combinatorics and polymer models). The power of the method is shown, for example, by the fact that it allows to deduce large deviations principles for non equilibrium models (see for example the breakthrough papers
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
17
]
). For simplicity, we illustrate the method for a system of particles with state space
\{0,1\}^{N}
and then show how our general approach applies. A probability measure represented by the matrix product ansatz often corresponds to a rational model with an infinite countable alphabet
B
. There is not a complete equivalence since in the MPA the matrices
may have complex entries, even if they are often real and non negative, thus corresponding to a rational model.

We consider the boundary driven totally asymmetric exclusion process (TASEP) on a one dimensional chain
\{1,2\dots,N\}
with N sites. This is a continuous time Markov process with generator given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,\dots N-1}\eta_{i}(1-\eta_{i+1})\left[f(\eta^{i,i+1})-f(\eta)\right]

(2.12)

\displaystyle+\alpha\left[f(\eta^{1,+})-f(\eta)\right]+\beta\left[f(\eta^{N,-})-f(\eta)\right]\,,

(2.13)

where

\eta^{i,j}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{k}&\textrm{if}\ k\neq i,j\\
\eta_{i}&\textrm{if}\ k=j\\
\eta_{j}&\textrm{if}\ k=i\end{array}\right.

(2.14)

and

\eta^{i,+}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{k}&\textrm{if}\ k\neq i\\
1&\textrm{if}\ k=i\,,\end{array}\right.\qquad\eta^{i,-}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{k}&\textrm{if}\ k\neq i\\
0&\textrm{if}\ k=i\,,\end{array}\right.

(2.15)

and
\alpha,\beta
with
\leq\alpha,\beta\leq 1
are positive parameters.
Informally, we have a chain of
N
sites where on each site at most one particle can be located; the variable
\eta_{i}
assumes the value
1
when there is a particle at
1\leq i\leq N
and assumes the value zero when the site
i
is empty. Particles jump with rate one to the nearest neighbor site to the right (i.e. from site
i
to site
i+1
) and the jump is suppressed if this site is already occupied; finally, particles are injected with rate
\alpha
in the leftmost site
1
, when it is empty, and particles in the rightmost site
N
are destroyed with rate
\beta
.

This is an important non equilibrium model whose invariant measure can be represented by the matrix product ansatz. To be aligned with the usual notation for this method we call the two matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a=0,1}
also as
M^{(0)}=E
and
M^{(1)}=D
. To have the invariant measure the following relations have to be satisfied for some vectors
\ket{x}
and
\bra{y}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}DE=D+E\,,\\
D\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\beta}\ket{x}\,,\\
\bra{y}E=\frac{1}{\alpha}\bra{y}\,.\end{array}\right.

(2.16)

For this model the matrix product ansatz states that the invariant measure of (
2.12
) is given by the rational measure (
2.8
) when the matrices satisfy (
2.16
). Equations (
2.16
) do not identify uniquely the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a=1,2}
, and there are several different representations for different values of the parameters; all of them, apart from very special cases, are obtained by infinite countable matrices. This is somehow unavoidable; as we will show in the next sections, the LDP principles are quite different in the cases of rational models with finite or infinite matrices. In the infinite case we will discuss only an example of matrix representation of the relations (
2.16
). The general strategy can, however, be applied quite generally.

3.
The finite case

In this section we consider the case when the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are finite.
Since our main aim is to illustrate the general method, we consider the simplest situation where all the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are irreducible and aperiodic and all the components of the vectors
\ket{x},\ket{y}
are strictly positive. We will discuss more general cases in a forthcoming paper
[
20
]
where also the mixture structure in
[
19
]
will be used to get a different representation. We refer to
[
22
]
for results in the general case, obtained by still another different approach. Our approach can also be adapted to the case of infinite matrices, as we will see in the next section. For simplicity, we compute the large deviations for some lower order algebraic and spatial empirical measures. Again with a more detailed analysis the result can be extended to higher order.

3.1.
Enlarged Markov bridges

Given the collection of matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
we define an
\left(|A||B|\right)\times\left(|A||B|\right)
matrix
\mathfrak{M}
having rows and columns labeled by elements of
A\times B
and defined as

\mathfrak{M}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}:=M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\,,\qquad a,a^{\prime}\in A\,;\ b,b^{\prime}\in B\,.

(3.1)

We discuss some preliminary lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

If the
B\times B
matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are irreducible and aperiodic, then the
(A\times B)\times(A\times B)
matrix
\mathfrak{M}
defined in (
3.1
) is irreducible and aperiodic.

Proof.

Since all the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are irreducible then also
M=\sum_{a\in A}M^{(a)}
is irreducible. Given any pair
b_{I},b_{F}\in B
then there exists a path
(b^{*}_{1},\dots b^{*}_{\ell})
such that
b^{*}_{1}=b_{I}
,
b^{*}_{\ell}=b_{F}
and
M_{b^{*}_{i},b^{*}_{i+1}}>0
. Let us call
a^{*}_{i}\in A
,
i=1,\dots,\ell-1
an element such that
M^{(a^{*}_{i})}_{b^{*}_{i},b^{*}_{i+1}}>0
. We need to show that for any
(a,b),(a^{\prime}.b^{\prime})\in A\times B
there exists a path
(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
that satisfies
(a_{1},b_{1})=(a,b)
,
(a_{k},b_{k})=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{M}_{(a_{i},b_{i}),(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})}>0
. Since by irreducibility there exists
b_{I}
such that
M^{(a)}_{b,b_{I}}
is strictly positive, we define
(a_{1},b_{1})=(a,b)
,
(a_{2},b_{2})=(a^{*}_{1},b_{I})
where
a^{*}_{i}
and
b^{*}_{i}
are the sequences associated, as before, with a path from
b_{I}
to
b_{F}=b^{\prime}
and then
(a_{i},b_{i})=(a^{*}_{i-1},b^{*}_{i-1})
,
i=2,\dots k
(observe that
a^{*}_{\ell}
can be fixed equal to
a^{\prime}
), and we have
k=\ell+1
. Aperiodicity follows easily by aperiodicity of all the
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
.
∎

Recall the Perron-Frobenius theorem in Section
A.1
in the appendix. We have the following relations among maximal eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrices
M
and
\mathfrak{M}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If all the
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are irreducible and aperiodic, we have that the maximal eigenvalue
\Lambda
of the matrix
\mathfrak{M}
is equal to the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda
of the matrix
M
, moreover if
\ket{e}
is the positive maximal eigenvector for
M
then the positive maximal eigenvector
\ket{\varepsilon}
of
\mathfrak{M}
is given by

\varepsilon(a,b):=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})\,.

(3.2)

Proof.

By the previous lemma,
\mathfrak{M}
is irreducible and aperiodic and therefore it has a unique maximal eigenvalue with a positive eigenvector by Perron Frobenius theorem (see theorem
A.1
). By irreducibility and the positivity of
\ket{e}
, we have that the vector
\ket{\varepsilon}
, as defined by formula (
3.2
), has all positive elements.

Taking the sum over
a\in A
on both sides of (
3.2
) we observe that

\sum_{a\in A}\varepsilon(a,b)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}M_{b,b^{\prime}}\underline{e}(b^{\prime})=e(b)\,.

(3.3)

To finish the proof it is enough to show that
\ket{\varepsilon}
is an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{M}
with eigenvalue
\lambda>0
and this follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\mathfrak{M}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime}}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})=\lambda\varepsilon(a,b)\,,

(3.5)

where we used (
3.3
) and the definition (
3.2
).
∎

We introduce the matrices:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}S_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}e(b)^{-1}M_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})\,,\\
\mathfrak{S}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\varepsilon^{-1}(a,b)\mathfrak{M}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\end{array}\right.

(3.6)

that by Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
and
A.3
, are stochastic, irreducible and aperiodic.
Introducing the
B\times B
diagonal matrix
E
having diagonal elements
E_{b,b}=e(b)
and the
(A\times B)\times(A\times B)
diagonal matrix
\mathcal{E}
having diagonal elements
\mathcal{E}_{(a,b),(a,b)}=\varepsilon(a,b)
, the formula (
3.6
) can be compactly written as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}S=\frac{1}{\lambda}E^{-1}ME\,,\\
\mathfrak{S}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathcal{E}^{-1}T\mathcal{E}.\end{array}\right.

(3.7)

We will not use the following lemma on the unique invariant measures directly, but we state and prove it anyway.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\theta=(\theta_{b})_{b\in B}
is the invariant measure of the stochastic matrix
S
, then

\Theta(a,b)=\theta(b)\frac{\varepsilon(a,b)}{e(b)}\,,\qquad a\in A,b\in B\,,

(3.8)

is the invariant measure of
\mathfrak{S}
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the matrices
\left(S^{(a)}\right)_{a\in A}
defined by
S_{b,b^{\prime}}^{(a)}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}e(b)^{-1}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})
.
A direct relation between
\mathfrak{S}
and the matrices
(S^{(a)})_{a\in A}
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\varepsilon^{-1}(a,b)M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a,b)}{e(b)}\right)^{-1}S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}{e(b^{\prime})}\right)\,.

Using the latter relation we can show that (
3.8
) is the invariant measure of
\mathfrak{S}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b}\Theta(a,b)\mathfrak{S}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}=\sum_{a,b}\Theta(a,b)\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a,b)}{e(b)}\right)^{-1}S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}{e(b^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\theta(b)S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}{e(b^{\prime})}\right)=\sum_{b\in B}\theta(b)S_{b,b^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}{e(b^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta(b^{\prime})\left(\frac{\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}{e(b^{\prime})}\right)=\Theta(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\,.

∎

We can now illustrate the general structure of the probability measures
\mathcal{C}_{N}
on the enlarged state space;
to simplify the notation we consider a generic state space
\mathbb{A}
instead that
A\times B
.
Consider a Markov chain on
\mathbb{A}
with transition probability

P
. Let
g,f:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be two positive functions, and define the measure on
\mathbb{A}^{N+1}
by

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{f,g}(\xi)=\frac{f(\xi_{1})\prod_{i=1}^{N}P_{\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1}}g(\xi_{N+1})}{Z_{N}}\,.

(3.9)

A special case is when
f(\cdot)=\delta_{\cdot,a}
and
g(\cdot)=\delta_{\cdot,a^{\prime}}
with
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{A}
and
\delta_{\cdot,\cdot}
denoting the Kronecker delta; in this case, the measure gives a positive weight only to trajectories that satisfy
\xi_{1}=a
and
\xi_{N+1}=a^{\prime}
. In this special case, the measure is called the Markov bridge with transition probability
P
between
a
and
a^{\prime}
in the discrete time window
1,\dots,N+1
and the normalization constant is given by

Z_{N}=P^{N}_{a,a^{\prime}}
. The process corresponds to the Markov chain with transition matrix
P
conditioned to start in
a
at time
1
and to end in
a^{\prime}
at time
N+1
. This is a Markov measure but with time non homogeneous transition probabilities. The general case (
3.9
) corresponds to a measure for which the initial and final points
(\xi_{1},\xi_{N+1})
are sampled according to a suitable distribution, and then the initial and final points are connected by a Markov bridge starting at
\xi_{1}
and finishing at
\xi_{N+1}
.
The initial and final points
(\xi_{1},\xi_{N+1})
are sampled according to the distribution

m(\xi_{1},\xi_{N+1})=\frac{f(\xi_{1})P^{N}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{N+1}}g(\xi_{N+1})}{Z_{N}}\,.

(3.10)

The Markov nature of a Markov bridge can be verified observing that

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{f,g}\left(\xi_{k+1}=a_{k+1}|\xi_{k}=a_{k},\dots,\xi_{1}=a_{1}\right)

depends just on
a_{k+1}
and
a_{k}
and the direct computation reveals the time non-homogeneous nature of the transition probabilities

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{f,g}\left(\xi_{k+1}=a_{k+1}|\xi_{k}=a_{k},\dots,\xi_{1}=a_{1}\right)=\frac{P_{a_{k},a_{k+1}}\sum_{\xi_{N+1}}P^{N-k}_{a_{k+1},\xi_{N+1}}g(\xi_{N+1})}{\sum_{\xi_{N+1}}P^{N-k+1}_{a_{k},\xi_{N+1}}g(\xi_{N+1})}\,.

(3.11)

If we need to specify the transition matrix
P
of the Markov bridge measure we use the notation
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{P,f,g}
.

By using the transformation (
3.6
), we have that the enlarged measure (
2.10
), is indeed the Markov bridge measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},f,g}
associated with the
(A\times B)\times(A\times B)
stochastic matrix
\mathfrak{S}
defined in (
3.6
) and with the functions
f,g;A\times B\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f(a,b)=y(b)\varepsilon(a,b)\\
g(a,b)=\varepsilon^{-1}(a,b)x(b)\,.\end{array}\right.

3.2.
The general statement

We can now state our main result in the finite case. We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{2}
a generic element
of
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{stat}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)
and by
\mathcal{C}^{1}
its one marginal, i.e.

\mathcal{C}^{1}(a,b):=\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A\times B}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]=\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A\times B}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}),(a,b)\right]\,,

(3.12)

where the second equality follows from the finite stationarity of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.
We instead denote by
\nu^{2}
a generic element
of
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{stat}(A^{2})
. We refer to
[
8
]
for the basic definitions on large deviations.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
|A|<+\infty
, every
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
is irreducible and aperiodic and
\ket{x},\ket{y}
have strictly positive components. When
\eta
is distributed according to
\mu_{N}
in (
2.8
), we have that
\widehat{\nu}^{2}_{N}
, defined in (
2.3
), satisfies a large deviation principle on
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{st}(A^{2})
with speed
N
and rate functional

I^{2}\left(\nu^{2}\right)=\inf\left\{\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in A;b,b^{\prime}\in B}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]\log\frac{\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]}{\mathcal{C}^{1}\left[(a,b)\right]M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}}+\log\lambda\right\}\,,

(3.13)

where the infimum is over

\left\{\mathcal{C}^{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{stat}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)\,:\,\sum_{b,b^{\prime}\in B}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]=\nu^{2}(a,a^{\prime})\,,\forall a,a^{\prime}\in A\right\}

(3.14)

and
\lambda
is the maximal eigenvalue of
M
. We have also that
\widehat{\Pi}^{2}_{N}
satisfies a large deviations principle on
\mathcal{M}([0,1];\mathcal{M}^{+}(A^{2}))
with rate functional

J^{2}\left(\Pi^{2}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{0}^{1}I^{2}\left(\Pi^{2}(x)\right)dx\,,&\textrm{if}\ \Pi^{2}=\Pi^{2}(x)dx\,,\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise}\,,\end{array}\right.

(3.15)

where
\Pi^{2}(x)\in\mathcal{M}^{1}_{st}(A^{2})
for almost all
x
.

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
we have that the matrix

\mathfrak{S}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\varepsilon(a,b)^{-1}\mathfrak{M}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}\varepsilon(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

(3.16)

is stochastic.
By the positivity of the vectors
\ket{x},\ket{y},\ket{\varepsilon}
we have that there exist positive constants
k,K
such that

k\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathrm{st}}\leq\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},f,g}\leq K\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathrm{st}}

where we called
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathrm{st}}
the stationary Markov measure with transition probability
\mathfrak{S}
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathrm{st}}(\eta,\zeta)=\Theta(\eta_{1},\zeta_{1})\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{S}_{(\eta_{i},\zeta_{i}),(\eta_{i+1},\zeta_{i+1})}\,,

(3.17)

where
\Theta
is the unique stationary measure of the transition matrix
\mathfrak{S}
and the value of
\eta_{N+1}
is irrelevant given the form of
\mathfrak{S}
.
We can therefore apply Lemma
A.4
deducing the large deviations for the empirical measure for
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},f,g}
from the large deviations of the Markov measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathrm{st}}
, that are well established (see for example
[
8
]
) since it is a finite state irreducible stationary Markov measure.

Let us call

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{2}_{N+1}:=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\delta_{\big((\eta_{i},\zeta_{i}),(\eta_{i+1},\zeta_{i+1})\big)}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{st}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)\,,

(3.18)

where as usual we have that the sums are taken modulo
N+1
. By the general theory of large deviations for Markov chains, see for example
[
8
]
, we have that (
3.18
) satisfies a LDP with rate functional
\mathcal{I}^{2}
on
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{st}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)
that is defined as follows. Let
\mathcal{C}^{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{st}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)
and recall that
C^{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(A\times B)
is its one marginal (
3.12
); we have then
[
8
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{2}(\mathcal{C}^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]\log\frac{\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]}{\mathcal{C}^{1}\left[(a,b)\right]\mathfrak{S}_{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}}\,,

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]\log\frac{\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]}{\mathcal{C}^{1}\left[(a,b)\right]M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}}+\log\lambda\,,

(3.20)

where the second inequality follows since
\mathcal{C}^{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{st}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)
.
We can now apply the contraction principle obtaining for
\nu^{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{stat}(A^{2})

I^{2}(\nu^{2})=\inf_{\{\sum_{b,b^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]=\nu^{2}(a,a^{\prime})\,,\forall a,a^{\prime}\}}\mathcal{I}^{2}(\mathcal{C}^{2})\,,

(3.21)

and this finishes the proof for the algebraic case. The proof of (
3.15
) can also be obtained following classic arguments for Markov measures.
∎

Large deviations principles for lower order empirical measures can be obtained by contraction, LDP for higher order empirical measures can be obtained following the same stategies used above and using the classic arguments for Markov measures (see, for example,
[
8
]
) on the enlarged state space and then applying the contraction principle.

In the general case it is difficult to get a more explicit form of the rate functional with respect to (
3.13
). We discuss, however, some special cases.

3.3.
Examples

3.3.1.
A general form

The critical condition in the minimization (
3.13
) is obtained considering several constraints that the measure
C^{2}
has to satisfy. The first collection of constraints is given by (
3.14
), while the other collection of constraints is obtained imposing
C^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{st}\left((A\times B)^{2}\right)
. To each constraint there corresponds a Lagrange multiplier. By a direct computation, the critical conditions are equivalent to

\frac{\mathcal{C}^{2}\left[(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right]}{\mathcal{C}^{1}\left[(a,b)\right]}=k^{-1}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})\frac{\gamma_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}}{\gamma_{a,b}}\,,

(3.22)

where the positive matrix
p
encodes the set of Lagrange multipliers for the constraints (
3.14
), while the numbers
\gamma
and the constant
k
are the Lagrange multipliers for the second group of constraints. Since on the left-hand side we have a stochastic matrix, we obtain that the minimizer is obtained when
\left(\gamma_{a,b}\right)_{a\in A,b\in B}
is the maximal eigenvector of the positive
(A\times B)\times(A\times B)
matrix
\left(M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})\right)_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}^{b,b^{\prime}\in B}
and
k
is the corresponding maximal eigenvalue.

Inserting (
3.22
) as the minimizer in (
3.13
) we obtain, using that
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is stationary and has
\nu^{2}
as
A\times A
marginal (that are the constraints (
3.14
)),

I(\nu^{2})=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\nu^{2}(a,a^{\prime})\log p(a,a^{\prime})+\log(\lambda/k)\,.

(3.23)

It can be shown that in (
3.22
) and (
3.23
) we can restrict to matrices
p(a,a^{\prime})
that are stochastic. Similar considerations can also be applied for the spatial empirical measure.

3.3.2.
Parallel eigenvectors

We consider the special case when

M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}=m(a)S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\frac{\phi(b)}{\phi(b^{\prime})}

(3.24)

where
(S^{(a)})_{a\in A}
is a collection of
B\times B
stochastic matrices,
(m(a))_{a\in A}
is a collection of positive numbers and
(\phi(b))_{b\in B}
is a positive vector. This corresponds to a family of positive matrices
M^{(a)}
, each having the same maximal eigenvector
\ket{\phi}
with corresponding maximal eigenvalue
m(a)
. Indeed, Lemma
A.3
implies that all the positive matrices having
\ket{\phi}
as a maximal eigenvector are of the form (
3.24
) for some stochastic matrix
S^{(a)}
. We have the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

For matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
of the form (
3.24
) we have that the maximal eigenvalue and eigenvector
k
,
\ket{\gamma}
of the
(A\times B)\times(A\times B)
matrix
\left(M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})\right)_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}^{b,b^{\prime}\in B}
are related to
\mu
,
\ket{\psi}
those of the
A\times A
matrix
\left(p(a,a^{\prime})m(a^{\prime})\right)_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}
by the relations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}k=\mu\,,\\
\gamma(a,b)=\psi(a)m(a)\phi(b)\,.\end{array}\right.

(3.25)

Moreover, we have that the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda
of the
B\times B
matrix
M
is
\sum_{a}m(a)
with corresponding eigenvector
\ket{\phi}
.

Proof.

Since a positive eigenvector is necessarily the one associated with the maximal eigenvalue, by a direct computation we have

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}M^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})\gamma(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

(3.26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}m(a)S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}\frac{\phi(b)}{\phi(b^{\prime})}p(a,a^{\prime})m(a^{\prime})\psi(a^{\prime})\phi(b^{\prime})

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\phi(b)m(a)\sum_{a^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})m(a^{\prime})\psi(a^{\prime})=\mu\gamma(a,b)\,.

(3.28)

The last statement follows also by the direct computation

\displaystyle\sum_{b^{\prime}}M_{b,b^{\prime}}\phi(b^{\prime})=\phi(b)\sum_{a}m(a)\sum_{b^{\prime}}S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}=\left(\sum_{a}m(a)\right)\phi(b)\,.

∎

We can now deduce the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

When the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
have all the same Perron eigenvector, then the large deviations
rate functional
I^{2}
in (
3.13
) coincides with the large deviations rate functional
for the pair empirical measure of a sequence of i.i.d. random variables taking values on
A
and having probability distribution
\mathbb{P}(a)=\frac{m(a)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}m(a^{\prime})}
,
a\in A
; i.e. we have

I(\nu^{2})=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\nu^{2}(a,a^{\prime})\log\frac{\nu^{2}(a,a^{\prime})}{\nu^{1}(a)\mathbb{P}(a^{\prime})}\,.

(3.29)

Proof.

Inserting the eigenvalues obtained in the previous lemma into the relation (
3.22
) for the minimizer we get

\mathcal{C}^{2}((a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))=\mathcal{C}^{1}(a,b)k^{-1}S^{(a)}_{b,b^{\prime}}p(a,a^{\prime})\frac{\psi(a^{\prime})m(a^{\prime})}{\psi(a)}\,,

and we can determine
p
satisfying (
3.14
) just summing over
(b,b^{\prime})\in B^{2}
the above equality getting

p(a,a^{\prime})=\frac{k\nu^{(2)}(a,a^{\prime})\psi(a)}{\nu^{(1)}(a)\psi(a^{\prime})m(a^{\prime})}\,.

(3.30)

Inserting this value in (
3.23
), recalling that
\nu^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{st}\left(A^{2}\right)
and the value of
\lambda
obtained in Lemma
3.5
, by a direct computation and using Lemma
3.5
we obtain (
3.29
),
which is the large deviations rate functional for the pair empirical measure for a product measure on
A
, assigning probability
\mathbb{P}(a)
to each element
a\in A
(see for example
[
8
]
).
∎

As in the general case, also in this case the rate functional for the empirical measures of order different from 2 and for the spatial empirical measures can be obtained by the same approach.

3.3.3.
Stochastic matrices

A special case of the situation discussed in the previous subsection is when all the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are stochastic. In this case all the Perron eigenvalues are equal to one and the distribution
\mathbb{P}(a)=\frac{1}{|A|}
is the uniform one. In this case, the results can also be obtained directly as follows.
The first observation is that, when all the matrices
(M^{(a)})_{a\in A}
are stochastic, the measure
\mu_{N}^{1,1}
associated with the vectors
\ket{x},\ket{y}
having all the coordinates identically equal to one, is the uniform measure on
A^{N}
. This follows by a direct check.

Let us call
|\ket{x}|_{M}=\sup_{a}x(a)
and
|\ket{x}|_{m}=\inf_{a}x(a)
that are both finite and strictly positive, we use a similar notation for
y
too. We have

|x|_{m}|y|_{m}\mu_{N}^{\underline{1},\underline{1}}\leq\mu_{N}^{x,y}\leq|x|_{M}|y|_{M}\mu_{N}^{\underline{1},\underline{1}}\,,

and from Lemma
A.4
we deduce

I^{k}(\nu^{k})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{a_{1}^{k}\in A^{k}}\nu^{k}(a_{1}^{k})\left(\log\nu^{k}(a_{1}^{k})-k\log|A|\right)&\textrm{if}\ \nu^{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{1}_{st}(A^{k})\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(3.31)

since the rate functional (
3.31
) is exactly the rate functionals associated with a product of uniform measures.
Similar considerations can also be done for the spatial empirical measures.

4.
The infinite case

For Markov measures in the finite case an irreducibility condition is sufficient to obtain a LDP both for the algebraic and spatial empirical measures. This is not the case for a countable infinite alphabet, where suitable conditions have to be satisfied, see
[
16
]
. In the example we consider only LDP for spatial empirical measures can be directly obtained, and we therefore restrict to that case. We apply the general results concerning eigenvalues and eigenvectors discussed in the finite case; a proof of the general statements in the infinite case is more subtle, but for our specific example we proceed by direct computation.

We consider now an example of a rational model with an infinite alphabet
B
; this is the boundary driven TASEP discussed in Section
2.5
. In particular, we consider a special representation of the matrices satisfying the conditions (
2.16
), which is valid when the parameters vary in the region
\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}{\alpha\beta}<1
, see for example
[

]
. This corresponds to the following matrices.

Consider the sets
A=\{0,1\}
,

M^{(0)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\

\vdots&\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\

(4.1)

These matrices have the following left and right eigenvectors:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bra{y}M^{(0)}=\frac{\bra{y}}{\alpha}\\

with:

\bra{y}=\widehat{k}\left[1,\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha},\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{2},\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{3},...\right]

(4.2)

The matrix

is the infinite matrix with all the elements on the main diagonal equal to

M_{b,b^{\prime}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\delta_{b,b^{\prime}}+\delta_{b,b^{\prime}+1}+\delta_{b,b^{\prime}-1}\,,&b\geq 1\,,\\
2\delta_{b,b^{\prime}}+\delta_{b,b^{\prime}+1}\,,&b=0\,.\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

The matrix

coincides with the matrix discussed in Section
A.3
with
\alpha=2
,

, the Perron eigenvalue is therefore

and the corresponding positive eigenvector is:

\ket{e}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
2\\
\vdots\\
n\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}

(4.5)

Exponential tightness

To show exponential tightness we need to construct a family of compact sets
\left(\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}
such that

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log P^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}\left((\widehat{z}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{B},\widehat{\Pi}^{I})\in\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\right)<-\alpha\,.

This statement allows to extend the following upper bound from compact set to closed ones. This is a technical issue that we do not discuss in detail and that can be treated considering
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{z}\times\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{B}\times\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{I}
and following the arguments in
[
7
]
Lemma 5.1.7 and
[
23
]
Section 4 of Chapter 10.

Lower bound

Consider a triple
(z,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})
that satisfies the conditions A, B, C and D and such that
\Pi^{B}(x)=m(x)\gamma^{B}(x)
and
\Pi^{I}(x)=(1-m(x))\gamma^{I}(x)
with
\gamma^{B},\gamma^{I}
that are strictly positive and Lipschitz continuous. Then, by Proposition
4.5
, it follows that, with respect to the measures
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}
the triple
\left(\widehat{z}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}_{N}^{B},\widehat{\Pi}_{N}^{I}\right)
converges in probability to the triple
(z,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})
and, moreover, by the formula (
4.26
) we have

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}h\left(\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}\Big|\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}\right)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}}\left[\log\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}}\right]=\mathcal{I}^{*}\Big(z,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I}\Big)\,.

(4.31)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
be the functional equal to
\mathcal{I}^{*}
for measures
\Pi^{B}=m\nu^{B}
and
\Pi^{I}=(1-m)\nu^{I}
with
\nu^{I},\nu^{B}
that are strictly positive and Lipschitz continuous and equal to
+\infty
otherwise.
By Theorem 3.4 in
[
25
]
we obtain a LD lower bound with rate functional that is
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{*}_{\textrm{lse}}
, the lower semicontinuous envelope of
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
. The lower bound is then obtained by showing that
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{*}_{\textrm{lse}}=\mathcal{I}^{*}
.

Upper bound

The upper bound is obtained by the classic argument; by the exponential tightness we can consider compact subsets
\mathcal{K}
. We have the following estimates. Let
\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}
be positive
x
dependent probability measures, then we have the following classic steps

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}\left((\widehat{z}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{B}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{I}_{N})\in\mathcal{K}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}}\left[\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\gamma^{I},\gamma^{B}}_{N}}{1\mskip-5.0mu{\rm I}}\left((\widehat{z}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{B}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{I}_{N})\in\mathcal{K}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\inf_{\left\{(\pi,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}\sum_{a,b}\int_{[0,1]}\left[\Pi^{I}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{I}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{I}_{a,b}}+\Pi^{B}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{B}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{B}_{a,b}}\right]\right\}\,.

Since the above estimate is true for any positive
\gamma^{B},\gamma^{I}
we can optimize getting

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{S},*}_{N}\left((\widehat{z}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{B}_{N},\widehat{\Pi}^{I}_{N})\in\mathcal{K}\right)\leq

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\{\gamma^{B},\gamma^{I}\right\}}\inf_{\left\{(\pi,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}\sum_{a,b}\int_{[0,1]}\left[\Pi^{I}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{I}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{I}_{a,b}}+\Pi^{B}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{B}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{B}_{a,b}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\inf_{\left\{(\pi,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}\sup_{\left\{\gamma^{B},\gamma^{I}\right\}}\sum_{a,b}\int_{[0,1]}\left[\Pi^{I}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{I}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{I}_{a,b}}+\Pi^{B}(a,b;dx)\log\frac{\gamma^{B}_{a,b}(x)}{\mu^{B}_{a,b}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\inf_{\left\{(\pi,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I})\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\Big(z,\Pi^{B},\Pi^{I}\Big)\,,

and this is the LD upper bound.
In the first inequality we optimized over
\gamma^{B}\gamma^{I}
; in the second equality
we used a minmax lemma (see for example lemmas 3.2 and 3.3 in Appendix 2 of
[
23
]
) to exchange infimum and supremum, and the last equality follows from a direct computation by using Lagrange multipliers.

4.2.
The contraction

By contraction we can deduce the LD rate functional for the empirical measure of the TASEP configuration (

Since

Appendix A
Some mathematical tools

We recall here some classic result and deduce some facts that we use in the paper.

A.1.
Finite Perron-Frobenius Theorem

Here we briefly recall the classic Perron Frobenius Theorem with also some auxiliary lemmas. We refer to
[
24
]
for a complete discussion. The results hold true for any finite alphabeth.

Theorem A.1

(Perron-Frobenius)
.

Consider a finite
B\times B
irreducible and aperiodic non-negative matrix
M
, then there exists a positive maximal eigenvalue
\lambda>0
such that all the remaining eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are such that
|\lambda_{i}|<\lambda
. The unique eigenvector
\ket{e}=(e(b))_{b\in B}
corresponding to the maximal eigenvalue can be fixed in such a way that
e(b)>0
for any
b\in B
.

Remark A.2
.

When the matrix
M
is irreducible but not aperiodic, Theorem
A.1
has to be modified and there exists still a maximal real eigenvalue
\lambda
and a corresponding strictly positive eigenvector
\ket{e}
; however, for the other eigenvalues we do not have a strict inequality, but instead
|\lambda_{i}|\leq\lambda
. If the period of the irreducible chain is
p
then there are exactly
p
eigenvalues of maximal modulus
\lambda
. We refer to
[
24
]
for more details.

As a consequence of the Perron Frobenius Theorem we have the following generalized Doob transform.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
M
be a non-negative irreducible and aperiodic matrix and let
\lambda
,
\ket{e}
be the corresponding maximal eigenvalue and positive right eigenvector; then the matrix

S_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}e(b)^{-1}M_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})\,\qquad b,b^{\prime}\in B\,,

(A.1)

is a stochastic matrix and we have

S^{k}_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k}}e(b)^{-1}\left(M^{k}\right)_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})\,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},.

(A.2)

If
M
is irreducible and aperiodic, the matrix
S
is also irreducible and aperiodic.

Proof.

The proof of (
A.1
) follows by a direct computation. We need just to check that the sum along each row is one

\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}S_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}e(b)^{-1}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}M_{b,b^{\prime}}e(b^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\lambda}e(b)^{-1}\lambda e(b)=1\,.

Formula (
A.2
) follows directly by the representation (
3.7
). Irreducibility and aperiodicity follow directly.
∎

Notice that in formula (
A.2
) the upper label
k
of the matrices
S,M
is an integer number, not an element of
A
, and it is denoting the
k

A.2.
Other tools

A simple but powerful result that we use is the following.

Lemma A.4
.

Suppose that we have two sequences of probability measures
\mu_{N}
and
\mu_{N}^{\prime}
such that there exist two sequences of positive constants
k_{N},K_{N}
such that

0\leq\liminf_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log k_{N}\leq\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log K_{N}\leq 0\,,

(A.3)

with
k_{N}\mu_{N}^{\prime}\leq\mu_{N}\leq K_{N}\mu_{N}^{\prime}
. If
\mu_{N}^{\prime}
satisfies a large deviations principle then also
\mu_{N}
satisfies a large deviations principle with the same rate.

Proof.

The proof follows from a direct verification of the validity of the lower and upper bounds.
∎

A.3.
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors for a class of infinite matrices

We consider the eigenvalue problem for the infinite-dimensional matrices, having rows and columns labeled by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, of the form:

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta_{1}&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&\cdots\\
\beta_{2}&\alpha&\beta_{1}&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\
0&\beta_{2}&\alpha&\beta_{1}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&\beta_{2}&\alpha&\beta_{1}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{bmatrix}

(A.4)

that is

A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\beta_{1}\delta_{i,j+1}\\
\alpha\delta_{i,j}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ i,j=0,1,2,...\\
\beta_{2}\delta_{i-1,j}\end{cases}

with
\alpha,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
. The spectral theory in the case of infinite matrices is more complex and we illustrate just a few computations. We refer, for example, to
[
24
]
for more details.

The Perron value for the matrix (
A.4
) is defined as

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sqrt[n]{\left(A^{n}\right)_{i,j}}\,,

(A.5)

and the limit does not depend on
i,j
,
[
24
]
. These matrices have a continuum of positive eigenvalues larger than the Perron value, for which the corresponding eigenvectors are positive. We have

Lemma A.5
.

The Perron value of the matrix (
A.4
) is given by
\alpha+2\sqrt{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}
and the corresponding column eigenvector is given by

\ket{e}=\left((n+1)\left(\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}}\right)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Proof.

We give only an outline of the argument, since the computation is equivalent to the classic combinatorial computation of the partition function based on bicoloured Motzkin and Dyck paths
[
30
]
.

We consider a discrete time random walk on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, that starts at zero and performs jumps of
0
or
\pm 1
. We assign the weight
\beta_{1}
to jumps of
+1
, the weight
\beta_{2}
to jumps of
-1
and the weight
\alpha
to jumps of
0
. The weights of paths of even length that start and end at
0
is given by

\displaystyle(A^{2n})_{0,0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{C}_{n-k}\binom{2n}{2k}\alpha^{2k}\beta_{1}^{n-k}\beta_{2}^{n-k}

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is the
n
-th Catalan number. By Stirling formula and classic asymptotic estimates, we deduce the Perron value. The eigenvector can be verified by a direct computation.
∎

In the special case with
\alpha=2
,
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=1
the Perron eigenvalue is
\lambda=4
and the eigenvector associated is
\ket{e}=(n+1)_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
