Effect of silver diamine fluoride and potassium iodide pretreatment on microleakage, penetration depth, and color changes of fissure sealants: an in vitro study
Authors: Hamide Cömert, Ecem Elif Çege
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of SDF- and SDF + KI-based pretreatment protocols, with or without NaOCl surface preparation, on microleakage, discoloration, and fissure sealant penetration depth in permanent teeth.MethodsSixty extracted mandibular permanent molars with non-cavitated initial enamel lesions (ICDAS score 1) were randomly allocated into six groups (n = 10 per group). According to the experimental design, SDF, SDF + KI, NaOCl surface preparation, and orthophosphoric acid etching protocols were applied before fissure sealant placement. Color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer according to the CIE Lab* system at baseline (T0) and after all procedures (T1). Microleakage and penetration depth were evaluated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Statistical analyses were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05).ResultsSignificant differences were observed among the groups for color change (ΔE) and lightness change (ΔL) (p < 0.001). SDF-treated groups showed significantly higher color change and greater lightness reduction compared with the control group. The mean ΔE values were 6.38 ± 5.42 in the control group, 15.78 ± 2.73 in the SDF group, and 12.60 ± 1.68 in the SDF + KI group. Most ΔE values exceeded the perceptibility (PT = 1.2) and acceptability (AT = 2.7) thresholds. NaOCl application following SDF resulted in significantly higher ΔL values compared with SDF alone. No statistically significant differences were observed among groups for microleakage or penetration depth (p > 0.05).ConclusionsSDF and SDF + KI pretreatments did not result in statistically significant differences in microleakage or penetration depth under the present experimental conditions, although SDF was associated with clinically relevant discoloration. KI showed only a limited effect in reducing this discoloration. NaOCl application was associated with less unfavorable lightness change in some comparisons; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Introduction

Dental caries is a dynamic process that progresses when the balance between the demineralization and remineralization phases shifts in favor of demineralization and may clinically begin with non-cavitated initial enamel lesions (white spot lesions) [1]. Pits and fissures are among the areas most susceptible to caries development in both primary and permanent teeth due to their morphology, which predisposes them to plaque and food retention. Fissure sealants aim to prevent caries development in these regions by forming a physical barrier [2, 3]. However, clinical success depends on factors such as surface preparation, adhesion quality, marginal sealing ability, and resistance to microleakage [4–7]. A fissure sealant must exhibit sufficient chemical and mechanical adhesion to the enamel tissue within pits and fissures. Fissure sealants that do not provide adequate adhesion may lead to marginal leakage and consequently allow caries progression [8, 9]. Therefore, contemporary minimally invasive dentistry approaches have increasingly focused on the use of surface pretreatments that not only enhance adhesive performance but also provide antibacterial properties [10, 11]. In this context, there is a growing interest in agents capable of modifying the enamel surface both biologically and mechanically.

Topical application of silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a non-invasive treatment that arrests active carious lesions and prevents the development of new caries [12]. A 38% SDF solution has also been used for purposes such as preventing pit and fissure caries, preventing the development of secondary caries, and promoting the remineralization of hypomineralized teeth [13]. Many studies have demonstrated that SDF is a non-invasive, inexpensive, and effective method for preventing pit and fissure caries when compared with other materials [14–16]. The mechanism of action of SDF is based on its reaction with hydroxyapatite crystals to promote the formation of fluorapatite, its inhibition of dentin matrix metalloproteinases, and the destructive effect of silver ions on bacterial cell walls and DNA replication. Through this multifaceted mechanism of action, SDF not only arrests caries progression but also increases the biological resistance of dental tissues [14, 15, 17]. However, the most important disadvantage of SDF is the dark discoloration of carious tissue [17]. The discoloration associated with SDF is thought to be related to the formation of silver phosphate (Ag₃PO₄) [18]. To reduce SDF-related discoloration, potassium iodide (KI) salt can be applied topically after the procedure [19–21]. The iodide ions in KI may react with free silver ions, thereby reducing Ag₃PO₄ formation and the resulting dark discoloration [20, 21].

Researchers have revisited the use of restorative materials such as fissure sealants that can mask SDF-treated tooth surfaces while simultaneously preventing plaque accumulation, due to the aesthetic disadvantage of SDF. In this context, it has been suggested that pretreating pits and fissures with SDF and subsequently applying fissure sealants may provide a significant benefit in caries prevention by combining the antimicrobial and remineralizing effects of SDF with the physical sealing property of the sealant. For this reason, several studies have investigated the combined use of SDF and fissure sealants [22, 23]. In an in vitro study conducted by Pérez-Hernández et al., molar teeth treated with SDF and molar teeth in the control group were restored using the same fissure sealant material, and no difference was observed between the groups in terms of the retention properties of the fissure sealant. In the same study, fissure sealant application was recommended to improve aesthetics in teeth treated with SDF. However, that study was conducted on non-carious teeth [22].

Fissure sealants that exhibit better adhesion tend to show lower microleakage values and therefore better clinical performance. In an in vitro study evaluating the microleakage of fissure sealants applied after SDF pretreatment, it was reported that pretreatment with SDF or nano-silver fluoride prior to fissure sealant application did not affect the microleakage of the sealant [23]. Studies evaluating the performance of fissure sealants on teeth treated with potassium iodide (KI) to reduce SDF-related discoloration are limited. Most studies on this topic have focused on bond strength to dentin. Koizumi et al. reported that SDF and KI may reduce dentin bond strength [24], whereas Soeno et al. demonstrated that this effect may be partially reversed with NaOCl application [25]. Although these findings are derived from dentin studies, they may provide indirect insight into the potential effects of SDF and NaOCl on surface interactions, including enamel, where evidence remains limited. The combined use of the antimicrobial and remineralizing effects of SDF with the physical sealing ability of fissure sealants may theoretically enhance caries control; however, the effects of SDF-related discoloration and changes in surface chemistry following SDF/KI application on the performance of fissure sealants remain unclear. Moreover, the available literature has largely evaluated these materials separately, and no studies have evaluated fissure sealant performance on enamel surfaces following combined SDF, KI, and NaOCl pretreatment. Therefore, in this in vitro study, the effects of SDF and SDF + KI pretreatments and surface preparation with NaOCl on the microleakage, color change, and penetration depth of fissure sealants in permanent molars were investigated.

The research question of this study was whether these pretreatment protocols affect the optical properties and sealing performance of fissure sealants placed on permanent molars. The null hypothesis was that SDF and SDF + KI pretreatment, with or without NaOCl deproteinization, would not significantly affect microleakage, penetration depth, color change (ΔE), or lightness change (ΔL) after fissure sealant placement.

Material and methods

In this in vitro study, mandibular permanent molar teeth (n = 60) indicated for extraction as part of routine treatment planning and without any structural anomalies other than caries were used. After extraction, the remaining soft tissue, plaque, and other debris on the teeth were cleaned using a polishing brush and subsequently rinsed with water. For disinfection purposes, all teeth were stored in a 0.1% thymol solution for 48 h and were then kept in distilled water in sterile containers until use. Since it is known that the solutions in which extracted teeth are stored and the storage duration may have adverse effects on bond strength, the extracted teeth were used within a maximum period of 3 months [26].

In order to detect existing enamel lesions in the teeth used in the study, the ICDAS-II (International Caries Detection and Assessment System) criteria were used. Extracted teeth presenting non-cavitated initial enamel lesions with a caries score of 1 according to the ICDAS II classification, which are visible only after air drying, were included in the study [22]. Teeth without carious lesions, teeth with an ICDAS score of 2–6, those presenting developmental anomalies, including fluorosis and enamel hypoplasia, or specimens damaged during extraction were excluded from the study.

To confirm the ICDAS score and the presence of fractures or structural defects, the teeth were examined under a stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) at ×20 magnification. The lesions were visually evaluated by two previously calibrated researchers under illuminated conditions and after air drying. Calibration was performed through two evaluations on 10 pilot teeth, and interobserver agreement was calculated using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.92).

The teeth were extracted for reasons unrelated to this study. Before inclusion in the study, all patients and/or their legal guardians were informed about the study protocol, and written informed consent was obtained in accordance with ethical principles. All procedures involving human-derived samples were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The overall experimental workflow of the study is shown in Fig. 1. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Ethics Committee on 30.12.2024 with decision number 2024/294.

Sample size

In this study, the sample size was calculated using the G*Power-3.1.9.2 software. At a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), the standardized effect size was determined as 0.6466 based on a similar study [27], and the minimum sample size was calculated as 57 with a statistical power of (1–β) = 0.95. In order to compensate for potential sample loss, a total of 60 teeth were included in the study.

Determination of the study groups

At the beginning of the study, the teeth were randomly allocated into six groups (Table 1). Randomization was performed using the website www.randomizer.org to ensure an unbiased and reproducible method. During this process, each tooth was assigned a number from 1 to 60, and these numbers were then randomly distributed by the system into groups of 10 teeth each (n = 10).

SDF application

The SDF solution (SDI Riva Star–Step 1, SDI Dental Ltd., Australia) was applied to the occlusal surfaces of the molar teeth in groups 2, 3, 5, and 6 in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Using microbrushes, one drop of SDF solution was used for the application to five specimens. The tip of the microbrush was brought into contact with the specimen surfaces with circular movements for 2 min, and after waiting for approximately 1 min in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, the specimens were dried using an air–water spray.

KI application

Immediately after the SDF application, the KI solution (SDI Riva Star–Step 2, SDI Dental Ltd., Australia) was applied to the occlusal surfaces of the molar teeth in groups 3 and 6 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After a clean microbrush was completely immersed in the solution, the application was performed on five specimens using two drops of KI solution. The KI solution was applied with circular movements until the creamy precipitate formed on the specimen surfaces became clear or for a maximum of 60 s, after which the specimens were dried with an air–water spray.

After completion of the SDF and KI applications, the teeth were stored in deionized water at 37 °C ± 1 °C for 7 days in order to allow the discoloration process of the SDF material to be completed and to simulate the effects of the oral environment to which it may be exposed. The deionized water was renewed every other day.

Surface preparation

At the end of the 7-day period, a 5.25% NaOCl solution (Promida, Turkey) was actively rubbed onto the occlusal surface of the specimens in groups 4, 5, and 6 for 60 s using a microbrush [10]. In the control groups (groups 1, 2, and 3), distilled water was applied with a microbrush.

Fissure sealant application

After this period, a pit and fissure sealant (Fissurit FX®, VOCO, Germany) was applied to all teeth according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, orthophosphoric acid (Scotchbond™ 35%, 3 M ESPE) was applied to the entire occlusal enamel surfaces of the teeth for 15 s, extending beyond the expected margins of the fissure sealant. After the acid was rinsed and dried, the fissure sealant was placed into the pits and fissures. The fissure sealant was polymerized by exposure to light at a wavelength of 450–470 nm and an intensity of 1100 mW/cm² for 20 s (Demi Plus™, KaVo-Kerr). The light intensity was verified daily before measurements using a radiometer (Bluephase® Meter II, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein).

Thermocycling procedure

The prepared specimens were placed in a thermocycling device (THE-1100, SD Mechatronik GmbH, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany) to simulate temperature changes in the oral environment. Thermocycling was applied between 5 °C and 55 °C with a dwell time of 60 s and a transfer time of 3 s for 2000 cycles [22]. Based on the widely accepted estimation that 10,000 cycles correspond to approximately one year of in vivo functioning [28], this protocol represents short-term thermal aging conditions. It was selected to simulate the early phase following fissure sealant application, during which initial adaptation and marginal integrity, as key factors influencing long-term clinical performance, are established.

Data collection

Color measurement

In order to calculate the color and brightness changes caused by the SDF, SDF + KI, and subsequently applied fissure sealant materials used in the study, color measurements were performed using the CIE L*a*b* color system with a spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade Advance, VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany). Color measurements were carried out at two time points. The T0 measurement was performed to determine the baseline color values of the specimens before any surface procedure was applied. The T1 measurement was performed after completion of the SDF/SDF + KI application, the 7-day storage period, surface preparation with NaOCl in the relevant groups, acid etching, fissure sealant application, and thermocycling procedures. Color change (ΔE) and lightness change (ΔL) were calculated using the difference between the T1 and T0 measurements. Measurements were obtained only from the treated surface, namely the occlusal enamel surface on which the fissure sealant was applied; therefore, the obtained ΔE and ΔL values were evaluated to reflect the overall optical change of the enamel–fissure sealant complex as clinically observed. Color measurements were performed on a white background after drying the specimens, under D65 standard illumination, with the probe tip positioned perpendicular (90°) to the specimen surface. Prior to the measurements, calibration of the spectrophotometer was performed and repeated after every 10 teeth. All measurements were performed by the same operator to eliminate inter-operator variability. Three consecutive measurements were performed at each time point (T0 and T1), and the average values were used for analysis to minimize measurement error and improve repeatability. For the clinical interpretation of color change, perceptibility and acceptability thresholds reported in the literature were taken into account. In the present study, ΔE values were interpreted with reference to a perceptibility threshold of 1.2 and an acceptability threshold of 2.7 [29–32].

Color evaluations were recorded in the CIE L*a*b* color space, where L* represents lightness (0–100), a* represents the green–red axis, and b* represents the blue–yellow axis [27, 29]. Microleakage measurement

After the color measurements, the apical foramen and the entire roots of each tooth were covered with two layers of nail varnish to prevent dye penetration. The specimens were then immersed for 24 h in a 0.1% Rhodamine B solution (C.I. Basic Violet 10; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) prepared with distilled water. At the end of the 24-h period, the specimens were removed from the solution and rinsed under running tap water.

The specimens were sectioned buccolingually into two equal halves using a precision cutting device (Struers Secotom 60, Denmark) under water cooling. CLSM imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (CLSM; Zeiss LSM 980, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 543 nm, appropriate for Rhodamine B fluorescence. The sections were examined at 10× magnification, and all images were obtained under standardized imaging conditions. Microleakage and penetration depth were evaluated using a scoring system based on previously defined criteria. For each specimen, three CLSM images obtained from different sections were evaluated, and the highest microleakage score and the lowest penetration score were recorded.

The microleakage scores were evaluated according to the criteria of Pardi et al. [33], which have also been used in previous studies (Fig. 2). The scoring was performed blindly and independently by two previously calibrated researchers. Calibration was carried out by performing two evaluations on 10 pilot CLSM images obtained from randomly selected specimens that were not included in the study, and interobserver agreement was calculated using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.86).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics of the data were presented as mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, and interquartile range (IQR). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons among three or more independent groups that did not show a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. When a statistically significant difference was detected in the Kruskal–Wallis test, Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to determine which groups were responsible for the difference. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses. In addition to p values, effect sizes for the Kruskal–Wallis tests were estimated using epsilon-squared (ε²) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.

Results

To quantitatively evaluate color change, the ΔE value (T1–T0) was calculated. Color change (ΔE) differed significantly among the groups (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001; ε² = 0.345, 95% CI: 0.154–0.635). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that Group 1 had significantly lower ΔE values than Groups 2 and 3 (p < 0.05; Table 2). Most ΔE values exceeded the clinical acceptability threshold (AT = 2.7), particularly in the SDF-treated groups, indicating clinically relevant discoloration.

Discussion

This in vitro study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of SDF- and SDF + KI-based pretreatment protocols, with or without NaOCl surface preparation, on microleakage, discoloration, and fissure sealant penetration depth in permanent teeth. The applied pretreatments significantly affected the aesthetic parameters but did not produce a statistically significant difference in microleakage or penetration depth. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected for color/lightness change but accepted for microleakage and penetration depth.

When the aesthetic findings of the study were evaluated, the ΔE* values exceeded the color perceptibility (PT = 1.2) and acceptability (AT = 2.7) thresholds defined in the CIE Lab* system [29–32], indicating clinically relevant discoloration. Moreover, the markedly high ΔE values observed particularly in the SDF-treated groups suggest clearly unacceptable discoloration. From a clinical perspective, such pronounced color alteration may limit the use of SDF-based pretreatment in aesthetically sensitive situations, even when fissure sealant placement is subsequently performed. This characteristic darkening associated with SDF is attributed to the formation of photosensitive Ag₃PO₄ and silver sulfide (Ag₂S) precipitates on the enamel surface and within dentinal tubules [18, 21]. The application of KI (Group 3), which has been proposed to overcome this aesthetic disadvantage, reacts with free silver ions to form silver iodide (AgI), and in the present study it showed a tendency to reduce SDF-related discoloration to some extent; however, this reduction did not result in a statistically significant difference. This finding is consistent with previous in vitro findings suggesting that KI application cannot completely prevent discoloration [21]. NaOCl application after SDF and SDF + KI pretreatment appeared to influence color and lightness changes. In the NaOCl-treated groups (Groups 4, 5, and 6), no significant difference in ΔE values was observed compared with Group 1, in which only the fissure sealant was applied (p > 0.05). In contrast, markedly higher color changes were observed in the SDF-treated groups (Groups 2 and 3) compared with the control group. In terms of lightness, the group in which NaOCl was applied following SDF treatment (Group 5) exhibited a more limited decrease in L* compared with the group treated only with SDF (Group 2), and the ΔL value was significantly higher, indicating a less unfavorable lightness change. This finding suggests that NaOCl application after SDF treatment may be associated with less unfavorable lightness change; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, as not all comparisons indicated a consistent statistically significant improvement in overall optical outcomes. The mechanism underlying this optical finding remains uncertain. Since no surface chemical analysis was performed in the present study, no definitive explanation can be made regarding the interactions between NaOCl and SDF-related reaction products. Further studies using surface characterization methods are needed to clarify this issue.

The findings of the present study regarding microleakage and penetration were based on CLSM evaluation. CLSM is frequently used to assess fissure sealant performance because it enables high-resolution visualization of dye penetration at the tooth–sealant interface [35]. In the present study, microleakage and penetration depth were evaluated as distinct outcomes. Although both parameters are related to fissure sealant performance, they do not represent the same phenomenon. Penetration depth reflects how far the sealant extends into the fissure system, whereas microleakage reflects dye penetration along the tooth–sealant interface. Therefore, adequate penetration does not necessarily indicate optimal marginal sealing. Accordingly, comparisons with studies focusing primarily on bond strength or adhesion should be made with caution, as these outcomes are related but methodologically and conceptually distinct.

In an in vitro study by Almusaad et al., the effects of SDF and SDF + KI applications on fissure sealant adhesion and microleakage were evaluated, and SDF application did not significantly reduce fissure sealant adhesion [36]. In the same study, KI provided some aesthetic benefit by reducing discoloration; however, it did not result in complete improvement in adhesion, marginal adaptation, or microleakage at the fissure sealant–enamel interface. These findings are generally consistent with the present results, in which SDF, SDF + KI, and NaOCl pretreatments did not significantly affect microleakage or penetration depth. Similarly, Pérez-Hernández et al. reported that fissure sealant retention could be maintained on enamel surfaces treated with SDF, with no statistically significant difference compared with the control group [22].

In the present study, fissure sealants were applied in some groups after NaOCl surface preparation following SDF and SDF + KI application. Previous studies have suggested that NaOCl pretreatment before acid etching may enhance the bonding performance of resin-based fissure sealants [10, 37], and favorable effects on microleakage have also been reported [38]. However, under the present experimental conditions, no statistically significant differences were found between the NaOCl-treated and non-NaOCl-treated groups in terms of microleakage or penetration depth. These findings may indicate that any potential benefit of NaOCl pretreatment was limited in the current study design. Further studies are needed before any definitive interpretation can be made.

It is known that discoloration after SDF application begins shortly after the procedure and becomes more stable over time due to the formation of Ag₃PO₄ [11]. Previous studies have shown that color change following SDF and SDF + KI applications may progress over time and continue up to the seventh day [21]. The timing of restorative material application may therefore influence the masking of this aesthetically important effect. Previous studies have also suggested that postponing restorative procedures rather than performing them on the same day as SDF application may help reduce SDF-related discoloration in restorative materials [39]. In the present study, the teeth were stored in deionized water at 37 °C for 7 days before fissure sealant placement to allow the discoloration process to stabilize and to simulate short-term oral environmental exposure [22].

This study has several limitations. One limitation of the present study is that, although the experimental design included multiple variables (SDF, KI, and NaOCl), the data were not analyzed using a factorial statistical model; therefore, potential interaction effects among these variables could not be evaluated. This was primarily due to the non-normal distribution of the data, the relatively small sample size, and the ordinal nature of some outcome measures, which led to the use of a non-parametric analytical approach. Future studies using appropriate factorial statistical models with larger sample sizes may provide further insight into the possible interaction effects among these variables. Another limitation of this study is the scoring approach used for outcome measurements. The use of the highest microleakage score and the lowest penetration score per specimen may have led to overestimation or underestimation of the true values, respectively, as it reflects a worst-case scenario rather than the overall behavior of the material, a method also adopted in previous studies [33–35]. The in vitro design limits the direct generalization of the obtained findings to clinical conditions. In the oral environment, factors such as the presence of saliva, pH fluctuations, biofilm formation, and mechanical wear may lead to different outcomes in terms of both color change and fissure sealant performance. In addition, during the microleakage evaluation in some specimens, it was difficult to distinguish the dark discoloration formed on the enamel surface after SDF pretreatment from the Rhodamine B dye used in the CLSM images. Furthermore, during the examination of the specimens under CLSM, the anatomical structures of the teeth and the fissure morphologies were observed to be highly heterogeneous. The depth, width, and configuration of fissures (e.g., U-, V-, I-, or Y-type fissures) are among the factors that may influence the penetration behavior of fissure sealant materials [40]. The inability to standardize these anatomical variations within the scope of the in vitro study may have contributed to the occurrence of microleakage at the restoration–enamel interface in some specimens, despite adequate penetration of the fissure sealant into the enamel tissue. Therefore, it is suggested that future studies incorporating subgroup analyses based on fissure morphology may contribute to a more detailed interpretation of penetration depth and microleakage findings.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings suggest that fissure sealant application after SDF or SDF + KI pretreatment may be feasible without a clear adverse effect on microleakage or penetration depth. However, the clinically relevant discoloration associated with SDF should be taken into account during treatment planning. Although KI and NaOCl may offer some optical advantage, their benefits in reducing discoloration appear limited under the conditions of the present study.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, SDF- and SDF + KI-based pretreatment protocols, with or without NaOCl deproteinization, did not result in statistically significant differences in microleakage or penetration depth under the present experimental conditions. SDF was associated with clinically relevant discoloration, whereas KI showed only a limited masking effect. NaOCl appeared to influence optical outcomes, particularly lightness change; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.
