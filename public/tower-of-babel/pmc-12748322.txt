Machine learning-Based integration develops a metastasis-Related rhythmic gene signature for improving outcomes in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Zhiyuan Cheng, Chuting Yu, Bin Quan, Kena Zhou, Chongxin He, Shuangjun Shen, Xiaowan Wu, Guo Yu, Leheng Liu, Zihao Guo, Haoran Sun, Rong Wan, Kai Zhao, Zhanjun Lu, Weiliang Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCircadian dysregulation has been shown to be associated with the progression of pancreatic cancer (PC). However, the circadian machinery comprises numerous circadian rhythm-related genes (CRRGs), whose roles in tumor biology remain unclear.MethodsA metastasis-related rhythmic gene signature (MRRGS) was established based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified from public PC datasets. A risk model was developed, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to explore functional differences between low-risk and high-risk groups. The impact of MRRGS was further assessed through mutation and copy number variation (CNV) analysis. The correlation between modeling genes and immune infiltration scores was examined using the ESTIMATE, xCell, and ssGSEA algorithms. Finally, potential therapeutic drugs were tested in various PC patient populations.ResultsFive CRRGs, including S100A10, CD47, UBXN1, ERBB3, and ST3GAL1, were identified and validated for the development of the risk model. GSEA analysis revealed that high-risk PC patients were associated with ribosome-related pathways and MHC class II pathways. Mutation and CNV analyses indicated that high-risk patients exhibited SMAD4 mutations and a higher frequency of CNVs, while low-risk patients displayed significant alterations in genes such as GSTM1, CFHR3, and RNF43. Furthermore, results from the ESTIMATE, xCell, and ssGSEA analyses demonstrated a notable negative correlation between the ERBB3 and S100A10 genes and immune infiltration-related genes. Based on drug screening outcomes, Sepatronium bromide is recommended for drug trials in high-risk populations, while SB505124 is suggested for low-risk populations.ConclusionsThe MRRGS constructed from S100A10, CD47, UBXN1, ERBB3, and ST3GAL1 may serve as predictors of prognosis and drug sensitivity for PC patients.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a prevalent digestive malignancy and one of the most lethal solid tumors. PC ranks as the seventh most common cause of cancer-related death, with increasing mortality rates for both men and women worldwide. It is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death in Western countries in the coming decades. Notably, the incidence and mortality rates of PC are four to five times higher in countries with a higher Human Development Index (HDI), with the highest incidence rates observed in Europe, Northern America, and Australia/New Zealand. Additionally, these rates are rapidly increasing in developing countries [1], suggesting that changes in lifestyle and environment may significantly influence the development of this highly lethal disease.

The poor prognosis associated with PC is primarily due to its tendency for early local invasion and metastasis, compounded by factors such as resistance to chemotherapy and the absence of effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Most cases of PC are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 11% in the United States, according to the latest statistics. Only 10–15% of patients diagnosed with PC are eligible for surgical resection, while 85–90% with locally advanced or metastatic disease; nearly 60% of patients present with metastatic disease and a poor performance status, which precludes standard surgical intervention [2]. Therefore, identifying the potential drivers of metastasis or recurrence may facilitate the development of new management strategies aimed at altering the disease’s trajectory.

Over the past few decades, the role of the circadian clock has become increasingly implicated in human health and disease [3]. Circadian misalignments resulting from genetic, behavioral, and metabolic changes have been closely linked to tumor initiation and metastasis. In 2007, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified ‘shift work that involves circadian disruption’ as potentially carcinogenic to humans (Group 2 A) [4]. Recent research suggests that light at night (LAN), a known circadian disruptor, may be a risk factor for PC, based on an epidemiological study involving 464,371 participants from the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study [5]. We have consistently focused on the physiological and pathological associations between the circadian clock and the pancreas, which is a well-characterized peripheral clock that harbors self-autonomous time-keeping systems regulating pancreatic biological processes at both the organ and cellular levels [6]. Our previous studies have shown that the local clock is a key determinant in regulating pancreatic fibrogenesis and exocrine function. Moreover, disruption of the pancreatic clock, specifically through the suppression of the core circadian gene Bmal1, promotes PC growth and chemoresistance [7]. However, significant perplexity remains regarding the functioning of the hierarchical timing system in PC progression and metastasis. It is known that the molecular clockwork comprises not only core clock genes but also hundreds of clock-controlled genes, both of which express and function in a rhythmic pattern. Nevertheless, the roles of these genes in tumor biology are not fully understood. In this study, we aimed to apply metastasis-related rhythmic genes (MRRGs) to develop a risk stratification signature for metastasis in PC patients from both public and in-house cohorts, and to investigate their association with tumor biological behavior, immune function, and therapeutic efficacy.

Methods

Data collection and processing

The high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) PC dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) PACA-AU dataset (https://dcc.icgc.org/projects/PACA-AU). The TCGA cohort comprises 147 PC patients with comprehensive clinical information, including 20 cases of localized tumors and 127 cases of metastatic tumors, categorized according to the staging of tumor metastasis. The ICGC PACA-AU cohort includes 1 case of localized tumor and 55 cases of metastatic tumors.

Using the ‘normalizeBetweenArrays’ function from the limma package in R software, we normalized and subsequently combined the data from the two datasets. A total of 5,113 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the localized and metastatic cases, adhering to the screening criteria of a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log(2) fold change (FC)| > 1.

The generation of the metastasis-related rhythmic gene signature (MRRGS) and the circadian rhythm-related metastatic signature (CRRMS) from machine learning-based integrative approaches

Genes associated with circadian rhythm were obtained from six gene sets available in the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and were compared with DEGs. A total of 133 circadian rhythm-related genes were analyzed using a univariate Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression model, with genes exhibiting a P-value < 0.05 selected for further analysis. These selected genes were subsequently subjected to Lasso regression using the ‘glmnet’ package, with the final set of genes and their respective coefficient values determined based on the lambda.min parameter following stepwise regression.

MRRGS validation

The ‘survminer’ package was used to perform Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis, evaluating the model’s performance in prognostic validation. Furthermore, the ‘rms’ package was utilized to visualize the potential value range of the modeled gene and its impact on the outcome event.

Functional pathway enrichment analysis

Functional pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis via the ‘clusterProfiler’ package. For Reactome pathway analysis, we employed the ‘ReactomePA’ package, while the ‘ggplot2’ package facilitated the visualization of the obtained data.

Mutation and CNV analysis

The R package maftools was utilized to identify significantly mutated genes, copy number variations (CNVs), and to construct a somatic mutation landscape for comparison between the low- and high-CRRMS groups. Subsequently, the somaticInteractions function was employed to detect mutually exclusive or co-occurring genes. The G.score was calculated by summing the squared gene-level GISTIC 2.0 values. Furthermore, genes with a q-value of less than 0.25 were selected for functional analysis.

Immune infiltration analysis

The ESTIMATE package utilizes the ESTIMATE algorithm, the xCell package employs the xCell algorithm, and the GSVA package implements the ssGSEA algorithm. In this study, we compared the immune scores generated by these three algorithms against the modeled genes. To evaluate the correlation between the genes and immune infiltration scores, we employed the Pearson correlation coefficient.

Drug-screening

The R package was employed to predict drug responses in cancer patients. The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, accessible at [https://www.cancerrxgene.org/], served as the training dataset.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated using a systematic approach: First, median drug response values were obtained separately for high-risk and low-risk groups, and these values were log-transformed to normalize their distribution. The GDSC is a publicly available repository that provides critical information on drug sensitivity in cancer cells, along with molecular markers that offer insights into drug response patterns. We utilized the oncoPredict (version 0.2) package to extract the GDSC gene expression profile and the corresponding dataset detailing drug response characteristics. Leveraging the gene expression profile, we predicted potential bioactive compounds that may be suitable for patients.

In the GSE144561 cohort, we analyzed 17 cases of localized PC and 18 cases of metastatic PC, identifying a total of 10,467 DEGs between the two groups. Subsequently, these DEGs were mapped to the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), an established repository of annotated gene sets. This process resulted in the identification of 52 genes, which were classified as CRRGs.

Development and evaluation of a metastasis-related rhythmic gene signature (MRRGS)

To establish a MRRGS for predicting the prognosis of metastatic PC, we conducted a univariate Cox regression analysis. Genes with a P-value of less than 0.05 were subsequently included in LASSO regression and stepwise Cox regression models (Fig. 2B). We identified five CRRGs with the strongest prognostic predictive power: S100A10, CD47, UBXN1, ERBB3, and ST3GAL1, which were selected as the final fitted genes. Following this, we constructed an individualized prediction model to estimate the survival probability of metastatic PC patients based on the expression levels of these five CRRGs (Fig. 2C). The risk score for each patient was calculated using the formula: risk score = (1.25 * expression value of ST3GAL1) + (− 1.11 * expression value of ERBB3) + (0.88 * expression value of UBXN1) + (1.05 * expression value of CD47) + (0.85 * expression value of S100A10). Patients with pancreatic cancer were then stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups based on the median risk score (Fig. 2D).

To enhance the clinical applicability of the MRRGS model, we further evaluated its performance across three datasets: the training set, which comprised 225 cases of clinically stratified PC from the TCGA cohort; the validation set, which included 81 cases of clinically stratified PC from the PACA-AU cohort; and the test set, which consisted of 172 cases of clinically stratified PC from the CPTAC cohort. The KM curve analysis demonstrated that the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer prognostic outcomes compared to the low-risk group across all three datasets (Fig. 3A–C). Further analysis revealed their significant upregulation (logFC > 1, adj.P.Val < 0.05) in metastatic versus primary tumors (Fig. 3D).

Furthermore, Reactome analysis (Fig. 4B) provided additional evidence supporting its role in translation, potentially regulating the expression of oncogenes and exerting biological effects.

The relationship between MRRGS and PC genomics

To further investigate the impact of MRRGS on patient functions, we examined their gene mutations and copy number variations (Fig. 4C, D). The findings revealed that patients in the high-risk group exhibited mutations in the SMAD4 gene and CNV. SMAD4, also known as small body dimer protein 4, is a crucial gene involved in cell signaling and the regulation of growth and development. It is part of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway and plays a significant role in signaling pathways that control cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation. In contrast, the low-risk group exhibited significant changes in genes such as GSTM1, CFHR3, and RNF43. GSTM1 encodes glutathione S-transferase mu 1, which is essential for antioxidant reactions and detoxification processes within cells. Mutations or defects in this gene may lead to increased sensitivity to environmental toxicants and are associated with a heightened risk of certain diseases and cancers. CFHR3, a member of the complement family, is associated with the immune system’s complement system. This gene encodes a protein that is part of the complement factor H-related proteins. RNF43, a tumor suppressor gene (Ring Finger Protein 43), encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase and functions as a negative regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway. The RNF43 protein ubiquitinates the Wnt receptor Frizzled, leading to its degradation and subsequently reducing the activity of the Wnt signaling pathway, a process that plays a role in various tumor types.

The genome plot (CNV) analyzed by GISTIC is presented in Fig. 4E and F. This study found that the high-risk group exhibited a greater frequency of copy number gains and losses compared to the low-risk group. This observation suggests that high-risk patients possess more copy number variations in their genomes, potentially contributing to their increased cancer risk. Enrichment analysis of genes with CNV indicated that the high-risk group showed significant enrichment in the positive regulation of cell projection organization, whereas the low-risk group demonstrated significant enrichment in the detection of chemical stimuli involved in sensory perception (Fig. 5A and B). Furthermore, mutation co-expression analysis revealed significant co-expression of KRAS and TP53 in both high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 5C and D). In the low-risk group, CDKN2A was also found to be significantly co-expressed with KRAS and TP53. CDKN2A is a cell cycle inhibitor gene, while KRAS and TP53 are associated with cell proliferation and apoptosis (cell death), respectively. The co-expression of these genes may drive the abnormal proliferation and survival of tumor cells.

The correlation between modeling genes and immune infiltration scores

Deconvolution algorithms were employed to further investigate the impact of mutations in ST3GAL1, ERBB3, UBXN1, CD47, and S100A10 on immune infiltration. ERBB3 exhibited a negative correlation with prognosis in the metastasis model (Fig. 2D) and was also negatively correlated with the degree of immune cell infiltration (Fig. 6A and B, and C). In contrast, S100A10 demonstrated a positive correlation with prognosis in the metastasis model (Fig. 2D), although it was negatively correlated with the degree of immune cell infiltration (Fig. 6A and B, and C). ST3GAL1 showed a positive correlation with prognosis in the metastasis model (Fig. 2D); however, aside from its positive correlation with Th2 cell infiltration, no correlations were observed with other immune cell infiltrations. UBXN1 was associated with stromal cell infiltration (Fig. 6A), while CD47 exhibited a partial association with immune cell infiltration (Fig. 6A and B, and C). Our results confirm that ST3GAL1, ERBB3, UBXN1, CD47, and S100A10 demonstrate statistically significant associations with metastatic potential (p < 0.05, Fig. 6D).

Screening for drug resistance

Using the OncoPredict algorithm, we identified drugs suitable for treatment in both high- and low-risk groups. Trametinib has been demonstrated to effectively induce tumor cell death and enhance patient prognosis in pancreatic cancer (PC). Given the differential sensitivity of immunotherapy to metastasis-related rhythmic genes, we employed cancer prediction methods to identify potential anti-cancer inhibitors (Fig. 6E). In our study, we recommend ABT737, Cytarabine, Uprosertib, Sepantronium bromide, and Trametinib for drug trials in high-risk groups. Conversely, the low-risk group may consider AZ6102, SB505124, Navitoclax, MK-8776, Sorafenib, PD173074, and UMI-77 for their drug trials.

Discussion

The circadian clock enables humans to perceive temporal changes in the external environment, and disturbances in circadian rhythms increase the risk of various conditions, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and tumors. Previous studies have highlighted the significance of the circadian clock in PC. This study identified five CRRGs associated with PC: S100A10, CD47, UBXN1, ERBB3, and ST3GAL1. A classification model for PC was developed utilizing these genes. Furthermore, drug sensitivity screening was conducted based on the correlation between biological clock genes and drugs, providing a foundation for future drug screening experiments.

ST3GAL1 is a sialic acid transferase responsible for catalyzing the transfer of sialic acid from CMP-sialic acid to substrates containing galactose. It plays a crucial role in the sialylation of N-linked and O-linked oligosaccharides on the surface glycoproteins of cancer cells. Previous studies have established a significant correlation between ST3GAL1 and the occurrence and progression of various cancers, including breast cancer [8], ovarian cancer [9], osteosarcoma [10], pancreatic cancer [11], melanoma [12], tongue cancer [13], malignant meningioma [14], and others. In the context of PC, Rodriguez demonstrated that the expression of ST3GAL1 in PC cells was notably upregulated, contributing to the synthesis of Siglec-7 ligands (a receptor for sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-type lectin), which further promotes the differentiation of tumor-associated macrophages, ultimately leading to poor clinical outcomes [15]. Sleep, an essential biological process regulated by the circadian rhythm system, has also been linked to ST3GAL1. Fei confirmed through whole exome sequencing that ST3GAL1 is associated with daytime sleepiness [16]. In drug screening, Szabo synthesized various α-Hydroxy-1,2,3-Triazole linked sialyltransferase inhibitors targeting ST3GAL1 genes to explore potential treatments for PC and other metastases [17]. Although the selective targeting results for ST3GAL1 were not satisfactory, this research provided a novel approach for drug screening in PC. Collectively, these studies suggest that the ST3GAL1 gene has the potential to serve as a prognostic marker for PC, possibly linked to the pancreatic circadian rhythm, and represents a promising target for further research, aligning with our screening efforts.

The ERBB3 (Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 3) gene, also known as HER3, encodes a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family characterized by its tyrosine kinase activity. Amplification and/or overexpression of this gene have been confirmed in various cancers. Han demonstrated that the resistance of PC cells to KRAS inhibitors can be mitigated through either genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition of ERBB3 [18]. Okuno identified a significant correlation between elevated ERBB3 expression levels and poor prognosis in PC patients, utilizing Andro-mediated regulation of ERBB3 and calcium signaling to reverse chemotherapy resistance to gemcitabine [19]. Chougoni illustrated that ERBB3 signaling is crucial for sustaining the growth of PC cells [20]. Rabia observed that the expression levels of EGFR, ERBB2, and ERBB3 increased in gemcitabine-resistant PC models, demonstrating that a combination of anti-EGFR, anti-ERBB2, and anti-ERBB3 antibodies can enhance sensitivity to gemcitabine [21]. Desai noted that the impact of ERBB3-targeted therapy on patients with solid tumors remains under-researched, suggesting that ERBB3 expression serves as an important predictive biomarker for patient response to such therapies [22]. In our study (Fig. 2C), we found that the expression level of ERBB3 significantly influences patient survival rates, supporting our hypothesis. Additionally, Li discovered that ERBB3 is involved in the formation of the plant RALF1-FER-EBP1 axis, resembling a transcriptional translation feedback loop regulated by circadian rhythms [23]. Currently, no research has reported progress regarding its relationship with the biological clock in PC or animal studies.

UBXN1 is a ubiquitin-binding protein. Jiao indicates that UBXN1 can promote the growth of hepatocellular carcinoma while inhibiting its apoptosis [24]. Oh suggests that the knockout of UBXN1 can significantly reduce the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells, as well as inhibit their migration and invasiveness [25]. However, no relevant research on UBXN1 has been identified in the context of PC or the biological clock. CD47 encodes a membrane protein that contributes to an increase in intracellular calcium concentration when cells adhere to the extracellular matrix. In PC, the overexpression of CD47 on the cell membrane is independently associated with poor clinical prognosis [26]. Nonetheless, research on the role of CD47 within the framework of the biological clock remains unexplored. S100A10 is the first defined member of the calcium-binding S100 protein family, which regulates various cellular functions, including cell cycle progression and differentiation. Ren found that S100A1 may be significantly related to the clinical outcomes of pancreatic cancer [27], but its role in the pancreatic biological clock has yet to be explored.

In our study, PC was categorized based on five circadian clock genes, which were utilized as a basis for drug screening tests. Similarly, Yan developed a novel RNs score (SVM-RFE and network topology score) and identified 17 genes as potential drug targets for PC in three independent expression microarray datasets [28]. Zhuang’s multidimensional analysis, which integrated gene expression, transcriptional regulation, and microRNA mechanisms, revealed that the CEBPA/miRNA32/PER2 signaling pathway, associated with circadian rhythm, may represent a promising target for PC treatment interventions [29]. This indicates that biological clock genes significantly influence the prediction and treatment of PC. However, there is a scarcity of research concerning the role of the five genes we have identified within the framework of the biological clock. Therefore, the findings of this study necessitate further enhancement and validation.

The results of the GO analysis indicated that patients in the high-risk group exhibited significant enrichment in ribosome-related pathways. This finding suggests that circadian clock genes may play a role in the regulation of cellular translation. Furthermore, the enrichment of circadian clock genes in the MHC class II pathway implies a potential correlation with the immune system. One possible explanation for this observation is that the biological clock regulates immune function. Additionally, although no definitive results have been obtained regarding the involvement of circadian clock genes in metabolic regulation and transfer, relevant studies suggest that this relationship warrants further investigation. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution, as database results may not necessarily reflect the actual organizational environment [2, 18, 30].

This article outlines several limitations. Firstly, the reliance on CTC-based analysis may not accurately represent the tissue environment, potentially leading to inconsistent results when compared to differential gene analysis in PC tissue biology. This limitation arises from the absence of PC tissue samples in the GEO database, necessitating their exclusion from the analysis. Secondly, the analysis presented in this paper aims to construct a predictive transfer grouping model based on clock-related genes, with a significant number of genes associated with metastasis being excluded. This limitation arises because the primary focus of this study is to investigate the prognosis, genomic stability, immune microenvironment, and drug sensitivity related to circadian rhythm-associated PC metastasis. Consequently, this focus may weaken the model’s predictive ability concerning metastasis. Additionally, MRRGS are associated with metabolic processes such as glycolysis and gluconeogenesis. However, since MRRGS is derived from a collective analysis of five gene combinations, the molecular mechanisms analyzed are not attributable to a single gene, but rather to the overall combination. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct further validation through single-cell metabolomics, recovery experiments, and other studies. Finally, the results obtained from retrospective database analysis have yet to undergo prospective validation in clinical settings and molecular biology experiments. Consequently, in the absence of clinical evidence to substantiate these findings, they should not be directly translated into clinical practice.

This study establishes a novel MRRGS. The MRRGS risk model not only categorizes patients into distinct prognostic groups but also suggests a complex interaction between circadian rhythm disorders and metastasis, thereby providing potential research directions for related studies. Notably, the MRRGS model elucidates the relationship between circadian rhythm genes and immune escape. Specifically, ERBB3 and S100A10 are negatively correlated with immune infiltration, indicating that these genes may inhibit anti-tumor immunity. The immune cold phenotype observed in high-risk patients aligns with their poor prognosis and resistance to conventional treatments. Drug sensitivity analysis underscores the practicality of MRRGS in guiding personalized treatment. Our work establishes a connection between circadian biology, metastasis, and tumor immunology, providing a mechanistic framework for understanding how dysregulation of circadian rhythms promotes the invasiveness of PC. The MRRGS has the potential to serve as a prognostic tool and a means of predicting drug response, thereby emphasizing the clinical relevance of circadian rhythms in precision oncology.
