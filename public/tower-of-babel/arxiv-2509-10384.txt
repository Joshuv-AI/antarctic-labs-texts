Flow Straight and Fast in Hilbert Space: Functional Rectified Flow
Jianxin Zhang, Clayton Scott

Abstract
Many generative models originally developed in finite-dimensional Euclidean space have functional generalizations in infinite-dimensional settings. However, the extension of rectified flow to infinite-dimensional spaces remains unexplored. In this work, we establish a rigorous functional formulation of rectified flow in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Our approach builds upon the superposition principle for continuity equations in an infinite-dimensional space. We further show that this framework extends naturally to functional flow matching and functional probability flow ODEs, interpreting them as nonlinear generalizations of rectified flow. Notably, our extension to functional flow matching removes the restrictive measure-theoretic assumptions in the existing theory of Kerrigan et al., 2024 . Furthermore, we demonstrate experimentally that our method achieves superior performance compared to existing functional generative models.

3
Rectified flows on Hilbert space

In this section, we extend rectified flows to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, demonstrating the fundamental property of marginal distribution preservation. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space. Given a probability triplet
(\Omega,\mathcal{S},\mathbb{P})
, denoting the sample space, sigma algebra, and probability measure, we take the perspective that the law of a stochastic process
\mathbb{X}:[0,1]\times\Omega\to\mathcal{H}
is the distribution of the function-valued random variable defined as
\omega\mapsto\mathbb{X}(\cdot,\omega)
. For simplicity, we denote the value of
\mathbb{X}
at time
t\in[0,1]
by
X_{t}
and the associated marginal measure by
\mu_{t}
. A random process
\mathbb{X}
will thus also be denoted
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
or simply
\{X_{t}\}_{t}
. Unless otherwise specified,
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
refers to the inner product in
\mathcal{H}
, and the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
refers to its induced norm.

3.1
Definitions of rectifiable and rectified flows

Given a process
\mathbb{X}
, the induced rectified flow is a random process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
that is defined in terms a quantity called the expected velocity of
\mathbb{X}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be a pathwise continuously differentiable random process on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The
expected velocity
of
\mathbb{X}
is defined as

v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{X_{t}}\mid X_{t}=x\right],\quad\forall x\in\operatorname{supp}(X_{t}),\forall t\in[0,1]

(2)

and is set to zero if
x\notin\operatorname{supp}(X_{t})
. Here,
\dot{X_{t}}
denotes the time derivative of
X_{t}
, and
\operatorname{supp}(X_{t})
the support of the random variable
X_{t}
.

Let
C^{1}
denote the space of continuously differentiable functions. An initial value problem (IVP) is a differential equation with a specified initial condition for the unknown function. To ensure the well-posedness of rectified flows in Hilbert space, we assume that the corresponding IVP admits a unique solution and a continuous solution map.

Assumption 2
.

Given a function
v:[0,1]\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, the initial value problem

z(t)=u+\int_{0}^{t}v(s,z(s))ds,\quad u\in\mathcal{H},

(3)

admits a unique solution
z(\cdot)\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathcal{H})
. Furthermore, the solution map
\Phi:\mathcal{H}\to C^{1}([0,1];\mathcal{H})
mapping from the initial value to the solution path, defined by
\Phi(u)=z
, is a continuous mapping from
(\mathcal{H},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}})
to
(C^{1}([0,1];\mathcal{H}),\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})
.

In the following assumption, we enforce that
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)
has a finite integral in time to ensure that the process does not exhibit pathological behavior, such as infinite total drift.

Assumption 3
.

Given a pathwise continuously differentiable random process
\mathbb{X}=\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}
,

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left\lVert v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)\right\rVert d\mu_{t}(x)dt<\infty

(4)

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\lVert\dot{X_{t}}\right\rVert\right]<\infty,

(5)

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the distribution of
X_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
for each
t\in[0,1]
.

With these notions, we can now define a rectified flow.

Definition 4
.

We say that a stochastic process
\mathbb{X}=\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is
rectifiable
if Assumption
2
holds for
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)
and Assumption
3
holds for
\mathbb{X}
.
In this case, the process defined by

Z_{t}=Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}v^{\mathbb{X}}(s,Z_{s})ds,\quad Z_{0}\sim X_{0},

(6)

is called the
rectified flow
induced by
\mathbb{X}
.

Unlike diffusion models, which rely on stochastic differential equations (SDEs), rectified flows are defined in terms of a deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE) guided by
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)
.

3.2
The marginal preserving property

A key property of a rectified flow is that it preserves the marginal distributions of the original stochastic process. This is formalized in the following theorem which generalizes
Liu et al., 2022
from a finite-dimensional Euclidean space to a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that an
\mathcal{H}
-valued stochastic process
\left\{X_{t}\right\}
is rectifiable, and let
\left\{Z_{t}\right\}
be the induced rectified flow. Then, for all
t\in[0,1]
,
Z_{t}\overset{d}{=}X_{t}.

The central challenge in proving the theorem lies in developing the superposition principle within a general separable Hilbert space setting. This requires establishing several nontrivial technical results and rigorously connecting the resulting measure-theoretic decomposition to the solution of Equation (
3
). We defer the proof to Section
A
in the appendix.

Note that
Z_{t}
derives its randomness entirely from the random initial condition
Z_{0}
.
Intuitively, this result states that while
Z_{t}
evolves deterministically given
Z_{0}
according to an ODE, it preserves the marginal distributions as
X_{t}
. When
X_{t}
is constructed to interpolate between two distributions, where
X_{0}
corresponds to a simple reference distribution (e.g., i.i.d. Gaussian noise) and
X_{1}
represents the target data distribution, then learning the velocity field
v^{\mathbb{X}}
enables sampling from the data distribution by solving (
6
) with randomly initialized
Z_{0}
.

The rectified flow method of
Liu et al., 2022
corresponds to a specific choice of
\mathbb{X}=\{X_{t}\}
given by

X_{t}=tX_{1}+(1-t)X_{0},

(7)

where
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
are independently sampled from the noise and data distributions, respectively. This formulation was shown to be effective in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces.

If we define
X_{t}=tX_{1}+(1-t)X_{0}
as in (
7
), where
X_{0},X_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
, then
\dot{X_{t}}=X_{1}-X_{0}
. The velocity field
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)
, modeled by a neural network
v_{\theta}
, can be trained to minimize

\min_{\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{X}}\left\lVert(x_{1}-x_{0})-v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right\rVert^{2}dt,

(8)

where
x
is a realization of the stochastic process and
x_{t}
is the value of
x
at time
t
. Note that the objective can be approximated by randomly sampled data
X_{1}
, noise
X_{0}
, and timestamp
t
.

For sampling, we numerically integrate the ODE:

Z_{t}=Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}v_{\theta}(s,Z_{s})ds,\quad Z_{0}\sim X_{0},

(9)

allowing us to generate new samples from
X_{1}
by evolving from
X_{0}
using the learned expected velocity
v_{\theta}
. This approach is computationally more efficient than stochastic sampling methods used in diffusion models, as it avoids the nuances of reversing an SDE.

3.3
Properties of rectified flow

The desirable properties of rectified flows (
7
) in finite-dimensional settings, as discussed in
Liu et al., 2022
—including transport cost reduction and the straightening effect—also hold in the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space setting. These results follow directly from the marginal-preserving property established in Theorem
5
, using arguments that largely mirror those in finite dimensions. As the extensions mainly involve technical but routine bookkeeping, we defer the detailed statements and proofs to Section
B
in the appendix.

4
Connections to other functional models

We place our work in the broader context of functional generative modeling by examining its relationship to functional flow matching
[
37
]
and functional probability flow ODE
[
62
]
.
Our framework accommodates interpolation paths of the form:

X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{1}+\beta_{t}X_{0},

(10)

where
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}
are continuously differentiable functions of time
t
. This generalized parameterization recovers rectified flow when
\alpha_{t}=t
,
\beta_{t}=1-t
. We call Equation (
10
) the nonlinear extension of the function rectified flow.
We show that both functional flow matching
[
37
]
and functional probability flow ODE
[
62
]
can be viewed as nonlinear extensions of functional rectified flow within our general framework.

To facilitate our discussion, we first define the Cameron–Martin space and the notion of a Wiener process in Hilbert space. Let
Q
be a trace-class, positive, symmetric operator on
\mathcal{H}
. The associated Cameron–Martin space is given by
\mathcal{H}_{Q}\coloneq Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
, the range of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
, equipped with the inner product
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\coloneq\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}x,Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}y\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. A
Q
-Wiener process
W_{t}
is a continuous Gaussian process in
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
W_{0}=0
and having independent, stationary increments, with
W_{t}-W_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(t-s)Q)
for all
0\leq s\leq t
.

4.1
Comparison with functional flow matching

By examining Equation (6) in
Kerrigan et al., 2024
, we can directly see that Equation (
10
) subsumes their proposed flow matching models as special cases. In Table
1
, we summarize how the “VP” and “OT” paths introduced in their work arise as specific instances of
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
.

The framework of
Kerrigan et al., 2024
relies on a strong measure-theoretic assumption that may not hold even in finite-dimensional Euclidean space. Suppose there exists a stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
interpolating
X_{0}
(noise) and
X_{1}
(data), with
\mu_{t}
denoting the marginal law of
X_{t}
, and
\mu_{t}^{x}
the conditional law of
X_{t}
given
X_{1}=x
.
Kerrigan et al., 2024
assume:

\mu_{t}^{x}\ll\mu_{t},

(11)

i.e.
, the conditional measure
\mu_{t}^{x}
must be absolutely continuous with respect to the marginal
\mu_{t}
for almost every
x
. As admitted by the authors in
[
37
]
, this assumption is
difficult to satisfy and verify, and generally fails even in finite-dimensional Euclidean space
. If
X_{0}
is a trace-class Gaussian process, this reduces to the requirement that
X_{1}
lies in the Cameron–Martin space of the initial Gaussian measure, i.e.,
X_{1}\in C_{0}^{1/2}(\mathcal{H})
when
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{0})
. As also noted in
Kerrigan et al., 2024
, even this condition is
highly restrictive and challenging to verify in practice
. Although we assume that the processes are pathwise continuously differentiable, this assumption is more plausible and readily satisfied in practical settings.

4.2
Comparison with functional probability flow ODE

Throughout this section, we adopt the convention that time
t=0
corresponds to the data distribution and
t=1
to the noise distribution. This is the reverse of the convention used in the rest of the paper, and is chosen to align with the standard formulation of score-based generative modeling and SDEs. This section also references technical concepts (e.g.,
Q
-Wiener process, Cameron-Martin space) that will be defined in the appendix.

The concurrent work of
Na et al., 2025
introduces an analogue of the probability flow ODE from
Song et al., 2021
, adapted to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Specifically, they consider the following variance-preserving SDE in
\mathcal{H}
:

dY_{t}=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}Y_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{\sigma_{t}}\,dW_{t},\quad Y_{0}\sim P_{\text{data}},

(12)

where
W_{t}
is a
Q
-Wiener process in
\mathcal{H}
,
\sigma_{t}
is a bounded, continuous function taking values on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Let
\{\zeta_{i}\}
denote an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenvectors of
Q
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
denote the corresponding Cameron–Martin space. To support the analysis, we recall the formal definition of the logarithmic gradient.

Definition 6
.

A Borel probability measure
\mu
is said to be Fomin differentiable along
h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}
if there exists a function
\rho_{h}^{\mu}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{H},\mu)
such that for all cylinder functions of the form

F(x)=f\left(\langle\zeta_{1},x\rangle,\dots,\langle\zeta_{m},x\rangle\right),\quad f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),\,m\in\mathbb{N},

(13)

we have

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\partial_{h}F(x)\,\mu(dx)=-\int_{\mathcal{H}}F(x)\,\rho_{h}^{\mu}(x)\,\mu(dx),

(14)

where
\partial_{h}
denotes the Gâteaux differential along
h
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. If there exists a function
\rho_{\mathcal{K}}^{\mu}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

\langle\rho_{\mathcal{K}}^{\mu}(x),h\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}=\rho_{h}^{\mu}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{H},\,h\in\mathcal{K},

(15)

then
\rho_{\mathcal{K}}^{\mu}
is called the logarithmic gradient of
\mu
along
\mathcal{K}
.

During training, a score network
S(t,Y_{t})
is learned to approximate the logarithmic gradient by minimizing the objective

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{0}\sim P_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t}\sim\mu_{t\mid Y_{0}}}\left\lVert S(t,Y_{t})-\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{\mu_{t\mid Y_{0}}}(Y_{t})\right\rVert dt,

(16)

where
\mu_{t\mid Y_{0}}
denotes the conditional distribution of
Y_{t}
given
Y_{0}
. Let
S^{*}
denote its minimizer.

For sampling,
Na et al., 2025
propose the following probability flow ODE, to be solved in reverse time from
t=1
to
t=0
:

dY_{t}=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}(Y_{t}+S^{*}(t,Y_{t}))\,dt,\quad Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q).

(17)

The following proposition shows how this ODE fits within our rectified flow framework.

Proposition 7
.

Equation (
17
) is the rectified flow induced by the process
Y^{\prime}_{t}
defined as

Y^{\prime}_{t}=\eta(t)\,Y^{\prime}_{0}+\sqrt{\kappa(t)}\,U,

(18)

where
\eta(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)
,
\kappa(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{\tau}d\tau\right)\sigma_{s}\,ds
, and
Y^{\prime}_{0}\sim P_{\text{data}}
,
U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
are sampled independently. Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}[\dot{Y}^{\prime}_{t}\mid Y^{\prime}_{t}]=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\left(Y^{\prime}_{t}+S^{*}(t,Y^{\prime}_{t})\right).

Thus, the probability flow ODE (
17
) corresponds to the time-reversal of the nonlinear rectified flow in Equation (
10
), with the interpolation weights given by
\alpha_{t}=\eta(1-t)
and
\beta_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa(1-t)}
.

5
Architectures for functional generative modeling

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of functions from some coordinate space
M
to
\mathbb{R}
. To implement functional rectified flows in practice, one must approximate the expected velocity field
v^{\mathbb{X}}(x_{t},t):\mathcal{H}\times[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}
, which is inherently defined over an infinite-dimensional domain.
Directly learning such a mapping is generally intractable. However, if
\mathcal{H}=L_{2}(M)
, under the conditions of Theorem 2 in
Franzese et al., 2023
, any
x\in\mathcal{H}
can be fully characterized by its pointwise evaluations
\{(x[p_{i}],p_{i})\}
, where
p_{i}\in M
. This insight enables the design of networks that act on sampled representations rather than abstract functional inputs, thus making implementation feasible. Following
Franzese et al., 2023
and
Kerrigan et al., 2024
, we consider three practical architectures for this purpose:
Implicit Neural Representations
(INRs),
Transformers
, and
Neural Operators
. We include this discussion of architectural choices for completeness but refer the reader to
Franzese et al., 2023
for a comprehensive treatment.

The first approach builds on
implicit neural representations
(INRs)
[
72
]
and the model
-
agnostic meta
-
learning (MAML) paradigm
[
19
]
.
Following the modulation strategy introduced for functional diffusion processes by
Franzese et al., 2023
, we represent the velocity field
v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)
with a network
n(\psi,t,\theta):M\to\mathbb{R}
where
\theta
is the shared base parameter and
\psi
is a
sample
-
specific
modulation vector that is adapted online to encode each
x_{t}
.
Formally,

v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)=n(\psi^{*},t,\theta),\quad\psi^{*}=\argmin_{\psi}\sum_{p_{i}}(n(\psi,t,\theta)[p_{i}]-x_{t}[p_{i}])^{2}.

(19)

For every
x_{t}
we initialize
\psi=\mathbf{0}
and perform a small number of gradient
-
descent steps, with
\theta
fixed, to minimize the residual in (
19
).
During training, the global parameters
\theta
are updated via the loss (
8
), whereas
\psi
is re
-
optimized from scratch for each
x_{t}
, enabling the network to have a functional representation while operating on finite discretizations
\{x_{t}[p_{i}],p_{i}\}
.

Our second approach employs
transformer architectures

[
77
]
, treating discretized function evaluations as sequences with positional information. For each
x_{t}
, we consider the set of finite discretizations
\{x_{t}[p_{i}]\}
as input features and their corresponding coordinates
\{p_{i}\}
as positions. Each
x_{t}[p_{i}]
is embedded into a higher-dimensional vector space, and summed with the positional encodings of
p_{i}
, resulting in a sequence of embeddings
\{y_{i}\}
. This sequence is then processed by a transformer to obtain
\{v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)[p_{i}]\}
. We note that
Cao, 2021
interpret transformers as mappings between Hilbert spaces, and
Kovachki et al., 2021
discuss the learned rather than guaranteed nature of resolution invariance of transformers. While these theoretical perspectives are relevant, we do not explore them further and refer interested readers to the original papers for a detailed discussion.

Neural operators
[
41
,
40
]
offer a principled framework for modeling functions in infinite-dimensional settings and have demonstrated success in various applications
[
64
,
67
]
. A neural operator learns a mapping
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}
, trained using finite evaluations of functions. Once trained, it maps a uniform discretization
\{(x[p_{i}],p_{i})\}
to output values at the same locations, producing
\{\mathcal{G}(x)[p_{i}]\}
. In our implementation, we directly parameterize the velocity field
v_{\theta}
using a neural operator. Similar to the transformer-based approach, the positions
\{p_{i}\}
are encoded via positional embeddings and appended to the embeddings of the corresponding
\{x_{t}[p_{i}]\}
. Although neural operators typically require inputs to be defined on fixed, uniformly spaced grids and produce outputs at the same grid locations—limiting flexibility for irregular or adaptive sampling—they have been shown to be highly effective for PDE data
[
37
]
.
Bond-Taylor and Willcocks, 2024
implement an infinite-dimensional generative model using a neural operator-based architecture but rely on a specialized diffusion autoencoder
[
66
]
, making their approach highly specialized and not applicable to our setting.

6
Experiments

We evaluate the proposed Functional Rectified Flow (FRF) model on three datasets: MNIST (MIT Licence)
[
44
]
, CelebA
64\times 64
(CC BY-SA 4.0)
[
57
]
, and the Navier-Stokes dataset (MIT Licence)
[
47
]
, using the INR, transformer, and neural operator implementations, respectively. Our goal is to assess whether extending rectified flow to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces yields competitive or superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art functional generative models under different architectural choices. 7
Conclusion, limitations and broader impact

We have introduced a functional extension of rectified flow by lifting it to general Hilbert spaces. Our theoretical results demonstrate that the marginal preserving property is preserved in the infinite-dimensional setting. This provides a principled and tractable foundation for functional generative modeling. We demonstrate experimentally that our method achieves superior performance compared to existing functional generative models. Notably, while most competing approaches develop specialized architectures tailored to their methods, we demonstrate the flexibility of our framework by applying it across three distinct model architectures—each originally designed for one of our competitors. Although our framework is broadly applicable, domain-specific architectures and inductive biases may still be required for optimal performance in high-complexity tasks.

As with all generative models, risks such as misuse in synthetic media generation remain, including the potential for creating misleading or harmful content. We encourage responsible use and further research into interpretability, robustness, and safety in functional generative models to mitigate these concerns. At the same time, functional generative models offer significant positive societal impacts, including advancing scientific discovery through improved simulation of complex physical systems and supporting creative industries with new tools for high-quality content generation.

A.1
Remarks on the existence of the velocity field

Under our setting, the existence of
v^{\mathbb{X}}
follows from standard results.

Proposition 8

(Proposition 1.10 of
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 1992
)
.

Assume that
E
is a separable Banach space. Let
V
be a Bochner integrable
E
-valued random variable defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{S},\mathbb{P})
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a
\sigma
-field contained in
\mathcal{S}
. There exists a unique, up to a set of
\mathbb{P}
-probability zero, integrable
E
-valued random variable
R
, measurable with respect to
\mathcal{G}
such that

\int_{A}V\,d\mathbb{P}=\int_{A}R\,d\mathbb{P},\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{G}.

(20)

The random variable
R
is denoted as
\mathbb{E}[V\mid\mathcal{G}]
and called the conditional expectation of
V
given
\mathcal{G}
.

In our case, the state space
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Banach space. Hence, it suffices to assume
\mathbb{E}\|\dot{X}_{t}\|<\infty
for integrability. Under this condition,
\mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_{t}\mid\sigma(X_{t})]
exists and is
\sigma(X_{t})
-measurable. By the Doob–Dynkin lemma, there exists a measurable function
v_{t}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

\mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_{t}\mid\sigma(X_{t})]=v_{t}(X_{t}).

(21)

Defining
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)=v_{t}(x)
for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(X_{t})
and
v^{\mathbb{X}}(t,x)=0
otherwise (which affects only a set of measure zero) yields the desired vector field. For completeness, we recall the Doob–Dynkin lemma:

Lemma 9

(Lemma 1.14 of
Kallenberg, 2021
)
.

Let
f,g
be measurable functions from
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
into measurable spaces
(S,\mathcal{S})
and
(T,\mathcal{T})
, where
S
is Borel. The following are equivalent:

1.

f
is
g
-measurable, i.e.
\sigma(f)\subset\sigma(g)
,

2.

there exists a measurable mapping
h:T\to S
such that
f=h\circ g
.

This shows that the velocity field
v^{\mathbb{X}}
is well-defined under mild integrability conditions.

A.2
Definitions and technical lemma

In this section, we present definitions and technical lemmas for the invocation of the superposition principle in Hilbert space. We first review Fréchet differentiability on a normed space.

Definition 10
.

Let
E
and
F
be normed vector spaces,
O
an open subset of
E
containing
0
. The derivative of
f:E\to F
at
x\in O
is a continuous linear map
D_{x}f
from
E
to
F
such that

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)-D_{x}f(h)}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}=0

(22)

When
E=\mathcal{H}
and
F=\mathbb{R}
,
D_{x}f\in\mathcal{H}^{*}
where
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is the dual space of
\mathcal{H}
. By the Riesz representation theorem, there is a unique
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that
D_{x}f(\cdot)=\left\langle\cdot,g\right\rangle
, and we denote such
g
by
\nabla f(x)
. We define the second-order derivative
\nabla^{2}f(x)
as the derivative of
x\in\mathcal{H}\to\nabla f(x)\in\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space with orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
and
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
. Define its asymptotic Lipschitz constant
L_{\varphi}(x)
at
x\in\mathcal{H}
by

L_{\varphi}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}L_{\varphi}(x,\epsilon),

where

L_{\varphi}(x,\epsilon)=\sup_{u,v\in B_{\epsilon}(x)}\frac{\left\lvert\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)\right\rvert}{\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert}.

Notice that
L_{\varphi}
is upper semicontinuous
[
75
]
.

Lemma 12
.

If
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is a twice-differentiable function with bounded second-order derivative, then
L_{\varphi}(x)=\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and
x\in\mathcal{H}
. We first show
L_{\varphi}(x)\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
.

Let
a,b\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
and
b\neq a
. By the mean value theorem (Theorem 3.2 of
Coleman, 2012
),

\displaystyle\varphi(b)-\varphi(a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\varphi(c),b-a\right\rangle

(23)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),b-a\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla\varphi(c)-\nabla\varphi(x),b-a\right\rangle

(24)

where
c=ta+(1-t)b
for some
t\in[0,1]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\varphi(b)-\varphi(a)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert+\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(c)-\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert+\sup_{c^{\prime}\in\left\{t^{\prime}x+(1-t^{\prime})c:t^{\prime}\in[0,1]\right\}}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\varphi(c^{\prime})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}}\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert\left\lVert c^{\prime}-x\right\rVert

(26)

We obtain the second inequality by applying the mean value inequality (Corollary 3.2 of
Coleman, 2012
) to
\nabla\varphi
. Suppose
\nabla^{2}\varphi
is bounded by a constant
B_{0}
. Note that both
c,c^{\prime}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lvert\varphi(b)-\varphi(a)\right\rvert}{\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert+B_{0}\epsilon

(27)

The choice of
a,b
is arbitrary, so
L_{\varphi}(x,\epsilon)\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert+B_{0}\epsilon
and
L_{\varphi}(x)\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
.

Now it remains to show
L_{\varphi}(x)\geq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
. WOLOG, we assume
\nabla\varphi(x)\neq 0
and let
v=\frac{\nabla\varphi(x)}{\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert}
. Let
y_{n}=x+\frac{1}{n}v,z_{n}=x,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. Then
\exists N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall n\geq N
,
y_{n}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
and by definition of Fréchet differentiability

\left\lvert\varphi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\varphi(x)-\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),\frac{1}{n}v\right\rangle\right\rvert\leq\alpha\left\lVert\frac{v}{n}\right\rVert=\frac{\alpha}{n}.

Hence,
\varphi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\varphi(x)\geq\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),\frac{1}{n}v\right\rangle-\frac{\alpha}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert-\frac{\alpha}{n}
.

For
y_{n}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
,

\displaystyle L_{\varphi}(x,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left\lvert\varphi(y_{n})-\varphi(z_{n})\right\rvert}{\left\lVert y_{n}-z_{n}\right\rVert}

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lvert\varphi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\varphi(x)\right\rvert}{\frac{1}{n}}

(29)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{{\varphi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\varphi(x)}}{\frac{1}{n}}

(30)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert-\alpha.

(31)

Since
\alpha
is arbitrary,
L_{\varphi}(x,\epsilon)\geq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
. Therefore,
L_{\varphi}(x)=\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert
.
∎

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the cylindrical functions on
\mathcal{H}
,
i.e.
,

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}:\varphi=\phi\circ P_{d},d\text{ is a non-negative integer},\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ has compact support}\right\}

(32)

where

P_{d}(x)=(\left\langle x,e_{1}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle x,e_{d}\right\rangle)
is a finite dimensional projection and

is the set of compactly supported function from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{A}
is an algebra (
i.e.

\mathcal{A}
by letting

. By Lemma
12

L_{\varphi}(x)=\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(x)\right\rVert,\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{A}
. Let

, for

be a vector field.
v_{t}
defines an operator
V_{t}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{A}

\varphi\in\mathcal{A}

(V_{t}\varphi)(x)=\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),v_{t}(x)\right\rangle

V_{t}(fg)=fV_{t}(g)+gV_{t}(f)

\left\lvert(V_{t}\varphi)(x)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),v_{t}(x)\right\rangle\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert v_{t}(x)\right\rVert L_{\varphi}(x)

We have to show one more lemma to invoke the results of

Lemma 14
.

Let
\varphi
be a Lipschitz, bounded function on
\mathcal{H}\times[0,1]

((x,t),(y,s))\to(\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then there is a sequence of uniformly bounded functions
\left\{\varphi_{k}\right\}
on
\mathcal{H}\times[0,1]
that are also Lipschitz with uniform Lipschitz constants, such that
\varphi_{k}(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{A}
for
t\in[0,1]
,
\lim_{k}\varphi_{k}=\varphi
pointwisely, and

\limsup_{k}L_{\varphi_{k}(\cdot,t)}(x)\leq L_{\varphi(\cdot,t)}(x),\forall(x,t)\in\mathcal{H}\times[0,1].

(33)

Proof.

Let
\{e_{k}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Let
C_{\varphi}
be the Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
.

For an integer
k>0

P_{k}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}

P_{k}(x)=(\left\langle x,e_{1}\right\rangle,\left\langle x,e_{2}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle x,e_{k}\right\rangle)

\pi_{k}(x):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

\pi_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle x,e_{i}\right\rangle e_{i}
and
\psi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

\psi_{k}(r,t)=\varphi(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}e_{i},t)

Note that
\left\lVert P_{k}(x)-P_{k}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}=\left\lVert\pi_{k}(x)-\pi_{k}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

.

Also note that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\psi_{k}(r,t)-\psi_{k}(\bar{r},s)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varphi}\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}e_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bar{r}_{i}e_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+(t-s)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{\varphi}\left(\left\lVert r-\bar{r}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}+(t-s)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(35)

So

is also

-Lipschitz.

Fix an integer

\displaystyle\eta(r):=\begin{cases}C\exp\left(\frac{1}{|r|^{2}-1}\right)&\text{ if }\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}<1\\

(37)

be the

Define the mollification of

\displaystyle\left\lvert h_{k,\epsilon}(r,t)-h_{k,\epsilon}(\bar{r},s)\right\rvert

(40)

\displaystyle=C_{\varphi}\left(\left\lVert r-\bar{r}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}^{2}+(t-s)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(41)

(43)

(44)

Therefore,

is also
C_{\varphi}
-Lipschitz and uniformly converges to

at a rate

when
\epsilon\to 0

\psi_{k,n}

A.3
Superposition principles

In this section, we leverage the abstract framework of Theorem 3.4 in
Stepanov and Trevisan, 2017
to establish the superposition principle in a separable Hilbert space. Intuitively, the principle states that any narrowly continuous curve of probability measures solving the continuity equation can be represented as a distribution over paths that solve the associated ODE. In this way, a statement about marginal laws is transformed into an ODE statement about sample trajectories. The former specifies the object that neural networks are trained to satisfy implicitly, while the latter specifies the mechanism through which new data are sampled with a trained model. By working directly with curves, we avoid introducing a Radon–Nikodym derivative between conditional and marginal laws as required in the FFM framework
[
37
]
. This pathwise view allows us to bypass the absolute-continuity assumption inherent to FFM.

A.3.1
From continuity equation to ODE solutions

Let
C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)
be the space of continuous function from
[0,1]
to
\mathcal{H}
. Let
E_{t}:z\in C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)\to z(t)\in\mathcal{H}
be the evaluation map,
i.e.
,
E_{t}(z)=z(t)
. Let
(\cdot)_{\#}
denote the pushforward. Specifically, if
z\in C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)
has law
\eta
,
i.e
, a probability measure on the space
C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)
, then
\left((E_{t})_{\#}\eta\right)(A)=\eta\left(z\in C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right):E_{t}(z)=z(t)\in A\right)
for a measurable set
A
in
\mathcal{H}
. Recall that
z:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}
is an absolutely continuous curve if and only if
z
is differentiable almost everywhere on
[0,1]
and
\dot{z}
is in
L_{1}([0,1];\mathcal{H})
(Remark 1.1.3 of
Ambrosio et al., 2008
). We first define the narrow continuity of measures.

Definition 15
.

Let
\left\{\mu_{t}\right\}
be a family of Borel probability measures on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, indexed by
t\in[0,1]
. We say that
\left\{\mu_{t}\right\}
is a narrowly continuous curve if and only if the map
t\mapsto\int f\,d\mu_{t}
is continuous for all bounded continuous functions
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
.

We say a probability measure
\eta
is concentrated on a set
A
if
\eta(A)=1
.

Now we move on to invoke Theorem 3.4 of
Stepanov and Trevisan, 2017
to develop the superposition principle.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\left\{\mu_{t}\right\}
be a narrowly continuous curve of Borel probability measures on the separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
v_{t}
be Borel vector fields such that
\int_{[0,1]}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left\lVert v_{t}(x)\right\rVert d\mu_{t}dt<\infty
. Suppose
(v_{t},\mu_{t})
solves the continuity equation in the sense that for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{A}
,

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi d\mu_{t}=\int\left\langle\nabla\varphi,v_{t}\right\rangle d\mu_{t}.

(78)

There exists a probability measure
\eta
over
C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)
concentrated on absolutely continuous curves
z:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}
such that
(E_{t})_{\#}\eta=\mu_{t}
for every
t\in[0,1]
and one has

\frac{d}{dt}\varphi(z(t))=(V_{t}\varphi)(z(t))

(79)

for

-
a.e.

z
and
a.e.

Proof.

Recall that the operator

is defined by

Note that constant functions are members of

By Lemma

Condition (3.4) requires that the continuity equation (

Finally, Lemma

[

, so

A.3.2

Before we move on to the proof of the main theorem, we show that Assumption

The existence and uniqueness are implied by Theorem 16.1 and Theorem 16.2 of
[
63
]

Theorem 20

)
.

Assume
\mathbb{X}
is rectifiable and

is its induced rectified flow. Then

in distribution for all

.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{A}
is the cylindrical functions on
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}
be the integral
w.r.t.
the path measure of process
\mathbb{X}
.
\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}
is the expectation
w.r.t.
the marginal
X_{t}
.
\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\varphi(X_{t+h})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]}{h}

(98)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\frac{\varphi(X_{t+h})-\varphi(X_{t})}{h}\right]

(99)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\varphi(X_{t+h})-\varphi(X_{t})}{h}\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\varphi(X_{t})\right]

(101)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\left\langle\nabla\varphi(X_{t}),\dot{X_{t}}\right\rangle\right]

(102)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}|X_{t}}\left[\left\langle\nabla\varphi(X_{t}),\dot{X_{t}}\right\rangle\right]

(103)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left[\left\langle\nabla\varphi(X_{t}),\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}|X_{t}}\left[\dot{X_{t}}\right]\right\rangle\right]

(104)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left[\left\langle\nabla\varphi(X_{t}),v^{\mathbb{X}}(X_{t},t)\right\rangle\right].

(105)

Note that following the mean-value theorem (Corollary 3.2 of
Coleman, 2012
) and Assumption
3
,

\left\lvert\frac{\varphi(X_{t+h})-\varphi(X_{t})}{h}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\frac{X_{t+h}-X_{t}}{h}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{\infty}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left\lVert\dot{X_{s}}\right\rVert<\infty

(107)

So for all
h
,
\left\lvert\frac{\varphi(X_{t+h})-\varphi(X_{t})}{h}\right\rvert
is bounded above and (
100
) follows the dominated convergence theorem (Theorem E.6. of
Cohn, 2013
). (
102
) follows the chain rule (Theorem 2.1 of
Coleman, 2012
). (
104
) is a direct consequence of Theorem E.11 of
Cohn, 2013
.

Let
\mu_{t}
denote the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
. Then,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]=\int\left\langle\nabla\varphi(x),v^{\mathbb{X}}(x,t)\right\rangle d\mu_{t}(x)
.
One the other hand,
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]=\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi(x)d\mu_{t}(x)
.
So
(v^{\mathbb{X}}(x,t),\mu_{t})
solves the continuity equation in Corollary

. Hence, there exists a probability measure

on
C\left([0,1];\mathcal{H}\right)
concentrated on absolutely continuous curves such that

for every

Theorem

\eta=\Phi_{\#}\mu_{0}
, which is the law of the rectified flow
\{Z_{t}\}
. Therefore,
(E_{t})_{\#}\eta=\mu_{t}
implies that

in distribution.
∎

A.5.1
Gaussian Measure in Hilbert Space

A linear operator
Q
on
\mathcal{H}
is called
positive
if
\left\langle Qx,x\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
, and
strictly positive
if
\left\langle Qx,x\right\rangle>0
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\{\zeta_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
, and let
Q
be a positive bounded linear operator. The trace of
Q
is defined as
\text{Tr}(Q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\langle Q\zeta_{k},\zeta_{k}\right\rangle
, which is independent of the choice of orthonormal basis. A self-adjoint, positive, bounded, linear operator
Q
is called
trace class
if and only if
\text{Tr}(Q)<\infty
.

Following
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{H}
is called
Gaussian
if for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
, the pushforward measure
B\mapsto\mu(\{x\in\mathcal{H}:\left\langle x,h\right\rangle\in B\})
is a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Any such Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{H}
admits a unique mean vector
m\in\mathcal{H}
and a unique self-adjoint positive bounded linear operator
Q
such that

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\left\langle h,x\right\rangle]=\left\langle m,h\right\rangle,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\left\langle h_{1},x-m\right\rangle\left\langle h_{2},x-m\right\rangle]=\left\langle Qh_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle,

(108)

for all
h,h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}

[
13
]
. The operator
Q
is called the
covariance operator
of a Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{H}
, and it is always trace class (see Section 2.3.1 of
[
13
]
). When
Q
is a covariance operator, there exists a complete orthonormal system
\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of eigenvectors of
Q
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

[
11
,
62
]
.

We define the Cameron–Martin space following
Pidstrigach et al., 2024
. For a Gaussian measure
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with a strictly positive
Q
, the Cameron–Martin space
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{Q}\coloneq Q^{1/2}(\mathcal{H}),\quad\text{with inner product}\quad\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}=\langle Q^{-1/2}f,Q^{-1/2}g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(109)

For completeness, we include the definition of an
\mathcal{H}
-valued
Q
-Wiener process:

Definition 21
.

Let
Q
be a trace-class, positive, self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
. A stochastic process
W=\{W_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
taking values in
\mathcal{H}
is called a
standard
Q
-Wiener process
if:

1.

W_{0}=0
,

2.

W
has continuous trajectories,

3.

W
has independent increments,

4.

W_{t}-W_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(t-s)Q)
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
.

Proof of Proposition

Lemma 22
.

Let

be a strictly positive trace-class covariance operator and

. Let

be a Gaussian measure with mean

and a strictly positive trace-class covariance operator

(110)

Remark 23
.

Note that although

does not necessarily belong to

,

and Remark 8 of

without loss of generality, assume

. Let
\{\zeta_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenvectors of
Q
, with corresponding eigenvalues be
\{\lambda_{i}\}
. Let
N_{1},N_{2}
be integers and

. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\left[\left\lvert\left\langle\sum_{i=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\zeta_{i},Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

(111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\left[\left\lvert\left\langle\sum_{i=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\zeta_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\zeta_{i}\right\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\right]

(112)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\left[\left\lvert\sum_{i=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

(113)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}{\left[\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle z,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]}

(114)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}{\left[\frac{\left\langle C\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]}

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{i,j=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}{\left[\frac{\lambda_{i}\left\langle\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]}

(116)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{i=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}{\left\lvert\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rvert^{2}}

(117)

Note that
Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\in\mathcal{H}
. So
\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}\left\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\zeta_{i}
is Cauchy. Hence,
{\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\zeta_{i},Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}
is Cauchy in

. Hence, for
z\in\mathcal{H}
,
\left\langle z,h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\coloneq\lim_{n}{\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left\langle z,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\zeta_{i},Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}
is well-defined in
L_{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\partial_{h}F(x)d\mu(x)=-\int_{\mathcal{H}}F(x)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(m-x),h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}d\mu(x)

(119)

Recall that
\{\zeta_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenvectors of
Q
. Let the corresponding eigenvalues be
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, and
F
is a cylindrical function. Define the projection operator
P_{k}:\mathcal{H}_{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},P_{k}(x)=[\left\langle x,\zeta_{1}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle x,\zeta_{k}\right\rangle]^{tr}
. Let
Y=P_{k}(X)
where
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
. Then
Y\sim\mu^{(k)}\coloneq\mathcal{N}(m^{(k)},\Sigma^{(k)})
where

,
m^{(k)}_{i}=\left\langle m,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle

\Sigma^{(k)}_{ij}=\left\langle C\zeta_{j},\zeta_{i}\right\rangle

Bogachev, 1998
and the characterizations of Gaussian measure on Hilbert space in
Na et al., 2025
. We first evaluate the Gâteaux differential
\partial_{h}F(x)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{h}F(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{F(x+\delta h)-F(x)}{\delta}

(120)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{f(P_{k}(x+\delta h))-f(P_{k}(x))}{\delta}

(121)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{f(P_{k}(x)+\delta P_{k}(h))-f(P_{k}(x))}{\delta}

(122)

\displaystyle=\partial_{P_{k}(h)}f(P_{k}(x))

(123)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f(P_{k}(x)),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}},

(124)

where the last equality follows the property of Gâteaux differential (Chapter 2 appendix,
[
10
]
).

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{H}}\partial_{h}F(x)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle\nabla f(P_{k}(x)),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}d\mu(x)

(125)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\langle\nabla f(y),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}d\mu^{(k)}(y)

(126)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}[\nabla f(Y)],P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}.

(127)

Recall that
\Sigma^{(k)}_{ij}=\left\langle C\zeta_{j},\zeta_{i}\right\rangle=\lambda_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=j}

is strictly positive. So
\Sigma^{(k)}
is invertible. By applying Stein’s Identity

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}[\nabla f(Y)]=[\Sigma^{(k)}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}[f(Y)(Y-m^{(k)})].

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{H}}\partial_{h}F(x)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}[\nabla f(Y)],P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}

(128)

\displaystyle=\left\langle[\Sigma^{(k)}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}[f(Y)(Y-m^{(k)})],P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}

(129)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}\left[f(Y)\left\langle[\Sigma^{(k)}]^{-1}(Y-m^{(k)}),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\right].

(130)

Now that the left-hand side of (

) has been reduced to an expectation of an inner product in

, we apply a similar procedure to the right-hand side.
Let
\pi_{k}h=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle h,\zeta_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\zeta_{i}
and

. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[F(X)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(X-m),h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\right]

(131)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[F(X)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(X-m),\pi_{k}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[F(X)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(X-m),h^{\perp}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\right].

(132)

It is sufficient to prove (

) by showing

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu^{(k)}}\left[f(Y)\left\langle[\Sigma^{(k)}]^{-1}(Y-m^{(k)}),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[F(X)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(X-m),\pi_{k}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\right]

(133)

and

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[F(X)\left\langle\kappa^{-1}(X-m),h^{\perp}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\right]=0.

(134)

We first show (
133
). Let

. Hence,

\displaystyle S^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\kappa^{-1}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(X-m),Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\pi_{k}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(135)

\displaystyle=\left\langle X-m,\kappa^{-1}Q^{-1}\pi_{k}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(136)

Note that
Q^{-1}\pi_{k}h
is well defined as it is supported on only a finite number of eigenvectors. So
S^{(k)}
is a Gaussian random variable on
\mathbb{R}
, by definition of Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{H}
.

Following the definition of the covariance operator

(Lemma 2.15 of

),

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(S^{(k)},Y_{j}-m_{j}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[\left\langle X-m,\kappa^{-1}Q^{-1}\pi_{k}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle X-m,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{k}h,\zeta_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(140)

Hence, we can apply the conditional mean estimator for a joint Gaussian distribution (Theorem 3 of

(141)

\displaystyle=\left\langle[\Sigma^{(k)}]^{-1}(Y-m^{(k)}),P_{k}(h)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}.

is measurable by

For

\displaystyle=0.

Again, by Theorem 3 of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S^{\perp}\mid Y]

\min_{S:[0,1]\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{0}\sim P_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t}\sim\mu_{t\mid Y_{0}}}\left\lVert S(t,Y_{t})-\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{\mu_{t\mid Y_{0}}}(Y_{t})\right\rVert^{2}dt,

where
S:[0,1]\times\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}
is measurable, and in practice parametrized by a neural network. This is equivalent to

\min_{S:[0,1]\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}_{0}\sim P_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}_{t}\sim\mu_{t\mid Y^{\prime}_{0}}}\left\lVert S(t,Y^{\prime}_{t})-\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{\mu_{t\mid Y^{\prime}_{0}}}(Y^{\prime}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}dt,

(159)

because the joint distribution
(Y_{0},Y_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y^{\prime}_{0},Y^{\prime}_{t})
.

This is a minimum mean squared error problem whose solution is the conditional mean

\displaystyle S^{*}(t,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{\mu_{t\mid Y^{\prime}_{0}}}(Y^{\prime}_{t})\mid Y^{\prime}_{t}=y\right]

(160)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\eta(t)Y^{\prime}_{0}-Y^{\prime}_{t}}{\kappa(t)}\mid Y^{\prime}_{t}=y\right]

(161)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{U}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\mid Y^{\prime}_{t}=y\right].

(162)

Note that
\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{\kappa(t)}=\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{2\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}
,
\dot{\eta}(t)=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\eta(t)
, and

\displaystyle\kappa(t)

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\eta^{-2}(s)\sigma_{s}ds,

(163)

\displaystyle\dot{\kappa}(t)

\displaystyle=2\eta(t)\dot{\eta}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\eta^{-2}(s)\sigma_{s}ds+\eta^{2}(t)\left(\eta^{-2}(t)\sigma_{t}\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{t}\eta^{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\eta^{-2}(s)\sigma_{s}ds+\sigma_{t}

(165)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}(1-\kappa(t)).

(166)

Hence,

\displaystyle\dot{Y}^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\dot{\eta}(t)\,Y^{\prime}_{0}+\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{2\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\,U

(167)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\,\eta(t)\Bigr)Y^{\prime}_{0}\;+\;\frac{\sigma_{t}\!\left[1-\kappa(t)\right]}{2\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\,U

(168)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\bigl[\eta(t)\,Y^{\prime}_{0}+\sqrt{\kappa(t)}\,U\bigr]\;+\;\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\,\frac{U}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}

(169)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\,Y^{\prime}_{t}\;-\;\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}\!\Bigl(-\frac{U}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\Bigr).

(170)

Therefore,

\dot{Y}^{\prime}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t}Y^{\prime}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t}(-\frac{U}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}).

So equation (
17
) is equivalent to

dY_{t}=-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{2}(Y_{t}+S^{*}(t,Y_{t}))dt=\mathbb{E}[\dot{Y}^{\prime}_{t}\mid Y^{\prime}_{t}=Y_{t}]dt.

(171)

∎

Appendix B
Convex transport costs and the straightening effect

In finite-dimensional spaces, rectified flows are known to reduce transportation costs and exhibit a straightening effect, both desirable properties in generative modeling
[
56
]
. These properties extend naturally to the setting of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and follow from the marginal-preserving property established in Theorem
5
. The proofs largely mirror those in the finite-dimensional case and involve routine but lengthy bookkeeping. We begin by defining the
\operatorname{RectFlow}(\cdot)
and
\operatorname{Rectify}(\cdot)
operations.

Definition 24
.

Let
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
be
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables. A coupling
\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
is called
rectifiable
if its linear interpolation process
\mathbb{X}=\left\{tX_{1}+\right.

\left.(1-t)X_{0}:t\in[0,1]\right\}
is rectifiable. In this case, the
\mathbb{Z}=\left\{Z_{t}:t\in[0,1]\right\}
in Equation (
6
) is called the
rectified flow
of coupling
\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
, denoted as
\mathbb{Z}=\operatorname{RectFlow}\left(\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)\right)
, and
\left(Z_{0},Z_{1}\right)
is called the
rectified coupling
of
\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
, denoted as
\left(Z_{0},Z_{1}\right)=\operatorname{Rectify}\left(\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)\right)
.

For any convex function
c
, The following theorem establishes that the
\operatorname{Rectify}(\cdot)
operator does not increase the transport cost
\mathbb{E}[c(Z_{1}-Z_{0})]
, which corresponds to a special case of Monge’s formulation of optimal transport (Section 1.2 of
[
1
]
). It generalizes Theorem 3.5 from
[
56
]
to the Hilbert space setting.

Theorem 25
.

Let
X_{0},X_{1}
be
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables such that the pair
(X_{0},X_{1})
is rectifiable, and define
(Z_{0},Z_{1})=\operatorname{Rectify}(X_{0},X_{1})
. Then for any convex function
c:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[c(Z_{1}-Z_{0})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[c(X_{1}-X_{0})\right].

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(\int_{0}^{1}v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(t,Z_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]

(172)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}c\left(v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(t,Z_{t}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

(173)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}c\left(v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(t,X_{t}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

(174)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}c\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\mid X_{t}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

(175)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\mid X_{t}\right]\mathrm{d}t\right]

(176)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

(177)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\right],

(178)

where we apply Jensen’s inequality at (
173
) and (
176
).
∎

Following
[
56
]
, we define a coupling
(X_{0},X_{1})
to be
straight
if it is a fixed point of the
\operatorname{Rectify}(\cdot)
operation. Such couplings are practically useful, as their associated process is linear and can be simulated exactly in a single step. As a direct extension of Theorem 3.6 from
[
56
]
to the Hilbert space setting, the following theorem characterizes this property.

Theorem 26
.

Let
X_{0},X_{1}
be
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables such that the pair
(X_{0},X_{1})
is rectifiable. Let
X_{t}=tX_{1}+(1-t)X_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}=\operatorname{RectFlow}\left(\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)\right)
. The following statements are equivalent.

1.

\exists
a strictly convex function
c:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that
\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\right]
.

2.

\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
is a fixed point of
\operatorname{Rectify}(\cdot)
, that is,
\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)=\left(Z_{0},Z_{1}\right)
.

3.

The rectified flow coincides with the linear interpolation process:
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{Z}
.

4.

The paths of the linear interpolation
\mathbb{X}
do not intersect:

V\left((X_{0},X_{1})\right):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{1}-X_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}\right]\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t=0.

Note that
V\left((X_{0},X_{1})\right)=0
indicates that
X_{1}-X_{0}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}\right]
almost surely when
t\sim
Uniform
([0,1])
, meaning that the lines passing through each
X_{t}
is unique, and hence no linear interpolation paths intersect.

Proof.

Clearly,
3\implies 2\implies 1
.

1\implies 4
:
\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\right]\implies
the equalities are achieved in inequalities (
173
) and (
176
). Since
c
is strictly convex,
(X_{1}-X_{0})|X_{t}
is a constant almost surely in
\mathbb{X}
and
t\in[0,1]
. So
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}|X_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}]
almost surely in
\mathbb{X}
and
t\in[0,1]
and
V(\mathbb{X})=0
.

4\implies 3:

V(\mathbb{X})=0\implies\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}|X_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}]
almost surely in
\mu_{t}
and
t\in[0,1]
. We have
\int_{0}^{s}\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{s}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}\right]\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{s}v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(t,X_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t
for
s\in(0,1]
. Hence,

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(X_{1}-X_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}t=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(s,X_{s}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(179)

By definition,
Z_{t}
also satisfy

Z_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}v^{\mathbb{X}}\left(s,X_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(180)

By Assumption
2
, for any fixed sample
\omega
, the solution is unique,
i.e.
,
\mathbb{X}(\cdot,\omega)=\mathbb{Z}(\cdot,\omega)
. So
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

We next present a Hilbert space analogue of Theorem 3.7 from
Liu et al., 2022
, which shows that repeated application of the rectified flow progressively improves the alignment of the coupling, resulting in more linear trajectories and reduced path overlap. Following
Liu et al., 2022
, the straightness of a continuously differentiable process
\mathbb{Z}=\{Z_{t}\}
is measured by by

S(\mathbb{Z})\coloneq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert(Z_{1}-Z_{0})-\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]dt.

Theorem 27
.

Let
X_{0},X_{1}
be
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables such that the pair
(X_{0},X_{1})
is rectifiable. Let
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
the
k
-th rectified flow of
\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
, that is,
\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}=\operatorname{RectFlow}\left(\left(Z_{0}^{k},Z_{1}^{k}\right)\right)
and
\left(Z_{0}^{0},Z_{1}^{0}\right)=\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
. Assume each
\left(Z_{0}^{k},Z_{1}^{k}\right)
is rectifiable for
k=0,\ldots,K
.
Then

\sum_{k=0}^{K}S\left(\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}\right)+V\left(\left(Z_{0}^{k},Z_{1}^{k}\right)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{1}-X_{0}\right\|^{2}\right].

Hence, if
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{1}-X_{0}\right\|^{2}\right]<+\infty
, we have
\min_{k\leq K}S\left(\mathbb{Z}^{k}\right)+V\left(\left(Z_{0}^{k},Z_{1}^{k}\right)\right)=O(1/K).

Proof.

\displaystyle\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}]\right\rVert^{2}

(181)

\displaystyle+2\left\langle X_{1}-X_{0}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}],\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}]\right\rangle

(182)

\displaystyle+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}]\right\rVert^{2}.

(183)

Then

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}=V((X_{0},X_{1}))+\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}|X_{t}\right\rVert^{2}]dt.

(185)

Note

\left\lVert(Z_{1}-Z_{0})-\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-2\left\langle Z_{1}-Z_{0},\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rangle+\left\lVert\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(186)

By moving the integral over
t
inside the inner product, we get

S(\mathbb{Z})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}dt-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right\rVert^{2}.

(187)

By construction,
\dot{Z_{t}}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{0}|X_{t}=Z_{t}]
. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\dot{Z_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}\mid X_{t}\right\rVert^{2}]
. Hence, by Equations (
185
) and (
187
),

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X_{1}-X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert Z_{1}-Z_{0}\right\rVert^{2}=S(\mathbb{Z})+V((X_{0},X_{1}))

(188)

Applying it to
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|Z_{1}^{k}-Z_{0}^{k}\right\|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|Z_{1}^{k+1}-Z_{0}^{k+1}\right\|^{2}\right]=S\left(\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}\right)+V\left(\left(Z_{0}^{k},Z_{1}^{k}\right)\right).

Summing over
k
leads to the desired inequality.
∎

A practical implication of Theorem
27
is that single-step sampling becomes feasible after several recursive applications of
\operatorname{RectFlow}(\cdot)
.

C.2
Architectural details and hyperparameter choices

C.2.1
INR on MNIST

We follow
Franzese et al., 2023
and adopt the original INR architecture proposed by
Sitzmann et al., 2020
. The network is a fully connected MLP with 8 layers, each containing 128 neurons and using sinusoidal activations
[
72
]
. We implement the modulation-based meta-learning framework as in
Dupont et al., 2022
and
Finn et al., 2017
. The base network parameters are optimized in the outer loop using the AdaBelief optimizer
[
84
]
, with a cosine learning rate schedule ending at
10^{-5}
. The inner loop adapts sample-specific modulation vectors via 3 steps of SGD with a learning rate of
10^{-2}
. Training is performed on 8 NVIDIA A40 GPUs with an effective batch size of
64\times 8
. The model is trained for
10^{6}
steps and takes approximately 3 days.

C.2.2
Transformer on CelebA

We follow
Franzese et al., 2023
and adopt the UViT backbone introduced in
Bao et al., 2023
. The model uses 2D sinusoidal positional embeddings, and we set the patch size to 1, effectively treating each pixel as a token. Our architecture matches that of
Franzese et al., 2023
, consisting of 7 transformer layers, each comprising an 8-head self-attention mechanism and a fully connected feedforward layer. Skip connections are employed following both
Franzese et al., 2023
and
Bao et al., 2023
. For optimization, we use the AdamW optimizer
[
58
]
with a cosine warm-up schedule, ending at a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
. The weight decay is set to
10^{-2}
. Training is conducted for
6\times 10^{5}
steps with an effective batch size of 32, and the learning rate is reduced by a factor of 0.1 during the final
10^{5}
steps. The model is trained on 8 NVIDIA A40 GPUs, and the full training takes approximately one week.

C.3
Neural operator on Navier-Stokes dataset

We follow
Kerrigan et al., 2024
and adopt the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) architecture proposed by
Kovachki et al., 2021
. The network comprises 4 Fourier layers with 32 modes and 64 hidden channels, along with 256-dimensional lifting and projection layers, mirroring the configuration used in
Kerrigan et al., 2024
. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
, which is reduced by a factor of 0.1 every 25 epochs. Following
Liu et al., 2022
, we add a small amount of noise to the input data for smoothing. To mitigate redundancy in the dataset, we follow
Kerrigan et al., 2024
and subsample
2\times 10^{4}
datapoints. The model is trained for 300 epochs with a batch size of 128 on a single NVIDIA A100 MIG device, with the full training taking approximately three hours.
