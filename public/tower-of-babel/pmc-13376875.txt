Production and characterization of gigastasin, a leech-derived inhibitor of complement and coagulation pathways
Authors: Aleksandra Blagojevic, Peter Rüthemann, Kevin Widmer, Marco Mannes, Dilara Caglar, Mateja Tiric, Mathew Ebin Sovichan, Richard B Pouw, Said Rabbani, Markus Huber-Lang, Daniel Ricklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complement and coagulation systems protect against injury and infection, but ill-controlled activation may cause clinical complications. Despite their efficacy in defined indications, approved complement and coagulation inhibitors remain poorly effective in complex thromboinflammatory disorders. Given their ability to broadly impair host defense, parasitic inhibitors provide a promising therapeutic avenue. Gigastasin from the giant Amazon leech was identified as complement initiation inhibitor, but its structural complexity hitherto hampered large-scale production. Here, we report high-yield bacterial expression of active, stable gigastasin, including HPLC purification and lyophilization into endotoxin-free preparations. Recombinant gigastasin potently inhibited complement activation, favoring the lectin over classical pathway, with comparable activity in human, mouse, and monkey serum. Target protease profiling revealed strong selectivity for active C1s over its zymogen, weak binding to C1r, and no interaction with other complement proteases. Intriguingly, similar selectivity patterns were observed for coagulation proteases, ascribing gigastasin potent activity against factor XIa with weaker effect on factor XIIa. Functional assays confirmed that gigastasin impairs coagulation via the intrinsic but not extrinsic pathway. Our study thereby identifies gigastasin as dual complement and coagulation inhibitor, which, alongside scalable production, favorable stability, and broad species specificity, supports the translational potential of recombinant gigastasin in thromboinflammatory disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52102-9.Keywords: Complement inhibitor, Leech protein, Gigastasin, Serine proteases, C1sSubject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

As part of the innate host defense, the complement system surveils the human body for pathogenic cells and is involved in homeostasis, pathogen elimination, and immune mediation1–3. Complement responses are initiated upon detection of pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMP and DAMP, respectively) via distinct activation routes. In the classical pathway (CP), C1q recognizes surface-bound IgG and IgM antibodies and activates the associated serine proteases C1r and C1s. Similarly, pattern recognition receptors of the lectin pathway (LP), i.e., mannose-binding lectin (MBL), ficolins, and collectins, recognize carbohydrate signatures on cell surfaces and induce MBL-associated serine proteases (i.e., MASP-1 and -2)4. Proteases of both pathways induce the downstream activation of the complement cascade by cleaving C4 and C2 to form C3 convertases on target surfaces, which subsequently activate C3 to deposit the opsonin C3b. The alternative pathway (AP) contributes to complement initiation via low-level hydrolysis of C3, yet also fuels an amplification loop when surface-deposited C3b engages with factor B (FB) and factor D (FD) to form new C3 convertases and increase the C3b density on the activating surface. Opsonization enables effector functions such as phagocytosis and lysis via the membrane attack complex, whereas the release of anaphylatoxins promotes immuno-inflammatory responses.

Despite its protective functions, inappropriate complement activation on host cells is contributing to various immune and inflammatory diseases5,6. Several pathway-directed drugs have meanwhile reached the market or clinical development7; while a majority of these entities impair complement amplification or effector generation, complement initiation inhibitors remain scarce and include approved monoclonal antibodies (mAb) that block C1s (sutimlimab; Sanofi) or MASP-2 (narsoplimab; Omeros) . Moreover, approved inhibitors are predominantly positioned for indications that represent well-defined complementopathies of the rare-disease spectrum, thereby leaving therapeutic needs for prevalent yet complex disorders7.

Alongside complement, coagulation represents another disease-relevant host defense system driven by cascading serine protease activation. Whereas current anticoagulants typically target the common pathway by blocking thrombin or activated factor Xa (FXa), the intrinsic pathway recently gained therapeutic interest due to the concept of ‘hemostasis-thrombosis uncoupling’ that assigns the intrinsic pathway a major role in thrombosis and the extrinsic pathway in maintenance of hemostasis8. Activated factors XI and XII (FXIa and FXIIa, respectively) are the central serine proteases of the intrinsic pathway; on damaged surfaces, FXII can be converted to FXIIa, which subsequently activates FXI to enable downstream thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation. While contributing to bleeding control, intrinsic pathway activation is primarily implicated in pathological thrombosis. Elevated FXIa levels have been associated with increased risks of venous thromboembolism, ischemic stroke, and myocardial infarction, whereas FXI-deficient individuals show bleeding rates similar to those without the deficiency9. FXIIa contributes to thrombus formation not only by generating FXIa but also by activating the kallikrein-kinin system to promote inflammation and vascular permeability10. An anti-FXIIa antibody has recently been approved (garadacimab, CSL), while specific inhibitors for FXIa (e.g., milvexian, BMS) are in late-stage clinical evaluation9.

Simultaneous activation of complement and coagulation drives thromboinflammation in conditions like ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), where hypoxia and DAMP exposure trigger defense cascades after blood flow restoration, potentially impairing organ function or causing complications11. Broad inhibition of involved effector pathways is considered promising, yet difficult to achieve with monospecific inhibitors currently in the clinic. Preparations of C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH), a physiological regulator with broad activity against serine proteases of the complement, coagulation, and contact cascades, are clinically available but only approved to treat hereditary angioedema12,13. Moreover, C1-INH is challenging to produce and is either isolated from donor plasma or recombinantly produced in transgenic rabbits. The treatment of IRI and other acute-phase conditions may therefore benefit from serine protease inhibitors with tuned specificity profiles against complement and coagulation pathways alongside facilitated production.

In this regard, parasites can be considered experts in host defense evasion and possess various complement- and/or coagulation-targeted inhibitors that may serve as templates or inspiration for therapeutic development14–16. An intriguing example is gigastasin, a protease inhibitor from the giant Amazon leech (Haementeria ghilianii) with reported activities against complement and, potentially, coagulation enzymes. Gigastasin (haemostasin, BD001), was initially identified as potent C1s inhibitor that blocks CP activation, while an impact on FXIIa was suggested but not fully investigated17,18. A later study revealed that gigastasin also blocks LP activation by inhibiting MASP-1 and -2, and provided a crystal structure of gigastasin in complex with C1s19. While these studies imply a broader involvement of gigastasin in host defense evasion, many questions regarding its mode-of-action or target selectivity remain unanswered. Herein, we describe the preparative and reliable recombinant expression of gigastasin in E. coli in a pure and active form, elucidate its stability, species specificity, and target selectivity profile, and evaluate the inhibitory efficacy of recombinant gigastasin toward the complement and coagulation cascades.

Results

Gigastasin can be produced in prokaryotic systems at high yield and purity

In previous studies, gigastasin was either isolated from the salivary glands of the giant Amazon leech or recombinantly expressed in eukaryotic systems17–19. This approach has likely been guided by the protein sequence that contains 20 cysteine residues forming 10 disulfide bridges, a glycosylation site, and 3 sulfotyrosine residues within a polypeptide chain of 122 amino acids. To facilitate large-scale production and structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies, we explored the prokaryotic expression of gigastasin by using E. coli strain Rosetta-gami 2(DE3) for enhanced disulfide bond formation20. The inclusion of an N-terminal 6xHis-tag, followed by a TEV protease cleavage site, in our construct (Fig. 1a) enabled single-step purification via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC; Fig. S1a). By optimizing expression conditions, soluble gigastasin could be extracted from the cytoplasmic fraction with an overall yield of ~ 1 mg/L culture when expressing at low temperature (18 °C) for 60 h. By isolating and refolding the remaining inclusion body fraction, the yield could be enhanced to 3–4 mg/L (Fig. 1b, Fig. S2). SDS-PAGE analysis, under reducing and non-reducing conditions, showed a pure protein fraction of the expected size (Fig. 1b). Mass spectrometry determined an exact molecular weight of 15,959 Da, which closely corresponds to the size of the gigastasin construct lacking posttranslational modifications (Fig. S3). To remove the His-tag, incubation of gigastasin with 6xHis-TEV protease, followed by IMAC purification, enabled the preparation of a pure, untagged gigastasin fraction at the expected size (Fig. 1c, Fig. S2).

Whereas disulfide-rich proteins are challenging to produce, they often offer favorable stability features21. To investigate whether this applies to recombinant gigastasin, we performed a thermal stability assay using nanoscale differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF), which monitors protein unfolding and aggregation by measuring the intrinsic fluorescence of aromatic residues over a temperature gradient22. The thermograms for gigastasin showed a change in the first derivative of the fluorescence ratio at ~ 40 °C that plateaued at ~ 60 °C, suggesting a transition of gigastasin to a new stable conformation in this temperature range. Importantly, this transition was fully reversible with the thermogram showing exactly mirroring curves during the heating and cooling ramps (Fig. 1e). The observed stability profile was reproducible in different buffer systems (i.e., HEPES, PBS, Tris) and for gigastasin that was preheated at 60 °C for 1 h. Only upon preheating at 90 °C, a loss of temperature-dependent fluorescence shifts was observed, presuming that the protein had irreversibly unfolded at high temperature. Initial long-term stability tests indicated that the thermal stability profile will likely translate into improved storage stability, since the C1s-inhibition activity of gigastasin was maintained for 30 days at 25 °C, whereas C1-INH lost activity under the same conditions (Fig. S4).

Although IMAC yielded pure gigastasin, residual impurities and endotoxin could limit applications in cell-based or in-vivo assays. Reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) was applied to remove these contaminants, leveraging the protein’s stability (Fig. S1b). The use of organic solvents also facilitated subsequent lyophilization to improve long-term storage and transport. SDS-PAGE analysis showed that protein prepared by this procedure was highly pure and homogeneous (Fig. 1b). More importantly, RP-HPLC reduced endotoxins levels from > 200 EU/mL to < 0.1 EU/mL, making the protein suitable for endotoxin-sensitive assays23,24.

Recombinant gigastasin acts as a potent inhibitor of C1s, FXIa, and FXIIa

Gigastasin had previously been identified as C1s inhibitor that prevents the cleavage of complement components C2 and C4 and, consequently, CP activation18,19. To validate the inhibitory activity of recombinant gigastasin against C1s in a quantitative manner, we employed a chromogenic substrate assay using plasma-purified C1s and a synthetic peptide substrate. Guided by structural insight19, a gigastasin derivative that lacks a key interaction residue for C1s binding (p.Arg65Gly; iGigastasin) was produced as negative control (Fig. S5). Whereas iGigastasin did not show activity in the tested concentration range, recombinant gigastasin dose-dependently inhibited the enzymatic activity of C1s (Fig. 2a). The inhibitory activity (IC50 ~ 20 nM; Table 1) was comparable to that reported for yeast-derived gigastasin (BD001; IC50 ~ 30 nM) and only slightly weaker than C1-INH (Fig. 2a; Table 1). The chromogenic substrate assay’s ease-of-use renders it ideal for in-process quality controls when producing recombinant gigastasin. By monitoring C1s-inhibition activity, we could show that the soluble cytoplasmic fraction of gigastasin had comparable activity to the refolded protein (Fig. 1d), that removal of the 6xHis tag did not impact inhibitory properties (Fig. S6), and that RP-HPLC was a suitable purification method. Intriguingly, the activity of gigastasin preparations were not only maintained after RP-HPLC purification, lyophilization, and reconstitution but even slightly improved (Fig. 1f), which may be attributed to the removal of residual impurities. Of note, and despite closely interacting with the active site of C1s, gigastasin itself does not seem to be cleaved upon target binding (Fig. S7a). In contrast, incubation of gigastasin with thrombin leads to a degradation of the inhibitor (Fig. S7b), as it has been suggested before18,19.

To confirm its mode of action, we tested whether gigastasin interferes with the conversion of its natural substrate by incubating plasma-purified C4 with C1s in presence and absence of inhibitors. SDS-PAGE analysis under reducing conditions showed native C4 as 3 bands that represent the α (97 kDa), β (75 kDa), and γ (33 kDa) chains (Fig. 2b). As expected, the C4 α-chain was cleaved into its α’ fragment (90 kDa) in presence of C1s. With increasing gigastasin concentrations, the C4 α’-chain band became weaker (Fig. 2b), confirming the inhibitory activity of recombinant gigastasin against C1s. C1-INH again showed a stronger potency with full inhibition of C4 cleavage at 1 µM, whereas residual levels of intact C4 were observed at equimolar gigastasin concentrations (Fig. 2b).

Finally, we investigated the impact of recombinant gigastasin on complement activation using pathway-specific ELISAs by measuring C3b deposition (i.e., opsonization) in comparison to therapeutic complement inhibitors or surrogates thereof. In the CP ELISA, gigastasin showed stronger dose-dependent inhibition when compared to C1-INH, while both inhibitors were less potent than a minibody surrogate of the anti-C1s mAb sutimlimab (Fig. 2c; Table 1). Neither iGigastasin nor a surrogate of the anti-MASP-2 mAb narsoplimab as LP inhibitor showed notable effects in the CP assay. As previous studies reported an LP-inhibitory activity of gigastasin19, we also performed a LP activation ELISA. Here, recombinant gigastasin showed stronger inhibitory activity than C1-INH, yet was not as potent as the anti-MASP-2 mAb (Fig. 2d; Table 1); iGigastasin and the anti-C1s minibody served as negative controls in this assay. These findings confirm the complement inhibitory potential of recombinant gigastasin with a preference for the LP over the CP.

When compared to gigastasin’s well-documented complement inhibition properties, a proposed impact of the leech protein on the coagulation system has not been sufficiently validated, experimentally18. We therefore employed a panel of chromogenic or fluorogenic substrate assays to assess the activity of gigastasin toward proteases of the coagulation and contact system. In contrast to the clinically exploited protein hirudin from the medical leech Hirudo medicinalis, gigastasin was not able to inhibit thrombin (Fig. S8) and was also found to be inactive in the plasma kallikrein (PKa) and FXa assays (Fig. 3a&d). Whereas bovine FXa had to be used as surrogate for human FXa for the main studies, control experiments with the human protease confirmed that FXa should not be considered a target of gigastasin (Fig. S9). Although we could experimentally confirm a previously suggested activity for FXIIa18, the observed inhibitory potency was comparatively weak (IC50 = 2.8 ± 1.5 µM; Fig. 3b). Importantly, our assay panel identified gigastasin as a highly potent inhibitor of the intrinsic pathway protease FXIa (IC50 = 9.7 ± 1.6 nM) with an activity in a similar range as for C1s (Fig. 3c).

To validate this finding, and explore the possibility that recombinant gigastasin may act as functional coagulation inhibitor, we employed rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) assays to assess blood clot formation upon initiation of the intrinsic or extrinsic coagulation pathway.25 In agreement with the observed target activity against FXIa and FXIIa, ROTEM results show that recombinant gigastasin impairs intrinsic pathway-induced clot formation, starting at a concentration of 1.25 µM and reaching full inhibition at 10 µM (Fig. 3e&f, Fig. S10). Importantly, no relevant effect was detected for extrinsic pathway conditions (Fig. 3e); alongside excluding the extrinsic pathway as functional target of gigastasin’s anticoagulant activity, the results also provide indirect evidence that gigastasin does not interfere with proteases of the common pathway, i.e., FXa and thrombin, as the readouts of both ROTEM studies depend on the integrity of the common pathway.

Recombinant gigastasin shows distinct target selectivity and species specificity profiles

Gigastasin inhibits the CP by binding active C1s, which is generated when C1r cleaves the inactive zymogen form, pro-C1s, within the C1 complex upon activation26. Whereas the binding between gigastasin and C1s had been described in atomic detail19, a potential target selectivity for C1s over pro-C1s has not yet been investigated. We therefore used surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to analyze the interaction of gigastasin with C1s and its zymogen. Recombinant gigastasin bound with high affinity to C1s (apparent KD = 118.5 nM), whereas no binding to the pro-C1s zymogen was observed (Fig. 4a&b). Despite slight deviations from a Langmuir 1:1 binding model, which may be attributed to surface heterogeneity upon protein immobilization, the SPR assay demonstrates gigastasin’s target selectivity towards C1s over pro-C1s. This finding was supported by structural analysis of C1s-bound gigastasin and free pro-C1s (Fig. 4c&d)19,27. Cleavage of the autolysis loop converts the zymogen into active C1s; this induces pronounced conformational rearrangements but leaves the two segments connected via disulfide bridges. The newly generated N-terminus (Ile-438) forms a stabilizing interaction with an Asp residue located adjacent to the substrate-binding site, thereby modulating the binding pocket and interfering with substrate binding. In particular, loops 1, 2 and D (according to the nomenclature by Perona and Craik28, which are relevant for the binding of gigastasin, undergo significant displacement (Fig. 4c&d)19,27. In pro-C1s, loop 2 obstructs the catalytic triad (i.e., Ser-632, His-475, Asp-529) in the active center; it also interferes with the formation of a critical salt bridge between Arg-65 of gigastasin and Asp-626 of C1s (Fig. 4c)19. The placement of loops 1 and D in pro-C1s further impairs the proper target interaction of gigastasin via Trp-17 in the N-terminal lobe, which acts as an important binding anchor for C1s. Molecular dynamics simulations indicated that gigastasin’s N-terminal region already dissociates from pro-C1s within the first 10 ns while the binding to active C1s remains stable (Fig. S11). This is consistent with the experimental observation that pro-C1s cannot act as a binding partner of gigastasin.

Direct binding assays further elucidated the target selectivity of gigastasin for various complement and coagulation factors. When employing SPR analysis, no notable interaction was observed for gigastasin with complement proteases C2, FB, and FD nor with contact/coagulation proteases PKa and FXa (Fig. 5). Conversely, and in agreement with the substrate cleavage assays, a strong interaction was observed in the case of FXIa and FXIIa (Table 2 and Fig. 5); the slower dissociation rate of FXIa when compared to FXIIa may explain the potency difference observed for inhibitory activity. Within the complement protease panel, binding signals were also observed for C1r, albeit at much lower intensity and with faster dissociation rate than for C1s, FXIa, or FXIIa (Fig. 5). While the distinct kinetic profile and dose-dependency indicate a specific binding event, the validity and biological relevance of the C1r-gigastasin interaction warrants further investigation. Overall, our target selectivity screening underscores the versatility of gigastasin, capable of modulating the complement’s classical and lectin pathways and the intrinsic coagulation pathway without broadly inhibiting other serine proteases.

So far, the complement-modulatory activity of gigastasin has only been shown in human systems18,19. As a subsequent use in biomedical or preclinical studies would involve animal models, we evaluated the complement-inhibitory activity of recombinant gigastasin in serum of relevant species, i.e., mouse (BALB/c) and non-human primates (NHP; cynomolgus monkey). Sequence analysis revealed similarity scores of at least 70% between the serine protease domains of C1s in different species (Fig. S12a). We utilized the published co-crystal structure of human C1s and gigastasin to identify amino acids that are involved in the binding of gigastasin (Fig. 6a)19. For NHP C1s, only 3 out of 59 interacting amino acids are not identical, whereas 18 residues differ between human and mouse C1s. When considering the large binding interface between gigastasin and C1s, and an anticipated flexibility in interactions, these dissimilarities were expected to have a limited impact on target binding. This structural hypothesis was confirmed in vitro when performing CP inhibition assays in animal sera: gigastasin inhibited CP-mediated C3b deposition both in 1% mouse serum (IC50 = 18.3 ± 10.8 nM) and 1% NHP serum (IC50 = 35.3 ± 13.5 nM; Fig. 6b). The same species tolerance was observed in the LP inhibition assay, where gigastasin prevented C3b deposition in mouse (IC50 = 2.1 ± 1.9 nM) and NHP serum (IC50 = 24.3 ± 11.5 nM; Fig. 6c). While the absence of co-crystal structures between MASPs and gigastasin prevents a molecular corroboration of the species specificity profile for LP inhibition, the high sequence similarity between human, NHP, and mouse MASP-2 (Fig. S12b&c) supports the experimental findings.

It is important to note that a direct comparison of absolute IC50 values between species should be avoided due to experimental assay differences and immune response variations. Nevertheless, it is intriguing to observe that the CP activity of gigastasin appears to be markedly improved for both NHP and mice when compared to human serum (Figs. 6b&c and 2c&d, respectively). Similarly, mouse activity was notably enhanced and NHP inhibition comparable to human serum in the LP assay. In contrast, human C1-INH showed reduced potency in both NHP assays and lacked activity in mouse serum (Fig. S13). Overall, our results demonstrate a broad species specificity of gigastasin and indicate its translational potential in complement inhibition studies (Fig. S14a&b).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to provide insight into the functional properties of the leech-derived inhibitor gigastasin, fill knowledge gaps about its molecular mechanisms, and explore whether recombinant forms can be produced in yields, purity, and activity suitable for biomedical and preclinical studies. Latter interest stems from unmet medical needs to prevent or mitigate acute thromboinflammatory complications as, for example, observed in IRI during stroke, myocardial infarction, or transplantation7,30,31, but also in sepsis or COVID-197,29,30. Given the dynamic involvement of multiple host defense pathways in such conditions, treatment options that broadly impair host‑defense‑mediated tissue damage may improve treatment success6,31,32. Parasite‑derived evasion proteins appear promising in this respect, as they target key pathways driving thromboinflammation and may offer benefits regarding tissue penetration or recombinant production16,33. The leech-derived thrombin inhibitor hirudin serves as best-known example for successful clinical translation from parasite inhibitor to approved drug34, and both the tick-derived complement C5 inhibitor nomacopan and the insect FXIIa inhibitor infestin-4 were considered clinical candidates35,36. Therapeutic potential was likewise suggested for gigastasin after its C1s activity was disclosed18 but, to our knowledge, no preclinical development has been attempted19,20. Similarly, functional studies on this leech protein have remained scarce18,19, and several of its observed or proposed activities (e.g., regarding C1r or FXIIa inhibition) have not been validated or described on a molecular level.

Among the reasons for the limited progress are the restricted access to native gigastasin and challenges with its recombinant production. Functional gigastasin was previously obtained from baculovirus or yeast expression, though yields and purities were not disclosed18,19. Recombinant production in mammalian cells has not been reported and corresponding attempts were not successful in our hands.19,20 Considering its disulfide-rich, sulfated, and glycosylated structure, a production of gigastasin in bacteria appears uncommon; if successful, however, it can provide advantages regarding scalable production and easy access to mutagenesis or isotope labeling for biomedical and structural studies. Importantly, posttranslational modifications were shown to be not essential for gigastasin’s activity18, and disulfide-bridge formation can be improved by optimizing expression conditions. In this study, the selection of a redox‑competent E. coli strain and expression at low temperature and extended culture time proved successful to obtain soluble protein at yields suitable for experimental studies. Remarkably for a disulfide-rich protein, yields could even be improved from 1 to 3–4 mg/L by refolding the inclusion-body fraction. While this study focused on establishing a reliable prokaryotic expression of gigastasin, yields could be further improved in future optimization trials, e.g., by varying strains, constructs, and/or conditions.

A two-step purification process based on IMAC and RP-HPLC proved efficient for obtaining pure and endotoxin-free gigastasin preparations. The placement of the 6xHis-tag at the protein’s N-terminus did not notably impact C1s-inhibitory activity, in agreement with structural insight that N‑terminal residues are less engaged in C1s interaction than the C-terminal tail19. While IMAC already resulted in pure protein, the exceptional thermal stability of recombinant gigastasin suggested that the protein may withstand RP-HPLC purification in organic solvents. The successful implementation of RP‑HPLC in the protocol not only served as polishing step to remove residual protein impurities but also profoundly reduced endotoxin levels to < 0.1 EU/mL, a threshold acceptable for biological drugs23. Finally, HPLC enabled lyophilization of recombinant gigastasin preparations, which, alongside the thermal stability, provide optimal conditions for transportation and long-term storage.

Chromogenic substrate assays served as quick and accessible method to assess C1s inhibition during in-process control and product characterization. The assay also confirmed the impact of posttranslational sulfation (Tyr‑117/119/121), which was reported to enhance C1s binding ~ 10‑fold19. Indeed, our prokaryotic protein closely matched the reported activity of yeast‑derived, non-sulfated gigastasin but was weaker than the native leech protein18. When reporting activity profiles for gigastasin, the production variant should therefore always be stated. It should be considered, however, that a direct quantitative comparison of activities across studies has limited power, especially when different readout parameters are involved (i.e., IC50 vs. Ki). On a functional level, quantitative complement activation assays confirmed gigastasin’s dual-pathway activity and revealed a ~ 25-fold selectivity for LP inhibition over CP inhibition. While this selectivity may depend on the sulfation status, previous studies with baculovirus-derived gigastasin also observed an LP preference in semiquantitative assays19. As expected, gigastasin did not match the potency of clinical anti-C1s and anti-MASP-2 antibody surrogates but showed 10-fold stronger LP inhibition and 2-fold stronger CP inhibition when compared to C1-INH.

The profound selectivity of gigastasin for active C1s over its zymogen has never been shown experimentally but is an important descriptor for the protein’s mode-of-action and affects inhibitory efficacy. For example, sutimlimab’s binding to pro-C1s contributes to the high dosing requirement, and next-generation anti-C1s mAb with selectivity for the active form are in clinical development37. Structural and computational analyses support the finding by predicting a minuscule probability for the formation of stable gigastasin:pro-C1s complexes. Previous studies showed that cleavage of pro-C1s displaces self-inhibitory loops to provide substrate access to the catalytic site38; the crystal structure confirmed that several loop residues engaged in contacts with gigastasin in active C1s are displaced in pro-C1s19. The weak-yet-notable binding of gigastasin to C1r supports previous, indirect observations18 and is intriguing when considering that other immune evasion proteins target C1r. Whether this dual targeting of CP proteases is functionally relevant warrants further investigation. Finally, the lack of activity for alternative pathway proteases corroborates that gigastasin interferes at the level of complement initiation rather than amplification. Overall, our studies confirm potent complement-directed activities of gigastasin, rendering it a rare, if not unique, example of leech-derived complement inhibitors. While the reasons of this property remain speculative, H. ghilianii’s reliance on a needle-like proboscis that is exposed to tissue fluids during blood collection could provide a rationale39.

Gigastasin’s impact on the coagulation cascade appears less surprising in the context of other leech-derived protease inhibitors40–43, but has never been experimentally explored. Despite sharing structural features with hirudin, such as a sulfotyrosine-containing tail region19,44, we did not observe relevant thrombin activity. Similarly, gigastasin did not inhibit FXa, another therapeutic target of the common pathway. Of note, bovine FXa was used as surrogate for human FXa throughout the study due to technical reasons. The high sequence homology between the proteins, particularly around the S1 pocket, and the activity of the clinical FXa inhibitor rivaroxaban as positive control suggest that bovine FXa serves as relevant selectivity probe. Indeed, limited tests with human FXa support the classification of FXa as non-target for gigastasin. Most importantly, any relevant inhibitory activity of gigastasin against human FXa or thrombin would have translated to extrinsic-pathway impairment in ROTEM assays, the readout of which depends on common pathway activity. ROTEM analysis thus not only served to functionally validate gigastasin’s anticoagulant activity but also delineate underlying mechanisms, excluding FVIIa of the extrinsic pathway, which acts in complex with tissue factor45, and FXa and thrombin of the common pathway as relevant serine-protease targets for gigastasin.

However, our study revealed a profound selectivity for key enzymes of the intrinsic pathway by potently blocking FXIa and, more weakly, FXIIa. Whereas FXIIa activity was previously suggested but not corroborated with experimental data18, the strong interaction with and inhibition of FXIa has not yet been described. The combination of assays established that gigastasin’s anticoagulant properties are intrinsic pathway-selective and mediated via inhibition of both FXIa and FXIIa. Our current selectivity panel did not include activated factor IX (FIXa) that is positioned downstream in the intrinsic pathway; although gigastasin’s strong effect can be largely attributed to FXIIa/FXIa inhibition, potential contributions by FIXa impairment cannot be excluded and remain to be assessed in extended selectivity studies. When compared to ROTEM values in literature,46 recombinant gigastasin appears to be more potent than C1-INH in impairing intrinsic pathway-mediated clot formation,44 though this warrants validation. Gigastasin’s selectivity for the intrinsic over the extrinsic and common pathways may align with the concept of hemostasis-thrombosis uncoupling, which builds on observations that intrinsic pathway inhibition may prevent early-stage thrombosis with minimal interference in hemostatic processes9,47. Though FXIa inhibitors are in clinical development, none has reached the market and some trials were halted due to a lack of efficacy48. It could be interesting to explore whether dual inhibition of FXIa and FXIIa may provide a therapeutic advantage over monotherapies.

Even compared to other parasite-derived host defense modulators, the broad-yet-selective target profile of gigastasin with hitherto 6 confirmed proteases is remarkable. Intriguingly, the leech protein consistently shows tandem inhibition of initiation proteases, with stronger activity for the downstream enzyme, of the classical (C1r < C1s), lectin (MASP-1 < MASP-2) and intrinsic pathways (FXIIa < FXIa)18. Regarding target spectrum, C1-INH may be considered the closest match to gigastasin12,30; while sharing the above-mentioned targets, C1-INH also interferes with PKa and tissue plasminogen activator function49. Despite similar selectivity, structural insights reveal different target interaction modes50: superimposition of the C1s: gigastasin and C1s: C1-INH structures shows overlap between areas covering the active site of C1s, but each molecule assumes a distinct orientation (Fig. S14). Moreover, C1-INH is known to act as covalent active-site inhibitor, whereas gigastasin binds reversibly and covers both the catalytic center and exosite of C1s12,19,50.

C1-INH preparations are approved for treating C1-INH-deficient patients suffering from hereditary angioedema, but an extended use in thromboinflammatory conditions showed limited efficacy51. This may be related to the fact that, in contrast to supplementing a missing protein in hereditary angioedema, providing C1-INH in other indications merely augments plasma levels of the endogenous regulator. In contrast, gigastasin is expected to benefit from its distinct binding mode as exogenous inhibitor, and its rather small size (< 20 kDa) compared to the C1-INH glycoprotein (> 100 kDa) may facilitate tissue penetration. Moreover, our study suggests an improved stability of gigastasin over C1-INH and easier recombinant production. In contrast, a small exogenous protein like gigastasin will likely face challenges regarding pharmacokinetic properties and immunogenicity. Even if such parameters are of less concern for the short-term treatment of acute-phase conditions in clinical settings, and other parasite-derived inhibitors such as lepirudin or nomacopan showed low immunogenicity33,52,53, these aspects need to be carefully assessed in future in-vivo studies before defining gigastasin’s therapeutic potential. The improved production and characterization in this study provides an important stepping stone, and the observed species tolerance with comparable complement inhibition in human, mouse, and non-human primate serum will facilitate translational studies. The species specificity profile of gigastasin reflects an evolutionary advantage, especially when considering that H. ghilianii is reported to feed on rodents (capybaras), reptiles (e.g., anacondas), and mammals (e.g., cattle)39,54. To our knowledge, this is the first report of gigastasin’s activity beyond human serum; and while cross-species comparisons should be interpreted cautiously55, the comparable activity-selectivity profiles support the description of gigastasin as complement inhibitor with broad species tolerance. Owing to their prevalence in IRI models, future assessments may include rats and pigs as relevant species, and it remains to be tested whether gigastasin’s species tolerance extends to anticoagulant properties.

Conclusion

In this study, we extended the perception of leech-derived protein gigastasin as potent and versatile host‑defense modulator. By combining interaction and inhibition analyses with functional assays, we could not only confirm gigastasin as complement modulator and shed light into its mode-of-action, but for the first time establish gigastasin a potent inhibitor of the intrinsic coagulation pathway. This also renders gigastasin an intriguing candidate for the development of therapeutic host-defense modulators to treat acute thromboinflammatory conditions. While additional studies are required to assess gigastasin’s therapeutic potential, the reliable prokaryotic production of active, pure, and endotoxin-free recombinant protein, the observed high stability and ease-of-lyophilization, and the broad species tolerance established in this study, suggest that recombinant gigastasin is a suitable candidate for preclinical assessment in relevant animal models.

Methods

Commercial proteins, antibodies, and reagents

C1r (active enzyme), C1s (zymogen and active enzyme), C2, C4, factor B (FB), and factor D (FD) were purchased from Complement Technology (Tyler, TX, USA). Coagulation enzymes included active forms of bovine factor X (FXa; New England Biolabs; P8010), human factor Xa (Merck, 233526), human factors XI and XII (FXIa, FXIIa; Innovative Research; IHUFXIA, IHUFXIIAA), and human plasma kallikrein (PKa; Sigma-Aldrich; K2638). C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) preparations were kindly provided by Sanquin (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and CSL Behring (Bern, Switzerland). The anti-C1s antibody (AH803; sutimlimab surrogate) was produced by the Geneva Antibody Facility (Geneva, Switzerland) in a minibody format with two scFv fused to a human IgG1 Fc part. The inhibitory anti-MASP2 mAb (narsoplimab surrogate) was purchased from BioVision (Milpitas, USA; No. A2203-100). The reference inhibitors asundexian (T39463, TargetMol), nafamostat (HY-B0190A, MedChemExpress), avoralstat (HY-16735, MedChemExpress), and rivaroxaban (Y0002187, Merck) were prepared as 5 mM DMSO stock solutions.

Normal human serum (NHS) used throughout this study was obtained by pooling sera of five anonymous healthy donors (AB+ blood group), provided by the Blood Donor Center Basel (Blutspende SRK Nordwestschweiz, Basel), collected with informed consent according to the local ethics committee and the Declaration of Helsinki. The NHS pool was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until use, thereby avoiding repetitive freeze-thawing. All assays involving human or animal blood products have been carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Recombinant expression and purification of soluble gigastasin

The DNA sequence encoding gigastasin18was codon-optimized for E. coli expression and synthesized at ATG: biosynthetics (Merzhausen, Germany). The construct encoding a N-terminal 6xHis tag followed by a cleavage sequence for tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease (Fig. 1a) was generated by PCR and ligated into a pET15b vector (Novagene, Lucerne, Switzerland) between NcoI and BamHI restriction sites prior amplification in E. coli DH5α (Table S1). A second construct in which Arg-65 was substituted by Gly-65 using site-directed mutagenesis to generate an inactive variant of gigastasin (iGigastasin) was similarly cloned (Fig. S5). After DNA minipreparation, construct correctness was verified by double-stranded DNA sequencing (Microsynth, Switzerland). To achieve soluble protein fractions at high yield and purity, several production parameters were tested, optimized and evaluated. These included several E. coli strains (BL21, RIPL, T7 shuffle, pLys, Rosetta-gami 2(DE3)), temperatures (18, 22, 30, 37 °C), IPTG concentrations (0.1 mM – 1 mM), culture times (1–3 days), as well as culture media and add-ons (e.g., ethanol). Both the amount of protein in the soluble cytoplasmic fraction and the presence of impurities were assessed (data not shown). The following production protocol is based on selecting most-suitable parameters. Plasmids were transformed into a chemo-competent E. coli strain (Rosetta-gami 2(DE3)) and grown in 3 × 1 L Lysogeny broth (LB) medium at 37 °C and 150 rpm. Upon reaching an OD600 of 0.8-1.0, cells were cooled to 18 °C, supplemented with isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Applichem, Darmstadt, Germany) to a final concentration of 1 mM and further grown for 60 h at 18 °C and 150 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation for 25 min at 5,300 × g and 4 °C (rotor: Fiberlite F9-6 × 1000), resuspended in binding buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 40 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and lysed using a cell homogenizer (FPG12800, Homogenising Systems, Harlow UK) at 1 bar pressure twice. The lysate was centrifuged at 15,500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred into ultracentrifugation tubes, centrifuged at 17,900 × g (rotor: Thermo Scientific; A27-8 × 50; 22,000 rpm) for 30 min at 4 °C and dialyzed against binding buffer overnight at 4 °C in dialysis tubes with a 7-kDa molecular weight cut-off (Roth, Switzerland). The sample was applied on a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (Cytiva) attached to an FPLC system (Äkta Pure, Cytiva, Switzerland). After a washing step with binding buffer the protein was eluted with 70% elution buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 400 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and the purity was analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

A functionally inactive gigastasin variant (iGigastasin, p.Arg65Gly) was expressed and purified as described for wildtype gigastasin above.

Refolding and purification of non-soluble gigastasin fractions

The insoluble fraction obtained during recombinant gigastasin expression was resuspended in 25 mL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mg/mL lysozyme, 50 µg/mL DNAse I) while stirring at 150 rpm and 4 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the suspension was centrifuged at 15,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The inclusion bodies were washed 3 times with 20 mL ice-cold washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 4 M urea, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA); each wash was followed by centrifugation at 8,200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was solubilized in 10 mL denaturation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 7 M guanidine hydrochloride, 1 mM DTT), shaken for 60 min at room temperature and 150 rpm, and centrifuged at 17,900 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was slowly added dropwise to 100 mL refolding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 9.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.75 M arginine, 6 mM reduced glutathione, 3 mM oxidized glutathione, 5 mM EDTA). The mixture was stirred at 100 rpm and 4 °C for 48 h. The refolded protein solution was dialyzed against 2 L dialysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 9.0, 300 mM NaCl) twice, each for 3–4 h; the third dialysis was performed against 2 L of binding buffer for 4 h. After dialysis, the solution was centrifuged at 17,900 × g for 60 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was filtered and applied on a 5 mL HisTrap HP column attached to an FPLC system (Äkta Pure, Cytiva). The column was washed with 12 column volumes of washing buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4 pH 8, 300 mM NaCl, 112 mM imidazole) and protein was eluted with buffer containing 292 mM imidazole. The eluted protein fractions were pooled and dialyzed against 2 L PBS using tubes with 7-kDa MW cut-off (SnakeSkin). The dialyzed sample was centrifuged for 1 h at 17,900 × g to remove aggregated or misfolded protein. The final purification was performed using RP-HPLC (see below). Of note, although the functional activity of refolded gigastasin was assessed and found to be comparable to the protein from the soluble cytoplasmic fraction, the two fractions were not combined and “gigastasin” used in this study typically refers to protein purified from the soluble cytoplasmic fraction.

Proteolytic removal of 6xHis-tag

To test whether the 6xHis-tag may interfere with functional activity, gigastasin samples after IMAC purification (100 µL, 1 mg/mL) were incubated with 6xHis-tagged TEV protease (10 µL, 100 µg/mL; kindly provided by Dr. R. Jakob (Biozentrum, University of Basel) in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT at 25 °C for 1 h. The mixture was diluted in 20 mL binding buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8) and applied to a Ni2+-NTA column. The column was washed with binding buffer and eluted with elution buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, pH 8). The flowthrough and eluted fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE under non-reducing conditions. Subsequently, the flowthrough containing tag-less gigastasin was concentrated using membrane filtration tubes with a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff, during which the buffer was exchanged to PBS.

Molecular characterization of recombinant gigastasin

The purity of recombinant gigastasin was verified by SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis. Samples were loaded on a 4–20% SDS-PAGE gradient gel (BioRad, Switzerland) under reducing or non-reducing conditions. After separation, gels were either stained with Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 for direct analysis or transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva) for western blot analysis. After the transfer, the membrane was blocked with 1% blocking reagent (Roche, Basel) in PBS overnight at 4 °C and gentle shaking. After washing with PBST (PBS pH 7.4 containing 0.1% Tween 20; Sigma, Switzerland), an anti-6xHis mAb (1:3000; Clone: His.H8, Invitrogen) in PBS containing 1% western blocking reagent was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature under gentle shaking. The membrane was washed with PBST, an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody (1:10,000; Sigma) in 1% western blocking reagent was added and incubated for 20 min at room temperature and gentle shaking. The membrane was washed again with PBST and the signal was visualized after addition of TMB substrate solution (Thermo Scientific). Gels and membranes were documented using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XS instrument with Image Lab software (v6.1; Bio-Rad).

The identity of the protein was further confirmed by electrospray mass spectrometry (ESI-MS; Functional Genomics Center, Zurich, Switzerland)56 in a mass range of 50-4000 Da with the sampling cone energy set at 40 V. The m/z data were deconvoluted into MSdata using the MaxEnt1 Software with an output mass resolution of 0.5 Da per channel 2/3 and a uniform gaussian damage model at the half height of 0.7 Da. As a theoretical reference, the molecular weight of gigastasin in reduced and non-reduced form was calculated with the Expasy Compute pI/Mw tool (www.expasy.org) based on the amino acid sequence of the recombinant protein.

Thermal stability assessment

The thermal stability of gigastasin was analyzed using nano-differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF; Prometheus NT.48, Nanotemper) in high-sensitivity quartz capillaries20. Thermal cycles were set at 1 °C/min increments from 20 °C to 75 °C and then back to 20 °C. Samples were prepared in Tris, HEPES and PBS buffers at pH 7.4 and protein concentrations of 0.3 mg/mL. Additionally, gigastasin samples were preheated to 60–90 °C for 1 h and included in the stability study. Each sample was measured in triplicates in two separate experiments, and the data was analyzed using Prism (v10, Graphpad).

RP-HPLC purification and lyophilization of gigastasin preparations

Affinity-purified gigastasin fractions potentially containing endotoxin and residual protein impurities were pooled and applied to reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC; Agilent, California USA) using a C8 column (ZORBAX 300 SB, 5 μm; Agilent, California USA). Separation was achieved with a linear gradient of 5 to 95% solvent B, 0.1% trifluoracetic acid (TFA) in acetonitrile in solvent A (0.1% TFA in water) over 22.5 min at a flow rate of 8.0 mL/min. Fractions were pooled, and lyophilization was carried out by flash freezing the sample in liquid N2 and drying on a Christ Alpha 2–4 LDplus freeze-dryer. To prepare for assays, lyophilized samples were resuspended in PBS, pH 7.4. If not used, protein preparations were frozen at -20 °C for long-term storage.

Measurement of endotoxin contamination

Endotoxin levels were determined under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions at the Hospital Pharmacy of the University Hospital Basel, Switzerland, using the EndoSafe nexgen PTS system (Charles River). Cartridges with a sensitivity of 0.05–5 EU/mL and 0.01–1 EU/mL were used for the measurements. Samples taken before RP-HPLC were diluted 1:1000 and measured in triplicates on a cartridge with 0.05–5 EU/mL sensitivity. After RP-HPLC, samples were diluted 1:10 and measured in triplicates on a cartridge with 0.01–1 EU/mL sensitivity.

Enzyme inhibition assays

The inhibitory activity of gigastasin toward several serine proteases of the complement and coagulation systems were evaluated using competitive enzyme inhibition assays. The chromogenic substrate format was based on previously reported assays (Table S2): first, a serial dilution (0.08–10 µM) of gigastasin was mixed in flat-bottom 96-well plates (PolySorp Nunc, Thermo Fischer Scientific) with one of the following active proteases: C1s, FXa, FXIa, FXIIa, PKa, and thrombin. The mixtures were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C prior the addition of the corresponding substrate; enzyme-specific parameters are listed in Table S2. The enzymatic assays included a 0% control (i.e., enzyme and inhibitor), a 100% control (i.e., enzyme and substrate), and a negative control (i.e., inhibitor and substrate) to assess the effect of the inhibitor on enzyme activity. In the case of FXIa, FXa and PKa, clinically relevant enzyme-specific inhibitors (asundexian, rivaroxaban and avoralstat, respectively) were included to validate the assay and compare activities; in other cases, the broadly active inhibitor nafamostat was used. The colorimetric reaction was measured at 405 nm on a Synergy HT plate reader (Biotek) at 37 °C for 30 min. FXIa activity was measured by monitoring fluorescence for 1 h, using an excitation wavelength of 375 nm and an emission wavelength of 445 nm. Assays were performed in triplicates with two technical replicates each. Every experiment has been normalized to the 100% and 0% controls. Curves were fitted using nonlinear regression to a normalized variable slope model in Prism (v10.3, GraphPad) to derive IC50 values.

Proteolytic stability of gigastasin

Whether gigastasin itself would be cleaved by active C1s upon target binding, recombinant gigastasin (10 µM) was incubated with C1s (464 nM) in PBS for 6.5 h at 37 °C. Samples were collected every 30 min (T0 – T11), heated to 99 °C for 5 min, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions. The previously reported susceptibility of gigastasin toward thrombin cleavage was confirmed in analogy by incubating recombinant gigastasin (10 µM) with thrombin (50 IU) and performing a time course analysis as described for C1s above.

C4 cleavage assay

To investigate the effect on natural substrate cleavage and account for both active site and exosite inhibition, we conducted a C4 cleavage assay as described before with minor adjustments 57,58. C1s (3 nM) and C4 (1.5 µM) were mixed and gigastasin was added at increasing concentrations (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0 µM) in PBS, pH 7.4. C1-INH at concentrations of 0.1 and 1 µM served as positive inhibition control. The cleavage reaction was performed at 37 °C for 1 h and stopped by the addition of 2x reducing SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Thermo Scientific) and incubation at 99 °C for 5 min on a thermomixer. Samples were loaded on a 4–20% SDS-PAGE gradient gel (Bio-Rad) and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue G-250. A positive (no inhibitor) and negative control (no C1s) were added alongside a protein standard marker (Bio-Rad). Gels were analyzed using a GelDocViever 3000 and Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Complement activation/inhibition and species specificity assays

The inhibitory activity of gigastasin toward the classical and lectin pathways of complement activation was determined by ELISA as described before59,60. Flat-bottom MaxiSorp 96-well plates (Thermo Fischer) were coated overnight at room temperature with 33 µg/mL IgM (Sigma) for classical pathway or 55 µg/mL mannan (Sigma) for lectin pathway activation, and plates were washed with PBST. Dilution series of gigastasin, iGigastasin, anti-C1s, anti-MASP-2, and C1-INH in 1% pooled normal human serum (NHS) were prepared in HBBT++ assay buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 containing 0.5% BSA, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% Twen 20) and samples were diluted 1:5 in 1% NHS. Negative controls included each inhibitor in assay buffer only, assay buffer only, and 1% (v/v) serum containing 10 mM EDTA, while HBBT++ containing 1% pooled NHS served as 100% control. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C and 700 rpm, plates were washed five times with PBST. 100 µL of a polyclonal biotinylated mouse anti-human C3b/iC3b antibody (1:1000; CEDARLANE) were added to each well and incubated at 700 rpm at RT for 1 h. Plates were washed five times with PBST. 100 µL of streptavidin conjugated with HRP (1:1000) (Invitrogen) were added to each well and incubated for 20 min at RT and 700 rpm. After incubation plates were washed five times with PBST and 100 µL of 1-Step™ Turbo TMB-ELISA solution (ThermoScientific) was added to each well. The reaction was left to develop until a light blue color was visible and stopped by addition of 100 µL 2 M sulfuric acid. The colorimetric readout was detected at 450 nm with a Synergy HT plate reader (Biotek).

To determine the inhibition potential of gigastasin for non-human primate and murine complement, plates were coated as described above and gigastasin was diluted 1:5 in 1% cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis) or BALB/c mouse serum, respectively, each obtained as commercial product (Innovative Research, USA). Gigastasin alone in assay buffer and 1% animal serum containing 10 mM EDTA served as 0% controls and HBBT++ containing 1% animal serum as 100% control. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C and 700 rpm, plates were washed five times with PBST. 100 µL of a polyclonal biotinylated mouse anti-human/mouse C3b/iC3b antibody (1:1000; CEDARLANE) was added to each well and incubated at 700 rpm at room temperature for 1 h. The rest of the ELISA was performed same as described above.

All assays were performed in triplicate and normalized to positive (no inhibitor, 1% serum in sample buffer) and negative (1% serum in EDTA buffer) controls. Inhibitory curves were fitted using nonlinear regression to a normalized variable slope model in Prism (v9, GraphPad).

Coagulation activation/inhibition in rotational thromboelastometry

To functionally evaluate the effect of gigastasin on the hemostatic system, rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM delta; Werfen GmbH, Munich, Germany) was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. After ethical approval (number #370/24, Local Independent Ethics Committee, University of Ulm) and obtaining written informed consent, citrated blood was drawn from healthy volunteers and exposed to increasing concentrations of gigastasin (1.25–10 µM); PBS was used as diluent and as negative control. Following re-calcification, specific activation of the extrinsic (EXTEM) or intrinsic (INTEM) pathway was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Target interaction studies

The interaction profiles between gigastasin and serine proteases of the complement system (C1r, pro-C1s, C1s, C2, FB, FD) and coagulation system (PKa, FXa, FXIa, FXIIa) was determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Experiments were performed on a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva) at 25 °C using research-grade CM5 sensor chips (Cytiva) and HBST (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% Tween 20) as running/sample buffer. Gigastasin (1 µM in 10 mM sodium acetate, pH 5) was immobilized using standard amine coupling; two flow cells were activated for 7 min with a 1:1 mixture of 0.1 M sulfo N-hydroxysuccinimide and 0.1 M 3-(N, N-dimethylamino)propyl-N’-ethylcarbodiimide at a flow rate of 10 µL/min. Gigastasin was immobilized on a single flow cell until a target density of ~ 650 RU was reached, after which both flow cells were blocked using 1 M ethanolamine, pH 8.0, for 7 min. To assess the gigastasin-target interactions, concentration series of each protease (0.47–30 nM for PKa (due to lower concentration of stock solution); 4.7–300 nM for other proteases) were injected for 300 s at a flow rate of 10 µL/min, followed by a dissociation phase of 600 s. After each injection cycle, the surface was regenerated using 0.1 M Na2CO3 for 60 s at a flow rate of 10 µL/min. Data was collected at 10 Hz, corrected for the molecular weight of the injected serine protease, evaluated using Biacore T200 Evaluation (v3.2.1, Cytiva), and plotted using Prism (v10; GraphPad).

Structural analysis of target binding

The 3D structures of active C1s in complex with gigastasin (PDB ID 5UBM19) and of unbound pro-C1s (PDB 4J1Y27) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and aligned in PyMOL (v 2.5.2, Schroedinger) to their catalytic triad (i.e., residues Ser-632, His-475, Asp-529). The electrostatic surface potential of the SP domain of C1s was calculated with the APBS plugin using default settings 61. PyMOL was used to visualize structures and create figures.

To investigate the dissociation of gigastasin from C1s, molecular dynamics simulations in explicit water were performed in the openMM software package and amber forcefield ff14SB62,63. Protein structures were prepared for the simulations by solvating them with the TIP3P water model in a cubic periodic boundary system, with a buffer of 10 Å between the protein atoms and the edge of the simulation box. To ensure that the systems were neutral, ions were added as necessary before relaxing and equilibrating the systems for 500 ps with 150 mM NaCl. To quantify the dissociation of gigastasin from C1s, the distance between the N-terminus or the core region of gigastasin and C1s was measured. These measurements were used to assess the stability of the complex and to identify potential dissociation events.
