Commentary on “Effectiveness of a Single‐Use Polyurethane Foam Positioning Pad Compared With Five‐Layer Foam Sacral Dressing for Pressure Ulcer Prevention in the Operating Room: A Randomized Controlled Clinical Trial”
Authors: Mostafa Javanian, Mohammad Barary, Soheil Ebrahimpour
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Elhihi and colleagues randomized 100 cardiac‐surgery patients to a single‐use polyurethane positioning pad versus a five‐layer sacral foam dressing and found a low overall pressure ulcer (PU) incidence (3%) with a nonsignificant difference between groups (6% vs. 0%) [1]. Their focus on intraoperative prevention is welcome. However, some methodological and interpretive issues temper inferences and policy relevance.

1.Analysis population and attrition: The CONSORT diagram shows 108 randomized, but only 100 analyzed. Exclusions post‐randomization (death, ECMO, short procedures < 4 h) indicate a per‐protocol analysis rather than intention‐to‐treat [2]. Excluding randomized patients can bias effect estimates, especially when exclusions are tied to surgical duration or clinical instability that also influence PU risk. A strict ITT analysis with sensitivity analyses would provide more credible estimates.

2.Open‐label design and outcome assessment: This study was necessarily unblinded, but the primary endpoint (incident PU over 7 days) included Stage I injuries and relied on visual assessment. Without blinded adjudication or inter‐rater reliability, detection bias is plausible. Adding objective co‐primary outcomes (e.g., device‐tracked repositioning exposure, pressure/immersion mapping) or blinded photographic adjudication could mitigate bias in future trials.

Open‐label design and outcome assessment: This study was necessarily unblinded, but the primary endpoint (incident PU over 7 days) included Stage I injuries and relied on visual assessment. Without blinded adjudication or inter‐rater reliability, detection bias is plausible. Adding objective co‐primary outcomes (e.g., device‐tracked repositioning exposure, pressure/immersion mapping) or blinded photographic adjudication could mitigate bias in future trials.

3.Handling of baseline and perioperative covariates: Although randomization helps, clinically meaningful imbalances remained (e.g., sex distribution, smoking history, BMI class). Moreover, perioperative exposures that shape PU risk, such as vasopressor use, cardiopulmonary bypass and cross‐clamp times, intraoperative temperature, positioning time, perfusion pressures, and glucose control, were not analyzed. A prespecified covariate‐adjusted model (or stratification by procedure duration and vasoactive exposure) would reduce residual confounding and improve precision.

Moreover, perioperative exposures that shape PU risk, such as vasopressor use, cardiopulmonary bypass and cross‐clamp times, intraoperative temperature, positioning time, perfusion pressures, and glucose control, were not analyzed. 4.Outcome window and clinical prominence: Follow‐up ended at 7 days. Deep‐tissue pressure injuries may declare after this window or evolve beyond Stage I [3]. Extending follow‐up to 14–30 days, reporting anatomic distribution and staging transitions, and distinguishing intraoperatively acquired versus ward‐acquired PUs would help clinicians judge downstream impact in future studies.

5.Economic analysis: The cost‐effectiveness analysis considered material costs only and interpreted a nonsignificant difference in clinical outcomes as economic dominance of the sacral dressing. Given event scarcity and short horizon, a cost‐minimization analysis might have been more appropriate.

Economic analysis: The cost‐effectiveness analysis considered material costs only and interpreted a nonsignificant difference in clinical outcomes as economic dominance of the sacral dressing. Given event scarcity and short horizon, a cost‐minimization analysis might have been more appropriate.

In summary, the trial adds useful data on intraoperative PU prevention, but per‐protocol analysis, unblinded subjective outcomes, limited power, sparse perioperative data, a short follow‐up window, and a simplified economic model limit the strength of the conclusions. A multicenter, adequately powered, intention‐to‐treat trial with blinded outcome adjudication, richer perioperative covariates, mechanism verification, and rigorous economic evaluation is warranted to determine whether positioning pads, sacral dressings, or their combination best prevent operating room‐related pressure injuries in cardiac surgery.
