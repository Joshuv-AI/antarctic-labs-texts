Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guideline for Diagnosis, Treatment, and Follow-Up of Keratoconus Patients
Authors: Andres Bustamante-Arias, Andres Rosas-Apraez, Carlos Alberto Restrepo, Martha Isabel Lizarazo, Douglas Tawse-Smith, Nestor I Carreño, Luis Daniel Holguin, Damien Gatinel, Martin Romero, Luis Gabriel Rocha, Andrea Diaz-Cely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKeratoconus is a progressive corneal ectatic disorder characterized by focal stromal thinning and conical protrusion, typically involving the central or paracentral cornea. Disease progression may lead to significant visual impairment and reduced quality of life. Reported global prevalence ranges from 0.17 to 40 cases per 1000 persons, reflecting differences in diagnostic criteria, imaging technologies, and study populations. Early detection, timely intervention, and risk-based follow-up are essential to limit progression and reduce visual morbidity. A systematic literature search did not identify existing clinical practice guidelines for keratoconus diagnosis and management with sufficient methodological quality to support adoption or adaptation. Therefore, a de novo evidence-based clinical practice guideline was developed. The objective was to provide recommendations for timely diagnosis, risk stratification, interventional treatment, and follow-up of pediatric and adult patients with keratoconus, aiming to standardize clinical criteria and support decision-making among healthcare professionals. The guideline was developed through systematic evidence synthesis and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to assess certainty of evidence and strength of recommendations. The evidence base included systematic reviews, diagnostic accuracy studies, randomized clinical trials, and observational studies, according to each clinical question. Recommendations addressed risk factors, diagnostic and staging criteria, progression assessment, corneal cross-linking protocols, intrastromal corneal ring segments, keratoplasty techniques, and individualized follow-up. Most recommendations were conditional, reflecting low or very low certainty of evidence in several domains. This guideline provides a practical framework for standardized keratoconus care while explicitly identifying areas where evidence remains limited and further research is needed.

Introduction

Keratoconus is characterized by focal stromal thinning and progressive central or paracentral corneal protrusion.1 The disease is typically bilateral, although frequently asymmetric, and may present with myopic, hyperopic, or mixed astigmatism. It was traditionally considered a disorder developing between puberty and 40 years of age.2 However, advances in corneal imaging have shown that keratoconus may manifest before puberty and that progression after 40 years of age is not uncommon. Reported prevalence varies widely according to geographic location, ethnicity, diagnostic criteria, and imaging modalities, ranging from 0.17 to 40 cases per 1000 persons.3,4 Early diagnosis, timely intervention, and appropriate follow-up are essential to limit disease progression and reduce visual morbidity.

Despite advances in corneal imaging, there is still no universally accepted definition of keratoconus progression. Existing staging systems vary in their reliance on anterior curvature, posterior elevation, pachymetry, biomechanical parameters, and visual function, which may lead to variability in disease classification and clinical decision-making.5 Although hyperopic astigmatism may be observed in early-stage or atypical presentations, it does not represent the typical refractive pattern of keratoconus progression.

This guideline provides evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis, interventional treatment, and follow-up of pediatric and adult patients with keratoconus. Its aim is to standardize clinical criteria, support evidence-informed decision-making, prevent progressive visual impairment, and optimize clinical outcomes. Optical rehabilitation was outside the predefined scope of this guideline.

Materials and Methods

The methodological standards established by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Working Group were followed for the development of this guideline.6,7 This guideline compiles available evidence on risk factors, diagnosis, interventional treatment, and follow-up of pediatric and adult patients with keratoconus. Optical rehabilitation, including spectacles and contact lens fitting, was outside the predefined scope because the prioritized objective was to address diagnostic criteria, disease progression, corneal imaging, and interventional or surgical management. The specific management of adverse events or treatment-related complications was also outside the scope, although safety outcomes and monitoring considerations were considered where relevant.

A systematic search of existing clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) on keratoconus was conducted. This search determined that none of the identified CPGs had sufficient methodological quality to support adoption or adaptation; therefore, a de novo clinical practice guideline was developed.

The development of the guideline involved two key groups: the guideline development group and an expert panel of ophthalmologists. The development group performed a systematic literature review (SLR) for each prioritized question. The databases consulted included MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CENTRAL. The expert panel defined the scope of the guideline, selected clinically relevant topics, formulated clinical questions, prioritized relevant outcomes, interpreted the results of the evidence synthesis, and actively participated in the formulation and review of recommendations, to ensure clinical relevance and applicability.

Systematic reviews in English or Spanish published within the previous five years were prioritized. In the absence of eligible evidence, the search strategy was systematically broadened to include primary studies or older evidence, as appropriate. The development group screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. For risk-of-bias assessment, validated instruments were used according to study design: Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) for systematic reviews, Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) for randomized clinical trials, Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) for diagnostic accuracy studies, and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies.8–11

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach, by outcome, considering the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Evidence profiles and summary of findings tables were prepared for the corresponding outcomes. Recommendation formulation was based on a structured synthesis of the clinical evidence and followed an evidence-to-decision framework.12

For each prioritized question, the panel considered the balance between desirable and undesirable effects, certainty of evidence, clinical applicability, feasibility, and variability in practice. These judgments were not anchored to any specific healthcare system or national context but were based on the global body of clinical evidence, with the aim of ensuring broad applicability across different care settings. When evidence certainty was low or very low, recommendations were formulated as conditional and accompanied by explanatory considerations to avoid overstatement of the evidence.

As part of the guideline development process, the expert panel translated the evidence-based recommendations and good practice statements into a practical clinical algorithm. The algorithm summarizes the proposed pathway for keratoconus care, including clinical suspicion, initial examination, diagnostic testing and staging, diagnostic confirmation, risk-factor control, treatment selection according to progression status, and risk-based follow-up. These questions generated 13 evidence-based recommendations and 5 good practice statements. Clinical Area I. Risk Factors

Question 1.What are the risk factors and/or comorbidities associated with the development of keratoconus?

What are the risk factors and/or comorbidities associated with the development of keratoconus?

Recommendation 1

In patients presenting with visual impairment and either a family history of keratoconus or a personal history of allergic disease, we suggest performing corneal tomography or topography to support early detection of keratoconus.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Low ⨁⨁◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

Three systematic reviews informed the assessment of factors associated with keratoconus. Seth et al (2023; 15 observational studies) identified significant associations with eye rubbing (OR = 5.22; 95% CI: 2.80–9.75), family history of keratoconus (OR = 6.67; 95% CI: 4.77–9.33), and allergic conditions (OR = 2.21; 95% CI: 1.57–3.13). This review was rated as having low risk of bias according to ROBIS. Based on these findings, the panel considered eye rubbing a key modifiable risk factor and issued a good practice statement to discourage this behavior. In addition, corneal tomography or topography was conditionally recommended in patients with visual impairment and either a family history of keratoconus or a history of allergic disease, given the overall low certainty of evidence related to risk of bias and inconsistency.13

Systemic allergic conditions, allergy as a broader clinical category, and ocular allergy were interpreted separately. Ocular allergy was not considered an independently demonstrated risk factor based on the pooled estimates from Seth et al; however, it remains clinically relevant because ocular pruritus may promote eye rubbing, which was the modifiable factor most strongly associated with keratoconus in this review. Therefore, the panel emphasized the identification and control of ocular allergy symptoms as part of risk-factor management.

In contrast, Akowuah et al (2022; 9 studies) found no statistically significant association between diabetes mellitus and keratoconus, including in subgroup analyses by diabetes type. This review was rated as having low risk of bias.14 Similarly, Hashemi et al (2020; 29 studies) did not demonstrate a significant association between keratoconus and sex (OR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.69–1.33), although this review was assessed as having high risk of bias due to limitations in the reporting of methodological quality assessment.3 Supporting evidence for Question 1 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area I).

Question 2.What is the effectiveness of anti-allergic ophthalmic treatments (antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, and dual-action agents) in controlling allergic conjunctivitis symptoms in patients with keratoconus or at risk of keratoconus?

What is the effectiveness of anti-allergic ophthalmic treatments (antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, and dual-action agents) in controlling allergic conjunctivitis symptoms in patients with keratoconus or at risk of keratoconus?

Recommendation 2

In patients with allergic conjunctivitis, we suggest topical anti-allergy eye medications (antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, or dual-action agents) as first-line therapy.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

The recommendation was informed by two systematic reviews evaluating topical treatments for allergic conjunctivitis and vernal keratoconjunctivitis. Leonardi et al summarized evidence showing that mast cell stabilizers, including sodium cromoglycate, nedocromil, and lodoxamide, increased the likelihood of perceived benefit compared with placebo by 4.9-fold (95% CI: 2.5–9.6). Another systematic review included by Leonardi et al found that olopatadine at concentrations between 0.05% and 0.20%, compared with placebo, significantly improved ocular pruritus (SMD = −1.33; 95% CI: −1.43 to −1.23) and reduced ocular hyperemia (SMD = −0.92; 95% CI: −1.19 to −0.65). A third study included by Leonardi et al suggested that olopatadine may be more effective than ketotifen for relieving pruritus; however, statistical heterogeneity was high (SMD = −0.32; 95% CI: −0.59 to −0.06; I2 = 83%). Other systematic reviews assessed by Leonardi et al could not be included in a meta-analysis because of heterogeneity in outcome measures.15

The evidence for this question was indirect. Available studies primarily evaluated symptoms and signs of allergic conjunctivitis, including pruritus and hyperemia, rather than direct effects on eye rubbing, keratoconus onset, or disease progression. Therefore, the recommendation reflects symptom control as a clinically relevant strategy to reduce ocular pruritus and potentially limit eye rubbing, rather than direct evidence of reduced keratoconus progression.

Roumeau et al assessed the efficacy of available treatments for signs and symptoms of vernal keratoconjunctivitis. Compared with placebo, significant improvement was observed with mast cell stabilizers (SMD = −3.19; 95% CI: −4.26 to −2.13), antihistamines (SMD = −0.89; 95% CI: −1.31 to −0.47), and dual-action agents (SMD = −1.29; 95% CI: −1.85 to −0.73). No significant differences were found among treatment classes.16

Overall, available evidence suggests that topical antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, and dual-action agents reduce symptoms of allergic conjunctivitis compared with placebo. Although the certainty of evidence was very low because of risk of bias, heterogeneity, and indirectness for keratoconus-related outcomes, the panel considered that the expected symptomatic benefits and favorable safety profile support their use as first-line therapy in patients with allergic conjunctivitis. Clinical Area II. Diagnosis, Staging, and Follow-Up

Question 3.What are the clinical and paraclinical parameters that lead to suspected keratoconus?

What are the clinical and paraclinical parameters that lead to suspected keratoconus?

Recommendation 3

In patients undergoing biomicroscopy or retinoscopy with findings such as corneal bulging, a scissoring reflex, prominent corneal nerve fibers, a Fleischer ring, oil droplet sign, or corneal thinning, we suggest performing additional corneal imaging with tomography and/or topography to evaluate suspected keratoconus.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Recommendation 4

In adult and pediatric patients with suspected keratoconus who undergo corneal tomography, we suggest considering the following parameters: maximum keratometry (Kmax), inferior-superior index (I-S), anterior asphericity coefficient (anterior Q), thinnest corneal point (TP), Ambrósio’s relational thickness maximum (ART Max), pachymetric progression index (PI), anterior and posterior elevation, total coma, root mean square of higher-order aberrations (RMS-HOA), and corneal volume.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

Regarding clinical and paraclinical parameters that lead to suspected keratoconus, no systematic reviews were identified; therefore, the evidence came from eight observational studies.17–24

In the study by Naderan et al, the most common biomicroscopy or retinoscopy findings in patients with keratoconus were corneal bulging (54.5%), scissoring reflex (54.5%), prominent corneal nerve fibers (45.5%), Fleischer ring (37.0%), oil droplet sign (32.5%), and corneal thinning (31.8%). Patients with these findings also had significantly higher mean keratometry (Kmean), anterior and posterior elevation, and significantly lower central corneal thickness (CCT) and thinnest corneal point (TP), as well as lower visual acuity, compared with those without these signs (p<0.05).17

Regarding corneal tomography parameters, studies conducted by Nicula et al, Hashemi et al, Li et al, Heidari et al, and Hashem et al, including studies in pediatric populations, reported parameters with high diagnostic performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.8. These parameters included Kmax, I-S, anterior Q, TP, ART Max, PI, anterior and posterior elevation, total coma, RMS-HOA, and corneal volume.18–22 The expert panel agreed that these parameters are among those most frequently used in clinical practice to support the diagnosis of keratoconus.

Two studies evaluated the diagnostic performance of optical coherence tomography (OCT)-derived parameters in keratoconus. Kim et al assessed elevation-based metrics obtained from swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT; ANTERION) in 126 eyes, including 43 keratoconus eyes and 46 control eyes. Anterior best-fit sphere (BFS) elevation demonstrated optimal discriminative capacity at a cutoff of 4.95 μm (sensitivity: 100%; specificity: 98%; AUC: 0.996). Anterior elevation from the best-fit toric ellipsoid showed comparable performance at a cutoff of 5.06 μm (sensitivity: 98%; specificity: 100%; AUC: 0.999). Posterior BFS elevation, using a cutoff of 9.88 μm, showed slightly lower accuracy (sensitivity: 95%; specificity: 89%; AUC: 0.981).23

Heidari et al evaluated corneal layer thickness parameters derived from spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) in 161 eyes. Central corneal thickness and stromal thickness showed the highest diagnostic performance. CCT, with a cutoff of 527 μm, achieved a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 87% (AUC: 0.900). Stromal thickness, at a cutoff of 446 μm, showed a sensitivity of 81.7% and specificity of 73.6% (AUC: 0.871).24

The certainty of the evidence was considered very low overall because the available information came primarily from observational studies. Although selected studies reported high AUC values for SS-OCT- and SD-OCT-derived parameters, these estimates were obtained under specific study conditions and do not, by themselves, establish external validity or routine clinical utility. The panel considered the limited availability and validation of normative databases, the heterogeneity of diagnostic thresholds and study populations, the device- and software-specific nature of the measurements, and the lack of sufficient independent and longitudinal validation. Therefore, the available evidence was considered insufficient to support a specific recommendation for the use of OCT-derived parameters as stand-alone tools in patients with suspected keratoconus. SS-OCT and SD-OCT may provide complementary information when interpreted together with established corneal tomography or topography and clinical findings. Further multicenter and longitudinal validation is required before specific OCT-derived parameters can be incorporated into a formal recommendation. Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

Evidence for Questions 4 and 5 was derived from five secondary sources, including systematic and narrative reviews and consensus-based literature, and complemented with 11 primary observational studies (cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs) due to limited direct evidence on standardized criteria.

The review by Henríquez et al showed that subclinical and forme fruste keratoconus are typically defined using combinations of clinical and tomographic criteria rather than single parameters. Commonly reported elements included keratoconus in the fellow eye, normal slit-lamp examination, and topographic abnormalities such as inferior-superior asymmetry or skewed radial axes, highlighting the absence of a standardized definition.25

Similarly, Niazi et al identified substantial variability in the parameters used to define disease progression, particularly across tomographic and pachymetric measures. Frequently used indicators included changes in Kmax, CCT, and corneal elevation. The review also emphasized the relevance of clinical risk factors, especially younger age and atopic disease, as factors associated with progression.26

Reviews by Kang et al and Song et al described both emerging artificial intelligence-based approaches and traditional classification systems for staging keratoconus. Although systems such as Amsler-Krumeich remain widely used, they do not fully reflect advances in corneal imaging, as noted by the Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases.27–29

Regarding biomarkers, Gupta et al found insufficient consistent evidence to support routine measurement of serum micronutrient levels, including zinc, copper, and selenium, for assessing keratoconus severity or progression. Although subgroup analyses suggested lower vitamin D levels in progressive disease, the overall certainty of evidence was low due to risk of bias and inconsistency.30

Across the included primary observational studies, there was marked heterogeneity in the criteria used to classify severity and define progression. However, a consistent pattern supported the use of combined clinical and tomographic parameters, particularly Kmax, CCT, refractive changes, anterior and posterior elevation, and higher-order aberrations, for monitoring disease course. Corneal topography and tomography are therefore considered key tools for both staging and follow-up, enabling early detection of structural changes and supporting clinical decision-making. The balance of benefits and harms favors their use, particularly given their noninvasive nature and potential to inform timely intervention. These findings support regular, individualized follow-up, especially in patients with established risk factors, to facilitate early identification of disease progression.31–41 Supporting evidence for Questions 4 and 5 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area II).

Clinical Area III. Treatment with Cross-Linking (CXL)

Question 6.For pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what is the effectiveness of CXL and its associated complications?

For pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what is the effectiveness of CXL and its associated complications?

Recommendation 8

In pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, we suggest the use of corneal cross-linking (CXL), as it may halt or slow disease progression by stabilizing corneal parameters and, in some cases, may lead to improvements in keratometry and visual acuity; however, patients should be informed about potential adverse events prior to treatment.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Low ⨁⨁◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

To address this question, systematic reviews focused on the effectiveness and complications of CXL were selected. The systematic review by Sarma et al assessed the effectiveness of different CXL protocols for the treatment of progressive keratoconus in patients aged 14 years or older. At 1 year, significant improvement was observed in Kmax and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the CXL group compared with the control group, with sustained benefits in Kmax after 2 and 3 years. However, no significant differences were found in spherical equivalent or CCT. Regarding adverse events, higher event rates were reported in the CXL group compared with the control group, including corneal opacity, corneal scarring, pain, punctate keratitis, persistent epithelial defect or delayed epithelial healing, glare, blurred vision, photophobia, hyperemia, and irritation.42

The systematic review by Kobashi et al evaluated the effectiveness of different CXL protocols for the treatment of progressive keratoconus in pediatric patients aged 18 years or younger. Significant reductions in Kmax and TP were observed at 1 year, along with improvements in uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) and BCVA. No significant changes were found in cylindrical refraction over 12 months; however, corneal endothelial cell density remained stable after 1 year. Although the certainty of evidence for CXL ranged from moderate to low, the panel considered that the risk-benefit balance favored intervention. Therefore, the available evidence supports the conditional use of CXL to stabilize or improve keratometric and visual acuity outcomes in patients with progressive keratoconus.43 Supporting evidence for Question 6 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area III).

Question 7.In pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what are the risks and benefits of CXL with a conventional protocol compared with an accelerated protocol?

In pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what are the risks and benefits of CXL with a conventional protocol compared with an accelerated protocol?

Recommendation 9

We suggest conventional corneal cross-linking (C-CXL) over accelerated corneal cross-linking (A-CXL) when feasible, particularly when the objective is to achieve a greater reduction in keratometric indices and a significant improvement in corrected visual acuity.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

The selected evidence came from two systematic reviews. Jiang et al found significant improvement in Kmean in favor of C-CXL compared with A-CXL at least 6 months after the procedure, with a weighted mean difference (WMD) of −3.15 (95% CI: −4.34 to −1.97; p<0.01). Similarly, there was a statistically significant difference in BCVA in favor of the C-CXL group, with a WMD of 0.05 (95% CI: 0.00 to 0.10; p<0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between A-CXL and C-CXL at least 6 months after the procedure for Kmax, Kmin, spherical equivalent, or CCT.44

Shajari et al conducted a comparative analysis of C-CXL vs A-CXL for the management of progressive keratoconus across different age groups. A statistically significant difference was found, with greater reduction in TP using C-CXL (p=0.0005). For BCVA and UCVA, no statistically significant differences were found between groups; similarly, no significant differences were observed in keratometric parameters (Kmax, Kmean, and Kmin) or re-epithelialization time. Regarding demarcation line depth, C-CXL demonstrated a deeper stromal demarcation line compared with A-CXL across all analyzed protocols. Statistical significance was reached in the 30 mW/cm2 and 9 mW/cm2 subgroups, while the 18 mW/cm2 protocol showed a similar trend without achieving statistical significance. In the overall pooled analysis, C-CXL was associated with significantly greater demarcation line depth (p<0.0001).45

Regarding complications, only two trials included in the systematic review documented adverse events. The first study reported delayed epithelial healing in two eyes treated with C-CXL, three eyes treated with 9 mW/cm2 A-CXL, and one eye treated with 30 mW/cm2 A-CXL. In addition, anterior stromal scarring was observed in two eyes following 9 mW/cm2 A-CXL. At 12-month follow-up, disease progression was reported in two eyes treated with A-CXL using 18 mW/cm2 and 30 mW/cm2 protocols. The second study reported trace or mild corneal haze in 10 eyes treated with C-CXL and in 10 eyes treated with 30 mW/cm2 A-CXL. Additionally, one case of severe central haze was identified in the C-CXL group at 1 month of follow-up. Eleven additional trials explicitly reported no complications during follow-up, while the remaining studies did not specify whether complications occurred. Overall, the number of reported adverse events was low in both C-CXL and A-CXL groups, without a clear predominance of complications in either protocol based on the available data.45

Overall, current evidence suggests that C-CXL may provide greater improvement in selected stabilization parameters compared with A-CXL, particularly in keratometric indices, BCVA, TP, and stromal demarcation line depth. However, the certainty of evidence was very low, and findings may change with future research. Therefore, the panel issued a conditional recommendation favoring C-CXL over A-CXL when feasible, particularly when the clinical objective is to maximize keratometric improvement and visual stabilization. Supporting evidence for Question 7 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area III).

Question 8.In pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what are the comparative benefits and risks of epithelium-off (Epi-off) vs epithelium-on (Epi-on) CXL?

In pediatric and adult patients with progressive keratoconus, what are the comparative benefits and risks of epithelium-off (Epi-off) vs epithelium-on (Epi-on) CXL?

Recommendation 10

In patients with progressive keratoconus requiring greater reduction in Kmax, we suggest the use of Epi-off over Epi-on CXL, as evidence indicates higher efficacy with the Epi-off approach. However, potential procedure-related risks should be carefully considered.

Clinical decision-making should be individualized, taking into account disease stage, corneal characteristics, recovery profile, and patient preferences, balancing expected benefits and potential harms.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Low ⨁⨁◯◯

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

Two systematic reviews directly compared the effectiveness and safety of Epi-off and Epi-on CXL techniques, and two additional systematic reviews evaluating transepithelial and iontophoresis-assisted transepithelial CXL were included as complementary evidence.

D’Oria et al compared these two protocols in adult and pediatric patients, finding significant differences in favor of Epi-off CXL for Kmax, with a mean difference (MD) of −1.69 (95% CI: −2.62 to −0.76; p=0.004). Significant improvement was found in BCVA for the Epi-on group, with an MD of 0.07 (95% CI: 0.04 to 0.10; p<0.001). Results for UCVA, CCT, and endothelial cell density were not statistically different. For adverse events and treatment failure, the Epi-on CXL protocol was significantly associated with a lower risk of delayed epithelial scarring, with a risk difference of 0.049 (95% CI: 0.003 to 0.0946; p=0.035), and persistent stromal opacity, with a risk difference of 0.0525 (95% CI: 0.0063 to 0.0986; p=0.026). No significant differences were found in keratoconus progression, sterile infiltrate, or infection risks between groups.46

The systematic review by Di et al compared the clinical outcomes of standard Epi-off CXL with Epi-on CXL in patients older than 18 years with keratoconus. For Kmax, Epi-off CXL provided a significant reduction, with an MD at 3 months of −0.74 (95% CI: −1.31 to −0.16; p=0.01), at 6 months of −0.33 (95% CI: −0.77 to 0.11; p=0.14), at 12 months of −0.85 (95% CI: −1.43 to −0.27; p=0.004), and at 24 months of −1.12 (95% CI: −1.96 to −0.29; p=0.009). For secondary outcomes, BCVA showed better results in the Epi-on group, with an MD of 0.11 (95% CI: 0.07 to 0.15; p<0.00001) at 6 months; no significant differences were found at 12 or 24 months. Regarding CCT, a significant reduction was found with the Epi-off technique when comparing both groups at 3 and 12 months. No differences were found in UCVA, manifest refraction, or spherical error.47

Additional evidence from transepithelial CXL techniques further supports the interpretation of the comparative findings between epithelial approaches. In pediatric populations, Fard et al observed statistically significant differences in corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) and UCVA, as well as in keratometric indices, after conventional (C-CXL) and accelerated (A-CXL) CXL when compared with transepithelial CXL (p<0.001). Specifically, BCVA, UCVA, and keratometric indices improved significantly after 12 to 24 months of follow-up in the C-CXL and A-CXL groups, whereas no significant improvement was observed in the transepithelial CXL group for the same outcomes. Furthermore, conventional and accelerated CXL continued to demonstrate significantly better visual and keratometric outcomes than transepithelial CXL after 24 to 120 months of follow-up (p<0.001).48

Similarly, Wan et al compared iontophoresis-assisted transepithelial CXL (I–CXL) with C-CXL in patients with progressive keratoconus. No statistically significant differences were observed in BCVA, UCVA, Kmax, Kmean, CCT, or aberrometric parameters. However, TP thinning was significantly lower in eyes treated with I–CXL, with a weighted mean difference of −0.25 (95% CI: −0.44 to −0.06). In addition, I–CXL was associated with a lower risk of treatment-related complications (OR = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.75), suggesting a more favorable postoperative safety profile while maintaining comparable functional outcomes.49

Epi-off CXL may offer greater reduction in Kmax compared with Epi-on CXL; however, given the low certainty of evidence, these benefits remain uncertain and should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, Epi-on CXL, although potentially less effective for Kmax reduction, may be associated with fewer procedure-related complications and a more tolerable postoperative recovery profile. Evidence from transepithelial techniques is consistent with this interpretation, suggesting that approaches preserving the epithelium may provide a more favorable safety profile, although they generally achieve less pronounced improvements in keratometric outcomes than conventional epithelial removal techniques. The decision between these techniques should balance expected benefits and potential harms, considering disease severity, rate of progression, corneal characteristics, recovery profile, and patient preferences. Supporting evidence for Question 8 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area III).

Clinical Area IV. Treatment with Intrastromal Corneal Ring Segments

Question 9.In patients with keratoconus, what is the effectiveness of intrastromal corneal ring segments and the associated complications?

In patients with keratoconus, what is the effectiveness of intrastromal corneal ring segments and the associated complications?

Recommendation 11

In patients with keratoconus, we suggest using intrastromal corneal ring segments (ICRS) to improve visual acuity and keratometric, refractive, and aberrometric outcomes.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Good Practice Statement

Clinicians should monitor adverse events after intrastromal corneal ring segment implantation, including white deposits, epithelial defects, extrusion, decentration, segment migration, halos, and glare, among others.

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

Four systematic reviews were selected. Janani et al synthesized evidence on the efficacy and safety of MyoRing for the treatment of keratoconus. Significant improvement was reported in visual parameters, such as UCVA at 3 and 6 months of follow-up, and BCVA at 3, 6, 12, and beyond 12 months. Similarly, improvement was found in keratometric and refractive parameters (Kmax, Kmean, Kmin, and spherical equivalent) at 3, 6, and 12 months. In terms of aberrometric parameters, results for higher-order aberrations (HOAs) showed significant improvement at 6 and 12 months.50

The systematic review conducted by Benoist et al compared ICRS alone or in combination with other interventions, such as CXL, PRK, or intraocular lenses. Regarding the results for ICRS alone, the authors reported statistically significant improvement in spherical equivalent (SMD = −0.98; 95% CI: −1.15 to −0.80; I2 = 87.8%; p<0.001). Improvements in visual parameters were also observed, including UCVA (SMD = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.07 to 1.44; I2 = 90.6%; p<0.001) and BCVA (SMD = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.64 to 0.87; I2 = 79.4%; p<0.001). Similarly, improvement was reported in keratometric parameters such as Kmax (SMD = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.69 to 1.01; I2 = 82.7%; p<0.001) and Kmean (SMD = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.69 to 0.98; I2 = 85.5%; p<0.001). The authors reported improvement in all parameters after ICRS implantation, with stability maintained for several years after the procedure. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted with caution given the high level of heterogeneity across studies.51

The positive standardized effect estimates reported by Benoist et al for Kmax and Kmean were retained as reported in the source review and interpreted according to the direction specified by that review, in which decreases in keratometric values represented favorable postoperative change. Therefore, these values should be understood as keratometric improvement or corneal flattening rather than increased corneal steepness. Given the high heterogeneity across studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Improvement in visual and keratometric parameters was also reported in the systematic review by Izquierdo et al, which assessed the effectiveness of ICRS in adult patients with keratoconus. Results were reported descriptively because no meta-analysis was conducted due to high heterogeneity. For UCVA, an improvement of 0.23 ± 0.28 logMAR was reported, and for BCVA, an improvement of 0.06 ± 0.21 logMAR was reported. In terms of refraction, sphere improved by 2.81 ± 1.54 diopters (D), and cylinder improved by 1.49 ± 0.83 D. Kmean showed a mean flattening of 3.41 ± 2.13 D at 12 months after ICRS implantation. The most frequently reported complications were white deposits (5.75%), epithelial defects (5.65%), extrusion (2.11%), decentration (1.41%), segment migration (0.6%), and halos and glare (0.6%).52

Lastly, the Cochrane review conducted by Zadnik et al assessed the effectiveness and safety of ICRS. The authors planned to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ICRS with eyeglasses or contact lenses; however, after reviewing 19 full-text studies, all were excluded. The authors determined that the RCTs did not meet the inclusion criteria, generally because they lacked a comparator of interest.53

The use of ICRS may provide more benefits than risks for patients with keratoconus, although the evidence was rated as very low certainty due to concerns related to inconsistency, statistical and clinical heterogeneity, and the quality of primary studies.

The evidence identified for this guideline did not allow a formal recommendation regarding the optimal timing or sequence of ICRS and CXL. When both procedures are considered appropriate, their timing should therefore be individualized according to documented disease progression, corneal characteristics, treatment objectives, and clinical judgment. ICRS is primarily intended to improve corneal geometry and visual function, whereas CXL is intended to stabilize the disease; accordingly, either a simultaneous or staged approach may be considered based on the needs of each patient.

Corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments (CAIRS) are intrastromal implants prepared from donor corneal tissue and have been proposed as an alternative to synthetic ICRS. Although CAIRS may offer potential advantages related to biocompatibility and may have a favorable complication profile compared with synthetic implants, the available evidence remains limited and is mainly derived from observational studies. CAIRS was not included as a predefined clinical question in the present guideline and therefore was not incorporated into the formal evidence-to-decision framework. Further comparative studies are required to define its long-term safety, efficacy, durability, and patient selection criteria. Future guideline updates should consider CAIRS as a specific intervention if sufficient comparative evidence becomes available. Supporting evidence for Question 9 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area IV).

Clinical Area V. Treatment with Keratoplasty

Question 10.In patients with keratoconus, what are the benefits and risks of performing deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) compared with penetrating keratoplasty (PK)?

In patients with keratoconus, what are the benefits and risks of performing deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) compared with penetrating keratoplasty (PK)?

Recommendation 12

In patients with severe keratoconus (Amsler–Krumeich stage IV), we suggest performing DALK rather than PK.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Good Practice Statement

Clinicians should routinely monitor adverse events after keratoplasty, including elevated intraocular pressure, cataract, corneal infection, and graft failure or rejection.

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

To address this question, two systematic reviews were selected. The most recent review was conducted by Shams et al and compared PK with DALK in adults. This systematic review included 30 studies: 2 RCTs, 25 cohort studies, and 3 cross-sectional studies. Given the substantial statistical and clinical heterogeneity, the continuous outcomes were interpreted cautiously. For CCT, the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD), calculated as PK minus DALK, was −0.10 (95% CI: −0.57 to 0.37; I2 = 85.9%), showing no statistically significant difference between the procedures. For spherical equivalent, the pooled SMD was 0.32 (95% CI: 0.10 to 0.54; I2 = 80.4%); however, its clinical interpretation was limited by the substantial heterogeneity. For BCVA, the pooled SMD was −0.01 (95% CI: −0.16 to 0.13; I2 = 65.1%), indicating no statistically significant difference between PK and DALK.54

For spherical equivalent, estimates were heterogeneous, with several estimates showing imprecision. MDs, calculated as the mean value after PK minus the mean value after DALK, ranged from 0.30 (95% CI: −0.15 to 0.75) to −0.04 (95% CI: −0.48 to 0.41) in two RCTs. Across 11 cohort studies, including 2 prospective studies, MDs ranged from −0.78 (95% CI: −1.15 to −0.41) to 1.12 (95% CI: 0.63 to 1.61). Similarly, BCVA estimates were heterogeneous, with two RCTs reporting MDs of −0.33 (95% CI: −0.79 to 0.12) and 0.07 (95% CI: −0.38 to 0.52), and 13 cohort studies, including 3 prospective studies, reporting MDs ranging from −0.58 (95% CI: −1.06 to −0.11) to 0.43 (95% CI: −0.08 to 0.94).54

Regarding adverse events, elevated intraocular pressure and cataract were more frequent after PK than after DALK, with RRs of 3.63 (95% CI: 2.36 to 5.59) and 3.02 (95% CI: 1.69 to 5.40), respectively. Overall graft rejection was also significantly more frequent after PK (RR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.53 to 2.39). No statistically significant differences were found for corneal infection (RR = 1.23; 95% CI: 0.58 to 2.63) or graft failure (RR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.71 to 1.09). Endothelial cell count was significantly higher after DALK (SMD = −2.49; 95% CI: −3.65 to −1.34), although substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 = 96.3%). The negative SMD reflects the direction of the comparison, calculated as PK minus DALK.54

The pooled analysis assessed overall graft rejection and should not be interpreted as specifically measuring endothelial rejection. From an anatomical and immunological perspective, successful DALK preserves the recipient Descemet membrane and endothelium and therefore eliminates the risk of endothelial graft rejection. However, epithelial or stromal rejection and other graft- or interface-related complications may still occur.

The systematic review conducted by Song et al compared PK and DALK for the treatment of keratoconus diagnosed through slit-lamp examination and corneal topography. The review included 13 studies (3 RCTs, 1 prospective study, 7 retrospective studies, and 2 cross-sectional studies). This review did not contribute new information or identify studies in pediatric populations that would supplement the evidence synthesis for this question.55

Considering that BCVA is a clinically relevant outcome and that the available evidence did not show consistent differences between the two techniques, the evidence did not clearly favor either PK or DALK in terms of visual effectiveness. However, the safety profile favors DALK, given the lower risks of elevated intraocular pressure, cataract, and overall graft rejection, as well as greater preservation of the recipient endothelium and endothelial cell count. The certainty of the evidence was rated as very low because most of the evidence came from observational studies, substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity was present, and limitations and inconsistencies were identified in the reporting of the included reviews.

From a technical perspective, DALK may require longer operative times and may necessitate intraoperative conversion to PK; both outcomes are influenced by surgeon experience. Supporting evidence for Question 10 is provided in the Supplementary Material (Clinical Area V).

Question 11.In patients with keratoconus who are candidates for DALK, how do the big-bubble (Anwar) and manual dissection (Melles) techniques compare in terms of effectiveness and safety?

In patients with keratoconus who are candidates for DALK, how do the big-bubble (Anwar) and manual dissection (Melles) techniques compare in terms of effectiveness and safety?

Recommendation 13

In patients with keratoconus undergoing DALK, we suggest the use of either the big-bubble (Anwar) or manual dissection (Melles) technique, as current evidence does not demonstrate clinically relevant differences in effectiveness or safety between these approaches.

Strength of Recommendation: Conditional in favor

Certainty of Evidence: Very low ⨁◯◯◯

Good Practice Statement

Clinicians should routinely monitor adverse events after DALK, regardless of whether the big-bubble (Anwar) or manual dissection (Melles) technique is used, including elevated intraocular pressure, cataract, corneal infection, and graft failure or rejection.

Evidence Synthesis and Justification for Recommendation

To address this question, one randomized controlled trial (RCT) and three cohort studies were included; no systematic reviews were identified.

The RCT by Baradaran et al compared the big-bubble (Anwar) and manual dissection (Melles) techniques. No statistically significant differences were observed in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), refractive outcomes, or aberrometric outcomes over 15 months of follow-up. Similarly, no differences were found in corneal biomechanical properties, endothelial cell density, endothelial cell loss, or corneal thickness. Reported complications included Descemet membrane detachment (Anwar: 2 vs Melles: 1), subepithelial rejection (Melles: 2), epithelial rejection (Anwar: 1), and one intraoperative failure to expose Descemet membrane in the Anwar group.56

The cohort study by Borderie et al, which included both techniques within the DALK group, reported comparable postoperative complication rates between the big-bubble and manual subgroups. The most frequent events were graft rejection (14.4% vs 19.1%), elevated intraocular pressure requiring treatment (9.3% vs 14.6%), cataract formation (2.5% vs 3.6%), non-immune graft failure (3.4% vs 1.2%), epithelial complications (2.5% vs 1.8%), and infection (2.5% vs 0.9%). Less frequent events included ocular trauma (0.9% in both groups) and immune deficiency (0% vs 0.9%). No cases of late endothelial dysfunction were reported.57

Han et al compared the big-bubble and manual dissection techniques, reporting intraoperative complications in the big-bubble group, including four cases of microperforation, one macroperforation requiring conversion to penetrating keratoplasty, and one case of graft refractive surgery. In contrast, the manual technique was associated with repeat grafting (n=3), microbial keratitis (n=2), epitheliopathy (n=2), Urrets-Zavalia syndrome (n=1), and graft refractive surgery (n=1).58 Knutsson et al reported postoperative complications in both groups, with four affected patients in the big-bubble group (including graft rejection, epithelial rejection, and stromal turbidity requiring regrafting) and three in the manual group, including graft rejection, epithelial rejection, and postoperative infection.59

The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for effectiveness outcomes and very low for safety outcomes; therefore, the overall certainty supporting the recommendation was considered very low. The main limitations were related to study design, small sample sizes, and incomplete reporting of patient characteristics and outcome definitions. Based on the available evidence, both the big-bubble and manual dissection techniques are acceptable options for DALK. The balance of desirable and undesirable effects does not favor one technique over the other, given the similarity in visual outcomes and the inconsistency in reported complications. Discussion

The overall certainty of evidence was predominantly low to very low across domains, reflecting the scarcity of high-quality randomized studies and the reliance on observational data, particularly in diagnostic and surgical decision-making.

From a diagnostic perspective, the findings reinforce the central role of corneal tomography and topography as key tools for keratoconus detection and monitoring. Although multiple tomographic indices demonstrated good diagnostic performance, the absence of standardized diagnostic thresholds and variability across studies highlights a critical gap in the field. Emerging modalities such as OCT-based parameters show promising diagnostic accuracy; however, current evidence remains insufficient to support their standalone routine implementation, underscoring the need for further validation.

Regarding disease classification and progression, the lack of consensus definitions remains a major limitation. The evidence consistently supports a multimodal approach incorporating keratometric, pachymetric, and aberrometric parameters, rather than reliance on single metrics. This is particularly relevant in clinical practice, where early identification of progression directly informs therapeutic decisions. The integration of clinical risk factors, such as age, allergic disease, and eye rubbing, further strengthens risk stratification and risk-based follow-up strategies.

Therapeutically, CXL remains the intervention with the most consistent evidence for halting or slowing disease progression. While conventional protocols may provide greater keratometric stabilization, differences between protocols and techniques are supported by low or very low certainty evidence and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Similarly, Epi-off approaches appear more effective in reducing Kmax, albeit at the cost of increased procedure-related adverse events and recovery burden, highlighting the importance of individualized decision-making.

ICRS reported consistent improvements in visual, refractive, keratometric, and aberrometric outcomes; however, high heterogeneity and lack of robust comparative data limit the strength of recommendations. In advanced disease, keratoplasty remains a surgical option, with DALK potentially offering a favorable safety profile compared with PK, despite similar visual outcomes. The choice of surgical technique should be guided by surgeon expertise and intraoperative considerations, as current evidence does not support clear superiority between approaches.

Overall, this guideline emphasizes the need for standardized definitions, higher-quality comparative studies, and integration of emerging technologies to improve diagnostic precision and therapeutic decision-making in keratoconus.

Implementation of these recommendations should consider local availability of diagnostic technologies, surgical expertise, patient preferences, and the low or very low certainty of evidence supporting several interventions. Conditional recommendations should not be interpreted as universal mandates but rather as structured guidance to support individualized clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This guideline provides a structured, evidence-based framework for the diagnosis, staging, interventional treatment, and follow-up of keratoconus, integrating heterogeneous evidence into clinically actionable recommendations.
