High sea ice algal primary production near marine-terminating glaciers linked to turbulence-driven nutrient fluxes across the ice–ocean interface
Authors: Dorte H. Søgaard, Søren Rysgaard, Tobias Reiner Vonnahme, Pedro Duarte, Cæcilie Erichsen Kudsk, Brian K. Sorrell, Lorenz Meire, Josefa Verdugo, Anne-Kirstine Dybdahl Kristensen, Mark A. Lever, John Mortensen, Lars Chresten Lund-Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sea ice primary production is a key component supplying carbon to higher trophic levels when few other resources are available. In bottom-ice habitats, this production is limited by light availability and nutrient supply from underlying seawater. Reports of low sea ice primary production from Greenland have reinforced the view that landfast ice is regionally unimportant. Here, we document a single early-spring observation of intense algal production in sea ice adjacent to marine terminating glaciers in West Greenland, an environment rarely examined in ice studies. The bloom included abundant pennate diatoms, including Nitzschia frigida, and reached a daily primary production of 146 ± 4.8 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ and biomass accumulation of 42.4 ± 1.6 mg Chlorophyll a (Chl a) m⁻², exceeding previous Greenland observations. A biomass-specific production of 3.40 mg C mg Chl a⁻¹ d⁻¹ and maximum quantum yield (ΦPSII_max) of 0.44 indicated an active community. Strong silicic acid depletion in the presence of significant nitrate and phosphate concentrations suggested that silicic acid was the primary limiting nutrient within the ice. We propose that inflow-driven fjord circulation likely enhanced nutrient availability beneath the ice, while turbulence-driven fluxes across the ice-ocean interface represent a plausible mechanism for sustaining the observed high sympagic production during this sampling event. Sea ice in fjords with marine-terminating glaciers may therefore support high early-season production under favorable local conditions.

Introduction

Sea ice algal production is a cornerstone of Arctic marine ecosystems, annually providing one of the earliest pulses of organic carbon to higher trophic levels when few other resources are available1–3. However, in most regions, sea ice algae are thought to be a minor contributor to overall production compared with pelagic phytoplankton production4. Studies from Greenland fjords often report low biomass and primary production in landfast ice5, reinforcing the perception that organic carbon production by sea ice algae is ecologically insignificant. The primary controls on sea ice algal production are nutrient and light availability, which regulate bloom magnitude and timing1,6–8. Silicic acid and nitrate are the principal limiting nutrients, and their replenishment depends on molecular diffusion and turbulence-driven exchange across the ice–ocean interface9,10. When turbulence is weak, nutrient fluxes are limited, leading to rapid nutrient exhaustion and reduced algal growth, whereas strong under-ice currents and shear-driven mixing can sustain nutrient supply and support high bottom-ice primary production by enhancing exchange across the ice–ocean interface9,10. Additional processes that can ameliorate nutrient limitation within the ice include microbial regeneration of nitrogen11, and intracellular nitrate storage, which allows ice algae to buffer short-term deficits and prolong bloom activity6.

Fjords with marine-terminating glaciers can promote the transport of deep, nutrient-rich bottom waters into the upper layer via upwelling12,13. Although this subglacial discharge-driven upwelling is strongest in summer14, low subglacial discharge in winter may also play an important ecological role15,16, particularly when sea ice limits wind-driven mixing17. Whether these nutrients are transferred to the bottom of the sea ice depends on two distinct bottlenecks. Weak water-column stratification and strong under-ice currents enhance the upward transport of nutrients to the ice–ocean interface. In contrast, strong stratification, for example due to a stable pycnocline, limits this supply. Even when nutrients reach the interface, transfer across the ice–ocean interface itself may represent an additional, small-scale constraint on nutrient availability to ice algae9,10,18. Fjords in Greenland that are fed by marine-terminating glaciers generally support higher water-column primary production than those influenced by land-terminating glaciers, owing to enhanced nutrient entrainment and upwelling12–14,19–23. However, how the processes of nutrient entrainment and upwelling affect sea ice algal primary production is still poorly understood. Studies from Svalbard fjords show that subglacial discharge plumes can enhance under-ice production, which could imply that similar mechanisms also occur in Greenland fjords17.

In Nuup Kangerlua, a fjord strongly influenced by marine-terminating glaciers14, elevated concentrations of nitrate, phosphate and silicic acid are frequently observed near glacier fronts, primarily driven by fjord circulation and summer subglacial discharge-induced upwelling12–14,21–23. Although winter subglacial discharges have not yet been observed in Nuup Kangerlua, localized discharge-related mixing may occur in some glacier-proximal settings, as reported from Svalbard and other Greenland fjords16,17. During winter, nutrients beneath landfast sea ice are therefore more likely redistributed and renewed by inflow-driven fjord circulation, supplemented by autumn wind mixing and thermohaline convection during sea ice formation15,24. The circulation-driven renewal of water masses in Nuup Kangerlua likely contributed to nutrient availability beneath the landfast sea ice and may therefore have supported the high sympagic algal production observed at the ice–ocean interface15–17,25,26.

Here, we present observations of high sea ice algal primary production during early spring at a glacier-proximal site in Nuup Kangerlua (Kangersuneq), West Greenland (Fig. 1). We hypothesize that this observed sea ice algal primary production was supported by turbulence-driven nutrient exchanges across the ice–ocean interface. To explore this hypothesis, we combined measurements of abiotic conditions (e.g. sea ice and under-ice water temperature and salinity), sea ice and under-ice water stable water isotope composition (δ¹⁸O and δ²H), sea ice algae species composition, nutrient concentrations (phosphate, nitrate and silicic acid), chlorophyll a (Chl a), the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPSII_max), and ¹⁴C-based primary production. We estimated vertical fluxes9,10 to identify plausible mechanisms of nutrient supply.

Results

Physical and nutrient characteristics

The sea ice was overlain by a uniform snow layer with an average thickness of 6.9 ± 0.7 cm, and the ice thickness was 36.4 ± 0.8 cm. The air temperature during sampling was − 3.7 °C. Vertical profiles of sea ice temperature and salinity were characteristic of early spring, showing progressive warming and desalination of the ice (Fig. 2a). Ice temperatures increased from − 2.6 ± 0.3 °C at the bottom-ice to − 0.8 ± 0.3 °C near the surface, while bulk salinity decreased from 14.3 ± 0.3 at the bottom to 7.3 ± 1.0 near the surface (Fig. 2a). Brine volume was high throughout the ice column (36 ± 10%), with especially elevated values in the lower ice (51% ± 13%; Table 1).

Nutrient concentrations exhibited a distinct vertical gradient through the sea ice to the under-ice water (Fig. 2b). Under-ice water concentrations were 7.9 ± 2.6 µM for nitrate, 4.9 ± 2.4 µM for silicic acid and 0.6 ± 0.3 µM for phosphate. Within the ice, bulk nitrate ranged from 2.4 ± 0.2 µM at the surface to 3.7 ± 1.2 µM at the bottom-ice, whereas bulk phosphate increased from 0.07 ± 0.03 µM to 0.30 ± 0.16 µM at the bottom-ice (Fig. 2b). Bulk silicic acid declined markedly from 0.7 ± 0.05 µM in the upper ice to 0.17 ± 0.05 µM in the bottom-ice layer. Consistent with the Redfield–Brzezinski ratio (106 C:16 N:15Si:1P), the elevated N: Si ratio (average ~ 9 and ~ 22 in the bottom-ice layer) suggests preferential depletion of silicic acid relative to nitrogen, indicating that silicic acid was likely the primary limiting nutrient within the ice.

In the underlying water column, there was a thermocline at ~ 5 m depth, with cold surface water (< −1.5 °C) overlying warmer water (> 1.0 °C; Fig. 2c). In the temperature–salinity (T–S) diagram, the observations formed a linear trend with a slope of 2.30 °C per salinity unit (p < 0.0001), closely matching the melt line for mixing between glacial ice meltwater and seawater (Fig. 2d). Surface water measurements plotted mainly above the freezing line, although a few fell below it (Fig. 2d). Stable water isotope values for the sea ice (δ¹⁸O = − 1.3 ± 2.6‰, δ²H = − 9.0 ± 19.9‰; Table 1) were close to the marine endmember, indicating that the integrated ice column was isotopically similar to seawater. However, more depleted values in the bottom-ice layers (down to δ¹⁸O = − 5.1‰ and δ²H = − 38.10‰; blue values in Table 1) suggest episodic incorporation of water of glacial origin. The under-ice water exhibited marine-like isotope values (δ¹⁸O = − 1.3‰, δ²H = − 7.8‰; Table 1), showing no distinct glacial signal.

Biotic parameters

A high-biomass sea ice algal bloom was observed during the sampling event, with pennate diatoms visually prominent and Nitzschia frigida identified from qualitative microscopy observations (Fig. 3a). Ice algal biomass reached 42.4 ± 1.6 mg Chl a m⁻², accompanied by high primary production of 146 ± 4.8 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ (Fig. 3b). In contrast, primary production in the under-ice water was two orders of magnitude lower (1.4 ± 0.6 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹; Fig. 3b). Dissolved O2 was undersaturated in both the sea ice (260 ± 4.2 µM) and underlying water column (330 ± 2.8 µM), indicating substantial heterotrophic activity within the sea ice and reduced oxygen concentrations in the underlying seawater (Fig. 3b). The vertical silicic acid flux, estimated assuming diffusion across a turbulence-controlled molecular sublayer, was 1.9 mmol Si m⁻² d⁻¹, which was of similar magnitude to the silicic acid requirement inferred from primary production (1.72 mmol Si m⁻² d⁻¹), when applying the Redfield–Brzezinski ratio (106 C:16 N:15Si:1P). This suggests that nutrient supply across the ice–water interface likely contributed to sustaining the observed bloom. Biomass-specific production in the ice reached 3.40 mg C mg Chl a⁻¹ d⁻¹, which is approximately one order of magnitude higher than the 0.52 mg C mg Chl a-¹ d-¹ measured in the underlying water column (Table 1). ΦPSII_max was similar in both the sea ice and in the under-ice water, with values of 0.44 ± 0.03 in ice and 0.41 ± 0.14 in under-ice water (Table 1). Under-ice photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was 33.03 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ (Table 1), indicating that sea ice algae were well adapted to low-light conditions.

Discussion

This study documents high sea ice algal primary production (146 ± 4.8 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹) during a single early-spring sampling event in the inner Nuup Kangerlua near a marine-terminating glacier. Although based on one site and one sampling date, the observations indicate that Greenland landfast sea ice can locally support substantially higher production than previously reported. Previous estimates for Greenland range from 0.1 to 21 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹5,27–29, whereas our results were nearly an order of magnitude higher. Our measurements are also higher than typical values measured in the Central Arctic (excluding algal mats), Barents Sea, and the Pacific Arctic (3 to 55 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹), matched only by measurements from the Canadian Arctic (2 to 463 mg Cm⁻² d⁻¹)1,30. Comparable rates of 157 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ have been observed in Resolute Bay, Canada31, largely due to strong tidal mixing and the inflow of nutrient-rich waters, which supports a high sympagic primary production5.

High algal biomass, elevated biomass-specific production, and high ΦPSII_max values in our study (Table 1) indicate continuous nutrient replenishment to the bottom-ice community. Strong silicic acid depletion within the sea ice (0.17 ± 0.05 µM in the bottom-ice layer) compared with under-ice water (4.9 ± 2.4 µM), identifies silicic acid as the primary limiting nutrient during the bloom. The calculated vertical silicic acid flux (1.9 mmol Si m⁻² d⁻¹), based on molecular diffusion regulated by turbulence, was of similar magnitude to the silicic acid demand (1.72 mmol Si m⁻² d⁻¹) derived from measured rates of primary production. This agreement is consistent with the interpretation that diffusion within a turbulence-controlled molecular sublayer may have supplied sufficient silicic acid to help sustain the observed primary production. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, which demonstrated that shear-induced mixing and under-ice turbulence enhance nutrient transfer across the ice–ocean interface, supporting algal growth when molecular diffusion alone is insufficient18.

The strong silicic acid drawdown, together with significant nitrate and phosphate concentrations, suggests that diatom growth was limited by silicic acid availability rather than by nitrogen or phosphorus. Physical conditions in the under-ice water column further supported efficient nutrient supply to the bottom-ice, as stratification was generally weak (N2 ≈ 0 with minor peaks around 0.04 s⁻² near 5 m depth), and the temperature–salinity relationship (slope = 2.29 °C per salinity unit) closely followed the melt line with a Gade slope (2.40 °C per salinity unit), indicating mixing between seawater and sea ice melt (Fig. 2d). Similar weakly stratified, shear-influenced under-ice conditions have been shown to enhance nutrient transport across the ice–ocean interface26.

The flux-demand balance together with the weakly stratified conditions suggest that turbulence-driven exchange at the ice–ocean interface may have contributed to sustaining the observed bloom. This interpretation should, however, be treated cautiously, as no direct measurements of under-ice current velocity or turbulence microstructure were obtained, and the friction velocity used in the flux calculation was adopted from published values rather than measured at the study site. Accordingly, the flux estimates are indicative rather than definitive. Turbulent exchange at the ice–ocean interface can be enhanced by shear, tidal forcing, and under-ice surface roughness, which intensify friction and mixing within the boundary layer and promote vertical nutrient transport from the underlying water column18,32,33,34,35,36. Nutrient supply during ice growth can also be influenced by brine drainage and convection within the skeletal layer33,37, although these processes are likely secondary in early spring when ice growth has ceased. Because the sea ice in this study was already melting (Fig. 2), the increased brine volume fraction (36 ± 10%; Table 1) and permeability may also have allowed brine flushing, potentially further enhancing nutrient exchange near the ice–ocean interface37,10.

The main source of nutrients for sympagic algae in marine-terminating glacier fjords during winter remains uncertain. We propose that winter inflow-driven fjord circulation represents the primary mechanism maintaining nutrient availability beneath landfast sea ice in Nuup Kangerlua, promoting the upward transport of nutrient-enriched coastal water through near-surface outflow and bottom-water inflow37. This interpretation is consistent with the depleted δ¹⁸O values (~ − 5‰) observed in bottom-ice layers, which suggest episodic incorporation of glacially modified water into the sea ice matrix. High silicic acid concentrations and enhanced biogenic silica fluxes reported near the marine-terminating glacier in Nuup Kangerlua12,38, further support the presence of a nutrient-rich environment in this region. Although winter subglacial discharge has not been detected in Nuup Kangerlua, secondary processes, including submarine melting of glacier fronts and iceberg–water interactions, may still contribute to localized mixing or upwelling beneath the sea ice15,17. Silicic acid stored beneath glacier-front ice may also be released into these upwelling waters39,40. On the other hand, an example from Svalbard suggests that proximity to marine-terminating glaciers can also suppress sea ice algal production when low-salinity sea ice becomes impermeable, thereby restricting nutrient exchanges with underlying seawater17. In this case, despite elevated nitrate and silicic acid concentrations near the glacier front, the conditions instead promoted an under-ice phytoplankton bloom17. Thus, the effect of marine-terminating glaciers on nutrient cycling varies with local winter conditions, including meltwater structure and frazil ice formation17,]25. Because sea ice algal biomass and production can vary substantially over small spatial scales, the present observations cannot determine whether the measured values were representative of a larger area or reflected a locally elevated patch. Broader spatial surveys, including repeated coring and under-ice imaging approaches, would be required to assess representativeness.

Conclusion

Collectively, our results indicate that the high sea ice algal production observed during this late-winter sampling event was associated with nutrient-rich waters and sufficient light availability. Weak stratification and glacier-driven circulation may have facilitated turbulent nutrient fluxes across the ice–ocean interface. Glacial-proximal sea ice environments may therefore sustain high early-season primary production under favorable physical and nutrient conditions, likely supported by interacting circulation and nutrient-supply processes. Our observations align with the broader understanding that Arctic early-season sympagic primary production is regulated by light availability and physical and nutrient-driven processes, rather than by light alone4. Glacial-proximal environments may represent overlooked hotspots of early spring primary production, with potential implications for carbon cycling, nutrient transfer, and food web structure in glacier-influenced Arctic fjord systems. Because this study represents a single site and a single early-spring sampling event, broader spatial, temporal, and process-based observations are needed to determine how persistent and widespread such conditions are, and to clarify whether glacial nutrient inputs contribute to sympagic primary production and fjord ecosystem dynamics more generally.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Sampling took place on 4 April 2024 in Kangersuneq, the innermost branch of Nuup Kangerlua (64º 38.5329’N, 50º 16.7174’W), approximately 15 km from the terminus of the marine-terminating glacier Narsap Sermia (NS; Fig. 1) and 45 km from Kangiata Nuaata Sermia (KNS; Fig. 1). Tidal currents with velocities up to 1 m s⁻¹ and periodic inflows of coastal water drive strong mixing across the sills at the entrance, exchanging several tens of cubic kilometers of water during each tidal cycle and creating a highly dynamic hydrographic regime14,]41. Winter circulation is important for bottom-water renewal with a net near-subsurface outflow and net deep-water inflow42.

Snow depths and ice thickness were measured with a ruler to the nearest 0.1 cm (n = 10), and sea ice cores were collected using a MARK II coring system (Kovacs Enterprises Ltd.). All samples were taken within a 10 m² (3.2 m × 3.2 m) quadrat following established methodology43. Triplicate sea ice cores were collected for physical, chemical, isotopic, chlorophyll a, PAM fluorescence, and algal community analysis, and were cut into 5 cm slices using a stainless-steel saw. Separate sea ice cores used for oxygen measurements were cut into 10 cm sections to provide sufficient sampling volume for Winkler titration. Additional intact cores of approximately 38 cm total thickness were collected for primary production incubations and divided into top-ice and bottom-ice sections (approximately 18 cm each). Under-ice seawater samples were collected using a Niskin bottle (General Oceanics, Inc., USA) at 1 m depth for chemical, isotopic and biological analyses. Vertical profiles of temperature and salinity in water below the sea ice were measured with a YSI handheld CTD (PRO30) with vertical resolution of 0.1 cm. Under-ice and surface PAR was measured with a LiCor sensor (Li-192).

Air temperatures were recorded 2 m above the sea ice surface, while temperature profiles in the sea ice cores were measured by a thermometer (Testo®) in 4 mm holes drilled into the ice. Light attenuation through the sea ice was used to estimate in situ sea ice primary production and was calculated following standard methods44. Sea ice sections were placed in polyethylene containers and transported in dark and thermally insulated boxes to the laboratory within 3 h.

Laboratory analysis

In the laboratory, sea ice sections were weighed to determine density, then melted in the dark over two days at 3 ± 1 °C. Bulk salinity of the samples was measured with an YSI handheld CTD (PRO30) and converted to brine salinity and brine volume45,]46.

To determine δ²H and δ¹⁸O, triplicate samples of melted sea ice and under-ice water (1 m) were transferred into 2 mL glass vials and analyzed using standard methods47. Stable water isotopes were measured using a cavity ring-down spectrometer (L2130-I Isotopic H₂O Picarro Inc., USA). Six injections were run per sample, of which the first three were discarded to reduce carryover effects and the final three were averaged for analysis. Calibration was performed against VSMOW2 and SLAP2 standards, with four standards measured at the beginning and end of each sample set. Standard errors of the mean field observations (n = 3) were within ± 0.02‰ for δ¹⁸O and ± 0.13‰ for δ²H47.

Inorganic nutrient concentrations NOx [NO3⁻ + NO2⁻] (hereafter referred to as nitrate, as > 90% of [NO3⁻ + NO2⁻] was in the form of nitrate), phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and silicic acid (Si(OH)4) were determined in triplicate samples of melted sea ice and under-ice water (1 m). Samples (10 mL) were filtered through 0.45 μm Q-Max GPF syringe filters, transferred to vials, and stored frozen at − 20 °C until analysis. Nutrients were measured by standard colorimetric methods using a Seal QuAAtro AutoAnalyzer, with a detection limit of 0.05 µM for nitrate and 0.5 µM for silicate. Triplicate analyses were used to assess measurement consistency, and average values are reported. To determine bulk concentrations of O2in sea ice, three sea ice cores were cut into 10 cm sections and placed immediately in gas-tight vacuum-packed plastic bags and transported back to the laboratory in thermally insulated boxes. O2 concentrations were determined using standard Winkler titration methods44. Average values of triplicate samples are reported.

For biological analyses, sea ice subsamples were thawed in a 3:1 ratio of filtered seawater to ice to minimize osmotic shock during sample processing48.

Primary production was measured using the H¹⁴CO₃⁻ incubation method49 in sea ice cores of approximately 38 cm total thickness, which were divided into two sections (top-ice and bottom-ice) of approximately 18 cm each. Measurements were also conducted in under-ice seawater collected just below the sea ice. Incubations were conducted at 3.0 ± 1.0 °C at eleven light intensities (456 ± 136, 298 ± 79, 196 ± 35, 145 ± 25, 102 ± 3, 77 ± 4, 55 ± 3, 40 ± 4, 25 ± 13, 18 ± 10, and 0 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹). Incubation times ranged from four to six hours. Potential production data (µg C l⁻¹ h⁻¹) were fitted to a photosynthesis-irradiance model using nonlinear regression in SigmaPlot43,]50. In situ primary production estimates were derived by integrating the lab-based production-light curves with PAR data obtained with 5-minute intervals recorded by Asiaq (www.asiaq.gl) at the entrance to Kangersuneq (Niaqornaa climate station located at 64.669361ºN, 50.895556ºW; Fig. 1). The in situ hourly PAR irradiance in the sea ice was calculated for each incubation using concurrent surface and under-ice PAR measurements obtained with a LiCor sensor (Li-192). Primary production estimates are based on duplicate incubation samples, and average values of these duplicates are reported.

For Chl a analysis, a known volume (100–200 mL) of melted sea ice from the 5 cm sea ice slices, and under-ice seawater, was filtered onto 25 mm Whatman GF/F filters. The filters were extracted in 96% ethanol at room temperature in the dark for 18 h and analyzed fluorometrically using a Turner TD-700 fluorometer (Turner Designs), before and after acidification with 200 µl of 1 M HCl51. The fluorometer was calibrated using a pure Chl a standard (Turner Designs). Average values of triplicate samples are reported.

The maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPSII_max) was assessed using pulse-amplitude modulated (PAM) fluorometry (Phyto-PAM Phytoplankton Analyzer, Heinz Walz GmbH, Germany). Triplicate dark-adapted samples from 5 cm ice slices and from under-ice seawater (1 m) were measured in a dark, temperature-controlled room (3.0 ± 1.0 °C)52. Instrument performance was checked prior to analysis following the manufacturer’s recommendations and published procedures52, and measurement variability is reported from triplicate samples.

For algal diversity analysis, sea ice samples and under-ice seawater samples were preserved in acidic Lugol’s solution (1% final concentration) and stored in the dark at 3 ± 1 °C. Samples were examined within two days using light microscopy and a standard sedimentation technique53. Taxonomic identification was qualitative and was conducted to the lowest practical taxonomic level; pennate diatoms were visually prominent, and Nitzschia frigida was identified in the sea ice samples.

Data analysis

Vertical nutrient fluxes across the ice–ocean interface were estimated using established methods9, which describe nutrient transport as molecular diffusion across a turbulence-controlled viscous sublayer at the ice–ocean interface, where fluxes depend on both the molecular properties of seawater and the intensity of shear near sea ice9,]10. Water column density (σ) was calculated from CTD-derived salinity and temperature profiles using the EOS-80 equation of state (data not shown)54. To diagnose meltwater mixing beneath the sea ice, a melt line was constructed with a marine endmember defined by salinity = 32.6 and temperature = 1.1 °C, and with a Gade slope of 2.40 °C per salinity unit. Observed temperature–salinity (T–S) relationships were compared to the melt line to infer the influence of glacial meltwater and water column mixing. Stratification strength was quantified using the Brunt–Väisälä frequency (N2): N2=(g/ρ0)·(∆ρ/Δz), where g = 9.81 m s⁻² is gravitational acceleration, ρ₀ is the in situ surface density (kg m-³) of the water (data not shown), and Δρ/Δz is the vertical density gradient. Low N2 values (N2; range: 1.9 × 10− 4 to 2.3 × 10- 3 s⁻²) indicate weak water column stratification and suggest efficient vertical exchange beneath the ice, consistent with enhanced turbulence beneath the ice.
