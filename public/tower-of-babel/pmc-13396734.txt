Excitatory paraventricular thalamus–posterior insular cortex circuit mediates pain–anxiety comorbidity in diabetic neuropathy
Authors: Min Wei, Jiayin Shou, Jing Yang, Siqing Cai, Qiyuan Bai, Jinwen Hou, Ye Jiang, Shixiong Shen, Zhuoying Yu, Xinru Zhao, Yongzheng Han, Jie Cai, Xue-wei Yang, Zhengqian Li, Daqing Ma, Guo-Gang Xing, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) is frequently accompanied by anxiety, yet the neural circuit mechanisms associated with nociceptive hypersensitivity to affective dysfunction remain unclear. Here we combined brain-wide c-Fos mapping, viral tracing, in vivo fiber photometry, whole-cell recordings, and projection-specific opto/chemogenetics in a mouse model of streptozotocin-induced PDN to define a thalamo–cortex circuit for pain–anxiety comorbidity. Within the posterior insular cortex (pIC), CaMKIIα⁺ excitatory neurons were recruited in PDN mice with comorbid anxiety, exhibiting heightened neuronal excitability and enhanced excitatory synaptic input. Bidirectional chemogenetic modulation of pICCaMKIIα neurons oppositely regulated anxiety-like behavior and mechanical hypersensitivity without locomotor or glycemic confounds. Circuit mapping identified a monosynaptic excitatory projection from the paraventricular thalamus (PVT) to the pIC; PVTCaMKIIα neurons were hyperactive in PDN, and projection-defined manipulation of the PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα pathway bidirectionally controlled the behavioral phenotypes. Cacna1c was upregulated in the PVT of PDN mice; its knockdown in pIC-projecting PVTCaMKIIα neurons attenuated both pain and anxiety-like behaviors. Together, these results delineate a thalamo–cortex excitatory pathway that couples nociceptive and affective dimensions of PDN and suggest that selective targeting of the PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα circuit may provide coordinated relief of pain and anxiety-related symptoms.

Introduction

Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN), a common and debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus, affects approximately one-third of diabetic patients and is characterized by spontaneous or evoked pain sensations such as burning, tingling, or electric shock-like discomfort [1]. In addition to sensory symptoms, PDN is frequently accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities, particularly anxiety disorders, which exacerbate pain perception and significantly affect quality of life [2]. Current pharmacological treatments, including tricyclic antidepressants, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and anticonvulsants, showed limited efficacy and are often associated with adverse side effects [3, 4]. These limitations underscore the need to delineate circuit mechanisms relevant to the coupling of nociceptive hypersensitivity with anxiety in PDN.

A growing body of evidence indicates that chronic pain and affective disorders such as anxiety may share common neural substrates [5, 6]. The posterior insular cortex (pIC), a key integrative hub for nociceptive, interoceptive, and emotional processing, has been implicated in both pain perception and emotional regulation [7]. Notably, PDN patients with heightened pain perception sensitivity exhibit increased thalamo–insular cortex functional connectivity, which correlates positively with neuropathic pain severity [8]. In addition, structural and functional alterations in the IC have been reported in various psychiatric conditions, including anxiety, further underscoring its role in integrating sensory and emotional states [9, 10].

Among thalamic nuclei projecting to the pIC, the paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT) is a relay integrating vigilance, affect, and nociceptive signals [11, 12]. The PVT is implicated in neuropathic [13], visceral [14, 15], and inflammatory pain [16], and it modulates anxiety and fear within limbic and stress-related circuits [17, 18]. Its widespread cortical and limbic projections are considered to shape the affective–motivational dimension of chronic pain [19, 20]. Projection-defined PVT outputs to amygdala-related circuits regulate fear- and anxiety-like behaviors, and the direct projection from the PVT to the BLA can bidirectionally modulate neuropathic pain together with anxiety-like phenotypes [21, 22]. Inputs from the posterior PVT to the vmPFC convey persistent pain-related signals that promote anxiety-like behavior [23]. Whether signaling from the PVT to the pIC couples neuropathic pain with anxiety in PDN remains unclear.

In a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced PDN mouse model, we combined c-Fos mapping, viral tracing, fiber photometry, whole-cell recordings, opto/chemogenetic manipulations, and cross-regional transcriptomics. We found that pICCaMKIIα neurons were recruited in PDN with comorbid anxiety and showed increased excitability with enhanced excitatory synaptic input; bidirectional chemogenetic modulation oppositely regulated anxiety-like behavior and mechanical hypersensitivity. Circuit mapping identified a monosynaptic excitatory projection from PVT to pIC; PVTCaMKIIα neurons were hyperactive in PDN, and projection-specific manipulation of the PVT to pIC pathway bidirectionally controlled both behaviors. Guided by transcriptomics, projection-restricted Cacna1c knockdown attenuated both pain-related hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behavior in PDN mice. Collectively, these data outline a thalamo–insular cortex pathway that may help explain the coupling of nociceptive and affective dimensions in PDN and, pending further validation, could inform circuit-guided strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Peking University Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. PUIRB-LA2023516) and complied with ARRIVE. Male and female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions on a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups before acclimation. Male and female mice underwent PDN induction and behavioral testing; mechanistic experiments were performed in male mice unless otherwise indicated. After a one-week acclimation, baseline body weight, random glucose, and nociception were recorded 24 h before induction.

PDN model

Mice were fasted for 4 h with free access to water, then received a single intraperitoneal injection of freshly prepared STZ (200 mg/kg, Sigma) in ice-cold sodium citrate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.5; Solarbio) [24]. Controls received citrate buffer. A 10% sucrose solution was provided for 48 h post-injection. Diabetes was confirmed 72 h later by tail-vein glucose tests; included if random glucose ≥16.7 mmol/L on three consecutive measurements. Body weight and glucose monitored weekly.

Unless specified, “PDN” denotes STZ-induced mice exhibiting both nociceptive hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behaviors, which were used for further experiments; others were excluded.

Behavioral assays

Mice were habituated for 3 days; on test days they acclimated 30 min to the apparatus. Anxiety assays were performed after the development of pain hypersensitivity. Experimenters were blinded to group allocation during behavioral test and outcome assessment. To minimize cross-task interference, open field test (OFT) preceded elevated plus maze (EPM) by 1 day. Videos were analyzed in SMART v3.0.

Von Frey

Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) was assessed with calibrated von Frey filaments (0.02–2 g; Stoelting, Wood Dale) applied to the left hind paw plantar surface until 90° bend for 5 s. Withdrawal or licking indicated a response; the lowest responding force was recorded, otherwise the next filament was used. Three trials per mouse were averaged.

Hargreaves test

Thermal sensitivity was assessed using the Hargreaves test (IITC Life Science). Radiant heat was applied to the left hind paw plantar surface, and paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was recorded. Three trials per mouse were averaged, with a 20 s cutoff to prevent tissue injury.

OFT

Mice were placed in the center of a 50 × 50 × 40 cm arena and recorded for 6 min. The arena comprised a 10-cm perimeter and a 30 × 30 cm center. Total distance and center time were quantified; reduced center time indicated anxiety-like behavior.

EPM

The apparatus comprised four 35 × 5 cm arms (two open; two closed with 15-cm walls) elevated 50 cm. Mice were placed on the central platform facing an open arm and recorded for 5 min. Open-arm entries and time were quantified; reduced open-arm exploration indicated anxiety-like behavior.

Viral vectors

AAV constructs used included AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-EGFP, AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hM3Dq-EGFP, and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP for chemogenetic manipulation; AAV1-CaMKIIα-Cre, AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-hM4Di-EGFP, AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP, and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-EGFP for circuit tracing and manipulation; AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f-EGFP for calcium imaging; and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hChR2-P2A-EGFP for functional verification of connectivity; AAV2/9-retro-CaMKIIα-mCherry for labeling the neurons which project to pIC; AAV2/9-retro-CaMKIIα-Cre, and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-EGFP-shRNA (Cacna1c or scramble) for Cacna1c knockdown in CaMKIIα⁺ neurons. All viral vectors were supplied by BrainVTA (China). For retrograde anatomical tracing, CTB-555 was used.

Stereotaxic injection surgery

Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (0.5%, i.p.) and placed in a stereotaxic frame. Meloxicam 5 mg/kg was given pre-incision, with 0.5 mg/kg (s.c.) as needed for postoperative analgesia. After midline scalp incision and antisepsis, bregma and lambda were exposed and the skull leveled (≤0.03 mm). Coordinates (from bregma): pIC AP − 0.10, ML ± 3.80, DV − 4.00; PVT AP − 0.94, ML 0.00, DV − 3.00. Burr holes drilled; virus pressure-injected (40 nL/min; 300 nL pIC; 200 nL PVT) via glass micropipette. To limit reflux, the pipette was held 5 min pre/post infusion, retracted 0.10 mm, held 5 min, then withdrawn. Mice recovered on a warming pad; monitored daily for ≥3 days before routine housing.

DREADD chemogenetics

For chemogenetic manipulation, hM4Di- or hM3Dq-expressing mice and DREADD-negative controls received intraperitoneal clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 2 mg/kg) or saline 30 min before behavioral testing, with identical handling and assays. Chemogenetic inhibition of the PVT to pIC pathway was performed in both male and female mice.

Fiber photometry

Fiber photometry signals were low-pass filtered and expressed as ΔF/F = (F − F0)/F0, using mean pre-event fluorescence as F0 (von Frey: −10 s; EPM open-arm entry: −2–0 s). ΔF/F was analyzed in −1–5 s around von Frey onset (0.4 g, plantar) and −2–10 s around open-arm entry, with 0–10 s quantified relative to −2–0 s. Artifact-contaminated trials were excluded; viral expression and fiber placement were verified by immunofluorescence.

Whole-cell patch clamp

After sevoflurane anesthesia, mice were decapitated and 300-µm coronal slices were cut in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF), recovered 20 min at 34–35 °C in 95% O₂/5% CO₂ ACSF, then held 1 h at room temperature. pIC and PVT neurons were recorded under IR-DIC (Zeiss Axioskop 2FS Plus) with amplifier (MultiClamp 700B) and pCLAMP; pipettes (3–5 MΩ) contained standard internal and slices were superfused with oxygenated ACSF. In current clamp we measured resting membrane potential (RMP), action potential (AP) threshold, rheobase, AP amplitude, and spike number in response to 500-ms depolarizing current steps(0–200 pA, 10-pA increments). In voltage clamp, sEPSC were recorded at −70 mV for 3 min. Both sEPSC frequency and amplitude were quantified. For chemogenetics, CNO (10 µM) was bath-applied and spontaneous firing compared before and after application. For optogenetics, 470-nm light (5–10 mW, 20 Hz, 5–15 ms pulses) was delivered to ChR2-expressing PVT neurons; responses were measured in current clamp. To verify monosynaptic PVT to pIC inputs, pIC neurons were held at −70 mV and light-evoked EPSCs were recorded in ACSF, then with TTX (1 µM) plus 4-AP (1 mM), and finally with CNQX (10 µM). Solutions are in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

Separate cohorts were perfused 90 min after nociceptive stimulation or EPM exposure. Mice were overdosed with 0.5% sodium pentobarbital (i.p.) and transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline then 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA (4 °C, 10 h), dehydrated in 20% then 30% sucrose, and sectioned coronally at 30 µm. Sections permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, blocked in 5% serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: c-Fos (rabbit, 1:1,000); c-Fos (guinea pig, 1:500); CaMKIIα (rabbit, 1:1,000); CaV1.2 (guinea pig, 1:100). Details of all antibodies are provided in Table S2. After PBS washes, sections incubated with species-appropriate secondary antibodies (1–1.5 h, room temperature); nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio) and sections mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 DIVE confocal microscope. Representative images were selected from sections included in the quantification.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

pIC and PVT were microdissected on ice, snap-frozen, and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted (TRIzol), RIN ≥ 7 verified, poly(A)-selected, and sequenced (NovaSeq X Plus, PE150). Reads were filtered (fastp), aligned to mm10 (STAR), counted, and analyzed with DESeq2 (|log₂FC | ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05). Overlap DEGs underwent GO (GOATOOLS) and KEGG (KOBAS 3.0) enrichment (P ≤ 0.05). Global concordance was assessed with rank–rank hypergeometric overlap 2 (RRHO2) using signed scores; heatmaps show −log₁₀(P).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

cDNA was synthesized from 100 ng RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323). qRT-PCR was performed with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex (Applied Biosystems) in 20 µL reactions; cycling was 95 °C for 30 s, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s; melt-curve analysis verified specificity. Relative expression was calculated by 2−ΔΔCt normalized to Gapdh. For qRT-PCR, n denotes mice per group; each dot represents one mouse. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Welch’s t test was used when variances were unequal. Behavioral outcomes were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests where appropriate. Two-tailed unpaired t tests compared saline- and CNO-treated groups in chemogenetic experiments and Scramble and shRNA groups in PDN knockdown experiments. Electrophysiological data were analyzed with two-tailed unpaired t tests or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey tests. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

c-Fos⁺ neurons were quantified across the pIC, basolateral amygdala (BLA), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), PVT, and periaqueductal gray (PAG). Sections (30 µm) were collected at 100 µm intervals, with one of every five slices immunostained. Three sections per brain region were analyzed, and the average number of c-Fos⁺ cells was compared between groups using two-tailed unpaired t tests. For retrograde tracing, CTB⁺ cells were quantified in upstream regions, and counts were normalized to the analyzed area to obtain cell density (cells/mm²). To define the cellular identity of activated neurons, CaMKIIα co-expression among c-Fos⁺ neurons in the pIC and PVT was quantified using ImageJ and reported as percentages.

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on our previous work with these assays [25, 26], and exact n values are reported in the figure legends.

To determine the temporal sequence of nociceptive and affective abnormalities in PDN, we assessed nociception weekly and initiated anxiety assays at week 6 after hypersensitivity emerged. By week 6, STZ mice showed reduced mechanical withdrawal thresholds and shortened thermal latencies, indicating established mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 1D, E). During the same interval, open-field center time and EPM open-arm exploration were comparable to controls (Fig. S1A–B). One week later, by week 7, STZ mice displayed robust anxiety-like behavior, characterized by reduced time spent in the center of the open-field and diminished open-arm entries and duration in the EPM, while locomotor activity was unaffected (Fig. 1F, G).

Female mice similarly exhibited sustained hyperglycemia and reduced body weight (Fig. S2A–D). A similar temporal sequence was also noted in female mice, with mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity at week 6 and anxiety-like behavior emerging at week 7 (Fig. S2E–H). These findings suggest that affective changes and hypersensitivity were established during chronic PDN progression.

To identify engaged circuits at this comorbid stage, we performed whole-brain c-Fos mapping 90 min after a standardized nociceptive stimulation (Fig. 1H). This timing aligns with established c-Fos protein kinetics [28]. Compared with controls, PDN mice exhibited widespread c-Fos activation across cortical–thalamic–limbic structures, including the ACC, PVT, BLA, and PAG (Fig. 1I, J). Notably, the pIC showed the strongest induction in this nociceptive mapping dataset (Fig. 1J). In a separate cohort, we mapped c-Fos following an anxiety-provoking EPM exposure (Fig. 1K), which increased c-Fos across distributed regions including the pIC, ACC, PVT, and BLA, whereas PAG did not reach significance (Fig. 1L, M). Based on these c-Fos mapping results, we selected the pIC as the target region for subsequent analyses.

To characterize the excitability profile of pICCaMKIIα neurons, whole-cell recordings were performed in EGFP-labeled neurons from acute slices (Fig. 2B). Neurons from PDN mice fired more spikes across depolarizing current steps (Fig. 2C, D), exhibited reduced rheobase (Fig. 2E) and lower AP thresholds (Fig. 2F), whereas RMP and AP amplitude were unchanged (Fig. 2G, H). In voltage clamp, sEPSC frequency was increased (Fig. 2I), while sEPSC amplitude was comparable between groups (Fig. 2J, K), indicating heightened excitability accompanied by elevated excitatory synaptic input under these recording conditions. To monitor population dynamics during nociceptive and anxiogenic challenges, we expressed GCaMP6f selectively in pICCaMKIIα neurons and recorded calcium signals via fiber photometry during behavioral assays (Fig. 2L). Event-triggered analyses revealed larger ΔF/F transients in PDN mice than in controls during mechanical stimulation (von Frey 0.4 g) and around open-arm exploration in the EPM (Fig. 2M, N), consistent with enhanced responsiveness of pIC excitatory neurons and motivating downstream causal tests.

Chemogenetic modulation of pICCaMKIIα neurons bidirectionally regulates pain and anxiety behaviors

To establish a causal relationship between pICCaMKIIα neuron activity and the behavioral expression of pain–anxiety comorbidity, we performed chemogenetic manipulations using inhibitory (hM4Di) and excitatory (hM3Dq) DREADDs.

Inhibition of pICCaMKIIα neurons in PDN mice increased mechanical withdrawal thresholds, prolonged center duration in the open-field, and enhanced open-arm exploration in the EPM, without affecting locomotion or blood glucose (Fig. 3A–E). Ex vivo validation confirmed that CNO significantly suppressed firing in hM4Di-expressing neurons (Fig. 3F). Conversely, chemogenetic activation of pICCaMKIIα neurons in naïve mice elicited the opposite phenotype. Together, these findings provide causal evidence that pICCaMKIIα neuronal activity is both necessary and sufficient for the expression of pain–anxiety comorbidity in PDN mice.

To assess functional alterations in the PVT, whole-cell recordings were performed from pIC-projecting PVTCaMKIIα neurons in acute slices (Fig. 4D). Compared with controls, neurons from PDN mice fired more spikes across depolarizing steps (Fig. 4E, F) and exhibited reduced rheobase and lower AP thresholds (Fig. 4G, H), whereas RMP and AP amplitude were unchanged (Fig. 4I, J). In voltage clamp, sEPSC frequency was increased, while sEPSC amplitude was comparable between groups (Fig. 4K–M), supporting enhanced excitatory synaptic drive. Complementary in vivo fiber-photometry recordings with CaMKIIα-driven GCaMP6f expression revealed larger ΔF/F transients in the PVT during mechanical stimulation and around open-arm exploration in the EPM (Fig. 4N–P). Taken together, PVTCaMKIIα neurons show heightened excitability in PDN, suggesting that PVT overactivation may promote insular circuit sensitization.

To test whether PVT activity causally influences behavioral outcomes, we performed bidirectional chemogenetic manipulations. In PDN mice, inhibition of PVTCaMKIIα neurons increased open-field center duration, increased open-arm time and entries in the EPM, and elevated mechanical thresholds, with locomotion and glycemia unchanged (Fig. S5A–E). DREADD-negative EGFP controls showed no behavioral or metabolic effects following CNO (Fig. S5F–J). Conversely, chemogenetic activation of PVTCaMKIIα neurons in naïve mice reduced open-field center duration, decreased EPM open-arm exploration, and lowered mechanical thresholds, without locomotor or metabolic effects (Fig. S5K–O).

These results support the view that the PVT functions as an excitatory hub whose activity is required for the expression of, and can drive key features of pain–anxiety comorbidity in PDN. Inhibiting PVTCaMKIIα neurons alleviated nociceptive and affective behaviors, whereas activation in naïve mice evoked a similar phenotype, consistent with a link between thalamic excitability and dual outcomes.

A direct excitatory PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα pathway bidirectionally regulates pain–anxiety comorbidity in PDN mice

Using an anterograde, trans-synaptic Cre strategy, AAV1-CaMKIIα-Cre was injected into the PVT and a Cre-dependent reporter (AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-EGFP) into the pIC. To establish functional connectivity, we expressed ChR2 selectively in PVTCaMKIIα neurons and performed whole-cell recordings (Fig. 5B). Blue-light stimulation reliably evoked action potentials in PVT somata and robust EPSCs in pIC neurons (Fig. 5C). We next tested whether selective manipulation of this projection could alter pain–anxiety behaviors. In PDN mice expressing the inhibitory DREADD hM4Di in the PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα pathway, CNO administration produced coordinated antiallodynic and anxiolytic effects, increasing open-field center exploration and EPM open-arm time and entries, and elevating mechanical thresholds, while locomotion and blood glucose remained unchanged (Fig. 5E–I). In female PDN mice, inhibition of the PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα pathway similarly increased mechanical thresholds and reduced anxiety-like behavior without affecting locomotion or blood glucose (Fig. S6A–E). Slice recordings verified that CNO suppressed firing in hM4Di-expressing projection neurons (Fig. 5J). Conversely, chemogenetic activation of the same projection with hM3Dq in naïve mice produced the opposite pattern, reducing open-field center time and EPM open-arm exploration and lowering mechanical thresholds, while locomotion and glycemia remained unchanged (Fig. 5K–O). In vitro validation confirmed that CNO increased firing in hM3Dq-expressing neurons (Fig. 5P). Control mice expressing EGFP in the projection pathway showed no CNO-induced changes in anxiety-like or nociceptive behaviors (Fig. S7A–E).

Together, these findings indicate that a direct excitatory pathway from PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα is functionally connected and, when up- or down-modulated, produces reciprocal changes in nociceptive and anxiety-related measures, linking thalamic activity to insular circuit output in PDN.

RRHO2 analysis showed strong same-direction concordance between the pIC and PVT transcriptomes across the full ranked gene lists, with minimal discordant overlap (Fig. 6E). Given that glutamine–glutamate cycling is a key process coupling synaptic metabolism to excitatory neurotransmission [35, 36], we next focused on glutamine-transport genes, identifying 10 shared candidates with directionally consistent expression trends across regions (Fig. 6F, G).

To corroborate these observations at the transcript level, we performed qRT-PCR for the ten glutamine-transport–related candidates. Gnai2 was significantly downregulated in both regions, whereas Cacna1c and Grin2c were increased in the PVT and Grm7 was increased in the pIC (Fig. 6H, I). These genes have established roles in excitability, synaptic signaling, and stress-related behaviors [37–40]. For the remaining 6 genes (Glul, Gng12, Slc38a3, Slc38a2, Shank3, Itpr1), qPCR results did not reach significance but showed directionally consistent trends with RNA-seq (Fig. S8).

Given that Cacna1c was identified by transcriptomic screening and validated as upregulated in the PVT, we next examined whether its protein product CaV1.2 is present in PVTCaMKIIα neurons, including those projecting to the pIC. Immunostaining revealed CaV1.2 signal within PVTCaMKIIα neurons, and CaV1.2 was also detected in retrogradely identified pIC-projecting PVTCaMKIIα neurons (Fig. 6J–K). To test whether Cacna1c in the PVT to pIC projection contributes to PDN, we selectively knocked down Cacna1c in pIC-projecting PVTCaMKIIα neurons using a projection-restricted viral strategy (Fig. 6L). qRT-PCR confirmed effective knockdown, showing an approximately 50% reduction in Cacna1c mRNA relative to scramble controls (Fig. 6M). Functionally, Cacna1c knockdown increased MWT in PDN mice (Fig. 6N) and produced anxiolytic-like behavioral changes, including increased OFT center duration without affecting total distance (Fig. 6O) and enhanced open-arm exploration in the EPM (Fig. 6P). Blood glucose levels were unchanged (Fig. 6Q). Together, these findings support a role for Cacna1c in pIC-projecting PVTCaMKIIα neurons in regulating pain and anxiety-like behaviors in PDN mice.

Discussion

PDN is frequently accompanied by anxiety, complicating management and worsening quality of life; therefore, we integrated brain-wide activity mapping, pathway tracing, in vivo and ex vivo physiology, projection-restricted causal tests, and cross-regional transcriptomics to delineate how nociceptive hypersensitivity may couple to anxiety-like behavior in this condition. Across modalities, the evidence converges on an excitatory projection from PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα neurons as a candidate substrate linking sensory and affective dimensions. Within the insular cortex, the posterior division was engaged during the comorbid stage; within the thalamus, the paraventricular nucleus showed heightened activity and provided monosynaptic glutamatergic input to the cortex. Projection-specific inhibition reduced allodynia and anxiety-like behavior, whereas activation produced the opposite pattern without locomotor or metabolic confounds, consistent with a circuit-level route for sensory–affective coupling.

The insular cortex comprises aIC and pIC subregions, both involved in nociceptive and emotional processing [41]. Neuroimaging evidence indicates that the pIC primarily encodes the sensory–discriminative dimension of pain, whereas the aIC contributes more to emotional–affective components [42–45]. In PDN, neuroimaging studies reported increased thalamo–insular functional connectivity that correlates with neuropathic pain severity [8, 46], providing clinical evidence of the thalamo–posterior insular circuit mechanism as we defined in our model reported in the current study. Beyond its sensory role, the pIC also participates in affective regulation, including stress-induced anxiety-like behavior [47], and pain-related mood disturbances [48, 49]. Inhibition of pain-responsive neurons within the pIC alleviates both hyperalgesia and anxiety [49, 50], and epidural pIC stimulation elicits frequency-dependent analgesic and anxiolytic effects [51, 52]. Extending these observations, our study demonstrates that pICCaMKIIα neurons are engaged and required for the concurrent manifestation of pain and anxiety in PDN, underscoring a disease-specific role for the pIC in integrating sensory and affective dysfunction.

Retrograde tracing and c-Fos mapping identified the PVT as an excitatory input to the pIC. In PDN mice, PVTCaMKIIα neurons were hyperexcitable; chemogenetically inhibiting these neurons alleviated both mechanical hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behaviors, while their activation produced the opposite effects. These results are in line with prior evidence implicating the PVT in chronic pain and affective dysregulation [13, 20, 22]. The PVT integrates nociceptive inputs from brainstem regions such as the parabrachial nucleus and PAG and relays them to limbic and cortical structures [11]. Previous studies have shown that the PVT is engaged during chronic, rather than acute, stress [53, 54], and increased excitability of PVTCaMKIIα neurons following REM-sleep deprivation prolongs hyperalgesia and anxiety-like behaviors [55]. Moreover, activation of these neurons in social contexts induces consolation and anxiety-like responses, highlighting their role in encoding psychosocial stress [56].

To establish projection specificity, we combined viral tracing and optogenetically assisted patch-clamp recordings to confirm a direct monosynaptic glutamatergic connection from PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα neurons. Chemogenetic inhibition of this projection in PDN mice attenuated both allodynia and anxiety, whereas activation of the same pathway in control mice was sufficient to induce these comorbid behaviors. These results identify a thalamocortical projection mediating the convergence of nociceptive and affective processing in PDN.

Comparable patterns have been observed in other PVT efferents. For example, modulation of the PVT–vmPFC projection affects both pain and anxiety, but downstream vmPFC nNOS⁺ neurons selectively regulate anxiety, illustrating projection-specific divergence [23]. In contrast, the PVT–BLA circuit exerts integrated control over both domains through glutamatergic signaling [22]. Our findings extend this organizational principle by identifying the PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα pathway as another efferent mediating convergent modulation of pain and affect. Importantly, this circuit-level insight provides a translational link between thalamo–insular dysfunction and neuromodulation strategies. Targeting the insular cortex using rTMS or focused ultrasound has shown analgesic effects, while thalamic DBS can alleviate central pain syndromes [57–59]. These findings raise the possibility that modulation of the PVT–pIC pathway may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for PDN-associated pain–anxiety comorbidity, although further validation in clinical settings is required.

Cross-regional transcriptomic analyses linked molecular and circuit-level mechanisms. The PVT and pIC of PDN mice shared 888 differentially expressed genes enriched in excitatory synaptic signaling, ion-channel regulation, and glutamine transport. RRHO2 analysis revealed strong same-direction concordance, indicating aligned transcriptional shifts across these nodes. This prompted us to move from pathway-level enrichment to candidate-level validation within the PVT to pIC projection.

Among these candidates, we prioritized Cacna1c for initial validation due to its key role in regulating neuronal excitability and excitatory synaptic signaling [60, 61], making it a suitable candidate for exploring molecular mechanisms within the PVT to pIC excitatory pathway.

Cacna1c encodes the L-type calcium channel CaV1.2, a locus implicated in human psychiatric vulnerability and emotional regulation [62]; neuroimaging work further links common CACNA1C risk variation to altered limbic reactivity [63]. In mice, perturbing Cacna1c signaling can modify anxiety-related behaviors, and downstream translational control can rescue anxiety-like phenotypes in Cacna1c-deficient adults [64, 65]. Consistent with these lines of evidence, we detected CaV1.2 immunoreactivity in PVTCaMKIIα neurons, including the subset retrogradely labeled from the pIC, and projection-restricted Cacna1c knockdown alleviated mechanical hypersensitivity and reduced anxiety-like behavior without affecting glycemia. These data provide initial support for Cacna1c involvement in both nociceptive hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behavior in PDN, and highlight its potential as a key molecular target in the PVT–pIC circuit. Future studies should further explore Gnai2 and other candidates differentially expressed in this pathway.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, CaMKIIα promoter driven viral tools and CNO DREADDs may cause off-target effects due to limited PVT/pIC specificity and potential CNO back-metabolism to clozapine, despite anatomical validation and EGFP/CNO controls. Second, excitability reflects intact synaptic transmission rather than isolated intrinsic membrane changes since antagonists have not been applied in the set of experiments. Third, bulk transcriptomics lacks cellular and projection resolution; although projection-restricted Cacna1c knockdown altered behavior, higher-resolution profiling and further investigation are needed, and circuits beyond the PVT–pIC axis were also unknown.

In summary, we delineate in PDN mice an excitatory thalamo–insular pathway from the PVT to the pIC that links nociceptive hypersensitivity with anxiety-like behavior. In PDN, PVTCaMKIIα to pICCaMKIIα shows heightened excitability, increased excitatory drive, and coordinated transcriptomic shifts, linking pain and anxiety. Additionally, projection-restricted Cacna1c knockdown in the PVT to pIC pathway was associated with reduced mechanical hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behavior. These findings support a mechanistic framework for co-regulation of chronic pain and negative affect and nominate thalamo-insular excitatory transmission as a candidate target for mechanism-informed interventions to relieve neuropathic pain and its emotional burden.
