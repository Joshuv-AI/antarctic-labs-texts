CEACAM1 in vascular homeostasis and inflammation
Authors: Lisa Götz, Uwe Rueckschloss, Süleyman Ergün, Florian Kleefeldt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe glycoprotein Carcinoembryonic Antigen‐related Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (CEACAM1), also known as CD66a, is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily. It is expressed in a variety of tissues including epithelial, immune, as well as endothelial cells, and is crucial to diverse physiological and pathological mechanisms. This review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of CEACAM1's multifaceted roles in vascular biology and inflammatory processes.MethodsDirected literature research was conducted using databases, such as PubMed, and relevant studies were categorized based on the physiological effects of CEACAM1.ResultsCEACAM1 plays a pivotal role in vascular homeostasis, particularly influencing the formation, maturation, and aging of blood vessels, as well as the endothelial barrier function. It supports endothelium‐dependent vasodilation and nitric oxide formation, thus promoting vascular integrity and regulating blood pressure. Additionally, CEACAM1 is of emerging importance to vascular inflammation and its potential clinical consequences.ConclusionCEACAM1 is a crucial regulator of vascular homeostasis and inflammation with significant implications for cardiovascular health. Despite the lack of understanding of tissue‐specific modulation and isoform‐dependent mechanisms, CEACAM1 could be a promising therapeutic target for the prevention of cardiovascular disease in the future.Keywords: cardiovascular, CEACAM1, homeostasis, inflammation

CEA FAMILY MEMBERS, STRUCTURE AND SIGNALLING

The CEA family of adhesion molecules is part of the immunoglobulin superfamily (Figure 1). CEACAMs exist as either transmembrane (CEACAM1, 3, 4) or membrane‐anchored (CEACAM5‐8) glycoproteins. Additionally, pregnancy‐specific glycoproteins (PSGs) represent genuine soluble members of the CEA family.

Structure of CEACAM family members. The members of the CEA family of adhesion molecules either exist as transmembrane (CEACAM1, 3, 4, 18, 19, 20, 21) or membrane‐anchored (GPI‐anchored; CEACAM5‐8) glycoproteins. The only exception is CEACAM16, which lacks a transmembrane domain and is secreted as a soluble protein. All CEACAM family members are characterised by the presence of an N‐terminal IgV‐like ectodomain (red) followed by multiple IgC‐like domains (blue). However, the number and arrangement of these Ig‐like domains varies between the individual proteins.

Prototypic human CEACAM1 is a single‐pass type I transmembrane protein. It contains a N‐terminal V‐set fold of the immunoglobulin superfamily ectodomain (IgV) followed by three immunoglobulin ectodomains (IgC2), a transmembrane sequence and a cytoplasmic tail. 1 However, due to alternative splicing, it can appear in 12 isoforms. Besides the number of IgC2 domains, splicing may affect the presence of the transmembrane domain by introducing a premature stop codon thereby generating secreted isoforms. 2 According to current knowledge, soluble variants can also be derived by cleavage of the ectodomains of CEACAM1. 3 It is assumed that both types of secreted proteins inhibit the dimerization of CEACAM1 on cell surfaces and thus presumably modulate its regulatory functions. 4

Finally, the length of the cytoplasmic tail may differ depending on the inclusion or exclusion of exon 7. This results in either a long form (L‐form, CEACAM1‐L, cytoplasmic tail: 71–73 amino acids) with an immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based inhibitory motif (ITIM)‐containing domain involved in signalling or a short form (S‐form, CEACAM1‐S, cytoplasmic tail: 10 amino acids) lacking the ITIM motif. 1 , 5 , 6

The N‐terminal IgV domain mediates intermolecular binding of CEACAM1. 7 , 8 CEACAM1 can couple to other CEA family members in either homophilic interactions (CEACAM1–CEACAM1) 8 or heterophilic interactions (CEACAM1–CEACAM5, 9 CEACAM1–CEACAM6, 10 CEACAM1–CEACAM8 11 ). Beyond dimerization, a recent study additionally indicates higher‐order oligomerization of human CEACAM1 presumably promoted by Zn2+ or Ni2+ ion binding. 12

The nature of the cytoplasmic tail determines signalling by CEACAM1. The L‐form of CEACAM1 can bind SH2 domain‐containing signalling molecules such as Src family kinases (SFKs) or SHP1/2 phosphatases via the ITIM motif. 13 , 14 In this context, the dimerization state of CEACAM1 is of importance. Whereas SHP1/2 phosphatases preferentially bind to CEACAM1 homodimers, SFKs bind to monomeric, homo‐ and heterodimeric CEACAM1 with similar affinity. 15 Accordingly, the expression ratio of CEACAM1 L‐ to S‐form affects preferential binding of SFKs or SHP1/2 phosphatases, thereby influencing the downstream signalling of CEACAM1. ITIM phosphorylation of CEACAM1 by SFKs augments interaction with SH2‐domain containing proteins, including SHP1/2 phosphatases. 16 Due to the recruitment of tyrosine kinases or phosphatases, CEACAM1 may act as a co‐receptor modifying phospho‐tyrosine‐dependent signalling of receptors, for example, the T cell receptor. 5

Additionally, both the L‐ and S‐forms of CEACAM1 bind calmodulin in a Ca2+‐dependent manner. 17 This interaction was reported to promote dissociation of CEACAM1 dimers. 18 Finally, other intracellular binding partners, like paxillin or actin, associate with CEACAM1. 19 , 20

CEACAM1 is expressed in a variety of different cells, for example, endothelial cells within the vasculature as well as non‐vascular cells like granulocytes, T cells, B cells and epithelial cells. 21 In quiescent vessels, the expression of CEACAM1 is relatively low. 22 , 23 , 24 However, CEACAM1 is upregulated during development and under conditions that exceed vascular homeostasis or stimulate the endothelium e.g. hypoxia/angiogenesis or aging/inflammation. 22 , 24 In the following sections, we will discuss in detail how CEACAM1 modulates those processes.

VASCULAR FORMATION

CEACAM1 plays a crucial role in vascular formation that is mediated either by vasculogenesis, angiogenesis or arteriogenesis. Vasculogenesis is defined as de novo formation of blood vessels during embryonic development directly from precursor cells called angioblasts. In contrast, during angiogenesis, new blood vessels are generated from pre‐existing vasculature via sprouting. Angiogenesis is involved in physiological processes, for example, the proliferation of the endometrium, as well as in pathological alterations, for example, tumour vascularization. Third, arteriogenesis represents a specialised form of arterial formation to maintain tissue perfusion by the development of collateral arteries from pre‐existing but blocked or narrowed main arteries. 25

CEACAM1 expression was shown in vessels of the developing rat brain 26 and in murine blood islands 27 suggesting a potential impact of CEACAM1 on vasculogenesis. In line with that, in murine embryonic stem cell‐derived embryoid bodies, vascular sprouting was reduced in the presence of anti‐CEACAM1 antibodies. 27 In contrast, homozygous CEACAM1‐deficient mice show regular embryonic development and no obvious morphological vascular abnormalities 28 that can be seen, for example, in the Flt1 and Flk1 knockout models. 29 , 30 This may be due to compensation by functionally redundant CEACAMs or other pro‐vasculogenic proteins.

First reports demonstrating a pro‐angiogenic function of CEACAM1 were published more than twenty years ago. In an in vivo chorion allantois membrane assay, soluble CEACAM1 was shown to act as a morphogenic effector of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF‐A) by enhancing endothelial proliferation, migration and tube formation resulting in increased vessel density. 22 Furthermore, cellular expression of CEACAM1 promotes pro‐angiogenic signalling in endothelial cells via upregulation of VEGF‐A and VEGFR2. 23 Conversely, VEGF‐A was shown to induce CEACAM1 expression in human microvascular endothelial cells. 22 This mutual upregulation of VEGF‐A and CEACAM1 may create a sustained pro‐angiogenic environment.

VEGF‐A expression is induced under hypoxic conditions via stabilization of the transcription factor HIF‐1α. 31 According to the VEGF‐A‐CEACAM1 interrelationship, endothelial CEACAM1 expression is also induced by decreased oxygen tension as can be found, for example, in the proximity of proliferating tumours indicating a role of CEACAM1 in tumour angiogenesis. 32 , 33 In line with these results, endothelium‐specific overexpression of CEACAM1 enhanced angiogenesis and vascular organization in an in vivo mouse model of ductal mammary adenocarcinoma. Vice versa, genetic deletion of CEACAM1 resulted in vascular instability associated with enhanced endothelial cell permeability and disruption of collagen IV matrix. Moreover, this in vivo tumour model revealed that CEACAM1 not only promotes formation but also maturation and stabilization of new vessels by enhanced integration of pericytes. 34

Similarly, CEACAM1 was also upregulated in hypoxic myocardial tissue 35 and promoted remodelling of collaterals after femoral artery ligation in a mouse hind limb ischemia model. 36 The latter finding links CEACAM1 function also with arteriogenesis.

Based on these data, CEACAM1 seems to be a key regulator of blood vessel formation by promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis while modulating angiogenic signalling pathways such as VEGF.

ENDOTHELIUM‐DEPENDENT RELAXATION AND NITRIC OXIDE FORMATION

Once the endothelium is formed, one of its main functions is the regulation of blood flow, particularly through endothelium‐dependent vasodilation, which is largely mediated by nitric oxide (NO). NO, produced by the endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS, NOS3), induces vascular smooth muscle cell relaxation that enhances local blood flow to ensure adequate oxygen and nutrient supply to tissues. 37

CEACAM1 affects eNOS‐dependent NO formation in many ways. Nouvion et al. were the first group to show that VEGFR2‐mediated eNOS activation is impaired in CEACAM1‐deficient primary murine lung endothelial cells. 28 Later, Najjar et al. demonstrated decreased eNOS expression and activity in CEACAM1‐deficient mice. 38 Under normal conditions, eNOS activation is stimulated by Akt kinase‐dependent phosphorylation. 38 , 39 However, in CEACAM1‐deficient mice, reduced Akt kinase activity likely contributes to impaired endothelial NO formation, further disrupting vascular function.

Additionally, proper activity of eNOS greatly depends on its localization in caveolae, specialised signalling compartments at the endothelial cell membrane. Co‐immunoprecipitation experiments suggest that CEACAM1 might serve directly as a membrane anchor for eNOS, securing its position at the cell membrane and ensuring its optimal function. 40 In CEACAM1‐deficient endothelial cells, reduced palmitoylation of eNOS, presumably due to enhanced expression of the depalmitoylating enzyme acyl‐protein thioesterase 1, leads to the mislocalization of eNOS from the cell membrane to the peri‐nuclear region. 40 This redistribution disrupts normal eNOS function and diminishes NO production, ultimately impairing endothelium‐dependent relaxation.

In addition to decreased NO production, CEACAM1 deficiency also leads to elevated formation of superoxide anions, which in turn can inactivate NO. These superoxide anions originate primarily from the mitochondrial respiratory chain and NADPH oxidases that have been reported to be upregulated in CEACAM1‐deficient mice. 38

VASCULAR BARRIER FUNCTION

Beyond regulating vascular perfusion, the endothelium controls the exchange of cells and molecules between the intravascular compartment and surrounding tissues, making proper endothelial barrier function essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis.

However, during inflammatory responses, it is necessary for leukocytes to overcome the endothelial barrier in order to fight pathogenic infections. Neutrophil granulocytes play a vital role in the first defence against bacterial infections, mediating allergic reactions and inflammatory processes, while monocytes mature into macrophages to engulf pathogens and present antigens to Tcells, linking innate and adaptive immunity. CEACAM1 is thought to be particularly involved in the initial step of adhesion and transendothelial migration of leukocytes, although its precise role in both types of immune cells is controversial discussed.

On the one hand, CEACAM1 was shown to enhance binding of granulocytes to human umbilical vein endothelial cells presumably via upregulation of integrin CD11b/CD18 on the neutrophil surface. 41 , 42 This interaction may be further augmented by CEACAM1 expressed on granulocytes since it is a major protein carrier of Lewis X and sialyl Lewis X carbohydrate groups that mediate binding to endothelial selectins. 43 , 44

On the other hand, aortic tissue of CEACAM1‐deficient mice showed enhanced immunohistochemical staining for monocyte markers indicating enhanced monocyte‐endothelial interaction in vivo. Furthermore, monocyte adhesion to aortic segments was enhanced due to CEACAM1 deficiency in an ex vivo assay. 38 These findings were confirmed later in vitro using myocardial endothelial cells. In this study, enhanced monocyte adhesion to CEACAM1‐deficient endothelial cells was attributed to an upregulation of endothelial ICAM‐1. 40 In addition, CEACAM1 deficiency upregulated endothelial expression of glycocalyx‐degrading enzymes, for example, MMP‐9, chondroitinase, and hyaluronidase‐2. Consequently, thickness and density of the endothelial glycocalyx were reduced in CEACAM1‐deficient cells that would further facilitate monocyte‐endothelial interaction. 40

These data suggest a cell type‐specific effect of CEACAM1, whereby it is essential for granulocyte binding while monocyte recruitment is compromised by CEACAM1. However, when considering the resolution of inflammation and the initiation of healing processes, the data for granulocytes and monocytes appear to be more consistent. 45 , 46 Singer et al. have shown that CEACAM1 delays spontaneous and Fas ligand‐induced apoptosis of granulocytes via ERK1/2 activation. 46 Similarly, CEACAM1 promotes human monocyte survival mediated by activation of the PI3K‐Akt‐signalling pathway. 47 As a result, CEACAM1 would contribute to a prolonged inflammatory response that presumably promotes tissue damage.

The transendothelial exchange of soluble components is mainly regulated by inter‐cellular tight junctions and adherens junction complexes. 48 Earlier reports indicated that CEACAM1 strengthens endothelial barrier function. In vivo experiments involving young adult CEACAM1‐deficient mice showed enhanced vascular permeability in established blood vessels such as the aorta. 49 This was accompanied by alterations in the distribution of inter‐endothelial adherens junctions as immunostaining for VE‐cadherin, the transmembrane component of the endothelial adherens junction complex, demonstrated inter‐cellular gap formation between adjacent endothelial cells. 38 Thus, in younger mice and under basal conditions in vitro, CEACAM1 promotes endothelial integrity by enhancing the interaction between VE‐cadherin and β‐catenin. 38 , 40 In the absence of CEACAM1, this interaction may weaken due to enhanced caveolin‐1 phosphorylation, as demonstrated in endothelial cells in vitro. 40 Similarly, CEACAM1 was reported to promote barrier integrity of developing vessels formed by angiogenesis in murine cancer models, presumably by maturation of inter‐endothelial adherens junctions. 28 , 34 Further analyses using mice of different age revealed that CEACAM1 modulates endothelial barrier function in an age‐dependent manner. Contrary to the barrier‐promoting effect of CEACAM1 observed in 3‐month‐old mice, increased endothelial CEACAM1 expression resulted in damage of barrier function in 9‐month‐old mice. 24 , 40 This reversal may be strongly related to aging‐associated alterations in the vascular anti‐/pro‐inflammatory balance, which will be discussed in more detail below.

VASCULAR INFLAMMAGING

Aging is characterised by a persistent low‐level systemic inflammation, a concept called inflammaging. Besides other pro‐inflammatory mediators, progressive age increases circulating levels and expression of TNF‐α within the vascular system. 50 , 51 , 52

Regarding the endothelial barrier, aging‐associated increased TNF‐α levels may be responsible for the transition of CEACAM1 from a barrier‐promoting to a barrier‐impairing agent. As mentioned before, CEACAM1 stabilizes inter‐endothelial adherens junctions in the absence of TNF‐α, for example, in younger mice or under basal conditions in vitro. However, TNF‐α induces tyrosine phosphorylation of adherens junction proteins leading to disassembly of the complexes and increased endothelial permeability. 40 , 53 , 54 Remarkably, this mechanism depends on the presence of CEACAM1. 40 Besides the increased endothelial permeability, CEACAM1 additionally mediates other widely accepted aging‐related vascular alterations, for example, fibrosis and oxidative stress. 24

Nevertheless, the most important finding in the context of inflammaging is the interrelationship of CEACAM1 and TNF‐α. Actually, it was shown that aortic expression of inflammatory mediators, including TNF‐α, was higher in CEACAM1‐deficient mice. 38 This may be caused by the increased endothelial permeability permitting the infiltration of pro‐inflammatory agents into the vessel wall, for example, LDL particles. 38 However, with progressive age the situation reverses. Our group confirmed upregulation of vascular TNF‐α expression with progressive age mainly in wild‐type mice. Consequently, at the age of 9 months, aortic expression of TNF‐α was more than 20 times higher in wild‐type mice compared to CEACAM1‐deficient mice. 24 Based on this finding, CEACAM1 can be regarded as a main driver of inflammaging. Vice versa, we found that age‐related upregulation of vascular CEACAM1 expression detected in humans and mice strictly depends on TNF‐α since it was lacking in Tnf −/− mice. 71 We identified NF‐κB and β‐catenin signalling as mediators of TNF‐α‐induced CEACAM1 upregulation in vitro. 71 This mutual upregulation of TNF‐α and CEACAM1 with progressive age establishes a vicious cycle that maintains a chronic pro‐inflammatory milieu within the vasculature and further promotes aging‐related alterations (Figure 2). Moreover, in conjunction with additional risk factors, the chronic pro‐inflammatory milieu may promote the transition from normal vascular aging into pathologic alterations, for example, the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. 55

Induction of CEACAM1 expression in endothelial cells by TNF‐α. In an early response, CEACAM1 is upregulated in TNF‐α‐stimulated endothelial cells via activation of NF‐κB. The delayed response involves TNF‐α‐induced adherens junction disassembly and nuclear translocation of released β‐catenin. CEACAM1 synergizes with TNF‐α in the further induction of TNF‐α formation.

CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASES

Atherosclerosis is the main pathology underlying cardiovascular events like myocardial infarction and stroke. It is characterised by the development of plaques within the arterial intima. These plaques arise from infiltrated leukocytes that take up oxidatively modified lipids in an unregulated manner eventually becoming so‐called foam cells. 56 Najjar et al. showed that under basal conditions, for example, regular diet mice harbouring a global deletion of Ceacam1 developed small aortic plaque‐like lesions. 38 It was shown that both global and hepatocyte‐specific CEACAM1 deficiency in mice induced hyperinsulinemia due to decreased hepatic insulin clearance. Consequently, these mice develop peripheral insulin resistance. 38 , 57 Insulin resistance is known to promote a pro‐inflammatory milieu. 57 , 58 , 59 This may have caused impaired endothelial barrier function favouring the development of small plaque‐like lesions as detected in 6‐month‐old Ceacam1−/− mice. 38 The crucial role of hepatic CEACAM1 expression for these alterations is further substantiated by the finding that hepatocyte‐specific re‐expression of CEACAM1 in a Ceacam1 −/− background rescued the metabolic phenotype as well as the endothelial and cardiovascular abnormalities. 60 , 61 In line with these observations, liver‐specific deletion of CEACAM1 in Ldlr −/− mice promoted inflammation, elevated oxidative stress and atherosclerotic plaque formation. 62 In contrast, endothelium‐specific CEACAM1 deletion in the same Ldlr −/− background did not affect atherosclerotic plaque size after high fat diet despite hypercholesterinemia and enhanced aortic inflammation. 63 In summary, these findings suggest a pro‐atherosclerotic effect of hepatic CEACAM1 deficiency that can be attributed to impaired insulin clearance. This is somewhat surprising considering the decisive role of CEACAM1 expression for aging‐associated impairment of endothelial barrier function and vascular inflammation as discussed above. 24 So far, the reasons for this seeming contradiction remain elusive and require further investigation.

Nevertheless, CEACAM1 expression is suggested to impact cardiovascular diseases in murine models as well as in humans. Hamo et al. used transcriptome analysis of endothelial cells harvested from the brachial vein of patients with different cardiometabolic risk factor burden. They showed a correlation of that risk factor burden with endothelial CEACAM1 expression. 64 Additionally, using single‐cell RNA sequencing, Tang et al. have shown enhanced endothelial expression of CEACAM1 in renal biopsies of patients with hypertensive nephropathy. 65

Furthermore, CEACAM1 was shown to affect the outcome after atherosclerosis‐related events like myocardial infarction (MI) or stroke. Interestingly, the effect of CEACAM1 expression differed between both cardiovascular events. Wang et al. showed that soluble CEACAM1 levels increased in the serum of patients with MI compared to healthy controls and in myocardial tissue of mice undergoing experimental MI. 66 Since total CEACAM1 upregulation was also observed in hypoxic rat neonatal cardiomyocytes, the authors suggested a VEGF‐dependent mechanism. 66 This was in line with a previous report showing VEGF‐A‐stimulated CEACAM1 expression in endothelial cells. 22 Interestingly, CEACAM1‐deficient mice showed reduced mortality, improved cardiac function and less cardiac remodelling after MI compared to their WT littermates presumably due to reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress‐induced apoptosis. 66 These data suggest a detrimental effect of CEACAM1 upregulation in MI.

In contrast, in a model of experimental stroke induced by temporary occlusion of the middle cerebral artery, CEACAM1 decreased mortality. 67 Following stroke induction, CEACAM1‐deficient mice showed increased stroke volumes accompanied by blood–brain barrier breakdown, edema and poor neurological performance. Mechanistically, this was attributed to enhanced neutrophil influx with subsequently increased secretion of neutrophil MMP9 in Ceacam1 −/− mice. 67 Here, CEACAM1 expression obviously exerts a positive effect by preventing excessive neutrophil activation. Therefore, enhanced CEACAM1 expression shown in patients with ischemic stroke compared to healthy controls can be interpreted as a protective mechanism. 68

CHALLENGES, FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

CEACAM1 plays a critical role in vascular homeostasis, including blood vessel formation, endothelial nitric oxide (NO) production, barrier integrity, and inflammation, making it a potential therapeutic target for various vascular disorders. However, significant gaps remain regarding the exact function of CEACAM1 in these processes, particularly its role in balancing pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory signals.

Therefore, a key challenge for future research is to uncover how CEACAM1 regulates inflammation within vascular tissues and beyond in more detail. While age‐dependent upregulation of CEACAM1 negatively impacts vascular function and contributes to inflammaging, its expression in the liver is crucial for maintaining metabolic homeostasis and promoting anti‐inflammatory conditions. A deeper understanding of how CEACAM1 affects inflammatory responses in a cell type‐ and organ‐specific manner is a prerequisite for the development of CEACAM1‐targeted therapeutic strategies, especially in the context of vascular diseases such as atherosclerosis, hypertension and ischemia–reperfusion injury.

In addition, the role of CEACAM1 isoforms that are generated by alternative splicing requires further investigation. Altered splicing patterns have been linked to tumorigenesis in breast cancer, and the ratio of CEACAM1 isoforms is crucial in determining downstream signalling pathways. 69 , 70 Therefore, imbalanced isoform expression could also contribute to the initiation and progression of vascular diseases. Accordingly, it remains elusive whether a chronic inappropriate CEACAM1 isoform ratio exacerbates vascular diseases, particularly in aging populations. Therefore, exploring the regulation of CEACAM1 isoforms and their impact on the development of vascular pathologies is an important avenue for future research.

In conclusion, while CEACAM1 presents a promising therapeutic target for vascular disorders, addressing these knowledge gaps is crucial to fully understand its therapeutic potential. Future research should focus on elucidating the cell type‐ and tissue‐specific effects of CEACAM1, the regulation of its isoform expression, and the long‐term implications of its modulation. These insights could pave the way for novel strategies to prevent and treat cardiovascular diseases by targeting CEACAM1.
