Contractile forces direct the chiral swirling of minimal cell collectives
Authors: Ghina Badih, Alexandre Schaeffer, Benoît Vianay, Pauline Smilovici, Laurent Blanchoin, Manuel Théry, Laëtitia Kurzawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceChirality, a fundamental property of living systems, manifests in cell collectives by their biased directional swirling. Despite the identification of the actomyosin cytoskeleton in driving the internal chiral symmetry break occurring in cells, little is known about the role of cellular forces in the development of handedness in collectives. Our findings establish that the level of mechanical work developed by pairs of confined endothelial cells modulates the direction of their rotation. Furthermore, we also identify the more contractile cell of the doublet as the cell driving this direction. This study sheds light on the importance of the generation of mechanical forces within a small collective in the determination of its chirality.

Chirality, defined as the property of an object not to be superimposable to its mirror image, is an evolutionary-conserved characteristic of living organisms (1, 2). Chirality notably manifests in the left–right (LR) asymmetry of tissues during embryonic development (3–5), where increasing evidence suggests that the LR bias of their constituting cells significantly contributes to this process (6, 7).

At the cellular level, chirality has been shown to manifest in various forms (8), among which collective cellular biased motion represents one of the most persistent depictions, likely due to the development of long-range correlations between individual cells (9). In most of the confined eukaryotic cells grown as collectives, a biased cellular alignment with respect to the system boundaries is indeed observed (10–13) and is eventually associated with the persistent chiral swirling of cells along the boundaries in both two-dimensional (14, 15) and three-dimensional systems (16, 17).

Although the LR bias of cells has been shown to strongly rely on the activity of several groups of actin-related proteins (5, 7, 10, 18, 19), the conditions leading to the emergence of chirality in motile multicellular groups is far from being understood. According to several studies, the presence of an intact actomyosin network notably appears as a key determinant of cellular chirality expression. Myosin activity has indeed been shown to be indispensable for both the chiral symmetry breaking of the actin cytoskeleton in single cells (20–22) and the biased alignment of cell ensembles (11), for which a LR-polarized distribution of myosin II is further described as concomitant with the appearance of cell chirality in the myocardium tissue (7). Thus, although cell mechanics appears to be a key factor affecting the chiral swirling observed in cell collectives, the exact role of cellular forces in chiral bias expression remains to be determined.

Importantly, what drives the expression of opposite handedness among different cell types sharing a priori common chiral actomyosin template remains unclear (14). These variations first reflect the dependence of chirality manifestation on additional unknown factors that are sensitive to environmental conditions (23), such as the size (24, 25) and geometry of the confinement (26), or the rigidity of the substrate on which cells are evolving (27). Second, although the chiral manifestation has been well established in big collectives, the constituting cells display a complex and heterogeneous behavior due to the local environment they interact with. This is well illustrated by the different morphologies or alignments cells adopt along the width of rectangles or rings (10, 28) and the counterrotations they display on geometries exhibiting several boundaries with opposite curvatures (14). Consequently, this intercellular variability interferes with the behavior of the collective and renders the identification of the critical parameters involved in the chiral phenotype expression within multicellular systems challenging.

In this study, we first aimed at reducing the number of cells in a moving collective in order to identify the minimal set of conditions allowing the emergence of a chiral movement. We then combined live monitoring of cell movement with traction force microscopy to investigate the role of contractile forces in the establishment of the chiral bias.

Results

Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cell (HUVEC) Doublets Represent a Minimal Cell Collective System Displaying Chirality.

Several studies have established that endothelial cells display a robust chiral bias, manifested by their biased alignment and/or chiral swirling when grown as collectives in confined environments (12, 14, 21, 29, 30). More recently, the chirality of endothelial cells was also demonstrated in vivo in vessels (13). Importantly, in both systems, chirality plays a crucial role in the modulation of the endothelium and vascular permeability (12, 13), highlighting its physiological role in these tissues. Here, we selected endothelial cells as a model collective system for our study. First, we sought to characterize their chiral bias in a simplified and controlled environment. To this end, we confined the cells on disk-shaped micropatterns, which display a plain 2D surface with only one external boundary, thereby enabling us to reduce the complexity of the geometries tested in previous studies (rings or tubes). We plated HUVECs at high-density on 250 µm diameter adhesive disks and imaged them for 8 to 14 h (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). By characterizing the rotation in these systems within this time window, we identified two classes of collectives: collectives in which the motion of individual cells displayed no coherence (designated by “no coherent rotation” or “NC”) and those where the majority of cells aligned and moved in a coordinated fashion, essentially along the periphery of the disk (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). On average, 60% of rotating cells demonstrated a coherent rotation (Movie S1), while 40% displayed no preferential orientation (Movie S2), showing that the majority of cells constituting the collectives were able to rapidly reorganize and polarize along the external boundary, ultimately leading to their coordinated motion (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). To test the impact of the initial cell density on the coherence of the motion, we counted the initial number of nuclei per collective for the two subpopulations and found no significant difference between them (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), indicating that the initial confluence of cell ensembles played a minimal role in triggering their coherent motion. Interestingly, around 90% of the collectives rotating in a coordinated fashion displayed a CW motion (Fig. 1C and Movie S3), in agreement with previous works (14). We next analyzed the speed of rotation of the nuclei located in different zones of the collective: along the external boundary in a radius of 70 µm (termed “OUT”) and in their inner part (termed “IN”) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). We observed that the cells at the periphery of the collectives moved more coherently and with a significantly higher speed than those in the internal part. These results indicate that, even in the absence of an internal boundary, big collectives still display a high heterogeneity in their rotational behavior, due to their localization among the collective. To simplify the endothelial collective to a more minimalistic configuration, we gradually reduced the number of HUVEC cells and examined the rotation of clusters constituted of either 4 (quadruplets), 3 (triplets), or 2 cells (doublets) confined on disks of 60 µm diameter (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). In all of these cases, the percentage of minimal collectives rotating persistently reached about 80 to 90% (Fig. 1E), which was significantly higher than the 60% rotation measured in the case of bigger collectives over the same period. This result suggests that reducing the number of cells constituting the collective tends to normalize the environment encountered by individual cells thereby favoring their coherent motion. These findings are in good agreement with the previous work showing that the persistence of coherent angular motion within a cluster of cells exhibits discontinuity above four cells and further requires their geometric rearrangement (31). Importantly, our quantification of the rotation direction within the population of persistently swirling cells showed that the CW-bias measured in big ensembles was maintained in the minimal collectives tested (Fig. 1F and Movie S4). In quadruplets, the CW-bias was lower than that in large ensembles but was still visible in 75% of the rotating minimal collectives. In triplets and doublets, the CW-bias was further decreased, reaching about 60% of the rotating collectives. However, this proportion turned out to be extremely robust across independent experiments (N = 16 experiments; n = 2,909 cells for doublets) and stable over time (up to 39 h) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 F and G and Movie S5). In order to characterize further the three subpopulations identified among cell doublets, we analyzed their angular velocities by tracking the movement of their junctions over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). We observed that the negative and positive angular velocities, registered for clockwise (CW)- and counterclockwise (CCW)-rotating doublets respectively, were quite stable over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I). For nonrotating doublets (NR), the values were oscillating around zero indicating the absence of motion in either direction (Movie S6). Importantly, the NR-doublets mean angular velocities were not overlapping with the mean angular velocities of rotating doublets (Fig. 1G), suggesting that the three subpopulations are distinct. Overall, these results demonstrate that the chiral bias displayed by large cell ensembles is reduced but conserved among cell doublets, which exhibited a higher frequency of coordinated rotation. Therefore, we decided to focus our study on endothelial cell doublets to investigate the critical parameters driving the CW or CCW directionality of the collective rotational motion.

The Mechanical Work (MW) Produced by the Doublets Is Correlated to Their Swirling Speed and Direction.

Interestingly, when measuring the angular velocities of the doublets (Fig. 1G), we found that their speed was significantly lower when rotating in the CCW direction than in the CW one. In the context of single cell migration, the classical view posits that myosin-mediated contraction defines the rear of migrating cells (32–36). In good agreement with this, we could observe a myosin cluster on one side and actin-rich protrusions on the opposite side in at least one of the two cells constituting the doublets (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). In cells expressing a fluorescent myosin light chain (MLC) construct, these myosin clusters were consistently observed at the rear of cells in both CW- and CCW-rotating doublets, as previously described by Lu et al. (17) in the case of 3D doublet rotation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B and Movie S7). However, monitoring the emergence of this polarized state by recording the spreading of cells on micropatterns prior to rotation showed that the symmetry-break resulting from contraction does not systematically determine the direction of motion of the doublets (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C and Movies S8–S12). Indeed, in certain cases, contraction of the cell rear preceded or occurred concomitantly with cell protrusion at the opposite pole. In others, myosin clusters formed near the leading edge. Alternatively, some cells initiated migration prior to any apparent myosin clustering (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Moreover, actin network contraction and myosin clustering were sometimes observed without any associated cell motion (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Consequently, we could not conclude whether the symmetry breaking event occurring in doublets stems from the protrusion of one cell or the contraction of the other.

In canonical migration models, both the speed and persistence of migration depend on cellular contractility levels (37–40). We therefore tested next whether CW- and CCW-rotating doublets could exhibit different contractile properties. To this end, we plated HUVEC cells on 60 µm disk-shaped micropatterns fabricated on polyacrylamide gels of 17 kPa rigidity and performed traction force measurements over the course of rotation (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). To assess the work produced by the doublets when deforming the substrate over the course of rotation, hereafter referred to as the doublets’ MW, we then computed the MW stored in the gel as the dot product of the force and displacement vectors (Fig. 2B, SI Appendix, Fig. S3B, and Movie S13). By tracking the junction, we could also measure the doublet angular velocity over time (Fig. 2C). Upon averaging the MW of distinct rotating doublets (Fig. 2D), we first noticed that the mean MW of the CCW-rotating doublets was significantly higher than that of the CW-rotating ones (Fig. 2 D and E). This was also accompanied by a significant decrease in the average length of the junction in CCW-rotating doublets (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), in line with previous works showing that the junction length depends on actomyosin contractility (41, 42). We then assessed whether this difference in the contractility level of the doublets could also be associated with a variation in their rotation speed on hydrogels. When plotting the angular velocity of the CW- and CCW-rotating doublets averaged over time (Fig. 2F), we observed that the angular velocity was lower in CCW-rotating cells compared to CW-rotating doublets, in line with our measurements on glass (Fig. 1G). Finally, plotting the mean angular velocity of doublets as a function of the corresponding magnitudes of mean MW revealed a weak negative correlation between these two parameters (Fig. 2G). Altogether, these results demonstrate the existence of a correlation between the contractility level of the doublets and their rotation direction.

The Level of Contractility of the Doublets Modulates the Directionality of the Chiral Bias.

To test whether variations in the forces developed by the doublets could affect their chiral bias, we further used chemical inhibitors to instantaneously modulate the contractility of the doublets.

We first treated the doublets with different concentrations of Rho kinase inhibitor (ROCKI) shortly after seeding the cells on micropatterns to decrease their contractility. As expected, we observed a significant decrease in phosphorylated MLC (p-MLC) levels upon treatment compared to the control conditions (Fig. 3A). These changes were also associated with a noticeable drop in the traction forces measured within the same doublets (Fig. 3B). We then monitored the doublets over time in the presence of increasing concentrations of ROCKI and quantified the percentage of rotation among the doublets (Fig. 3C and Movie S14). A gradual and significant decrease in the percentage of rotating doublets was registered upon increasing the inhibitor concentration, indicating that a minimal contractility level was required for the onset of rotation. Importantly, we recorded a slight but significative increase in the percentage of CW-rotating doublets with increasing concentrations of ROCKI (Fig. 3D). The same trend was also observed in response to increasing concentrations of Blebbistatin (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C).

Using Calyculin A (CalyA), an inhibitor of protein phosphatase 2A and 1, we then tested the effects of a force increase on the rotational chiral bias. As expected, low doses of CalyA induced a significant increase in the amount of p-MLC compared to control conditions (Fig. 3E). This phenotype was also associated with a marked increase in traction stresses exerted by the doublets (Fig. 3F). Interestingly, low doses of CalyA led to a significant decrease in the percentage of rotating doublets (Fig. 3G and Movie S15), showing that an excess in contractility impairs the rotation. More interestingly, the percentage of CW-rotating doublets gradually decreased with increasing CalyA concentrations (Fig. 3H).

Taken together, these results first show that an intermediate level of contractility is required to induce doublet rotation. This biphasic behavior is consistent with previous studies showing the existence of an optimal adhesion strength and contractility in the regulation of single cell migration speed (37, 43, 44). More importantly, our results also demonstrate that low doublet contraction favors a CW bias whereas high contractility induces a CCW bias.

The Mechanical Asymmetry between the Constituting Cells of Rotating Doublets Correlates with the Bias.

When considering collective motion, the magnitude and distribution of the forces exerted by individual cells can affect the behavior of the collective (45, 46). This led us to investigate the mechanical contribution of individual cells within rotating doublets to the determination of their chiral bias.

In order to isolate the contractile forces of individual cells within rotating doublets, we contoured each cell of the pairs and computed their individual MW over time (Fig. 4A). Despite noticeable fluctuations, we could see that, on average, one of the two cells was slightly more contractile than the other. We will hereon refer to the cell displaying the higher magnitude of MW within the pair as the “Stronger cell” and to its partner as the “Weaker cell.” In order to visualize the distribution of traction forces between the Stronger and Weaker cells of the doublets, we first rotated the MW maps of all the analyzed doublets and aligned them in such a way that the Stronger cells would be on the right side of the image (Movie S16). Then, we averaged the aligned MW maps over time and independently overlaid the resulting averaged maps for CW- and CCW-rotating doublets (Fig. 4B). This representation highlighted the localization of traction forces at the junctional edges of doublets rotating in either direction, consistent with the typical positioning of adhesions previously described in migrating cells. Interestingly, we noticed, toward the back of Stronger cells, a distinctive hot spot of traction forces, which seemed to be more prominent among CCW-rotating doublets. To characterize further the asymmetry in MW between the cells constituting the doublets, we calculated the difference in the mean MW of the two cells (i.e., Stronger cell MW − Weaker cell MW) for both CW- and CCW-rotating cell pairs (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, we noticed that CCW-rotating doublets, which were associated with higher average doublet MW levels, demonstrated a greater mechanical asymmetry between their constituting cells. This observation prompted us to assess more closely the correlation between the MW of individual cells and that of the doublets. To this end, we independently plotted the MW of both the Stronger and Weaker cells as a function of the MW of their corresponding doublets (Fig. 4D). We observed that, regardless of the rotation direction, the MW of Stronger cells demonstrated a better correlation (less dispersion) with the MW of doublets than that of Weaker cells (Correlation coefficient R2 = 0.93 for Stronger cells versus R2 = 0.85 for Weaker cells). More importantly, we noticed that at higher levels of doublet MW, where the difference between the fits of the Stronger and the Weaker cells became more pronounced, Stronger cells appeared to have an overall greater contribution, as they became increasingly contractile. This result implied that the mechanical distinction of CCW-rotating doublets, characterized by higher doublet MW as well as a greater MW asymmetry, could be mostly attributed to Stronger cells. Indeed, when comparing the MW of individual Stronger and Weaker cells belonging to CW- and CCW-rotating doublets (Fig. 4E), the difference between the average MW levels of their respective Stronger cells was more significant compared to that existing between the MW of their Weaker cells. These findings suggest that the Stronger cells in rotating doublets could have a greater impact on the determination of their chiral bias.

The More Contractile Cell of the Doublet Drives the Chiral Bias of the Pair.

To challenge the mechanical role of the Stronger cells in the chiral rotation of doublets, we tried to modulate the contractility levels of the Stronger cells within the population by introducing a more contractile cell type than HUVECs into our doublet. In the light of a previous study (47), we hypothesized that Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts (MEFs), which displayed a more prominent actomyosin network compared to HUVECs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C), would be more contractile than HUVECs. To verify this, we performed TFM measurements in MEFs and HUVECs homotypic doublets (Fig. 5 A and B) in a common experimental setup (60 µm disks and 17 kPa PAA gels). As expected, our results showed that MEF doublets demonstrated significantly higher MW levels than HUVEC ones (Fig. 5B). We then checked the impact of the higher contractility levels displayed by MEF homotypic doublets on the rotation by simultaneously monitoring both types of homotypic doublets using live imaging. We first noticed that the rotation percentage among MEF doublets was lower compared to HUVEC ones (Fig. 5E). Interestingly, homotypic MEF doublets exhibited a predominance for CCW rotation (60%, n = 434) unlike HUVEC doublets, demonstrating the expected CW-bias (60%, n = 358) (Fig. 5F). This further confirmed our conclusion that high cellular contractility levels favor a CCW rotation.

To assess the effect of the more contractile cell on the chiral rotation, we created a heterotypic system, in which doublets were composed of a less contractile HUVEC in pair with a more contractile MEF. As expected, compared to homotypic HUVEC and MEF doublets, heterotypic doublets displayed an intermediate contractility level as evident by the magnitude of their MW (Fig. 5 C and D) and actomyosin content (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C). By monitoring heterotypic doublets, in which MEFs were prelabeled with Calcein-AM, we noticed that, contrary to HUVECs and MEFs homotypic doublets in which the individual cells often occupied a comparable space on the micropattern, MEFs occupied most of the space on the disk. HUVECs were confined to the periphery of the micropattern, along which they moved more than the central MEF (Movie S17). In addition, heterotypic doublets exhibited an intermediate rotation percentage (70%, Fig. 5E) compared to both HUVEC (90% of rotation) and MEF (60% of rotation) homotypic doublets. However, their chiral bias was CCW (60%, n = 163) as is the case of MEF doublets (Fig. 5F). Altogether, these results support our hypothesis that the more contractile cells within the doublets could actually drive the chiral motion of the collectives.

Discussion

Our findings first reveal the link between the chiral bias of migrating cells and their mechanical work, supporting the idea that cell chirality is a mechanosensitive process relying on actomyosin contractility (11, 27). Indeed previous work reported the loss of chiral bias upon a disruption of the actomyosin network (20, 27, 48). In addition, the localization of myosins was found to be correlated with the orientation of the rotation in cell doublets (17) and in tissues (7, 49). Here, by directly measuring both the forces and the speed of migrating cells, and by modulating these forces over a broad range with chemical drugs, we demonstrated that the chiral bias is determined by the magnitude of contractile forces. In the endothelial and fibroblasts cells we tested, high forces appear to promote CCW bias, whereas low forces tend to impose a CW bias. Whether this relationship can be generalized to other cell types would require further investigation. However, it is interesting to hypothesize that the cellular contractility levels determine the corresponding chiral bias of their tissues.

In addition, the analysis of the MW of individual cells within the doublet consistently revealed differences between the two cells. In larger cell ensembles, a similar heterogeneity and force imbalance was previously described to impact the polarization and migration of the collective, with follower cells pulling on the future leader cell (45). Interestingly, the emergence of a leader cell, which ensures front–rear polarization and guidance among other cells via mechanical signaling, is associated with the exertion of higher traction forces (50). We found that the more contractile cell of the doublet imposes its own bias on its partner. Since a mechanical coordination can happen by the transmission of contractile forces between adjacent cells (51), it is tempting to speculate that the biased distribution of these forces can also be transmitted and impact the chirality of the collective. These data overall provide additional support to the hypothesis that chiral organization in multicellular groups is determined by the chirality developed in individual cells (6, 10, 52, 53). However, despite numerous studies on the migration of individual cells, only a few reported the spontaneous development of chiral bias in cell trajectories (54–56). This suggests that it is the integration of individual cells in a moving collective that allows their dominant bias to be revealed.

Because the chiral manifestation in individual cells is rare and transient (20), its underlying mechanism remains unclear. Several studies suggest that a combination of cytoskeleton and motor proteins can generate an individual cellular torque (4, 19, 20, 25). The imbalance between radial fibers polymerization, executed by formins, and myosin-driven contractility of transverse arcs has been proposed to control the direction of the chiral bias (20, 57). According to this hypothesis, myosin II activity would regulate the strength of the CW bias by modulating the prominence and integrity of transverse arcs at the expanse of radial fibers. Our results are consistent with this hypothesis, but the exact role of the migration process and of the integration within a collective requires further investigation. Taken together, these studies point at the need to study not only the architectures, but also the intracellular displacement and dynamics of networks to understand the propagation of chiral bias from the right-handed actin filaments to the individual cell and the chiral bias of multicellular collectives.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture.

HUVECs (HUVEC-h-TERT2 – Evercyte CHT-006-0008) were cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (Lonza EGM BulletKit CC- 3121 & CC-4133) supplemented with growth factors, 2% fetal bovine serum, and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco 15240062). The culture flasks were coated with Gelatin (Sigma G1890) 0.1% in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS Gibco 14200075) for 30 min before use. MEF cells (immortalized by stable expression of SV40 large T antigen) generated by the lab of John Eriksson (58) were a gift from the lab of Robert Goldman (59). They were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution. The cells were grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and tested regularly for Mycoplasma contamination using VenorGeM Advance (11-7024) PCR kit.

Micropatterned Glass Coverslips (CS) Preparation.

Protein micropatterns on glass CS were prepared as previously described (60).

Cleaning.

CS (EPREDIA) 20 × 20 mm or 25 × 25 mm #1.5 (CS) were washed and sonicated in acetone (Carlo Erba 528203) for 30 min, isopropanol (Sigma 34863) for 30 min, and in MilliQ water for 30 min. CS were then air-dried.

Polystyrene (PS) coating.

Clean CS were activated by an air plasma treatment (Diener electronic GmbH & Co KG - Zepto) then placed inside a container heated at 75 °C in the presence of Hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS Sigma 440191) for at least 6 h. Alternatively, a layer of Ti-Prime was spin-coated (Laurel – WS-400BBX-6NPP) on the CS for 30 s at 1,000 rpm. The CS were then placed in the spin coater (Laurell Technologies), covered with a solution of PS 1% (Acros Organics AC404720250) in toluene (108325), and spun for 30 s at 1,500 rpm.

PLL-PEG (poly(l -lysine)-poly(ethylene glycol)) coating–passivation.

The PS-coated CS were activated by air plasma treatment and then coated with PLL-PEG solution (PLL-PEG JenKemTechnology ZL187P072) 0.1 mg/mL in HEPES (H3375) 10 mM, pH 7.4 in MilliQ water for 30 min at room temperature. After incubation, the CS were dewetted using HEPES 10 mM and stored at 4 °C for at least 1 h before use.

Deep UV (ultraviolet) micropatterning.

PLL-PEG-coated CS were placed on a vacuum holder in contact with a quartz-chrome printed photomask (Toppan Photomask). The sandwich was then placed in a UVO cleaner (Jelight–Model No. 342A-220), with a power of 6 mW/cm2, for 5 min. After exposure, the patterned CS were gently detached from the mask using vacuum.

Protein coating.

The patterned CS were coated with a protein solution, composed of fibronectin (FN Sigma F1141) 20 µg/mL ± Fibrinogen from Human Plasma, Alexa Fluor 546 or 647 Conjugate (FNG Invitrogen F13192 or F35200) 10 µg/mL in sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3 Sigma S6297) 100 mM in MilliQ water for 30 min. After incubation, the patterned CS were washed three times with NaHCO3 and dewetted. Before seeding the cells, the CS were washed with sterile DPBS.

PAA (Polyacrylamide) Gel Micropatterning for TFM.

Patterned hydrogels were prepared according to the “Glass Method” described in ref. 61. 22 × 22 mm #1.5 glass CS were passivated with PLL-PEG, deep UV-patterned, and coated with adhesive proteins (FN 20 µg/mL or a mixture of FN and Col (Collagen I, rat tail Gibco A1048301) 10:10 µg/mL) as previously described. A mixture of 40% Acrylamide (Sigma A4058) and 2% Bis-acrylamide (Sigma M1533) in MilliQ water (experimental Young modulus of 16.7 kPa) was prepared according to ref. 62, degassed in a vacuum bell during protein incubation, mixed with the passivated 200 nm fluorescent beads (FluoSpheres Carboxylate-Modified 200 nm PS Microspheres Invitrogen F8810 – 580/605 nm or F8807 – 660/680 nm), sonicated for homogenization, and kept on ice. 1 µL of Ammonium persulfate (APS Sigma A3678) and 1 µL of N,N,N′,N′-Tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED T9281) were added to the 165 µL PAA mix. 25 µL of the latter solution was added on each of the 22 × 22 mm patterned CS. Silanized 20 × 20 mm CS were flipped on top of the PAA drops, and the gel was allowed to polymerize for 40 to 50 min. At the end of the incubation, the gels were gently detached from the patterned CS. Micropatterned PAA gels were then stored in NaHCO3 at 4 °C until use. Before seeding the cells, the gels were washed twice with sterile DPBS and once with warm medium.

Cells Seeding on Micropatterns.

Cells were trypsinized and seeded at the appropriate density on the micropatterns in their culture medium. For heterotypic doublets, MEFs and HUVECs were mixed at a ratio of 1:2 using the HUVECs culture medium and seeded on the micropatterns. In both cases, cells not attached to the micropatterns were removed by washing with medium before the start of imaging.

Cytoskeletal Drug Treatments.

All the chemical inhibitors were added to the cells shortly after seeding (30 min) and kept in the medium during the time of observation. Concentrations of ROCKI (Calbiochem 555550) and CalyA (Sigma 208851) are indicated in the figures.

Cells Transfection.

Cells were transfected with MLC GFP plasmid, kindly provided by Gregory Giannone’s laboratory, using the Amaxa Nucleofector kit for HUVECs. Following their trypsinization, the cells (106 cells/mL) were centrifuged at 200× g for 10 min and resuspended in 100 µL of the nucleofector solution. A volume corresponding to 3 µg of DNA was added to the mix before transferring it to the cuvette and running the dedicated program on the Amaxa Nucleofector II device. 500 µL of prewarmed medium was then added to the mix, which was then transferred into 6-well plate gelatin-coated wells containing 1 mL of warm medium. The transfected cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 5 to 6 h, after which the medium in the wells was replenished. The cells were imaged 24 to 48 h following transfection.

Immunofluorescence.

Cells were fixed for 15 min at room temperature in cytoskeleton buffer [CB = MES 10 mM, KCl (ROTH Art. No. 6781.1) 138 mM, MgCl (ROTH Art. No. KK36.1) 3 mM, EGTA (ethylene glycol-bis(beta-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N’,N’-tetraacetic acid) (Sigma E0396) 2 mM in Milli-Q water] supplemented with Sucrose (ROTH Art. No. 4661.2) 10%, Triton-X100 (EUROMEDEX 3617818) 0.1%, Glutaraldehyde (Polysciences 00216) 0.1%, and Paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15710) 4%. For p-MLC stating, fixation was preceded by a brief permeabilization using Triton-X100 0.05% in CB with 10% Glycerol (Carlo Erba 453742). The CS were rinsed three times with PBS, incubated in sodium borohydride (NaBH4 Sigma 452882) 1 mg/mL in PBS for 10 min and washed three times with PBS. The blocking solution [Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA A7030) 3% – Tween-20 (Sigma P2287) 0.1% –PBS] was then added for 45 min at room temperature. The CS were successively incubated with the primary and secondary antibodies diluted in the blocking solution for 1 h each at room temperature. After both incubations, the CS were rinsed three times using PBS-Tween 0.1%. Then, the CS were labeled with Phalloidin Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen A12379) at 1/200 and Hoechst (Invitrogen H3570) diluted in the blocking solution. Finally, the slides were rinsed three times in PBS-Tween 0.1%, once in PBS, and once in Milli-Q water and were then mounted in Mowiol 4-88 containing TetraSpeck™ Microspheres 0.5 μm (Invitrogen T7281) at 1/200.

Rabbit Anti-Phospho-Myosin Light Chain 2 (Ser19) Antibody (Cell Signaling 3671) was used at 1/100 and secondary Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 546 antibody (Invitrogen A11035) was used at 1/500.

Image Acquisition.

Time-lapse imaging of the cells rotation was performed on an epifluorescence system consisting of a Nikon inverted microscope (Ti2) equipped with a Prime BSI Express Camera (Photometrics), a pE-4000 Fluo lamp (CoolLED), and a top stage incubator for controlled temperature 37 °C and CO2 5% (Okolab). The following objectives were used: 4× Phase Contrast, 20× DIC (differential interference contrast), and 40× DIC. Time-lapse courses were followed using the nikon imaging software elements software.

Traction force microscopy was performed on a Nikon confocal spinning-disk system (Eclipse Ti-E) equipped with a CSUX1-A1 Yokogawa confocal head, an Evolve EMCCD camera (Photometrics), and a top stage incubator for controlled temperature 37 °C and CO2 5% (TOKAI). The microscope was operated by MetaMorph software (MetaImaging). Phase Contrast 40× (air) or 40× oil objectives were used for the acquisition of images and movies.

Images of the immunofluorescence stainings were acquired on a Zeiss LSM900 Airyscan 2 confocal microscope (Axio Observer) using 63× objective (Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 oil).

In all the cases, only cells that were fully spread on the micropatterns were selected for imaging.

TFM Analysis.

Following the method previously described in ref. 63, TFM data were analyzed in Fiji (64) using a macro calling different plugins (https://sites.google.com/site/qingzongtseng/imagejplugins). Beads images were paired and realigned using template matching. Displacement fields were calculated by particle imaging velocimetry (PIV), which used a normalized cross correlation–based method with an iterative scheme. Final vector-grid size was 4.6 μm × 4.6 μm.

Only doublets fully spread on the micropatterns and rotating for about 3 h were selected for further analysis.

The TFM analysis was performed in two steps. First, Fourier-transform traction cytometry was used to compute the traction force field, with a regularization parameter of 5 × 10–10, Poisson coefficient of 0.5, and a Young Modulus of 16.7 kPa. A circular region of interest was defined around the doublets, such that its area was larger than that of the micropattern. Force vectors located outside this ROI (region of interest) were discarded during the calculation of the doublets MW. The MW was computed as the sum, over the field of view, of the dot products of displacement (in meters, extracted from the PIV) and the force (in Newton) on each PIV interrogation window. The computed force on an interrogation window is the traction force (in Pascal i.e., N/m2) calculated from the fourier transform traction cytometry, times the PIV interrogation window size, set to 4.6 × 4.6 µm2 in our analysis.

The second step consisted of extracting the magnitude of forces exerted by individual cells by reprocessing the previously generated traction force files and MW maps based on the manual definition of the cell–cell junction at every time point (65). Briefly, the defined force region was split at the user-defined junction into two areas, and then the corresponding forces were assessed. The sum of the TF (traction force) vectors under each area represented the imbalance or the intercellular force. As the sum of the TF vectors in a doublet was zero, the imbalance across both cells was equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

In the process, the junction was fitted to an ellipse, whose long axis was rescaled to the size of the micropattern and used to compute the instantaneous angular displacement that provided both the direction and magnitude of doublet angular velocity.

For representing the average MW maps, the mechanical work was projected over time. The maps of distinct doublets rotating were then averaged.

For MW asymmetry visualization, the ROIs obtained by contouring the cells were used to realign the MW map at each time point, before projecting them over time. This method was used for all the rotating doublets of each subpopulation, such that all the cells displaying the higher MW in average, termed “the stronger cell,” were located to the right (Movie S16). Finally, the resulting realigned MW maps of all CW- and CCW-rotating doublets were separately projected to generate the aligned, average MW map corresponding to each subpopulation.

Image Analysis.

Analyses were performed using Fiji software.

Quantification of rotation and bias of doublets.

Time-lapse movies were examined by the user, who manually identified the rotating collectives and indicated their directionality. To decrease the influence of inherent user bias on the outcomes, the analysis of the data generated from different experiments was performed by two, three, or four independent users.

Collectives in which the cells retracted from the micropatterns or reverted their direction of rotation were eliminated from the population that was subsequently used to quantify the statistics. The reversion phenotype indeed represented a stochastic event occurring in less than 10% of the collectives. The percentage of rotating collectives within the entire cell population was computed. The bias was then quantified among the subpopulation of rotating collectives.

The angular velocity of doublets belonging to the three subpopulations described was quantified by following the displacement of the junction over time using the same method employed for isolating the MW of individual cells within doublets.

Generation of vector plots.

For big collectives, nuclei tracks were computed using Trackmate plugin in Fiji. Parameters were fine-tuned for each tracking. Aberrant tracks (when nuclei detection was lost over time or upon inaccurate detection) were manually discarded. Nuclei trajectories were represented by color-coded lines on defined periods specified in the figures. The representations display the endpoint of the trajectories (circles). The start- and endpoints of the tracks were subsequently used to generate vectorial displacement maps in Fiji using the plot function.

Actin and phosphomyosin immunofluorescence signal quantification.

Following inhibitors treatment, the total myosin signal was measured in Fiji on single plane images after contouring the doublets and then normalized by the intensity of tetraspeck fluorescent beads of 1 µm (Invitrogen T7282).

For immunofluorescence quantifications, a maximum projection of the acquired Z-stacks was first performed. The total actin and phosphomyosin signals were measured in a region of interest of constant area and then normalized by the intensity of tetraspeck fluorescent beads of 0.5 µm (Invitrogen T7281).

Data Representation and Statistical Analysis.

Mechanical work maps, traction stress maps, and vector plots were created in Fiji.

Data plotting and graph design were done on Graphpad Prism 8 (http://www.graphpad.com).

The statistical tests, indicated in each figure legend, were all performed using Graphpad Prism 8. For unpaired t tests, a nonparametric Mann–Whitney test was used.
