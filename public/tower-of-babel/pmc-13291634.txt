The Associations Between Dietary Flavonoid Intake and Neurodegenerative Diseases Risk: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Chenzhong Zong, Yang Dong, Xue Yu, Yu He, Xiaoqing Zhang, Kuo Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe incidence of neurodegenerative diseases (NDs), primarily Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, has been steadily increasing, constituting a significant global public health challenge. This study aims to evaluate the associations between dietary flavonoid intake and the risk of NDs. Data from 13,573 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were analyzed. Weighted logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, weighted restricted cubic splines, subgroup analysis, and sensitivity analysis were employed to assess the associations. Overall, higher total flavonoid intake was associated with lower odds of NDs after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.31–0.90). Among flavonoid subclasses, flavones (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.30–0.79) and flavonols (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33–0.86) showed the strongest inverse associations. In addition, restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant non‐linear relationships, with the lowest odds of NDs observed at intake levels of approximately 432 mg/day for total flavonoids, 1.7 mg/day for flavones, and 29 mg/day for flavonols. These findings suggest that moderate intake of flavonoids, particularly flavones and flavonols, may contribute to the observed inverse association between flavonoid intake and NDs.

Introduction

With population aging and changing living environments, the incidence of neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) has been steadily increasing, posing a major global public health challenge (Gitler et al. 2017; Lamptey et al. 2022; Su et al. 2020) and representing a leading cause of disability and mortality (Gitler et al. 2017).

Among NDs, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent (Erkkinen et al. 2018) and the primary cause of dementia worldwide. Currently, AD affects over 6.2 million individuals in the United States (Mogavero et al. 2021) and approximately 50 million people globally (Andronie‐Cioara et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021), with an annual growth rate of about 3.1%. Projections estimate that by 2050, the global prevalence will exceed 130 million cases (Scheltens et al. 2021), with approximately 68% of cases occurring in low‐ and middle‐income countries. The prevalence of AD rises significantly with age, with the risk in individuals aged 65–85 years increasing over 15‐fold compared to younger populations, and reaching up to 50% in those over 85 years (Erkkinen et al. 2018). Clinically, AD is characterized by progressive memory loss and cognitive impairment, with hallmark pathological features including extracellular β‐amyloid (Aβ) plaque deposition and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles formed by hyperphosphorylated tau protein (Scheltens et al. 2021).

Parkinson's disease (PD), the second most common NDs, affects approximately 1 million individuals in the United States and over 10 million globally (Ball et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2020). Its prevalence is increasing at an annual rate of approximately 4.7% (Zhu et al. 2025), with significant rises projected in the coming decades. Clinically, PD is characterized by motor symptoms, including tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability, alongside neuropsychiatric symptoms and cognitive decline (Opara et al. 2017). Pathologically, PD is marked by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the formation of cytoplasmic Lewy bodies, primarily composed of insoluble protein aggregates (Bertram and Tanzi 2005; Lamptey et al. 2022).

The pathogenesis of AD and PD is complex and not fully elucidated. Previous studies indicate that these diseases result from intricate interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors (Chin‐Chan et al. 2015; Monteiro et al. 2023). Key pathological processes include abnormal protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which trigger cellular cascades leading to oxidative stress, loss of synaptic plasticity, neuronal apoptosis, and disruption of neural networks (Klemmensen et al. 2024; Liem et al. 2017). In recent years, mounting evidence has highlighted the central role of neuroinflammation in NDs (Andronie‐Cioara et al. 2023; Dhapola et al. 2021; Twarowski and Herbet 2023). Aberrant activation of microglia and astrocytes leads to the release of inflammatory cytokines, promoting oxidative stress and excessive production of reactive nitrogen species (RNS), thereby disrupting redox homeostasis. The vicious cycle between oxidative stress and neuroinflammation is considered a critical pathogenic basis of NDs (Beura et al. 2022; Firdous et al. 2024; Simpson and Oliver 2020). Current therapeutic strategies, such as levodopa for PD and cholinesterase inhibitors for AD, provide only symptomatic relief and fail to halt or reverse neurodegeneration, often accompanied by adverse effects (Van Bulck et al. 2019). Consequently, identifying risk factors for NDs and exploring interventions targeting their fundamental mechanisms are critical for disease prevention and management.

Against this backdrop, natural bioactive compounds have garnered significant attention (Calderaro et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). Flavonoids, a class of polyphenolic compounds widely found in fruits, vegetables, teas, plant‐based foods, and herbal medicines, are categorized into six major subclasses based on their chemical structure: anthocyanidins, flavan‐3‐ols, flavanones, flavones, flavonols, and isoflavones (Wang et al. 2024). Increasing evidence suggests that flavonoids exert multi‐target, multi‐pathway effects, including inhibition of neuroinflammation, reduction of oxidative stress, and prevention of pathological protein aggregation, thereby intervening in the complex pathological networks of NDs (Chen et al. 2023; Maleki et al. 2019). As such, their potential in the prevention and treatment of NDs is receiving growing emphasis.

Previous population‐based studies have provided limited evidence on the association between flavonoid intake and NDs risk. While some studies have reported a reduced ND risk associated with flavonoids (Parmenter et al. 2025), others have found no significant association (Sandoval‐Insausti et al. 2024). The conflicting findings among previous studies may be explained by differences in the disease endpoints examined (e.g., Parkinson's disease versus broader chronic disease outcomes), study population characteristics, approaches used to assess flavonoid exposure (total flavonoid intake versus flavonoid diversity or specific subclasses), as well as variations in the control of potential confounding factors.

Given the conflicting findings and limitations in the existing literature, including a lack of comprehensive analyses from the perspective of the whole NDs, limited understanding of the specific effects of flavonoid subclasses, small sample sizes, and incomplete adjustment for covariates, this study aims to address these gaps. In addition, we conducted dose–response analyses and performed a series of sensitivity and subgroup analyses to further assess the robustness, stability, and consistency of the observed associations. We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis using nationally representative data from three cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; 2007–2010 and 2017–2018) to evaluate the association between dietary flavonoid intake, including its six major subclasses, and NDs risk. This study provides a novel perspective with large‐scale population evidence to inform dietary strategies for NDs prevention.

Methods

Data Source

The data were obtained from three NHANES cycles spanning 2007–2010 and 2017–2018. NHANES utilizes a stratified, multistage probability sampling approach to gather nutrition and health information from a nationally representative, non‐institutionalized USA sample (Patel et al. 2016). The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethical approval for NHANES was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and written informed consent was provided by all participants (details of NCHS Ethics Review Board approval are available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html). All data are publicly accessible online (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Study Participants

This study initially screened 29,940 participants from the 2007–2010 and 2017–2018 cycles of the NHANES. After applying inclusion criteria, 29,481 participants remained, based on the following: (1) complete medication data for Parkinson's disease or Alzheimer's disease (n = 29,660) and (2) non‐pregnant status (n = 29,481). Exclusion criteria included: (1) age less than 20 years (n = 12,158), (2) incomplete data on total flavonoid intake (n = 3750), and (3) missing weights (n = 0). Following inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 13,573 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

The flowchart for participant selection.

Identification of AD

In the NHANES database, medications were categorized based on their therapeutic indications. Participants using medications designated for AD were identified as AD patients. The medications employed for AD identification included rivastigmine, galantamine, donepezil, and memantine (Zhao et al. 2022).

Identification of PD

Patients with PD were identified using specific PD medications, including benztropine, carbidopa, levodopa, ropinirole, methyldopa, entacapone, amantadine, trihexyphenidyl, selegiline, pramipexole, and bromocriptine (Huang et al. 2025; Yong et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2022).

Assessment of Flavonoid Intake

Data of flavonoid intake from foods and beverages were sourced from the Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS), U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) Food and corresponding NHANES dietary data (https://www.ars.usda.gov/northeast‐area/beltsville‐md‐bhnrc/beltsville‐human‐nutrition‐research‐center/food‐surveys‐research‐group/docs/fndds‐flavonoid‐database/). The flavonoid content (mg/100 g) of each beverage or food item was determined by the USDA Nutrient Data Laboratory (Harnly et al. 2006). The daily intake of each flavonoid was calculated using the following equation: Flavonoid intake (mg/day) = Σ [Food consumption (g/day) × Flavonoid content (mg/100 g)/100]. Flavonoid intake was assessed using two 24‐h dietary recalls. The average of the total flavonoid intake from the first and second days was calculated. Participants with data available only for the first day were excluded (Wang et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023).

Total flavonoids were classified into six major groups, comprising 29 subclasses. These include anthocyanidins (cyanidin, delphinidin, malvidin, pelargonidin, peonidin, and petunidin); flavan‐3‐ols encompassing (−)‐epicatechin, (−)‐epicatechin 3‐gallate, (−)‐epigallocatechin, (−)‐epigallocatechin 3‐gallate, (+)‐catechin, (+)‐gallocatechin, theaflavin, theaflavin‐3,3′‐digallate, theaflavin‐3′‐gallate, theaflavin‐3‐gallate, and thearubigins; flavanones (eriodictyol, hesperetin, and naringenin); flavones (apigenin and luteolin); flavonols (isorhamnetin, kaempferol, myricetin, and quercetin); and isoflavones (daidzein, genistein, and glycitein) (Table S1).

Specific cycles (2007–2008, 2009–2010, and 2017–2018) provided complete flavonoid intake data and utilized consistent dietary assessment methods. These cycles yielded a sufficiently large sample size for robust statistical analysis, minimizing potential temporal variations in dietary patterns.

We carefully reviewed the questionnaires and examination protocols across different NHANES cycles. Following the official NHANES guidelines and documentation, we harmonized variables and measurements to ensure consistency and comparability across survey years.

Covariates Assessment

Covariates were selected based on prior research and clinical relevance, incorporating potential confounding variables associated with NDs to minimize bias. The analysis integrated data from demographic characteristics, questionnaire responses, physical examinations, and laboratory measurements.

Covariates were categorized as follows: age was divided into three groups (20 ≤ Age < 40, 40 ≤ Age < 60, and Age ≥ 60 years); gender was classified as Male or Female; Race was grouped into Mexican American, Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic, and Other/Multiracial categories; Education level was categorized as Less than 9th grade, 9–11th grade, High school graduate, Some college or Associate's degree, and College graduate or above; marital status was classified as Married/Living with partner, Widowed/Divorced/Separated, or Never married; family income to poverty ratio (PIR) was grouped into < 1.0, 1 ≤ PIR < 3, and ≥ 3.0 (Ma, Zhang, et al. 2024); and pregnancy status was categorized as Yes, No, or Unknown. Physical activity was quantified by calculating the metabolic equivalent (MET) level multiplied by the weekly minutes of moderate and vigorous activities. It was categorized into three groups: Insufficient (MET < 600), Moderate (600 ≤ MET < 3000), and High (MET ≥ 3000). Data with a cumulative weekly duration exceeding 84,000 min were deemed implausible and excluded. Smoking status was divided into three categories: Current Smoker, Former Smoker, and Never Smoker (Zhang et al. 2023). Alcohol consumption was classified into two groups: Non‐drinker and Drinker. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the standard formula and treated as a continuous variable.

Comorbidities included in the analysis were hypertension (Cao et al. 2023), hypercholesterolemia (Grundy et al. 2019), diabetes (Guo et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023), chronic kidney disease (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Glomerular Diseases Work Group 2021), stroke, and heart diseases (Zhang and Yao 2025).

Detailed diagnostic criteria for variables are shown in Table S2.

Other Indicators

Additional participant indicators were evaluated, including a standard biochemistry profile comprising serum uric acid (mg/dL), serum creatinine (mg/dL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L). Indicators related to hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease included antihypertensive therapy, systolic blood pressure (mm Hg), diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg), antihyperlipidemia therapy, triglycerides (mg/dL), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C, mg/dL), total cholesterol (mg/dL), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C, mg/dL), use of insulin, use of diabetic pills, plasma fasting glucose (mmol/L), glycohemoglobin (%), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2), and C‐reactive protein (mg/L). The complete blood count included white blood cell count (WBC, 1000 cells/μL), lymphocyte count (1000 cells/μL), monocyte count (1000 cells/μL), neutrophil count (1000 cells/μL), red cell distribution width (RDW, %), platelet count (PLT, 1000 cells/μL), and red blood cell count (million cells/μL).

This study incorporated inflammatory biomarkers known to play a significant role in various diseases. Inflammatory biomarkers from the NHANES database included the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte‐to‐monocyte ratio (LMR), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII). The formulas for these biomarkers are as follows: NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, PLR = platelet count/lymphocyte count, LMR = lymphocyte count/monocyte count, SIRI = (neutrophil count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count, and SII = (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count (Cai et al. 2023; Lan et al. 2023; Ma, Cai, et al. 2024; Zhan et al. 2024).

Statistical Analyses

Direct exclusion of participants with incomplete data may introduce selection bias and reduce the generalizability of findings. The covariates with the highest proportions of missing data were Alcohol Status (24.68%), Family Income to Poverty Ratio (9.22%), and Body Mass Index (BMI) (1.05%). To address missing data, a multilevel imputation approach tailored for survey data was employed (Muntner et al. 2020). The Jomo package was used to generate five imputed datasets, with 500 burn‐in iterations and 500 updates performed to ensure stochastic independence among the imputed datasets.

To account for the complex survey design of NHANES, strata, primary sampling units, and probability weights were applied to address clustering, stratification, nonresponse probability, and oversampling of specific population groups. In this research, we chose the WTDR2D for 2007–2010 and 2017–2018.

Statistical analyses were conducted using appropriate NHANES sampling weights, accounting for the complex multistage cluster survey design. Baseline characteristics were reported as unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages for categorical variables, and as weighted medians with 25th and 75th interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Comparisons of baseline characteristics were performed using design‐based methods, specifically Rao‐Scott adjusted χ 2 tests for categorical variables and design‐based Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Weighted logistic regression models were employed to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between total flavonoid intake, as well as its six subgroups, and the risk of NDs. Total flavonoid intake and five of its subgroups were categorized into weighted quartiles (Q1–Q4) to evaluate their associations with neurodegenerative disease risk, expressed as OR and 95% CI. Due to 37.6% of isoflavone values being zero, isoflavones were divided into two groups (Q1 and Q2) based on the weighted median. The groups corresponding to Q1 to Q4 are respectively assigned the numbers 1–4 to calculate the p for Trend. Three analytical models were employed in this study. Model 1 was unadjusted for covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender. Model 3 was adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, including age, gender, race, education level, marital status, family income to poverty ratio, physical activity (MET), smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart diseases, and stroke. p‐values for trends were assessed by treating the quartile levels as an ordinal variable.

Under Model 3, with adjustment for all covariates, flavonoid levels were treated as a continuous variable. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the discriminative ability of flavonoid levels in predicting NDs status. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated as a summary measure of predictive accuracy, with 95% CI estimated using bootstrap resampling.

Weighted restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to explore and visualize the dose–response relationships between total flavonoid intake, as well as its six subgroups, and the NDs. The number of knots was determined by minimizing Akaike's information criterion while ensuring curve smoothness across different node configurations (3, 4, or 5), balancing optimal model fit and the risk of overfitting. Ultimately, three knots were selected for the RCS analysis, placed at Harrell's recommended percentiles (5th, 50th, and 90th), to stabilize nodes against extreme intake distributions while preventing overfitting. Non‐linearity was assessed using the Wald test, with a p‐value < 0.05 indicating evidence of a non‐linear association.

For the Total Flavonoids, Anthocyanidins, Flavan‐3‐ols, Flavones, and Flavonols that showed differences in Logistic analysis, we conducted a stratified analysis. Stratified analyses were performed to assess effect modification by variables including age, gender, race, education, marital status, family income to poverty ratio, physical activity (MET), smoking status, alcohol status, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart diseases, and stroke. All covariates were adjusted, excluding the stratification variable itself. Interaction analyses were performed by including interaction terms between the total flavonoids, anthocyanidins, flavan‐3‐ols, flavones, flavonols and the stratification variables within the regression models. Subgroup interactions were assessed using likelihood ratio tests. Results for total flavonoids, anthocyanidins, flavan‐3‐ols, flavones, flavonols as a categorical variable are shown in Table S3–S7.

To enhance the reliability of the study findings, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted. Following Rubin's Rules, five imputed datasets were used to compute weighted logistic regression models, estimating ORs and 95% CIs for the association between total flavonoids, their six subgroups, and NDs risk. The results from the five datasets were subsequently pooled. Additionally, participants were stratified based on the presence of Parkinson's disease or Alzheimer's disease to explore the association with flavonoid intake.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2, with a two‐tailed alpha value of 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Participants Characteristics by NDs

A total of 13,573 participants were included, of whom 2.2% had NDs. Compared with those without NDs, NDs participants were older, more likely to be female and non‐Hispanic White, had lower educational attainment, were less physically active, and differed in marital status distribution (all p < 0.05). Clinically, they had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, heart disease, and stroke, accompanied by greater use of antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering therapy. Laboratory findings showed higher fasting glucose and glycated hemoglobin, lower eGFR, reduced red blood cell counts, and elevated inflammatory markers. Nutritionally, total flavonoid intake and several flavonoid subclasses were significantly lower in the NDs group. After Bonferroni correction, differences in age, marital status, physical activity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, heart disease, stroke, eGFR, glycated hemoglobin, red blood cell counts, and intakes of flavones and luteolin remained statistically significant. Participants Characteristics by Quartiles of Total Flavonoids Intake

Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), participants in the highest quartile (Q4) were generally younger, had higher educational attainment and household income, and were more likely to be non‐Hispanic White. They were less likely to be current smokers and exhibited lower body mass index. Clinically, Q4 participants demonstrated more favorable profiles, with higher HDL cholesterol and lower systemic inflammation. Specifically, they showed reduced levels of C‐reactive protein, white blood cell counts, and neutrophils, as well as lower values of the composite inflammatory indices SIRI and SII, both of which increase with greater inflammatory burden. Nutritionally, Q4 participants had substantially higher intakes of total flavonoids and major subclasses (flavan‐3‐ols, flavones, flavonols, isoflavones, and anthocyanidins). Overall, higher flavonoid intake was associated with healthier demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic characteristics, along with reduced systemic inflammation. Discriminative Ability Based on ROC

Under Model 3, ROC analyses showed substantial discrimination of the fully adjusted model incorporating total flavonoid intake (six subclasses) and conventional covariates in predicting NDs (Figure 2). Overall, total flavonoids and their major subclasses exhibited moderate discriminative ability for NDs risk, suggesting dietary intake has limited explanatory power but aligns with expectations (due to numerous confounding factors).

Dose–Response Relationships Based on RCS

RCS models were applied to examine the dose–response relationships between total flavonoid intake and its six subclasses with the risk of NDs (Figure 3). Significant non‐linear associations were observed for total flavonoids, flavones, and flavonols (all p overall < 0.05, p non‐linear < 0.05). The lowest risk points were identified at approximately 432 mg/day for total flavonoids, 1.7 mg/day for flavones, and 29 mg/day for flavonols. Anthocyanidins and flavan‐3‐ols showed borderline evidence of non‐linearity, but the associations did not reach statistical significance. No significant linear or non‐linear associations were detected for flavanones and isoflavones. These findings suggest that moderate intake of total flavonoids and selected subclasses may be linked to lower risk of NDs, with evidence of non‐linear dose–response patterns.

Subgroup Analysis

In stratified analyses of total flavonoid intake, a significant interaction was observed with marital status in the “Never Married” subgroup (p for interaction = 0.025). However, the limited number of events in this subgroup across quartiles (6, 9, 2, and 4 cases in Q1–Q4, respectively) resulted in unstable OR estimates with wide confidence intervals. For instance, the OR for Q2 was 10.82 (95% CI: 1.41–83.07), likely reflecting insufficient sample size and random variation rather than true effect modification. Consequently, this result should be interpreted cautiously.

For anthocyanidins and flavan‐3‐ols, no statistically significant interactions were observed in any stratified analyses (all p for interaction > 0.05). In contrast, stratified analyses of flavones revealed a significant interaction with physical activity level (p for interaction = 0.038). Among individuals with high physical activity (MET ≥ 3000, n = 4189, 34.2% of the sample), a decreasing trend in NDs risk was observed across intake quartiles, with a significant protective effect in the highest quartile (Q4; OR: 0.16; 95% CI: 0.08–0.33; p < 0.001).

In stratified analyses of flavonols, a significant interaction was observed with age (p for interaction = 0.022). In the 20–40‐year age group, extremely low OR were noted in some intake quartiles (e.g., Q2: OR = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.00–0.19); however, the total number of NDs cases in this age group was only 14, with just 1 event each in Q2 and Q3, leading to highly unstable estimates. This suggests that these findings likely reflect insufficient sample size and random variation rather than true effect modification. In contrast, among individuals aged ≥ 60 years, a more consistent and robust protective effect was observed (Q3: OR = 0.51; 95% CI: 0.30–0.86; Q4: OR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.36–0.99; p for trend = 0.015), suggesting that higher flavonols intake may be associated with reduced NDs risk in older populations.

Sensitivity Analysis

In analyses based on a single dataset, higher intakes of total flavonoids, anthocyanidins, flavan‐3‐ols, flavanones, flavones, and flavonols were consistently associated with reduced risk of NDs, whereas isoflavones showed no significant associations. Specifically, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the highest quartile (Q4) of total flavonoids was associated with a 48% lower risk in the fully adjusted model (Model 3: OR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.31–0.90; p for trend = 0.016). Flavones and flavonols showed the strongest and most consistent protective effects (flavones: OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.30–0.79; p = 0.006; flavonols: OR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.33–0.86; p = 0.013; both p for trend < 0.05). Anthocyanidins (Q4: OR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.30–0.97; p = 0.039) and flavan‐3‐ols (Q4: OR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.90; p = 0.020) also demonstrated inverse associations, though results were borderline significant in some models. Flavanones exhibited inconsistent results, with significance lost after full adjustment, and isoflavones remained nonsignificant.

To enhance robustness, five imputed datasets were generated using the jomo approach and pooled according to Rubin's Rules. Imputation did not significantly alter the direction or trend of estimates but reduced statistical variability, and estimates became more precise and statistical significance strengthened. Notably, anthocyanidins and flavan‐3‐ols, which previously showed borderline associations, demonstrated clear inverse associations after pooling (anthocyanidins Q4: OR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.92; p = 0.024; flavan‐3‐ols Q4: OR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.34–0.87; p = 0.011). Flavones (Q4: OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.32–0.76; p = 0.002) and flavonols (Q4: OR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.34–0.83; p = 0.005) continued to show strong and consistent protective effects across models, while isoflavones remained nonsignificant (Table S10).

Collectively, these findings indicate that the observed association between higher flavonoid intake and lower odds of neurodegenerative diseases remained consistent across multiple analytic strategies. The use of multiple imputation confirmed the consistency of the results and reduced the influence of potential bias due to missing data.

In the analysis of PD, higher intake of total flavonoids was associated with a reduced risk. Participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly lower odds compared with the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.34–0.96; p = 0.034), with a clear dose–response trend (p for trend = 0.026). Among flavonoid subclasses, flavan‐3‐ols, flavones, and flavonols showed the most consistent inverse associations. For flavan‐3‐ols, the highest quartile was linked to a significantly lower risk (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.36–0.96; p = 0.033), with a linear decreasing trend (p for trend = 0.040). Flavones demonstrated a strong protective effect, with risk decreasing progressively from Q3 (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.38–0.96; p = 0.032) to Q4 (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.74; p = 0.001), and a robust dose–response relationship (p for trend = 0.001). Flavonols exhibited the most robust association, with significant reductions across all higher quartiles (Q2: OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.40–0.82; Q3: OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.41–1.00; Q4: OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32–0.84) and a consistent trend (p for trend = 0.014). In contrast, anthocyanidins displayed a suggestive but weaker protective effect (Q4: OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.36–1.14; p = 0.134), while flavanones (Q4: OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.55–1.25; p = 0.370) and isoflavones (p for trend = 0.371) showed no significant associations with PD risk. Overall, these findings indicate that flavonols, flavones, and flavan‐3‐ols showed the strongest inverse associations with PD among the flavonoid subclasses examined (Tables S11–S16).

In the analysis of AD, higher total flavonoid intake was generally associated with lower odds of AD, although the associations appeared weaker than those observed for PD. Participants in the highest quartile of total flavonoid intake tended to have lower odds of AD compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.16–1.17 in Model 3), although the association did not reach statistical significance. Among flavonoid subclasses, anthocyanidins showed the most consistent inverse associations. Significant associations were observed in both the third quartile (Q3 vs. Q1: OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.15–0.81) and the fourth quartile (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.11–0.76) in Model 3, accompanied by a significant trend across intake categories. Flavan‐3‐ols also demonstrated an inverse association, particularly in the highest intake group (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.17–1.12 in Model 3), although the trend was not statistically significant (p = 0.177). Flavones showed a similar pattern, with the strongest inverse association observed in Q3, whereas the association in Q4 was not statistically significant. Flavonols exhibited the strongest associations overall. Participants in Q3 had substantially lower odds of AD (OR = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.12–0.76), and a significant trend was observed in Model 2 (p for trend = 0.023), although this pattern was less consistent after further adjustment in Model 3. In contrast, flavanones and isoflavones were not significantly associated with AD (Tables S17–S19).

Collectively, these findings suggest that anthocyanidins and flavonols, and to a lesser extent flavan‐3‐ols, exhibited the most consistent inverse associations with AD among the flavonoid subclasses examined, although the associations were generally less pronounced than those observed for PD.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this cross‐sectional analysis of 13,573 NHANES participants, higher total flavonoid intake was associated with lower odds of NDs after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.31–0.90). Among flavonoid subclasses, flavones (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.30–0.79) and flavonols (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33–0.86) showed the strongest inverse associations. In addition, restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant non‐linear relationships, with the lowest odds of NDs observed at intake levels of approximately 432 mg/day for total flavonoids, 1.7 mg/day for flavones, and 29 mg/day for flavonols.

Comparison With Previous Studies

In the UK Biobank, a study involving over 120,000 participants demonstrated that higher dietary flavonoid diversity was associated with reduced all‐cause mortality and risk of major chronic diseases. When flavonoid diversity was held constant, individuals in the highest quintile of flavonoid intake exhibited approximately 20% lower risk of NDs compared to the lowest quintile (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.68–0.94) (Parmenter et al. 2025).

A study from the NHANES among individuals aged ≥ 60 years found that the highest quartile of anthocyanidins intake was significantly associated with a reduced risk of AD compared to the lowest quartile (OR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.97–0.99) (Chen et al. 2025). Although inclusion criteria and covariate adjustments differed slightly from our study, consistent findings were observed in both the overall population and stratified analyses of those aged ≥ 60 years, indicating a significant association between high anthocyanidin intake and reduced AD risk.

A community‐based study further reported that higher intake of pelargonidin, a subclass of anthocyanidins found in strawberries, was associated with fewer AD‐related neuropathological changes, particularly a negative correlation with neurofibrillary tangles formed by hyperphosphorylated tau protein (Agarwal et al. 2022).

Another NHANES analysis revealed that moderate intakes of total flavonoids (OR: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.23–0.93), flavan‐3‐ols (OR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.31–0.96), and flavonols (OR: 0.48; 95% CI: 0.27–0.87) were significantly associated with reduced PD risk (Gong and Fei 2025). In our study, which incorporated additional PD screening criteria based on medication use and imputed covariates, a larger PD population was identified. Beyond confirming these findings, we also observed that high flavones intake was associated with reduced PD risk.

However, clinical evidence regarding flavonoids in PD prevention remains limited and controversial. In a prospective study involving 49,281 men (Health Professionals Follow‐up Study) and 80,336 women (Nurses' Health Study), followed for 20–22 years, 805 PD cases were identified. Among men, the highest quintile of total flavonoid intake was associated with a 40% lower PD risk compared to the lowest quintile (HR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.43–0.83; p for trend = 0.001), but no significant association was observed in women or the overall cohort (Gao et al. 2012). After extending follow‐up to 30–32 years (80,701 women and 48,782 men), 1390 incident PD cases were identified. Baseline total flavonoid intake (Q5 vs. Q1) showed no significant association with PD risk (men: HR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.69–1.14; women: HR: 1.27; 95% CI: 0.98–1.64) (Sandoval‐Insausti et al. 2024). However, among 599 women and 652 men with newly diagnosed PD, higher flavonoids intake (particularly anthocyanins and flavan‐3‐ols) and consumption of flavonoid‐rich foods (e.g., berries and red wine) were associated with lower mortality risk (Zhang et al. 2022).

Studies on cognitive impairment, closely linked to NDs, have shown similar results. In the Rush Memory and Aging Project (921 participants, 220 ad events), flavonols intake was significantly inversely associated with AD risk. Compared to the lowest quintile, the highest quintile had reduced risk for total flavonols (HR: 0.52; 95% CI: 0.33–0.84), kaempferol (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.77), myricetin (HR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.40–0.97), and isorhamnetin (HR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.39–0.98) (Holland et al. 2020).

Epidemiological evidence also supports the protective role of flavonoids against subjective cognitive decline (SCD). Higher intakes of flavones (OR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.57–0.68), flavanones (OR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.58–0.68), and anthocyanins (OR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.72–0.84) were significantly associated with reduced SCD risk (all p for trend < 0.001) (Yeh et al. 2021).

In a 23‐year follow‐up of the Danish Diet, Cancer, and Health Study (55,985 participants), moderate intakes of flavonols (HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.82–0.99), anthocyanins (HR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.76–0.93), flavanones (HR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.80–0.99), and flavones (HR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.77–0.95) were associated with reduced incident dementia risk. Although total flavonoid intake showed no statistically significant association with AD, a clear non‐linear inverse trend was observed (Bondonno et al. 2021).

In another prospective UK Biobank study (121,986 participants), adherence to a strictly flavonoid‐rich dietary pattern (particularly tea, red wine, and berries) was associated with significantly reduced dementia risk (HR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.57–0.89) (Jennings et al. 2024). Another study suggested that this protective effect may be partly mediated by reduced white matter hyperintensity volume, a marker closely linked to AD and related dementias (Shishtar et al. 2020).

Overall, evidence from diverse populations and cohorts suggests that higher flavonoid intake is associated with reduced risks of NDs, cognitive impairment, and dementia, though results vary by subclass, gender, and follow‐up duration. These differences may stem from variations in study population characteristics, dietary assessment methods, and covariate adjustments. Our findings align with most prior literature and provide additional epidemiological evidence by focusing on the general population with NDs.

Mechanisms

Genetic susceptibility and various environmental factors contribute to the onset and progression of NDs by inducing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, pathological protein aggregation, and cellular homeostasis imbalance (Langworth‐Green et al. 2023; Moujalled et al. 2021; Sies 2023; Simon et al. 2020). These pathological processes further impair the function of neurons, glial cells, and immune cells in the central nervous system (CNS), ultimately leading to neuronal loss (Moujalled et al. 2021). Epidemiological evidence suggests that inflammation is not merely a passive response but likely a key upstream driver of NDs progression. Peripheral inflammatory conditions, such as hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes, and inflammatory bowel disease, are associated with an increased risk of PD (Simon et al. 2020). Current treatment strategies for AD and PD primarily focus on symptomatic relief and are often accompanied by significant adverse effects. Thus, exploring natural bioactive compounds with higher safety profiles that mitigate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress holds significant research and clinical importance (Sengupta and Kayed 2022).

Regulation of Oxidative Stress and Activation of Endogenous Antioxidant Pathways

Oxidative stress is a central pathological mechanism in the development and progression of NDs. Impaired endogenous antioxidant defense systems, coupled with excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a high proportion of unsaturated fatty acids in neuronal membranes, render neurons more susceptible to oxidative damage, ultimately leading to cellular injury and death (Yoon et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025).

Flavonoids exert antioxidant effects through multiple mechanisms. The phenolic hydroxyl groups in their structure enable direct scavenging of ROS, neutralizing free radicals and reducing neuronal oxidative damage (Jomova et al. 2025; Rębas 2025). Flavan‐3‐ols and their metabolites act as scavengers of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (Aron and Kennedy 2008) and inhibit apoptosis by modulating cellular signaling pathways (Spencer et al. 2001).

More importantly, flavonoids activate endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms. The Keap1‐Nrf2‐ARE pathway is critical in regulating cellular antioxidant responses. Studies demonstrate that flavonoids from Cistanche deserticola inhibit Keap1, preventing Nrf2 degradation, thereby promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and binding to antioxidant response elements. This induces the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) (Zhao et al. 2025). Similarly, apigenin enhances SOD and GPx activity (Calderaro et al. 2022).

Suppression of Neuroinflammation and Regulation of Glial Cell Function

In the CNS, sustained overactivation of microglia and astrocytes is a key driver of neuroinflammation (Filannino et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2025; Zhang, Ma, et al. 2025; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2025). Under pathological conditions, microglia and astrocytes polarize toward pro‐inflammatory M1 and A1 phenotypes, respectively, releasing large amounts of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic substances that accelerate neuronal damage and disease progression (Hu et al. 2025; Zhang, Ma, et al. 2025; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2025). Flavonoids effectively suppress glial cell overactivation and promote their polarization toward anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective M2/A2 phenotypes, thereby restoring CNS immune homeostasis (Filannino et al. 2025; Zhang, Ma, et al. 2025; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2025).

Additionally, flavonoids inhibit the maturation of peripheral dendritic cells by reducing CD80 and CD86 expression (Bourque and Hawiger 2023), thereby decreasing pro‐inflammatory cytokine release and downregulating adaptive and innate immune responses (Jomova et al. 2025).

At the molecular level, flavonoids modulate inflammation by interfering with signaling pathways. Nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB) is a critical regulator of inflammation, and flavonoids such as luteolin (Li et al. 2019) and baicalein (Kuwar and Kalia 2025) inhibit its activation, blocking transcription of pro‐inflammatory genes. Flavonoids like quercetin and myricetin also suppress the activation of protein kinase C (PKC), tyrosine kinases, and phosphatidylinositol kinases, reducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) (Yokoyama et al. 2015).

The anti‐inflammatory effects of flavonoids extend beyond the nervous system. Atherosclerosis, recognized as an inflammatory disease, involves plaque formation linked to adhesion molecule activation (Del Bo' et al. 2016). Major citrus flavonoids, such as quercetin, naringin, hesperidin, and rutin, target these molecules, reducing atherosclerosis risk and indirectly mitigating the adverse effects of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular inflammation on NDs (Ebrahimi et al. 2023).

The relationship between alcohol consumption and AD risk remains controversial. Chronic heavy drinking increases dementia and AD risk by inducing inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction (Piazza‐Gardner et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2008), whereas moderate drinking has been associated with reduced risk (Chandrashekar et al. 2023; Piazza‐Gardner et al. 2013). In our study, the proportion of alcohol consumers was lower in the NDs group than in the non‐NDs group, suggesting a potential dose‐dependent biphasic effect of alcohol, the mechanisms of which require further elucidation.

The association between smoking and AD also warrants attention. Although smoking is a major risk factor for lung cancer, nicotine has been reported to exert neuroprotective effects, including promoting neuronal survival, reducing injury, and mitigating excitotoxicity (Dong et al. 2020). In this study, the proportion of current smokers was lower in the NDs group than in the non‐NDs group.

Analysis of inflammatory biomarkers further indicated higher systemic inflammation in NDs patients. Before full adjustment, the NDs group exhibited higher neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR: 2.14 vs. 1.93, p = 0.028), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR: 124 vs. 118, p = 0.042), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII: 509 vs. 468, p = 0.039), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI: 1.13 vs. 1.03, p = 0.038), and a lower lymphocyte‐to‐monocyte ratio (LMR: 3.67 vs. 3.88, p = 0.045) compared to controls. In quartile analyses of total flavonoid intake, SIRI and SII levels decreased from Q1 to Q3 but slightly increased from Q3 to Q4, consistent with the lowest risk point for total flavonoid intake (~432 mg/day, within Q4) identified in rcs spline analyses. This partly reflects higher inflammation levels in NDs patients and suggests a potential “optimal range” effect for total flavonoids intake.

Public Health

In stratified analyses of flavones, physical activity level (MET) exhibited a significant interaction (p for interaction = 0.038). Among individuals with high physical activity (MET ≥ 3000), a decreasing trend in NDs risk was observed across intake quartiles, with significantly lower odds of NDs in the highest quartile (Q4) (OR: 0.16; 95% CI: 0.08–0.33; p < 0.001). Existing literature suggests that physical activity may act synergistically in ND prevention and intervention by enhancing neuroplasticity through multiple pathways, including promoting neurotrophic factor release, modulating neuroinflammation, reducing oxidative stress, and strengthening synaptic connections and neurogenesis (Ben Ezzdine et al. 2025). Therefore, physical activity may interact with flavone intake through shared anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroplasticity‐related pathways, potentially contributing to the stronger inverse association observed between flavone intake and NDs among individuals with higher levels of physical activity.

Beyond this significant interaction, no other notable interaction effects were observed across sociodemographic characteristics or health conditions. This suggests that the inverse association between flavone intake and NDs was generally consistent across most subgroups, indicating a robustness of the observed association. This finding is particularly important, as dietary factors such as total energy intake, vitamin B12, or iron may vary significantly across populations (Li et al. 2025; Pasricha et al. 2021). The consistent inverse association between flavone intake and NDs across most subgroups suggests that this relationship may be broadly applicable across diverse populations.

Notably, our study identified a potential non‐linear relationship between NDs risk and the intake of total flavonoids and certain subclasses, where the inverse association appeared to plateau at a certain intake level and may even reverse at higher levels. This finding aligns with non‐linear associations observed for flavonoids in other chronic diseases (Kong et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2023), suggesting an “optimal moderation” principle, whereby both excessively high and low intake levels may be associated with less favorable outcomes than a moderate intake range.

This phenomenon may be explained by several mechanisms: (1) Certain flavonoids, such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) (Galati and O'Brien 2004) or those with phenol B ring structures (e.g., apigenin, naringenin), may generate ROS when exposed to transition metal ions or through peroxidase/H2O2‐driven oxidation reactions (Galati et al. 1999, 2001); (2) In some cases, long‐term high‐dose polyphenol dietary supplements may adversely affect organs such as the thyroid (Rebas et al. 2020); (3) Certain flavonoids (e.g., quercetin) can cross the blood–brain barrier, potentially inducing toxicity in brain tissue at excessive levels (Youdim et al. 2004). Therefore, although flavonoids have been associated with favorable neurological outcomes in experimental and epidemiological studies, their optimal intake levels warrant further investigation.

Notably, the first dietary recommendations for flavonoids were issued in 2022, suggesting a daily intake of 400–600 mg of catechins for potential cardiometabolic benefits (Crowe‐White et al. 2022). Our findings further suggest that appropriate flavonoid intake may be associated with favorable neurological outcomes in addition to its potential cardiometabolic benefits.

In summary, these findings provide epidemiological evidence supporting further investigation of flavonoid intake in the context of dietary recommendations and public health strategies aimed at promoting healthy neurological aging.

Multi‐Omics Technologies and Treatment Challenges

In recent years, the rapid advancement of multi‐omics technologies has substantially enhanced our understanding of disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies (Jia et al. 2026). Integrative analyses incorporating transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and microbiome profiling have revealed the molecular mechanisms underlying the initiation and progression of various diseases, including Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, across multiple biological layers (Chang et al. 2025; Clark et al. 2021; Jin et al. 2026; Paul et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023).

A recent study established a systems‐level network pharmacology framework by integrating computational analyses, experimental validation, and epidemiological evidence, thereby characterizing the pharmacological properties of flavonoids in the treatment of neurological and cardiovascular diseases (Fujisaki et al. 2026). Such findings highlight the value of combining population‐based evidence with systems biology approaches to elucidate the complex biological effects of dietary bioactive compounds.

Several medications, such as donepezil and memantine, have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the symptomatic treatment of AD. However, these drugs exhibit considerable inter‐individual variability in efficacy and are often associated with significant economic burdens and adverse effects, including gastrointestinal symptoms and heart rate alterations (Tonda‐Turo et al. 2018).

Compared to existing treatments, flavonoids target multiple AD‐related biomarkers through diverse pathways, demonstrating the advantage of multi‐target intervention (Liga et al. 2023). Their use may not only reduce the adverse effects of approved drugs and improve patient adherence (Li et al. 2022) but also offer a potential alternative to synthetic drugs that rely on single‐mechanism approaches (Hasan et al. 2023; Minocha et al. 2022).

Neural stem cells (NSCs) play a critical role in the pathogenesis and repair of neurodegenerative diseases. NSCs can regenerate and differentiate into neurons and astrocytes, thereby compensating for neuronal loss while upregulating nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) to enhance synaptic plasticity and functional connectivity. Polyphenolic compounds with antioxidant properties, such as resveratrol, promote NSC proliferation by activating the SIRT1 signaling pathway (Kumar et al. 2016). Similarly, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) modulates the CREB/BDNF pathway, facilitating NSC differentiation into functional neurons and enhancing NSC survival and differentiation through increased NGF levels (Ding et al. 2017). These findings suggest that flavonoids may represent a key therapeutic avenue for neurodegenerative diseases by modulating NSC function.

Despite robust preclinical evidence highlighting the therapeutic potential of flavonoids in neurodegenerative diseases, their clinical translation faces multiple challenges. Major obstacles include limited blood–brain barrier permeability, poor oral bioavailability due to high lipophilicity, extensive hepatic and intestinal metabolism, and suboptimal photothermal stability (Alharbi et al. 2023; Almatroodi and Rahmani 2025). Even for flavonoids capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier, their cerebral concentrations and retention times are often insufficient to produce sustained pharmacological effects. This “mismatch between target accessibility and drug exposure” is a primary reason why many flavonoids demonstrate significant effects in cellular and animal models but perform poorly or fail in clinical trials.

To address these limitations, chemical modification, nano‐delivery systems, freeze‐drying, and cold plasma processing are possible strategies to enhance flavonoid bioavailability and stability include (Cao et al. 2021). These emerging technologies offer promising directions for overcoming translational bottlenecks in flavonoid applications, potentially improving clinical outcomes and patients' quality of life.

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers several key strengths. (1) Unlike prior UK Biobank studies that explored associations between flavonoids and various chronic diseases, including NDs, this investigation is the first to focus specifically on NDs, employing stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria alongside systematic covariate adjustment in a large‐scale analysis to enhance specificity. (2) Dietary flavonoid intake was assessed using the validated USDA FNDDS, combined with two 24‐h dietary recalls, which provide a more accurate reflection of habitual intake compared to single dietary surveys or food frequency questionnaires. (3) Comprehensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of the findings. (4) For handling missing data, the jomo method, designed for survey‐weighted data, was applied to preserve the correlation structure among variables in complex sampling designs, minimizing biases associated with traditional imputation methods. Five imputed datasets were generated and pooled using Rubin's Rules, yielding estimates consistent with the single dataset but with narrower confidence intervals and enhanced statistical significance, thereby improving the reliability of the results. Data missing rate of covariates and other indicators were shown in Table S20.

Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. (1) The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, limiting the findings to associations between flavonoid intake and NDs. (2) Although NHANES data are nationally representative of the US population, the study was restricted to US adults, potentially limiting generalizability to other populations. (3) Although the total sample size was 13,573 participants, cases of AD and PD were primarily identified based on self‐reported medication use. While this approach has been widely adopted in NHANES‐based studies, it may underestimate the true prevalence of these diseases because early‐stage cases not yet receiving pharmacological treatment, as well as undiagnosed, untreated, or atypically managed patients, are less likely to be captured. Consequently, the number of identified cases remained relatively small. Future epidemiological studies should include biomarker‐based (amyloid‐β and tau biomarkers for AD (Kobayashi et al. 2026), emerging α‐synuclein‐based biomarkers for PD (Soto 2024)) assessments and validated clinical evaluations to enhance diagnostic precision and reduce disease misclassification. (4) The reliance on two 24‐h dietary recalls for flavonoid intake assessment may be subject to recall bias and may not fully capture long‐term dietary habits. (5) Although multiple known confounders were adjusted, unmeasured factors such as genetic polymorphisms and gut microbiota (Wang et al. 2026) differences may still influence flavonoid metabolism and NDs risk. (6) Despite the use of trend analyses and multiple sensitivity analyses, the large number of subgroup and interaction tests performed may have increased the risk of false‐positive findings. In particular, interaction effects with modest statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously. However, supplementary FDR correction yielded results largely consistent with the primary analyses (Table 1 vs. Table S8; Table 2 vs. Table S9).

Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future large‐scale, prospective studies with long‐term follow‐up are needed to validate the causal relationship between flavonoid intake and NDs and to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study utilized data from the NHANES 2007–2010 and 2017–2018 cycles, encompassing 13,573 participants, to systematically evaluate the association between total flavonoid intake, including its six major subclasses, and the risk of NDs. Overall, higher intakes of total flavonoids, flavones, flavonols, anthocyanidins, and flavan‐3‐ols were significantly inversely associated with NDs risk. This relationship remained robust even after adjusting for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities.

Dose–response analyses using rcs further revealed significant non‐linear associations between NDs risk and intakes of total flavonoids, flavones, and flavonols. The lowest risk was observed at intake levels of approximately 432 mg/day for total flavonoids, 1.7 mg/day for flavones, and 29 mg/day for flavonols, suggesting that moderate intake may confer greater health benefits than simply pursuing high doses.

These findings deepen our understanding of the protective role of plant‐based foods in NDs prevention and provide critical evidence to inform more precise dietary flavonoid guidelines. With the global prevalence of NDs steadily rising, integrating pharmacological treatments with non‐pharmacological interventions, such as optimized dietary strategies, may offer a practical and effective approach to comprehensive NDs prevention. Future large‐scale prospective studies and clinical investigations are warranted to validate these findings and further elucidate the temporal and potentially causal relationships between flavonoid intake and NDs.
