Performance Evaluation of Molecular Detection of Enteroviruses: Results of 18 Years of Quality Control for Molecular Diagnostics (QCMD) External Quality Assessment Program, 2005−2022
Authors: Kimberley S. M. Benschop, Harry Staines, Emily Mckloud, Elaine McCulloch, Dorothy Montgomery, Greg Sutton, Oliver Donoso Mantke, Anton van Loon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe gold standard for enterovirus (EV) detection is the polymerase chain reaction based on the detection of the 5' untranslated region of the virus. Correct detection of EV is crucial for patient and public health purposes. The performance of diagnostic and public health laboratories on molecular EV‐detection was analyzed using data from the external quality assessment program distributed by Quality Control for Molecular Diagnostics (QCMD) between 2005 and 2022. Overall performance on core samples of both laboratory types has improved over the years for in‐house or commercial assays (overall performance rate > 94.8%) since 2013. A similar improvement was observed for negative/specificity samples. Performance on EV‐positive samples varied, with the lowest performance observed on samples containing enterovirus D68 (B3 strain) and echovirus 11. Significant differences in performance were observed between laboratory and assay types. Performance of diagnostic laboratories (91.8) was significantly higher than of public health laboratories (89.9%; p < 0.0001). Additionally, a significant higher performance of diagnostic laboratories using commercial assays was observed (92.5% vs. 91.2% for in‐house assays; p < 0.0001). In contrast, the performance of public health laboratories using in‐house assays (90.1%) was higher than commercial assays (89.2%; p = 0.3608). The data document the improved performance of diagnostic and public health laboratories using either commercial or in‐house assays.

Introduction

Human enteroviruses (EVs) belong to the family Picornaviridae, genus Enterovirus, and are classified into four genetically distinct EV species (A−D) that infect humans. The genus Enterovirus also includes three genetically distinct Rhinovirus (RV) species (A−C) [1]. The different EV types are widespread and have varying epidemiological patterns. They are the main cause of a wide range of diseases, such as acute flaccid paralysis/myelitis (AFP/AFM, e.g., poliovirus and EV‐D68), aseptic viral meningitis (e.g., echovirus [E]30), and hand foot and mouth disease (e.g., EV‐A71, coxsackievirus [CV]A6 and CVA16). There are no effective antiviral treatment options available for EV infections. Nonetheless, accurate and reliable detection and typing of EVs are essential to diagnose and monitor these infections, understand their circulation, and guide appropriate patient management and public health interventions [2, 3]. Also, detection and typing of EVs is crucial to exclude poliovirus circulation, wild or vaccine‐derived, and monitor the circulation, and public health impact of non‐polio enteroviruses (NPEVs) [3, 4, 5].

The gold standard for the detection and diagnosis of EV infections is the reverse‐transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), based on the conserved 5' untranslated region (5'UTR) of the virus. This enables the detection of all known EV types [3, 6]. Over the years, several assays have been developed that are defined as either in‐house or commercial assays. However, some assays may fail to detect certain EV types [7, 8] or EVs at a low viral load, while other assays make no distinction between EV or RV types [9].

As part of ISO15189 (Requirements for Quality and Competence of Medical Laboratories) or equivalent standards and increasing requirements of in vitro diagnostic regulations [10], diagnostic and public health laboratories are required to monitor the performance of their assays by participation in external quality assessment (EQA) programs.

Quality Control for Molecular Diagnostics (QCMD, Glasgow, Scotland [11]) is a not‐for‐profit, independent, international EQA provider with programs covering a comprehensive range of infectious disease pathogens. QCMD has offered an Enterovirus EQA program for molecular detection since 1998 for laboratories worldwide.

The purpose of this study was to analyze the performance of the participating diagnostic and public health laboratories that use in‐house and/or commercial assays using the data from QCMD EQA challenges for molecular EV detection between 2005 and 2022.

Methods

Challenges, Panels, and Data Sets

Annually, QCMD offers an EQA program for molecular detection of EVs. The program consists of a panel of samples (core and/or educational) distributed either as a single annual challenge (S) or as a biannual challenge (C1, C2) (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). In 2015, flexibility in the number of challenges was introduced to meet regulatory needs. A laboratory may test the samples within a panel on various workflows (either in‐house, commercial, or both) resulting in various data sets returned (Table 1).

Panel Composition

Panels consisted of four types of sample: (i) core EV‐positive samples at clinically relevant levels, (ii) core negative samples, (iii) educational EV‐positive samples, and (iv) specificity samples containing closely related viruses, for example, human parechoviruses or human RVs. Each panel was composed of a number of only core samples or core and educational samples (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Core samples are defined as samples of clinical and public health importance, containing viral loads that should be detected by laboratories (Figure S1). Core samples are reviewed regularly by the QCMD Scientific Experts on the basis of scientific information, clinical and public health importance, current literature/scientific information and, where appropriate, professional clinical guidelines. Educational samples may include rare or emerging genotypes or strains (such as CVA24 or the EV‐D68 B3 strain) or diluted core samples to help participants assess and, if needed, improve the quality of their assays and workflows. Both core and educational samples may contain high and low viral loads. As such analysis of core and/or educational samples cannot be used as a proxy to assess the performance on sensitivity or lower limit of detection of assays used in this study. Of note, educational samples included dilution series in earlier years and were used to assess and compare analytical sensitivity (data not included).

Sample Preparation

Samples included different virus types (Supporting Information S1: Table 2), harvested from cell cultures and prepared in a transport medium as the sample matrix. They were distributed in a lyophilized format (before 2014) or liquid frozen format (from 2014 onwards). If diluted samples were used within a panel, these came from the same stock or batch in a 10‐fold dilution.

Performance Assessment

Laboratories are expected to test the samples of each panel using their routine molecular assays and workflows and report their results (positive or negative) through the dedicated ITEMS online reporting system of QCMD. The overall performance on each panel is defined as the percentage of correct results reported on EV‐positive samples (as positive), negative samples (as negative), and specificity samples containing parechovirus or RV (as negative) compared to the total number of samples tested.

Performance was analyzed separately for core samples and all samples (core and educational samples combined). For clarity, results in the main text are presented for core samples alone and results based on all samples are shown in Figures S2−S4, as results for core were similar to results for all samples.

Statistics

Medians are presented for continuous data (e.g., C t values). Binary endpoints (e.g., performance) are presented as percentages and Clopper−Pearson exact 95% confidence intervals. Univariate and bivariate binary logistic regression models were fitted to test for differences across factors (e.g., laboratory type). The resulting odds ratios, their 95% confidence intervals and p‐values are computed using Wald's normal approximation.

Statistics were performed using the statistical package SAS, version 9.4. All tests were two‐sided with significant levels displayed without multiplicity adjustment.

Results

Panels and Retuned Data Sets Analyzed by Laboratory Type and Assay Type

From 2005 to 2022, performance data on a total of 32 panels were analyzed and 3675 data sets were returned by 699 laboratories worldwide that had registered for participation in the EV EQA challenges. Of these, 621 were diagnostic laboratories and 78 were public health laboratories. Table 1 shows the number of data sets returned for each panel tested by laboratory type and assay type. The data sets consisted of a total of 44 434 samples analyzed, 41 087 core samples and 3347 educational samples (Supporting Information S1: Table 3).

In 2005, in‐house assays were used on 88.5% and 87.5% of the returned data sets in diagnostic and public health laboratories, respectively. Over the years, diagnostic laboratories steadily switched toward increasing the use of commercial assays with 41.6% of the returned data sets resulting from in‐house assays in 2022 (Table 1). In contrast, the returned data sets from in‐house assays decreased only marginally in public health laboratories in the early years and remained at the 70%−80% level later on. The trend is also reflected in the number of samples tested by laboratory type and assay type (Supporting Information S1: Table 3).

Overall Performance by Laboratory Type: Diagnostic and Public Health Laboratories

The overall performance on core samples of both diagnostic and public health laboratories increased over the study period (Figure 1A). The overall performance on core samples ranged from 76.1% for diagnostic laboratories and 76.0% for public health laboratories in 2005 to 94.8%−99.9% from 2013 onwards for both laboratory types. There was a large variation seen in the performance over time, particularly for public health laboratories (Figure 1A). When results for each core sample were pooled over the years and analyzed by laboratory type, the overall performance on core samples of diagnostic laboratories (91.8%) was significantly higher than that of public health laboratories (89.9%) (Figure 1C, black triangles) with odds ratio = 1.26 (95% CI: 1.14−1.40); p < 0.0001 (data not shown). Similar results were observed when analyzing all samples (core and educational samples) (Figure S2).

Overall performance on core samples tested by laboratory type (A and B by year and C pooled over time) and assay type (B by year and C pooled over time) within the QCMD Enterovirus EQA program for molecular detection from 2005 to 2022.

Performance by Technology: In‐House Versus Commercial Assays

To investigate whether the performance is related to the use of in‐house or commercial assays, the data were further analyzed separately by assay type. Figure 1B shows the increase over time in overall performance on core samples of in‐house and commercial assays separately for each laboratory type. A larger variation for commercial assays was observed compared to in‐house assays. The performance on core samples over the years was consistently lower when commercial assays were used than when in‐house assays were used, particularly for diagnostic laboratories. When results for each core sample were pooled over 18 years and analyzed by assay type alone and irrespective of laboratory type, the overall performance on core samples was significantly higher for commercial assays (92.3% for commercial assays and 91.1% for in‐house assays; odds ratio = 1.18 [95% CI: 1.10−1.27]; p < 0.0001, data not shown).

Taking laboratory type and assay type into account, pooled data from all the years combined showed a significantly higher performance of commercial assays used by diagnostic laboratories (91.2% for in‐house assays and 92.5% for commercial assays) (Figure 1C, left panel) with odds ratio = 1.190 (95% CI: 1.103−1.284); p < 0.0001 (data not shown). For public health laboratories, the opposite was observed (90.1% for in‐house assays and 89.2% for commercial assays [Figure 1C, right panel]) with an odds ratio = 0.904 (95% CI: 0.729–1.122); p = 0.3608 (data not shown).

The binary logistic regression model on pooled data of core samples for both laboratory type and assay type showed very similar results with an adjusted odds ratio for commercial assays (odds ratio = 1.16 [95% CI: 1.08−1.24; p < 0.001]) and an adjusted odds ratio for diagnostic laboratories (odds ratio = 1.23 [95% CI: 1.11−1.36]; p < 0.001) shown in Figure 1C.

Similar results were observed when analyzing all samples (core and educational samples) (Figure S2).

Commercial Assay‐Related Performance

Up to 25 different commercial assays were used, with the top five assays from Cepheid (n = 6221, 34.6%), Biomerieux (n = 2732, 15.2%), Argene (n = 1641, 9.14%), Fast‐track diagnostics (n = 1182, 6.6%), and ELITech (n = 689, 3.8%). Amongst these assays, the overall performance on core samples varied significantly from 87.1% (commercial assays from Argene) to 99.7% (commercial assays from ELITech) (see Figure 2 for the odds ratios).

Performance on EV‐Positive Core Samples

The performance on EV‐positive core samples alone was 71.0% in 2005 and 96.4% in 2022 (Table 2). Analysis per EV type showed a high yet varying performance on EV‐positive core samples ranging from 79.7% for poliovirus type 3 positive core samples to 99.0% for echovirus 18 positive core samples (Figure 3A). Considering only the NPEV types, the lowest sensitivity on core samples was observed for echovirus 11 positive core samples (85.2%). The variation in performance on positive core samples was low, particularly when enough samples were analyzed.

The performance on EV‐positive core samples was similar when analyzing the data by laboratory type (Figure 3B) or by assay type (Figure 3C). However, the variation in public health laboratories was greater than for diagnostic laboratories as seen with the overall performance (Figure 1B). In addition, a marked difference in performance on EV‐positive core samples was observed between in‐house and commercial assays for echovirus 9, Enterovirus D68 B3, and poliovirus 3 positive core samples. A lower sensitivity was observed when commercial assays were used (Figure 3C).

Due to the changing epidemiology of EV‐D68 in recent years and the predominant circulation of the EV‐D68 B3 strain [9, 12, 13, 14], the B3 strain was included in the 2020 challenges as an educational sample next to the original prototype Fermon strain (as core). The performance on the EV‐D68 B3 educational positive sample in 2020 was lower (67.9%) than on the prototype strain core sample (98.4%) (data not shown). In the 2021 and 2022 challenges, the EV‐D68 B3 strain was included as a core sample. The performance on the EV‐D68 B3 core positive sample was 82.4% in 2021 and 91.2% in 2022 (data not shown) and was lowest when tested with commercial assays (Figure 3C). Similar results were observed when analyzing all samples (core and educational samples) (Figure S4).

Performance on Negative Samples

The performance on negative samples over the study period was measured as the proportion of incorrectly detected samples (false positivity). The overall false positivity was low (2.5%) and varied over the years (Table 2). From 2005 through 2011, a high false positivity rate was observed, with the highest rate in 2007 (7.8%). After 2011, a decrease in the false positivity rates was observed ranging from 0.3% in 2017 to 2.2% in 2020. There was no significant difference in the proportion of false positivity rates by laboratory type (odds ratio = 1.18 [95% CI: 0.68–2.05]; p = 0.5459, data not shown). However, commercial assays had a significantly lower false positivity rate than in‐house assays (1.45%−3.32%, odds ratio = 0.43 [95% CI: 0.28−0.65]; p < 0.0001, data not shown).

Performance on Specificity (RV/PEV) Samples

The performance on specificity samples over the study period was measured as the proportion of incorrectly detected samples (specificity). RV 16 and parechovirus type 1 and 3 containing samples were included in the panels as specificity controls in the years 2005, and 2007 through 2011 (Supporting Information S1: Table 2). The overall percentage of incorrectly detected specificity samples as EV positive was 5.7% and was highest for RV 16 (12.8%), followed by parechovirus type 1 (Table 2). There was no significant difference in the proportion of incorrectly detected specificity samples by laboratory type (odds ratio = 1.02 [95% CI: 0.57−1.83]; p = 0.9365, data not shown) or assay type (odds ratio = 0.67 [95% CI: 0.42−1.08]; p = 0.1005, data not shown).

Discussion

EQA of molecular diagnostics is a cornerstone in maintaining high‐quality diagnostic and public health testing. Here we analyzed for the first time the consecutive EQA testing results for the molecular detection of EVs conducted from 2005 through 2022 by diagnostic and public health laboratories that use in‐house and/or commercial assays.

The samples in the provided panels were predominantly tested by in‐house assays in both laboratories. However, over the years, an increase in the use of commercial assays was observed for diagnostic laboratories while public health laboratories were more likely to retain the use of in‐house assays. A previous study by Hayes et al. [15], evaluating the performance of EVs and parechoviruses testing with in‐house and commercial assays used in public health and diagnostic laboratories, also showed the higher propensity of public health laboratories to use in‐house assays. The increasing trend of using commercial assays is most likely also fueled by their increasing availability. However, their performance varies, as seen in this study with frequent use of BioMerieux R‐gene Kit, the Cepheid Xpert EV, Argene, ELITech group, and the FTD Real‐Time PCR. Also, the new IVDR regulatory requirements [10] will probably also have an impact on the availability and use of commercial assays.

The overall performance on core samples increased over the years for both diagnostic and public health laboratories that use in‐house and/or commercial assays. This is also shown for analysis of both core and educational samples combined. After 2013, the overall performance of both laboratory types was high and around 95%. The sudden increase in overall performance did not coincide with a significant switch in the use of either in‐house or commercial assays. The sample preparations did change after 2014 from lyophilized format to liquid frozen format. However, this does not explain the increase in the year 2013 when lyophilized format was still used. Furthermore, around this time the new ISO15189 norm was published (ISO15189:2012) with a section on laboratory information management that required laboratories to continuously monitor their systems and in the case of EQA optimize assays when needed. This could explain the increased performance after 2013. Unfortunately, registration of ISO15189 accreditation is not included in the returned data information and we cannot corroborate this hypothesis.

Another shift that was observed in the data was a drop in false positivity rates after 2011 and factors that contributed to this drop remain unknown.

We observed a consistently higher performance over the years of in‐house assays over commercial assays in both laboratory types, similar to Hayes et al. [15]. The observed lower performance of commercial assays over in‐house assays over the years is a concern, particularly with new strains such as EV‐D68 B3 showing lower performance rates, and in view of the trend that laboratories, particularly diagnostic laboratories, are switching to the use of commercial assays.

Univariate and bivariate analysis of the pooled data from all the years combined showed that commercial assays have a significantly higher performance over in‐house assays, and mainly for diagnostic laboratories in our study. The higher number of data sets and samples tested by diagnostic laboratories increasingly using commercial assays might have skewed the pooled data.

In addition, a higher pooled performance of diagnostic laboratories over public health laboratories was found, which was also different than observed by Hayes et al. [15]. The differences in pooled performance compared to Hayes et al. [15] could be due to the study set up, where the study by Hayes et al. reflects one data set distributed in 1 year and here we analyzed 18 years' worth of data sets. Analysis of specific individual years does show similar results compared to Hayes et al. [15]. The composition of the challenges between Hayes et al. and this study should be noted as well. Hayes et al. [15] used RNA transcripts that were immediately tested in the detection/typing assays. The use of RNA transcripts enables the inclusion of difficult‐to‐culture EVs and parechoviruses, yet does not reflect the entire workflow of the laboratory, including extraction methods. On the contrary, QCMD uses virus‐ positive (cultured) samples, which enable testing of the performance of the entire workflow of the laboratory, but does not enable the inclusion of difficult‐to‐culture types. The performance described in the study by Hayes et al. [15] was further dependent on the viral load in the samples. In this study the performance was only analyzed on core (or core and educational combined) samples, which is a limitation in our study. Core samples are not defined by viral load, but by clinical and public health importance and performance of the samples in previous years. As such, low viral load samples may also be defined as a core sample (Figure S1) and performance on sensitivity could not be assessed in this study. Similarly, educational samples do not always reflect low viral load samples to enable them as a proxy for assay sensitivity. Dilution series are not consistently included in the panels any more, but can be reconsidered for types such as EV‐D68 and EV‐A71 for example.

Performance on EV‐positive core samples varied per virus type. There are over 200 EV types, and while it is not possible to include all these EV types, the panels have been carefully curated to contain different types of significant clinical and public health importance. As acquired mutations which occur in the primer‐probe regions used to detect EVs can have a significant effect on the performance of an assay [7, 8, 9], inclusion of recently circulating new strains of a given type should be key in designing the challenges as well. In the last 2 years, the challenges have included the recently circulating EV‐D68 B3 strain [9, 12, 13, 14]. While the commercial assays have improved to the level of in‐house assays, inclusion of the EV‐D68 B3 strain in the last 2 years in the study period has led to a lower percentage of correctly detected core samples positive for the B3 strain than for the prototype strain. Of interest was that the inclusion of EV‐D68 B3 strain in 2021 and 2022 as a core sample coincided with a slight drop in the overall performance on core samples when commercial assays were used in both diagnostic and public health laboratories (Figure 1B).

Of further concern was the low sensitivity for poliovirus type 3 core positive samples, particularly in commercial assays. It should be noted that poliovirus type 3 positive core samples were only included in the challenges in 2006−2009 (Supporting Information S1: Table 2) but were removed due to the constraints from the polio eradication Global Action Plan (GAP)III/IV [16]. The low sensitivity could be related to the low sensitivity observed in the first half of the study period (Figure 1A,B). As this type is no longer part of QCMD panels, we could not assess whether detection of this type has also improved over the years. Also, the low percentage of correctly detected echovirus 11 core samples, which were included until 2018, is another concern. Recent outbreaks of echovirus 11 have been associated with severe hepatitis and mortality among neonates [17, 18] and highlight the need to improve the accuracy and reliability of EV testing and that this type should be (re‐)included in future challenges.

The percentage of incorrectly reported specificity samples was highest for RV 16, which is not surprising considering their close genetic relation to EVs, in particular within the 5'UTR region used for the assays. Most commercial assays also do not distinguish between RVs and EVs, and only with further typing can the two viruses be distinguished.

Our study details the performance of diagnostic and public health laboratories that use commercial and/or in‐house assays over time and shows the value of EQA. Failure to detect a type due to either low sensitivity or specificity should entail further optimization of the assays. The inclusion of different EV types of clinical and public health importance remains a crucial part of the EQA, as differentiation between these EV types should be regularly evaluated in light of their varying disease patterns, changing epidemiology, and emergence of new strains.
