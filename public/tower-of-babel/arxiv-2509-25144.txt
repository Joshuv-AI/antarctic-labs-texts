Paired by the Teacher: Turning Unpaired Data into High-Fidelity Pairs for Low-Resource Text Generation
Yen-Ju Lu, Thomas Thebaud, Laureano Moro-Velazquez, Najim Dehak, Jesus Villalba Center for Language and Speech Processing, Johns Hopkins University {ylu125, tthebau1, laureano, ndehak3, jvillal7}@jhu.edu

Abstract
We present Paired by the Teacher (PbT), a two-stage teacher–student pipeline that synthesizes accurate input–output pairs without human labels or parallel data. In many low-resource natural language generation (NLG) scenarios, practitioners may have only raw outputs, like highlights, recaps, or questions, or only raw inputs, such as articles, dialogues, or paragraphs, but seldom both. This mismatch forces small models to learn from very few examples or rely on costly, broad-scope synthetic examples produced by large LLMs. PbT addresses this by asking a teacher LLM to compress each unpaired example into a concise intermediate representation (IR), and training a student to reconstruct inputs from IRs. This enables outputs to be paired with student-generated inputs, yielding high-quality synthetic data. We evaluate PbT on five benchmarks—document summarization (XSum, CNNDM), dialogue summarization (SAMSum, DialogSum), and question generation (SQuAD)—as well as an unpaired setting on SwitchBoard (paired with DialogSum summaries). An 8B student trained only on PbT data outperforms models trained on 70 B teacher-generated corpora and other unsupervised baselines, coming within 1.2 ROUGE-L of human-annotated pairs and closing 82% of the oracle gap at one-third the annotation cost of direct synthesis. Human evaluation on SwitchBoard further confirms that only PbT produces concise, faithful summaries aligned with the target style, highlighting its advantage of generating in-domain sources that avoid the mismatch, limiting direct synthesis.



1
Introduction

Many NLG tasks, such as summarization and question generation, lack readily available input–output pairs, often referred to as source–target pairs.
Creating these pairs requires experts to read each document or dialogue and write its summary or questions. This process is slow, costly, and must be repeated for every new domain. It also raises privacy concerns when conversations contain sensitive information.
To solve this problem, we introduce
Paired by the Teacher
(PbT). Given only unpaired in-domain texts and a small set of example summaries or questions, PbT generates high-quality synthetic source–target pairs without any human pairing. This pipeline turns raw data into ready training material that matches the target style and speeds up adaptation across domains.

Figure 1:
PbT consistently outperforms the strongest synthetic baselines from GPT and LLAMA70B (by up to +3.3 BERTScore points) and closes the gap to oracle (real) data across five summarization and QG tasks.

Existing work shows that synthesis models can be trained using only unpaired data, such as back-translation
(
Lample et al. 2018
)
, GAN-based, or VAE-based summarization
(
Wang and LEE 2020
;
Bražinskas et al. 2020
)
. More recent techniques leverage instruction-tuned teacher LLMs to generate training pairs, such as DiaSynth with GPT-4o
(
Suresh et al. 2025
)
, MixSumm with LLaMA-70B
(
Sahu and Laradji 2024
)
, and ChatGPT self-chat corpora
(
Xu et al.
)
. In few-shot scenarios, smaller teachers like GPT-3.5 or in-model synthesis adapters can also guide the pairing process
(
Mishra et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2025
)
.
However, these methods rely heavily on the teacher’s generation, refinement, and filtering abilities. This drives up inference costs and makes deployment in resource-constrained settings challenging, or requiring some ground-truth labeled data to ensure quality.

Paired by the Teacher (PbT) is a two-phase teacher–student pipeline that transforms unpaired inputs and outputs into high-quality source–target pairs without any manual annotation. In the first phase, a teacher model extracts a concise IR from each raw source example, and a smaller student model is fine-tuned via parameter-efficient methods to invert this mapping and reconstruct the source.
In the second stage, the teacher model annotates IR for each unpaired target with the source-side IR example as in-context demonstrations. Each generated IR is then passed through the trained student model to synthesize an aligned source example.
Because PbT asks the teacher to output only short IRs, the heavy text generation is offloaded to the student. The teacher’s knowledge still guides the process, but its compute cost is a fraction of end-to-end generation. At the same time, the student has been trained on real in-domain documents and dialogues. As a result, the synthetic sources it produces remain in-domain, unlike direct synthesis where mismatched sources hurt downstream performance. This in-domain alignment is the key reason PbT generalizes better at test time.

We evaluate PbT on five benchmarks—document summarization (XSum, CNNDM), dialogue summarization (SAMSum, DialogSum), and question generation (SQuAD).
An 8B student trained only on PbT data outperforms models trained on 70B teacher-generated or self-generated data, and comes on average within 1.2 ROUGE-L points of human-annotated pairs, recovering 5.6 of the 6.8 ROUGE-L gap (82% closure) compared with the best synthetic-only baseline.
We further build an unpaired setting on SwitchBoard dialogue-only dataset, paired with summaries from DialogSum and train the summarization model on purely cross-domain data.
Human evaluation on SwitchBoard shows that only PbT adheres to the target summary lengths with concise, fluent, and faithful outputs. In contrast, all baselines produce overly long summaries due to a domain mismatch between the synthetic training data and the real test inputs. This highlights PbT’s core advantage: by generating sources through a student trained on real inputs, PbT keeps training data aligned with test-time conditions.

Major contributions:

•

Teacher–Student Pairing Pipeline.
PbT transforms unpaired sources and targets into high-fidelity training pairs, enabling a student model to learn from realistic source examples without any human annotations. Unlike direct synthesis, PbT’s student-generated sources remain in-domain, ensuring alignment between training and test distributions.

•

Robust Intermediate Representation.
We conduct the experiments with various IR formats and annotated by larger, same-sized, or smaller teachers, reducing dependence on model scale while maintaining data quality. Also, the cost for IR annotation is
3\times
smaller than direct synthesis from the teacher model.

•

Intra-and Cross-Dataset Experiments:
PbT not only surpasses teacher/student-only baselines across all five benchmarks in automatic metrics and model-based evaluation, but also yields a functional summarizer on the label-free SwitchBoard corpus by borrowing DialogSum summaries—adhering to their style and outperforming baseline systems in both human and GPT evaluation.

Related Work

Unpaired and Low-Resource NLG with Model Selection

Before the emergence of LLMs, unsupervised text generation used cycle objectives or auto-encoding on smaller models. Back-translation let dual translators learn without parallel data
(
Lample et al. 2018
)
. Similar cycle or reconstruction ideas powered unsupervised summarization, sentence compression, and opinion summarization
(
Wang and LEE 2020
;
Baziotis et al. 2019
;
Bražinskas et al. 2020
)
, as well as large-scale pseudo-pre-training from news leads
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
. Cycle consistency was later applied to data-to-text generation
(
Guo et al. 2020
)
. Because no gold validation exists, these studies chose checkpoints with round-trip BLEU, reconstruction loss, or discriminator scores, which correlate well with true quality. We follow the same principle, using IR-to-document reconstruction to guide model selection for LLM-scale training.

LLM-Driven Pseudo-Labeling and In-Context NLG

With instruction-tuned LLMs, generating synthetic corpora for low-resource tasks has become widespread. MixSumm
(
Sahu and Laradji 2024
)
and PPSL
(
Sahu et al. 2025
)
prompt LLaMA-3-70B to synthesize documents and pseudo-summaries, then fine-tune a 100–300 M summarizer to match fully-supervised ROUGE with only 5 % gold pairs
(
Sahu et al. 2025
)
.

Mishra et al. 2023
use GPT-3.5 to pseudo-label customer-service chats; with just 10 % human data, their BERT-based model recovers 95 % of full-data performance.
MRDS
(
Lu et al. 2025
)
further intertwines dialogue synthesis and summarization LoRA adapters inside a single LLM with pseudo-pair generation.
In parallel, self-chat methods like Baize
(
Xu et al.
)
collect
\sim
100 k ChatGPT-generated dialogues to LoRA-tune LLaMA, approaching proprietary chatbot quality, and task-oriented work
(
Surana et al. 2025
)
turns product reviews into synthetic recommendation dialogues for ReDial
(
Li et al. 2018
)
and INSPIRED
(
Hayati et al. 2020
)
benchmarks.
Finally, zero-shot and in-context prompting with GPT-3 can produce news summaries preferred by humans over gold references
(
Goyal et al. 2022
)
, though such methods lack a lightweight deployable model, motivating hybrid approaches that combine in-context learning with pseudo-fine-tuning.

3
Methodology

Paired by the Teacher
(PbT) is a unified teacher–student pipeline that converts unpaired inputs and outputs into high-fidelity source–target pairs without any human labeling.

PbT Overview

Figure
2
provides a high-level illustration of the PbT pipeline. The process unfolds in three phases.
In the Source IR Induction phase, the teacher LLM compresses each unpaired source into a concise intermediate representation (IR), and the student model is trained to reconstruct the original input from this IR.
In the Target IR Annotation phase, the teacher annotates each unpaired target with a plausible IR, which the trained student then expands into a synthetic source, forming paired examples
(\tilde{x},y)
.
Finally, in the
Downstream Fine-tuning
phase, a summarization or question-generation model is fine-tuned on these synthetic pairs.
By shifting heavy generation to the student while keeping the teacher’s guidance at the IR level, PbT achieves both efficiency and alignment (see Appendix
A.3
for concrete IR examples).

3.1
Source-side IR Learning

Our pipeline begins by teaching the student model to reconstruct in-domain source data from the IR.
We first use a large teacher model
\mathcal{T}
to extract a structured IR from each raw source example, and then fine-tune a smaller student model
\mathcal{S}
to invert that mapping.

IR Extraction (Teacher)

As illustrated in Figure
3
a, for every unpaired source instance
x
(dialogue, article, paragraph, …), the teacher
\mathcal{T}
produces a concise IR
m_{x}
(e.g., a topical outline, chain-of-thought, or related facts). This yields a semi-supervised dataset of
(x_{i},m_{x_{i}})
pairs:

\{(x_{i},m_{x_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{N}.

The form of
m_{x_{i}}
is task-dependent. For dialogue and document summarization, the teacher is instructed to split the input into paragraph groups and write a topic sentence with key entities. For question generation, the teacher extracts the answer-containing sentence and writes supporting facts as bullets. These task-specific IRs provide the information needed to reconstruct the source
x_{i}
(see Table
12
).

IR-to-Source Induction (Student)

We then fine-tune the student model
\mathcal{S}
with parameter-efficient methods (PEFT) on these source-side annotations to learn the inverse mapping

\mathcal{S}_{m\rightarrow x}:m\mapsto x.

By optimizing on
(x_{i},m_{x_{i}})
, the student internalizes the source domain’s structural patterns and can expand IRs into realistic source texts
\hat{x}=\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(m)
. The model is trained with the reconstruction loss

\theta=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{R}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(m_{x_{i}}),x_{i}),

where
\mathcal{L}_{R}
is the cross-entropy loss. These learned synthesis skills later allow
\mathcal{S}
to generate candidate sources for unpaired targets.

3.2
Target-side IR Annotation

IR Annotation (Teacher)

As shown in Figure
3(b)
, we prompt the teacher LLM
\mathcal{T}
to annotate each unpaired target example
y
with a plausible IR
\tilde{m}_{y}
. To ensure consistent formatting,
\mathcal{T}
is provided with a few in-context examples of
(x,m_{x})
pairs from the source-side training phase. This yields a set of target annotations:

\{(y_{j},\tilde{m}_{y_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\,.

(1)

Because the teacher does not observe the corresponding source
x_{j}
, the generated
\tilde{m}_{y_{j}}
are not aligned with the actual sources. Such explicit alignment is unnecessary, as the student generates synthetic sources directly from these IRs.

IR-to-Source Generation (Student)

Once the student model
\mathcal{S}_{m\to x}
has learned to expand IRs into full source texts, we apply it to each target-side representation
\tilde{m}_{y_{j}}
.
The student then produces a synthetic source
\tilde{x}_{j}
for each target
y_{j}
, resulting in a synthetic paired corpus

\{(\tilde{x}_{j},\,y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\,,

(2)

where every target is matched with a candidate source generated solely from its corresponding IR.

3.3
Target Task Training

Finally, we fine-tune a downstream student model
\mathcal{S}_{x\to y}
on the synthetic pairs
\{(\tilde{x}_{j},\,y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{y}}
. The model learns to map synthetic sources to targets:

\mathcal{S}_{x\to y}\;:\;\tilde{x}\;\mapsto\;y.

(3)

The training objective is

\phi=\arg\min_{\phi}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\mathcal{L}_{S}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{\phi}(\tilde{x}_{j}),\,y_{j}\bigr),

(4)

yielding predictions
\hat{y}=\mathcal{S}_{\phi}(\tilde{x})
.

PbT mimics the effect of having true training pairs for two reasons.
First, it generates only synthetic source inputs
\tilde{x}
and pairs them with authentic targets
y
, avoiding propagation of synthesis artifacts into outputs.
Second, by training the student on real source data, PbT ensures that synthetic sources remain in-domain.
As a result, at test time, PbT-generated pairs better match the distribution of real inputs, unlike direct synthesis, which suffers from mismatched sources.

Experiments

Intra-Datasets Experiment

We evaluate PbT on three tasks with five benchmarks: dialogue-summarization (DialogSum
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
and SAMSum
(
Gliwa et al. 2019
)
), document summarization (XSum
(
Narayan et al. 2018
)
and CNNDM
(
Hermann et al. 2015
)
), and question generation (SQuAD
Rajpurkar et al. 2016

For each dataset, we perform intra-dataset experiments by partitioning the data into "source-only” (dialogues, documents, and paragraphs) and "target-only” (summaries and questions) subsets, training the model without any paired examples.

Cross-Datasets Experiment

Additionally, we assess the cross-domain transfer ability of PbT by training it on SwitchBoard
(
Godfrey et al. 1992
)
, a conversation-style dialogue-only dataset, containing 1155 dialogues without any available summary labels.
We train the PbT model with the dialogues from SwitchBoard and summaries from DialogSum, aiming to train a summarization model that can summarize the dialogues from SwitchBoard into DialogSum-style summaries.

4.1
Alternative Methods

We compare our PbT method with several unpaired summarization alternative approaches, divided into two categories: methods using the pre-trained Llama3 model without fine-tuning and methods fine-tuned on real or synthetic data.

Pre-trained Methods

-

Zero-shot:
Zero-shot summarization performance of LLAMA8B.

-

ICL:
Summarization performance of LLAMA8B using in-context learning with
k=5
summaries examples.

Fine-tuned Methods

-

Direct Synthesis
: Using a fixed teacher or student model to generate source from the summaries with in-context source examples.
Then we fine-tune the student summarization model from those synthetic source-target pairs.

-

Best-of-5 Filtering (BoN-5)
: Applied to both baselines and PbT, this strategy generates five candidate pairs and filter as in Genie
(
Yehudai et al.
)
. GPT-4o-mini is first used to filter inconsistent document–summary pairs, and a QRM-Gemma-2-27B ranker
(
Dorka 2024
)
selects the top candidate.

-

Oracle Paired
: Fine-tuning the student summarization model with the oracle summaries for the source shots used in other experiments.

-

DiaSynth
: Fine-tuning the student summarization model on synthetic data from
(
Suresh et al. 2025
)
, which leverages LLM CoT reasoning. The amount of synthetic training data is aligned with the other experiments.

4.2
Implementation Details

Teacher/Student Selection

Our primary experiments use Llama3.1-8B-Instruct
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
as the student model (denoted LLAMA8B). We train LoRA adapters of rank 16 and
\alpha=32
on top of the frozen base weights. To generate IRs, we rely on two large teachers: the Llama3.3-70B-Instruct model (LLAMA70B) and GPT-4o mini (GPT), and then fine-tune LLAMA8B on the synthetic data they produce.

Training Settings (Summarization, QG, and Source-synthesis).

Both the summarization / question-generation models and the IR
\!\rightarrow
source synthesizer are trained with the same optimization recipe from
(
Lu et al. 2025
)
.
During synthesis training, the student sees only the IR without summaries, forcing them to reconstruct the source from the IR alone.
For the proposed methods in the main experiments, we run the synthesis and training process three times and compute the average.
The hyperparameters and prompts for IR extraction, source generation, and downstream training are listed in Appendix

5
Results

5.1
Unpaired Document Summarization

For document summarization, we conduct experiments at two different scales—200-shot and 1,000-shot source training—on the XSum and CNNDM datasets. For validation and testing, we randomly sample 500 and 1,000 examples, respectively, from each split. In addition to ROUGE and BERTScore, we also report G-Eval
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, a rigorously designed model-based evaluation framework that uses fixed prompts and the GPT-4 model, and has shown high correlation with human judgments.

XSum

As shown in Table
1
, XSum is challenging dataset, as it requires generating a one-line summary from a long news article. Both zero-shot and ICL experiments yield ROUGE-2 scores below 10%, and direct synthesis performs even worse than ICL (7.9 vs. 5.0 and 6.5 R-2), falling far short of the oracle paired-data baseline (18.7 R-2). In contrast, the proposed PbT approach achieves a ROUGE-2 score of 16.1, closing more than 80% of the gap to the oracle performance. Incorporating the Best-of-5 (BoN-5) filtering strategy does not further improve ROUGE-based scores, suggesting limited gains under reference-based evaluation. Results for the medium-scale XSum setting are provided in Appendix
A.2
.

XSum G-Eval

Table
1
also presents G-Eval metrics on XSum, including Coherence (Coh.), Consistency (Con.), Relevance (Rel.), and Fluency (Flu.). We compute over the first 200 test examples across 20 runs. We find that the direct-synthesis baselines fall short in coherence and relevance. In contrast, PbT outperforms the real paired data on three metrics, achieving an overall average of 3.22 compared to 3.01. With BoN-5 filtering, although no further improvements are observed in Ref-based scores, PbT achieves a new best overall G-Eval score of 3.43, showing consistent gains in coherence, consistency, and relevance. This indicates that BoN-5 effectively refines the quality of synthetic data under unpaired scenarios when evaluated by human-aligned metrics.

CNNDM

We also conduct experiments on CNNDM, a dataset with higher risk of pretraining contamination
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
. As a result, the gap between pretrained models and oracle paired data is relatively small—only 2–4% in ROUGE-L (21.0, 24.6 vs. 25.7)—compared to the much larger gap on XSum (12.5, 18.2 vs. 32.6). In this setting, direct synthesis offers only modest ROUGE-2 gains, whereas PbT outperforms both ICL and oracle paired data. Moreover, G-Eval shows that PbT achieves the best overall average of 4.26 (vs. 4.11 for oracle), with notable improvements in relevance (4.53 vs. 4.21) and coherence (4.61 vs. 4.51), highlighting that PbT yields more human-aligned quality gains even when reference-based improvements are limited.

6
Conclusion

Paired by the Teacher (PbT) addresses the shortage of aligned input–output pairs in natural language generation by combining teacher-annotated intermediate representations with student-generated sources. This design converts unpaired corpora into usable supervision without human labeling, while shifting most text generation from the teacher to the student.
Across five benchmarks covering document summarization, dialogue summarization, and question generation, PbT consistently surpasses teacher- and student-only baselines. An 8B student trained with PbT data comes within 1.2 ROUGE-L points of human-labeled systems, closing 82% of the gap over the strongest synthetic baseline at only one-third the annotation cost of direct synthesis.
On the unlabeled SwitchBoard corpus, PbT further shows better style adherence and fluency than competing approaches. This advantage comes from PbT’s in-domain alignment: a student trained on real inputs generates sources close to the true distribution, avoiding the mismatch that limits direct synthesis.
PbT thus offers a fast, cost-effective way to train compact models for specialized or privacy-sensitive applications, even when labeled input–output pairs are scarce.

Limitations

While synthesizing data, we train the student to reconstruct the source text in an open-ended manner. PbT generally introduces fewer hallucinations than directly synthesizing from the target. However, because the IR inevitably omits some details, the generated sources and summaries still require careful human review in high-fidelity applications.
All current experiments use English corpora only. We have not yet studied other languages or cross-lingual scenarios such as summarizing documents or dialogues written in a different language.

Ethical Considerations

As we extend the frontiers of NLP, we remain attentive to the ethical dimensions of our work. Generating synthetic data with large-scale models reduces our reliance on vast real-world datasets that could include personal or sensitive information. To uphold privacy and meet regulatory standards, we draw exclusively on openly available corpora under permissive terms (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 and CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) and enforce stringent anonymization measures.
When PbT is applied to copyrighted web or news material, the synthetic sources can echo portions of the originals. Practitioners should verify redistribution rights or ensure that outputs are sufficiently transformed. For conversational data, intermediate representations may reveal private entities or sensitive details, so names should be anonymized before teacher annotation. Finally, because PbT makes it easier to create large paired datasets, the same pipeline could be exploited to mass-produce malicious content. To limit misuse, we will release the code under a research-only license.
