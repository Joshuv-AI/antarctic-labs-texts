The Chemistry and Thermodynamics of Point Source CO2 Capture by Liquid Chemical Absorption and Its Impact on Process Performance
Authors: Graeme Puxty, Marcel Maeder, Thomas Moore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Point source carbon capture is a technology that has been developed to separate carbon dioxide (CO2) from gas mixtures prior to emission to the atmosphere. It is considered a crucial technology to manage CO2 emissions from fossil fuel-based heat and power and industrial processes as part of emissions reduction strategies. The most mature technology is reactive chemical absorption using aqueous amines, with other options emerging. In this review we have described the chemistry of liquid-based reactive chemical absorption and examined the current state-of-the-art in terms of the molecules being investigated. We have also highlighted the critical properties relevant for an absorbent to be effective for carbon capture. The chemical and physical properties have also been considered in terms of how they influence process performance, both positively and negatively, with emphasis on the multifaceted nature of this relationship and the importance of understanding both the chemistry and chemical engineering when endeavoring to make improvements.

Introduction

The role of carbon dioxide (CO2) as a greenhouse gas is now accepted globally, and the impact of its historical and ongoing emission to the atmosphere are being felt in the form of climate change. To address this, global agreements are in place under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, such as the Paris Agreement, with the goal to drastically reduce CO2 emissions over the coming decades. Many nations have also committed to net-zero CO2 emissions targets by 2050. While large reductions in CO2 emissions can be achieved by transitioning energy systems to renewable generation and the electrification of industrial processes and transportation, CO2 capture from point source emissions in the energy sector and in heavy industries where such alternatives are infeasible remains critical. While point source capture will be relied upon primarily as a means to directly remove CO2 from process emissions for permanent geological storage, it will also provide a carbon source for utilization applications such as integration into building materials via mineral carbonation and displacement of fossil fuels in polymer and fuel production.

Point source CO2 capture refers to technologies that can selectively separate CO2 from gas mixtures produced by combustion and other industrial processes prior to emission to the atmosphere. These processes offer advantages over more challenging direct air capture because the elevated partial pressure of CO2 (relative to air) results in lower energy requirement and cost for separation. The focus of this review is on the separation of CO2 from point source emissions and will not address the fate of the CO2 once separated. Many different types of point source capture technology have been developed including adsorption, absorption, chemical looping, membrane separation and cryogenic distillation approaches. Other techniques such as precombustion capture and oxy-firing also exist but these are specific to particular processes and not generally applicable. Chemical absorption of CO2 into aqueous amine and/or carbonate solutions, often called CO2 ‘solvents’ in carbon capture literature, is by far the most technically mature approach. It has been used for decades in industries such as natural gas sweetening and ammonia synthesis due to its applicability to a broad range of gas streams of wide-ranging CO2 content, its scalability, and tunability through modification of the absorbent composition. The other techniques each have challenges that need to be addressed before large scale deployment can be realized. For example, solid handling and heat integration is difficult in adsorption using solids and chemical looping based processes, physical adsorption and cryogenic processes do not work well for low CO2 partial pressure applications, membranes suffer from stability issues in industrial environments, and the kinetics of CO2 interactions is much slower for gas–solid processes. Chemical absorption using aqueous amines is now being deployed at large scale in coal and gas combustion processes and industrial processes such as cement and ethanol production. These absorption-based carbon capture technologies rely on reversible reactive chemical absorption of CO2 and are at their heart chemical processes. This review will focus on understanding the chemical and thermodynamic properties of the absorbents used and how this is intimately coupled to process performance. It will consider the current state-of-the-art and emerging chemical absorbent-based technologies, the opportunities that exist for further improvement and the challenges posed by operation at large scale.

The Chemical Absorption-Based CO2 Capture Process

While many process improvements have been made over the years the process of chemical absorption of CO2 has remained fundamentally unchanged since the original patent by Robert Bottoms in 1930. Figure is an illustration from the Bottoms patent showing all of the main features of a thermal swing acidic gas chemical absorption process including: a low temperature (40–60 °C) absorption column (10); high temperature (100–150 °C) desorption or stripping column (15) with steam heating at the base (18) and overhead condenser (24); a cross-flow heat exchanger to recover heat input into the stripping column (22); and a trim cooler to cool the absorbent liquid before entry to the absorber column (23).

Process configuration for an acidic gas capture process from Robert Bottoms in 1930.

Figure is a simplified illustration of a chemical absorption-based CO2 capture process with a view to highlighting the role of the absorbent. The absorption column is a vertical column used to provide contact between the gas stream to be treated and the absorbent. Gas is introduced at the base and CO2 lean absorbent liquid at the top and they are contacted counter-currently within the column internals at relatively low temperatures. Modern columns typically contain a structured packing that provides for a large surface area of contact between the gas and liquid with minimal resistance to flow. The liquid flows under the effect of gravity over the packing as a thin film, and the gas moves up through voids in the packing. Older columns may use random packing or trays. In this region where the gas–liquid contacting occurs CO2(g) is physically absorbed or dissolved into the liquid phase followed by chemical reaction. This chemical reaction transforms the absorbed CO2 into other compounds allowing more CO2 to enter the liquid phase. The gas with CO2 removed exits the top of the absorption column. Typically, there will also be a wash section at the top of the column to remove any volatile compounds (e.g., amines or degradation products) that may have been entrained with the exiting gas stream. The liquid having absorbed CO2 collects in the sump of the column. The CO2 rich solution is then pumped via a cross-flow heat exchanger to the top of the desorption column. This heat exchanger preheats the CO2 rich solution by heat transfer from the hot CO2 lean solution that exits the desorption column sump. The desorption column also contains packing material which the CO2 rich solution flows over under gravity. Steam generated by heating the absorbent in the sump of the column travels upward and condenses, heating the CO2 rich absorbent. This heating, combined with dilution of released CO2 by steam results in conditions in which CO2(g) is released from the absorbent. This CO2(g) then exits the top of the desorption column, now having been separated from the other gases in the gas mixture being treated. Some water vapor and other volatile compounds from the absorbent may exit with the CO2, so desorption columns typically have a reflux condenser at the exit and a rectification section at the top of the column above the CO2 rich solution feed where the condensate is returned. The hot CO2 lean solution is then returned to the absorption column via the cross-flow heat exchanger and a trim cooler to remove any excess heat.

An illustration of the basic components of a chemical absorption-based CO2 capture process.

The Chemistry of CO2 and Its Reactions

Chemistry takes place at the surface of the reacting molecules. Thus, in order to understand the chemistry of the CO2 molecule, its electronic surface has to be analyzed. CO2 is a highly symmetric, linear molecule, with the carbon atom at the center and the two oxygen atoms at opposite sides. The carbon atom has a formal positive charge of +4, while each oxygen atom has a formal −2 charge. However, these formal charges are not realized. Theoretical calculations for gaseous CO2 reveal a partial positive charge of about +0.69 e on the carbon while each oxygen atom has a negative charge of about −0.345 e as shown in Figure . These charges are not well-defined and they depend on the environment around the molecule, be it in the gas phase, in an aqueous phase or any other solution.

Partial charges on the carbon (black) and oxygen (red) atoms of a linear CO2 molecule.

There are two types of reactions of the CO2 molecule. In redox reactions the formal charges of any of the atoms is changed, e.g.: the carbon atom of CO2 can be reduced to a formal +2 charge (as in formic acid, HCOOH, or in carbon monoxide, CO); it can be reduced to a formal 0 charge (as in carbon, C, or formaldehyde, HCOH); it can be further reduced to a formal −2 charge (as in methanol, H3COH); or to the minimum formal −4 charge (as in methane, CH4). Redox reactions are usually very slow, and they are often essentially irreversible and thus not useful for carbon capture; however, they are the core of CO2 utilization reactions.

The other type of reaction involves substitutions at one or more atoms of the CO2 molecule. All reactions relevant for carbon capture are of this nature; they can be fast and they are reversible. The important point is that any reaction partner with a negative charge will ‘attack’ CO2 at the carbon atom, a nucleophilic attack, while positively charged reaction partners will attack at the oxygen atoms, an electrophilic attack. It turns out that most relevant reactions of CO2 in the present context involve a nucleophilic attack at the carbon atom.

Molecules which attack the positive charge of the carbon in CO2 feature a full or partial negative charge; they are called Lewis bases. The reaction partner of a Lewis base is a Lewis acid which features a partial or full positive charge. It is important to note that that the protonation equilibria are essentially instantaneous as the kinetics of these reactions is very fast on the time scale of CO2 capture. Consequently, they are quantified by an equilibrium constant only. We use the double arrow (↔) to distinguish these fast protonation equilibria from the slower equilibrium reactions (⇄) which are quantified by two rate constants. In CO2 capture, these slower reactions occur typically on a second to minute time scale. Also, the value of logK 8 is equivalent to the pK a value of the conjugate acid of the amine. pK a is the typical way to represent amine basicity (the larger the pK a the more basic the amine).

An additional reaction between amines and the bicarbonate ion has also been identified. 3 (R,H)N+HCO3−⇄k4k−4(R)NCOO−+H2O

The forward reaction, k 4, is not significant under capture conditions as the other reactions of the amine (with CO2 or H+) are significantly faster. However, the back reaction is the main path for the decomposition of the carbamate at high pH. The logarithm of the protonation constant for the carbamate is around 6, thus, at high pH the carbamic acid has a very low concentration and subsequently the decomposition via the carbamic acid, k –3, is very slow. Values for the carbamate protonation constant, K 7, as well as of the rate constant, k –4, of the back reaction at high temperature in the desorber column are not known; it appears that this reaction is fast enough and the actual values for the constants are of little importance.

The task for the chemist is to develop amines with favorable properties. Rational design of amines can be based on the development of Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship (QSAR) or it can be based on chemical insight. As the discussions around Figure suggest, the stronger the electron pair of the Lewis base, the faster the reaction, k 3, with CO2 and the larger the equilibrium constant K 3. The standard way of determining the strength of a Lewis base is to determine its strength toward the simplest Lewis acid, which is the proton. The constant for the protonation equilibrium is defined by K 8. Many protonation constants are published, otherwise they are easily determined by simple potentiometric titrations. Thus, we expect a correlation between the protonation constant K 8 and the rate constant k 3 as well as the equilibrium constant K 3. This relationship between K 8 and k 3 is known as a Brønsted relationship and has been reported a number of times. − The substituents on an amine molecule can influence the electronic environment of the amino group, and the value of K 8 , by either withdrawing (e.g., −OH) or adding (e.g., −CH3) electron density. In addition, bulky substituents in close proximity to the amino group can physically restrict or sterically hinder access. − Figure displays log­(k 3) and log­(K 3) versus log­(K 8) for a series of primary and secondary amines. Both correlations are very strong for sterically unhindered amines. Sterical hindrance will be less important for the small proton, compared with the bulkier CO2 molecule, thus we expect a reduced strength of interaction with CO2 when sterical hindrance is present. This is evident for 2-AP, SBA and DEA. NH3 also features a low interaction with CO2 which is ascribed to its small size and specific solvation interactions with water, as its structure is significantly different from the other primary and secondary amines.

The Cyclic Capacity

A crucially important property of a CO2 absorbent is its cyclic capacity, the amount of CO2 that is released in the desorber column per unit of absorption solution. It is quantified by the difference in the equilibrium constant for the overall reaction at low temperature (in the absorber) and high temperature (in the desorber) columns. 4 CO2+H2O+(R,H)N⇄KH2OHCO3−+(R,H)NH+⁣KH2O=K1K8K6CO2+2(R,H)N⇄Kamine(R)NCOO−+(R,H)NH+⁣Kamine=K3K8K7

The reaction of CO2 follows one of two pathways: the initial formation of carbonic acid followed by immediate deprotonation and protonation of an amine, and the initial formation of carbamic acid followed by deprotonation and protonation of a second amine. Both equilibria are quantitatively defined by their equilibrium constants, which are in turn determined by the previously given equilibria (see eq –eq ).

Both equilibria and thus the overall equilibrium is strongly influenced by the protonation constant, K 8, of the amine. As Figure demonstrates, an amine with large protonation constant is desirable as its reaction with CO2 will be fast, additionally its equilibrium constant is large as well resulting in large absorption capacity.

Concentrating on the amine path, the left, low temperature, side of Figure attempts to summarize the important aspects of the overall absorption process. At low absorber temperature the forward rate is defined by the activation barrier, the formation of an activated complex, between the amine and CO2. Of equal importance is the overall equilibrium constant K amine,LT which encompasses carbamic acid formation, its deprotonation, and the protonation of the amine. The exothermicity, ΔH LT, of the overall amine reaction is also relevant, it increases the temperature in the absorber column and thus decreases the absorption equilibrium constant and results in the so-called temperature bulge or rise within the absorber. If required, this temperature bulge can be managed by engineering solutions such as absorber intercooling.

A significant amount of energy is required to increase the temperature of the absorption solution and provide energy for the endothermic CO2 desorption reactions. There is an interesting apparent paradox, a large reaction enthalpy appears to be disadvantageous as more energy is also required to break bonds and drive the endothermic CO2 desorption reactions. However, the large reaction enthalpy increases the cyclic capacity by increasing the amount of CO2 that is desorbed at elevated temperature. This results in a trade-off between the chemical (reaction enthalpy) and physical (sensible heat requirement to raise the temperature of the absorbent and steam generation to dilute desorbed CO2 and maintain the driving force for desorption) contributions to the energy requirements. See Section of this contribution for more detailed information on the interplay between these chemical and physical processes.

Oxidative and Thermal Stability, and Corrosion

The focus of most research and development of absorbents for CO2 capture is primarily and rightly focused on reactivity with CO2 and related properties such as mass transfer, absorption capacity and stripping energy duty. However, when it comes to scale-up and commercial deployment long-term absorbent stability can be as or even more important. Degradation of an absorbent comes at a cost resulting in loss of performance and requiring makeup and reclamation. Degradation also comes with emissions of volatile products such as ammonia and aldehydes. When commercial realities and regulatory requirements of volatile emissions are considered, it may be preferable to have a more stable absorbent than the lowest possible energy duty or equipment costs. This represents a bit of a balancing act, as the properties that make an amine reactive toward CO2, such as the lone electron pair on a nitrogen, also makes it susceptible to oxidation and molecular rearrangement. Just as molecular structure has a large impact on CO2 capture performance, it also has a large impact on the stability of an amine toward degradation. Degradation also occurs on a longer time scale than the capture process operation. It is only through long duration piloting experiments with exposure to industrially realistic gas streams that stability can be effectively determined.

This section gives an introduction to the degradation reactions of aqueous amine absorbents which have been widely investigated for stability and degradation. This is not an exhaustive review of this topic, which is extensive, but rather an introduction to the type of chemistry that occurs. More work is needed to understand degradation of more recently developed absorbents, such as nonaqueous and biphasic systems. The primary routes of aqueous amine absorbent degradation in a process are oxidation via the presence of O2 or NO2 in the gas stream being treated; and thermal rearrangement when exposed to elevated temperature particularly at the base of the stripper. , Typically, these degradation processes do not occur in isolation, with the products of thermal degradation potentially undergoing oxidation, and the thermal cycling in a process accelerating some oxidation reactions. As an aside, the presence of SO2 in a gas stream is also often considered to cause degradation. This is not strictly correct, as absorbed SO2 does not directly react with amines but rather is converted to sulphurous/sulfuric acid. This leads to acidification of the absorbent and loss of performance, but the amines themselves are not chemically altered. However, the acidification of the absorbent can lead to enhancement of other degradation reactions that have a pH dependence.

The O2 derived oxidative degradation products of aqueous amine formulations are specific to the structure of the amines being used. However, the degradation reaction is generally considered to be initiated by hydrogen abstraction from a carbon in proximity to the nitrogen, or electron abstraction from the nitrogen, leading to unstable imine formation and subsequent reactions. Figure represents a general mechanism for the initial steps in the oxidation of primary amines to aldehydes, carboxylic acids and amides via an imine intermediate. These compounds can then proceed to react with each other, and the starting amine to form other products. The thermal cycling that occurs in a CO2 capture process can facilitate some of these reactions. Secondary amines can undergo similar reactions but with loss of more complex RNH2 groups, while tertiary amines tend to be more stable due to their inability to easily form an imine intermediate. This general mechanism also highlights why NH3 emissions are typically associated with amine degradation, with loss of NH3 featuring.

A general mechanism representing the oxidative degradation of primary amines.

Amines can also be oxidized by NO2 present in a flue gas. The mechanism is complex, however in this case the oxidizing agent is generally considered to be an NO2 radical under absorber column conditions and nitrous acid (HNO2) formed by aqueous dissolution of NO2 under desorber column conditions. ,− The free radical reaction leads to amine radical formation by hydrogen abstraction which can then react with NO. Primary amines form an unstable nitrosamine, that further reacts to an alcohol or amine dimer via loss of N2. Secondary amines can form stable nitrosamines. Nitramines may also form by reaction of amine radicals with NO2, but this is a minor pathway. Generalized mechanisms are given in Figure . If an amine dimer, or secondary amine, is formed from a primary amine, it is then possible for this degradation product to form a stable nitrosamine. Stable nitrosamines are problematic as they are carcinogenic. If formed in a process steps must be taken to eliminate them, for example exposure to UV light or ozone or elevated temperature. Thermal destruction will then impact the extent of thermal degradation and operating costs. Tertiary amines are less susceptible to oxidation by NO2. There is evidence they can undergo some reaction but to a much lesser extent than primary and secondary amines.

A general mechanism showing the reaction of primary and secondary amines with absorbed NO2 as an NO2 radical or as nitrous acid (HNO2).

Thermal degradation occurs where temperatures are highest in the capture process, the reboiler and hot side of the cross-flow heat exchanger. Thermal degradation generally proceeds via the carbamic acid/carbamate formed from reaction with CO2 with alkanolamines undergoing carbamic acid polymerization. A combination of polymerization and ring closure reactions result in the formation of dimers and cyclic structures such as oxazolidinones from the commonly used amine monoethanolamine (MEA) and its derivatives. An example of oxazolidinone formation is shown in Figure . An important point to note is that even though MEA and its derivative 2-amino-2-methyl-1-proponal (AMP) both form an oxazolidinone, AMP is much more thermally stable as it only forms a very small amount of carbamic acid compared to MEA. This suggests that amine structure can be utilized to limit degradation pathways and develop more stable compounds. Tertiary amines, which form no carbamate, tend to undergo demethylation/methylation reactions which may result in the formation of secondary amines that are then able to undergo carbamic acid polymerization. Diamines, such as ethylendiamine, can also undergo ring closure reactions to form imidazolidinones or other multinitrogen heterocycles.

Thermal degradation mechanism of oxazolidinone formation from MEA and its derivatives.

Aqueous amine-based CO2 capture plants are typically constructed from carbon or stainless steel. These steels can suffer corrosion due to exposure to CO2 and aqueous amines. This is a common issue for any industrial process where steels are used with acidic or basic materials. However, a particular issue for carbon capture is that the solubilization of metal ions such as Fe2+ and Cu2+ from the steel can enhance oxidative degradation of aqueous amines. − The metal ions can act as catalysts for the redox reactions that occur during oxidation and dramatically increase the rate of degradation. We will not go into the details of corrosion mechanisms here, however it is important to note that corrosion can have an operational impact on equipment lifetime and also on the longevity of the absorbent.

Aqueous Amines and Amino Acids Used for Carbon Capture

Aqueous Amines

As already noted, aqueous amines as chemical absorbents for CO2 have been used for many decades and are ubiquitous in this field. In this section we will provide an overview of the most prominent amines investigated for this application in recent history. Due to the extensive literature available, we have limited the scope to amines for which there have been at least 5 publications over the preceding 15 years. Amines have the unique ability to interact with CO2 via two pathways. As detailed in Section , amines are Lewis bases, and primary and secondary amines can form a direct N–C bond with CO2 via loss of a labile proton. Amines are also Brønsted bases, and all types can accept protons released from other reactions with CO2. This dual functionality of amines makes them uniquely suited to CO2 absorption. Their use in aqueous solution stems from the fact that amines are polar molecules with good aqueous solubility. Additionally, the boiling point of water is well matched to the temperature required for CO2 desorption and concomitant generation of water vapor required for the functioning of a desorption column. Water also allows for the rapid transport of protons between molecules at rates orders of magnitude faster than the diffusion of molecules. This means proton transfer is not rate limiting, with absorption rates being controlled by molecular diffusion and the reactions of CO2.

The pre-eminent amines used for decades as chemical absorbents are monoethanolamine (MEA), diethanolamine (DEA), 2-amino-2-methyl-propanol (AMP), N-dimethylethanolamine (MDEA) and piperazine (PZ). Their structures are given in Figure . The primary and secondary amines MEA, DEA and PZ have been used as single amine absorbents as each reacts rapidly with CO2 to form a carbamate, while also playing the role of Brønsted base to accept protons released by carbamate and bicarbonate formation. Stoichiometrically, 2 amine molecules are consumed per CO2 molecule for MEA and DEA (see eq ). Because PZ is a diamine, its absorption capacity is greater as a single PZ molecule has amine functionality that can potentially play both roles. The capacity is not double as the amino groups of PZ do not behave independently due to their close proximity, but fewer than 2 PZ molecules are consumed per CO2. In contrast, AMP and MDEA do not form carbamates (at least to any appreciable extent, MDEA as it is a tertiary amine and AMP for steric hindrance reasons) and undergo acid–base reactions only. This means that in absorbents based on these amines CO2 reacts only with water (or OH–) to form bicarbonate via a kinetically relatively slow reaction, with AMP/MDEA acting primarily to accept the protons released. This consumes 1 amine molecule per CO2 molecule yielding a larger absorption capacity compared to MEA, DEA and PZ. Additionally, they may also increase the rate of the reaction with water via a base catalysis mechanism, but even with this enhancement the rates of CO2 absorption remain impractically slow unless high pressure CO2 is used. For this reason, AMP and MDEA are frequently formulated in binary mixtures with carbamate forming amines that are able to react more rapidly with CO2. By doing so, the properties of fast reaction kinetics are combined with increased absorption capacity with the resulting formulation having better CO2 capture performance than the individual components.

Molecular structures of the most common amines used as CO2 chemical absorbents as reported in publicly available literature.

Besides PZ, all these amines also have alcohol functional groups (alkanolamines). The −OH groups are generally considered not directly involved in reaction with CO2. There is recent evidence that alkyl carbonates can form at high CO2 loading, but to a much lesser extent than carbamate and bicarbonate. It remains unclear if this reaction is of relevance under practical CO2 absorption conditions. It has been postulated that −OH groups reduce the vapor pressure of these molecules in aqueous solution by the ability of the polar alcohol group to hydrogen bond with water. However, −OH groups also have an electron withdrawing effect when in close proximity to an amino group reducing the Lewis and Brønsted basicity.

As discussed in Section , the 2 secondary amines PZ and DEA, and secondary amines in general, may have a propensity to form nitrosamines or nitramines by reaction with NO2 that may be present in some gas streams. Nitrosamines and nitramines are known human carcinogens, so their formation is a concern both in terms of worker health and community health if emitted to the environment. Also noted in Section , engineering controls exist that can be used to manage nitrosamine and nitramine formation. However, these techniques are not always available, or suitable for use with some amines.

In recent decades a vast number of other amine molecules in aqueous solution have been investigated for CO2 absorption applications. The reviews of aqueous amine solutions by Meng et al. (2022) and Bernhardsen and Knuutila (2017) identified 118 amine molecules in addition to the 5 shown in Figure . These provide an excellent snapshot of the amines that have been investigated and the relationship between amine class, chemistry, and CO2 absorption behavior. In practical terms, many of those amines listed are not suitable for various reasons such as low boiling point and therefore excessive volatility or are too basic which limits the thermal reversibility of the absorption reactions. Besides evaporative losses in the absorber due to high gas flows, amines with boiling points below or near water will be substantially lost to the vapor phase under desorption conditions.

As mentioned above, amines that are too basic may not have the thermal reversibility properties necessary to perform well in carbon capture applications. This is an important concept to explore, as more basic is not always better. Amines that are excessively basic will have too low a CO2 partial pressure for effective desorption at typical desorption temperatures, even though they have a larger capacity for CO2 absorption. Figure shows the change of CO2 partial pressure with CO2 loading (CO2­ loading is the ratio of moles of CO2 absorbed to the moles of amine in solution – mol CO2 / mol amine) for 5 M MEA and 2 hypothetical MEA-like amines. The MEA like-amines have the same reaction parameters as MEA, but have pK a,ref values at 25 °C of 10.5 and 11.0, compared to 9.5 for MEA (pK a,ref refers to the pK a value at a specified reference temperature). This has been simulated using the chemical model and parameters for MEA in Puxty and Maeder (2013). The figure shows the curves for 2 temperatures typical of CO2 capture processes, absorption at 40 °C and desorption at 120 °C. The figure also shows the cycle of absorption, heating, desorption and cooling for MEA, with an analogous cycle occurring for the MEA-like amines. If we assume an idealized process with a rich CO2(g) loading at 15 kPa CO2(g) and a lean CO2 loading at 0.15 kPa CO2, the CO2(g) partial pressure at 120 °C, and hence the base of a desorption column, is 55 kPa, 35 and 20 kPa for MEA, and an MEA-like amine with pK a,ref values of 10.5 and 11.0 respectively. Desorption columns need to operate above atmospheric pressure, typically at 150–200 kPa, to generate adequate flow while avoiding excessive thermal degradation. For the MEA-like amines with their smaller CO2(g) partial pressure at elevated temperature, more steam is needed to maintain the desorption column pressure and dilute the CO2(g) to maintain stripping driving force resulting in increased energy demand. There are no hard and fast rules, but the point is that considering reversibility is crucial when assessing molecules for CO2 capture.

Deep insight into the effects of basic parameters such as the protonation constant of the amine, as discussed above, are possible only once the complete set of chemical reactions are quantitatively determined. Such procedures can substantially reduce experimental investigations.

Alkanolamines

Alkanolamines are historically the class of amine used for CO2 capture applications as noted above, and they still feature prominently in the research literature. In this section we will focus on alkanolamines not covered so far that have shown promise across many of the desirable chemical and physical properties detailed in Table . The structures of the molecules considered are given in Figure and include primary, secondary and tertiary amines as well as a polyamine and cyclic amine.

Selected alkanolamines that have shown promising chemical and physical characteristics.

2-(Methylamino)­ethanol (MAE) − is a derivative of MEA. It is more basic due to the addition of an electron donating methyl substituent and reacts more rapidly with CO2 as a consequence. It has larger CO2 absorption capacity at low temperature and similar capacity to MEA at elevated temperature, resulting in a larger cyclic capacity. It has been characterized as a single amine and in a formulation with 2-(2-aminoethylamino)­ethanol (AEEA). Being a secondary amine, it may have a propensity to form nitrosamines in the presence of NO2 that may be present in some flue gases.

2-(Ethylamino)­ethanol (EAE) − is structurally very similar to MAE but with an ethyl substituent further increasing basicity to the point where CO2 desorption may be problematic. Its main point of difference is that it is known to react very rapidly with CO2 and has been used as an absorbent rate promoter in formulations with tertiary amines 3-(dimethylamino)­propan-1-ol and diglycolamine. A drawback is that it is also a secondary amine and would likely form nitrosamines. Also there is little information about its stability or degradation. 2-(Isopropylamino)­ethanol (IPAE) ,, is a sterically hindered derivative of EAE with a methyl substituent on one of the amino group’s α-carbons. The steric hindrance enhances its CO2 absorption and cyclic capacity by reducing the affinity for carbamate formation in favor of bicarbonate, but decreases its rate of reaction with CO2 relative to EAE. It has seen small-scale pilot plant operation in a water-lean formulation with some success. It is also a secondary amine but the presence of steric hindrance may reduce its affinity for nitrosamine formation, although this has not been investigated. MAE, EAE and IPAE are all relatively low viscosity and are completely miscible with water and do not form precipitates.

2-(2-Aminoethoxy)­ethanol (AEE), also commonly known as diglycolamine (DGA), has been used for CO2 capture applications for some time and has continued to see sustained interest. − As a result its chemical, physical, and mass transfer properties have been well characterized to the extent that it is present alongside the common amines of Figure as a component in a number of the commonly used process modeling packages such as ProTreat (Optimized Gas Treating Inc.), ProMax (Bryan Research & Engineering, LLC) and Aspen PLUS (AspenTech). Its stability and degradation characteristics are also known and AEE is more stable than MEA but has slower rates of mass transfer and smaller absorption capacity at low CO2 partial pressure. Previously AEE has been applied to natural gas sweetening, in particular low pressure but high CO2 fraction applications as a single aqueous amine. The more recent publications focus on its use in biogas upgrading applications which have a similar low pressure but high CO2 fraction composition.

Similarly diisopropanolamine (DIPA) has been used for high pressure CO2 capture applications, is well characterized and is incorporated into process modeling packages. More recent work involving DIPA has focused on characterizing its formulation with other amines, typically to improve mass transfer performance which is slow, but still with high pressure applications in mind. − Being a secondary amine, nitrosamine formation may be an issue in NO2 containing gas streams.

2-Amino-2-hydroxymethyl-1,3-propanediol (THAM, AHPD or TRIS) is a sterically hindered amine that is similar in structure to AMP, with the α-carbon methyl substituents replaced by methyl alcohol. − The electron withdrawing effect of the additional alcohol groups reduces the basicity of the amino group, limiting capacity at low CO2 partial pressure. Surprisingly, the additional alcohol groups do not seem to have a particularly detrimental impact on viscosity (at least in the absence of CO2) and the vapor pressure in aqueous solution is low relative to AMP due to the additional molecular weight and possibly enhanced hydrogen bonding capacity. For most applications formulation with a faster reacting primary or secondary amine that is not sterically hindered will be necessary to achieve useful mass transfer rates. Its thermodynamic behavior (large protonation enthalpy and good thermal reversibility) is typical of noncarbamate forming sterically hindered amines, but little is known about its stability.

Even though they are tertiary amines, and therefore do not undergo direct reaction with CO2, N,N-dimethylethanolamine (DMEA) ,− and N,N-diethylethanolamine (DEEA) ,,,− have seen considerable activity in terms of publications. Being tertiary amines, they cannot directly react with CO2 and they primarily act as proton acceptors (and possibly base catalysts) for the slow bicarbonate formation reaction pathway. Consequently, they have been used in formulations with carbamate forming amines such as MEA. DMEA and DEEA are structurally very similar, DEEA is more basic due to ethyl substitution of the amino groups and lends itself to more rapid mass transfer of CO2. Consequently, it has been studied in more detail and appeared more frequently in literature. Its primary appeal is that it has a smaller CO2 absorption enthalpy and viscosity and is more basic than both AMP and MDEA (the sterically hindered and tertiary amine workhorses), which yields mass transfer and absorption capacity benefits. However, with regard to AMP, it exhibits a reduced thermally driven CO2 cyclic capacity and is not as resistant to degradation. DEEA certainly provides an alternative to AMP as a reasonably robust and low-cost amine for formulation with primary and secondary amines, but it would have alternative optimum operating conditions due to its different thermodynamic properties.

1-Dimethylamino-2-propanol (1DMAP or DMA2P) is another tertiary amine that has been the focus of considerable research recently. ,,− It is similar in structure to DMEA, with a methyl branch in the 2-position. This methyl group is unlikely to interact with the amino group but is in close proximity to the alcohol group of the molecule. This will likely have an effect on the solvation environment, particularly in aqueous solution where the alcohol group will hydrogen bond with water. It has a similar basicity to the structurally similar tertiary amines already discussed. There is an odd discrepancy in the cited literature in regard to whether it has an unusually large or unusually small enthalpy of CO2 absorption. The focus so far has been on determination of its properties and it has only been considered primarily in formulations with MEA, EAE and PZ. It certainly warrants further investigation, in particular to better understand its thermodynamic properties and the role of the additional methyl group, and also its stability. 4-Diethylamino-2-butanol (DEAB) has similarities to DEEA but with an additional C-atom separating the alcohol and amino groups and a methyl branch in the 2-position. ,− The net effect of greater distance to the electron withdrawing alcohol is greater basicity compared to DEAB and the other amines already discussed (pK a of conjugate acid >10.2 at 25 °C compared to 9.9 for DEEA). This imparts it with greater absorption capacity and a more rapid mass transfer behavior compared to other tertiary amines (due to more basic conditions in solution). However, formulation with other primary or secondary amines is still necessary to achieve practically useful mass transfer. Little information is currently available about its thermodynamics and therefore cyclic capacity or its stability.

2-(2-Aminoethylamino)­ethanol (AEEA) is one of the few alkanol diamines. ,− At first glance it appears similar to AEE, however the replacement of the ethoxy group with a secondary amine is significant. Both amine groups are reactive with CO2 to form a carbamate, changing the chemistry considerably including the formation of stable protonated carbamates at high CO2 loading. The spacing between amino groups is such that although both amines can be protonated (conjugate acid pK a values of 10.05 and 7.21 respectively at 20 °C) only the more basic primary amine is relevant under CO2 absorption conditions. AEEA reacts rapidly with CO2 and studies have focused mainly on vapor–liquid equilibria and CO2 reaction kinetics. Little is known about its thermodynamic and cyclic capacity properties across a broad temperature range nor its stability.

The vast majority of alkanolamines considered for CO2 capture are acyclic aliphatic compounds. 2-Piperidineethanol (2-PE) is one of the few cyclic alkanolamines considered and has some interesting properties. ,− Piperidine, from which 2-PE is derived, is itself is not suited to CO2 capture applications as its basicity is too great to allow adequate reversibility of the absorption reactions under moderate thermal swing (conjugate acid pK a = 11.14 at 25 °C). , The addition of the ethyl alcohol reduces the basicity to a useful range (conjugate acid pK a = 10.48 at 25 °C). The proximity of the ethyl alcohol group to the amino group results in no carbamate formation caused by either steric hindrance or intramolecular hydrogen bonding between the alcohol and amine groups. The fact that 2-piperidinemethanol behaves similarly in terms of carbamate formation suggests steric hindrance is the more likely reason. As with the other secondary amines considered, nitrosamine formation will be of concern in NO2 containing gas streams.

Alcohol Free Amines

The second major class of amine molecules that feature as aqueous amine absorbents are amines with no alcohol functionality present. These tend to be polyamines or aliphatic cyclic amines or both, as in PZ. The preference for polyamines is because they combine multiple sites within a single molecule that are potentially reactive with CO2 or function as Brønsted bases. Additional amino groups generally improves aqueous solubility and reduces vapor pressure through increased molecular weight and enhanced hydrogen bonding. Acyclic monoamines tend not to be favored as low molecular weight forms have low boiling points and high molecule weight forms lack aqueous solubility due to lengthy nonpolar C-atom backbones. Alternatively, mono-, di- and tri- amines feature as cyclic molecules with PZ being the most ubiquitous. Cyclic structures constrain the C-atom backbone in a way that increases the availability of the nitrogen lone electron pair improving reactivity toward CO2, aqueous solubility and increasing basicity compared to the equivalently sized acyclic backbone. Cyclic amines also tend to be more stable toward thermal and oxidative degradation. Ring opening is harder to do than the cleavage or substitution reactions that lead to the degradation of acyclic amines.

Ethylenediamine (EDA) is by far the most mature of the acyclic polyamines in terms of its application. ,,− Structurally, it is MEA with the alcohol group replaced by a second amino group. It has been used in pilot plant trials but also continues to be the focus of fundamental studies of the characteristics of linear diamines. The amino groups have similar properties to that of MEA, although EDA is more basic and does react more rapidly with CO2. The proximity of the amino groups means that once one is protonated, protonation of the second amino group occurs at a pH value lower than is relevant for CO2 capture. However, it is able to form a dicarbamate. EDA is more volatile and is less stable than MEA which limits its utility.

There has also been interest in the use of hexamethylenediamine (HMDA). It is similar to EDA, but has 6 instead of 2 carbons in the chain separating the amino groups. This greater separation allows the amino groups to function almost independently with the protonation constant of both amino groups remaining in a useful range. HMDA is marginally more basic than EDA and has a larger absorption capacity and a faster reaction rate with CO2, making it a potential rate promoter in conjunction with tertiary or sterically hindered amines. More work is needed to determine its cyclic capacity and initial studies of stability indicate it has good thermal stability but poor oxidative stability. ,

3-(Methylamino)­propylamine (MAPA) has been the focus of quite a bit of research activity. ,− Compared to EDA it has one additional carbon atom separating the amino. In addition, one of the groups is methylated resulting in MAPA containing a primary and a secondary amino group. It is more basic than EDA, and while the second protonation constant is significantly smaller than the first, it remains in a useful range for CO2 absorption. MAPA has exhibited fast reaction kinetics with CO2 and good cyclic capacity. It has generally been considered for use in formulations with other tertiary and sterically hindered amines and sarcosine, as high viscosity limits its use alone at high concentration. Its thermodynamics have been well characterized, and its cyclic capacity is good. A downside appears to be its stability as it degrades rapidly in the presence of oxygen. The presence of a secondary amino group also limits its utility in the presence of NO2 due to the potential for nitrosamine formation.

Bis­(3-aminopropyl) amine (APA) is the only triamine identified among the group of acyclic polyamines. It has been studied less than the other polyamines already discussed, and mostly by the same authors. − Its structure is symmetrical with a central secondary amine and 2 propylamine substituents attached. It certainly shows promise as it has low viscosity, fast reaction with CO2 and the separation of the primary amino groups mean they all have the potential to be active proton acceptors and carbamate formers. The data collected to date is focused on physical properties, equilibrium studies of capacity and a small amount on absorption rate. More data needs to be collected especially as a function of temperature to fully characterize its performance as an absorbent. Investigation of the chemical speciation with respect to protonation and carbamate formation would also provide valuable information. No stability data has been collected, and given the number of reactive amino groups, it may be expected to degrade quite rapidly in the presence of oxygen and high temperatures, and form nitrosamines at the secondary amine site in the presence of NO2.

The first row of cyclic amines shown in Figure are all derivatives of PZ. They contain secondary amino groups so nitrosamine formation will likely be an issue. Like PZ, these compounds are likely to be more tolerant toward thermal degradation allowing them to operate at higher CO2 desorption temperature. These higher temperatures can destroy any nitrosamines formed. 1-Methylpiperazine (1-MPZ) has seen limited study and has generally been used to better understand the properties of PZ. − It has a secondary and tertiary amino functionality and is less basic than PZ but retains the fast reaction with CO2. It is less stable than PZ but still one of the more stable amines studied to-date. It does seem to have a reduced CO2 absorption capacity likely due to the inability to form a dicarbamate. 2-Methylpiperazine (2-MPZ) is very similar, however the location of the methyl group means it retains 2 secondary amino groups, but one is sterically hindered. 2-MPZ has been studied more intensively than 1-MPZ. ,,− It has been found to be more stable than 1-MPZ but still less stable than PZ. CO2 mass transfer has also been found to be slower for 2-MPZ even though its basicity sits between that of 1-MPZ and PZ. It is unclear what factors are responsible for the differences in rate as both 1-MPZ and 2-MPZ have a sterically free secondary amine able to react with CO2, and 2-MPZ can form a dicarbamate at high CO2 loadings. This points to why there is particular interest in 2-MPZ as it has better CO2 cyclic capacity than 1-MPZ and PZ while still retaining good rates of reaction with CO2 and better stability than most other amines. There has also been some interest in 1-(2-aminoethyl)­piperazine (AEP) ,,− particularly as a rate promoter. It is a triamine consisting of a primary, secondary and tertiary amino group but does not appear to show any particular advantage over the smaller 1-MPZ and 2-MPZ molecules. The mass transfer benefits of the other PZ derivatives are lost and speciation studies indicate carbamate formation occurs principally at the primary amino group. It is unclear why this is the case given the secondary amines of PZ are known to react very rapidly with CO2. A possibility is that the dangling primary amino group is able to interfere with the secondary amino group within the ring. Although stability has not been investigated extensively, it is likely AEP would also be less stable than the other PZ derivatives due to the additional primary ethyl amino substituent which would be more susceptible to degradation.

Selected amines that contain no alcohol functionality and that have shown promising chemical and physical characteristics.

The final row of Figure contains 2 derivatives of piperidine, as is alkanolamine 2-PE which was already discussed. Morpholine (MOR) results from the insertion of an oxygen atom within the ring structure of piperidine. ,,,− This leads to a significant reduction in basicity compared to piperidine (conjugate acid pK a reduced from 11.1 to 8.5 at 25 °C) to a value more useful for reversible CO2 absorption. MOR is more stable than PZ, likely due to fewer reactive amine groups but suffers from a lower rate of reaction with CO2 consistent with its smaller basicity. Although it still provides reasonable rates of mass transfer, CO2 absorption capacity and cyclic capacity to warrant ongoing study. 2-Methylpiperdine (2MPD or 2-MP) is an alternative piperidine derivative with a methyl group in the 2-position of the ring. − The proximity of the methyl group on a carbon α to the amino group results in steric hindrance which suppresses formation of carbamate (carbamate forms to a small extent). Unlike acyclic amines the methyl group in the 2-position reduces the basicity of the amino group marginally compared to piperidine due to steric strain effects, however it is still more basic than would be desirable.

Benzylamine (BZA) is somewhat of a structural outlier compared to the other amines considered. − It contains an aromatic benzene ring with a pendant methyl amino group. Unlike the other cyclic structures, the amino group is primary which limits the possibility of nitrosamine formation. Also, the combination of cyclic structure and aromaticity means the ring is extremely robust and any degradation will occur via the pendant arm. Having a single carbon spacing between the ring and the amino group is the minimum required for reactivity with CO2, as direct attachment of the amino group such as in aniline results in the lone pair of the nitrogen atom becoming less available and unreactive toward CO2. BZA has been found to react rapidly with CO2 and have a good absorption capacity and excellent cyclic capacity. Its enthalpy of protonation and subsequently enthalpy of absorption are large and negative yielding good cyclic capacity but shifting the optimal lean and rich CO2 loadings in a process compared to the more typical thermodynamic values of other amines. It forms a precipitate at high concentration upon reaction with CO2 which can be problematic. Also it does suffer some stability issues that need further investigation.

Aqueous Amino Acids

Amino acids are amine compounds that also contain additional carboxylic acid functionality. Many amino acids are naturally occurring and at least 22 play a biological function and are the building blocks of proteins. Figure shows a selection of amino acids that have been investigated for CO2 capture applications. The amino group in these compounds is able to react with CO2 via the same mechanism as amines, and they perform similarly in terms of mass transfer. The amino groups have similar protonation constants to amines and alkanolamines. Neutral amino acids are zwitterionic and have low reactivity with CO2 due to predominant protonation at the amine group, rendering it unreactive toward CO2. The labile proton can be removed by reaction with hydroxide or other strong base, and once removed the amino acid anion that remains carries a negative charge and is reactive toward CO2. Once protonated as part of the reaction with CO2, the zwitterion formed is also a nonvolatile species. The fact that all differently protonated forms of amino acids are primarily ionic yields the functional advantage of negligible vapor pressure, which has process benefits by eliminating the need to wash out or condense organic compounds carried from the absorption and desorption columns with the exiting gas streams. Figure shows an example for glycine. The first reaction is removal of a proton from glycine by hydroxide to form a glycinate anion, and the second the overall reaction between glycinate and CO2 to form a carbamate and glycine zwitterion. This reaction with CO2 is equivalent to that seen in primary amines and alkanolamines. Many amino acids are resistant to oxidative degradation and also have the advantage of being nontoxic and environmentally benign, at least in the case of naturally occurring amino acids.

Chemical structures of some amino acids considered for CO2 capture applications.

Reaction of glycine with hydroxide to generate the glycinate anion and the overall reaction of glycinate with CO2 to form a carbamate and glycine zwitterion.

A challenge posed by many amino acids is that they have limited aqueous solubility compared to amines and often readily precipitate following reaction with CO2. This can limit the absorption capacity of amino acid–based absorbents. To-date pilot plant trials of single-phase amino acid–based systems have not produced favorable results. , Novel process designs that utilize this biphasic behavior and allow for separation of the solids to overcome the capacity loss have been developed. In this case the precipitate formed can be either the neutral, zwitter-ionic amino acid or CO2 containing products, depending upon the amino acid used. A second challenge is that amino acids tend to have poor thermal stability compared to amines. A topic that requires further investigation is the role of the counterion that is in solution as a consequence of the hydroxide salt used when converting amino acids to their anion. Typically, hydroxides of sodium, potassium or lithium are used resulting in a high concentration of background positive ions present. , This counterion can influence many factors including solubility, reaction kinetics and CO2 absorption capacity due to ionic strength effects and the particular properties of the counterion, however very little investigation of this has occurred. For completeness it should be noted that the limited absorption capacity is not an issue for applications to dilute CO2 streams. In fact while not point source capture, for applications with very high gas-to-liquid ratios and high oxygen content such as direct air capture, their nonvolatility and resistance to oxidative degradation are attractive features. −

Water-Lean and Nonaqueous Absorbents

Water-lean absorbents are typically but not exclusively based on amines like those discussed already, but where water has been partially or fully replaced by an organic solvent. A very comprehensive review of water-lean absorbents by Wanderley et al. was recently published. The reader is referred to this work for a more complete discussion of this area, including a historical perspective. This section will only provide a brief summary of more recent work. Ionic liquids would also fall into this class of absorbents but will not be discussed in detail as significant further development is needed before they could be applied practically. Ionic liquids can consist of ion pairs that physically dissolve CO2 or have reactive functionality incorporated as well. , The main issues relate to high viscosity, lack of information about stability, and scale-up. Technically, deep eutectic absorbents in which an amine is added to a deep eutectic solvent such as choline chloride − also fit the definition of a water-lean absorbent. A deep eutectic absorbent is a mixture in which the melting point of the mixture is lower than that of the individual components. However, they are currently far from being practically useful due to very high viscosity and poor absorption at low to moderate CO2 partial pressure. As such, they are mentioned for completeness but will not be discussed.

The motivation for replacing water is to reduce the heat capacity and therefore the latent heat requirements of CO2 stripping, minimize water vaporisation during CO2 stripping, and increase the physical solubility of CO2. A general drawback of water-lean absorbents is they tend to have a high viscosity (>100 cP). Most aqueous amines used in traditional CO2 capture processes have viscosities in the 2–30 cP range. Above 30 cP heat and mass transfer, pumping, flow characteristics and wetting of packing materials can become problematic. Maintaining the water balance in processes where the feed gas contains moisture may also be challenging. Additionally, the lack of water can require the use of a stripping gas or vacuum stripping which can be problematic for applications requiring high purity CO2. Water-lean absorbents also introduce other factors such as changes in the solvation environment and polarity that can both positively and negatively influence amine-CO2 reaction kinetics and equilibria and the solubility of ionic species.

Studies of stability under conditions that represent long-term operation, or even long-term operation itself are currently lacking for water-lean absorbents. It is unclear what the impact of water-lean compositions will be on the degradation of amines. Also, it is possible the organic solvent itself will undergo degradation or reaction with amine degradation products.

The vapor–liquid-equilibria behavior of water-lean absorbents differs from aqueous absorbents in that carbamic acid/carbamate formation is somewhat destabilized in a lower polarity solvent environment. This leads to reduced enhancement due to chemical reaction and therefore reduced absorption capacity at low CO2 partial pressure. Alternatively, at high CO2 partial pressure where chemical reaction plays a lesser role and physical absorption dominates, the capacity is enhanced due to the increased physical solubility of CO2 in organic solvents.

Alcohols feature prominently as solvents for water-lean absorbents. − Methanol and ethanol are the most common as they provide low viscosity, good amine and reaction product solubility and generally enhanced physical CO2 solubility relative to water only. Glycols also feature such as di-, tri-, and poly ethylene glycol. Examples of their structures are given in Figure . Alcohols are appealing because they are generally miscible with water and amines. Also being protic solvents the reactions between CO2 and amines proceed via essentially the same mechanism as in water with protons free to migrate between molecules. A drawback is that the vapor pressure of smaller alcohols can be high leading to losses which need to be managed. A trade-off is that alcohols of larger molecular weight have reduced vapor pressure but also tend to have larger viscosity.

Examples of common alcohol solvents featured in water-lean formulations.

A broad range of other organic compounds have been tested as solvents but one that stands out in terms of prominence is N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone ,− (NMP) which is shown in Figure . NMP features a large physical solubility for CO2, is known to be stable, is of low viscosity and moderately polar. It has been used as a physical absorbent in its own right for high pressure CO2 applications. Mass transfer in mixtures with amines such as MEA remains good.

Another interesting hydrophobic and low vapor pressure example of a water-lean system not based on typical alcoholic solvents is that consisting of equimolar fluorinated amine and fluorinated alcohol also shown in Figure . CO2 appears to react with the amine via the typical mechanism already described with the fluorinated alcohol acting as a diluent only. The viscosities under CO2 free and CO2 loaded conditions are acceptable (<30 cP at 40 °C). Little is known about the system in terms of mass transfer or stability but it has an interesting feature of large variability of the absorption enthalpy with temperature. It also demonstrates a low CO2 affinity at elevated temperature and may be able to release CO2 at high pressure.

A recent water-lean example from Zheng et al. tested a system using the single component N-(2-ethoxyethyl)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (2-EEMPA) shown in Figure . It stands out and warrants special mention due to its unusual single component composition. Water loading is small at only around 1.5 wt %. 2-EEMPA is a derivative of morpholine and contains both a secondary and tertiary amino group. Based on vapor–liquid-equilibria results it has been inferred that 2-EEMPA reacts to form carbamate and protonated amine products in a manner equivalent to aqueous amines. Additional investigation of the speciation upon reaction with CO2 would help in understanding the chemical behavior. Viscosity is relatively high (∼100 cP at 30 °C and high CO2 loading) which poses a challenge. Investigations of the potential of nitrosamine formation via the secondary amino group and absorbent degradation still need to be undertaken.

Alkoxide chemistry can be an alternative to amine chemistry for reaction with CO2. A hurdle for applicability of alkoxides is the stability of oxygen anions in solution. One way of making the oxygen atom a good nucleophile for rection with CO2 in standard solutions is demonstrated in the CO2BOL class of absorbents.

A CO2BOL is a CO2 binding organic liquid. It is distinct from an amine based aqueous system because the amino group is not the site for reaction with CO2 and distinct from an ionic liquid because no ions are present (other than zwitterions) until CO2 is absorbed. They are based around a guanidine core structure with an alkanolamidine group attached, the guanidine group providing a highly basic nitrogen lone pair. In the liquid phase the base will deprotonate the alcohol moiety giving an alkoxide which may then react with CO2. The concept originated from combining guanidine with aliphatic alcohols to promote reaction with CO2. In the molecules in Figure , both functions are combined in one. The ring structure surrounding the guanidine lends stability against hydrolysis to the guanidinium ion generated in CO2 binding.

Example CO2BOL molecules combining the guanidine and alcohol functionality in single molecules.

As already noted, high viscosity is the common hurdle for water-lean absorbents which also applies to CO2BOLs. To avoid this Malhotra et al. used chemical synthesis and computer modeling to study how hydrogen bonding, degrees of freedom, and cation/anion charge solvation affected absorbent viscosity. They found that intramolecular hydrogen bonding leads to lower viscosity under CO2 loaded conditions and studied which components of a molecule led to which kind of hydrogen bonding. Using this approach, they developed the molecule 1-MEIPADM-2-BOL (Figure ) which has 60% lower viscosity than the original 1-PADM-2-BOL at 25% CO2 loading, reduced from 171 cP to 75 cP. CO2 uptake for the molecules was 9.0 and 7.3 wt.% (44 and 35.5 mol % respectively). The absorption capacity of CO2BOLs is similar to amine-based absorbents but high regeneration temperatures are required for conventional thermal regeneration (∼159 °C).

A feature of CO2BOLs is that they can operate in thermal swing or polarity swing assisted mode. , Adding an antisolvent like hexadecane can destabilize the ions and thereby remove CO2 from the absorbent. This can reduce the required temperature for regeneration from 159 to 86 °C with a theoretical minimum of 65 °C. The lower desorption operating temperature when using polarity swing could also reduce absorbent attrition due to thermal degradation, although thermal and oxidative stability of CO2BOLs is not well investigated.

Biphasic absorbents also represent a water-lean type of absorbent as they have only a small amount of or no water content. They consist of multiple components, typically amines, where at least one has low polarity. Upon absorption of CO2 phase separation occurs into an organic and an aqueous or polar phase, with ionic species bound to CO2 residing in the aqueous/polar phase. The CO2 containing phase is then processed for CO2 stripping leading to reduced energy requirements as the CO2 content has been concentrated in a smaller amount of material. In some instances it is a solid phase that forms with CO2 containing ion pairs precipitating out of solution.

Aqueous amine biphasic absorbents generally consist of aqueous solutions of sparingly soluble amines with more organic character or lower polarity. Upon reaction with CO2 the resulting ionic species of protonated amine, carbamate and bicarbonate are insoluble in the neutral amines and phase separation occurs with the ions favoring the aqueous phase. Examples include aqueous binary mixtures of 3-(methylamino) propylamine (MAPA, Figure ) or dibutylamine (DBA) with N,N-diethlylethanolamine (DEEA, Figure ) which form 2 phases upon reaction with CO2. It may be that in aqueous solution the neutral amines tend to form clusters solvated by water, but as the ionic strength increases following reaction with CO2 these clusters break down and phase separation occurs.

Nonaqueous biphasic absorbents operate according to the same principle as aqueous ones but with water replaced by an alcohol or another organic compound. The compounds used are similar to those used for water-lean absorbents, although long chain alcohols are favored such as 1-heptanol or 1-octanol to facilitate phase separation. Smaller alcohols tend to result in a solid–liquid phase transition. For the longer chain alcohols, the CO2 products form a separate ionic-liquid like phase with the alcohol in the second phase. Both single amines and mixtures of those commonly used in aqueous solution have been used. N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) has also been used with AMP to form a solid–liquid biphasic absorbent.

Thermomorphic biphasic absorbents are a unique form of biphasic absorbent in which phase separation occurs as a function of CO2 absorption and temperature. − These absorbents consist of aqueous solutions of relatively nonpolar amines (not alkanolamines) that only have limited and weak molecular interactions with water at room temperature. Some examples are shown in Figure . The interactions are strong enough to result in a homogeneous solution at room temperature. Upon heating these molecular interactions break down and phase separation occurs. When CO2 is introduced the resulting ions favor the aqueous phase upon phase separation in a similar manner to the aqueous biphasic absorbents already mentioned. These absorbents and the thermomorphic phase change behavior is very sensitive to the composition which would make process operation a challenge and somewhat more complex than a typical process.

The Role of Thermodynamic Properties in Process Performance

Enthalpy of Absorption

The total energy requirement for CO2 desorption, q tot (kJ/g CO2), in a desorption column is made up of 3 terms. 6 qtot=|qabs|+qsens+qvap

In this equation, q abs, is the heat of absorption of CO2, which must be returned in the stripper to drive the reverse, endothermic CO2 desorption reaction. The second term, q sens, is the sensible heat energy required to raise the temperature of the absorbent exiting the cross-flow heat exchanger to the stripper temperature. The size of this term is exacerbated by the fact that the cross-flow heat exchanger is cool-side pinched: the thermal mass of the cool, rich absorbent is greater than that of the lean, hot absorbent, as the former contains more CO2. Hence, there is insufficient energy in the hot stream to heat the cool, rich absorbent to stripper temperature. When combined with the finite approach temperatures at the cool side of the cross-flow heat exchanger, it is not uncommon for the rich absorbent entering the stripper to be 15 °C below stripper temperature. The third term, q vap, is the latent heat of vaporisation of any steam which exits with CO2 at the top of the stripper and must subsequently be condensed.

A great deal of absorbent development work has focused solely on reducing q abs, but this is often misguided, as the 3 terms of eq are interdependent. Liquid absorbent-based CCS processes are classically temperature-swing processes, and are driven by a shift in reaction equilibrium with temperature. CO2 absorption is exothermic, so Le Chatellier’s principle implies that as the temperature is increased, reaction equilibria will shift to drive CO2 out of solution. Quantitatively, if the enthalpy of absorption, ΔH abs, is independent of temperature, eq , which can be derived from the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation, implies that the change in CO2 partial pressure with temperature for an absorbent at constant composition is directly related to ΔH abs. 7 ln(pCO2(T2)pCO2(T1))=ΔHabsR(1T2−1T1)

Reducing q abs reduces one term in eq , but according to eq it also makes the CO2 partial pressure less sensitive to temperature swings. This has several knock-on effects. First, it tends to decrease the cyclic loading, αrich - αlean, which increases the liquid circulation rate and in turn q sens. Second, it tends to reduce the partial pressure of CO2 exiting the top of the stripper. For example, according to eq , the partial pressure of CO2 in an aqueous K2CO3 solution (q abs = −35 kJ/mol) will only increase 10-fold between 40 and 110 °C. If the partial pressure of the CO2 in the gas entering the absorber at 40 °C is 5 kPa, the partial pressure of CO2 leaving the stripper at 110 °C is thermodynamically capped at 50 kPa (and in reality will be less due to the presence of finite driving forces). If q abs = −80 kJ/mol (as is the case for MEA) p CO2 can increase up to 400-fold over the same temperature range. The lowering of p CO2 in the stripper typically leads to an increase in the steam mole fraction at the top, increasing q vap. It also precludes the use of high-pressure stripper designs, which rely on very large shifts in CO2 partial pressure to produce high-pressure CO2 streams, reducing downstream compression duties.

The question of identifying the thermodynamically optimal enthalpy of absorption is highly contested, and has been discussed in multiple studies. ,,− While it is tempting to imagine that a purely thermodynamic study could identify an optimal absorbent, practical engineering constraints (and the assumptions that are made to deal with them) tend to have an outsized influence on the ‘optimal’ properties predicted by these studies. For example, many amines have stability issues above 130 °C, but the relative stability of piperazine combined with the advanced flash stripper design (which reduces the stripper’s residence time) allows stripper temperatures to exceed 150 °C. Studies which consider the possibility of such high temperature stripper operation typically recommend more exothermic absorbents in order to fully exploit the large temperature swing, while studies which consider more moderate stripper temperatures typically find smaller optimal values for q abs .

Of course, the ‘thermodynamically optimal absorbent’ is a mathematical idealization, and any physical instantiation will have practical drawbacks. While piperazine’s stability and reasonably large ΔH abs (along with its excellent kinetics) are strengths, it can experience issues with crystallization at lean CO2 loadings in the absorber. This places a lower limit on the CO2 partial pressure achievable in the gas leaving the absorber, making it more challenging to use for high capture rates (e.g., 99% capture) or more dilute flue gases, unless engineering modifications are made to avoid or tolerate crystallization. Similarly, MEA combines a large ΔH abs with a very low CO2 partial pressure at moderate loadings (e.g., 5 M MEA can draw CO2 from air at 40 °C when the loading is less than 0.3 mol CO2/mol MEA). From a thermodynamic perspective it would be a natural candidate for direct air capture applications, however due to its high volatility extensive post-treatment of the scrubbed flue gas is required in this application

Thermodynamics of Nonaqueous Absorbents

Many nonaqueous absorbents have been investigated for carbon capture, including ionic liquids, CO2 binding organic liquids, and deep eutectic absorbents. , Nonaqueous systems have several potential advantages over aqueous systems. First, as discussed above, a traditional stripper using an aqueous absorbent effectively acts as kettle with its lid open: it is constantly boiling water from the absorbent, and releasing it as steam mixed with the product CO2 stream. This process of generating steam and immediately condensing it wastes a lot of energy, leading to the parasitic q vap term in eq above. In principle, nonaqueous absorbents could be used to produce a pure CO2 stream directly from the stripper, eliminating or significantly reducing this term. Second, many nonaqueous absorbents have heat capacities roughly half that of water, reducing q sens. Third, nonaqueous absorbents can exhibit high physical CO2 solubilities, potentially improving absorption kinetics.

In practice, these potential advantages can be difficult to realize. Many nonaqueous systems suffer from high viscosity, leading to larger absorber sizes (due to both poor hydrodynamics and poor mass transfer rates, see Section ). Furthermore, efficient steam-free stripping processes are difficult to design. Steam plays two roles in a conventional stripper: it facilitates heat transfer along the column, and it also displaces CO2 from the gas phase, reducing p CO 2 and assisting the desorption of CO2 from the fluid. If steam is not used for stripping, the partial pressure of CO2 at the bottom of the column will equal the stripper pressure, requiring higher temperatures to obtain a given lean loading compared with a conventional aqueous system, where (due to displacement by steam) p CO 2 at the bottom of the stripper is typically 10 to 100x lower than the stripper pressure. On the other hand, if steam is directly contacted with the absorbent, entrainment of steam with the CO2 stream again becomes an issue, and the water fraction in the absorbent must be managed. Furthermore, modern stripper designs utilizing a cool-rich bypass allow 60–80% recovery of the parasitic q vap term, reducing the urgency of eliminating these losses. , That said, recent analyses of the nonaqueous absorbent 2-EEMPA suggest that efficient stripping can be conducted while maintaining small quantities of water (<2 wt %), potentially providing the promised benefits of reduced heat capacity, reduced steam vaporisation, and improved wetting.

The Role of Kinetics and Physical Properties

Absorber Design and Performance

The absorption column is the largest unit operation within a CCS process, and contributes roughly a quarter of the total process CAPEX. The engineer’s goal in designing an absorber is to minimize its size (reducing CAPEX) and gas-phase pressure drop (reducing blower duties) while achieving a specified CO2 capture rate (e.g., 95% CO2 removal). The size of the absorption column is a function of (1) the superficial velocities of the gas and liquid phases, (2) the rate of CO2 absorption per unit volume of packing, and (3) the size of supporting equipment such as liquid redistributors and intercoolers. In setting the gas and liquid velocities, care must be taken to avoid “flooding,” in which the absorber is transformed into a fluidized bed with gas, liquid and (in the worst case) column internals spouting out the top. Somewhat unintuitively, as a rule the gas and liquid superficial velocities are set as high as is safetypically at 60–80% of the flooding velocities (their velocity ratio is fixed by the cyclic absorbent capacity, αrich – αlean, which depends on how the stripper is operated). Operation at faster gas and liquid velocities leads to taller, narrower columns. This increases the pressure drop (a significant downside) but minimizes the column’s area footprint, simplifies access to column internals, and facilitates more uniform liquid flow. Increased absorbent viscosity leads to reduced superficial liquid and gas velocities, increasing the column’s cross-sectional area and the overall CAPEX.

As fluid velocities are predominantly a function of fluid hydrodynamics, the most significant lever for reducing absorber size is increasing the rate of CO2 absorption per unit volume of packing. The local volumetric absorption rate depends on the gas–liquid interfacial area per unit volume of column, a w , the overall mass transfer coefficient, K G , and the driving force, which is most easily expressed as the difference between the gas-phase CO2 partial pressure, p CO2 (p,y CO 2 ), and the CO2 partial pressure at equilibrium with the liquid at its current temperature and CO2 loading, p CO2 eq(T,α): 13 Rate[mol CO2m3s]=KGaw(pCO2−pCO2eq) CO2 absorption can be thought of as a resistance-in-series problem, in which the overall resistance to mass transfer, 1/K G , is the sum of the resistance CO2 molecules encounter in the gas phase and in the liquid phase: 14 1KG=1kG+1HEkL where k G and k L are the gas- and liquid-side mass transfer coefficients, H is the Henry’s constant, and E is an enhancement factor, which accounts for the effect of chemical reactions on the gas flux. For most aqueous amines, p CO2 eq ≪ p CO2 throughout most of the absorber, so improving kinetics is largely a matter of increasing the gas–liquid area, a w and the overall mass transfer coefficient, K G .

Wetted Area, Pressure Drop, and Fluid Properties

The most important factor determining the gas–liquid interfacial specific surface area, a w , is the specific area of the structured or random packing filling the column, a, which varies from ∼ 100 m2/m3 (e.g., traditional random packings) to ∼ 1000 m2/m3 (achievable with modern, structured packings). Increasing a increases a w (they are often roughly proportional) but it also decreases the flooding velocities (leading to shorter, wider columns more susceptible to liquid maldistribution) and increases the gas-phase pressure drop. The importance of minimizing pressure drop may be seen via a simple back-of-the-envelope calculation. The minimum energy required to isothermally compress a 40 °C ideal gas stream from 100 to 110 kPa is 15 Wmin=RTln⁡p2/p1=250J/mol

If the stream contains 5 mol % CO2, this equates to roughly 5 kJ/mol CO2; an actual blower is likely to use ∼ 30% more work than this minimum limit. For comparison, a state-of-the-art CCS process has total equivalent work requirements (by equivalent work requirements, we mean, for example, the net loss in electrical output from a power plant due to addition of a CCS process) of 30–40 kJ/mol CO2. Hence, it is a critical design constraint that absorber pressure drops not exceed ∼ 10 kPa.

The relationship between the packing area and the pressure drop is well described by the Generalized Pressure Drop Correlation (GPDC) shown in Figure (a). This correlation has one packing-dependent parameter, the ‘packing factor’ F p ; a lower F p means a lower pressure drop, all else being equal. Historically, this correlation evolved from flooding point correlations of Walker and Sherwood themselves derived from the Darcy-Weisbach friction factor correlation. In these early correlations, F p was given explicitly by a/ε 3, where ε is the void fraction of the packing. As packings were improved, lower pressure drops could be achieved for a given value of a/ε 3, and the a/ε 3 term was replaced with the packing-specific term, F p . As shown in Figure (b), the progression from Raschig Rings (‘generation 1 packings’) to Pall rings (‘generation 2 packings’) to modern random packings such as IMTP or CMR (‘generation 3 packings’) resulted in a significant decrease in F p , plateauing around F p ∼ 0.5a/ε 3. Modern structured packings, such as Mellapak X and Y designs, can achieve packing factors as low as F p ∼ 0.1a/ε 3 (Figure (c)).

Reactive Mass Transfer

The field of reactive interfacial mass transfer is broad and deep, and we cannot begin to do justice to it here. Classic texts include those of Sherwood, Astarita and Danckwerts, and simplified accounts may be found in Levenspiel and Bird, Stewart and Lightfoot. In this review, we will only sketch the fundamentals, and discuss how they apply to CCS.

There exist two broad classes of absorbents for carbon capture: chemical absorbents (which react with CO2) and physical absorbents (which do not). Chemical absorbents have higher CO2 capacities (because CO2 is dissolved physically and stored in chemically bound form) and faster CO2 kinetics because the reactive consumption of CO2 enhances mass transfer at the gas–liquid interface. Physical absorbent processes (e.g., the Rectisol and Selexol processes) get around these kinetic and thermodynamic limitations by operating the absorber at high pressure: this is reasonable for, e.g., CO2 separation from a pressurized natural gas stream, but is uneconomical for low pressure applications (see Section ).

One approach to predicting mass transfer rates in absorption columns is to directly simulate the unsteady, gravity driven, multiphysics, two-phase flows occurring in these systems. Unsurprisingly, this is computationally difficult. For example, the dynamic model of Singh et al. of a 15 cm tall, bench-scale absorber took 3 days to run on 72 cores. Fortunately, dimensional scaling arguments have been used for many decades to develop relatively robust correlations for mass transfer coefficients for physical absorbents. For example, Onda et al. reported that the liquid-side mass transfer coefficient into a physical absorbent is given by 17 kL=0.0051(ρLμLg)−1/3(LawμL)2/3(μLρLDL)−1/2(atDp)0.4 where D L is the diffusivity of the absorbed gas in the liquid, and D p is a characteristic packing size. Dozens of k L and k G correlations have been developed, as they are typically estimated by dividing measured values of K G a w by estimated values of a w , they inherit the uncertainty found in a w correlations (Figure ), and the same warnings apply.

Chemical reactions typically increase the rate of gas absorption. Diffusive mass transfer is inherently a dynamic, two-way process: during absorption, CO2 molecules move randomly about in all directions according to the laws of Brownian motion, and the net CO2 flux is the sum of all these random movements. When CO2 reacts with and is bound to liquid-phase molecules, the dynamic balance of these Brownian motions is altered: the bound CO2 molecules are ‘removed’ from the process, and are no longer able to randomly diffuse back into the gas phase. This increases the net flux of CO2 into the fluid, often by a factor of 100 or more. This is clearly seen in the steepening of the CO2 concentration profiles between physical and chemical absorbents, due entirely to the reactive consumption of CO2 (Figure ). If gas-side mass transfer resistance is ignored, the Enhancement Factor, E, equals the ratio of the flux in a chemical absorbent to the flux in a hypothetical physical absorbent under the same physical and hydrodynamic conditions, but with the reaction ‘switched off’. Mathematically, it is defined such that the flux into a chemical absorbent is given by 20 J=EkL(cCO2i−HpCO2eq) where k L is given by physical absorbent correlations such as eq . The enhancement factor may be estimated from idealized mass transfer theories, such as the film theory, surface renewal theory, penetration theory or eddy-diffusivity theory. In each of these theories, the complex dynamics of two-phase flow are reduced to an idealized dynamic process with a single, tunable hydrodynamic parameter. Knowledge of k L (from correlations such as eq ) allows the tunable parameter to be calculated, and then the flux into a chemical absorbent with arbitrary kinetics may then be calculated using the idealized model. For example, in the film theory, the liquid phase is modeled as a thin, stationary film of width δ (the model’s hydrodynamic parameter) sitting on top of a well-mixed bulk fluid. If no reactions occur, Fick’s law predicts that the flux of gas equals: 21 J=kL(cCO2i−HpCO2eq)=DCO2δ(cCO2i−HpCO2eq)→δ=DCO2kL

After calculating δ via a k L correlation, the complex problem of dynamic, multiphase reactive mass transfer may be exchanged for a much simpler problem: 1-dimensional diffusion with reaction in a stagnant film of width δ. Note that, even when analyzing a chemical absorbent, it is the concentration profile of physically dissolved CO2, c CO 2 , which determines the flux. Chemical reactions simply alter the shape of this profile.

The same process is followed in other theories. The penetration theory treats absorption as if the fluid near the surface is stationary and undergoing an unsteady 1-dimensional diffusion-reaction process, such that every τ seconds the concentrations in the fluid are returned to the bulk concentration. τ may be related to k L in the nonreactive case, then the model may be numerically solved with reactions added back in to calculate E. The surface renewal theory is identical, except that regular, evenly spaced renewals are replaced by stochastic renewals governed by a Poisson process with some frequency s (the tunable parameter). The eddy-diffusivity theory incorporates an extra parameter associated with the rate of turbulent eddy diffusion, which again can be estimated from physical absorbent data and then applied to estimating E in a chemical absorbent. ,

Though all these theories are superficially different, they often predict very similar gas fluxes. Fundamentally, this is because they each capture the expected asymptotic behavior in the limit as the governing dimensionless groups become very big or very small. The most important dimensionless groups in reactive mass transfer are the Hatta number and E i , the enhancement factor in the limit of infinitely fast chemical kinetics. For a second order reaction, 25 CO2+R→P the Hatta number (Ha) is given by 26 Ha2=k2cRbulkDCO2kL2 where c R bulk is the bulk concentration of the liquid-phase reactant, and k 2 is the second order rate constant. For a first-order reaction eq applies. 27 Ha2=kDCO2kL2

A second-order reaction approaches first order behavior when c R ≈ c R bulk everywhere; under these conditions, the system is said to behave in a pseudo-first order manner with rate constant k = k 2 c R bulk.

The Hatta number can be expressed as δ/δ r , where δ is the typical concentration boundary layer width for a physical absorbent (eq ) and δr=DCO2/k is the typical distance a CO2 molecule will travel into the chemical absorbent before it is consumed (Figure ). Alternatively, it may also be expressed as (τ/τ r )2, where τ = D CO 2 /k L 2 is a characteristic time scale associated with the hydrodynamic flow (e.g., the time between surface renewals in the penetration theory) and τ r = 1/k is the characteristic time for the first-order diffusion-reaction problem to settle to steady-state. In an absorbent with first-order (or pseudo-first order) kinetics, a large Hatta number implies that the absorbents’ flux is independent of the hydrodynamics, as the concentration gradient at the gas–liquid interface is determined entirely by a diffusion-reaction process that occurs on length-scales much shorter (and time-scales much faster) than the fluid flow. As far as the CO2 molecules are concerned, they are diffusing into a stationary fluid – they react so fast and over such short a length-scale that fluid motion does not influence the mass transfer rate.

In a fluid with second-order kinetics, a second dimensionless group, E i , also plays a critical role. This is defined as 28 Ei=1+cRbulkDRcCO2DCO2 where D R is the diffusivity of the liquid-phase reactant. E i quantifies the relative ease with which CO2 can diffuse from the surface to the bulk as compared with the rate at which reactive molecules in the fluid can diffuse from the bulk to the surface. If E i ∼ 1, the CO2 flux will be determined by how quickly reactive molecules can diffuse to the surface to react, while if E i ≫ 1 getting reactive molecules to the surface is easy, and the rate of CO2 diffusion and reaction in the vicinity of the interface will determine the CO2 flux.

Increasing the fluid’s motion affects the flux into a chemical absorbent if either E i ∼ 1 (mixing helps move reactants to the surface) or if Ha ≪ E i (in which case fluid motion helps move CO2 into the bulk). Nonaqueous Absorbents

CO2 is a hydrophobic molecule: the equilibrium concentration of CO2(aq) in 30 wt % MEA exposed to the flue gas from a natural gas combined cycle turbine is <1 mM. As discussed above, the low CO2(aq) solubility limits the kinetics of these absorbents, as even in a chemical absorbent the mass transfer rate is proportional to the concentration gradient of CO2(aq) at the gas–liquid interface, and the flux typically scales in proportion to the Henry’s constant, H (see, e.g., eq ). For this reason, it is plausible that organic, less polar absorbents with larger Henry’s constants for CO2 could have faster absorption rates even if their intrinsic reaction kinetics were slower than those of aqueous amines (as discussed in Section , under some conditions nonaqueous absorbents may also have intrinsic thermodynamic advantages). This hypothesis is borne out in the data on CO2BOLs, which, under favorable conditions, can exhibit mass transfer rates superior to those seen in aqueous amines.

However, the large CO2(aq) solubility in nonaqueous absorbents leads to unexpected mass transfer behavior. Most notably, CO2BOLs typically exhibit a decrease in K G with increasing temperature, even as reaction kinetics and species diffusivities increase (Figure ). This behavior is driven by a shift in reaction equilibria: at low temperatures (<30 °C), CO2BOLs react relatively irreversibly with CO2, but at moderate temperatures (∼40–60 °C) the equilibrium extent of the reaction decreases substantially, reducing the enhancement factor, E, and the mass transfer rate. The decrease in the physical solubility with temperature also contributes to this behavior, and can lead to similar phenomena in aqueous amines at higher temperatures.

As emphasized in Section , the rational development of novel chemistries for nonaqueous absorbents cannot be divorced from practical chemical engineering concerns. As a rule, low viscosity is preferrable (it improves mass transfer and process hydrodynamic performance), as are fast reaction kinetics and increased physical CO2 solubility. But the optimal enthalpy of absorption, and the optimal temperature at which p CO2 eq shifts from <5 kPa (absorbing regime) to >50 kPa (stripping regime) at intermediate CO2 loadings (a function of the enthalpy and entropy of absorption) can only be assessed in concert with engineering analyses.

Conclusions

The development of reactive chemical liquid absorbents for carbon capture applications is a uniquely multifaceted problem. A balance must be found between mass transfer, absorption capacity, thermodynamic behavior and reversibility, process emissions and stability during operation. We have outlined how these properties relate to the performance of a CO2 capture process and provided an overview of the state-of-the-art in terms of molecules being investigated for this application. We have also highlighted that these properties cannot be considered in isolation. Reasonable performance in all these aspects is necessary for an absorbent to be considered for large scale deployment.

Amines are the dominant chemical species used for carbon capture due to the unique affinity and reversibility of the reaction between nucleophilic amino groups and the electrophilic carbon of CO2. These take a few flavours with aqueous amines being dominant as both single components and in formulations. Water-lean/nonaqueous and phase change absorbents are emerging as having the potential to offer performance improvements but also pose their own challenges in terms of requiring different operating conditions. It has also been noted that when considering large scale deployment long-term stability of an absorbent is a crucial factor. It can be difficult to completely assess this stability in a laboratory environment, and it is often only through long duration piloting, with exposure to the myriad components that are present in an imperfect industrial gas stream, that stability can be fully assessed.

There is still significant scope for improvement in the chemical and physical properties of absorbents for carbon capture applications. A great deal of fundamental information about absorbent properties now exists, and this is being augmented by experience from long duration process operation. The key is relating and understanding the interplay between the fundamental properties of an absorbent (chemistry) and how it will behave in a process (chemical engineering) through both experimentation and modeling approaches, and feedback from real world experience. This allows for improvements to be realized from both sides of the problem, the chemical behavior and process design and operation.
