Examining the impact of naming equity within perinatal substance use and mental health policy: a scoping review
Authors: Sana Z Shahram, Lisa Knox, Megan Black, Alex Kent, Bernie Pauly, Lenora Marcellus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ways in which we understand and respond to perinatal substance use are explicitly and implicitly driven by policy. Perinatal substance use is a visible manifestation of intersecting systems of oppression, shaped by racist, colonial and sexist discourses surrounding mothering and drug use. Policies related to perinatal substance use and mental health have far-reaching impacts on families, communities and generations. This scoping review examined the conceptualization and operationalization of equity within foundational perinatal policies in one Canadian province, British Columbia (BC), between 2005 and 2025. Documents were included if they were: (1) provincial-level policy documents that either (a) guided perinatal service delivery generally or (b) focused on mental health promotion, prevention of mental disorders or harms of substance use; (2) publicly available; and (3) published within the study timeframe. Inductive content analysis and sensitizing questions were used to explore how equity concepts were conceptualized and integrated. In total, 30 documents met inclusion criteria. Equity was mentioned in 15, but only 4 provided explicit definitions. Often, implied proxy terms for equity were substituted to discuss equity considerations, such as risk, disadvantage, social determinants of health, vulnerable, marginalized and harm reduction, with limited direction on public health system roles or accountability in addressing root causes of inequities. Content analysis yielded the following missed opportunities in the results: an absence of clear definitions; focus on accessibility; inattention to structural conditions; and incoherent concepts of equity. Given BC’s reputation as a leader in equity-oriented perinatal substance use policy, these gaps are notable. In the context of an expanding drug poisoning crisis and increasing global recognition of equity as foundational to public health, it is imperative to examine how equity is understood and actioned in policy to strengthen alignment between intentions and outcomes.Keywords: Equity, Perinatal, Substance use, Scoping review, Policy

Introduction

Addressing and attending to persisting gaps in health outcomes and access to quality healthcare within and between populations worldwide is an urgent ethical, social and economic imperative for governments and health systems [1–4]. Accordingly, governmental health policies and health sector mandates are increasingly concerned with reducing health inequities and promoting health equity as an important focus and goal of health system activities. Health inequities are avoidable and unjust, arising from systemic barriers that prevent some groups from enjoying their full health potential on the basis of gender, age, race, ethnicity, class, socioeconomic status and/or other socially determined circumstances [5]. In contrast, health equity requires that all people have the access, power, resources and opportunities that create the conditions for optimal health, as measured by the most advantaged in society [6]. For our purposes, we align with a three-part definition of equity offered by Plamondon & Shahram [7], where equity is defined as: an idea, reflective of normative discourses about justice; a benchmark, reflective of society’s aspirations; and, an experience, both individual and communal [8]. This definition recognizes equity as a dynamic relational concept, whereby the determinants of equity are defined as the distribution of power, resources and opportunities. Within this conceptualization of equity and its determinants, then, policies, both formal and informal, which shape and are shaped by the distribution of power, resources and opportunities within health systems are also implicated in and of themselves as a driving determinant of equity within health systems [8].

Despite growing international commitments to health equity, recent years have seen a troubling rise in political movements – particularly in the United States and parts of Europe – that are increasingly hostile to equity-focused work. Far-right political agendas have gained traction, often accompanied by rhetoric that undermines social justice and policies that defund or delegitimize efforts to address systemic inequities. These developments risk eroding hard-won progress in public health and threaten to normalize regressive policy shifts. In this sociopolitical climate, the need for clear, actionable and evidence-driven equity policy becomes even more urgent, not only to advance justice but also to safeguard it from retrenchment.

Mental health and substance use are areas where inequities are readily visible, shaped by the intersecting impacts of poverty, colonialism and gender-based violence – structural conditions that disproportionately burden pregnant and parenting people and undermine their access to care, stability and support. These inequities are further entrenched through the confluence of neoliberal, capitalist, racist, sexist and colonial policies that intertwine with drug use in ways that work to exacerbate existing inequities by perpetuating surveillance, stigma and exclusion rather than healing and justice. In the context of population and public health, there has been an enduring focus on attending to mental health and substance use issues as urgent priorities, not only for advancing equity, but also, in the past decade, as essential components of responding to ongoing and persistent related crises such as drug poisoning, housing, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and other interrelated issues that reach across health and social systems [9]. Research indicates that between 1% and 5% of pregnant people report using unregulated drugs during pregnancy [10], with some estimates as high as 9% [11]. These figures are likely underestimated owing to the potential consequences of disclosure within health and social care systems that have historically marginalized people who use substances. In this context, substance use during pregnancy must be understood not only as an individual health issue but also as the outcome of social and structural inequities such as intergenerational trauma, racism, colonialism, intimate partner violence, sexism, mental health challenges, poverty and homelessness that disproportionately shape risks for both pregnant people and their infants [10, 11–14].

Bridging this with the similarly enduring life course approach to the field, where interventions within the perinatal period and early years are recognized to offer staggering and compounding returns [15, 16] on investment for improving both equity and health and social outcomes [4, 17, 18], it follows that the policy context of perinatal substance use and mental health offers a powerful window through which to interrogate the operationalization of equity within public health systems. Moreover, because of the way the harms related to perinatal substance use and mental health issues reverberate across communities and generations, improving policy in this topical area also offers similarly compounding and interrelated impacts across health and social systems.

The public health system has an important leadership role to play in reducing health inequities and promoting health equity. In Canada, responsibility for healthcare lies with the 10 provinces and three territories, which fund and deliver most services without cost to individuals. These governments set priorities for local public health programming and decide on funding allocations. The establishment of public health standards within each jurisdiction has been shaped by contextual influences such as legislation, historical developments, leadership approaches and jurisdictional goals and mandates [19]. Canada is one of the signatories on the 2011 Rio Declaration asserting the role of health systems in reorienting their work towards addressing equity [3]. In British Columbia (BC), Canada, the Ministry of Health’s Framework for Core Functions in Public Health clearly outlines that “public health has a duty, as one of its fundamental tasks, to work to reduce inequalities in health” (p. 48) [20]. For the public health system, as well as other government and nongovernmental organizations, formal documents and policies can be seen as “essential tools that guide thinking and action in relation to establishing authority and processes as well as framing the problem, making recommendations and tracking implementation” (p. 4) [3]. Therefore, document analysis of governmental policies can be employed as a way to understand dominant values, discourses and discursive dynamics that influence the uptake and prioritization of (or lack thereof) health equity in perinatal public health services and programs.

In 2012, Pinto et al. extended similar logic to conduct a qualitative analysis of policies to understand the implementation of equity in public health standards in two Canadian provinces. This paper extends their approach to a scoping review of BC’s provincial perinatal policies to understand if and how key equity concepts are applied and operationalized within the policy landscape of perinatal health and perinatal substance use and mental health. Recognizing that pregnancy represents a window of opportunity for upstream intervention, with impacts across populations and generations, it is an important policy space within which to get these foundational and guiding documents right, to build coherence and impacts that are likely to generate the outcomes intended. It was within this rich and aspirational policy environment that we conducted this study, with the aim of recognizing the ways in which we might best convert intentions into evidence-based action and, ultimately, lasting impact.

Study context

This study is part of The Equity Lens in Public Health (ELPH) program of research that commenced in 2011 to study the application of a health equity lens in BC health authorities [21]. Within Canada, BC is a leader in formalized equity-oriented approaches to public health. This leadership is reflected in a range of provincial policies and guidelines developed over the past two decades, which have aimed to embed equity considerations into perinatal mental health and substance use services across the province’s public health system. The BC Ministry of Health released the BC Guiding Framework for Public Health in 2017, refreshed in 2024, as a strategic framework outlining public health strategies and core programs to address populations and inequity lenses including: health improvement; prevention of disease, injury and illness; environmental health; and public health emergency management [22]. In the 2024 refresh, maternal child health is addressed under the priority “best start in life”, with a call to build “foundations for good health by reducing unfair disadvantages for pregnant people, children and families” through the advancement of a set of interrelated core public health functions and enabling functions [23]. “Population mental health and wellness”, and “prevention and reduction of substance-related harms” are two other identified priorities in the framework, with a focus on perinatal substance use and mental health effectively encompassing half of the framework’s six priority areas. Building from the “population” and “equity” lenses that preceded this most recent iteration, this framework now applies three foundational principles as lenses across the framework, including: (1) truth, rights and reconciliation; (2) health equity and antiracism; and (3) system capacity. Under the health equity and antiracism lens, the goal of this principle is described as systems-level work “to eliminate preventable health disparities so that no one is left behind”. The framework emphasizes that this is accomplished by influencing the social, ecological and structural determinants of health.

Materials and methods

Study design

This scoping review was driven by the research question: How is the application of an equity lens conceptualized and operationalized within foundational and guiding policies related to mental health promotion, prevention of mental disorders and harms of substance use within perinatal programs in BC?

Scoping reviews offer a systematic approach for rapidly collecting and summarizing the available published documents to provide a snapshot of the breadth, depth and nature of research and/or policy activity on a given topic; but, unlike literature reviews or systematic reviews, scoping reviews do not assess the quality or validity of included sources [24]. We conducted a scoping review to systematically examine key perinatal documents in BC from a 20-year period (2005–2025) to understand the context shaping attitudes towards the role of health equity in supporting pregnant and parenting women1 and their families, with a focus on substance use and mental health. The review focused specifically on foundational provincial-level perinatal systems documents and their supporting collaborative models related to mental health promotion, and the prevention of mental disorders and harms of substance use. This timeframe was intentionally selected to capture policy developments influenced by BC’s Core Functions Framework and related equity-oriented public health reforms.

Data collection

Drawing inspiration from Pinto and colleagues’ “Equity in public health standards: a qualitative document analysis of policies from two Canadian provinces” [19], we adopted a collaborative approach to data collection, engaging both academic and public health partners in an iterative, inductive content analysis of foundational documents that articulate the goals, visions, mandates and strategic direction of perinatal care in BC. Our comprehensive search and selection strategy focused on provincial-level perinatal system documents and supporting service delivery frameworks published between 2005 and 2025. This date range was intentionally selected to capture the influence of the core functions framework in BC. With the support of a regional health authority librarian, we initially conducted a grey literature search using Google to identify targeted documents that guide perinatal service delivery, either generally or specifically in relation to mental health and substance use. We used the following search terms: “Perinatal” OR “maternal” AND “British Columbia” OR “policy” OR “guidelines” OR “services” AND “PDF”. Sample

The initial Google grey literature search yielded 73 records after removing duplicates. Over the course of the study, eight additional records were identified for consideration through key informants and citation searching (Fig. 1). After screening the records for eligibility, 51 records were excluded, to produce a final sample of 30 documents. The included documents were subsequently categorized as either (a) a foundational document that guides perinatal service delivery generally (n = 14) or (b) a document that guides perinatal service delivery in relation to mental health promotion, prevention of mental disorders and harms of substance use as its primary focus (n = 16) (see Appendix A for a list of included documents). Materials comprised evidence reviews, practice and policy frameworks, program papers, clinical care pathways and clinical guidelines intended for healthcare providers in public health and acute care settings.

To adapt their analysis for our research purposes, probing questions were adapted to reflect the different scope of the research (e.g. removing any mention of Ontario) and to directly align our analysis with our three-part definition of equity (see Table 1 for adapted probes that were used to review the data set).

We employed constant comparative analysis drawing on grounded theory methodology to achieve higher levels of abstraction and uncover sociocultural and structural influences through multiple readings [19, 26]. Specifically, documents were read, reread and coded in three stages of increasingly nuanced questions related to our threefold definition of equity [19]. Prior to coding and analysis, an initial readthrough of included documents was conducted by a single researcher (M.B., L.K. or A.K.) to obtain a general sense of health equity discourses in perinatal health policy documents in BC. After the initial readthrough, M.B., A.K. or L.K. systematically applied the adapted three-stage questions and constant comparative analysis. During the second readthrough (stage 1, “equity as an idea”), M.B., A.K. and L.K. examined whether and how the terms “equity” or “inequity” were referenced, whether any definitions were provided that identified proxy2 language for equity and whether novel or unclear interpretations of equity were introduced that departed from ideas typically associated with equity and/or our prescribed definition. The third readthrough (stage 2, “equity as a benchmark”) focused on whether advancing equity was identified as an explicit objective, examining links to social justice, structural determinants, recommended actions and the presence of accountability mechanisms. Finally, the fourth readthrough (stage 3, “equity as experienced”) explored how equity intersected with concepts such as accessibility, references to specific priority populations and whether certain standards or programs neglected to address equity altogether [19]. Missed opportunities reviewed contexts where structural conditions were identified, but their impact on inequities was not further interrogated. The full set of guiding questions used in each stage of analysis is summarized in Table 1. At each stage, the analytical process was documented through coding the documents on the basis of the guiding questions; recording memos to reflect on coding decisions, questions and emerging ideas; and organizing notes in a data extraction table for synthesis and interpretation.

Results

There were 30 provincial level policy documents included and reviewed in the analysis. Over half (n = 16) of the documents were focused on perinatal service delivery in relation to mental health promotion, prevention of mental disorders and harms of substance use; the remaining 14 were general documents that guided prenatal service delivery.

A variety of terms were used to discuss health equity, including “equity, equities, equitable, equal, equality, inequity, inequities, inequality, inequalities, inequitable”. Equity was explicitly mentioned in 16 of the 30 documents, and when it was discussed, it was most frequently referenced as “inequalities”, followed by “inequities” and “equity”. Across all documents, a variety of proxy terms for equity considerations were used to relate to health equity and most commonly by naming groups “at risk”, “with risk conditions”, “vulnerable”, “disadvantaged”, or “marginalized”. Other proxy terms for equity considerations commonly found included the “social determinant of health or disease” and “universal, targeted, enhanced or proportionate interventions” (e.g. screening, counselling, and education). Missed opportunities in the explicit mentioning of equity included (a) discrepancies in (or absence of) a clear definition, (b) no demonstration of practical application, (c) limiting the understanding of equity to access to health services, (d) minimal interrogation of structural conditions, and (e) missing discussion around targeting within universalism. In 14 documents where equity was referenced, consideration of the structural determinants of equity was present. In the remaining documents, the most commonly overlooked detail was acknowledgement of the structural conditions that produce inequities, which can be understood as the “interpersonal, institutional, and systemic mechanisms that organize the distribution of power and resources differentially across lines of race, gender, class, sexual orientation, gender expression, and other dimensions of individual and group identity” [27] (p. 7). Further, equity of access (including consideration for meaningful participation across roles and contexts within the health system for various populations), how to practically apply an equity lens (including attention to capacity building) and/or examples of equity in action were also missing across many of the documents. Missed considerations

Absence of clear definitions: Equity was rarely defined, with only 4 out of the 16 documents providing a clear description of its meaning. Rooming-in Guideline for Perinatal Women Using Substances (Rooming-in Guideline) [28] and The Provincial Blueprint for a Perinatal Substance Use Continuum of Care (the Blueprint) [12] both equate the definition of equity to “equitable access”. Specifically, the Blueprint defines equitable access as, “…involv[ing] fair distribution of care according to population need, and providing access to healthcare services to people regardless of their gender, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or where they live” [12] (p. 36). The First Nations Health Authority’s BC First Nations and Aboriginal Maternal, Child and Family Strategic Approach defines inequity as differences “…in rates of illness, disease, health outcomes, or access to healthcare…” between population groups [29] (p. 58). However, the definition omits a key distinguishing and significant social justice feature: To address health inequities, we must acknowledge issues of power imbalance and resource allocation, and actions and inactions within these systems [8]. In the documents where equity was mentioned (n = 15), it was more common for equity to be implicitly discussed and addressed (n = 10) through the previously described proxy terms, than to be an explicit goal (n = 5). When achieving health equity was named as an explicit goal, it was through aspirational language such as “advancing health equity” or achieving equitable health outcomes”, but these goals were not accompanied by clear statements on how this would be achieved or what concrete actions would be required and by whom to realize those aspirations.

Focus on accessibility: Equity or inequities are mentioned in various contexts and applied to diverse programs. Throughout the documents, approaches for achieving health equity included the identification of vulnerable groups, unmet population needs, population health indicators and implementation of universal and/or targeted programs and/or services. Examples of programs included education for patients, providers and decision-makers within and beyond the health system, as well as screening and counselling. Other interventions described included advocating, promoting and legislating healthy public policies, increasing access to health services, applying a health equity lens and carefully considering the patient-facing barriers that may impact one’s ability to access and use health services. In most of the documents in our sample, equity was positioned primarily as an issue of access to services. While three documents appeared to assume that all populations can access services without extra supports, others mentioned considerations such as: the financial burden and/or need for affordable services [25–28, 30–33]; a need for the equitable delivery and distribution of health services for different populations [28–31, 33–36]; and, a need for resources and health services to be culturally relevant and safe, as deemed by users [12, 28, 30, 37].

Inattention to structural conditions: Across documents, explicitly named or identified social determinants of health included, in order of frequency: Aboriginal/Aboriginal Status/First Nation/Indigenous (n = 13) [12, 20, 28, 29, 33, 34–37, 36–42]; vulnerable/at-risk/high risk (n = 5) [22, 28, 31, 35, 38]; women (people) who use drugs (or substances) [28, 36, 38, 43, 44] (n = 5); LGBTQI+/gay/lesbian/gender identities (n = 4) [12, 20, 35, 43]; poverty/low income (n = 2) [31, 34]; ethno-cultural communities/people of colour/subpopulation/racial, ethnic minority (n = 3) [12, 20, 39]; disabilities (n = 3) [20, 31, 35]; immigrant(s)/refugee(s) (n = 2) [33, 42]; and socioeconomic status (n = 1) [12]. However, even in cases where determinants of health are named, only a few documents alluded to public health systems’ responsibility for addressing health inequities or taking action on these determinants, though these obligations were still only named implicitly. Of the sample, five documents spoke to vague direction that aligned with those offered in either the Healthy Start Initiative [30] to guide the distribution of opportunities for well-being based on people’s needs and/or the Rooming-In Guideline [28] direction that “addressing historical, current and ongoing colonization requires meaningful, consistent action at multiple levels” (p. 3). Indeed, many documents focused on colonialism as a key driver of perinatal health inequities for Indigenous mothers, with a pronounced shift in language and rhetoric throughout documents following the release of the Elder’s Visioning Perinatal Substance Use Toolkit [45] in 2021. Documents from this point forward, including notably the Blueprint (2021) released alongside this toolkit had much more explicit attention to the use of red intersectionality [46], cultural safety [47] and grounding perinatal substance use within historical and neocolonial contexts [48]. When considering the notion that equity is an experience, attention to the ways in which people feel within health systems (such as through attention to cultural safety whereby safety of interactions are defined by the person receiving care) is an important dimension to building attention to equity within and across health systems.

Incoherent concepts of equity: The conflation of access with equity, combined with a general failure to name the root causes of health inequities or to articulate public health’s responsibility in addressing them, gives rise to a number of curious and conceptually inconsistent ideas about equity across the documents reviewed. For the purposes of our review, we looked for both incoherence in conceptualizations and operationalizations of equity against our threefold definition, but also within the document itself between its stated intentions, goals or foci and the substantive content of the document. For instance, some materials suggest that simply increasing access to services or improving referral pathways will reduce the prevalence of smoking during pregnancy – without acknowledging the broader structural conditions that shape this behaviour and/or the role of the system in addressing these conditions [41]. Gender equity is another area marked by oversimplification: While men’s involvement in reproductive and family health is discussed, it is framed narrowly in terms of access to sexual health services and fertility support. This framing overlooks the complex systems of power and resource distribution – such as violence, legal policy and systemic trauma – that disproportionately affect women and children [30]. Similarly, efforts to support fathers’ roles in parenting are largely interpreted through the lens of service delivery, without addressing the deeper societal norms or policy-level barriers (such as parental leave) that shape paternal engagement [43]. In some of the earlier documents, the mother–baby dyad (referring to the interconnected, holistic unit of a mother and infant and their shared biological, social and psychological relationship) is often considered independent of one another in the context of clinical decision-making, notably prior to the introduction of rooming-in practices. In some cases, documents focus on protecting child health through surveillance and reporting to child protection services, while neglecting to propose supportive interventions that strengthen the mother–child bond or account for the social context of substance use [33]. Finally, some documents invoke the principle of proportionate universality but fail to apply it meaningfully. They often reduce health inequities to individual risk factors, ignoring the systemic and structural determinants that actually drive inequity [30, 49]. Collectively, these examples illustrate a fragmented and sometimes contradictory approach to equity that limits the potential for transformative public health action.

Discussion

This review of policy and strategic documents in one Canadian province spanning a 20-year period reveals a growing effort to define and integrate terms such as “equity” and “inequity” into public health strategies. However, this integration has been inconsistent and, at times, superficial. Despite increasing references to equity, documents often fail to articulate clear definitions, or to connect equity to social justice or structural determinants. Few, if any, concrete directives are provided on the role of public health systems in interrupting or acting on these root causes of inequities. As a result, ambiguous or contradictory interpretations emerge, weakening the impact and coherence of policy directives.

The power of language and the importance of clear definitions

Words create worlds. When foundational concepts such as health equity are poorly defined or inconsistently applied, the resulting policy documents lack the clarity needed to guide meaningful action. These challenges are likely to persist in the context of continued erosion of public health expertise within health systems, and point to the opportunity and need to purposefully integrate people with the equity expertise, experience and training to ensure policy documents align with the best available evidence and practice [50]. A lack of attention to this issue of expertise is especially problematic in top-down policy implementation processes, where vague or misaligned language can lead to the dissipation of policy goals and a failure to sustain impact [4]. From global to local contexts, it is clear that the lack of progress towards equity since the calls to action from the WHO in 2008, following the release of the often cited Closing the Gap document [2], is driven, at least in part, by a lack of clear, shared understandings of health equity whereby policy and practice remain disconnected and health outcomes continue to suffer. In BC, while concerted investment in equity-advancing frameworks and directives have resulted in an increasingly clear understanding of equity, this analysis demonstrates that the risks of rhetorical equity remain real.

Across the documents, policies reflect a long-standing issue of reducing equity to a question of access, failing to grapple with deeper systemic issues [3, 8, 51]. When policy documents focus solely on service availability or individual-level factors, they obscure the broader structural conditions – such as poverty, colonialism and gender-based violence – that shape perinatal health inequities [3, 8, 51, 52]. This reinforces individualized, medicalized and deficit-based lenses that collectively contribute to the growing incoherence between named “equity approaches” that prioritize the levelling-down and targeting of services and programs, rather than supporting equity-oriented, structural approaches to care and policy that advance proportionate universalism approaches to advancing population health and equity [53].

The mother–baby dyad

Across our analysis, the focus of policies consistently defaulted to the neonate, often at the expense of a holistic understanding of the mother–baby dyad. While child protection is a legitimate concern, overemphasis on surveillance and fetal outcomes can lead to punitive approaches that undermine maternal support and autonomy. Documents rarely addressed the ethical tension between protecting infant health and enabling the well-being of the mother–child unit as a whole [54]. Attention to equity science reminds us that individual clinical decisions occur within a broader sociopolitical context and that attention to this context would better prepare policies for advancing care that is likely to address root causes of inequities [8, 55]. Policies must support practitioners in navigating these complexities ethically, and this begins with principle-based guidance that foregrounds equity and justice, rather than clinical risk alone, Policies must support practitioners in navigating these complexities ethically, and this begins with principle-based guidance that foregrounds equity and justice, rather than clinical risk alone, especially within the context of perinatal mental health and substance use where the mother’s well-being, and her perceived and/or implied goodness (and that of her family and community) are inextricable from the well-being of the fetus and/or baby [56, 57]. In fact, counter to the primacy of concern for the baby within these documents, it is well-established that supports that improve the living conditions, well-being, opportunities and health of the mother (and other people within the baby’s circle of care) offer the most impactful and long-reaching impacts for improving the life course and health outcomes of the baby. Policies focused primarily on infant well-being to the exclusion of the mother paradoxically can create more harm for both baby and mother [11, 58]. Despite this evidence, policies in health and social fields (such as reproductive care, administration of blood products, fetal surgery and child protection) continue to weigh in favour of the infant, often reflecting internal and external biases and stigmas of the health providers who are influencing and making decisions.

Policy implementation and the role of institutional infrastructure

The inconsistent ways in which equity is defined and understood also reflects broader challenges in policy implementation. It is not enough for documents to articulate principles – mechanisms for putting them into practice are equally essential. Without investments in the deep work and expertise that drives good policy, we are left in a chicken and egg scenario where it is hard to pinpoint which intervention needs to come first. The case of the Centre of Excellence for Women’s Health (CEWH) offers a compelling example: As an institution specifically resourced and mandated to translate knowledge into action, its outputs have endured over time and their impacts in shaping practice are evident even to this day across women’s health policy and programming within the province and within this review [59]. This demonstrates the importance of sustained, targeted investment in institutions that bridge the gap between policy and practice that are equipped with both the topical expertise and the understanding of the implementation systems within which that expertise needs to be distilled within. In contrast, many world-leading innovative and integrated maternity and perinatal programs developed in BC to support pregnant and early parenting people with mental health and substance use challenges – such as Families in Recovery (FIR) Square, Sheway, and Best Babies – continue to operate in precarious funding environments, often external to health authority budgets. This limits their scalability, integration and long-term sustainability [55].

While there is emerging and exciting attention to implementation science more generally within public health systems, it is also urgently important to remember that policies are only as effective as the implementation plans and sustainable institutional supports behind them [60]. When these are lacking, promising models remain fragmented or fail to be embedded systemically and, it is somewhat unrealistic to expect excellence in policies where topical expertise is not prioritized or supported. Encouragingly, it remains evident that when there is attention to and investment in process and broad engagement of expertise in the development of policies, as was done in BC in 2020 as part of the development of the 2021 Blueprint [12] and Elders’ Visioning Toolkit [45], what is produced creates sustained impacts across the system. Indeed, our review revealed a sustained shift upstream to the root causes of inequities for Indigenous mothers following these documents. This speaks to the immense promise of investing in the deep foundational work of including diverse knowledges and science in the development of these policies so that we are equipped to meet the challenges of our time through the advancement of the best and wisest practices and evidence.

When thinking about how policy moves from a simple document to being embedded within a system, it bears recognizing that embedding is rare – certainly, the initial core functions framework for BC’s public health system is over 20 years old, and we are similarly approaching the 20-year anniversary of the WHO’s Closing the Gap in a Generation [2] document with limited markers of success in responding to their aspirations. Similarly rare, and important, is the act of “undoing” or deimplementation [61] within healthcare to let go of things that do not work, in favour of creating space for new approaches. Part of the challenge is certainly driven by the abovementioned inattention to resourcing and specificity of policies – but the second part of this issue stems from the fact that many services addressing inequities, including within perinatal health, are nonprofit organizations or groups external to the “official” health system’s funding and priorities, resulting in a lack of sustainability and growth in these domains. Merging these siloed areas of action is one key piece of attention to infrastructure that is essential to moving aspirational policies into day-to-day practices within public health systems, rather than seeing much of this work as happening outside of the system’s responsibilities and roles [54, 62]. Conversely, when programming is moved from one context (community) to another (health systems), there is also a threat of having the philosophical approach that underpins the success of that programming being subsumed by biomedical approaches and so attention to contextual fidelity is also paramount. As demonstrated in this review, however, there are recent promising shifts in attention to these issues and the foregrounding of upstream investments in advancing equity – it remains to be seen how it is actualized as the threats to equity action and rhetoric appear to be once again increasing in shifting political landscapes.

Facilitators and barriers: systemic influences and missed opportunities

While our review focused on documents, facilitating development and implementation of equity-oriented policy requires more than well-written documents; it demands supportive ecosystems capable of buffering public health systems from the inherently political nature of governance. Similar to other public institutions, public health is not immune to political influence or the shifting winds of public sentiment, and negating this context while asking for more precise and inherently political language within our formal documents is at best naïve and at worse harmful towards those navigating the very real constraints of our health systems. As governments change and societal priorities shift, so too can the policy directions and funding mechanisms that shape service delivery. This makes it essential to develop and maintain infrastructure that can function across systemic levels and, where possible, at arm’s length from direct political control.

In BC, supportive ecosystem elements have included institutions such as the Centre of Excellence for Women’s Health, the National Collaborating Centres for Public Health, Perinatal Services BC, Health Canada, Public Health Agency of Canada and the Women’s Health Research Institute. These organizations play a vital role in translating evidence into action, sustaining institutional memory and bridging gaps between policy and practice. Their existence and impact highlight how much it matters to have a favourable political climate – or, in its absence, to establish mechanisms that shield equity-oriented work from political volatility through legislative or policy safeguards. The more such buffers exist within a system, the more resilient and consistent equity-focused work can be, even amidst political shifts [50].

Indeed, over the span of this review, changes in provincial leadership – that often swing widely from socialism to conservativism extremes – clearly shaped policy tone and direction. During periods of political misalignment between provincial and federal governments, efforts such as gender-based analysis (GBA+) saw little uptake provincially. Only when greater coherence emerged between federal and provincial priorities did implementation of equity-focused visions gain traction.

At the same time, the broader sociopolitical climate had specific and deeply consequential effects on the perinatal health policy landscape. The erosion of the public health nursing workforce, particularly in the post-COVID period, reflects a troubling trend: the levelling down of services despite strong evidence supporting their effectiveness. Core functions – such as home visiting programs – have been lost or deprioritized, even though they are foundational to equitable perinatal care [23]. This decline points to a broader erosion of expertise, institutional knowledge and applied understanding within the public health system [63].

These systemic losses are not incidental – they are manifestations of the policy incoherence identified in this review. Inconsistent or underdeveloped equity language in policy documents mirrors, and may even contribute to, the weakening of core public health infrastructure. When foundational concepts such as equity, prevention and community-based care are poorly understood or inconsistently applied in policy, the capacity of the system to deliver on its equity commitments is fundamentally compromised. This underscores why precision, accountability and clarity in policy language – and the political and institutional supports that uphold them – matter so profoundly. Language and political and social contexts also manifested in this review in the ways in which risks and actions were positioned within the space of perinatal health and well-being.

Recommendations and implications for policy

This review highlights key gaps in how equity is understood, defined and operationalized in perinatal mental health and substance use policy in BC. Despite the province’s reputation for innovation and leadership in equity-oriented health services, many guiding documents lack clear accountability mechanisms or expectations for public health actors to address inequities in a sustained and measurable way [52]. Previous ELPH findings have shown that sustained action for equity in health systems requires embedding health equity as a core value and organizational priority – through structural supports, dedicated resources, inclusive decision-making and government mandates – while addressing dominant values and conceptual gaps that act as barriers to its prioritization [52]. There is a notable absence of mechanisms to monitor the uptake of practice principles and guidelines, especially in documents designed for frontline providers. Without such accountability and monitoring, even well-intentioned equity frameworks risk limited impact.

While this scoping review was focused on perinatal mental health and substance use policy in the context of stated provincial equity priorities of supporting families impacted by mental health and substance use, we hypothesize that expanding this lens to general health service delivery will reveal even broader gaps. Health equity should be an explicit and actionable goal in policy development across sectors, supported by frameworks that guide movement from concept to practice and include concrete examples. Policies must extend beyond vague references to “vulnerability” or “social determinants” and instead offer direct guidance on addressing structural and systemic causes of inequities. In addition, policy evaluation should include specific metrics and processes to assess impact over time, ensuring that equity commitments translate into measurable improvements for communities experiencing the greatest disadvantage.

To strengthen equity in perinatal substance use policy, Marcellus and colleagues [64] recommend five areas of action, including: (1) advocating for reproductive and human rights-based guidelines and legislation; (2) ensuring policies are explicitly antiracist and gender-informed; (3) amplifying women’s voices in drug policy and guideline development; and (4) engaging in cross-national policy comparisons to learn from jurisdictions with innovative and effective models.

Together, these recommendations, alongside attention to building the scaffolding and infrastructure for sustained action towards equity-oriented systems goals, highlight the need for multilevel policy action that is grounded in justice, informed by those most impacted and committed to transforming the systems that continue to reproduce perinatal health inequities.

These recommendations align with global public health commitments, such as the Rio Declaration, and have broad international relevance. Countries grappling with rising rates of perinatal substance use and intersecting crises – including gender and race-based inequity, poverty and drug toxicity – can draw on these findings to inform more coherent, accountable and justice-oriented policy responses. In particular, this review offers practical relevance for health systems in high-income countries that, similar to Canada, are navigating complex tensions between equity-oriented mandates and neoliberal austerity measures. This review also holds relevance for countries and regions undergoing political shifts that threaten equity-focused work, where policy backsliding and funding cuts risk deepening existing health inequities. In such contexts, evidence-informed, rights-based approaches to perinatal care are especially critical to safeguarding and advancing public health goals. By demonstrating how policy analysis can reveal both gaps and opportunities in the operationalization of equity, this review offers transferable insights for health systems worldwide committed to improving maternal and infant health outcomes through socially just and sustainable policy design.

Limitations

We note several limitations of the study design. First, as a scoping review, this work is largely exploratory and opens up opportunities for a more in-depth investigation of attitudes, thinking and action in relation to mental health promotion and the prevention of mental disorders and harms of substance use within perinatal programs in BC and elsewhere. Similar to Pinto and colleagues [19], without corroborating evidence such as key informant interviews, we are limited in what conclusions we can make about the actual uptake and implementation of policy directives. However, previous interviews within the BC health system by the ELPH project have noted similar patterns of thought, uptake and implementation of an equity lens in public health [3, 65]. Interviews with policy-makers, public health decision-makers and frontline staff would also provide a more complete picture of the broader context shaping the application of a health equity lens within perinatal programs in BC. Lastly, while our inclusion criteria targeted foundational and guiding policy documents, the intent, scope and audiences of each likely shaped what their authors chose to include. Depending on their goals, some elements of our analysis may have been considered out of scope or redundant. Nevertheless, this analysis remains important: Across many documents – similar to policies in other contexts – equity and related terms are invoked as relevant but rarely defined or deeply engaged. Because this pattern was consistent, and no defining or foundational document emerged to anchor the others, we conclude that, as a collective, these policies reveal persistent gaps and incoherencies that warrant attention.

Conclusions

While more recent documents in this review show greater attention to process, community knowledge and systems-level thinking, the impact of these shifts remains to be seen. What is clear from this review is that equity must be more than a rhetorical gesture; equity’s definition as an idea, aspiration and experience must be structurally embedded, ethically grounded and practically implemented. The complexity of perinatal mental health and substance use, particularly when shaped by factors such as the erosion of public health nursing practice, the opioid crisis and child welfare interventions, demands nuanced, coherent and sustained responses. Building on what works, such as embedded knowledge translation models and topical hubs of expertise and implementation, will be key.

Ultimately, policy relevance depends on whether it meets the needs of those it aims to serve. For public health leaders, clinicians, researchers and policy-makers with commitments to advancing equity, the call is clear: Define equity rigorously, implement it strategically, sustain it systemically and evaluate its impact continuously to ensure accountability and improvement over time.
