Protocol to stain phagocytic macrophages and assess phagocytosis function in phagocytic cells
Authors: Mostafa Changaei, Seyed Adnan Kashfi, Sara Soudi, Mohammad Adel Ghiass, Seyed Mahmoud Hashemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe yeast phagocytosis assay serves as a foundational tool for directly visualizing and quantifying a phagocyte’s ability to bind and internalize foreign targets. Here, we present a protocol for analyzing phagocytosis in mouse peritoneal macrophages. We describe steps for yeast staining, phagocytosis of stained yeast, hematoxylin staining of macrophages, and light microscopy imaging. We then detail procedures for phagocytic metric analysis, which are also applicable to any phagocytic cells. This protocol can also be used for time-lapse visualization of phagocytosis.Subject areas: Cell Biology, Immunology, Microbiology, Microscopy

Before you begin

We summarized the steps for harvesting mouse peritoneal macrophages in Figure 1; however, for a detailed, and step-by-step protocol, please refer to De Jesus et al.1 In addition to peritoneal macrophages, this protocol can be applied to various macrophage/monocyte cell lines, including RAW 264.7 and J774A.1, BAC1.2F5.

Schematic summary of peritoneal macrophage isolation

(A) 1 mL of 3% (w/v) thioglycollate was intraperitoneally injected.

(B) On day 4, mice were humanely sacrificed.

(C) 5 mL of cold (8°C) PBS containing 0.5 mM EDTA was injected into the peritoneal cavity, then the abdomen was gently tapped for 30 sec to detach the adherent cells.

(D) The peritoneal fluid was collected using a 5 mL syringe.

(E) The collected fluid was transferred to a 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 500 G for 5 min.

(F) The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL RPMI 1640 media containing 10% FBS (complete media).

(G) The cell suspension was seeded in a 6 cm diameter cell culture Petri dish, the appropriate volume of complete media was added, and the dish was incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 30-60 min. Macrophages are adherent cells, so after 2 hrs., the supernatant was discarded and replaced with new media to remove non-adherent cells.

Innovation

This modified procedure presents a specific color contrast (blue host cell versus magenta target), and creates a clearer, unambiguous distinction between the phagocyte and its target compared to other staining methods, such as Giemsa staining. In addition, in this method, the yeasts are first stained and then added to macrophages. This provides ideal contrast for real-time observation and examination of phagocytosis; neither of the other staining techniques can be used for this purpose. Also, because the stained yeasts can be stored for a long time, this significantly reduces the testing time for subsequent experiments.

Staining yeast cell wall polysaccharides

Timing: 10 min

Here, we describe steps for staining yeast cell wall using Schiff’s reagent (Figures 2F–2J).

Note: The Schiff’s reagent contains basic fuchsin that has been decolorized with sulfurous acid (H2SO3). This decolorization process creates a colorless solution known as leucofuchsin. The aldehyde groups (-CHO) generated in previous part now react with the leucofuchsin in the Schiff solution. This reaction forms new chemical bonds and reorganizes the molecule, restoring the dye's conjugated quinoid structure. This restoration of the chemical structure brings back the intense magenta color and fixes it at the site of the original carbohydrates.

Expected outcomes

This protocol provides a simple, fast, and robust method for visualizing and quantifying phagocytosis function. Successful execution will yield high-contrast images ideal for analysis with standard light microscope. Upon completion of the co-culture, fixation, and staining steps, macrophages will be identifiable by their pale blue to gray cytoplasm and distinct dark blue nuclei, as revealed by hematoxylin staining. The yeast cell walls, due to their high polysaccharide content, will be vividly stained magenta-red by Schiff’s reagent. In a successful experiment, macrophages will be well-adhered and spread. Phagocytosis is clearly visualized as magenta-red yeast cells engulfed by the pale blue macrophage cytoplasm (Figures 6A–6C). Expected quantitative results (e.g., phagocytic index) will depend on macrophage subtype (M1 or M2), activation state, polarization, and the yeast-to-macrophage ratio.

Limitations

The present protocol has some limitations. First, the killing ability of engulfed particles by phagocytic cells cannot be assessed using this technique. Second, it can be difficult to filter out yeast clumps completely; in this case, the yeast preparation step should be repeated. Third, during real-time observation of phagocytosis, the optimal incubation condition (e.g., CO2, humidity, and temperature) for cells is not applicable. Fourth, the cell culture Petri dish limits the field of view to the center of the plate when using a conventional light microscope. Finally, there is no an efficient fully automated method to measure the phagocytosis metrics and assessment is mainly dependent on individual observation and manual manners.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

There are dye precipitates or crystals due to undissolved dye particles or rapid drying during staining.

Potential solution

Filter hematoxylin working solution before use (0.45 μm syringe filter is recommended), and avoid drying during staining by covering the cell culture plates or slides.

Problem 2

Overstaining or understaining. In this situation, nuclei are very dark or even black, and cytoplasmic detail is obscured. In understaining, nuclei are pale, and contrast is too low.

Potential solution

Adjust the staining incubation time or the concentration of hematoxylin dye.

Problem 3

The yeast clumps still remain after filtration.

Potential solution

Hardly vortex the stained yeast suspension and redo the filtration step with more cotton pieces.
