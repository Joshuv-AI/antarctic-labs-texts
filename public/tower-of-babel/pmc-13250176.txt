Healthcare professionals’ perspectives on point-of-care testing quality and access in Australian healthcare services: a qualitative study
Authors: Jacqueline Prestedge, Alissar El-Murr, Deborah A Williamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore healthcare professionals’ perceptions and experiences with point-of-care testing (PoCT) implementation within Australia’s healthcare system and identify potential strategies for effective governance and improvements to regulation and guidelines that facilitate coordinated integration of quality PoCT into healthcare systems.DesignA descriptive qualitative study with thematic analysis with codes derived inductively from the transcriptions and a structured framework analysis using the six building blocks of the WHO Health Systems Framework exploring barriers and enablers to PoCT implementation.SettingsHealthcare settings in Australia.ParticipantsHealthcare workers with patient care responsibilities and healthcare administrators with oversight or regulatory responsibility for a service. 18 participants were interviewed with four from hospitals, four from primary care, three from community care, three from pathology laboratories, two from emergency care and two from pharmacy settings.ResultsHealthcare professionals’ perspectives on PoCT implementation were grouped into three main themes: (1) community-centric pathology testing, (2) connectivity and continuity of care and (3) quality and governance. Participants identified insufficient health system financing and health information systems as significant barriers to PoCT implementation. Improved practical guidance for workforce training and health service delivery to interpret the current regulation outside of laboratory settings was viewed as a potential enabler to establishing PoCT within healthcare services.ConclusionParticipants reflected that Australia has several examples of successful PoCT programmes providing patient-centred pathology to improve healthcare equity. However, the lack of information systems, funding, workforce training and diagnostic strategies prohibits the implementation of PoCT at-scale. It was recommended that adequately financing PoCT to encompass costs for testing, quality management and workforce training would incentivise services to obtain accreditation and ensure quality healthcare delivery. Despite the potential of PoCT to improve equitable healthcare access and patient outcomes, health system strengthening is required to integrate PoCT effectively across Australian healthcare services.

Introduction

Point-of-care testing (PoCT) refers to diagnostic testing performed at or near the site of patient care, providing rapid results for immediate use.1 There are commercially available point-of-care (PoC) tests for various conditions including communicable and non-communicable diseases.2 3 Some PoC tests are restricted to healthcare worker use; other PoC tests are available in self-test formats, allowing individuals to perform tests and interpret results without clinical supervision (eg, COVID-19 rapid tests and pregnancy tests).4 As PoCT technology expands and becomes more common in healthcare, services must adhere to safety and quality standards to ensure reliable, timely results for effective patient management and public health responses.

PoCT has been implemented worldwide, particularly in primary and community care where access to pathology laboratories is limited. For patients, PoCT enables diagnosis and treatment in a single visit, alleviating uncertainty, improving communication and promoting patient engagement with faster treatment initiation.5 These benefits are amplified when PoCT is used in tailored models of care for communities where access to traditional healthcare services is challenging due to stigma and discrimination or resource limitations. PoCT has proven beneficial for underserved communities, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, people living in rural and remote areas, and key populations at greater risk of blood-borne viruses and sexually transmissible infections including people who inject drugs and men who have sex with men.69 PoCT procedures with often simplified sample collection can align better with patients’ cultural and safety needs and may be preferred by patients and healthcare workers compared with pathology laboratory testing.10 11

Despite the benefits of PoCT in improving patient care, there are barriers to the implementation of PoCT within healthcare services. Several studies have reported that failure to fund the true cost of PoCT is a key barrier to its widescale adoption.12 13 In Australia, the federal government funds and oversees national health programmes including the financing of medical services (Medicare), regulation of diagnostic tests and other therapeutic goods (Therapeutic Goods Administration), medical workforce registration and quality and safety of health services.14 State and territory governments manage hospital, emergency and community health services, leading to varied implementation of national guidelines across jurisdictions. Australia’s federal healthcare system provides universal access to pathology testing through Medicare (subsidised or free medical services for patients and reimbursements to healthcare providers). While Medicare covers the cost of most laboratory tests, coverage for PoCT is limited and condition-specific (eg, diabetes management and sexually transmissible infection testing in remote areas). Medicare reimbursement for pathology testing is only provided for sites which comply with the National Pathology Accreditation Advisory Council (NPAAC) and ISO 15189:2022, medical laboratories standards, both of which include requirements for PoCT.15 16 Non-Medicare-funded PoCT are instead funded through hospital budgets, government or donor funded pilot programmes or consumer out-of-pocket payments. As most PoCT services are ineligible for Medicare funding, health services lack a financial incentive to comply with non-mandatory regulatory frameworks. This gap reduces motivation for adherence and increases the risk that PoCT may be performed without appropriate quality oversight.17

To integrate PoCT into healthcare systems requires an understanding of regulatory requirements, healthcare practices and resource allocation. The WHO Health System Framework promotes systems thinking to improve healthcare quality and outcomes.18 The Health System Framework disaggregates complex health systems into interacting components, facilitating the translation of research findings into policy and practice. It was selected for this study to enable participants’ experiences to be mapped to health system-level components without requiring familiarity with health systems concepts, thereby supporting the inclusion of participants with diverse levels of experience. In doing so, the framework helps inform strategies to strengthen the health system for broader implementation of PoCT to achieve high-quality, equitable and effective improvements in healthcare.

Understanding how current health systems support or hinder PoCT implementation will become more critical as PoCT technology expands and is adopted by a wider range of healthcare practitioners outside of specialised pathology environments. Findings from a preceding scoping review revealed a lack of information on how PoCT regulation and quality standards inform PoCT implementation and translate into improved healthcare services.19 To address this gap, this qualitative study uses the WHO Health System Framework to explore healthcare professionals’ experiences with PoCT implementation and perceived impacts of PoCT regulation. In doing so, we aim to identify barriers, enablers and potential strategies for effective governance and integration of quality PoCT into healthcare systems.

Methods

Study design

Qualitative in-depth interviews were used to explore healthcare professionals’ perspectives and experiences on PoCT and its associated regulation in Australia. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (2023-25999-40022-4).

Data collection

Participants included healthcare workers (HCW) involved in patient care delivery and healthcare administrators (HCA) responsible for policy and decision-making. A purposive sampling strategy was used to invite representatives from professional associations and sector representative organisations, experts in PoCT and healthcare professionals (HCWs and HCAs) within healthcare settings previously identified to have regulatory or governance mechanisms for PoCT including hospital, laboratory, emergency, primary care, pharmacy and community care.19 This study included participants with and without PoCT accreditation experience to capture either similar or opposing viewpoints on enablers and barriers to PoCT implementation. Participants were identified through the professional associations of our project’s Steering Committee (comprised of experts in PoCT implementation and policy) and the leadership of sector representative organisations and health sector websites. Snowballing recruitment increased participation with a total of 46 email invitations sent and recruitment ceased when data saturation was reached.

Data were collected from participants between May and October 2023. Participants completed pre-interview questionnaires to obtain written consent, demographic information and previous PoCT experience (online supplementary table 1). Interview guides (online supplementary table 2 and 3) were developed to structure the interviews with topics for exploration including concerns and benefits of PoCT within healthcare, perceived barriers and facilitators in the implementation of PoCT and feedback on the role of formal governance frameworks or regulation for PoCT. Two interview guides were used, comprising the same themes but with role-specific wording variations to ensure relevance for healthcare workers who deliver healthcare and healthcare administrators who oversee service governance and service quality. Semi-structured interviews were held virtually (Zoom Video Communications Inc.) for approximately 1 hour, facilitated by AE-M (an experienced qualitative social researcher) or JP (a doctoral candidate with PoCT experience). Participants had no prior relationship with their interviewer and were informed that the team was investigating PoCT implementation in Victorian healthcare settings. The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed using the Zoom automatic transcription function, and the transcripts were cleaned by JP and AE-M.

Data analysis

Pre-interview questionnaire data were analysed descriptively in Excel. Cross-tabulations explored associations between healthcare service type, experience and familiarity with PoCT regulations which provided contextual data for interview findings.

Inductive analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis, a widely adopted method for systematically analysing qualitative data.20 Each phase was implemented as follows: Cleaned, anonymised interview transcripts were imported into NVivo software (QSR International, V.14). JP and AE-M reviewed the interview verbatim transcripts multiple times for familiarisation with the data, with observations of recurring points and potential codes documented and discussed. JP generated initial codes across the dataset using an open coding approach using NVivo software. Initial codes were then reviewed and grouped to identify themes, resulting in an initial thematic list comprising three themes and nine sub-themes. The initial themes were reviewed by JP and AE-M and iteratively refined to ensure alignment with codes and research objectives with sub-themes merged to improve conceptual clarity. Themes and sub-themes were then classified according to their meaning and scope. Finally, themes were integrated into a narrative using illustrative quotes to highlight the diversity of participant views.

A framework analysis, following the process by Gale et al, was then conducted using the WHO Health Systems Framework.18 21 22 The inductively derived codes from the initial thematic analysis (see above) were mapped by JP against the WHO Health Systems Framework’s six building blocks (service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, medical products, health system financing and leadership and governance). Data were interpreted to report enablers and barriers to PoCT implementation across the WHO Health Systems Framework.18

To ensure analytical rigour, the same verbatim transcript data were analysed using both Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis (for exploratory breadth of the data) and Gale et al’s framework analysis (for structured, policy-relevant interpretation) for methodological triangulation.20 21 23 Additionally, participants were emailed a summary of findings and invited to provide feedback on the themes and interpretations. Two participants responded in support of the findings, and no requested changes were received from any participants, supporting the credibility of the analysis.

Results

18 participants including 14 healthcare workers (HCW) and four healthcare administrators (HCA) from primary and tertiary healthcare settings were interviewed (table 1). Only one participant worked in exclusively non-metro healthcare settings; four participants worked across metro and regional or remote services; and 13 participants worked exclusively in metro areas. All participants described experience with PoCT in their health service with a range of tests across biochemistry, haematology and microbiology disciplines, most commonly blood glucose and COVID-19 testing. Only six (33%) of participants provided accredited PoCT services; these were all in hospitals and laboratory settings, whereas no participants in decentralised services such as primary care, pharmacy or community care reported accreditation of PoCT. There was also less familiarity with PoCT regulation.

Thematic analysis

Theme 1: community-centric pathology testing

Healthcare workers and administrators perceived PoCT as a valuable tool for enhancing healthcare delivery and promoting health equity, particularly for communities underserved by traditional healthcare services. All participants acknowledged that patient benefits, particularly improved healthcare access and outcomes, were the primary motivation for performing PoCT. This patient-centric focus was consistently reported as a reason to ensure the quality of PoCT despite the implementation barriers described in themes 2 and 3.

Sub-theme 1.1: Increased access to diagnostics

Participants emphasised the importance of PoCT in improving diagnostic access for patients in rural and remote areas where limited pathology services can delay diagnosis and treatment which worsens patient outcomes. As one healthcare administrator with experience in non-metro settings stated “Australia’s problem is access, and we don’t want to limit how practitioners roll [PoCT] out. We do want to make sure patient safety is paramount.” (Pharmacy HCA 1). Respondents in both metro and non-metro settings agreed that the standard of PoCT should be comparable to laboratory testing to ensure equitable access to high-quality diagnostic services.

Sub-theme 1.2: Increased engagement in healthcare

Same-day PoC test-and-treat models were described by healthcare professionals as having a positive impact on patients’ understanding of their disease, willingness to act on referral pathways, and greater uptake of treatment, leading to improved health outcomes. Healthcare workers attributed increased patient engagement to curiosity about PoCT which facilitated more in-depth health discussions between patients and providers. For example, after PoCT, some patients that were initially reluctant became more receptive to laboratory tests for co-infections or co-morbidities requiring traditional collection and testing methods.

Patients love it, they love sitting there seeing their results coming out straight away. (Hospital HCW 1)

By providing faster results, PoCT reduces patient anxiety about their health, reduces delays in treatment and reduces loss to follow-up, where patients do not return for test results and subsequent care.

PoCT certainly reduces the level of anxiety [compared with] waiting for pathology test results which may take several days or a week or more in remote communities. (Community Care HCW 2)

Sub-theme 1.3: Increased testing uptake for underserved communities

Sub-theme 1.1 demonstrates that PoCT can increase access to pathology; however, increased physical access alone does not necessarily translate into increased testing uptake, particularly for underserved communities. Participants highlighted the importance of understanding the epidemiological and cultural context of the communities they serve to inform the design and implementation of PoCT initiatives. The comments below from interviewees working with different underserved communities illustrate that PoCT, when delivered by culturally competent providers, supports patient trust and comfort with services leading to increased testing and subsequent treatment uptake.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people

PoCT can better align with the cultural beliefs of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people through less invasive sample collection and on-site testing, which removes the need to transport patient samples to unfamiliar laboratories.24 Increased cultural safety promotes trust in healthcare services and greater patient engagement in the continuation of care, thus leading to a reduction of loss to follow-up.

Aboriginal people generally don’t like to see their blood taken from them …with PoCT seeing their blood sample being taken in front of them, placed onto the device and the result occurring. [It] has more [cultural] relevance …and are willing to come back. (Community Care HCW 2)

Individuals with living or lived experience of injecting drug use

PoCT often uses less invasive, less painful and faster sample collection, such as finger prick blood collection. These more accessible procedures increase feelings of safety for patients, particularly for the underserved community of people who inject drugs, improving engagement in testing and ongoing care.

[Patients] prefer PoCT particularly when it’s a finger prick vs having to get venepuncture … [for] people who inject drugs where poor vein access is often a major barrier to presenting for testing. (Community Care HCW 1)

Theme 2: connectivity and continuity of care

Sub-theme 2.1: Technological connectivity

Technological connectivity refers to the seamless integration of data across health information systems and stakeholders involved in a patient’s care journey. This includes the interoperability that enables the exchange of PoCT results and clinical data among healthcare providers, laboratories and electronic health record systems. Participants reported significant barriers posed by legacy systems and the lack of real-time data integration, which hinder effective service delivery and PoCT implementation.

The lack of standardised PoCT devices and information systems used in healthcare services required individual services to each address data connectivity challenges. This approach led to inefficiencies through expensive middleware and limited the potential benefits of PoCT to provide cost-effective, patient-focused healthcare delivery.

Connectivity solutions have significant price structures associated with the number of services …it’s on a multiplying sort of scale, depending on the size of your network and it becomes very prohibitive to provide a cost-effective connectivity solution. (Community Care HCW 2)

However, respondents from services which had invested in digital infrastructure for managing and communicating PoCT results reported this as a critical enabler of efficiency. Interoperability facilitated centralised monitoring of PoCT networks to ensure quality testing and connected pathology support to clinical areas.

Connectivity is an issue. There is an initial significant financial investment but in the long run being able to view all of your point of care service remotely does help with efficiency … it’s an overall cost saving. (Hospital HCW 2)

Sub-theme 2.2: Patient connectivity

Effective continuity of care depends on connected and coordinated patient pathways across healthcare services. Healthcare workers expressed a sense of powerlessness and frustration with the existing digital infrastructure which usually failed to effectively integrate PoCT results into decision-making processes and hindered their ability to inform healthcare service delivery. Participants stated the need for information systems and clinical algorithms that embed PoCT results into decision-making. The absence of clear guidelines outlining the permissible actions and clinical pathways based on PoCT results led to duplicate PoCT and laboratory testing in some services, frustrating clinical and laboratory staff due to wasted time, cost and resources.

If you can't provide appropriate communication of process and result to action. Then why do the [point-of-care] test? Where is the benefit in testing? (Laboratory HCW 1)

Despite their best efforts, many participants felt unfairly constrained by systems, preventing them from leveraging the full potential of PoCT to improve patient care and healthcare services.

Australia is a real leader in the roll out of point of care testing, it’s just that systems are not yet fully functional to be able to support that at scale. (Community Care HCW 1)

Theme 3: quality and governance for PoCT excellence

Sub-theme 3.1: Regulation and governance

Healthcare workers recognised the importance of quality measures such as training, competency assessments and performance monitoring in ensuring the safe and effective implementation of accurate PoCT.

If [quality assurance and performance monitoring] are poorly handled, the potential for test errors, misinterpretation of test results or potential misdiagnoses because of poor quality, can impact patient safety. (Community Care HCW 2)

However, there was considerable variation among participants in the awareness and understanding of current regulatory methods designed to ensure quality PoCT with 50% (9/18) of participants extremely or very familiar, 17% (3/18) moderately familiar and 33% (6/18) slightly or not familiar with PoCT regulation (table 1). Healthcare professionals in hospital and laboratory settings had the most familiarity and were supportive of the current PoCT regulatory methods which align with laboratory testing.

There are NPAAC standards that cover PoCT, and they should be applied to any healthcare practitioner that is performing PoCT… whether that’s a regional healthcare drop-in clinic with no doctor available vs a flash pathology laboratory in a hospital. (Laboratory HCW 1)

Participants reported that familiarity with PoCT accreditation processes reduced the perceived complexity over time, with one healthcare worker commenting, “Because we’ve been through accreditation so many times before, we understand what is actually being asked of us.” (Hospital HCW 2).

Views on the appropriateness of these regulatory methods varied, and other accounts, particularly from participants in pharmacy, community and primary care, were critical of the current regulatory mechanisms for PoCT. Several participants perceived current regulatory guidance as unsuitable for non-laboratory environments and called for a more pragmatic response to PoCT accreditation by considering the location where PoCT was being conducted or the complexity of the PoCT device, assuming less risks with simpler PoCT.

There’s no way that many of the sites that will be delivering PoCT are going to have the resources or the infrastructure to be able to operate like a lab. (Community Care HCW 1)

Laboratory professionals emphasised the importance of regulation for quality management in PoCT. However, they acknowledged the success of alternative approaches to ensure quality including decentralised networks, where a multidisciplinary team shares the responsibilities for PoCT management to reduce the burden on individual services to develop, implement and monitor PoCT.

I think the quality challenges are best handled, not by the poor people trying to do the point of care testing as well as doing everything else they're expected to do in their job, but by a group of people who are dedicated to supporting the point of care testing, to make sure that it works. (Laboratory HCA 1)

Sub-theme 3.2: Workforce and training support

All participants expressed concerns about the additional workload and training requirements associated with PoCT implementation, particularly in settings with already limited financial and staffing resources. They emphasised the need for adequate funding and support to ensure sustainable implementation of PoCT into health service delivery.

If you’re able to test and diagnose and treat more people, it means that you’ve got more and more clinical workload …the staff at the sites essentially are expected to do more without additional implementation funds and additional nursing support. (Community Care HCW 1)

A common view among interviewees was the importance of oversight and support from specialised areas, including disease experts and pathology laboratories, to ensure PoCT results improved patient care. Respondents noted that expertise in quality assurance, test validation and result interpretation from laboratory specialists could ease the burden on clinical staff less familiar with these functions.

[Healthcare providers] should have access to a pathologist when they have PoCT results they don’t understand. (Laboratory HCW 1)

Sub-theme 3.3: Resourcing sustainably

Healthcare workers expressed concerns about the current funding models and reimbursement mechanisms (Medicare) for PoCT services, which were perceived as inadequate for the implementation and maintenance of connectivity and quality assurance processes. Few participants utilised Medicare reimbursements for PoCT but noted this funding was insufficient to cover costs.

The MBS (Medicare) item numbers are not fit for purpose. They don’t include aspects related to the IT, the connectivity, the operator training, the delivery of the test, an external quality assurance program or a competency sort of program. (Community Care HCW 1)

In contrast to the wide Medicare coverage for laboratory pathology tests, few Medicare item numbers exist for PoCT. To fund PoCT, services were reliant on specific PoCT programme funding from governments, absorbing costs within the healthcare service, or charging patients directly. Moreover, lack of Medicare item numbers for PoCT limits the financial incentive for services to obtain accreditation, especially given the investment required to meet and maintain the regulatory requirements. Without these incentives, respondents expressed concerns that unaccredited PoCT may not adhere to best practice that ensures quality service delivery for patient care.

Some people do think about [accreditation] as just a ‘Well, if you want to claim Medicare, you need to be accredited’. But on the other side it is about patient care as well. (Hospital HCW 2)

Sub-theme 3.4: Supporting PoCT excellence

Participants acknowledged the potential risks of PoCT without specialised oversight and regulatory frameworks and emphasised the importance of ongoing monitoring and quality management to ensure patient benefit and effective healthcare service delivery.

There are always risks inherent in using a technology that’s previously been used in a specialist area with less oversight from those specialists, but I also would think that PoCT should only be used in a system where its use is well governed and people are trained in its use and there’s some connection with an overseeing lab (Emergency HCA 1)

Healthcare workers consistently emphasised the need for practical guidance and support to effectively conduct PoCT; for example, one participant commented, “The regulations are specifically written with pathology laboratories in mind… What you need is guidance on how to translate those national regulations into something that works for [PoCT] sites.” (Laboratory HCW 1). Therefore, tailored guidance, practical resources (for example, instructive guides, quality management checklists and training materials) and specialised pathology support can empower healthcare services to integrate PoCT into their clinical care pathways and facilitate the effective implementation of PoCT.

If there were procedures and policies that were already developed around [PoCT] that could just be followed and implemented, that would make it a whole lot easier. Right now, we develop all that ourselves from scratch. (Pharmacy HCW 1)

Framework analysis

The WHO Health Systems Framework highlighted the connection between barriers and enablers to PoCT implementation (table 3). The selection and sustained supply of PoCT devices (medical products) supported accessible and community-centric pathology testing (service delivery). Conversely, the reported lack of funding for PoCT (health system financing) directly constrained workforce capacity (health workforce) and limited digital infrastructure to support documentation, communication and action on PoCT results (health information systems). Participants emphasised that addressing these interconnected implementation challenges would require national strategies or policies (leadership and governance) to support system-level coordination of PoCT integration across health services rather than within disease-specific areas.

Leadership and governance

Participants viewed that effective PoCT implementation would require national strategies to promote PoCT and provide appropriate funding aligned with regulations. At the local health service level, participants saw multi-disciplinary governance as a solution for PoCT implementation, acknowledging that input from clinicians, scientists, industry and consumer representatives, in particular Indigenous knowledge and cultural perspectives, was important to translate national regulations into practical advice for specific healthcare services.

Health workforce

All participants recognised that PoCT increases staff workload which is amplified in services using multiple PoCT devices for different conditions, through training and competency requirements, quality management processes and test-and-treat models involving additional patient interactions. Participants reported introducing PoCT without an increase in workforce capacity prevented staff with existing time pressures from using PoCT and maximising the investment in their training “If you’re doing PoCT all the time you gain more confidence and you get more efficient” (Pharmacy HCW 1).

Health information systems

As reported in theme 2, poor integration of PoCT results within clinical information systems limited data accessibility across services, constraining implementation and clinical decision-making.

Health system financing

Participants reported that PoCT is inadequately funded, with limited Medicare reimbursements and poor recognition of its full costs beyond instruments and consumables. Current mechanisms where “PoCT is being funded through this sort of disease specific and somewhat piecemeal approach.” (Community Care HCW 1) were seen as a barrier to widespread adoption of PoCT.

Service delivery

Leveraging PoCT to improve service delivery was the strongest enabler of implementation, reflecting participants’ motivation as described in theme 1, to provide community-centric pathology and increase patient access to quality healthcare.

Medical products

Participants noticed the increasing availability of PoCT technologies that are simpler and more robust, enabling implementation in decentralised settings by a broader range of healthcare professionals. However, non-laboratory participants identified challenges in selecting appropriate devices: “In the hands of non-laboratory trained operators, the PoCT performance may not be as optimal as that achieved by a manufacturer in controlled conditions.” (Community Care HCW 2). Accordingly, participants viewed pathology leadership as a solution to ensure quality PoCT implementation.

Discussion

This study explored healthcare professionals’ experiences with implementation of PoCT and the role of regulation and governance in PoCT healthcare services. Our analysis highlighted several barriers to effective PoCT implementation, including variable technological infrastructure and lack of connectivity, concerns about quality assurance and patient safety, and inadequate funding and resourcing. Despite these challenges, healthcare workers consistently identified the delivery of community-centric pathology services as motivation to navigate these barriers within their health services. Healthcare settings differed in views on the most appropriate mechanism to achieve quality PoCT service delivery including formal accreditation, local governance structures and minimal oversight perceiving current regulation as overburdensome.

Our results reinforce previous research demonstrating the positive impact of PoCT in underserved communities, highlighting its role in bridging healthcare gaps and promoting health equity.25 PoCT enhances healthcare delivery through increased diagnostic access and patient engagement, offering culturally aligned processes for global First Nations communities and benefits for underserved groups. PoCT’s simplified sample collection and on-site, rapid results can increase comfort and privacy for diverse populations and support timely patient management in settings with limited access to follow-up care. These aspects make PoCT particularly beneficial for individuals living in rural and remote areas and those with experience of homelessness or injecting drug use.26 27

Healthcare workers and administrators interviewed in this study described challenges in complying with current national regulations and adhering to available standards for PoCT. Participants from decentralised healthcare services (eg, primary care, community care and pharmacy) reported low uptake of accreditation in services and variable familiarity with PoCT regulation and quality standards. The gap between regulatory awareness and implementation of formal accreditation indicates misaligned expectations between regulators and these decentralised health services, rather than deficiencies in service quality. Some PoCT programmes have implemented their own initiatives to incorporate data connectivity and quality assurance measures outside of accreditation frameworks.8 28 29 However, these ad hoc, program-funded solutions remain uncoordinated and have not led to systemic changes, leaving other settings and disease areas to address these challenges independently. Participants recommended stronger financial incentives and simplified regulatory requirements to support decentralised services to obtain accreditation and access Medicare funding for PoCT services. This finding supports previous recommendations that policy efforts should focus on developing proportionate, incentivised and context-appropriate governance options for decentralised PoCT.30 Further, some healthcare workers thought current regulation was overly bureaucratic for often relatively simple PoCT. In contrast to Australia, the US Food and Drug Administration regulates in vitro devices according to complexity, with the low-complexity tests receiving a ‘waiver’, permitting services to use these tests with minimal regulatory oversight which may encourage the introduction of PoCT in healthcare settings.31 In contrast, participants from hospital and laboratory settings, where regulatory accreditation and specialist pathology support are well established, viewed accreditation as necessary for all PoCT services and highlighted its importance for ensuring quality oversight including in decentralised settings. Future research should explore implementation challenges and successes within accredited PoCT environments to demonstrate how effective regulation and governance influence service delivery for patients and providers. This could inform policy changes and guidance to facilitate accreditation and Medicare reimbursement for PoCT, particularly as Medicare item numbers expand in line with evidence of cost-effectiveness and improved patient outcomes, as seen with other health technologies.32

Our findings highlight the need for a coordinated, system-wide approach to PoCT implementation, driven by clear guidelines and governance from relevant authorities. Healthcare workers and administrators called for governments or other trusted professional bodies to develop practical guidance to interpret the existing regulations for non-laboratory services. This could include support tools (templates and training resources) for standardisation of practices, as well as workforce training programmes and knowledge-sharing initiatives, which support implementation of national guidelines.33 Additionally, investment in digital systems and data integration across all healthcare sectors was identified as important to ensure that PoCT results are effectively utilised to inform clinical decision-making and optimise patient care pathways.

National leadership was perceived as a key requirement for widespread integration of PoCT within the healthcare system. Including PoCT in diagnostic and national strategies could increase uptake and secure funding for training, quality assurance programmes, data connectivity and testing as demonstrated in areas such as Hepatitis C management.34 Furthermore, integrating PoCT into national policies and healthcare workers’ scope of practice could enable consistent utilisation across settings and foster responsibility for PoCT services. Coupled with appropriate resourcing, this could encourage continuous improvement in PoCT implementation through recognition of PoCT as central to professional practice, rather than a non-essential, administratively burdensome procedure.35

Our exclusion of patient perspectives, key stakeholders in the health system, is a notable limitation of this study. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients find PoCT acceptable and can improve care, but affordability remains a concern.36 37 Nevertheless, within our study, patient experiences are required to validate healthcare professionals’ reports that PoCT strengthens patient-provider relationships and improves patient outcomes. The inclusion of diverse healthcare professionals, including healthcare administrators across settings, strengthens the generalisability of the findings. Including participants from both accredited and non-accredited PoCT services aligned with the study’s focus on regulatory and governance implementation for PoCT, although this may have introduced bias through participants reporting perceived, rather than experienced barriers to delivering quality-assured PoCT. Despite these limitations, by capturing perspectives across a range of healthcare settings, this study contributes to understanding PoCT regulation adoption and identifies opportunities to enhance the implementation of quality PoCT in healthcare services.

Conclusion

PoCT is increasing in use across health systems globally, although implementation is somewhat uncoordinated with healthcare services needing to address similar implementation challenges in digital systems, workforce training and financing. Importantly, this qualitative study examined PoCT at a health system level, extending prior research that has largely focused on disease- or setting-specific implementation. This approach directly interrogates the gap between PoCT uptake and the health system improvements required to support its sustainable, widespread implementation. Investing in technology improvements, including more affordable PoCT devices, and incentivising accreditation through expanded funding could support the implementation of quality-assured PoCT. These recommendations emphasise preparedness across the healthcare workforce, structures and systems to enable widespread PoCT implementation in response to emerging health challenges, including infectious disease outbreaks and the increasing burden of chronic diseases in Australia’s ageing population.
