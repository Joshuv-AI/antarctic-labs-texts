As the implantation and sophistication of these devices continue to increase, it is imperative for perioperative clinicians to be knowledgeable in their management. Introduction

Annually, nearly half a million patients undergo implantation of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), with millions more having existing devices (1,2). As many of the indications for these devices are associated with age-related comorbidities, the predominance of patients with CIEDs is over the age of 65 years and thus nearly twice as likely to have a surgical procedure each year (3). As such, anesthesiologists will continue to have a high and increasing frequency of encounters with patients having CIEDs and undergoing surgical procedures.

Patients with CIEDs are at risk for device malfunction during surgery due to electromagnetic interference (EMI) from electrocautery, radiofrequency (RF) ablation, or lithotripsy, which can result in inappropriate or absent pacing and defibrillation (4,5). In fact, in patients with CIEDs, a nearly 2% incidence of EMI per patient-year has been reported (5). As such, knowledge of and comfort with the perioperative management of these devices is of utmost importance. This is particularly important as technologies have advanced to include more complex devices, including leadless and subcutaneous and extravascular devices (6). Many organizations have previously published guidelines to facilitate this, though several new iterations of these guidelines have emerged in the last several years. In this manuscript, we aim to provide readers with updated guidance based on the latest guidelines, practice advisories, and expert consensus statements. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0434/rc).

Methods

The initial search was performed using the PubMed database with a focus on locating guidelines, consensus statements, and practice advisories from major national and international organizations from January 2020 to September 2025 (Table 1). Search terms included “Anesthesia”, “anesthesiologists”, “CIED”, “guideline”, “practice advisory”, “consensus”, “perioperative”, “periprocedural”, “cardiac implantable electronic device”, and “interference”. Only articles in the English language were considered. Relevance was determined based on the title and abstract. The primary search only focused on guidelines, practice advisories, and consensus statements, and original studies that did not meet this description were excluded from the initial search. Eligibility of articles thought to be relevant was established by review of the full manuscript. The main results are shown in Table 2 (5-9). Secondary searches were subsequently conducted in an unstructured manner based on the content discussed and references within the initial papers in order to provide a more complete description of CIEDs as deemed appropriate by the authors and as requested by peer reviewers.

Perioperative management of CIEDs

Device identification and assessment

Brief overview of CIEDs

Permanent pacemakers

Permanent pacemakers are the most commonly implanted CIEDs and are designed to treat symptomatic bradyarrhythmias (10). Pacemakers are typically classified by the number of chambers paced. Single-chamber devices pace either the right atrium or right ventricle, whereas dual-chamber devices pace both chambers to maintain atrioventricular synchrony (10).

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs)

ICDs are designed to detect and terminate life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias through high-energy shocks and/or antitachycardia pacing and are indicated for both primary and secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death. ICDs may be configured as single-chamber or dual-chamber systems and often provide backup antibradycardia pacing (1,11).

Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) devices

CRT devices are used in selected patients with symptomatic heart failure, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, and evidence of electrical dyssynchrony, most commonly, left bundle branch block. CRT aims to improve ventricular coordination and cardiac output through synchronized pacing of both ventricles (12).

CRT systems include pacing-only devices (CRT-P) and systems combined with defibrillation capability (CRT-D). Both configurations typically employ three leads: a right atrial lead, a right ventricular lead, and a left ventricular/coronary sinus lead. The left ventricular lead is advanced through the coronary sinus and positioned in a lateral or posterolateral cardiac vein (13,14).

Leadless pacemakers

Leadless pacemakers represent an important technological advancement by eliminating transvenous leads and subcutaneous generator pockets. These self-contained devices are implanted percutaneously via the femoral vein and secured directly within the endocardium (14-16).

Practical approach to device identification

Physical examination

Physical examination may reveal a generator pocket in the infraclavicular region. Larger, more prominent generators favor ICDs or CRT-D systems, whereas smaller, thinner devices are more consistent with pacemakers. Palpation alone, however, is insufficient for definitive identification and must be supplemented by imaging (1).

Identification of device manufacturer

Accurate identification of the device manufacturer is often necessary for interrogation and programming. Manufacturer identification may be obtained through patient device cards, electronic medical record documentation, or chest radiographic characteristics such as generator shape, header configuration, and lead connectors. Several manufacturers provide smartphone applications and online tools that assist with identification using chest radiographs. When uncertainty persists, consultation with electrophysiology services or direct contact with device representatives is appropriate (17-19).

Chest radiography

Chest radiography is the most valuable initial tool for CIED identification (Table 3) and some algorithms, such as the cardiac rhythm device identification algorithm using X-rays (CaRDIA-X), have been designed for this purpose (20). On chest radiography, pacemakers are characterized by a relatively small generator and one or two thin transvenous leads (Figure 1). The atrial lead often forms a J-shaped curve within the right atrial appendage, while the ventricular lead is positioned at the right ventricular apex or septum (20,21). A single transvenous lead terminating in the right ventricle is most consistent with a single-chamber pacemaker or ICD (20,21). The presence of two leads—typically one in the right atrium and one in the right ventricle—suggests a dual-chamber system (20,21). Three transvenous leads indicate CRT. The distinguishing feature is the left ventricular lead, which courses posteriorly through the coronary sinus and follows a lateral or posterolateral trajectory along the cardiac silhouette (Figure 2). Recognition of this leads reliably differentiates CRT systems from standard dual-chamber devices (20,21). ICD generators are larger than pacemaker generators due to higher energy storage requirements. The ventricular lead is thicker than standard pacing leads and typically contains one or two shock coils positioned in the right ventricle and sometimes the superior vena cava. Subcutaneous ICDs represent a distinct subtype and are identified by the absence of intracardiac leads and the presence of a parasternal subcutaneous electrode connected to a laterally positioned generator. Leadless pacemakers appear as small, capsule-shaped metallic structures within the right heart, with no associated transvenous leads or generator pocket. Implantable loop recorders appear as thin, rectangular structures without intracardiac leads (20,21). Notably, these cardiac devices must be distinguished from non-cardiac devices that may have a generator in a similar location (deep brain stimulator, vagal nerve simulator, hypoglossal nerve stimulator, etc.) (22-24). Attention to the lead trajectory can help determine the device type, although this may still be challenging, as some devices, such as the remedē (for sleep apnea) and Optimizer (cardiac contractility device), may have leads that course near/through the heart (25,26).

Frequency of device interrogation

Professional societies provide broadly concordant recommendations emphasizing regular scheduled follow-up (Table 4), with additional interrogation when clinical circumstances change. Remote monitoring is endorsed by multiple societies as an effective alternative or adjunct to in-person interrogation for stable patients, allowing earlier detection of device- or rhythm-related abnormalities (11,12,27).

Across societal guidance, additional interrogation is recommended when there is a change in clinical status suggestive of device malfunction or arrhythmia, device alerts or remote monitoring notifications are triggered, battery depletion or lead issues are suspected, prior to or following procedures or exposures that may affect device function, and after delivery of ICD therapy, including shocks or antitachycardia pacing (11,12,27-29).

Methods of device interrogation

In-person interrogation by electrophysiology services

Standard interrogation is performed using manufacturer-specific programmers by trained operators. This approach allows full diagnostic review and device reprogramming when indicated. Most tertiary and many community centers maintain local device services to support inpatient and outpatient interrogation (1,10-12).

Manufacturer device representatives

Industry-employed device representatives frequently assist with interrogation and troubleshooting, particularly in urgent or after-hours settings. While representatives provide technical expertise with proprietary programmers, clinical decision-making and programming changes are typically performed under the direction of the treating physician (11,12,29).

Remote monitoring systems

Remote interrogation and monitoring systems allow scheduled or event-triggered transmission of device data to monitoring centers. These systems are increasingly recommended by professional societies for routine follow-up, early detection of device malfunction, and surveillance of arrhythmia burden (11,12,27-29). Remote monitoring reduces the need for in-person visits while maintaining safety and clinical oversight (27).

EMI

The management of a CIED perioperatively requires assessment of the impact of EMI on device function. Procedures with no risk of EMI do not need further CIED alteration. Otherwise, EMI is potentially problematic in that it can be misinterpreted by the device as cardiac activity. This can result in inappropriate inhibition of pacing and/or delivery of tachytherapy (17).

Monopolar electrosurgery

The most common source of EMI in the operative setting is the monopolar electrosurgical unit (ESU), which cauterizes tissue by passing alternating current between the instrument tip electrode and a large dispersion pad affixed to the patient. If a CIED is located within the electromagnetic field path, the device may misinterpret the interference as a physiologic signal and inappropriately trigger anti-tachyarrhythmia therapies or inhibit anti-bradycardic pacing known as “oversensing” (5). Modern CIEDs are less susceptible to EMI due to technological improvements such as hermetic shielding, bandpass filtering, and bipolar leads (30). Nevertheless, when there is concern for intraoperative EMI, several measures can help mitigate the risk of device interference (Table 5). This includes reducing the ESU power setting and using cut instead of coagulation mode (31). In addition, limiting electrocautery to short bursts may be necessary for patients who cannot tolerate periods of bradycardia from oversensing. If bipolar forceps can be used, the risk of EMI is essentially zero. If a CIED is inactivated, transcutaneous pacing backup and/or external defibrillation capability should be immediately available (6).

Other sources of EMI

EMI may also occur with harmonic scalpels, RF emitting instruments, argon laser, direct current cardioversion, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), lithotripsy, and ionizing radiation, among others. In addition to peri-procedural sources, certain patient implantable devices have also been reported to cause EMI, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation units, neurostimulators (spinal cord and deep brain stimulators), cochlear implants, programmable hydrocephalus shunts, left ventricular assist devices, and cardiac contractility management devices (32).

Factors affecting EMI

Grounding format type positioning

For supraumbilical procedures, the location of the dispersion electrode pad is instrumental in preventing CIED interference. It should be affixed to the patient at least 15 cm from the CIED and in close proximity to the active element of the instrument. Ideally, the pad should be positioned in such a manner that the current path is directed away from the CIED (31,33). Modern electrosurgical equipment allows for automated generator shutoff if poor contact is detected to help prevent unintended tissue injury (34).

Capacitive vs. traditional grounding

While traditional dispersion electrodes have a predictable current vector, this is not the case for underbody or whole-body electrodes, which work by non-contact capacitive energy transfer and require a large surface area (Figure 3) (35). Cases of EMI-induced inappropriate ICD discharge in association with underbody dispersion pad use have been published, including during infraumbilical procedures (36,37). Other recent reports have implicated failed dispersion pad grounding in pediatric burn injuries, which led to a recall and warning about not using Megadyne return electrode for patients under 12 years old (38). Consequently, recent updates in several societal guidelines now include specific recommendations on CIED management when underbody electrodes are used (Table 6) (6,9,39).

Transvenous pacemaker management

Pacemaker leads can sense and pace, and their functions are denoted by a five-letter code as described by the North American Society of Pacing and Electrophysiology/British Pacing and Electrophysiology Group Generic Pacemaker Code (Table 7) (40). For example, a pacemaker set to VOO will asynchronously ventricularly pace while a pacemaker set to DDD will sense in the atria and pace only if a native impulse is not detected at the lower rate setting, then pause and repeat the process in the ventricle. The fourth site, rate modulation, denotes the ability of the pacemaker to increase heart rate to augment cardiac output in instances where there is perceived exertion. When changes are detected, such as acceleration or increased minute ventilation, pacemaker impulse rate increases accordingly. This is an important function to understand, as nearly 90% of pacemakers have this function enabled and mechanical ventilation, intraoperative temperature changes, and movement of the drapes can all be inappropriate triggers (41). The final position, multisite pacing, indicates whether there is more than one lead in a solitary chamber, there is biatrial (rare) or biventricular pacing or a combination of both (40).

When planning non-cardiac surgery for a patient with a permanent transvenous pacemaker, a few key points to take into account are pacemaker dependence [defined as (I) heart rate < 50 bpm resulting in symptoms, atrioventricular block, or sinus arrest, or (II) lack of an escape rhythm], likelihood and type of EMI, and the location of surgery (5,17). Importantly, since a pacemaker may be set with a lower rate greater than 50 bpm, it is possible for a patient to be 100% paced without necessarily meeting this definition of “pacemaker dependent”.

While there is general agreement that the device should be interrogated recently, that exact timing varies by societal recommendation (Table 4). Notably, these societies recommend multi-disciplinary case planning and a tailored approach taking into account type and location of surgery, nature of EMI and pacemaker dependence (17). Unfortunately, they do not offer a universal recommendation regarding magnet placement or device reprograming (Table 8).

Furthermore, there are some notable variations that exist between manufacturers. Biotronik pacers are notable for only responding to a magnet for 8 hours unless the magnet is removed and then replaced several seconds later (43). Biotronik pacers also have 3 programmable responses to magnet placement (auto, asynchronous and synchronous), making preoperative interrogation and knowledge of what mode the pacemaker is set to critical (44). In auto mode, which is the default, application of a magnet will result in 10 asynchronous beats at a rate of 90 bpm (or lower if battery is low) after which the pacer will reset to its original settings at the lower rate limit (44). In asynchronous mode, pacing will occur asynchronously at 90 bpm, while in synchronous mode, pacing will occur at the original lower rate limit (44). Another unique aspect of some older Medtronic devices is that the magnet functionality will be compromised for one hour after the device has been programmed (5). These device-specific attributes should be kept in mind, especially when devices do not appear to respond to magnets in the expected manner.

Transvenous internal cardioverter-defibrillator management

All transvenous ICDs have pacing capability, but not all patients with transvenous ICDs require pacing. Many patients have conditions that put them at risk for sudden cardiac death but have intact sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodal function. With all ICD manufacturers, a magnet should inhibit the ICD function. There are currently no transvenous ICDs being manufactured where placement of a magnet will result in an asynchronous pacing mode. For patients with transvenous ICDs undergoing procedures, some information must be gathered to make a perioperative plan for device management.

The first is the reason for device placement. Some ICDs are placed for primary prevention in patients who are at high risk for lethal arrhythmias, but who have not had sudden cardiac death (45). These patients may have decreased left ventricular systolic function, known arrhythmias, family history of sudden cardiac death, or hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. Some ICDs are placed in patients who have previously had an episode of sudden cardiac death; this is termed secondary prevention. Another piece of information is the underlying rhythm of the patient. Some patients with ICDs do not require pacing by the device. Patients who have no history of sinoatrial or atrioventricular nodal conduction pathology will typically have a single-chamber ICD. This type of device can deliver tachytherapy to the ventricle, but it cannot sense atrial electrical activity for sequential atrial and ventricular pacing. Patients with ICDs who have pacing needs often get a dual-chamber or biventricular ICDs to allow for sequential pacing (45).

The guidance from the different societies on how to manage EMI with ICDs varies to some degree (Table 10). Generally, if a patient with an ICD is pacemaker dependent, and there is a high chance of EMI inhibiting pacing, the device should be reprogrammed to both suspend ICD function and place the pacemaker in asynchronous mode (reprogrammed) (17). In a patient with a transvenous ICD who does not require pacing, application of a magnet is appropriate to suspend tachytherapy during the operative period if the generator is accessible. The magnet response may differ according to ICD brands (Table 11), but typically, if the pacemaker needs to be placed in asynchronous mode, the device should be reprogrammed (17). If a magnet is used to suspend tachytherapy, the magnet should be removed if the patient develops a tachyarrhythmia that would respond to therapy from the ICD. Notably, although a general rule uses the umbilicus as a reference point to determine whether EMI is likely, we believe that tachytherapies should be disabled when EMI is used regardless of the distance from the umbilicus (37). The sensing of EMI occurs at the tip of the ICD lead in the right ventricle and not at the insertion site of the device, which can be problematic, especially in short patients (37).

One special circumstance can occur in patients with some older Sorin (now Microport) transvenous ICDs. In addition to suspending the tachytherapy function, a magnet may increase the set rate of a Microport/Sorin transvenous ICD. At the beginning of its battery life, the rate will be 96 and decrease to 80 bpm at the elective replacement interval. Importantly, this does not cause a change in pacemaker mode (17). Because Microport/Sorin has an overall small market share of pacemakers in the United States, this is a less common problem than in other areas of the world. Regardless, this is unlike the response of other ICDs. It is important for perioperative physicians to know that this rate change may be seen when a magnet is placed on a Microport/Sorin transvenous ICD to avoid the assumption that any change in pacing rate means that a patient has a transvenous pacemaker and not an ICD (Table 11) (17).

Management of non-traditional CIEDs

Non-traditional ICDs

Two non-traditional devices that involve leads are the subcutaneous ICD (Boston Scientific) and the extravascular-ICD (Medtronic) (6). These devices can reduce complications both periprocedurally (cardiac/vascular injury, pneumothorax) and in the long-term (vascular stenosis/thrombosis, valve injury/infection) (46). The subcutaneous ICD consists of a generator that is typically implanted on the left side along the mid-axillary line (Figure 5) (6,47). The lead, which contains a shock coil, is placed subcutaneously anterior to the sternum. This device is primarily designed for patients who may need defibrillation for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Notably, it does not provide antitachycardia pacing. Furthermore, while it can provide pacing at a rate of 50 bpm for up to 30 s after a shock is delivered, it does not otherwise offer pacemaker capability. Defibrillation therapy can be turned off either by reprogramming or by use of a magnet, which should be placed along the upper or lower edge of the generator (47). An audible tone is typically heard for 60 s with magnet placement, although this may not be heard for various reasons, such as disabling the feature, prior exposure to a magnetic field, or deep implantation of the generator. Removal of the magnet should resume prior device function (47).

The Aurora extravascular-ICD (Medtronic) is similar to the subcutaneous ICD in that it has a similar location of the generator and has an extravascular lead (Figure 5) (6). In contrast, the lead, which is recognizable by its sinusoidal shape, is placed substernally (46). As it is closer to the heart, lower energy can be used to achieve defibrillation, which therefore allows for a smaller generator (46). In addition, this device is capable of providing antitachycardia pacing as well as “pause prevention”, a feature that allows for pacing at a rate of 40 bpm. Normally, the pacing activates after a 5-s pause, but this can be increased to 15 s if necessary (6). Application of a magnet will deactivate antitachycardia therapy without interfering with the pause-prevention feature (6). Notably, it should not be assumed that the pause prevention feature is active as some patients may have had this feature deactivated due to it causing discomfort (46).

Leadless pacemakers

Leadless pacemakers are a major class of non-traditional devices that have emerged in the last decade and can avoid many of the aforementioned complications. These small devices are implanted and completely contained in the heart in either the atrium or the ventricle (Figure 5). The two major devices are the Micra (Medtronic) and the AVEIR (Abbott). The Micra VR and AV are both implanted in the right ventricle, but the AV is able to provide AV synchrony through an accelerometer that mechanically senses atrial contraction (48). Second generations of both devices, the VR2 and AV2, were subsequently released (49). An important feature of the Micra AV is the AV conduction mode switch, which allows for intermittently changing the mode from VDD to VVI at 40 bpm to allow for native conduction in patients who have a ventricular rate above 40; if the ventricular rate is below 40 bpm, the mode automatically switches back to VDD. If the ventricular rate is >40 bpm, however, the device will remain in VVI, which may be problematic for patients who cannot tolerate bradycardia or those who have a sinus rate above 80 bpm with 2:1 AV block (50). With the Micra AV2, however, the conduction mode switch rate can be programmed between 40–70 bpm, allowing greater flexibility. This switch can also be turned off in either model. Unfortunately, the Micra devices are not magnet-responsive, so changes require reprogramming (6).

The AVEIR can be used either as a single system (VR) for right ventricular pacing, right atrial pacing (AR) or as a dual system (DR) for both right atrial and ventricular sensing and pacing (6). The DR is the only dual-system available and requires implantation of separate devices into each chamber, which then communicate to provide atrioventricular synchrony. Unlike the Micra, the AVEIR is magnet-responsive and will change to an asynchronous mode at a rate of 100 bpm but may decrease to as low as 85 bpm depending on the battery life (Table 9, AOO/VOO/DOO). In cases where there is a loss of communication between the two devices in the DR system, various backup methods exist depending on whether there is a complete or partial communication loss (48). Remote monitoring is possible with both devices. While the Micra uses RF for communication, the AVEIR uses a novel method of conductive telemetry (51). While not specific to leadless pacemakers, it is possible that artificial intelligence could be utilized to enhance remote monitoring by detecting CIED problems sooner or streamlining serious alerts (52).

Leadless pacemakers are also being investigated for novel uses. First, they can be implanted in patients with challenging anatomy, including single ventricles with Fontan physiology (53-55). Other potential uses include left bundle branch area pacing (56). In addition, the EMPOWER LP system (investigational) could potentially provide antitachycardia pacing and work in conjunction with the subcutaneous ICD (57). Biotronik is also investigating a new leadless pacemaker called LivIQ (58). Finally, the Wireless Stimulation Endocardially for Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (WiSE CRT) system offers left ventricular pacing and can be combined with a right ventricular leadless pacemaker for biventricular pacing (57,59).

Perioperative management strategies

The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) practice advisory, European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) consensus, and British Heart Rhythm Society guideline do not offer any specific guidance for leadless pacemakers other than lack of magnet responsiveness for the Micra (5,8). The American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) statement mentions that the principles of device management are similar to transvenous pacemakers; furthermore, it states that interrogation can be obtained within 3 months prior to the scheduled procedure to obtain the relevant information (6). Use of a magnet may be challenging compared to transvenous pacemakers, especially in obese patients. The ACC/AHA perioperative guidelines state that a leadless pacemaker only needs reprogramming to an asynchronous mode when EMI is above the umbilicus in a pacemaker-dependent patient; otherwise, no change is necessary (9).

The subcutaneous and extravascular ICDs may be more prone to EMI given the increased sensing area (9,46). Regarding the subcutaneous ICD, the ACC/AHA perioperative guideline suggests that defibrillation therapy should be turned off if EMI will be present above the groin, but that no intervention is required if EMI will be below the groin. If a magnet is used, the audible tone should be present, and the device should be reprogrammed if no tone is heard. Furthermore, a significant amount of EMI above the diaphragm or any position other than supine should prompt consideration of reprogramming over a magnet (9). While no recommendation was made for the extravascular ICD, the considerations may be similar since both the subcutaneous and extravascular ICDs are more prone to EMI compared to transvenous systems (6). The EHRA consensus suggests caution in patients undergoing sternotomy and disabling therapy during RF ablation.

Management of CIEDs in special situations

Several medical procedures outside the operating room can produce electromagnetic or mechanical interference with CIEDs (5). The following section briefly summarizes practical recommendations for several non-surgical sources of EMI—direct-current cardioversion, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), RF ablation, extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy, and ECT (Table 12).

External cardioversion

External cardioversion can be performed safely in patients with contemporary pacemakers and ICDs, but device function may still be affected. Key procedural steps include placing pads in an anteroposterior position to avoid current flow across the device, using biphasic shocks at the lowest effective energy, and disabling ICD therapies before the procedure. Post-shock interrogation remains advisable, given occasionally delayed changes in thresholds or device settings (5-7).

MRI

MRI exposes CIEDs to static, gradient, and RF fields that can influence device behavior and the lead-tissue interface. The static magnetic field can exert mild translational and torque forces on the generator due to residual ferromagnetic components, although modern devices contain very small amounts of ferromagnetic material and clinically significant displacement does not occur. Gradient magnetic fields may induce currents along pacing and defibrillation leads, resulting in oversensing, pacing inhibition, or unintended myocardial capture without structural injury to the leads. RF fields can couple with leads and cause heating at the electrode-tissue interface, occasionally producing transient increases in pacing thresholds, the principal theoretical concern for non-conditional systems (60). Advances in device engineering, including reduced ferromagnetic content, more effective shielding of internal circuitry, improved feedthrough filtering, revised lead coil configurations, and predictable Hall-sensor mechanisms, have improved the resilience of modern pacemakers and ICDs to MRI fields. MRI-specific programming modes further limit interference by disabling ICD therapies and modifying pacing behavior (60).

Clinical evidence from evaluations of MRI-conditional pacemakers and ICDs demonstrates that 1.5-T scanning is safe when manufacturer restrictions are followed (61,62). Consensus documents recommend pre-scan interrogation to confirm system integrity and MRI conditionality, identify pacing dependence, and program asynchronous pacing when needed (5-7,61,63-65). Non-tracking or inhibited modes are appropriate for patients with a stable underlying rhythm. During imaging, continuous physiological monitoring with ECG (when interpretable) and pulse oximetry is advised, with advanced cardiac life support-trained personnel and external defibrillation capability immediately available. Device settings should be restored afterward, with attention to any changes in sensing, impedance, or threshold measurements.

MRI may also be performed in patients with non-conditional (legacy) systems when necessary, provided strict institutional protocols are followed (6,7,61,63-66). ICD therapies must be suspended, pre- and post-scan interrogation performed, and continuous monitoring maintained. Evidence from large registries shows a very low rate of clinically important events, with power-on reset being the most frequent interaction and generally manageable without long-term consequences (67). Recent guidance from the Joint British Societies (63) and the AHA scientific statement(6) supports MRI in non-conditional systems when MRI is the preferred diagnostic study and safeguards are in place.

Specific device and lead configurations warrant additional caution. Abandoned or epicardial leads may experience greater RF coupling in bench models, although a recent systematic review has not demonstrated significant adverse effects when scanning is performed under controlled conditions (62). Leadless pacemakers often lack a reliable magnet response and may require direct programming to an asynchronous mode in pacing-dependent patients (5,6,62). Subcutaneous and extravascular ICDs, which rely on larger sensing vectors, may be more prone to oversensing MRI-related signals and should follow ICD-specific MRI precautions (5,6,62).

RF catheter ablation

RF ablation can produce transient electrical interference in patients with pacemakers and ICDs, most often appearing as brief oversensing, undersensing, pacing inhibition, or a switch to noise-reversion pacing during energy delivery. These effects resolve once ablation stops and have not been associated with permanent generator malfunction in contemporary systems. Mechanical interaction with transvenous leads is possible during catheter manipulation, particularly when leads are newly implanted. Dislodgment is uncommon and occurs almost exclusively in leads that have not yet matured (68,69). Thermal effects near the lead tip may occur if RF lesions are delivered very close to the distal electrode (70).

Recommended measures include suspending ICD therapies before ablation, programming asynchronous pacing in pacemaker-dependent patients, avoiding RF delivery directly on or immediately adjacent to lead electrodes, and interrogating the device afterward to confirm stable parameters. When these measures are used, RF catheter ablation is considered safe in patients with implanted devices (5-7).

ECT

ECT can produce brief autonomic shifts that may trigger transient arrhythmias, but modern pacemakers and ICDs generally tolerate the procedure well, with no device malfunctions reported and only rare, self-limited arrhythmias (71). Serious cardiac events are uncommon and typically reflect underlying cardiovascular disease rather than device interference. Transient oversensing or brief pacing inhibition may occur during the stimulus or early seizure phase, but sustained pacing failure or permanent device dysfunction has not been observed with modern systems (72,73).

For ICDs, tachyarrhythmia therapies should be suspended during treatment, with continuous electrocardiogram monitoring and immediate access to external defibrillation. Pacemakers generally require no reprogramming, although demand-mode inhibition may occasionally occur; post-treatment interrogation is advisable to confirm stable sensing, capture, and lead parameters (5-7).

Extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy

Extracorporeal lithotripsy can interfere with pacemakers and ICDs through mechanical vibration and electromagnetic discharge, but clinically important device malfunction is uncommon (74). Guidelines recommend avoiding positioning the shock-wave focus near the pulse generator, as proximity increases the likelihood of oversensing or temporary pacing inhibition (5-7). ICD therapies should be disabled during treatment, with continuous electrocardiogram monitoring and external defibrillation capability available. Pacemakers generally do not require reprogramming unless pacing dependence or oversensing is noted. A post-procedure device check is reasonable if inhibition, arrhythmias, or symptoms occur.

CIED infection

CIED infection is associated with increased morbidity and mortality and has a reported incidence of up to 3.4% (75). The highest periods of risk are likely during the time of implantation and any subsequent interventions concerning the device. Risk factors include implanters with less experience, long procedures, requiring lead adjustments, an abdominal pocket, and having more leads (75). Other risk factors related to patient comorbidities include anticoagulation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, and end-stage renal disease, which confers the highest risk. The other typical scenario for infection is bacteremia. CIED infection is serious, given that it typically must be removed. Proper sterile technique is critical, and some implanters have also utilized additional measures such as sponges containing antibiotics (75,76). Antibiotic administration is crucial during CIED implantation. Secondary prophylaxis is usually reserved for patients who are undergoing either device replacement because of infection or incision and drainage at another infected area. In contrast, antibiotic prophylaxis is not typically needed for patients who undergo unrelated procedures, such as cardiac, gastrointestinal, dental, or respiratory (75).

Strengths and limitations

This narrative review draws from several consensus-type documents within the last several years to inform clinical decision-making around CIED management perioperatively. The strengths relate to reporting from multiple sources that are recent as well as the comprehensive depth. Nevertheless, a complete summary of all the available guideline documents is not possible in a single narrative review, and referring to the individual guidelines may be required for specific details. Furthermore, these guideline documents may not include all the most recent literature relating to CIEDs as they tend to focus on the broader picture. Finally, there is device-specific literature apart from the guidelines that can direct management, which can be particularly useful with nontraditional devices such as leadless pacemakers and non-transvenous ICDs (46,48,77,78).

Conclusions

We have summarized herein the most recent major societal guidance regarding the perioperative management of CIEDs. While the major principles are generally similar between all sources, a universal approach is lacking. Nevertheless, clinicians should be aware of the various considerations for both traditional and non-traditional devices to be able to safely care for patients perioperatively.
