The Linearized Vlasov–Maxwell System as a Hamiltonian System ( Second Revision, Clean Version )
Dominik Bell, Martin Campos Pinto, Stefan Possanner, Eric Sonnendrücker

Abstract
We present a Hamiltonian formulation for the linearized Vlasov–Maxwell system with a Maxwellian background distribution function. We discuss the geometric properties of the model at the continuous level, and how to discretize the model in the GEMPIC framework [ 17 ] . This method allows us to preserve the structure of the system at the semi-discrete level. To integrate the model in time, we employ a Poisson splitting and discuss how to integrate each subsystem separately. We test the model against the direct delta-f method, which is the non-geometric pendant of our model. The first test case is the weak Landau damping, where our model exhibits the same physical properties for short simulations, but enjoys better long-time stability and energy conservation due to its geometric construction. These advantages becomes even more pronounced for the simulation of Bernstein waves, our second test case, where the noise in the direct delta-f method washes out all features of the dispersion relation whereas our model is able to reproduce the full spectrum correctly. The model is implemented in the open-source Python library STRUPHY [ 32 , 37 ] .

Introduction

Like many other physical models
[
29
]
, the Vlasov–Maxwell system
[
39
]
possesses a rather rich structure, most prominently, there exists a non-canonical Hamiltonian system
[
24
,
30
,
28
]
which consists of a Hamiltonian functional, that has the interpretation of physical energy, and a Poisson bracket, which generates the equations of motion. The Casimir functionals of this bracket correspond to physical conservation properties, most importantly the divergence-free magnetic field and Gauss’ law.

Geometric methods aim to discretize the infinite-dimensional system so that the physical (Hamiltonian) structure is also present in the finite-dimensional approximation space, rather than simply discretizing the model equations of motion. This construction guarantees long-time stability due to the boundedness of the Hamiltonian
[
8
]
. Since their inception, geometric Particle-in-cell (PIC) methods have been discussed and extended manifold in the literature
[
41
,
23
,
35
,
9
]
. The structure-preserving discretization techniques used in this work are closely related to the GEMPIC
[
17
,
31
,
16
]
framework. It consists of two components: the discretization of the distribution function
f
using a PIC method and the Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC)
[
1
,
2
]
method using B-splines as basis functions
[
4
]
for the discretization of the electromagnetic fields. Discretizing the Hamiltonian structure in this way guarantees structure-preservation at the semi-discrete level.

The PIC method is a popular choice for transport equations because markers are evolved along the characteristics of the equation and integrals are computed in the sense of a Monte Carlo expectation value. However, the distribution function
f
appearing in the Vlasov–Maxwell model is defined on a six-dimensional phase space, which makes simulations very costly if one wants to achieve acceptable noise levels. There are (at least) two ways to improve upon this situation: either a model reduction such as averaging out the gyration of the particles around the magnetic field lines yielding 5-dimensional models
[
11
,
5
]
or considering the model near thermal equilibrium where a fluid description is a good approximation
[
19
]
. However, this has the downfall of potentially not capturing all the physical effects. The other way is to improve the numerical algorithms. For particle methods, the goal is often to reduce the noise.

One such noise reduction method is the control variate method, which assumes that one can analytically evaluate expectation values against a function subtracted from the distribution function to compute integral values more accurately, thus increasing the overall accuracy. To illustrate the advantage of a control variate, let us look at an example: assume that we want to sample the function

f(x)=1+\delta\,\cos\left(2\pi\frac{x}{L}\right)

(1)

on a domain
[0,L]
with
\delta
some small number,
\absolutevalue{\delta}\ll 1
. In this case, the control variate is the simple function
f_{0}(x)=1
, and we only draw markers from
f_{1}(x)=\delta\,\cos\left(2\pi x/L\right)
. The difference between drawing from the full
f(x)
and drawing only from
f_{1}(x)
is represented in fig.
1
(both using the same pseudo-random sampling technique). For a number of markers
N_{p}=10^{5}
the control variate achieves a variance (with respect to the analytical solution) which is three orders of magnitude lower than the full-
f
method. However, applying this procedure to the distribution function in the Vlasov–Maxwell model does
a priori
break the structure of the model because the control variate terms are introduced into the equations.

The aim of this work is to bring the concepts of Hamiltonian structure-preservation and noise reduction using a control variate together. If it is known that the solution of the Vlasov-Maxwell system will stay close to a given equilibrium, one can consider the linearized model equations instead of solving the full system; this equilibrium is then used as the control variate. We present a Hamiltonian framework for the linearized Vlasov–Maxwell system using an equilibrium as a control-variate and discretize it in a structure-preserving manner. We compare our model to non-geometric discretizations of the non-linear Vlasov–Maxwell equations and of the linearized equations. For the weak Landau damping, we find that our model reproduces the correct physical predictions for short times, i.e., the damping rate and damping level are the same for both. However, the structure-preservation shows its advantages in terms of long-time stability and energy conservation. The weak Landau damping is simulated both in Cartesian and curvilinear coordinates. As a second test, we check for plasma waves appearing under a background magnetic field, where we see that our code reproduces correctly the dispersion relation including the electromagnetic waves as well as the Bernstein waves.

This work is organized as follows: In section

Then we discuss the discretization of this geometric delta-f model in a structure-preserving way using the GEMPIC framework. The PIC method is presented in sec.
3

[
17
]
. We derive the particle equations of motion, and subsequently discretize the field in sec.
4
, yielding the fully semi-discrete system. In order to integrate the system in time with energy conservation, we employ a Poisson-splitting and show how to numerically integrate each subsystem them in time (sec.
5

Python
-library
STRUPHY

[
32
,
37
]
. In sec.
6

7

.

The following bracket is a Poisson bracket

(8a)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\int_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{v}-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta f_{1}}\right]f_{0}\;\text{d}\mathbf{x}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\int_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}f_{0}\left[\mathbf{v}\cdot\left(\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\right)\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta f_{1}}-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta f_{1}}\right)\right.

(8c)

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left.\left(\mathbf{E}_{0}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\right)\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta f_{1}}-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta f_{1}}\right)\right]\;\text{d}\mathbf{x}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}\,.

Proof:
We prove that this bracket (

Curvilinear Coordinates

Following
[
13
]

3.1
Discrete Particle Equations of Motion

We consider a transport equation with a source term in curvilinear coordinates

\partial_{t}f_{1}+\mathbf{A}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}f_{1}+\mathbf{A}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f_{1}=S({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\,,

(23)

where we assume that always

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cdot\left(\sqrt{g}\mathbf{A}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=0\,,

(24)

as is the case for (
2a
) and (

).

Computing integrals of arbitrary functions

against the distribution function

can be done either by directly plugging in the PIC approximation

f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\approx f_{1,h}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}w_{p}(t)\;\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t))\;\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\,,

(25)

or multiplying the integrand by
\frac{s({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}
, with

a time-independent probability distribution function that is constant along the characteristics, such that the weights are related to

w_{p}(t)\equiv\frac{f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}=\frac{f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s(t=0,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t=0),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t=0))}=\frac{f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s_{0,p}}

is not conserved along the characteristics. In both cases, the particles’ positions

\sqrt{g}\,s({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})

The equations of motion for the particles are derived by demanding that (
25

23
) in a weak sense. We start by looking at the time derivative of the integral of

multiplied by a test function

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}=\int h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\partial_{t}f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(27a)

\displaystyle\quad=\int h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\left[-\mathbf{A}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{A}_{2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})+S({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\right]\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(27b)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\int f_{1,h}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\left[\mathbf{A}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{A}_{2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\right]\sqrt{g}\,\frac{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(27c)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int S({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\,\frac{h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(27d)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{w_{p}(t)}{N}\left[\mathbf{A}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))+\mathbf{A}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\right]

(27e)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}S({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\,\underbrace{\frac{h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}}_{=\frac{h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))}{s_{0,p}}}\,,

(27f)

where we used the Monte Carlo method to approximate the integral in line (
27d
). Comparing this with the expression obtained by directly plugging in (
25
)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int f_{1,h}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})h({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}=\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}w_{p}(t)h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))

(28a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\left[h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\frac{\text{d}w_{p}}{\text{d}t}+w_{p}(t)\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\cdot\frac{\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}{\text{d}t}+w_{p}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}h({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\cdot\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_{p}}{\text{d}t}\right]\,,

(28b)

we can read off the equations of motion for the particle positions
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}
, velocities
\mathbf{v}_{p}
, and weights
w_{p}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\,,

(29a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\,,

(29b)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}w_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{0,p}}S({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))\,.

(29c)

Applying this to (
20
), the equations of motion for the particle positions, velocities, and weights read:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}(t)\,,

(30a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}DL^{-T}\mathbf{E}_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t))+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbf{v}_{p}(t)\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\,DL\,\mathbf{B}_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t))\,,

(30b)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}w_{p}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{0,p}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\left[DL^{-T}\mathbf{E}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t))\right]\cdot\mathbf{v}_{p}(t)f_{0,p}\,,

(30c)

where we abbreviated
f_{0,p}=f_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t))
and also
s_{0,p}=s({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}(t=0),\mathbf{v}_{p}(t=0))
.

3.2
Discrete Hamiltonian Structure

We now discretize the Hamiltonian framework given by (
21
) and (
22
).

3.2.1
Discrete Conserved Hamiltonian

We recall the Hamiltonian (
21
)

H=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}\int_{\hat{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{f_{1}^{2}}{f_{0}}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}+H_{\text{Maxwell}}\,.

(31)

The first term can be discretized using the Monte Carlo method

\displaystyle\int\frac{f_{1}^{2}}{f_{0}}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\left(f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})\right)^{2}}{f_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}\frac{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v})}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(32a)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\frac{\left(f_{1}(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\right)^{2}}{s(t,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\,f_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})}

(32b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\frac{s_{0,p}\,w_{p}(t)^{2}}{f_{0,p}}\,.

(32c)

We will discuss the discretization of the fields in more detail below, so for now we replace
\mathbf{E}_{1}\to\mathbf{E}_{h}
and
\mathbf{B}_{1}\to\mathbf{B}_{h}
. We note that the weak Ampère equation (
19
) is discretized as:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int\mathbf{F}_{h}\cdot G^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{h}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\times\mathbf{F}_{h}\right)\cdot G\,\mathbf{B}_{h}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\epsilon}\int DL^{-T}\mathbf{F}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}f_{1,h}\,\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(33a)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\times\mathbf{F}_{h}\right)\cdot G\,\mathbf{B}_{h}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{F}_{h}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{p}\,w_{p}\,,

(33b)

where we have plugged in the PIC approach (
25
) for the distribution function
h
.

3.2.2
Discrete Poisson Bracket

In order to discretize the bracket (
22
) we need to generalize the discussion in
[
17
]
in order to figure out what the discrete versions of

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\,,

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}

(34)

are. We demand that the variation of a functional
\mathcal{F}[f_{1}]
shall not change under the discretization (at least in a weak sense). We also know that such a functional will become a function
F[w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}]
of the particle positions, velocities, and weights. We demand that the variation of both these quantities be the same

\delta\mathcal{F}[f_{1}]=\delta F[w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}]\,.

(35)

The left-hand side reads:

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{F}[f_{1}]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\delta f_{1,h}\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(36a)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\sum_{p}\left[\frac{\partial f_{1,h}(w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})}{\partial w_{p}}\delta w_{p}+\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}f_{1,h}(w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right.

(36b)

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59605pt\left.+\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_{p}}f_{1,h}(w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\vphantom{\frac{\partial h_{h}}{\partial w_{p}}}\right]\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(36c)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\left[\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\delta w_{p}+w_{p}\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right.

(36d)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\left.+w_{p}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_{p}}\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\right]\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(36e)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\left[\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\delta w_{p}-w_{p}\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right.

(36f)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\left.-w_{p}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\right]\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(36g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\int\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\delta w_{p}+w_{p}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}+w_{p}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\right]\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\delta(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{p})\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}

(36h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\delta w_{p}+w_{p}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}+w_{p}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\right]\,,

(36i)

where we used that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})=-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\delta({\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\,.

(37)

The right-hand side of (
35
) reads

\delta F[w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}]=\sum_{p}\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\delta w_{p}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(38)

and by comparing terms, we find the following replacement rules

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{w_{p}}{N}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{w_{p}}{N}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\,.

(39)

Thus, we can discretize (
22
) using the Monte Carlo method, and plugging in these replacement rules yields

\displaystyle\left\{F,G\right\}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\times\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{h}}\right)\cdot G\,\frac{\delta G}{\delta\mathbf{B}_{h}}-\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\times\frac{\delta G}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{h}}\right)\cdot G\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{h}}\right]\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

(40)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\left[\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\delta G}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{h}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})-\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mathbf{E}_{h}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p})\right]\cdot DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &+\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}\,w_{p}}\left[DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\right)\right.\\
&\hskip 99.58464pt\left.+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbf{E}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\sqrt{g}DL\,\mathbf{B}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\right)\right]\,.\end{aligned}

We note that in comparison to (
22
), we have removed the factor of
\frac{1}{2}
in the last term, which was there to produce the correct equations of motion. Since the notion of integration by parts does not exist in the discrete setting, this factor would still appear in the equations of motion, and hence we removed it. It can be easily checked that this bracket and the Hamiltonian from sec. (
3.2.1
) produces the correct particle equations of motion.

We follow the notation of
[

,
13
]
by denoting the basis functions using
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{k}
for scalar-valued spaces and
\mathbb{\Lambda}^{k}
for vector-valued spaces, where
k
indicates in which FE space
V_{h}^{k}
we are. So the charge density
\rho
lives in
H^{1}(\hat{\Omega})
; its FE approximation will be written as

H^{1}(\hat{\Omega})\ni\quad\rho({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\approx\rho_{h}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})={\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\quad\in V^{0}_{h}\,,

(42)

and analogously for any function
f
that lives in
L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})

L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})\ni\quad f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\approx f_{h}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\mathbf{f}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{3}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\quad\in V^{3}_{h}\,.

(43)

For the vector-valued spaces, we have, for example, the electric field
\mathbf{E}\in H(\text{curl},\hat{\Omega})
and
\mathbf{B}\in H(\text{div},\hat{\Omega})
, which are approximated as follows

\displaystyle H(\text{curl},\hat{\Omega})\ni\quad\mathbf{E}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\approx\left(\mathbf{E}_{h}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\quad\in V^{1}_{h}\,,

(44a)

\displaystyle H(\text{div},\hat{\Omega})\ni\quad\mathbf{B}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\approx\left(\mathbf{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\quad\in V^{2}_{h}\,.

(44b)

We also denote mass matrices in these spaces by
\mathbb{M}_{k}
.

4.2
The Full Semi-discrete System

After the FEEC discretization, the particle equations of motion (
30
) become

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}

\displaystyle=DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(45a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{v}_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{g({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})}}\,DL({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{b}_{0}\,,

(45b)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}w_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{0}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\epsilon}\left[DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{v}_{p}\,f_{0,p}\,.

(45c)

The Ampère equation (in weak form) (
33
) becomes

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}=\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}w_{p}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(46)

and Faraday’s law (in strong form) (
18
) becomes

\,.\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{b}_{1}=-\mathbb{C}\mathbf{e}_{1}

(47)

The Hamiltonian (
21
) is finally discretized as

H=\frac{1}{2N}\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}\sum_{p}\frac{s_{0,p}\,w_{p}^{2}}{f_{0,p}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}\,,

(48)

and the Poisson bracket (
40
) becomes (under the replacement rules found in
[
17
]
)

\displaystyle\left\{F,G\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{T}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{b}_{1}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{b}_{1}}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}

(49a)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\left[\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1})^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}\mathbb{M}^{-1}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\cdot DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}\right]

(49b)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\left[DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\right)\right.

(49c)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left.+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1})^{T}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2})^{T}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\right)\right]\,,

(49d)

where we have suppressed all arguments for increased readability.

The bracket (
49
) is in fact a Poisson bracket as it is manifestly anti-symmetric and verifies the Leibniz rule. It remains to show the Jacobi identity

\left\{\left\{U_{i},U_{j}\right\},U_{k}\right\}+\left\{\left\{U_{k},U_{i}\right\},U_{j}\right\}+\left\{\left\{U_{j},U_{k}\right\},U_{i}\right\}=0\,,

(50)

for
U_{i}\in\left\{({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})_{i},(\mathbf{v}_{p})_{i},w_{p},(\mathbf{e}_{1})_{i},(\mathbf{b}_{1})_{i}\right\}
. We only consider non-vanishing, non-constant brackets. We also note that

\left\{U_{p},U_{q}\right\}=\delta_{p,q}\qquad\qquad\forall\,U\in\left\{w,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v}\right\}\,,

(51)

i.e., the particle part of the bracket vanishes if the arguments are not from the same particle. We start with

\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\}=-\left\{\mathbf{e}_{1},w_{p}\right\}=\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\epsilon}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\,\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\right)

(52)

which is a function of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}
and
\mathbf{v}_{p}
, thus only a further bracket with
w_{p}
does not vanish. Then the Jacobi identity reads

\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{w_{p},w_{p}\right\},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{e}_{1},w_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=0

(53)

because of the antisymmetry of the bracket. Next, we consider

\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}=-\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},w_{p}\right\}=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\,DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}

(54)

which vanishes by the same argument. Finally

\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}=-\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},w_{p}\right\}=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\right)

(55)

and also this gives a zero Jacobi identity, so (
50
) is verified for all coordinate functions and therefore for arbitrary functions of them.

We show that the bracket (
49
) is in fact a Poisson bracket. Antisymmetry and Leibniz rule are again manifest from the construction. We still need to show the Jacobi identity

\left\{\left\{U_{i},U_{j}\right\},U_{k}\right\}+\left\{\left\{U_{k},U_{i}\right\},U_{j}\right\}+\left\{\left\{U_{j},U_{k}\right\},U_{i}\right\}=0\,,

(56)

for
U_{i}\in\left\{({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})_{i},(\mathbf{v}_{p})_{i},w_{p},(\mathbf{e}_{1})_{i},(\mathbf{b}_{1})_{i}\right\}
. We only consider non-vanishing, non-constant brackets. We also note that

\left\{U_{p},U_{q}\right\}=\delta_{p,q}\qquad\qquad\forall\,U\in\left\{w,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\mathbf{v}\right\}\,,

(57)

i.e., the particle part of the bracket vanishes if the arguments are not from the same particle.

We start with

\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\}=-\left\{\mathbf{e}_{1},w_{p}\right\}=\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\epsilon}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\,\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\right)\,,

(58)

which is a function of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}
and
\mathbf{v}_{p}
, thus only a further bracket with
w_{p}
does not vanish. Then the Jacobi identity reads

\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{w_{p},w_{p}\right\},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{e}_{1},w_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=0\,,

(59)

because of the antisymmetry of the bracket. Next, we consider

\displaystyle\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},w_{p}\right\}=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\,DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(60a)

\displaystyle\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\}=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\right)\,,

(60b)

which depend on all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}
,
\mathbf{v}_{p}
, and
w_{p}
, and thus have non-vanishing brackets with
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}
and
\mathbf{v}_{p}
. The following Jacobi identity terms

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{w_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},w_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(61a)

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(61b)

as well as

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{w_{p},w_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(62a)

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}-\left\{\left\{w_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(62b)

vanish by the same argument as above. However, there are also mixed terms

\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}\,,

(63)

which could be non-vanishing. To check this, we have to compute the nested brackets

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\right)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{p}}\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}

(64a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}^{2}}\,DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\,,

(64b)

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\,\left(DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{p}}\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\}

(64c)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\,\left(DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}w_{p}^{2}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(DL^{-T}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\right)\right)\,,

(64d)

and so we see immediately that

\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}=-\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}\,,

(65)

and so

\left\{\left\{w_{p},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{\mathbf{v}_{p},w_{p}\right\},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}\right\}+\left\{\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\},w_{p}\right\}=0\,,

(66)

because
\left\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p},\mathbf{v}_{p}\right\}=0
. Therefore (
56
) is verified for all coordinate functions and thus for arbitrary functions of them.

5
Poisson Splitting & Time Discretization

We employ a Poisson splitting of the matrix (
49
) in order to preserve the Hamiltonian (
48
) exactly in each substep. We split five terms and subsequently arrive at five substeps, which we can combine to achieve any order in time. The framework of
STRUPHY

[
32
,
37
]
allows us to choose flexibly between the Lie-Trotter splitting (a first-order method in time) or Strang splitting (a second-order method).

5.1
Maxwell Substep

The first term in the bracket (
49
) reads

\left\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\right\}_{1}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\mathbf{b}_{1}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\mathbf{b}_{1}}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}\,,

(67)

and yields the discrete Maxwell equations in vacuum

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{b}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{C}\mathbf{e}_{1}\,.

(68b)

This system of equations can be written as a
2\times 2
system

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\\
-\mathbb{C}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(69a)

which can be solved using a Crank-Nicolson scheme as the discretization in time and the Schur method
[
12
]
. Since the Hamiltonian is at most quadratic in the variables, the Crank-Nicolson scheme is a discrete gradient and therefore energy-preserving.

The update for
\mathbf{e}_{1}
then reads

(M/D)\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n+1}=\left(\mathbb{M}_{1}+\mathbb{A}\mathbb{H}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n}-2\mathbb{A}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{n}\,,

(70)

with the matrices

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta t\;}{2}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\,,

(71)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta t\;^{2}}{4}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbb{C}\,,

(72)

\displaystyle(M/D)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{1}-\mathbb{A}\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{M}_{1}+\frac{\Delta t\;^{2}}{4}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbb{C}\,,

(73)

and the update for
\mathbf{b}_{1}
reads

\mathbf{b}_{1}^{n+1}=\mathbf{b}_{1}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t\;}{2}\mathbb{C}\left(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n+1}+\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n}\right)\,.

(74)

Note that for solving (
70
), we have to invert the matrix
(M/D)
, and thus energy conservation can only be guaranteed up to the accuracy of the inversion solver.

5.2
Coupling Substep

The second term of the Poisson bracket,

\left\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\right\}_{2}=\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial w_{p}}DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1})^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{1}}\mathbb{M}^{-1}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\cdot DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial w_{p}}\right]\,,

(75)

couples the particles and the fields through the weights and the electric field. The equations read

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}w_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\left[DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(76a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}w_{p}\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\cdot\left(DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\,.

(76b)

We can rewrite this by introducing a vector and matrices with sizes of the particle number:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{W}\right)_{p}

\displaystyle\equiv w_{p}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{W}\right)_{p}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\left(DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\right)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\right)_{p}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\,\epsilon}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}\cdot\left(DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\,,

(77)

which have sizes

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle\,\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}

\displaystyle\,\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{p},N_{1}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\,\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{1},N_{p}}\,.

(78)

(
76
) can be written as a
2\times 2
system

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{W}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathbb{E}\\
\mathbb{W}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{W}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(79)

so again, we can do an energy-conserving discretization in time using the Crank-Nicolson scheme and use the Schur complement
[
12
]
to solve the resulting system. Here, the number of markers is usually much larger than the number of basis functions of
V_{h}^{1}
:
N_{p}\gg N_{1}
, so using the Schur complement shows its strength in computational cost while still being energy-conserving. We find that the update rule for the electric FE coefficients

(M/D)\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n+1}=\left[\mathbb{M}_{1}-\frac{\Delta t\;^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}\right]\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n}-\Delta t\;\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}\,,

(80)

where

(M/D)=\mathbb{M}_{1}-\frac{\Delta t\;^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}\,.

(81)

The quantities
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}
include summation over the particles, which is called accumulation in
STRUPHY

[
37
]
. Written out, they read

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\,v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{p}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\cdot\left(DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\left(DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\right)^{T}\,,

(82a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N\lx@nobreakspace\,\epsilon}\sum_{p}w_{p}\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,.

(82b)

Note that the accumulation matrix
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}
is symmetric. Finally,
\mathbf{W}^{n+1}
is computed as

\mathbf{W}^{n+1}=\mathbf{W}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t\;}{2}\mathbb{W}\left(\mathbf{e}^{n+1}+\mathbf{e}^{n}\right)\,,

(83)

which can be computed element-wise: for each component (i.e., each particle), we have

w_{p}^{n+1}=w_{p}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t\;}{2}\frac{f_{0,p}}{s_{0,p}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}\,\epsilon}\left(DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\right)\cdot\left[\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\right)^{T}\left(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n+1}+\mathbf{e}_{1}^{n}\right)\right]\,.

(84)

5.3
Space Advection Substep

The third term of the Poisson bracket reads

\left\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\right\}_{3}=\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\left[DL^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}}\right)\right]\,,

(85)

and yields the equations of motion

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}=DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,.

(86)

This can be solved very accurately using an explicit Runge-Kutta method of order 4, which will not conserve the energy exactly. For our use cases
n_{0}(\mathbf{x})=const.
, and therefore the Hamiltonian does not depend on the positions and is thus conserved during this step.

5.4
Electric Lorentz Substep

The fourth term of the bracket

\left\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\right\}=\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}\epsilon}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\left(DL^{-T}(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1})^{T}\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\right)\,,

(87)

is the electric part of the Lorentz force of the background fields

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{v}_{p}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{e}_{0}\,,

(88)

and can be solved analytically.

5.5
Magnetic Lorentz Substep

The fifth term of the Poisson bracket

\left\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\right\}=\frac{N}{\alpha^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}\epsilon}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{s_{0,p}w_{p}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}DL\,(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2})^{T}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial w_{p}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial w_{p}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{p}}\right)\,,

(89)

is the magnetic part of the Lorentz force of the background fields

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{v}_{p}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbf{v}_{p}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{g({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})}}\,DL({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{b}_{0}\,.

(90)

This system can also be solved analytically.

6.2
Landau Damping

Landau damping
[
21
,
20
]
is a widely known effect in plasmas where an initial perturbation is damped exponentially by the particle-field interaction. The initial condition for the distribution functions reads

f(t=0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{\text{th}}^{2}}^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{\bv}^{2}}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\right)\,\left[1+\delta\cos\left(kx\right)\vphantom{\sum}\right]\,,

(92)

with parameters
v_{\text{th}}=1
,
k=0.5
, and
\delta=10^{-3}
. The initial electric field is computed from the initial distribution function by solving a Poisson equation. The magnetic field is initialized as zero and should remain zero over time.

6.2.1
Linear Vlasov–Ampère Model and Setup

Since this is an electrostatic test case, we can reduce our model by setting
\mathbf{B}_{1}=0
, hence we effectively solve a Vlasov–Ampère model. We also simulate this test case using the Vlasov–Maxwell model, which we discuss below. Furthermore, the background fields are identically zero, meaning the Vlasov equation only contains a transport in
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. The units of scale are chosen such that
\alpha=1
and
\epsilon=-1
(since it carries the sign of the charge; for the simulations, the sign does not play a role). So finally (
20
) and (
19

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{f}_{1}+\mathbf{v}\cdot DL^{-T}\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{f}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}DL^{-T}\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}\hat{f}_{0}\,,

(93a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\hat{\Omega}}\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot G^{-1}\,\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{1}\,\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}DL^{-T}\,\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,\hat{f}_{1}\,\sqrt{g}\;\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\;\text{d}\mathbf{v}\,,

(93b)

which leads to the following semi-discrete equations of motion

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}

\displaystyle=DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(94a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{v}_{p}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(94b)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}w_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{0,p}}\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\left[DL^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})(\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p}))^{T}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{v}_{p}\,f_{0,p}\,,

(94c)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p}w_{p}f_{0,p}\,\mathbb{\Lambda}^{1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})DL^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{p})\mathbf{v}_{p}\,,

(94d)

which we split and solve in time exactly as mentioned above. The remaining substeps are the coupling of weights and electric field (
5.2
) and the transport in position space by velocity (
5.3
).

For our model, the initial conditions translate to

\displaystyle f_{0}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{\text{th}}^{2}}^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{\bv}^{2}}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\right)\,,

(95a)

\displaystyle f_{1}(t=0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{\text{th}}^{2}}^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{\bv}^{2}}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}\right)\cos\left(kx\right)\,,

(95b)

and the initial

Since we want to simulate the 1D case, the domain is chosen as

6.3
Bernstein Waves

Bernstein waves
[
3
]
exist in plasmas subject to an external magnetic field. In contrast to the Landau waves discussed above, these modes are undamped and exist irrespective of the strength of the magnetic field. This discontinuous jump in existence of modes between vanishing and a small background magnetic field is known as the Bernstein–Landau paradox. Because the wavelengths of the Bernstein waves are of the order of the gyro-radius, they can only be captured by kinetic models, thus presenting a perfect candidate for another test case.

6.3.1
Setup

We take the domain to be
\Omega=[0,144]\times[-0.4,0.4]^{2}
and simulate until
T=2000
with a time-step size of
\Delta t=0.25
. The number of cells is chosen to be
1024\times 1\times 1
with degree 3 splines in the first direction. The background magnetic field is taken as the unit vector in
z
-direction:
\mathbf{B}_{0}=e_{z}
. We take the background Maxwellian (
3
) with a thermal velocity of
v_{\text{th}}=0.2
and a constant density
n_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1
such that no background electric field exists
\mathbf{E}_{0}=0

7
Summary & Outlook

In this work, we presented a Hamiltonian framework of the linearized Vlasov–Maxwell model with a Maxwellian background distribution function
f_{0}
and consistent electromagnetic background fields
\mathbf{E}_{0}
and
\mathbf{B}_{0}
. The system consists of the Maxwell equations (
2
) and a Vlasov-type equation (
2a
), which has a source term proportional to the electric field perturbation. The Hamiltonian of the model (
7
) is quadratic in all three variables, which is also to be expected when deriving it from the energy–Casimir method
[
14
,
28
,
33
]
. Furthermore, a non-canonical Poisson bracket was found that generates the equations of motion, completing the Hamiltonian system. The equations were then formulated in curvilinear coordinates using a diffeomorphism
L
as a mapping (
13
) to enable using the model for simulations of realistic tokamak or stellerator geometries.

The discretization was done using the GEMPIC framework, in which the electromagnetic fields are understood as coefficient functions of forms living in an exact de Rham sequence. This FEEC approach lets us keep the geometric properties of the theory at the discrete level. The distribution function was discretized using a particle-in-cell method, for which we developed the tools necessary to handle the source term. We also discretized the Hamiltonian and the Poisson bracket. To integrate the system in time, we employed a Poisson splitting and found numerical methods to solve each of the resulting subsystems. The implementation was done in
STRUPHY

[
32
,
37
]
.

The model was tested on two test cases: firstly, the Landau damping, both in 1D Cartesian coordinates and in a 2D curved geometry, where we compared it to the Vlasov–Maxwell system with a control variate method and the linearized Vlasov–Maxwell model using the direct delta-f method. Our model gave consistent results in the domain of validity with the comparison models, i.e., a correct damping rate and final magnitude of the electric field energy. But in long-time simulations, it is where our geometric methods trump the simple noise reduction technique (fig.
3
and fig.
7
): the amplitude of the electric field energy is kept at a low value while the numerical errors accumulate in the comparison models and blow up eventually. Also, energy conservation (fig.
4
) is constantly respected (up to solver accuracy). The second test case was the dispersion relation under a constant magnetic background field (fig. (
8
)), where our model correctly reproduced the analytical dispersion relation, in particular the electron Bernstein waves for larger wave-numbers
k
. We observed other, unphysical modes at multiples of the hybrid frequencies which were found to be discrete Case–van Kampen stemming from the discretization.

One obvious follow-up work is the generalization of this model to more background distribution functions. The model now does not allow for shifts or anisotropies in the velocity part (c.f. eq. (
3
)). A first step would be the introduction of shifts and different thermal velocities in all directions. However, the constructive way of the energy–Casimir method does not work with more general backgrounds, for example, a shift in the background distribution function and vanishing background fields
