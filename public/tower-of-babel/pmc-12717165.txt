Soft kidney allocation improves HLA matching and maintains allocation order
Authors: Sapir Israeli, Eytan Mor, Maya Mor Cohen, Ronit Pasvolsky Gutman, Ron Loewenthal, Benaya Rosen Zvi, Moshe Israeli, Ruth Rahamimov, Eviatar Nesher, Yoram Louzoun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney allocation scores aim to balance transplant efficacy and equity but often deprioritize Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) matching, leading to reduced graft lifespan and a higher risk of donor-specific antibodies (DSA). This study introduces a “soft allocation” strategy to improve HLA match rates while minimally disrupting the allocation queue or equity.MethodsDonors include directed-living (D-LD), non-directed-living (ND-LD), and deceased (DD) types. In the “soft allocation” system, instead of automatically assigning the kidney to the first person in line, the kidney is offered to the best HLA match among the top patients on the waiting list. The predefined size of the top patient group (typically 5–20 patients) is referred to as K. This approach preserves blood type and age compatibility while protecting patients with rare HLA types.ResultsAnalysis of 95,250 US DD transplants shows an increase in HLA-A&B&DR match scores from 1.45 to 1.77. In 42 recent DD cases and 230 ND-LD transplants in Israel, match scores improved from 2.33 to 2.54 and from 1.34 to 2.03, respectively. Similar enhancements are observed in related donor–recipient pairs using crossover transplants, achieving a three-fold match improvement without worsening any recipient’s match.ConclusionsFor predefined allocation rules, soft allocation enables significant HLA matching improvement with minimal impact on allocation order, benefiting both unrelated and related transplants. Future work will extend this approach to include sensitized donations.Subject terms: Kidney, Medical research

Introduction

Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) matching between donor and recipient improves long-term kidney transplant survival, with each additional matched allele incrementally enhancing graft longevity1–4. According to the United Network for Organ Sharing database, average 10-year graft survival rates are 82% with 0 mismatches, 76% with 3 mismatches, and 71% with 6 mismatches3. HLA matching also prevents sensitization in patients who may need future transplants, as re-transplantation is common, especially for recipients under 50 who often face multiple transplants over their lifetime5.

In the US, the Kidney Allocation System (KAS) prioritizes transplant candidates based on clinical and immunological factors, including waiting time (1/365 points per day), sensitization (CPRA score, 0–202.10 points), and the Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) matched to recipient longevity3. Pediatric and urgent cases receive additional priority, and geographic proximity (0–4 points) also influences organ allocation. HLA match has a limited effect (0–4 points) since it was shown to bias the allocation to frequent HLA alleles6.

Israel’s allocation system uses a points-based method considering waiting time (1.5 points per year), HLA match (up to 4 points), age, sensitization, and priority points for registered donors and their families7. Due to high waiting times (averaging over 6 years), live donor transplants are preferred. In Israel, altruistic donations have become prominent through the “Matnat Haim" organization, which has facilitated over 250 non-directed live transplants annually, a rate exceeding 60% of all live donor transplantations8.

In the US, the National Kidney Registry (NKR) improves matching for highly sensitized patients through a large donor pool, enabling HLA amino acid level matching (eplet matching) and achieving favorable outcomes, even among higher-risk patients9. The NKR’s Standard Voucher Program allows donors to schedule surgery conveniently and donate early, supporting patients not yet in End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD)10. The program has gained popularity over traditional paired kidney exchanges due to its flexibility and the ability to accommodate the timing needs of both donors and recipients11.

We propose that for deceased and non-directed-living donors, allowing for a soft allocation of the top K, (5 ≤ K ≤ 20) candidates in the allocation queue (the ranked list of compatible candidates produced by the existing allocation system), where one chooses the optimally HLA-matched recipient within this limited pool, can drastically improve the HLA matching, with practically no effect on the queue, ensuring both equity and efficacy.

In directed donors, allowing for a similar paired soft matching can ensure that no recipient has worse HLA matching than initially planned, but many have a much better match, with practically no effect on the time of the transplant.

While many approaches were proposed to improve the queue for efficiency or for equity12,13, we propose an alternative approach for any allocation system that maintains the priority order set by the current system (or actually any scoring system). We propose to replace the top of the queue (the K next candidates) with a soft allocation. Instead of allocating the next kidney to the next candidate, we propose to assign it to one of the top-K candidates with the best HLA match.

HLA Imputation and matching

For the Israeli cohort, the first field of most HLA-A,B,C,DQB1,DRB1 is given for the donor/recipient, while for some loci (varying between the donors/recipients), the typing is missing (Supplementary Material Table S3). Given the limited size of this cohort, we aimed to conserve as many samples as possible. All HLA typings of the Israeli cohort were imputed to the 5-locus genotype in 2 fields using MR-GRIMM15. The frequencies used to impute the typing were computed using a multi-race expectation maximization(EM) algorithm - GRIMME16 based on the Ezer Mizion Bone Marrow Donor Registry, the largest Israeli HLA typing registry. Since the donor and recipient origins are unknown, the frequencies used are of the entire Israeli population as a single origin.

Technically, we considered for each donor/recipient the most probable genotype from the imputation. The imputation also provided the genotype frequencies. All alleles (those that were fully known from the typing and those that were imputed) were converted to antigen level using py-ard17.

Matching can be performed at multiple levels. For the Israeli cohort, we performed the analysis at the serology and at the 4-digit genetic levels. The analysis for the US cohort was performed only at the serology level, and only for pairs with all HLA information for the specific analysis. We defined a match per locus i between donor and recipients by 2 − xi, when xi is the number of alleles that appear in the donor but not in the recipient (see Supplementary Material Table S4 for example).

Limited perturbation of the queue

Given the current queue by the position scoring approach of each transplant center, we looked for each donor for the K patients at the top of the list on the donation date. We chose among those the candidate with the appropriate age and blood type with the best HLA matching. If a few candidates have the same maximum matching/all have a match of 0, we choose the first that matches in age and blood type. To ensure that a patient does not wait for more than K positions beyond his/her original position in the queue if he/she has waited for K − 1 turns, he/she is automatically assigned the next ABO and age-compatible donor, regardless of HLA match (see Fig. 1C, Algorithm 1).

Cycles optimal solution

We created a directed graph matching based on the Monteiro et al. algorithm18. Each pair is represented as a node. If the donor from node ni has a suitable blood type to donate to the recipient from node nj, the age criteria are met, and if nj represents an unpaired pair or nj represents a paired pair and the HLA match is higher than his paired donor, then we create an edge from node ni to nj, with a weight of the HLA match score. An edge from ni to itself was created with a weight of the HLA match score if the blood and age criteria are met. We found all cycles up to size K in the graph.

Results

We analyzed two populations (the US and Israel), and in each population, we analyzed living and deceased donors. For each population, we tested the HLA match with the current allocation and the HLA match with the same allocation combined with the soft allocation. We denote the soft allocation as the Top-K algorithm - Given a donor, choose the patient with the highest HLA matching among the top-K positions in the current queue (the queue by the current allocation system) that fits the age and blood type restrictions; To ensure that no patient waits for more than K positions beyond the original queue placement, if K − 1 turns have passed, he/she automatically receives the next blood type and age-matched transplant, respectively of his score, since he already was expected to receive a donation. In practice, the number of candidates considered (K) is not fixed but represents a policy choice. We examined values between 5 and 20, reflecting a balance between improved HLA matching and minimal changes to waiting time. This range corresponds to a small group of top-ranked candidates on the list and ensures that no patient is delayed beyond a few positions. For example (Fig. 1C), assume all donors and recipients have the same blood type, the age criteria hold for all pair combinations, and K = 3. Here we use K = 3 only for illustration, as it makes the schematic easier to follow; in practice, our analyses considered higher values of K. The first line represents the current queue, while the ID numbers represent the order in the queue. The middle line represents the donors that can be chosen in each step; each step refers to a new kidney becoming available. In the first step, we can choose among IDs-1,2,3, and we chose ID-2 with the highest HLA match. Then, we can choose among the new top 3, but in step 3, we must choose ID-1 since we do not want the patient to wait more than 3 turns. The last line represents the queue order by the best from the top 3. Further details of the model and data processing are in the Supplementary Methods.

We present here a simplified model that does not account for many details of the transplant, such as the high turn-down rate of DD in the US (above 26%19), the logistic limitations, and center size differences. We propose that, within the current allocations and limitations, replacing the rigid score-based allocation with a soft allocation would drastically improve HLA matching, with no effect on equity or average wait time, and with no patient being moved more than a very limited number of positions in the allocation queue.

The exchange of transplants and donors between centers in Israel is possible since the two centers are geographically close. Thus, for the Israeli cohort, our analysis combines donors and patients from the two centers studied. In the US, although the exchange of transplants is possible, some centers are too far away to allow the exchange of organs between them. For the US cohort, we repeated the analysis when we either allowed exchange only within the state (see Supplementary Material Table S5 for the number of transplants after the pre-process in each state), or we allowed exchange between all states. The latter situation is not very realistic and only demonstrates the effect on all transplant hospitals. Another difference between Israel and the US is the treatment of ND-LD. In Israel, there is a very large number of ND-LD (they constitute the majority of the donors), these donors are interested in donating as soon as possible and are usually assigned to a patient from the waiting list, while in the US the percentage of ND-LD is low, and they are assigned to the patient from the waiting list or to the exchange program with the D-LD.

Transplant match level

We examined the fraction of matching HLA in living-unrelated donor–recipient pairs in the Israeli cohort, and deceased donor–recipient pairs in both the Israeli and US cohorts (for the definition of HLA matching, see Supplementary Methods). We used 3 HLA loci combinations, following different approaches to limit DSA20: (1) A, B, and DR, (2) DQ and DR (class II), (3) all 5 loci (A, B, Cw, DR, and DQ). In the Israeli cohort, for ND-LD, the average A, B, and DR matching is 1.34 out of 6 possible matches, the average DQ and DR matching is 1.7 out of 4 possible, and the average match over all 5 loci is 3 out of 10 (Fig. 2A, Table 1). For deceased donors, the average HLA matching is higher than the ND-LD but still low, 2.33 out of 6 in A, B, and DR, 2.55 out of 4 in DQ and DR, and 4.36 out of 10 in all 5 loci (Fig. 3A, Table 1). In the US cohort, for deceased donors, the average HLA matching is lower than the Israeli DD, 1.45 out of 6 in A, B, and DR, 1.44 out of 4 in DQ and DR, and 2.67 out of 10 in all 5 loci (Fig. 3D, Table 1).

Improved HLA matching with limited perturbation of the queue in Israeli ND-LD and DD

We tested the effect of the Top-K method on the HLA match. We first analyzed ND-LD and DD in Israel. K was varied from 1 to 25. Even at K = 20, the increase remains significant, with a typical 50-100% improvement. In general, the HLA match increases with K (Figs. 2A–C, 3, Table 1, Supplementary Material Figs. S2, S3A).

To ensure the increase in the average HLA matching is not the result of rare matches, and that most transplants gain from it, we checked the difference in the matching for all the recipients, and indeed most of the recipients (77-91% with the maximal increase for A,B,DR) receive a better HLA match or with no change (Supplementary Material Fig. S4). The same results hold when the analysis is performed at the four-digit level (Supplementary Material Fig. S5).

We also evaluated the equity implications of the algorithm by testing its effect on recipients with rare haplotypes. Genotype frequency, used here as a proxy for population-level HLA variation (and thus potential ethnic diversity), was not correlated with the change in queue position or with the change in HLA match score under the Top-K method (Supplementary Material Fig. S6). This suggests that the algorithm does not disproportionately disadvantage individuals with rare genotypes.

To validate that no patient is moved more than K positions, we measured the average and the standard deviation (Std.) of the difference between the position by the Top-K method and the current position for 1 ≤ K ≤ 25. The average is always 0 (as expected), and the Std. is approximately K2 (Figs. 2D, 3C, F).

In Israel, unrelated living donations preferably have an age difference of less than 30 years. We did not add that as a constraint. However, the resulting age difference distribution is similar following our algorithm to the current practice (Supplementary Material Fig. S7).

Improved HLA match in US DD

The US-DD donor case is more complex following the extremely high turn-down rate. Moreover, in practice, many other factors are more heavily weighted by transplant clinicians than HLA match, including donor age, donor terminal creatinine, donor comorbidities, organ recovery type, and biopsy results. We do not propose to change the selection criteria of clinicians; instead, we propose a soft allocation to different patients of any arriving kidney. To avoid the need for modeling the complex decision process, we simply use the kidneys that were actually used. This is a simplification, but the drastic improvement in HLA match shows the advantages of the top-K algorithm in DD in the US. In practice, multiple adaptations of the algorithm will be required for DD.

We repeated the Top-K analysis for the US-DD cohort (1) in states of at least 500 DD transplants (39 states), allowing cross-only within each state. (2) in states of at least 500 DD transplants, allowing cross-only between all states. (3) In states of at least 3000 DD transplants (11 states). In case 1, K varied from 1 to 25, and in cases 2 and 3, K varied from 1 to 50. The Top-K shows similar results in all cases for similar K. However, the most significant improvement is for K = 50, which we allowed only for cases 2 and 3 (since in the smaller states it increases the waiting time). For A&B&DR, in case 1, the improvement is from 1.45 to 1.98 with K = 20, and in case 3, the improvement is from 1.44 to 2.3 with K = 50; similar results were obtained for all HLA combinations (Fig. 3D, E, Table 1, Supplementary Material Figs. S3B, C, S8).

Note that this solution applies to all types of donors allocated to the waiting list and any scoring method. Also, it is extremely fast and does not depend on the queue size, as such, it can be applied even to very large centers in real-time.

Paired donations - Israel population

The results above were obtained assuming no required pairing. For paired donations, the donor may require that the match be at least as good as the original donation. We thus adapted the algorithm to propose alternative donors only if the alternative donor obeys the blood and age restrictions, and if the new donor has a better match than the original one. We used that to check whether, even considering these strict restrictions, it is possible to improve matching for related donations using crossover/cycles. Note that donor–recipient pairs are often first or second-order relatives and may share a haplotype, and as such, they often already have a high matching probability. For the Israeli cohort, we tested different sizes of maximum cycle sizes (2–5). Cycles are allowed only for transplants in the range of four months (January–April, May–August, September–December) in order not to delay the transplants for a long time. For most recipients, their related donor is the best donor for them, or they cannot be replaced without reducing the match for another pair. However, for those whom we found a better donor, we show a significant increase in matching, for A&B&DR 3 fold increase, and for class II and all 5 loci 2 fold increase, Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Material Figs. S9, S10, Table S6. As can be seen, there is no point in having cycles of more than 3 pairs.

All living donation - paired and unpaired in Israel and the US

We tested the contribution of mixing the ND-LD and the D-LD donor. In Israel, there are a lot of ND-LD, and they want to donate as soon as possible; thus, the cycle method that applies every four months cannot be assumed.

When combining paired and unpaired donors, we tested here whether we could maximize the overall match in the population rather than the maximal match for a specific donor. As such, we aimed at maximizing, on the one hand, the average HLA match per cycle, and on the other hand, the HLA match in the top-K patients.

Formally, for each of the top-K patients, we decided whether to use the top-K method as reported above, or to include it in a cycle by comparing the HLA match it could get in the top K and the average it could get in a paired cycle of paired donor-patients in a four-month window, and chose the higher of those. If the patient was chosen for the top-K method, we assigned them the top-K best match. Otherwise, we used it for the optimal cycle computation.

The average HLA matching score of all living donors (related and unrelated) is 1.74 for HLA-A&B&DR. When applying the Top-K and Cycles method separately, the HLA matching score for HLA-A&B&DR is 2.29. However, when applying the combined method of Top-K with the related donor, the HLA matching score for HLA-A&B&DR is 2.19. Similar results were shown for DQ&DR and for all 5 loci (Supplementary Material Table S7).

For the US, we combined the ND-LD with the D-LD, and included states with at least 500 transplants between 2015 and 2023 (25 states). We applied the cycles method (similar to only D-LD in Israel) for each four months and allowed crossover transplants between centers in the same state. For the ND-LD, we did not coerce a better HLA matching than the original donor. For both donor types, the blood type should match in biological terms (similar to only D-LD criteria) to exchange pairs, and the age criteria are similar to all the above scenarios. Note, if we scramble between the donors and the recipients of the ND-LD, the results will not change; thus, in a prospective scenario, this algorithm can apply to unrelated donors and the top candidates from the waiting list, when they are randomly paired. We tested different sizes of maximum cycle sizes (2–5). In contrast to Israel, around half of the recipients (depending on the maximum cycle size) could not find a better match using this method, and for the other half of the recipients, a better donor was found. For those whom we found a better donor, we show a significant increase in matching, for A&B&DR and class II 2.5-3 fold increase, and for all 5 loci 2 fold increase. While larger cycle sizes do not drastically change the average HLA match improvement, they include more pairs (Fig. 4C, D, Supplementary Material Table S8).

Discussion

Kidney allocation balances efficacy, ensuring graft and host survival, and equity, promoting fair access despite HLA, blood type, or DSA differences13. Most countries use a points-based system for allocation21,22, balancing these goals but potentially limiting HLA matching. Our analysis shows that optimizing matches within the top-K candidates can significantly enhance HLA compatibility with minimal impact on waiting times, averaging less than a month for K = 10 in Israel. This method maintains the current allocation queue but chooses the best match among the top K, ensuring no delay beyond K allocations.

The choice of K is context-dependent and can be adjusted according to the allocation system. Larger values of K increase the gain in HLA matching but may extend waiting time, especially in centers with fewer transplants per month. By contrast, smaller values of K already provide substantial improvement while keeping delays negligible. Thus, K should be viewed as a policy parameter that can be tailored to local transplant activity and priorities.

Importantly, a candidate who is not chosen in a given allocation step is not permanently bypassed. Our approach ensures that no recipient can be delayed by more than K − 1 allocation steps. If a candidate reaches this limit, they automatically receive the next compatible kidney according to the allocation system’s rules. Thus, the Top-K method does not bypass clinical allocation criteria; it only adds flexibility within the top-K candidates, while preserving all standard compatibility and priority requirements.

The approach can be adapted for various allocation systems and both deceased and living donors. For deceased donations, we tested using the existing ND-LD queue and simulated the DD queue. For related donations, slight modifications ensure recipients do not receive worse matches, still enhancing overall matching. While serological-level matching remains the clinical standard, our model also supports genetic-level HLA data, as shown in the Supplementary Material.

Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that increased HLA compatibility is associated with improved long-term graft survival and reduced sensitization risk1–4. This study focused on showing that substantial improvements in HLA matching can be achieved with minimal disruption to allocation order. Given the established association between HLA matching and post-transplant outcomes, these improvements are likely to have meaningful clinical implications. Further research is needed to directly assess the impact of soft allocation strategies on graft survival and rejection rates through prospective or retrospective outcome-based analyses.

In the US cohort, our analysis is restricted to kidneys that were ultimately transplanted, without explicitly modeling the high discard rate or the clinical decision-making process underlying organ acceptance. While this simplification may limit the generalizability of the results, most kidneys reaching the final stages of allocation are likely to be accepted, and we believe the observed improvement in HLA matching remains informative. As well, in the US cohort, the soft allocation analysis was restricted to candidates within the same state to reflect logistical feasibility and minimize the impact of organ transport and ischemia time. In the Israeli cohort, all transplants were drawn from two geographically close centers, where transportation constraints are negligible.

Our focus here is on non-sensitized patients, but the method can be adapted for sensitized patients, factoring in DSA requirements, albeit with potentially longer waits. Applied to mixed waiting lists, the algorithm ensures that sensitized patients follow the standard protocol, while non-sensitized patients benefit from the Top-K method. Future work will adapt this approach for sensitized patients. We only considered kidneys already transplanted, assuming appropriate quality.
