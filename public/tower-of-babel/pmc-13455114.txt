Triazatrinaphthylene-based metal-free polycyclic aromatic frameworks for electrochemical nitrate reduction to ammonia
Authors: Ananda Basak, Nilmadhab Mukherjee, Supratim Ghosh, Rahul Kumar Sharma, Avishek Roy, Ekta Nehra, Yusuke Nishiyama, Biswarup Pathak, Uttam Kumar Ghorai, Rahul Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR) offers a sustainable route to convert waste NO3− into valuable ammonia (NH3) using renewable electricity. However, the multielectron nature of the NO3RR necessitates the use of efficient, earth-abundant catalysts. While noble metals show high activity, their scarcity motivates the exploration of carbon-based alternatives. Here, we synthesized a triamine derivative based on the triazatrinaphthylene unit (TNP-NH2), a nitrogen-doped planar carbon structure, for the synthesis of six highly porous, crystalline covalent organic frameworks (COFs) as metal-free NO3RR catalysts. All the COFs exhibited good to moderate ammonia yields, achieving the highest yield rate of 2282 µg h−1 mgcat−1 and the highest faradaic efficiency of 82% at −1.0 V vs. RHE. Long-term chronoamperometry confirmed the structural durability of the COF during catalysis. DFT calculations further identified the specific active sites and reaction energetics on the COF backbone responsible for stabilizing key intermediates, consistent with the observed catalytic performance.

Introduction

Nitrate (NO3−) contamination of water bodies is a global challenge, primarily driven by intensive agricultural practices, the use of nitrogen-rich fertilizers, and the discharge of industrial waste.1,2 Conventional nitrate-removal methods, such as biological denitrification, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, and electrodialysis, often suffer from high energy requirements, poor selectivity, or the generation of secondary pollutants.3,4 As a result, the electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR), which simultaneously achieves pollutant removal and value-added ammonia (NH3) synthesis, Although noble metals (Pt, Rh, Pd, and Au) exhibit NO3RR activity due to their favorable hydrogenation energetics and low activation barriers, their high cost and scarcity hinder large-scale deployment.11–15 In response, earth-abundant electrocatalysts, such as transition metals, metal oxides, and bimetallic alloys, have been extensively explored.16–20 Alternatively, metal-free carbon-based systems, such as defective graphene, N-doped graphene, and carbon nanotubes, have demonstrated that heteroatom-induced charge polarization and vacancy sites enhance catalytic performance by modulating adsorption energies and stabilizing critical intermediates.21–24 However, despite their immense tunability, the application of intrinsically metal-free porous carbon architectures in the NO3RR remains underdeveloped.

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs), a class of crystalline, permanently porous materials formed through dynamic covalent chemistry, offer unparalleled design flexibility.25,26 Their π-conjugated backbones, uniform channels, and high surface areas create well-defined environments for charge transport and mass diffusion.27,28 Importantly, COFs enable the placement of heteroatoms (N, O, S, etc.) within the skeleton, allowing for the rational tuning of electron density, binding affinity, and catalytic site geometry. Such structural programmability has enabled COFs to perform efficiently in the CO2RR, ORR, water oxidation, H2O2 formation, and organic electrosynthesis.29–34 Several reported COF-based NO3RR systems rely on the integration of COFs with inorganic catalysts or the incorporation of metal sites within the framework. Although these systems often exhibit good catalytic performance, the intrinsic activity of the COF backbone remains unclear, as the reduction process is predominantly governed by the metal centers. Consequently, the potential of fully metal-free COFs as active catalysts for the NO3RR remains largely unexplored.35–39

Motivated by this gap, we designed a nitrogen-rich planar triazatrinaphthylene (TNP)-based triamine linker (TNP-NH2) to mimic a segment of a N-doped graphene. This extended polycyclic aromatic heterocycle enhances π-electron delocalization and introduces electron-rich pyridinic N sites. By condensing TNP-NH2 with six structurally distinct aldehydes, we synthesized a family of crystalline COFs spanning different linker lengths, functional groups, and electronic environments. Among the six COFs, TNP-DMDA, with electron-donating –OCH3 groups, exhibited the highest NH3 yield (2282 µg h−1 mgcat−1) and the highest faradaic efficiency (82%) at −1.0 V vs. RHE, supported by in situ FT-IR and 15N-labeling confirming nitrate as the sole nitrogen source. The catalyst showed stability over repeated cycles and 40 hours of continuous operation. DFT calculations revealed that the methoxy groups lower the rate-determining energy barrier, illustrating how framework functionalization and heteroatom doping govern NO3RR activity and selectivity. This library provides a coherent platform to interrogate how subtle differences in heteroatom distribution and framework electron density regulate NO3RR activity.

Results and discussion

We synthesized triazatrinaphthylene triamine (TNP-NH2) via a condensation reaction between 2,4,6-trimethoxy-1,3,5-benzenetricarbaldehyde and tert-butyl(4-aminophenyl)carbamate in ethanol, yielding tri-tert-butyldibenzo[b,j]quinolino[2,3-f][1,7]phenanthroline-2,8,14-triylcarbamate (3, Fig. 1 and Section S-I).403 was then subjected to deprotection using trifluoroacetic acid in dichloromethane, followed by neutralisation with a 5% (w/v) aqueous NaOH solution, affording the desired triamine TNP-NH2. The triamine was then allowed to undergo Schiff base condensation reactions with different dialdehydes to synthesize six different covalent organic frameworks. These COFs were prepared by condensing TNP-NH2 with terephthaldehyde (PDA), [1,1′-biphenyl]-4,4′-dicarbaldehyde (BDA), 5′-(4-formylphenyl)-1,1′:3′,1″-terphenyl-4,4″-dicarbaldehyde (TAB), 2,5-dihydroxyterephthaldehyde (DHDA), 2,5-dimethoxyterephthaldehyde (DMDA), and 4,4′,4″-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)tribenzaldehyde (TTA).

Synthesis of the building unit—triazatrinaphthylene triamine—and the structures of six covalent organic frameworks.

All the synthesized COFs exhibited intense and well-defined powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns, confirming high crystallinity and long-range structural order (Fig. 2 and S3). For TNP-PDA, the (100) plane reflection was observed at 2.8° (2θ), with additional reflections from the (110), (210), and (001) planes appearing at 4.9°, 7.5°, and 26.0°, respectively. In the case of TNP-BDA, due to the extended length of the biphenyl linker, the reflection from (100) planes shifted to a lower angle of 2.2° (2θ), and the (110), (210), and (001) plane reflections were located at 4.0°, 6.2°, and 25.2° (2θ), respectively. TNP-TAB showed its most intense diffraction peak at 4.0° (2θ), corresponding to the (100) plane reflections, with additional reflections at 6.9°, 8.1°, 10.7°, and 25.4° (2θ), which were assigned to the (110), (200), (120), and (001) planes, respectively. TNP-DHDA exhibited characteristic PXRD peaks at 2.7°, 4.8°, 7.4°, and 26.1° (2θ) corresponding to the (100), (110), (210), and (001) plane reflections, while TNP-DMDA displayed nearly identical PXRD features with peaks at 2.7°, 4.7°, 7.4°, and 26.2° (2θ). For TNP-TTA, reflections corresponding to the (100), (110), (210), (120), and (001) planes were clearly observed at 4.0°, 6.9°, 8.0°, 10.6°, and 26.4° (2θ), respectively. All PXRD patterns were further refined, yielding the most plausible structure.41 The refinement showed good agreement between the experimental and simulated data, with refinement parameters Rp ranging from 1.9 to 4.9% and Rwp from 3.7 to 6.1%. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were collected at 77 K to investigate the porosity of the as-synthesized COFs (Fig. 3a). The surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method and validated using the Rouquerol criteria (Fig. S4–S9).42 The BET surface areas were determined to be 1237, 841, 1030, 688, 2006, and 1746 m2 g−1 for TNP-PDA, TNP-BDA, TNP-TAB, TNP-DHDA, TNP-DMDA, and TNP-TTA, respectively. To confirm the formation of imine linkages within the frameworks, solid-state 13C CP-MAS NMR spectra were recorded (Fig. 3b and c and S10 and S11). The aromatic carbon signals appeared in the 100–155 ppm region, and the imine carbon was detected as a broad resonance in the 155–160 ppm range. The broadening of the imine signal likely appeared from the merging of the imine carbon signal with that of the pyridinic carbon in the triazatrinaphthylene core. Notably, the methoxy group in TNP-DMDA showcased a distinct signal at 53 ppm, and the triazine carbon of TNP-TTA showed a downfield shift to 168 ppm. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy further supported the formation of imine linkages (Fig. S12). All COFs exhibited characteristic stretching bands in the range of 1620–1626 cm−1, attributed to the –HCNH– vibration of the imine bond, indicating successful Schiff base condensation. Additional bands were observed around 1600 cm−1, corresponding to aromatic –CHCH– stretching, and at 1694 cm−1, indicative of residual carbonyl (–CO) groups from unreacted aldehyde functionalities. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of TNP-PDA, TNP-BDA, and TNP-TAB revealed a fibrous nature, which gave rise to a flower-like morphology (Fig. 3d). In contrast, TNP-DMDA, TNP-DHDA, and TNP-TTA exhibited no specific morphology but instead formed agglomerated structures composed of thin crystallites.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to investigate the chemical environments of carbon and nitrogen atoms within the frameworks (Fig. S13 and S14). The deconvoluted C 1s spectra exhibited two dominant peaks: one at 284.7–284.8 eV, corresponding to aromatic –CHCH– carbons, and another at 285.6–286.5 eV, assigned to –CHN– imine carbons. In the cases of TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA, an additional peak was observed at 288.2–288.5 eV, attributed to the –C–O– carbons. High-resolution N 1s spectra revealed two characteristic peaks: the peak at 398.5–399.1 eV was assigned to imine nitrogen atoms, while the peak at 399.6–400.8 eV originated from pyridinic nitrogen atoms within the triazatrinaphthylene (TNP) core. Additionally, TNP-TTA displayed a distinct peak at 401.2 eV, attributed to the triazine-associated imine nitrogen.

The nitrate reduction efficiency of the COFs was determined by depositing the COF inks onto carbon paper electrodes and using the electrodes in a standard H-type cell. The ink was prepared by ultrasonically mixing 1.6 mg of as-synthesized COF and 10 µL of Nafion solution (5%) in 150 µL of isopropanol. 80 µL of the ink was drop-cast onto a carbon paper electrode with a catalyst loading of ∼0.8 mg cm−2 (Section S-I). All the COFs showed an increase in current density when 0.2 M NaNO3 was added to 0.2 M Na2SO4 (Fig. 4a and b). TNP-DMDA showcased the highest increase in current density among all the COFs in the potential range of 0 to −1.0 V vs. RHE. Faradaic efficiency for the ammonia production process was evaluated by performing chronoamperometry for 30 minutes on each COF-coated electrode. After the chronoamperometry experiment, ammonia was detected by UV-Vis spectroscopy using the indophenol blue method. TNP-DMDA demonstrated the highest faradaic efficiency of 82% at −1 V vs. RHE amongst all the COFs (Fig. 4c). The other side products detected include NO2−, H2, and a small amount of hydrazine (Fig. S15). TNP-DMDA also exhibited the highest yield rate of 2282 µg h−1 mgcat−1 at −1.0 V vs. RHE (Fig. 4d). The double-layer capacitance of the COFs was measured by performing CV in a non-faradaic region, and the Cdl values were calculated to be 272.6, 202.4, 167.7, 111.4, 96.4, and 58.9 µF cm−2 for TNP-DMDA, TNP-DHDA, TNP-TAB, TNP-BDA, TNP-TTA, and TNP-PDA, respectively (Fig. 4e and S16). The higher Cdl value represents a higher electrochemically active surface area for TNP-DMDA than those of the other COFs, demonstrating higher catalytic activity (Table S3). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed to assess the charge-transfer resistance at the electrode surface (Fig. 4d). The Nyquist plot revealed the lowest charge-transfer resistances for TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA among the six COFs, indicating superior conductivity and efficient electron transfer.

An isotope labeling experiment was performed using 15N-labeled NaNO3 to verify the source of the ammonia formed (Fig. 5a). In the 1H NMR spectrum, a triplet was observed with a coupling constant of 52.5 Hz, referenced to 14NH4+. However, when the nitrate source was replaced with Na15NO3, a doublet appeared with a coupling constant of 73 Hz. When the reaction was performed in the absence of any nitrate source, no NMR peak was found in the spectrum. Hence, the data indicate that the nitrogen source for ammonia comes only from externally added nitrate, not from catalyst breakdown. To monitor the electrolytic process, in situ Attenuated Total Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed at −1.0 V vs. RHE at 2 minute intervals (Fig. 5b). A strong absorption band at 1522 cm−1 corresponds to the vibration of *NO. Notably, distinct peaks at 1263, 1551, and 1174 cm−1 were assigned to key reaction intermediates, namely *NO2, *NH2, and *NH2OH, respectively. The formation of NH3 was confirmed by a prominent peak at 1106 cm−1. Interestingly, the spectral data revealed a clear correlation between the applied potential and the intensity of the ammonia signal, highlighting the role of potential in governing product formation. The long-term durability of TNP-DMDA was evaluated through cycling and chronoamperometry tests. In the cycling stability test, a single electrode was operated at −1.0 V vs. RHE for eight consecutive cycles (Fig. 5c). The yield rate showed only a minor drop, decreasing from 2282 to 2110 µg h−1 mgcat−1, and a drop in faradaic efficiency from 82% to 76.5% after the eighth cycle. This minimal performance loss highlights the robust catalytic nature of TNP-DMDA under repeated operation. Additionally, chronoamperometry at −1.0 V vs. RHE over a single continuous run up to 40 hours demonstrated a minute loss in current density of 10 mA cm−2 and an overall faradaic efficiency of 81.14% (Fig. 5d). These results collectively confirm the strong operational stability of TNP-DMDA over an extended period. Notably, an amorphous polymer—analogous to TNP-DMDA was synthesized, having a surface area of 300 m2 g−1, which showcased a faradaic efficiency of 64% toward ammonia production (Fig. S17).

Post-catalytic characterization after 40 hours of the NO3RR revealed modest changes in the FT-IR and XPS spectra of TNP-DMDA. The FT-IR spectrum exhibits slight broadening of the CO band with partial overlap with the imine stretching (Fig. S18). The deconvoluted N 1s spectrum shifts from 398.5 to 399.4 eV, consistent with protonation of imine nitrogen (Fig. S19). In the C 1s spectrum, the appearance of a new component at 288.7 eV suggests limited imine hydrolysis (∼10%), leading to the formation of aldehyde carbon. SEM images show no noticeable morphological change (Fig. S20). However, N2 adsorption measurements indicate a decrease in surface area to 380 m2 g−1, and the reduced PXRD reflections suggest loss of long-range order, likely due to weakened π–π stacking (Fig. S21).

To rationalize the activity trends of the synthesized COFs, surface charges were measured using zeta potential (Table S1). The measurements were conducted by dispersing the COFs in pure water and in a 1 mM NaNO3 solution. The results show that TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA exhibit negative shifts of 6.7 and 2 mV, respectively, indicating adsorption of NO3− anions on the surface. In contrast, TNP-PDA and TNP-TTA display smaller shifts of 0.9 and 0.4 mV, suggesting weaker anion adsorption. Meanwhile, TNP-TAB and TNP-BDA show positive shifts of 3.1 and 2 mV, respectively, indicating predominant adsorption of Na+ cations. These observations qualitatively correlate with the superior NO3RR activity of TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA compared to the other COFs. Moreover, all the COFs used in this work contain imine and pyridine nitrogen atoms. However, TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA additionally possess electron-donating –OMe and –OH groups, respectively. These functional groups are believed to enhance their reduction performance (Table S4). Importantly, TNP-DMDA and TNP-DHDA, which display the best electrocatalytic performance, do not contain the highest levels of any of the metal impurities identified by ICP-MS analysis (Table S2). This lack of correlation between impurity concentration and catalytic activity indicates that the observed performance trends cannot be attributed to trace metal contamination. Instead, the electrocatalytic behavior is predominantly dictated by the intrinsic characteristics of the COFs.

To further validate the experimental observations, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP; Fig. 6 and Section S-VI).43,44 To gain molecular-level insight, single molecular units of TNP-PDA, TNP-TTA, and TNP-DMDA, each terminated with a methyl group, were modeled and optimized. The charge distribution varies across different atomic sites within each COF unit, confirming the presence of heterogeneous active sites (Fig. 6a, S22–S24 and Table S5). Further, electronic structure analysis using partial density of states (PDOS) exhibits a downshifted p-band center for both N and C atoms in TNP-DMDA compared to TNP-PDA and TNP-TTA, indicating that the methoxy substituent plays a key role in modulating the electronic structure and thereby optimizing intermediate adsorption energy (Fig. S25). To evaluate the NO3RR activity in a neutral medium, key reaction intermediates were adsorbed at these distinct sites. Apart from heterogeneous N and C atoms in the framework, we also simulated the reaction at S8 to evaluate the impact of the methoxy group in TNP-DMDA. The adsorption energies (Eads) for each intermediate at the most stable positions of the COFs have been summarised (Fig. 6b), along with the site-specific relative Eads (Tables S6–S8). Each site exhibits a unique interaction with intermediates due to the varied positioning of N atoms. Note that the adsorption of *NHOH at sites S2, S6, S7, S8, and S9 led to large distortions in the framework and was therefore excluded from further analysis.

Further, the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) model was employed to assess catalytic activity by identifying the most endothermic reaction step at 0 V, following the adsorbate-evolution pathway via an NRA2 mechanism,45,46 which mainly includes the experimentally observed intermediates (Table S9). To analyze the reaction energy changes, all elementary steps are listed (Tables S10–S12), and the most endothermic rate-determining steps (RDS) are summarized (Fig. 6c). Among the studied systems, TNP-DMDA exhibits the lowest endothermic value (0.17 eV at S4) for the rate-determining step (RDS), followed by TNP-TTA (0.65 eV at S10) and TNP-PDA (0.67 eV at S4) (Fig. 6d and S26–S28). Additionally, TNP-DMDA with a lower theoretical work function correlates linearly with the lower (more positive) experimental onset potential (Fig. S29). The most active sites of TNP-DMDA exhibit lower endothermic barriers for the RDS compared to TNP-TTA and TNP-PDA, indicating its higher activity, which aligns well with the experimental observation.

To evaluate the competition between the NO3RR and HER, hydrogen evolution activity was investigated on the identified active sites of the COFs. The adsorption of the *H intermediate was examined at different heterogeneous sites (Tables S13–S15), and the most stable adsorption configuration was evaluated. The HER activity was assessed using the Gibbs free energy of the adsorbed H atom (ΔG*H), where values close to 0 eV indicate optimal HER performance (Fig. S30). The calculated HER activity follows the order TNP-TTA > TNP-DMDA > TNP-PDA, as reflected by the ΔG*H of −0.01, 0.14, and 0.19 eV, respectively. The near thermoneutral ΔG*H of TNP-TTA indicates the most favorable HER activity among the three COFs. Interestingly, the majority of active sites in TNP-DMDA exhibit relatively higher ΔG*H values, indicating diminished HER activity and improved NO3RR.

Conclusions

This work demonstrated the design and synthesis of triazatrinaphthylene triamine and six covalent organic frameworks for electrochemical nitrate reduction to ammonia. The COFs are highly crystalline in nature and have a high surface area. Among all the COFs, TNP-DMDA, which has methoxy functional groups, exhibited the highest yield (2282 µg h−1 mgcat−1) and the highest faradaic efficiency (82%) at −1.0 V vs. RHE. 15N labeling confirmed ammonia originated from nitrate reduction, while in situ IR identified the key intermediates. The catalyst retained its performance over 8 cycles with minimal loss and operated continuously for 40 hours without a significant loss in current density, demonstrating excellent durability. Overall, this study establishes triazatrinaphthylene-based COFs as a new class of metal-free electrocatalysts for nitrate reduction. By demonstrating how rational framework engineering tunes the electronic structure and catalytic-site chemistry, our work offers a blueprint for designing next-generation porous organic materials for sustainable NH3 synthesis and environmental remediation.
