Fused Partial Gromov–Wasserstein for Structured Objects
Yikun Bai, Shuang Wang, Huy Tran, Hengrong Du, Juexin Wang, Soheil Kolouri

Abstract
Structured data, such as graphs, is vital in machine learning due to its capacity to capture complex relationships and interactions. In recent years, the Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance has attracted growing interest because it enables the comparison of structured data by jointly accounting for feature similarity and geometric structure. However, as a variant of optimal transport (OT), classical FGW assumes an equal mass constraint on the compared data. In this work, we relax this mass constraint and propose the Fused Partial Gromov-Wasserstein (FPGW) framework, which extends FGW to accommodate unbalanced data. Theoretically, we establish the relationship between FPGW and FGW and prove the metric properties of FPGW. Numerically, we introduce Frank-Wolfe solvers and Sinkhorn solvers for the proposed FPGW framework. Finally, we evaluate the FPGW distance through graph matching, graph classification and graph clustering experiments, demonstrating its robust performance.

Background: Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) Problems

Note, graph-structured data can be measured as a metric measure space (mm-space) consisting of a set
X
endowed with a metric structure, that is, a notion of distance
d_{X}
between its elements, and equipped with a Borel measure
\mu
.
As in
(
18

, we will assume that

is compact and that
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)=X
.
Given two probability mm-spaces
\mathbb{X}=(X,d_{X},\mu)
,
\mathbb{Y}=(Y,d_{Y},\nu)

\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)

L:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
(e.g., the Euclidean distance or the KL-loss), the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) matching problem is defined as:

GW_{r,L}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r}(\cdot,\cdot),d_{Y}^{r}(\cdot,\cdot))),

(1)

where
r\geq 1
and

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mu,\nu):=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y):\gamma_{1}=\mu,\gamma_{2}=\nu\}.

(2)

For brevity, we employ the notation
\gamma^{\otimes 2}
for the product measure
d\gamma^{\otimes 2}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=d\gamma(x,y)d\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. If

L(a,b)=|a-b|^{q}
,
for
1\leq q<\infty
, we denote
GW_{r,L}(\cdot,\cdot)
by
d_{GW,r,q}^{q}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
In this case, the expression (
1
) defines an equivalence relation
\sim
among probability mm-spaces, i.e.,
\mathbb{X}\sim\mathbb{Y}
if and only if
d_{GW,r,q}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=0
1
1

1

Moreover, given two probability mm-spaces
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
,
d_{GW,r,q}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=0
if and only if there exists a bijective isometry
\phi:X\to Y
such that
\phi_{\#}\mu=\nu
.
In particular, the GW distance is invariant under rigid transformations (translations and rotations) of a given probability mm-space.
. A minimizer of the GW problem (
1

\min
. Moreover, similar to OT, the above GW problem defines a distance for probability mm-spaces after taking the quotient under

In particular, two equivalent extensions of the Gromov-Wasserstein problem, named

\displaystyle PGW_{r,L}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r}))+\lambda(|\mu^{\otimes 2}-\gamma_{1}^{\otimes 2}|+|\nu^{\otimes 2}-\gamma_{2}^{\otimes 2}|)

(3)

\displaystyle MPGW_{r,L}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(\mu,\nu)}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r})).

(4)

2.1
Fused Gromov-Wasserstein problem

Given two

\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y},\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\geq 0

\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}=1
, a cost function

\displaystyle FGW_{r,L}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\omega_{1}\gamma(C)+\omega_{2}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r})).

(7)

Similar to the original GW problem, the above problem admits a minimizer. In addition, when
C(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{q}
, and
L(\cdot_{1},\cdot_{2})=|\cdot_{1}-\cdot_{2}|^{q}
, it defines a semi-metric
[
30
]
.

2.2

The above formulation relies on the equal mass assumption, i.e.,
|\mu|=|\nu|

]

\displaystyle FUGW_{r,L,\lambda}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(X\times Y)}\omega_{1}\gamma(C)+\omega_{2}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r}))+\lambda(D_{\phi_{1}}(\gamma_{1}^{\otimes 2}\parallel\mu^{\otimes 2})+D_{\phi_{2}}(\gamma_{2}^{\otimes 2}\parallel\nu^{\otimes 2})),

In

\displaystyle FMPGW_{r,L,\rho}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\rho}_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\omega_{1}\gamma(C)+\omega_{2}\gamma^{\otimes 2}(L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r})).

(10)

3.2
Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

The above FPGW problem (
11

\displaystyle FPGW_{\rho}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in\gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(p,q)}\underbrace{\omega_{1}\langle C,\gamma\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle(M-2\lambda)\circ\gamma,\gamma}_{\mathcal{L}_{C,M-2\lambda}}\rangle+\underbrace{\lambda(|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2})}_{\text{constant}}\rangle

(12)

are defined in the previous subsection,

k

Step 1. Gradient computation.

Suppose

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C,M-2\lambda}(\gamma)=\omega_{1}C+\omega_{2}(M+M^{\top}-4\lambda)\circ\gamma.

Next, we aim to find the optimal

\displaystyle\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}:=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C,M-2\lambda}(\gamma^{(k-1)}),\gamma^{k}\rangle.

(13)

Step 2. linear search algorithm.

In this step, we aim to find the optimal step size

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}:=\arg\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathcal{L}_{C,M-2\lambda}((1-\alpha)\gamma^{(k-1)}+\alpha\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}).

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\leq 0,a+b\leq 0,\\

\text{clip}(\frac{-b}{2a},[0,1]),&\text{if }a>0\end{cases},\begin{cases}a&=\omega_{2}\langle(M-2\lambda)\circ\delta\gamma,\delta\gamma\rangle\\

4.1
Toy example: Graph clustering

Given a set of unlabeled graphs
\{G_{1},\ldots G_{K}\}
, we compare the performance of FGW and FPGW in the graph clustering task.

Dataset setup.

We adapt the dataset generated by
[
24
]
. Each graph follows a simple Stochastic Block Model, and the groups are defined w.r.t. the number of communities inside each graph and the distribution of their labels. The dataset contains three different types of graphs, and each type contains five graphs. Each graph contains 30 or 40 nodes, and the feature of each node is randomly selected in a compact set
[-2,2]
.

In addition, we randomly generate outliers and add them to graphs. In particular, we select 50% of graphs and add
30\%
outliers. These outlier nodes are randomly connected or connected to the original nodes. For each outlier node, we define its feature as a random value in
[2,2+2\text{std}]
, where
std
is the standard deviation of all features of nodes in the graph.
Similar to the setting of graph classification, for each graph
G
, we suppose
N_{G}
is the number of nodes that are not outliers. We assume
N_{G}
is known in this experiment. We refer to figure
2
for the visualization of these graphs.

Baseline: FGW K-means.

We consider the FGW K-means method introduced in Section 4.3 of
[
24
]
as the baseline method. Specifically, given graphs
\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}
and the number of clusters
K^{\prime}\leq K
, the method iteratively alternates between the following two steps:

–

Step 1.
For each
i\in[1:K]
and
j\in[1:K^{\prime}]
, compute the distance between each graph
G_{i}
and centroid
G_{j}^{\prime}
. Assign each graph
G_{i}
to the closest centroid based on the computed distances.

–

Step 2.
Using the updated assignments, recompute each centroid
G_{j}^{\prime}
, where
j^{\prime}\in[1:K^{\prime}]
, as the center of the graphs assigned to it.

In the FGW K-means method, the Fused-GW distance is used to define the distance in Step 1, while the Fused-GW barycenter is used to compute the “center” in Step 2.

Our method: FPGW K-means.

Inspired by the FGW K-means method, we introduce the FPGW K-means method. In summary, we adapt the FMPGW discrepancy to measure the distance in Step 1, and introduce the
FPGW barycenter
to define the “center” in Step 2.

First, we convert the graphs to mm-spaces using the formulation introduced in Section
L
. Specifically, given a graph
G=(V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\},E)
, we define

\mathbb{X}=\left(V,C,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N_{G}}\delta_{v_{i}}\right),

where
C=[c(v_{i},v_{i^{\prime}})]_{i,i^{\prime}\in[1:N]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and
c(v_{i},v_{i^{\prime}})
is the “shortest path distance” determined by
E
. Here,
N_{G}
is the number of nodes that are not outliers, and its value is known based on the experiment setup. The feature distance between nodes
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
is defined as the Euclidean distance. We then use the Fused-PGW discrepancy (
10
) to measure the distance between each pair of graphs and centroids in Step 1 of the K-means method.

For Step 2, suppose the set of graphs
\bm{G}^{k}\subset\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}
is assigned to cluster
k^{\prime}
for each
k^{\prime}\in[1:K^{\prime}]
. We define the “center” using the following
Fused-PGW barycenter
:

\displaystyle\min_{G}\sum_{G_{k}\in\bm{G}^{k}}\frac{1}{|\bm{G}^{k}|}\text{FMPGW}_{\rho_{k}}(G,G_{k}),

(18)

where
\rho_{k}=1
for all
k
. The solution to this problem provides the updated centroid for cluster
k^{\prime}
. Details of the formal formulation and solver are provided in Appendix
J

We iteratively repeat the above two steps until all centroids/assignments converge.

Other parameter setting.

In this experiment, we set
\omega_{2}=0.999
for both the FGW and FPGW methods. In addition, we set the number of clustering
K^{\prime}=3

Performance analysis.

5
Summary.

In this paper, we proposed a novel formulation called “fused-partial Gromov-Wasserstein” (fused-PGW) for comparing structured objects. Theoretically, we demonstrated the metric properties of fused-PGW, and numerically, we introduced the corresponding Frank-Wolfe solver, Sinkhorn Solver and barycenter algorithms. Finally, we applied fused-PGW to graph matching and clustering experiments, showing that it achieves more robust performance due to its partial matching property.

Appendix B
Background of optimal transport problems

The Optimal Transport (OT) problems
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, with transportation cost
c(x,y):\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
being a
lower-semi continuous function, is defined as:

\displaystyle OT(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\gamma(c),

(19)

\displaystyle\text{ where }\quad\gamma(c):=\int_{\Omega^{2}}c(x,y)\,d\gamma(x,y)

and where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all joint probability measures on
\Omega^{2}:=\Omega\times\Omega
with marginals
\mu,\nu
, i.e.,
\gamma_{1}:=\pi_{1\#}\gamma=\mu,\gamma_{2}:=\pi_{2\#}\gamma=\nu
,
where
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:\Omega^{2}\to\Omega
are the canonical projections
\pi_{1}(x,y):=x,\pi_{2}(x,y):=y
. A minimizer for (
19
) always exists
[
7
,
48
]
and when
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{p}
, for
p\geq 1
,
it defines a metric on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, which is referred to as the “
p
-Wasserstein distance”:

\displaystyle W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\Omega^{2}}\|x-y\|^{p}d\gamma(x,y).

(20)

The Partial Optimal Transport (POT) problem

[
8
,
49
,
50
]
extends the OT problem to the set of Radon measures
\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega)
, i.e., non-negative and finite measures. For
\lambda>0
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega)
, the POT problem is defined as:

POT(\mu,\nu;\lambda):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega^{2})}\gamma(c)+\lambda(|\mu-\gamma_{1}|+|\nu-\gamma_{2}|),

(21)

where, in general,
|\sigma|
denotes the total variation norm of a measure
\sigma
, i.e.,
|\sigma|:=\sigma(\Omega)
.
The constraint
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega^{2})
in (
21
) can be further restricted to
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
:

D.4
Future’s direction.

Note, the general version for (the p-th power of) fused-Gromov Wasserstein distance is defined by

\displaystyle d_{FGW,r,q,p}^{p}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\left(\omega_{1}\|x-y\|^{q}+|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|^{q}\right)^{p}d\gamma^{\otimes 2}.

(51)

Inspired by the above formulation, we propose the following generalized fused-partial Gromov Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle d_{FGW,r,q,p}^{p}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\left(\omega_{1}\frac{\|x-y\|^{q}}{|\gamma|}+|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|^{q}+\lambda(\frac{|\mu|^{2}}{|\gamma|^{2}}+\frac{|\nu|^{2}}{|\gamma|^{2}}-2)\right)^{p}d\gamma^{\otimes 2}.

The fused-PGW distance defined in (

holds for the above general form. We leave the theoretical study of the metric property for our future work.

Appendix E
Frank Wolf Algorithm for the Fused Mass-constraint Partial Gromov Wasserstein problem

In discrete setting, the FMPGW problem (
10
) becomes the following:

\displaystyle FMPGW_{\rho}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=\min_{\gamma\in\gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(p,q)}\underbrace{\omega_{1}\langle C,\gamma\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle M\circ\gamma,\gamma}_{\mathcal{L}_{C,M}}\rangle

(53)

where
\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(\mathrm{p},\mathrm{q}):=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}:\gamma 1_{m}\leq\mathrm{p},\gamma^{\top}1_{n}\leq\mathrm{q},|\gamma|\geq\rho\}
,

C=[C(x_{i},y_{j})]_{i\in[1:n],j\in[1:m]}
,
M_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}:=L(C^{X},C^{Y}):=[L(C^{X}_{i,i^{\prime}},C^{Y}_{j,j^{\prime}})]_{i,i\in[1:n],j,j^{\prime}\in[1:n]}
,
M\circ\gamma=[\langle M[i,j,\cdot,\cdot],\gamma\rangle]_{i,j}
.

Similar to the Fused Gromov-Wasserstein problem, we propose the following Frank-Wolfe algorithm as a solver: The above problem will be solved iteratively. In every iteration, say
k
, we will adapt the following steps:

Step 1. Gradient computation.

Suppose
\gamma^{(k-1)}
is the transportation plan in the previous iteration; it is straightforward to verify:

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C,M}(\gamma)=\omega_{1}C+\omega_{2}((M+M^{\top})\circ\gamma),

where
M^{\top}=[M_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},i,j}]_{i,i^{\prime}\in[1:n],j,j^{\prime}\in[1:m]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times n\times m}
.
Next, we aim to find
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(\mu,\nu)
for the following problem:

\displaystyle\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}:=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(\mu,\nu)}\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C,M}(\gamma^{(k-1)}),\gamma\rangle.

(54)

which is a mass-constraint partial OT problem.

Step 2. linear search algorithm.

In this step, we aim to find the optimal step size
\alpha^{(k)}\in[0,1]
. In particular,

\displaystyle\alpha^{(k)}:=\arg\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathcal{L}_{C,M}((1-\alpha)\gamma^{(k-1)}+\alpha\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}).

Let
\delta\gamma=\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}-\gamma^{(k-1)}
, the above problem is essentially quadratic problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}((1-\alpha)\gamma^{(k-1)}+\alpha\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}})=\alpha^{2}\underbrace{\langle\omega_{2}M\circ\delta\gamma,\delta\gamma\rangle}_{a}

\displaystyle+\alpha\underbrace{\langle\omega_{2}(M+M^{\top})\circ\gamma^{(k-1)}+\omega_{1}C,\delta\gamma\rangle}_{b}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\langle\omega_{2}M\circ\gamma^{(k-1)}+\omega_{1}C,\gamma\rangle}_{c}

(55)

and
\alpha^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\leq 0,a+b\leq 0,\\
0&\text{if }a\leq 0,a+b>0\\
\text{clip}(\frac{-b}{2a},[0,1]),&\text{if }a>0\end{cases}.

(56)

Then
\gamma^{(k)}=(1-\alpha^{*})\gamma^{(k-1)}+\alpha^{*}\gamma^{(k)}{{}^{\prime}}
.

,
\omega_{1}\in[0,1],\rho\in[0,\min(|\mathrm{p}|,|\mathrm{q}|)]
.

\gamma^{(final)}

for

// Compute gradient

// Solve the POT problem.

Compute

via (

In this section, we introduce some fundamental results for tensor computation.

Lemma G.1

\displaystyle\langle M\circ\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}\rangle

Appendix H
Convergence Analysis

As in
[

H.1
Fused-MPGW

Consider the minimization problems

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\rho}_{\leq}(\mathrm{p},\mathrm{q})}\mathcal{L}_{C,M}(\gamma):=\omega_{1}\langle C,\gamma\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle M\circ\gamma,\gamma\rangle

that corresponds to the discrete fused-PGW problem (
11
).

The objective functions

\gamma\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\hat{M}}(\gamma)=\omega_{1}\langle C,\gamma\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle M\circ\gamma,\gamma\rangle,

is non-convex in general.
However, the constraint set
\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(p,q)
are convex and compact on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
(see Proposition
C.4
.)

Consider the
Frank-Wolfe gap
of
\mathcal{L}_{C,M}
at the approximation
\gamma^{(k)}
of the optimal plan
\gamma
:

g_{k}=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}})}\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{M}}(\gamma^{(k)}),\gamma^{(k)}-\gamma\rangle_{F}.

(71)

It provided a good criterion to measure the distance to a stationary point at iteration
k
. Indeed, a plan
\gamma^{(k)}
is a stationary transportation plan for the corresponding constrained optimization problem in (
H.1
) if and only if
g_{k}=0
. Moreover,
g_{k}
is always non-negative (
g_{k}\geq 0
).

From Theorem 1 in
[
56
]
, after
K
iterations, we have the following upper bound for the minimal Frank-Wolf gap:

g_{K}:=\min_{1\leq k\leq K}g_{k}\leq\frac{\max\{2L_{1},\mathrm{Lip}\cdot(\text{diam}(\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}})))^{2}\}}{\sqrt{K}},

(72)

where

L_{1}:=\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{M}}(\gamma^{(1)})-\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}})}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{M}}(\gamma)

is the initial global sub-optimal bound for the initialization
\gamma^{(1)}
of the algorithm;
Lip
is the Lipschitz constant of function

\gamma\mapsto\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{M}}
; and

\text{diam}(\Gamma_{\leq}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}}))=\sup_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{F}

is the
\|\cdot\|_{F}
diameter of
\Gamma_{\leq}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.

The important thing to notice is that the constant
\max\{2L_{1},D_{L}\}
does not depend on the iteration step
k
. Thus, according to Theorem 1 in
[
56
]
, the rate in
\tilde{g}_{K}
is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{K})
. That is, the algorithm takes at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})
iterations to find an approximate stationary point with a gap smaller than
\varepsilon
.

Next, we will continue to simplify the upper bound (
72
). We first introduce the following fundamental results:

Lemma H.1
.

In the discrete setting, we have

\displaystyle{\rm diam}(\Gamma_{\leq}(p,q))\leq 2\rho.

(73)

Proof.

Choose
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\leq}^{\rho}(p,q)
,
we apply the property

\displaystyle(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2},\quad\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

(74)

and obtain

\displaystyle\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}|\gamma_{i,j}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i,j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}2|\gamma_{i,j}|^{2}+2|\gamma^{\prime}_{i,j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left[\left(\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}\gamma_{i,j}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}\gamma^{\prime}_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2(|\gamma|^{2}+|\gamma^{\prime}|^{2})

(75)

\displaystyle=2(\rho^{2}+\rho^{2})=4\rho^{2},

and thus, we complete the proof.
∎

Lemma H.2
.

The Lipschitz constant term in (
72
) can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Lip}\leq\omega_{2}nm\max(|M|)^{2}.

(76)

In particular, when
L(\mathrm{r}_{1},\mathrm{r}_{2})=|\mathrm{r}_{1}-\mathrm{r}_{2}|^{p}
where
p\geq 1
, we have

\text{Lip}\leq nm(2^{p-1}C^{X}+2^{p-1}C^{Y})^{2}
.

Proof.

Pick
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\leq}({\mathrm{p}},{\mathrm{q}})
we have,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{M}(\gamma)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C,M}(\gamma^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(\omega_{1}C+\omega_{2}(M\circ\gamma+M^{\top}\circ\gamma))-(\omega_{1}C+\omega_{2}(M\circ\gamma^{\prime}+M^{\top}\circ\gamma^{\prime}))\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{2}^{2}\|M\circ(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}+2\omega_{2}^{2}\|M^{\top}\circ(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\left[M\circ(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right]_{i,j}\right)^{2}+2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\left[M^{\top}\circ(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right]_{i,j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}M_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)^{2}+2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}M^{\top}_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|M_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}||\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\right)^{2}+2\omega_{2}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|M^{\top}_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}||\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\omega_{2}^{2}\underbrace{\max(M)^{2}}_{A}\cdot\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{n,m}|\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\right)^{2}}_{B}

For the second term, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{n,m}|\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}^{n,m}\left(nm\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{n,m}\left|\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}m^{2}\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}_{F}

(77)

For the first term, when
L(\mathrm{r}_{1},\mathrm{r}_{2})=|\mathrm{r}_{1}-\mathrm{r}_{2}|^{p}
, from the inequality (
49
), we have:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\max M\leq\max\{2^{p-1}(C^{X})^{p}+2^{p-1}(C^{Y})^{p}\}^{2}.

where

\max\{2^{p-1}(C^{X})^{p}+2^{p-1}(C^{Y})^{p}\}:=\max\{\max_{i,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},j^{\prime}}2^{p-1}(C^{X}_{i,i^{\prime}})^{p}+2^{p-1}(C^{Y}_{j,j^{\prime}})^{p}\}.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\text{Lip}\leq\frac{\max(2^{p-1}(C^{X})^{p}+2^{p-1}(C^{Y})^{p})nm\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{F}}{\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{F}}=\omega_{2}nm\max(2^{p-1}(C^{X})^{p}+2^{p-1}(C^{Y})^{p}).

and we complete the proof.
∎

Appendix I
Sinkhon Algorithm for Fused Partial Gromov Wasserstein problem

For convenience, in this section we default to setting

L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{R})=\|d_{X}-d_{Y}\|^{2},

while all propositions, algorithms and proofs extend without loss of generality to a generic loss function
L(d_{X}^{r},d_{Y}^{r})
.

I.1
Sinkhorn Algorithm for FPGW metric

Given mm-spaces
\mathbb{X}=(X,d_{X},\mu),\mathbb{Y}=(Y,d_{Y},\nu)
, the general unbalanced Fused-Gromov Wasserstein setting, the entropic problem is defined as the following:

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\gamma)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\gamma^{\otimes 2}\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

(86)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\omega_{1}\int_{X\times Y}d(x,y)d\gamma+\omega_{2}\int_{X^{2}\times Y^{2}}|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2}d\gamma^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda(D_{\phi}(\gamma_{1}^{\otimes 2}\parallel\mu^{\otimes 2})+D_{\phi}(\gamma_{2}^{\otimes 2}\parallel\nu^{\otimes 2}))

where
D_{\phi}
is
\phi-
divergence,

D_{KL}(\mu\parallel\nu)=\begin{cases}\underbrace{\int\ln(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu})d\mu}_{\bar{D}_{KL}(\mu\parallel\nu)}+|\nu|-|\mu|&\text{if }\mu\ll\nu\\
+\infty&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}.

In the Fused Partial GW setting,
D_{\phi}
becomes

\displaystyle D_{PTV}(\mu\parallel\nu):=\begin{cases}|\mu-\nu|_{TV}=|\nu-\mu|&\text{if }\mu\leq\nu\\
+\infty&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}.

(87)

Note, from the definition of (
87
), we can restrict the searching space for
\gamma
to
\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
:

\displaystyle EFPGW(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})

\displaystyle:=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\gamma^{\otimes 2}\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

(88)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\gamma)=\omega_{1}\langle c,\gamma\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2}\otimes\gamma^{\otimes 2}\rangle+\lambda(|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2}-2|\gamma|^{2})

Problem (
88
) can be further relaxed as:

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma,\pi\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\mathcal{F}(\gamma,\pi)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\pi\otimes\gamma\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

(89)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu):=\omega_{1}\langle d(x,y),\frac{\gamma+\pi}{2}\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2},\gamma\otimes\pi\rangle+\lambda(|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2}-2|\gamma||\pi|)

It is clear
\mathcal{F}(\gamma,\gamma)=\mathcal{F}(\gamma)
. Thus,
(\ref{eq:entropy-fpgw-2})\leq(\ref{eq:entropy-fpgw})
, and we denote (
89
) as
LB-FPGW_{\lambda}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
(lower bound of Fused Partial Gromov Wasserstein). Essentially, the Sinkhorn algorithm aims to solve
LB-FPGW
.

We first introduce the following fundamental proposition. Note, a similar version can be found in
[
20
, Proposition 4]
:

Proposition I.1
.

Given a fixed
\pi\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
, considering the problem:

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\mathcal{F}(\pi,\gamma)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\pi\otimes\gamma\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2}),

it is equivalent to solve the following entropic optimal partial transport problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c_{\pi}(x,y)d\gamma+\lambda|\pi|(|\mu|+|\nu|-2|\gamma|)+\epsilon|\pi|D_{KL}(\gamma\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)

(90)

where

\displaystyle c_{\pi}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{1}d(x,y)+\omega_{2}[|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2}\circ\pi](x,y)+\epsilon\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)

\displaystyle[|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2}\circ\pi](x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|d\pi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

Proof.

Fix
\pi\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
in (
89
).
Since
\pi,\gamma\leq\mu\otimes\nu
, we have
\pi,\gamma\ll\mu\otimes\nu
, thus we have:

\displaystyle D_{KL}(\pi\otimes\gamma\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\ln(\frac{d\pi d\gamma}{d\mu\otimes\nu\cdot d\mu\otimes\nu})d\mu d\gamma+(|\mu||\nu|)^{2}-|\pi||\gamma|

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\left[\int_{X\times Y}\ln(\frac{d\pi}{d\mu\otimes\nu})d\pi\right]d\gamma+\int_{X\times Y}\left[\int_{X\times Y}\ln(\frac{d\gamma}{d\mu\otimes\nu})d\gamma\right]d\pi+(|\mu||\nu|)^{2}-|\pi||\gamma|

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}d\pi\overline{D}_{KL}(\gamma\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)+\int_{X\times Y}d\gamma\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)+(|\mu||\nu|)^{2}-|\pi||\gamma|

\displaystyle=|\pi|D_{KL}(\gamma\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)+|\gamma|\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)+((|\mu||\nu|)^{2}-|\pi||\mu||\nu|)

(91)

we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\pi,\gamma)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\gamma\times\pi\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\langle d,\frac{\gamma+\pi}{2}\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2},\gamma\otimes\pi\rangle+\lambda(D_{PTV}(\gamma_{1}\otimes\pi_{1}\parallel\mu^{\otimes 2})+D_{PTV}(\gamma_{2}\otimes\mu_{2}\parallel\nu^{\otimes 2}))

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon D_{KL}(\gamma\times\pi\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\langle d,\frac{\gamma+\pi}{2}\rangle+\omega_{2}\langle|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2},\gamma\otimes\pi\rangle+\lambda(|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2}-2|\gamma||\pi|)+\epsilon D_{KL}(\gamma\times\pi\parallel(\mu\otimes\nu)^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\omega_{1}\langle d,\pi\rangle+\lambda(|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2})+\epsilon[(|\mu||\nu|)^{2}-|\pi||\mu||\nu|]}_{constant}

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\frac{1}{2}\omega_{1}d+\omega_{2}|d_{X}-d_{Y}|^{2}\circ\pi+\epsilon\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu),\gamma\rangle-2\lambda|\pi||\gamma|)+\epsilon|\pi|D_{KL}(\gamma\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)

where we use the fact (
91
) and

\displaystyle|\gamma|\epsilon\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu)=\langle\epsilon\overline{D}_{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu),\gamma\rangle.

If we ignore the constant part, the remaining part is exactly the entropic partial OT (up to a constant
\lambda|\pi|(|\mu|+|\nu|)
), and we complete the proof.
∎

Note, the partial OT problem (
90
) can be solved by the classical Sinkhorn algorithm. See e.g.
[
57
,
58
]
.

Algorithm 4

Sinkhorn Partial OT

Input:

c,\epsilon,\lambda,\mathrm{p},\mathrm{q}

Output:

\gamma

Initialize

–

Adjacency indicator function:

Let

in the feature space.

The feature similarity function
d_{\mathcal{F}}
is set as follows:

–

Continuous feature graph:
Since
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

–

Discrete feature graph:

[

Wall-clock time analysis.

The wall-clock times are reported in Table
5
. All graph data are formatted as NetworkX graphs using the
NetworkX library
. Continuous features are represented as 64-bit float NumPy vectors, while discrete features are stored as 64-bit integers.

For discrete feature graphs, WLK, GK, and Vertex Histogram are the fastest methods, while FGW and FPGW have similar wall-clock times. In contrast, Lovász Theta and Random Walk Kernel are the slowest. For continuous feature graphs, “Propagation” and “GraphHopper” are significantly faster than FGW and FPGW.

Among the three fused-GW-based methods, FGW is the fastest overall, while FUGW is the slowest. A potential reason for this difference is that FGW and FPGW leverage a C++ linear programming solver for the OT/POT solving step. In addition, FUGW adapts the Sinkhorn solver, leading to an accuracy-efficiency trade-off. Specifically, a smaller
\epsilon
can reduce the accuracy gap introduced by the entropic term; however, it increases the number of iterations required for convergence.

All experiments presented in this paper are conducted on a computational machine with an AMD EPYC 7713 64-Core Processor, 8
\times
