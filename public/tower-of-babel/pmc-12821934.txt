Smart devices, head posture, and malocclusion: is there a relationship? A prospective controlled study
Authors: Mariam Mohammad Al-Abdallah, Mira Khaldoun Salem, Shafiq Adel Masoud, Bushra As’ad Alhaj, Ahmad Ashraf AlSamhan, Soukaina Tawfiq Ryalat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsidering the widespread and growing use of smart devices, this study aimed to investigate the association between smart device use and malocclusion, potentially mediated by changes in head posture.MethodsDental patients scheduled to undergo lateral cephalograms at a university dental hospital and who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. A questionnaire was completed to collect baseline demographics (age, gender, and parental education) and assess the severity of smart device use (MPPUS-10). Craniocervical and craniofacial measurements, hyoid bone position, and malocclusion characteristics were assessed using lateral cephalometric analysis.ResultsA total of 97 patients (aged 9 to 20 years) completed the questionnaire. Patients with moderate smart device use (n = 50) were excluded, and only mild users (G1 = 23) and severe users (G2 = 24) were included in the final analysis. The orientation of the maxilla relative to the vertical axis was significantly more clockwise in G2 (P = 0.013). Skeletal pattern assessment using ANB and Wits analyses showed that G1 had significantly more Class I skeletal patterns, while G2 had a higher prevalence of Class III patterns. Although severe users demonstrated more forward bending of the cervical column with smaller craniocervical angles, these differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionThis study suggests an association between severe smart device use and a higher prevalence of Class III malocclusion. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between smart device use, head posture, and craniofacial development.Keywords: Smart devices, Malocclusion, Head posture, Craniocervical orientation

Background

Occlusion encompasses more than just the static contact between maxillary and mandibular teeth; it reflects the dynamic relationship among skeletal structures, soft tissues, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), in addition to neuromuscular maturation, oral development and function [1]. The development of occlusion, craniofacial structures, and the cervical vertebral column is a complex embryological process, with most structures sharing origins in the notochord and neural crest cells [2, 3].

Malocclusion is defined as any deviation from normal occlusion that compromises aesthetics and/or function. The World Health Organization has categorized malocclusion under “Handicapping Dento-Facial Anomalies,” warranting intervention for physical and emotional well-being [4]. It is a multifactorial condition influenced by genetic, environmental, and postural factors.

The cranio–mandibular–cervical complex, which includes the cranial base, cervical vertebrae, mandible, TMJ, and dentoalveolar structures, functions as an integrated biomechanical system. Proper coordination among craniovertebral centric relation, craniomandibular centric relation, and dental occlusion (maximum intercuspation) is essential for balanced craniofacial growth, optimal function, and prevention of musculoskeletal strain [5, 6].

A growing body of research supports an association between head posture and craniofacial morphology, especially in the sagittal and vertical planes. Studies have shown that individuals with altered craniocervical posture—often due to airway obstruction or habitual postural compensation—exhibit features such as Class II malocclusion, anterior crowding, and dolichocephalic facial types [7, 8]. Solow and Tallgren’s foundational studies [9–12] described the correlation between craniofacial morphology and head posture. They found that patients with true forward growth rotation of the mandible were presented with head extension (increased craniocervical angle) and those with true posterior growth rotation presented with head flexion (reduced craniofacial angle) [10]. They also found that on average a small craniocervical angle was associated with a horizontal facial development and a large craniocervical angle was associated with a vertical facial development [11]. They then introduced the “soft tissue stretching hypothesis,” suggesting that postural changes in the head and neck region alter the soft tissue envelope, thereby influencing skeletal and dental development [12]. This was later extended to explain how head extension (increased craniocervical angle) may inhibit sagittal dentoalveolar development and contribute to anterior crowding. This was termed as “the differential forces on the skeleton in relation to postural changes” [13–15].

Subsequent studies have reinforced associations between craniocervical posture and orthodontic features such as skeletal pattern, overjet, arch deficiencies, and cervical vertebral anomalies [16–19]. While these findings suggest a bidirectional relationship, the causal pathway remains unclear. Recent literature further emphasizes the clinical relevance of craniovertebral alignment and orthopedic stability in cranio-mandibular function and orthodontic diagnosis. Šedý et al. (2022) highlighted the neural mechanisms underlying cervicogenic orofacial pain and the importance of maintaining proper coordination within the cranio–mandibular–cervical complex [5]. Demirovic et al. (2024) demonstrated that orthopedic instability and temporomandibular joint function directly influence cephalometric interpretation and orthodontic planning [6].

In parallel, the widespread and intensive use of smart devices has introduced new lifestyle-related postural habits. Global access to these devices ranges from 20% in low-income regions to nearly 100% in developed countries [20]. Problematic use has been linked to musculoskeletal strain, particularly in the head, neck, and shoulder regions, and is associated with symptoms like fatigue, headaches, and concentration difficulties [21–23]. Studies have demonstrated that smart device use significantly alters head flexion and cervical posture compared to neutral standing positions, with additional impacts on respiratory function and TMJ health [24–26].

Despite intensive investigation into both craniocervical posture and malocclusion, no study has clearly identified the initiating factor. The habitual postures associated with smart device use present a unique, contemporary opportunity to explore this relationship. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between problematic smart device use and orthodontic malocclusion, mediated through changes in head posture. We hypothesized that if craniocervical posture contributes to the development of malocclusion, then individuals with problematic device use would exhibit more pronounced and characteristic craniofacial and occlusal features than mild users. The null hypothesis posits that smart device use has no effect on malocclusion characteristics.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Research Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry at the University of Jordan and the Ethics and Research Committee of Jordan University Hospital (IRB number: 2020/52). To ensure sufficient statistical power for detecting clinically meaningful differences in cephalometric outcomes, a priori sample size calculation was conducted. Based on prior literature, we determined that a sample of 21 participants per group would be required to detect a mean difference of 3 in cephalometric measurements (assumed to be clinically significant), with an estimated standard deviation of 3, a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 90% (β = 0.10). These assumptions correspond to a standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of 1.0, representing a large effect. The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for comparing two independent means: n = 2 × [(Z₁₋⍺/2 + Z₁₋β)² × σ²]/(µ₁ − µ₂)².

Where: Z₁₋⍺/2 = 1.96 (for α = 0.05), Z₁₋β = 1.28 (for 90% power), σ = 3 (standard deviation), µ₁ − µ₂ = 3 (expected mean difference). Substituting these values:

n = 2 × [(1.96 + 1.28)² × 9]/9 = 2 × 10.4976 ≈ 21 participants per group [27].

The study sample included patients aged from 9 to 20 years who attended a university dental hospital, and were scheduled to have digital cephalometric radiographs for diagnostic and treatment purposes. Patients with significant medical history, upper airway obstruction, neck or backbone problems, cerebral palsy, severe skeletal discrepancies, craniofacial syndromes and clefts, or previous history of orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery were excluded. A total of 97 patients, who provided informed consent to participate in the study, completed the MPPUS-10 under researcher supervision in a neutral, non-judgmental environment, with clarification of any unclear items. Problematic mobile phone use was measured by the MPPUS-10 (Mobile Phone Problem Use Scale) [28]. This is a shorter version of the original MPPUS-27 [29] that was developed to address different aspects of smart device addiction, which includes loss of control, withdrawal, negative life consequences, craving and peer dependence [23]. The ten items have to be answered on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not true at all) to 10 (extremely true). The participants were divided according to the MPPUS scale total results into 3 groups: mild users with scores less than or equal to 39 (G1), moderate users with scores more than 39 but less than 59, and sever users with scores more than or equal to 59 (G2) [22]. Participants in the severe group and the mild group were included in the analysis, while moderate users were excluded from the primary analysis due to the absence of well-defined thresholds and their heterogeneous usage patterns, which could obscure group differences [28]. This binary classification was designed to maximize contrast and facilitate the detection of significant differences in cephalometric and postural outcomes [30–32].

Collected data were inserted in an excel file and when data collection was finalized the file was converted to SPSS file format. SPSS program version 25.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp., 2017) was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics and frequency tables were generated and data were checked for normality to explore the distribution. Baseline demographic data and pattern of smart device use were compared between G1 and G2 using t-test (age) and chi-square test (gender, data on smart device use). Independent sample t-test was used to compare the cephalometric variables between G1 and G2. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to assess the magnitude of differences. To compare the skeletal patterns, OJ and OB among the two groups we used chi-square test with Post Hoc analysis. Cramer’s V was used to quantify the strength of association for categorical comparisons. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

The number of patients completed the questionnaire was 97 patients (54 females (55.7%) and 43 males (44.3%)), age ranged from 9.1 to 20.8 years (mean = 15.2 years, SD = 2.65). As none of the respondents reported lack of access to or use of a smart device, the formation of a control group comprising non-users was not possible. Responders were divided according to the severity of smart-device use based on the MPPU-10 scale. Patients with mild use (G1 = 23) and severe use (G2 = 24) were included in the statistical analysis for comparison, while patients with moderate use (n = 50) were excluded to avoid bias [28, 30–32].

The sociodemographic characteristics of the study sample (n = 47) are presented in Fig. 2. The mean age in Group 1 (G1) was 15.04 years (SD = 2.31), and in Group 2 (G2) was 16.21 years (SD = 2.44), with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.098; 95% CI: − 2.57 to 0.23). Similarly, there were no significant differences between groups in gender distribution or in the reported pattern of time spent using smart devices. Most participants in both groups owned their own smart device. Only six participants used a family member’s device, and these were evenly distributed across both groups, with no significant group difference. Given the statistical comparability of the groups in terms of age, gender, and device use patterns—and supported by our theoretical framework—multivariate adjustment for these variables was deemed unnecessary, as it would not meaningfully alter the interpretation of the study findings.

Cephalometric measures by compared groups are reported in Table 3. For the cervical column orientation presented by OPT-horizontal angle and CVT-horizontal angle, G2 showed more forward bending of the cervical column (head flexion) with smaller measured angles (87.43 and 77.83 respectively) compared to G1 (91.31 and 81.87 respectively). Although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.176 and P = 0.166, respectively), the effect sizes indicated a moderate association (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.40), suggesting a potentially meaningful clinical trend. In addition, all cranio-cervical angles were comparable between the groups, with no statistically significant differences and negligible effect sizes, indicating minimal association. The orientation of the cranial base, maxilla, and mandible relative to the vertical axis was reduced in the G2 group (104.61°, 96.91°, and 68.44°, respectively) compared to the G1 group (108.30°, 101.73°, and 71.63°, respectively), suggesting a clockwise rotation of these planes consistent with forward head posture (head flexion) when the profile faces right. Although the difference reached statistical significance only for the maxillary angle (P = 0.013), all three parameters demonstrated moderate to strong effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.557, 0.752, 0.475 respectively), indicating potentially meaningful clinical differences despite limited statistical significance. The remaining facial measurements, which assessed sagittal and vertical relationships as well as facial bone lengths, showed no significant differences between the groups and exhibited mild to negligible associations. Dental measurements of upper and lower incisor proclination were higher in G2, while overbite (OB) and overjet (OJ) values were higher in G1; these differences were not statistically significant but showed moderate associations (0.2 < Cohen’s d < 0.5). Measurements related to the position of the hyoid bone were similar between groups, with no significant differences and small to negligible associations (Cohen’s d < 0.2).

Discussion

Problematic usage of smart devices is increasing worldwide. Concurrently, smartphone users often adopt a specific posture, holding the device below eye level and looking down at it while typing or watching, which can contribute to a forward flexion of the head. While the relationship between head and cervical posture and malocclusion on one hand, and head and cervical posture and problematic smart device use on the other, has been extensively investigated, the association between smart device use and malocclusion has not been previously explored. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the potential relationship between smart device usage and malocclusion through changes in head posture. The participants were categorized based on the severity of smart device use, as assessed using the MPPU-10 scale. The analysis focused on patients with mild use (G1 = 23) and severe use (G2 = 24) for comparative purposes. A control group of non-users could not be included, as none of the respondents reported not using or lacking access to a smart device.

The decision to exclude moderate users was a methodological choice aimed at reducing misclassification and improving internal validity. Prior studies have shown that moderate users often demonstrate inconsistent behavioral profiles, potentially introducing noise into statistical comparisons [28, 32]. While this limits generalizability to the full spectrum of smartphone use, it enhances our ability to detect meaningful differences between well-defined usage extremes [30, 31]. Future research could explore gradient effects across all usage levels, ideally through longitudinal or population-based designs.

Regarding cervical column orientation, as determined by the OPT-horizontal angle and CVT- horizontal angle, our study found that severe users (G2) exhibited a greater forward bending of the cervical column with smaller measured angles compared to mild users (G1). However, these differences were not statistically significant but had moderate assocation. In comparison, a previous study that considered young age group (15 to 19 years) illustrated that smartphone use addiction caused significant alterations in cervical posture and head anteriorization [39].

Measurements assessing the orientation of the cranial base, maxillary plane, and mandibular plane relative to the true vertical line were higher in G1 than in G2, reflecting greater head extension in mild smart device users (G1) and increased head flexion in severe users (G2). While only the maxillary plane orientation showed a statistically significant difference between groups, moderate to strong effect sizes was observed across all three parameters indicating potentially meaningful clinical differences that warrant further investigation.

When the skeletal pattern was analysed, our findings revealed that the proportion of patients with Class I skeletal pattern in mild smart device users (G1) was significantly higher than severe users, while the proportion of patients with Class lll skeletal pattern in severe users (G2) was significantly higher than mild users (G1) as determined by the ANB angle and Wits analysis. These results are consistent with the work conducted by Hedayati et al. (2013), who reported that participants with skeletal Class III malocclusion tend to exhibit greater head flexion compared to those with Class I malocclusion [40]. Similarly, Marcotte (1981) and Bjork (1951) observed that people with retrognathic facial profile and an obtuse cranial base angle tend to maintain a more extended head position. Conversely, individuals with prognathic profiles usually have a more acute cranial base angle and tend to flex their heads toward their chests [19, 41]. Additionaly, Vukicevic and Petrovic (2016) suggested that changes in the craniocervical angle could result in a deterioration of Class ll or Class lll malocclusions [42]. Recently, Peng et al. (2024) carried out a study to investigate the correlation between posture and craniofacial morphology in individuals with different skeletal malocclusions, and their results align with ours, highlighting that patients with skeletal Class III displayed a flexed cervical spine [43]. In contrast, the findings of Solow and Tallgren (1977) indicated that there was no correlation between the craniocervical angle and the sagittal relationship of the jaws [44].

The study by Solow and Siersbaek-Nielsen in 1992 indicated that head posture or alterations in posture could influence the development of the lower face, rather than being affected by it. However, they did not include any Class III malocclusions in their study, focusing only on Class I and Class II malocclusions [11]. This aligns with the soft tissue stretching hypothesis proposed by Solow and Kreiborg, which suggests a mechanism linking posture and craniofacial development [12]. Nevertheless, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2022 investigated head and cervical posture and its relation to malocclusion, and concluded that, due to small sample sizes and high heterogeneity among the studies included, the current evidence is insufficient to support this relationship [45].

In the current study, the results regarding overbite and overjet demonstrate that there are no significant differences among the compared groups. In contrast, a study conducted on Saudi subjects indicated a significant relationship between overjet and head posture, regardless of age or gender. Furthermore, they observed that flexion of the head (small craniocervical angle) was associated with deep overbite [46].

The findings of this study suggest that problematic smart device use is associated with altered head posture, which may, in turn, contribute to the development or worsening of malocclusion. This has important clinical implications for both preventive and interceptive orthodontic care, particularly among children and adolescents whose craniofacial structures are still developing.

Although several cephalometric differences were observed between mild and severe smart device users, most were modest in magnitude. The orientation of the maxillary plane relative to the vertical axis was significantly more clockwise in the severe-use group, suggesting greater head flexion. Other craniofacial and postural measurements—such as cranial base and mandibular plane orientation—showed moderate effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5–0.7) but did not reach statistical significance. Dental measurements also reflected small to moderate associations: upper and lower incisors were more proclined, and overbite and overjet were smaller in the severe-use group, though these differences were not statistically significant.

Notably, skeletal classification assessed via ANB angle and Wits analysis showed a statistically significant shift toward a Class III pattern in the severe-use group, with moderate associations (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.25–0.40). While these findings may reflect early trends linking problematic smart device use to craniofacial adaptations, they remain insufficient to support firm clinical conclusions and should therefore be interpreted as preliminary.

Given the growing prevalence of smart device use, orthodontists and dental professionals may consider incorporating screen-time assessments into routine patient histories to identify behavioral factors that could influence posture and craniofacial growth. However, further longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to establish causal relationships and to determine the threshold at which behavioral differences translate into clinically significant outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a true control group with no exposure to smart devices limited the ability to compare findings against a completely unexposed population. However, given the near-universal use of smart devices among adolescents and young adults, such a group was not practically attainable, highlighting the growing relevance of investigating usage severity rather than presence or absence.

Second, the inclusion of a broad age range may have introduced variability in growth status and duration of smart device exposure. Some participants may not have completed their pubertal growth spurt, and differences in exposure time could influence craniofacial development. Nevertheless, this age distribution reflects the real-world demographics of digital device users and supports the external validity of the findings.

Third, while the sample size (n = 47) was modest, it met the requirements of an a priori power analysis, which indicated that 21 participants per group were sufficient to detect a clinically meaningful difference in cephalometric variables with 90% power at α = 0.05. Both groups exceeded this threshold (G1: n = 23; G2: n = 24), and the sample size is comparable to that of foundational studies in the field. This supports the internal validity and methodological soundness of our design, which focused on contrasting behavioral extremes rather than population-level stratification. Notably, our sample size aligns with prior foundational studies on craniofacial development and postural adaptations [10, 11, 47–49], reinforcing the methodological soundness of our approach.

Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits inferences about causality. Longitudinal research with larger, more diverse cohorts is needed to establish temporal and directional relationships between smart device use, head posture, and craniofacial development. Such studies will be critical in translating current observations into evidence-based clinical recommendations, including guidelines for usage duration, ergonomic device handling, and early intervention strategies in growing individuals.

Conclusions

The most notable finding of this study was the significant association between the severity of smart device use and skeletal pattern. Mild users were more likely to exhibit a Class I skeletal relationship, while severe users showed a higher prevalence of Class III skeletal patterns.
