PrEP public policies for HIV prevention in South America: an intersectional analysis
Authors: Kris Herik de Oliveira, Breno de Oliveira Ferreira, Fernando Joaquim da Silva Junior, Luiz Fernando Greiner Barp, Carolina Bonomi de Menezes Guerra, Flávia Danielle da Silva Câmara, Marianna Agnes de Almeida Soares, Gabriela Bercht, Isabelle Brambilla Honorato, Agostina Aixa Gagliolo, María Guadalupe García, Ramon Pereira dos Reis, Guilherme Rodrigues Passamani, Bruno Silva Kauss, Fernando Ruiz Vallejo, Julio Cesar Aguilera Hurtado, André Luiz Machado das Neves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective biomedical prevention strategy, recommended by the World Health Organization since 2015. However, in South America, its adoption has been uneven, reflecting structural, political, and socio-cultural inequalities. This study maps and analyzes national PrEP policies across 13 South American countries through an intersectional lens, identifying challenges and opportunities for more equitable HIV prevention strategies.MethodsWe conducted a documentary survey of national PrEP-related policies, clinical guidelines, regulations, and public communications from Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, Peru, Suriname, Uruguay, and Venezuela. Searches were performed on institutional websites in Portuguese, Spanish, English, and French using predefined keywords. In the absence of official policy documents, information was collected from media and civil society sources. Data from 118 documents were coded and analyzed using iterative summative content analysis, integrating intersectionality theory to assess how policies address (or neglect) structural inequalities.ResultsTen countries have formal national PrEP policies (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay), with Brazil pioneering implementation in 2017. Three countries (Bolivia, Suriname, Venezuela) lack comprehensive national guidelines. Most countries recommend daily oral PrEP; on-demand dosing is approved in fewer contexts, often with restrictions. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir has been approved only in Brazil, French Guiana, and Peru, but remains largely unavailable. Geographic coverage ranges from national to highly localized models. Prioritized groups include men who have sex with men, transgender women, sex workers, serodifferent couples, and, in some countries, people who use drugs. Policy formulation involves ministries of health, academia, international organizations, and, variably, civil society. However, inclusion of marginalized populations and integration of intersectional approaches remain limited. There are inequalities in the incorporation of social markers of difference in PrEP policies across the region, with greater emphasis on sexuality, gender, class, and health conditions, and limited attention to race/ethnicity, territory, generation, and disability.ConclusionPrEP policy adoption in South America demonstrates significant heterogeneity, shaped by intersecting social, political, and institutional factors. While the region has made notable advances, access remains uneven, with structural barriers such as stigma, regulatory gaps, and geographic inequities limiting impact. Incorporating intersectional perspectives into policy design and implementation is essential to ensure that PrEP – in all its modalities – is accessible as a right to health, addressing differences across multiple social, geographic, and economic dimensions. Background

Global estimates of the number of people living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) increased from 27.2 million in 2000 to 39.9 million in 2023. The majority are from countries on the African continent, an estimated number of 26 million people, representing almost 1 in 25 (3.4%) of the adults and two-thirds of people living with HIV worldwide. This increase reflects the continued transmission of HIV, despite reductions in its incidence. The significant expansion of access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has contributed to a decline in HIV/ Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS)-related deaths, especially since 2004, when mortality peaked [1].

The American continent has the second largest number of people living with HIV in the world, about 4 million in 2023, with South America accounting for approximately 1.8 million, a significant increase from the 677,700 cases reported in 2000 [1]. This expansion of approximately 165.5% in just over two decades is indicative of the persistent obstacles encountered by vulnerable populations in obtaining effective prevention, testing, and ongoing care strategies. It also highlights the resurgence of the AIDS epidemic in Latin America and the Caribbean. The region is marked by deep structural inequalities in access to health care, expressed by disparities in resource distribution, inadequate health system coverage, HIV-related stigma, and multiple socioeconomic barriers to diagnosis and treatment [2].

Considering these difficulties, the World Health Organization (WHO) [3] and the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) [4] have acknowledged HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) as a viable strategy for addressing the HIV/AIDS epidemic within the framework of the combined prevention approach. With the goal of improving the efficacy of preventive measures and lowering disparities in access to healthcare, this approach combines behavioral, biomedical, and sociostructural interventions that are coordinated and tailored to each person’s unique needs, life circumstances, and vulnerabilities [5].

Oral PrEP was first approved for use in 2012 by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Then, it was recommended by the WHO in 2015 as part of global HIV prevention strategies. The most recent long-acting injectable PrEP (Cabotegravir) was approved by the FDA on December 20, 2021, and recommended by the WHO in July 2022. The high effectiveness of PrEP in preventing HIV, whether in oral (continuous/on-demand) or long-acting injectable form, has been proven in extensive randomized clinical trials and demonstration studies conducted in different settings and populations [3, 6].

Although its effectiveness is widely proven, the implementation of PrEP as a public health strategy faces structural, political, and cultural challenges in several regions, especially in South American. As mentioned, this reflects and reproduces the historical patterns of structural inequalities that characterize the region [6]. According to the most recent data [7], South America has recorded 237,011 PrEP initiations, of which 236,688 were oral and 1,313 were injectable. Brazil accounts for the vast majority, with 217,683 initiations (216,370 oral and 1,313 injectable), followed by Colombia (6,670), Peru (4,383), Argentina (3,927), Chile (1,893), Ecuador (1,661), Paraguay (852), Guyana (692), and Uruguay (240). In contrast, Bolivia, Suriname, Venezuela, and French Guiana reported no data on PrEP initiations during the period considered.

Socioeconomic inequalities, institutional racism, gender-based violence, discrimination related to sexual orientation and gender identity, stigma surrounding HIV, and the criminalization of key populations, such as men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender women (TGW), sex workers (SW), and people who use drugs (PWUD), directly impact the effectiveness of public PrEP policies [2]. Such barriers compromise access, adherence, and continuity of PrEP use, even in contexts where the medication is available free of charge through public health systems [8–12]. The context of neoliberalization of prevention, which centralizes strategies on the individual, is added to this scenario at the expense of collective approaches [13].

The trajectory of PrEP implementation and use has not yet been widely assimilated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The promise of sexuality free from fear of HIV has sparked both enthusiasm and resistance, especially in contexts marked by conservative sexual moralities and structural inequalities. Thus, understanding PrEP not only as a clinical tool but as part of a complex ethical, political, and affective field is fundamental to reducing social inequalities in health in South America. This implies recognizing that the effectiveness of PrEP depends both on pharmacological adherence and on the capacity of health systems to accommodate the multiple dimensions that cut across care, such as stigma, pleasure, fear, and desire, as well as to build public policies that are sensitive to the realities and subjectivities of those who would most benefit from this form of prevention [14–16].

Although there are studies on experiences with public PrEP policies in South American countries such as Brazil [17–19], Colombia [20], and Peru [21], there is still a lack of comparative and critical analysis of these policies that considers the sociopolitical contexts and structures of inequality that shape the response to HIV in each location. This gap limits the ability of governments, multilateral organizations, and social movements to formulate more equitable, culturally sensitive public policies focused on the needs of the populations most impacted by the epidemic [6, 11, 22].

Public policies regarding PrEP are defined in this context as the official guidelines established by a national government to regulate the administration, use, and access of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis. These guidelines should be publicly available on institutional platforms and cover aspects such as registration and approval of drugs, definition of priority population groups, physical criteria and possible restrictions on use, tests required for initiation and follow-up, dispensing modalities, and geographical coverage of services. The development and updating of these policies should be aligned with WHO recommendations, as outlined in its technical guidelines and periodic reviews [3, 23].

Moreover, it is imperative to engage in a dialogue with social movements in the health sector to directly address the political dimension of prevention, which is viewed as a means of promoting social justice and advocating for rights [24]. While this distance persists, it restricts the capacity of governments, multilateral institutions, and social movements to develop more culturally aware and equitable policies that prioritize the needs of the groups most affected.

In this sense, intersectionality in health is understood as an analytical perspective that highlights how multiple social markers of difference—such as sexuality, gender, race/ethnicity, class/socioeconomic status, territory, generation/age, disability, and health conditions—overlap and interact in producing health inequities. Rather than the sum of isolated factors, intersectionality allows for the analysis of the combined effects of these dimensions and their resulting oppressions, offering critical tools to understand specific vulnerabilities and to propose more equitable public health responses [25].

Applied to the field of HIV/AIDS and PrEP, this approach remains incipient in South America, particularly in comparative analyses of public policies [24, 26]. This study seeks to advance this discussion by examining how national PrEP policies incorporate or neglect debates on structural and contextual dimensions of inequality. Thus, intersectionality is presented here both as a theoretical framework and as an interpretive lens to map who is included, excluded, or rendered invisible in the normative and programmatic arrangements of 13 countries in the region.

Therefore, this article maps and analyzes national public policies on PrEP for HIV prevention in thirteen South American countries, combining documentary research with an intersectional perspective. The study seeks to specifically identify characteristics, challenges, and opportunities for implementation in contexts marked by structural vulnerabilities, offering insights for more contextualized and effective policies to reduce health inequalities.

Methods

This research, focused on public policies on PrEP in South America, was carried out in three stages: (i) document review; (ii) content analysis and data tabulation; and (iii) discussion of results.

In the first stage, research was conducted on documents related to national PrEP policies on institutional websites of the thirteen countries of South America: Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, French Guiana, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, Suriname, Uruguay, and Venezuela. The survey focused on clinical and therapeutic protocols, regulations, guidelines, ordinances, legislation, and informational materials.

Searches on institutional websites were conducted using combinations of terms in Portuguese, Spanish, English, and French to cover different nomenclatures and terminological variations associated with PrEP. In Portuguese, the terms used were: PrEP, Truvada, TDF-FTC, tenofovir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarato, emtricitabina, cabotegravir, guia, guia clínica, diretrizes, recomendações, estratégia nacional, HIV, HIV/AIDS and profilaxia pré-exposição. In Spanish: PrEP, Truvada, TDF-FTC, tenofovir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarato, emtricitabina, cabotegravir, guía, guía clínica, lineamientos, recomendaciones, estrategia nacional, VIH/SIDA and profilaxis previa a la exposición. In English: PrEP, Truvada, TDF-FTC, tenofovir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, emtricitabine, cabotegravir, guideline, clinical guideline, recommendations, national strategy, HIV, HIV/AIDS and pre-exposure prophylaxis. In French: PrEP, Truvada, TDF-FTC, ténofovir, ténofovir disoproxil fumarate, emtricitabine, cabotegravir, guide, guide clinique, recommandations, stratégie nationale, VIH, VIH/SIDA and prophylaxie pré-exposition.

In the absence of national policy documents, news articles and press releases from civil society organizations evidencing that a policy for PrEP does not exist in the country were collected. The survey identified and systematized 118 documents published between 2013 and 2024, covering the 13 South American countries/nations analyzed. The documents were classified into seven categories: epidemiological bulletins (n = 3), outreach materials (n = 16), regulations (n = 18), news items (n = 31), clinical protocols and therapeutic guidelines (n = 18), technical-administrative records (n = 24), and other formats (n = 8). The distribution of documents by country was as follows: Brazil (n = 19), Chile (n = 13), Guyana (n = 13), French Guiana (n = 12), Argentina (n = 9), Peru (n = 8), Suriname (n = 8), Paraguay (n = 7), Venezuela (n = 7), Colombia (n = 6), Ecuador (n = 6), Bolivia (n = 5), and Uruguay (n = 5).

The second stage of the research was a content analysis of the documents collected and data tabulation. The information included in the database was limited to that which addressed the approval of or access to PrEP for HIV prevention, methods of availability, regulations, clinical protocols and therapeutic guidelines. Following the methodological approach to public policy analysis in PrEP, iterative summative content analysis was applied [6, 27].

At this stage, intersectionality was operationalized through a systematic analysis of the presence or absence of eight social markers in normative documents on PrEP: sexuality, gender, race/ethnicity, class/socioeconomic status, territory, generation/age, disability, and health conditions. Table 1 presents the search terms employed. This procedure allowed us to identify the groups explicitly recognized as priorities in each country, as well as the silences and invisibilities produced by policies, enabling a comparative reading of national convergences, gaps, and specificities.

The data were discussed in dialogue with the literature on intersectionality in public policy. This articulation sought to recognize the diversity of experiences surrounding the use of PrEP and to highlight how different social markers intersect and influence the way actors relate to HIV prevention policies [25]. The intersectional analysis addresses the patterns of privilege and oppression that operate simultaneously at the structural, institutional, and microsocial levels, enabling a deeper understanding of the barriers and facilitators to access to PrEP [24].

Overall, most South American countries have approved and implemented oral PrEP as a national public policy, with published clinical protocols and therapeutic guidelines. Brazil and French Guiana were pioneers in implementing oral PrEP in 2017, followed by Chile, Colombia, Guyana, and Peru in 2019. In 2021, Argentina and Paraguay joined the strategy, while Uruguay incorporated it in 2022. More recently, Ecuador and Suriname began offering it in 2023, although the latter does not have a national public policy.

However, significant gaps remain: Bolivia and Venezuela have not yet introduced PrEP into the public system, although in Bolivia it is possible to obtain it from non-governmental organizations (NGOs) located in major cities. There is also a diversity of institutional arrangements and partnerships involving national governments, international organizations and agencies such as UNAIDS and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), as well as civil society organizations. In Peru, for example, initial distribution took place in 2019 through an NGO, while in Argentina, implementation also involved initiatives and partnerships between civil society organizations and state policies aimed at HIV prevention.

The availability of long-acting injectable PrEP based on cabotegravir is still very recent and limited in South America. Among the countries analyzed, only Brazil and Peru approved the use of the drug under national public policies in 2023, and French Guiana in 2024. In all three cases, implementation has not yet been effective.

Among South American countries with public policies on PrEP, geographical coverage is mostly national in Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Argentina, and Guyana, although with different levels of capillarity. In contrast, Bolivia (La Paz, Santa Cruz de la Sierra, and Cochabamba), Ecuador (Quito, Guayaquil, Machala, and Manta), Suriname (through Fundação Lobi), and Uruguay (specialized hospital units) have adopted more local or regional models.

Institutions and actors involved in policy formulation include public institutions, NGOs, universities, and international organizations. While some progress has been made, the adoption of telemedicine and innovative dispensing strategies, such as automatic machines in subway stations in São Paulo, Brazil, remains limited. In addition, barriers persist regarding the explicit inclusion of TGW and other transgender populations, SW, and PWUD in certain guidelines, as well as specific restrictions on the use of on-demand and injectable PrEP.

The structuring of PrEP policies

Concerning terms of institutionalizing PrEP as public policy, there has been more robust consolidation in countries such as Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay. In these countries, PrEP is included in national protocols, supported by ministerial regulations, and linked to combined prevention strategies. In Brazil, for example, PrEP has been incorporated into the Unified Health System (SUS) since 2017, with expansion to primary care and the incorporation of telemedicine technologies, demonstrating a strategy of dissemination and decentralization of care. Across Colombia and Peru, the policy was formally established with recent ministerial resolutions that guarantee free access, including for adolescents and key populations, in line with international recommendations.

However, the formal provision of PrEP modalities, such as continuous oral and on-demand, still varies substantially between countries. Based on scientific evidence, the on-demand modality is widely adopted in Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Uruguay, but there are countries that have not yet fully regulated this form of use or restricted it based on questionable clinical and epidemiological criteria. Noteworthy is the case of Uruguay, which, although it offers the on-demand modality, excludes cis women, trans women, and heterosexual men with hepatitis B, suggesting the need for a technical review based on more recent evidence.

In Argentina, the PrEP policy has a well-established legal and regulatory framework. The national regulatory agency for medicines (ANMAT) has approved, through provisions such as No. 6114/2019, No. 4792/2020, and No. 3458/2023, medicines containing tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine with a specific indication for HIV prevention, and national legislation mandates coverage by all health subsystems: public, social security (obras sociales), and private medical companies. Distribution takes place across various services, including public hospitals and institutions within the healthcare network, in accordance with current regulations.

Bolivia, Suriname, and Venezuela are countries with incipient or nonexistent policies. Despite civil society initiatives and international cooperation (notably with Ecuador and PAHO), there is no consolidated regulation or robust national clinical guidelines. Bolivia, in particular, has low political commitment and weak institutional leadership, reinforcing the gap between global commitments and local public health practices. The case of Suriname illustrates well the centrality of local partnerships, such as the Lobi Foundation, in leading implementation in fragile state contexts. In Venezuela, PrEP is available in some pharmacies.

The unique situation of French Guiana stands out, where prevention policy fully follows French metropolitan guidelines. In France, PrEP is fully institutionalized, with universal and free access since 2016, highlighting the impact of administrative integration with European policies in overseas territories. In French Guiana, the implementation of oral PrEP began in 2017 and is currently available in hospitals and health centers throughout the territory. Injectable PrEP was approved in 2024, but there are still no indications of its implementation.

A comparative analysis of the documents reveals striking inequalities in the structuring of PrEP policies in South America, conditioned by state capacities, political interests, international agreements, civil society action, and health regulation. Most countries that have advanced in institutionalization have also sought to expand delivery modalities and integrate PrEP into combined prevention strategies. However, challenges remain in terms of equitable access, transparent drug regulation, effective universal coverage, and overcoming structural and symbolic barriers, such as the stigma associated with HIV and the emphasis on biomedicalization of prevention. These limitations restrain the potential of PrEP as a transformative public policy in South America.

Coverage and forms of access

Strong heterogeneity was noticeable in the geographical coverage and forms of access to PrEP between countries. This diversity stems from varied and interrelated social and political-administrative factors, such as the specificities of institutional arrangements (laws and governance structures), differentiated implementation strategies (decentralization and logistical models), and the nature of social participation in the territories (NGO involvement and community actions).

Countries such as Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru, and Guyana have consolidated national coverage of PrEP, despite varying degrees of capillarity, while others still limit its availability to specific regions. Bolivia (La Paz, Santa Cruz de la Sierra, and Cochabamba through NGOs), Ecuador (Quito, Guayaquil, Machala, and Manta), Suriname (through the Lobi Foundation), and Uruguay (specialized hospital units) are examples of this geographical fragmentation, which is different from a decentralization of the service. Argentina also has national coverage, supported by Law No. 27,675/2022, which establishes a comprehensive response to HIV, viral hepatitis, and other sexually transmitted infections with a good diversity of access options, although there are still regulatory setbacks related to TDF-FTC.

This heterogeneity reaches its peak when we compare Venezuela and French Guiana. In the Venezuelan case, unlike free access to ART through the advocacy work of civil society organizations and investments from the United Nations Global Fund, PrEP is not yet a structured public policy, it is restricted to authorization for sale in private pharmacies. As a result, access remains driven by a strictly commercial logic, in which prophylaxis is purchased on the pharmaceutical market (consumer market) and not offered as a guaranteed right through public health services (user market). This format imposes obstacles on the most vulnerable populations, given that PrEP, treated as a restricted commodity, ends up being conditioned by pre-existing social inequalities.

French Guiana, on the other hand, due to its status as an overseas territory, has adopted the French metropolitan model, with universal and free provision of PrEP in hospital pharmacies and public health units, which guarantees, at least in formal terms, greater availability of this prevention technology as a state policy.

Brazil stands out in terms of capillarity, with innovative decentralization strategies in South America, offering PrEP in hospitals, basic health units, pharmacies, universities, and even through automatic machines in São Paulo subway stations (with a strong focus on avoiding social stigma and facilitating access), complemented by public telemedicine. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain in terms of expanding geographical coverage, especially in regions further away from large urban centers and state capitals. In other words, there are still internal inequalities in the distribution and forms of access to PrEP.

Colombia and Paraguay have also diversified access points to PrEP, including hospitals, private clinics, and CSOs. Although this model is not always linked to telemedicine, both countries, like Brazil, are the only ones to offer remote public services for prophylaxis. In the case of Colombia, given the fragmentation of the healthcare system, access to PrEP may vary depending on the type of scheme (subsidized versus contributory/special) and on how each managing entity structures effective access.

In countries with less structured health systems, such as Guyana and Suriname, PrEP is mainly provided through community-based strategies. In Guyana, even with other forms of public and private access, health workers operate in remote areas with support from PAHO/WHO. In Suriname, the Lobi Foundation is conducting a limited reach pilot project.

In Chile and Peru, PrEP is available in public health services nationwide, integrated into combined prevention policies, and coordinated with other HIV care strategies. Ecuador, although it does not yet have national coverage, has recently started to make PrEP available in 12 dispensing units, signaling a gradual expansion that may indicate progress toward universal access.

National PrEP guidelines in South America present different types of restrictions, both clinical and population-based. Regarding daily oral PrEP, most countries establish contraindications for individuals with confirmed or suspected HIV diagnosis, renal insufficiency, and, in some cases, hepatic insufficiency or hepatitis B and C coinfection (as in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Guyana). In the case of on-demand oral PrEP, a more exclusionary pattern is observed: several countries restrict its use to MSM, discouraging or contraindicating this modality for cisgender women, TGW, transgender men engaging in vaginal/frontal sex, and people with chronic hepatitis B. Such restrictions have been documented, for example, in Brazil, Colombia, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay.

In addition, some protocols condition eligibility on the individual’s ability to plan sexual encounters or to demonstrate good adherence. With respect to injectable PrEP, only Colombia explicitly lists contraindications (even though it has not been approved or made available in the country), including HIV diagnosis, hepatotoxicity, and hypersensitivity to cabotegravir. This overview highlights both convergences (such as the systematic exclusion of people living with HIV and individuals with renal impairment) and divergences, particularly in the regulation of on-demand use, where restrictions persist based on gender, sexual practices, and population profiles.

In summary, the South American landscape shows that, although there have been significant advances in the institutionalization of PrEP in several countries, coverage and access modalities remain markedly unequal. The combination of structural, political, and social factors shapes contrasting realities, ranging from universal and integrated models to public health policies to restricted and commercialized formats. This heterogeneity reinforces the need for coordinated regional strategies capable of promoting greater territorial equity, overcoming regulatory and logistical barriers, and strengthening community participation to ensure that PrEP is effectively accessible as a right to health and not as a privilege conditioned by geographic context or socioeconomic status.

Actors involved

The formulation of national PrEP policies in South America reveals a collaborative and multisectoral effort involving ministries of health, international organizations, universities, civil society, health professionals, and HIV/AIDS experts. This diverse composition reflects the countries’ commitment to aligning themselves with international guidelines based on scientific evidence and sensitivity to local needs.

Health professionals and/or institutions are present in all countries with public PrEP policies (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay). For example, in Argentina, there is the Dirección de Respuesta al VIH, ITS, Hepatitis Virales y Tuberculosis of the Ministerio de Salud de la Nación; in Brazil, the Departamento de Doenças de Condições Crônicas e Infecções Sexualmente Transmissíveis of the Secretaria de Vigilância em Saúde of the Ministério da Saúde; in Colombia, the Instituto de Evaluación Tecnológica en Salud of the Ministerio de Salud y Protección Social; in Ecuador, the Subsecretaría de Vigilancia, Prevención y Control de la Salud of the Ministerio de Salud Pública; and in French Guiana, the Centre Hospitalier de Cayenne. These agencies lead the technical formulation of guidelines, highlighting the government’s leading role in conducting these policies.

Likewise, universities, research institutions, and associations play an important role, providing technical and scientific support in nine countries (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, and Uruguay). In Argentina, Universidad de Buenos Aires (UBA) stands out; in Brazil, the Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (Fiocruz) and the Universidade Federal de São Paulo (UNIFESP); in Chile, researchers from public health, psychology, and epidemiology participate (e.g., Hospital Clínico Universidad Católica de Chile); in Ecuador, the Universidad Tecnológica Equinoccial (UTE); and in Uruguay, the Universidad de la República (Udelar).

International institutions and/or consultancies are present in eight countries (Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, and Uruguay). In general, these countries have the collaboration of PAHO, the UNAIDS, and the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), which signals strong international technical and financial influence in the formulation of regional PrEP policies. In the case of Venezuela, it was the combination of civil society advocacy and Global Fund investments that enabled the maintenance of free access to ART, although PrEP has not yet been established as a structured public policy and remains limited to the private market. In Bolivia and Suriname, PAHO plays a key role by providing technical assistance, supporting regional coordination, and facilitating community initiatives—such as the program led by the Lobi Foundation—in contexts marked by institutional fragilities.

Civil society participation was identified in seven countries (Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, and Paraguay). However, the visibility of this participation in the documents varies: it is more significant in Colombia (NGOs, associations, and community leaders), Ecuador (LGBTQIAPN + associations), and Paraguay (NGO networks), and less visible in countries such as Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Guyana, and French Guiana, which may indicate different levels of institutionalization and access to policy formulation. This inclusion represents a historic achievement for social movements and ensures that policies reflect the demands of different groups.

Finally, medical associations (Chile, Colombia, Paraguay, and Uruguay) and private professionals or institutions (Chile, Colombia, Guyana, and French Guiana) are present in four countries each. In Chile, the participation of the Sociedad Chilena de Infectología stands out; in Colombia, Profamilia; in Guyana, there is a record of public-private partnerships; in Paraguay, the Sociedad Paraguaya de Medicina Interna and the Sociedad Paraguaya de Infectología; and in Uruguay, the Sociedad de Infectología Clínica del Uruguay. In addition, there are contracted consultants, especially in the medical field.

A comparison between countries reveals relevant nuances in the organization and participation of actors. Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, and Uruguay have adopted relatively comprehensive models, bringing together government, academia, civil society, and international organizations in varying proportions. In Brazil, the diversity of collaborators and institutional multiplicity stand out, reflecting the federal structure and the SUS. Colombia and Ecuador also mobilize various sectors, although with less involvement from civil society. In Chile and Uruguay, there is greater centralization among health specialists and professionals, with less significant community participation. Guyana and French Guiana, on the other hand, have more restricted compositions, possibly due to political, structural, and resource limitations.

The diversity of institutions and actors involved is in line with contemporary approaches to public policy analysis in HIV/AIDS, notably: (i) network governance with multiple state and non-state actors; (ii) an evidence-based approach, marked by academic involvement; (iii) social participation, through the inclusion of civil society; and (iv) international influence, with a strong presence of PAHO and UNAIDS.

This multiplicity expresses central principles of current public health: intersectorality, community participation, scientific basis, and global alignment. However, it also brings challenges, such as: coordination between multiple actors, requiring articulation between different sectors and institutional levels; reconciliation between technical rigor and community demands, especially in contexts with a history of social exclusion; adaptation to local contexts, respecting the specificities of each territory and population group; effective inclusion of key populations, which is still uneven in some countries; structural and financial limitations, especially in politically unstable contexts; stigma and cultural barriers, which still hinder PrEP implementation in many contexts.

The analysis shows that the configuration of actors varies according to the political context, the trajectory of social movements, and the structure of health systems. Although there is a general profile of cooperative approaches, inequalities in the composition and strength of actors imply differences in the effectiveness and equity of the policies implemented. The formulation of national PrEP policies in South America reflects a regional effort toward collaborative governance, albeit one permeated by institutional and contextual asymmetries.

Priority groups and social markers

The implementation of PrEP in South America has been oriented mainly at population groups defined as “key” or “priority” based on epidemiological analysis of increased risk of infection. Nevertheless, the definition of these groups reflects not only clinical and epidemiological criteria but also social processes of marginalization, unequal access to health services, and structural intersections of race, gender, class, and territory.

The minimum age for starting PrEP varies significantly among South American countries. While Brazil and Ecuador set the starting age at 15, Colombia and Guyana allow access at 12 and 13, respectively, covering adolescents at risk. In contrast, Chile and Peru set the minimum age at 18, restricting access to adults. In some countries, such as Argentina, French Guiana, Paraguay, and Uruguay, there is no explicit indication of an age limit in the documents consulted. In others, such as Bolivia, Suriname, and Venezuela, no specific information was found due to the absence of formalized national protocols for PrEP.

The most frequently mentioned priority groups are: (i) MSM; (ii) TGW; (iii) SW; (iv) SDC; and (v) PWUD. Other groups are also mentioned, such as people ≥ 12 years old and ≥ 35 kg, individually assessed as being at substantial risk of HIV infection due to sexual practices without condoms, multiple partners, recurrent Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs), repeated use of Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP), sex in exchange for money/drugs, or high prevalence origin/region. In the cases of Bolivia, Suriname, and Venezuela, the absence of a formal national policy implies the lack of specific criteria and guidelines for mapping these groups in the population.

All countries with public PrEP policies mention MSM, TGW, SW, and SDC as priority groups. Seven countries include PWUD (not only injecting drug users, considering chemical sex practices) as a priority (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Guyana, French Guiana, and Paraguay). Nine countries also consider other groups as priority groups (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, French Guiana, Paraguay, and Uruguay). In some documents, the choice of these groups is justified by recent HIV infection statistics; in others, the rationale is not explained.

Beyond statistics, mapping risk behaviors and populations that are socially vulnerable to HIV can be conducted in a collaborative manner, actively involving the subjects of the policies themselves, not just experts or managers. This process should value listening to the lived experiences and obstacles faced in everyday life. For PrEP to fulfill its potential as a tool for equity, it must be rooted in widespread and decentralized public policies, integrating scientific evidence, the characteristics of the territories, their social markers, and the defense of human rights.

Important dialogues emerge regarding sexuality and gender. All countries with PrEP policies include MSM and TGW as key populations, reflecting the epidemiological centrality of these groups. In some cases, the scope is broader: Paraguay explicitly includes travestis and non-binary people, while Colombia and Uruguay also recognize cisgender women in vulnerable situations. French Guiana and Brazil incorporate trans people at the same level as MSM, with the latter being the only country to systematically mention travestis.

In contrast, the category of race/ethnicity is among the most unevenly addressed. Most countries (Argentina, Ecuador, Paraguay, Uruguay, and Guyana) make no explicit mention of racial inequalities. Only Brazil identifies Black and Indigenous populations as priorities; Chile includes Indigenous peoples (such as Mapuche and Aymara); Peru adopts an intercultural approach, valuing ethnocultural practices and knowledge; and French Guiana highlights migrants and foreigners as vulnerable groups, without using direct racial categories. This disparity reveals an important gap, given the historical weight of racial inequalities in the HIV/AIDS epidemic.

The marker of class/socioeconomic status appears frequently. Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Paraguay, and Peru explicitly recognize sex workers and situations of poverty or social exclusion as risk factors. Uruguay and Guyana emphasize sex exchange for money, drugs, or housing as an eligibility criterion, while French Guiana associates vulnerability with prostitution and social precariousness.

With respect to territory, national policies acknowledge regional inequalities: Brazil highlights the Amazon and urban peripheries; Chile identifies higher rates in the North and the Metropolitan Region; Ecuador and Paraguay emphasize specific provinces; Peru adopts national decentralization; and French Guiana underscores the importance of Centres Délocalisés de Prévention et de Soins (CDPS) for Amazonian and border populations.

As noted previously, the marker of generation/age presents significant variation across countries. The disability marker appears only in Brazil, which explicitly mentions accessibility. Regarding health conditions, however, there is strong convergence: all countries prioritize people with recurrent STIs, coinfections (hepatitis, tuberculosis), people who use drugs (including in chemsex contexts), and serodifferent partnerships. This standardization contrasts with the invisibility of markers such as disability and race/ethnicity in much of the region, revealing uneven progress in acknowledging multiple social vulnerabilities.

Discussion

Global health policies related to HIV have undergone major changes in the last decade, a period in which the development and distribution of new biomedical technologies, such as PrEP, converged in UNAIDS’ global strategy of Treatment as Prevention and, later, in the so-called combined prevention [28].

Following global trends expressed in recommendations and agreements with international organizations and agencies such as PAHO, WHO, and UNAIDS, several countries in South America have adopted the recommendation to include and prioritize the use of pharmacological prevention tools for “key populations,” especially PrEP and PEP [29]. These actions range from the creation of guidelines for the provision of combined prevention, clinical protocols and therapeutic guidelines to the formulation of laws that place the territorialization, decentralization, and autonomy of these “key populations” at the center of public debate. In other words, consideration is given to how these people access health facilities (institutions and supplies), how they are treated, their adherence or non-adherence to prevention and treatment schemes, and professional practice beyond the traditional doctor-patient relationship.

The analysis of documents related to PrEP policies showed that their adoption by public health systems in South America is recent and uneven. Brazil stands out for having more systematic data, but there is evidence of a concentration of supply in large urban centers and limited reach to certain social segments [30]. In other countries, implementation is fragmented, with strong participation from scientific and medical institutions, international consultancies, and CSOs. This institutional heterogeneity directly impacts equity of access and continuity of use.

From an intersectional perspective in public policies [25], with a focus on HIV prevention [24, 26, 31], data analysis indicates that the institutional barriers identified are not limited to the field of health. The overlap of social markers of race/color, class, gender, sexuality, generation, territory, and migratory status can intensify processes of vulnerability. These patterned inclusions and omissions indicate that PrEP policies in South America still only partially operationalize intersectionality.

The territory/region, linked to other social markers of difference, emerges as a critical factor in this analysis of public policies on PrEP. Studies establish that populations in non-metropolitan, rural, border, or impoverished areas face complex barriers: lack of specialized services, long distances to distribution centers, marked social stigma, lack of privacy, absence of tailored information campaigns, and indirect travel costs. In these locations, structural precariousness and stigma interact to reduce adherence and continuity of PrEP use [32, 33].

Policy design and eligibility criteria are shaped by intersecting social, political, and institutional factors that influence who can access, initiate, and persist on PrEP. Given this scenario, expanding access to PrEP requires intersectoral strategies that involve not only health but also areas such as education, justice, and social assistance [6]. It is necessary to diversify the profile of users, expand the supply, adapt communication campaigns to different cultural and linguistic contexts, and strengthen primary health care as a privileged gateway to prevention.

Advancing equity will require: (i) aligning eligibility and modalities (continuous, on-demand, and injectable) with the best available evidence; (ii) expanding decentralized delivery with attention to territorial specificities; and (iii) systematically incorporating intersecting social markers—including sexuality, gender, race/ethnicity, class/socioeconomic status, generation/age, disability, health conditions, territory, and migration status —into policy design, service organization, and monitoring. Conclusions

This study mapped and analyzed PrEP policies in South America from an intersectional health perspective. The research was limited to the analysis of publicly available institutional documents, which may not fully reflect the actual implementation of PrEP policies in the countries analyzed, especially in contexts where undocumented practices or informal local initiatives play a significant role. Although all thirteen countries/nations in the region have approved the oral modality, only ten have formal national policies (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, French Guiana, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay), while three others (Bolivia, Suriname, and Venezuela) have no specific guidelines. The adoption of injectable PrEP is in its early stages, approved in only three countries (Brazil, French Guiana, and Peru), with practical availability limited to an implementation study in Brazil.

Brazil has emerged as a pioneer since 2017, offering free access to different vulnerable groups, while in other countries its use is still restricted to groups considered high risk. The picture that emerges, marked by historical processes such as colonization and neoliberal reforms, reveals a central paradox: the coexistence of the widely proven effectiveness of the strategy and its deeply unequal distribution in South America. Overcoming this situation requires incorporating an intersectional perspective into public policies, not only to describe inequalities, but to transform them through integrated actions.

An analysis of social markers reveals asymmetric incorporation across national guidelines. Sexuality and gender are consistently foregrounded—most countries prioritize MSM and TGW, with some also recognizing sex workers, serodifferent couples, and, variably, people who use drugs. By contrast, race/ethnicity and disability are largely invisible: only a handful of countries name Black and Indigenous peoples as priorities, and disability is seldom addressed beyond generic references to “accessibility.” Territorial inequalities are acknowledged (e.g., Amazonian, border, rural, or peripheral urban areas), yet concrete mechanisms to guarantee decentralized provision and continuity of care remain limited. Age thresholds and adolescent access vary considerably, reinforcing uneven protection for younger populations at substantial risk.

These patterned inclusions and omissions indicate that PrEP policies in South America still only partially operationalize intersectionality. Convergences exist (e.g., prioritization of people with recurrent STIs, coinfections, chemsex contexts, and serodifferent partnerships), but they coexist with exclusions in on-demand eligibility (often restricted to MSM) and with gaps in addressing structural drivers of vulnerability (racism, poverty, gender-based violence, criminalization, and migration).

In practical terms, intersectionality should function as both a theoretical reference and an implementation framework: setting disaggregated targets; resourcing tailored strategies for vulnerable territories and populations; embedding community participation in governance; and strengthening data systems to monitor coverage, uptake, adherence, and persistence by key social markers. Doing so would help shift PrEP from a technology unevenly distributed by geography and social position to an enforceable right to HIV prevention across the South America.
