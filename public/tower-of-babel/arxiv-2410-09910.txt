A note on the standard zero-free region for L L -functions
Sun-Kai Leung

Abstract
. In this short note, we establish a standard zero-free region for a general class of L L -functions for which their logarithms have coefficients with nonnegative real parts, which includes the Rankin–Selberg L L -functions for unitary cuspidal automorphic representations.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11F66, 11M41

1.
Introduction

In 1899, de la Vallée Poussin proved a quantitative prime number theorem

\displaystyle\psi(x)=x+O(x\exp(-c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}))

for some absolute constant
c_{1}>0
by establishing the classical zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
of the form

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c_{2}}{\log(|t|+3)}

for
s=\sigma+it.
This generalizes to a zero-free region for a wide class of
L
-functions
L(f,s)
of the form

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{d^{4}\log(\mathfrak{q}(f)(|t|+3))},

where
d
is the degree and
\mathfrak{q}(f)
is the analytic conductor at
s=0
, with the possible exception for a simple real zero, in which case
f
is self-dual (see
[
IK04
, Theorem 5.10]
).

However, in order to apply
[
IK04
, Theorem 5.10]
, the existence of the Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions
L(f\otimes f,s)
and
L(f\otimes\overline{f},s)
are necessary. In particular, when
L(f,s)=L(s,\pi\times\tilde{\pi})
for some unitary cuspidal automorphic representations
\pi
with
\tilde{\pi}
being the contragredient representation of
\pi
, the existence of the associated Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions has yet to be confirmed in general; nonetheless, this would follow from the Langlands functoriality conjecture.

Following the effectivization of the Langlands–Shahidi method by Sarnak
[
Sar04
]
, Goldfeld and Li
[
GL18
]
proved that for any cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi
of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
which is unramified and tempered at every non-archimedean place outside a set of Dirichlet density zero, the Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,\pi\times\tilde{\pi})
has no zeros in the region

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c_{\pi}}{(\log(|t|+3))^{5}}

for some constant
c_{\pi}>0
depending on
\pi,
provided that
|t|\geqslant 1.

Extending upon their work, Humphries and Brumley
[
HB19
, Theorem 1.9]
employed sieve methods to improve the zero-free region of
Goldfeld–Li to

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c_{\pi}}{\log(|t|+3)}

while removing their restriction that
K=\mathbb{Q}
and
\pi
is unramified at every place. The automorphic representation

\pi,
however, is still required to be tempered at every non-archimedean place outside a set of
Dirichlet density zero. Furthermore, their zero-free region concerns only the
t
-aspect. More recently, Humphries and Thorner
[
HT22
, Theorem 5.1]
proved an unconditional refinement with improved uniformity in
\pi.

In this short note, we generalize their results to a general class of
L
-functions for which their logarithms have coefficients with nonnegative real parts.

2.
Main result

We begin with introducing a general class of
L
-functions.
Throughout the paper, let

\displaystyle L(f,s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{f}(n)}{n^{s}}

be an
L
-function which is absolutely convergent and zero-free on
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1.

We denote the gamma factor as

\displaystyle\gamma(f,s):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\pi}^{-\frac{s+\kappa_{j}}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+\kappa_{j}}{2}\right),

where
d
is the degree and
\{\kappa_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
are the Langlands parameters satisfying
\mathrm{Re}(\kappa_{j})>-1
for
j=1,\ldots,d.

The coefficients of the Dirichlet series

\displaystyle\log L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(n)}{n^{s}\log n}

satisfy the bound
|\Lambda_{f}(n)|\leqslant dn\log n
for
n\geqslant 1.

The complete
L
-function is defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda(f,s):=q(f)^{\frac{s}{2}}\gamma(f,s)L(f,s)

with conductor
q(f),
which admits an analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
except possibly poles of finite order at
s=1.
Moreover, it satisfies the function equation

\displaystyle\Lambda(f,s)=\epsilon_{f}\Lambda(f,1-s),

where
\epsilon_{f}
is the root number.

1

1

It is plausible to extend Theorem
2.1
to a wider class of Dirichlet series, as the functional equations do not play a significant role here (see Section
3
for details).

Remark 2.1
.

The
L
-functions considered here do not necessarily have an Euler product, as the multiplicative structure of integers is not involved in establishing a zero-free region within the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant 1.
Nevertheless, the existence of the Euler product

\displaystyle L(f,s)=\prod_{p}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{j}(p)}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1}

for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1,
where
\{\alpha_{j}(p)\}_{p,j}
are the Satake parameters, guarantees that there are no zeros in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1.

We now state the main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
L(f,s)
be an
L
-function in the class above.
Suppose
\mathrm{Re}(\Lambda_{f}(n))\geqslant 0
for
n\geqslant 1
and
L(f,s)
has at most a simple pole at
s=1.
2
2

2

Note that
L(f,1)\neq 0
since by assumption
\mathrm{Re}\left(\Lambda_{f}(n)\right)\geqslant 0
for
n\geqslant 1.
Then there exists an (effectively computable) absolute constant
c>0
such that
L(f,s)
has no zeros in the region

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log\left(\mathfrak{q}(f)(|t|+3)^{d}\right)}

for
s=\sigma+it,
with the possible exception for a simple real zero
\beta_{f}<1,

where
\mathfrak{q}(f)
is the analytic conductor
\mathfrak{q}(f,s)
at
s=0,
i.e.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(f):=q(f)\mathfrak{q}_{\infty}(0)=q(f)\prod_{j=1}^{d}(|\kappa_{j}|+3).

The following examples are immediate consequences.

Example 2.1

(Dirichlet
L
-functions)
.

Let
\chi\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
be a nontrivial Dirichlet character. Then there exists an (effectively computable) absolute constant
c>0
such that the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
has no zeros in the region

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log\left(q(|t|+3)\right)}

except possibly for a simple real zero
\beta_{\chi}<1

(see
[
Dav00
, p. 93]
) by applying Theorem
2.1
to the product
\zeta(s)L(s,\chi).

Example 2.2

(Dedekind zeta functions)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
d
. Then there exists an (effectively computable) absolute constant
c>0
such that the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(s)
has no zeros in the region

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log\left(|d_{K}|(|t|+3)^{d}\right)}

except possibly for a simple real zero
\beta_{K}<1
, where
d_{K}
is the discriminant (see
[
LO77
, Lemma 8.1 & Lemma 8.2]
).

Example 2.3

(Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions)
.

Let
\pi
be a unitary cuspidal automorphic representation of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{K}).

Then there exists an (effectively computable) absolute constant
c>0
such that the Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,\pi\times\tilde{\pi})
has no zeros in the region

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log\left(\mathfrak{q}(\pi\times\tilde{\pi})(|t|+3)^{d^{2}}\right)}

except possibly for a simple real zero
\beta_{\pi\times\tilde{\pi}}<1
(see
[
HT22
, Theorem 5.1]
).

Remark 2.2
.

The nonnegativity of
\Lambda_{\pi\times\tilde{\pi}}
is guaranteed by the local Langlands conjecture for
GL_{d}
over
p
-adic fields, proved by Harris–Taylor (see
[
IK04
, Remark, p.138]
). Also, since

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(\pi\times\tilde{\pi})\leqslant\mathfrak{q}(\pi)^{2d}3^{d^{2}[K:\mathbb{Q}]}

(see
[
IK04
, equation (5.11)]
), in practice, the zero-free region (
2.1
) can be replaced by

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\frac{c}{d\log\left(\mathfrak{q}(\pi)(|t|+3)^{d[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\right)}.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

The method of de la Vallée Poussin remains applicable in the absence of the Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(f\otimes\overline{f},s)
, as long as
\mathrm{Re}(\Lambda_{f}(n))\geqslant 0
for
n\geqslant 1
(though one must be wary of what one assumes, as pointed out in
[
HB19
, Remark 1.2]
).

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
\rho_{0}=\beta+i\gamma
be a zero of
L(f,s)
with
\beta\in\left[3/4,1\right]
and
\gamma\neq 0.
We denote the squared de la Vallée Poussin product by

\displaystyle L(g,\sigma):=|L^{3}(f,\sigma)L^{4}(f,\sigma+i\gamma)L(f,\sigma+2i\gamma)|^{2}.

For
n\geqslant 1,
we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{g}(n)

\displaystyle=2\left(3+4\cos(\gamma\log n)+\cos(2\gamma\log n)\right)\Lambda_{f}(n)

\displaystyle=|1+n^{-i\gamma}|^{4}\Lambda_{f}(n).

Since
\mathrm{Re}\left(\Lambda_{f}(n)\right)\geqslant 0,
taking the real part of both sides yields

\mathrm{Re}\left(\Lambda_{g}(n)\right)\geqslant 0.

Let
\sigma>1.
Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(g,\sigma)=-6\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma)-8\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma+i\gamma)-2\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma+2i\gamma)\geqslant 0.

Recall
[
IK04
, Proposition 5.7]
that for any
s
in the strip
\sigma\in[1,5/4],
we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,s)+\frac{r}{s}+\frac{r}{s-1}=\sum_{\kappa_{j}\;:\;|s+\kappa_{j}|\leqslant 1}\frac{1}{s+\kappa_{j}}+\sum_{\rho\;:\;|s-\rho|\leqslant 1}\frac{1}{s-\rho}+O\left(\log\mathfrak{q}(f,s)\right),

where
r=0
or
1
is the order of pole at
s=1,

\rho
’s are the zeros of
L(f,s)
and
\mathfrak{q}(f,s)
is the analytic conductor.
Since
\mathrm{Re}(\kappa_{j})>-1
and
\mathrm{Re}(\rho)\leqslant 1
, we have both

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{1}{s+\kappa_{j}}\right),\quad\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{1}{s-\rho}\right)>0.

Taking the real part of both sides of (
3.2
) at
s=\sigma,\sigma+i\gamma,\sigma+2i\gamma
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma)\geqslant-\frac{r}{\sigma-1}+O\left(\log\mathfrak{q}(f,\sigma)\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma+i\gamma)\geqslant-\frac{r(\sigma-1)}{(\sigma-1)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma-\beta}+O(\log\mathfrak{q}(f,\sigma+i\gamma)),

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma+2i\gamma)\geqslant-\frac{r(\sigma-1)}{(\sigma-1)^{2}+4\gamma^{2}}+O(\log\mathfrak{q}(f,\sigma+2i\gamma))

respectively. Since by definition
d<\log\mathfrak{q}(f)
and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(f,s)\leqslant\mathfrak{q}(f)(|s|+3)^{d}

(see
[
IK04
, equation (5.8)]
), combining the previous three inequalities with (
3.1
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\sigma-\beta}\leqslant\frac{3r}{\sigma-1}+\frac{4r(\sigma-1)}{(\sigma-1)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+\frac{r(\sigma-1)}{(\sigma-1)^{2}+4\gamma^{2}}+A\mathcal{L}.

for some absolute constant
A>10,
where
\mathcal{L}:=\log(\mathfrak{q}(f)(|\gamma|+3)^{d}).

Suppose for now
r=0
or
|\gamma|>(5A\mathcal{L})^{-1}.

Then,
substituting
\sigma=1+(10A\mathcal{L})^{-1}
into the inequality followed by careful
rearrangement, we conclude that

\displaystyle\beta<1-\frac{1}{400A\mathcal{L}}.

Otherwise, if
r=1
and
0<|\gamma|\leqslant(5A\mathcal{L})^{-1},
then
\overline{\rho_{0}}=\beta-i\gamma
is also a zero of
L(f,s)
satisfying
|\sigma-\overline{\rho_{0}}|\leqslant 1.
Also, since by assumption
\mathrm{Re}(\Lambda_{f}(n))\geqslant 0
for
n\geqslant 1,
it follows from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+\frac{2(\sigma-\beta)}{(\sigma-\beta)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}-B\mathcal{L}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma)\leqslant 0

for some absolute constant
B>0.
To simplify notation, note that we can replace both
A
and
B
by
C:=\max\{A,B\}.
Similarly, substituting
\sigma=1+(4C\mathcal{L})^{-1}
into the inequality followed by careful
rearrangement, we conclude that

\displaystyle\beta\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{60C\mathcal{L}}.

Finally, let
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
be real zeros of
L(f,s)
in the segment
\left[\frac{3}{4},1\right).
Since

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(f,\sigma)\geqslant 0,

it follows again from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{\sigma-\beta_{j}}<\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+O\left(\log\mathfrak{q}(f)\right).

Let
c_{2}>0.
Suppose there are
n\geqslant 0
real zeros
\beta_{j}
’s in the segment
\left[1-c_{2}(\log\mathfrak{q}(f))^{-1},1\right).
Then, substituting
\sigma=1+2c_{2}(\log\mathfrak{q}(f))^{-1}
into the inequality gives

\displaystyle\frac{n\log\mathfrak{q}(f)}{3c_{2}}<\frac{\log\mathfrak{q}(f)}{2c_{2}}+O(\log\mathfrak{q}(f)),

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{n}{3}<\frac{1}{2}+O(c_{2}).

Therefore, if
c_{2}
is sufficiently small, then
n\leqslant 1,
i.e. there is at most one simple zero in the segment. Finally, the theorem follows from choosing

c=\min\left\{\frac{1}{400C},c_{2}\right\}.

∎
