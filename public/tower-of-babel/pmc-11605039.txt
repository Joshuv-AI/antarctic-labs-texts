Risk Factors for Intravenous Acetaminophen-Induced Hypotension in Patients with Repeated Acetaminophen Administration
Authors: Sung-Ryeol Kim, Nak-Hoon Son, Kyung Hee Park, Jung-Won Park, Jae-Hyun Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeIntravenous (IV) acetaminophen-induced hypotension is a clinically significant issue that remains difficult to predict. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the factors associated with hypotension in patients with repeated IV acetaminophen administration.Materials and MethodsThis observational cohort study included patients who received IV acetaminophen in the critical care unit of the Yongin Severance Hospital in 2020. All IV acetaminophen administration records for each patient were reviewed, and the blood pressure records within 2 h after IV acetaminophen administration were examined. Changes in blood pressure within 2 h of IV acetaminophen administration were monitored to identify hypotension, defined as a systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg, a decrease in systolic blood pressure by 30 mm Hg, or a decrease in mean arterial pressure by 15%.ResultsThere were 1547 instances of IV acetaminophen administration among 398 patients. Of these, 416 instances (26.9%) resulted in hypotension among 204 patients (51.3%). A history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension did not predict subsequent hypotensive episodes, and there was no consistent tendency. The use of beta-blocker [odds ratio (OR)=1.50], gastrointestinal (GI) infection (OR=1.42), and septic shock (OR=1.68) were significant risk factors for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension in multivariate analysis. In subgroup analysis of cases with beta-blocker, heart failure (OR=1.91), urinary tract infection (OR=2.16), GI infection (OR=1.83) were significant risk factors.ConclusionSevere infections, heart failure, and the use of beta-blockers are associated with IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. However, IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is inconsistent and depends on the patient’s condition.

INTRODUCTION

Acetaminophen, also known as paracetamol, is widely used as an analgesic and antipyretic agent.123 All forms of acetaminophen are generally known to be safe; however, intravenous (IV) acetaminophen can cause hypotension in some patients.45678 The incidence of acetaminophen-induced hypotension is reported to be between 8.2% and 59%.9 Monitoring acetaminophen-induced hypotension is particularly important in the intensive care unit (ICU) since it is more likely to occur in critically ill patients with unstable vital signs.110 Furthermore, acetaminophen can cause fatal hypotension. Previous studies have shown that more than 33% of patients with acetaminophen-induced hypotension require active interventions.1112

IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is different from nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)-induced hypersensitivity. NSAIDs-induced hypersensitivity presents as typical hypersensitivity symptoms, including urticaria, angioedema, and anaphylaxis, due to the inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX) or rarely, IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity.13 In contrast, IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension does not involve symptoms related to COX inhibition or rare, type I hypersensitivity other than hypotension.4 Additionally, in one study, patients who experienced hypotension with IV acetaminophen administration rarely developed hypotension after taking oral acetaminophen.14 Therefore, IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is suggested to be a pharmacokinetic response rather than a hypersensitivity reaction.15

The risk factors for acetaminophen-induced hypotension identified in several studies include fever, high Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II scores, use of vasopressors or sedative agents, advanced age, hypoalbuminemia, brain injury, mechanical ventilation, antihypertensive drugs, and sepsis.912161718 However, the currently known risk factors have not shown consistent results across different studies.912161718 Therefore, the currently known risk factors are not significantly helpful in predicting IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. Since IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is a phenomenon related to the pharmacokinetic response characteristics involving the interaction between the patient and the drug, a history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension could be an important risk factor. However, studies to date have not determined whether a history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is a risk factor for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. In the present study, we evaluated the blood pressure response in patients repeatedly exposed to IV acetaminophen, analyzed the pattern of acetaminophen-induced hypotension, and identified risk factors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population and hypotension

Patients receiving IV acetaminophen in the ICU of Yongin Severance Hospital between March 2020 and February 2021 were enrolled. ICU patients were chosen since their vital signs are measured at least hourly. Patients with a history of acetaminophen hypersensitivity or those who developed hypersensitivity reactions after IV acetaminophen administration were excluded. Acetaminophen-induced hypersensitivity was defined as the occurrence of urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, laryngeal involvement, or severe gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms immediately after administration.19 All IV acetaminophen administration records for each patient were reviewed, and the blood pressure records within 2 h after IV acetaminophen administration were examined. In each case, changes in blood pressure within 2 h of IV acetaminophen administration were investigated to confirm the occurrence of hypotension. Hypotension was defined as systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg, a decrease in systolic blood pressure of 30 mm Hg, or mean arterial pressure decrease of 15%.1220 Patients with hypotension before acetaminophen administration were also excluded. In addition, to adjust for potential confounding factors and analyze risk factors, underlying diseases, drug histories, and blood test results (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, hemoglobin, platelet count, protein, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, total CO2, C-reactive protein, pH) were also examined. This study was approved by the Severance Hospital ethical review board (9-2021-0083), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographic variables were presented as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Normality tests (Shapiro–Wilk test) were performed for continuous variables to confirm the statistical assumptions. In general situations, where no special mention was made, statistical significance was determined at a 5% significance level. For patients who were administered multiple doses of acetaminophen, the impact of multiple administrations on the occurrence of hypotension was observed as the dependent variable. Measurements such as height, weight, age, sex, and underlying disease were used as independent variables for fixed effects that did not change at each dosing point, whereas blood test results and the type of drug administered were used as time-dependent variables that changed at each administration point. As a random effect, we parameterized the structure of covariance for random components of repeated administration in individual patients and designed an analysis that considered the effects within patients but not across patients, at each time point. We presented the results of this study using a mixed-effects model, which considers both fixed and random effects, and the GLIMMIX procedure in SAS. Furthermore, we conducted a case-based analysis, using the same variables used in patient-based analysis, to test our hypothesis that the occurrence of hypotension was more likely associated with patient characteristics at the time of administration, and presented the results. The patient’s condition at each administration was variable enough to suggest that the relationship was independent of other administrations. In addition, sensitivity analysis was performed using logistic regression, assuming that the time points were independent of each other. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and R (version 4.2.2; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

A total of 398 patients, with a total of 1547 IV-acetaminophen administrations, were included in this study (Table 1). Patients received IV acetaminophen an average of 3.9 times during their hospital stay, with 140 patients receiving only a single dose. Hypotension occurred in 204 (51.3%) patients. Of the 1547 acetaminophen administrations, 416 (26.9%) resulted in hypotension, and there were no cases exhibiting hypersensitivity reactions. The average age of the patients was 66 years. Hypertension was the most common underlying condition (71.6%), followed by diabetes mellitus (42.7%), GI infection (40.7%), cardiovascular disease (37.3%), and pneumonia (27.5%) (Table 1). The frequent administration of IV acetaminophen was associated with an increased incidence of hypotension [odds ratio (OR)=1.35]. An association between septic shock and IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension was observed (OR=3.06).

The results of repeated IV acetaminophen administration in each patient are shown in Fig. 1. The frequency of acetaminophen-induced hypotension was observed to increase with repeated administrations (Table 1). However, there was no clear trend in the pattern of hypotension occurrence at each specific time point (Fig. 1). IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension tended to occur irregularly, rather than repeatedly, in certain patients. Albumin (OR=0.76), blood urea nitrogen (OR=1.01), and creatinine (OR=1.14) showed significant differences in the univariate analysis but not in the multivariate analysis (Table 2). Among the underlying diseases, diabetes mellitus (OR=1.29), renal disease (OR=1.41), urinary tract infection (UTI) (OR=1.69), GI infection (OR=1.39), and septic shock (OR=1.90) showed significant differences in the univariate analysis. However, diabetes mellitus, renal disease, and UTI did not show significant differences in the multivariate analysis. GI infection (OR=1.42) and septic shock (OR=1.68) were associated with IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension in the multivariate analysis. Among the medications, beta-blocker (OR=1.39) and norepinephrine (OR=1.33) were associated with IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension in the univariate analysis. In the multivariate analysis, beta-blocker (OR=1.50) was associated with hypotension, whereas norepinephrine was not.

A subgroup analysis was also conducted in cases with beta-blocker (Table 3). Blood tests showed that albumin levels (OR=0.60) were associated with IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. However, albumin levels (OR=0.65) did not show significant differences in the multivariate analysis. Among the underlying diseases, a history of heart failure (OR=1.91), UTI (OR=2.16), and GI infection (OR=1.83) were significantly associated with IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension.

When IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension occurred, hydration and inotropic treatments were often administered. However, it was not clearly documented in the medical records whether these treatments were specifically for addressing hypotension due to IV acetaminophen or managing the underlying conditions. Despite the frequent use of inotropes (norepinephrine, vasopressin, dopamine), there was no statistically significant association between these inotropes and the occurrence of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension (Table 1). Additionally, there were no reported deaths due to IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension in this study.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we aimed to identify the risk factors for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension and the blood pressure responses in patients repeatedly exposed to IV acetaminophen. Septic shock significantly increased the incidence of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension (OR=3.06), and repeated acetaminophen administration was associated with a higher frequency of hypotension (OR=1.35). However, a history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension did not consistently predict subsequent hypotensive episodes, and there was no significant correlation. Further analysis independently considering each case revealed that beta-blocker use (OR=1.50), GI infection (OR=1.42), and septic shock (OR=1.68) were significant risk factors for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. In the subgroup analysis of cases with beta-blocker, heart failure (OR=1.91), UTI (OR=2.16), and GI infection (OR=1.83) were risk factors.

IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is suggested to be a type A adverse drug event, which results from its pharmacological effect. The increase in IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension observed with repeated acetaminophen administration in this study (OR=1.35) is related to its pharmacological effect. However, the underlying mechanism has not yet been elucidated. IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension may be associated with resetting of the thermoregulatory setpoint, as vasodilation, which lowers the body temperature and can decrease blood pressure.21 IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension may also be associated with COX inhibition.2122 Endothelial COX plays an important role in the endothelium-dependent contractions of smooth muscle in blood vessels. Therefore, COX inhibition by acetaminophen can reduce or inhibit the endothelium-dependent contraction of blood vessels.21 This can diminish the body’s compensatory mechanisms of vasodilation, leading to a decrease in blood pressure. Vasodilation induced by IV acetaminophen may be associated with its metabolites. A recent study has suggested that N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), a metabolite of acetaminophen, induces hypotension by activating the Kv7 potassium channel, an important regulator of arterial tone.4 Another study objectively confirmed the acetaminophen-induced vasodilation.23 Boyle, et al.23 reported that IV acetaminophen increased blood flow to the skin.

IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension may be associated with mannitol, which is included as a stabilizing compound (up to 3.91 g/100 mL IV acetaminophen).5 Mannitol is secreted into the lumen of the nephrons, leading to natriuresis that creates a fluid shift. This draws water from the peritubular blood into the nephron lumen due to osmosis, further increasing urine volume and consequently decreasing blood volume.5 Therefore, owing to its diuretic nature, mannitol can cause hypotension-related hypovolemia.24 However, in a study conducted on healthy volunteers, IV mannitol, unlike IV acetaminophen, did not cause a decrease in blood pressure.25 Nonetheless, as IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension primarily occurs in critically ill patients, it is difficult to rule out the possibility of blood pressure reduction due to mannitol.

In this study, septic shock and GI infection were risk factors for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. Previous studies have also reported an association between infections and IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension.461026 The primary cardiovascular response observed in sepsis is vasodilation.27 Therefore, vasodilation caused by IV acetaminophen, combined with vasodilation due to sepsis, may make patients more susceptible to IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension during severe infection. Infections are risk factors for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension and may be related to NAPQI. NAPQI formation increases under inflammatory conditions, such as sepsis, causing vasodilation and potentially leading to hypotension.4 In this study, beta-blockers were also found to be a risk factor for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. Beta-blockers appear to cause IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension by inhibiting the vasoconstrictive effect, thereby limiting the body’s compensatory response to IV acetaminophen-induced vasodilation.

The occurrence of hypotension upon re-administration of IV acetaminophen in patients who have previously experienced IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension has not been well-studied. In this study, we investigated the response of patients who experienced IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension after acetaminophen administration (Fig. 1). The results showed that IV acetaminophen administration post-IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension was not associated with subsequent IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. Therefore, it can be inferred that a history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension cannot predict the occurrence of subsequent IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. This suggests that IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension is more closely related to a patient’s overall condition at the time of IV acetaminophen administration than to their baseline characteristics. The factors identified in this study that contribute to such conditions include infections that cause vasodilation and the use of beta-blocker that inhibit compensatory responses to vasodilation.

Due to the observed association between the use of beta-blocker and hypotension in the multivariate analysis, a subgroup analysis was conducted specifically on cases with beta-blocker. In the subgroup analysis of cases with beta-blocker, heart failure (OR=1.91) was a risk factor for IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. Since acetaminophen has been reported to reduce the cardiac output,2829 the hypotension observed in patients with heart failure who are using beta-blockers may be related to a reduction in cardiac output caused by acetaminophen. However, the mechanisms underlying the effects of acetaminophen on cardiac output remain unclear. On the other hand, animal studies have reported that acetaminophen does not affect cardiac output.30 Therefore, the effects of acetaminophen on cardiac output remain controversial.

The results of this study indicate that caution is needed when administering IV acetaminophen in patients with severe infections, especially in those using beta-blockers. However, since fever often occurs with infections, the use of acetaminophen is frequently necessary. In these cases, oral administration of acetaminophen is recommended. Although there are reports that oral administration can cause hypotension, the associated risk is lower than with IV administration.31 Additionally, one study found that hypotension was rarely observed when acetaminophen was administered orally in patients who experienced IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension.14 Furthermore, oral administration of acetaminophen is not inferior to IV acetaminophen for controlling fever. In a randomized controlled study on the antipyretic effect of acetaminophen, there was no significant difference at the 2-h mark.32 Therefore, the purpose of using acetaminophen can be achieved with oral administration.

This study had some limitations. First, it did not account for all potential confounding variables. For instance, variations in fluid management, use of other medications that might influence blood pressure, and overall severity of illness were not controlled. These factors could potentially affect the occurrence of hypotension and the observed associations. Second, the analysis in this study was presented as a mixed-effects model in which IV acetaminophen was repeatedly administered to the same patient, and the response to each administration was considered based on statistical assumptions. However, there are several reasons why the actual administration of acetaminophen may be treated as an independent event, such as the variations in the time between blood test results and acetaminophen administration being slightly different depending on the circumstances or the type of medication administered but not the order, which may not accurately reflect the clinical situation in which cumulative effects or patient-specific factors may affect hypotension. Third, although the study evaluated blood pressure changes due to IV acetaminophen administration, it did not adequately address clinical outcomes or the impact on patients’ quality of life. These limitations suggest that caution should be exercised when interpreting the study results. Considering these limitations, additional studies are required to better understand and manage IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension.

In conclusion, our findings indicated that GI infections (OR=1.42), septic shock (OR=1.68), and the use of beta-blocker (OR=1.50) significantly increased the incidence of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension. However, a history of IV acetaminophen-induced hypotension did not consistently predict subsequent hypotensive episodes, suggesting that hypotension was more closely related to the patient’s overall condition at the time of administration. Clinicians should perform individualized patient assessments rather than relying solely on past medical history, and monitoring should be done during and after the administration of IV acetaminophen to promptly identify and manage hypotensive episodes. Additionally, oral administration of acetaminophen can be considered as a safer alternative, particularly for patients at higher risk of hypotension. To build on these findings, future research should focus on exploring the underlying mechanisms of acetaminophen-induced hypotension and validating the results through prospective studies.
