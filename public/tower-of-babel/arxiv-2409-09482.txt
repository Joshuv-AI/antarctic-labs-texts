Almost Commutative Terwilliger Algebras of Group Association Schemes II: Primitive Idempotents
Nicholas L. Bastian

Abstract
This paper is a continuation of Almost Commutative Terwilliger Algebras of Group Association Schemes I: Classification [ 1 ] . In that paper, we found all groups G G for which the Terwilliger algebra of the group association scheme, denoted T ⁡ ( G ) T(G) , is almost commutative. We also found the primitive idempotents for T ⁡ ( G ) T(G) for three of the four types of such groups. In this paper, we determine the primitive idempotents for the fourth type.

Terwilliger algebra, Camina group, association scheme, group association scheme, Schur ring, centralizer algebra

3
Terwilliger Algebras

Definitions of Terwilliger algebras and Schur rings can be found in
[
1
]
. One particular definition we remind the reader of is that given a finite group
G
with conjugacy classes
C_{0},C_{1},\cdots,C_{d}
, we can define
R_{i}=\{(x,y)\in G\times G\colon yx^{-1}\in C_{i}\}
. Then
\mathcal{A}=(G,\{R_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
is an association scheme called the
group association scheme
. We let
T(G)
denote the Terwilliger algebra for the group association scheme of group
G
. For any
x\in G
, we define the primary-T(x) module in
[
1
]
. This definition will be used in the next important definition.

Recall that for any
x\in G
, the Terwilliger algebra
T(x)
is
almost commutative
if every non-primary irreducible
T(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional.

Theorem 3.1

(Tanaka,
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(\Omega,\{R_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
be a commutative association scheme. Let
\mathcal{T}(x)
be the Terwilliger algebra of
\mathcal{A}
for some
x\in\Omega
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

Every non-primary irreducible
\mathcal{T}(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional for some
x\in\Omega
.

(ii)

Every non-primary irreducible
\mathcal{T}(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional for all
x\in\Omega
.

(iii)

The intersection numbers of
\mathcal{A}
have the property that for all distinct
h,i
there is exactly one
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0

(0\leq h,i,j\leq d)
.

(iv)

The Krein parameters of
\mathcal{A}
have the property that for all distinct
h,i
there is exactly one
j
such that
q_{ij}^{h}\neq 0

(0\leq h,i,j\leq d)
.

(v)

\mathcal{A}
is a wreath product of association schemes
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
where each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is either a
1-
class association scheme or the group scheme of a finite abelian group.

Moreover, a Terwilliger algebra satisfying these equivalent conditions is triply regular.

Definitions of intersection numbers and Krein parameters are given in
[
1
]
. For the definition of the wreath product of association schemes see
[
6
]
. We note that a Terwilliger algebra for a commutative association scheme is almost commutative if it satisfies the five equivalent conditions in Theorem
3.1
.

In
[
1
]
, we found those groups for which

is almost commutative. We mention this result here.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let

be a finite group. Then

is almost commutative if and only if

is isomorphic to one of the following groups

•

A finite abelian group

•

The group
(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
, where

is the quaternions of order

•

(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r-1}}
for some prime
p
and

.

•

A non-abelian Camina
p-
group, for some prime
p
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G
have nilpotency class
2
, where
|G|=p^{n}
and
|Z(G)|=p^{k}
. Then
\dim T(G)=(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-1)(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-2)+p^{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
,
T(G)
is almost commutative. Then by Theorem
3.1
,
T(G)
is triply regular. Therefore,
\dim T(G)=|\{(i,j,h)\colon p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0\}|
. As
G
has nilpotency class
2
, its lower central series is
\{e\}\triangleleft G_{1}\triangleleft G
. We have
G_{1}\leq Z(G)
and
G/G_{1}\leq Z(G/G_{1})
. Note that
G/G_{1}
. Thus,
G^{\prime}\leq G_{1}\leq Z(G)
. So
G^{\prime}\leq Z(G)
. By Lemma
2.1
,
Z(G)\leq G^{\prime}
. So
G^{\prime}=Z(G)
.

As
G
is a Camina group the conjugacy classes of
G
are those inside of
G^{\prime}=Z(G)
and the non-trivial cosets of
G/G^{\prime}
. As
|G/G^{\prime}|=p^{n-k}
there are
p^{n-k}-1
conjugacy classes that are non-trivial cosets of
G/G^{\prime}
. Since
G^{\prime}=Z(G)
the classes of
G
in
G^{\prime}
are just singleton elements. There are
p^{k}
of these. Then
G
has
p^{n-k}-1+p^{k}
classes. We shall denote these classes as
C_{i}
. We consider cases for fixed
i,h
to find the number of nonzero constants
p_{ij}^{h}
.

Case 1:

i\neq h
. As
T(G)
is almost commutative, by Theorem
3.2
there is a unique
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. Thus there is a unique
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. We have
p^{n-k}+p^{k}-1
choices for
C_{i}
. We then have
p^{n-k}+p^{k}-2
choices for
C_{h}
. Therefore, in total we have
(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-1)(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-2)
triples
(i,j,h)
with
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
in this case.

Case 2:
i=h
and
C_{i}=\{g\}\subseteq Z(G)
. For any
C_{j}\not\subseteq Z(G)
we have
C_{j}=xZ(G)
,
x\not\in Z(G)
. Then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{gxZ(G)}=\overline{xZ(G)}
, which does not contain
g\in Z(G)
. Thus,
p_{ij}^{h}=0
in this case. If
C_{j}=\{x\}\subseteq Z(G)
, then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=gx
. This equals
\overline{C_{h}}
if and only if
x=e
. Thus, for
i=h
, there is a unique
C_{j}=\{e\}
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. We have
p^{k}
choices for
i=h
, so there are a total of
p^{k}
triples
(i,j,h)
with
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
in this case.

Case 3:
i=h
with
C_{i}\not\subseteq Z(G)
. Then
C_{i}=gZ(G)
,
g\not\in Z(G)
. For any
C_{j}\not\in Z(G)
we have
C_{j}=xZ(G)
for
x\not\in Z(G)
. Then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=|Z(G)|\overline{gxZ(G)}
. As
g\in gZ(G)
, for
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
we must have
gxZ(G)=gZ(G)
. Then
xZ(G)=Z(G)
, which is false. Thus,
p_{ij}^{h}=0
for all
C_{j}\not\subseteq Z(G)
. For any
C_{j}\subseteq Z(G)
we have
C_{j}=\{x\}\subseteq Z(G)
. Then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{gxZ(G)}=\overline{gZ(G)}=\overline{C_{h}}
. So
p_{ij}^{h}=1
in this case. Hence, for any
C_{j}\subseteq Z(G)
we have
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. We have
p^{k}
choices for
C_{j}
for each
C_{i}=C_{h}
. There are
p^{n-k}-1
choices for
C_{i}
. Thus, in total we have
p^{k}(p^{n-k}-1)
triples
(i,j,h)
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
in this case.

Having considered every possible case we obtain:

\dim T(G)=(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-1)(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-2)+p^{k}+p^{k}(p^{n-k}-1)=(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-1)(p^{n-k}+p^{k}-2)+p^{n}.\qed

.

.

Proposition 4.4

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
have nilpotency class
2
with
|G|=p^{n}
. Suppose
|Z(G)|=p^{k}
Then the Wedderburn decomposition of
T(G)
is

T(G)=V\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p^{n-k}-1}\bigoplus_{\alpha}\mathcal{W}_{i}(\alpha),

where
V
is the primary component and each

is a one-dimensional ideal generated by a matrix

(using the notation from Theorem
4.1
), where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{k})
with each
\alpha_{j}\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,p-1\}
and not all
\alpha_{j}=0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
,
\dim T(G)=p^{2n-2k}+3p^{n}-3p^{n-k}+p^{2k}-3p^{k}+2
. We know
V
is an irreducible ideal with dimension equal to the number of conjugacy classes of
G
squared. From the proof of Proposition
4.1
,
\dim(V)=(p^{n-k}-1+p^{k})^{2}
. We can then write
T(G)=V\oplus R
where
\dim(R)=p^{n}-p^{k}-p^{n-k}+1=(p^{k}-1)(p^{n-k}-1)
.

From Proposition
4.4
we have, for each choice of
C_{i}\not\subseteq G^{\prime}
, idempotents
W_{i}(\alpha)
all of which are orthogonal. Clearly for
i\neq j
we have
W_{i}(\alpha)
and
W_{j}(\beta)
are orthogonal as they are nonzero in different blocks. For any fixed
C_{i}\not\subseteq G^{\prime}
, we consider
W_{i}(\alpha)
when
\alpha=(0,0,\cdots,0)
. We have

W_{i}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(E_{i}^{*}A_{j}E_{i}^{*})^{a_{j}}.

By Lemma
4.6
,
\prod_{j=1}^{k}(E_{i}^{*}A_{j}E_{i}^{*})^{a_{j}}=E_{i}^{*}A_{\ell}E_{i}^{*}
where
C_{\ell}=\{z_{1}^{a_{1}}z_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots z_{k}^{a_{k}}\}
. This means
W_{i}(\alpha)
is a scaled sum of the
C_{i},C_{i}
blocks of all the adjacency matrices corresponding to an element in
G^{\prime}
.

Let
C_{t}
be any class of
G
outside of
G^{\prime}
. Say
C_{i}=xZ(G)
and
C_{t}=yZ(G)
. Then
C_{i}C_{t}=xyZ(G)
. Suppose
x\in xyZ(G)
. Then
x=xyz
for some
z\in Z(G)
and then
y^{-1}=z
. This implies
y^{-1}\in Z(G)
, which is false. Therefore,
C_{i}\not\subseteq C_{i}C_{t}
. By Lemma
4.1
the
C_{i},C_{i}
block of
A_{t}
is all
0
. Hence, every adjacency matrix corresponding to a class outside
G^{\prime}
is
0
in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block. As the sum of all the adjacency matrices is the all
1
’s matrix, the sum of all the adjacency matrices corresponding to classes outside
G^{\prime}
must be
0
in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block. Hence, the sum of all the adjacency matrices corresponding to
C_{t}\subseteq G^{\prime}
must be the all
1
’s matrix in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block. Hence,
W_{i}(\alpha)
has all
1
’s in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block and is
0
outside of this block. Therefore, it is equal to the basis element of

that is the all
1
’s matrix in block

and
0
outside this block. We denote this as

.

Then for all
\beta\neq(0,0,\cdots,0)
we have
\langle W_{i}(\alpha),W_{i}(\beta)\rangle=0
, so
\langle v_{ii},W_{i}(\beta)\rangle=0
. Clearly
\langle v_{st},W_{i}(\beta)\rangle=0
for all
s,t
not both equal to

. Hence, for all
\beta\neq(0,0,\cdots,0)
we have
W_{i}(\beta)
is orthogonal to

. Then

arbitrarily, this is true for all

.

Now let
\mathcal{W}_{i}(\beta)
be the ideal generated by

. Then for all

,

. There are a total of

. From the proof of Proposition

there are

. Hence, we have found

. This equals the dimension of

must be

Proposition 4.7

3
. Suppose
|Z(G)|=p^{k}
,
k>0
, and
|G^{\prime}\colon Z(G)|=p^{n}
. Suppose that
Z(G)=\langle z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{k}\rangle

G^{\prime}/Z(G)=\langle g_{1}Z(G),g_{2}Z(G),\cdots,g_{n}Z(G)\rangle
. Let
C_{i}\subseteq G\setminus G^{\prime}
be a class. For
1\leq j\leq k
we let
C_{j}=\{z_{j}\}
be a central class of
G
. Let
\zeta
be any primitive
p
th root of unity. Let
X_{i}(\beta)
be any idempotent of
T(G)
from Theorem
4.3
for any
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots\beta_{k})
with
\beta_{j}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}
not all
0
. Then
X_{i}(\beta)
is orthogonal to
V
, where

is the primary component of
T(G)
.

Proof.

For any
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{k})
we have

X_{i}(\beta)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}(E_{i}^{*}A_{j}E_{i}^{*})^{a_{j}}.

Since
X_{i}(\beta)
is
0
outside of the
C_{i},C_{i}
block, the inner product of
X_{i}(\beta)
with any matrix is the same as if we only considered the
C_{i},C_{i}
block of each. For this reason we set

X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}(A_{j}^{\prime})^{a_{j}}

where
A_{j}^{\prime}
is the
C_{i},C_{i}
block of the matrix
A_{j}
.
Let
P
be the
p\times p
matrix in (
4.3
).
Then using an argument similar to that used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
we have

X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}I_{p}\otimes\zeta^{\alpha_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}=\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}.

Let us now consider
X_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)
when
\alpha=(0,0,\cdots,0)
. We have

X_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}I_{p}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}=\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}.

We claim that
\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}=J_{p^{k}}
. We proceed by induction on
k\geq 1
. For
k=1
,

\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}P^{i}=J_{p}

so the result is true for
k=1
. Suppose the result is true for some
m\geq 1
. That is,
\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}P^{a_{j}}=J_{p^{m}}
. We consider
m+1
. We have

\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m+1}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m+1}P^{a_{j}}=\sum_{a_{1}=0}^{p-1}\sum_{a_{2},a_{3},\cdots,a_{m+1}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m+1}P^{a_{j}}=\sum_{a_{1}=0}^{p-1}P^{a_{1}}\otimes\sum_{a_{2},a_{3},\cdots,a_{m+1}}\bigotimes_{j=2}^{m+1}P^{a_{j}}.

The first term is a sum of all the powers of
P
, and is thus
J_{p}
. The second term, by the inductive hypothesis, is
J_{p^{m}}
. Then this equals
J_{p}\otimes J_{p^{m}}=J_{p^{m+1}}
.
This proves the inductive step. Hence,
\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}=J_{p^{k}}
. We then have
X_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}
.

Now for any
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{k})
, with not all the
\beta_{j}=0
, we have by Proposition
4.6
,
\langle X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta),X_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)\rangle=0
. Thus,

0=\langle X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta),X_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)\rangle=\text{tr}\left(\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)\left(\overline{\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}}\right)^{T}\right)

=\text{tr}\left(\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}\right)\right)=\text{tr}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}\right).

We note since the first term in the Kronecker product is

this trace is just the trace of

\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}
multiplied by
\frac{p^{n}}{p^{2k}}
since in the trace computation we just add up the trace of
\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}
a total
p^{n}
times. We then have

0=\frac{p^{n}}{p^{2k}}\text{tr}\left(\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}\right).

So
\text{tr}\left(\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}\right)=0
.

We now let
v_{ii}
be the basis element of
V
with all
1
’s in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block. We let
v^{\prime}_{ii}
be the
C_{i},C_{i}
block of
v_{ii}
. We have
v^{\prime}_{ii}=J_{p^{n+k}}=J_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}
. Observe

\langle X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta),v^{\prime}_{ii}\rangle=\text{tr}\left(\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)\left(\overline{J_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}}\right)^{T}\right)

=\text{tr}\left(\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}I_{p^{n}}\otimes\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)\left(J_{p^{n}}\otimes J_{p^{k}}\right)\right)=\text{tr}\left(\frac{1}{p^{k}}J_{p^{n}}\otimes\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}\right).

Since the first term in the Kronecker product has all
\frac{1}{p^{k}}
’s along its main diagonal, the trace we are computing is
\frac{p^{n}}{p^{k}}\text{tr}\left(\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}}\zeta^{\beta_{j}a_{j}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}P^{a_{j}}\right)J_{p^{k}}\right)=0
. Hence,
\langle X_{i}^{\prime}(\beta),v^{\prime}_{ii}\rangle=0
, and so
\langle X_{i}(\beta),v_{ii}\rangle=0
. For any
s,t
not both
i
we have
\langle X_{i}(\beta),v_{st}\rangle=0
as
X_{i}(\beta)
is only nonzero in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block and
v_{st}
is all
0
in the
C_{i},C_{i}
block. Hence, for any
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots\beta_{k})
with
\beta_{j}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}
not all
0
,
X_{i}(\beta)
is orthogonal to
V
.
∎

We now consider the third type of idempotent for

using a similar argument to the first two.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
G

3
. Suppose
|Z(G)|=p^{k}
,

Proof.

4.3

(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell}E_{i}^{*})_{xu}(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})_{uy}=1

There are
p^{k}
different choices for

, namely those in

, if

. This gives a total of

nonzero terms in the sum, each of which equals 1. Thus, if

, the

entry of
(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell}E_{i}^{*})(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})
is

. If

, then
(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell}E_{i}^{*})_{xu}(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})_{uy}=0
for all

as either

, or
y\not\in ug_{m}^{\ell}Z(G)
. Thus, the
(x,y)
entry of

is
0
in this case.

Hence, the

entry of
(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell}E_{i}^{*})(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})

if and only if

this is identical to the condition for the

to be

. Hence,
[(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell}E_{i}^{*})(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})]_{xy}=p^{k}(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell+1}E_{i}^{*})_{xy}
for all

. Thus,

E_{i}^{*}A_{\beta}E_{i}^{*}=(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})^{\ell+1}=p^{(\ell-1)k}(E_{i}^{*}A_{m\ell}E_{i}^{*})(E_{i}^{*}A_{m,1}E_{i}^{*})=p^{\ell k}E_{i}^{*}A_{m,\ell+1}E_{i}^{*}.

Then by induction we have

for all

.

Lemma 4.10
