Elucidating the Role of Oxidative Stress-Associated Genes FKBP Prolyl Isomerase 5 in Osteoarthritis Development and Immunological Milieu
Authors: Liangkun Huang, Zhongyu Peng, Ya Wen, Ze Zhang, Zijie Pei, Hanzhe Xu, Liangyuan Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxidative stress arises from disrupted equilibrium between reactive oxygen species generation and cellular antioxidant capacity, serving as a pivotal mechanism in inflammatory pathology, particularly osteoarthritis development. The progression of osteoarthritis-related pain may additionally involve oxidative stress through its activation of nociceptive signaling pathways, but the mechanism of its influence on the progression of osteoarthritis is not yet unanimous.MethodsThe study utilized publicly available sequencing data, which included specimens from patients with osteoarthritis and matched healthy controls, retrieved from GEO database, and combined them after removing batch effects. A multi-step analytical approach was employed to identify pivotal diagnostic biomarkers for osteoarthritis. This strategy encompassed examining differential gene expression, constructing protein-protein interaction networks, applying machine learning algorithms, and assessing co-expression patterns. Subsequently, a diagnostic framework incorporating a nomogram was developed based on these candidate genes. To discern distinct patient subgroups, cluster analysis was conducted. Further investigations into the underlying biology revealed divergent functional pathways and immune cell infiltration landscapes across the identified subtypes, highlighting the putative roles of the key biomarkers. We investigated the effects of FKBP5 on osteoarthritis through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, examining chondrocyte proliferation capacity, apoptosis, cellular senescence, mitochondrial function, and cartilage degeneration.ResultWe successfully screened the key genes PPARGC1A, FKBP5, and MMP13 for the diagnosis of osteoarthritis, constructed a diagnostic model and a nomogram of osteoarthritis, and verified their excellent diagnostic ability. Based on these core genes we classified osteoarthritis into two subtypes. Enrichment analysis and immune infiltration analysis confirmed that FKBP5 regulates the immune microenvironment in osteoarthritis and influences the progression of osteoarthritis. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that FKBP5 overexpression enhances chondrocyte viability, proliferative capacity, and mitochondrial function under inflammatory conditions, while reducing cellular senescence, apoptosis, and cartilage degeneration. FKBP5 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for osteoarthritis intervention.ConclusionOur study identified distinct molecular subtypes of osteoarthritis by analyzing genes associated with oxidative stress. Furthermore, we found that FKBP5 exerts a positive regulatory effect on chondrocyte function and mitigates cartilage degeneration in the osteoarthritis environment. This provides new insights for personalized treatment of osteoarthritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12575-026-00336-2.

Introduction

With the development of an aging population, age-related diseases are one of the great challenges facing the world today [1]. Osteoarthritis (OA) is a whole-joint disorder driven by cartilage degradation, synovitis, and pain, whose prevalence increases markedly with age [2, 3].The prevalence of OA is about 50% in people over 60 years, and as high as 80% in people over 75 years, with 20%-30% of the patients presenting with clinical signs and symptoms (e.g., joint pain, stiffness, and dysfunction). The disability rate can be as high as 53% [4, 5]. The incidence of osteoarthritis is nowadays younger. OA severely diminishes life quality and elevates the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, ranking as the globe’s third leading cause of disability, behind only diabetes and dementia [6, 7]. Current evidence establishes oxidative stress as a key pathogenic driver in numerous disorders, such as inflammatory conditions, cancer, and illnesses mediated by immune dysfunction [8, 9]. This state arises when the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) under pathological stimuli surpasses the clearance capability of the endogenous antioxidant defenses, leading to a disruption of the redox equilibrium [10]. Physiologically, ROS/RNS are important signaling molecules involved in immune defense and cellular homeostasis regulation [11, 12]. However, excess ROS attack proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, inducing cell damage and apoptosis [13, 14]. During chronic inflammation, mediators such as IL-1β and TNF-α can induce NADPH oxidase activation, which in turn augments reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. This interplay establishes a self-perpetuating cycle linking oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [15, 16]. Oxidative stress is not only related to local joint damage, but also involves systemic metabolic disorders and dysregulation of cellular energy metabolism [17]. In recent years, theories such as glycolysis-oxidative stress axis, mitochondrial autophagy imbalance, and gut-joint axis regulation have been explored to decipher how oxidative stress influences OA pathogenesis [18–21]. However, scholars have not yet had a consistent view on the intervention targets and influence mechanisms of oxidative stress on the development of OA. This investigation highlights the clinical utility of oxidative stress-related genes for OA prevention, diagnosis, and personalized therapy. We developed a diagnostic model and nomogram incorporating PPARGC1A, FKBP5, and MMP13, which demonstrated high discriminatory power and enabled patient stratification into distinct subtypes, thereby informing tailored treatment strategies. Furthermore, experimental validation confirmed that FKBP5 positively regulates chondrocyte function under inflammatory conditions, supporting its potential as a viable therapeutic target for OA.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

A systematic search of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository was conducted using the keyword “osteoarthritis” to identify human transcriptional profiles. This yielded five eligible datasets—GSE51588, GSE55457, GSE55235, GSE82107, and GSE169077—comprising a total of 76 OA and 42 healthy control specimens. Using the search term “oxidative stress” within the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/), 1792 genes encoding proteins related to oxidative stress were obtained (Relevance score > 8). For diagnostic model construction, GSE51588, GSE55457, and GSE55235 were merged as the training set, while GSE82107 and GSE169077 were merged as an independent test set. To eliminate batch effects arising from non-biological factors during dataset integration, the removeBatchEffect function from the R package “limma” was applied to the training set and test set separately. To evaluate the effectiveness of batch effect removal, Principal Component Analysis was performed on both datasets before and after correction using the R packages “FactoMineR” and “factoextra”, and the sample distributions were visualized.

Differential Expression Analysis

Differential expression analysis between OA and control samples in the training set was conducted with the “limma” R package, applying a threshold of |log2FC| > 0.7 and adj. P value < 0.05. The resulting OA DEGs were cross-referenced with oxidative stress-related genes to identify DEORGs, visualized via a Venn diagram generated by the “venn” package. Correlation patterns among DEORGs were displayed in a heatmap created with “corrplot”. Subsequently, a protein‑protein interaction (PPI)network for the DEORGs was built utilizing the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape.

Access to Key Genes for OA Diagnosis

The “randomForest” R package was employed to rank DEORGs by importance. To balance inclusiveness and stringency in gene selection under the constraint of sample size, the top 60% of genes were retained as high-value candidates [22–25]. Separately, four network centrality measures, including BottleNeck, Closeness, MNC, and EcCentricity, were derived from the PPI analysis using the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. For each centrality measure, the top 60% of genes were also selected. The final set of diagnostic key genes for OA was defined as the intersection of genes identified by all five ranking methods, ensuring that only genes consistently prioritized across different analytical perspectives were retained. Their co-expression relationships were visualized via correlation heatmap and chord diagram, generated with the “corrplot” and “circlize” packages, respectively.

Development of an OA Diagnostic Model and Nomogram Utilizing Key Genes

ROC curves for the key genes were generated with the “ROCR” package to determine their diagnostic performance, quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). Genes demonstrating an AUC greater than 0.7 were selected for inclusion in a multivariate logistic regression model, built using the “glmnet” package, to formulate the final diagnostic classifier. Subsequently, a diagnostic nomogram was developed with the “rms” and “rmda” packages, and its predictive accuracy was evaluated via calibration and decision curve analyses. The entire modeling process, established in the training cohort, was subsequently validated in an independent dataset.

Identification of Oxidative Stress-related OA Subtypes

To facilitate personalized therapeutic strategies for osteoarthritis, molecular subtyping of OA was performed according to the expression profiles of the key genes. The “ConsensusClusterPlus” package was applied to categorize OA samples, testing cluster numbers (k) from 1 to 9. The optimal number of subtypes was determined by assessing the consensus matrix heatmap, cumulative distribution function, and tracking changes in the area under the CDF curve. Finally, the robustness of the identified subtypes was confirmed in the validation cohort.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To delineate the molecular mechanisms distinguishing OA subtypes, DEGs were identified for each cluster. These DEGs then underwent comprehensive functional annotation, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), executed with the “clusterProfiler” package. Furthermore, a separate, focused enrichment analysis was conducted on the key genes to elucidate the specific biological pathways through which they potentially influence OA pathogenesis.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

The immune landscape across OA subtypes was profiled using the “GSEABase” and “GSVA” packages. This analysis assessed disparities in the immune microenvironment, visualized through box plots depicting immune signature scores and immune checkpoint expression. A heatmap generated with the “pheatmap” package illustrated the immune infiltration patterns, while correlations between key genes and immune scores were displayed in a heatmap created by the “corrplot” package.

Isolation and Culture of Murine Chondrocytes

Primary chondrocytes were isolated from articular cartilage harvested from 5-day-old wild-type mice. All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow cabinet (ESCO, Singapore). The harvested cartilage tissue was minced into fine pieces using sterile ophthalmic scissors. Sequential enzymatic digestion was performed: the tissue fragments were first incubated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by digestion with 0.2% type II collagenase (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ, USA) in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 6 h at 37 °C. After two centrifugation steps, the cell pellet was resuspended in complete DMEM/F12 medium (Corning, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The cell suspension was transferred to T25 culture flasks (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every two days. To maintain phenotypic stability, only passage 1 or 2 cells were used for subsequent experiments. Cell adherence and morphological characteristics were routinely monitored using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

FKBP5 Overexpression and Protein Level Verification

The pFKBP5 overexpression plasmid (PCMV-FKBP5-Neo) and its corresponding negative control (OE nc) were commercially synthesized by AuGCT DNA-SYN Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The plasmid construction incorporated an insert amplified using the following primer pair: FKBP5-F, 5’-GAATTCATGGCCGAGGAGGAGCAG-3’ and FKBP5-R, 5’-CTCGAGTGTTGGTGATGGGGGACTC-3’. Chondrocytes underwent transfection with these constructs via Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol; untransfected cells served as an additional control. Successful transfection and endogenous FKBP5 levels in OA versus normal chondrocytes were assessed by Western blotting. Total protein was isolated with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Equal amounts of protein samples were resolved on 8% SDS-PAGE gels and then electrophoretically transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then subjected to sequential incubations with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Band intensities from three independent blots were quantified with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and protein expression was normalized to β-actin and presented as target-to-control ratios.

Cytochemical Staining for Proteoglycan Deposition

The synthesis and localization of proteoglycans in cultured chondrocytes were assessed cytochemically. To label cell surface chondroitin sulfate, live cells were incubated with 0.1% toluidine blue solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 60 min in the dark. Glycosaminoglycan content was similarly visualized by treating parallel cultures with a 0.02% safranin O solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 45 min. After each staining, any non-specifically bound dye was eliminated through three 5-minute washes with PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Finally, the stained cell monolayers were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, Shanghai, China) and documented under an inverted bright-field microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell Proliferation Assessment by EdU Staining

For proliferation analysis, chondrocytes were plated in 6-well plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. After removing the culture medium, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 10 min at ambient temperature and then rinsed three times with PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The EdU detection procedure was conducted according to manufacturer specifications using the Click-iT™ EdU Imaging Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA): cells were incubated with EdU reaction mixture (light-protected environment) followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After final PBS washes, samples were visualized by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

After respective treatments, chondrocytes were allowed to adhere in 12-well plates (Corning, NY, USA) over a 24-hour period. The existing medium was then removed and substituted with a pre-warmed JC-1 solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at a final concentration of 5 µM. Incubation with the mitochondrial probe proceeded for 30 min at 37 °C in the absence of light. Post-incubation, samples underwent three gentle washes with ice-cold PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) to eliminate unbound JC-1. Fluorescence visualization was conducted using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with dual-channel detection: JC-1 aggregates (indicative of polarized mitochondria) were imaged at 590 nm emission, while monomers (depolarized mitochondria) were captured at 530 nm.

Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Measurement

Cellular ROS production was measured employing the fluorogenic compound DCFH-DA (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Chondrocyte suspensions were loaded with the probe at a 1:1000 dilution in serum-free medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and maintained at 37 °C for 30 min in darkness. After this incubation, cells were rinsed thrice with warm HBSS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) to eliminate any unincorporated dye. Fluorescent images were captured immediately using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the mean signal intensity was determined through quantification with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). All experimental conditions included at least three independent replicates for statistical evaluation.

Detection of Cell Apoptosis

To assess apoptosis, chondrocytes representing each experimental condition were plated in 6-well plates (Corning, NY, USA). The cells underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 15 min at 25 °C and were subsequently rinsed three times with PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Permeabilization was achieved by a 5-minute exposure to 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at ambient temperature, followed by a single PBS wash. A 100 µL aliquot of TUNEL reaction mixture (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was then applied to cover the cells, which were incubated for 60 min in a dark environment at room temperature. Following three final PBS rinses, nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The stained samples were finally imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of Cellular Senescence via SA-β-Gal Activity

Chondrocyte senescence was determined through senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) detection. After a 72-hour exposure to TBHP and βOHB, cells from respective experimental subgroups were fixed using a mixture of 2% formaldehyde and 0.2% glutaraldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min at ambient temperature. Subsequently, the fixed cells were subjected to overnight incubation at 37 °C with the SA-β-gal staining reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) maintained in a light-proof environment. The samples underwent three PBS washes (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) post-staining, and enzymatic activity was identified by the emergence of green precipitates under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Animal Experiments

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were utilized throughout the study. Osteoarthritis was induced in the knee joints based on an established protocol [26]: under general anesthesia, medial meniscus resection (DMM) was performed in the OA model group, whereas sham-operated animals underwent only medial joint capsulotomy. In line with earlier reports, a group size of 10 mice was considered adequate for OARSI scoring comparisons [27]. Animals were randomized into three experimental groups: Sham + PBS, DMM + PBS, and DMM+FKBP5. Beginning after surgery, the DMM+FKBP5 group received weekly intra-articular injections of 10 µL FKBP5 plasmid suspension; the other groups were administered the same volume of PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), continuing until week 7. At week 8, all mice were euthanized and knee cartilage specimens were harvested intact. Tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, Shanghai, China) for 24 h and subsequently decalcified in 10% EDTA solution (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 4 weeks. Histological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and Safranin O-Fast Green (SOFG) staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The stained sections were visualized under a bright-field microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For immunohistochemical analysis, following deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were blocked with 3% BSA containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 60 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL2A1 (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and MMP13 (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with color development controlled for 3–5 min, followed by nuclear counterstaining with hematoxylin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 s. All sections were mounted with neutral resin and imaged under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of China Huatongwei Testing Co., Ltd. (Animal Ethics Approval No.: QRB149–08).

Statistical Methods

Data analysis was performed with R software (v4.4.3). Experimental data from Western blot and cellular assays represent three independent biological replicates. The gene expression profiles utilized in this study are publicly accessible in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Between-group differences were assessed by two-tailed Student’s t-tests, considering p-values below 0.05 as statistically significant.

Identification of Pivotal Biomarkers for Developing an OA Diagnostic Model and Predictive Nomogram

To further screen for key genes with potential diagnostic value for OA, we performed additional selection of DEORGs in the training set (merged GSE51588, GSE55457, and GSE55235 datasets) using machine learning methods (Random Forest) and PPI network analysis. As shown in Fig. 3A, the top 60% of DEORGs ranked in importance from RF method were displayed. We ranked the importance of DEORGs based on BottleNeck, Closeness, MNC, and EcCentricity values and took the top 60% as the key genes affecting OA diagnosis. Figure 3B shows the PPI network of DEORGs with the top 60% ranked by Closeness value. The consensus genes derived from the integration of these analytical approaches were defined as the key diagnostic determinants for osteoarthritis. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, the intersection across all five gene selection strategies yielded a final set of six pivotal genes. The co-expression relationships among these six key genes were assessed, with the correlation matrix and network interactions visualized as a heatmap and chord diagram in Fig. 3D and E, respectively. Analysis revealed significant positive and negative regulatory relationships within this gene set. To evaluate their individual diagnostic performance, ROC curves were generated for each key gene, with corresponding AUC values presented in Fig. 4A-F. In the training cohort, the AUC values were as follows: JUN (0.588), MMP13 (0.708), PPARGC1A (0.737), PTGS2 (0.627), ALB (0.695), and FKBP5 (0.854). Subsequently, we selected MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5 with AUC values greater than 0.7 to construct a diagnostic model for OA using multifactorial logistic regression, expressed as Riskscore= MMP13 expression*0.3558228 - PPARGC1A expression *0.5172689- FKBP5 expression *0.9778753, and plotted the ROC plot of the diagnostic model. The results are shown in Fig. 4G, and the diagnostic model achieved an AUC of 0.858 in the training cohort. We also constructed a nomogram for predicting OA based on three key genes, PPARGC1A, FKBP5, and MMP13, and the results are shown in Fig. 4H. Its calibration curve and decision curve are shown in Fig. 4I, J, confirming that the nomogram has high accuracy and disease prediction ability. To examine the differential expression of the three key genes MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5, we plotted their differential expression violins in the training set, and the results are shown in Fig. 4K-M. PPARGC1A and FKBP5 demonstrated significantly reduced expression in OA tissues relative to normal controls. Conversely, MMP13 showed marked upregulation in the OA group.

Classification of OA Subtypes Based on Oxidative Stress

To assess the clinical utility of MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5 for osteoarthritis management, we conducted molecular subtyping of OA specimens using these biomarkers to identify potential personalized therapeutic approaches. Unsupervised clustering results demonstrated optimal partition of OA samples into two distinct subtypes (k = 2), supported by the cumulative distribution function (Fig. 5B), delta area plot (Fig. 5C), and cluster consensus heatmap (Fig. 5D). Figure 5A shows the excellent clustering of OA samples when k is 2. OA was successfully stratified into distinct molecular subtypes. Figure 5E-G presents the expression patterns of MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5 in different OA subtypes using box plots. We found that FKBP5 expression was significantly higher in the C2 subtype compared to the C1 subtype, whereas MMP13 showed the opposite trend. To validate the reliability of our diagnostic model and subtype classification, we evaluated these results in an independent validation cohort, namely the test set generated by merging the GSE82107 and GSE169077 datasets. The diagnostic power of the three biomarkers in this cohort yielded AUC values of 0.917 (MMP13), 0.703 (PPARGC1A), and 0.719 (FKBP5) (Fig. 6A-C). The combined diagnostic model maintained strong performance with an AUC of 0.854 (Fig. 6D). Consistent with the training set, validation data confirmed significantly reduced PPARGC1A and FKBP5 levels alongside marked MMP13 upregulation in OA samples versus controls (Fig. 6E-G). Figure 6H-K shows that OA samples in the validation set could still cluster well into two subtypes based on MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5. All of the above results confirmed that the diagnostic model and subtype delineation we obtained had very high stability.

Exploring Potential Biological Differences Between OA Subtypes

To investigate the heterogeneity between the C1 and C2 OA subtypes, we identified their DEGs and conducted functional enrichment interpretation using GO, KEGG, and GSEA. The GO enrichment outcomes are displayed as circle and bar plots in Fig. 7A and B, respectively. Analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways critical for cartilage integrity, encompassing molecular functions, cellular components, and biological processes such as collagen binding, glycosaminoglycan binding, extracellular matrix structural constituent, fibrillar collagen trimer, banded collagen fibril, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, collagen fibril organization, and cartilage development. The KEGG results are similarly presented using radiograph and bar visualizations in Fig. 7C and D, respectively. Key KEGG pathways identified encompassed Rheumatoid arthritis, Malaria, Protein digestion and absorption, Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, as well as AGE-RAGE and Toll-like receptor signaling in diabetic complications and the IL-17 signaling pathway. GSEA revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in immunoregulatory processes, oxidative stress response, and osteoarthritis pathways, such as T cell differentiation and activation, leukocyte-mediated immunity, eosinophil chemotaxis, primary immunodeficiency, interleukin 17 signaling, HP osteoarthritis, chondrocyte differentiation, and lung macrophage cell function (Fig. 7E). The enrichment profile demonstrated strong associations not only with osteoarthritis pathogenesis and chondrocyte biology but also with extensive immune network activation. Consequently, we propose that the distinct molecular characteristics of the C1 and C2 subtypes are intrinsically linked to alterations in the immune microenvironment, suggesting potential variations in immune infiltration patterns across OA subtypes.

Western blot analysis was performed to assess FKBP5 levels in chondrocytes from normal and OA samples. As demonstrated in Fig. 10A, FKBP5 expression was significantly reduced under osteoarthritic conditions. Subsequently, we transfected chondrocytes with pFKBP5 and empty plasmid as a way to construct a chondrocyte model of FKBP5 overexpression, and the empty transfected group and the untreated group were considered as controls. We verified the successful construction of the FKBP5 overexpressing chondrocyte model by western blot, as shown in 10B. Chondrocytes were stimulated with IL-1β to simulate an osteoarthritic condition. The experimental groups comprised untreated controls, IL-1β-treated cells, IL-1β-treated cells transfected with a control plasmid (OE nc), and IL-1β-treated cells overexpressing FKBP5 (OE). Reversing Effect of FKBP5 on Cartilage Destruction in OA Environment

A total of 30 wild-type mice were allocated into three experimental conditions: sham surgery plus PBS injection, medial meniscus resection (DMM) plus PBS injection, and DMM combined with FKBP5 overexpression plasmid injection; these were labeled as Sham + PBS, DMM + PBS, and DMM+FKBP5 groups, respectively. Following seven weeks of treatment, knee-joint soft tissues were harvested for immunohistochemical and histological staining. Quantitative immunohistochemistry for COL2A is presented in Fig. 12A, with the corresponding bar graph shown in Fig. 12E. Representative MMP13 immunohistochemistry images appear in Fig. 12B, and its quantification (Fig. 12F) displayed an inverse pattern compared with COL2A. Cartilage morphology was assessed via HE staining (Fig. 12C) and SOFG staining (Fig. 12D). Discussion

Osteoarthritis (OA), a prevalent and debilitating chronic joint disorder, has articular cartilage degradation and osteophyte formation as its core pathological features, primarily affecting knee, hip, and hand joints [28]. Global Burden of Disease data indicate its prevalence reaches approximately 50% in individuals aged over 60 and 80% in those over 75. Among these, 20%-30% experience clinical manifestations like pain, morning stiffness, and impaired function, with disability rates as high as 53%—a figure rising with population aging [4, 5]. Current clinical management focuses on symptom alleviation and functional improvement, employing a step-up approach: foundational interventions encompass health education, exercise therapy, and weight control; pharmacotherapy primarily utilizes nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular injections (e.g., hyaluronic acid); while end-stage disease necessitates joint replacement surgery [28–30].However, the existing means can only delay symptoms but not reverse cartilage degeneration, and the lack of targeted drugs that effectively slow disease progression highlights the urgency of research on pathomechanisms and the development of novel therapies [31, 32]. The heterogeneity of OA and the interaction of complex pathological networks, such as inflammation, immunity, and mechanical stresses, remain a central challenge in today’s research [33, 34]. A pathological imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the antioxidant defense system, triggered by harmful stimuli, defines the state of oxidative stress [35]. In physiological state, ROS are mainly produced by mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) and NADPH oxidase (NOX) pathways, which are involved in immune defense and cell signaling [36, 37]. However, excess ROS attack proteins, lipids, and DNA, leading to cellular dysfunction or death [13, 14]. A self-perpetuating vicious cycle involving oxidative stress and inflammation is established in inflammatory pathologies [38, 39]. Accumulating evidence substantiates that oxidative stress serves as a pivotal driver of cartilage degradation in osteoarthritis [40]. Normal chondrocytes, as hypoxia-adapted cells, have low ROS levels. However, in the pathological environment of OA, factors such as inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β) and mechanical stress activate NADPH oxidase and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to excessive ROS accumulation [41]. Excess ROS exacerbate joint damage through multiple pathways [42, 43]. Nevertheless, a consensus regarding the precise intervention targets and mechanistic pathways through which oxidative stress influences OA progression remains elusive. Furthermore, the stratification of OA into distinct subtypes based on oxidative stress profiles has not been established, thereby limiting the development of targeted therapeutic strategies [44, 45]. Consequently, a deeper investigation into the mechanisms and targets through which oxidative stress modulates OA is warranted.

This study acquired five datasets with 76 OA and 42 normal samples from GEO, which were randomly split into a training set and a validation set for batch effect processing. Genes related to oxidative stress (relevance score > 8) were acquired from Genecards. The 78 DEORGs were identified by intersecting DEGs with these oxidative stress-associated genes, and significant positive or negative correlations between DEORGs were found, suggesting a co-expression pattern of these genes. We screened the top 60% of high-value genes by machine learning RF method and PPI analysis of BottleNeck, Closeness, MNC, and EcCentricity values, respectively, and subsequently took the intersection to obtain 6 key genes JUN, MMP13, PPARGC1A, PTGS2, ALB, and FKBP5 affecting OA diagnosis. The six key genes demonstrated individual diagnostic capacities for OA, with AUC values of 0.588 (JUN), 0.708 (MMP13), 0.737 (PPARGC1A), 0.627 (PTGS2), 0.695 (ALB), and 0.854 (FKBP5), and their expression levels were significantly intercorrelated. We then built a diagnostic model via multifactorial logistic regression using MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5, all of which had AUCs exceeding 0.7. This model showed outstanding performance in the training set, achieving an AUC of 0.858. A nomogram constructed from these genes also exhibited strong predictive accuracy, as verified by its calibration and decision curves. Consistent with the model’s formula (Riskscore = [MMP13 expression * 0.3558228] - [PPARGC1A expression * 0.5172689] - [FKBP5 expression * 0.9778753]), PPARGC1A and FKBP5 were significantly downregulated in OA training samples, while MMP13 was upregulated. Unsupervised clustering analysis based on these three genes revealed an optimal two-subtype classification (C1 and C2) of OA samples at K = 2, offering new potential for patient stratification and personalized treatment. The robustness of both the diagnostic model and the subtypes was confirmed in the validation set, where the diagnostic efficacy of the key genes, the composite model, and the subtype classification remained highly stable, affirming the substantial clinical relevance of our findings.

To investigate the distinct pathological mechanisms across OA subtypes and enable patient stratification, we identified subtype-specific DEGs and conducted functional enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG, GSEA). GO analysis revealed subtype variations in molecular functions, cellular components, and biological processes related to cartilage integrity, such as collagen fibril organization, cartilage development, fibrillar collagen trimer, banded collagen fibril, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, collagen binding, glycosaminoglycan binding, and extracellular matrix structural constituent. KEGG analysis identified immunity-associated pathways including Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection and IL-17 signaling pathway, along with established OA progression pathways like Toll-like receptor signaling and AGE-RAGE signaling pathway [46, 47]. GSEA enrichment yielded immune related pathways, osteoarthritis, oxidative stress related pathways such as chondrocyte differentiation, eosinophil chemotaxis, T cell differentiation, T cell activation, leukocyte mediated immunity, HP osteoarthritis, primary immunodeficiency, interleukin 17 signaling, lung macrophage cell, oxidative stress response. These outcomes further implicated numerous immune-associated pathways, leading us to postulate distinct immune microenvironments in the C1 and C2 subtypes. Subsequent immune infiltration analysis validated this hypothesis, revealing a substantially elevated immune state in the C2 subtype relative to C1. This heightened activity was particularly notable across various immune compartments: T cell co-inhibition, HLA, inflammation promoting, Cytolytic activity, CCR, TIL, Th2 cells, Th1 cells, NK cells, Neutrophils, B cells, and aDCs. Our results align with recent investigations. Lu et al. [48], through co-culture models, reported that OA synovial fibroblasts enhance B cell activation and differentiation. Kang et al. [49] proposed that modulating T cell receptor signaling to reduce effector CD4 + T cell (including Th17) functionality could represent a viable arthritis treatment. Song et al. [50], via single-cell analysis, established a strong link between OA progression and immune cell infiltration levels, specifically involving T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes, and macrophages. Furthermore, we observed marked variations in immune checkpoint expression—CD44, CD86, TNFSF4, TNFRSF9, CD200, CD40LG, TNFRSF4, TNFRSF25, CTLA4, TNFSF18, BTNL2—across OA oxidative stress subtypes. This consistency extends to the work of Lv et al. [51], who engineered a chondrocyte-targeted DNA origami system (OCS) for siRNA delivery to osteoarthritic cartilage. This nanostructure incorporates anti-CD44 aptamers for selective targeting and can be loaded with Stat3-targeting siRNAs. Jiang et al. [52] found that silencing LOXL1 in chondrocytes decreased CD86, inflammatory cytokine expression, attenuated MMP13 and chondrocyte apoptosis in co-cultured macrophages.

Experimental validation by Yu et al. [53] established TNFRSF9 as an activator of the NF-κB pathway, linking it to inflammatory processes within chondrocytes. These collective findings underscore the strong involvement of the aforementioned immune checkpoints in OA pathogenesis. Our data further demonstrate that the key biomarker FKBP5 exhibited significant positive correlations with numerous immune signatures. Notably, among the three pivotal genes investigated, FKBP5 was uniquely upregulated in the C2 subtype.

Subsequently, we performed enrichment analysis of FKBP5 alone to explore its potential biological pathways affecting OA progression. GO enrichment revealed that FKBP5 was mainly associated with chondrogenesis, inflammatory response, and immune-related pathways, including glycosaminoglycan binding, extracellular matrix binding, banded collagen fibril, complex of collagen trimers, collagen containing extracellular matrix, fibrillar collagen trimer, regulation of stress activated MAPK cascade, positive regulation of leukocyte migration, positive regulation of angiogenesis, chondrocyte differentiation, acute inflammatory response, regulation of granulocyte chemotaxis, chronic inflammatory response, and macrophage activation involved in immune response. The KEGG enrichment results also included some immune-related pathways, such as Chemokine signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway, as well as recognized OA pathways, such as MAPK signaling pathway and NF-kappa B signaling pathway. GSEA enrichment revealed that FKBP5 was mainly associated with cartilage formation and inflammatory response, including AAA, and FKBP5 was mainly associated with chondrocyte differentiation, macrophage activation, muscle cell proliferation, response to oxidative stress, HALLMARK apoptosis, T cell receptor signaling pathway, proteoglycans, cellular senescence, collagen formation, and diseases of immune system. The above results suggest that FKBP5 may influence OA progression by affecting immune infiltration, apoptosis or senescence, and cartilage structure.

FKBP5 (FK506 binding protein 51) is located on human chromosome 6p21 and encodes an important co-chaperone protein belonging to the highly conserved immunophilin family. This protein exerts multiple regulatory functions within cells, with its core role centered on modulating glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling. FKBP5 expression is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. The gene promoter region contains multiple glucocorticoid response elements (GREs). Under stimulation by stress or inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), cortisol significantly upregulates FKBP5 transcription through GRE binding, forming a “stress amplification loop.” Zhang et al. reported that FKBP5 expression is downregulated in OA and may participate in the pathological mechanisms of OA [54]. Li et al. also identified FKBP5 as a valuable methylation-based biomarker for OA [55]. Shen et al. indicated that elevated FKBP5 promotes the proliferation of osteogenic marker genes while reducing BMSC apoptosis [56]. It should be noted that an earlier study reported significantly higher FKBP5 mRNA expression in bone marrow CD34 + cells from RA patients compared to OA patients, suggesting that this elevation may represent an intrinsic abnormality of RA bone marrow CD34 + cells rather than being secondary to systemic inflammation [57]. However, that study did not directly compare FKBP5 expression levels between OA and healthy control tissues. In recent years, multiple independent studies have consistently shown that FKBP5 is downregulated in OA joint tissues [54, 58], which aligns with our findings. A Mendelian randomization study further revealed that an elevated DNMBP/FKBP5 ratio is significantly associated with an increased risk of hip OA, whereas the FKBP5/MITD1 ratio is associated with a reduced risk of hip OA, suggesting that the direction of FKBP5 dysregulation is consistent with a potential loss of its protective role [58]. Although the mTOR signaling pathway has been shown to be activated in OA, FKBP5 and the classic mTOR-binding protein FKBP12 (encoded by FKBP1A) are functionally distinct proteins [59–61]. As a co-chaperone, FKBP5 primarily participates in GR signaling and stress responses; therefore, the established role of mTOR in OA does not directly predict the expression pattern of FKBP5, and the functional relationship between these two proteins requires further investigation [60, 61].

Our study is the first to identify the independent impact of FKBP5 on OA, incorporating it into the construction of an OA diagnostic model and subtype classification, and exploring the connections among FKBP5, the immune microenvironment, and OA. Basic experimental results showed that under inflammatory conditions, chondrocyte senescence and apoptosis were enhanced, while cell proliferation capacity, cell viability, mitochondrial function, and chondrocyte injury repair ability were suppressed. FKBP5 overexpression partially reversed these trends, which is highly consistent with our earlier enrichment analysis results for FKBP5. These findings confirm that FKBP5 overexpression can attenuate chondrocyte injury and death in the OA environment, thereby inhibiting OA progression.

In summary, we constructed a robust OA diagnostic model based on the oxidative stress-related genes MMP13, PPARGC1A, and FKBP5, and established OA subtype classifications to facilitate stratified management of OA patients. From the perspective of immune infiltration, we also elucidated the mechanism by which FKBP5 influences OA progression. By integrating bioinformatics analysis with basic experimental validation, we demonstrated the strong translational potential of FKBP5 as an interventional target for OA. It should be acknowledged that due to inherent limitations of publicly available OA microarray datasets, the sample size included in this study is relatively small, introducing a certain degree of overfitting risk. Although we employed multiple strategies to mitigate this risk, including screening key genes with AUC greater than 0.7, using multivariate logistic regression to construct the diagnostic model, and strictly partitioning the test set, the generalizability of our findings still requires further validation. In addition, due to sample size constraints, the 60% cutoff threshold used in our multi‑marker crossover strategy was empirically determined based on the current data conditions to balance inclusiveness and stringency, and its feasibility was indirectly supported by subsequent ROC validation. We did not conduct systematic sensitivity analyses to assess the stability of gene selection under different percentile thresholds. Future large‑scale, multicenter human OA study cohorts are necessary to validate the accuracy of this diagnostic model and the therapeutic value of FKBP5, and to systematically evaluate the stability of gene selection through sensitivity analyses across different cutoff thresholds.

Conclusion

Our study, based on oxidative stress-related genes, identified distinct OA subtypes and further revealed the positive regulatory role of FKBP5 on chondrocytes and cartilage tissue in the OA environment. This offers new perspectives for personalized treatment of osteoarthritis.
