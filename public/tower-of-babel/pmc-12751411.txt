Exploring the integration of AI and national quality courses in China: a study on teaching practices in nursing smart education
Authors: Zhao Jing, Zang Mouhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe 2024 Global MOOC and Online Education Conference, held in the UK with the theme "Reconstructing Future Higher Education in the Intelligent Age," first proposed that global higher education has entered the "Year of Smart Education." With the global promotion of education digitalization, the trend of "AI+higher education" is becoming increasingly prominent. As a key component of China's education reform, the deep integration of national high-quality curricula and artificial intelligence is crucial for developing digital learning models. This study utilizes generative AI to develop personalized curriculum enhancement models (such as AI teaching/learning partners), using the Chinese national high-quality course"Nursing Ethics" as an example, to investigate smart education models through teaching reform. Its objectives include integrating AI into Nursing Ethics, constructing an intelligent curriculum system, evaluating application effectiveness, and summarizing reform achievements.MethodFour complete classes were randomly assigned to either the control group or the experimental group. Introduction

The 2024 World Conference on MOOC and Online Education, held in the United Kingdom on December 12, 2024, under the theme “Reconstructing Future Higher Education in the Intelligent Era”, officially launched the global “Year of Intelligent Education” in higher education and released the World Higher Education Digital Development Report (2024) [1]. The conference proposed a new educational scenario of a “trinity of teachers, machines, and students”, highlighting that generative AI technologies are pioneering new models for teaching applications [2]. The last decade of progress in educational informatization has substantially improved the effectiveness, equity, and efficiency of education systems worldwide [3]. For instance, generative AI has exerted a positive impact on nursing education in the United States [4], while Germany has tested the application value of large language models in medical education and examinations [5]. Thailand has achieved notable success in STEAM education using digital virtual classrooms, surpassing outcomes from traditional teaching methods [6]. Japan has experimented with online courses in traditional medical education [7] and introduced Virtual Reality (VR) technology into nursing practice curricula [8]. Actively embracing the MOOC construction wave, Chinese universities have launched over 97,000 MOOCs, attracting more than 1.39 billion learners, ranking first globally in both metrics. A UNESCO report outlines the current implementation status of AI curricula [9], which have been adopted and recognized in more than ten countries, including Armenia, Austria, Belgium, China, and India. In March 2024, the Department of Higher Education of the Chinese Ministry of Education emphasized that AI technology poses new challenges for the transformation of educational models and advocated for developing a “Smart+” higher education ecosystem [10].

However, despite the promising prospects, significant gaps remain in current research on nursing ethics education. A review of high-quality studies from the past five years (2020–2025) shows that while scholars have actively investigated innovative approaches like narrative pedagogy, flipped classrooms, online learning, ethics seminars, and embedded case scenarios. These approaches confirm their short-term effectiveness in enhancing moral sensitivity and reasoning skills, however, most of the investigations remain confined to traditional technological approaches. These studies indicate that the long-term stability of teaching outcomes and the efficiency of translating knowledge comprehension into clinical decision-making capabilities remain critical challenges to be addressed [11–21]. This situation highlights the necessity of incorporating intelligent technological tools to deepen and consolidate educational outcomes. The application of artificial intelligence in medical and nursing education is attracting growing interest. The nursing profession, both domestically and internationally, has begun exploring the integration of digitalization, knowledge informatization, and intelligent nursing scenarios [22], charting a course for intelligent nursing practice [23, 24]. Currently, AI in medical education primarily supports learning assistance, student assessment, and course optimization [25]. Specific educational applications include adaptive assessment technologies, the integration of educational and social robotics systems, and intelligent tutoring systems that provide personalized guidance and feedback based on students’ learning patterns and knowledge levels. For instance, existing AI tools have been utilized to facilitate personalized, self-paced learning within courses, uncover new insights for targeted interventions, strengthen decision-making processes and clinical reasoning skills in nursing students, provide AI-assisted diagnostics, improve academic writing, and apply these advancements in patient care [26–31]. Most of the studies mentioned above incorporate established AI tools into specific teaching units or for accomplishing isolated instructional tasks. A systematic description of the processes required for the deep integration of artificial intelligence across entire courses is notably absent.

A recently published systematic review on artificial intelligence in nursing education and practice [32] identifies the transformation of nursing education as a critical area. There is an urgent need to update curriculum systems by integrating AI-driven teaching tools to cultivate technological proficiency and ethical decision-making capabilities among nursing students and practitioners. At the same time, core challenges such as resistance to AI adoption, the absence of standardized education, and disparities in technology access must be confronted. Existing research methodologies present several limitations: on one hand, mainstream studies primarily rely on qualitative interviews, often feature small sample sizes, and include few randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The generally small overall sample sizes limit the generalizability and statistical power of the conclusions [33–37]. To address these limitations, this study proposes a blended educational approach that integrates AI with a national-level high-quality courses, implementing reforms in curriculum content, teaching methods, and course evaluation. Specifically, the curriculum content system is built upon resources from the National-Level High-Quality Course. Building upon this foundation, we collaborated with specialized companies to embed a vertically tailored AI model into the course, providing deeply integrated, personalized teaching assistance, learning support, and evaluation aid throughout the entire learning process. Key functionalities include a lesson preparation assistant, one-click test generation, an intelligent teaching companion, an intelligent learning companion, and automated grading. AI scoring is embedded into a tripartite assessment system comprising “theoretical examinations + case analysis + situational simulations,” with AI participating forming a closed data loop across all stages. Using a cluster randomized controlled trial design, this study explores and evaluates the application effect of artificial intelligence technology in nursing ethics education. It aims to investigate two core questions: the feasibility of integrating AI into the national-level high-quality course “Nursing Ethics” and its impact on student satisfaction; and the effect of this integrated model on students’ academic performance.

Methods

Research background and course selection

Nursing majors are purposefully selected as research subjects for three reasons: Nursing students frequently face complex ethical dilemmas during clinical internships, making them an ideal group for evaluating AI assisted ethical decisions; the National Health Commission has listed nursing as a key major for digital transformation, which can receive sufficient policy and resource support; the major’s standardized curriculum and uniform assessment system effectively control for confounding variations across different courses.

Nursing Ethics was selected for this study based on the following considerations: First, policy Alignment: As a core compulsory module specified in the National Standards for Teaching Quality of Nursing Programs (2024), the course’s objective of fostering “ethical decision-making competence” directly corresponds to the national requirement of “strengthening medical students’ professionalism and ethical reasoning,” making it irreplaceable from a policy perspective. Second, AI-Specific Relevance to Ethics Education: Ethical decision-making represents a high-order competency in nursing practice that uniquely integrates empirical evidence (e.g., clinical guidelines) with value-based judgments (e.g., patient autonomy). AI, with its capabilities for multimodal data integration and causal reasoning, is particularly well-suited to simulate this dual-track “scientific-humanistic” thinking process, which is a dimension difficult to concretize using traditional teaching methods. Third, Structural Compatibility: The course’s fixed 32-class-hour structure aligns with the modular teaching reform pilot criteria outlined in the Ministry of Education’s Implementation Guidelines for National High-Quality Course Development. Its original 4-class-hour unit on “Ethical Dilemma Case Analysis” can be seamlessly replaced with an AI-based ethical decision simulation module, without altering the total course hours or assessment weighting. Finally, validity Control: The course has not been part of any university-level teaching reform initiatives in the past three years, and its standardized case database has been consistent for five years. This stability effectively minimizes confounding effects from prior interventions when evaluating the influence of the AI-based teaching approach.

Sampling and grouping allocation

All 185 nursing students enrolled in 2023 were stratified by gender and admission scores to ensure baseline balance. Four intact classes were then assigned to either the control group or the experimental group using a cluster randomized controlled trial design. The experimental group introduced AI assisted blended learning mode, while the control group used traditional teaching mode. The study conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis of two groups of students by evaluating and comparing their satisfaction with course quality, teacher performance, and AI teaching assistance, as well as their total scores and excellence rates. The specific process is as follows: The target population for the sampling frame consisted of all 4 administrative classes of the 2023 nursing major cohort, totaling 185 students. Class information: Classes 2301, 2302, 2303, and 2304, with numbers of 48, 48, 44, and 45 respectively. Baseline stratification (to ensure comparability), with 2 × 2 stratification based on “gender” and “admission scores (top 50%/bottom 50%)” to ensure similar composition within each class. After stratification, there was no statistically significant difference in gender ratio and performance distribution among the classes (Table 1). The four classes were randomly grouped using SPSS 27.0, numbered alphabetically (2301 = 1, 2302 = 2, 2303 = 3, 2304 = 4). A one-time random sampling command generated 4 random numbers. Sorting these numbers from smallest to largest assigned the first two classes to the “experimental group” (2301, 2302) and the last two classes to the “control group” (2303, 2304), achieving a 1:1 whole class allocation. Allocation concealment and implementation, random results sealed in opaque envelopes, kept by research secretaries who have not participated in teaching. One day before the course started, the envelope was delivered to the course manager for on-site opening and group announcement, ensuring neither teachers nor students could predict group assignment before randomization. To ensure intervention fidelity and prevent cross-contamination between the experimental and control groups, a series of technical and administrative measures were implemented. Technically, AI learning platform accounts were bound to students’ personal student IDs and mobile numbers, employing single sign-on authentication while monitoring for anomalous login activity. Administratively, core AI-platform learning activities were scheduled within supervised sessions in designated computer labs to minimize unnecessary dissemination of account credentials. Furthermore, all participants were explicitly informed of their data confidentiality obligations and academic integrity requirements. Through these measures, the study successfully implemented a cluster randomized controlled trial, maintaining ecological validity of the educational environment while ensuring strict separation between the intervention and control groups.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received formal approval from the Ethics Committee of West Anhui Health Vocational College (Approval No: [2024 − 0128]), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The consent form detailed the study aims, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, assuring participants of the voluntary nature of their involvement and their right to withdraw unconditionally at any time without penalty. To ensure confidentiality, all personally identifiable information was removed after data collection, and each participant was assigned a unique anonymous code for analysis. Signed consent forms were stored securely in locked cabinets, physically separate from the anonymized research data. All electronic data were stored on a password-protected, encrypted server, and results are presented only in aggregate form to prevent participant identification.

Control of confounding variables

During the design phase, cluster randomization was applied to balance known baseline characteristics, including gender, age, and admission scores, between the two groups (Table 1). The same experienced teacher was assigned to teach both the experimental and control groups to prevent bias from instructor effects. During implementation, all courses were scheduled in classrooms with similar conditions and strictly followed a unified, detailed lesson plan. With the exception of the artificial intelligence module, the textbooks, courseware, and case materials used were identical for both groups. The final assessment was independently graded by a grader who is unaware of the grouping situation, ensuring blind grading.

Experimental cycle

The teaching experiment was conducted continuously for 16 weeks, covering weeks 1–16 of the 2023 autumn semester. The first week was used for baseline assessment, teaching intervention was implemented from the 2nd to the 15th week, and the post assessment and satisfaction survey were completed in the 16th week.

Teaching design and implementation

The AI-assisted blended teaching model adopted in this study fundamentally transcends a traditional Learning Management System (LMS) that merely provides static resources. By integrating the “DeepSeek R1 250528” generative AI model, it created a learning environment capable of dynamic content generation and intelligent real-time interaction. The specific implementation process and the unique contributions of generative AI are as follows:

Structuring and Enhancement of Course Resources:

Resource Integration and Upload: Resources from the National-level high-quality course (videos, exercise banks) and self-developed materials (textbooks, PPTs, academic papers) were first uploaded to the platform. This step, similar to a traditional LMS, aimed to build a comprehensive learning resource repository.b) Construction of Course Knowledge Graph: Unlike the simple file lists in traditional LMSs, the platform utilizes AI technology to semantically interlink these resources, thus automatically building a structured course knowledge graph. This enables students to navigate and connect related knowledge based on logical conceptual pathways rather than through simple folder hierarchies.

Instead, the teaching team designed a series of highly structured pedagogical prompt templates based on disciplinary characteristics (e.g., “Knowledge Point Q&A: Four Principles of Nursing Ethics”, “Generate an Ethical Dilemma Case in End-of-Life Care”, “Reconstruct the Following Case from a Nurse’s Perspective”). For example, on the topic of “informed consent,” the AI could instantly create complex scenarios involving different cultural backgrounds, patient ages, and disease severities for student analysis and debate, thereby overcoming the limitations of scarce and slow-to-update cases in traditional teaching. (ii) Socratic Dialogue and Immediate Feedback: Students could ask the AI questions on any topic at any time. Instead of returning links to static answers, the AI acted as a “tireless tutor,” guiding students toward deeper thinking through techniques like follow-up questions, counter-questions, and requests for clarification (Socratic dialogue), while providing immediate, personalized explanations and feedback. This capacity for one-on-one, in-depth dialogue is unmatched by traditional forums or quiz systems. (iii) Personalized Learning Path Recommendations: Based on analysis of student learning behavior data and interactive Q&A, the AI could dynamically generate personalized learning path suggestions, e.g., “Your performance on exercises related to “Justice in Healthcare Resource Allocation” is relatively weak. Evaluation and Optimization: The platform continuously collects data generated during these generative interactions (e.g., types of questions asked, engagement in case discussions, depth of dialogues with the AI), going beyond mere quiz completion records. By analyzing these rich learning behavior characteristics, instructors could more precisely refine both instructional design and AI prompt strategies, forming a continuous enhancement feedback loop among “teaching, AI, and students.” The “AI assistance” in this model is far more than digital resource access. Its essence lies in leveraging generative AI to inject “generative” and “intelligent” core capabilities into teaching activities, achieving a paradigm shift from static content delivery toward dynamic, interactive, and personalized knowledge construction.

In contrast, the traditional teaching model operated without AI assistance. Before class, teachers typically distributed pre-class materials in either print or digital format for students to review independently. This approach did not allow instructors to monitor student preparation or comprehension in real time. During class sessions, instruction primarily consisted of lectures and blackboard presentations, with limited integration of case analyses and discussion activities. Teachers could not gather immediate student feedback to adjust their instructional pacing. After class, case analysis was manually graded by teachers, with a long feedback cycle and difficulty in providing personalized review and extension resources on the basis of individual differences. The traditional teaching model has obvious shortcomings in terms of precise assessment, personalized teaching, and interactivity, making it difficult to effectively enhance students’ deep learning abilities and practical application levels.

This study adopted an integrated three-phase teaching model of pre class guidance, classroom application, and post class expansion, aiming to enhance students’ understanding and practical ability of nursing ethics knowledge. Teaching with the theme of “Value of Life”: Personalized preview materials were pushed through an intelligent learning platform before class, a pre-class task list was designed to collect feedback, and a preview test was generated on the AI platform to evaluate the effectiveness of self-directed learning; in the classroom, intelligent scoring systems and data analysis techniques were used to accurately evaluate learning outcomes. Through case analysis, knowledge point breakdowns, and mind mapping exercises, students develop deeper understanding of core concepts regarding life’s value; group debates and peer evaluations activities enhance comprehensive grasp of both intrinsic and extrinsic values of life; after class, students completed extension tasks on contemporary issues like gene editing after class, where students collected information through intelligent learning partners to write study reports and receive intelligent ratings. Teachers pushed personalized review resources based on the learning reports generated by the learning platform to help students fill knowledge gaps and consolidate core content. The combination of intelligent tools and interactive learning activities effectively promoted the improvement of students’ understanding of the theory of life value and practical application ability (Table 2). Throughout the semester, students used the system 1,244 times collectively, averaging 12.95 uses per person, with total usage time reaching 512 h and an average of 5 h 20 min per student.

Teaching effect evaluation

This course employed a multi-faceted assessment approach integrating theoretical examination, case analysis, and situational simulation to comprehensively evaluate students’ mastery of ethical knowledge, problem-solving capabilities, and professional competence. The program aimed to cultivate nursing professionals with heightened ethical sensitivity and responsibility, enabling them to deliver high-quality patient care. To ensure scoring objectivity, consistency, and reproducibility, we developed structured grading rubrics for each assessment component and conducted unified training and calibration sessions for all evaluating instructors prior to scoring. The evaluation process was implemented sequentially, with a total possible score of 100 points.

Theoretical examination (50% of total Score)

The theoretical examination assessed students’ grasp of fundamental nursing ethics knowledge, including theories, principles, legal regulations, and professional standards. Question types comprised multiple-choice, true/false, short-answer, and essay questions.

Objective Questions (Multiple-choice/True-false): These were scored against standardized answer keys, with points awarded for correct responses.

Short-answer Questions: Grading emphasized accuracy and completeness. For example, the rubric for “List the elements of informed consent” included:

Case analysis (30% of total Score)

Students analyzed one to two given nursing ethics cases to identify ethical issues and propose solutions. This component evaluated students’ ability to apply ethical principles to practical problems using the following rubric:

Ethical Issue Identification (25%): Accuracy and comprehensiveness in recognizing core ethical dilemmas (e.g., informed consent, privacy, resource allocation).

Ethical Principle Application (35%): Correct and appropriate use of relevant ethical principles (e.g., autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice).

Solution and Justification (30%): Feasibility and rationality of proposed solutions, supported by clear ethical reasoning.

Structure and Expression (10%): Logical organization, coherent analysis, and precise language.

Situational simulation (20% of total Score)

Role-playing simulations of clinical ethical conflicts assessed students’ moral decision-making, communication, and adaptability in realistic scenarios. Trained observer instructors scored performances in real-time using this rubric:

Moral Sensitivity and Decision-making (40%): Prompt identification of ethical issues and decisions consistent with ethical standards.

Communication and Empathy (30%): Effective, empathetic communication with “patients” or “family members,” with clear explanation of situations.

Professionalism and Adaptability (20%): Maintained professional demeanor under pressure and responded flexibly to scenario developments.

Team Collaboration (where applicable) (10%): Effective cooperation with other healthcare providers in the simulated scenario.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire was designed to assess students’ overall evaluation of the course and the instructor. It comprised three core items: overall satisfaction with the course, the accessibility of timely answers and support when students encountered problems during instruction, and the overall evaluation of the instructor’s teaching. To ensure the reliability and scientific validity of subsequent empirical research, reliability and validity analyses were conducted on the questionnaire, with results detailed below.

Reliability was assessed through internal consistency analysis. As shown in Table 3, the questionnaire yielded a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.873, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.8 and indicating high internal consistency and satisfactory reliability. Corrected Item-Total Correlation (CITC) was further analyzed to evaluate the relationship between each item and the total score. The CITC values were 0.805 for Q1, 0.680 for Q2, and 0.817 for Q3. All values surpassed the acceptable threshold of 0.5, confirming each item’s effective contribution to the latent construct measured by the scale.

Result

In accordance with the research design, a statistical analysis was conducted to examine score differences between the experimental group (n = 96) and the control group. First, an independent samples t-test was performed to compare total scores. Results indicated a violation of the homogeneity of variance assumption (Levene’s F = 18.38, p < 0.001); therefore, Welch’s corrected t-test was applied. The analysis revealed a statistically significant difference between the experimental group (M = 93.99, SD = 2.81) and the control group (M = 91.28, SD = 5.68), t(126.47) = 4.07, p < 0.001. The mean difference was 2.71, with a 95% confidence interval [1.40, 4.03]. Cohen’s d = 0.61 indicated a moderate effect size, with the experimental group scoring significantly higher than the control group (Table 6).

This unadjusted difference provides descriptive evidence for the intervention’s potential effectiveness.

Second, a detailed examination of interaction effects pointed to context-specific effects. Notably, the “group × gender” interaction approached marginal significance (p = 0.056). This finding strongly implies the intervention’s effect may vary by gender, as pooled analyses could obscure subgroup-specific effects (e.g., females or males). This provides a precise direction for future research.

Finally, the study establishes a methodological foundation for subsequent investigations. Key covariates (entrance exam scores, gender, age) showed weak direct associations with post-test scores in this sample, aiding future studies in sample size estimation and variable selection. The experimental intervention protocol was feasible and acceptable, offering an operational blueprint for replication in larger or targeted populations.

Discussion

Advantages

This study aimed to address two core questions: first, whether artificial intelligence could be effectively incorporated into the national-level high-quality course “Nursing Ethics” and how this integration would affect student satisfaction; and second, how this combined approach would influence students’ academic achievement. The findings demonstrated that the blended learning model based on “AI + National High-Quality Course” yielded positive outcomes in both aspects. The experimental group significantly outperformed the control group, which received traditional instruction, in both examination scores and multiple satisfaction indicators. The following discussion elaborates on these findings by examining them in relation to the two research objectives.

Feasibility of AI integration and its role in driving satisfaction

This study confirmed the high feasibility of integrating AI technology with national-level high-quality course resources for teaching “Nursing Ethics,” and indicated that this model was widely welcomed by students. The nearly 45-percentage-point higher proportion of students in the experimental group reporting they were “very satisfied” with the overall course was not coincidental. We proposed that the central explanation resided in how AI tools directly tackled persistent difficulties in conventional ethics education.

Immediate Instructional Support: Ethical decision-making in nursing is often complex and uncertain, and students are prone to confusion during self-directed learning after class. In this study, the high satisfaction rate of 86.46% with the “instructional support services” could be directly attributed to the AI’s 24/7 intelligent learning companion functionality. Learners could access resource searches, knowledge point inquiries, and case analysis support whenever needed. This mechanism of “on-demand resolution” [38, 39] significantly enhanced learning confidence and experience, serving as the key driver for the nearly 66-percentage-point increase in satisfaction.

Personalized Learning Experience: AI allowed students to explore learning content according to their own pace and interests, which was consistent with previous research concluding that personalized instruction enhances engagement [40]. In nursing ethics education, students naturally varied in their comprehension levels and topic preferences concerning subjects like “informed consent” and “confidentiality.” The AI-enhanced blended model offered the adaptability needed to respect these individual differences.

Mechanisms underlying the impact on academic performance

The increase in the total scores and the rise in the excellence rate among students in the experimental group may be attributable to the effectiveness of AI-blended learning.This outcome might be associated with the following mechanisms:

Strengthened Practice Cycle for Ethical Decision-Making: A core objective of nursing ethics education is to cultivate students’ decision-making ability in real-world scenarios. Traditional teaching is often limited to in-class case discussions, whereas the AI platform in this study provided a low-risk, repeatable decision-making practice environment through simulated Q&A and writing assistance. Students could explore the same ethical dilemma from different perspectives with the AI, thereby deepening their understanding and application of ethical principles. This directly enhanced their ability to analyze cases and solve problems in examinations.

Deep Internalization of High-Quality Resources: The national high-quality course provided a systematic knowledge framework, while AI technology helped students repeatedly master key concepts and overcome learning obstacles as they arise. This model of “high-quality content + intelligent tutoring” established a virtuous cycle among “teacher-machine-student,” ensuring that students were not only exposed to top-tier resources but could also genuinely understand and absorb them.

Positioning and future directions in nursing ethics education

Our findings strongly aligned with calls to incorporate artificial intelligence into medical education systems [41–44]. Particularly significant is how this study supplies empirical backing for using AI specifically in nursing ethics, a subject area rooted in humanities within the curriculum. While previous research has predominantly focused on AI applications in clinical skills or basic medical sciences [45], this study demonstrated AI’s considerable potential in cultivating higher-order competencies such as critical thinking and ethical judgment in nurses. Students’ positive attitudes toward AI [46], as concretely reflected in this study, offer a new and compelling direction for integrating AI-related curricula, training, and practical learning into nursing education. Future research should further explore AI’s role in simulating more complex ethical conflicts and in formative assessment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, methodological constraints include the use of a quasi-experimental design rather than individual randomization and the absence of a pre-test using the same assessment tools as the post-test. These factors limit infer causality, that is to say, the current results should be regarded as indicating a promising association rather than conclusive evidence of causation. Second, regarding measurement instruments, the satisfaction questionnaire consisted of only three items. While it successfully measured fundamental dimensions of the educational experience, its condensed format and restricted content coverage, together with possible social desirability bias in self-reports, narrow the depth of possible conclusions. Finally, in terms of intervention fidelity, although administrative measures such as account binding were implemented, the natural educational setting precludes complete elimination of potential information contamination between groups. Additionally, this study is limited by the extrapolation of a sample of four classes from a single university and the impact of a small number of classes on statistical validity.In light of these limitations, the findings of this study should be considered preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of AI-assisted instruction. Additional verification through randomized controlled trials using thoroughly validated multi-faceted instruments and objective behavioral metrics is justified.

Conclusion

This quasi-experimental study was conducted within a nursing ethics course at a health vocational college. The results indicated that students participating in the “AI + National High-Quality Course” blended learning model demonstrated more positive outcomes in final examination scores and course satisfaction surveys compared to those receiving traditional instruction. These outcomes provide initial support for the possibility that merging artificial intelligence with high-quality curricular assets could represent both a workable and student-approved tactic for improving nursing ethics education. Nevertheless, these conclusions demand careful consideration. Because quasi-experimental designs inherently cannot manage all potential confounding variables, the present findings should be interpreted as revealing encouraging correlations rather than confirming definite causal connections, while simultaneously supplying preliminary support and guidance for future, more methodologically stringent research that includes pre-test arrangements.

The deep integration of AI into nursing education continues to face multiple challenges, including data privacy, faculty receptivity, and technical preparedness. Future work should prioritize conducting multi-center, large-sample randomized controlled trials to validate the effects observed in this study and explore the use of objective behavioral data to assess the long-term impact of AI on students’ ethical decision-making abilities. Simultaneously, creating uniform data ethics guidelines and delivering organized AI education training for faculty remain vital for securing consistent quality and effectiveness improvements in such technology-enhanced courses.
