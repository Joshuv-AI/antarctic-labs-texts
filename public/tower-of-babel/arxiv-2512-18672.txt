Two point functions and quantum fields in the anti-de Sitter universe Dedicated to the memory of Henri Epstein
Ugo Moschella

Abstract
We construct a manifestly covariant and coordinate-free plane-wave representation of scalar two-point functions in d d -dimensional anti-de Sitter spacetime. The construction is based on a new class of holomorphic plane waves defined globally on the universal covering of the AdS via chiral cones in the complex null cone. Imposing AdS invariance, locality, positive definiteness and a spectral condition, we obtain integral representations as superpositions over relative homology cycles, reproducing the standard maximally analytic solutions in terms of Legendre functions of the second kind. In Poincaré coordinates, the two-point functions diagonalize into a Källén-Lehmann superposition of ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -dimensional Minkowski correlators where the weight is a product of Bessel functions. This diagonalization clarifies the relation between Euclidean and Lorentzian AdS quantum field theory and allows Wick rotation of Euclidean Feynman diagrams to Lorentzian integrals supported on a single Poincaré patch while preserving full AdS covariance.

Introduction

The concept of a cyclic universe is deeply rooted in many cultures and long predates the notion of a linear, historical cosmos. In the Western tradition, Pythagoras, who is credited with introducing the term “cosmos”, was also the first to speak of a universe governed by recurring cycles. Porphyry, in his
Life of Pythagoras
, conveys the Pythagorean cosmological beliefs explicitly referring to the idea of cosmic cycles
[
1
]
:

“He taught that […] after certain specified periods, the same events occur again.”
In Eastern traditions, the idea of the universe as a cycle is often even more deeply ingrained. Hindu cosmology speaks of the Great Era, a vast cycle of creation, preservation, and destruction, while Buddhist thought highlights endless ages of arising and dissolution. Similarly, Chinese philosophy envisions the cosmos as continuously renewing itself through alternating cycles.
Mircea Eliade, in his book
Le mythe de l’éternel retour
observed that virtually all ancient societies conceived of time as an endlessly recurring cycle, and rejected the notion of a singular, linear historical timeline
[
2
]
:
“No event is unique, occurs once and for all… but it has occurred, occurs, and will occur perpetually; the same situations are reproduced that have already been produced in previous cycles and will be reproduced in subsequent cycles — ad infinitum.”

From a branch of philosophy and religion, cosmology evolved into a scientific discipline very recently, with the advent of Einstein’s general theory of relativity in 1915. In his initial cosmological model of 1917, Einstein assumed the universe to be static and unchanging, much like the skies of Ptolemy and Copernicus. To eliminate the notion of absolute space, he represented the universe as spherical, which led him to incorporate the cosmological constant—the key term to ensure staticity of a spherical dusty universe. Edwin Hubble’s 1929 observations later showed that the universe is actually expanding and consistent with Einstein’s original equations and therefore
if there is no static universe away with the cosmological constant

[
3
]
. Then, in 1998, two teams, independently studying distant supernovae, found that the expansion of the universe is accelerating. This surprising discovery revived interest in the cosmological constant and suggested that it may constitute the dominant component of the universe’s energy content.

If the cosmological constant is truly nonzero, could it be negative? A negative cosmological constant entails fundamentally different physics: the vacuum energy density is negative and the gravitational effect attractive, in contrast to the repulsion caused by a positive cosmological constant. Recent research
[
4
,
5
]
however indicates that a negative cosmological constant, when combined with other dark energy components, can be compatible with observational data, potentially allowing for a broader range of cosmic evolutions.

A universe devoid of matter and energy except for a negative cosmological constant has the anti-de Sitter (AdS) geometry. It features a constant negative curvature and exhibits closed timelike curves; while the light-cone ordering holds locally, it breaks down globally. In this respect, the AdS universe has a more evocative resemblance to the old cyclical cosmological worlds of Pythagoras rather than the modern relativistic expanding universes of Friedmann and Lemaître.

While the cyclical vision of time provided ancient peoples with a sense of continuity and meaning beyond the relentless progression of historical events, it is perceived as a scandal by physicists who are deeply committed to the causality principle and find such cyclicality at odds with the very idea of physical law:

“many physicists are unhappy with the closed timelike curves and seek to assuage their feelings of guilt by claiming to pass to the universal covering spacetime. In this way they feel that they have exorcised the demon of “acausality”. However, therapeutic uttering these words may be, nothing is actually
gained in this way.”
(quoted from: G. Gibbons
[
6
]
).

The presence of closed timelike curves in AdS spacetime constitutes a genuine conceptual challenge in physical modeling.
Calling it a scandal is perhaps an overstatement but, nevertheless, the word highlights a real and significant tension with causality.
The challenge remains unresolved by passing to the universal covering spacetime: the geodesics originating from one point converge at the antipodal point after a half-period of
“six great months”
and again at the starting point after a full
”great era.”
The universal cover removes the identification of points along the closed timelike curves but the periodic focusing of geodesics persists, providing no real gain in resolving the issues related to cyclic time in AdS (see Figure
2
).

This conceptual challenge explains why even apparently simple questions—such as finding an explicitly AdS-covariant expression for the correlation functions of free quantum fields—have remained unresolved to date. In the standard approach to AdS quantum field theory, which mainly relies on imposing boundary conditions at spacelike infinity for modes decomposed in specific coordinate systems
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
, the problem seems intractable. This method tends to obscure the global analyticity properties of both the AdS manifold and the correlation functions
[
11
]
. Neglecting these properties implies that the difficulties introduced by closed timelike curves could only be overcome with substantially greater effort.

This is one of the issues we tackle in the present work. We develop a new representation for the two-point functions of scalar quantum fields in a
d
-dimensional anti-de Sitter universe, based on a novel family of holomorphic plane waves that we introduce here and previously announced in
[
10
]
. These plane waves are almost always quasi-periodic functions on the AdS manifold and naturally reside on its covering space. The sole exception arises when the mass parameter takes integer values; in such cases, the plane waves become genuinely periodic.

Here is, deferring all detailed explanations, our formula for the two-point function of a massive scalar field:

W^{(d)}_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2})=c_{d}(\lambda)\int_{\gamma(z_{1})}(z_{1}\cdot\zeta)^{\lambda}(z_{2}\cdot\zeta)^{-\lambda-d+1}d\mu_{\gamma}(\zeta).

(1.1)

This integral representation is a superposition of plane waves; it does not separate space and time variables, nor does it depend on any particular coordinate system on the AdS manifold or any specific class of special functions. It is explicitly invariant under the anti-de Sitter group and clearly reveals the primitive analyticity domain of the two-point function
[
11
]
. Correlation functions for tensors and spinors can be straightforwardly derived by applying suitable differential operators to the right-hand side of this expression.

The significance of the new representation (
1.1
) is evident: it provides the closest possible analogue, within the AdS framework, to the plane-wave Fourier representation of the two-point function for a massive scalar field in (complex) Minkowski space
M_{d}^{(c)}
with positive energy spectrum
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle{W}^{{M}_{d}}_{m}({{\rm z}}_{1},{{\rm z}}_{2})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int e^{-i{\rm p}\cdot{\rm z}_{1}}e^{i{\rm p}\cdot{\rm z}_{2}}\theta({\rm p}^{0})\delta({\rm p}^{2}-m^{2})d{\rm p}.

(1.2)

Manifestly covariant momentum space representations of quantum fields provide the physically intuitive ingredients of Feynman diagrammatics
[
13
]
and a great deal of the predictive power of quantum field theory depends on them. Furthermore, many crucial mathematical developments such as the PCT and the Spin-Statistics theorems
[
12
]
and the Euclidean formulation of quantum field theory
[
14

are based on the plane-wave Fourier representation of the correlation functions and the positivity of the energy spectrum. The importance of the Fourier representation (
1.2
) and of the corresponding representation of the Feynman propagator cannot be overestimated and we expect that (
1.1
) to occupy the same position in AdS QFT.

The second major topic addressed in this work pertains to the horospherical, or Poincaré, coordinate system, which covers exactly one half of the AdS manifold. We analyze the Fourier transform of the plane waves w.r.t. the Poincaré coordinates and show how this transform diagonalizes the two-point function (
1.1
). We present the final, remarkably elegant result here deferring detailed explanations to Sections
13
and
14

\displaystyle W^{(d)}_{\lambda}(z_{1}({\rm z}_{1},u),z_{2}({\rm z}_{2},u^{\prime})=

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(uu^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{2(2\pi)^{d-2}}\int e^{-i{\rm k}({\rm z}_{1}-{\rm z}_{2})}\theta({\rm k}^{0})\theta({\rm k}^{2})J_{\frac{d-1}{2}+\lambda}\left(u\sqrt{{\rm k}^{2}}\right)J_{\frac{d-1}{2}+\lambda}\left(u^{\prime}\sqrt{{\rm k}^{2}}\right)d{\rm k}

(1.4)

The crucial role played by the global analyticity properties of the plane waves, which are inherited from our foundational axioms, will appear clearly in this calculation.

Moreover, this analysis yields an unexpected insight: Euclidean Feynman diagrams constructed on Lobachevski space can be mapped, via Wick rotation, onto diagrams entirely supported in the Poincaré patch. Multiple examples are given that substantiate the conjecture that any Feynman or Witten diagram computed by integration solely over a Poincaré patch of the real AdS manifold remains AdS covariant, even though the patch itself is not invariant.

The structure of the paper is as follows: in Section
2
, we describe the geometric and group-theoretical foundations, detailing the real AdS spacetime as an embedded manifold, its complexification, and the universal covering of the real manifold.

In Section
3
we introduce the null cone in the complex ambient space and explore its connection to geodesic motion in the AdS universe following the approach developed in
[
15
]
for the geometrically simpler de Sitter case.

In Section
4
we describe the chiral tuboids originally introduced in
[
11
]
: these are complex analytic domains that play a pivotal role in the study of quantum fields on AdS spacetime. Within our framework, analyticity in these tuboids replaces the conventional approach, which relies on imposing boundary conditions at spacelike infinity on individual modes.

Section
5
investigates the analytic structure of two-point functions, which must meet the fundamental requirements of AdS covariance, locality, and positive definiteness—essential principles for any quantum field theory.
However these general properties alone do not uniquely determine the solutions to the field equations of a given model; additional physical input is necessary to distinguish physically meaningful solutions from the vast set of mathematical possibilities. Here, as in Minkowski space, this role is played by a spectral condition, providing both a physical and a mathematical criterion for quantization. For two-point functions the spectral condition is equivalent to the maximal analyticity of the correlation functions in a certain complexification of the AdS manifold.

In Section
6
, we construct plane wave solutions to the AdS-Klein-Gordon equation by exploiting the relationship between the pseudo-Laplacian in the ambient space and the AdS d’Alembertian. Plane waves are labeled by null vectors in the ambient space and by a mass parameter; in general they are well-defined only locally; the introduction of chiral cones in Section
7
provides a means to extend them into objects globally well-defined on the universal covering of the AdS manifold. Some mathematical aspects of our chiral cones are provided in Appendix
A
.

In Section
8
we return to the global analytic structure of the two-point functions for Klein-Gordon fields and recall their expression in terms of Legendre functions of the second kind whose argument is the scalar product of two complex AdS events, lifted to a complex domain of the Cartesian product of two copies of the covering manifold.

Section
9
and
10
focus on
AdS_{2}

as a testing ground for the general case. We provide a detailed proof of the plane-wave expansion (
1.1
) and derive several novel integral representations of the two-point functions previously described in Section
8
. The main challenge is the proper identification of the integration cycle
\gamma
on the complex cone, a difficulty that reflects the intricate topological features of the AdS universe. Additionally, we address the bulk-to-boundary limit, which is crucial for holographic correspondences, and introduce the shadow transformation—a mathematical relation among dual waves of a given mass. These issues are resolved first in the two-dimensional context, which, while simpler, captures the critical aspects of the general case discussed in Section
11
. Furthermore, we present in an example the value of our new formula (
1.1
) to derive nontrivial theorems concerning Legendre functions of the second kind.

In Section
12
, we analyze the relevant integration cycles of the two-point function (
1.1
) within the particularly significant Poincaré coordinate system. In the subsequent Sections
13
and
14
, we compute the horospherical-Fourier transform of the plane waves and show how this transform can be utilized to diagonalize the two-point function. The calculation fully exploits the holomorphic structure of the plane waves and the connection between the ambient AdS chiral tuboids and the Minkowskian tubes of the Poincaré leaves.

This approach allows for clarifying the issue of Wick rotations illustrating the relation between Lorentzian and Euclidean calculation in AdS. In the last Section
15
we derive a formula for the Feynman-Schwinger propagator corresponding to (
1.4
) and show how all banana Euclidean Feynman diagrams can be Wick-rotated into banana diagrams in the real manifold, where the integration is restricted solely to a Poincare patch. We also explore examples of diagrams with external legs where the integration is again restricted to a Poincaré patch and find that the result is AdS invariant.

The concluding remarks reflect on the broader significance of the results, their potential applications to perturbative quantum field theory in AdS, and the continuing evolution of both mathematical and physical understanding in this area.

5
The analytic structure of two-point functions

The geometrical properties discussed in the previous section allow to fully characterize the two-point functions of scalar fields on either
\widetilde{}AdS_{d}
or on the uncovered manifold
AdS_{d}
for integer values of the mass parameters. We assume the following general properties
[
11
]

1.
Covariance:

{\cal W}
is invariant under
the action of the group
\widetilde{G}_{0}
(resp.
G_{0}
), i.e.

for all

(resp.
G_{0}
).

2.
Locality:
if
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are space-like separated then
the commutator

{\cal C}({x_{1}},x_{2})={\cal W}({x_{1}},x_{2})-{\cal W}({x_{2}},x_{1})

(5.38)

vanishes.

3.
Positive Definiteness:
For any smooth test function
f
in a suitable test-function space
{\cal T}(\widetilde{}AdS_{d})
the following positivity property holds:

{\cal W}(\overline{f}f)=\int{\cal W}(x_{1},x_{2})\overline{f(x)}f(y)d\sigma(x)d\sigma(y)\geq 0.

(5.39)

A reconstruction procedure essentially identical to that
of relativistic quantum field theory
[
12
]
then provides a Fock-Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, a continuous unitary
representation
g\mapsto U(g)
of
\widetilde{G}_{0}
(resp.
G_{0}
); a unit vector

under

and a representation of the field as an operator valued distribution

such that

\displaystyle{\cal W}(fg)=(\Omega,\ \varphi(f)\varphi(g)\Omega)\ \ for\ all\ \ f,g\in{\cal T}

(5.40)

and that

U(g)\,\varphi(f)\,U(g)^{-1}=\varphi(f_{\{g\}}),\ \ \ \ f_{\{g\}}(x)=f(g^{-1}x),\ \ \ \ U(g)\,\Omega=\Omega\

(5.41)

for all
g\in\widetilde{G}_{0}
(resp.
G_{0}
).

Now we need a physical criterion to select among all possible quantizations realizing the above conditions. Operators representing elements of
C_{+}
(see Eq.
4.28
) may be interpreted as Hamiltonian operators; a spectral condition selecting a class of relevant quantization amounts to assuming positivity of the spectra of such operators. More precisely, to every element
M\in C_{+}

we can associate the one-parameter subgroup

of

\widetilde{G}_{0}
(resp.
G_{0}
) and
a self-adjoint operator
\widehat{M}
acting in

{\cal H}
such that
\exp it\widehat{M}=U(\exp tM)
for all
t\in{\bf R}
.
With these notations, we assume the following

4.
(Strong) Spectral Condition:

For every
M\in C_{+}
, every
\Psi\in{\cal H}
, and every
{\cal C}^{\infty}

function
\widetilde{f}
with compact support contained in

(-\infty,\ 0)

\int_{{\bf R}}\left(\int\widetilde{f}(p)\,e^{-itp}\,dp\right)\,U(\exp tM)\,\Psi\,dt=0\ .

(5.42)

Equivalently
\widehat{M}
has its spectrum contained in
{\bf R}_{+}
.

The strong spectral condition actually broadens to cover general (interacting) QFT’s whose truncated
n
-point functions do not vanish
[

. When restricted to the case of two-point functions
the condition tells us that the
two-point function is not just any distribution on

but is realized as a boundary value of a certain analytic function:
[
11
]
:

4^{\prime}
.
Normal analyticity for two-point functions:

\mathcal{W}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)

is the boundary value of a function

holomorphic in the domain

AdS invariance then implies that to

there corresponds a function
of a single complex variable that can be identified with the scalar product

when
z_{1}\in{\cal Z}_{-}
and

; the function

Complex AdS invariance and normal analyticity then imply the following

.
Maximal analyticity property
:

extends to a function

\widetilde{\Delta}
of the

For theories single-valued on

is analytic in

Note that the two-point function of any field

6
Klein-Gordon equation, homogeneous functions and plane waves

Let us begin by recalling the relationship between the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Box_{g}=g_{\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}

g_{\mu\nu}
of the ambient space
{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}
, and the anti-de Sitter d’Alembert operator, which we will denote simply as
\Box

To this end let
{D}
be the infinitesimal generator of dilations so that

({D}f)(x)=x^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f(x)
and let
{\cal M}_{\mu\nu}=(x_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}-x_{\nu}\partial_{\mu})

{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}

{\cal M}^{2}={\cal M}^{\mu\nu}{\cal M}_{\mu\nu}=2x^{2}\Box_{g}-2(d-1){D}-2{D}^{2}.

(6.44)

If
f
and
h
are smooth functions on
{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}

and
h
is
G_{0}
-invariant, then

{\cal M}_{\mu\nu}(h(x)f(x))=h(x){\cal M}_{\mu\nu}f(x)\ .

(6.45)

This remains true if

is a
G_{0}
-invariant distribution
such as
\delta((x\cdot x)-1)

In particular the operators
{\cal M}_{\mu\nu}
and
{\cal M}^{2}
commute with
the restriction to invariant submanifolds of
{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}
and also
with dilations.
If
f
is a smooth function or a distribution
homogeneous of degree 0
on the open set

\Omega=\{x\in{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}\ :\ (x,x)>0\}

then

\Box(f|_{AdS_{d}})=(\Box_{g}f)|_{AdS_{d}}=\frac{1}{2}{\cal M}^{2}f|_{AdS_{d}}

(6.46)

because a homogeneous function of degree zero
is dilation invariant.
Since any smooth function or any distribution defined on
AdS_{d}
has a unique homogeneous extension
of degree zero in the open set
\Omega

\Box\phi=\frac{1}{2}{\cal M}^{2}\phi

(6.47)

\phi
defined on
AdS_{d}
.

Suppose now that
f
, defined in the ambient space
{\bf R}^{d+1}_{2}

and homogeneous of degree
\lambda

\Box_{g}f=0

6.44

{\cal M}^{2}f(x)=-2\lambda(\lambda+d-1)f(x).

(6.48)

Eqs. (

(6.50)

For real

and real

\lambda_{d}=-\frac{d-1}{2},\ \ \ \ m^{2}_{\lambda_{d}}=-\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}.

(6.51)

It may be useful to introduce the complex parameter

by subtracting

\nu=\lambda+\frac{d-1}{2}

(6.52)

so that
\nu=0

(6.53)

In the following we will use

and

Plane waves are the most important example of homogeneous solution of the massless equation in the ambient space:

\widetilde{}AdS_{d}

This issue will be addressed in detail in the next section.

7.1
Plane waves again

We are now in position to give a global definition of the anti-de Sitter plane waves. Given any complex number
\lambda

{\cal Z}_{+}\times\overline{{\cal C}}_{-}
and
{\cal Z}_{-}\times\overline{{\cal C}}_{+}

(7.70)

A previous attempt
[
18
]
to introduce global waves was based on a local
i\epsilon

m^{2}_{\lambda}=\lambda(\lambda+d-1).

In the following we will also need to consider plane waves in subsets of
{\cal Z}_{+}\times\overline{{\cal C}}_{+}
and
{\cal Z}_{-}\times\overline{{\cal C}}_{-}

This condition secures the integrity of the analytic continuation of the waves and preserves the properties necessary for their role in the anti-de Sitter spacetime framework.

9
Plane wave analysis of the two-point function: spacetime dimension
d=2

With all ingredients in place, we begin by analyzing the plane-wave expansion of two-point functions within the two-dimensional spacetime
\widetilde{AdS_{2}}
. Though simpler than in the more general

d
-dimensional setting, this simpler case already illustrates many important features of plane-wave expansions while avoiding some of the geometrical complications encountered in higher dimensions.

The fundamental object is the two-point function

\mathfrak{W}^{(2)}_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2})=c_{2}(\lambda)\int_{\gamma}(z_{1}\cdot\zeta)^{\lambda}(z_{2}\cdot\zeta)^{-\lambda-1}d\mu_{\gamma}(\zeta),\ \ z_{1}\in{\widetilde{\cal Z}_{-}},z_{2}\in\widetilde{\cal Z}_{+}.

(9.81)

In this definition, the dependence on the integration cycle
\gamma
is left implicit on the left-hand side. On the right-hand side, we have the integral of a closed differential form, where the cycle
\gamma
is a curve on the complex cone
\mathbb{C}_{2}^{(c)}
which generally depends on the points
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
that have been chosen. By construction, equation (
9.81
) defines a solution of the Klein–Gordon equation with respect to both variables and is analytic in
\mathcal{Z}_{-}\times\mathcal{Z}_{+}
(provided the integral converges).

Our objective is to determine those choices of the integration cycle
\gamma
that yield an AdS-invariant result; in such cases, the resulting expression must necessarily be a linear combination of (
8.72
) and (
8.76
).
The selection of the integration cycle is crucial to enforce the AdS symmetry and obtain covariant quantizations of the Klein-Gordon field.

9.1
A closed cycle and the special case of half-integer mass parameter

Let us proceed by mimicking the de Sitter case
[
16
,
17
]
and consider the integral
(
9.81
) over a complete closed cycle
\gamma
on the two-dimensional real cone
{\cal C}_{2}
that does not depend on the two points
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
; let us consider the integral over the upper component
\gamma^{+}_{S(2)}
of the spherical basis of
{\cal C}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{S(2)}^{+}=\left\{\xi=(\sin\phi,1,\cos\phi),\ \ -\pi<\phi<\pi\right\},\ \ d\mu_{\gamma^{+}_{S(2)}}(\xi)=d\phi.

(9.82)

Properties 3 and 4 of Sect.
7
guarantee that the integrand is always finite and therefore the integral converges. Unfortunately, in general, the integrand is not univalued on
{\cal C}_{2}
; after one revolution it gets multiplied by the global phase factor
\exp(-4\pi i\lambda)
.
The conclusion is that Stokes theorem cannot be applied to deform the contour and the result is not AdS invariant.

Exceptions occur when
\lambda=l
integer or
\lambda=l+\frac{1}{2}
half-integer. When
\lambda=l
the integrals over the two adjacent half-circles exactly cancel each other, causing the total integral to vanish identically.
In contrast, when
\lambda=l+\frac{1}{2}
the integrand has a period of

\pi
and the contributions from the two halves combine coherently to yield, in a nontrivial manner, the invariant maximally analytic solution (
8.72
):

\mathfrak{W}^{(2)}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})=W^{(2)}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\gamma_{S(2)}^{+}}(z_{1}\cdot\xi)^{l+\frac{1}{2}}(z_{2}\cdot\xi)^{-l-\frac{3}{2}}d\mu_{\gamma_{2+}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}Q_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(z_{1}\cdot z_{2}).

(9.83)

9.2
General case: relative homology

For general complex
\lambda
integrating over a closed real cycle on the cone
{\cal C}_{2}
such as
\gamma^{+}_{S(2)}
gives a non-invariant result.
Extending the integration to cycles on the covering space of the cone does not resolve this issue and is physically unsound, as it would lead to an overcounting of possible momentum directions. In fact, even a complete cycle of the type considered above already entails such an overcounting, which partly accounts for the vanishing of the integral (
9.81
) when

\lambda
is an integer.

The solution for a general complex
\lambda
is nevertheless given by Eq. (
9.81
), provided the integration cycle
\gamma
is specified appropriately; here we generalize the results of
[
20
]
where integer values of
\lambda
were studied: for
{\rm Re\,}\lambda>-1
the cycle
\gamma
should be open and have its endpoints on the plane orthogonal to
z_{1}
; more precisely, for
{\rm Re\,}\lambda>-1
the cycle should belong to a relative homology class of
H_{1}({\cal C}_{-},\{\zeta:\,\zeta\cdot z_{1}=0\})
;
for
{\rm Re\,}\lambda<0
the cycle should belong to a relative homology class of
H_{1}({\cal C}_{+},\{\zeta:\,\zeta\cdot z_{2}=0\})
; the two possible choices actually give the same results.

Let us examine the first option. For
{\rm Re\,}\lambda\geq 0
let us choose a relative cycle
\gamma(z_{1})
in
H_{1}({\cal C}_{-},\{\zeta:\,\zeta\cdot z_{1}=0\})
i.e. a path in
{\cal C}_{-}
whose extremal points belong to the plane
\zeta\cdot z_{1}=0
:

\mathfrak{W}^{(2)}_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\gamma(z_{1})}(z_{1}\cdot\zeta)^{\lambda}(\zeta\cdot z_{2})^{-\lambda-1}d\mu_{\gamma(z_{1})}(\zeta),\ \ z_{1}\in{{\cal Z}_{-}},z_{2}\in{\cal Z}_{+}.

(9.84)

•

The value of the integral is independent of the chosen representative within the relative homology class. This follows from the fact that the integrand is a locally well-defined closed differential form, and that it vanishes on the relative boundary.

•

Convergence of the integral then allows to relax the condition to
{\rm Re\,}\lambda\geq-1
. The independence on the chosen representative in the relative homology class remains true.

•

AdS invariance follows by construction from the relation
\gamma(gz_{1})=g\gamma(z_{1})\subset{\cal C}_{-}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}^{(2)}_{\lambda}(gz_{1},gz_{2})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{g\gamma(z_{1})}(z_{1}\cdot g^{-1}\zeta)^{\lambda}(g^{-1}\zeta\cdot z_{2})^{-\lambda-1}d\mu_{g\gamma(z_{1})}(\zeta)=

(9.85)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\gamma(z_{1})}(z_{1}\cdot\zeta)^{\lambda}(\zeta\cdot z_{2})^{-\lambda-1}d\mu_{\gamma(z_{1})}(\zeta).

(9.86)

\mathfrak{W}_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2})
is thus a function of one complex variable, namely of the scalar product
z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\in\Delta
; it is univalued only for integer
\lambda=l
. o evaluate this function, we choose the two points in the respective tuboids as follows:

\displaystyle z_{1}=(\sin(-iv),0,\cos(-iv)),\ \ v\geq 0,

\displaystyle z_{2}=(\sin(iu),0,\cos(iu)),\ \ u>0.

(9.87)

A representative of the relevant relative homology class
is the following cycle in
{\cal C}_{-}

\displaystyle\gamma(z_{1})=\left\{(\sin(\phi-iv),1,\cos(\phi-iv)),\ \ -\pi/2<\phi<\pi/2\right\}

(9.88)

In the special case when
v=0

In all cases Eq. (

A similar formula holds true for the permuted two-point function

Taking the difference of the boundary values gives the commutator:

9.3
Application to Legendre functions: a multiplication theorem for
Q_{\lambda}

The key new feature of Eq. (
9.84
) is that it provides an integral representation of

Q_{\lambda}(z_{1}\cdot z_{2})
in terms of two distinct points through their scalar product, opening up a wide range of potential applications. Equation (
9.84
) can be used to derive numerous new integral representations of Legendre functions with more complicated arguments (see, for instance, Eqs. (
13.166
) and (
13.176
)). Moreover, Eq. (
9.84
) can also be employed to generate new formulas for Legendre functions of the second kind.

To give an example let us choose
z_{1}=e_{d}
and
z_{2}=(x\sin(iu),\sqrt{x^{2}-1},x\cos(iu))
; Eq. (
9.84
) gives

\displaystyle Q_{\lambda}(x\,\mbox{ch\,}u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\cos\phi)^{\lambda}\left(x\cos(\phi-iu)-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)^{-\lambda-1}d\phi.

(9.105)

By replacing the second factor in the integrand on the right-hand side with its binomial series, we are led to consider a series of integrals. These integrals can be evaluated using yet another new integral representation for associated Legendre functions of the second kind, which is derived in Eq. (
11.129
) below:

\displaystyle\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\cos\phi)^{\lambda}(\cos(\phi-iu))^{-\lambda-1-n}d\phi=\frac{2^{1+\frac{n}{2}}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1+\lambda)e^{-\frac{1}{2}in\pi}Q_{\frac{n}{2}+\lambda}^{\frac{n}{2}}(\mbox{ch\,}(u))}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\right)\Gamma(1+n+\lambda)(\mbox{sh\,}u)^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

(9.106)

What follows is a nontrivial — and possibly previously unknown — multiplication theorem for associated Legendre functions of the second kind:

\displaystyle Q_{\lambda}(x\,\mbox{ch\,}u)=\frac{1}{x^{1+\lambda}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+n)}\,\left(1-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right)^{n}\frac{Q_{n+\lambda}^{n}(\mbox{ch\,}u)}{(2\,\mbox{sh\,}u)^{n}},\ \ x>1.

(9.107)

Bulk-to-boundary limit and the shadow transformation

The plane-wave expansion (
9.84
) offers the possibility to study the boundary behaviour of the two-point function in a very simple way by sending
z_{1}
to the boundary of
\widetilde{AdS_{2}}
here identified with the covering of real cone
{\cal C}_{2}
. To this aim let us choose

\displaystyle z_{1}(t,R)=\frac{\hat{z}_{1}(t,R)}{R}=\frac{1}{R}{\left(\sqrt{R^{2}+1}\sin t,1,\sqrt{R^{2}+1}\cos t\right)},\ \ z_{2}=(\sin(iu),0,\cos(iu))

(10.108)

\displaystyle\gamma(z_{1})=\{\xi=(\sin\phi,1,\cos\phi),\ \ t-t_{R}<\phi<t+t_{R}\},\ \ t_{R}=\arccos\left((R^{2}+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(10.109)

Note that
z_{1}(t,R)\cdot z_{1}(t,R)=1
while
\hat{z}_{1}(t,R)\cdot\hat{z}_{1}(t,R)=R^{2}
and we are going to take the limit
R\to 0
.
Suppose that
R>0
; when
R\to 0
the integration is over the small interval
[t-R,t+R]
; the plane-wave representation (
9.84
) of the two-point function immediately gives

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{\lambda}(z_{1}(t,R),z_{2})\simeq\frac{(\cos(t-iu))^{-\lambda-1}}{4\pi R^{\lambda}}\int_{-t_{R}}^{t_{R}}\left(\sqrt{R^{2}+1}\cos\phi-1\right)^{\lambda}d\phi

(10.110)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2^{-\lambda-2}\Gamma(\lambda+1)R^{\lambda+1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\lambda+\frac{3}{2}\right)}(\hat{z}(t,R)\cdot z_{2})^{-\lambda-1}.

(10.111)

For
R<0
the integration path tends instead to the whole circle
[t-\pi,t+\pi]
but the result does not change.
For
\lambda>-1
the rhs of (
10.111
) tends to zero but the following expression has a limit

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to 0^{\pm}}R^{-\lambda-1}W^{(2)}_{\lambda}(z_{1}(t,R),z_{2})=\frac{2^{-\lambda-2}\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\lambda+\frac{3}{2})}\left({\xi_{\pm}\cdot z}\right)^{-\lambda-1}

(10.112)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{\pm}=\pm(\sin t,1,\cos t)

(10.113)

are the null vectors of
{\cal C}_{2}
representing the relevant limit points of the boundary.
The bulk-to-boundary two-point function is thus proportional to a plane wave.

10.1
The shadow transformation

If we now let the second point
z_{2}
approach the boundary while keeping

16
Concluding remarks

AdS quantum field theory admits a formulation that is structurally rich as its Minkowskian Wightman counterpart provided one fully exploits it on the intrinsic complex geometry of AdS rather than working in specific coordinates. By introducing holomorphic AdS plane waves and identifying suitable relative homology cycles supported on the complex null cone, the scalar two-point function is expressed in a manifestly covariant, coordinate-free integral representation that implements covariance, locality, maximal analyticity in a unified way, while addressing some of the conceptual difficulties associated with closed timelike curves. Its diagonalisation in Poincaré coordinates, encoded in a Källén–Lehmann-type superposition over lower-dimensional Minkowski two-point functions with Bessel kernels, not only yields explicit Schwinger and Feynman propagators but also helps to clarify the status of the Poincaré patch and the precise sense in which Euclidean Feynman diagrams on Lobachevski space can be Wick-rotated to diagrams supported entirely in a single AdS patch without loss of AdS covariance.

Altogether, the framework provides a robust and versatile starting point for perturbative AdS QFT and offers a new setting in which to revisit and refine holographic constructions that rely on AdS correlation functions and their boundary limits.
