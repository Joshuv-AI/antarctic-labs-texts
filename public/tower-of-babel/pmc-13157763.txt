Medication errors in practice: A mixed-methods study from Diwan Polyclinic-Muscat
Authors: Zainab Ali Mohammed Al-Mukhaini, Ingrid Passarin, Edna M. Mora Palomares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo explore healthcare professionals’ experiences with medication errors (MEs) in terms of types and factors that contribute to them.DesignA mixed-methods study was conducted to explore the MEs experiences of healthcare professionals. Quantitative data were collected through a cross-sectional, self-administered questionnaire, whereas qualitative data were collected via face-to-face semi-structured interviews.SettingThe study was conducted at Diwan Polyclinic in Muscat, a primary and secondary healthcare institute.ParticipantsThe study population included healthcare workers who were practising (not retired) and actively involved in patient care and the medication-use cycle, either by prescribing, dispensing or administering medication. The total number of participants was 83 (55 females and 28 males) healthcare professionals, comprising doctors (38), nurses (32), pharmacists and assistant pharmacists (13). Omani participants accounted for 72%, whereas non-Omani participants accounted for 28%. Six of 83 healthcare professionals were purposefully selected to provide additional qualitative insights into their experiences with MEs and the measures they use to reduce them.InterventionsNone.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes focused on identifying types, causes and effects of MEs, and the secondary outcomes aimed to explore the emotional and professional implications on healthcare workers as well as suggested strategies to minimise MEs.ResultsA total of 83 participants (55 females and 28 males) were included, with 72% Omani and 28% non-Omani. Of these, 44 participants (53%) encountered MEs during their practice at Diwan Polyclinic. The most common type of MEs was prescribing errors (51%), followed by dispensing errors (39%) and administering errors (10%). Incorrect dosing was the most typical cause of prescribing errors.In the qualitative part of the study, the interviewed participants implemented these measures to reduce MEs: 1) double-checking the prescribed medicines, patients ‘names and identity before dispensing and administering drugs, 2) patient education on polypharmacy and 3) alerting prescribing doctors to errors in their prescriptions or orders. Several measures have been suggested to mitigate MEs, including better communication among health professionals and between them and patients, focusing on staff well-being and development and innovative built-in modules and programmes in electronic medical records for drug verification.ConclusionThe most common type of MEs was prescribing errors. Focusing on better communication and staff well-being, as well as the innovative development of electronic medical records, will help minimise MEs.

Introduction

Medication Errors (MEs) constitute a major and persistent challenge to healthcare systems worldwide and are widely recognised as one of the most preventable causes of patient harm.1 2 Despite their clinical and policy significance, MEs have no universally accepted definition, and considerable variability exists across the literature. 35Lisby et al demonstrated that this heterogeneity in definition contributes substantially to the wide variations in reported prevalence rates of MEs across studies.6

MEs have been broadly defined as “any error in the prescribing, dispensing, or administration of a drug, irrespective of whether such errors lead to adverse consequences.”2 Other definitions emphasise the preventable nature of these events. For example, the Ministry of Health in Oman defines a medication error as “any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm while the medication is in the control of the healthcare professional.”7 These definitions underscore the multifaceted nature of MEs and their occurrence across multiple stages of the medication-use process.

MEs may occur at any point along the medication-use cycle and typically involve three principal groups of healthcare professionals: physicians during prescribing, pharmacists and assistant pharmacists during dispensing and nurses during administration. MEs at any of these stages can compromise patients’ safety and undermine the quality of care.

Despite ongoing advancements in healthcare systems and patient safety initiatives, preventing MEs remains a significant challenge. International and regional studies consistently report a high prevalence of MEs, particularly within Middle Eastern healthcare settings.8 9 Naseralallah et al systematically reviewed publications on MEs in outpatient and ambulatory settings and found that the prevalence of MEs in prescribing drugs ranged from 23% to 92%.8 In a systematic review, Alqenae et al reported a median ME rate of 53% among adult patients, with medication discrepancies reaching up to 50% following hospital discharge.9 Similarly, Aidah et al identified prescribing errors as the most frequently reported type of MEs in Middle Eastern countries, followed by administration errors.10

However, the body of scientific literature addressing MEs in Oman remains limited. Most studies do not comprehensively examine the prevalence, types or contributory factors of MEs within the Omani healthcare system. Sheikh and Al-Ruzaiqi explored community knowledge and perceptions of medical errors in general, with MEs forming only a component of the broader analysis.11 Likewise, Al-Balushi et al investigated factors associated with self-reported MEs among healthcare workers in Oman, identifying sex, burnout, professional role, workplace bullying and nationality as significant correlates.12 Nonetheless, these studies did not specifically focus on MEs across the medication-use cycle.

MEs can be classified in several ways. In this study, classification is based on the stage of the medication-use process at which the error occurs—namely, prescribing, dispensing or administering—consistent with internationally recognised frameworks.3 13 This approach allows for systematic identification of error patterns and targeted intervention strategies.

Multiple factors have been implicated in the occurrence of MEs. Common contributory factors include inadequate knowledge or training, excessive workload, fatigue, look-alike and sound-alike medications, use of ambiguous abbreviations, incorrect dosing or units due to non-standardised documentation practices and patient misidentification, particularly when patients’ names are similar and not appropriately verified using identification numbers or medical records.1417 These factors often interact within complex healthcare environments, increasing the risk of error.

Oman is a developing country with a strong commitment to improving patient safety and quality of care. Although national initiatives to strengthen patient safety systems have been implemented, comprehensive national-level data on MEs remain scarce.18 This knowledge gap underscores the importance of this study, which aimed to address this deficiency by examining the experiences, types, contributing factors and mitigation strategies related to MEs among healthcare workers at Diwan Polyclinic in Oman. The findings will contribute to building a conceptual understanding of MEs in Oman. To the authors’ knowledge, this study represents the first focused examination of MEs in Oman.

Methods

Study design

A mixed-methods study was conducted between 1 January 2023 and 6 April 2023 to assess healthcare professionals’ experiences with MEs over the years of their work at Diwan Polyclinic in Muscat. Quantitative data were collected through questionnaires, whereas qualitative data were obtained through semi-structured interviews.

Setting

The study was conducted at Diwan Polyclinic in Muscat. Diwan Polyclinic provides primary and secondary healthcare services. It consists of seventeen sections (paediatrics, internal medicine, obstetrics and gynaecology, general practitioner, accident and emergency, Ear-Nose-Throat, dermatology, ophthalmology, dental, physiotherapy, sport medicine, nutrition and dietetics, laboratory, radiology, pharmacy, medical informatics (medical records) and medical co-ordination). It receives around 500 patients daily. The total number of practising healthcare professionals and other allied healthcare workers in this polyclinic at the time the study was conducted was 191.

Medical records are purely electronic and include patients’ clinical notes, doctors’ orders to nurses, investigations requested, all diagnostic procedures and prescribed medication. All documentation in the medical record is in English. All healthcare workers in the study context and in Oman in general can read, write and speak English fluently. For a smooth workflow, all patients are assessed by doctors who write prescriptions based on the diagnosis of patients’ conditions. Only doctors are allowed to prescribe medications. Prescriptions are received electronically at the pharmacy, where pharmacists review them and patients’ clinical notes to check, prior to dispensing the medication, for any possible errors in the prescription, such as wrong dose, contradicting medication or drug-drug interaction. If the pharmacist identifies any MEs, the prescribing doctor will be alerted about the error, and the pharmacists will keep a record of these MEs in their system for educational and improvement purposes. The MEs incidents are also usually reported through the internal electronic portal, which goes to the quality assurance section at the polyclinic. Pharmacists are also playing a crucial role in counselling patients for polypharmacy.

Participants

All healthcare professionals who were directly involved in patients’ medication-use cycle by prescribing, administering or dispensing were eligible to take part in the study. Other medical and paramedical personnel who were not directly involved in patients’ medication were excluded. 85 out of 191 healthcare workers were eligible for this study who belong to three groups: doctors, nurses and pharmacists and assistant pharmacists.

Sample size

Sample size calculation is essential to determine the minimum number of participants needed to provide a good representation of the target population with good study statistical power.19 20 In this study, all staff members who were actively practising and involved in the medication-use cycle were invited to take part in the survey. By knowing the sample size and comparing it to the response rate, the researchers can ensure good representation of the population, which allows them to draw conclusions from the results with confidence. Yamane’s (1967) formula was used to calculate the sample size for the quantitative part of the study. With a population size (N) of 85, a margin of error of 5%, a Confidence Interval (CI) of 95% and a study power of 80%, the sample size was calculated to be 71.

Sample size and sampling for the qualitative study

For the qualitative part of the study, a sample was selected by purposive sampling to yield richness in data.21 The participants were intended to reflect the three groups of the involved healthcare workers (doctors, nurses, pharmacists and assistant pharmacists), both sexes, diversity in nationality and diversity in work experience. Hence, they consisted of two individuals (one male and one female) from each healthcare worker group, a mixture of Omani and non-Omani and with different lengths of work experience. Six participants were selected for one-to-one interviews. The final number of participants will depend on reaching saturation. 22 23 The plan was to increase the number with the same selection strategy if saturation was not reached. Saturation was reached with the fourth candidate, but we continued with the other two to maintain the heterogeneity of the group that was purposefully selected. Reaching data saturation increases the credibility of the result.23

Research tool for the quantitative study (questionnaire)

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire was developed through brainstorming and a literature review. The authors listed all possible causes and factors associated with MEs from brainstorming. From the literature review on MEs, all causes mentioned were extracted and listed.24 5 15 16 2428 By comparing the two lists, a final list was generated that includes all possible causes and factors related to MEs. These causes (items) were grouped into four main groups: prescribing MEs, administration MEs, dispensing MEs and other reasons. The questionnaire was designed to cover different aspects of MEs, as well as other essential data such as demographic information, years of experience and whether patients experienced harm due to MEs.

Description of the questionnaire

The questionnaire comprised two main parts. The first part contains five questions covering demographic data: sex, nationality, department, job, total years of work experience and work experience within the Diwan Polyclinic.

The second part included 17 main questions with their branches. Candidates were instructed to answer all questions in this part based on their work experience in Diwan Polyclinic. They were encouraged to indicate all types of MEs they encountered, not necessarily choose only one. The first question in this part (sixth in the sequence of the whole questionnaire) asked whether the candidate encountered MEs during their work at the Diwan Polyclinic. If the answer was ‘yes’, then further details were pursued, namely, frequency of MEs they encountered, whether it was reported and how it was reported (verbally or by writing an incident report). Then, the seventh question asked, “What was the most common medication error they came across?” They were given options to choose prescribing error, administering error or dispensing error.

Questions 8–11 were about the causes of MEs. Questions 8–10 focused on each type of MEs (prescribing, dispensing and administering MEs) separately to learn about the possible causes for each one. For prescribing MEs, the listed causes were wrong medicine, wrong dose, contraindicated drug, omission of available warnings and drug allergy not documented. For dispensing MEs, causes included dispensing with incorrect information on the label, with incorrect verbal instructions to the patient, incorrect patient name or ID, similar names or covers, orders misread and interruptions during dispensing. For administering MEs, identified causes included incorrect dose, expired drug, incorrect instructions by the doctor and wrong drug preparation, wrong administration techniques, wrong route of drug administration and documentation error.

Question 11 was about listing other possible causes of MEs, which included a combination of personal and workplace causes. Question 12 was an enquiry about the MEs led to patient injury. If the answer was ‘yes’, then the candidates were asked to select the level of harm between minor and severe. To be more objective while deciding on the level of harm in question 12, a standardised classification of harm was adopted from The Harm Associated with Medication Error Classification (HAMEC) by Gates et al.13 The definition of each harm level was placed between brackets next to the harm level label so that it is easier for participants to choose the appropriate choice. Subsequent questions addressed participants’ perceptions of empowerment, presence of policies, documentation of the communication regarding MEs, and on whom does the responsibility of MEs lie

The questionnaire was in English because English is the official language of medical documentation in Oman, and all healthcare workers can speak, read and write it.

Validation of the questionnaire

Validity of an instrument refers to the extent the instrument measures what it should measure.19 Having the questionnaire items already mentioned in the literature on the same topic indicates right concept and good content validity. The presence of convergent agreement between authors’ views and the literature in identifying questionnaire items of MEs causes strengthens the questionnaire’s validity.

After placing the questions together and organising the questionnaire in its final format, three experts assessed and approved the questionnaire for its content validity. At least three individuals are required for the content validity check of a questionnaire.19 (Experts’ feedback, the questionnaire and detailed calculation of content validity ratio for each item and for the whole questionnaire are provided in onlinesupplemental appendices 15). Face validity was established by piloting the questionnaire with colleagues from the medical field and the community (public) to assess its clarity. A very minor amendment was suggested by one person only to enhance the clarity of one question. The questionnaire showed a high level of internal consistency and reliability with a Cronbach coefficient alpha of 0.884. Any value of Cronbach coefficient alpha >0.7 is considered good.19 This degree of validity check was deemed sufficient, as this questionnaire is not a psychometric scale that requires extensive validity assessment.

Procedure

After obtaining ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee at The Directorate General of Medical Services-Diwan of Royal Court-The Sultanate of Oman (REC 02/2022), a list of doctors, nurses, pharmacists and assistant pharmacists was obtained. Self-administered questionnaires were distributed to all medical practitioners and staff from the above-mentioned categories who were in direct contact with patients and medication delivery.

In the quantitative study, participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire by considering only their work experience at Diwan Polyclinic-Muscat over the years of their practice. Thus, the data mainly reflected their experience from the time they joined Diwan Polyclinic through the date of data collection. If any participant encountered MEs during the study period, they might have included them in their response, but without differentiating between past and present. As the main aim of this study was to determine whether healthcare workers have come across any MEs during their work at Diwan polyclinic, they were encouraged to report all MEs they encountered; thus, one person might report several MEs. It is not necessary that they committed the MEs themselves, but they might have witnessed them or heard about them at the workplace or in their departmental meetings.

Participants were instructed to return their answers to an allocated box in their department, which would be collected later by the researchers, so candidates’ identities were secured.

Additionally, six participants from these professions were purposefully selected for individual face-to-face interviews to provide in-depth insights into their experiences with MEs and perceptions of contributing factors. The selection ensured diversity in work specialty, sex, work experience and nationality. Each interview lasted between 30 and 40 min. Informed consent was taken from each participant. Participants were assured of the privacy and confidentiality of provided data, and informed that their refusal to participate would not affect them. All participants were keen to share their experiences and thoughts.

A list of probes was prepared to guide the interviews and ensure that the most critical issues were addressed. These probes were informed by the literature and brainstorming sessions to cover all necessary areas related to the aim of this study.

Data processing and data analysis

Data were initially entered into an Excel spreadsheet and, after checking for accuracy, transferred to SPSS version 22 for statistical analysis. For easy presentation, assistant pharmacists and pharmacists were combined into pharmacists. All assistant pharmacists were highly trained and experienced, and their diploma programme lasted 3 years.

Patients and public involvement

As the study participants were all healthcare professionals, patients were not involved. The public was involved during questionnaire clarity verification and face validity check. The questionnaire for quantitative data collection was distributed to some colleagues from outside medical field (public) to check on the clarity and flow of the questionnaire. Findings of this study might be disseminated later (after publication) to patients to know their feedback and suggestions for improvement and MEs control.

In the dispensing phase, 39% of pharmacists, 32% of doctors and 19% of nurses reported that they encountered dispensing MEs. The common cause of MEs was interruption while dispensing, which was reported by 19 (23%) participants, followed by similar names or packages (21%), incorrect patient’s name or identity (19%), wrong information on the label (18%), wrong verbal instructions to the patient (17%), orders misread (13%) and other reasons (5%) (figure 1).

Participants indicated several causes of MEs related to the administering phase in which 9% of nurses, 8% of pharmacists and 5% of doctors had encountered administering MEs. The most common cause was administering incorrect drug, which was reported by 29% of the participants. Other reported causes had similar weight, namely, administering expired drug, wrong drug timing and documentation errors, which were reported by 13%, whereas incorrect instructions by the doctor and wrong administration techniques were reported by 11% of participants. Other causes of administering MEs were wrong drug preparation (7%), similar units writing (7%), wrong routes of drug administration (6%) and other reasons (5%) (figure 1).

Participants reported other causes of MEs, mainly workplace factors. The most reported cause was excessive workload (39%), followed by distraction of healthcare professionals (28%).

Qualitative data analysis results

Six healthcare professionals were interviewed individually to gain in-depth insight into their experiences with MEs. The interviewees included two doctors, two pharmacists and two nurses, with different levels of work experience, ranging from 10 to 30 years. Although all encountered MEs at the polyclinic, none resulted in harm, as the errors were intercepted before they affected patients. All interviewees unanimously agreed that the responsibility for MEs lies with all healthcare professionals involved in patient care, including doctors, nurses and pharmacists, depending on the type of error. The verbatim content analysis of the interviews identified three main themes: (1) current measures to prevent MEs, (2) proposed measures to prevent MEs and (3) the emotional impact of MEs.

Current measures to prevent or minimise medication errors

Steps taken to avoid repetition of MEs, primarily implemented by pharmacists, included (1) colour-coding for look-alike and sound-alike medications; (2) multiple checks by different pharmacists before dispensing medications; (3) confirming the patient’s name and identity before dispensing; (4) clinical pharmacists checking for adverse drug reactions, drug–drug interactions, and contradictions, especially if patients were seen by two different clinics on the same day and prescribed medication from both places; and (5) patient counselling on polypharmacy management. Nurses also conducted double-checks.

Verbal warnings and meetings on MEs were supported as an initiative to record ‘lessons learnt’, alongside documenting errors using the pharmacy’s internal form. As part of the patient safety protocol in the pharmacy, a designated pharmacy team was responsible for managing MEs. Incidents of MEs were also reported electronically via the institutional portal to the Department of Quality Assurance, as everybody indicated.

When participants were asked about policies to prevent MEs, the majority reported being unaware of any formal guidelines. Given the extent to which one is empowered to address MEs, writing an incident report and speaking to a superior were commonly mentioned actions. These practices were applied synonymously for both documentation and recording communication. Disclosing the MEs to the superior, alerting the prescribing doctor about the error and informing patients and their families were emphasized.

Suggested measures to prevent or minimise medication errors

Several strategies have been proposed to help in avoiding or minimising MEs, in addition to existing measures.

Communication

Participants highlighted the need to improve communication among staff and between staff and patients to achieve better outcomes. Better communication between staff can be achieved not only through verbal communication but also through good documentation of all aspects of patient care. Pharmacists should document any changes in dispensed medications compared with the original prescription, which will ensure that the treating doctor is aware of the patient’s updated medication list for subsequent follow-up visits. Similarly, doctors should regularly update patients’ medical records regarding their current health status and any side effects to help pharmacists countercheck the selection of planned medications and identify potential drug interactions.

Improving medical records

This involves improving the patient’s electronic medical record by incorporating in-built calculations for correct dosing and an integrated drug interaction alert module to reduce MEs and prescription processing time in the pharmacy.

Patient involvement

Patient involvement in medication use cycle was identified as a step in avoiding MEs. This can be achieved by explaining the medication process to the patient by both the treating doctor (in the prescribing phase) and the pharmacist (in the dispensing phase). Any discrepancies can be identified by the patient.

Staff well-being

Attention must be given to staff well-being by reducing stress, improving knowledge through specialised training and increasing their awareness of drugs and MEs. Developing an orientation programme and possibly short training sessions for new staff could help achieve this.

Emotional impact of medication errors

Regarding emotional impact, all interviewees expressed their feelings of guilt, disappointment and insecurity.

Discussion

This study’s findings revealed that 53% of healthcare professionals had encountered MEs, and the most common type of MEs was prescribing MEs, with the incorrect dose as the most common cause of error. These findings are consistent with studies from Middle Eastern countries and globally.89 1417 28 29

In this study, all pharmacists (100%) reported having encountered MEs, whereas only 55% of doctors and 31% of nurses did. One possible explanation for why all pharmacists reported having encountered MEs more than other health professionals is that prescriptions are dispensed at the pharmacy, so pharmacists are more likely to spot errors before physicians.29

According to this study, prescribing MEs were committed by doctors only, based on the study context, in which only doctors are allowed to prescribe drugs. However, these prescribing MEs were detected mainly by pharmacists. As mentioned previously in the study setting, pharmacists at Diwan Polyclinic assess each prescription for any MEs prior to dispensing the medication; hence, they are more likely to spot prescribing MEs than any other health professionals. For this reason, almost all prescribing MEs are near misses (detected before reaching the patients), which was reflected by 64% reporting no harm and further supported by the qualitative study results. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia showed that pharmacists detected 75% of reported MEs,30 which reflects the pivotal role of pharmacists in patient safety from an ME control perspective. Preventing MEs and guarding patients’ safety is the duty of everyone who works in the healthcare system,31 and literature shows that nurses and pharmacists detect 30–70% of prescribing MEs.31

This study provides insights into the workplace factors that significantly contribute to MEs. Excessive workload, which leads to fatigue, was identified as a contributing factor by 39% of the participants, and fatigue itself was reported by 17%. Other important workplace factors included distraction (28%), poor communication among staff (19%) and inadequate information about the available formulation (23%). These findings are consistent with the literature. 13 17 28 29 32Moreover, poor communication was not attributed to a language barrier but to a lack of feedback and documentation. Such a cause of MEs is well documented in the literature.27

In addition to workplace factors, participants mentioned several personal factors related to MEs, such as incorrect doses given to patients, incorrect drugs, incorrect patient names or identities, incorrect verbal instructions to patients, non-adherence to medication administration protocols and misread orders. These findings are consistent with the literature.29 30 33

Regarding measures to mitigate MEs, the study participants highlighted several measures that were implemented to mitigate look-alike and sound-alike MEs, such as the use of colour-coding to differentiate similar medicines. This measure adheres to the Ministry of Health’s Pharmaceutical Policy and Guidelines.7 Pharmacists also mentioned that they counsel and educate patients regarding polypharmacy, which is an important step in reducing MEs. Patients’ education about their medication and how to take it plays a significant role in MEs reduction.33 34 Other implemented measures were double-checking prescriptions and patients’ names and identity before dispensing or administering the medication.

Good communication between healthcare providers themselves is essential to avoid wrong assumptions or misinterpretations when developing management plans for patients, as indicated by participants in this study. Hence, communication should be patient-centred to mitigate the consequences of communication faults on patients’ safety.32

Patient-centred healthcare, which involves patients in decisions about their health, is a fundamental measure.34 Good communication between healthcare providers and patients is crucial to achieve this goal. Communication should not only extend to educating patients and their families but also the community, raising awareness about MEs prevention. For example, people should be encouraged to check their medication details, such as the name on the label, identification number, expiry date and the correct mode of administration and storage. This is crucial for the prevention of MEs.

Participants suggested mitigating measures, including (1) staff empowerment through training and addressing knowledge gaps, (2) staff well-being by managing excessive workload to avoid employee fatigue, (3) encouraging ME-incident reporting, (4) improving staff communication skills and (5) improving the electronic medical record to include a module or programme that helps correctly calculate doses. A recently published systematic review on community pharmacy dispensing errors found that implementing a comprehensive automated system with integrated verification, monitoring and training would reduce MEs by up to 98%.29 Improving staff communication skills through training programmes is equally important.29 32 33

While most of the findings in this study are consistent with the regional and international literature, it presents the first documented study of healthcare professionals’ experience with MEs in Oman.

Implications and recommendations

As we work towards a patient-centred healthcare model for medication safety, empowering patients with knowledge, giving them a voice and fostering communication between healthcare providers and patients would help reduce the risk of MEs. This supports placing more patient safety systems in healthcare institutions in Oman.18

Applying transparency will reduce workloads, foster collaboration and improve resource utilisation. The healthcare system should focus on staff well-being to achieve better service outcomes. These factors will collectively foster a culture of learning and enhance patient safety in healthcare.

Limitations

As this is a cross-sectional study examining past experiences, data may be subject to recall bias where participants might not remember all MEs they encountered during their work experience at Diwan Polyclinic. In addition, the timing of the most recent ME incident was not captured, limiting our ability to assess temporal trends. Moreover, a single-centre setting might limit generalisability. Despite these limitations, this study provides a valuable foundation for further investigation of this phenomenon and offers guidance for further research. Using a mixed-methods approach might serve as a buffer against potential recall bias in data collected via the questionnaire.

Conclusion

In summary, MEs remain a significant challenge to patient safety. Although measures have been implemented to minimise MEs, more robust actions are still needed to further minimise MEs and ensure patient safety. A comprehensive revision of the healthcare system is needed to encourage ME-reporting practices, improve communication between patients and healthcare professionals and prioritise staff development and well-being.
