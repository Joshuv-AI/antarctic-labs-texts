Aortic arch surgery in real-life practice: is the frozen elephant trunk always a better option than conventional aortic arch replacement?
Authors: Maxime Zimmermann, Matteo Pozzi, Fadi Farhat, Marco Vola, Jean-François Obadia, Antoine Millon, Daniel Grinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveManagement of aortic arch disease (AAD) is complex due to its involvement in multiple aortic segments and the variety of patient presentations. We compared the clinical outcomes of conventional aortic arch repair (CAAR) and the Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET) technique across the main clinical indications: acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), chronic dissection and atheromatous aneurysm.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-centre cohort study of 102 consecutive patients who underwent aortic arch repair between January 2012 and June 2023. Indication-specific subgroup analyses were the primary analytical framework. Patients were divided according to the surgical technique: CAAR (n = 45, 44.1%) or FET (n = 57, 55.9%). Pre- and post-operative CT-angiograms were analysed. The primary endpoint was one-year survival. Secondary endpoints included post-operative morbidity, aortic diameter evolution, and freedom from a composite aortic event. Time-to-event endpoints were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.ResultsOne-year mortality was 27.5% (CAAR 28.9% vs. FET 26.3%; not statistically different in indication-specific subgroups). Mortality was driven by ATAAD (35.4%) and chronic dissection (30.8%); it was 4.2% in atheromatous aneurysm. In ATAAD, FET was associated with favourable aortic remodelling, with a 24.3% reduction in isthmus diameter compared with a 25.8% increase after CAAR (p = 0.0005). In chronic dissection, FET resulted in a 13.6% reduction in isthmus diameter at follow-up. In aneurysmal disease, FET stabilised aortic diameters whereas CAAR was associated with a 20.8% increase in isthmus diameter (p < 0.05). Reinterventions were more frequent after FET (42.1% vs. 17.8%); the great majority were planned staged TEVAR extensions.ConclusionIn experienced centres, the FET technique appears safe and is associated with favourable aortic remodelling. It represents a valuable option when downstream aortic disease or future endovascular extension is anticipated. Given the non-randomised design and indication imbalance, our data do not support a definitive claim of superiority over CAAR. Surgical strategy should remain anatomy- and indication-driven within a lifetime aortic management framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04381-6.Keywords: Cardiac surgery, Aortic arch, Aortic dissection, Frozen elephant trunk, Aortic remodelling

Introduction

Aortic arch pathologies present significant challenges for several reasons. (1) Aortic arch disease (AAD) is rarely isolated, typically occurring as part of a more extensive pan-aortic process [1]; treatment often requires addressing concomitant disease in the aortic root, ascending aorta and/or thoracoabdominal segments. (2) The patient profile undergoing aortic arch repair is highly variable, ranging from life-threatening emergencies to asymptomatic patients with chronic aneurysms benefiting from elective preventive surgery [2]. (3) Patients often present with a wide range of comorbidities and complex aortic anatomies. (4) AADs are chronic conditions, often evolving over time and frequently requiring further interventions [3].

Several techniques have been developed to address these complexities. Total aortic arch replacement (CAAR) with a straight graft was pioneered in 1957 [4], the elephant trunk concept introduced in 1983 [5], and the Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET) technique developed in 1996 [6] as an integrated solution for extensive aortic disease. Endovascular and hybrid options have since expanded the therapeutic armamentarium [7, 8]. Despite continuous improvements, AAD treatment remains high-risk, and a granular understanding of outcomes across techniques and indications is essential.

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients undergoing aortic arch repair at our institution, with indication-specific analyses (ATAAD, chronic dissection and atheromatous aneurysm) as the primary framework for comparison.

Methods

Study design and patient population

We conducted a monocentric, retrospective, observational study. Consecutive patients who underwent surgical aortic arch repair at our institution from January 2012 to June 2023 were included. Patients younger than 18 years or with congenital aortopathy were excluded. The need for informed consent was waived by our institutional ethics committee.

Data collection

Data were retrospectively retrieved from our institutional database and grouped into preoperative, operative and postoperative variables. Vital status was ascertained through the French national MatchID database; vital-status follow-up was complete. Pre- and post-operative aortic CT-angiograms were analysed with the Endosize® software (Therenva). Maximum aortic diameters were measured at predefined anatomical landmarks (aortic root, sino-tubular junction, mid-arch, isthmus, mid-descending aorta) by a single investigator, blinded to the surgical technique whenever possible. Presence and location of entry tears, endoleaks, patent false lumen and aneurysmal reperfusion were collected. Imaging follow-up was performed as part of routine clinical care; no protocolised study CT was scheduled.

Definitions

Preoperative renal impairment was defined as any of the following: documented chronic kidney disease, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (CKD-EPI) at admission, or acute kidney injury related to the index aortic event. Established CKD (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for more than three months) is reported separately.

Postoperative neurological events were classified following the consensus statement of the International Aortic Arch Surgery Study Group [9]. Grade ≥ II refers to a permanent or non-resolving deficit at hospital discharge. Spinal cord injury (SCI) was managed with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, used reactively during the early study period and prophylactically since 2020 for cases with extensive distal coverage or previous abdominal aortic surgery.

Aortic-related death was defined as death due to aortic rupture, aortic dissection extension, or early/late complications of aortic surgery (including bleeding, malperfusion or graft infection).

Surgical strategy and decision-making

Our institutional strategy evolved markedly during the study period and can be described in two phases. In the first phase (January 2012 to end of 2022), aortic arch cases were performed by several surgeons, each operating on a limited number of cases per year. The intraoperative choice between CAAR and FET was based on anatomical considerations on the preoperative CT-angiogram (extent of disease, descending aortic anatomy, distal landing zone, and contraindications to FET such as a landing zone < 26 mm, severe descending aortic tortuosity or true-lumen compression in chronic dissection), but was ultimately left to the operating surgeon’s discretion and experience. As a consequence, technique selection during this period was operator-dependent rather than protocolised.

In the second phase (from 2023 onwards), our institution restructured the aortic arch programme. Cases were centralised on two dedicated operators, planned and discussed within a structured multidisciplinary aortic team, and the FET technique became the near-exclusive default approach when arch replacement was indicated. In parallel, our technical strategy progressively shifted toward zone 2 distal anastomosis with systematic LSCA revascularisation (carotid-subclavian bypass) and a transition from “island” to selective supra-aortic vessel reimplantation. The FET prostheses used were mostly the E-vita Open Plus / E-vita Open Neo (Artivion). The Thoraflex Hybrid (Vascutek/Terumo Aortic) was used in fewer than 10% of cases. The distal landing zone targeted Th6 to Th8, with a stent length of 100 to 130 mm; oversizing was 10 to 15% relative to the true lumen in degenerative aneurysms and size-to-size in acute dissections.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was one-year survival, chosen because it captures both operative risk and the early post-discharge phase, during which most aortic arch-related deaths occur. Secondary endpoints were post-operative morbidity (defined per the International Aortic Arch Surgery Study Group consensus [9]), the evolution of aortic diameters between preoperative, postoperative and follow-up CT-angiograms, and freedom from a composite aortic event (aortic-related death, aortic rupture, aortic reintervention).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages) and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation, or median (interquartile range) when appropriate. Categorical variables were compared with the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables with the two-sample t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Time-to-event endpoints (overall survival, freedom from aortic reintervention and freedom from the composite aortic event) were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups with the log-rank test.

Given the heterogeneity of indications, indication-specific subgroup analyses (ATAAD, chronic dissection, atheromatous aneurysm) were considered the primary analytical framework. Pooled CAAR-vs-FET comparisons across the whole cohort are reported descriptively only and should be interpreted with caution because of marked between-group imbalance in indication. Given the limited size of each subgroup, the study is underpowered to demonstrate equivalence between techniques; absence of statistical significance does not imply equivalence.

All tests were two-sided, with α = 0.05. Analyses were performed with R (R Core Team).

Results

A total of 102 patients were included. Aortic arch repair was performed for ATAAD (n = 48), chronic aortic dissection (n = 26), atheromatous aneurysm (n = 24) and atypical pathologies (n = 4, mycotic aortic arch aneurysm, thoracic endograft infection). Forty-five patients (44.1%) underwent CAAR and 57 (55.9%) underwent FET (Table 1). The pooled one-year mortality was 27.5% (CAAR 28.9% vs. FET 26.3%); this pooled comparison should be interpreted descriptively given the marked indication imbalance (ATAAD: 73.3% of CAAR vs. 26.3% of FET; chronic dissection: 4.4% of CAAR vs. 42.1% of FET). Reinterventions were more frequent in the FET group (42.1% vs. 17.8%); their nature is detailed in the corresponding subgroup sections below.

Mortality breakdown by indication

Surgical procedures

Aortic root replacement was performed in 51.5% of CAAR and 66.7% of FET (p = 0.50). Aortic valve replacement rates were 33.3% (CAAR) and 20.0% (FET) (p = 0.50). Distal anastomosis was most often in zone 3 (64.6%), and supra-aortic vessel reimplantation by the “island” technique was more frequent in the FET group (80.0% vs. 33.3%; p = 0.007). Circulatory arrest, cross-clamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times did not differ statistically between groups.

Postoperative course

Major adverse events did not differ statistically between groups. Permanent neurological deficits (Grade ≥ II) occurred in 8 patients; one CAAR patient sustained spinal cord injury (Grade ≥ II). Postoperative CT-angiograms were similar between groups except at the isthmus, which was larger in the FET group (39.5 ± 6.5 mm vs. 33.8 ± 5.7 mm; p = 0.011). Three patients had a residual entry tear in the descending aorta, and 24 patients had a patent false lumen postoperatively (CAAR 54.5% vs. FET 40.0%; p = 0.13). Procedure-related new entry tears at the distal anastomosis were observed in CAAR patients only (4 cases, 12.1%). Thirty-day mortality was 35.4% (CAAR 42.4% vs. FET 20.0%); no additional deaths occurred between 30 days and 1 year. Aortic-related one-year mortality was 31% (15/48).

Follow-up and reinterventions

Mean follow-up was 3.5 ± 2.9 years. During follow-up, 20 patients (41.7%) died; 10 underwent aortic reintervention at a mean interval of 634 ± 734 days. Reinterventions were performed on the aortic arch in 1 CAAR patient, on the descending aorta in 5 patients (3 CAAR / 2 FET), on the abdominal aorta in 2 (1 CAAR / 1 FET), on the lower limbs in 2 CAAR patients, and on supra-aortic vessels in 3 patients (2 CAAR / 1 FET). After classification, 4 of 4 reinterventions in the FET group were planned staged TEVAR extensions; in the CAAR group, 4 of 6 were unplanned (false-lumen progression or new entry tear), 2 were elective.

Aortic diameter evolution

CAAR patients showed a 25.8% increase in isthmus diameter, compared with a 24.3% decrease in the FET group (p = 0.0005; Fig. 1). Descending aorta diameter increased by 21.7% in CAAR patients and decreased by 2.1% in the FET group (p = 0.036; Fig. 2).

Chronic aortic dissection

Demographics and preoperative data

Of 26 patients, 24 (92.3%) underwent FET and 2 underwent CAAR. The cohort was predominantly male (73.1%); mean age was 64.4 ± 12.0 years; hypertension was prevalent (88.5%). Preoperative entry tears were located in the ascending aorta (n = 3, 11.5%), aortic arch (n = 17, 65.4%) and descending aorta (n = 8, 30.8%). Mean aortic isthmus and descending aorta diameters were 52.8 ± 11.7 mm and 53.4 ± 13.3 mm, respectively.

Surgical procedures

Fifteen patients (57.7%) had previous cardiac surgery, mostly related to ATAAD. Aortic root replacement was performed in 15.4% of cases and aortic valve replacement in 11.5%. Distal anastomosis was in zone 3 in 88.5% of patients, and 80.8% had supra-aortic vessel reimplantation by the “island” technique.

Postoperative course

Postoperative neurological deficits (Grade ≥ II) occurred in 3 patients (11.5%), with one case of spinal cord injury (3.9%). Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (Grade III) was noted in 5 FET patients (20.8%). Re-exploration for bleeding was required in 9 patients (37.5%). CT-angiograms identified a residual descending entry tear in 5 patients (19.2%) and a patent false lumen in 11 (45.8%). Thirty-day mortality was 30.8%; aortic-related one-year mortality was 30.8%.

Follow-up and reinterventions

Mean follow-up was 3.7 ± 2.6 years; aortic-related death occurred in 10 patients (38.5%). Twelve patients (50%) underwent reintervention, all consisting of TEVAR (with extension below the renal arteries in 3). All 12 FET reinterventions were planned staged TEVAR extensions, consistent with the lifetime aortic management strategy. On follow-up CT-angiograms, 4 patients (16.7%) had a patent false lumen.

Aortic diameter evolution

A reduction in isthmus diameter (-13.6%) and descending aorta diameter (-6.1%) was observed between postoperative and follow-up CT-angiograms (Figs. 3 and 4).

Atheromatous aneurysm

Demographics and preoperative data

FET was performed in 16 patients (66.7%) and CAAR in 8 (33.3%). The sex ratio differed between groups (CAAR 12.5% vs. FET 68.8% male; p = 0.030). Patients in the CAAR group were older (mean 72.5 ± 9.4 vs. 63.3 ± 13.8 years; p = 0.071). Cardiovascular risk factors were prevalent (hypertension 83.3%, dyslipidaemia 37.5%, smoking 41.7%). Mean preoperative aortic diameters were: aortic root 37.5 ± 9.2 mm; supra-sinusal aorta 47.5 ± 11.2 mm; aortic arch 51.8 ± 16.1 mm; isthmus 40.5 ± 14.6 mm; descending aorta 46.9 ± 21.3 mm.

Surgical procedures

Previous cardiac surgery was reported in 33.3%. Concomitant aortic root replacement was performed in 25.0% and aortic valve replacement in 33.3%. Distal anastomosis was in zone 3 in 66.7% of cases, and 62.5% had supra-aortic vessel reimplantation by the “island” technique.

Postoperative course

Permanent neurological deficits (Grade ≥ II) occurred in 3 patients (12.5%); 2 FET patients sustained spinal cord injury (12.5%). Re-exploration for bleeding was required in 4 patients (2 CAAR / 2 FET; 25.0% vs. 12.5%; p = 0.016). CT-angiograms revealed aneurysmal reperfusion in 3 FET patients (18.8%, all with a descending aorta endoleak). Thirty-day mortality was 4.2%; aortic-related one-year mortality was 4.2%.

Discussion

This study analysed the outcomes of aortic arch repair across acute and chronic indications, comparing two main techniques: conventional aortic arch replacement (CAAR) and the Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET) technique. The FET technique can be conceptualised as a CAAR with an integrated distal endograft. Therefore, the relevant clinical question is: what are the advantages and disadvantages of integrating the distal endograft in this particular patient?

Does the endograft improve anastomotic sealing and bleeding control? FET may help control bleeding by providing additional structural support to the aortic wall. We did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in re-exploration for bleeding after FET compared with CAAR (15.6% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.36). The shorter operative times observed in the FET group may suggest improved intraoperative haemostasis. Our findings do not fully reproduce the results from previous literature [10] or the interim NEOS data [11], where re-exploration rates were lower. The 21.6% pooled rate in our cohort reflects an ATAAD-dominated case-mix, a substantial proportion of redo surgery (28.4%) and a low institutional threshold for re-exploration when chest tube output exceeds 200 mL/h.

Does the endograft increase the risk of spinal cord injury? Spinal cord injury is a recognised concern after FET surgery. With the implementation of a comprehensive spinal cord protection strategy following published recommendations [12], this risk can be kept at an acceptable level. In our cohort, 4 patients (3.9%) sustained Grade ≥ II SCI, three of them in the FET group (5.3%); two were transient and two were permanent. The SCI rate did not differ across indications. These figures are in line with previous literature [13].

Does the endograft reduce malperfusion in dissection? FET may stabilise the aorta and reduce malperfusion in dissection [14]; in our cohort, FET was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in postoperative malperfusion syndrome (24.2% vs. 33.3%, p > 0.05). Our study is underpowered to detect a moderate effect.

Does the endograft improve aortic remodelling? One of the most consistent advantages observed in our cohort was favourable aortic remodelling after FET [15]. Patients treated with FET showed greater reductions in aortic diameter, particularly in the isthmus and descending aorta, during follow-up compared with CAAR patients with ATAAD. This is consistent with previous literature reporting long-term remodelling benefits with FET [16] and with reduction in false-lumen patency and aneurysmal degeneration [17]. Given the variable timing of follow-up imaging and the absence of a competing-risk analysis for death, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Does the endograft prepare for future endovascular extension? Reinterventions were more frequent in the FET group than in the CAAR group; this finding is consistent with previous reports [18]. After classification, the great majority of FET reinterventions in our cohort were planned staged TEVAR extensions (12 of 12 in chronic dissection, 8 of 8 in aneurysmal disease, and 4 of 4 in ATAAD), reflecting deliberate use of FET as the proximal scaffold for downstream endovascular completion. Connective tissue disorders, DeBakey type and false lumen status were similar between reoperated and non-reoperated patients (Supplementary Table S1). The higher reintervention rate in the FET group should therefore be interpreted as a feature of a planned lifetime strategy rather than as a sign of technique failure.

Can FET always be performed when arch replacement is indicated? With current devices, models and techniques, very few situations preclude FET. The main contraindication is a distal landing zone < 26 mm, occasionally encountered in adolescents and young adults, or in chronic aortic dissection with a false lumen compressing the true lumen (where surgical fenestration may still allow FET implantation).

Should FET always be preferred when arch replacement is indicated? Our data suggest that, in experienced centres, FET can be performed with morbidity and one-year mortality comparable to CAAR in both acute and chronic settings. Reintervention rates were higher with FET but largely driven by planned staged TEVAR extension. Given the non-randomised design, indication imbalance and limited sample size, our results do not support a definitive claim of superiority. We believe FET is a valuable option when downstream aortic disease or future endovascular extension is anticipated, consistent with a lifetime aortic management framework [19]. The decision between CAAR and FET should remain anatomy- and indication-driven.

Mortality contextualisation

Our pooled one-year mortality (27.5%) is high in absolute terms but reflects a case-mix dominated by ATAAD (47%) and chronic dissection (25%, often after previous ATAAD repair), with 28% redo surgery. These figures are within the published range for tertiary European aortic centres operating on unselected ATAAD and complex chronic patients. In the aneurysmal subgroup, one-year mortality was 4.2%, in line with international benchmarks for elective aortic arch replacement.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single-centre, retrospective analysis, it is subject to selection and information bias and the findings may not generalise to centres with different surgical practices and patient management protocols. Second, the indication imbalance between groups (ATAAD: 73% CAAR vs. 26% FET; chronic dissection: 4% CAAR vs. 42% FET) precludes any unbiased pooled CAAR-vs-FET comparison. Indication-specific subgroup analyses were therefore the primary framework, but with 15 to 24 patients per subgroup the study is underpowered to demonstrate equivalence between techniques; absence of statistical significance does not imply equivalence. Third, our institutional strategy evolved markedly during the study period. In the first phase (2012 to 2022), cases were performed by several surgeons with limited individual case-volume, and technique selection was operator-dependent rather than protocolised. In 2023, the programme was restructured: cases were centralised on two dedicated operators, planned within a structured multidisciplinary aortic team, and FET became the near-exclusive default approach. In parallel, our technical strategy progressively shifted toward zone 2 distal anastomosis, systematic LSCA revascularisation when zone 2 was used, and a transition from island to selective supra-aortic vessel reimplantation. This dual evolution, organisational and technical, introduces a learning-curve and operator-effect bias that we cannot fully control for and that may favour the FET group, predominantly used during the second phase of the study. Fourth, follow-up imaging was not protocolised; CT timing was variable, which may bias the remodelling analysis. Fifth, no formal competing-risk analysis with death as a competing event was performed for reinterventions or remodelling, as group sizes are too small to support reliable estimation. Despite these limitations, our results reflect real-life practice in a high-volume European tertiary aortic centre, and the heterogeneity of our cohort offers an integrated view of the complexity of contemporary aortic arch surgery.

Conclusion

In experienced centres, the FET technique can be performed with morbidity and one-year mortality comparable to CAAR, and is associated with favourable aortic remodelling, particularly in ATAAD and chronic dissection. FET appears as a valuable option when an extended distal repair or future endovascular extension is anticipated. Given the higher reintervention rate (mostly planned staged TEVAR), the non-randomised design and the limited sample size, our results do not support a definitive claim of superiority of FET over CAAR. The choice between techniques should remain anatomy- and indication-driven, integrated within a lifetime aortic management strategy in experienced centres.

In experienced centres, the Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET) appears safe and is associated with morbidity and one-year mortality comparable to conventional aortic arch replacement (CAAR) and with favourable aortic remodelling, particularly in acute and chronic dissection.

Perspective statement

FET is a valuable option for aortic arch repair when downstream aortic disease or future endovascular extension is anticipated. Given the non-randomised design, group imbalance and the higher rate of (mostly planned, staged) reinterventions, our data do not support a definitive claim of superiority over CAAR. The choice between techniques should remain anatomy- and indication-driven, integrated within a lifetime aortic management strategy in experienced centres.
