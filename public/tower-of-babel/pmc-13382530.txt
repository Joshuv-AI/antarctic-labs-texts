Human Immunodeficiency Virus Type 2 Vpx-mediated Degradation of TASOR Activates Innate Immune Sensing of Nucleic Acids in Infected Cells
Authors: Heather M Hanson, Madeline R Damkot, Louis M Mansky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe HIV-2/SIVsm lineage of lentiviruses encodes for Vpx, which is a virus-associated protein that is absent from HIV-1. Vpx enhances HIV-2 replication by degrading host proteins that possess antiviral activity. For example, Vpx is known to degrade the host dNTPase SAMHD1 (which reduces dNTP levels to inhibit viral DNA synthesis in macrophages) and components of the epigenetic regulator complex HUSH (where degradation enhances proviral gene expression and viral replication). Here, we used a transcriptomic analysis to investigate host gene expression response to HIV-2 infection of THP-1 cells in the presence and absence of Vpx. Analysis of HIV-2 WT and ΔVpx infected cells revealed an increase of gene expression of several interferon stimulating genes (ISGs), with an increase in ISG expression in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to that of HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells. Analysis of Vpx mutants implicated that Vpx-mediated induction of ISG expression was due to Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR, one protein of the HUSH complex. Based upon these observations, we sought to determine whether Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR and subsequent inhibition of HUSH complex activity induces ISG expression. We found that TASOR degradation by Vpx led to increased LINE-1 activity. Furthermore, we found that Vpx-mediated ISG induction relied on both RNA sensing that was mediated by MAVS signaling and DNA sensing mediated by cGAS/STING signaling. Taken together, these findings support a model in which Vpx-mediated inhibition of the HUSH complex is a key variable how Vpx affects the host response to HIV-2 replication in monocytic cells.Keywords: lentivirus, transcriptome, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome

Introduction

HIV-1 and HIV-2 are closely related lentiviruses but are genetically distinct and emerged from distinct spillover events into humans. There are four distinct lineages of HIV-1 that are classified in groups M, N, O, and P. Each was due to an independent spillover of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV). Group N and M HIV-1 strains are derived from SIV from chimpanzees (SIVcpz) [1–3], while group P strains originate from SIV from gorilla (SIVgor) [4]. The origin of HIV-1 group O remains unclear [5,6]. There are 9 distinct lineages of HIV-2 which each emerged in humans from distinct spillover events of a SIV from sooty mangabeys (SIVsm) [7]. Due to the different non-human lentivirus origins, the HIV-1 and HIV-2 genomes share only 55% sequence identity. Both viruses contain many conserved genes encoding a polyprotein (Gag-Pol) that contains the Gag structural protein and the viral enzymes (i.e., reverse transcriptase, protease, integrase) that are required for virus replication. Both viruses express Tat and Rev, which allow for transcriptional activation of the integrated provirus and export of the genome out of the nucleus, respectively. Both HIV-1 and HIV-2 encode for the accessory proteins Vif, Vpr and Nef, but only HIV-1 encodes for Vpu and only HIV-2 encodes for Vpx. Differences in genome organization likely contribute to the distinct infectivity and pathogenicity of these viruses. For example, HIV-1 Vpu is an accessory protein that can inhibit NFκB signaling [8], while the HIV-2 Vpx protein can promote HIV-2 replication by targeting host cell restriction factors for proteasomal degradation, inhibiting NF-κB signaling, and promoting nuclear import of the viral genome [9–11].

While Vpx can promote replication, there may be antiviral consequences of Vpx expression. For instance, Vpx has been shown to induce interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression [12]. Furthermore, Vpx-mediated ISG expression may be linked to DBB1 and CUL4 associated factor 1 (DCAF1) binding [12]. The interaction of Vpx with DCAF1, a receptor for the E3 ubiquitin ligase CRL4, has been shown to mediate proteasomal degradation of host cell proteins. Two key cellular proteins that are targeted for degradation by Vpx are sterile alpha motif and histidine/aspartic acid domain containing protein 1 (SAMHD1), an HIV restriction factor, and transcription activation suppressor (TASOR), one of the components (along with MPP8 and periphilin) of the human silencing hub (HUSH) complex [13–21]. The HUSH complex protects the host genome from invasion of retroelements and regulates gene expression by recruiting the H3K9me3 methyltransferase, SETDB1 [16,22]. Inhibition of the HUSH complex has also been reported to increase ISG expression [23].

In this study, we have used a transcriptomic analysis to investigate the host gene expression response to HIV-2 infection of THP-1 cells in the presence and absence of Vpx. Analysis of HIV-2 WT and ΔVpx infected cells revealed an increase of gene expression of several ISGs, with an increase in ISG expression in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to that of HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells. Analysis of Vpx mutants implicated that Vpx-mediated induction of ISG expression was due to Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR, one of the proteins in the HUSH complex. Based upon these observations, we sought to test the hypothesis that Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR and subsequent inhibition of HUSH complex activity may induce ISG expression. We report that TASOR degradation by Vpx led to increased LINE-1 activity and Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation induction of ISG expression was observed to rely on both RNA sensing that was mediated by MAVS signaling and by DNA sensing mediated by STING signaling. Collectively, our observations help to support a model in which Vpx-mediated inhibition of the HUSH complex represents an important aspect for how Vpx affects the host cell response to HIV-2 replication in monocytic cells.

Results

HIV-2 infection induces ISG expression in a Vpx-dependent manner

To investigate the consequences of Vpx expression during HIV-2 infection, transcriptome analysis of PMA-treated, THP-1 cells infected with ROD14 HIV-2 (either WT virus or a ΔVpx mutant) was conducted. THP-1 cells were differentiated to a macrophage-like state for transcriptome analysis after 64 h PMA treatment. To estimate the relative rate of infection of THP-1 cells infected with WT or ΔVpx ROD14 HIV-2, immunofluorescence staining was used to determine the proportion of cells that were positive for HIV-2 protein gp120. Across three biological replicates, 26% of HIV-2 WT infected cells were gp120 positive and 27% of HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells were gp120 positive (Supplemental Figure 1). Two differential expression analyses were conducted, one comparing gene expression in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to mock infected cells and another comparing gene expression in HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells relative to mock infected cells. In both differential expression analyses, a total of 641 genes were identified as differentially expressed with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 and adjusted p-value less than 0.05 in cells infected with HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 ΔVpx relative to mock infected cells (Figure 1A and B). There were 253 genes that were differentially expressed in both differential expression analyses. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was used to summarize the gene expression changes in HIV-2 infected cells by identifying biological pathways that are enriched for significantly upregulated genes from the RNAseq analysis. This analysis identified enrichment of GO terms that are indicative of the general response to virus infection, such as the inflammatory response, the apoptotic process, and the positive regulation of gene expression for both HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells (Figure 1C and D). The GO analysis also reported enrichment of GO terms relating to several different innate immune sensors unique to either the HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells (Figure 1E and F). Of note, in HIV-2 WT infected cells there was enrichment of GO terms involving the regulation of the RIG-I signaling pathway. In HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells, GO analysis indicated enrichment of genes involved in the positive regulation of MDA-5 signaling pathway (Figure 1E and F).

The observed gene expression changes of individual ISGs from cells infected with either HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 ΔVpx relative to mock infected cells further support that Vpx induces ISG expression. In this regard, in cells infected with either HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 ΔVpx, there were 46 ISGs from a list of 422 genes designated as ISGs that were differentially expressed relative to that observed from mock infected cells. Among the 46 ISGs, all but two were upregulated relative to that observed from mock infected cells. To directly compare expression changes in WT and ΔVpx HIV-2 infected cells we normalized the log2 fold change in gene expression determined by RNAseq analysis to Gag expression determined by RT-qPCR because Gag expression was higher in ΔVpx HIV-2 infected cells despite prior normalization (Supplemental Figure 1). This analysis demonstrates greater relative induction of select ISGs in WT HIV-2 infected cells than ΔVpx HIV-2 infected cells (Figure 1G). IL6 and MT1G RNA abundance were significantly increased 2.57 and 8.05 times respectively in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to cells infected with HIV-2 ΔVpx (Figure 1G). The observation that there was a greater upregulation of ISGs in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to that of HIV-2 ΔVpx supports the conclusion that Vpx activity plays a role in inducing ISG expression from HIV-2 infection. To further validate this, we measured gene expression of select ISGs by using the same RNA samples from WT and ΔVpx HIV-2 infected THP-1 cells used from the transcriptome analysis. In particular, we used RT-qPCR to measure the gene expression changes of CCL5, Interferon β (IFNβ), IFIT1, IFIT2, MT1G, and OAS2 from HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells relative to that of mock infected cells, normalized to β-actin gene expression [12]. Specifically, the fold changes of MT1G and OAS2 were found to be 2.76 and 2.43 times greater in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT than that observed from cells infected with HIV-2 ΔVpx (Figure 1H). This analysis helped to validate the transcriptome analysis and helps provide further support of the conclusion that HIV-2 Vpx induces ISG expression.

HIV-2 induction of ISG expression is a consequence of Vpx mediated TASOR degradation

Our transcriptome analysis is limited in attributing Vpx mediated ISG induction to any specific Vpx function. One possible explanation for reduced ISG expression in HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells is reduced HIV-2 integration due to SAMHD1 mediated dNTP depletion. This possibility cannot be directly ruled out without measuring relative integration efficiency for HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 ΔVpx. Induction of ISGs mediated by HIV-2 Vpx has been previously noted, where this activity is independent of Vpx-mediated degradation of SAMHD1 but dependent on Vpx-DCAF1 binding [12]. This observation suggests that Vpx-mediated proteasomal degradation of another host cell protein may result in increased ISG expression. Notably, it has been previously reported that inhibition of the HUSH complex induces ISG expression due to an increase in dsRNA sensing from increased LINE-1 activity [23]. This suggests that Vpx antagonism of the HUSH complex via TASOR degradation could help provide an explanation for Vpx-dependent induction of ISG expression.

To test whether Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation plays a role in ISG induction, we analyzed ISG expression in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 with a mutation in Vpx that inhibits Vpx mediated TASOR degradation (HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA). To validate reduced Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation of HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA we detected TASOR protein levels by immunoblot in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2. There was no change in TASOR protein levels in cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to cells infected with HIV-2 ΔVpx (Figure 2A and B). TASOR protein abundance was reduced in cells infected with HIV-2 WT that was produced in cells overexpressing WT Vpx relative to HIV-2 WT produced in the absence of Vpx overexpression (Figure 2A and B). Because there was no apparent TASOR degradation in cells infected with HIV-2 produced in the absence of Vpx overexpression all experiments were done using HIV-2 produced in the presence of Vpx overexpression. There was no significant difference in TASOR protein abundance in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA produced in the presence or absence of Vpx QV47,48AA (Figure 2A and B). Together these data validate Vpx mediated degradation of TASOR in our study by HIV-2 WT Vpx and confirm that QV47,48AA amino acid substitutions inhibit Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation. The infectivity of HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA produced in cells with Vpx overexpression and HIV-2 ΔVpx was measured to ensure differences in ISG expression are not due to different rates of infectivity. Infectivity was measured by detecting fluorescent reporter proteins expressed by HIV-2 vectors using flow cytometry. There was no significant difference in the proportion of cells expressing fluorescent reporter proteins across HIV-2 ΔVpx, HIV-2 WT, and HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA produced in cells in the presence or absence of Vpx (Figure 2C).

To assess Vpx-mediated ISG induction, we monitored ISG expression induction by using a THP-1 reporter cell line that expresses a luminescence reporter, Lucia, under the control of several ISG response elements (ISRE). Cells infected with HIV-2 WT had an increase in luminescence relative to that of mock infected cells, providing evidence that there was an ISG response to HIV-2 infection (Figure 2D). Relative to HIV-2 WT infected cells, cells infected with HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA had reduced ISG expression (Figure 2D). Because HIV-2 ROD14 Vpx can also degrade host protein SAMHD1 we conducted the ISRE assay in THP-1 cells stably expressing shRNA targeting SAMHD1. We confirmed that SAMHD1 expression was reduced at the RNA and protein level by using RT-qPCR and immunoblot respectively in shSAMHD1 THP-1 cells relative to controls cells expressing a scrambled shRNA (Supplemental Figure 1). A similar trend in ISG expression was observed in THP-1 shSAMHD1 cells and WT cells. Specifically, there was induction in ISG expression in shSAMHD1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT and ISG expression was reduced in HIV-2 QV47,48AA infected shSAMHD1 cells (Figure 2E). This suggests that Vpx mediated induction of ISG expression is independent of SAMHD1 degradation.

Measuring the gene expression of specific ISGs also provided evidence that Vpx mediated inhibition of the HUSH complex induces ISG expression. Expression of ISGs, IFNβ, IFIT1, IFIT2, CCL5 and OAS2 was detected in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 by using RT-qPCR. IFIT1 and OAS2 RNA levels were increased in THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to mock infected cells (Figure 2F and G). In THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA the relative abundance of IFIT1 and OAS2 mRNA was significantly reduced relative to HIV-2 WT infected cells (Figure 2F and G). These results add support to the observations made using the ISG reporter cell line and suggest that Vpx mediated inhibition of the HUSH complex induces a response of the innate immune system.

To further investigate whether Vpx induction of ISG expression is mediated by TASOR degradation we measured ISG expression in 293 T cells stably expressing a shRNA targeting TASOR. We confirmed that stable shTASOR RNA expression reduced TASOR expression at the RNA and protein level by using RT-qPCR and immunoblot respectively (Supplemental Figure 1). Expression of specific ISGs including IFNβ, IFIT1, IFIT2, CCL5 and OAS2 was detected by using RT-qPCR. In cells expressing a control nontargeting shRNA (shSCR) IFNβ, IFIT1, and CCL5 mRNA abundance was increased in HIV-2 infected cells relative to mock infected cells (Figure 2H–J). This recapitulates ISG induction in HIV-2 infected cells observed in THP-1 cells. In shTASOR expressing cells there was no increase of IFNβ, IFIT1, and CCL5 mRNA abundance in HIV-2 infected cells relative to mock infected cells (Figure 2H–J). The lack of induction of a subset of ISGs in TASOR knockdown cells and in cells infected with a TASOR degradation mutant HIV-2 suggest that Vpx mediated induction of ISG expression is a result of Vpx targeting TASOR for proteasomal degradation. We also compared the expression of ISGs that were induced in a Vpx-dependent manner in mock infected shSCR and shTASOR cells. IFIT1 and OAS2 mRNA abundance was significantly increased about 5-fold and 10-fold respectively in shTASOR cells relative to control cells (Figure 2K). There was no change in IFNβ, CCL5 or IFIT2 expression in mock infected TASOR KD cells relative to control cells (Figure 2K). Increased IFIT1 and OAS2 expression in mock infected TASOR knockdown cells relative to control cells suggest that lack of TASOR expression can lead to increased ISG expression independent of HIV-2 infection. However, the expression of other ISGs was not increased in mock infected cells with reduced TASOR expression which implies additional requirements for Vpx-mediated ISG expression.

It has been previously shown that Vpx expression in cells alone does not induce ISG expression [12], and that TASOR inhibition more significantly induces ISG expression when cells were treated with IFNβ relative to untreated cells [23]. While IFIT1 and OAS2 expression was increased in TASOR knockdown cells in the absence of HIV-2 infection, the expression of IFNβ, CCL5, and IFIT2 was not increased independent of HIV-2 infection. This suggests that HIV-2 innate immune system priming perhaps due to the presence of the HIV-2 genome may support ISG induction associated with TASOR degradation. To test whether innate immune system priming plays a role in Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR inducing ISG expression we treated cells with IFNβ to prime the innate immune system and treated cells with virus-like particles (VLPs) containing Vpx. We measured IFIT2 mRNA abundance by RT-qPCR in THP-1 cells treated with IFNβ and virus-like particles carrying Vpx WT or Vpx QV47,48AA. No significant increase of IFIT2 mRNA levels was observed in cells with Vpx WT or Vpx QV47,48AA without IFNβ treatment. Relative to empty VLPs and to VLPs with Vpx QV47,48AA in THP-1 cells, delivery of Vpx WT led to a greater increase in IFIT2 RNA abundance in IFNβ-treated cells relative to that of untreated cells (Figure 3). These data suggest that immune system priming contributes to Vpx-mediated ISG induction in HIV-2 infected cells. Whether HIV-2 infection priming of the innate immune system has the same effect as IFNβ priming requires further investigation.

Vpx-mediated inhibition of the HUSH complex increases nucleic acid sensing

Given that HIV-2 Vpx induction of ISG expression correlates to Vpx mediated degradation of TASOR, we sought a mechanism that would induce ISG expression in response to TASOR degradation. It has been previously concluded that HUSH inhibition can induce ISG expression due to increased LINE-1 activity which led to an increase in the generation of dsRNAs in the cytoplasm, and therefore dsRNA sensing, which can lead to elevated ISG expression [23].

To determine whether Vpx associated induction of ISGs is due to HUSH complex inhibition, LINE-1 activity was measured in cells infected with either HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA. Changes in LINE-1 activity were assessed by comparing the amount of LINE-1 mRNA detected by RT-qPCR using primers targeting the 5′ end of the LINE-1 genome and located in ORF1. THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT expressed greater amounts of LINE-1 mRNA than mock infected cells (Figure 4A). This suggests that HIV-2 Vpx presence in cells is associated with increased LINE-1 transcription. Additionally we provide evidence that Vpx associated increased LINE-1 activity is due to Vpx mediated degradation of TASOR and therefore inhibition of the HUSH complex as cells infected with HIV-2 encoding Vpx QV47,48AA also has reduced LINE-1 mRNA abundance relative to HIV-2 WT (Figure 4A). To ensure increased LINE-1 mRNA abundance in HIV-2 WT infected cells is due to HIV-2 infection we also detected LINE-1 mRNA in cells treated with HIV reverse transcriptase (RT) inhibitor Azidothymidine (AZT) 2 h before infection. AZT treatment led to about a 90% reduction in the proportion of infected cells infected with HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 QV47,48AA relative to DMSO treated cells (Figure 4B). Relative to DMSO treated mock infected cells there was no increase in LINE-1 mRNA abundance in mock infected, HIV-2 WT infected or HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48 AA infected cells treated with 1 μM AZT (Figure 4A). Increased LINE-1 transcription due to Vpx degradation of HUSH complex component TASOR, may explain the Vpx-mediated induction of ISG expression as increased LINE-1 activity has been previously shown to induce RNA and DNA innate immune sensing, though the precise nucleic acid products that are detected are unknown [24,25].

Next, we analyzed the role of nucleic acid sensing pathways in Vpx-mediated ISG expression. To do this, ISG expression changes were measured via the ISRE luminescence reporter assay in cells without a key protein in the dsRNA sensing pathways – i.e., mitochondrial anti-viral signaling protein (MAVS), melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) – and cells without DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING), an adaptor protein downstream of sensors including cGAS. We identified a statistically significant decrease in ISRE reporter activity in HIV-2 WT-infected MAVS knockout cells compared to that of WT cells (Figure 5A). These data support a role for MAVS signaling and therefore RNA sensing in the Vpx-mediated induction of ISG expression. The ISRE reporter activity was significantly increased in MDA5 KO cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to WT THP-1 cells infected with HIV-2 WT and there was no significant difference in ISRE reporter activity in RIG-I KO cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to HIV-2 infected WT THP-1 cells (Figure 5A). In MDA5 and RIG-I KO cells we detected increased ISRE reporter activity in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA infected cells (Figure 5A). There was no significant difference in ISG expression detected in the ISRE reporter assay in MAVS knockout cells infected with HIV-2 WT or HIV-2 QV47,48AA (Figure 5A). These data indicate that TASOR degradation dependent Vpx-mediated ISG induction is maintained in cells without MDA5 and RIG-I. These observations suggest that dsRNA sensing by RIG-I and MDA5 alone is not sufficient for TASOR degradation dependent Vpx-mediated ISG induction. The comparison of ISRE activity in STING knockout cells also suggested a role of DNA sensing in HIV-2 Vpx-mediated ISG expression. As was observed in MAVS knockout cells, in STING knockout cells infected with HIV-2 WT there was a decrease in ISG induction compared to WT cells, as measured by ISRE reporter activity (Figure 5A). In STING knock out cells infected with HIV-2 WT there was no change in ISRE luminescence relative to WT cells infected with HIV-2 WT and the level of ISG expression in STING knockout cells infected with HIV-2 WT was indistinguishable from STING knockout cells infected with HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA (Figure 5A). These results suggest that STING signaling also is required for Vpx-induced ISG expression. Because STING has also been reported to be involved in the MAVS pathway, we repeated the infections in cGAS knockout cells which has not been reported to interact with in the MAVS signaling pathway. Relative to WT cells infected with WT HIV-2, there was reduced ISG expression in cGAS knock cells infected with HIV-2 WT (Figure 5A). In cGAS knockout cells there was no difference in the level of ISG induction when cells were infected with HIV-2 WT or QV47,48AA and there was no significant difference in ISRE luminescence signal in WT or cGAS knockout cells infected with HIV-2 QV47,48AA (Figure 5A).

To confirm that ISG expression changes measured by ISRE activity in the luminescence assay are due to events associated with HIV-2 replication we measured ISRE activity in cells treated with 1 uM AZT. In all cell lines AZT treatment reduced infectivity by at least 80% for both HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 QV47,48AA (Figure 5B). In all THP-1 KO cell lines except for RIG-I KO cells there was no induction of ISG expression in WT cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to cells infected with HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA (Figure 5A). This suggests that the ISG response in cells that were not treated with AZT is specific to HIV-2 replication. The increased ISRE activity in AZT treated THP-1 RIG-I KO cells infected with HIV-2 WT relative to HIV-2 Vpx QV47,48AA suggests that the observed ISG response in cells that are not treated with AZT is not specific to HIV-2 replication. Therefore, the role of RIG-I in TASOR degradation dependent, Vpx-mediated ISG expression requires further investigation. These data suggest that both RNA and DNA sensing pathways activated by increased LINE-1 expression due to Vpx mediated TASOR degradation induce ISG expression.

Discussion

The host cell response to viral infection is complex and involves a wide array of changes in cellular gene expression. In this study, we utilized transcriptome analysis to help provide insights into host cell gene expression changes due to HIV-2 infection of macrophage-like THP-1 cells in the presence and absence of the viral accessory protein Vpx. Transcriptome analysis of cells infected with either HIV-2 WT or ΔVpx revealed an increase of gene expression of several ISGs, with an increase in ISG expression in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to that which was observed with HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells. Because Vpx has several functions that could induce ISG expression we investigated the innate immune response in cells infected with HIV-2 with different Vpx phenotypes. The analysis of ISG expression in cells infected with HIV-2 with a mutation in Vpx that inhibits TASOR degradation (QV47,48AA) and cells with reduced TASOR protein levels led to the observation that Vpx-mediated induction of ISG expression was due to Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR (a member of the HUSH complex). Additionally, Vpx-mediated ISG induction due to TASOR degradation was maintained in cells with reduced SAMHD1 protein abundance. Our findings suggest that to an extent, priming of the innate immune system is needed for Vpx-induced ISG expression. More experimentation is needed to further investigate this mechanism as the expression of some ISGs was increased independent of HIV-2 infection.

TASOR degradation by Vpx was found to lead to increased LINE-1 mRNA levels. Furthermore, we found that ISG induction due to Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation relies on both RNA sensing mediated by MAVS signaling and DNA sensing mediated by cGAS/STING signaling. However, our investigation demonstrated that neither RIG-I nor MDA5 alone mediated TASOR degradation dependent, Vpx-mediated ISG induction. Vpx-mediated ISG induction associated with increased LINE-1 activity in HIV-2 infected cells. Gene ontology analysis of differentially expressed genes in HIV-2 WT infected cells relative to mock infected cells indicated an enrichment of genes involved in RIG-I regulation, suggesting that RIG-I involved signaling is important in Vpx-mediated ISG induction in HIV-2 infected cells. We suggest a model in which Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation induces ISG expression via MAVS and cGAS/STING nucleic acid sensing of LINE-1 replication products. TASOR degradation is predicted to reduce HUSH complex epigenetic silencing that could increase LINE-1 replication. Increased LINE-1 retrotransposon activity has been shown to increase dsRNA production as well as lead to the accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA [24–26]. The RNA or DNA species detected by the innate immune system and the specific mechanisms by which they are detected to result in Vpx-mediated ISG induction remain an open question to be addressed in future studies.

The observations made in this study are in good agreement with other studies that have shown that HIV-2 and SIV Vpx can induce ISG expression [12,27]. A recent transcriptomic analysis of THP-1 cells expressing HIV-2 Vpx reported differentially expressed genes involved in the type I interferon signaling pathway among others [28]. Furthermore, other studies have shown that Vpx induction of ISG expression is mediated by DCAF1 binding but is independent of SAMHD1 degradation [12]. Interestingly, inhibition of each of the HUSH complex components (i.e., TASOR, MPP8, and Periphilin) have been previously shown to increase ISG induction independent of HIV-2 infection [23]. In particular, MPP8 inhibition had the greatest impact on ISG expression relative to that of the other HUSH complex components. TASOR inhibition slightly reduced induction of ISG expression, which further supports the requirement for HIV-2 priming of the innate immune system for Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation to induce ISG expression.

Two recent studies have reported a novel function for TASOR paralog, TASOR2. They demonstrate that TASOR2 interacts with IRF2 and recruits HUSH complex components MPP8 and Periphilin to form the HUSH2 complex which represses ISG expression [29,30]. This work shows competition between HUSH and HUSH2 for shared components MPP8 and Periphilin, which predicts ISG repression in response to Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation increasing the availability of MPP8 and Periphilin to form the HUSH2 complex. However, HUSH2 ISG repression and LINE-1 activity driving ISG expression from cGAS/STING and MAVS signaling are not mutually exclusive. While it has been reported that Vpx expression represses known HUSH2 targeted ISGs including, IFIH1, TAP1, B2M, DDX60, and DDX60L [30], these genes were not identified as having Vpx dependent upregulation in this study. cGAS/STING and MAVS signaling activates IRF3 mediated transcription, therefore it is possible a different set of ISGs is activated by TASOR degradation. Additionally, our study and others demonstrate that Vpx expression alone does not induce ISG expression and that some innate immune priming from HIV-2 infection is required [12]. It remains to be determined whether Vpx can target TASOR2 for proteasomal degradation. TASOR2 is made up of similar domains that are organized in a similar order as TASOR and Alpha-Fold modeling predicts similar structures of key domains [29,30]. If Vpx can also target TASOR2 for proteasomal degradation Vpx-mediated ISG induction in HIV-2 infected cells may also be due to loss of HUSH2 mediated ISG repression.

Induction of ISG expression due to Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation proposes an interesting fitness tradeoff for HIV-2. TASOR degradation can limit restriction of HIV-2 gene expression due to HUSH complex silencing of transcription from the HIV-2 LTR [16,20,21]. However, data in the present study suggests Vpx mediated TASOR degradation induces ISG expression. However, further testing is required to test the hypothesis that ISG expression induced by Vpx-mediated TASOR degradation negatively impacts HIV-2 replication. Another possibility is that other Vpx functions that are mediated by regions that overlap with residues involved in TASOR degradation can also promote HIV-2 fitness and offset the consequences of increased ISG expression. Notably, SAMHD1 has been reported to negatively regulate the innate immune response [31]. This would suggest that Vpx inhibition of SAMHD1 could also contribute to induction of ISG expression. However, our results and that of others demonstrate a greater change in ISG expression when TASOR degradation is inhibited rather than SAMHD1 degradation. It is important to note that not all HIV-2 strains encode for a Vpx protein that is capable of degrading SAMHD1. While the HIV-2 strain used in this study (ROD14) does encode for a Vpx protein that targets SAMHD1 for proteasomal degradation the relationship between Vpx mediated proteasomal degradation of host proteins and ISG expression could differ depending on strain. Additionally, a recent study reported that Vpx proteins from various HIV-2 strains have different rates of TASOR degradation [32]. This study reported that HIV-2 Rod Vpx does target TASOR for degradation. HIV-1 does not encode for Vpx and HIV-1 infection has not been reported to inhibit the HUSH complex. While HIV-1 and HIV-2 have many similarities, HIV-2 globally does not infect as many individuals as HIV-1 and this has been attributed, in part, to the lower viral loads in infected individuals [33–35]. The tradeoff of HIV-2 Vpx-induced ISG expression due to HUSH complex inhibition could be one factor that contributes to reduced HIV-2 viral loads in vivo in comparison to HIV-1. Future studies of Vpx-mediated degradation of TASOR will help further establish how Vpx contributes to HIV-2 replication.

Materials and Method s

Cell culture, cell lines and plasmids

The cell lines used in this study included WT THP-1 cells (ATCC), which were provided as a gift from Dr. Nathaniel Landau (New York University), THP1-Lucia ISG cells (Invivogen), THP1-Dual KO-STING cells (Invivogen), THP1-Dual KO-cGAS cells (Invivogen), THP1-Dual KO-MAVS (Invivogen), THP1-Dual KO-MDA5 (Invivogen), and THP1-Dual KO-RIGI (Invivogen). TASOR and SAMHD1 knockdown cells were made by transducing 293 T and THP-1 cells with shRNA in the pLKO.1 vector, a gift from David Root (Addgene plasmid #10878; https://n2t.net/addgene:10879; RRID:Addgene_10879) [36], according to the manufacturer protocol. The following DNA oligos were annealed and cloned into the pLKO.1 TRC-vector, TASOR F 5′ CCGGGAGG AAGCTTGAGGATCTACTCGAGTAGATCCTCA AGCTTCCTCTTTTTG 3′, TASOR F 5′ AATTCAAAAAGAGGAAGC TTGAGGATCTACTCGAGTAGATCCTCAAGCTTCCTC 3′, SAMHD1 F 5′ CCGGGCAGATGACTA CATAGAGATTCTCGAGAATCTCTATGTAGTCATCTGCTTTTTG 3′, and SAMHD1 R 5′ AATTCAAAAAGCAGATG ACTACATAGAGATTCTCGAGAATCTCTATGTAGTCATCTGC 3′. Cells were transduced with the pLKO.1 scramble shRNA vector, a gift from David Sabatini (Addgene plasmid #1864; https://n2t.net/addgene:1864; RRID:Addgene 1864) [37], to make negative control shRNA knockdown cells. All THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Life Technologies, Grand Island NY) supplemented with 10% Hyclone Fetal Clone 3 (FC3) serum (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Logan UT) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Life Technologies, Grand Island NY). The 293 T cells (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM (Mediatech, Vanassas VA) supplemented with 10% FC3 and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. shRNA knock down cells were maintained in media with 1 μg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich). Replication-competent HIV-2 was produced from a full-length infectious HIV-2 molecular clone, pROD14, a gift from Dr. Keith Peden (Food and Drug Administration, Rockville, MD). The HIV-2 ΔVpx derivative was created from pROD14 by introducing a frame shift in the vpx gene that has been previously described to interfere with Vpx expression [38]. A HIV-2 vector in which the env gene was deleted and in its place is a reporter gene cassette consisting of mCherry-IRES-GFP was produced from the previously described pROD-MIG plasmid [39]. A HIV-2 ΔVpx vector was produced from pROD-MIG with the Vpx mutations M1T and M62STOP introduced. The HIV-2 vector Vpx mutation QV47,48AA was introduced into the pROD-MIG plasmid with the corresponding substitutions introduced by using Gibson assembly. For experiments in which Vpx expression was done from an expression construct, Vpx was expressed from the expression vector, pTARGET HIV-2 Vpx. The Vpx QV47,48AA amino acid substitutions were introduced by using Gibson assembly. A codon-optimized HIV-2 Gag expression construct, pN3 HIV-2 Gag, has been described previously [40]. VSV-G was produced from pHCMV-G plasmid, which was a kind gift from J. Burns (University of California, San Diego).

Infection of cells with HIV-2 or HIV-2 vectors

Infectious virus stocks of HIV-2 were produced by transfecting 293 T cells with pROD14 molecular clone by using PEI at a 1:3 wt to volume ratio. At 48 h post transfection, cell culture supernatants were filtered by passing through a 0.22 μm filter and concentrated 1,000× at 10,000g for 3 h by using a JA-25.50 rotor (Beckman). Concentrated supernatant was treated with DNase for 1 h at 37 C. Sixteen hours prior to infection, THP-1 cells that were seeded at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL were activated with 50 nM phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA, Calbiochem). THP-1 cells were then infected with HIV-2 stocks normalized by immunoblot detection of p24 by using an HIV-1 p24 antibody (6G9) (SantaCruz Biotechnology). 48 h post infection immunofluorescence was done to detect infection by using an HIV-2 ST gp120 polyclonal serum (NIH HIV Reagent Program). For single-cycle infectivity assays using the HIV-2 vector (or Vpx mutant derivatives) virus was produced in 293 T cells by transfecting pROD-MIG, pTARGET HIV-2 Vpx, and VSV-G at a 6:4:1 ratio using PEI. Vector virus was collected, concentrated, and normalized as described above. Cells, either WT THP-1 or the THP1-Lucia ISG, were plated at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL and were infected by spin-inoculating at 1,200g for 1 h (SW4750A, Beckman). When indicated cells were incubated with DMSO or 1 μM Azidothymidine (AZT, Cayman Chemical Company) for two hours before infection. Infection was determined at 48 h through detection of mCherry and GFP reporter gene expression by using Cytek Aurora flow cytometer.

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriptome analysis was conducted on differentiated, macrophage-like THP-1 cells by using RNAseq (Azenta Life Sciences) to conduct differential gene expression analysis of gene expression in infected cells relative to mock infected cells (parallel experiments done in triplicate). Total RNA was extracted from differentiated THP-1 cells after 64 h PMA treatment and 48 h post infection with the HIV-2 molecular clone by using the Qiagen Rneasy mini kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. An mRNA library from the total RNA used for RNAseq was created by using poly A selection. Libraries were sequenced by using Illumina HiSeq 2 with 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing. Samples were sequenced average of 53 million reads per sample. The raw data was analyzed by FastQC to ensure high-quality. Across 9 samples 94.2–98.1% of reads had a quality score of 30 or higher. Sequence reads were trimmed to remove possible adaptor sequences and nucleotides with poor quality using Trimmomatic v.0.36. The trimmed reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome (ENSEMBL) using the STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene hit counts were calculated by using featureCounts from the Subread package v.1.5.2. Unique gene hit counts were used to conduct two differential expression analyses using DESeq2. One to compare gene expression in mock infected cells and HIV-2 WT infected cells and another to compare gene expression in mock infected cells and HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells. To determine statistically significant gene expression changes the Wald test was used to generate p-values and log2 fold changes. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 were considered differentially expressed. To compare the gene expression changes in HIV-2 WT and HIV-2 ΔVpx infected cells the log2 fold change value from each differential expression analysis adjusted for average HIV-2 Gag expression in each sample was compared. Gene ontology analysis was performed by inputting the list of statistically significant upregulated genes into GeneSCF software v.1.1-p2. The significance of enrichment of each gene ontology pathway is calculated using Fisher’s Exact test with standard multiple testing correction methods by utilizing available PERL statistical modules.

Vpx incorporation into virus-like particles

Vpx was incorporated into HIV-2-like particles by transfecting 293 T cells with pN3 HIV-2 Gag, pTarget HIV-2 Vpx, and pHCMV-G at a 9:3:1 ratio by using PEI at a 1:3 wt to volume ratio. At 48 h post transfection, cell culture supernatants were filtered by passing through a 0.22 μm filter and concentrated 1,000× at 10,000g for 3 h by using a JA-25.50 rotor (Beckman). Concentrated supernatant was treated with DNase for 1 h at 37 C. Concentrated HIV-2 like particles were added to THP-1 cells seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and spinoculated for 1 h at 1,200g (SW4750A, Beckman).

Reverse transcriptase-quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells by using the Qiagen Rneasy mini kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total cDNA was made from the total RNA by using the Biorad iSelect cDNA synthesis kit following the manufacturer’s instructions for oligo-dT primers by using 100 ng of RNA as the template in each reaction. The cDNA was then diluted six times and used as the template in qPCR reactions by using the Biorad SYBR green master mix following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 2 μl of diluted cDNA was used in each reaction with primers targeting CCL5, IFNβ, IFIT1, IFIT2, LINE-1, and OAS2 mRNA each at 500 nM. Gene expression changes were determined by calculating the difference between qPCR C(t) values of RNA from infected and mock infected control cells and normalized for the difference in qPCR C(t) values of β-actin of RNA from infected and control cells and expressed as a log 2-fold change.

ISRE reporter assay

At 48 h post infection of THP1-Lucia ISG cells, cell culture supernatants were collected from HIV-2 infected and mock infected control cells. The supernatants were then mixed with QUANTI-Luc reagent (Invivogen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was detected by using a luminometer.
