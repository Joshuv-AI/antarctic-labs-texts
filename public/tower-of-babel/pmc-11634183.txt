Discovery of Gene Fusions in Driver-Negative Cancer Samples From the National Cancer Institute-Molecular Analysis for Therapy Choice Screening Cohort
Authors: Stefan T. Kaluziak, Elizabeth M. Codd, Rashi Purohit, Beatrice Melli, Prinjali Kalyan, Jo Anne Fordham, Grace Kirkpatrick, Lisa M. McShane, Ting-Chia Chang, Guangxiao Yang, Jinglan Wang, P. Mickey Williams, Chris Karlovich, Jeffrey Sklar, A. John Iafrate
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe National Cancer Institute-Molecular Analysis for Therapy Choice (NCI-MATCH) trial was implemented to identify actionable genetic alterations across cancer types and enroll patients accordingly onto treatment arms, irrespective of tumor histology. Using multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) next-generation sequencing, NCI-MATCH genotyped 5,540 patients, discovering gene fusions in 202/5,540 tumors (3.65%). This result, substantially lower than the fusion detection prevalence of 8.5% across all patients with cancer screened at Massachusetts General Hospital's (MGH) clinical laboratories, supported reanalysis of NCI-MATCH samples identified as mutations-of-interest (MOI)–negative. The assay used by NCI-MATCH requires previous knowledge of both fusion genes, cannot detect novel fusions, and may underestimate fusion-positive patients. Anchored multiplex PCR (AMP) technology permits fusion detection with knowledge of just one gene of the fusion partners.METHODSUsing AMP-based kits, we reprocessed 663 MOI-negative samples. 200 ng of RNA per sample were shipped from the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group-American College of Radiology Imaging Network biorepository to MGH (n = 319) and Yale University (n = 344), processed, and sequenced on the NextSeq550. Reported fusions were manually reviewed, and novel fusions orthogonally verified via reverse-transcription PCR and Sanger sequencing.RESULTSAMP identified 148 fusions in 142/663 MOI-negative patients (21% [95% CI, 18 to 25]), of which 28 were covered by the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay (OCA) panel but missed, while 120 were not covered by OCA. Among AMP-identified positive patients, 32 had actionable fusions, 24 contained novel fusions, and six had two fusion events. We identified fusions in 12/34 (35% [95% CI, 20 to 54]) cholangiocarcinomas and 43/109 (39% [95% CI, 30 to 49]) sarcomas.CONCLUSIONTechnology and awareness of actionable fusions have improved since the NCI-MATCH trial. With AMP-based technology, we identified 142 patients with fusions not detected during NCI-MATCH screening, many potentially actionable. These striking data underscore the need to optimize the fusion-detection capabilities of genotyping assays used in precision medicine.

INTRODUCTION

To investigate the scope of mutationally guided, targeted drug therapies for cancer patients with rare tumors or advanced disease, collaborators at the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group-American College of Radiology Imaging Network (ECOG-ACRIN) implemented the NCI-Molecular Analysis for Therapy Choice (NCI-MATCH; study no. EAY131) study. As one of the largest precision medicine trials to date, NCI-MATCH screened tumor nucleic acid from 5,954 individuals, approximately 60% of whom were diagnosed with a rare or uncommon cancer type that had progressed on standard treatment.1,2 Centralized tumor screening and genotyping was supported by four academic medical centers: MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC), Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH), NCI's Molecular Characterization Laboratory, and Yale University (Yale).3 NCI-MATCH investigators leveraged a next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based biomarker discovery assay, Thermo Fisher's Oncomine Comprehensive Assay (OCA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), to systematically profile patient tumors to identify molecular aberrations of DNA representing likely therapeutic targets, including amplifications,4,5 mutations,6 or fusions/translocations.7 Using vetted algorithms to detect mutations-of-interest (MOIs) and actionable MOIs (aMOIs), the trial successfully assayed 5,540 tumor specimens, assigning 985 patients whose tumors contained aMOIs to one of 38 treatment arms.1,8-10

Given our research group's longstanding interest in gene fusion diagnostics and the critical role of fusions as targets for therapy, we retrospectively analyzed the NCI-MATCH genotyping data, focusing on gene fusions.11-14 We noted a very low prevalence of fusion-positive patients; only 202 of 5,540 samples (3.65%) had pipeline-called fusions, and a noticeable paucity of fusions was found by OCA in some tumor types, including cholangiocarcinomas (where FGFR2 fusions are common15) and sarcomas.16-18

The identification of only 202 fusion-positive patients among 5,540 patients screened (3.65%) was substantially lower than our clinical laboratory experience; sequencing of over 7,500 cancer samples by MGH's clinical laboratory over the past 7 years identified a fusion-positive prevalence of 8.5% (unpublished data). This difference between our clinical findings and those of NCI-MATCH in an advanced solid tumor population led us to consider that the study may not have detected patients with identifiable tumor fusions because of technical limitations of the OCA and to design a reanalysis to provide a broader picture of the fusion landscape in the NCI-MATCH screening cohort (Fig 1).

A limitation of multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based fusion detection is the requirement to know both gene partners and all possible breakpoints. Thus, novel fusions or breakpoints not covered in the multiplex PCR panel may not have been detected by the OCA.19 An additional challenge to multiplex PCR–based fusion detection is that some important fusion genes, such as FGFR2 in cholangiocarcinoma, can have many partners.15 We previously developed the NGS library construction technique known as anchored multiplex PCR (AMP), which addresses such assay limitations.20 AMP facilitates fusion detection when only one fusion gene partner is known by using PCR primers specific to the gene(s) of interest and ligated adapter sequences that provide the reverse PCR primer site (Fig 2).

The low fusion percentage in NCI-MATCH could possibly be explained by sampling bias, such as the likely omission of ALK and ROS1 fusion-positive lung cancer patients during NCI-MATCH screening, but we further hypothesized that a sizable number of fusions were not detected during NCI-MATCH active screening. We also hypothesized that the more comprehensive AMP-based assay would have detected many more fusions than reported, and that AMP would increase the identification of novel, previously unknown fusion driver events.

We focused on MOI-negative NCI-MATCH patients, given that samples with OCA-identified MOIs would be unlikely to harbor additional MOIs, since many fusions are known to be mutually exclusive with other MOIs.11,21 We expected our analysis to help detail the limitations and areas for improvement that may be broadly applicable to the many laboratories performing clinical sequencing of tumors.

METHODS

We identified 884 NCI-MATCH patients whose tissue samples had been determined to be MOI-negative (MOIs are predefined genomic variants)3 by the OCA pipeline (Fig 1). All patients received informed consent to the NCI-MATCH protocol, which was reviewed and approved by NCI Central Institutional Review Board for Early Clinical Trials as the institutional review board of record. This correlative study was additionally approved by the local Institutional Review Boards of Yale and MGH. Of the 884, 693 had sufficient RNA remaining at the ECOG-ACRIN central specimen repository at MDACC to permit fusion reanalysis. For each patient, approximately 200 ng of total RNA, previously extracted during NCI-MATCH from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue, were deidentified and shipped to MGH or Yale. Of these, 30 samples failed quality control (RNA integrity number < 6 or <50 ng total-RNA), resulting in 663 samples that were analyzed for fusions at MGH (n = 319) and Yale (n = 344). Samples were processed according to vendor specifications with the ArcherDX FusionPlex Pan Solid Tumor v2 kit (kit: AB0137). Final libraries of approximately 30 samples were multiplexed and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq550 (Illumina, Inc, San Diego, CA; kit: 20024908).

Sequencing data were processed with BCL Convert v4.2.1 (Illumina, Inc, San Diego, CA), producing fastq files that were uploaded to Archer Analysis Unlimited v7.1.0-14 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Boulder, CO), where raw reads were quality-controlled, aligned to GRCh37,22,23 and fusion calling was performed. Additionally, we leveraged a custom pipeline, implemented in Bash49 and R (v4.3.1),50 to quality-control fastq reads with TrimGalore,24 align against GRCh37 with STAR,25 and identify fusions using Arriba.26 High-confidence fusions from ArcherDX and Arriba were aggregated for each patient and manually verified (A.J.I. and J.S.). All additional analyses were implemented in R v4.3.1 (June 16, 2023),27 using the following packages: ComplexHeatmap v2.18.0,28 circlize v0.4.15,29 dplyr v1.1.4,30 GenomicFeatures v1.54.3,31 ggplot2 v3.4.4,32 gridBase v0.4.7,33 knitr v1.45,34 and RColorBrewer v1.1.3.35 The code for this project and requisite input files are available on GitHub.36

Fusion novelty was assessed via automated queries of publicly available databases, including ArcherDx's Quiver,37 COSMIC,38 and FusionGDB,39 as well as peer-reviewed publications from the past 10 years accessible on PubMed and Google Scholar. To determine the potential actionability of AMP-identified fusions, each individual gene partner was intersected with OncoKB's database of Level 1 (Food and Drug Administration [FDA]–approved) and Level 2 (standard care) therapeutic interventions, selecting for alterations labeled as fusions.40,41

Orthogonal verification of novel fusions was performed with reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and Sanger sequencing. Using PrimerQuest from IDT, we designed custom PCR primers spanning each novel fusion breakpoint to produce amplicons of <200 base pairs. Two hundred nanograms of additional RNA were shipped to MGH from MDACC. Of the 24 patients with novel fusions, 21 had sufficient RNA remaining (Data Supplement, Table S1). RT-PCR was performed on these samples with Thermo Fisher's RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA; kit: K1621), followed by PCR with custom settings. Post-PCR cleanup was performed using a minimally modified protocol, with the PCR product run on a standard 1% agarose gel with TAE (Tris-acetate-EDTA) buffer and stained with GelRed Prestain (Biotium, Fremont, CA; kit: 41011; Data Supplement, Figs S1-S4). The purified PCR product was Sanger-sequenced at the MGH DNA Sequencing Core (Data Supplement, Table S2).

Statistical Analyses

Estimates of percentages are accompanied by 95% two-sided exact binomial CIs (binom.test, R v4.3.1 [June 16, 2023]).27 Comparisons of the percentages between groups were based on two-sided Fisher's exact test (fisher.test, R v4.3.1 [June 16, 2023]).27 To compare continuous variables between groups, two-sided t tests were performed (t.test, R v4.3.1 [June 16, 2023]).27 Two-sided P values <.05 are reported as significant.

RESULTS

For the 5,540 patients with successfully reported results in the NCI-MATCH screening cohort, 4,656 MOIs were identified; 2,024 of them were actionable (Fig 1). Resource limitations prevented fusion reanalysis of the entire NCI-MATCH screening cohort; thus, we only resequenced patients identified by NCI-MATCH as MOI-negative (n = 693), a subgroup of specimens lacking any identified MOIs. We successfully reanalyzed 96% (663 of 693) of the MOI-negative samples received from MDACC. Within these 663 patients, we identified 142 patients with at least one gene fusion (21% [95% CI, 18 to 25]; Table 1; complete sample-level data are provided in the Data Supplement, Table S3).

The 142 fusion-positive patients involved 84 unique gene partner combinations, of which 23 were identified in multiple patients. Six patients had two fusion events involving distinct gene pairs. Overall, fusions were detected in many tumor types, with a high number of fusions discovered in sarcoma, head-and-neck cancers, GI tumors (including cholangiocarcinoma), and urogenital tumors (Fig 3A). Large percentages were found in sarcomas of bone (50%), soft tissue sarcomas (39%), and cholangiocarcinomas (35%), congruent with our initial hypothesis. Additionally, we identified a high fusion prevalence in head-and-neck tumors (40%), largely because of the detection of MYB-NFIB fusions in adenoid cystic carcinomas (ACCs). Notably, the mean age of fusion-positive patients (56.3 years old) was significantly lower than that of fusion-negative patients (59 years old) across all tumor histology types (P < .03). However, within histologic categories, as well as within specific tumor types, there was no such significant difference, suggesting that the histology types enriched for fusions were composed of a younger patient demographic. Additionally, fusion positivity was not significantly associated with sex.

Given the high prevalence of fusion detection using AMP across the MOI-negative cohort, we assessed potential reasons why fusions had not been detected by OCA. For each AMP-detected fusion, we annotated the specific gene partners and exact breakpoints involved. By intersecting the gene and breakpoint coordinates with the currently available OCA primer list, we concluded that the OCA panel was incapable of detecting 120 of the 148 AMP-identified fusion events (81%), as there were no combinations of forward and reverse primers theoretically capable of amplifying the relevant fusion transcripts (Fig 3B).

Importantly, this subset included some well-known fusions, such as the EWSR1-FLI1 fusions identified in sarcomas, which were intentionally excluded from the OCA by NCI-MATCH because of a lack of actionability. This subset also included novel fusions, undetectable by the OCA, as it is designed to only detect known fusions. The remaining 28 identified fusions (19%) theoretically should have been detected (Fig 3B). Why these fusions were not detected by OCA is unclear; however, we suspect that the impact of limited assay sensitivity and/or potential single nucleotide variant (SNV) occurring under PCR primer binding sites may have contributed. It is also possible that, for a limited subset of samples in the NCI-MATCH screening cohort, the RNA tested may have been of unsatisfactory quality for OCA analysis at the time, but a MOI-negative DNA-based result was reported. Finally, the presence of fusions in certain tumor subtypes was less appreciated at the study's outset, which likely influenced targets included in the Oncomine panel.

To consider how technical differences may have limited fusion detection by multiplex PCR–based OCA, we highlight two examples of fusions detected in the MOI-negative cohort: FGFR2 (Fig 3C) and MYB-NFIB fusions (Fig 3D). A total of 15 FGFR2 fusions were identified by AMP in cholangiocarcinoma (n = 9), breast (n = 2), ovarian (n = 2), and not otherwise specified (n = 2; Fig 3C). Of these 15, three fusions involved novel gene partners (SIRT2, BCAT2, and a nonannotated gene), and an additional six patients involved known fusions not included in OCA (Fig 3C). Finally, six other patients involved gene partners that were represented in the OCA panel (eg, FGFR2-CCDC6 or FGFR2-BICC1), five of which should have been detected, as they contained breakpoints explicitly covered by the OCA.

Within the MOI-negative cohort, AMP identified 23 fusions in ACCs, 19 MYB-NFIB, and four MYBL1-NFIB fusions that were not detected by the OCA (Fig 3D). Of the 19 MYB-NFIB fusions, 10 involved breakpoints not within the OCA, while nine breakpoints, all involving exon 11 of NFIB, should have been detected (Fig 3D). Three of four MYBL1-NFIB breakpoints are absent from the OCA panel, with the exception of fusions involving exon 11 of NFIB.

We identified 25 fusions (in 24 patients) that can be considered novel (Table 2), defined here as an anchored gene's fusion partner that has not been previously observed. To determine whether or not a fusion was novel, we cross-referenced public fusion databases and performed automated literature searches (see Methods). We had sufficient RNA for 21 of these fusion-positive patients to permit orthogonal verification using RT-PCR and Sanger sequencing (Data Supplement, Table S2). Of these 25 novel fusions, seven were GI tumors, six were urogenital, and three sarcoma. These included three novel JAZF1 fusions, two in endometrial stromal sarcoma and one in follicular lymphoma; two FGFR3 fusions in urogenital patients and one FGFR2 fusion in GI carcinoma; and two BRAF fusions, one in cholangiocarcinoma and one in a neuroendocrine tumor. Of the novel fusions, eight would potentially have qualified to be enrolled in an NCI-MATCH treatment arm (Table 2). The novel NRCAM-BRAF fusion (NRCAM exon 29 to BRAF exon 7) was identified in an adrenal pheochromocytoma (Fig 4), with 179 well-supported in-frame reads supporting the fusion breakpoint, which includes the BRAF kinase domain (Fig 4). To date, there are only a handful of BRAF fusions reported in pheochromocytoma.42,43

We assessed the actionability of the fusions detected with AMP by cross-referencing the AMP-identified fusion genes against OncoKB's Therapeutic Level of Evidence V2 database. Of the 142 patients, 32 contained potentially actionable fusions (Table 3). Twelve patients had a fusion that matched an entry in OncoKB's Therapeutic Level 1 (FDA-approved drugs) and a matching eligible diagnosis for the listed drug. These included eight cholangiocarcinoma patients with FGFR2 fusions, one lung adenocarcinoma with MET-14 exon skipping, and three patients that were positive with actionable FGFR3 (urothelial), NTRK1 (pancreas), or NTRK3 (leiomyosarcoma) fusions.

DISCUSSION

The NCI-MATCH Trial was one of the largest trials to date of mutationally targeted therapy across varying tumor types. As a basket trial, it attempted to assess the efficacy of drugs targeted to specific mutations, regardless of the tumor type in which those aMOIs were found.9 NCI-MATCH yielded important insights into the molecular dynamics of cancer,2 expanded the availability of effective treatment options for patients with rare or difficult-to-treat cancers, and contributed findings that will improve clinical outcomes.2 Consistent with progress on all those fronts, our data suggest that gene fusions were more common in the NCI-MATCH screening cohort than reported originally. These data further indicate how common gene fusions may be in many different tumor types.

We confirmed our hypothesis that an AMP-based assay, designed to overcome some limitations of multiplex PCR–based fusion detection, could identify more fusions than the NCI-MATCH's unexpectedly low number (3.65%) of detections.20 The AMP-detected fusion frequency of 21% in NCI-MATCH samples previously deemed MOI-negative by multiplex-PCR included actionable fusions. AMP fusion detection for this subset of patients from a rare and advanced tumor population also exceeded MGH findings in a clinical cohort of unselected patients. We note, however, that disparities between the broader NCI-MATCH screening cohort and the subset MOI-negative cohort analyzed here could contribute to appreciable differences in fusion prevalence and subsequent detection. For example, the MOI-negative cohort lacks SNVs, and thus would be expected to be enriched to some degree for fusions. Also, the overall NCI-MATCH study design, which aimed to recruit rare and difficult-to-treat cancers, may have led to potential ascertainment bias of the screening cohort and, consequently, the MOI-negative cohort analyzed here. Despite these differences, our study's detection of more than six times as many fusions as the OCA, 142 fusion-positive patients in 663 tested, with 32 of 142 potentially actionable, indicates the importance of continued improvement toward more robust gene fusion detection technology.

The high prevalence of detection with AMP was of particular interest for specific tumor subtypes, including sarcoma and cholangiocarcinoma patients. Detection of novel fusions was also higher than we anticipated, with 24 of 142 patients (17%) having previously unreported gene fusion partners, as identified by extensive searches of databases and literature for previous entries. When possible, the authenticity of these novel fusions was investigated and orthogonally identified by using direct uniplex RT-PCR with primer pairs different from those used in the AMP-based assay.

From a diagnostic assay evaluation standpoint, we believe that the factors contributing to OCA's inability to detect many fusions are likely multiple. Considering the overlap between the primer combinations used as part of the OCA during NCI-MATCH and the fusions identified in this study, 28 of 142 (20%) of the MOI-negative patients analyzed here should have been reported as fusion-positive by OCA. Although we have not conclusively determined why these patients were not detected, we suspect a major impact from the sensitivity of the assay, as well as potential problems in the exact read-count cutoffs implemented during postsequencing analysis, and/or differences in the quality of the RNA samples processed for AMP versus OCA analysis.

The remaining 80% of the fusion-positive patients were missed because the exact primers required to detect these fusions with a multiplex PCR–based approach were not present in the OCA panel. These patients encompassed fusion events involving both known and novel gene partners, as well as fusion events missing the exact breakpoints required. Importantly, a perfect fusion assay has yet to be developed, and AMP has the limitation of not being as comprehensive as whole-transcriptome or whole-genome sequencing. The OCA has some potential advantages in genotyping tumors,44 including the ability to effectively genotype samples with low nucleic acid inputs. Furthermore, the OCA cannot be faulted for not detecting targets excluded as unnecessary during the initial NCI-MATCH study design. The finding of novel fusions in our study could well lead to future modifications of the OCA or other sequencing panels with primers able to cover those fusions.

For certain tumor types, such as cholangiocarcinomas, where patients have been identified as driver mutation–negative by a multiplex-PCR assay or a DNA-based assay, testing with AMP may be useful in identifying any actionable fusions not initially recognized by existing panels. A similar approach was leveraged by Benayed et al45 to reanalyze 2,522 mutation driver–negative lung adenocarcinomas, initially profiled by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center's Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets (IMPACT) DNA sequencing assay. The IMPACT assay identified 195 (7.7%) gene fusions and 119 (4.7%) METex14 alterations. Benayed et al subsequently used AMP to reanalyze 254 samples, previously deemed driver-negative by the IMPACT analysis, to further investigate for potentially missed fusion events. Of the 254 reanalyzed samples, 36 (14%) were shown to have an alteration missed by the IMPACT analysis, 33 of which were actionable.

Among the 142 fusion-positive patients reported in our study, 32 were deemed actionable according to the strict criteria that the fusions could be targeted by FDA-approved agents. Many more of the identified fusions would be considered actionable in a broader sense, such as the ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusions in alveolar soft part sarcoma. Five such fusions were detected in this study. These fusions are now being linked to positive responses to immunotherapy, despite not being a directly targeted molecular alteration.46 We found additional rearrangements that could be targetable, but likely only within a purely investigational approach. One example is the ALK alternative transcript initiation (ALK-ATI), which we found in three patients. However, conflicting evidence exists for the use of ALK inhibitors in ALK-ATI–positive patients, thus warranting additional clinical evaluation47,48 (Data Supplement, Table S3).

Taken together, our findings demonstrate the importance of more comprehensive assays for identifying fusions and the need for increased attention to the limitations of screening assays. The previously undetected actionable fusions we identified strongly support the use of sequencing technologies that can detect novel fusions and suggest that access to more comprehensive fusion detection can improve the selection of specific therapies for patients with rare or advanced tumors. Greater accuracy and timeliness in identifying fusion events in these tumors is therefore a matter of urgency. Improved access to this information, and its impact on patient decision making, should yield beneficial clinical outcomes for the patients who seek access to the expanding advantages that contemporary oncology can offer.
