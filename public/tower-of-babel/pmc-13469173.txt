Pre‐Excited Atrial Fibrillation Degenerating Into Ventricular Fibrillation Captured by an Automated External Defibrillator
Authors: Mari Nakayama, Kodai Shinzato, Yuki Norita, Makoto Edayoshi, Takanori Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An automated external defibrillator (AED) captured the real‐time transition from pre‐excited atrial fibrillation to ventricular fibrillation in a previously undiagnosed patient with WPW syndrome. This case highlights the malignant potential of rapidly conducting accessory pathways.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) with antegrade accessory pathway conduction, referred to as pre‐excited AF, in patients with Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome may result in extremely rapid ventricular activation and can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation (VF) [1]. The risk of pre‐excited AF is known to be higher in patients with multiple pathways and a short antegrade accessory pathway effective refractory period (< 250 ms), which allows rapid conduction and predisposes patients to VF and sudden death [1]. However, real‐time documentation of this malignant transition is rare [2]. We present an educationally valuable case in which an automated external defibrillator (AED) documented pre‐excited AF degenerating into VF.

A 38‐year‐old man with no prior medical history or known evidence of WPW syndrome collapsed with cardiac arrest immediately after sitting down in a chair during a rest break while working a night shift at a factory. Bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately, and an AED delivered four shocks for a shockable rhythm, resulting in return of spontaneous circulation. On arrival at the hospital, the patient remained unconscious and required endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation.

The initial 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated a narrow QRS regular tachycardia at 206 beats/min (Figure 1A). Following synchronized cardioversion, sinus rhythm with delta waves consistent with a left‐sided accessory pathway was evident (Figure 1B). Laboratory findings and transthoracic echocardiography were unremarkable.

(B) Twelve‐lead electrocardiogram after restoration of sinus rhythm demonstrating manifest ventricular pre‐excitation with delta waves. (C) Post‐ablation electrocardiogram showing complete disappearance of delta waves.

The AED ECG tracing showing the transition to VF is presented in Figure 2A. To reduce chest compression artifacts, the tracing was digitized using WebPlotDigitizer (version 5.2), and data points were manually sampled at 10‐ms intervals to reconstruct the ECG waveform in Microsoft Excel. Six compression artifacts with comparable morphology were manually selected, temporally aligned, and averaged to generate a template waveform. The template waveform was then subtracted from the reconstructed ECG waveform to estimate the underlying ECG signal with reduced chest compression artifacts (Figure 2B). The processed tracing demonstrates the transition from wide QRS irregular tachycardia (pre‐excited AF) to VF more clearly. Figure 2C shows the processed tracing at a paper speed of 25 mm/s. The mean RR interval of the seven consecutive RR intervals immediately preceding VF was 206 ± 50 ms (Figure 2C).

(C) Horizontally expanded tracing displayed at 25 mm/s to facilitate visualization of the irregular wide QRS tachycardia consistent with pre‐excited atrial fibrillation. Electrophysiological study performed the following day identified a left posterolateral accessory pathway with both antegrade and retrograde conduction. The antegrade effective refractory period was extremely short at 220 ms, and the shortest pre‐excited RR interval during induced AF was 230 ms, indicating a high‐risk accessory pathway. Maximum pre‐excitation is shown in Figure 3 during atrial burst pacing at a cycle length of 230 ms. These electrophysiological findings suggest that the wide QRS irregular tachycardia recorded by AED was consistent with pre‐excited AF.

Surface electrocardiogram and intracardiac recordings during electrophysiological study. One‐to‐one atrioventricular conduction with maximum ventricular pre‐excitation during rapid atrial burst pacing at a cycle length of 230 ms. CS, coronary sinus; HIS, His bundle; RV, right ventricle.

Orthodromic atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia was also inducible. Accessory pathway mapping localized the pathway to the posterolateral mitral annulus. Radiofrequency catheter ablation successfully eliminated both antegrade and retrograde conduction simultaneously (Figure 4). No recurrence of accessory pathway conduction was observed thereafter.

Fluoroscopic image showing the successful ablation site of the left posterolateral accessory pathway at the mitral annulus (arrow).

No VF was induced by programmed ventricular stimulation, and pilsicainide challenge testing did not reveal a Brugada electrocardiogram pattern. Coronary angiography revealed no significant coronary artery disease or anomalous origin of the coronary artery.

Exercise stress testing showed no QT prolongation or ventricular arrhythmias suggestive of catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. The patient recovered without neurological deficits and was discharged on hospital Day 9. No recurrence of malignant arrhythmia or delta waves was observed after ablation (Figure 1C). Because VF was considered secondary to rapid antegrade accessory pathway conduction during pre‐excited AF, which was successfully eliminated by catheter ablation, implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implantation was deemed unnecessary.

This case provides rare real‐time AED documentation of pre‐excited AF degenerating into VF in a patient with WPW syndrome. Takahashi et al. [2] previously reported a similar case in a 14‐year‐old girl with known WPW syndrome. In contrast, the present patient had no prior diagnosis of WPW syndrome, which was first recognized after the AED‐recorded event. Furthermore, chest‐compression artifact subtraction clarified the underlying rhythm during ongoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

The discrepancy between the shortest pre‐excited RR interval during the clinical event and that observed during the electrophysiological study may be explained by differences in autonomic tone. Shift work has been associated with an increased risk of AF in younger individuals, possibly through alterations in autonomic regulation [3]. Sympathetic nerve activity has been shown to increase during AF [4], and sympathetic stimulation shortens the effective refractory period of accessory pathways and facilitates antegrade conduction [5]. Therefore, heightened sympathetic activation during the night shift, followed by the onset of pre‐excited AF, may have transiently shortened the accessory pathway refractory period.

This AED recording directly visualizes the electrophysiological transition from pre‐excited AF to VF and highlights the risk of rapidly conducting accessory pathways in patients with WPW syndrome.
