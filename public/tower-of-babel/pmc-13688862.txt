Influencing factors and individualized intervention strategies for inadequate bowel preparation before colonoscopy in high-risk populations: a review based on the clinical practice of painless colonoscopy in China
Authors: Huibin Lai, Ting Sun, Xiujing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInadequate bowel preparation (IBP) significantly reduces the diagnostic accuracy of painless colonoscopy and increases complication risks. High-risk populations account for 28% of painless colonoscopy cases in China, with IBP rates ranging from 42% to 62%, but targeted nursing interventions remain underdeveloped.ObjectiveTo systematically analyze IBP-related influencing factors and evaluate the effectiveness of individualized nursing interventions for high-risk populations undergoing painless colonoscopy in China.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted across the MEDLINE (via the PubMed interface, Cochrane Library, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang Data, and VIP from January 2020 to April 2024 using keywords related to bowel preparation, high-risk populations, and nursing intervention. Studies on Chinese high-risk populations undergoing painless colonoscopy were eligible if they reported IBP rate, bowel preparation score, or intervention adherence. RCTs, systematic reviews, and clinical guidelines were included. Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted data, and assessed methodological quality using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool, JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, and AGREE II Instrument. Data synthesis involved descriptive analysis and random-effects meta-analysis.ResultsA total of 43 studies (32 RCTs, 7 systematic reviews, 4 guidelines) were included. Six high-risk populations were identified: obese patients (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²), patients with abdominal/pelvic surgery history, neurological disease patients, long-term medication users, children, and diabetic patients. Key influencing factors included gastrointestinal motility disorders, psychological anxiety, and medication interactions. Individualized interventions (e.g., dose-adjusted laxatives, nurse-led psychological counseling) reduced IBP rates by 23%–48% (pooled OR = 0.36, 95%CI:0.29–0.45, I²=45%).ConclusionIBP in high-risk populations is driven by multi-dimensional factors. Context-adapted nursing interventions (e.g., WeChat-guided education, parental participation training) significantly improve preparation quality. Standardized protocols for primary hospitals and child-specific regimens are urgently needed. These findings translate into a risk-stratified, nurse-pharmacist-led preparation pathway that can be implemented immediately—especially in primary hospitals—to reduce IBP and improve safety. And these findings pertain to six high-risk populations—obese, post-surgical, neurological disease, long-term medication users, children, and diabetics—as predefined in the Introduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05161-1.

Introduction

Clinical context of IBP in painless colonoscopy in China

With the widespread use of painless colonoscopy in China, bowel preparation has become a key determinant of examination quality, especially in patients with physiological vulnerabilities or comorbidities [1–3]. In this review, the relevance of painless colonoscopy is mainly clinical and operational: inadequate bowel preparation (IBP) impairs mucosal visualization, lowers lesion detection, increases repeat procedures, and consumes endoscopy and anesthesia resources [4–6]. This problem is particularly important in high-risk populations, who make up a considerable share of painless colonoscopy recipients in China and have higher IBP rates than the general adult population [7, 8]. Therefore, this review focuses on IBP-related risk factors and individualized interventions in these high-risk groups rather than on anesthesia itself.

Bowel preparation quality: a determinant of diagnostic efficacy and healthcare economics

Bowel preparation quality is the foundation of colonoscopy accuracy, and international guidelines such as ESGE 2024 and ASGE 2023 recommend a minimum adequacy rate of 85% [4]. China’s Medical Quality Control Indicators for Digestive Endoscopy Diagnostic and Therapeutic Technology (2022 Edition) likewise identifies adequate bowel preparation as a prerequisite for high-quality colonoscopy and notes that it can reduce missed lesion diagnoses by 40% [9]. Adequate preparation, commonly defined as a BBPS score ≥ 6 or Ottawa score ≤ 6, allows reliable visualization of mucosal lesions, including small adenomas that may precede CRC [6]. By contrast, IBP has major clinical consequences. A multicenter study of 5,832 patients showed a strong correlation between bowel cleanliness and polyp detection (r = 0.78, p < 0.001) [10], while later meta-analyses found that IBP triples adenoma miss rates and reduces CRC detection by 35% [5]. In severe cases, it leads to procedure abandonment in 15%–20% of patients [11].

The burden is even greater in painless colonoscopy because poor preparation may require repeat anesthesia and repeat procedures. A 2023 Chinese multicenter study of 10,000 patients reported that IBP-related repeat examinations used 18% of endoscopy center capacity and prolonged waits for urgent cases from 3 to 14 days [12]. IBP also increases procedural risk: perforation rises 2.3-fold because of blind instrumentation [13], and aspiration risk is higher in patients with residual gastric contents (12% vs. 5%) [8, 14]. These findings weaken the safety and efficiency advantages of painless colonoscopy [1].

Elderly patients and those with end-stage renal disease are clinically important and are discussed for context, but they were not included in pooled analyses because they were not predefined core analytic subgroups and the available studies showed substantial overlap or used highly specific regimens that limited comparability. Pregnant women are also discussed for context only and are not included in pooled analyses or primary conclusions.

These patient factors are compounded by gaps in standardized care. A 2024 survey of 200 hospitals found that many Chinese centers still rely on “one-size-fits-all” protocols, including 80% of primary hospitals and 40% of tertiary hospitals [6, 9] Only a minority adjust doses for obese patients, use pediatric-specific formulations, or provide pregnancy-specific guidance [35]. Even when guidelines are available, implementation remains limited, especially in primary hospitals [36]. In addition, PEG—the most widely used laxative—has poor tolerability in vulnerable groups, particularly older adults and patients with heart or kidney disease [37].

Evidence for targeted interventions: beyond basic preparation

Emerging evidence suggests that personalized interventions can address the specific causes of IBP in high-risk populations. In post-abdominal or pelvic surgery patients, integrating pain control into bowel preparation improves adherence. A 2023 study of 296 patients found that oral acetaminophen plus topical lidocaine significantly reduced pain and increased laxative completion compared with standard care [38]. Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital’s Fast Recovery After Surgery system similarly showed that multimodal peri-procedural analgesia reduced abdominal pain during preparation and promoted earlier recovery of intake and motility [39].

Caregiver training is another promising strategy, especially for elderly, pediatric, and neurologically impaired patients. A 2024 RCT of 350 patients showed that standardized 30-minute caregiver training improved caregiver competency and reduced IBP rates [40, 41]. Training materials supported by national service-improvement initiatives included pictorial instructions and videos, and most rural caregivers reported greater confidence after training [42]. Related clinical practice in Hebei also found that family supervision improved full laxative intake compared with self-management [43, 44].

Although these approaches complement standard measures such as dietary guidance and digital reminders, implementation is still limited in primary hospitals because of staffing and cost constraints.

Research gaps and the rationale for this review

Despite the importance of bowel preparation in high-risk groups, current evidence has several limitations. First, Chinese studies are concentrated in eastern tertiary hospitals, while central, western, and primary-care settings remain underrepresented [6].

Second, most domestic studies focus on a single subgroup—such as diabetic, elderly, or post-surgical patients—without integrating evidence across special populations [18, 32, 43, 45]. As a result, common barriers such as poor tolerance, taste aversion, and physical dependence are insufficiently synthesized across groups [28].

Third, there is a persistent gap between international recommendations and Chinese practice. Although ESGE and ASGE advocate individualized dosing, split-dose regimens, and low-fiber diets [4], these approaches are not always feasible in China. For example, 2 L PEG plus ascorbate is still unavailable in some rural pharmacies, limiting implementation [46]. At the same time, culturally and system-relevant strategies—such as WeChat-based education and caregiver training—remain underrepresented in global reviews [10, 24, 47].

This systematic review therefore aims to synthesize IBP prevalence and risk factors across six key high-risk populations in China, evaluate the effectiveness of nursing-led interventions, and propose practical strategies adapted to Chinese clinical settings. By centering Chinese evidence and implementation realities, this review seeks to improve IBP management in the patients most at risk.

Methods

This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [48, 49]. The methodological conduct and reporting of the review were also cross-checked against the key domains of AMSTAR II to further improve transparency and completeness [50].

Literature search strategy

We performed a systematic literature search across the MEDLINE (accessed via the PubMed search platform), Cochrane Library, and Embase (for English-language studies), as well as CNKI, Wanfang Data, and VIP (for Chinese-language studies). For relevant guidance, the official portals of the Chinese Society of Gastroenterology and the National Healthcare Security Administration were searched. To ensure high sensitivity, our search procedures integrated both controlled vocabulary and free-text entry terms. Through the PubMed interface (for MEDLINE), we sought and identified relevant descriptors such as ‘Cathartics’ [MeSH], ‘Colonoscopy’ [MeSH], and ‘Nursing Care’ [MeSH]. The final English search string was constructed using a combination of these standardized headings and free-text keywords: (‘bowel preparation’ OR ‘colonoscopy preparation’) AND (‘obese’ OR ‘postoperative’ OR ‘neurological disease’ OR ‘long-term medication’ OR ‘child’) AND (‘China’ OR ‘Chinese’) AND (‘nursing intervention’ OR ‘patient education’). For Chinese databases, we used corresponding subjects and keywords including ‘肠道准备’ (bowel preparation), ‘清肠’ (gut cleansing), ‘肥胖’ (obese), and ‘护理干预’ (nursing intervention). The search timeframe was fixed from January 2020 up to April 2024. All analytical framework and results followed the six predefined high-risk populations. Results relating to pregnancy or end-stage renal disease were summarized strictly as contextual evidence and were excluded from those primary pooled analyses to preserve subgroup comparability. Consistent cross-referencing markers were utilized throughout the internal reporting to ensure clear organizational hierarchy. Details regarding the finalized search strings for all six databases are reported in Supplementary File 1: Search Strategy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria. They were conducted in Chinese populations undergoing painless colonoscopy, involved one of the high-risk populations defined above, and reported at least one relevant outcome, including the IBP rate, a bowel preparation score (e.g., BBPS or Ottawa scale), or intervention adherence. Eligible study designs included RCTs, systematic reviews, and clinical guidelines. In addition, participants had to belong to one of the six high-risk populations defined in "The disproportionate burden of IBP in Chinese high-risk populations" section. Studies focused on elderly patients, pregnant women, or patients with end-stage renal disease were narratively summarized but were not included in the pooled analyses in order to preserve subgroup comparability and avoid overlap with other high-risk categories.

Studies were excluded if they involved non-Chinese populations, did not concern painless colonoscopy, or were case reports or reviews with fewer than 100 participants.

Quality assessment and data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data relevant to sample size, intervention characteristics, and IBP rates, subsequently evaluating the methodological quality of the included records. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [51], which evaluates domains such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, and selective reporting. Studies were not excluded solely on the basis of a high risk of bias. Instead, the risk-of-bias assessment was used to evaluate methodological quality and to support the interpretation of the overall pooled findings. Systematic reviews were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist [52], whereas clinical guidelines were scrutinized according to the AGREE II Instrument [53]. Formal certainty grading of the overall body of evidence was not performed because this review synthesized mixed evidence types and clinically heterogeneous populations, interventions, and outcomes, which limited the feasibility of applying a uniform outcome-level certainty framework across all pooled and narrative findings. In cases where original studies provided insufficient procedural detail regarding specific intervention components, researchers coded the modality as “insufficient procedural detail” and documented this as a reproducibility limitation. Any disagreements encountered during the quality assessment process were resolved via discussion or through consultation with a third reviewer for reached consensus.

Data synthesis

Descriptive analysis summarized influencing factors and interventions. Meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method) to calculate the pooled OR for IBP rate reduction. This model was justified by the intrinsic clinical heterogeneity expected among the six diverse high-risk populations (e.g., pediatric vs. neurological disease patients) and their varying responses to individualized intervention models. Bowel preparation quality in the included studies was assessed using validated scales, primarily the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) and, in some studies, the Ottawa Bowel Preparation Scale. To ensure comparability across studies, we harmonized these instruments at the level of bowel preparation adequacy rather than raw total scores. IBP was defined as BBPS < 6 or Ottawa score > 6, while adequate preparation was defined as BBPS ≥ 6 or Ottawa score ≤ 6, consistent with established thresholds [4, 54].Accordingly, meta-analysis was conducted on the dichotomous outcome of IBP rate reduction, whereas raw scale values were summarized descriptively when different scoring systems were used. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic (I²>50% indicating high heterogeneity), with potential sources of variance further explored through subgroup analyses grouped by hospital tier and intervention modality. Additionally, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to verify the stability of the pooled estimates. Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed because the pooled analyses combined clinically heterogeneous interventions across distinct high-risk populations, and several subgroup analyses included fewer than 10 studies, limiting the interpretability of funnel plots and small-study effect tests. Results were then effectively pooled using the random-effects estimator.

Multimodal analgesia and incisional sustained-release devices

To enhance reproducibility, we recommend that incisional sustained-release devices be reported and implemented according to a standardized framework.

Commonly used devices can be categorized into three types: (A) local drug-eluting implants or biodegradable carriers, such as liposomal anesthetic formulations; (B) suture- or patch-based drug-releasing membranes; and (C) catheter- or pump-based continuous local infusion systems. Studies should report the generic active compound and mechanism in addition to commercial names, to facilitate replication across centers.

The timing of application was generally similar across studies. Devices were usually placed at the end of the procedure, before wound closure, or immediately after closure. Continuous infusion systems should be initiated in the operating room and maintained for 24–72 h postoperatively, according to institutional protocols.

The reporting checklist should include several essential details, including device type (A/B/C), drug name, route of administration (intracutaneous, subcutaneous, or intracavitary), time of administration (pre-closure or immediate post-closure), duration or dwell time, co-interventions (such as systemic analgesics, education, and digital reminders), adverse-event monitoring, and whether the intervention was nurse-led or pharmacist-led. For infusion devices, infusion rate range and removal criteria should be specified in line with local anesthesiology/pharmacy standards.

Regarding safety and documentation, use of these devices should be integrated into peri-procedural care bundles, with documentation of operator, insertion and removal time, adverse-event monitoring, and patient/guardian informed consent.

Results

Literature characteristics and quality assessment

A total of 4,826 records were retrieved, with 43 studies included (PRISMA Flowchart, Fig. 1). Of 32 RCTs (total sample size 28,600), 24 (75%) were classified as high quality because they were judged to be at low risk in at least five bias domains. The remaining 8 studies showed high or unclear risk in one or more domains. All 7 systematic reviews and 4 guidelines met high-quality criteria. Geographically, 65% (28/43) of studies were from eastern China (Jiangsu, Zhejiang), 35% (15/43) from central/western regions.

Risk factor correlation analysis

Meta-analysis of 18 observational studies showed that gastrointestinal motility disorders (e.g., delayed gastric emptying) had the strongest correlation with IBP (pooled OR = 4.1, 95% CI:3.2–5.2), followed by psychological factors (OR = 3.2, 95% CI:2.5–4.1) and medication interactions (OR = 3.0, 95%CI:2.4–3.8). Random-effects weighting was utilized here to account for sample variance across these different pathological subgroups. For China-specific factors, lack of protocolized care (e.g., no post-surgical preparation guidelines) was associated with 2.8-fold higher IBP risk (95% CI:2.1–3.7).

Meta-analysis of IBP rate reduction

Thirty-two RCTs (total sample size 28,600) were included in meta-analysis. Figure 2 provides a visual summary of the overall pooled effect of individualized interventions on IBP rate reduction. Individualized interventions significantly reduced IBP rate (pooled OR = 0.36, 95% CI:0.29–0.45, p < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis (Fig. 3) showed differences by both hospital type and intervention type. Tertiary hospitals achieved a more pronounced reduction in IBP rates (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.25–0.43) with lower heterogeneity (I²=32%) than primary hospitals (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.28–0.54, I²=52%). Across intervention types, multidisciplinary education delivered through pharmacist-nurse collaboration was the most effective modality (OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.20–0.42), followed by laxative regimen optimization (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.28–0.49), psychological counseling (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.27–0.54), and parental training in pediatric populations (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.30–0.56).

Sensitivity analysis

Sequential exclusion of individual studies showed no significant changes in pooled OR (range: 0.34–0.38), confirming the stability of meta-analysis results. These findings suggest that the overall result was not driven by any single included study, including studies with relatively lower methodological quality.

Adverse event analysis

Pooled analysis of 32 RCTs showed that individualized interventions reduced adverse events by 58% (pooled OR = 0.42, 95%CI:0.33–0.54, I²=39%). The most common adverse events were abdominal distension (intervention: 8.2% vs. control: 22.5%) and hypoglycemia (diabetic subgroup: 3.1% vs. 12.1%). No severe adverse events (e.g., aspiration, AKI) were reported in intervention groups, compared with 2.3% in control groups.

Discussion

This review is the first to quantify IBP risk factors across 6 high-risk populations in China. The data show that neurological disease patients have the highest baseline IBP rate (58.6%), driven by dysphagia (45.3%) and prolonged colonic transit time (84 ± 12 h in Parkinson’s patients). For children, the 30.2% taste refusal rate highlights the need for age-adapted interventions—consistent with global data [55] but with higher refusal rates in China, possibly due to limited child-specific laxative flavors [36].

Notably, primary hospitals have 18% higher IBP rates than tertiary hospitals (pooled OR = 0.39 vs. 0.33), reflecting resource disparities: only 20% of primary hospitals have access to HRV devices for DAN screening [56], and 80% lack post-surgical preparation protocols [36]. This aligns with China’s national healthcare survey showing 35% lower nursing staffing in primary hospitals [7].

The meta-analysis confirms that individualized interventions reduce IBP rates by 64% (1–0.36), with the strongest effect in pharmacist-nurse collaboration (OR = 0.29). This is because long-term medication users face complex drug-laxative interactions (e.g., opioids slowing motility), and pharmacist involvement reduces medication-related IBP by 40% [57]. For children, parental training achieves 92.1% adherence—critical in China’s family-centric care model [36, 55].

The moderate heterogeneity (I²=45%) is primarily driven by primary hospital studies (I²=52%), where intervention protocols varied (e.g., WeChat reminder frequency: daily vs. twice-daily). Subgroup analysis resolves 13% of heterogeneity, indicating that standardizing protocols in primary hospitals could improve result consistency.

In light of potential patient-specific factors recognized in broader clinical intervention models [58], we observed systematic variation in the effectiveness of multimodal analgesia and individualized preparation strategies across different high-risk populations. Several mechanisms may account for these differences:

Long-term medication users (opioids, anticholinergics)

Chronic opioid exposure leads to tolerance and altered nociceptive processing, reducing responsiveness to standard analgesics. Many long-term medications also impair gastrointestinal motility, thereby lowering the effectiveness of lavage regimens. Interventions combining incisional sustained-release devices with pharmacist-led medication review may therefore yield greater benefit in this group by simultaneously improving pain control and bowel motility.

Children

The markedly higher adherence to parental training observed in pediatric populations likely reflects both heightened parental vigilance and direct parental execution of preparation tasks (timely dosing, fluid intake, monitoring diarrhea). Child-specific interventions are also often accompanied by more intensive behavioral components—visual aids, demonstrations, and direct reminders—further enhancing compliance. Thus, success in this group depends largely on family engagement and educational intensity rather than pharmacological adjustments alone.

Obese and diabetic patients

Obesity may mechanically reduce distribution efficiency of lavage solutions, while diabetic patients frequently exhibit autonomic neuropathy with delayed gastrointestinal transit. In diabetic patients, mechanisms affecting bowel preparation are not limited to diabetic autonomic neuropathy. Antidiabetic pharmacotherapy may also contribute to impaired preparation quality, particularly when medications influence gastric emptying or intestinal motility. In particular, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists have attracted increasing attention because of their potential to delay gastric emptying and alter gastrointestinal transit [59, 60]. Emerging evidence also suggests that these agents may be relevant to bowel preparation quality in endoscopic practice [61]. Although this factor was not consistently evaluated in the studies included in our review, it is clinically relevant and should be considered when interpreting IBP risk and planning individualized preparation in diabetic patients. In such populations, analgesia alone does little to improve cleansing quality; more effective outcomes require coupling analgesia with motility-enhancing or regimen-tailoring strategies (e.g., adjusted fluid volume, timing of split dose, or use of prokinetics under pharmacist supervision).

Post-surgical patients

Adhesions or altered anatomy after abdominal/pelvic surgery hinder uniform distribution of lavage solutions. Sustained-release incisional analgesia may improve comfort and cooperation but does not address mechanical limitations, underscoring the need for longer preparation times or more intensive lavage in this group.

Overall, differential effects across populations stem from both biological factors (motility, pain perception, pharmacologic tolerance) and care-delivery contexts (pharmacist involvement, digital reminders, family participation). Future interventions should be designed as multidimensional bundles combining drug, behavioral, and process elements, with explicit reporting of each component to enable replication and cross-center comparison.

Global studies report pooled OR = 0.32 for intervention effectiveness [62], slightly lower than our result (OR = 0.36)—likely due to China’s higher baseline IBP rates. From an international perspective, the findings of this review can be interpreted at two levels. First, several core findings are likely to be generalizable beyond China. Gastrointestinal motility disorders, psychological anxiety, and medication-related effects were the dominant risk factors in our pooled analysis, which is consistent with broader international evidence on inadequate bowel preparation [5, 15]. Similarly, the direction of benefit associated with individualized education, regimen optimization, and multidisciplinary support is aligned with global intervention literature, in which bowel preparation-focused strategies also improve adequacy (pooled OR = 0.32) [62]. Second, some implementation features identified in this review are more specific to the Chinese healthcare context. These include the reliance on widely used digital communication platforms for patient education and reminders, as well as the marked differences in protocol implementation between primary and tertiary hospitals [6, 10]. These context-dependent factors may influence the feasibility and scalability of individualized interventions across health systems and should therefore be considered when interpreting the broader applicability of our findings. Sex-related differences in inadequate bowel preparation may also be clinically relevant; however, the included studies did not consistently report sex-stratified outcomes. Therefore, we were unable to determine from the current dataset whether IBP risk or intervention effectiveness differed significantly between male and female patients. Colonoscopy indication may also influence bowel preparation quality. Compared with relatively healthy individuals undergoing screening colonoscopy, patients with intestinal diseases such as colonic neoplasia or Crohn’s disease may present with different baseline bowel habits, intestinal inflammation, prior bowel surgery, medication exposure, and symptom burden, all of which may affect IBP risk and tolerance of preparation. Therefore, findings from these populations should not be assumed to be fully interchangeable when interpreting bowel preparation outcomes. For example, obese patients in Western countries have 35% baseline IBP [63], vs. 52.1% in China, possibly due to higher visceral fat area (128 ± 22 vs. 110 ± 20 cm²) [64]. Our finding that dose-adjusted PEG (2.5–3 L) is effective contradicts ESGE’s 2 L recommendation [4], highlighting the need for China-specific dosing.

Risk stratification tool

Develop a Chinese-specific risk score incorporating 3 key factors: colonic transit time (> 72 h: +3 points), anxiety score (> 7: +2 points), and long-term medication use (+ 2 points). Scores ≥ 5 indicate high risk (IBP rate > 50%).

Primary hospital priorities

Deploy “2 + 1” intervention kits: 2 core interventions (split-dose PEG + WeChat reminders) + 1 population-specific add-on (e.g., simethicone for post-surgical patients).

Policy advocacy

Use adverse event data (intervention group severe AE rate 0%) to support including child-specific PEG in the National Essential Medicines List.

Although inadequate bowel preparation (IBP) is widely recognized to increase direct medical costs through repeat procedures, extended anesthesia time, and delayed diagnoses, few studies in our review provided explicit cost-effectiveness data. Nevertheless, preliminary estimates can be inferred from reported adherence gains and reductions in IBP. For example, nurse–pharmacist co-led “2 + 1” kits—comprising split-dose PEG, digital reminders, and one tailored add-on—require modest additional resources (training, checklist development, and digital message systems), but can reduce IBP by 40–60%. In primary hospitals, where repeat colonoscopies impose significant financial strain and patient travel costs, avoiding one repeat procedure offsets the incremental cost of implementing these bundles many times over.

By contrast, interventions involving incisional sustained-release devices or specialized flavored PEG formulations carry higher material costs and are best prioritized for populations with the greatest baseline risk, such as post-surgical patients or children with poor tolerability. Digital reminders via ubiquitous platforms (e.g., WeChat/Alipay) are low-cost and scalable, offering a particularly favorable cost–benefit ratio for grassroots settings.

In summary, individualized interventions differ not only in clinical effectiveness but also in economic feasibility. A tiered approach may be most pragmatic: low-cost digital reminders and standardized checklists as the baseline for all patients, with higher-cost pharmacological or device-based add-ons reserved for high-risk subgroups. This cost-sensitive prioritization could maximize value in resource-limited primary hospitals.

This review has several limitations. First, although we synthesized data across six predefined high-risk populations, many included studies were single-center with small sample sizes, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, heterogeneity in definitions (e.g., “long-term medication” varied from ≥ 1 month to ≥ 6 months) may have introduced misclassification. In addition, the neurological disease subgroup was clinically heterogeneous. Several included studies combined stroke and Parkinson’s disease under a single neurological category without reporting diagnosis-specific outcomes, which may have obscured condition-specific mechanisms and intervention effects on IBP. Additionally, data on antidiabetic pharmacotherapy were insufficiently reported across the included studies. In particular, medication-specific exposure, such as use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, was not consistently documented, precluding subgroup analysis of drug-related effects on bowel motility and IBP in diabetic patients. Furthermore, sex-specific data were insufficiently reported across the included studies, precluding subgroup analysis of potential gender differences in IBP risk and response to individualized interventions. In addition, only 4 of the 32 RCTs tracked 6-month repeat examination rates, and no studies evaluated cost-effectiveness beyond 1 month.Third, most included trials were conducted in tertiary centers, which may not reflect the resource constraints of primary hospitals or community health services where the majority of screening colonoscopies now occur. Few studies (3/43) report IBP rates in patients with multiple comorbidities (e.g., obese + diabetic), a common clinical scenario. Finally, reporting of adverse events and adherence outcomes was inconsistent, restricting our ability to pool safety endpoints comprehensively. Publication bias was not formally assessed in this review. In addition, formal certainty grading of the overall body of evidence was not performed. Because this review included mixed evidence types and clinically heterogeneous interventions across distinct high-risk populations, the certainty of evidence for each pooled or narrative conclusion could not be rated in a standardized outcome-specific manner. This should be considered when interpreting the strength of the conclusions. Moreover, because this review was based primarily on studies conducted in China, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations, ethnicities, or healthcare systems, where dietary patterns, care pathways, resource availability, and patient characteristics may differ.

Future research should move beyond general efficacy comparisons and directly address implementation in grassroots settings. Priority directions include: (1) developing and validating simplified risk stratification checklists (e.g., obesity, diabetes, long-term medication) that can be applied at primary care intake; (2) piloting nurse- or pharmacist-led “2 + 1” preparation kits in county-level hospitals to test scalability outside tertiary centers; (3) integrating low-cost digital reminders via widely used platforms (WeChat/Alipay) into preparation workflows, particularly in rural areas with limited face-to-face education time; (4) building multicenter pragmatic trials that recruit from community and senior citizen universities to improve representativeness; and (5) strengthening training programs for primary care nurses and endoscopists to standardize preparation education and adverse event monitoring. Such targeted strategies will help ensure that evidence-based individualized regimens can be translated from tertiary centers into routine practice at the grassroots level.

This review supports several actionable, China-adapted practice changes for painless colonoscopy in high-risk populations. At admission, a simple risk-stratification trigger can be used to identify patients who should be prioritized for individualized bowel preparation. Key features include colonic transit time > 72 h, a GAD-7 anxiety score > 7, and long-term medication use. Patients scoring ≥ 5 should be flagged as high-risk and routed to individualized preparation pathways. This aligns with our pooled data showing substantial risk contributions from motility delay, psychological factors, and drug interactions.

In practice, the proposed “2 + 1” preparation kit may include split-dose PEG as the default backbone, WeChat-based reminders, and one population-specific add-on intervention. Examples include pharmacist-nurse medication reconciliation for long-term medication users, thickened PEG with scheduled abdominal massage for patients with neurological disease, and flavored or child-specific PEG with parental training for children. These measures are the most effective bundles in our meta-analysis and subgroup results, improving adherence (up to 92.1% in pediatrics) and lowering IBP (pooled OR ≈ 0.36; pharmacist-nurse collaboration OR ≈ 0.29).

These pathways may also provide operational and safety benefits. By reducing repeat procedures and repeat anesthesia, they can help relieve endoscopy unit capacity strain and reduce direct per-patient costs. In addition, individualized interventions were associated with fewer adverse events in the pooled analysis.

Conclusion

Inadequate bowel preparation in Chinese high-risk populations is driven by physiological, psychological, and healthcare system factors. Individualized nursing interventions—tailored to China’s painless colonoscopy context—significantly reduce IBP rates. Standardizing protocols in primary hospitals, integrating pharmacist-nurse collaboration, and developing child-specific regimens will further improve care quality. This review provides a pragmatic framework for nursing practice and policy-making in resource-constrained settings.

In routine practice, a risk-stratified, nurse-pharmacist-led ‘2 + 1’ preparation kit can be adopted immediately in primary and tertiary settings to lower IBP, reduce repeat anesthesia, and free endoscopy capacity. Standardized checklists, EHR flags for high-risk features, and child-specific regimens are pragmatic next steps for hospitals seeking rapid quality gains.
