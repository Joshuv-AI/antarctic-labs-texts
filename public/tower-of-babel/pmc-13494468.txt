In Regard to Regan et al
Authors: Erkan Topkan, Efsun Somay, Ugur Selek
Published: 2026

We congratulate Regan et al1 for their study, which evaluated the use of concurrent and adjuvant nivolumab in combination with chemoradiation for high-risk p16+ oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma. Although there is a strong rationale for using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in this context, the study results revealed no significant survival benefit and pointed out considerable toxicity. Several factors may account for the lack of ICI benefit, and it is crucial to consider these factors because they may influence future research on the combination of ICIs and chemoradiation therapy in this patient population.

First, radiation-induced immune suppression may have blunted nivolumab’s potential efficacy in this study because elective nodal irradiation, a standard component of head and neck radiation, can impair antigen presentation by sterilizing draining lymph nodes that serve as critical sites for T-cell priming.2 This fact may explain the contrasts in therapeutic efficacy observed between patients who initially present with metastatic disease, in which the nodal basins remain intact, and those who undergo definitive chemoradiation, where the effectiveness appears to diminish.

Second, the sequencing of treatments may not have been optimal. Evidence from non-small cell lung cancer suggests that concurrent chemoimmunoradiation may not provide a survival benefit. In contrast, adjuvant-only immunotherapy has shown improved outcomes, as demonstrated in the PACIFIC trial.3 Similarly, recent studies in head and neck cancer, such as CheckMate-141 and KEYNOTE-412,4,5 have not shown enhanced outcomes with concurrent immunotherapy, suggesting that induction or adjuvant-only approaches may prove to be more effective.

Third, the high rates of toxicity and treatment discontinuation in this study may have significantly affected overall drug exposure. Notably, 69% of patients who were receiving nivolumab ended their treatment prematurely, mainly because of severe mucositis, dysphagia, difficulty swallowing, and failure to thrive, indicating a substantial decline in overall health and nutritional status. This premature discontinuation likely diminished the full therapeutic potential of nivolumab, thereby impacting the overall efficacy of the treatment regimen.

In conclusion, the failure of nivolumab in this trial likely reflects a combination of radiation therapy-related immune suppression, unfavorable sequencing, and high treatment-related toxicity. Therefore, future studies may consider omitting elective nodal irradiation and adopting induction or adjuvant-only immunotherapy strategies to better exploit the potential synergy between chemoirradiation and ICIs. Finally, we extend our gratitude to the authors for providing us with the opportunity to comment on this critical topic within the field of head and neck oncology.
