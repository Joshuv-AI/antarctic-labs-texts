Neonatal hypoxia-ischemia alters the events governing the hippocampal critical period of postnatal synaptic plasticity leading to deficits in working memory in mice
Authors: Pritika Parmar, Harisa Spahic, Charles Lechner, Mark St Pierre, Katherine Carlin, Michael Nugent, Raul Chavez-Valdez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostnatal critical periods of synaptic plasticity (CPsp) are characterized by profound neural network refinement, which is shaped by synaptic activity and sculpted by maturation of the GABAergic network. Even after therapeutic hypothermia (TH), neonatal hypoxia-ischemia (HI) impairs two triggers for the initiation of the CPsp in the hippocampus: i) PSA-NCAM developmental decline and ii) parvalbumin (PV) + interneuron (IN) maturation. Thus, we investigated whether neonatal HI despite TH disturbs other events governing the onset, consolidation and closure of the postnatal CPsp in the hippocampus. We induced cerebral HI in P10 C57BL6 mice with right carotid ligation and 45 m of hypoxia (FiO2 = 0.08), followed by normothermia (36 °C, NT) or TH (31 °C) for 4 h with anesthesia-exposed shams as controls. ELISA, immunoblotting and immunohistochemistry were performed at 24 h (P11), 5 days (P15), 8 days (P18) and 30 days (P40) after HI injury. We specifically assessed: i) BDNF levels and TrkB activation, controlling the CPsp, ii) Otx2 and NPTX2 immunoreactivity (IR), engaging CPsp onset and iii) NogoR1, Lynx1 IR, PNN formation and myelination (MBP) mediating CPsp closure. Pups aged to P40 also received a battery of tests assessing working memory. Here, we documented deficits in hippocampal BDNF levels and TrkB activation at P18 in response to neonatal HI even with TH. Neonatal HI impaired in the CA1 the developmental increase in PV, Otx2, and NPTX2 between P11 and P18, the colocalization of Otx2 and PV at P18 and P40, the accumulation of NPTX2 in PV+ dendrites at P18 and P40, and the expression of NogoR at P40. Furthermore, neonatal HI decreased BDNF and impaired PNN development and myelination (MBP) at P40. Most of these abnormalities were insensitive to TH and correlated with memory deficits. Neonatal HI appears to disrupt many of the molecular and structural events initiating and consolidating the postnatal hippocampal CPsp, perhaps due to the early and delayed deficits in TrkB activation leading to memory deficits.Keywords: CA1, Interneurons, Memory, Parvalbumin, Neonatal hypoxia-ischemia, hippocampus, Synaptic plasticity

Introduction

In the hippocampus, undisturbed progression of the postnatal critical period of synaptic plasticity (CPsp) is crucial for functional maturation (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017). Profound refinement and remodeling of hippocampal circuits with extensive synaptic outgrowth and pruning occur during this CPsp (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Catts et al., 2013; Gao et al., 2018; Hensch, 2005; Sayin et al., 2015; Travaglia et al., 2016a; Zhang et al., 2011). Successful progression of the CPsp results in stable neural networks sculpted by experience (Hensch, 2005; Travaglia et al., 2018). Unlike the earlier CPsp in somatosensory cortex and amygdala, the hippocampal CPsp is constituted by multiple waves of synaptic refinement with specific neurobehavioral correlates, beginning with mastering of memories linked to objects and culminating with complex circumstances (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017). This collective hippocampal multi-wave process extends into early adulthood in humans (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Catts et al., 2013; Gao et al., 2018; Sayin et al., 2015; Travaglia et al., 2016b; Zhang et al., 2011). Impaired initiation of CPsp results in oversimplified neural networks, while impairment in the closure results in excessive synaptic rewiring. Either of these disturbances may translate into neurodevelopmental deficits and neuropsychiatric disorders (Bavelier et al., 2010).

The developmental events leading to the initiation and closure of the CPsp have been well established in the visual cortex using models of ocular dominance, (Di Cristo et al., 2007; Hensch, 2005) but evidence suggest that these events are largely preserved across brain regions including in the hippocampus (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Djordjevic et al., 2015; Nugent et al., 2023; Rizzi et al., 2011; St Pierre et al., 2022; Travaglia et al., 2016b; Travaglia et al., 2018). Among the triggers for initiation of the CPsp, the expression of poly-sialic acid modification of the Neural Cell Adhesion Molecule (PSA-NCAM), which is essential during early brain development for normal neurogenesis, migration, and anchoring of pyramidal cells (PCs), (Bonfanti, 2006; Cremer et al., 2000; Durbec and Cremer, 2001; Gascon et al., 2010; Nacher et al., 2013; Senkov et al., 2012) must decline to allow the maturation and stabilization of the GABAergic networks (Belanger and Di Cristo, 2011; Di Cristo et al., 2007). Neonatal HI prevents the decline of PSA-NCAM in the hippocampus for up to eight days after the insult in mice (~2 year old human brain equivalent) (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a), which may result in cognition and memory impairments as shown previously by us (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019; Spahic et al., 2022) and others (Higuera-Matas et al., 2009; Minana et al., 2000; Sharma and Kaur, 2008; Zharkovsky et al., 2003). Equally importantly, neonatal HI persistently disrupts the normal GABAergic network development in the mouse dorsal CA1 subfield decreasing the number interneurons (INs) expressing parvalbumin (PV), inducing somatodendritic attrition of those PV+ INs that survive injury and producing persistent deficit in GAD65/67+ buttons (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Spahic et al., 2022) Whether increased PSA-NCAM expression and impaired GABAergic maturation prevents the onset and consolidation of the CPsp in the dorsal hippocampus after neonatal HI is unknown.

In addition to PSA-NCAM and GABAergic development, the boundaries of the CPsp are governed by the rise of brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a regulator of activity-dependent synaptic plasticity, (Goren, 2016) and by the peak activation of the cognate BDNF receptor, TrkB, which leads maturation of PV+ INs (Zheng et al., 2011) and initiation of the CPsp. In the rodent hippocampus, the TrkB activation via phosphorylation plateaus around P17-P18 (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Carstens et al., 2016; Travaglia et al., 2016a; Travaglia et al., 2016b; Ye et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2016). Following BDNF binding to the ectodomain of TrkB, various residues auto-phosphorylates leading to activation of differential downstream cascade and effects (McCarty and Feinstein, 1999; Middlemas et al., 1994). For instance, Y515 TrkB phosphorylation facilitates the binding of the adaptor protein Shc, which via PI3 Kinase/Akt and MAPK cascades promotes neuronal differentiation and synaptic formation, while Y816 phosphorylation facilitates the association of the phospholipase Cɣ1, which via PI3 and diacylglycerol cascades promotes CREB-mediated gene expression to enhance cell survival and synaptic plasticity (Huang et al., 2019; Minichiello et al., 2002; Reichardt, 2006). BDNF binding to TrkB receptor is also required for synthesis and release of neuronal pentraxin 2 (NPTX2), which modulates excitatory synapse formation, plasticity and inputs (Gomez de San et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2013; Mariga et al., 2015; Maynard et al., 2020; Pelkey et al., 2015). NPTX2 produced by pyramidal cells (PCs) localizes on AMPA receptors to allow development and stabilization of excitatory synapses onto PV+ INs and consolidation of the CPsp (Angulo et al., 1997; Chang et al., 2010). Formation of perineural nets (PNNs) supporting NPTX2 proximity to PV+ INs and myelination closes the CPsp, (Akbik et al., 2012; Fournier et al., 2001; Hensch, 2005; Lee et al., 2017; Makinodan et al., 2012; Xiao et al., 2017) events that are typically completed during late adolescence (>P30) in the rodent hippocampus (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Carstens et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2018; Guirado et al., 2014; Travaglia et al., 2016b). Knowing that PSA-NCAM also modulates BDNF function thereby facilitating the neurotrophin-receptor interaction, (Rutishauser, 2008; Vutskits et al., 2001), we aim to investigate whether neonatal HI despite TH disturbs the events governing the onset, consolidation and closure of the postnatal CPsp in the hippocampus, hypothesizing that disturbance in BDNF-TrkB would result in disruption of downstream biochemical and structural events governing the boundaries of CPsp. Pre-clinical and clinical data suggest that therapeutic hypothermia (TH), the standard of clinical care for full-term infants suffering of HI encephalopathy (Shankaran et al., 2005), neither prevents memory impairments, (Azzopardi et al., 2014; Burnsed et al., 2015; Cainelli et al., 2021; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019; Shankaran et al., 2012) nor persistently protects the hippocampus (Burnsed et al., 2015; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019), a brain region crucial for memory processing. Thus, we also hypothesize that TH will not prevent disturbances in the hippocampal CPsp.

Materials and methods

Animals

For these experiments 208 C57BL6 mice (35 litters) of both sexes (98 males and 110 females) were used, with a HI model mortality of 16 %, which resulted in 188 mice included in the final analysis. Litters culled to 6 (equalized to 3 males and 3 females) were purchased from Charles River (Charles River Laboratories, Inc.; Wilmington, MA). ARRIVE guidelines were followed for the animal protocol which was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Johns Hopkins University – School of Medicine. The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals provided by the NIH, US Department of Health and Human Services 85–23, 1985 was also followed. Experimental design is shown in Fig. 1A.

General Methods Diagram.

(A) C57BL6 mice received isoflurane for anesthesia to be either treated as a control sham or receive carotid artery ligation and hypoxia for 45 min (FiO2 0.08) and then randomized to normothermia (NT, 36 °C) or hypothermia (TH, 36 °C) for 4 h. Pups were killed at P11, P15, P18 or P40 for either hippocampal microdissection or 4 % PFA brain perfusion and coronal sectioning using a freezing microtome. (B) Hippocampus was homogenized with sonication, prior to clarification and aliquot was collected to run GFAP, α-fodrin and validation experiments. Following clarification, supernatant was used for BDNF ELISA and pellet was used for TrkB WBS. (C) Following sectioning of perfused and cryoprotected brains in 50 μm-thick sections, 2 sections 600 μm apart with embedded dorsal hippocampus, were used for staining and evaluation under confocal microscopy in two contiguous high magnification z-stacks in the CA1 and CA3 subfields.

Neonatal HI injury and TH

Pups from each litter were assigned in a 1:2 ratio to sham: HI treatments. Cerebral HI was induced using the Vannucci model modified for mice at postnatal day (P) 10 in 124 C57BL6 mice (Graham et al., 2004). Mice were exposed to anesthesia using isoflurane at 3 % (induction, 30–60 s) and 1 % (maintenance, 60 to 120 s) during right carotid artery ligation. After 1 h recovery with the dam, mice were exposed to 45 min of hypoxia (FiO2 = 0.08), followed by randomization to normothermia (NT, 36 °C) or TH (31 °C) for 4 h (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019). Continuous rectal temperature monitoring was performed using PowerLab data acquisition system (Ad Instruments, Inc., Colorado Springs, CO). This preclinical model of TH provides neuroprotection sustainably in the cortex, but transiently in the hippocampus (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017). The histopathological evolution of the injury produced by the model in our hands has already been reported elsewhere (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019). In total, 64 pups were assigned as sham (P11 = 12, P15 = 11, P18 = 13, P40 = 28) while 63 pups were randomized to HI/NT (P11 = 13, P15 = 8, P18 = 13, P40 = 29) and an additional 61 were randomized to HI/TH (P11 = 13, P15 = 9, P18 = 14, P40 = 25).

Brain processing

Mice sacrificed at P11, P15, P18, and P40 were deeply anesthetized by exposure to isoflurane. Mice assigned to immunohistochemistry (IHC) received a 10 mL trans-cardiac infusion of 0.1 M PBS pH 7.4 followed by 4 % paraformaldehyde (4 % PFA) in 0.1 M PBS for 5 min (4 mL/min) for brain fixation. Brains were cryoprotected in sucrose gradient until sinking, snap-frozen in dry-ice cold 2-methylbutane, to store at −80 °C for later coronal sectioning (50 μm) using freezing microtome to study the dorsal hippocampus as previously reported (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a). For fresh tissue collection, mice were decapitated after anesthesia, and brains were transferred onto a freezing plate where the hippocampus proper was micro-dissected then snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C (Fig. 1B).

BDNF Sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Hippocampal tissues underwent extraction protocol using an acid-extraction buffer (50 mmol/L sodium acetate, 1 mol/L NaCl, 0.1 % Triton X100 10 %, and acetic acid to pH of 4.0) with 20 % (v:v) glycerol and protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich; 11,836,153,001), according to manufacturer’s instructions (Biosensis Pty Ltd., Thebarton, South Australia). Hippocampi were sonicated (5–7 s, x2, 30 min apart) in acid-extraction buffer at 10:1 [w (mg): v (μL)] on ice. Half of the mixture was saved for immunoblotting and the remaining was centrifuged (30 min, 10,000 x g, 4 °C), to produce clarified supernatant. Total protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay. Sample diluent was prepared mixing 3:1 (v:v) acid-extraction buffer and incubation/neutralization buffer (1 mol/L phosphate buffer = 0.1 mol/L KH2PO4 + 0.1 mol/L Na2HPO4, pH 7.6). Clarified supernatants were diluted 1:1 (v:v) with sample diluent prior to incubating in duplicate in the BDNF antibody pre-coated well. A standard curve was produced by sequential dilution of lyophilized antigen in sample diluent (7.8 to 500 pg/mL). The detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP conjugate were diluted 100-fold with Assay Diluent A (from manufacturer). The 450 nm absorbance was read using a colorimetric plate reader with no incubation. Intra-assay and inter-assay variability were 2.4 % and 3.6 % in our hands.

Western blotting for validation of antibodies used for IHC, assessment of brain injury using α-fodrin and GFAP and evaluation of TrkB activation

Methods were previously described (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Lechner et al., 2021; Spahic et al., 2022). Hippocampal crude homogenates were prepared from sham mice at P11, P15, P18 and P40. Homogenates were prepared using homogenization buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Twenty percent (w/v) glycerol was added for cryoprotection and protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford, 1976). A 3:1 (v:v) dilution of 25 μg of protein homogenate in 4× loading buffer (reducing conditions), then loaded in 4–20 % mini-protean TGX polyacrylamide precast protein gels (Biorad Inc., Hercules, CA), subjected to electrophoresis and finally gels were transfer to nitrocellulose membrane using TransBlot Turbo Midi-size (Biorad Inc). Membrane was stained with Ponceau S then blocked with 2 % normal goat serum (NGS) instead of standard BSA to replicate the conditions used for the immunohistochemistry (IHC) experiments. NGS was diluted to 2 % using 0.1 % Tween-20 in 50 mM Tris buffered saline (TBS-T, pH 7.4). Nitrocellulose blots were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies at 1:500. After exposure to primary antibody, membranes were washed with TBS-T, exposed to secondary antibodies for 1 h and then developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Clarity Western ECL Substrate, Biorad Inc.). Specificity of the antibodies for immunofluorescent (IF)-IHC experiments were: i) the identification of a single or multiple bands at molecular weights described for the target proteins and ii) lack of non-specific bands at molecular weight not reported for the protein target, unless well reported post-transcriptional modifications were detected with the antibody. The reliability of sample loading and protein transfer was verified by staining nitrocellulose membranes with Ponceau S before immunoblotting.

For assessment of brain injury, we followed the methods described above and sections were exposed to the following primary antibodies: 1) α-fodrin (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale NY, USA, FG6090; RRID: AB_2050678) mouse monoclonal IgG1 antibody raised against chicken blood cell membranes following hypotonic lysis and mechanical enucleation (0.25 μg/mL), detecting bands at 250, ~150, and ~ 120 kDa and 2) GFAP (ProteinTech, 16,825–1-AP; RRID: AB_2109646) rabbit polyclonal IgG antibody raised against GFAP fusion protein (ProteinTECH, Ag10423) (0.2 μg/mL), detecting a band at ~50 kDa.

For TrkB activation we used the following antibodies: 1) Y515 TrkB (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# PA5–36695, RRID: AB_2553666) rabbit polyclonal IgG antibody raised against synthetic phosphopeptide derived from human Trk B around the phosphorylation site of Tyrosine 516 (0.5 μg/mL), detecting a band at ~150 kDa; 2) Y817 TrkB (MyBioSource, MBS4750089; RRID: AB_2938791) rabbit polyclonal IgG antibody raised against a synthesized peptide derived from human phosphotyrosine 817 TrkB (1 μg/mL), detecting a band at ~150 kDa and 3) TrkB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5–78405; RRID: AB_2736725) rabbit polyclonal IgG antibody raised against carrier-protein conjugated synthetic peptide encompassing a sequence within the C-terminus region of mouse TrkB (The exact sequence is proprietary) (0.04 μg/mL; 1:1000), detecting a band at ~150 kDa and a truncated form at 90 kDa.

Multichannel IF-IHC

Floating IHC was performed as previously described (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Lechner et al., 2021; Spahic et al., 2022). IHC was performed in two 600 μm-apart coronal sections to evaluate the dorsal CA1 or CA3 subfields of the hippocampus in two contiguous high-magnification fields (Fig. 1C). For these experiments, brain sections were classified as anterior as previously reported (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Spahic et al., 2022). Sections (50 μm-thick) were washed in TBS pH 7.2 for 10 min followed by antigen retrieval with sodium citrate buffer pH 6.0 incubated for 90 min at 80 °C. Tissues were permeabilized using Triton X in TBS for 15 min at RT at concentrations of 0.2 % for P11, 0.4 % for P15 and P18, and 0.6 % for P40 sections. Blocking step was performed using 10 % NGS in 0.1 % Tween/TBS for 1 h at RT, prior to exposure primary antibodies. Sections were exposed to the following antibody combinations: chicken anti-PV (Novus Cat# NBP2–50036, RRID: AB_2814697; 1: 250) combined with one of the following antibodies: 1) rabbit anti-Otx2 (Proteintech Cat# 13497–1-AP, RRID: AB_2157176; 1:400), 2) rabbit anti-NPTX2 (Proteintech Cat# 10889–1-AP, RRID: AB_2153875; 1:400), 3) rabbit anti-NogoR1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# PA5–77534, RRID: AB_2736244; 1:400) or 4) Rabbit anti-Lynx1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# PA5–70413, RRID: AB_2690510; 1:400). Additionally, monoclonal mouse anti-purified anti-Myelin Basic Protein antibody (MBP, Bio-Legend Cat# 808402, RRID: AB_2564742; 1:2000) was combined with polyclonal rabbit anti-Olig 2 (Millipore Cat# AB9610, RRID: AB_570666; 1:500). Primary antibodies were mixed in 4 % NGS in TBS (for NogoR1, Lynx1) or TBS-T (for PV, Otx2, NPTX2, MBP and Olig2) overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation with each primary antibodies and washes, tissues were incubated in the dark for 2 h at RT to goat anti-chicken IgY Alexa Fluor 488, goat anti-rabbit or anti mouse conjugated to either Alexa Fluor 568 or 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA). Secondary antibodies were mixed in 4 % NGS /TBS-T. Following exposure to secondary antibodies, tissues were incubated for 5 min in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/mL) in TBS, washed in TBS, and mounted and dried for 30 min prior to coverslip with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies Corp., Carlsbad, CA).

Antibodies

All antibodies used for IHC experiments were tested for specificity using western blotting ensuring specificity and that the molecular weight of the detected protein corresponds to expected size. Positive controls were used when available. Negative controls were used for all IHC experiments. These included no primary antibody and subtype and species specific immunoglobulin replacing primary antibody at identical concentrations. The used subtype and species-specific immunoglobulin were as follows: 1) rabbit IgG isotype (Vector Laboratories, Inc.; Burlingame, CA, USA; RRID: AB_2336355); 2) mouse IgG isotype (Vector Laboratories, Inc.; AB_2336354) and 3) chicken IgY isotype (R&D Systems; RRID: AB_354263).

Wisteria floribunda lectin (WFA) fluorescent staining for PNNs

Sections were washed in 1× PBS for 10 min, blocked with 10 % NGS in PBS at RT for 90 min, and incubated in green fluorescein conjugated - WFA (Vector Labs, FL-1351-2; at 1:400) in 4 % NGS in PBS for 6 h at RT. WFA is a lectin that preferentially binds to chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan glycosaminoglycan (CSPG) side chains which is enriched in PNNs (Pizzorusso et al., 2002; Reinhard et al., 2019). Following PBS washes, slices were incubated with rabbit anti-Otx2 (Proteintech Cat# 13497–1-AP, RRID: AB_2157176; 1:400) in TBS and lastly in 1 μg/mL DAPI solution for 5 min, washed in PBS, and mounted and dried for 30 min prior to coverslip with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies Corp.).

Quantification of IF-IHC experiments

The dorsal CA1 analyzed included the region limited by the sub-iculum (septally) and the CA2 subfield (temporally). The analysis was performed exclusively in the pyramidal cell layer (PCL) of the dorsal CA1 subfield in two z-stacks per animal and at two levels 600 μm apart (Fig. 1A). Representative microphotographs of brain sections exposing the dorsal CA1 subfield of the hippocampus are shown in Fig. 1C. For only MBP/Olig2, the focus was instead on the CA3 as myelinated axons trajectory can be more clearly followed, incoming from the Mossy Fibers and outgoing towards the CA1 from the Schaffer Collaterals. Confocal microscopy. Z-stacks were taken at 1440 × 1440 pixels, 16-bit depth, and averaged X2, captured using a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective and 1.0 zoom to produce an uncompressed image of 101.54 × 101.54 μm at the most septal and temporal portions of the dorsal subfields included for the respective protein target. Z-stacks were set for slicing at 1 air unit (0.8 μm) to the maximal wavelength used in the experiments (639 nm) using a Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope LSM700 AxioObserver from Carl Zeiss AG (Oberkochen, Germany). The same configurations for pinhole, gain and offset were used to capture repeats for each experiment. Detection wavelengths were 300–483 nm for DAPI, 493 to 550 nm for Alexa 488, 560–600 nm for Alexa 568 and 644–800 nm for Alexa 647. Z-stacks were saved in .czi format for processing uncompressed. Analysis using ZenLite and ImageJ. Total IF signal within the PCL of the CA1 subfield was quantified with standard software-generated threshold using the histogram function in the Zen 2.3 blue edition (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany). Quantification of dorsal CA1 area. CA1 area was measured using the freehand selection function of Image J software and calibrated to mm2. Quantification of NOGOR1, Otx2 and Lynx1 immunofluorescence (IF). Following 3D reconstruction, the total IF was calculated relative to the evaluated area of the CA1 PCL (mm2). Image processing using Imaris. Image processing of PNNs, NPTX2 and MBP/Olig2 experiments were performed using Imaris x64 v9.8 software (Bitplane AG. Belfast, UK) as we have reported previously (Nugent et al., 2023; St Pierre et al., 2023; St Pierre et al., 2022). Volumetric analysis: Following automatic creation of an algorithm and definition of channel source and level of surface detailing, the detected surface renderings were adjusted automatically after background subtraction and masking to zero voxels inside surface were applied. Split touching object function was used with the seed point diameter set to the average diameter of the cell/object of interest. Mean statistics for cell/object count, volume, and immunofluorescence intensity (IF) were retrieved for analysis with the volume of the region of interest (ROI; single PNNs for WFA, CA1 PCL for NPTX2 and total z-stack volume for MBP. Additional measurements included length (μm) and area (μm2) for filament analysis (MBP). Thus, all measurements reported were adjusted for the field volume (μm3) of ROI within the z-stack in which they were imaged. Filament reconstruction for MBP. An automatic filament detection protocol was used to batch processing of conjugated MBP (deep red, Alexa 647). Creation parameters involved starting point threshold range covering 10 % of the upper third quartile for even distribution throughout the field. Dendritic complexity analysis: an automatic dendrite detection protocol was used using the green channel (fluorescein conjugated for WFA). We created new filaments starting with the automatic algorithm setting to calculate diameter of filament. Starting points, the largest dendritic diameter, and seed points, the smallest dendritic diameter, were identified in slice mode and adjusted using automatic and manual thresholds. Detected points were then automatically connected with lines following the image intensity. Detailed, average values statistics were then exported for analysis.

Behavioral testing

Behavioral testing included open field (OF), Y maze testing (YM) and object location task (OLT) as previously described by our group and others with randomized testing order (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019). OF at P39-P40 measures interest in new environments as well as general anxiety and locomotion patterns. During OF, mouse is placed in the center of the area and given 5 min for free exploration to determine percentage time spend in both, center and periphery. YM testing at P34–35 for phase 1 and P38–39 for phase 2 is performed using a plexiglass YM with 3 arms (120° apart) to assess spatial working memory with calculation of spontaneous alternation performance (SAP) and percent of time spend exploring the re-opened (novel) arm. Lastly, OLT, conducted at P37, uses time spent exploring a novel object to assay spatial and working memory. After a habituation trial, a mouse is given 5 min in an area with 4 novel objects. After a 30 min rest period, the mouse is given another 5 min trial to explore the same 4 objects after 2 of them are swapped. Exploratory behavior was considered when the mouse gets within 1 cm of the object with nose in direction the object. Total time exploring objects was measured and broken down into percentage of time exploring familiar and novel location. The exploratory preference (EP) score is the time exploring novel location divided by time exploring familiar location. Lower EP relates to impaired working memory. The ANY-Maze tracking software (Stoelting CO.; Wood Dale, IL) was used to measure all behavioral parameters described above.

Statistics

Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H one-way ANOVA (stratified by sex and not) was applied with post-hoc pair analysis using Dunn-Bonferroni’s test. All results were presented as hybrid box and whisker /scatter vertical plot, where the box was limited by the 25th and 75th percentiles and the solid line represented the median and all experimental data points are shown. Significance was assigned by p-value ≤0.05 in all cases, with adjustment for multiple comparisons. Non-parametric Spearman Rho correlations were applied and a best-fit regression line was calculated. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24v (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Results

Dysregulation of BDNF and TrkB activation at early and delayed time points after neonatal HI

In the rodent hippocampus rise in BDNF and peak TrkB activation occurs at P17-P18 (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Carstens et al., 2016; Travaglia et al., 2016a; Travaglia et al., 2016b; Ye et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2016). Similarly, in sham C57BL6 mice, hippocampal BDNF levels plateaued at P18 and remained at stable levels until P40 (Fig. 2A). Following neonatal HI, hippocampal BDNF levels appeared similar to those in sham at P11 and P15 (Fig. 2A). However, the increase in α-fodrin 150 kDa fragment (KW ANOVA p = 0.02, H 7.5, df 2; p = 0.02 vs. sham) over the 120 kDa fragment 24 h after neonatal HI (Fig 2B1 vs. 2B2) and its correlation with higher BDNF levels in (R2 0.43 [cubic], p < 0.001, Fig. 2C). At P15, higher GFAP levels in the hippocampus (KW ANOVA p = 0.002, H 12.9, df 2; p < 0.001 sham vs. HI; p = 0.04 sham vs. TH; Fig 2D1 &D2, which correlated with lower BDNF levels (R2 0.32 [quadratic], p < 0.001; Fig. 2E). The rise in BDNF levels documented in uninjured hippocampus at P18 (KW ANOVA p < 0.001, H 14.4, df 2; Fig. 2A) and P40 (KW ANOVA p = 0.02, H 7.7, df 2) was attenuated by neonatal HI (p < 0.001 vs. sham [P18], p = 0.02 vs. sham [P40]) even after treatment with TH (p = 0.004 vs. sham [P18], p = 0.01 vs. sham [P40]).

Although no deficits in BDNF were documented at P11, the HI-injured hippocampus demonstrated an 87.5 % lower level of phosphorylated (p) TrkB at the Y515 site (KW ANOVA p = 0.003, H11.9, df 2; p = 0.002 vs. sham, Fig. 3A) and a 72.7 % lower Y515 pTrkB to total TrkB (KW ANOVA p = 0.02, H 7.7, df 2; p = 0.007 vs. sham, Fig. 3D), which persist even with TH treatment (p = 0.004 vs. sham [Y515 TrkB] and p = 0.05 [Y515 TrkB/total TrkB]. As expected the BDNF levels did not correlate with Y515 pTrkB/TrkB ratio (Fig. 3E), nor with Y817 pTrkB/TrkB ratio (data not shown), supporting the concept that preserved levels of BDNF at P11 did not support a neurotrophic effect and instead may represent a marker of injury instead. At P15, a mild decrease in pTrkB at the Y817 site was also documented (KW ANOVA p = 0.02, H 8.1, df 2; p = 0.01 vs. sham, Fig. 3B) along with decrease TrkB levels (KW ANOVA p = 0.02, H 7.6, df 2; p = 0.02 vs. sham, Fig. 3C), not resulting in a change in pTrkB / TrkB ratio (Fig. 3D). Deficits in BDNF levels in the injured hippocampus at P18 (Fig. 2A) did result in deficit in Y515 pTrkB (KW ANOVA p = 0.04, p = 0.04 vs. sham; Fig. 3A) and Y515 pTrkB/TrkB ratio (KW ANOVA p = 0.05, H 5.4, df 2; p = 0.04 vs. sham; Fig. 3D). Unlike at P11, BDNF levels did correlate with Y515 pTrkB/TrkB ratio (Rho p = 0.03; Fig. 3F) at P18. Altogether these data support deficits in the BDNF / TrkB pathway at P11 and P18, two time points essential for the initiation and progression of the CPsp in the hippocampus.

Neonatal HI decreases nuclear localization of transcription factor Otx2 in PV expressing INs, disrupts PNN formation and decreases NPTX2

Along with BDNF/TrkB, the extracortical homeobox-containing transcription factor Otx2 is transferred to PV+INs, the most abundant subpopulation of GABAergic INs in the hippocampus, to support their maturation (Gibel-Russo et al., 2022; Mueller-Buehl et al., 2022; Tripathi et al., 2014). We documented that while at P11 both PV and Otx2 expressions were minimal in the uninjured CA1 (Fig. 4A), nuclear Otx2 increased at P15 along with a greater number of heavily PV expressing INs (Fig. 4B). By P18, heavily PV expressing INs in uninjured hippocampus showed minimal levels of Otx2, while those with low PV expression still showed high nuclear Otx2 (Fig. 4C). In contrast to sham, HI-injured hippocampus demonstrated lower Otx2 expression (KW ANOVA, p < 0.001, H 20.9, df 2; p = 0.001 vs. sham, Fig 4F1) and lower nuclear Otx2 (KW ANOVA p < 0.001, H 21.5, df 2; p < 0.001 vs. sham; Fig 4F2, 4D and 4G) at P15, becoming minimal by P18 (Fig. 4E). Otx2 supports the developmental formation and maintenance of PNN, which wraps most PV+ INs (Bernard and Prochiantz, 2016; Mueller-Buehl et al., 2022). PNN formation in the dorsal hippocampus was greatly increased by P40 (Fig. 5A), the time at which nuclear Otx2 was identified in sham. In the hippocampus injured by neonatal HI, PNN and Otx2 wrapping the cell was decreased even after TH (Fig. 5B). At P40, disruption of PNNs was quantified by the decreased WFA area (KW ANOVA p = 0.001, H 13.6, df 2; p = 0.001 vs. sham; Fig 5C1) and volume (KW ANOVA p < 0.001, H 17.1, df 2; p = 0.001 vs. sham; Fig 5C2) as exemplified in Fig. 5D, which is not prevented by TH. Increased neuronal pentraxin 2 (NPTX2) in excitatory synpases onto PV+ INs allows consolidation of the CPsp (Angulo et al., 1997; Chang et al., 2010) a process supported by the formation of PNNs (Akbik et al., 2012; Fournier et al., 2001; Hensch, 2005; Lee et al., 2017; Makinodan et al., 2012; St Pierre et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2017) In the uninjured CA1, the increase in NPTX2 expression paralleled that of PV (Fig. 6A, C & 6E) and stayed in close proximity to PV+ IN dendritic processes (Fig. 6G). Neonatal HI unequivocally decreases NPTX2 expression in the CA1 at P18 (Fig. 6D) and P40 (Fig. 6F). Specifically, 30 days after HI (P40), NPTX2 was decreased in immunofluorescence (IF) by 31.6 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.003, H 11.5, df 2; p = 0.03 vs. sham; Fig 6H1) and in number of buttons by 55.5 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.002, H 10.2, df 2; p = 0.04 vs. sham; Fig 6H2).

Decreased NogoR1 and impaired myelination in the HI-injured hippocampus

Although NogoR1 binds to several myelin inhibitors, it binds more avidly to Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), crucial components of the PNNs, which inhibit axonal growth to close the CPsp (Dickendesher et al., 2012; Galtrey and Fawcett, 2007). While NogoR1 increased between P18 and P40 (Fig. 7A & 7B) in the uninjured CA1, NogoR1 expression was decreased by 80.8 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.01, H 8.8, df 2; p = 0.01; Fig. 7C & 7E) in the HI-injured hippocampus even after treatment with TH (p = 0.04 vs. sham, Fig. 7D & 7E). Another molecule closing the CPsp is Lynx1, which limits plasticity in presence of normal cholinergic innervation in the primary visual cortex (Morishita et al., 2010). Lynx1 expression increased by 3.4-fold between P18 and P40 in the uninjured hippocampus (MWU test p = 0.003, Z-score 2.98; Fig 8A1–3), but neonatal HI did not produce any changes at P40 (KW ANOVA p = 0.07, H 5.3, df 2; Fig. 8B–D). Oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination are among the last events closing the CPsp (Lee et al., 2017; St Pierre et al., 2022; Takesian and Hensch, 2013). Neonatal HI decreases the MBP+ processes length by 25.5 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.018, H 8.1, df 2; p = 0.01 vs. sham, Fig 9A1), area by 30.7 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.03, H 6.9, df 2; p = 0.01 vs. sham, Fig 9A2) and volume by 33.3 % (KW ANOVA p = 0.03, H 6.5, df 2; p = 0.01 vs. sham, Fig 9A3). However, unlike the rest of our results, we identified attenuation by TH in the MBP+ processes length (p = 0.02 vs. HI/NT, Fig 9A1) and area (p = 0.05 vs. HI/NT, Fig 9A2). Representative reconstructions shown in Fig. 9B. While neonatal HI decreased the number of Olig2+ oligodendrocytes (KW ANOVA p = 0.01, H 9.3, df 2; p = 0.03 vs. sham, Fig 9A2), surprisingly TH did not attenuate the decrease (p = 0.003 vs. sham, Fig 9C1 & 9D). Olig2 + oligodendrocytes strongly correlated with MBP length (Spearman Rho 0.61, p = 0.006), area (Spearman Rho 0.74, p < 0.001) and volume (Spearman Rho 0.71, p = 0.001) following neonatal HI. Interestingly, when plotting separately the three treatment groups, the correlation was lost between MBP volume and number of Olig2+ cells, supporting greater myelination from fewer oligodendrocytes (Fig 9C2).

BDNF and TrkB receptor activation along with events closing the CPsp relate to impaired working memory following neonatal HI

Next, the relationship of some of the changes described above were correlated with working memory outcomes at P40. As shown in Fig. 10, higher BDNF levels correlated with higher percent of time spend in the center of the OF arena and higher EP in OLT, indicative that lower BDNF levels at P40 as seen in NT and TH groups after HI related to worse working memory (Fig. 2A). While increased phosphorylation at the y515 site of the TrkB receptor at P40 related to worse spatial memory in OLT at P40, increased phosphorylation of the y817 site related to improved outcomes in both OLT and YM (Fig. 10). Two main events known to close the CPsp were assessed in association to memory outcomes: PNN formation and myelination. As shown in Fig. 10, both events directly related to memory outcomes as such higher PNN volume, area and immunofluorescence as well as higher MBP length, area, volume and Olig2 counts correlated with higher SAP and lower overall memory impairment in YM and higher EP in OLT.

Discussion

The CPsp is characterized by profound refinement and remodeling of neural circuits resulting in mature networks sculpted by experience. Neonatal HI brain injury prevents the physiologic evolution governing the CPsp in the hippocampus including the decline of PSA-NCAM expression on PCs and the development of GABAergic PV+ INs (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Spahic et al., 2022). Here, we show that neonatal HI also results in a delayed decline in BDNF and TrkB activation with minimal nuclear translocation of Otx2 despite TH, events required for maturation of PV+ INs and initiation of the CPsp. Low BDNF levels in the injured hippocampus persist to P40, time at which NPTX2 expression is also decreased in the CA1 pyramidal cell layer supporting the notion of disrupted consolidation of the CPsp. The closure of the CPsp is characterized by PNN formation to stabilize the proximity of excitatory synapses to PV+ INs (Akbik et al., 2012; Fournier et al., 2001; Hensch, 2005; Lee et al., 2017; Makinodan et al., 2012; Xiao et al., 2017) and to promote myelination (Dickendesher et al., 2012; Galtrey and Fawcett, 2007). Neonatal HI leads to disrupted PNN formation and perhaps degradation, which occurs along with decreased NogoR1, Olig2+ cells counts and decreased myelination. Low BDNF levels along with disrupted PNN formation and myelination correlate with worse working memory in these animals. Thus, neonatal HI at the very minimum delays many of the events governing the initiation, consolidation and closure of the CPsp resulting in deficits in working memory.

Nearly all of these changes are not prevented by TH except for myelination, which may be the result of hypertrophic post-mitotic myelinating oligodendrocytes. Indeed, TH allows myelination to proceed perhaps allowing the closure of the CPsp; however, the poor development of PNNs shown here along with the already decreased PV+ IN counts found in TH-treated injured hippocampus (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Spahic et al., 2022), suggest long-lasting hippocampal network deficits, which may explain the memory deficits already reported by us previously in this model (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019; Spahic et al., 2022) and in neonates surviving HIE in the cooling era (Azzopardi et al., 2014; Cainelli et al., 2021; Shankaran et al., 2012).

The rise in BDNF and TrkB activation, which peak at P18 in rodents, govern the initiation of the hippocampal CPsp (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Carstens et al., 2016; Travaglia et al., 2016a; Travaglia et al., 2016b; Ye et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2016). At P11, the preserved hippocampal BDNF levels associated with α-fodrin breakdown products may suggest release from intracellular sources secondary to cell death and, as expected, was not associated with preservation but instead decreased in TrkB receptor activation. Because astrocytes can provide paracrine neuroprotection by releasing BDNF (de Pins et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2017), we studied the relationship between GFAP and BDNF expressions at P15, P18 and P40, to find that only at P15 was GFAP inversely related to BDNF levels, perhaps suggesting that 5 days after HI, astrocytes still have a pro-inflammatory instead of a homeostatic phenotype as we have described previously in the preclinical model and human biomarker work (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021a; Chavez-Valdez et al., 2021b; Duck et al., 2024). At P18, HI injury of the hippocampus prevents the developmental peak in BDNF levels and TrkB activation, which are required for the initiation of the CPsp to proceed. BDNF is also required for synthesis and release of NPTX2 to consolidate of excitatory synapses onto PV+ INs (Gomez de San et al., 2022), while Otx2 supports PV+ IN maturation and PNN formation (Gibel-Russo et al., 2022; Mueller-Buehl et al., 2022; Tripathi et al., 2014). Thus, the decrease in BDNF levels and Otx2 nuclear translocation in PV+ INs following neonatal HI also results in decreased NPTX2 in the PC layer of hippocampal CA at P18. Altogether, these disrupted events in the mouse hippocampus, suggest that neonatal HI prevents the normal progression to initiation and consolidation of the CPsp between P15 and P18 (toddler to early childhood human equivalent).

Many of the events closing the CPsp in the hippocampus are also disrupted by neonatal HI. We identify that PNN formation is disrupted 30 days after neonatal HI (P40) along with decreased in NPTX2 levels. The binding of NogoR1 to CSPGs, a crucial component of the PNNs, also inhibits axonal growth and closes the CPsp (Dickendesher et al., 2012; Galtrey and Fawcett, 2007) as shown by the reopening of the CPsp in several circuits upon NogoR1 genetic deletion, (Takesian and Hensch, 2013) which increases the dendritic spine turnover to juvenile levels (Akbik et al., 2013). In our experiments, 30 days after neonatal HI, NogoR1 abundance on PV+ INs is decreased explaining delayed oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination, the last events closing the CPsp (Lee et al., 2017; St Pierre et al., 2022; Takesian and Hensch, 2013). Although TH does not protect against the loss of Olig2 + cells, it does attenuate deficits in myelin (MBP). Since nuclear olig2 marks oligodendrocytes at any stage of development, a potential explanation is that TH may prevent the death of post-mitotic myelinating oligodendrocytes, but not pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes, likely secondary to ongoing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation (Back et al., 1998; Back et al., 2005; Folkerth et al., 2004; Motavaf and Piao, 2021; Ziemka-Nalecz et al., 2018). Alternatively, as a compensatory mechanism, surviving myelinating oligodendrocytes treated with TH may become hypertrophic. The combined underdevelopment of PNNs, decrease NogoR1 and decrease myelination, support a disruption in the closure of the CPsp, with only partial attenuation by TH.

Learning, memory and other hippocampal-dependent functions emerge during early brain development and refine for decades after birth. (Alberini and Travaglia, 2017; Carstens et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2018; Guirado et al., 2014; Travaglia et al., 2016b). Electrophysiology demonstrate the evolution of the CPsp in the dorsal hippocampus spaning from week two to eight in rats (Sakimoto et al., 2022), and mice (Spahic et al., 2022). In our experimental model of HI brain injury in P10 mice, we have extensively documented working memory deficits, but the mechanisms have been only partially investigated. (Burnsed et al., 2015; Diaz et al., 2017; Goffigan-Holmes et al., 2019; Spahic et al., 2022) Here, we performed 3 behavioral tests, OF, YM and OLT, in our mouse cohort aged to P40 and correlated them to some of the key events governing the CPsp. Thirty days after neonatal HI (P40), low BDNF levels, low y817 TrkB phosphorylation, underdeveloped PNNs and impaired myelination correlate with worse performance in the YM and the OLT testing. On the other hand, increased TrkB phosphorylation at the y515 site at P40 correlate with decreased time on the center of the OF, suggesting anxiety-like behavior and decreased time exploring the novel objects in the OLT, suggesting impaired spatial working memory. Thus, the TrkB phosphorylation site appears to be exquisitely specific to the developmental stage as are the changes produced by neonatal HI. After HI, TrkB phosphorylation is deficient at the y515 site 24 h and 8d later, with deficits not prevented by TH, while deficits in the phosphorylation at the y817 site occur at 5d and 30d, with deficits prevented by TH. For instance, phosphorylation of the TrkB y515 site is linked to hippocampal protection in mice acutely undergoing kainic acid micro-infusion by allowing the TrkB-Shc-Akt signaling pathway to proceed, (Huang et al., 2019) while phosphorylation of the TrkB y817 site is linked to pre-synaptic retrograde feedback and perhaps modulation of microglial function. (Spencer-Segal et al., 2011) Thus, it is entirely plausible that y515 and y817 TrkB phosphorylation sites functionally relate to different developmental processes governing the onset and closure of the postnatal CPsp in the hippocampus.

Although our data support the hypothesis that neonatal HI alters many of the events known to govern the postnatal CPsp in the hippocampus, which relate to behavioral deficits, the mechanistic aspects need still further investigation. Future experiments are needed to study manipulation of the BDNF-TrkB signaling cascade to observe effects in downstream events following neonatal HI. Since TH after HI at P10 does not prevent the deficits of y515 TrkB phosphorylation at P18, manipulation at delay times is more likely to be fruitful in future experiments. Another limitation is that despite the importance of the dorsal hippocampus for memory outcomes, several other regions including the entorhinal cortex, amygdala and the pre-frontal cortex and the ventral hippocampus are also essential to study to better understand the deficits in connectivity. Lastly, although we have recently showed deficits in long-term depression in at P18 and P40 in extracellular field potential of mice undergoing HI at P10, (Spahic et al., 2022) which may result from the deficit of GABAergic PV+ INs counts, (Chavez-Valdez et al., 2018), the natural progression to this work is determine the persistence of memory, cognitive and learning deficits during late adulthood, linked to earlier changes in the events governing the hippocampal CPsp.

Conclusion

Neonatal HI injury results in many alterations in events known to govern the postnatal CPsp in the hippocampus. Since undisturbed progression of the CPsp across the brain regions is essential for the development of mature networks sculpted by experience, the correlation of many of the changes produced by neonatal HI in the dorsal hippocampus with behavioral deficits, for instance working memory, may support the notion of altered synaptic connectivity and cognitive function. TH, the standard of care for the treatment of HI brain injury, does not appear to prevent most of these changes, although it seems to attenuate deficits in myelination, this treatment does not prevent memory deficits seen in neonates. (Azzopardi et al., 2014; Cainelli et al., 2021; Shankaran et al., 2012).
