Generalized Robust Adaptive-Bandwidth Multi-View Manifold Learning in High Dimensions with Noise
Xiucai Ding, Chao Shen, Hau-Tieng Wu

Abstract
Multiview datasets are common in scientific and engineering applications, yet existing fusion methods offer limited theoretical guarantees, particularly in the presence of heterogeneous and high-dimensional noise. We propose Generalized Robust Adaptive-Bandwidth Multiview Diffusion Maps (GRAB-MDM), a new kernel-based diffusion geometry framework for integrating multiple noisy data sources. The key innovation of GRAB-MDM is a view-dependent bandwidth selection strategy that adapts to the geometry and noise level of each view, enabling a stable and principled construction of multiview diffusion operators. Under a common-manifold model, we establish asymptotic convergence results and show that the adaptive bandwidths lead to provably robust recovery of the shared intrinsic structure, even when noise levels and sensor dimensions differ across views. Numerical experiments demonstrate that GRAB-MDM significantly improves robustness and embedding quality compared with fixed-bandwidth and equal-bandwidth baselines, and usually outperform existing algorithms. The proposed framework offers a practical and theoretically grounded solution for multiview sensor fusion in high-dimensional noisy environments.

Introduction

Advances in sensing technology now allow researchers to interrogate a system through heterogeneous sensors, producing multiple datasets with distinct structures and properties collected simultaneously. Following common terminology, we refer to the dataset from each sensor as a
view
of the system. Although it is often assumed that more data yield more information, this assumption may fail when additional data arise from heterogeneous sources. Integrating such data, variously termed sensor fusion
[
13
,
23
,
24
,
30
]
or multi-view learning
[
52
,
53
,
55
]
, remains a fundamental challenge.
To our knowledge, beyond naive concatenation, which increases dimensionality, or highly parameterized models, which risk overfitting and inefficiency, there is still no consensus or systematic framework for handling heterogeneous views, particularly under nonlinearity, high dimensionality, and noise. The central goal of sensor fusion is to combine information from multiple sensors into a unified representation that reflects the system’s intrinsic latent structure. In practice, however, different views may contribute unequal or incomplete information, may exist in incompatible formats, and may be corrupted by distinct noise sources or artifacts. These issues collectively make effective sensor fusion a difficult and largely unresolved problem.

Despite these challenges, the practical importance of multi-view data integration has motivated substantial research activity. In the two-view setting, canonical correlation analysis (CCA)
[
16
]
and its variants, including kernel CCA (KCCA)
[
1
,
15
]
and NCCA
[
31
]
, among others, have been extensively studied. These methods seek maximally correlated linear or nonlinear projections across two families of measurable functions, with the main differences lying in how these function classes and their associated correlations are defined. Notably, NCCA is closely related to the alternating diffusion map (ADM)
[
25
,
43
]
, a kernel-based method derived from diffusion maps
[
3
]
to uncover shared latent manifold structure across two views. ADM, NCCA, and KCCA are all kernel-based approaches.
For more than two views, several empirical generalizations of single-view algorithms have been proposed.

[
27
,
28
,
22
,
35
]
proposed multiview versions of the diffusion map, called multiview diffusion maps (MDM), which extends the original diffusion map (DM) algorithm
[
3
]
. ADM is applied to fuse multiple views by concatenating all binary pairs
[
22
]
.
[
17
,
34
,
38
,
56
]
developed multiview versions of locally linear embedding. Furthermore,
[
9
,
21
,
34
,
51
]
introduced multiview variants of t-Stochastic Neighbourhood Embedding, while
[
9
]
proposed a multiview extension of UMAP
[
29
]
and
[
34
]
developed a multiview version of Isomap. Comprehensive reviews are available in
[
34
,
52
]
.
A parallel line of work develops deep-learning-based architectures, and readers can find tons of them in the review articles
[
53
,
42
]
.
While powerful, these models are typically applied in an ad hoc, black-box manner and lack rigorous theoretical guarantees, especially in high-dimensional or noisy regimes, raising concerns about interpretability and reproducibility.
For non-deep-learning approaches, theory remains limited once the number of views exceeds two. Aside from the analysis supporting ADM
[
22
]
, rigorous results for multi-view fusion are scarce. Although the MDM framework
[
28
]
is designed for general multi-view settings, existing theoretical work is restricted to the noise-free, two-view case.

In this paper, motivated by the practical challenges of analyzing high-dimensional, noisy multiview data and by the limited theoretical understanding of multiview fusion under noise, we introduce a new kernel-based algorithm, the
Generalized Robust Adaptive-Bandwidth Multiview Diffusion Maps
(GRAB-MDM). Building on the multiview diffusion maps (MDM) framework, our method constructs a diffusion process using a novel data-driven bandwidth selection that propagates information across views to achieve robust sensor fusion. Specifically, given
\mathsf{K}\geq 2
views, GRAB-MDM constructs a
\mathsf{K}\times\mathsf{K}
block kernel affinity matrix
\mathcal{K}
, where each block is of size
n\times n
(see (
4
) below), designed to extract common information across views through a convolution-type procedure (see (
3
) below). Based on this affinity matrix, we construct a normalized transition matrix (see (
6
) below).
Since the resulting matrix is of size
\mathsf{K}n\times\mathsf{K}n
, it admits a natural eigendecomposition that yields a joint spectral embedding of all samples across views. This construction also provides a flexible framework for defining different embedding. For example, in tasks such as simultaneous clustering, one may average embeddings across views to better exploit cross-view information rather than relying on view-specific embeddings alone (see (
8
)).

A critical component of GRAB-MDM is the choice of bandwidth, which must adapt to the geometry and noise level of each view. To address this, we propose a theoretically grounded, sensor-dependent bandwidth selection strategy (see Algorithm
1
below) that enables a principled multiview diffusion construction under noise. The procedure consists of two stages: first, a view-specific quantity is selected to capture signal characteristics unique to each sensor; second, a global scaling parameter is determined by leveraging cross-view information.

GRAB-MDM is theoretically sound and geometrically interpretable. Mathematically, we consider that each view is represented by a high-dimensional point cloud
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}:=\{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell}},1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}
, so that
n
samples are observed through
\mathsf{K}
sensors of possibly different dimensions. We refer to
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}
as the
i
-th sample recorded by the
\ell
-th sensor
. In practice, each observation is corrupted by noise,

\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell},\ 1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K},\ 1\leq i\leq n\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}
denotes the clean signal and
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell}
the corresponding noise. Since sensors often have disparate physical characteristics, there is generally no simple relationship between
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell}
and
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell^{\prime}}
when
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}

\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n}
across all views, using only the noisy observations
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}
.

To render the problem theoretically tractable, we assume that the clean signals
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n}
lie on a common low-dimensional, smooth, closed latent manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and that each sensor observes data on a manifold diffeomorphic to

. This setting is formalized by the generalized common manifold model (see Assumption
3.1

3.3

4.2

4.4
, respectively.
In Section

[
28
]
when
\mathsf{K}>2
.
Finally, while the richness of the limiting operators highlights the ability of GRAB-MDM to capture complex geometric structure under the common manifold model, these operators are not simple weighted Laplace-Beltrami operators. A full understanding of their spectral behavior would require substantially new analytical tools beyond existing work
[
10
,
41
,
47
,
49
,
37
]
. We leave a detailed investigation of this direction for future research.

2
The proposed algorithm and bandwidth selection procedure

Motivated by MDM
[
28
]
and by the need to handle noisy data in practical applications, we now introduce our proposed algorithm, the
Generalized Robust Adaptive-Bandwidth Multiview Diffusion Maps
(GRAB-MDM). Algorithmically, GRAB-MDM differs from MDM primarily in its principled selection of sensor-dependent bandwidths and its embedding strategy for multiview datasets.

2.1
The proposed GRAB-MDM algorithm

For each view
1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}
and some bandwidth
\epsilon_{\ell}
selected from Algorithm
1
below in Section
2.2
, we define
\mathbf{K}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
so that for
1\leq i,j\leq n

\mathbf{K}^{\ell}(i,j)=K\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon_{\ell}}\right),\ 1\leq i,j\leq n\,,

(2)

where
K:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{0\}
is a non-degenerate kernel with sufficient regularity and decay satisfying some mild regularity conditions (see Assumption
4.1
below for more details); for example, a Gaussian kernel.
Based on (
2
), for
1\leq\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\leq\mathsf{K},
we define

\mathbf{K}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\mathbf{K}^{\ell_{1}}\mathbf{K}^{\ell_{2}}\,.

(3)

Using (
3
), we can further define the block-wise asymmetric kernel affinity matrix
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\mathsf{K}\times n\mathsf{K}}
as follows

\mathcal{K}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}&\mathbf{K}^{1,2}&\mathbf{K}^{1,3}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}^{1,\mathsf{K}}\\
\mathbf{K}^{2,1}&\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}&\mathbf{K}^{2,3}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}^{2,\mathsf{K}}\\
\mathbf{K}^{3,1}&\mathbf{K}^{3,2}&\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}^{3,\mathsf{K}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{K},1}&\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{K},2}&\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{K},3}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

Denote the diagonal degree matrix
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\mathsf{K}\times n\mathsf{K}}
that

\mathcal{D}(i,i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n\mathsf{K}}\mathcal{K}(i,j)\,,

(5)

which is nondegenerate by the construction of
\mathcal{K}
.
Then define the transition matrix as

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{D}^{-1}\mathcal{K}.

(6)

Since
\mathcal{A}
is similar to a symmetric matrix
\mathcal{D}^{-1/2}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{D}^{-1/2}
, we have eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathcal{A}
. Denote
\mathcal{A}
’s eigenvalues and right eigenvectors as
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n\mathsf{K}}
and
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n\mathsf{K}}
, where
1=\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\eta_{n\mathsf{K}}\geq-1
. The eigenvalue bound is due to the normalization and the spectral norm controlled by the
\ell^{\infty}
norm of
\mathcal{A}
.
Fix
1\leq m<n\mathsf{K}
,
t\geq 0
and construct

Q_{m,t}=\texttt{diag}(\eta^{t}_{2},\ldots,\eta^{t}_{m+1})[\mathbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{m+1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\mathsf{K}}\,.

(7)

Note that all entries of
\mathbf{u}_{1}
are the same due to the construction of
\mathcal{A}
.

In practice, the data embedding is constructed from (
7
) depending on the downstream tasks. If one aims to obtain distinct embeddings for each view, a natural approach is to embed

\Phi^{(\ell)}_{m,t}:\,\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\to Q_{m,t}e_{j+(\ell-1)n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

for the
\ell
-th view, where
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\mathsf{K}}
is the unit vector with the
j
-th entry equal to
1
. When
m=n
, this embedding is reduced to
[
28
, (21)]
.
When only a single embedding for each sample from all across views is needed, one can consider a joint embedding by mapping

\Phi_{m,t}^{(\texttt{joint})}:\,\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}}\to[Q_{m,t}e_{j};Q_{m,t}e_{j+n};\ldots;Q_{m,t}e_{j+(\mathsf{K}-1)n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\mathsf{K}}\,.

Similarly, when
m=n
, this embedding is reduced to
[
28
, (23)]
. See Sections
4
and
5
for a theoretical justification of these embeddings when
\mathsf{K}\geq 2
.
In this paper, motivated by the theoretical analysis in Sections
4
and
5
, we propose to also consider

\displaystyle\Phi_{m,t}:\,\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}}\to\frac{1}{\mathsf{K}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}}Q_{m,t}e_{j+(\ell-1)n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(8)

which averages the embeddings across all views.
With the chosen embedding, we can proceed to downstream tasks such as clustering and visualization. These tasks typically rely on the Euclidean distance between embedded points, which in the literature is often referred to as the
diffusion distance
. This terminology arises because the eigenvalues and eigenvectors used to construct the embedding are derived from a diffusion process defined on the dataset. To formalize this relationship, denote the associated diffusion distances as

\displaystyle d_{m,t}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i},\,\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j})

\displaystyle\,:=\left\|\Phi_{m,t}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i})-\Phi_{m,t}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j})\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}},\ \ \ell=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}\,,

\displaystyle d_{m,t}^{(\texttt{joint})}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}},\,\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\,:=\left\|\Phi_{m,t}^{(\texttt{joint})}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})-\Phi_{m,t}^{(\texttt{joint})}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m\mathsf{K}}}\,,

\displaystyle d_{m,t}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}},\,\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\,:=\left\|\Phi_{m,t}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})-\Phi_{m,t}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,,

respectively, where
d_{m,t}^{(\ell)}
is view-dependent. Note that,
d_{n-1,t}^{(\texttt{joint})}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}},\,\{\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{K}})=\|e_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{A}^{t}-e_{j}^{\top}\mathcal{A}^{t}\|_{2}
, and similar expressions hold for the other distances. Here,
\mathcal{A}
is a row-stochastic matrix describing a diffusion process on the dataset. At a high-level,
\mathcal{A}
can be interpreted as the transition matrix of a Markov chain defined on the union of all views such that for each observation
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell},1\leq i\leq n,1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K},
the one-step transition probability is given by
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{\ell_{2}})=\frac{\mathbf{K}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(i,j)}{\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{i},\mathsf{i})}
, where
\mathsf{i}=(\ell_{1}-1)n+i
and
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
. In this sense, the terminology diffusion distance is well justified.

In general, the choice of
t
and
m
in (
7
) depends on the specific downstream task. To keep the algorithm automatic, we follow standard practice in diffusion geometry and fix
t=1
. For selecting
m
, we extend the elbow method, classically used in PCA via scree plots
[
19
]
, by adaptively incorporating the eigen-ratios of
\{\eta_{i}\}
; see Appendix
E
of our supplement for the detailed algorithm. This strategy yields robust performance in the numerical experiments of Section
6
. A comprehensive study of optimal choices of
t
and
m
, and more generally of embedding designs tailored to particular applications, is beyond the scope of this work and remains an interesting direction for future research.

Remark 2.1
.

In the two-view case,
\mathsf{K}=2
, the ADM algorithm has been analyzed in
[
25
,
43
,
7
]
, while
[
31
]
studied NCCA. Although both methods superficially resemble GRAB-MDM when
\mathsf{K}=2
, they differ in an essential way: rather than normalizing after multiplying the kernel affinity matrices as in (
2
), ADM and NCCA normalize each kernel separately before multiplication, leading to distinct operators and interpretations.
Beyond the two-view setting, canonical extensions of these approaches remain unclear. With the exception of the recent effort in
[
22
]
, there is still no principled framework for generalizing these algorithms to settings with more than two views.

Remark 2.2
.

Heuristically, the construction of
\mathcal{A}
forces the Markov chain to transition only across different views, prohibiting within-view moves. This design mirrors the strategy in
[
11
]
, where suppressing lazy walks improves robustness to high-dimensional noise in diffusion maps. As shown in Section
5
, the same principle applies here and significantly enhances noise robustness.

On the other hand, if one inserts the blocks
\mathbf{K}^{\ell,\ell}
back into the diagonal of
\mathcal{K},
it would yield a matrix
\mathcal{K}^{\circ}
, with associated degree matrix
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
and diffusion matrix
\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
. The rank of
\mathcal{K}^{\circ}
is at most
n
and admits the outer-product representation
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\mathsf{K}\times n}
. Consequently, the eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
can be simplified by computing the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathcal{D}^{\circ-1/2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}=:\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}
, which is computationally more efficient. Furthermore, this SVD reduces to the SVD of each
n\times n
block
\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
. Specifically, if the SVD of
\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
is
U_{\ell}D_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\top}
, then the SVD of
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{1};\ldots;\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}
can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&U_{2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&U_{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}D_{1}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&D_{2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&D_{\mathsf{K}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\mathsf{K}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
. For each view, the SVD of
\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
differs from the eigenvalue decomposition of the random walk matrix typically used in DM. Because the shared information is primarily captured in
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
, this relationship suggests that inserting the diagonal blocks, or equivalently, allowing view-level lazy walks, may diminish the effectiveness of information fusion, even for clean datasets. A systematic quantification of this phenomenon lies beyond the scope of the present paper.

Remark 2.3
.

For notional simplicity, we use the same kernel function
K(\cdot)
in (
2
) for all views
1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}.
Our results and algorithms extend straightforwardly to view-specific kernels
K_{\ell}
, provided they each satisfy Assumption
4.1
. Although such a generalization may offer empirical flexibility, it adds no conceptual insight to the analysis, and we omit it for brevity.

2.2
The bandwidth selection procedure in GRAB-MDM

A key component of GRAB-MDM is the bandwidth selection procedure in Algorithm
1
, designed to recover the underlying geometric structure of the clean samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}
from the noisy observations
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\}
. A theoretical justification is provided in Section
5.2
. The resulting bandwidth,
\epsilon_{\ell}:=\mathsf{c}h_{\ell}
in (
12
), decomposes into a view-specific term
h_{\ell}
and a global scaling factor
\mathsf{c}
.

The construction of
h_{\ell}
follows
[
6
]
, where it is introduced as a data-adaptive local scale parameter. Specifically,
h_{\ell}
is defined as the
\omega_{\ell}
-percentile of the empirical cumulative distribution of the pairwise squared distances in the
\ell
-th view, with
\omega_{\ell}
a tunable hyperparameter. Theoretically, any fixed
\omega_{\ell}\in(0,1)
yields the same asymptotic behavior. Practically, to reduce tuning and improve stability, we recommend the resampling strategy of
[
6
]
, detailed in Appendix
E
of our supplement. In this way,
h_{\ell}
captures the intrinsic geometry of each noisy point cloud
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
,
1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}
.

The global scaling factor
\mathsf{c}
, in contrast, reflects geometry shared across all views and its order is theoretically linked to the underlying geometry. As shown in Section
5.1
,
\mathsf{c}
must lie in an appropriate range: excessively large or small values destabilize the algorithm in Section
2.1
, particularly the spectral properties of the transition matrix in (
6
). When
\mathsf{c}
is appropriately selected, however, the spectrum is stable. Algorithm
1
determines
\mathsf{c}
via a grid search using the spectral distance as in (
10
)
[
14
,
20
,
26
]
, which is small when the corresponding
\mathsf{c}
values are close. A hyperparameter
\delta
controls the stability threshold needs to be chosen. For implementation, we recommend the calibration procedure of
[
8
]
; see Appendix
E
for details.

Section
6
presents extensive numerical experiments. Across all scenarios, particularly in high-noise regimes, GRAB-MDM consistently outperforms ten competing methods, demonstrating substantial robustness.

Algorithm 1

The bandwidth selection procedure

Input:
Observed samples
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}

\omega_{\ell}\in(0,1),1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K},

For

Remark 2.4
.

In the MDM algorithm

Remark 2.5
.

Bandwidth selection is a critical challenge in general statistical tasks. To our knowledge, in the single-view case (

3
The common manifold model and limiting operators

In this section, we introduce a mathematical model for the clean signals
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}
in (
1
), which serves as the basis for analyzing GRAB-MDM under noise. For notional simplicity, we denote the
\mathsf{K}
clean point clouds as
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n},1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}.
Our objective is to construct simultaneous embeddings across all views by exploiting their shared structure. To this end, we adopt a common manifold model for the clean views, formalized in Assumption
3.1
. Under this framework, the proposed algorithm corresponds, in the large-sample limit, to certain differential operators defined on the underlying manifold.

3.1
The model and basic definitions

We generalize the common manifold model
[
43
,
7
]
to facilitate our analysis. Consider the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
(Generalized common manifold model).

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a

\mathrm{g}
.
Take a random vector
X:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathsf{P})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}

\mathsf{K}\in\mathbb{N}

\ell=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}

\iota_{\ell}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell}}
so that
\iota_{\ell}

\mathcal{M}

Denote the random vector

Let

(13)

0<\delta_{0}<1
so that for all

\delta_{0}\leq\varrho(z)\leq\delta_{0}^{-1}.

(14)

When

Then, i.i.d. sample
n

to construct the point clouds

\ell=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}
; that is,
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}:=\iota_{\ell}(x_{i})
, where

is i.i.d. sampled from

See Figure

[

]
.
This mild structural assumption captures cross-view dependence and enables recovery of shared geometric information.

For each
\ell=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\varrho_{\ell}(y)=\varrho(\iota_{\ell}^{-1}(y))\left|\det(D\iota^{-1}_{\ell}(y))\right|\frac{\sqrt{\det G(\iota_{\ell}^{-1}(y))}}{\sqrt{\det G_{\ell}(y)}}\,,

(17)

\delta^{*}\leq\varrho_{\ell}(\iota_{\ell}(z))\leq(\delta^{*})^{-1}.

(18)

Note that in general

, and even if

is uniform, in general the sampling of each

is not uniform.
Moreover, using the relation (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}(g(X_{i},X_{j}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}(g(X_{i},\vartheta_{ji}(X_{i})))=\int_{\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})}g(x,\vartheta_{ji}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}(x)\,,

(19)

where
\mathbb{E}_{i}
denotes expectation under the law of

.

Remark 3.2
.

For concreteness, and motivated by our target applications, we focus on the setting where all datasets arise from a single underlying Riemannian manifold equipped with a Riemannian metric, and each view is generated through a different embedding of this manifold. In principle, the framework can be extended to richer geometric structures, including multiple latent spaces with hierarchical relationships, a direction we leave for future investigation.

3.2
The limiting operators

Based on the common manifold model, we introduce the limiting operators associated with our algorithm proposed in Section
2
. To this end, observe that for a
\mathsf{K}\times 1
block vector
v=[v_{1}^{\top},\ldots,v_{\mathsf{K}}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\mathsf{K}}
with blocks of size
n\times 1
, where
v_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
l
-th block for
l=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}
, we have

(\mathcal{D}^{-1}\mathcal{K}v)_{i}(l)=\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\big(\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\mathbf{K}^{i}(l,\beta)\mathbf{K}^{j}(\beta,\alpha)\big)v_{j}(\alpha)}{\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\big(\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\mathbf{K}^{i}(l,\beta)\mathbf{K}^{j}(\beta,\alpha)\big)}\,.

At the purely algebraic level, this operator is a combination of matrix-matrix and matrix-vector multiplications.
However, its asymptotic interpretation under the manifold model requires care because each kernel matrix lives on a potentially different embedded manifold, and summation indices implicitly mix coordinates across views.
We therefore need to make explicit the geometric meaning of each component.

First, for the product
\mathbf{K}^{i}(l,\beta)\mathbf{K}^{j}(\beta,\alpha)
, note that
\mathbf{K}^{i}(l,\beta)=K\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}^{i}_{l}-\mathbf{y}^{i}_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon_{i}}\right)
and
\mathbf{K}^{j}(\beta,\alpha)=K\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}^{j}_{\beta}-\mathbf{y}^{j}_{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon_{j}}\right)
are kernels defined on different embedded manifolds,
\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
and
\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})
, respectively. To interpret the summation indexed by
\beta
involving
\mathbf{y}^{i}_{\beta}
and
\mathbf{y}^{j}_{\beta}
,
we introduce a
transfer kernel

\widetilde{K}_{ij}:\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})\times\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathbb{R}
defined for
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
and
y\in\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{ij}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}[K_{\epsilon_{i}}(x,X_{i})K_{\epsilon_{j}}(\vartheta_{ji}(X_{i}),y)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})}K_{\epsilon_{i}}(x,w)K_{\epsilon_{j}}(\vartheta_{ji}(w),y)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}(w),

(20)

where

K_{\epsilon_{i}}(x,w):=K\left(\|x-w\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}_{2}}^{2}/\epsilon_{i}\right).

(21)

By sampling
n
points,
\mathbf{x}^{i}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{i}_{n}
, on
\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
following
\mathbb{P}_{i}
and set
x
and
y
to be
\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l}
and
\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha}=\vartheta_{ji}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{\alpha})
respectively, (
3.2
) is discretized into

\widetilde{K}_{ij}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha})\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}K_{\epsilon_{i}}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l},\mathbf{x}^{i}_{\beta})K_{\epsilon_{j}}(\vartheta_{ji}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{\beta}),\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{K}^{i,j}(l,\alpha)\,,

where
\approx
will be precisely quantified below in the proof.

The tricky part is the matrix-vector multiplication
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\mathbf{K}^{i,j}(l,\alpha)v_{j}(\alpha)
, since we need to specify the quantity where
v_{j}
is discretized from. Because the index
\alpha
corresponds to the
j
-th view, it is natural to interpret
v_{j}
as a discretized function defined on
\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})
; that is,
v_{j}(\alpha):=f_{j}(\iota_{j}(z_{\alpha}))
, where
f_{j}\in C(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
.
Define an integral operator
\omega_{ij}:C(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))\to C(\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M}))
so that for all
f_{j}\in C(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
and
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
,

\omega_{ij}f_{j}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{j}[\widetilde{K}_{ij}(x,X_{j})f_{j}(X_{j})]}{\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{E}_{j}[\widetilde{K}_{ij}(x,X_{j})]}=\frac{\int_{\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})}\widetilde{K}_{ij}(x,z)f_{j}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}(z)}{\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})}\widetilde{K}_{ij}(x,z)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}(z)}\,.

(22)

By sampling
n
points,
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
following
\mathbb{P}
and set
x=\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l}
and
v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
v_{j}(\alpha)=f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha})
, where
f_{j}\in C(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
, the numerator of (
22
) is discretized into

\int_{\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M})}\widetilde{K}_{ij}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l},z)f_{j}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}(z)\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\widetilde{K}_{ij}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{l},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha})f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\alpha})\approx\frac{1}{n}[\mathbf{K}^{i,j}v_{j}](l)\,,

and similarly for the denominator, where
\approx
will be made precise in subsequent analysis. Below, denote
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{\mathsf{K}})^{\top}\in C(\iota_{1}(\mathcal{M}))\times\ldots\times C(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{M}))
to be a vector of functions defined on different embedded manifolds with the evaluation
\mathbf{f}(x)=(f_{1}(\iota_{1}(x)),\cdots,f_{\mathsf{K}}(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(x)))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{K}}
when
x\in\mathcal{M}
, or
\mathbf{f}(x^{\prime})=(f_{1}(\vartheta_{1i}(x^{\prime})),\cdots,f_{\mathsf{K}}(\vartheta_{\mathsf{K}i}(x^{\prime})))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{K}}
when
x^{\prime}\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
for any
i=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}
. These evaluations are all equivalent, and we choose one or another depending on the context. In practice, we cannot access
\mathcal{M}
but can only access
\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
, so the second evaluation is more natural for the algorithm analysis, while the first evaluation helps explore the latent relationship.
We now introduce the limiting operator.

Definition 3.3
.

Define an operator
\Omega:C(\iota_{1}(\mathcal{M}))\times\ldots\times C(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{M}))\rightarrow C(\iota_{1}(\mathcal{M}))\times\ldots\times C(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{M}))
such that for
\mathbf{f}\in C(\iota_{1}(\mathcal{M}))\times\ldots\times C(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{M}))
,
\Omega\mathbf{f}
satisfies

[\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}{\omega_{ij}(f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})}\in C(\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M}))\,,

where
[\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{i}
is the
i
-th component of
\Omega\mathbf{f}
.
In other words, for
x\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
[\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{i}(\iota_{i}(x))=\sum_{j\neq i}\omega_{ij}f_{j}(\iota_{j}(x))\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Viewing
\mathbf{f}
as a
\mathsf{K}
-dim vector with functional entries, we may write

\Omega\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{f}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is the functional analogue of
\mathcal{K}
, a
\mathsf{K}\times\mathsf{K}
matrix whose entries are operators,

\mathbf{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega_{12}&\omega_{13}&\cdots&\omega_{1\mathsf{K}}\\
\omega_{21}&0&\omega_{23}&\cdots&\omega_{2\mathsf{K}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\omega_{\mathsf{K}1}&\omega_{\mathsf{K}2}&\omega_{\mathsf{K}3}&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(23)

Its structure mirrors that of the corresponding matrix in the discretized setting.

Asymptotic analysis

In this section, we provide the asymptotic analysis of our algorithm for the clean signals
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. A robust analysis extending these results to the noisy observations described in (
1
) is deferred to Section

.

4.1
Connection with the Laplace-Beltrami operator: Bias analysis

In this section, we analyze the bias of the sequence of operators introduced in Definition
3.3
and clarify its connection to, as well as its distinction from, the classical Laplace-Beltrami operator
[
39
]
.
We begin by stating our assumptions on the kernel function
K.
For
r,l,k=0,1,2,\ldots,
define

\mu_{r,l}^{(k)}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{l}\partial_{k}K^{r}(\|x\|)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Assumption 4.1
.

Assume that the kernel function
K\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
is bounded,
K(0),K(1)
are away from
0
by some constant, and
K
is normalized so that
\mu_{1,0}^{0}=1
.

We begin by introducing notations related to the geometry of the manifolds.
For the common manifold
\mathcal{M}
and its embedded images
\iota_{k}(\mathcal{M}),1\leq k\leq\mathsf{K},
denote for each
z\in\mathcal{M}
the tangent space
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
and the embedded tangent space
T_{\iota_{k}(z)}\iota_{k}(\mathcal{M})=(\iota_{k})_{*}T_{z}\mathcal{M},
. Let
\nabla^{(\ell)}
and
\Delta^{(\ell)}
denote, respectively, the covariant derivative and Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\iota_{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
. For
x=\iota_{k}(z),1\leq k\leq\mathsf{K},
write
\textup{I}\!\textup{I}_{x,k}
for the second fundamental form of
\iota_{k}(\mathcal{M})
at
x
, and let
s_{k}(x)
denote the scalar curvature. Define

\mathsf{w}_{k}(x):=\frac{1}{3}s_{k}(x)-\frac{d}{12|\mathsf{S}^{d-1}|}\frac{\mu_{1,3}^{(1)}}{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}\int_{\mathsf{S}^{d-1}}\textup{I}\!\textup{I}_{x,k}^{2}(\theta,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta,

(24)

where
|\mathsf{S}^{d-1}|
is the volume of the canonical
(d-1)
-dim sphere.
The main result of this section is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
and
4.1
hold. Fix
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{K}
. Denote
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{\mathsf{K}})\in C^{4}(\iota_{1}(\mathcal{M}))\times\ldots\times C^{4}(\iota_{\mathsf{K}}(\mathcal{M}))
. For
i=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}
, denote
\bar{f}\in C^{4}(\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})))
as

\bar{f}_{i}(x):=\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d}\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))f_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))}{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d}\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))}\,.

When
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{\mathsf{K}}
are sufficiently small and satisfy
\epsilon_{i}\asymp\epsilon_{j}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,\mathsf{K}
, we have for
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
,

\displaystyle[\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{i}(x)=\,\bar{f}_{i}(x)+\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\left[\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d+1}\big([\Delta^{(j)}(f_{j}\varrho_{j})-\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j}f_{j}]\big|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}-[\Delta^{(j)}\varrho_{j}-\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j}]\big|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}\bar{f}_{i}(x)\big)}{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d}\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))}\right.

(25)

\displaystyle\left.+{\epsilon_{i}\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d}\left(\left[\Delta^{(i)}(\varrho_{i}\varrho_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})-\mathsf{w}_{i}\varrho_{i}\varrho_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}\right]-\bar{f}_{i}\left[\Delta^{(i)}(\varrho_{i}\varrho_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})-\mathsf{w}_{i}\varrho_{i}\varrho_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}\right]\right)(x)}{\sum_{j\neq i}\epsilon_{j}^{d}\varrho_{i}(x)\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))}}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathrm{O}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}(\epsilon^{2}_{i}+\epsilon^{2}_{j})\Big)\,.

From the asymptotic expansion in (
25
), we observe that the
i
-th component of
\Omega\mathbf{f}
involves nontrivial interactions among
all views
. The first first-order term contains operators acting on the non-
i
-th manifolds, and the second first-order term involves those on the
i
-th manifold. Since there is no simple invariance formula for the Laplace-Beltrami operator under a general diffeomorphism, one generally has
\Delta^{(i)}(f\circ\vartheta_{ji})(x)\neq\Delta^{(j)}f(\vartheta_{ji}(x))
when
f\in C^{\infty}(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
and
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
, unless
\vartheta_{ji}
is an isometry. Consequently, in general (
25
) cannot be further simplified, and it is not transparent from (
25
) alone how information is exchanged across views or how each view benefits from the others.

To parse (
25
), we start with assuming
\mathsf{K}=2
. In this case, when
i=1
, we have
\bar{f}_{1}=f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21}
, and the terms in (
24
) vanish so that

\displaystyle[\Omega\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle]_{1}(x)=f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21}(x)+\epsilon_{1}\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\left(\Delta^{(1)}(f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21})(x)+2\nabla^{(1)}\log(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21})(x)\cdot\nabla^{(1)}(f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21}))(x)\right)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{2}\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\left((\Delta^{(2)}f_{2})\circ\vartheta_{21}(x)+2(\nabla^{(2)}\log(\varrho_{2})\cdot\nabla^{(2)}f_{2})\circ\vartheta_{21}(x)\right)+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{2}_{1}+\epsilon^{2}_{2})\,.

In this setup,
\frac{[\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{1}-f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21}}{\epsilon_{1}}
approximates a mixed Laplacian
\Delta^{(1)}(f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21})+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}(\Delta^{(2)}f_{2})\circ\vartheta_{21}
that involves a first order differentiation term
2\nabla^{(1)}\log(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21})\cdot\nabla^{(1)}(f_{2}\circ\vartheta_{21}))+2\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}(\nabla^{(2)}\log(\varrho_{2})\cdot\nabla^{(2)}f_{2})\circ\vartheta_{21}
, which depends on the sampling scheme. Note that the mixed Laplacian depends on
\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}
, which is asymptotically constant under the assumption
\epsilon_{1}\asymp\epsilon_{2}
. Consequently, the choice of
\epsilon_{i}
plays a role in enhancing the desired Laplacian.

However, the situation becomes complicated when
\mathsf{K}\geq 3
. In this case,
\bar{f}_{i}
is in general no longer equal to
f_{l}\circ\vartheta_{li}
for any
l\neq i
. The resulting mixed Laplacian involves not only first order differentiation terms depending on the sampling scheme, but also a zeroth order term,
(\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j})|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}\bar{f}_{i}(x)-(\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j}f_{j})|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}
. which involves curvature and is generically nonzero. Note that even if
\mathbf{f}
is chosen so that
f_{i}=f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}
, we still cannot guarantee
(\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j})|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}\bar{f}_{i}(x)-(\mathsf{w}_{j}\varrho_{j}f_{j})|_{\vartheta_{ji}(x)}=0
, since the density functions are not comparable (
17
); that is,
\varrho_{j}\neq\varrho_{i}\circ\vartheta_{ij}
. This suggests that the presence of a single view with large curvature can impact the embedding through its weight
\mathsf{w}_{i}
.

To gain further insight when
\mathsf{K}>2
, consider a special case that
\iota_{k}=c_{k}\iota
for some
c_{k}>0,1\leq k\leq\mathsf{K},
where
\iota:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a fixed embedding. In other words, all views observe the same latent manifold but with possibly different dilation factors. In this setup, all quantities scale nicely. Specifically,
c_{j}^{d}\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))=c_{j}^{d}\varrho_{j}(c_{j}x/c_{i})=c_{i}^{d}\varrho_{i}(x)
for
i\neq j
since
\vartheta_{ji}(x)=c_{j}x/c_{i}
when

. Therefore,
\sum_{j\neq i}\varrho_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))=c_{i}^{d}\varrho_{i}(x)\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{c_{j}^{d}}
and
\bar{f}_{i}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}f_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))}{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}}
.
The metrics satisfy
\vartheta_{ji}^{*}g^{(j)}=\frac{c_{j}^{2}}{c_{i}^{2}}g^{(i)}
, so the curvature dilates squared inversely like
\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{c_{j}^{2}}
; that is,
\mathsf{w}_{j}(\vartheta_{ji}(x))=\mathsf{w}_{j}(c_{j}x/c_{i})=\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{c_{j}^{2}}\mathsf{w}_{i}(x)

(\nabla^{(j)}f)(\vartheta_{ji}(x))=(\Delta^{(j)}f)(c_{j}x/c_{i})=\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j}}\nabla^{(i)}(f(c_{j}x/c_{i}))=\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j}}\nabla^{(i)}(f\circ\vartheta_{ji})(x)
, where
f\in C^{2}(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
and
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
. Moreover, Laplace-Beltrami operator dilates accordingly; that is,
(\Delta^{(j)}f)(\vartheta_{ji}(x))=(\Delta^{(j)}f)(c_{j}x/c_{i})=\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{c_{j}^{2}}\Delta^{(i)}(f(c_{j}x/c_{i}))=\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{c_{j}^{2}}\Delta^{(i)}(f\circ\vartheta_{ji})(x)
, where
f\in C^{2}(\iota_{j}(\mathcal{M}))
and
x\in\iota_{i}(\mathcal{M})
. Putting all these together, if we further assume that
\epsilon_{1}=\ldots=\epsilon_{\mathsf{K}}=\epsilon
, (
25
) is reduced to

\displaystyle[\Omega\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle]_{i}(x)=\bar{f}_{i}(x)+\epsilon\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{i}^{2}c_{j}^{-(d+2)}\big[\Delta^{(i)}((f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})\varrho_{i})-\bar{f}_{i}(x)\Delta^{(i)}\varrho_{i}-(\mathsf{w}_{i}\varrho_{i}(f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji}-\bar{f}_{i}))\big](x)}{\varrho_{i}(x)\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}}

\displaystyle+\epsilon\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}\big[(\Delta^{(i)}(\varrho_{i}^{2}f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})-\mathsf{w}_{i}\varrho_{i}^{2}f_{j}\circ\vartheta_{ji})-\bar{f}_{i}(\Delta^{(i)}(\varrho_{i}^{2})-\mathsf{w}_{i}\varrho_{i}^{2})\big](x)}{\varrho_{i}^{2}(x)\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}}+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

which shows that
\frac{([\Omega\mathbf{f}]_{i}-\bar{f}_{i})(x)}{\epsilon}
approximates a mixed weighted Laplaciain with mixing weights
c_{j}^{-d}
and
c_{i}^{2}c_{j}^{-(d+2)}
, combined with first order and zeroth order differentiation terms. If we further assume

so that
\bar{f}_{i}(x)=f_{i}(x)
, it is reduced to

\displaystyle[\Omega\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle]_{i}(x)={f}_{i}(x)+\epsilon\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{i}^{2}c_{j}^{-(d+2)}}{\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{-d}}\big[\Delta^{(i)}f_{i}-\nabla^{(i)}\log(\varrho_{i})\cdot\nabla^{(i)}{f}_{i}\big](x)

\displaystyle+\epsilon\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{2d}\big[\Delta^{(i)}f_{i}+4\nabla^{(i)}\log(\varrho_{i})\cdot\nabla^{(i)}f_{i})\big](x)+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

If we further assume
c_{1}=\ldots=c_{\mathsf{K}}=1
so that
\varrho_{1}=\ldots=\varrho_{\mathsf{K}}
, and choose
f_{1}=\ldots=f_{\mathsf{K}}=f
, then we further simply (
25
) and obtain

\displaystyle[\Omega\mathbf{f}(x)]_{i}=f(x)+\frac{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{d}\Delta f(x)+\frac{3\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}{d}\nabla\log(\varrho(x))\cdot\nabla f(x)+\mathrm{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

for any

, yielding an expression structurally similar to the standard DM framework
[
3
]
, except for the appearance of the constant
3
rather than
1
. As shown in the proof, this factor of

arises from three distinct contributions: the gradients associated with the two different views and an additional term generated by applying the product rule to their interaction.

Remark 4.3
.

In the DM literature and its extension
[
3
,
10
,
41
]
for the single-view setting, a common practice to recover the Laplace-Beltrami operator without non-uniform sampling impact is the
\alpha
-normalization
, with parameter
\alpha\geq 0
. In particular, choosing
\alpha=1
removes the influence of the sampling density from the Laplacian term, yielding an operator independent of the underlying distribution.
In the multiview setting, when the goal is to recover the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
, one might attempt to apply the same idea by normalizing the sampling density for each dataset before constructing
\mathcal{K}
. Alternatively, one could normalize the kernel in (
3.2
) after constructing
\mathcal{K}
. However, neither approach produces the desired effect.
The key obstruction is the

step,
\mathcal{D}
, which mixes the geometries of different views that may differ by a diffeomorphism. Consequently, even if each view is individually normalized so that its kernel compensates for sampling density, the joint normalization reintroduces unwanted cross-view terms. Due to the page limitation, this important issue is made explicit in Theorems
D.2
and
D.3
of the supplement. In short, the similar
\alpha
normalization, either sensor-wisely or jointly, leads to more complicated mixing of different views that limited insight can be directly caught.

\displaystyle\frac{2d}{\mu_{1,2}^{(0)}}\mathcal{E}^{-1}\,

49

Assumption 5.1

Assume that for each

Note that in (

The centralization assumption of the embedded manifold is mild, as GRAB-MDM depends only on pairwise distances, so any global translation leaves the output unchanged. This assumption is introduced mainly for notational convenience.

Next, we explain that how the analysis of our algorithms with (

) reduces to that of the spiked covariance matrix model
[

]
. For a similar discussions for the single-view case, see

. For

and all

there exists an rotation matrix

for all

is supported in, which is assumed to be fixed as

Denote the covariance matrix of the first

as

If we further construct a matrix

\overline{U}_{\ell}:=\begin{pmatrix}U_{\ell}&\mathbf{0}\\

where

.

5.1
Robustness of the proposed GRAB-MDM

We establish the robustness of GRAB-MDM to high-dimensional noise in two steps.
First, under an oracle assumption, we analyze how bandwidth and SNR interact, thereby demonstrating robustness at the population level.
Second, we show that Algorithm

provides effective bandwidth estimates in practice.
We focus on the commonly used high-SNR regime in statistics
[
5

6
,
54
]

Recall that
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}

Our goal is to relate the empirical matrix

To this end, we impose the following assumptions, which characterize the relationship among bandwidth, signal strength, overall noise level, and the kernel function
K
. For all
1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K}
, denote

\mathsf{R}_{\ell}:=2\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}+p_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}{\epsilon_{\ell}}.

(35)

The numerator
\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}+p_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}
in
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}
represents the
total energy
, which is asymptotically of the same order as
\|\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\|^{2}_{2}
over the high-SNR regime. Thus,
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}
is the ratio of squared diameter of embedded manifold and
\epsilon_{\ell}
.

For clarity and simplicity, we will adopt the following decay rate assumption for the remainder of the paper on the kernel function that for
t\geq 0

\left|(\log K)^{\prime}(t)\right|=\mathrm{O}(1).

(36)

It is easy to check that commonly used kernel functions, such as
K(t)=e^{-t}
or
K(t)=(1+t)^{-\beta}
for some large
\beta
, satisfy (
36
).

The bandwidth parameter
\epsilon_{\ell}
is essential for our proposed algorithm, and we control it via the relationship between
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}
and
K
. More specifically, we require the kernel
K
and
\epsilon_{\ell}
to be chosen appropriately so that asymptotically

K(\mathsf{R}_{\ell})\gg n^{-1}.

(37)

Note that (
37
) can be easily achieved in practice. For instance, when
K(t)=e^{-t}
, since the underlying manifold is assumed to be fixed, under the high-SNR assumption, (
37
) is equivalent to requiring that
\epsilon_{\ell}\gg(\log n)^{-1}
.

The result is stated in Theorem
5.2
. Bandwidths chosen under this assumption ensure that the discrepancy between
\mathcal{A}
and
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
is controlled by the SNR, thereby providing the foundation for our bandwidth selection strategy.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
4.1
,
5.1
, and (
36
) and (
37
) hold.
Denote the error control parameter
\Psi
as

\Psi:=\frac{\Psi_{0}}{\min_{\ell}K(\mathsf{R}_{\ell})\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}K(\mathsf{R}_{\ell^{\prime}})},

(38)

where

\Psi_{0}:={(\mathsf{K}-1)\max_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell}p_{\ell}}}\right)+\sum_{1\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq\mathsf{K}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell^{\prime}}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell^{\prime}}p_{\ell^{\prime}}}}\right)}.

(39)

Assume for some constant
0<\mathsf{c}\equiv\mathsf{c}(n)<\infty,
the bandwidth satisfies

\epsilon_{\ell}\asymp\mathsf{c}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \Psi=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{c}).

(40)

Then we have that

\left\|\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\right\|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{\Psi}{\mathsf{c}}\right).

Theorem
5.2
shows that when the bandwidths satisfy
\epsilon_{\ell}\asymp\mathsf{c}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}
, where
\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}
is the signal strength of the
\ell
th view and
\mathsf{c}
is a global scaling factor, the discrepancy between
\mathcal{A}
and
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
is controlled by
\frac{\Psi}{\mathsf{c}}
.
We mention that
\mathsf{c}
here plays the same role of
\mathsf{c}
in Algorithm
1
.
Consequently, the embeddings in (
7
) are robust to noise in the sense that the leading eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{A}
and
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
remain close under perturbation as long as (
40
) is satisfied. The assumption
\Psi=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{c})
forces
\Psi\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, which combined with (
37
) implies
\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Before concluding this section, we provide a few remarks on the SNR. In this work, for concreteness, we focus on the commonly used high SNR regime in statistics, where the signal strength dominates the noise. When the underlying manifold is fixed, or equivalently when
\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}
is fixed, this assumption reduces to requiring
\sigma_{\ell}=\mathrm{o}(p_{\ell}^{-1/2})
, a standard condition in statistical theory. In practice, one may wish to relax this assumption. A natural approach is to first reduce the ambient dimension
p_{\ell}
to a much smaller dimension
\mathsf{s}_{\ell}\ll p_{\ell}
, so that it suffices to assume
\sigma_{\ell}=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{s}_{\ell}^{-1/2})
. This reduction can be achieved via (random) sketching with sparse sensing matrices, motivated by the model-reduction step in (
32
), which suggests that the informative signals resides in only a few coordinates. Concretely, one can consider
\mathring{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\ell}=\mathrm{S}_{\ell}\mathring{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{s}_{\ell}},
where
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{s}_{\ell}\times p_{\ell}}
is a sparse sensing matrix such that, with high probability,

\|\mathring{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\ell}-\mathring{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\ell}\|\approx\|\mathring{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\ell}-\mathring{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\ell}\|.

As illustrations, Appendices
D.2
and
F
of the supplement present numerical experiments showing that when the signal fulfills some structure and
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}
is chosen as a Haar or orthogonal wavelet matrix, GRAB-MDM remain robust even under weaker SNR conditions. A detailed theoretical and practical investigation of how to design
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}
and select
\mathsf{s}_{\ell}
is deferred to future work.

5.2
Effectiveness of the bandwidth selection algorithm

To rigorously establish the robustness of GRAB-MDM, we must justify the bandwidth selection procedure in Algorithm
1
, since in practice the bandwidth cannot rely on the oracle information required to directly invoke Theorem
5.2
. In practice, both
\mathsf{c}
and the total signal energy
\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}
are unknown and must be estimated. Because the underlying data structures are nonlinear, classical estimators based on outlier eigenvalues of sample covariance matrices
[
4
,
18
,
32
]
are no longer applicable. To overcome this difficulty, we show that the proposed estimator
h_{\ell}
, chosen via (
9
) in Algorithm
1
, provides a reliable approximation of the overall signal strength.

Proposition 5.3
.

For any
\omega_{\ell}\equiv\omega_{\ell}(n)\in(0,1),
under the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
, for
h_{\ell}
chosen according to (
9
) using
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\}
, we have that

\frac{h_{\ell}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}}=1+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}_{\ell}p_{\ell}}}\right).

(41)

Proposition
5.3
demonstrates that once the signal dominates the noise so that the error term on the right-hand side of (
41
) vanishes, our bandwidth selection procedure successfully captures the overall signal strength. This occurs when the total noise energy is asymptotically dominated by the squared manifold diameter.
Theoretically, Proposition
5.3
holds for any constant
\omega_{\ell}\in(0,1)
, indicating that the procedure is robust to the choice of
\omega_{\ell}
; this robustness is also confirmed numerically in Section
6
.
To further automate the selection, we recommend the resampling approach of
[
6
]
, described in Appendix
E
of the supplement.
Finally, note that the right-hand side of (
41
) aligns with the high-SNR intuition that the total noise energy should be much smaller than the manifold diameter.

With Proposition
5.3
and (
40
), for
1\leq\ell\leq\mathsf{K},
we may choose

\hat{\epsilon}_{\ell}=\hat{\mathsf{c}}h_{\ell},

(42)

where
\hat{\mathsf{c}}
is determined according to (
11
). Here,
\hat{\mathsf{c}}
serves as an estimate of
\mathsf{c}
. In practice, we propose to select
\mathsf{c}
via the grid-search procedure in Algorithm
1
, choosing the value that satisfies the condition in Theorem
5.2
.
Observe that by assumption,
\min_{\ell}K(\mathsf{R}_{\ell})\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}K(\mathsf{R}_{\ell^{\prime}})
is bounded away from
0
if
\epsilon_{\ell}
, and hence
\mathsf{c}
, is chosen not too small so that
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}
is asymptotically of order
1
. In this regime, since
\Psi_{0}\to 0
asymptotically, we also have
\Psi\to 0
asymptotically. Therefore, we can allow
\mathsf{c}\to 0
asymptotically as long as
\mathsf{c}
is not taken so small that it falls below
\Psi
, the robustness result in Theorem
5.2
guarantees recovery of the desired
\mathcal{A}
. Consequently, we propose to apply coordinate-wise z-score normalization to the datasets, and then simply specify an interval with small upper bound with a nonzero lower bound, over which we perform a grid search.
For sufficiently large
n
, we find empirically that the range
[10^{-3},0.5]
is adequate; as demonstrated in Section
6
, this choice yields strong empirical performance in both simulations and real data.

6.1
Multiview spectral clustering

In this section, we consider the problem of multi-view clustering, with a particular focus on spectral clustering. In this framework, embeddings obtained from multiple views are first integrated to form a unified representation, which is subsequently partitioned into clusters via the K-means algorithm. We begin by describing the simulation setup used for dataset generation, following the procedure outlined in Section
1
. Specifically, we examine the case of three views, i.e., we set
\mathsf{K}=3
.

We begin by generating synthetic datasets with well-defined cluster structures, as described below. For
1\leq i\leq n:=3\mathsf{n},\,\mathsf{n}\in\mathbb{N}
we consider that

\mathbf{w}_{i}\sim\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathbf{1}_{(j-1)\mathsf{n}+1\leq i\leq j\mathsf{n}}\mathbf{a}_{j},

(43)

where
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ d=3\mathsf{d},\ \mathsf{d}\in\mathbb{N},\ 1\leq j\leq 3,
are some independent random vectors with density function
\rho_{j}
which will be specified below. For convenience, decompose each
\mathbf{a}_{j}
into three blocks:

\mathbf{a}^{\top}_{j}=(\mathbf{a}_{j1}^{\top},\mathbf{a}_{j2}^{\top},\mathbf{a}_{j3}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\mathsf{d}},\ \mathbf{a}_{j\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}\ \text{for}\ 1\leq\ell\leq 3,

so that
\mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}=(\mathbf{w}_{i1}^{\top},\mathbf{w}_{i2}^{\top},\mathbf{w}_{i3}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\mathsf{d}}.

We now describe how to generate the datasets in accordance with (
1
). For some function
\mathsf{g}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
, the clean signals are constructed for
1\leq\ell\leq 3
as follows

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{g}_{\ell}\odot\mathbf{w}_{i\ell}\\
\mathbf{0}_{p_{1}-\mathsf{d}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell}},

where
\odot
means entrywise operation. In what follows, we choose
\mathsf{g}_{1}(x)=x^{2}+x,\ \mathsf{g}_{2}(x)=10\log(x+2),\ \mathsf{g}_{3}(x)=0.8x.

Regarding
\rho_{j},1\leq j\leq 3,
we consider the following two setups in our simulations.

(1).

\rho_{j}(\bm{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}(x_{k}\in[a_{jk},b_{jk}])\frac{1}{b_{jk}-a_{jk}},\ \bm{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{\top}
is the density function of the uniform distribution on the Cartesian product space
\prod_{k=1}^{d}[a_{jk},b_{jk}].
In our simulations, we consider

a_{1k}=\begin{cases}2.5,&1\leq k\leq\mathsf{d}\\
1,&\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 2\mathsf{d}\\

1,&2\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 3\mathsf{d}\end{cases},\ \ b_{2k}=\begin{cases}3.5,&1\leq k\leq\mathsf{d}\\
6,&\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 2\mathsf{d}\\

a_{3k}=\begin{cases}1.5,&1\leq k\leq\mathsf{d}\\
3,&\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 2\mathsf{d}\\
-1,&2\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 3\mathsf{d}\end{cases},\ \ b_{3k}=\begin{cases}2.5,&1\leq k\leq\mathsf{d}\\
7,&\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 2\mathsf{d}\\
3,&2\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 3\mathsf{d}\end{cases}.

(2).

\rho_{j}(\bm{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\varphi_{jk}(x_{k}),
where
\varphi_{jk}
are the density functions of independent truncated Gaussian random variables with parameters
(\mu_{jk},\sigma^{2},\mu_{jk}-3\sigma,\mu_{jk}+3\sigma).
Here
\mu_{jk}
and
\sigma^{2}
are the mean and variance of the Gaussian random variables and
\mu_{jk}-3\sigma
and
\mu_{jk}+3\sigma
are the truncation levels. In our simulations, we use that

and

2,&2\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 3\mathsf{d}\end{cases},\ \ \mu_{3k}=\begin{cases}2,&1\leq k\leq\mathsf{d}\\
5.5,&\mathsf{d}+1\leq k\leq 2\mathsf{d}\\

Finally, for the noise, we consider

6.2
Manifold learning

In this section, we compare the performance of different methods by assessing the quality of their low-dimensional embeddings in terms of neighborhood preservation, as measured by the trustworthiness metric
[
45
,
48
]
. To facilitate comparison with existing two-view methods and streamline our discussion, we focus on the two-view setup. Specifically, for samples
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, independently drawn from certain geometric objects, we construct the clean samples as described in (
1
) so that

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{s}_{i}\\
\mathbf{0}_{p-d}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \text{for}\ \ell=1,2.

To construct the noisy datasets, we choose

\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{p},\upsilon^{2}_{\ell}\mathbf{I}_{p}),\ \upsilon_{1}<\upsilon_{2}.

That is, view 1 corresponds to a relatively clean signal, whereas view 2 represents a noisier signal, though both share the same underlying clean structure which will be specified later. The trustworthiness is computed with respect to the underlying geometric objects using the samples
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\}
.
For simplicity and ease of comparison, we set
m=d
in this application. Therefore, the trustworthiness
[
45
]
here is defined as

T(k)=1-\frac{2}{nk(2n-3k-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in U_{k}(i)\backslash V_{k}(i)}(r(i,j)-k).

Here,
k
denotes the neighborhood size,
U_{k}(i)
is the set of
k
nearest neighbors of point
i
in the embedding, and
V_{k}(i)
is the set of
k
nearest neighbors of
i
in the original data
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\}
. The quantity
r(i,j)

\{\|\mathbf{x}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\ell}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
. When
n
is sufficiently large, we have

X_{(k)}\geq\frac{C}{\log^{4t}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}.

(C.17)

Together with (
C.14
) and (
C.12
), we find that when
n
is sufficiently large, there are at least
k

\{\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfies

{X_{(k)}-\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(p_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}+\sigma_{\ell}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg)\,,}

which follows from the fact that the first
k
elements
\{X_{(i)}:1\leq i\leq k\}
are no smaller than
X_{(k)}
. As a result, the
k
-th largest element
Y_{(k)}
in
\{\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfies

X_{(k)}-Y_{(k)}={\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(p_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}+\sigma_{\ell}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg).}

(C.18)

On the other hand, we claim that there are at least
n-k+1
elements in
\{\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfying

{\|\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{\ell}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-X_{(k)}={\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(p_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}+\sigma_{\ell}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg)},}

which follows from Lemma
C.2

are no greater than
X_{(k)}
. As a result, we also have

(C.19)

Combining the above discussions, under the assumption of Theorem
5.2
, using the definition (

\left|\frac{h_{\ell}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,i}}-1\right|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}(\ell)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}{(\ell)p_{\ell}}}}\right),

which concludes our proof.

As discussed therein, denote
a_{ij}=\|\mathring{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\ell}-\mathring{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\ell}\|,

b_{ij}=\|\mathring{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\ell}-\mathring{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\ell}\|
and
c_{ij}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\ell}\|,
one can follow the discussion between (
C.5
) and (
C.7
) to obtain that

|K(a_{ij}/\epsilon_{\ell})-K(c_{ij}/\epsilon_{\ell})|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{|a_{ij}-b_{ij}|}{\epsilon_{\ell}}+\frac{\mathsf{s}_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,j}}+\frac{\sigma_{\ell}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{\ell}}\lambda_{\ell,j}}}\right).

Consequently, if one can design a sparse sensing matrix
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{s}_{\ell}\times p_{\ell}}
with
\mathsf{s}_{\ell}\ll p_{\ell}
such that
|a_{ij}-b_{ij}|=\mathrm{o}_{\prec}(\epsilon_{\ell}),
the requirement on the SNR can be substantially relaxed. For instance, when the manifold is fixed and
\epsilon_{\ell}\asymp 1,
it would suffice to assume
\sigma_{\ell}\ll\mathsf{s}_{\ell}^{-1/2}
rather than the stronger original condition
\sigma_{\ell}\ll p_{\ell}^{-1/2}.

We illustrate in Figure
3
that the performance of our proposed method with sketching can be further enhanced when the data sets are noisier, highlighting the practical usefulness of the proposed ideas. In this example, we consider setup (1) in Section
6.1
with
\mathsf{s}_{1}=\mathsf{s}_{2}=\mathsf{s}_{3}=12
, and take
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}
to be a block of the
p_{\ell}\times p_{\ell}
(
p_{\ell}=100
) Haar wavelet matrix constructed using the Python package PyWavelets. Specifically, we select the top 15 columns corresponding to the transformed components with the highest energy. A systematic investigation and theoretical analysis of the optimal selection of
\mathrm{S}_{\ell}
and
\mathsf{s}_{\ell}
are beyond the scope of the present paper and will be deferred to future work.

E
Automated tuning parameter selection

In this section, we discuss practical strategies for selecting various tuning parameters. To enhance the automation of our algorithm, we propose procedures for automatically determining the embedding dimension
m
in (
7
) and the percentiles
\omega_{\ell}
used in (
9
). These choices are naturally associated with the larger eigenvalues of the corresponding matrices. In practice, our approach relies on scree plots of eigenvalues and the use of eigen-ratios, techniques that are widely employed in random matrix theory for identifying the number of spikes and signals
[
6
,
8
]
, consistent with the model reduction discussed in Section
5
. At a high level, these methods generalize the well-known elbow method
[
19
]
based on scree plots in PCA.

We first discuss how to select
m
using the eigenvalues
\eta_{2}\geq\eta_{3}\geq\cdots
of the matrix
\mathcal{A}
in (
6
). Given a threshold
\mathsf{e}>0
, we define

m:=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq\sqrt{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta_{i}}{\eta_{i+1}}\geq\mathsf{e}.

That is, we choose the embedding dimension so that the leading eigenvalues (corresponding to the signals) are large and well separated from the rest (corresponding to the noise). This ensures that the selected eigenvectors capture the essential information contained in the signal. The use of the argmin helps stabilize the numerical procedure. In our simulations, for practical purposes, we set

\mathsf{e}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname*{max}_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq\sqrt{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta_{i}}{\eta_{i+1}}-1\right).

(E.1)

Then we discuss how to choose
\omega_{\ell}
, following the approach in
[
5
]
. For a pre-selected sequence of percentiles
\{\omega_{\ell,i}\}_{i=1}^{T},
compute the associated
h_{\ell,i}
according to (
9
), and denote them by
\{h_{\ell,i}\}_{i=1}^{T}.
For each
1\leq i\leq T
, calculate the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{K}^{\ell}_{i}
, constructed using the bandwidth
h_{\ell,i}
as in (
2
). Denote these eigenvalues in decreasing order by
{\lambda_{\ell,k}^{(i)}}_{k=1}^{n}
. For some
\mathsf{s}>0
(for example, defined analogously to (
E.1
) using the corresponding eigenvalues), define

\mathsf{k}(\omega_{\ell,i}):=\max_{1\leq k\leq\sqrt{n}}\left\{k\bigg|\frac{\lambda_{\ell,k}^{(i)}}{\lambda_{\ell,k+1}^{(i)}}\geq 1+\mathsf{s}\right\}.

Choose the percentile
\omega
such that

\omega_{\ell}=\max_{i}[\operatorname{argmax}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{\ell,i}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{k}(\omega_{\ell,i})].

In particular, the final step can be replaced with alternative criteria depending on the user’s objectives. Here, we follow
[
5
]
and select the largest criterion to enhance robustness.
