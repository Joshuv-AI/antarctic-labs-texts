Molecular-gated MRI via αIIbβ3: ligand-triggered relaxation switching for efficient imaging of activated platelet-driven thrombosis
Authors: Xuehao Yu, Jinlong Zhang, Qiang Zhang, Yuanyuan Guo, Kai Sheng, Jiahua Wang, Lingyue Shen, Yuehua Li, Yu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective management of thromboembolic diseases is critically limited by the absence of imaging tools that can distinguish biologically active thrombi from inactive clots, thereby impeding personalized therapeutic strategies. To address this, we designed a molecular-gated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) probe that converts the specific binding event between a fibrinogen-mimetic ligand and activated platelet integrin αIIbβ3 into a bright T1 signal. Mimicking the natural fibrinogen, our fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicle probe (denoted as H12-PEG-Gd-NV) is engineered with the specific H12 peptide, a fibrinogen γ-chain-derived dodecapeptide (HHLGGAKQAGDV, H12), to target αIIbβ3. Its diagnostic signal is switched “on” exclusively by binding to the active conformation of αIIbβ3 on activated platelets at a thrombus site, which defines the ligand-triggered relaxation switching mechanism. This provides a high contrast relative to conventional “always-on” agents, which lack target-dependent signal control. Microfluidic assays confirmed the specific binding of H12-PEG-Gd-NV to αIIbβ3 on activated platelets under physiological shear flow. In vivo, this molecular-gated imaging strategy enabled high-contrast visualization of thrombi in both carotid artery thrombosis and cerebral thromboembolism models, with the probe exhibiting prolonged circulation and precise accumulation at thrombotic sites. Comprehensive toxicological evaluations revealed a favorable biosafety profile, with no significant adverse effects observed across platelet function, hematological, biochemical, coagulation, and histopathological analyses. By directly imaging αIIbβ3 status, this molecular-gated MRI approach fulfills a critical unmet need for noninvasive thrombus detection and holds strong potential to guide personalized antithrombotic therapy.Keywords: Molecular-gated MRI, Thrombus imaging, Activated platelets, Integrin αIIbβ3, Fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicle

Introduction

Thromboembolic diseases, including ischemic stroke, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction, impose a staggering global health burden [[1], [2], [3]], yet a fundamental diagnostic blind spot persists: the inability to distinguish biologically active thrombi from quiescent, stabilized clots. While current imaging modalities excel at delineating anatomical features of vascular occlusion [4,5], they provide only static snapshots of thrombus morphology. This limitation leaves clinicians unable to assess a critical determinant of clinical outcome: thrombus biological activity, governed by the ongoing activation of platelets that directly determines clot propagation, embolization risk, and therapeutic resistance [[6], [7], [8]]. The clinical consequences are profound, driving an unmet need for probes capable of reporting the biological state of thrombi, not merely their anatomical presence [9].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers high spatial resolution, deep tissue penetration, and freedom from ionizing radiation, making it an attractive platform for molecular interrogation of thrombotic processes [10,11]. However, conventional gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents operate in an “always-on” mode, generating persistent signal in both lesions and background tissue regardless of molecular context [12]. This indiscriminate signal output severely limits sensitivity for detecting thrombi and accurately delineating the margins of actively evolving clots. Biologically active thrombi are dynamic lesions characterized by ongoing platelet activation and recruitment, thrombin generation, fibrin deposition and remodeling, and inflammatory cell involvement. In contrast, stabilized thrombi progressively undergo fibrin cross-linking, collagen deposition, cellular remodeling, and structural organization. These differences may influence thrombus stability and the responsiveness to anticoagulant or thrombolytic therapy [13,14]. Accordingly, several molecular targets have been explored for active-thrombus imaging, including platelet activation-associated markers such as activated integrin αIIbβ3 and P-selectin, as well as coagulation-related targets such as fibrin, thrombin activity, and factor XIIIa [[15], [16], [17]]. Among these candidates, platelet activation-associated targets are particularly attractive because they may provide a localized molecular readout of platelet-rich thrombi undergoing active recruitment and aggregation. And what is needed is not merely a targeted agent, but a smart probe whose MRI signal is conditionally switched “on” only upon engaging a specific molecular signature of disease.

Among platelet activation-associated targets, integrin αIIbβ3 is highly expressed on platelets and plays a central role in platelet aggregation. As shown in Fig. 1a-ⅰ and 1a-ⅱ, in resting platelets, αIIbβ3 adopts a bent, low-affinity state. Upon vascular injury, it undergoes a dramatic structural transition to an extended, high-affinity conformation, exposing binding sites for fibrinogen and enabling platelet aggregation [[18], [19], [20]]. The conformational switch enables fibrinogen to crosslink activated platelets via specific recognition motifs, particularly the dodecapeptide (HHLGGAKQAGDV, H12) on the fibrinogen γ-chain (γ400-411), as well as RGD-containing sequences at the α-chain (α95-98 and α572-575) [21,22], culminating in platelet aggregation and thrombus formation. Accordingly, the ligand-binding state of αIIbβ3 represents a biologically relevant molecular feature of local platelet activation within platelet-rich thrombi. Although activated αIIbβ3 is not an exclusive biomarker for every type of biologically active thrombus, its increased availability may provide valuable information for the early identification of recently formed or propagating platelet-rich thrombi before extensive structural stabilization occurs. Previous molecular imaging approaches have targeted thrombus using RGD-based ligands, achieving enhanced thrombus visualization in preclinical models. However, RGD motifs lack specificity for activated platelets and bind multiple integrins expressed on endothelial and tumor cells [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Moreover, most existing probes rely on passive accumulation and constitutive signal generation, limiting their ability to distinguish active thrombi from stabilized clots [[27], [28], [29]]. Some small molecular MRI contrast agents also face challenges of rapid blood clearance and limited thrombus retention [30]. In contrast, the fibrinogen γ-chain-derived H12 peptide exhibits preferential binding to the activated conformation of αIIbβ3 at thrombus sites, offering higher biological specificity. An ideal probe would transduce the specific molecular event of ligand H12-αIIbβ3 binding directly into a detectable MRI signal change.

Within an activated platelet-rich thrombus, the local T1 relaxation environment remains relatively slow before effective probe engagement, resulting in an MRI signal “OFF” state. Platelet activation induces αIIbβ3 to adopt a ligand-binding conformation, which functions as a biological switch associated with the pathological state of activated thrombi. Upon specific recognition of ligand-binding αIIbβ3 by H12-PEG-Gd-NV, the Gd-containing nanovesicles accumulate at the platelet-rich thrombus interface. This H12-mediated targeting event increases the local Gd availability and enhances longitudinal relaxation of surrounding water protons, generating focal T1 signal enhancement and switching the thrombus MRI signal “ON”.

Herein, we report the design of a fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicle probe (H12-PEG-Gd-NV) that embodies this “molecular-gated” MRI principle. By conjugating the fibrinogen γ-chain-derived H12 peptide to a PEGylated, Gd-containing nanovesicle (Fig. 1a-ⅲ), we engineered a system in which T1 signal output is regulated by a biological binding event associated with activated platelet-rich thrombi. As shown in Fig. 1b, platelet activation induces αIIbβ3 to adopt a ligand-binding state, which serves as a biological switch associated with the pathological state of activated thrombi. Upon H12-mediated recognition of ligand-binding αIIbβ3, the intact Gd-containing nanovesicles are retained at the thrombus interface, increasing the local Gd availability and enhancing longitudinal relaxation of surrounding water protons. This focal T1 signal enhancement, defined here as ligand-triggered relaxation switching, does not require a conformational change of the Gd chelate itself; rather, the ligand-binding state of αIIbβ3 determines whether H12-PEG-Gd-NV remains dispersed in the circulation or accumulates at the thrombus site to generate enhanced local T1 relaxation. H12-PEG-Gd-NV exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 124.0 ± 5.5 nm with a PDI of 0.16, and preferential binding to activated platelets under physiological flow. In the FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombosis model, H12-PEG-Gd-NV produced a maximal signal enhancement at 30 min after administration, three-fold higher than that of PEG-Gd-NV, and this enhancement was substantially attenuated by αIIbβ3 blockade with eptifibatide. In the cerebral thromboembolism model, H12-PEG-Gd-NV generated a thrombus-associated signal enhancement of 8.13 ± 1.52, compared with 2.42 ± 0.24 for PEG-Gd-NV and 2.51 ± 0.47 for Gd-DTPA. Eptifibatide pretreatment reduced this enhancement to 2.81 ± 0.88. The probe also exhibited prolonged circulation (106.7 ± 33.5 min) and favorable biosafety profile at the imaging-relevant dose. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV translates the biological activity of activated platelet-rich thrombi into localized T1 contrast and provides a molecular-gated MRI strategy for the noninvasive assessment of thrombotic diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), cholesterol, chloroform (CHCl3), and anhydrous ethanol (EA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). PE-DTPA (Gd) and egg liss Rhod PE were purchased from Avanti. Cys-H12 peptide (Cys-HHLGGAKQAGDV) was obtained from Scierbio (Hefei, China). DSPE-PEG2000-maleimide (DSPE-PEG-Mal) was purchased from Xi'an Ruixi Biological Technology (Xi'an, China). Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Triton X-100, bovine serum albumin (BSA), sodium chloride (NaCl), calcium chloride (CaCl2), dimethylformamide (DMF), deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6), and dialysis membranes were purchased Adamas-beta® (Shanghai, China). Collagen, the Avanti extruder set, polycarbonate membranes, and filter supports were purchased from Rainbio (Shanghai, China). Flow chambers Vena8 Fluoro + BioChips (width, 0.04 cm; height, 0.01 cm; length, 2.8 cm) were purchased from Cellix Ltd. (Dublin, Ireland).

Synthesis of DSPE-PEG-H12

The DSPE-PEG-H12 was synthesized through a thiol-maleimide click chemistry (TMCC)-mediated reaction. Briefly, H12 peptide (Cys-HHLGGAKQAGDV) was pre-dissolved in DMF followed by dilution with pH 7.0 PBS in a reaction flask. The DSPE-PEG-Mal was also dissolved in DMF. Under continuous stirring, the DSPE-PEG-Mal solution was added dropwise to the flask at a 1.0:1.1 molar ratio (Mal:H12) to prepare the DSPE-PEG-H12. The reaction mixture was agitated continuously for 24 h at room temperature. The excessive H12 was removed by dialysis (MWCO = 2 kDa). The final product of DSPE-PEG-H12 was characterized by a 400 MHz 1H NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) and a 600 MHz 13C NMR spectrometer in DMSO-d6.

Preparation and characterization of H12-PEG-Gd-NV

The Gd- and H12-coated, and PEGylated nanovesicles, denoted as H12-PEG-Gd-NV, were fabricated via a thin-film hydration followed by extrusion through 200 nm polycarbonate membranes. Briefly, DPPC (10 mg), cholesterol (2 mg), DSPE-PEG-H12 (2 mg), and PE-DTPA (Gd) (0.7 mg) were co-dissolved in a chloroform/ethyl acetate mixture (2/1, v/v) to ensure molecular homogeneity. Following solvent evaporation using a rotary evaporator (40 °C, 120 rpm), a uniform lipid film was formed and then hydrated with 5 mL pH 7.4 PBS under stirring at 40°C for 30 min. To achieve narrow size distribution, the nanovesicles were sonicated and then extruded with an Avanti lipid extruder through 200 nm polycarbonate membranes. PEG-Gd-NV without H12 peptide was prepared for comparisons according to the similar process described above. Hydrodynamic diameters, polydispersity indexes (PDI) and zeta potentials of the nanovesicles were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis (Zetasizer Lab, Malvern, UK). The morphology of H12-PEG-Gd-NV was measured by 120 kV transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

MR imaging capacity measurements

All MR imaging experiments were performed using a horizontal bore 9.4T uMR imaging system (United Imaging Life Science Instrument, Wuhan, China) equipped with an 86-mm inner diameter volume coil for radiofrequency transmission and VTC42 phased-array receiver coil. The relaxation efficiency was obtained by fitting the value of 1/T1 to the gadolinium concentration. To quantify the MR imaging capability of H12-PEG-Gd-NV, nanovesicle dispersions were prepared in PBS with varied Gd concentrations at 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.025, 0.05, and 0.1 mM. T1-weighted gradient echo sequences were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 4.4 ms, echo time (TE) 1.78 ms, field of view 80 × 80 mm2, slice thickness 1.5 mm, matrix size 256 × 256. For longitudinal relaxivity (r1) determination, variable flip angle (VFA) mapping was performed using four excitation angles (4°, 9°, 15°, 25°) under constant TR/TE conditions. Signal intensity data were fitted to the Ernst equation using vendor-provided reconstruction software to generate T1 relaxation time maps. The r1 values (mM−1s−1) were subsequently calculated through linear regression of inverse T1 relaxation rates versus Gd concentrations. The clinical T1-MRI contrast agent gadopentetic acid (Gd-DTPA) solution at the different concentration in PBS was used as a control.

In vitro thrombus MR imaging

Fresh whole blood was collected from healthy rats (n = 5) and immediately transferred into centrifuge tubes. Thrombin was added to induce thrombus formation, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The freshly formed thrombi were subsequently incubated with PBS, Gd-DTPA, PEG-Gd-NV, or H12-PEG-Gd-NV for 1 h. After incubation, the thrombi were washed with PBS to remove unbound probes and then placed in the MR scanning system for analysis.

To evaluate the imaging performance at different thrombus stages, thrombin-induced thrombi were maintained for 2 h, 3 d, or 7 d at 4 °C. At each time point, thrombi were incubated with H12-PEG-Gd-NV for 1 h under identical conditions. The thrombi were then washed with PBS to remove unbound probes and subjected to MR imaging using the same protocol described above.

Thrombus specific targeting in a microfluidics system

Citrated blood from SD rats was pre-incubated with DIOC6 and then perfused in the collagen-coated channels at a shear rate of 1000 s−1. Extension of perfusion time up to 10 min enhanced green (platelet) fluorescence, suggesting that stable nonocclusive thrombi were formed. Subsequently, the channels were washed by perfusion with PBS buffer and then perfused with Rhod-labeled PEG-Gd-NV and H12-PEG-Gd-NV at a shear rate of 1000 s−1. To investigate the specific targeting of our probe to αIIbβ3, we performed a blocking experiment using eptifibatide (ep), a specific αIIbβ3 inhibitor, prior to perfusion with Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV. The changes in the green (platelet) and red (nanovesicles) fluorescence were monitored after 10 min.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were performed in strict agreement with the Principles of Animal Care and Use with an ethical approval obtained from the Animal Research Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People's Hospital (ethical approval number: 2022-0188). Healthy male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (∼220 g) and Kunming (KM) mice (∼20 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.

In vivo thrombus MR imaging in the carotid thrombosis model

The left common carotid artery in an SD rat was first exposed. A piece of filter paper (1 × 2 mm) was saturated with 10% FeCl3 solution (w/v) and placed directly on the carotid artery for 3 min to form a thrombus. Immediately after FeCl3-associated thrombus induction, the animals were positioned in a 9.4T uMR imaging system. The animals were connected to a respiratory rate monitor, and the flow of anesthetic gas was constantly regulated to maintain a breathing rate of 60-80 min−1. A fast spin echo (FSE) pulse sequence was used to acquire scout T1-weighted MR images. The following parameters were used: TR = 923 ms, TE = 18.48 ms, FOV = 34 × 47 mm, matrix = 174 × 240, slice = 30. The area covered the common carotid artery, the carotid bifurcation, and the distal carotid artery. Contiguous cross-sectional images were obtained perpendicular to the long axis of the neck. The animals were scanned at baseline (20 min after thrombus induction) and longitudinally over a 2-h period (after a bolus injection of the contrast agent) to follow the course of enhancement. After thrombus induction, a baseline MR imaging scan was performed. To demonstrate the in vivo specificity of H12-peptide to the integrin αIIbβ3 expressed on activated platelets, a competitive inhibition experiment was performed. Eptifibatide (ep) was injected at the dose of 72 μg kg−1 to saturate the integrins before H12-PEG-Gd-NV administration. Thereafter, H12-PEG-Gd-NV was injected and the postscan procedure was performed according to the protocol previously described. Quantification of MR imaging signal (contrast to noise ratios (CNR)) enhancement analysis was performed. The signal intensity (SI) of the thrombus in T1-weighted images was quantified as SIthrombus. Additional regions of interest (ROI) were placed in the paravertebral muscles and in a motion-free region outside the animal to determine the signal intensity of the reference tissue (SImuscle) and the signal intensity of noise signal (SInoise), respectively. Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3) and graphed by using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Differences between two groups were analyzed for significance by using an unpaired Student's t-test and comparisons among multiple groups were assessed for significance using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. Differences were statistically significant when the p-value was < 0.05.

Results and discussions

Preparation and characterizations of fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicle probes

To prepare the lipid-based fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicles incorporating the H12 peptide from the γ chain of fibrinogen, a terminal thiol group-containing Cys-H12 peptide (Cys-HHLGGAKQAGDV) was first synthesized, and its structure was confirmed through a liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC/MS) in Fig. S1. The resulting Cys-H12 was then conjugated to DSPE-PEG-maleimide (DSPE-PEG-Mal) through a thiol-maleimide click chemistry (TMCC) reaction to obtain the DSPE-PEG-H12 (Fig. S2a). The successful conjugation of H12 to DSPE-PEG-Mal was confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S2b and S2c). In the 1H NMR spectrum of DSPE-PEG-Mal, the characteristic signals of the DSPE-PEG backbone were observed at approximately δ 0.85-0.90 ppm (terminal methyl), δ 1.20-1.35 ppm (alkyl methylene), the methylene protons adjacent to the ester carbonyl groups at δ 2.25-2.35 ppm, δ 3.50-3.75 ppm (PEG backbone). The diagnostic maleimide olefinic proton signal at approximately δ 7.0 ppm was clearly present in DSPE-PEG-Mal but disappeared after reaction with the cysteine-containing H12 peptide. Consistently, the 13C NMR signal corresponding to the maleimide C=C bond at approximately δ 134 ppm was absent in DSPE-PEG-H12 (Fig. S2c), whereas the PEG signal at approximately δ 70 ppm and lipid-chain signals in the aliphatic region were retained. These results support the consumption of the maleimide double bond through thiol-maleimide Michael addition and validate the successful preparation of DSPE-PEG-H12. The fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicles (denoted as H12-PEG-Gd-NV) were then fabricated by DPPC, cholesterol, PE-DTPA(Gd) and DSPE-PEG-H12 through a thin-film hydration followed by extrusion through 200-nm polycarbonate membranes (Fig. 2a). The PEGylated, Gd-containing nanovesicles without H12 peptides, denoted as PEG-Gd-NV, were constructed for comparison (Fig. 2b).

As shown in Fig. 2c, the morphology of fresh H12-PEG-Gd-NV was investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to be spherical. As shown in Table S1 and Fig. S3, both PEG-Gd-NV (115.3 ± 2.0 nm) and H12-PEG-Gd-NV (124.0 ± 5.5 nm) exhibited narrow, intensity-weighted DLS size distributions. The slight numerical increase observed after H12 modification was not statistically significant, indicating that H12 incorporation did not result in a substantial change in hydrodynamic diameter under the tested formulation conditions. Furthermore, both nanovesicle systems showed the polydispersity index (PDI) values between 0.15 and 0.16, indicating a monodisperse size distribution. Zeta potential values of the H12-PEG-Gd-NV were −30.2 ± 0.2 mV and −28.8 ± 0.3 mV for PEG-Gd-NV, suggesting similar surface charge properties. Stability of the H12-PEG-Gd-NV was first examined by monitoring the size change during storage at 4 °C. As shown in Fig. S4a, there was no significant size change after 7 days of storage at 4 °C. To further assess the physiological stability of H12-PEG-Gd-NV, the nanovesicles were incubated in 50% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C under gentle shaking for 7 days. As shown in Fig. S4b and S4c, neither the hydrodynamic diameter nor the zeta potential of H12-PEG-Gd-NV showed substantial changes during incubation, indicating that H12-PEG-Gd-NV maintained favorable colloidal stability under physiologically relevant conditions. In addition, the longitudinal relaxivity (r1) of H12-PEG-Gd-NV was evaluated through a 9.4T uMR scanner and the clinical T1-MRI contrast agent gadopentetic acid (Gd-DTPA) was used as a control. As illustrated in Fig. 2d–f, H12-PEG-Gd-NV exhibited effective T1-weighted MRI contrast performance (r1 = 3.970 mM−1s−1), about 1.32 times stronger than that of Gd-DTPA (r1 = 2.995 mM−1s−1). Besides, a hemolysis assay was used to investigate the hemocompatibility of probes. As shown in Fig. S4d, the level of hemolysis was less than 1% for the H12-PEG-Gd-NV, PEG-Gd-NV, and Gd-DTPA, suggesting that all probes had high biocompatibility in blood.

Activated platelet targeting and in vivo distribution of H12-PEG-Gd-NV

The binding behavior of H12-PEG-Gd-NV towards αIIbβ3 on activated platelets at a thrombus site was first analyzed using a fluorescence microscope. Activated platelets were immobilized onto a collagen-coated coverslip and then incubated with either Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV or Rhod-labeled PEG-Gd-NV for 12 h. As shown in Fig. 3a, compared to PEG-Gd-NV, the H12-PEG-Gd-NV exhibited considerably stronger adhesion towards αIIbβ3 on the activated platelets. Quantitative analysis of Rhod fluorescence intensity revealed that H12-PEG-Gd-NV achieved approximately fourfold higher binding affinity than that of PEG-Gd-NV (Fig. 3b). To further characterize the activation-dependent binding of H12-PEG-Gd-NV, resting platelets, thrombin-activated platelets, and eptifibatide (ep)-pretreated activated platelets were incubated with Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV and monitored at different time points. As shown in Fig. S5a, activated platelets exhibited a progressive increase in Rhod fluorescence over time, while resting platelets showed only minimal signals. Pretreatment with eptifibatide markedly attenuated the platelet-associated Rhod fluorescence in activated platelets. Quantitative analysis (Fig. S5b) confirmed that the fluorescence intensity of the activated platelet group rose substantially with time, whereas both the resting and eptifibatide-blocked groups remained at relatively low levels. These findings demonstrate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV preferentially binds activated platelets in a time-dependent manner, and that this binding is mediated by activated αIIbβ3 rather than by total αIIbβ3. Furthermore, the selective binding of the Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV towards αIIbβ3 on activated platelets at a thrombus site was validated by an in vitro thrombus MR imaging. As shown in Figs. 3c , 0.5 mL blood samples were placed in a centrifuge tube and allowed to thrombosis by adding thrombin at 37 oC for 2 h. After thrombus formation, PBS, Gd-DTPA, PEG-Gd-NV or H12-PEG-Gd-NV were added to the clots at the same Gd concentration (0.1 mM Gd per clot). The clots were then washed with PBS and were transferred to the 9.4T uMR scanner for T1-weighted imaging and T1 mapping assay. As shown in Fig. 3d, the H12-PEG-Gd-NV-treated clot exhibited high brightness and high intensity in both T1-weighted images and pseudocolor images, which is significantly stronger than that of Gd-DTPA or PEG-Gd-NV. The quantitative assessments of T1 relaxation time according to signal intensity in T1 maps were shown in Fig. 3e. It was found that H12-PEG-Gd-NV achieved shorter T1 relaxation time than that of Gd-DTPA or PEG-Gd-NV, suggesting an enhanced MR imaging due to the higher binding affinity towards αIIbβ3 on the activated platelets at a thrombus site. To further evaluate the imaging performance of H12-PEG-Gd-NV across thrombi at different formation stages, thrombi formed for 2 h, 3 d, or 7 d were incubated with H12-PEG-Gd-NV under identical conditions. Representative T1-weighted images and pseudocolor images are shown in Fig. S6a. The 2 h freshly formed thrombi exhibited the strongest T1-weighted signal enhancement, while the contrast progressively decreased in the 3 d and 7 d thrombi. Quantitative analysis of T1 signal further confirmed this stage-dependent imaging pattern (Fig. S6b). These results indicate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV preferentially enhances MR imaging of freshly formed thrombi, supporting its sensitivity for detecting biologically active thrombi. We note that this characterization is based on in vitro thrombus models, and that systematic evaluation in aged-thrombus animal models will further extend the assessment of probe selectivity for biologically active thrombi in vivo.

In addition, a microfluidic system was further employed to consolidate the selective targeting of H12-PEG-Gd-NV towards αIIbβ3 on activated platelets under a physiological flow at a shear rate of 1000 s−1. As shown in Fig. 3f(i), the 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DIOC6) pre-treated blood was perfused in the collagen-coated biochip channels at a shear rate of 1000 s−1 for 10 min. Stable nonocclusive thrombi were formed (Fig. S7), and the platelet-associated green fluorescence was observed by fluorescence microscopy. As illustrated in Fig. 3f(ii), the fluorescence changes, green for platelets and red for probes, were monitored after the thrombus-containing channel was perfused with Rhod-labeled probes at 1000 s−1 for 10 min. As shown in Fig. 3g and h, perfusion with Rhod-labeled PEG-Gd-NV caused a negligible red fluorescence staining at thrombi, indicative of negligible targeting towards αIIbβ3 on activated platelets at a thrombus site under a physiological flow. In contrast, perfusion with Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV alone resulted in nearly complete colocalization of red nanovesicle fluorescence with green platelet fluorescence, confirming highly efficient and specific targeting of αIIbβ3 on activated platelets within the thrombus. It is interesting to note that, once αIIbβ3 expressed on the surface of activated platelets were inhibited by eptifibatide (ep), there was only a marginal red fluorescence at thrombus in the H12-PEG-Gd-NV + ep experimental group. As quantitative analysis shown in Fig. 3g, the thrombus treated with H12-PEG-Gd-NV displayed about threefold higher fluorescence intensity than that of Rhod-labeled PEG-Gd-NV and H12-PEG-Gd-NV + ep, demonstrating its ability to maintain effective and specific targeting towards αIIbβ3 on the activated platelets at a thrombus site in the shear-stress environment under physiological flow conditions.

To evaluate the in vivo circulation behavior of the nanovesicle probes, Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used as the animal model. Briefly, the rats were intravenously administered different formulations at the same dose of Rhod-labeled H12-PEG-Gd-NV and PEG-Gd-NV, respectively. Then, blood samples were taken at specific time points, and the fluorescence intensity in the plasma was assessed for quantitation. As shown in Fig. 4a, long circulation time was observed in both H12-PEG-Gd-NV and PEG-Gd-NV groups, probably due to the protective properties of PEGylated lipids in the nanovesicles [33,34]. In addition to the observed long blood retention profile, the half-lives (t1/2) of both H12-PEG-Gd-NV and PEG-Gd-NV were calculated to be 106.7 ± 33.5 and 97.2 ± 35.0 min, respectively (Fig. 4b). This data is consistent with the previous report by other researchers that the circulation half-life of the lipid-based nanovesicles [35,36].

Furthermore, the distribution of H12-PEG-Gd-NV in vivo was tracked through MRI. Clinical Gd-DTPA was used as a control. After H12-PEG-Gd-NV and Gd-DTPA were injected intravenously into the tail vein of Kunming (KM) mice, the real-time MRI of various organs was monitored during the period of 150 min post injection. As shown in Fig. 4c and d and S8a, the MR signals from H12-PEG-Gd-NV and the Gd-DTPA were mainly found in the liver and kidney, indicating that the nanovesicles could accumulate in metabolic organs. As expected, after injection of H12-PEG-Gd-NV and Gd-DTPA, the quantitative signal intensity of H12-PEG-Gd-NV group in the major organs (Fig. 4e and f, S8b and S8c) were higher than those in the Gd-DTPA group, further consolidating that H12-PEG-Gd-NV could prolong the retention time in blood.

In vivo MR imaging of FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombi

To validate our central hypothesis that MRI contrast is dependent on the specific binding event between H12 and αIIbβ3 in vivo, we evaluated our H12-PEG-Gd-NV probe in two pathophysiologically distinct thrombosis models.

We first employed a FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombosis model in rats to simulate in situ thrombus formation [37,38]. As shown in Fig. 5a, firstly, the left common carotid artery in an SD rat was exposed. A piece of filter paper (1 × 2 mm) was saturated with 10% FeCl3 solution (w/v) and placed directly on the carotid artery for 3 min to form a thrombus. Subsequently, a T1-weighted MR imaging was monitored at various time points through a 9.4T uMR imaging system, followed by intravenous injection with probe. Critically, pre-contrast T1-weighted images (0 min) showed very low inherent signal at the thrombus site (Fig. 5b). Following intravenous injection, the imaging performance diverged markedly between groups. As shown in Fig. 5c, administration of H12-PEG-Gd-NV probes resulted in a strong, time-dependent signal enhancement at the thrombus, reaching its maximum ΔCNR (contrast to noise ratio) at about 30 min post-injection. In contrast, the structurally analogous PEG-Gd-NV and the clinical Gd-DTPA produced only minimal and transient signal enhancement at the thrombus site, which was significantly lower than that achieved by the targeted probe of H12-PEG-Gd-NV at all key time points. On one hand, this demonstrates that H12-PEG-Gd-NV exhibits prolonged retention and higher concentration accumulation at the thrombus site. On the other hand, it also indicates that the nanovesicle probe possesses superior relaxation performance, which aligns with our previous experimental data (Fig. 3c–e). This target-specific enhancement of MRI was further confirmed by a competitive inhibition experiment. As shown in Fig. 5b, the robust signal improvement elicited by H12-PEG-Gd-NV was substantially abolished in rats pre-administered with eptifibatide (ep). This indicates that H12-PEG-Gd-NV specifically binds to integrin αIIbβ3 in vivo, resulting in an enhanced signal distinguishable from the vascular wall, which is consistent with the experimental findings in the flow-based microfluidic system (Fig. 3f–h). These results provide in vivo corroboration of the mechanism of ligand-triggered relaxation switching for efficient MRI previously demonstrated in vitro. Furthermore, the signal persisted for over 120 min, demonstrating a sustained imaging capability that constitutes a distinct practical advantage over the transient contrast provided by conventional agents.

In vivo MR imaging of cerebral thromboembolic thrombi

To evaluate the imaging performance of H12-PEG-Gd-NV in a clinically relevant distal thromboembolism setting, a cerebral thromboembolism model was established in SD rats. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, autologous thrombi were prepared in PE tubing and injected through the external carotid artery into the internal carotid artery toward the bifurcation of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA). After thrombus injection, the external carotid artery was ligated and blood flow through the common carotid artery was restored. As shown in Fig. 6b-ⅰ, prior to the surgical procedure, 3D time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (3D-TOF MRA) was performed to evaluate the normal cerebral vasculature and blood flow in rats. Following the induction of cerebral thromboembolism, a combination of 3D-TOF MRA and T1-weighted gradient echo (GRE) bright-blood imaging was utilized to assess vascular occlusion and hemodynamic alterations. In addition, as shown in Fig. 6b-ⅱ, functional magnetic resonance imaging, specifically diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and correlated apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, was employed to evaluate focal diffusion restriction resulting from cerebral ischemia induced by thromboembolism. As depicted in Fig. 6c, before the surgical procedure, the cerebral vasculature of the rat appeared symmetrical with intact blood flow signals, as demonstrated by preoperative 3D-TOF MRA. Following the surgical of cerebral thromboembolism, the perfused brain confirmed a Y-shaped thromboembolism on the left (Fig. 6d, red arrow) compared with the void cerebral vessel on the right (Fig. 6d, blue arrow). As demonstrated in Fig. 6e, the 3D-TOF MRA clearly revealed post-surgical occlusion of the left cerebral vasculature (indicated by the red arrow), while the right cerebral vasculature remains patent with preserved blood flow (indicated by the blue arrow). Fig. 6f (axial) and Fig. 6g (coronal) obtained from the T1-weighted GRE bright-blood sequence demonstrated complete disappearance of blood flow signals in the left cerebral vasculature (indicated by the red arrow), while preserved vascular filling and patent flow are maintained on the right side (indicated by the blue arrow). To further evaluate the brain damage resulting from the cerebral blood flow obstruction, functional magnetic resonance imaging was performed. DWI (Fig. 6h) and the corresponding ADC map (Fig. 6i) showed a distinct focal area of diffusion restriction consistent with post-ischemic cytotoxic edema. Additionally, 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining of brain also confirmed the presence of a well-defined cerebral infarct zone (Fig. 6j). Quantitative analysis of the DWI-positive region was performed by measuring the infarct area across different sections relative to the Bregma point (Fig. 6k), demonstrating stable and reproducible model establishment. These findings collectively indicate that distal thromboembolism induces a hypoxic-ischemic microenvironment leading to severe cerebral infarction. The results are corresponded with the absence of flow signal on time-of-flight angiography and reduced vascular filling on T1-weighted gradient-echo bright-blood sequences (Fig. 6e–g), thereby validating the successful construction of the experimental thromboembolic model. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 6l) revealed occlusion of the left cerebral vasculature (red arrow) and the absence of thrombus formation in the patent right cerebral vasculature (blue arrow), corresponding to the hemodynamic alterations observed in MRI. A well-demarcated ischemic infarct area was delineated by a white dashed circle, consistent with the functional imaging findings, further confirming the induction of targeted cerebral infarction in the established thromboembolic model.

Following the successful establishment of the model, we further designed a probe-targeted thrombus enhancement experiment. As illustrated in Fig. 7a, fast spin echo (FSE) T1-weighted imaging was first performed to scan and record the baseline signal intensity in the suspected thrombus region post-surgery. At 30 min post injection of the nanovesicle probe, FSE T1-weighted imaging was repeated to quantify the signal intensity at the thrombus site, allowing for further assessment of H12-triggered relaxation switching for MRI. Except for nanovesicle probe H12-PEG-Gd-NV, non-targeted nanovesicle probe PEG-Gd-NV, the clinical Gd-DTPA, and inhibition group H12-PEG-Gd-NV + ep were set as controls. Furthermore, at 30 min post injection, a clear, focal, and pronounced T1 hyperintensity, precisely colocalizing with the thrombus in the left cerebral vessel, was observed exclusively in the H12-PEG-Gd-NV group (Fig. 7b and c). In contrast, the non-targeted control and clinical agent failed to generate diagnostically meaningful contrast at the thrombus site, with their signal intensity remaining indistinguishable from the background. The critical role of H12-αIIbβ3 binding was further proven by competitive inhibition, as the observed hyperintensity was markedly attenuated in the group H12-PEG-Gd-NV + ep. This once again demonstrates the specificity of the nanovesicle probe to αIIbβ3 for efficient targeted MR imaging.

Together, these results demonstrate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV enables targeted MR visualization of cerebral thromboembolic thrombi in vivo. The consistent contrast enhancement in both carotid artery thrombosis and cerebral thromboembolism models supports the applicability of this probe for imaging activated platelet-rich thrombi in distinct vascular beds.

Biosafety profiles in vivo

Prior to the in vivo biosafety evaluation, we first assessed whether H12-PEG-Gd-NV directly affected platelet function or promoted blood-clot formation in vitro. As shown in Fig. S9, H12-PEG-Gd-NV at 1×, 5×, and 10× the imaging-relevant concentration did not induce appreciable platelet aggregation compared with the PBS group, whereas the agonist-treated positive-control group exhibited substantial increased platelet aggregation. A flow cytometric fibrinogen-binding assay was further performed to evaluate potential functional activation of platelet integrin αIIbβ3. As shown in Fig. S10, the Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated fibrinogen signals in the H12-PEG-Gd-NV-treated groups were comparable to those of the PBS control across all tested concentrations, while thrombin-stimulated platelets showed enhanced fibrinogen binding. In addition, the modified halo whole-blood coagulation assay (Fig. S11) showed that H12-PEG-Gd-NV at 1×, 5×, and 10× the imaging-relevant concentration produced absorbance values comparable to the PBS control, whereas the thrombin-containing positive-control group exhibited reduced absorbance due to erythrocyte retention within the formed thrombus. Collectively, these findings indicate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV does not measurably induce platelet aggregation, functional αIIbβ3 activation, or whole-blood coagulation under the tested conditions. These observations are mechanistically consistent with the physiological context of H12-αIIbβ3 interaction. Although H12 is derived from the fibrinogen γ-chain and might in principle compete with endogenous fibrinogen, several factors limit such interference at the imaging dose: endogenous fibrinogen circulates at high physiological concentrations and is further enriched at activated platelets via α-granule release [39], whereas H12 delivered by H12-PEG-Gd-NV is far less abundant. The absence of detectable platelet activation or coagulation across a 10-fold concentration range further supports a wide safety margin. Nevertheless, future translational studies will need to address the effects of H12-PEG-Gd-NV on established thrombus growth, thrombus stability under flow, and interactions with antithrombotic therapies (such as aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin, and thrombolytics).

As shown in Fig. 8a, no significant alterations in coagulation parameters, including fibrinogen (FIB), thrombin time (TT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and prothrombin time (PT), were observed following treatment with H12-PEG-Gd-NV compared to the PEG-Gd-NV and PBS groups. Due to the accumulation of H12-PEG-Gd-NV mainly in the kidney and liver (Fig. 4c–f), the main biochemical indexes of kidney (uric acid: UA, creatinine: Crea, and Urea) and liver (alkaline phosphatase: ALP, aspartate aminotransferase: AST, and alanine aminotransferase: ALT) were also examined (Fig. 8b), showing similar levels in the H12-PEG-Gd-NV treated rats as compared to PEG-Gd-NV and PBS. Furthermore, the hematology results in Fig. 8c showed that post treatment with H12-PEG-Gd-NV, no obvious changes to the blood indexes were observed, including inflammatory cells (white blood cells: WBC, granulocytes: Gran, lymphocytes: Lym and monocytes: Mon) and other main indexes (monocytes percentage: %Mon, red blood cell: RBC, hemoglobin: HGB, hematocrit value: HCT, mean corpuscular hemoglobin: MCH, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration: MCHC, coefficient of variation of red blood cell volume distribution width: RDW-CV, standard deviation of red blood cell volume distribution width: RDW-SD, platelet: PLT, mean platelet volume: MPV, platelet distribution width: PDW, thrombocytocrit: PCT, platelet large cell count: P-LCC, and platelet large cell ratio: P-LCR). To evaluate potential unwanted side effects, the main organs of SD rats were further harvested for H&E staining. Fig. 8d shows the H&E staining of the dissected liver, heart, spleen, lung, and kidney after injection with H12-PEG-Gd-NV. There was no evidence of pathological differences or inflammatory cell infiltration in all those tissue sections, demonstrating the good in vivo biocompatibility of H12-PEG-Gd-NV. The hemorrhagic risk of H12-PEG-Gd-NV was further evaluated using a tail bleeding assay [40]. As shown in Fig. 8e, a distal 1-cm segment of the KM mouse tail was amputated to induce bleeding, and the bleeding time and volume were recorded at 2 h post injection of H12-PEG-Gd-NV, PEG-Gd-NV and PBS, respectively. Fig. 8f shows that the complete blood coagulation occurred 2.56 ± 1.32, 2.40 ± 1.34 and 2.51 ± 1.27 min in the PBS, PEG-Gd-NV and H12-PEG-Gd-NV, respectively. Fig. 8g shows that the bleeding volume induced by PBS (28.4 ± 9.5 μL) was similar to PEG-Gd-NV (25.8 ± 11.1 μL) and H12-PEG-Gd-NV (28.3 ± 11.3 μL). The bleeding index, as defined by the product of bleeding time and bleeding volume, was then calculated to evaluate the hemorrhagic risk [41]. As shown in Fig. 8h, the bleeding index in mice treated with PBS (0.07 ± 0.04 min × mL) was similar to that in the PEG-Gd-NV (0.06 ± 0.05 min × mL) and H12-PEG-Gd-NV (0.07 ± 0.05 min × mL) treatment group. These findings indicate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV did not cause detectable hemorrhagic side effects under the tested conditions. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 8i, after injection of PEG-Gd-NV or H12-PEG-Gd-NV in SD rats, no noticeable body weight loss was detected within 7 days. Collectively, all these multifaceted biosafety evaluations, including the coagulation parameters, organ function biomarkers, hematological indices, tissue histopathology, hemorrhagic risk and spanning systemic physiology (body weight), demonstrate that H12-PEG-Gd-NV probe exhibits excellent biocompatibility in vivo.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a fibrinogen-mimetic nanovesicle probe, H12-PEG-Gd-NV, that enables molecular-gated MRI of thrombus through a ligand-triggered relaxation switching mechanism. This approach directly transduces the conformational activation of platelet integrin αIIbβ3, a precise biological switch, into a target-dependent T1 signal enhancement, thereby overcoming the fundamental limitation of conventional “always-on” contrast agents which cannot distinguish biologically active thrombi from quiescent background tissue.

The probe's design integrates three key conceptual advances. First, it establishes a conditional signal gate, where MRI contrast is dependent on the specific binding event between the H12 peptide and the ligand-binding state of αIIbβ3 on activated platelets. These switching mechanism yields high target-to-background contrast and enables the sensitive detection of early and micro-thrombi. Second, it employs the fibrinogen γ-chain-derived H12 peptide, which preferentially recognizes activated αIIbβ3 and minimizes off-target binding compared with promiscuous RGD motifs. Third, it leverages a biocompatible lipid nanovesicle platform that provides prolonged circulation, thrombus-selective accumulation, and a favorable biosafety profile without measurable perturbation of platelet function, αIIbβ3 activation, or whole-blood coagulation, even at concentrations up to 10× the imaging dose.

Validation across in vitro microfluidic assays and in vivo models of carotid and cerebral thrombosis confirms the probe's specific binding under physiological flow and its ability to deliver high-contrast visualization of activated platelet-driven thrombosis, outperforming clinical contrast agent and non-targeted controls. This molecular-gated imaging strategy holds significant translational potential. It may guide personalized antithrombotic therapy by discriminating active from stable thrombi, enable early intervention through detection of high-risk micro-thrombi, and provide a dynamic biomarker for real-time therapeutic monitoring. Beyond thrombosis, the underlying principle of ligand-triggered signal switching represents a generalizable design paradigm for functional molecular imaging across a spectrum of diseases.

Several aspects merit further investigation to advance the clinical translation of H12-PEG-Gd-NV. First, the current evidence for differentiating active and stabilized thrombi is primarily derived from in vitro thrombus models, and systematic evaluation in aged-thrombus animal models will be needed to extend this assessment in vivo. Second, more detailed evaluations of the potential influence of H12-PEG-Gd-NV on established thrombus growth, thrombus stability under flow, and interactions with antithrombotic therapies (such as aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin, and thrombolytics) will be important for clinical development. Third, although the present thin-film hydration and membrane-extrusion process yielded H12-PEG-Gd-NV with a relatively narrow DLS distribution and low PDI, microfluidic fabrication offers advantages for further refining size uniformity and batch-to-batch reproducibility. By providing controlled solvent exchange and tunable formulation parameters, microfluidic platforms can produce lipid nanovesicles with improved physicochemical consistency [[42], [43], [44]], which is particularly relevant for MRI probes whose performance depends on particle size and surface composition. Future studies will explore microfluidic fabrication to further optimize the uniformity and scalability of H12-PEG-Gd-NV production. Fourth, the present 7-day evaluation provides preliminary evidence of colloidal stability, but extended stability assessments under varied storage and formulation conditions will be required for long-term clinical translation.
