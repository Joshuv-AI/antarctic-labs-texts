Quantum State Characterization of Gravitational Waves via Graviton Counting Statistics
Kristian Toccacelo, Thomas Beitel, Ulrik Lund Andersen, Igor Pikovski

Abstract
Although gravitational waves are now routinely observed, the detection of individual gravitons has long been regarded as impossible. Recent work, however, has demonstrated that single-graviton detection can be achieved and may be feasible in the near future. Here we show that beyond mere particle detection, these detectors provide access to the quantum state and particle statistics of gravitational waves. We show that graviton detection probabilities enable the discrimination between squeezed, coherent, and thermal radiation. We further demonstrate that the full quantum statistics contained in the second-order correlation function of the passing wave can be directly measured at the detector, independent of the weak gravitational interaction strength. Building on recent quantum-optical techniques, this capability opens the way to full quantum state tomography of Gaussian states. Our results demonstrate that single-graviton detection is not only of foundational significance but also of practical value, allowing for the characterization of quantum statistics and the states of the gravitational radiation field, which remain currently unknown.

Introduction

Whether and how gravity is quantized remains one of the most fundamental open questions in physics. While general relativity successfully describes gravity at macroscopic scales, its reconciliation with quantum mechanics has been the subject of theoretical studies for nearly a century

The result is possible due to now routine LIGO detections of passing GWs to which a single particle detector could cross-correlate, and recent advancements in the cooling and measurement of individual energy transitions of acoustic modes in macroscopic systems

Here, we show that, beyond mere detection, single-graviton detectors can also provide new input in our understanding of the quantum composition of gravitational waves. Quantum theory predicts that gravitational waves, like their electromagnetic counterparts, could come in a variety of quantum states, ranging from classical-like coherent states to highly non-classical states, like squeezed states, or even non-Gaussian states with negative Wigner functions and sub-Poissonian counting statistics. The non-linear nature of gravity and the GW production process indicates that passing GWs may indeed deviate from coherent states
14
;
17
;
25
;
11
. However, identifying which states can be distinguished observationally remains an open challenge. The classical position detection method used for GW detection has limited sensitivity to features that determine the quantum state—unless it is exponentially enhanced
32
;
31
;
18
;
19
;
1
—and mostly confirms the classical mean corresponding to the intensity. But the same intensity can be produced by a variety of quantum states. In particular, Gaussian states, characterized by a positive Wigner function and fully described by their first and second moments, are a wide class of different possible states, which play a central role in quantum optics and continuous-variable quantum information
37
;
6
;
5
. It remains an open question what exact states of gravitational radiation astrophysical sources may produce, and which of these states may be detectable on Earth.

In this work, we propose a robust method to determine gravitational wave states through graviton counting statistics in graviton-click detectors. By analyzing the statistical properties of phonon number distributions in the detectors, we derive experimental criteria that distinguish Gaussian characteristics of the passing GWs and can even provide their full quantum statistics and state tomography. This framework provides a potential diagnostic for detecting the statistical particle nature of gravitational radiation and establishes a close link between graviton detection and modern methods in quantum optics.

II
Interaction Hamiltonian and equations of motion

We first briefly summarize the interaction dynamics between GWs and a resonant detector that leads to the ability to detect gravitons—see Ref.
40
and App.
A
. We consider a gravitational wave incident on a macroscopic bulk acoustic resonator, which we assume to be cylindrical, of length
L
, and total mass
M
. The interaction Hamiltonian between the quantized modes of the gravitational radiation field
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, and the collective phononic modes
\hat{b}_{\ell}
and
\hat{b}_{\ell}^{\dagger}
, where
\ell
is an odd integer, and
\omega_{\ell}
the corresponding resonator frequency, can be written as
13
;
30
;
40
;
24
;
23

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=\hbar\,\gamma_{\text{g}}\left(\hat{a}\,e^{-i\nu t}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{i\nu t}\right)\left(\hat{b}_{\ell}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\right).

(1)

Here,
\nu=\nu(t)
is the gravitational wave frequency,
\gamma_{\text{g}}
is the coupling strength given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{g}}=\sqrt{(-1)^{\ell-1}\frac{8\pi GM\nu^{3}L^{3}}{\omega_{\ell}\,c^{2}V\pi^{4}\ell^{4}}},

(2)

G
is Newton’s constant,
c
the speed of light in vacuum,
V
the characteristic volume of the gravitational wave, and
\omega_{\ell}
the frequency of the resonator. In writing (
1
), we assume a large GW wavelength compared to the detector and that the interaction involves a single mode of the gravitational field, that is, the mode on resonance with the acoustic resonator at time
t
(in general, the GW frequency will chirp but is still well described by a single mode). Given that the coupling is typically smaller than the resonator’s frequency, it is possible to further approximate the interaction Hamiltonian by employing the rotating wave approximation (RWA), in which case

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=\hbar\,\gamma_{\text{g}}\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\,\hat{a}\,e^{-i\nu t}+\hat{b}_{\ell}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{i\nu t}\right).

(3)

The dynamics generated by the Hamiltonian (
3
) are easily solved in the Heisenberg picture, where we can view the time dependence of the mode operators as the beamsplitter transformations

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\hat{b}_{\ell}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{\ell}t}\left[\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{b}_{\ell}-i\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{a}\right],\\
\hat{a}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{\ell}t}\left[\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{a}-i\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{b}_{\ell}\right],\end{gathered}

where
\hat{b}_{\ell}=\hat{b}_{\ell}(0)
and
\hat{a}=\hat{a}(0)
. The solutions to the equations of motion given by (
II
) hold when the resonator to GW detuning parameter, defined as
\Delta=\omega_{\ell}-\nu
, vanishes; in the Apps.
B.1
and
B.2
, we respectively show the solutions for
\Delta\neq 0
, and the solutions with the RWA condition relaxed. Given the initial state of the detector and the gravitational wave modes, the solutions in Eq. (
II
) allow us to determine the expectation values of any observable of interest, for any Gaussian or non-Gaussian initial state.

II.1
Dynamics for general Gaussian gravitational wave states

In Ref.
40
, a coherent GW state was assumed, and it was shown that measurement of
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\hat{b}_{\ell}
yields direct information about single graviton absorption and emission. In the following, we will be interested in characterizing the dynamics for general Gaussian states and other observables. Since we are in a regime where the interaction between the two modes is quadratic in the mode operators [see Eq. (
1
)], all states that are initially Gaussian will evolve into final Gaussian states. This remains true when tracing out any subsystem, for instance, the reduced state
\rho_{\text{bar}}(t)
of the detector system, which will be relevant for us in the following discussion. In general, an
N
-mode Gaussian state with quadrature operators
\hat{\bm{r}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{N},\hat{p}_{N})^{\text{T}}
, is completely characterized by the first and second moments, namely, by the covariance matrix
\bm{\sigma}
and displacement vector
\bar{\bm{r}}
defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{r}_{i},\hat{r}_{j}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{r}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{r}_{j}\rangle,\,\text{and}\quad\bar{r}_{i}=\langle\hat{r}_{i}\rangle,

(6)

where, given the operator
\hat{A}
, the expectation value is
\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\text{tr}[\rho\hat{A}]
. The evolution of Gaussian moments under a quadratic Hamiltonian is determined by the symplectic transformations

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(t)=S\bm{\sigma}(0)S^{\text{T}},\quad\bar{\bm{r}}(t)=S\,\bar{\bm{r}}(0),

(7)

where
S=e^{\bm{\Omega}Ht/\hbar}
,
\bm{\Omega}
is the symplectic form, and
H
is the Hamiltonian matrix

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{T}}H\hat{\bm{r}}+\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{T}}\bar{\bm{r}}.

(8)

Using this formalism, let us consider the dynamics of a general Gaussian gravitational wave state
\rho_{\text{grav}}
interacting with a resonant detector prepared in the ground state
\rho_{\text{bar}}=|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|
. In this case, the gravitational wave state can be written as a combination of the squeezing and displacement operators acting on a thermal state for a certain choice of displacement
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, squeezing
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, and average thermal occupation number
\bar{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that an arbitrary Gaussian gravitational wave state can be cast in the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{grav}}=\hat{D}_{\alpha}\,\hat{S}_{\xi}\,\rho_{\bar{n}}\,\hat{S}^{\dagger}_{\xi}\,\hat{D}^{\dagger}_{\alpha},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\xi}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\left({\xi^{*}}\hat{a}^{2}-{\xi}\,{\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}}\right)},\quad\hat{D}_{\alpha}=e^{\alpha\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a}},

(10)

are the squeezing and displacement operators, respectively, and
\rho_{\bar{n}}
is the thermal state

\displaystyle\rho_{\bar{n}}=\frac{1}{1+\bar{n}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{\bar{n}+1}\right)^{n}|n\rangle\!\langle n|.

(11)

At the level of the covariance matrix and displacement vector, the general state (
9
) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{\text{grav}}(0)=\bm{F}_{\xi}\,\bm{\sigma}_{\bar{n}}\,\bm{F}_{\xi}^{\text{T}},\quad\bar{\bm{r}}_{\text{grav}}(0)=\sqrt{2}\left(\text{Re}(\alpha),\text{Im}(\alpha)\right)^{\text{T}},

(12)

where
\bm{F}_{\xi}=\cosh r\,\mathds{1}_{2}-\sinh r\,\mathfrak{R}_{\varphi}
is the squeezing transformation,
\xi=re^{i\theta}
,
\mathfrak{R}_{\theta}
is the matrix

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

(13)

and
\bm{\sigma}_{\bar{n}}
the covariance matrix of a thermal state with average thermal occupation
\bar{n}
, given by

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{\bar{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+\bar{n}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}+\bar{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

For the detector initialized in the vacuum state, the state at
t=0
is specified by

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{\text{bar}}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{2},\quad\bar{\bm{r}}_{\text{bar}}(0)=0.

(15)

The joint state of the gravitational wave and detector system after having resonantly interacted through the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
), can be determined via the corresponding symplectic transformations (
7
)—see App.
C
for details. In particular, the reduced state of the detector at time
t
is given, up to a local phase shift, by the first and second moments

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\bm{\sigma}_{\text{bar}}(t)=\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\bm{\sigma}_{\text{bar}}(0)+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\mathcal{}\bm{\sigma}_{\text{grav}}(0),\\
\bar{\bm{r}}_{\text{bar}}(t)=\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\bar{\bm{r}}_{\text{grav}}(0).\end{gathered}

For what we are about to consider, it is convenient to represent the first and second moments of the Gaussian state using the creation and annihilation operators. For
N
modes, we define the ladder operator

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{x}_{j}+i\hat{p}_{j}\right),\quad\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{x}_{j}-i\hat{p}_{j}\right),

(18)

which, like the canonical quadrature operators, can be collected in the column vector
\hat{\bm{a}}=(\hat{a}_{1},\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger},\dots,\hat{a}_{N},\hat{a}_{N}^{\dagger})^{\text{T}}
. In this representation, a Gaussian state is characterized by the first and second moments

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{{a}}_{i},\hat{{a}}_{j}\}\rangle-\bar{a}_{i}\bar{a}_{j}^{*},\quad\bar{a}_{i}=\langle\hat{a}_{i}\rangle.

(19)

We can go from the representation in Eq. (
6
) to the representation in Eq. (
19
) through the linear transformation

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{a}}=\bm{M}\,\bar{\bm{r}},\quad\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{M}\,\bm{\sigma}\,\bm{M}^{\dagger}

(20)

where
\bm{M}
is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{M}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}M_{j},\quad\text{with}\quad M_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
1&-i\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

III
Graviton counting statistics

The beamsplitter transformations (
II
) tell us that if the incident gravitational wave is in a coherent state
|\alpha\rangle_{\text{grav}}
and the detector is prepared in its ground state
|0\rangle_{\text{bar}}
, the joint state at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{t}\left(|\alpha\rangle_{\text{grav}}\otimes|0\rangle_{\text{bar}}\right)=|\alpha\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\rangle\otimes|\!-i\alpha\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\rangle,

(22)

where
\hat{U}_{t}=\exp\left(-\frac{i\,t}{\hbar}\hat{H}\right)
is the time-evolution operator. In this case, since the interaction with the gravitational wave induces a coherent state in the detector system, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{n}
of detecting
n
GW-induced excitations in the detector is simply the Poisson distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)}\frac{\left(|\alpha|^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\right)^{n}}{n!},

(23)

which comes about when projecting the state of the detector onto the
n
-th Fock state
|n\rangle
. This is the basis of single-graviton detection
40
, as the detection of one excitation in the detector corresponds to the resonant swap of exactly one quantum from the GW. When the gravitational field is initially in the general Gaussian state
\rho_{\text{grav}}
given by Eq. (
9
), the reduced state of the detector after interacting with the GW is not necessarily pure. Therefore, determining
\mathbb{P}_{n}
by projecting onto the Fock state
|n\rangle
like above is not trivial. Instead, we resort to the phase space representation. In general, for an arbitrary state
\rho
the probability
\mathbb{P}_{n}=\text{tr}[\rho\,\hat{m}_{n}]
, where we defined the fock state projector
\hat{m}_{n}=|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|
, can be written as the overlap integral

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}=\int\text{d}^{2}\bm{\alpha}\,Q_{\rho}(\bm{\alpha})P_{n}(\bm{\alpha}).

(24)

Here,
Q_{\rho}(\bm{\alpha})=\langle\alpha|\rho|\alpha\rangle/\pi
is the Husimi
Q
-function of the state
\rho
,
P_{n}(\bm{\alpha})
is the Glauber–Sudarshan
P
-representation of the projector
|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|
, and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
.

III.1
Counting excitations in Gaussian states

To explicitly determine the probabilities (
24
), we use the representation of the first and second moments in terms of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\bar{\mathbf{a}}
defined in (
19
). The
Q
-function is then represented by

\displaystyle Q_{\rho}(\bm{\alpha})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\bm{\Sigma_{Q}})}}e^{-\left(\bm{\alpha}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}\,\right)^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma_{Q}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\alpha}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}\,\right)/2},

(25)

where
\bm{\Sigma_{Q}}=\bm{\Sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}
. The
P
-representation of the Fock state
|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|
, on the other hand, is singular, but can be represented using Dirac delta functions, namely,

\displaystyle P_{n}(\bm{\alpha})=\frac{e^{|\alpha|^{2}}}{n!}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha\,\partial\alpha^{*}}\right)^{n}\delta(\alpha)\,\delta(\alpha^{*}).

(26)

If we plug Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
) into Eq. (
24
), we can show that the probabilities are expressed in terms of a generating function
20
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}=\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha\partial\alpha^{*}}\right)^{n}\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\right|_{\bm{\alpha}=0},

(27)

where the generating function is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma_{Q}}^{-1}\bar{\bm{a}}}}{n!\sqrt{\det(\bm{\Sigma_{Q}})}}\exp\left[{\frac{1}{2}\bm{\alpha}^{\text{T}}\bm{A}\bm{\alpha}+\bm{F}}\bm{\alpha}\right],

(28)

and where we defined

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\left(\mathds{1}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{Q}^{-1}\right),\quad\bm{F}=\bar{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{Q}^{-1}.

(29)

We note that the probabilities (
27
) can also be expressed in terms of the so-called loop Hafnian
20
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{Q}^{-1}\bar{\bm{a}}}}{n!\sqrt{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{Q})}}\ell\text{haf}(\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}),

(30)

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}
is matrix constructed from
\bm{A}^{(n)}=\bm{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{n}
by replacing the diagonal entries with the
2n
-dimensional column vector
\bm{F}^{(n)}
obtained repeating
n
times the components of
\bm{F}=\bar{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{Q}^{-1}
, and
\ell\text{haf}
is the loop hafnian, defined as

\displaystyle\ell\text{haf}(B)=\sum_{p\in\text{P}^{1,2}_{2k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in p}B_{ij}\,,

(31)

B
being a
2k\times 2k
symetric matrix, and
\text{P}^{1,2}_{2k}
being the set of partitions of size 1 and 2 of
2k
elements
3
.

III.2
Graviton statistics of Gaussian gravitational wave states

We now use the formalism outlined in the previous section to determine the transition probabilities
0\to n
induced by the gravitational wave on the detector system. In the creation and annihilation operators representation, the reduced state of the bar resonator at time
t
after having resonantly interacted with the incident Gaussian gravitational radiation is given by

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{bar}}(t)=\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{bar}}(0)+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{grav}}(0),\\
\bar{\bm{a}}_{\text{bar}}(t)=\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\bar{\bm{a}}_{\text{grav}}(0).\end{gathered}

From Eq. (
30

is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)=\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{a}}_{\text{bar}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{bar}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{2})^{-1}\bar{\bm{a}}_{\text{bar}}\right]}{\sqrt{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{bar}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{2})}},

(34)

while for the transition
0\rightarrow 1
we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{1}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)\left[\mathfrak{a}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{1}\mathfrak{f}_{2}\right]

(35)

and, similarly, for the transition
0\to 2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)\left[\left(\mathfrak{a}_{2}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(\mathfrak{a}_{2}+\mathfrak{f}_{1}^{2}\right)+4\mathfrak{f}_{1}\mathfrak{f}_{2}\mathfrak{a}_{1}+2\,\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2}\right],

where
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
,

,
\mathfrak{f}_{1}
, and

\bar{n}
, and the relative phase between the displacement and squeezing phases. Without loss of generality, we choose the relative phase so that it coincides with the squeezing angle
\theta
. Explicitly, in the case where

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)=\frac{2\exp\left\{-\frac{4|\alpha|^{2}e^{2r}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)}{2(2\bar{n}+1)\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+e^{2r}[\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+3]}\right\}}{\sqrt{(2\bar{n}+1)\cosh(2r)\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)[\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+3]+(2\bar{n}+1)^{2}\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+\left[\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+1\right]^{2}}},\\
\mathbb{P}_{1}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)\!\left\{\!1\!-\!\frac{2}{2(2\bar{n}+1)e^{2r}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+3}\!-\!\frac{4(2\bar{n}+1)e^{2r}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+2e^{4r}\left[\left(4|\alpha|^{2}+1\right)\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+3-4|\alpha|^{2}\right]}{\left[2(2\bar{n}+1)\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+e^{2r}(\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+3)\right]^{2}}\!\right\}.\end{gathered}

estimate that the number of squeezed quanta from compact binary mergers as detected on Earth could be relatively large, on the order of
n_{\text{q}}/n_{\text{grav}}\sim 0.01
.

While the above single-phonon excitation is well suited for
n_{\text{grav}}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)^{2}\approx 1
, i.e., a detector that mostly measures single graviton clicks, one can similarly derive the detection probabilities for better detectors that absorb more than one graviton, using Eq. (
30
)—see also Fig.

IV
Second-order correlations

In quantum optics, the statistics of a light field can be characterized by second-order correlation functions that measure correlations between intensities
22
. These tools are also applicable to the statistics of gravitational radiation. In this section, we will be interested in the zero-time-delay
g^{(2)}(0)
function, defined as

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(0)=\frac{\langle\hat{n}^{2}-\hat{n}\rangle}{\langle\hat{n}\rangle^{2}}.

(43)

We can use the same formalism detailed in the previous section for determining excitation probabilities of Gaussian states to determine the expectation values needed to compute the second-order coherence (
43
). In particular, the
s
-ordered moments of an arbitrary state
\rho
can be expressed in terms of derivatives of the
s
-ordered characteristic function
\chi_{s}(\bm{\alpha})=e^{s|\alpha|^{2}/4}\text{tr}[\rho\hat{D}_{\bm{\alpha}}]
, namely
2
;
8

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger n}\,\hat{a}^{m}\rangle_{s}=\left.\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\right)^{n}\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right)^{m}\chi_{s}(\bm{\alpha})\right|_{\bm{\alpha}=0}.

(44)

Here,
s=1
corresponds to normal ordering,
s=0
corresponds to symmetric ordering, and
s=-1
corresponds to ant-normal ordering. For a Gaussian state with covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
and displacement
\bar{\bm{a}}
, the characteristic function is given by

\displaystyle\chi_{s}(\bm{\alpha})=\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\bm{Z}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{s}\bm{Z}\,\bm{\alpha}+\bar{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\bm{Z}\,\bm{\alpha}}\right),

(45)

where we defined

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{s}=\bm{\Sigma}-\frac{s}{2}\mathds{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(46)

Notice that
\bm{\Sigma}_{Q}=\bm{\Sigma}_{-1}
. Indeed, the
Q
-function (
25
) is related to the anti-normal ordered characteristic function by a Fourier transform. As for the excitation probabilities discussed above, it is possible to relate the
s
-ordered moments to the loop-Hafnian of an appropriately defined matrix—see Ref.
8
.

IV.1
Measuring graviton counting statistics

We consider the case in which the initial joint state of the gravitational wave and detector system is
\rho=\rho_{\text{grav}}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{\text{bar}}
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{n}_{\text{bar}}(t)\rangle=\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle,

(47)

and similarly

\displaystyle\langle\hat{n}_{\text{bar}}^{2}(t)\rangle=\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle\hat{n}^{2}_{\text{grav}}\rangle+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle.

(48)

Consequently, for the
g^{(2)}(0)
function we find

\displaystyle g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\hat{n}^{2}_{\text{grav}}\rangle-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle}{\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle^{2}}\equiv g_{\text{grav}}^{(2)}(0),

(49)

which is valid for arbitrary states of the gravitational field
\rho_{\text{grav}}
, and for
\sin(\gamma_{g}t)\neq 0
.
Remarkably, we see that the
g^{(2)}(0)
correlation function is directly transferred from the GW onto the detector, and it does not depend on the coupling strength
\gamma_{\text{g}}
, regardless of the quantum state of the gravitational wave. We will comment on this behavior below. This is in contrast to other metrics of non-classicality, such as the Mandel
Q
parameter, which in our case takes the form
Q_{\text{bar}}=\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle\big(g_{\text{grav}}^{(2)}(0)-1\big)

\gamma_{\text{g}}t
, whereas the full
g^{(2)}(0)
does not.

The measurement of the second-order correlations (

g_{\text{grav}}^{(2)}(0)

IV.2
Renormalization of the vacuum state second-order correlation function and thermal noise

The apparent interaction-independence of the
g^{(2)}(0)
function observed above is a consequence of the way in which the
g^{(2)}(0)
function is normalized and the fact that the initial state of the detector mode is the vacuum.
In fact, the second-order coherence as defined by Eq. (
43
) is ill-defined in the case of a vacuum state, since
g^{(2)}(0)=0/0
. It is possible to attribute a limiting value if the vacuum state is defined as a limit of a general Gaussian state, but such a limit would not define a unique value for the correlation function. For example, we can equally view the vacuum state as a thermal state where the thermal occupation number approaches zero, that is,
|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{\text{bar}}=\lim_{n_{\text{th}}\to 0}\rho_{n_{\text{th}}}
, or as a coherent state where
\alpha\to 0
, namely
|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{\text{bar}}=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}|{\alpha}\rangle\!\langle{\alpha}|
. In the first case, the vacuum limit yields the correlation function
g^{(2)}(0)=2
, while the latter yields
g^{(2)}(0)=1
.

In light of this, the initial value of the second-order correlation function should be consistent with the state preparation process that brings the detector to its ground state. Physically, for the detectors envisioned here, this corresponds to the ground-state cooling of the mechanical mode of the bar resonator. To illustrate the last point, let us consider the case in which the detector is initially in a thermal state with thermal occupation number
n_{\text{th}}

(52)

V
State tomography through phase sensitive measurements

We now show that we can also go beyond counting statistics of the GWs and obtain full tomography of Gaussian states. When the detector is fixed to a reference state (as in our discussion so far, where it was assumed to be initially in the ground state), the second-order correlation function (
50
) contains only partial tomographic information of the Gaussian signal. To completely determine the Gaussian state and ultimately achieve full tomography, it is necessary to access the phase of the incoming gravitational wave signal
24
;
23
. In other words, if we define the generalized gravitational wave quadrature operator

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{\phi}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[e^{-i\phi}\hat{a}(t)+e^{i\phi}\hat{a}^{\dagger}(t)\right],

(53)

our goal is to determine the value of the quadrature for different choices of
\phi
; this can be accomplished by preparing the state of the detector in a reference state with a known phase. By varying the reference phase and recording the corresponding response of the detector, different quadratures can be accessed.

V.1
Homodyne intensity correlation measurement scheme

For optical signals, the standard phase-sensitive measurement schemes are Homodyne or Heterodyne measurements. Here, we consider the so-called homodyne intensity correlation scheme
43
. The strength of this method lies in the fact that, by using correlation measurements, detector inefficiencies only appear as multiplicative factors that don’t spoil the signal
43
. This is unlike standard homodyne measurement schemes. The scheme consists of measuring the intensity correlations of a signal that has been superimposed on a beamsplitter with a reference local oscillator. In our case, the signal is the GW, and the mixing of the signal to the local oscillator happens through the gravitational interaction with the bar detector. We consider then the unnormalized time-delayed second-order coherence of the detector, given by

\displaystyle G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}(\tau)=\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{b}^{\dagger}(t+\tau)\hat{b}(t+\tau)\hat{b}(t)\rangle.

(54)

Recalling the beamsplitter relations in Eq. (
II
), and assuming that the resonator is in a coherent state of amplitude
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, we see that the correlation function (
54
) is composed of five terms each of order
|\beta|^{k}
, with
k\in\{0,\dots,4\}
. That is,
G^{(2)}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{4}G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},k}(\tau)
. As pointed out in Ref.
43
, not all of the contributions are detectable in correlation measurements. We are therefore interested in separating the different contributions. To that end, it is convenient to compare the short- and long-time delay correlations, since physical correlation functions tend to de-correlate for long time delays. Thus, we consider the quantity

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}=G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}(0)-\lim_{\tau\to\infty}G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}(\tau).

(55)

Given the weakness of the coupling of the GW modes to the resonator, i.e.,
\gamma_{\text{g}}t\ll 1
, we have
G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},4}(0)\approx\lim_{\tau\to\infty}G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},4}(\tau)
, which implies
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},4}\approx 0
. We are then left with four contributions. In particular, we find the zeroth-order term

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},0}=\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[\langle:\!\hat{n}^{2}_{\text{grav}}(t)\!:\rangle-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(\infty)\rangle\right],

(56)

the first-order term

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},1}=&\sin^{3}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)|\beta|\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[2\langle:\!\hat{h}_{\phi}(t)\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(t)\!:\rangle\right.\\
&-\left.\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(\infty)\rangle-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(\infty)\rangle\right],\end{split}

(57)

the second-order term

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},2}=&\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)|\beta|^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[\langle:\!\hat{h}^{2}_{\phi}(t)\!:\rangle\right.\\
&\left.-\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(\infty)\rangle+\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(t)\rangle-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}(\infty)\rangle\right],\end{split}

(58)

and, finally, the third-order contribution

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},3}=

\displaystyle\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)|\beta|^{3}\cos^{3}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(t)\rangle-\langle\hat{h}_{\phi}(\infty)\rangle\right].

(59)

Gravitational waves are generally not stationary signals. However, we can assume stationarity of the signal around the resonance frequency in the detection window
\Delta t
. In that case, the only remaining contributions are

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},0}=\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle:\!(\Delta\hat{n}_{\text{grav}})^{2}\!:\rangle,

(60)

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},1}=\sin^{3}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)|\beta|\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle:\!\Delta\hat{h}_{\phi}\,\Delta\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\!:\rangle,

(61)

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},2}=\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,|\beta|^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle:\!(\Delta\hat{h}_{\phi})^{2}\!:\rangle,

(62)

where we defined the deviation from the mean of an operator
\hat{A}
as
\Delta\hat{A}=\hat{A}-\langle\hat{A}\rangle
, and
:\!\hat{\Pi}\!:
stands for the normal-ordering of products
\hat{\Pi}
of creation and annihilation operators. Now, supposing
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}
has been measured, we need further criteria to separate the remaining terms in Eqs. (
60
)–(
62
).

V.2
Physical interpretation and separation of effects

The zeroth-order contribution in Eq. (
60
)—or equivalently, Eq. (
56
) for the non-static case—corresponds to the normal-ordered intensity fluctuation correlations of the gravitational field. This term can be independently measured in the limit
|\beta|\to 0
. In fact, up to normalization, Eq. (
60
) corresponds precisely to the zero-time delay second-order autocorrelation function in Eq. (
49
) discussed in the previous section.

The first-order term
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},1}
in Eq. (
61
) contains critical information about the GW state. It represents the normal-ordered correlation between the quadrature and intensity fluctuations of the field. This term exactly vanishes for many types of radiation, like Fock states, coherent states, squeezed states, or thermal states—see App.
F
. In contrast, it is non-vanishing for displaced squeezed and displaced thermal states, the states of interest here. It thus provides a means to discern a simple, coherent state GW input from a more general state, and is obtained from the measurement scheme described above.

Finally, the second-order term
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},2}
in Eq. (
62
) contains the normal-ordered quadrature fluctuations of the gravitational field. For Gaussian states, the measurement of this term allows for full state tomography. To discriminate between this contribution and the others, one can notice the different periodicity with respect to the phase
\phi
, which enables one to isolate this term in a practical experiment, by varying the phonon state
\left|\beta\right\rangle
. Thus, absorption measurements alone are sufficient to obtain full information about the passing GW state, exploiting the ability to prepare different input states of the bar and perform counting statistics at different times.

We now discuss a concrete example. For GWs whose average number of non-coherent excitations
n_{\text{q}}
is comparable to the total average occupation number
n_{\text{grav}}
, the three terms (
60
)–(
62
) are all of order unity, and can therefore be detected in this measurement scheme. For example, for a displaced squeezed gravitational wave (
\bar{n}=0
) with the relative phase
\theta=0
, starting from the expressions derived in App.
F
, and considering the limit of large squeezing
r\gg 1
, we find

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},1}\approx\frac{1}{2}|\beta|\sin^{3}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\cos(\phi)|\alpha|e^{2r},

(63)

for the first-order term, and

\displaystyle\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},2}\approx\frac{1}{2}|\beta|^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\sin^{2}(\phi)e^{2r},

(64)

for the second-order term. This shows, explicitly, the different phase dependence of the two terms. Furthermore, depending on the quadrature measured, i.e., on the choice of
\phi
, the term (
63
) can acquire negative values. In general, this could lead to an overall sub-Poisson statistic. However, in this particular case, the addition of the zeroth order term, which for
r\gg 1
is
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar},2}\simeq\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)e^{4r}
, ensures that the overall
\Delta G^{(2)}_{\text{bar}}
is consistent with super-Poissonian counting statistics, as should be the case for Gaussian states. For more exotic GW states (if these exist), this need not be the case, and the first-order term could contribute to signatures of sub-Poissonian statistics.

V.3
Limits to quantum state tomography

While we have shown above that quantum state tomography can be achieved for Gaussian GW signals, it will have practical limitations. In particular, there will be an experimental limit on how well
G^{(2)}(\tau)
can be determined, due to imperfections, finite timing, detector noise, and other deleterious sources. This will limit the ability to determine the states of the GW. If we denote the imprecision by
\delta_{\textrm{exp}}=|G^{(2)}-G^{(2)}_{\text{exp}}|
, then our method can determine GW states with quadrature variances of order
\langle(\Delta h_{\phi})^{2}\rangle\gtrsim\delta_{\text{exp}}/(\gamma_{\text{g}}^{2}t^{2}\,|\beta|^{2})
, where
\beta
is the local oscillator amplitude, in this case, the initial state in the bar detector. Thus, in practice, the ability to discern arbitrary GW states will be limited, and it is best achieved with a strong acoustic drive and for long times, i.e., for continuous sources, such as ring-downs of neutron star mergers
21
;
41
or pulsars
16
;
39
. Recently, an acoustic superfluid resonator was excited to beyond
|\beta|^{2}>3\times 10^{4}
and its
g^{(2)}(0)
-function measured
33
, demonstrating the feasibility of such experiments.

However, as first noted in Ref.
29
, in the homodyne correlation measurement scheme, one is also limited by classical noise coming from amplitude fluctuations in the local oscillator. This is because such noise is not balanced out like in traditional homodyne schemes. As a result, there is a trade-off between signal amplification on the one hand and noise on the other. To illustrate this, let us consider a simple analysis of noise originating from classical Gaussian amplitude fluctuations of the detector. We denote the fluctuations around
\beta
by
\delta\beta(t)
and assume that the time averages of the fluctuations satisfy

\displaystyle\overline{\delta\beta(t)}=0,\quad\overline{\delta\beta(t)\beta(t+\tau)}=\overline{(\delta\beta)^{2}}e^{-\kappa\tau},

(65)

where
\tau>t>0
,
\kappa
is the correlation decay timescale of the fluctuations, and
\overline{(\delta\beta)^{2}}
is the variance of the fluctuations. Taking into account such classical noise, we can compute the second-order correlation (
54
) averaged over the fluctuations. Notably, in taking the difference (
55
), the leading order term
|\beta|^{4}
no longer cancels, rather, we find
43

\displaystyle\Delta\overline{G_{\text{bar},4}^{(2)}}\approx 4\cos^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)|\beta|^{2}\overline{(\delta\beta)^{2}},

(66)

where we assumed that
\overline{(\delta\beta)^{2}}/|\beta|^{2}=\varepsilon\ll 1
. If we are interested in the measurement of the quadrature variance from Eq. (
62
), by comparing with the leading order fluctuation term (
66
), we get the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\Delta G_{\text{bar},2}^{(2)}}{\Delta\overline{G_{\text{bar},4}^{(2)}}}\right|=\frac{\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle:\!(\Delta\hat{h}_{\phi})^{2}\!:\rangle}{4\,\varepsilon|\beta|^{2}}.

(67)

From Eq. (
67
), we see that if the amplitude of the detector is of the same order of magnitude as the quadrature variance of the signal, namely, if
|\beta|^{2}\sim\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\langle:\!\!(\Delta\hat{h}_{\phi})^{2}\!\!:\rangle
, the ratio (
67
) becomes of order
1/\varepsilon\gg 1
. This shows the trade-off explicitly; by increasing the amplitude
|\beta|
, the signal in Eq. (
62
) is boosted, but at the same time the SNR decreases. Nonetheless, the relation Eq. (
67
) also shows that the SNR can remain large for larger amplitudes
|\beta|
, if the ratio
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, giving leeway to boost weaker GW signals.

V.4
Weak coupling not a limitation

A key feature of the described approach to tomography is that access to the second-order correlations of the GW is enabled through intensity-correlations measurements, even when the graviton–matter coupling is extremely weak and detector efficiencies are very low. The use of correlation measurements for state reconstruction is well established in quantum optics due to their inherent robustness against detector inefficiencies
29
;
43
;
36
. We find that the same rationale applies to gravitational waves, making the detection of gravitational-wave statistics possible despite the weakness of the interaction.

Beyond the close analogy with quantum-optical detection methods, important differences arise in practical implementation. In standard optical homodyne detection, the local oscillator can typically be made arbitrarily strong, and excess noise can be suppressed through careful power balancing, leading to precise quadrature measurements. In contrast, the effective local oscillator in our scheme is realized by a driven mechanical mode of the detector, whose amplitude cannot be increased arbitrarily without introducing excess amplitude noise. As discussed above, this noise limits the achievable signal-to-noise ratio by introducing additional spurious correlations that cannot be balanced out. A further experimental challenge is the control of the phonon-mode phase, which is required to perform full Gaussian state tomography. We also highlight that the method requires measurements over a long time-period, meaning that either several detectors have to be used, or that continuous GW sources, such as pulsars
39
, are desirable.

These considerations imply that progress in detecting the quantum statistics of gravitational waves, through the methods described in this manuscript, will be driven primarily by improvements in detector coherence, thermal occupation, and classical noise suppression, circumventing the weak graviton–to-matter coupling and without the need for near-unit detector efficiency.

VI
Conclusions

Our work establishes a bridge between quantum optics and gravitational-wave physics at the level of quantum measurement theory. We show that graviton detection can serve as a practical tool for extracting the quantum statistics of gravitational waves and determining their state as well as their potential quantum features. Graviton detection was only recently shown to become experimentally accessible in the near future
40
due to rapid advances in quantum acoustics of macroscopic resonators, and the ability to extract single quanta of energy from passing waves. Here, we derive the graviton-to-phonon conversion probabilities for general Gaussian gravitational waves and show that these probabilities allow for the discrimination of different Gaussian radiation states. In addition, we demonstrate that the second-order correlation function of the gravitational wave is directly transferred to the detector, irrespective of the weak gravitational coupling, enabling direct measurement of GW counting statistics. Building on this result, we present a scheme based on coherent acoustic driving with known reference phases that enables full tomography of Gaussian gravitational radiation states. Thus, in addition to tests of linearized quantum gravity
38
, graviton counting also opens new insights into the composition and sources of gravitational waves, and can potentially reveal non-classical features of the radiation. By showing how standard measurement tools of quantum optics can be adapted to the gravitational context, this work opens a pathway toward characterizing the quantum states of gravitational radiation and exploring quantum aspects of gravity using near-future quantum sensing technologies.

(71)

M

Appendix B
Heisenberg evolution

B.1
Evolution in the rotating wave approximation

If we perform the rotating wave approximation, we can write the full Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{\ell}=\hbar\omega_{\ell}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\hat{b}_{\ell}+\hbar\,\gamma_{\text{g}}\left(\hat{b}_{\ell}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{i\nu t}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\,\hat{a}\,e^{-i\nu t}\right).

(72)

We can very easily solve the dynamics in the Heisenberg picture. We are interested in determining the evolution of the operators

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\hat{b}_{\ell}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\hat{b}_{\ell}\right],\quad\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\hat{a}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\hat{a}\right]+\frac{\partial\,\hat{a}}{\partial\,t}.

(73)

We see that this determines a system of coupled differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\hat{b}}_{\ell}=-i\left(\omega_{\ell}\,\hat{b}_{\ell}+\gamma_{\text{g}}\,\hat{a}\right)\\
\dot{\hat{a}}=-i\left(\nu\,\hat{a}+\gamma_{\text{g}}\,\hat{b}_{\ell}\right).\end{cases}

(74)

The general solution to this system is of the form

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\ell}(t)=e^{-i\left(\omega_{\ell}+\nu\right)t/2}\left[\hat{a}(0)f(t)+\hat{b}(0)g_{-}(t)\right],\quad\hat{a}(t)=e^{-i\left(\omega_{\ell}+\nu\right)t/2}\left[\hat{b}(0)f(t)+\hat{a}(0)g_{+}(t)\right],

(75)

where

\displaystyle f(t)=e^{-it\lambda_{\text{g}}/2}\frac{\gamma_{\text{g}}\left(1-e^{it\lambda_{\text{g}}}\right)}{\lambda_{\text{g}}},\quad g_{\pm}(t)=e^{-it\lambda_{\text{g}}/2}\frac{\lambda_{\text{g}}+\left(\lambda_{\text{g}}-\Delta\right)e^{it\lambda_{\text{g}}}\pm\Delta}{2\lambda_{\text{g}}},

(76)

and where we defined
\lambda_{\text{g}}=\sqrt{4\gamma_{\text{g}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
, and the detuning
\Delta=\nu-\omega_{\ell}
. At resonance, i.e., when
\Delta=0
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\ell}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{\ell}t}\left[\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{b}_{\ell}(0)-i\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{a}(0)\right],\,\,\hat{a}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{\ell}t}\left[\cos(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{a}(0)-i\sin(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\,\hat{b}_{\ell}(0)\right].

(77)

Having solved the equations of motion for the ladder operators, we can compute the time evolution of any observable we might be interested in.

(82)

B.3
Warm-up: transfer of squeezing from a squeezed vacuum gravitational wave signal

To build an intuitive understanding of the dynamics determined by the Hamiltonian (
3
) in the rotating wave approximation, we consider the case where the incident gravitational wave is a squeezed vacuum state, and the local resonator (the detector) is in its ground state, that is,
|\Psi\rangle=|\xi\rangle_{\text{grav}}\otimes|0\rangle_{\text{bar}}
. Here,
|\xi\rangle=\hat{S}_{\xi}|0\rangle
is the single-mode squeezed vacuum state, with squeezing
\xi=re^{i\varphi}
, which in the Fock basis
\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
can be written as

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh(r)}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-i\varphi}\tanh r\right)^{n}\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}|2n\rangle.

(83)

Let us consider the dynamics in the resonant case, i.e.,
\Delta=0
. Having solved the Heisenberg equations of motion (
II
), we know the GW’s interaction with the local oscillator is a beam-splitter interaction. This means that any squeezing present in the gravitational wave state should, in principle, be transferred to the detector on a timescale dictated by the coupling
\gamma_{\text{g}}
. Let us try to estimate the magnitude of the squeezing transfer.

An arbitrary quadrature
\hat{q}
is said to be squeezed if
\Delta\hat{q}(t)<\Delta\hat{q}_{\text{zp}}
, where
\Delta\hat{q}_{\text{zp}}
is the zero point variance. Let us define the rotated quadrature

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\vartheta)=\hat{x}\cos(\vartheta)+\hat{p}\sin(\vartheta)

(84)

where

\displaystyle\hat{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}\right)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{p}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}\right).

(85)

We have dropped the
\ell
index since we are in the resonant case
\Delta=0
, and we have normalized each quadrature so that the zero point expectation values are
\Delta x_{\text{zp}}=\Delta p_{\text{zp}}=\Delta q_{\text{zp}}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Given that the oscillator state is the vacuum state and the gravitational wave is a squeezed vacuum state,
\langle q(\vartheta)\rangle=0
, and we have
\Delta q(\vartheta)=\sqrt{\langle q(\vartheta)^{2}\rangle}
. The expectation value we are after is given by

\displaystyle\langle q(\vartheta)^{2}\rangle=\langle x^{2}\rangle_{t}\cos^{2}(\vartheta)+\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}\sin^{2}(\vartheta)+\langle\{x,p\}\rangle_{t}\cos(\vartheta)\sin(\vartheta),

(86)

where
\{a,b\}=ab+ba
. Minimizing the expression above with respect to
\vartheta
, we find that the maximum amount of squeezing transferred to the Weber bar resonator at a given time is

\displaystyle\langle q(\vartheta_{\text{min}})^{2}\rangle=\frac{\langle x^{2}\rangle_{t}+\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{(\langle x^{2}\rangle_{t}\!-\!\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t})^{2}+(\langle xp\rangle_{t}\!+\!\langle px\rangle_{t})^{2}}}{2},

(87)

where
\vartheta_{\text{min}}
corresponds to the angle

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\text{min}}=\tan^{-1}\left[\!\frac{\langle xp\rangle+\langle px\rangle}{\langle x^{2}\rangle\!-\!\langle p^{2}\rangle\!-\!\sqrt{(\langle x^{2}\rangle-\langle p^{2}\rangle)^{2}+(\langle xp\rangle+\langle px\rangle)^{2}}}\!\right].

(88)

Therefore, using the time-dependent operators derived in the appendix
B.1
, we find that

\displaystyle\langle q(\vartheta_{\text{min}})^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left(e^{-2r}-1\right)\right].

(89)

As expected, for
t=0
, no squeezing is present in the resonator. As
t
increases, so does the squeezing of the quadrature
\hat{q}(\vartheta_{\text{min}})
, until it reaches its maximum at
t_{\text{swap}}=\pi/(2\gamma_{\text{g}})
for which all the squeezing present in the gravitational wave is transferred to the resonator, i.e.,
\Delta q(\vartheta_{\text{min}})=e^{-r}/\sqrt{2}
. Given the very small value of the coupling
\gamma_{\text{g}}
, the timescale
t_{\text{swap}}
is several times the age of the universe. Due to the Heisenberg uncertainty relations, the quadrature
\pi
-rotated with respect to
\vartheta_{\text{min}}
will be maximally anti-squeezed, namely

\displaystyle\langle q(\vartheta_{\text{max}})^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left(e^{2r}-1\right)\right],

(90)

where
\vartheta_{\text{max}}=\vartheta_{\text{min}}+\pi
. For gravitational waves detectable by LIGO, the quantity
\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)e^{2r}
can be of order unity, given the very high graviton occupation number
\langle\hat{n}_{\text{grav}}\rangle\simeq 10^{35}
—see Eq. (
39
).

Appendix C
Unitary evolution of Gaussian moments

For Hamiltonians quadratic in the canonical operators, like the interaction Hamiltonian (
1

N

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{r}_{i},\hat{r}_{j}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{r}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{r}_{j}\rangle,\,\text{and}\quad\bar{r}_{i}=\langle\hat{r}_{i}\rangle,

(91)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{T}}H\hat{\bm{r}}+\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{T}}\bar{\bm{r}},

(92)

and the evolution of an initial Gaussian state specified by
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
and

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(t)=S\bm{\sigma}_{0}S^{\text{T}},\quad\bar{\bm{r}}(t)=S\bar{\bm{r}}_{0},

(93)

where

, and
\bm{\Omega}
is the
N

, meaning that

is a symplectic transformation.

C.2
Open diffusive dynamics of the detector mode

Above, we considered the unitary evolution of the joint gravitational wave and detector system. In practice, however, the resonant detector will be subject to environmental noise. \zeta\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, differentiating with respect to
\zeta
and
\zeta^{*}
, through Eq. (
44
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\bar{n}\right)\cosh(2r)-\frac{1}{2},

(120)

and

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\hat{a}^{2}\rangle\!=\!\frac{1}{8}\!\left[8|\alpha|^{2}\!\left(|\alpha|^{2}\!-2\right)\!-\!8|\alpha|^{2}(2\bar{n}+1)\!\cos(\theta)\sinh(2r)\!+\!8\!\left(2|\alpha|^{2}\!-\!1\!\right)\!(2\bar{n}+1)\!\cosh(2r)\!+\!3(2\bar{n}+1)^{2}\!\cosh(4r)\!+\!4\bar{n}(\bar{n}+1)\!+\!5\right].

E.1
Effect of detector thermal noise in the determination of the zero time-delay second-order correlations

To study how thermal noise affects the determination of the second-order coherence, we compute the
g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)
for a detector initially in a thermal state with average thermal occupation number
n_{\text{th}}
. From the general expressions of the time evolution determined in App.
C
, that is, from Eq. (
98
) and Eq. (
99
), we find that the state of the detector is specified by Eq. (
C.1
), except that now

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{\text{bar}}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}n_{\text{th}}+\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&n_{\text{th}}+\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(122)

Then, we can determine
g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)
exactly as above, yielding

\displaystyle g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)=\frac{\mathcal{I}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{I}_{2}(t)+\mathcal{I}_{3}(t)}{8\left[n_{\text{th}}+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left(n_{\text{grav}}-n_{\text{th}}\right)\right]^{2}},

(123)

where

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\mathcal{I}_{1}(t)=4(2\bar{n}+1)\cosh(2r)\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[2|\alpha|^{2}+\left(2n_{\text{th}}+1-2|\alpha|^{2}\right)\cos(2\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+2n_{\text{th}}-1\right],\\
\mathcal{I}_{2}(t)=\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left[8|\alpha|^{4}-8|\alpha|^{2}(2\bar{n}+1)\cos(\theta)\sinh(2r)+3(2\bar{n}+1)^{2}\cosh(4r)+(2\bar{n}+1)^{2}\right],\\
\mathcal{I}_{3}(t)=4\left[(2n_{\text{th}}+1)\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)-1\right]\left[(2n_{\text{th}}+1)\cos^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)+4|\alpha|^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)-1\right].\end{gathered}

In the limit
n_{\text{th}}\to 0
of Eq. (
123
), the time dependence drops and we recover the expression in Eq. (
49
). In the limit
\bar{n}\to 0
, which corresponds to a displaced squeezed gravitational wave, we find

\displaystyle g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)=2-\frac{\left[|\alpha|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2r)\right]^{2}\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)}{\left[n_{\text{th}}+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)(|\alpha|^{2}+\sinh^{2}r-n_{\text{th}})\right]^{2}}.

(127)

The calculation above reveals how imperfections in the state preparation of the detector affect our ability to learn the statistical properties of the gravitational signal. In general, however, we also have to account for noise during the measurement itself. Using the evolution determined by the Markovian diffusive model defined by Eq. (
C.2
), which for a detector initialized to its ground state corresponds to the covariance matrix and displacement vector defined in Eq. (
C.2
), we find that the second-order correlation function is

\displaystyle g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}(0)=2+\frac{\sin^{4}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\left\{(2\bar{n}+1)\sinh(2r)\left[(2\bar{n}+1)\sinh(2r)-4|\alpha|^{2}\right]-4|\alpha|^{4}\right\}}{2\left[\bar{N}(e^{\kappa t}-1)+n_{\text{grav}}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)\right]^{2}}.

(128)

As expected, in the limit
t\to\infty
of Eq. (
128
),
g_{\text{bar}}^{(2)}\to 2
. Assuming
\kappa=\omega/Q\ll 1
, where
Q
is the resonator’s quality factor, we arrive at the condition
k_{\text{B}}T\,t/\hbar Q=\Gamma_{\text{th}}\,t<n_{\text{grav}}(\gamma_{\text{g}}t)^{2}
, where
k_{\text{B}}
is Boltzmann’s constant,
T
the temperature of the thermal bath, and we used the approximation
\bar{N}\approx k_{\text{B}}T/\hbar Q
. This condition corresponds to the requirement that less than one environmental phonon enters the resonator throughout the duration of the experiment.
