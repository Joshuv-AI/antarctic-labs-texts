Effects of battle rope training on sporting performance: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yingying Cao, Yuanyuan Luo, Mohamad Nizam Nazarudin, Denise Koh, Shuairan Li, Peng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of battle rope training (BRT) on upper-limb explosive power, lower-limb explosive power, grip strength, upper-limb muscular endurance, and core endurance.MethodsThis study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. PubMed, Web of Science, Wiley Online Library, Scopus, EBSCO, and CNKI were systematically searched to identify controlled intervention studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.5.0. Effect sizes were expressed as Hedges’ g, a standardised mean difference, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pooled effects were estimated using a random-effects model. Subgroup analyses were used to preliminarily explore the potential effects of training status, type of control condition, and intervention duration on the effects of BRT interventions.ResultsA total of 21 studies involving 590 participants aged 11–25 years were included. Sex was reported for 553 participants (433 males, 120 females), whereas it was not reported for the remaining 37 participants. Compared with control conditions, BRT significantly improved upper-limb explosive power (Hedges’ g = 1.59, 95% CI: 0.77–2.42, p = 0.001), grip strength (g = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.30–1.67, p = 0.011), upper-limb muscular endurance (g = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.04–2.30, p = 0.044), and core endurance (g = 1.67, 95% CI: 0.30–3.03, p = 0.016). The pooled effect of BRT on lower-limb explosive power did not reach statistical significance (g = 1.17, 95% CI: -0.21–2.55, p = 0.088). Subgroup analyses indicated that training status may moderate the effect of BRT on upper-limb explosive power. Training status and type of control condition may influence effects on upper-limb muscular endurance, while type of control condition may also influence effects on core endurance.ConclusionBRT may improve upper-limb explosive power, grip strength, upper-limb muscular endurance, and core muscular endurance, whereas its effect on lower-limb explosive power remains uncertain. Given the substantial between-study heterogeneity and the exploratory nature of the subgroup analyses, the robustness of these findings requires further confirmation through additional research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01970-9.Keywords: Battle rope training, Explosive power, Grip strength, Muscular endurance, Core endurance

Introduction

Battle rope training (BRT) is a physical training method that involves swinging a thick, heavy rope synchronously or alternately with both arms to create continuous or intermittent rope waves. The training load can be adjusted through rope specifications, movement performance, swing frequency, range of motion, and exercise-recovery time structure [8, 58]. During training, the upper limbs repeatedly and rapidly exert force,the hands and forearms continuously grip and control the rope, while the trunk and lower limbs contribute to postural stability and force transfer [10, 40]. Given these movement characteristics, BRT may affect multiple aspects of physical performance, including upper-limb strength, grip strength, upper-limb and core muscle endurance, and trunk stability. Due to its low venue requirements, diverse movement forms, and easy adjustment of training load, BRT has been increasingly applied to general physical training, school sports, and athlete-specific physical training [24, 40].

Acute studies have shown that a single BRT session can significantly increase heart rate, oxygen uptake, and energy expenditure, and that different movement patterns and body postures can lead to distinct muscle recruitment patterns [9, 10, 26]. These results indicate that BRT can simultaneously impose a high cardiopulmonary metabolic load and neuromuscular stimulation in a short period of time, providing a physiological basis for its potential long-term physical adaptation. However, acute indicators only reflect immediate responses during or after a single training session and are mainly used to describe the load characteristics of BRT,they are difficult to use to infer stable and sustained physical adaptation from long-term training.

Long-term intervention studies have shown that several weeks of BRT may influence participants' physical performance, including upper- and lower-limb strength, explosive power, upper-limb and core muscle endurance, and left- and right-hand grip strength. BRT may also improve agility, cardiorespiratory endurance, and body composition. Furthermore, some studies have reported improvements in specific sport performance [8, 12, 56, 66]. However, these studies generally had small sample sizes, and participants varied in age, training background, and sport. Training protocols also lacked consistency in rope specifications, movement patterns, training intensity, training frequency, and intervention duration, limiting direct comparisons between research results.

Based on the characteristics of BRT movements and the reporting of outcome indicators in existing studies, upper- and lower-limb explosive power, left- and right-hand grip strength, upper-limb muscle endurance, and core muscle endurance are directly related to the main movement requirements of BRT and are also outcome indicators with a relatively large number of reports and comparable data. Upper- and lower-limb explosive power reflects the body's ability to generate force rapidly over a short period; left- and right-hand grip strength reflects the ability of the hand and forearm muscle groups to generate grip force and to maintain continuous control of the rope; upper-limb muscle endurance reflects the participant's ability to repeatedly complete rope-wave movements; and core muscle endurance reflects the body's ability to maintain trunk stability and complete force transfer under continuous perturbation conditions [16, 40, 59]. In contrast, aerobic endurance, agility, body composition, and some specific sports abilities have fewer reports, and related studies vary greatly in terms of measurement tools, indicator definitions, and data reporting methods, making it difficult to form sufficiently comparable quantitative evidence.

Therefore, this study employs a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the overall effects of BRT on the primary reported outcomes—upper- and lower-limb explosive power, left- and right-hand grip strength, upper-limb muscle endurance, and core muscle endurance. An exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to assess whether effect estimates varied by potential moderators, including participants' training status, control conditions, and intervention duration. This study aims to determine the overall effect of BRT across physical performance dimensions, evaluate the consistency and applicability of existing evidence, and explore potential sources of discrepancies in research findings, thereby providing a basis for the rational application of BRT in sports training and school physical education, as well as for the design and optimisation of subsequent training programmes.

Materials and methods

The design, conduct, and reporting of this systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [50]. The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under registration number CRD420251122117.

Literature search

An updated systematic literature search was conducted on 29 July 2026. The databases searched were PubMed, Web of Science, Wiley Online Library, Scopus, EBSCOhost, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). Before conducting the formal search, the research team reviewed the titles, abstracts, keywords, and indexing terms of relevant studies to identify alternative expressions for BRT. To maximise search sensitivity, the revised strategy focused on intervention-related terms without imposing an outcome-based search restriction. The core search terms were: (“battle rope*” OR “battling rope*” OR “undulating rope*” OR “undulation rope*” OR “heavy rope training” OR “heavy rope exercise*” OR “conditioning rope training” OR “rope wave training” OR “rope slam training” OR “rope training”). The syntax and searchable fields were adapted to the functions of each database, with searches restricted to titles, abstracts, and keywords where supported. No language restrictions were applied. In addition, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify potentially eligible records. The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study used the PICO framework to formulate the inclusion and exclusion criteria, covering participants, interventions, comparator conditions, and outcomes. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants were healthy adolescents or young adults, including athletes and non-athletes, with no restrictions on sex or training level; (2) BRT was the primary intervention; (3) a comparable control group that did not receive BRT was included, such as usual daily activities, regular physical education classes, sport-specific training, traditional resistance training, functional training, or other active control conditions; (4) a randomised or quasi-randomised controlled intervention design was used; and (5) in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook, different tests were classified within the same outcome domain only when they reflected the same construct at the conceptual level and higher values consistently represented better physical performance. The influence of differences in measurement methods was examined through subgroup analyses by test type or sensitivity analyses [31, 46].

The same construct of upper-limb explosive power included tests of throwing distance and throwing velocity. Both types of measures are derived from rapid, ballistic upper-limb throwing movements and reflect the ability of the upper limbs to generate force rapidly over a short period and transfer that force to an external object. Previous studies have shown that both the distance and velocity of a seated medicine-ball throw can serve as reliable indicators of upper-limb explosive power [29, 64].

The same construct of lower-limb explosive power included the vertical jump, standing long jump, and standing triple jump tests. All three tests require the lower-limb extensor muscles to generate a large amount of force rapidly over a short period. Previous studies have shown that the vertical jump, standing long jump, and standing triple jump have high loadings on the lower-limb explosive-power factor [11, 28, 44].

The same construct of handgrip strength included handgrip tests of the dominant and non-dominant hands. Both measures use a handgrip dynamometer to assess the ability of the hand and forearm muscles to produce maximal isometric gripping force [34].

The same construct of upper-limb muscular endurance included push-up, pull-up, flexed-arm hang, and repeated-load barbell bench-press tests. These tests require the muscles of the upper limbs and shoulder girdle to produce force continuously or repeatedly until a predefined test endpoint is reached, the participant can no longer maintain the required technique, or the participant voluntarily stops. Previous studies have shown that push-ups, pull-ups, repeated-load barbell bench presses, and flexed-arm hangs assess the strength and endurance of the upper-limb and shoulder-girdle musculature [15, 47, 60].

The same construct of core endurance included plank and bridge tests. Both types of tests require participants to maintain a prescribed trunk and pelvic position against gravity until they can no longer continue because of fatigue or are unable to maintain the required technique. These tests primarily reflect the ability of the abdominal, back, hip, and pelvic stabilising muscles to sustain isometric contractions and resist fatigue. Previous studies have shown that the prone bridge or plank test can provide a valid and reliable assessment of core muscle fatigue and trunk muscle performance. The prone bridge and unilateral supine bridge tests have also demonstrated good-to-excellent test–retest reliability for core endurance [7, 17].

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate publications or studies using overlapping data; (2) non-empirical studies, including reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, conference abstracts, and letters; (3) studies without a control group, including cross-sectional studies, single-group pre–post studies, and descriptive studies; (4) studies in which BRT was not the primary intervention; (5) studies providing an insufficient description of the BRT intervention protocol, such as failing to report the intervention duration, training frequency, duration of each session, or principal battle rope exercises; and (6) studies in which the intervention was not fully implemented or the available data were insufficient to calculate an effect size.

Data extraction and coding

Study screening was conducted independently by two reviewers. After duplicate records were removed, titles and abstracts were screened to exclude studies that clearly did not involve BRT or the predefined outcomes. Potentially eligible studies were then assessed in full text, and final decisions were made in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer made the final decision.

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardised data extraction form. The extracted information included: (1) study characteristics, such as first author, year of publication, country or region, and study design; (2) participant characteristics, including sample size, age, sex, training background, sport type, and baseline training level; (3) intervention characteristics, including intervention duration, weekly training frequency, session duration, battle rope exercise mode, rope length, rope diameter and weight, work–rest structure, intensity monitoring method, load progression strategy, and any combined training content; (4) control conditions, including passive control, usual daily activities, regular sport-specific training, traditional resistance training, or other active control protocols; and (5) outcome characteristics, including the type of physical performance outcome, assessment tool, testing procedure, and pre- and post-intervention means and standard deviations.

For continuous outcomes, means and standard deviations reported in the original studies were preferentially extracted. If these were not reported directly, they were calculated from the available pre- and post-intervention data. When required for meta-analysis, the necessary data were also derived from statistical information such as p values, confidence intervals, standard errors, or t statistics [31]. If results were presented only in graphical form, the data were digitised using GetData Graph Digitizer 2.26 [20]. For studies with missing key data, the research team contacted the corresponding authors by email. If the required data could not be obtained after contact, the study was excluded from the quantitative analysis of the corresponding outcome.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

This study used the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (Risk of Bias 2, RoB 2) as the primary instrument for assessing the internal risk of bias of the included studies [6]. RoB 2 comprises five domains: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was judged as having a low risk of bias, some concerns, or a high risk of bias, and these domain-level judgements were used to derive an overall risk-of-bias judgement for each study.

Assessment of the randomisation process considered random sequence generation, allocation concealment, and baseline differences between groups. When a study reported only that participants were “randomly allocated” but did not adequately describe the method used to generate the random sequence or the allocation-concealment procedure, this domain was judged as having some concerns. Studies using predictable quasi-random allocation methods were judged as having a high risk of bias in this domain.

The PEDro scale was used as a supplementary item-level assessment tool to describe specific features of exercise intervention studies relating to trial design, implementation, and outcome reporting [18]. The scale contains 11 items. Item 1 assesses eligibility criteria but is not included in the total score. The remaining 10 items assess random allocation, concealed allocation, baseline comparability, blinding of participants, blinding of intervention providers, blinding of assessors, completeness of follow-up, intention-to-treat analysis, between-group comparisons, and the reporting of point estimates and measures of variability.

Given that summary quality scores may obscure the specific methodological limitations of individual studies, this review primarily presented the item-level PEDro assessments and did not classify studies as being of high, moderate, or low quality on the basis of their total scores. The domain-based RoB 2 judgements served as the primary basis for interpreting the internal validity of the studies and the credibility of the pooled effects, whereas the PEDro findings were used to identify recurring methodological limitations relating to allocation concealment, blinding, completeness of follow-up, and intention-to-treat analysis.

Statistical method

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0). Because the included studies used different assessment tools and testing procedures to evaluate similar physical performance constructs, the standardised mean difference was used as the effect-size metric and expressed as Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given that some included studies had small sample sizes, Hedges’ g was used to correct for small-sample bias. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies in participant characteristics, training protocols, control conditions, and outcome measurement methods, a random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled effect size. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I2 statistic, and τ2. Where applicable, prediction intervals were also reported to indicate the possible range of true effects that may be observed in future similar studies.

When sufficient data were available for a given outcome, and each subgroup contained at least two independent comparisons, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether participants’ training status, type of control condition, and intervention duration could explain between-study heterogeneity. Training status was classified according to the participant background reported in the original studies. Participants with continuous, systematic exercise training or sport-specific training experience were classified as trained, whereas healthy participants without reported systematic training experience were classified as untrained.

Control conditions were classified based on whether they included structured exercise or training content. Control groups that received regular sport-specific training, physical education classes, resistance training, or other planned exercise were classified as active controls, whereas those that maintained only usual daily activities or received no additional training were classified as passive controls. The intervention duration across the included studies ranged from 4 to 12 weeks, with a median of 8 weeks. Therefore, using 8 weeks as the cut-off point, intervention duration was classified as short-term (≤ 8 weeks) or longer-term (> 8 weeks). Given the limited number of studies included in some subgroups, the subgroup analysis results should be regarded as exploratory findings rather than interpreted causally and should be interpreted with caution. When at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome, funnel plots were used to assess potential publication bias, and Egger’s test was further applied to evaluate funnel plot asymmetry.

Results

Literature search

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 793 records were initially identified through database searches. After duplicates were removed using EndNote X9, 154 records remained for title and abstract screening. Before consensus was reached, the percentage agreement between the two reviewers was 96.7%, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.895. After resolving disagreements, 125 records that did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria were excluded, and the remaining 29 articles underwent full-text assessment. For full-text screening, the percentage agreement was 93.1%, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.827. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. Eight articles were subsequently excluded for failing to meet the eligibility criteria. Finally, 21 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, comprising 13 English-language articles, 7 Chinese-language articles, and 1 Korean-language article. The overall agreement rate for data extraction was 97.3%.

Characteristics of the included studies

The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. A total of 21 studies involving 590 participants were included. Sex was reported for 553 participants, comprising 433 males and 120 females. Participants ranged in age from 11 to 25 years and were mainly secondary school students, university students, healthy young adults, military trainees, and athletes from various sports, including basketball, volleyball, tennis, golf, handball, baseball, rhythmic gymnastics, and discus. Most studies used randomised or quasi-randomised controlled intervention designs. The experimental groups used BRT as the primary intervention, whereas the control groups received usual physical activity, regular physical education classes, sport-specific training, traditional resistance training, shuttle-run training, or other routine training. Accordingly, the control conditions varied across studies and included both passive and active controls.

The BRT protocols differed substantially across studies, particularly in exercise movements, equipment parameters, load adjustment, and combined training content. The battle rope exercises used included double-arm waves, alternating waves, power slams, spiral waves, lateral waves, hip-driven waves, jump slams, and other combined battle rope exercises. Some studies also combined BRT with jumps, lunges, body-weight resistance exercises, sprint running, or sport-specific technical training. Rope length, diameter, and weight also varied across studies. Training intensity was monitored or controlled using methods such as heart rate, rating of perceived exertion, work–rest ratio, movement cadence, and progressive increases in training volume. Intervention duration ranged from 4 to 12 weeks, training frequency from 2 to 5 sessions per week, and session duration from 20 to 180 min.

The outcomes spanned multiple domains of physical performance, including upper-limb explosive power, lower-limb explosive power, left- and right-hand grip strength, upper-limb muscular endurance, and core endurance. However, assessment methods for comparable physical performance outcomes were inconsistent across studies. For example, upper-limb explosive power was assessed using dumbbell throws, chest passes, throwing distance, or throwing velocity, whereas core endurance was assessed using sit-ups, plank tests, prone bridge tests, side-bridge tests, or trunk endurance tests. Therefore, differences in intervention protocols and outcome measurement methods should be carefully considered when interpreting the pooled effects (Figs. 2 and 3).

Summary of risk of bias across included studies

Risk of bias assessment for individual studies

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane RoB 2 assessment showed that, for bias arising from the randomisation process, five studies (23.8%) were judged to be at low risk, whereas 16 studies (76.2%) raised some concerns, mainly because of insufficient reporting of random sequence generation or allocation concealment. Regarding bias due to deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, and outcome measurement, five, one, and three studies, respectively, were judged to be at high risk. Selective reporting was the most prominent concern, with nine studies (42.9%) rated as being at high risk. The supplementary PEDro assessment showed that scores ranged from 5 to 8. Most studies reported baseline comparability, between-group statistical comparisons, and point estimates with measures of variability; however, limitations remained regarding allocation concealment, blinding of participants and intervention providers, assessor blinding, and intention-to-treat analysis (see Supplementary Table S2). Overall, insufficient reporting of randomisation procedures, difficulties in implementing blinding, and the high risk of selective reporting reduced confidence in the stability and credibility of the pooled effects. The findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously in conjunction with the observed heterogeneity, confidence intervals, and prediction intervals.

Meta-analysis

Upper-limb explosive

The random-effects meta-analysis results for upper-limb explosive power are shown in Fig. 4. Compared with control conditions, BRT significantly improved upper-limb explosive power (Hedges’ g = 1.59, 95% CI: 0.77 to 2.42, p = 0.0014). However, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 85.8%, τ2 = 1.4166, χ2 = 77.67, p for heterogeneity < 0.0001), and the prediction interval ranged from −1.15 to 4.33. In the subgroup analysis by test type, the pooled effect size for throwing distance tests was Hedges’ g = 1.65 (95% CI: 0.43 to 2.87; I2 = 87.3%, τ2 = 1.8248, χ2 = 55.04, p for heterogeneity < 0.0001). For throwing velocity tests, the pooled effect size was Hedges’ g = 1.49 (95% CI: −0.31 to 3.29; I2 = 86.7%, τ2 = 1.1149, χ2 = 22.59, p for heterogeneity < 0.0001). However, this effect did not reach statistical significance because the 95% CI crossed zero. The test for subgroup differences indicated no statistically significant difference between throwing distance and throwing velocity tests (χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.8364).

Discussion

The findings of this meta-analysis suggest that BRT is associated with improvements in upper-limb explosive power, upper-limb muscular endurance, handgrip strength, and core endurance, whereas the evidence for an effect on lower-limb explosive power remains limited. Notably, relatively large pooled effect sizes were observed for upper-limb explosive power (Hedges’ g = 1.59, I2 = 85.8%), upper-limb muscular endurance (g = 1.17, I2 = 82.1%), and core endurance (g = 1.67, I2 = 88.5%); However, most outcomes showed substantial between-study heterogeneity, and their prediction intervals crossed zero, indicating that the above findings remain uncertain and require further verification through additional studies. Overall, the available evidence suggests that BRT may be a comprehensive training method with potential application value for improving multiple components of physical fitness.

Nevertheless, the RoB 2 assessment showed that some studies had a high risk of bias in the randomisation process and selective reporting of results. The item-level PEDro assessment further indicated recurring methodological limitations in allocation concealment, blinding, and intention-to-treat analysis. Meanwhile, sensitivity analyses based on the RoB 2 risk-of-bias assessment showed that the overall findings of this study did not change substantially after studies at high risk of bias were excluded, although the robustness of the evidence varied across outcomes. Therefore, the available evidence can only cautiously support the use of BRT as a supplementary training method for improving upper-limb and core physical performance. The magnitude and consistency of its effects still require further verification through studies with larger sample sizes, more rigorous research designs, and standardised outcome measures.

The meta-analysis results showed that BRT was associated with significant improvements in upper-limb explosive power. Alternating waves and double-arm slams are typical BRT exercises that require participants to rapidly overcome the mass and movement inertia of the ropes within a short period, thereby placing considerable demands on rapid force production by the upper limbs [10, 40]. From the perspective of neuromuscular adaptation, these rapid and repetitive muscle contractions may facilitate the recruitment of type II muscle fibres and increase the rate and synchronisation of motor-unit activation, all of which are closely associated with explosive-power performance [1, 16]. In addition, BRT involves not only the principal force-producing muscles of the shoulders and upper limbs but also the core musculature, which contributes to postural stability and force transmission [36]. Therefore, greater core stability may provide a stable base of support for rapid upper-limb movements and help improve the efficiency of force transmission through the kinetic chain. Subgroup analysis indicated that training experience may be a potential moderator of the effect of BRT on upper-limb explosive power, with individuals who had more extensive training experience potentially showing greater improvements. This may be related to the better neuromuscular control and motor coordination of trained individuals, which may enable them to translate the training stimulus generated by BRT more effectively into improvements in upper-limb explosive power [3, 62].

This study found that BRT was associated with a statistically significant improvement in upper-limb muscular endurance. This finding is consistent with the interval-training characteristics of BRT, although the included studies did not directly assess the physiological mechanisms underlying this association. In typical BRT protocols, participants generally perform prescribed exercises at a high intensity or near-maximal effort for a specified duration. For example, some protocols involve 30 s of continuous exercise followed by a brief rest period. Such repeated exercise-and-recovery cycles may place substantial demands on the cardiovascular system and skeletal muscle metabolism [8]. Previous long-term HIIT studies have shown that regular training may improve muscular oxidative capacity, buffering capacity, and lactate metabolism, thereby helping to delay fatigue and maintain muscular endurance performance [22, 43]. Therefore, the sustained and repeated upper-limb movements performed during BRT, particularly the repeated involvement of the shoulder and upper-arm muscles, may provide a training stimulus favourable for local muscular endurance adaptations [19]. Subgroup analysis indicated that the type of control condition may influence the effect of BRT on upper-limb muscular endurance. Compared with passive control conditions (i.e., no additional training), BRT may be more beneficial for improving upper-limb muscular endurance. However, compared with active control conditions (conventional training), BRT did not show a more apparent advantage. This may indicate that BRT provides an effective training stimulus relative to no additional training, whereas conventional training itself may also maintain or improve upper-limb muscular endurance, thereby reducing the effect of BRT relative to active control conditions [12].

Compared with upper-limb-related outcomes, the present meta-analysis could not confirm that BRT consistently improves lower-limb explosive power. Although the point estimate of the pooled effect was relatively large (Hedges’ g = 1.17), the 95% confidence interval was wide and crossed zero (− 0.21 to 2.55, p = 0.0875), between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 83.8%), and the prediction interval was similarly wide and crossed zero (− 2.83 to 5.18). These findings indicate that the estimated effect of BRT on lower-limb explosive power had limited precision and that the true effect may vary considerably across different study settings. From the perspective of training specificity, one possible explanation is that, in many BRT exercises, the lower limbs primarily contribute to postural stability and force transmission. Participants commonly maintain a semi-squat or lunge position, during which the lower-limb muscles may contract mainly isometrically or near-isometrically and receive relatively limited stretch–shortening cycle stimulation [10]. Although some BRT variations, such as jumping slams, involve explosive lower-limb movements, these exercises were not the principal training components in most of the included studies. Therefore, the training stimuli applied to lower-limb explosive power may have varied across BRT protocols. In contrast, plyometric training or high-load resistance training can provide more direct stimuli for developing rapid force-production capacity in the lower limbs [30, 45]. BRT may also indirectly influence lower-limb function by improving core stability and whole-body coordination. However, the included studies did not directly assess these mechanisms, and the current findings are insufficient to confirm that such indirect adaptations produce greater improvements in lower-limb explosive power relative to the control conditions [21]. Therefore, the current findings should not be interpreted as indicating that BRT is completely ineffective for lower-limb explosive power. Rather, the precision and consistency of the available evidence remain insufficient to confirm a stable positive effect. When the primary training objective is to improve lower-limb explosive power, BRT may be more appropriately used as an adjunct to targeted lower-limb training.

This study found that BRT was associated with improvements in handgrip strength. From a biomechanical perspective, this finding is plausible because participants must maintain a continuous and forceful grip on the ropes while performing various wave and swing movements [40]. An electromyographic study showed that, across five common BRT exercises, activation of the palmaris longus exceeded 40% of maximal voluntary isometric contraction, with high levels of muscle activation observed during most exercises. These findings suggest that BRT may impose substantial loading on the forearm muscles involved in grip-force production and wrist stabilisation [61]. Maintaining a continuous grip on the ropes may place considerable demands on the forearm flexors and other muscles responsible for wrist stabilisation and require participants to sustain sufficient force output despite fatigue. Repeated exposure to this type of training stimulus may promote neuromuscular adaptations associated with improvements in handgrip strength [55]. In addition, some studies have reported moderate correlations between handgrip strength and lower-limb explosive power, which may reflect the influence of overall neuromuscular function on both outcomes [49]. Therefore, the beneficial effect of BRT on grip strength supports its use as a functional training tool with broad practical applicability.

This study also found that BRT was associated with a statistically significant improvement in core endurance. During BRT, rapid and large-amplitude upper-limb movements may generate substantial inertial loads that act on the trunk through the shoulder girdle, thereby placing continuous demands on core stability. To perform these movements effectively while maintaining appropriate posture, the abdominal, back, and hip muscles may need to remain continuously engaged to resist external moments that could cause trunk rotation, flexion, and extension and to maintain a relatively neutral spinal position [12]. Electromyographic studies have shown that alternating-wave and double-arm-wave exercises elicit moderate-to-high levels of activation in the external oblique and lumbar erector spinae muscles, indicating that BRT may impose a certain loading stimulus on the muscles involved in trunk stabilisation [10]. The continuous and dynamic involvement of the core musculature in postural control and force transmission during training may partly explain the improvement in core endurance observed in this study. Subgroup analysis showed that the effect of BRT on core endurance was more pronounced than with active control conditions, suggesting that BRT may provide a more targeted dynamic core stimulus than some conventional training approaches. However, this finding was derived from exploratory study-level subgroup comparisons and therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence that the type of control condition has a definitive causal moderating effect.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study provides valuable insights into the training effects of BRT, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the principal limitation of this study was the substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. Potential sources of heterogeneity included differences in participant characteristics, such as age, sex, sport-specific background, developmental stage, and training level; differences in BRT intervention protocols, such as battle-rope specifications, exercise selection, training intensity, and intervention duration; and differences in outcome measurement methods. For example, even when different studies assessed upper-limb explosive power, they may have used different testing methods, including the seated medicine-ball throw, bench-press tests, and other throwing tests. These tests may differ in their movement patterns, biomechanical characteristics, primary muscle groups involved, and energy metabolism requirements [41, 53]. Although this study used a random-effects model and subgroup analyses to address between-study heterogeneity, its potential sources were not fully explained. Therefore, the pooled effect sizes reported in this study should be understood as average effect estimates across different study conditions and should be interpreted cautiously in practical applications, taking into account the specific population, training protocol, and measurement method.

Second, the BRT protocols used in the included studies lacked standardisation. At present, there are no widely recognised BRT training guidelines, resulting in substantial variation in the selection of training variables across studies [37, 65]. Rope weight, length, and diameter directly influence training load, while different movement patterns activate distinct muscle groups. This inconsistency in protocols makes it difficult to determine the optimal combination of training parameters and limits the generalisability of the findings. Future studies should aim to establish and validate standardised BRT protocols and report all training variables in detail to facilitate comparisons across studies and practical application [25].

Third, the overall methodological quality of the included studies was moderate. Some studies had limitations in allocation concealment, blinding, selective reporting, and data completeness, all of which may affect the reliability of the conclusions.

Fourth, although we developed the literature search strategy to be as comprehensive as possible, the possibility that some relevant studies were missed cannot be entirely excluded.

Fifth, although this study classified different tests according to predefined physical fitness constructs, the tests within the same construct may differ in movement patterns, primary muscle groups involved, and task requirements. These differences may increase the heterogeneity of the pooled effects and affect the stability of the findings across studies. In addition, potential publication bias, small sample sizes, the use of quasi-randomised designs in some studies, the underrepresentation of female participants, and the concentration of the available studies in a limited number of geographical regions may also restrict the general applicability of the study conclusions.

Finally, all included studies had relatively short intervention durations, ranging from 4 to 12 weeks, and lacked data on long-term training effects and effect maintenance. Short-term training adaptations may primarily reflect neural improvements, whereas longer-term structural adaptations, such as muscle hypertrophy, generally require longer intervention periods [38].

Future research should therefore conduct longer-term randomised controlled trials with follow-up assessments to evaluate the long-term effects of BRT and the retention of its benefits after detraining. In addition, future studies should explore dose–response relationships between training variables, such as rope weight, training volume, and rest interval, and training outcomes, and compare BRT with other training modalities, such as kettlebell training and circuit training [14, 27]. Study populations should also be expanded to more diverse groups, including female athletes, middle-aged and older adults, and athletes from different sports, to examine the broader applicability of BRT. Finally, integrating electromyographic, kinematic, and physiological measurements to investigate the mechanisms underlying BRT-induced improvements in physical performance represents an important direction for future research. In addition, future studies should prioritise the use of consistent and standardised outcome measurement tools. When sufficient evidence is available, separate meta-analyses should also be conducted for different test types to further examine the robustness of the pooled findings at the construct level.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis preliminarily indicated that, as an efficient multimodal training method, short-term BRT interventions may have positive effects on upper-limb explosive power, bilateral handgrip strength, upper-limb muscular endurance, and core endurance. However, the existing evidence is insufficient to support a significant beneficial effect of BRT on lower-limb explosive power. It should be noted that the funnel plot and Egger’s test for the core endurance outcome indicated the possible presence of small-study effects or publication bias, and the pooled effect may therefore have been overestimated. In practice, BRT may be used as a supplementary training method for developing upper-limb and core physical fitness. When the primary training goal is to improve lower-limb explosive power, more targeted lower-limb training methods should also be incorporated.
