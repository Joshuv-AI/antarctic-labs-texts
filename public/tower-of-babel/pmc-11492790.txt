Effects of adverse childhood experiences on university students’ ability to lead healthy lives: an exploratory qualitative study in Lahore, Pakistan
Authors: Rabia Fatima, Javeria Saleem, Muhammad Ishaq, Humera Zaib Khan, Gul Mehar Javaid Bukhari, Mahwish Naz, Aneela Fatima, Gholamreza Abdi, Mukul Jain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse Childhood Experiences are a significant global public health concern as these experiences have adverse effects on all the health determinants.MethodsThe interpretive phenomenological research design was used to explore the implications of adverse childhood experiences on the adult life of university students. An interpretive thematic analysis method was employed to analyze this study.ResultsFive major themes emerged after a comprehensive review of the information obtained from the interviews with the ten selected participants. These were (1) Chronic health struggles (2) Mental health disorders (3) Emotional and social difficulties (4) Paranoia and distrust and (5) Academic and professional under-performance.ConclusionThis study concludes that adverse childhood experiences have long-lasting negative effects on university students’ health and behaviour, preventing them from leading healthy lives and excelling academically and professionally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20391-2.

Background

It is scientifically established that poor health behaviors, such as drug abuse, eating poorly, and not exercising regularly, put people at risk for several chronic illnesses, including diabetes, liver dysfunction, several cancers, and an increase in cardiovascular diseases [1]. The frequency of these diseases is increasing every year especially in developing countries like Pakistan. For instance, some contributing factors of these diseases such as substance abuse and consumption have become widespread in young population of Pakistan despite laws against drugs and alcohol, limitations on tobacco use, and the availability of alternatives (such as patches) [2]. Likewise, no public health activity has been able to reduce the increasing rate of obesity; consequently, young women’s body mass index (BMI) has been increasing with each generation and over the life span, which can result in multiple morbidities [3]. Therefore, we must focus on the secondary factors that might predispose adults to adopt poor health choices. One of these factors is Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs).

Two types of adversities are included in the term adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). One is being abused during childhood, and the other is having a dysfunctional childhood. Childhood abuse can be of a psychological, sexual, emotional, and physical nature. A dysfunctional household may include exposure to substance abuse, physical or emotional neglect from parents, mental illness at home, having a household member incarcerated, and witnessing domestic violence against a father or mother during childhood [4].

According to recent studies, the prevalence of experiencing childhood adversities ranges between 46.4% in nationally representative British research and 72% in a representative Canadian sample to 82% in a small Saudi Arabian study [5]. Research shows that ACEs have many negative effects on the life of victims. Past studies in South Asia show positive associations between adverse childhood experiences and mental health issues in women [6]. Despite the availability of research-based evidence about a positive relationship between experiences of adversity in childhood and declining mental health, which also includes suicidal ideation, depressive disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder symptoms, only a few studies have focused on the presence of adverse childhood experiences as a contributing factor for unhealthy behaviour, compromised physical health, and psychological health issues using a community-based sample [5, 7].

According to a study conducted on women in South Asian countries, including Pakistan, women of this demographic experience some severe forms of domestic violence as well as social violence due to cultural patriarchal mindset, socioeconomic adversities, including poverty, domestic conflicts, and gender discrimination, which, consequently, enhance the probability of ACEs [8]. The types of ACEs that mostly result in poor health are interpersonal violence and mental illness in the household [8]. Katherine LeMasters conducted a study on women in Pakistan, according to it the occurrence of experiencing at least one ACE was 58% and 7% in cases of experiencing four or more ACEs. The most frequent types were physical and emotional abuse and neglect. Psychological adversity, domestic violence, and witnessing community violence were associated with a major depressive episode and its severity [9]. There are only a few studies conducted in Pakistan relating to ACEs and most of them focused only on the mental health issues because of ACEs and in female population only. There is a research gap in studying the other consequences of ACEs which include the effect on social and emotional health in both genders and student population of Pakistan. This study aims to explore the ACE-related adverse effects on adult health, including physical, social, emotional, and mental health, as well as on educational and career in both genders among university students of Lahore, Pakistan using a interpretive phenomenological method.

Methods

Research team and reflexivity

To report the findings of this study, consolidated criteria for writing a qualitative research checklist (COREQ) were used [10]. All the male and female researchers in this study, who were responsible for organizing and conducting the interviews, hold doctorates in public health and medical sciences (PharmD, MBBS, Public Health and Sociology) and have prior relevant research-related work experience.

The inclusion criteria for choosing participants required them to be enrolled in a university and to have an ACE score of 3 or higher, which was determined by examining the responses to the ACE-International Questionnaire that were gathered using the WHO-recommended methodology. Due to the cultural sensitivity of this research topic, telephone interviews were conducted, each lasting 40–60 min after verbal consent was obtained from the participants. They were allowed to quit the study anytime they wanted to, and interviews were conducted with those who agreed to participate with their informed consent.

Setting

This is part of a mixed methods study, and this paper covers the qualitative portion only as the quantitative portion is under review for publication. Stratified sampling method was used to select three universities in Lahore, which ensured a representative sample of students with different educational backgrounds. Students from each group were then chosen at random, guaranteeing that the study accurately represented all students and then an online version of the ACE International Questionnaire was circulated in different departments of these universities. This is a sensitive topic from cultural perspective, so the online version of the ACE-IQ [11] was used to ensure privacy and confidentiality. The following were the selected universities:

Sample size and study design

Ten students were interviewed, and selected through purposive sampling, after analyzing their responses to the ACE-IQ. After ten interviews, the data achieved data saturation, so no more interviews were conducted. An interpretive phenomenological study design was used for interviews to explore the role of Adverse Childhood Experiences in creating challenges to lead a healthy life of study participants. This design helped examine the implications of childhood adversities on study participants’ lives, as they were allowed to discuss the challenges they faced in detail.

Data collection tool

Telephonic interviews were conducted by using the semi-structured interview guide developed considering the instructions provided by the World Health Organization to explore the effect of ACEs on the life of the study participants. The interview guide given by the WHO has detailed information about interview skills, asking questions, providing clarifications, topics to discuss, probing, common responses, handling refusals and language issues.

Interpretive thematic analysis

Over two months, from January 1 to March 1, 2023, telephonic interviews, lasting 40 to 60 min each, were conducted. The study participants permitted the interviews to be recorded, and the researchers then transcribed the interviews verbatim. The transcripts were later translated into English. For a month, the researchers thoroughly and repeatedly read the translated data to find codes, which were combined into categories using NVIVO 12. Initial themes were identified and discussed with all the authors. After profound discussion with all the authors in multiple sittings, the data’s major themes were finalized by the researchers, who then used exact quotes from the study participants and the available scientific literature to explain these themes. There was no reason to conduct additional interviews (Fig. 1).

Ethical considerations

Before conducting telephonic interviews to gather data for the study, an informed written consent was obtained from each participant. The study participants were briefed on the research topic and its objectives. Only adults were enrolled in this study, and the preapproved interview guide provided by the World Health Organization was strictly followed. The confidentiality of participants was carefully maintained by removing any identifying marks from the transcripts. An institutional review board (IRB) letter (Ref no. D/42/FIMS) was obtained from the University of Punjab Institutional Review Board before conducting this study.

Conceptual framework

This study is based on the concept of adverse childhood experiences, which is a term defined by Centre for Disease Control (CDC) as traumatic events that occur in a child’s life 0–17 years and include abuse, neglect, witnessing violence, having a family member attempt or die by suicide, having an environment at home that can undermine a child’s safety, stability, and bonding such as living with a family member who had any mental illness, substance abuse problem or was incarcerated or parental separation/death. The expected prevalence of ACEs is 30–70% around the globe, and it has become a public health concern as the negative impacts are of an extensive range. A growing body of international studies has linked ACEs as a distal predisposing factor for developing chronic diseases of the heart or lungs, diabetes, obesity, and mental health disorders in adult life [5]. In Pakistan, a limited number of studies have been conducted to understand the prevalence of ACEs and their role in creating challenges to living a healthy life. A few national studies have linked ACEs with depression in women and impulsivity in students. This study has explored the implications of ACEs in the form of issues with physical health, mental health, social health, emotional health, personality development and the effect on academic and professional life of university-going students in Lahore.

Chronic health struggles

Since childhood, most of the research participants have occasionally experienced psychosomatic symptoms. Physical illnesses or ailments that are brought on by or made worse by a mental element, like stress, are known as psychosomatic symptoms. These consist of migraines, stomach problems, obesity or low body weight, irregular sleep patterns, bad eating habits, and allergies. A small number of female participants reported having recurrent infections. In terms of physical well-being, P1 said, “There have always been health problems going on with me as far as I can remember. I eat a lot when I am stressed, which is most of the time. I always had stomach issues. I have always had migraines. I have allergy issues. I get seasonal infections, but sometimes they are difficult to treat. I get vaginal infections often, and some urinary tract infections keep recurring. There have always been some issues going on”. Another female participant, P2, said, “I experience psychosomatic symptoms quite a lot, like physical pain is very frequent. I get migraines a lot. There are very few days when I do not get any migraine”. All female participants experienced these psychosomatic headaches.

Another female participant, P3, who was very weak, said about her health, “I feel like I have taken so much stress that nothing (food) affects me now; that is why I am weak. I get headaches frequently, as well as stomach issues. My immunity is weak, and these issues have stayed with me for so long that I think even medication does not affect me or I have become careless as I know it will not get okay. This keeps happening, and I also have allergy issues”. Regarding eating habits one of the female participants who lived in a dysfunctional household; P5, said, “It is like that with my siblings and me that when we are stressed, we eat a lot. I have seen that my friends and other people eat less when stressed. They do not feel hungry. However, when stressed, we eat a lot, so we also have had obesity issues for a long time”. These symptoms were more prominent in female participants than male participants, who only had the headaches frequently. However, when asked about substance abuse, one of the male participants admitted to using smoking, alcohol, and recreational drugs.

Most female study participants with ACE scores of four or above were overweight from childhood, but a few male and female participants were also severely underweight.

Mental health disorders

All the participants revealed having symptoms of disturbed mental health, including stress, depression, and anxiety, from an early age, which were still persistent. Some were clinically diagnosed with various mental health disorders and had to get help through medication and sessions. In this regard, a female participant, P2, said, “I was diagnosed with MDD (Major Depressive Disorder), and I often get its episodes. I have been very disturbed regarding my mental health for a long time”. The relationship between ACEs and depression in women has also been reported in recent study reports. Another female participant, P1, who had faced multiple childhood adversities, also mentioned her mental health issues: “I was diagnosed with borderline personality disorder a couple of years ago, and my therapist told me that it was due to my traumatic childhood”. She added that she has also been facing other mental health issues: “I have always shown symptoms of depression. Like sadness so deeply rooted in my soul. In addition, emptiness and not wanting to talk to anybody. I would cry a lot, however, only in solitude. This was initially. Then, there were symptoms of anxiety, feeling restlessness and fears about the future life”. She also mentioned developing a mental state issue: “In fact, in college, I got a condition called trichotillomania which is hair-pulling mania. I used to pull my hair all the time. My forehead would get bare often, and people would tell me to stop doing that, but I could not help it”. Apart from depression and anxiety, fear of the future was also observed in many female study participants. Only two of the participants sought help from a professional. They were diagnosed with Major Depression, Borderline personality disorder and a condition known as trichotillomania, in which the patient pulls and plucks their hair.

Another female participant, P7, who lost her mother early, mentioned her mental health issues: “I still feel depression and anxiety. Often, I feel so empty that I do not want to talk to anybody. Everything feels bad and meaningless. It feels odd to say, but sometimes I do not feel like spending time with my children either”. Another female participant, P5, who witnessed domestic violence against her mother, mentioned her mental health issues as: “Depression and anger stay with me, I feel like staying all alone only and not interacting with anyone. I do not want to go out with my friends. I do not want to talk with anyone”.

Emotional and social difficulties

Most individuals reported abnormal emotional and social well-being. Compared to others, some participants said that their inability to socialize with others interfered with their capacity to lead a healthy social life. They also felt the extremity of some specific emotions, such as annoyance, impatience, and rage, which made it difficult for them to interact with people in a healthy way. A male participant, P4, who experienced domestic violence in childhood, described, “I cannot communicate with my teachers, I get terrified. Words do not even come out of my mouth at that time. My personality has become like that I feel difficulty when I have to talk to someone who is in a higher position or authority than me”. Another participant, P1, who experienced multiple adversities in her life, including sexual abuse, psychological abuse, witnessing domestic violence, and living a mentally ill family member, explained her social and emotional health as follows: “I was always slightly socially distant and interacted with the least people. I was not interested in social life until I started university. However, before that, I would prefer not to talk to people like my relatives. I would get nervous sometimes if I had to talk in front of people or if the teacher asked me a question. My mind goes blank sometimes”. In addition, she added that “I was mostly sad and upset in the start, then I used to be angry and anxious all the time. I have felt like I live in extremes. I laugh and have fun a lot, but I have never truly felt happy emotions until I married. There was guilt and shame and frustration too.” Lack of confidence and fear of being humiliated in front of everyone was dominant in many study participants. Another critical change the study participants felt was that they all preferred to be alone rather than being around people. They felt that socializing was meaningless for them and wanted to be left alone.

Some study participants talked about having abnormal emotional health, as they experienced a few emotions that were dominant in their personality, such as anger or sadness and feelings of emptiness or indifference. One of the study participants, P2, who experienced domestic violence at home, described her emotional health: “I used to stay depressed often. I felt sadness, hopelessness, anger, and irritable feelings mostly. This was common when I stayed home”. Another participant who experienced the loss of her father discussed her emotional health in these words: “Now it is like when someone talks to me in any strange way, I feel a surge of anger in me, and I just feel like shutting them up. I feel a lot of frustration that I cannot ignore things. I used to stay calm or silent in such matters, but now I cannot control my response. Anger has filled in me so much that it comes out violently, or I start crying. People usually talk oddly, and then I do not want to talk to anyone”. According to a few of the study participants, they were bottling up their emotions, such as anger and frustration, for many years. And even now they cannot control the expression of these emotions, and they often come out as rude statements or actions.

Another participant, P9, who lost her mentally disturbed father in her childhood, experienced physical abuse, described her emotional health as: “I got irritable, angry, and rude. I had so much aggression in me. I did not usually listen to anybody. It worsened after my father’s death. I got very irritable and frustrated. Whenever I saw a child with his father, I got angry that he had his father, and I did not. All these things got worsened after his death”.

Paranoia and distrust

Many of the study participants explained different personality changes after facing adverse childhood experiences. These included the Development of trust issues, insecurities, attachment issues, impulsivity, Inability to maintain healthy relationships, confusion, and Inability to decide confidently. In this regard, a female participant, P1, mentioned, “Once something clicks with my mind, I feel like I have to do it no matter what, and I would not feel okay without it. I have been living on my impulses. I still do. If I have a task on my mind, I cannot truly rest until I complete it”. She added, “I have always had deeply rooted trust issues because I initially used to trust blindly and got myself hurt. Then, I was just unable to trust anyone. I have had so many insecurities, which I still do. In addition, there is always the fear of abandonment, like everyone will leave me eventually, so only I can protect myself—like a defensive mechanism. I do not get attached to people; I always keep a safe distance first. And guilt, I used to feel guilt all the time, like issues with my life were all my mistakes. And like I am a bad person. Bad Muslim. And always felt embarrassed”. The development of severe trust issues, fears, paranoia, and insecurities were the reasons expressed by most of the study participants due to which they were unable to have healthy relationships.

Another female participant, P2, said, " I could not develop a sense of trust in myself because of my parents. I never had a sense of security. I always felt like I was in a toxic relationship as I had at home. I could never trust anyone from the start. My relationships always deteriorated eventually”. She added, “I doubt myself a lot, and I cannot decide if something is good for me and whether I should do something. I feel unable to move forward with things, and I could never take the first step”. Additionally, I used to have so much aggression as far as I can recall”. She mentioned her aggravated emotions as: “I used to be so angry most of the time. Due to this anger once, I even left my home because nobody used to listen to me; I thought if I left, then I would struggle, and I would achieve something.” Most of the study participants from dysfunctional and abusive households observed a lack of self-confidence and poor strength to decide about important matters in their lives.

Another participant, P3, who lost her father and was bullied and neglected, discussed her behavior in relationships and that she was always full of insecurities and feared abandonment. She said, “I used to be very close with my friends, but now it is like people come into my life, and it is friendship, but I don’t truly feel anything.”

Another female participant, P5, who experienced domestic violence and physical abuse at home mentioned that she cannot even think that any relationship can be healthy and said in this regard that, “When any of my friends get married, I get this feeling that this marriage is also going to be like that of my parents. It never occurred to me that a relationship can be healthy”. She added, “I have observed that people take little things very normally, but we siblings overreact to everything, and I feel like this is the consequence of our adverse childhood experiences”.

Having trust issues, insecurities, and fear of abandonment were the major contributing factors towards becoming a paranoid personality, which was observed to different extents in all the study participants.

Academic and professional under-performance

Many of the study participants faced issues with their academic performance because they could not focus on their studies properly. Some participants revealed that they lost interest in their studies altogether. In this regard, P5 mentioned, “There were only a few tests that I prepared with proper attention because of tension at home due to my parent’s unstable relationship. Then, again in college life, I went through the same issues, and things got even more critical. And it was the same in my university life as well”.

In this regard, another female participant, P3, said, “I have always been an average student. I could never express what was going on in my mind. I never shared it. Therefore, I could also never focus on my studies. I always avoided discussing anything in class, and I never liked questioning by teachers. It made me uncomfortable”.

Some participants also mentioned that they could not normally interact, which caused a lag in their academic life. In this regard, P1 said, “I feel I could have done much better if my life wasn’t like this. Because I could not even ask questions in class, I lagged somehow. And in my profession, I am also susceptible to different attitudes like being dominated by male bosses; it makes me uncomfortable. And sometimes I also still feel a lack of confidence and focus. And we need these things to make a good career. I could have done much better if my life were normal”. In this regard, P2 said, “There was always a doubt cast by my parents on the personality that I can or can’t do things. In the classroom, when teachers used to ask me about stuff, even if I knew the answer, I could never reply. I was always doubtful if my answers were right or not” " She added, “I was so afraid of interactions and what others would think about me if I gave a wrong answer. And my teachers might insult me over that (a wrong answer)”.

Discussion

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are a term used for many childhood adversities categorized into two themes; childhood abuse and dysfunctional household [12]. According to recent studies, the probability of an individual reporting at least one childhood adversity varies from 46.4 to 82% globally [5]. Childhood adversities are a significant global public health concern because they have a long-term negative impact on health [7]. A number of recent global studies have established that ACEs are associated with adverse health outcomes, which include physical health [13–16] and psychological issues [17, 18], risky health behavior [12], issues regarding personality development and increased healthcare utilization [19]. In Pakistan, previous research has only linked the ACEs to physical and mental health outcomes among a female population in a rural setting. According to the current study findings, most participants were experiencing psychosomatic symptoms to varying levels. Female participants had a higher frequency of these symptoms, especially migraine headaches and persistent gastric issues, as reported in the theme chronic health struggles. No such study has been previously conducted in the demographic region of Pakistan, so this study was one of its kind. In most international studies, childhood adversities have been associated with an increased ratio of migraine headaches in children and adolescents [20]. However, the relationship has not been adequately studied in adults previously, even though migraine has been associated with increased disability in the daily life of adults [21].

A study conducted by Crouch & Radcliff showed that increasing ACE scores correlate with learning and behavior problems [12] and obesity [19]. In another study, researchers argued that there is a strong relationship between ACE scores and lifetime or recent depressive episodes. This conforms to our study’s findings under the theme of mental health issues as almost all of the study participants had either symptom of depression, anxiety or increased severity of their diagnosed mental health issues. One of the female participants was also diagnosed with borderline personality disorder which is also a consequence of childhood maltreatment reported in the theme mental health issues [22]. In the previous studies conducted in Pakistan these results were gathered from only female population and in a rural setting.

Most of the female study participants in this study went through behavioral and emotional changes, including internalization, anger issues, and lack of interest in socializing, as reported in the theme emotional and social difficulties. A similar result was observed in another study which concluded that early childhood adversities could cause behavioral problems in children starting as early as nine. They are also associated with internalization or externalization behaviors and early Development of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder [23] and a strong relationship between ACEs and antisocial behavior, depression symptoms, and substance use during adulthood. This was the most unique aspect of this study as it has been the least studied and there has been no such study performed in this demographic region.

Research shows that trauma that occurs early in childhood, prolonged exposure to adversity, and trauma with high severity is associated with an increased ratio of impulsive nature due to the diminished capability of the brain to modulate emotions. A similar outcome was observed in our study participants as many of them were impulsive, especially in the form of impulsive eating and decision making. This study also explored the impact of childhood adversities on social and emotional health as well as behavior and personality development which has not been studied previously in this demographic region, explained in the theme, paranoia and distrust. According to the theme of academic and professional under-performance, the study’s findings are consistent with the body of knowledge that poor childhood experiences have a variety of detrimental effects on young adults’ lives, including learning and cognitive difficulties in both educational and professional settings. It is a very important and unique aspect of this study as most of the population of this region is young and failing to excel in education or career will have a prominent negative effect on the personal growth of individuals and the economy as well.

This study explored the implications of adverse childhood experiences in a qualitative interpretive phenomenological study design that was previously not utilized. It is a strength of this study as it allowed more profound insights into the possible consequences of ACEs. Also, only a few studies on this topic are focused on the effects of ACEs on young adults, especially university students, who can provide valuable information about the challenges they face to lead a healthy life. The limitation of this study is that it does not establish a direct causal relationship between them, as it was a qualitative retrospective study, so it should be further studied with a larger cohort and the inclusion of other confounding factors. Furthermore, adverse childhood experiences may have various implications, but this study focused on different determinants of health, educational and professional life, and interpersonal behavior. The remaining areas should also be further studied.

Recommendations

This study recommends that there should be a department in academic and professional institutes whose purpose should be to identify children and adults who are having difficulties in learning or performing or are not behaving normally and in a healthy way. Additionally, such departments can be utilized to determine the root causes and provide them with the required suggestions/therapy or if they need to meet with their guardians to identify and alleviate the problems at an early stage. A similar system is recommended to be introduced in the current healthcare system of Pakistan, where they screen out patients having some traumatic experience in the past and then help them to get better with a ‘Trauma Induced Care’ approach that incorporates all adverse childhood experiences and provides trauma-related therapy in their care plan.

Conclusion

This study concluded that Adverse Childhood Experiences among university students of Lahore have a wide array of negative implications on the determinants of health and behavior, which consequently restrains them from performing well in academia and their professional lives. This study has also explored the impact of ACEs on the personality development, social health, and emotional health of university students. These factors were not studied in past research and needed to be explored further because the personality of adults affects how they behave socially and what health choices they adopt in their lives. Adverse childhood experiences are one of the distal factors that can predispose individuals to chronic and severe physical illnesses, which also impose an economic burden on people and the healthcare system. ACEs are also related to underperformance in academics and professional life.
