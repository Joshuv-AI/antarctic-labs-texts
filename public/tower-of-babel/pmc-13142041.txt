Stimuli-responsive 4D-bioprinted constructs for musculoskeletal tissue regeneration: Shape-morphing mechanisms, cell-laden bioink engineering, and preclinical outcomes
Authors: Suleiman Ibrahim Mohammad, Asokan Vasudevan, Jasgurpreet Singh Chohan, Ahmed Fekry Salman, Manveet Singh, Ripendeep Singh, Pardeep Singh Bains, Parvoz Abdulkhakimov, Mohammad Ebrahim Astaneh, Narges Fereydouni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMusculoskeletal disorders impose a substantial global disability burden. Conventional 3D bioprinting cannot replicate the dynamic, anisotropic architecture of bone, cartilage, skeletal muscle, and cardiac muscle. Four-dimensional (4D) bioprinting addresses this by integrating stimuli-responsive materials into constructs, enabling programmed shape transformation and adaptive behavior following implantation.MethodsThis narrative review examines primary experimental research on stimuli-responsive 4D-bioprinted musculoskeletal constructs, drawing on in vitro, in vivo, and combined outcomes from leading peer-reviewed journals.ResultsEvidence spans four stimuli modalities — magnetic actuation, near-infrared (NIR) photothermal response, thermoresponsive swelling-shrinking transitions, and shape memory polymer (SMP) recovery — applied across bone, cartilage, skeletal muscle, and cardiac constructs. Bioink formulations from silk fibroin-gelatin composites and alginate-polydopamine inks to GelMA-based hydrogels and polyester SMPs present trade-offs between printability, shape fidelity, and cellular compatibility. Cross-study synthesis identifies stiffness trajectory, architectural anisotropy, and dynamic deformation as primary mechano-biological axes directing cell fate decisions. Preclinical studies document encouraging ossification and chondrogenesis outcomes, though constructs fall short of native tissue mechanical benchmarks.ConclusionTranslational barriers range from fundamental physical constraints — including the mechanical performance gap and stimulus penetration depth limitations — to incremental engineering challenges amenable to near-term resolution. Passive hydration-driven deployment represents the most clinically tractable strategy, while multifunctional bioinks integrating stimuli-responsiveness, bioactive factor presentation, and cell-instructive surface chemistry define the primary material development direction.

Introduction

The musculoskeletal system encompasses a diversity of load-bearing, mechanosensitive, and architecturally anisotropic tissues whose functional properties arise not merely from chemical composition but from spatial organization at multiple hierarchical scales. Articular cartilage exhibits a depth-dependent arrangement of collagen fibers and proteoglycan gradients that produce zone-specific biomechanical behavior fundamentally incompatible with isotropic scaffold fabrication [1,2]. Cortical bone derives its fracture resistance from the lamellar organization of mineralized collagen fibers oriented along principal stress directions [3,4]. Skeletal muscle generates directional contractile force only when myofibers are aligned within a three-dimensionally organized extracellular matrix framework [5]. Conventional tissue engineering strategies — including three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting — have achieved considerable progress in replicating the gross geometry of such tissues, yet static scaffolds remain incapable of matching the dynamic, time-dependent mechanical and morphological adaptations that characterize native musculoskeletal biology [6,7].

Four-dimensional (4D) bioprinting extends the additive manufacturing paradigm by incorporating time as a functional design variable, enabling the fabrication of constructs that undergo programmed geometric transformation, stiffness modulation, or biochemical activation in response to defined external or physiological stimuli [8,9]. When embedded within cell-laden bioinks, stimuli-responsive properties confer constructs with the capacity to self-organize into biomimetically curved, tubular, or anisotropically oriented geometries that more faithfully reproduce the native tissue microenvironment — and to impose the dynamic mechanical stimuli that drive cell differentiation in ways static architectures fundamentally cannot [10,11].

The conceptual foundations of biomimetic 4D printing were established compellingly by Gladman et al. [12], whose landmark 2016 study in Nature Materials demonstrated that composites of cellulose fibrils and acrylamide could be programmatically swelled to adopt flower-inspired architectures closely replicating the hygroscopic movements observed in plant organs. While the original system used no living cells, the mathematical and materials framework it articulated — encoding local anisotropy into the ink composition to direct spatially heterogeneous swelling responses — has profoundly influenced the trajectory of bioink design for cell-laden 4D constructs in subsequent years. Every stimuli-responsive approach reviewed here is, in some conceptual sense, a descendant of that framework, even when the stimulus modality and biological application differ substantially.

For the musculoskeletal system specifically, the rationale for 4D bioprinting rests on properties that static scaffolds cannot replicate: adaptation to cyclically varying mechanical loads [8,13]; capacity for minimally invasive delivery followed by in situ self-deployment into irregular defect geometries [14,15]; and active generation of the topographic and mechanical cues that direct the differentiation of mechanosensitive cell populations — osteoblasts, chondrocytes, tenocytes, and cardiomyocytes — whose tissue-specific phenotypic commitment is driven as much by scaffold biophysics as by soluble biochemical signals [16].

This review critically appraises the current state of stimuli-responsive 4D-bioprinted constructs across the principal musculoskeletal tissue categories — bone, cartilage, skeletal muscle, and cardiac muscle — with particular attention to the relationship between bioink formulation, shape-morphing mechanism, and biological outcome. Studies were identified through targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science using terminology spanning 4D bioprinting, stimuli-responsive scaffolds, shape memory polymers, and musculoskeletal tissue engineering, and were selected on the basis of reporting original primary experimental data — in vitro, in vivo, or both — involving a defined stimuli-responsive or shape-morphing component published in internationally recognized peer-reviewed journals. Given the recency of 4D bioprinting as a distinct research domain, the search prioritized the most recent literature while incorporating earlier foundational studies whose mechanistic contributions remain conceptually central. This review adopts a narrative approach suited to a field at this developmental stage, aiming to synthesize mechanistic diversity, identify conceptual convergences, and contextualize preclinical findings within the broader tissue engineering landscape, rather than to aggregate quantitative outcomes across heterogeneous experimental systems.

Conceptual foundations of 4D bioprinting

The time dimension: why static architecture is insufficient

To understand why the addition of temporal programmability represents more than an incremental refinement of 3D bioprinting, it is instructive to consider what static scaffolds fundamentally cannot accomplish. A conventional extruded hydrogel construct, regardless of how faithfully its geometry replicates that of the target tissue, exists in a fixed mechanical and morphological state from the moment of fabrication. It cannot adapt to the evolving mechanical environment of a healing bone defect, cannot unfold to match the curved surface of a joint, and cannot exert the topographical guidance cues that direct myofiber alignment in maturing skeletal muscle [17,18]. In biological terms, native musculoskeletal tissues are not static entities — they remodel, contract, stiffen, and adapt continuously in response to mechanical loading, growth factor gradients, and cell-mediated matrix deposition [19]. A scaffold that remains invariant throughout this process is, at best, a temporary structural placeholder.

4D bioprinting addresses this gap by encoding stimuli-responsiveness directly into the material composition and printing architecture of the construct. The 'fourth dimension' is not simply the passage of time but the occurrence of a defined, programmable transformation over time upon exposure to a specific trigger [8,9]. This transformation may be geometric — as in the self-folding of a flat-printed sheet into a tubular airway scaffold [20] — or functional, involving changes in stiffness, porosity, or surface topography without gross shape change [8,21]. The distinction matters because different tissue engineering applications demand different types of temporal behavior. Bone repair may benefit most from a scaffold that transitions from a soft, deliverable state to a mechanically competent load-bearing architecture after implantation. Cartilage repair may demand a construct injectable in a collapsed form that self-organizes into a curved surface matching the articular geometry. Cardiac tissue engineering requires constructs whose curvature mirrors the helical arrangement of cardiomyocytes in the ventricular wall.

Shape-morphing mechanisms: A Mechanistic Taxonomy

The primary mechanisms by which 4D-printed constructs undergo shape transformation can be grouped into four principal categories, each operative at a different length scale and driven by distinct physicochemical processes.

The first category is differential swelling, in which spatial gradients in material composition or crosslink density within the construct produce heterogeneous volumetric expansion upon hydration, driving macroscopic bending or folding in a manner directly analogous to the bilayer curl that Gladman et al. [12] demonstrated in plant-inspired composite systems. Jensen et al. [21] applied this principle to a polyacrylamide-based composite hydrogel incorporating a swelling component and a stiffer structural phase, achieving precisely programmable deformation amplitudes by controlling the relative proportion and spatial arrangement of the two phases. The deformation was reversible and modulated by varying the degree of crosslinking in the swelling layer, a design feature with direct implications for minimally invasive deployment scenarios.

The second category is shape memory behavior, exploited in thermoplastic polymers with glass transition temperatures (Tg) within the physiologically accessible range. Shape memory polymers (SMPs) can be fabricated into a primary configuration, deformed and fixed into a secondary shape at an elevated temperature, and then recovered toward the primary shape upon reheating above Tg [22]. Du et al. [22] leveraged this property in a polyester-based SMP scaffold for bone defect repair: the scaffold is compressed at elevated temperature, cooled to fix the compact form for implantation, and thermally triggered in situ to recover its original defect-filling geometry. Miao et al. [23] employed a related strategy in cardiac applications, using PLMC-based SMPs with a Tg of approximately 37 °C to fabricate constructs that progressively recover their programmed curvature at body temperature, inducing cardiomyogenic differentiation in mesenchymal stem cells.

The third category is photothermal actuation, in which light-absorbing agents — most commonly polydopamine nanoparticles — convert NIR radiation into localized heat, producing deformation in surrounding thermosensitive or heat-expandable matrix components [24,25]. Luo et al. [25] demonstrated this approach using alginate/polydopamine bioinks in which the NIR-induced thermal gradient drove differential swelling deformation, enabling cell-laden constructs to adopt preprogrammed shapes without requiring contact-based heating. Choudhury et al. [24] applied a similar NIR-triggered deployment mechanism to bone scaffolds fabricated from GelMA and NIR-responsive agents, achieving self-fitting behavior into irregular defect geometries that resist matching with rigid pre-formed implants.

The fourth category is magnetically actuated morphing, in which magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) dispersed within the hydrogel matrix respond to externally applied magnetic fields by generating torque, translational force, or magnetothermal heating [14,26]. Zhang et al. [26] incorporated MNPs into a gelatin-fibrin octopus-inspired scaffold for auricular cartilage regeneration, demonstrating that magnetic actuation could drive the construct to adopt a complex three-dimensional curvature while simultaneously stimulating chondrocyte proliferation and matrix deposition through magnetomechanical transduction. Chakraborty et al. [14] used silk fibroin-gelatin bioinks loaded with MNPs to produce shape-morphing cartilage constructs whose deformation amplitude was tunable by adjusting field strength, a property with practical value for intraoperative fit adjustment. BIOINK engineering for stimuli-responsive musculoskeletal constructs

Hydrogel systems: design principles and compositional diversity

The engineering of bioinks for 4D musculoskeletal applications demands simultaneous satisfaction of competing requirements that are genuinely difficult to reconcile in a single formulation. Printability — the capacity of the ink to flow through a nozzle under applied pressure and then rapidly solidify to maintain the deposited geometry — generally favors high viscosity and rapid gelation kinetics [33,34]. Cell viability during and after extrusion, by contrast, requires avoiding shear stresses that exceed cellular tolerance thresholds and maintaining aqueous, protein-permissive environments [35,36]. Stimuli-responsiveness adds a third constraint: the formulation must incorporate elements whose physical or chemical properties change detectably and reversibly upon exposure to the target stimulus, without compromising the mechanical integrity of the construct or generating cytotoxic byproducts [10].

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) has emerged as a particularly versatile base matrix for 4D bioinks because it combines the biocompatibility and cell-adhesive RGD sequences of gelatin with photo-crosslinkable methacrylate groups that enable tunable stiffness and network density [10]. Several studies incorporated GelMA as the primary structural component while adding responsive agents — polydopamine particles, magnetic nanoparticles, or swelling polymers — to confer stimuli-responsiveness [24,28]. The challenge with GelMA-based formulations is that the methacrylation degree must be carefully calibrated: excessive crosslinking reduces swelling capacity and limits shape-morphing amplitude, while insufficient crosslinking compromises mechanical integrity under physiological loading.

Silk fibroin represents an alternative biopolymer backbone whose hierarchical beta-sheet crystalline domains contribute both mechanical toughness and slow biodegradation kinetics well matched to the long timescales of musculoskeletal tissue remodeling [37,38]. Kim et al. [31] demonstrated that silk hydrogels could be rendered thermoresponsive by blending with PNIPAM derivatives, enabling temperature-induced volume transitions that drive construct morphing at temperatures near the physiological range [39]. The silk-gelatin composite used by Chakraborty et al. [14] for magnetic cartilage constructs benefited from silk's slow degradation profile and high water retention, properties that support the sustained chondrocyte biosynthetic activity needed for hyaline matrix deposition over extended culture periods.

Alginate, despite well-characterized limitations in long-term cell culture — notably its lack of mammalian cell-adhesive sequences and the tendency of ionically crosslinked networks to swell or disintegrate in physiological ionic environments — remains widely used as a shape-morphing component in composite bioinks because of its exceptional printability and tunable swelling behavior [40]. Luo et al. [25] exploited alginate's differential swelling response to construct bilayer NIR-responsive inks in which one layer swelled more extensively than the other upon hydration, generating the curvature needed for shape morphing. The polydopamine content in the responsive layer added photothermal conversion capacity without requiring any synthetic modification of the alginate backbone itself.

Collagen occupies a distinct position in this landscape because it is the primary extracellular matrix protein of virtually all musculoskeletal tissues, conferring exceptional biological authenticity to constructs fabricated from it [29,41]. Patrawalla et al. [29] developed an approach to fabricating anisotropic collagen scaffolds with 4D properties derived not from a synthetic responsive component but from the inherent fibrillar self-assembly behavior of collagen, which can be directionally guided during gelation to produce constructs with defined fiber orientation. This purely biogenic approach to 4D behavior — using the material's intrinsic assembly dynamics rather than an embedded synthetic actuator — is conceptually distinct from other strategies reviewed here and warrants serious consideration as a potentially more biocompatible path forward, particularly for tendinous applications where collagen fiber alignment is a direct determinant of mechanical function. Shape memory polymer matrices

Thermoplastic shape memory systems operate on a design logic distinct from hydrogel-based approaches. Rather than relying on fluid-mediated swelling or magnetically induced reorientation, SMPs exploit the viscoelastic transition between glassy and rubbery states to encode and recover geometric configurations [42,43]. For musculoskeletal applications, the most critical engineering challenge is matching the Tg to a stimulus temperature that is both accessible in the clinical context and safe for embedded cells or surrounding tissue.

PLMC — poly(lactide-co-trimethylene carbonate) — was the SMP system employed by Miao et al. [23] for cardiac constructs, representing a family of biodegradable polyesters whose Tg can be tuned between approximately 30 °C and 55 °C by varying the trimethylene carbonate content, offering a workable window for physiologically triggered shape recovery. The polyester employed by Du et al. [22] operated at a higher recovery temperature and was used in an acellular bone scaffold context, where the absence of embedded cells relaxed the thermal constraint and allowed optimization purely for mechanical recovery fidelity and osteogenic surface properties. The distinction between cell-laden and acellular SMP scaffolds is important and sometimes obscured in comparative discussions: acellular SMPs can be more aggressively crosslinked and operated at higher temperatures than constructs that must maintain cell viability throughout the morphing event.

SOEA — soybean oil epoxidized acrylate — appeared in the work of Miao et al. [32] for skeletal muscle constructs, selected specifically for its capacity to produce anisotropic filament alignment during printing that mimics the longitudinal organization of myofibers. While SOEA is not a shape memory polymer in the strict thermal-switching sense, the oriented architecture produced during printing constitutes a form of programmed structural anisotropy whose expression occurs over the maturation time of the construct rather than in response to a discrete stimulus event [44].

Nanoparticle functionalization: magnetic and photothermal agents

The integration of nanoparticles into 4D bioinks has become one of the field's most productive areas of innovation because nanoparticles can transduce an external stimulus — a magnetic field, NIR light, or thermal gradient — into a local mechanical, thermal, or chemical response within the construct without requiring the bulk polymer matrix to itself be directly responsive [45]. Magnetic nanoparticles typically consist of iron oxide cores (Fe3O4) coated with biocompatible surface ligands such as polyethylene glycol, dextran, or silica [14,26]. Their dual functionality — providing mechanical force when actuated by a static or oscillating magnetic field, and generating heat when driven by an alternating magnetic field through Néel relaxation — makes them attractive for both shape morphing and local stimulation of osteogenic or chondrogenic pathways [14,46].

Polydopamine nanoparticles occupy a complementary niche as photothermal agents. Their high NIR absorbance, combined with a surface chemistry that promotes adhesion of cells and proteins, makes them particularly well suited for integration into cell-laden bioinks without requiring prior surface functionalization [[25], [47]]. Choudhury et al. [24] demonstrated that increasing polydopamine content in GelMA scaffolds predictably increased NIR-to-heat conversion efficiency and the speed of self-fitting deformation, but also increased cytotoxicity at high concentrations — a dose-response trade-off that remains a genuine constraint on the maximum stimuli-responsiveness achievable in cell-laden systems. Managing this trade-off through nanoparticle surface passivation or controlled spatial distribution within the construct architecture is an active engineering challenge that none of the reviewed studies has fully resolved.

Mechano-biological coupling in stimuli-responsive constructs

Substrate stiffness and stem cell fate decisions

A persistent limitation of earlier discussions of 4D bioprinting is the tendency to treat shape transformation as a geometric accomplishment — a structural event whose biological significance lies principally in the improved fit between scaffold and tissue defect. What this framing undervalues is the mechanobiological dimension: the physical properties of a stimuli-responsive construct are not incidental to its biological performance but are themselves the primary determinants of cell fate, operating through the same mechanotransductive machinery that governs development and repair in native tissues [16,48]. Cells actively probe the resistance of their substrate through cytoskeletal tension, integrin-mediated adhesion complexes, and focal adhesion kinase signaling cascades that translate mechanical input into transcriptional regulatory outcomes. For 4D constructs, these inputs are not fixed — they evolve dynamically as the shape-morphing event unfolds, as hydrogel crosslinks mature, and as cells deposit new matrix over weeks of culture.

The swelling-stiffening scaffold developed by Liu et al. [15] makes this principle explicit in a way that most 4D studies do not. By engineering a hydrogel whose compressive modulus increased approximately three-fold during hydration-driven shape recovery — transitioning from a compliant, injectable state to a stiffer construct in the chondrogenic induction range — the authors effectively programmed a temporal stiffness trajectory that mirrors the mechanical progression of the periosteal environment during cartilage anlage formation in development. The elevated Sox9 expression and proteoglycan deposition observed in cells experiencing this dynamic stiffening, compared with cells held in statically equivalent mechanical environments, makes a compelling case that the trajectory of stiffness change — not merely the endpoint value — constitutes a biologically instructive signal. This matters for construct design: it implies that 4D scaffolds should be engineered to replicate not just the final mechanical state of the target tissue but the developmental history of stiffness progression that progenitor cells would experience during normal morphogenesis.

The implications differ across tissue categories. For osteogenic applications, the relevant question is not whether a construct immediately achieves cortical bone stiffness — it cannot, and the attempt is mechanically futile — but whether it delivers a stiffness in the range that initiates osteoprogenitor commitment while permitting the progressive reinforcement driven by matrix mineralization [16,48]. Shape memory scaffolds such as those employed by Du et al. [22] and Choudhury et al. [24] address part of this problem through macroscopic deployment, but the local stiffness evolution at the cell-matrix interface — the scale at which mechanotransductive signaling actually operates — deserves more deliberate engineering attention than current designs typically afford it. Constructs that couple shape recovery to a defined local stiffening trajectory at the cellular scale represent an underexplored but mechanistically motivated design direction.

Architectural anisotropy and contact guidance

Contact guidance — the alignment of cells along topographic features at the substrate surface — is among the most reproducible and functionally consequential phenomena in cell biology [17], and it is central to the function of every anisotropic musculoskeletal tissue reviewed here. In skeletal muscle, myoblast fusion into multinucleated myotubes depends critically on prior cell alignment, which dictates both the orientation of sarcomere assembly and the directionality of contractile force generation [32,49]. In cardiac muscle, cardiomyocyte alignment along the helical fiber axes of the ventricular wall determines the mechanical efficiency of systolic contraction [30], [50]. In tendon, the longitudinal alignment of tenocytes within type I collagen fascicles is the primary determinant of tensile stiffness and creep resistance [29].

The anisotropic architectures achievable through 4D bioprinting engage this biology through mechanisms operating at multiple scales simultaneously. At the macro-scale, the corrugated surface topology produced by the staircase printing strategy of Miao et al. [32] provides directional contact guidance grooves that orient myoblast elongation along the filament axis — a topographic cue whose effectiveness in promoting myogenic alignment is well grounded in the broader literature on micro-patterned substrates [17]. At the micro-scale, the photolithographically patterned microgrooves of Miao et al. [23] direct stem cell alignment for cardiomyogenic differentiation through a contact guidance mechanism operating at dimensions comparable to individual cell widths, achieving alignment precision that macro-scale printing alone cannot produce. The PSTS strategy's combination of both scales generated differentiation outcomes exceeding either scale alone [23], a result that argues for deliberate multiscale architectural design as a general principle for demanding differentiation targets, rather than a feature specific to this particular fabrication strategy. For the field more broadly, this finding suggests that optimizing print resolution and the nano-to micro-topographic surface features of the bioink matrix — not just the macro-scale construct geometry — is as important a design variable as material composition.

The silk fibroin-gelatin constructs of Chakraborty et al. [14] add a further dimension: magnetic actuation during culture not only morphed the construct shape but also generated oscillating compressive and tensile strains within the matrix, imposing a dynamic topographic perturbation superimposed on the static printed architecture. The observation that chondrogenic differentiation was enhanced relative to non-actuated controls suggests that repeated mechanical actuation contributed a biologically meaningful inductive cue independent of soluble growth factor supplementation — a finding with practical implications for reducing the exogenous cytokine burden required for efficient in vitro chondrogenesis. Dissociating the contributions of static architecture, dynamic actuation-induced strain, and any magnetothermal effects within these systems remains an unresolved experimental challenge, but one that is tractable through appropriately designed control experiments.

Dynamic deformation as a mechanotransductive signal

Perhaps the most conceptually distinctive feature of 4D constructs — and the one that most clearly distinguishes them from their 3D-printed counterparts — is their capacity to subject encapsulated cells to programmed mechanical deformation events after fabrication. This is not merely an aesthetic property of shape morphing; it constitutes a mechanotransductive stimulus whose magnitude, frequency, and directionality can, in principle, be engineered into the construct architecture as deliberately as any chemical component.

The magnetomechanical actuation systems of Zhang et al. [26] and Chakraborty et al. [14] exemplify this principle at the cellular scale. External magnetic field application to MNP-loaded constructs generates local torque and translational displacement within the hydrogel matrix, producing microscale strains at the cell-matrix interface that are transduced through integrin-cytoskeletal connections into intracellular mechanosignaling cascades [46]. The chondrogenic differentiation enhancement observed in both studies — elevated Sox9, aggrecan, and type II collagen expression — is consistent with the well-characterized sensitivity of chondroprogenitor cells to cyclic compressive stimulation, the loading regime that normally drives chondrocyte biosynthesis through joint articulation in vivo [51]. In this framing, magnetic actuation functions not only as a geometric deployment tool but as an embedded bioreactor mechanism — delivering the mechanical stimulation that chondrocytes require for matrix production without the external loading devices that conventional dynamic culture systems demand.

Thermoresponsive transitions introduce a related but distinct mechanotransductive mode. The volumetric changes accompanying PNIPAM-driven phase transitions in systems such as those of Kim et al. [31] and Wu et al. [28], or the SMP recovery of Miao et al. [23], generate transient compressive and tensile strains on encapsulated cells during the morphing event itself. Whether these strains contribute positively to differentiation, are tolerated without cellular damage, or are mechanobiologically inconsequential at the magnitudes typically produced by hydrogel volume changes remains an undercharacterized question in the reviewed literature. The few studies reporting viability during, rather than only after, the morphing event suggest tolerance is maintained [20,25], but systematic characterization of mechanotransductive contributions of the morphing event itself — separate from the final geometric state — would substantially strengthen the biological rationale for dynamic construct design and provide a more mechanistically complete justification for the added complexity that stimuli-responsiveness entails.

The practical implication of this analysis is that the physical parameters of 4D constructs — stiffness trajectory, anisotropy direction, actuation strain magnitude and frequency — should be specified as explicit design variables alongside material composition and cell type, rather than as incidental consequences of fabrication choices. A scaffold engineered with defined mechanotransductive intent, grounded in the known mechanical sensitivity windows of the target cell population, represents a more powerful and reproducible tool for directed tissue engineering than one whose biological effects on cell fate are characterized post hoc.

Bone tissue engineering via 4D-PRINTED scaffolds

NIR-triggered and shape-memory approaches to defect filling

Bone defect repair represents one of the most demanding applications for 4D-printed scaffolds because of the combination of mechanical requirements — constructs must ultimately support physiological loads in the range of several megapascals for cortical sites — and the biological complexity of intramembranous and endochondral ossification [52]. The ability to deliver a scaffold in a compact, minimally invasive configuration and then trigger in situ expansion to match an irregular defect geometry has direct clinical relevance: current grafting procedures often require aggressive surgical exposure to achieve precise defect filling with rigid implants [53].

Choudhury et al. [24] addressed this challenge directly, fabricating a GelMA-based scaffold incorporating polydopamine nanoparticles and hydroxyapatite in a formulation designed for NIR-triggered self-fitting. Upon irradiation, the polydopamine converted NIR light to heat, driving differential swelling of the GelMA matrix into the negative space of an irregular defect model. The authors demonstrated that the scaffold could fill a non-uniform defect geometry approximately 40% larger than the initial scaffold footprint, with residual compressive mechanical properties in the range of 50–150 kPa — values appropriate for cancellous bone analogs but falling substantially short of cortical bone requirements. Osteogenic differentiation assays using bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) showed elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and calcium deposition consistent with osteoblastic commitment, with the hydroxyapatite component appearing to provide both osteoconductive surface chemistry and structural reinforcement.

Du et al. [22] pursued a fundamentally different strategy, using an acellular shape memory polyester scaffold without embedded cells and focusing on the scaffold's capacity to promote bone repair through its mechanical properties and surface chemistry following shape recovery. The polyester system underwent shape recovery at temperatures achievable using mild hyperthermic treatment, regaining approximately 90% of its programmed geometry within minutes of triggering. When implanted in a rat cranial bone defect model, the shape-recovered scaffold showed substantially greater bone ingrowth and vascularization at eight weeks compared with non-shape-recovered controls of identical composition. The authors attributed this difference to improved mechanical contact between scaffold and defect walls following shape recovery, which would reduce interfacial micromotion and promote osteoblast adhesion and matrix mineralization. This is a plausible mechanistic interpretation, but one that was not directly tested — the study lacked the experimental arm needed to dissociate the geometric fit effect from any potential mechanotransductive or surface activation effects associated with the thermal treatment itself. This interpretive ambiguity is a limitation the authors would do well to address in follow-up work.

Growth factor integration and osteogenic bioink performance

Wu et al. [28] introduced an important variant on cell-laden 4D bone constructs by incorporating transformable living bioink formulations capable of sustained local growth factor presentation. Their system used a GelMA base functionalized with heparin microspheres loaded with bone morphogenetic protein, achieving controlled release kinetics over a 21-day period while the underlying construct underwent shape transformation in response to differential swelling. The combination of shape morphing and sustained bioactive factor presentation in a single construct represents an advance over systems that address these requirements separately. That said, the study's biological validation remained at the in vitro level, making it premature to draw conclusions about in vivo efficacy. The release kinetics were characterized under static conditions, and the extent to which the dynamic mechanical environment of shape morphing alters drug diffusivity through the hydrogel matrix — a question of direct practical importance — was not investigated.

Liu et al. [15] approached the bioactive scaffolding problem from a different direction, using a 4D hydrogel scaffold with swelling-stiffening properties programmed to produce a defined mechanical stiffness upon hydration-driven shape recovery. The rationale was that matching scaffold stiffness to the target tissue through passive physicochemical evolution — rather than requiring active cellular remodeling — would provide an earlier and more consistent mechanotransductive induction signal for encapsulated MSCs. The scaffold's stiffness increased approximately three-fold upon full swelling from its compact delivery state, transitioning from a soft, injectably deformable material to a stiffer construct with compressive moduli in the 15–30 kPa range appropriate for early-stage chondrogenic induction. The in vitro data showing elevated Sox9 expression and proteoglycan deposition in dynamically stiffening constructs compared with static mechanical controls is one of the more rigorously designed biological experiments in the reviewed literature, though the authors would note that 15–30 kPa represents stiffness appropriate for cartilage rather than bone, and the study's applicability to bone repair specifically is therefore limited [54].

Cartilage regeneration: anisotropy, hydration gradients, and magnetic guidance

Self-folding constructs and tracheal cartilage engineering

Articular and tracheal cartilage share a structural need for organized, zone-specific matrix composition and a curved or tubular geometry that is difficult to achieve with conventional 3D printing without post-processing steps [55]. Chiesa et al. [20] capitalized on the self-folding potential of 4D constructs to engineer tracheal cartilage analogs from a composite hydrogel containing GelMA and hyaluronic acid. The flat-printed construct, when placed in aqueous medium, spontaneously folded into a semi-tubular geometry driven by differential crosslink density between the inner and outer faces, achieving an internal diameter and curvature closely matching those of the tracheal lumen targeted. Chondrocytes encapsulated within the construct maintained viability exceeding 85% over seven days and produced glycosaminoglycan-rich matrix colocalizing with type II collagen — the principal collagen of hyaline cartilage. The self-folding geometry appeared to support chondrocyte biosynthesis more effectively than flat controls of identical composition, an observation the authors attributed to the compressive microenvironment generated within the curved architecture, though direct mechanical measurement of this intraconstruct stress distribution was not reported and remains a gap in the mechanistic analysis.

Jensen et al. [21] approached cartilage-relevant applications from the angle of programmable swelling-shrinking behavior, developing a composite hydrogel with on-demand volumetric transitions actuated by temperature changes near the physiological range. While the biological validation in this study was limited primarily to cytocompatibility assessment, the material engineering was unusually thorough: swelling amplitude, deformation directionality, and recovery kinetics were each characterized as a function of composition variables, providing a more complete mechanistic picture than most bioink studies offer. The system showed particular promise for minimally invasive cartilage delivery because the compact, shrunken configuration could theoretically be injected through an arthroscopic port and then triggered to expand to fill an osteochondral defect in situ — a deployment strategy with obvious clinical appeal that has yet to be validated in a tissue-specific biological model.

Magnetic actuation for auricular and articular cartilage repair

The outer ear presents a cartilage engineering challenge distinct from joint cartilage: the three-dimensional geometry of the auricular template is intricate, the tissue is mechanically non-load-bearing, and the primary clinical driver is aesthetic restoration rather than functional recovery. Zhang et al. [26] developed a magnetoresponsive scaffold — designated FOGS (fibrin-gelatin octopus scaffold) — specifically for auricular cartilage, incorporating iron oxide MNPs within a gelatin-fibrin matrix designed to match the compressive stiffness of native auricular cartilage. Magnetic actuation drove the scaffold to adopt the target auricular geometry with a shape fidelity of approximately 85–90% as assessed by geometric deviation analysis. Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) seeded on the magnetically actuated constructs showed increased Sox9, aggrecan, and type II collagen expression relative to non-actuated controls, suggesting that the magnetomechanical stimulus during actuation provided a chondrogenic inductive cue independent of soluble growth factor supplementation. This finding, if replicable across cell types and field parameters, would have considerable practical significance by potentially reducing the exogenous growth factor burden needed for efficient chondrogenic induction in scaffold-based systems.

Chakraborty et al. [14] employed a silk fibroin-gelatin composite bioink loaded with MNPs for articular-type cartilage applications. This study is notable for the systematic characterization of the relationship between MNP content, magnetic field strength, and deformation amplitude — a parameter-response mapping that is essential for clinical translation but frequently absent from proof-of-concept studies in this space. The optimal MNP concentration identified (approximately 2 mg/mL) balanced sufficient actuation capacity with acceptable cell viability: at higher concentrations, the MNPs appeared to generate localized oxidative stress that compromised chondrocyte survival, a cellular toxicity mechanism consistent with iron-mediated Fenton chemistry. Mechanical testing of the constructs following magnetic actuation and 14-day culture showed compressive moduli in the range of 40–80 kPa, values approximating the properties of superficial zone articular cartilage but falling well below those of the deeper zones, where compressive loading demands are substantially higher. This zonal mechanical gradient remains one of the most difficult engineering targets in cartilage tissue engineering, and none of the 4D approaches reviewed here has yet demonstrated a solution.

Anisotropic collagen approaches and biological authenticity

Patrawalla et al. [29] departed from the magnetic and thermoresponsive paradigms by developing a fabrication strategy for anisotropic collagen scaffolds in which 4D behavior arises from the inherent fibrillar self-assembly and contraction dynamics of collagen itself, guided during gelation to produce constructs with defined fiber orientation and curvature. This purely biogenic approach — using the material's intrinsic assembly dynamics rather than an embedded synthetic actuator — is conceptually distinct from all other strategies reviewed here. Tendon-relevant applications were the primary focus, given that tendons are composed almost entirely of aligned type I collagen fibers whose orientation directly determines tensile mechanical properties. The principal limitation of the approach is that the degree of architectural control is governed by the thermodynamics of collagen self-assembly, which is sensitive to temperature, ionic strength, and protein concentration in ways that may challenge batch-to-batch reproducibility in manufacturing contexts. A more fundamental question — whether construct-scale fiber alignment produced during self-assembly faithfully replicates the sub-micron fibrillar organization of native tendon, rather than simply its macroscopic directionality — was not fully addressed in this study and warrants future investigation using high-resolution structural characterization methods.

Skeletal muscle and cardiac tissue engineering

Anisotropic constructs for myofiber alignment and functional organization

The generation of functional skeletal muscle tissue requires not only the presence of appropriate cell types — typically myoblasts or multipotent muscle progenitors — but also a microenvironmental organization that enables the differentiation of these cells into elongated, multinucleated myotubes aligned in a common direction [49]. Without directional alignment, individual myofibers contract asynchronously and in random orientations, producing no net contractile force along any given axis and failing to replicate the coordinated mechanical output of native muscle [32,56].

Miao et al. [32] addressed this challenge through a staircase-effect printing strategy in which alternating deposition of stiff and soft material layers produced a construct with built-in mechanical anisotropy and differential swelling capacity that drove self-alignment of the printed filaments upon hydration. The SOEA-based construct showed myoblast alignment along the filament direction, with immunostaining confirming the presence of myosin heavy chain (MHC)-positive multinucleated myotubes organized in fascicular-like bundles. The staircase geometry simultaneously produced a corrugated surface topography that appeared to promote contact guidance of differentiating myoblasts, adding a topographic inductive cue to the mechanical alignment effect. One analytical limitation of this study is that the relative contribution of topographic versus mechanical anisotropy to myotube alignment was not experimentally deconvoluted, making it difficult to determine which design feature was primarily responsible for the observed differentiation outcomes. Such deconvolution — achievable by comparing topographically flat but mechanically anisotropic controls with topographically structured but mechanically isotropic ones — would considerably strengthen the mechanistic interpretation and provide actionable design guidance for future iterations.

The near-infrared approach to myoblast construct development was explored by Luo et al. [25], who demonstrated that NIR irradiation of alginate/polydopamine bioinks caused local heating that drove differential swelling-induced folding, producing a curved construct architecture around which myoblasts organized and differentiated. The polydopamine component served a dual function: as the photothermal agent driving shape morphing, and as an adhesion-promoting surface coating that enhanced myoblast attachment to the alginate matrix, which intrinsically lacks mammalian cell-adhesive sequences. Cell viability at four days post-printing exceeded 90%, though the longer-term outcome of myogenic differentiation under sustained NIR stimulation protocols was not fully characterized. The extent to which the NIR-induced thermal elevation during shape morphing affected myoblast differentiation state — either positively through heat shock protein induction or negatively through thermal stress — was not systematically investigated, representing a gap that is mechanistically important given the known sensitivity of myogenic commitment to thermal stimuli.

Cardiac constructs: curvature engineering and cardiomyogenic differentiation

Myocardial repair presents a stringent set of requirements that arguably exceed those of any other tissue category in this review. The left ventricular wall has a complex helical fiber arrangement, transitions continuously between systolic and diastolic configurations under pressures of up to approximately 15 kPa, and must synchronize electrically with the surrounding myocardium to avoid arrhythmia [57], [58], [59]. A scaffold that fails to replicate myofiber alignment will produce mechanical strain on implanted cells inconsistent with native cardiomyocyte loading, potentially triggering apoptosis or phenotypic drift away from the contractile cardiomyocyte lineage [30,60].

Wang et al. [30] fabricated a 4D-printed cardiac construct from a GelMA/MeHA composite in which aligned printing paths embedded directional structural anisotropy, producing a construct whose curvature could be adjusted post-fabrication by altering the swelling ratio between anisotropic and isotropic layers. The adjustable curvature feature was specifically designed to enable the construct to match the varying curvature radii encountered across different regions of the ventricular wall, addressing a fundamental geometric compatibility problem that rigid cardiac patches inherently cannot solve. Cardiomyocytes cultured on these constructs showed aligned sarcomere organization, elevated connexin-43 expression indicating gap junction formation, and spontaneous synchronized beating — functional metrics that substantially exceed what most static cardiac scaffolds achieve. When implanted in a rat myocardial infarction model, the constructs produced measurable improvements in ejection fraction relative to scaffold-free controls at four weeks, though the authors appropriately acknowledged the difficulty of attributing this functional improvement unambiguously to the specific 4D properties of the scaffold as opposed to the material composition or cell delivery effect.

Miao et al. [61] employed the PSTS (photolithographic-stereolithographic-tandem strategy) to fabricate 4D smart scaffolds from PLMC-based SMPs for cardiomyogenic differentiation of stem cells. The distinctive feature of this study was the tandem fabrication approach that produced a scaffold with two distinct structural scales: a macro-scale SMP architecture that underwent shape recovery at body temperature to generate curvature and mechanical compression on the scaffold, and a micro-scale photolithographically patterned surface that directed stem cell alignment through contact guidance. The combination produced a differentiation outcome — as measured by cardiomyogenic marker expression and calcium transient activity — that surpassed what either structural scale alone achieved, supporting the concept that multiscale architectural design is more effective than single-scale optimization for demanding differentiation targets. This multiscale principle is underutilized across the field and deserves greater attention as a design strategy, particularly for cardiac and skeletal muscle applications where functional output depends on coordinated organization at nano-, micro-, and macro-scales simultaneously.

Kim et al. [31] contributed a thermoresponsive silk hydrogel system of broad applicability to musculoskeletal tissue engineering. The silk-PNIPAM composite exhibited a reversible LCST transition near 32–37 °C, enabling volumetric transitions and associated cell alignment changes in response to temperature cycling. In vitro culture studies demonstrated that smooth muscle cells encapsulated within the thermoresponsive silk hydrogel aligned along the direction of thermal gradient-induced contractile strain, a self-organizing behavior with clear implications for vascular graft and cardiac patch equivalents. The reversibility of the LCST transition in this system is both a strength — enabling repeated actuation — and a potential concern for long-term in vivo stability, since the transition temperature could theoretically be modulated by changes in the ionic and protein environment of the implant site.

Growth factor delivery and dynamic bioactive environments

The biological efficacy of any tissue engineering construct depends not only on its structural properties but on the spatiotemporal availability of bioactive signals — growth factors, morphogens, and adhesion ligands — that guide cell fate decisions and matrix biosynthesis [62]. This is particularly consequential in 4D systems, where the construct's changing geometry and mechanics create a dynamically evolving biophysical environment that either reinforces or undermines the biochemical signals present [28,63].

Wu et al. [28] addressed this directly by engineering a 4D construct with sustained, localized growth factor presentation integrated into the shape-morphing architecture. Their heparin microsphere delivery system provided controlled release of BMP-2 over three weeks, with release kinetics modulated by the crosslink density of the surrounding GelMA matrix. Because crosslink density varied spatially within the shape-morphing construct, the growth factor release rate was inherently heterogeneous — higher in regions of looser crosslinking, lower where the matrix was more densely crosslinked. The authors framed this as a feature enabling spatially graded differentiation induction, but this interpretation requires caution: the relationship between local crosslink density, local growth factor flux, and local cell differentiation state was not mapped experimentally with sufficient spatial resolution to confirm the proposed gradient mechanism. The concept is compelling, but spatially resolved measurement of both growth factor concentration and cell differentiation markers within the construct volume would be needed to move this from a plausible hypothesis to a validated mechanistic claim.

The swelling-stiffening scaffold developed by Liu et al. [15] illustrates an alternative approach to dynamic bioactive signaling through passive mechanical evolution rather than active pharmacological delivery. The concept that a scaffold's stiffness progression through physiologically relevant ranges — from the compliance appropriate for soft tissue delivery through the stiffness range that drives chondrogenic commitment — provides an endogenous mechanobiological induction signal is theoretically well grounded in the mechanosensing literature [48], [64]. The in vitro data reported in this study, showing elevated Sox9 expression in MSCs experiencing dynamic stiffening compared with those in static mechanical environments, represents one of the more mechanistically rigorous biological validations in the reviewed literature. Whether this mechanobiological advantage persists in vivo, where the dynamic stiffening of the scaffold is occurring simultaneously with the stiffening effects of matrix deposition by the cells themselves, is an open and important question.

Across the reviewed literature, the dominant approach to growth factor biology in 4D constructs has been the incorporation of established osteogenic or chondrogenic induction factors (BMP-2, TGF-β3) rather than exploration of less conventional signaling molecules or the physical cues — substrate topology, matrix stiffness, fluid shear — that are increasingly recognized as co-equal drivers of cell fate with soluble factors [65]. The capacity of 4D constructs to generate precisely tunable physical stimuli at the cell-matrix interface represents an underexploited opportunity to investigate the physical basis of musculoskeletal differentiation in ways that static systems simply cannot achieve.

Translational considerations: preclinical outcomes, limitations, and unanswered questions

In vivo validation: what the evidence shows and what it does not

Of the primary studies reviewed, in vivo experimentation was conducted in a subset, and the gap between in vitro demonstration and in vivo validation remains one of the most conspicuous unresolved challenges in the field. Du et al. [22] reported in vivo bone defect repair in a rat cranial model, demonstrating statistically improved bone regeneration in shape-recovered scaffold groups relative to non-shape-recovered controls of identical composition at eight weeks. Wang et al. [30] reported functional cardiac construct performance in a rat infarction model, with ejection fraction data suggesting partial functional recovery compared with scaffold-free controls, though follow-up was limited to four weeks and sample sizes were modest. Zhang et al. [26] conducted in vivo auricular cartilage regeneration experiments in a rabbit auricle defect model, demonstrating that FOGS-implanted defects produced cartilage-rich tissue with histological architecture resembling native auricular cartilage at twelve weeks.

These in vivo findings are genuinely encouraging, but several considerations temper their direct translational significance. Rodent and rabbit models are widely recognized to have substantially greater regenerative capacity than human musculoskeletal tissues, which are notoriously poor regenerators — articular cartilage in adults has essentially no intrinsic capacity for regeneration beyond the tidemark, and cortical bone repair depends critically on the vascular supply and periosteal integrity that small animal models overestimate relative to clinical defect scenarios [66,67]. The defect geometries used in these models are also small relative to clinically relevant defect sizes in humans, meaning that the stimulus delivery strategies — which depend on energy penetration depth and spatial field uniformity — may perform substantially differently at scale. Magnetic field uniformity decreases with the cube of distance from the source, NIR radiation is attenuated by approximately 99% within the first centimeter of soft tissue at 808 nm, and thermal gradients dissipate rapidly through conductive tissue [68]. At clinically relevant scaffold dimensions and tissue depths, the stimulus delivery physics may differ so fundamentally from the preclinical scenario that essentially a new set of engineering solutions will be required.

Mechanical performance: A persistent and serious gap

The mechanical properties of 4D-bioprinted musculoskeletal constructs, while improving, remain consistently below those of the native tissues they are intended to replace. This gap is not trivial. Native cortical bone has a compressive strength of approximately 130–200 MPa and a compressive modulus of approximately 15–25 GPa; the highest-performing scaffolds reviewed here reach compressive moduli in the tens to hundreds of kilopascals at best [22,69]. Native articular cartilage has a dynamic compressive modulus of approximately 0.5–5 MPa depending on zone and loading frequency [51]; the cartilage constructs reviewed achieve 40–80 kPa under static compression [14], [70]. Skeletal muscle generates active contractile stresses of up to approximately 200 kPa in the cross-sectional direction; the constructs reviewed produce appropriate alignment and differentiation markers but quantified contractile force output was not reported in most cases [32].

This mechanical shortfall is not simply a fabrication limitation — it reflects the fundamental challenge that the biological processes responsible for generating the mechanical properties of native tissue require extended in vivo maturation periods that in vitro culture of days to weeks cannot replicate. Matrix mineralization in bone, proteoglycan water-retention in cartilage, and myofibrillar protein assembly in muscle each require timescales of weeks to months and depend on mechanical loading, vascularization, and complex cellular signaling cascades that preclinical in vitro models approximate poorly. The implication is that 4D constructs should probably be evaluated not at the time of fabrication, but following a maturation period — whether in a bioreactor or in vivo — during which cellular and matrix processes driving mechanical development can operate. Studies reporting mechanical properties of freshly bioprinted constructs without post-fabrication maturation may therefore be measuring properties of limited relevance to clinical performance, and more nuanced maturation timeline reporting would substantially improve the interpretive value of these data.

Stimulus safety and clinical accessibility

The clinical deployment of 4D constructs requires stimuli that can be applied safely to patients in a surgical or post-surgical setting. Thermal stimuli at temperatures above approximately 42 °C risk protein denaturation and tissue damage; NIR radiation at the wavelengths and powers used in preclinical studies (typically 808 nm, 1–2 W/cm2) must be delivered percutaneously through potentially centimeters of overlying soft tissue with attendant attenuation and collateral heating; alternating magnetic fields for magnetothermal actuation require specialized equipment, have frequency-specific tissue interaction profiles, and may be contraindicated in patients with ferromagnetic implants [71]. Each constraint is manageable in principle but represents a real translational barrier that is rarely addressed with appropriate seriousness in proof-of-concept studies.

The passive hydration-driven deployment strategy explored by Liu et al. [15] and Jensen et al. [21] — in which the construct is delivered through a small access port and undergoes shape recovery driven entirely by the osmotic and swelling thermodynamics of the implant site without any active energy input — offers a potentially more clinically tractable approach than energy-dependent actuation. This is because it requires no post-implantation stimulus delivery infrastructure and no patient cooperation or compliance with an external actuation protocol. The trade-off is that passive swelling-driven morphing provides less precise geometric control than active magnetic or photothermal actuation: the deformation is governed by the local ionic environment and osmotic gradients of the implant site, which are patient-variable and not directly controllable by the surgeon. The choice between active and passive actuation strategies therefore involves a genuine clinical trade-off between geometric control and practical simplicity that the field has not yet explicitly acknowledged or systematically evaluated.

Bioink standardization and reproducibility

A survey of the bioink formulations across the primary literature reveals a diversity that, while intellectually productive, complicates the construction of any meaningful cross-study comparative framework. GelMA concentrations range from 5% to 15% w/v; MNP loadings range from 0.5 to 5 mg/mL; crosslinker concentrations, UV exposure times, and gelation temperatures vary substantially across laboratories [14,21,24,26]. In the absence of standardized fabrication protocols and material benchmarking assays, it is genuinely difficult to determine whether differences in biological outcomes between studies reflect principled differences in construct design or simply differences in fabrication quality and material consistency [33,72].

This challenge is not unique to 4D bioprinting — it pervades the broader biofabrication literature — but it acquires additional weight in stimuli-responsive systems because small changes in crosslink density or nanoparticle distribution can fundamentally alter the shape-morphing response. A construct whose swelling behavior is designed for a specific deformation amplitude will perform differently if batch-to-batch variability in polymerization efficiency exceeds a few percent. The field would benefit substantially from the development and adoption of standardized material characterization protocols — rheological fingerprinting, swelling kinetics testing, shape-recovery quantification under defined loading conditions — reported consistently across laboratories to enable genuine cross-study comparison and facilitate regulatory evaluation pathways.

Translational hierarchy: distinguishing fundamental barriers from addressable challenges

An honest assessment of the field's translational trajectory requires distinguishing between limitations that represent fundamental physical or biological constraints — those whose resolution demands conceptual breakthroughs rather than engineering refinements — and those that are tractable through foreseeable advances in materials science, fabrication technology, or regulatory frameworks. Conflating these two categories produces an undifferentiated list of challenges that neither accurately reflects the pace of likely progress nor helps to prioritize the research investments most likely to accelerate clinical impact.

Certain barriers belong clearly in the fundamental category. The mechanical performance gap for load-bearing bone and deep-zone cartilage is not primarily an engineering problem — it is a biological one, rooted in the fact that the relevant mechanical properties emerge from years of cell-mediated matrix remodeling under physiological loading, vascular supply, and innervation conditions that no current ex vivo fabrication system can replicate. A construct that achieves 500 kPa compressive modulus today is not on a trajectory to reach 20 GPa through incremental optimization; closing that gap requires a shift from scaffold-as-substitute toward scaffold-as-template, in which the implanted construct primarily guides the host's own regenerative biology rather than attempting to preemptively replicate the mechanical state of mature tissue [52,69]. Stimulus penetration depth for NIR and alternating magnetic field strategies similarly reflects physical constraints — electromagnetic field geometry and tissue optical properties — that do not yield to iterative construct-level refinement at the bench scale [68]. These are constraints that demand either a change in strategy (accepting reduced geometric control, shifting to acoustically or radiofrequency-responsive systems, or confining NIR applications to surgically accessible sites) or a fundamental advance in physics, not incremental progress in bioink formulation.

By contrast, several other challenges in the field are more tractable. Bioink standardization is a solvable technical problem: the community has successfully established rheological and printing quality standards for conventional hydrogel bioinks [33,72], and extending such frameworks to include stimuli-responsive performance metrics — standardized swelling kinetics assays, shape-recovery fidelity scoring, and nanoparticle distribution characterization protocols — is a matter of community coordination and resource allocation rather than fundamental discovery [73]. The current dominance of small rodent in vivo models is an institutional and resource constraint as much as a scientific one; progression to ovine or porcine large-animal critical-size defect models with longer follow-up windows is achievable with existing technologies and would dramatically improve the translational relevance of preclinical evidence. Fabrication reproducibility can be substantially improved through tighter raw material specification, online process monitoring approaches adapted from pharmaceutical manufacturing, and the adoption of digital fabrication quality records that enable cross-laboratory benchmarking [72].

Regarding the specific clinical deployment contexts most likely to see early translation: passive hydration-driven shape recovery represents the nearest-term pathway because it is compatible with existing surgical workflows, requires no post-implantation energy delivery infrastructure, and faces a regulatory profile closer to that of established injectable hydrogel systems than to novel energy-delivery devices [15,22]. Magnetic actuation systems occupy an intermediate position — static and alternating magnetic field devices are already in clinical use for other indications, the toxicological profile of iron oxide nanoparticles at appropriate concentrations is well characterized in the oncology imaging literature [46], and the translational challenges are primarily construct-level rather than regulatory-conceptual. NIR-responsive constructs, elegant in their bench-scale execution, face the most formidable translational barriers arising from tissue penetration physics; their nearest realistic clinical application context is likely superficial anatomy — craniofacial bone, auricular cartilage, or surgically exposed joint surfaces — where fiber-optic NIR delivery can provide spatially controlled actuation without transmissive tissue penetration [24,68]. Recognizing this application-specific translational hierarchy, rather than treating all stimulus modalities as equivalently promising for clinical deployment, would allow the field to channel experimental investment toward the preclinical models, scale parameters, and safety studies most likely to generate a credible path to first-in-human use.

Conclusions

The body of evidence reviewed here establishes that stimuli-responsive 4D bioprinting has moved substantially beyond its origins in soft robotics and plant-inspired materials to become a genuinely productive strategy for musculoskeletal tissue engineering. The field has demonstrated, across multiple tissue types and stimulus modalities, that programmed shape morphing can be integrated with cell-laden bioinks to produce constructs that self-organize into geometrically complex architectures, guide cell differentiation through biomimetic topographic and mechanical cues, and initiate tissue regeneration in preclinical models. The foundational framework articulated by Gladman et al. — encoding local anisotropy into the material to direct macroscopic shape transformation — remains the conceptual backbone of the field, even as the materials, triggers, and biological targets have diversified considerably.

Across the reviewed literature, the mechanistic bridge between construct physical properties and biological outcomes is increasingly well-defined: substrate stiffness trajectory, architectural anisotropy, and dynamic deformation during actuation each engage distinct mechanotransductive pathways that direct cell fate decisions as powerfully as any soluble biochemical signal. This convergence of evidence suggests that the physical parameters of 4D constructs — not just their material composition — warrant specification as explicit design variables in future work, grounded in the known mechanical sensitivity of the target cell population rather than characterized post hoc.

What the evidence does not yet provide is a demonstration that 4D-bioprinted constructs can match the mechanical performance, long-term structural integrity, and scalable biological activity needed for clinical translation in demanding musculoskeletal applications. The mechanical gap between fabricated constructs and native tissue remains substantial and is inadequately addressed in most studies. In vivo validation has been confined to small animal models with limited defect sizes and observation periods. The safety and accessibility of the required stimuli for human application has not been systematically evaluated. The diversity of bioink formulations and fabrication parameters across the literature makes cumulative evidence-building difficult.

The most productive near-term directions converge on three strategies: the development of multifunctional bioinks that combine stimuli-responsiveness with sustained bioactive factor presentation and cell-instructive surface chemistry in a single formulation; the systematic evaluation of 4D constructs under biomechanically relevant dynamic loading conditions that more closely approximate the in vivo mechanical environment; and the prioritization of passive, hydration-driven shape recovery strategies as the most immediately clinically tractable actuation paradigm. Beyond these material and engineering priorities, the field would benefit from explicitly acknowledging the translational hierarchy discussed here — distinguishing challenges that require fundamental biological or physical breakthroughs from those addressable through incremental engineering advances — so that experimental investment can be directed most efficiently. Progress grounded in the mechanistic and mechano-biological insights accumulated across the primary literature reviewed here will determine whether 4D bioprinting ultimately fulfills its considerable promise for musculoskeletal regenerative medicine.
