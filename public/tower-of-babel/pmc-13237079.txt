Impact of holiday periods on glioblastoma surgery: a Dutch nationwide population-based cohort study
Authors: Lucas G Westerink, Kiki I Slingerland, Vincent K Y Ho, Tatjana Seute, Niels Verburg, Pierre A J T Robe, Philip C de Witt Hamer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring high-quality surgical care is challenging during holiday periods due to potential reduction of capacity as well as availability of specialised personnel. Recently, quality of care for patients with gastric cancer in the Netherlands was found to be lower during the holiday periods. The effect of the holiday periods on quality of care on brain cancer, specifically glioblastoma surgery in the Netherlands, has not been investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether surgery during holiday periods is associated with differences in survival, early mortality, extent of resection (EOR), complications, weekly number of operations and time from diagnosis to treatment. This nationwide retrospective cohort study utilised data from the Dutch Brain Tumour Registry (DBTR) to analyse 4053 glioblastoma patients undergoing first-time resective surgery between 2014 and 2021. Three holiday periods were studied to reflect different levels of healthcare disruption: broad, narrow and summer. We compared holiday and non-holiday periods in terms of survival, early mortality (< 30 days), residual tumour presence after surgery, complications (Clavien-Dindo classification ≥ 3), performance decline (Karnofsky performance decline ≥ 10 at six weeks after surgery), weekly number of operations and time from diagnosis to treatment (> 21 days). Analyses were performed using chi-squared statistics, Kaplan-meier curves, Cox-regression, Poisson and logistic regression models. Surgery during holiday periods was not associated with differences in survival (adjusted hazard ratio 1.048, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.949–1.158; p = 0.350), early mortality (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.087, 95% CI 0.608–1.939; p = 0.779), complications (aOR 1.118, 95% CI 0.794–1.574; p = 0.524), residual tumour presence (aOR = 0.779, 95% CI 0.603–1.059; p = 0.119), performance decline (aOR 0.858, 95% CI 0.719–1.023; p = 0.088) or time from diagnosis to treatment > 21 days (aOR 0.868, 95% CI 0.710–1.062; p = 0.169). There was a significantly lower number of weekly operations in the holiday group compared to the non-holiday group (incidence rate ratio 0.920, 95% CI 0.858–0.986; p = 0.018). Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most malignant and common type of primary malignant brain cancer in adults1. Despite standard treatment consisting of maximal safe surgical resection combined with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, median survival is 15 months2.

Postoperative therapy for glioblastoma generally follows the Stupp protocol, consisting of radiotherapy with concurrent and adjuvant chemotherapy (temozolomide), ideally initiated within six weeks after surgery3,4. For elderly or frail patients, or those with a specific molecular profile (unmethylated MGMT promoter), radiotherapy monotherapy or temozolomide monotherapy may be offered instead3.

Organisation of care plays a crucial role in offering optimal treatment for patients5–9. The quality of the (surgical) treatment relies on several critical organisational elements, such as team cohesion, multidisciplinary approaches, and the availability of specialised teams9–14.

However, ensuring the quality of care can be particularly challenging during holiday periods due to potential reductions in the number of operations and the availability of specialised personnel. Recent research in the Netherlands on gastric cancer surgery has shown a higher rate of tumour-positive resection margins for procedures performed during holiday periods, raising concerns about compromised surgical quality during holiday periods15. Moreover, studies from Sweden and Italy on various conditions, including breast, colorectal, head-and-neck, prostate, kidney and bladder, pancreatic, and thyroid cancer, melanoma, and leukemia, have demonstrated increased overall mortality for patients diagnosed or undergoing surgery during holiday periods16–19. Multiple reasons for this have been proposed, including reduced staffing levels, lower availability of specialised surgical and perioperative teams, delays in diagnosis or treatment, decreased postoperative monitoring capacity, and reduced team cohesion due to temporary or less experienced staff.

Given these observations, it is of interest to investigate whether similar trends extend to patients with glioblastoma. However, until now, no studies have examined the impact of surgeries performed during holiday periods on the outcomes of brain tumours, specifically glioblastoma. Moreover, there have been no studies investigating the effects of holidays on diseases other than gastric cancer in the Dutch population. Such insights hold implications for healthcare planning and resource allocation in the world and provide insights into whether holiday surgery is a systematic issue in the Netherlands for other diseases as well. Therefore, in this study we analysed the effect of holiday periods on survival, early mortality, residual tumour presence after surgery, complications, numbers of operations, and time from diagnosis to treatment of glioblastoma surgery in the Netherlands.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This nationwide population based retrospective cohort study retrieved patient records from the expanded neuro-oncology section of the Netherlands Cancer Registry (NCR), the Dutch Brain Tumour Registry (DBTR). The NCR/DBTR is notified of new histologically confirmed glioblastoma diagnoses by all Dutch pathology laboratories. Patient characteristics, histomolecular diagnosis, radiology reports and therapeutic interventions are collected from hospital records by data managers of the NCR. Patients’ vital status (deceased/alive) is assessed through annual record linkage with the Municipal Personal Records database (Gemeentelijke Basisadministratie), which was last updated on 31-01-2023. The study design, data abstraction process, and storage protocols were approved by the national supervisory committee of the NCR, following the principles of the codes of good conduct (Gedragscode Gezondheidsonderzoek) issued by the Committee on Regulation of Health Research (COREON)20 and were in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The requirement for informed consent for the study was waived by the NCR’s supervisory committee.

Study population

We included all patients who had first-time resective glioblastoma surgery between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2021 in the Netherlands. Tumours were histologically confirmed according to the WHO criteria21. The following patient characteristics were recorded: sex, age at diagnosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, baseline Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS), year of surgery, tumour laterality and adjuvant treatment (concomitant chemoradiotherapy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy or no adjuvant therapy).

Holidays

School holidays are nationally issued in the Netherlands by the Dutch government and consists of 5 holiday periods: spring (1 week), May (1 week), summer (6 weeks), autumn (1 week) and Christmas (2 weeks)22. Spring, summer and autumn holidays vary by region (north, central and south). In each year, specific holiday weeks according to Dutch authorities were registered.

For the main analysis we used a broad definition of the holiday periods which included weeks during spring in which at least one of the regions has spring holidays (2 weeks), May (1 week), weeks during summer in which at least one of the regions has summer holidays (9 weeks), weeks during autumn in which at least one region has autumn holidays (2 weeks), and Christmas holiday (2 weeks), resulting in a total of 16 weeks. For the sensitivity analysis we used a summer only period and a narrow period. The summer period included the overlapping weeks of summer holidays in all regions (4 weeks) and the narrow definition included summer holidays (4 weeks), May (1 week) and Christmas holidays (2 weeks), resulting in a total of seven weeks.

These periods were chosen based on the following reasons. Firstly, broad period, a time where the majority of people in the Netherlands take holidays, including those without children who are not bound by school holidays. Secondly, a narrow period, a time when all schools in all regions are on holiday. Lastly, the summer period, to assess the potential impact at the centre of a long holiday period, during which the most disruption to healthcare organisations is anticipated.

Outcomes

The study assessed multiple outcomes to evaluate the impact of holiday periods on glioblastoma surgery. Survival over time was analysed, with follow-up defined as time from histological diagnosis to death of any cause or end of follow-up. Other outcomes were weekly number of operations, mortality within 30 days after surgery, performance decline after surgery, complications after surgery, residual tumour presence after surgery and time from diagnosis to treatment.

Performance decline was defined as a decline of 10 points or more in the pre-operative and 6-weeks postoperative karnofsky performance scale (KPS). Postoperative complications were assessed using the revised Clavien-Dindo classification (CDC), which ranks complications based on the treatment required for these complications and consist of five grades; I: any deviation from normal, not requiring treatment, II: requiring medication, III: requiring an intervention, IV: requiring intensive care management, and V: death24. Significant postoperative complications were defined as CDC ≥ 3. Residual tumour presence is defined as the presence of residual tumour on postoperative MRI (yes/no). Time from diagnosis to treatment is the number of days between the earliest date of either neurological consultation or imaging due to suspected glioblastoma, whichever occurred first, and the day of resective surgery. This is dichotomized based on national median of 21 days.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised by frequencies with percentages, or means with standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Differences in characteristics between surgery in holiday and non-holiday periods were tested with Pearson’s chi-squared tests. Number of weekly surgeries was calculated and Poisson regression modelling was utilised for comparison between holiday and non-holiday periods, with incidence rate ratio (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Kaplan-Meier estimator was conducted to visualise survival times, with log-rank test to determine differences in survival distributions. Survival analysis was conducted using univariable and multivariable Cox regression, with non-holiday period as the reference, with 95% CI. To account for heterogeneity between surgical centres, a shared frailty term at the hospital-level was included in the Cox models. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used for all other outcome measures, also including a hospital-level random effect.

Potential patient related confounding factors for survival and outcomes were included in multivariable models, i.e. age at diagnosis (below and above 65 years), sex (male; female) and baseline KPS (10–60; 70; 80–90; 100). Additionally, time from surgery to adjuvant treatment (continuous variable) and adjuvant treatment type (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy) were included in multivariable Cox regression analysis for survival. Performance decline was not adjusted for KPS, as it contains baseline KPS for its calculation. Complete case analysis was performed per outcome. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Number of operations

The number of weekly operations was lower in the holiday group compared to the non-holiday group (incidence rate ratio 0.920, 95% CI 0.858–0.986, p = 0.018). The median number of weekly operations in the non-holiday group was 10.43 (IQR 10.04–10.78) compared to 8.40 (IQR 8.20–8.75) in the holiday group.

Survival

Kaplan-Meier analysis (Fig. 1) showed that surgery during the holiday period did not affect survival outcomes of glioblastoma surgery. Median survival was 12.7 months (IQR 7.2–21.9) for non-holiday group and 12.9 months (IQR 7.0–21.9 months) in holiday group. Furthermore, surgery during holiday periods did not affect risk of mortality in univariable (HR 1.015, 95% CI 0.944–1.109; p = 0.684) and multivariable (aHR 1.048, 95% CI 0.949–1.158; p = 0.350) analysis. There was no missing follow-up survival data.

Sensitivity analysis

Results of the analyses using the other holiday period definitions (narrow and summer) were similar to the main analyses. The results are presented in supplementary material.

Discussion

This Dutch nationwide population based retrospective cohort study shows that outcomes of glioblastoma surgery in the Netherlands did not differ during holiday periods in terms of survival, early mortality, residual tumour presence, complications, performance decline and time diagnosis to treatment, despite a significant decrease in the number of operations.

The effect of surgery during holiday periods on outcomes in patients with brain tumours, including glioblastoma, has not previously been described in the literature. A recent study from the Netherlands has raised concerns about the quality of surgical care during holiday periods in the Netherlands, with evidence indicating a higher risk of tumour-positive resection margins in gastric cancer surgery during holiday periods15. Additionally, several nationwide studies, primarily conducted in Sweden, have linked cancer surgery during holidays with adverse mortality outcomes16–18. Further supporting evidence from a single centre study in Italy indicates that surgeries performed during holidays also influence the risk of severe morbidity in Whipple procedures19. We found no effect of the holiday periods in outcomes of glioblastoma surgery. This is in line with studies performed on radical cystectomy and esophageal cancer in Sweden, which show no differences in holiday surgery either25,26. We propose three possible explanations for the sustained outcomes observed in glioblastoma patients during holiday periods in the Netherlands.

One possible explanation involves the postponement of more complex surgery during holiday periods. The more complex procedures may be deferred due to factors such as age, tumour location, involvement of eloquent brain areas, or the need for (awake) intraoperative stimulation mapping. For example, glioblastomas located near eloquent cortex may require stimulation mapping, in which language, motor or cognitive functions are tested during surgery (awake or under general anaesthesia) to identify functional boundaries and enable a maximal safe resection. These cases are typically performed by neurosurgeons and perioperative teams with dedicated neuro-oncological expertise equipped with specialised mapping and monitoring techniques. Neuro-oncological procedures that necessitate specialised neuro-oncology teams might be postponed to non-holiday weeks when such teams are available. The more complex glioblastoma surgeries yield poorer outcomes27,28. Therefore, postponing them from holidays to non-holidays could potentially lead to a relative improvement in outcomes during holidays compared to non-holidays. This could contrast with other oncological procedures, where a broader range of surgeons and hospitals perform major resections and where temporary reductions in specialist availability may more readily influence outcomes because complex cases are less often deferred to non-holiday periods. However, this theory is not supported by our results, as there were no differences in time from diagnosis to treatment and any disparities in patient characteristics were minimal and not clinically relevant. Notably, the proportion of patients with high-risk preoperative status (KPS 10–60) was identical between holiday and non-holiday periods. This group is often considered poor surgical candidates, although selected patients may still benefit from surgery because postoperative functional improvement is sometimes achievable, particularly when symptomatic mass effect is present.

Furthermore, neuro-oncology teams may employ other strategies that safeguard care continuity, such as strategic surgical planning and adjusted staff scheduling. Although the overall surgical volume declines during these times, such organisational strategies may help sustain the quality of surgical and perioperative care. A reason for this robustness could be the structured nature of neurosurgical care in the Netherlands. Glioblastoma resections are performed in 12 neurosurgical centres, six of which are academic centres, with only a small number of medium-volume general hospitals contributing to surgical care. Although there is considerable practice variation between these neurosurgical centres in the Netherlands, all centres participate in (regional) neuro-oncology networks in which multidisciplinary tumour boards coordinate referral pathways and treatment decisions29. This system may limit vulnerability during holidays. Similar suggestions were made in a Swedish study on oesophageal cancer, where structured care planning and referral systems were suggested as possible explanations for the lack of a holiday effect on outcomes26. Interestingly, outcome of gastric cancer surgery was not well maintained in the Netherlands in their nationwide cohort study15. The authors speculate about the origin of this shortfall during holiday periods and suggest that one potential explanation could be the prevalence of extensive specialisation in the current medical landscape, leading to a shortage of specialised surgeons during holiday periods. There may be instances where surgeons who do not typically perform a specific surgery are required to fill in, resulting in a lack of expertise in these procedures. The authors plead for more extensive collaboration, next to centralisation, to ensure adequate quality of care.

A second potential explanation could be that the investigated and available set of patient outcomes does not fully capture the impact of specialised neuro-oncological surgical teams on glioblastoma care. Other relevant quality indicators may include patient-reported outcomes, cognitive performance, or health-related quality of life. In addition, detailed surgical nuances are not recorded in a nationwide database such as resected tumour volume, the rate of supramarginal resections, or the proportion of surgeries performed in or near eloquent brain regions. Nonetheless, a study from the United Kingdom demonstrated improved survival among patients treated by specialist neuro-oncological surgeons compared to general neurosurgeons14, suggesting that reduced availability of such specialists during holiday periods could potentially affect survival.

A third explanation could be that, during holiday periods, glioblastoma patients are more frequently allocated to best supportive care without undergoing surgery and without obtaining a histological diagnosis of glioblastoma. This could theoretically occur during weeks when the available teams during holidays may judge certain cases, particularly those requiring specialised expertise, to be too complex (because of tumour location or size) and/or too high-risk (because of patient characteristics) to operate at that moment. During non-holiday periods, some of these patients might otherwise have been considered operable. As these patients were not included in the current database, this may have contributed to the observed reduced number of operations and a relative improvement in observed outcomes during holiday periods.

Strengths of our study are a large population-based nationwide cohort, complete and accurate follow-up survival data based on Municipal Personal Records database and the comprehensive nature of this study, contrary to some studies, adjusting for multiple covariates and various outcomes. However, missing data may have biased our findings. Nevertheless, the missing data is considered to be missing at random, as it mainly resulted from the start date of data collection, which was the same for both groups. Inherently associated with nationwide databases, certain variables, such as resective surgery type (with/without intraoperative stimulation mapping, supramarginal, with/without intraoperative fluorescence), exact understaffing data across different hospitals, exact amount of resection (partial, subtotal, gross total, or volumetric) and other functional outcome measures were unavailable. In addition, our findings span an eight-year period, during which temporal surgical and organisational developments are not fully captured in this database. Surgical temporal shifts may have occurred during this time, including broader adoption of supramarginal resections and increased use of intraoperative fluorescence (5-aminolevulinic acid) for tumour visualisation. However, the organisational structure of glioblastoma care in the Netherlands has remained largely stable during these years. Multidisciplinary collaboration within neuro-oncology and referral pathways have seen no major structural changes. Finally, given the overall poor prognosis of glioblastoma, smaller holiday-related effects on survival cannot be fully excluded.

The observed differences in outcomes between glioblastoma surgery and gastric cancer surgery within the same healthcare system highlight the need for further investigation into the reasons behind this disparity. Similarly, disparities in outcomes between different diseases and across different European countries highlight the importance of understanding the complex interplay between healthcare organisation and patient outcomes in cancer care. Further research should focus on detecting the reasons for these differences, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes in all weeks of the year for all diseases.

Conclusion

Outcomes of glioblastoma surgery in the Netherlands did not differ between holiday and non-holiday periods in terms of survival, early mortality, residual tumour presence, complications, performance decline and time diagnosis to treatment, despite a significant decrease in the number of operations.
