Piezocatalytic reforming of plastics into high-value chemicals
Authors: Zongnuo Sha, Fang Chen, Cheng Hu, Hongwei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpcycling of various plastic wastes and their mixtures into clean fuels at ambient conditions holds great potential, but challenging the mild catalytic technologies in terms of product selectivity. Herein, we propose constructing mechanically-driven piezocatalytic reforming system as a universal plastic reforming platform, based on [PO4] unit-substituted BiOIO3 nanorods. The [PO4] tetrahedra acting as polarization vacancies disrupt the symmetric dipole alignment of [IO3] units to beak the see-saw effect between effective active sites and piezoelectric polarization intensity, achieving polarization reorientation and constructing I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP frustrated Lewis acid-base pairs, which undergo kinetically-favorable dynamic transformation under periodic stress. This system successfully produces H2 and CO from typical plastic including polyester, polylactic acid, polyethylene, and their mixtures with selectivity beyond 87% in gas. Especially, methanol and ethanol with the total yield of 91.84 mmol⋅g−1⋅h−1 can be directly obtained from polyester in liquid, setting a mechanical-to-chemical conversion efficiency of 4.96%.Subject terms: Renewable energy, Heterogeneous catalysis

Introduction

The widespread use of plastic products has generated enormous quantities of non-degradable waste, which ultimately accumulates in landfills or escapes into natural environments, polluting the ecosystems in soil, ocean and atmosphere. In particular, microplastics (MPs, ranging from 0.1 to 5 mm in size) pose challenges for recycling due to their homogeneous dispersion and degradation tolerance in environments1,2. Currently, mechanical recycling can regenerate low-quality plastic pellets at a relatively low cost, but its inherent downcycling fails to close the loop in terms of material performance and value3. In contrast, chemical recycling deconstructs plastic polymer chains into higher-value products. Techniques such as catalytic pyrolysis and hydrogenolysis can directly convert waste plastics into fuels (e.g., diesel, wax) and chemical feedstocks4–6, achieving upcycling. However, the harsh reaction conditions (high temperature and high pressure) along with unpredictable product distributions, result in significant energy consumption and scalability challenges. While photothermal catalysis7–9, which leverages solar energy, substantially reduces the demand for intensive external energy, it is constrained by low photothermal conversion efficiency and reaction discontinuity. Therefore, upcycling waste plastics into fuels and platform chemicals under mild conditions is of great importance for advancing the carbon neutrality of the economy.

Tandem chemical depolymerization with photo-/electro-reforming systems offers a promising route for upcycling waste plastics into fuels and high-value chemicals under ambient conditions (Fig. 1a). For instance, polyols derived from polyethylene terephthalate (PET) can be selectively oxidized to carboxylic acids (such as glycolic acid, acetic acid, and formic acid) and glycolaldehyde, accompanied by the generation of H2, CO, and CH410–13. Qiao et al. reported that TiO2 photocatalysts modified with atomically dispersed Pd species enable selective conversion of polyethylene (PE) to ethylene and propionic acid14. In a separate study, Zhang et al. transformed biodegradable polylactic acid (PLA) into H2 and pyruvic acid via a photo-reforming pathway15. Moreover, electrocatalysis shows great potential for converting plastic-derived intermediates into value-added products such as long-chain α-hydroxy acids16, amino acids17, and organosulfur compounds18 through C–C, C–N, and C–S coupling. However, due to the diversity of chemical bonds in different plastics, these mild catalytic systems (while exhibiting high selectivity for single polymers) often struggle to maintain such selectivity when dealing with various plastic wastes and their mixtures, increasing the cost of product separation and purification.

Piezocatalysis, capable of driving redox reactions using the mechanical energy widely present in nature (e.g., water flow, tides, wind, and noise), has emerged as a research hotspot in clean fuel production. Based on the band theory and screening charge effect19, active charges generated/separated in piezocatalysts can undergo hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and plastic oxidation process on distinct surfaces, dynamically ameliorating thermodynamic reaction pathways and circumventing the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) with slow kinetics, which is expected to produce syngas for the indirect synthesis of alcohol fuels20. Moreover, the efficient charge separation and self-adaptive redox potential endow piezocatalysts with the capacity of activating O-H, C-H, and C-O bonds in plastic monomers, promoting the cleavage of C-C bonds21. However, studies on the recycling of carbon and hydrogen resources through mechanically activated piezocatalytic reforming (PIR) of plastics remain unexplored.

In this work, we develop an efficient piezocatalyst based on [PO4]- substituted BiOIO3 and verify the feasibility of the PIR system for different plastics and their mixtures. BiOIO3 (BIO) possesses a crystal structure featuring the oriented alignment of polar [IO3] groups, which induces significant macroscopic polarization and is considered a high-performance piezocatalyst22. However, akin to challenges faced by most piezocatalysts, BIO exhibits a pronounced see-saw effect between effective active sites and piezoelectric polarization intensity during piezocatalytic reactions. Specifically, the directional growth of BIO single crystal nanorods along the [001] orientation generates ordered stacking of [IO3] polar units, amplifying the crystal polarity23,24. However, the effective separation and migration of active charges along the [001] polar axis renders the reduced utilization active sites on the dominantly-exposed {010} facets25 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The substitution with [PO4] groups induces structural distortion of [IO3] tetrahedra and disrupts the dipole symmetry of BIO along the b-axis, establishing a new polarization field along the [010] direction and enhancing the crystal polarity, which provides a charge transfer pathway for electrons to migrate toward effective active sites. Concurrently, the charge redistribution arising from the I-OB-P bonds formed between [IO3] and [PO4] groups establishes I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP frustrated Lewis acid-base pairs (FLPs) composed of I atoms and terminal oxygen (OP) in [PO4], creating a dynamically reconfigurable I–O–H–OP reaction channel that enhances H2O molecule activation within the periodic stress (Fig. 1b). Under ultrasonic, the as-prepared catalyst achieves efficient PIR process to produce H2, CO, and light hydrocarbons (CH4, C2H4, C2H6 and C2H2) from PET, PLA, PE, and their mixtures with the overall selectivity of H2 and CO exceeding 87% in gas. Besides, methanol and ethanol, as liquid products in PET reforming, can be directly generated at a rate of 91.84 mmol⋅g−1⋅h−1. It provides a universal platform for the plastic-upgrading synthesis of renewable fuels.

Results

Catalyst synthesis and characterization

BIO nanosheets (BIO-S) and nanorods (BIO-R) were synthesized via an HNO3-assisted hydrothermal process under different pH conditions. The BIO nanorods exhibit a thickness of ~ 15 nm, with lengths reaching several micrometers, which facilitates the oriented alignment of polar [IO3] groups to enhance the macroscopic polarization (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subsequently, a mild phosphorization treatment under an inert atmosphere was employed to introduce [PO4] groups into the crystal structure of BIO-R (Supplementary Fig. 3). The doping concentration was facilely modulated by varying the phosphorization time and dosage (BIO-RPX, X = 1-5, representing different phosphorization conditions). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and transmission electron microscope (TEM) images revealed that the diffraction peaks of the phosphorized BIO samples undergo no significant changes, and the surfaces remained smooth and flat without observable facet dissolution or nanoparticle formation (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). This indicates that the mild phosphorization treatment preserved the intrinsic crystal structure of BIO while preventing the precipitation of metallic atoms. As shown in energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings, the homogeneously distribution of Bi, I, O and P elements in BIO-RP3 further validates the nanorod morphology and doping of P species (Fig. 2d). To determine the occupied location of P dopant, the surface atomic structures of BIO-RP3 were characterized by using high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), because of the intensity dependence of atomic number. The lattice spacing of 0.284 nm and 0.288 nm in Fig. 2a corresponds to the (200) and (002) crystal planes of BIO, respectively, indicating that the directional growth of nanorods along the [001] direction formed the predominantly exposed {010} facets, in which the long range ordered lattice structures composed of alternating arrangements of Bi and I atoms can be observed. The incorporation of P atoms with a strong coordination effect and a smaller atomic radius is a priority to bond with O atoms in or around the [IO3] groups due to the high stability of the internal [Bi2O2]2+ layer, breaking the periodic atomic arrangement and producing some lattice disorder regions (marked with the pink rectangle). As depicted in Fig. 2b and c, the intensity distribution of the corresponding atoms along the two orthogonal blue rectangular lines was found to be decreased, attributing to P atoms (atomic number: 15) substituting I atoms (atomic number: 53). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) reveals that with the increase of P doping concentration, the content of I in BIO samples gradually deviates from its theoretical value (31.73 wt%) and decreases accordingly (Supplementary Table 1). Hence, the moderate phosphorization process results in partial substitution of P atoms for I atoms in [IO3] units, thereby achieving atomic dispersion of P.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and Raman spectroscopy were employed to further elucidate the local coordination environment of P atoms. Compared to pristine BIO, the FTIR spectra of P-substituted BIO nanorods exhibited broad vibration bands in the ranges of 900–1200 cm−1 and 530–650 cm−1, corresponding to the P-O bonds26, whose intensity gradually increased with higher doping concentrations. This observation further confirms that P atoms replaced I atoms in the [IO3] groups and bonded with the neighboring O atoms (OB). Simultaneously, a new characteristic peak emerged near 520 cm−1 in the low-wavenumber region, attributed to the bending vibration modes of O-P-O bonds. The vibration peaks at 767 cm−1 and 673 cm−1, assigned to I-O bonds, shifted toward higher wavenumbers after P substitution. These shifts indicate that the enhanced interaction between [IO3] groups and P atoms altered the chemical bond strength and local environment of I-O, facilitating the bonding of terminal oxygen in [IO3] with P atoms via I-OB-P linkages to form [PO4] tetrahedra (Fig. 2e). In the Raman spectra, the emergence of v1 symmetric stretching vibration (979 cm−1) and v3 asymmetric stretching vibration (1034 cm−1) bands of [PO4] groups coincided with the attenuation of I-O vibration peaks (620–820 cm−1), consistent with the FTIR results (Fig. 2f).

Subsequently, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was analyzed to investigate the chemical bonding between [IO3] groups and P atoms. The Bi 4 f and P 2p spectra confirmed the stable presence of Bi3+ and effective incorporation of P atoms after phosphorization (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Compared to BIO-R, the characteristic peaks of I 3 d spectra located at 623.0 eV (I5+ 3d5/2) and 634.5 eV (I5+ 3d3/2) shifted toward higher binding energies by approximately 0.3 eV for BIO-RP3, which can be attributed to the charge redistribution within [IO3] units induced by the I-OB-P bonding configuration, increasing the electron density of OB atoms (Fig. 2g). The shift of the I-O characteristic peak at 530.2 eV in the O 1 s spectrum of BIO-RP3 towards lower binding energy (0.3 eV) compared to BIO-R (530.5 eV) further confirms this phenomenon (Fig. 2h). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy indicates that partially substituting I atoms with a small amount of P atoms generated negligible oxygen vacancies in BIO-RP3 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Combined with the structural models optimized by first-principles calculations in Fig. 2i, it can be concluded that the partial substitution of I atoms by P atoms induces structural distortion and charge redistribution in the adjacent [IO3] groups, forming the distinctive I-OB-P bonding configurations to stabilize P species as [PO₄] tetrahedra within the lattice.

Piezocatalytic performance

Following phosphorization, the BIO nanorods exhibited a significantly narrowed bandgap, which is advantageous for enhancing the piezoresistive sensitivity and accelerating the transfer of piezoelectrically active charges (Supplementary Figs. 9–11). First, the piezocatalytic H2 production performance of the samples was evaluated in pure water without any cocatalysts. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, the time-dependent H2 production under ultrasonic excitation revealed that the sonolysis of water is negligible. All the P-substituted BIO nanorods demonstrated enhanced piezocatalytic H2 evolution activity, and the yield of H2 exhibited a volcano-shaped trend with increasing the concentration of P species. BIO-RP3 possessed the highest catalytic activity, with H2 evolution rate reaching 1691.47 µmol⋅g−1⋅h−1, 3.3-fold and 2.2-fold higher than those of BIO-S (513.37 µmol⋅g−1⋅h−1) and BIO-R (775.77 µmol⋅g−1⋅h−1), respectively. This indicates that incorporating [PO4] groups significantly improves the piezoelectric potential output and surface reaction kinetics of BIO. Excessive phosphorization of BIO nanorods may cause the reduction of I5+ and formation of abundant oxygen vacancies, which evolved into charge recombination traps and damage the intrinsic polar structure, thereby suppressing H2 evolution (Supplementary Fig. 12). Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was detected as the corresponding oxidative product generated by combining two hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) from water cleavage, maintaining the high-purity H2 in gaseous products (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Trapping experiments verified that piezoelectrically induced active electrons were the primary reactive species for H2 evolution, and the production rate showed a positive correlation with ultrasonic power, further confirming that ultrasound-induced piezoelectric potential drives the water-splitting reaction (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16).

To study the stability of the piezocatalytic system, long-term H2 production was conducted. Over 10 hours of ultrasonic vibration, a total yield of 16875.26 µmol⋅g−1 H2 was produced by BIO-RP3 (Supplementary Fig. 17a). A slight decline in the piezocatalytic efficiency was observed after four reaction cycles (Supplementary Fig. 17b). XRD and FTIR spectra of BIO-RP3 indicate the robustness of the phase structure (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). The change in the peak intensity ratios observed in the post-reaction XRD pattern may be caused by the variation in the exposure ratio of the crystal facet resulting from the fracture of the nanorods, together with the random powder orientation during the XRD sample preparation process. SEM and TEM images demonstrate that the average aspect ratio of the nanorods decreases from 6.18 to 5.46 after the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 20), which confirms the partial fracture of nanorods under the transient high pressure induced by ultrasonic cavitation. The fracture of nanorods disrupts the dipole alignment in BIO-RP3, thereby reducing macroscopic polarization. The decrease in the nanorods' aspect ratio is also unfavorable for bending deformation for mechanical energy capture. Besides, after each catalytic cycle, a small amount of catalyst is inevitably lost during the recollection by centrifugation and drying for the next run. The above reasons are collectively responsible for the slight decline in the cycling runs. Nonetheless, BIO-RP3 exhibits competitive piezocatalytic performance for water splitting (Supplementary Fig. 21).

The piezocatalytic water splitting activity of P-substituted BIO nanorods indicates their potential in MPs aqueous-phase reforming for the synthesis of value-added chemicals. PET, PLA, and PE were selected as model plastics to evaluate the reactivity of the PIR system. EG, lactic acid (LA), and succinic acid (SA), which are the monomers and derivatives of PET, PLA, and PE, respectively, were examined under ultrasound employing BIO-RP3 as the piezocatalyst. It should be noted that terephthalic acid (TPA) is one of the hydrolysis products of PET, but contributes negligibly to C-based fuel production during PIR (Supplementary Fig. 22). These three plastic derivatives can all be converted into H2, CO, and light hydrocarbons (CH4, C2H4, C2H6, and C2H2) in gas. The total amount of H2 and CO produced by the PIR process of EG (111.3 g/L), LA (47.5 g/L), and SA (50 g/L) reached 17.35, 15.25, and 31.84 mmol⋅g−1 in 2 h, respectively, showcasing the potential for high value conversion of mixed plastic wastes (Fig. 3c). The sharp decrease in gas production in the absence of catalysts ruled out the ultrasound-induced dissociation of substrates (Supplementary Fig. 23). Simulating real-world plastic reforming was performed after a simple crushing and hydrothermal pretreatment. It can be seen that BIO-RP3 demonstrated efficient reforming activity for transforming the derivatives of PET, PLA, and PE to H2 (8.37, 10.76, and 10.59 mmol⋅g−1), CO (1.78, 4.25, and 4.01 mmol⋅g−1), and light hydrocarbons (0.93, 2.24, and 1.37 mmol⋅g−1) in 2h. More importantly, this PIR system is equally efficient for their mixtures, producing H2 and CO of 16.81 mmol⋅g−1 with the selectivity exceeding 89% in gas-phase products (Fig. 3d). This plastic reforming system exhibited a moderate performance decay after four cycles with longer reaction time, as observed in the piezocatalytic H2 production cycles (Supplementary Fig. 24). The high selectivity of H2 and CO is conducive to the further high-value conversion of waste plastics into alcohol fuels.

The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Supplementary Figs. 25–27) were employed to monitor the liquid products of different plastics after PIR reaction. The PIR of PLA and PE derivatives generated no signal of liquid products in the 1H NMR spectra. The proportions of H2 and CO produced by PLA reforming account for 62.36% and 24.66%, respectively, while those produced by PE reforming account for 66.31% and 25.11%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 28). In contrast, for ethylene glycol derived from PET, methanol and ethanol are the major reforming products (accounting for 72.60% of the total product yield). This could be attributed to the multi-step bond-cleavage process during polyol oxidation, which generates numerous molecular fragments and intermediate products. BIO-RP3 demonstrated an efficient PIR activity in 1 g/L PET hydrolysate with the conversion of 9.05%, producing methanol and ethanol at the rate of 91.84 mmol⋅g−1⋅h−1 (Fig. 3e). The mechanical-to-chemical (MTC) conversion efficiency of this PIR system was determined to be 4.96%. It can be seen that higher PET concentration is not conducive to the oxidation of EG monomers, which can be attributed to the quenching of ⋅OH by the increased OH− (Supplementary Fig. 29). Given that gaseous and liquid products are easily separable, and high selectivity towards alcohols, H2, and CO can be achieved for different plastic substrates in the two phases, the piezocatalytic reforming system holds practical potential in the upcycling of plastics.

In situ electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the activation behavior of EG monomers in the PIR process under an argon atmosphere with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap. As shown in Fig. 3f, the ESR spectrum contains a 6-line signal that was assigned to a hydrogen doublet (hyperfine coupling constant (αHβ) = 2.17 mT), in which each line further splits into a nitrogen triplet (αN = 1.52 mT). The coupling to β-hydrogen is more favorable than that to the nitrogen atom, implying that the trapped radical was centered on a carbon atom. The obtained β-hydrogen coupling constant, which reflects the feature of the radicals trapped by DMPO, matches well with that of DMPO/ ⋅ CH2OH radical adduct27, illustrating that the fracture of C-C bonds in EG was materialized to produce methanol. A strong DMPO- ⋅ OH signal was detected under ultrasound, while no DMPO- ⋅ O2− signal peak was observed (Supplementary Fig. 30). This confirms the presence and continuous generation of ⋅OH by piezocatalytic water cleavage, which acts as an active species to oxidize EG. Combining with the reported reaction mechanism of the PET reforming system28–30, the two conversion pathways of EG via the PIR process can be speculated (Fig. 3g). In pathway I, EG first undergoes nucleophile dehydrogenation steps by ⋅OH or holes generated/separated in BIO-RP3 to form the adsorbed HOCH2-CHO* intermediates and release H2, followed by cleavage of the C-C bonds and C-O bonds prior to hydrogenation to generate methanol and ethanol, respectively. The alcohol can proceed further reaction on the catalyst surface (dehydrogenation, C-C cleavage, C-O cleavage) to produce CO and CH4. Pathway II initiates by dehydration in an alkaline environment to form CH3-CHO* intermediates, which subsequently combine with OH− for the obtention of acetate or undergo C-C breaking to generate CO and CH4. Besides, the coupling of carbon-centered free radicals can produce a small amount of C2 light hydrocarbons. Hence, the developed PIR system exhibits precise molecular scissors for the efficient activation of C-H and O-H bonds, boosting the cleavage of C-C and C-O bonds to co-generate H2, CO, and alcohol fuels with a competitive efficiency (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32, and Table2).

Piezoelectricity and charge transfer analysis

The surface band bending induced by the internal polarization field can modulate the surface work function of the semiconductor, manifesting as the variation in surface potential, which can be monitored via Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). As shown in Fig. 4a–c, BIO-R exhibited a larger contact potential difference (CPD) (~45.2 mV) than BIO-S, benefiting from the dipole superposition elicited macroscopic polarization enhancement. The surface potential of BIO-RP3 further increased to 58.5 mV, nearly 3.2-fold higher than that of BIO-S (18.3 mV), indicating that the incorporation of [PO4] groups induces structural distortion and promotes asymmetric charge distribution between [IO3] units and P atoms. This result was corroborated by the piezoelectric force microscope (PFM), where the BIO samples displayed uniform piezoelectric responses (PFM phase and amplitude maps in Supplementary Fig. 33 matched with their morphologies). Notably, BIO-RP3 exhibited the steeper butterfly-shaped amplitude loops compared to BIO-S and BIO-R, signifying its better piezoelectric performance (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 34). The enhanced piezoelectric polarization in BIO-RP3 facilitates the generation of a stronger piezoelectric potential to efficiently drive redox reactions. Finite element method (FEM) simulations of the piezoelectric potential distribution across BIO samples revealed that BIO-RP generates a potential difference of 8.86 V under 100 MPa pressure, surpassing the outputs of BIO-S (4.96 V) and BIO-R (7.74 V), consistent with the PFM results (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 35). Piezo-electrochemical measurements were conducted to investigate the charge transport properties of piezocatalysts. Under ultrasonic vibration, BIO-RP3 demonstrated the highest current density from linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves, and a significantly reduced charge transfer resistance (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 36). Additionally, BIO-RP3 exhibited the highest charge density, as calculated from Mott-Schottky plots, reflecting the optimal piezoelectric activity and efficient charge diffusion capability (Fig. 4g, h and Supplementary Fig. 37). Thus, the phosphorization strategy enhances the crystalline dipole of BIO nanorods, improving the piezoelectric polarization and accelerating the transfer of reductive and oxidative charges to participate in plastic reforming reactions.

Polarization reorientation

Experimental and simulated results confirmed that [PO4] doping enhances the piezoelectric polarization of BIO nanorods, despite disrupting the aligned orientation of polar [IO3] units. Exploration of the microscopic mechanisms underlying polarization enhancement is necessary. DFT calculation of the electrostatic potential (EP) distribution along [010] direction was performed to quantify the built-in electric field in BIO-R, BIO-RP, and pressurized BIO-RP. As shown in Fig. 5a, the EP of BIO-R exhibited mirror-symmetric distribution along the b-axis, generating no electrostatic potential difference (△V). It arises from the cancellation of the dipoles generated by [IO3] and stereoactive lone-pairs of Bi3+ along the b-axis, resulting in a net dipole moment of zero. Upon P substitution for I atoms in BIO, the distribution of EP showed obvious asymmetric characteristics, generating a potential difference of 2.05 eV in the b-axis (Fig. 5b), indicating that P atoms disrupt the symmetric dipole arrangement in BIO-R and induce a built-in electric field perpendicular to the exposed facets. This field was further amplified under stress (△V2 = 2.20 eV), providing a strong driving force for charge separation and transfer along the [010] direction (Fig. 5c). Calculations of dipole moments along different crystallographic axes in BIO samples further elucidated the origin of the built-in field (Fig. 5h). BIO-R exhibited a dipole moment of 6.51 eÅ along the c-axis, attributed to the superposition and compensation of dipoles in the vertical direction. Notably, P substitution induced a larger dipole moment along the b-axis (6.86 eÅ) compared to the c-axis (3.29 eÅ). This can be ascribed to the nearly zero net dipole of the symmetric [PO4] groups, which act as polarization vacancies to disrupt the symmetric dipole distribution of [IO3] colony along the b-axis, leading to incomplete dipole neutralization (Supplementary Fig. 38). Evidently, [PO4] incorporation not only enhances the crystal polarity of BIO but also rotates its polarization direction, favoring the migration of active charges toward exposed facets. Under stress, the dipole moments of BIO-RP further increase to 9.35 eÅ (b-axis) and 5.90 eÅ (c-axis), consistent with the EP distribution results.

The polarity of BIO crystals is primarily contributed by [IO3] units, whose local structural evolution is modulated by [PO4] groups and stress induces the transitions in polar axis orientation and polarization intensity. It can be revealed by Raman mapping and in-situ high-pressure (ISHP) Raman spectroscopy. For BIO samples, Raman peaks at 678.8 and 702.6 cm−1 correspond to symmetric stretching vibrations (v3 [IO3]), while the peak at 775.4 cm−1 is assigned to asymmetric stretching vibrations (v1 [IO3]). The v1 [IO3]/v3 [IO3] ratio can reflect the dynamic evolution of I–O bond in polar units. Raman mapping of P-substituted BIO nanorods exhibited a higher v1 [IO3]/v3 [IO3] ratio compared to BIO-R, with uniform distribution across the scanned regions, indicating that the enhanced interactions between [IO3] and [PO4] groups via I-OB-P bonding induce structural distortion in [IO3] units (Fig. 5d). Under the high pressure, the v3 [IO3] peak of BIO-RP3 at 702.6 cm−1 progressively weakened and eventually vanished, while the vibrational band at 678.8 cm−1 shifted significantly. The v1 [IO3] peak gradually shifted to higher wavenumbers, accompanied by the emergence of a new Raman peak at 3.7 GPa (Fig. 5e–g). These anomalous changes of Raman peaks typically indicate an increase in the average I-O bond length31, exacerbating the degree of disorder in the crystal, which disappeared after atmospheric pressure was restored. Thus, the external stress will further promote structural distortion of [IO3] units, enhance their polarity, and generate more significant asymmetric dipole arrangements. The dipole moment of [PO4] groups displays a negligible alteration before and after pressurization, excluding the influence of their structural distortion on the polarization state (Supplementary Fig. 39). Collectively, these results demonstrate that [PO4] groups not only act as polarization vacancies to induce asymmetric dipole alignment along the b-axis but also trigger structural distortion in neighboring [IO3] units via I-OB-P bonding, amplifying the asymmetry feature and overcoming the see-saw effect of BIO-R. Moreover, [PO4] substituted BIO nanosheets (BIO-SP3) were successfully prepared, which also demonstrate improved polarization properties and piezocatalytic activity, further validating the effectiveness of this strategy (Supplementary Figs. 40–43). The acoustic pressure generated by ultrasonic cavitation further strengthens these effects, increasing the distortion degree of [IO3] units and enhancing crystal polarity (Fig. 5i).

Dynamic frustrated Lewis acid-base pairs

In terms of the established plastic PIR system, activating H2O molecules to form ⋅OH, which contributes to the dehydrogenation oxidation of EG monomers, is crucial for the production of platform chemicals. To explore the crucial role of [PO4] substitution in the H2O adsorption and activation of BIO, the local electronic structures of catalysts were analyzed by electron localization function (ELF) and Bader charge analysis (Supplementary Fig. 44). In BIO-R, the [IO3] groups are arranged evenly and isolated from each other, without any interactions or charge transfer between the groups (Fig. 6a). In contrast, an obvious charge dissipation region around the P atom can be observed in BIO-RP model, revealing that the replaced P atom is able to serve as an electron donor to increase the electron density of connected oxygen atoms (OB and OP). The electron density of OB (−7.21 e) and OP (−7.38 e) in BIO-RP increases significantly compared to that of BIO-R, altering the oxidation state of I atoms in adjacent [IO3] groups via the chemical bonding of I-OB, which manifests as the increment of Bader charge of I atom from +4.56 e in BIO-R to +4.61 e in BIO-RP (Fig. 6b). Combined with the optimized structural models of H2O and proton (*H) adsorbed BIO-RP, it can be demonstrated that I atoms in [IO3] and OP atoms in [PO4] can act as the Lewis acid (LA) and base (LB) sites, respectively, forming a I-O-H-OP configuration and enhancing the H2O adsorption and H-O bond cleavage through the typical push-pull effect. Moreover, BIO-RP exhibits higher electron density and acid-base properties of the steric hindered I and OP atoms compared with BIO-R, indicating more active features for the frustrated Lewis acid–base pairs (FLPs). With the applied stress, the electron deficiency of P atoms becomes more pronounced, and the distance between I and OP atoms increases from 3.32 Å to 3.55 Å (Fig. 6c, d). The dynamic changes in the geometrical and electronic structures of BIO-RP under ultrasonically induced periodic stress will further suppress the acid-base neutralization at the sites, lowering the activation energy for water splitting by facilitating tensile fracture of the H-O bond.

Pyridine-IR (Py-IR) spectra were collected to distinguish LA sites and Brønsted acid (BA) sites in BIO-RP3 (Fig. 6e). A typical characteristic peak of LA sites at 1448 cm−1 was observed, while only a weak peak of BA sites at 1541 cm−1 appeared, proving that the acid sites mainly existed in the form of LA32. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) was calculated to clarify the orbital interactions between FLPs and adsorbed H2O. It can be seen that BIO-RP exhibits noticeable bonding states in I-O via the orbital interactions between I 5p and O 2p in H2O, as well as the enhanced orbital distributions of OP 2p-H 1 s to form the OP-H bonds (Fig. 6g). Such the strong orbital hybridization of I 5p-O 2p is also validated by the projected density of state (PDOS) analysis, which reveals that the 5p orbital of I site displays a large amount of overlap with the 2p orbital of O in H2O (Fig. 6h). This calculation result is intrinsically consistent with the adsorption mechanism between FLPs and H2O molecules, due to the O and H atoms in H2O are regarded as the classic Lewis pair to react with LA (I atoms) and LB (OP atoms) sites, respectively. Hence, the charge redistribution and structural distortion induced by [PO4] substitution in BIO generates highly active FLP sites (I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP) to enhance the adsorption of H2O, which were contributed by the p-p orbital interactions of I-O and s-p hybridization of OP-H shaping the I-O-H-OP reaction channel.

The strong interaction between H2O molecules and BIO samples can be visualized by the charge density difference analysis. As shown in Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 45-47, an obvious charge enrichment and depletion occurs at the interface of OP-H and I-O, respectively, and the charge transfer level of BIO-RP is greater than that of BIO. The O atom in H2O molecule donates 0.19 e− to the LA site of I, while the electron-deficient H atom accepts 0.22 e− from the LB site of OP, consistent with the -pCOHP results. This charge transfer with push-pull characteristic can be further intensified with the applied stress, which contributes to the improved electron exchange by the piezoelectric field at the interface. In addition, the stress lengthened I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP sites further increase the bond length and bond angle of H2O molecule to 1.02 Å and 104.92°, respectively, promoting the cleavage of H-OH bond and formation of ⋅OH. The *H will be adsorbed at the OP site and combine with each other or be reduced by the electron from LB sites to produce H2. The increased charge transfer level between *H and OP sites further demonstrates the important role of the piezoelectric field (Supplementary Figs. 48–51). Thus, [PO4] induced I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP FLPs improve the adsorption and electron transfer of H2O on BIO-R, which is further dynamically enhanced by the piezoelectric field generated by the periodic stress, resulting in the lowering of adsorption energy and activation energy barriers, ultimately enhancing H2 and ⋅OH evolution activity (Supplementary Figs. 52–55).

In general, P substitution not only enhances the polarization strength and rotates the polarization direction in BIO-R by inducing structural distortion of [IO3] units and breaking the symmetric arrangement of dipoles, but also introduces I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP FLPs to enhance the adsorption and activation of H2O with the I-O-H-OP reaction channel. Under periodic stress, the structural asymmetry of BIO-RP is further strengthened, generating a stronger polarized electric field that promotes electron exchange at the reaction interface, dynamically modulating the distance between the LA and LB sites to lower the activation energy for water splitting and ⋅OH formation. As a result, the C-H and O-H bonds in plastic monomers and their derivatives can be effectively activated, accelerating the cleavage of C-C and C-O bonds for co-production of H2 and CO via the PIR system.

Discussion

In summary, an efficient PIR system was developed for producing valued chemicals from PET, PLA, PE, and their mixtures by applying the [PO4] substituted BIO nanorods as delicate piezocatalysts, in which P atoms substituted I atoms in BIO and bonded with adjacent O atoms in [IO3] units, forming an I-OB-P bonding configuration that stabilizes P species as [PO4] tetrahedra. The [PO4] tetrahedra act as polarization vacancies to disrupt the symmetric dipole alignment of [IO3] units along the [010] direction and induce their structural distortion. This promotes polarization reorientation and enhancement in BIO nanorods, providing a strong driving force for rapid charge transfer to exposed facets and overcoming the see-saw effect between the effective active sites and the piezoelectric polarization intensity. Additionally, the interaction between [PO4] and [IO3] groups triggers charge redistribution, generating reactive I ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ OP FLPs on the surface and increasing the density of active sites. Under periodic mechanical stress, the amplified piezoelectric polarization, improved interfacial charge transfer, and dynamic stretching of FLPs synergistically enhance the activation of H2O molecules. As a result, the prepared catalyst reformed the mixed plastics into H2 and CO (16.81 mmol⋅g−1 in 2 h) with selectivity over 87% in gas. Especially for PET, methanol and ethanol can be directly obtained in liquid form, contributing to a high MTC efficiency of 4.96%. This work is conducive to advancing mechanically-driven plastic reforming technologies.

Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this work are of analytical grade (AR) and utilized without further treatment. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure water. Bismuth nitrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, AR) was obtained from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. Iodine pentoxide (I2O5, AR) was purchased by Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. Nitric acid (HNO3, 65.0-68.0%) and sodium hypophosphite monohydrate (NaH2PO2·H2O, AR) were supplied by Beijing Chemical Industry Group Co., Ltd. Potassium iodide (KI, AR), Ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate (H32Mo7N6O28, AR), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, AR), ethylene glycol (EG, 98.0%), methanol (MeOH, 99.5%), ethanol (EtOH, 99.8%), sodium acetate (C2H3NaO2, AR), DL-lactic acid (LA, 80.0%), and succinic acid (SA, AR) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Terephthalic acid (TPA, AR) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of BIO-S and BIO-R

Sheet-shaped BIO-S and long rod-shaped BIO-R were synthesized via a one-pot hydrothermal approach. In a typical synthesis procedure, 3 mmol of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O and 1.5 mmol of I2O5 were dispersed in a mixture composed of 35 ml of deionized water and a specific volume (4 mL or 0 mL) of 65.0-68.0% HNO3. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 hour. Subsequently, the resultant suspension was transferred into a 50 ml stainless steel high-pressure vessel lined with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and then heated in an oven at 453 K for 18 hours. The powder was collected by centrifugation, washed alternately with distilled ethanol and water three times, and finally dried at 333 K for one night.

Synthesis of BIO-RPX (X = 1-5)

A series of BIO-RP samples were synthesized by a mild phosphorization treatment under an inert atmosphere. Among them, BIO-RP1, BIO-RP2, and BIO-RP3 were heated at 573 K (3 K/min) under Ar conditions, with a mass ratio of BIO/ NaH2PO2·H2O = 2.5:1, in which calcination times of 1 hour, 2 hours, and 3 hours, respectively. Meanwhile, the products with different mass ratios (BIO/NaH2PO2·H2O = 1.5:1 and 1:1) were annealí under Ar at 573 K (3 K/min) for 3 h to obtain BIO-RP4 and BIO-RP5. After annealing, the above samples need to be washed several times with alcohol and deionized water, and dried at 333 K for one night.

Catalyst characterization

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted on a Bruker D8 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation to probe the phase structures of catalysts. Microstructural features of the piezocatalysts were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon). JEM-2100 (JEOL) was employed to acquire transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) images. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was performed on a Talos F200X (FEI). The electronic structures and chemical states of catalysts were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using an ESCALAB 250Xi instrument (Thermo Fisher). Piezoelectric properties of BIO nano catalysts were recorded via AFM equipped with Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) and piezoelectric force microscope (PFM) modules. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) were conducted to analyze the band structures of catalysts within BaSO4 as a reference (Varian Cary 5000). Raman spectra of samples were acquired on LabRAM HR Evolution confocal Raman microscope with 532 nm laser excitation. 1H NMR spectra before and after the reaction were recorded on 600 MHz Agilent spectrometer.

Piezo-electrochemical test

Piezo-electrochemical tests for charge dynamics characterizations were carried out using a CHI760E electrochemical workstation. A three-electrode system was assembled by applying platinum (Pt) as the counter electrode, Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference electrode, and 30 mL of 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution as the electrolyte. To fabricate the working electrodes, 10 mg of piezocatalysts were dispersed into 1 mL of ethanol by ultrasound. The homogeneous suspension was uniformly coated onto a conductive adhesive attached to an indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrate with an area of 25 × 40 mm, and subsequently allowed to dry naturally in air for 12 hours33. The three-electrode system was placed in an ultrasonic cleaner (100 W, 40 kHz) for electrochemical measurements. Linear sweep voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. The frequency range of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was set from 0.01 Hz to 1 MHz, with an applied amplitude of 5 mV. All potentials are not iR corrected unless otherwise specified.

Piezocatalytic water splitting experiment

In a standard procedure, 10 mg of the piezocatalyst was dispersed into 100 mL of water under stirring. After complete sealing and vacuuming, the reaction vessel was purged with high-purity argon (Ar) gas. The piezocatalytic water splitting experiment was conducted in an ultrasonic cleaner (240 W, 40 kHz) accompanied by frequent water replacement, maintaining the reaction temperature within the range of 288–293 K. 1 mL of gas was sampled from the vessel after reaction, and the H2 was quantified by gas chromatography (GC 7900, Tianmei) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). For H2O2 detection, 500 µL of centrifuged reaction suspension was mixed with 50 µL of 0.01 M H32Mo7N6O28 solution (100 mL) and 2 mL of 0.1 M KI solution (500 mL), followed by a 10-minute reaction. The absorbance at 352 nm measured by a UV-visible spectrophotometer can be identified as H2O2. The concentration of ⋅OH was detected by a fluorescence spectrophotometer with the aid of terephthalic acid (TPA). In this procedure, 100 mL of TPA solution (0.05 mmol TPA in a 2 mM NaOH solution) was used to replace water for the piezocatalytic experiment. After the reaction, 5 mL of the liquid was collected to measure the fluorescence spectrum at an excitation wavelength of 315 nm.

Substrate pretreatment

A total of 2 g of PET powder was soaked in 40 mL of 2 M NaOH aqueous in a sealed vial for 12 h at 333 K with stirring to obtain the hydrolysis solution. 2 g of PLA powder was dispersed in 40 mL of water by a hydrothermal reaction at 433 K for 10 h. 0.6 g PE powder was dispersed in 15 ml 7% nitric acid solution and maintained at 433 K for 8 h.

Plastic PIR experiment

The experiments of piezocatalytic plastic reforming are similar to that of water splitting, except that the water is replaced with 100 mL of a plastic derivative solution in which 5 mg of catalyst is dispersed. The produced H2, CO, CH4, C2H4, C2H6 and C2H2 were detected by gas chromatography (Shimadzu GC-2014) equipped with both a flame ionization detector (FID) and a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The oxidation products were quantitatively measured by 1H NMR spectra. The NMR samples were prepared by mixing 540 µL solution after piezocatalysis with 60 µL D2O. The peaks of D2O in all 1H NMR spectra were removed from 4.0 to 5.5 ppm. Calibration curves for each product were constructed using standard solutions of determined concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 25).
