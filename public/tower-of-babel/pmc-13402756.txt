Improving the elevated temperature behaviour of foamed concrete through nano titania addition and microstructure control
Authors: Md Azree Othuman Mydin, Dina E. Tobbala, Roshartini Omar, Khairunisa Muthusamy, Blessen Skariah Thomas, Mohd Mustafa Al Bakri Abdullah, Paul O. Awoyera, Olaolu George Fadugba, Nadhim Hamah Sor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effectiveness of nano-titania (NT) as a nano-additive for enhancing the performance and elevated-temperature resistance of foamed concrete (FC). The novelty of this research lies in the comprehensive evaluation of the residual mechanical, transport, thermal, and microstructural behaviour of NT-modified FC after exposure to temperatures of up to 800 °C. NT was incorporated at dosages of 0.5–2.0% by weight of cement, and the performance of the resulting FC was assessed in terms of fresh-state properties, shrinkage, mechanical strength, ultrasonic pulse velocity, transport behaviour, thermal properties, and microstructural characteristics. The results showed that NT reduced workability and shortened setting time, while significantly improving the overall performance of FC under both ambient and elevated-temperature conditions. Among the tested mixtures, the incorporation of 1.5% NT provided the optimum performance. At room temperature, this dosage increased the compressive strength by approximately 40% compared with the control mix. More importantly, after exposure to 800 °C, the residual compressive strength ratio increased markedly from 18% for the control FC to 57% for the FC containing 1.5% NT, indicating a substantial enhancement in thermal resistance. NT also improved the pore structure by reducing the average pore diameter from 580 to 440 µm at 20 °C and from 820 to 620 µm at 800 °C, which contributed to lower permeability and porosity, as well as improved thermal stability. Microstructural observations from SEM and MIP confirmed that NT promoted a denser and more homogeneous matrix through pore refinement and improved hydration development, leading to enhanced residual performance after high-temperature exposure. Overall, the findings demonstrate that NT is an effective nano-modifier for producing FC with superior strength, durability, and fire resistance, with 1.5% NT identified as the optimum dosage.

Introduction

Concrete is widely acknowledged as one of the most utilized construction materials worldwide. The usage of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) is growing to a degree that it is estimated that by the year 2050, the need for this primary binder in the manufacture of concrete will rise by 200% more than in 2010, reaching a total of 6 billion tons annually. The growing demand for concrete is attributed to its robustness, resilience, and durability in the face of threats from the environment. Nevertheless, due to its inherent permeability, concrete remains susceptible to environmental hazards. Thus, it is imperative to guarantee both the robustness and effectiveness of a concrete in order to enhance its lifespan1. Concrete is a hybrid material that exhibits fast changes in its characteristics when exposed to elevated temperatures. Elevated temperatures commonly result in the development of interfacial ruptures in concrete. Nevertheless, these gaps can be repaired through post-fire curing. Restoring bridging cracks can significantly impact the regaining of its strength properties2. Multiple investigations have already been conducted to assess the concrete performance when subjected to elevated temperatures and fire. Besides, supplementary cementing materials (SCMs) were employed to boost the concrete properties3. When cementitious composites are exposed to high temperatures, they lose their mechanical strength, crack, and explode. Thermal incompatibility among constituents, dehydration of hydration products, and vapor pressure accumulation within dense matrices govern these phenomena. Recent research has methodically examined the thermo-mechanical behavior of cement-based and alkali-activated mortars subjected to rapid heating and fire-like scenarios, demonstrating significant decreases in compressive and tensile strength and emphasizing spalling as a pivotal failure mechanism, especially in high-strength systems3. At the same time, the construction sector is under increasing pressure to improve sustainability by incorporating waste-derived and low-density constituents without sacrificing safety and serviceability4. A recent state-of-the-art review on e-waste in concrete construction showed that electronic waste can be reused in cementitious composites as aggregate, fibers, and glass, providing opportunities for natural resource conservation, lighter composites, and improved thermal functionality, although important trade-offs in mechanical performance, durability, and design standardization remain unresolved5.

With the ongoing advancement of modern urbanization and the growing number of structures, ensuring the fireproofing of structures has become a crucial matter. During a fire, the different parts of a building are susceptible to being damaged to different extents and may even be at risk of collapsing, which creates a significant danger to occupants and the buildings themselves6. Thus, it is imperative to guarantee the fire-resistant performance of buildings throughout the construction phase of diverse structures and infrastructure projects. Expanded polystyrene (EPS) materials have been employed extensively in creating porous materials and improving thermal insulation. Studies involving cement and geopolymer mortar modified by EPS materials indicate enhanced insulation properties and decreased susceptibility to spalling, especially when mixed with alkaline activators. These properties were, however, strongly dependent on pore interconnectivity and the composition of the matrices. In addition, recent field-based infrastructure assessments have highlighted that building safety should be interpreted in a broader multi-hazard resilience framework, since poor construction quality, insufficient maintenance, and structural irregularities continue to amplify the vulnerability of the built environment under extreme events7.

An effective method to improve the properties of concrete under high temperatures is to include nanoparticles into it. This technique originated in the last decade of the 1980s and has been more focused and widespread since the beginning of the modern era. The latest innovations in technology have sparked substantial interest in utilizing nanoparticles as a strengthening substance in concrete. The unique chemical and physical characteristics of nanoparticles can augment the qualities of concrete at micro and nanoscale when exposed to high temperatures, leading to improved performance. Nanoparticles exhibit reactivity due to their broader surface area and diminutive particle size. This reactivity serves to fortify the transitional layer within mixed mortar, which leads to enhanced strength properties and reduced permeability8. Furthermore, nanoparticles possess significant advantages in increasing the fire-resistant properties of concrete9. The use of nanomaterials has been found to be a promising method for the improvement of durability and residual strengths of concrete under extreme exposure. Nano-silica, nano-clay, nanocellulose, and carbon nanomaterials have been found to have a refining effect on the pore structure of concrete, which helps in enhancing the post-fire resistance of concrete.

Foamed concrete (FC) is produced from a mortar paste which comprises evenly distributed air bubbles created by protein or synthetic foaming agents10,11. Its benefits include easy construction, self-flowing, excellent thermal and acoustic insulation. In civil engineering, it is used most as filling materials. Nevertheless, its acoustic and thermal properties imply it might be good building material. Although it has lower mechanical properties than regular concrete, FC can serve as a wall panel in low-rise residential structures. For FC to fully optimize its capabilities in building construction, it is crucial that its mechanical and thermal qualities are reliable and consistent, at both ambient and high temperatures. Although several studies have examined the mechanical and thermal characteristics of lightweight aggregate concrete at both high and ambient temperatures, there is hardly any systematic study on FC at higher temperatures. In this numerical and experimental work, the thermal characteristics of FC at high temperatures were examined and determined. Like regular weight concrete, FC requires a lot of energy to liberate free water in the pores and some chemically bound water in the hydrated cement paste at high temperatures. A few authors have written about the high-temperature reactions in cement-based materials such as FC. Around 100 ºC, free water evaporates. Evaporable water normally evaporates about 120 ºC. Chemically bound water is released when C–S–H and carboaluminate hydrates breakdown between 180 and 300 °C9. Hardened FC paste may react at temperatures as high as 400–600 °C. Decomposition of the hydration product and breakdown of the C–S–H gel follow complete desiccation of the pore system6. Because lime expands, heating calcium hydroxide into lime and water vapor can result in catastrophic damage12,13. These modifications will impact on the thermal characteristics of FC. The thermal conductivity of FC is influenced by the presence of air pores, which contribute to its porous nature. Air pores are governed by the principles of low temperature heat conduction. The coefficient of radiant heat transfer is directly related to the cube of the temperature. Consequently, radiation becomes increasingly significant at elevated temperatures. The effective thermal conductivity of FC at high temperatures is influenced by the radiation effects occurring within air pores, as well as the thermal conductivities associated with cement and air14. Alkali-activated and geopolymer binders have also been proven to possess greater thermal stability compared to ordinary Portland cement. The reason behind this improved thermal stability of alkali-activated binders lies in their lower calcium content coupled with greater resistance of their aluminosilicate gel frameworks to thermal conditions. Research studies on alkali-activated slag and one-component alkali-activated concrete have proven residual strength and microdamage resistance to high temperatures15.

Nanoparticle reinforced concrete has the prospective to reform the building industry. The presence of nanoparticles in cementitious material improves the bending properties, workability of mix, chemical-resistant, and promote environmentally friendly concrete. Nanotechnology has enabled the production of cementitious composites that are multifunctional, customized, and possess exceptional mechanical and durability properties. These materials demonstrate exceptional ductility, qualities that regulate fractures, the ability to mend themselves, low electrical conductivity, the ability to detect themselves, and self-cleaning abilities. Nanoparticles possess dimensions that are less than 100 nm in diameter. The addition of NT to reactive powder concrete can strengthen it by lowering its placement of Ca (OH)2, enhancing its rigidity, and producing pores and microcracks. Different hydration stages of cement can be strengthened with the right amount of NT16. NT based concrete had better mechanical performance and durability because of the C–S–H gel and NT barrier. Following impregnation, the application of sprayed-on NT effectively eliminated methyl orange and acetaldehyde17. Concrete’s long-term chlorine resistance properties with the presence of NT increase its durability performance18. NT encourages carbonization of CH. Room-temperature pore size, homogeneity, and sphericity are all reduced by NT as demonstrated by three-dimensional X-Ray imaging and encouraging CH carbonation is NT. Furthermore, for hydration, NT seems to increase strength. At 20 °C, 3D X-Ray microscopy reveals that NT produces spherical, homogenous, and smaller holes. In this way, nanoparticles will be strengthened19.

Previous research has shown that nano-titanium dioxide (nano-TiO₂) can greatly change the microstructure and performance of cementitious materials. However, we still don’t know enough about how it works in FC, especially at high temperatures. In traditional concrete and mortar systems, nano-TiO₂ has been shown to speed up the hydration of cement, make the pore structure more refined, and make the interfacial transition zone denser. This leads to better mechanical properties and better resistance to thermal damage at moderate temperatures (≤ 400–600 °C)20. Research on FC without nanomodification shows that high temperatures cause pore coarsening, more air voids to connect, hydration products to break down, and a big drop in residual mechanical strength, especially above 600 °C21,22. Some studies have shown that adding nano-TiO₂ to FC or lightweight cementitious composites can make the average pore size smaller and the matrix more compact at room temperature23. However, not much research has been done on how this modified pore system changes when the temperature rises. Furthermore, current research frequently examines thermal conductivity or room-temperature strength in isolation, failing to connect microstructural alterations to residual performance post-heating. As a result, there is still no systematic understanding of how nano-TiO₂ affects the thermal stability, pore structure evolution, and mechanical performance of FC after exposure. This gap must be addressed to evaluate the appropriateness of nano-TiO₂-modified FC in fire-exposed or high-temperature contexts, which constitutes the principal objective of the current study22.

The novelty of this study lies in its systematic investigation of the residual damage mechanisms and thermal stability of nano-titanium dioxide (NT)–modified FC after exposure to elevated temperatures. Unlike previous research that focused either on the ambient performance of NT-modified cementitious materials or the elevated-temperature of unmodified FC, this work directly links NT-induced pore refinement to high temperature mechanical, thermal, and transport performance. The study provides the first integrated experimental evidence demonstrating how NT mitigates heat-induced pore coarsening, microcrack development, and permeability increase in FC at temperatures up to 800 °C. Furthermore, it identifies an optimal NT dosage that maximizes residual strength and thermal stability while limiting deterioration under severe thermal exposure. In normal conditions, NT speeds up hydration, improves pore structure, and lowers permeability and shrinkage in traditional concrete and FC. However, too much NT can cause nanoparticles to clump together and make them less efficient. A 3 wt.% NT dosage makes the material more evenly spread out, denser, and more durable at room temperature. High temperatures produce pore coarsening, dehydration of hydration products, microcracking, and a considerable loss of residual strength, especially over 600 °C. This is not the case with unaltered FC. Most of the research done so far has been on the ambient conditions of NT-modified FC or the behavior of untreated FC at high temperatures. We don’t know much about how NT affects thermal stability, damage development, and performance after exposure. It is not known how heat-induced deterioration changes the NT-refined pore topologies20. This study investigates the remaining mechanical, thermal, transport, and microstructural properties of NT-modified FC following exposure to extreme temperatures to address this deficiency. This study connects NT-induced microstructural refinement to high-temperature damage mechanisms and residual performance, showing how nano-modified FC may resist heat in applications where it would be exposed to elevated-temperature.

The selected NT dosages of 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0% by weight of cement were chosen based on previous studies on nano-modified cementitious materials, which reported that small amounts of nanoparticles can significantly improve matrix densification, hydration activity, and pore refinement28,29. However, excessive nanoparticle incorporation may cause agglomeration, reduce workability, and adversely affect the uniformity of the cementitious matrix. Therefore, the selected range was intended to capture both the beneficial effects of NT and the possible reduction in performance at higher dosages, allowing the optimum content to be identified experimentally.

Foamed concrete mix design

The FC mixtures were prepared using a controlled step-by-step procedure to ensure uniformity and reproducibility. Figure 4 shows the FC production process First, the dry materials, consisting of ordinary Portland cement (OPC), fine sand, and nano-titania (NT), were thoroughly dry-mixed for approximately 2–3 min to achieve a homogeneous distribution of particles. NT was incorporated as a partial addition by weight of cement at the specified dosages (0–2.0%). Subsequently, approximately 70% of the total mixing water was gradually added to the dry mixture and mixed for an additional 3–5 min until a consistent mortar slurry was obtained. The remaining water was then introduced to adjust the workability and ensure proper dispersion of NT within the matrix. In parallel, preformed foam was generated using a protein-based foaming agent and a Portafoam TM-2 foam generator (Fig. 5a), producing stable foam with uniform bubble size (Fig. 5b). The generated foam was gently added into the mortar slurry and mixed at low speed to avoid bubble collapse (Fig. 5c). The incorporation of foam was continued until the target fresh density of 1000 kg/m3 was achieved, which was verified using a standard density test. The fresh FC mixture was then immediately cast into steel moulds of the required dimensions and lightly compacted to remove large, entrapped air voids without disturbing the foam structure. The specimens were covered to prevent moisture loss and demoulded after 24 h. Thereafter, all specimens were sealed and cured under control conditions until the testing age of 28 days. NT additions (1–2%) improve mechanical properties, densify microstructure, and improve functional characteristics without affecting workability. Higher contents may cause detrimental effects due to nanoparticle agglomeration and processing challenges. Optimizing high-performance cementitious matrices revealed peak mechanical and photocatalytic performance near 2% TiO₂, at which point benefits plateau or reverse due to dispersion difficulties and poor fresh-state workability30. Systematic evaluations indicate that nanoscale TiO₂ increases viscosity and reduces slump with higher content, indicating dose limits. Excessive nanoparticles might cause clustering, compromising load transfer and composite integrity31. The higher dosages (> 2%) risk agglomeration, poor dispersion, and material workability and performance32. Elevated temperature of specimens

An electric furnace was employed to heat the samples to different predefined temperatures, as depicted in Fig. 7. The kiln temperature was adjusted to follow the typical cellulose combustion profile. Figure 8 illustrates the time–temperature profile of the electric kiln in comparison to the normal cellulose fire curve. After 28 days of curing, the specimens were exposed to elevated temperatures of 20, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, and 800 °C using an electric furnace. The furnace temperature was controlled by a programmed processor and monitored using a Type K thermocouple, with a temperature accuracy of ± 0.5 °C. The specimens were heated to the target temperature and maintained at that temperature for 30 min. An unstressed residual test method was adopted, in which specimens were heated without preload, cooled to room temperature, and then tested to determine their residual properties. Fresh-state properties included slump flow and setting time; transport properties included water absorption, apparent porosity, and intrinsic air permeability; mechanical properties included compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, Young’s modulus, and ultrasonic pulse velocity; pore-structure analyses included mass loss, SEM, and MIP; and thermal properties included thermal conductivity, specific heat, and thermal diffusivity. Fresh-state properties

The workability of FC was assessed using a flow table test, following the guidelines outlined in ASTM C230/C230M-1433. The determination of the setting time of FC paste involves the utilization of a Vicat needle to measure the penetrating resistance, adhere to the regulations outlined in BS EN 196-3 (2016)34.

Transport properties

A water absorption test, an intrinsic air permeability test, and a fast chloride permeability test were the three independent tests that were carried out to establish the transport characteristics of various FC combinations. An initial drying process was performed on the samples that had been prepared. This process was carried out at a temperature of 105 °C for a period of 24 h, or until the weights of the samples reached a state of equilibrium. In addition, the evaluation of the intrinsic air permeability was carried out with the use of FC cylinders that were constructed in accordance with the criteria that were supplied by Cabrera et al.35 and had the dimensions of 45 mm in diameter, and the height is 50 mm. A disc formed of FC with dimensions of 100 mm in diameter and 50 mm in thickness was used to conduct a fast chloride permeability test in compliance with ASTM C120236.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of FC were evaluated using compression, splitting tensile, flexural and Young’s modulus test. The compression test was performed in accordance with the standards outlined in BS12390-337. A GOTECH-7001-M5 universal testing machine was used, which can handle 3000 kN. The load speed was 0.5 N/mm2/second. The evaluation employed cylindrical samples with dimensions of 100 × 200 mm2. A flexural test was conducted using a GOTECH-7001-C10 on a prism with dimensions of 100 × 100 × 500 mm, adhering to the specifications provided in BS12390-538 standard. The tensile tests were carried out in accordance with the protocols that were outlined in the BS12390-639 standard, Using GOTECH-7001-BS300 universal testing machine on cylindrical specimens of 100 × 200 mm2. The specimens of the previous tests were tested after 28 days of curing. After a period of 28 days, the Young’s modulus of the UFC specimens was evaluated according to the specifications provided in the ASTM C469/C469M-1440 standard. The evaluation employed cylindrical samples with dimensions of 100 × 200 mm2. Three specimens were employed to ascertain the average values. The ultrasonic pulse velocity test was conducted according to BS12504-441 specifications. At 28 days of age, a prism measuring 100 × 100 × 500 mm was tested.

Mass loss and pore structure assessment

A series of tests were conducted to assess the pore structure of the FC. The investigations comprised of measuring mass loss, scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging, analyzing pore size distribution, and calculating permeable porosity. By employing electron microscopy, the precise positioning and organization of FC pores were successfully ascertained. SEM was used to analyze the FC specimens’ morphology. Following ISO 1670042, the experiment utilized samples with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm utilizing a Quanta FEG 650 microscope in conjunction with an Oxford Instruments X-Max. In order to gain a deeper comprehension of the distribution of the pores in various FC mixtures, an assessment was conducted using mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) in line with ASTM D4404-184.

Thermal properties

Through an investigation on the thermal characteristics of FC, three key parameters were determined specifically the thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity and thermal diffusivity. The thermal properties were determined using the guarded-hot-plate method, which involves monitoring the electrical power output of a hot plate to facilitate directional heat transmission. As per this technique, the principles specified in the ASTM C177-1944 are followed. The FC samples utilized for testing had dimensions of 22 × 22 × 12 mm.

Results and discussion

The improvement in the performance of FC with the incorporation of NT can be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. Due to its ultra-fine particle size and high surface area, NT acts as a filler material, occupying micro-voids within the cement matrix and leading to a denser microstructure. In addition, NT promotes nucleation sites for hydration products, accelerating the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and improving interfacial bonding. This results in reduced porosity, enhanced matrix integrity, and improved resistance to thermal and mechanical degradation. The following sections will discuss the effect on NT on varying properties.

Fresh-state properties

Slump flow diameter

The slump flow diameter of FC mixes containing different proportions of NT was measured and compared to the mix without NT, as depicted in Fig. 9. The addition of 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%, NT resulted in a reduction in flow diameter of 0.4%, 1.2%, 1.6%, and 2.0%, respectively. It has been shown that chemical bonding processes and mix adjustments may decrease the viscosity of cementitious materials when nanomaterials are combined with cementitious materials because of their large specific surface areas. The phenomenon can be elucidated by the nano-particle size of NT in comparison to cement, resulting in greater water absorption in NT paste than in cement paste. On the other hand, because CaCO3 in the cement matrix can improve the workability of cementitious composites, it is possible that the NT addition had no significant effect on the workability of cementitious composites, as pointed out by Sobhy et al. and Ying et al.45,46. This clarifies why there is a little decrease in FC fluidity correlated with an increase in NT concentration47.Figure 9Slump flow diameter of NT-FC.

Slump flow diameter of NT-FC.

Initial and final setting time

NT, with its extensive surface area and strong affinity for water, modifies the arrangement of unbound water in cement-based materials, hence affecting the duration of the setting process. Figure 10 demonstrates that the inclusion of NT resulted in a decrease in both the initial and final setting times. Furthermore, the reduction percentages of both times rose as the percentages of NT increased. At a concentration of 2%, the addition of NT resulted in a 22.05% decrease in the initial setting time of the cement paste and an 11.7% decrease in its ultimate setting time. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Ma et al.48. The studies conducted by Chen and Lee49,50 reported that cementitious materials have shorter setting and hardening durations when the NT content is higher and the particle size is lower. The rapid increase in water absorption rate due to NT 's large specific surface area is the primary cause of the reduced setting time. In addition, NT exhibits nucleation activity, which accelerates the hydration process in cement. This speeds up the process of interstitial bridging by increasing the consumption of free internal water. Solidification of a material is accelerated when viscosity increases significantly51.Figure 10Initial and final setting time of NT-FC.

Transport properties

Water absorption

The permeability of FC allows for water infiltration. Water facilitates the scattering and transportation of chloride ions and other harmful ions within the materials. Figure 11 illustrates the proportion of water absorption exhibited by the specimens. The water absorption percentage in NT specimens at ambient temperature is less than that in F0 specimens. The increased production of hydrated compounds in the series of NT specimens at the standard curing temperature may be the fundamental cause. As previously stated, NT nanoparticles expedite the creation of cement hydrates, necessitating an increased amount of water to generate these compounds. Thus, when curing at a temperature of 20 °C, the NT series of concrete requires a greater amount of water compared to the F0 series. Subsequent enhancements are found when the concentration of NT is elevated to 1.5%, however, an adverse impact is witnessed when the concentration is further increased to 2%.Figure 11Water absorption of NT-FC.

Water absorption of NT-FC.

This trend is consistent with the broader nano-TiO₂ literature. Nazari and Hiari52 reported that TiO₂ nanoparticles improve resistance to water penetration by refining the pore system and densifying the cement paste, while later-age water absorption becomes lower than the control once hydration has progressed sufficiently. Likewise, NT improves concrete homogeneity and chloride resistance, with the optimum range centered around about 1.5–2.0% NT, beyond which the benefit declines because of poorer workability and dispersion32. More recently, nano-TiO₂-modified concrete with 1.5% NT was reported to show a compressive-strength increase of 28.7% and marked permeability reduction due to pore filling and densification, again supporting the existence of an optimum dosage range. Therefore, the current FC results follow the same general mechanism reported for normal-weight concrete, but the improvement is even more significant in FC because its initially higher connected porosity makes it more responsive to nanoscale pore refinement. Exposing F0 series concrete to temperatures up to 800 °C causes the pore structure to degrade, leading to increased water permeability compared to specimens cured at normal temperatures. The data shown in the figure demonstrates that the water absorption percentage in the NT series decreases as the amount of NT nanoparticles increases up to 1.5% at temperatures up to 800 °C. As a result, the amount of water absorbed increases when the NT concentration exceeds this limit, eventually reaching 2%.

Compared with previous studies, the present findings are scientifically meaningful because most published work on NT has focused on water absorption under ambient curing, whereas the current study demonstrates that the beneficial effect of NT remains evident even after thermal exposure. This is an important distinction. Existing studies on FC at high temperatures show that dehydration, pore coarsening, and microcrack growth strongly increase water ingress after heating. The present results show that NT partly counteracts this deterioration by reducing permeable channels and improving the interfacial transition zone. The fact that the optimum dosage remains 1.5% after heating is also consistent with the widely reported optimum range in NT-modified concrete, whereas the decline at 2% is reasonably attributed to nanoparticle agglomeration, which reduces the effective filler/nucleation function and may locally create defects rather than eliminate them53.Insufficient dispersion of nanoparticles in the cement paste may be the cause when the concentration of nanoparticles exceeds 1.5%. Thus, it may be inferred that subjecting the curing process to higher temperatures, along with increasing the duration and concentration of NT nanoparticles, can result in a decrease in permeable gaps. The observed outcome is a direct result of the significant level of activity and capacity to fill space of NT nanoparticles. Additionally, it has been found that the presence of NT nanoparticles in concrete leads to an improvement in the interfacial transition zone due to their high reactivity and ability to act as fillers. This discovery largely supports the findings of the experiments carried out by Nazari et al.52.

Apparent porosity

The porosity of cementitious materials is essential in determining their strength and durability under high temperature exposures. An in-depth examination of porosity is essential for understanding the relationship between porosity and material properties, as porosity has a substantial impact on the features of FC. Augmenting the level of porosity in FC generally results in a reduction of its mechanical characteristics. As the temperature increases, the density decreases, leading to an increase in both porosity and void dimensions. Thus, any alteration in the microstructure of FC resulting from changes in the void system can significantly impact on the mechanical properties, particularly in terms of density. The permeability of cementitious materials is a critical determinant of their strength and ability to withstand high temperatures. Mass loss and pore structure assessment

Mass loss

Figure 25 demonstrates that as the temperature climbed, each sample had noticeable decreases in mass. The decrease in FC may be attributed to the presence of both unbound water and water that is chemically bound. The density of FC is directly correlated with the amount of accessible water, with higher densities corresponding to greater quantities of unbound water. As the temperature rises, the water molecules that are chemically attached to the FC escape into the air by evaporation, causing the FC to become less dense53. Through the analysis of the loss data, the process may be classified into three clearly defined phases. Phase 1 of the NT-FC involves a temperature range of 20–200 °C and includes several important reactions. First, the water that can turn into vapor and a portion of the water that is connected through chemical bonds are released. Once the temperature surpasses 200 °C, it can be confidently deduced that all the unbound water has vaporized. Furthermore, when exposed to temperatures between 120 and 140 °C, the gel formed by calcium silicate hydrates (C–S–H) breaks down, causing the liberation of a fraction of the chemically bound water. Moreover, the breakdown of ettringite, a mineral commonly found in cement-based materials, takes place at around 200 °C. At this stage, the loss did not exceed 8% of the starting mass. Between temperatures of 200–400 °C (phase 2), the chemical reaction occurs when the calcium silicate hydrates (C–S–H) gel and sulfo-aluminate phases in the cement matrix decompose, resulting in the liberation of chemically bound water. During this stage, 75% of the water that has undergone chemical bonding is converted into vapor. Currently, the investigation found that the fall at this stage did not exceed 11% of the initial weight. During phase 3, specifically at a temperature of 450 °C, within the temperature range of 400–800 °C, there is an extra step of removing water. This process is linked to the breakdown of calcium hydroxide (CH) into calcium oxide (CaO) and water (H2O). The procedure reaches completion at a temperature of 530 °C. Currently, the remaining 25% of the chemically bound water has also been transformed into vapor. Moreover, within the temperature range of 500 to 650 °C, a transition from α-quartz to β-quartz takes place in a crystalline form68. Additionally, a further stage of reducing body weight can be attributed to the separation of carbon dioxide (CO2) from calcium carbonate (CaCO3), wherein CaCO3 breaks down into calcium oxide (CaO) and CO2. This reaction occurs predominantly at a temperature of 800 °C. The current study revealed that the loss at this stage ranged from 12 to 15% of the initial mass69.Figure 25Original mass (%) of NT-FC.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the effect of nano-titania (NT) on the residual performance of foamed concrete (FC) exposed to elevated temperatures up to 800 °C. The novelty of this work lies in its integrated assessment of the fresh-state, mechanical, transport, thermal, and microstructural behaviour of NT-modified FC under both ambient and post-heating conditions. Unlike previous studies that mainly focused on either NT-modified cementitious material at room temperature or conventional FC at elevated temperatures, the present work demonstrates how NT improves the fire resistance and residual performance of FC through pore refinement, matrix densification, and mitigation of heat-induced damage. Based on the experimental results, the following conclusions can be drawn:The incorporation of NT reduced the workability and accelerated the setting process of FC. At the optimum dosage of 1.5% NT, the slump flow diameter, initial setting time, and final setting time decreased by 1.6%, 18%, and 8.9%, respectively, compared with the control mixture.NT significantly improved the mechanical properties of FC up to an optimum dosage of 1.5%. At room temperature, the compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, Young’s modulus, and ultrasonic pulse velocity increased by 40%, 105%, 86.7%, 229%, and 40%, respectively, relative to the control mix. These improvements are attributed to the filler effect of NT and its ability to promote hydration and densify the cementitious matrix.NT markedly enhanced the residual mechanical performance of FC after exposure to high temperatures. At 800 °C, the residual ratios of compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, Young’s modulus, and ultrasonic pulse velocity increased from 0.18, 0.12, 0.14, 0.07, and 0.23 in the control mix to 0.57, 0.54, 0.58, 0.36, and 0.61, respectively, for the mixture containing 1.5% NT. This confirms the strong potential of NT in improving the structural stability of FC under severe thermal conditions.The addition of NT improved the transport-related properties of FC by reducing pore connectivity and fluid ingress. At ambient temperature, the incorporation of 1.5% NT reduced water absorption, apparent porosity, and intrinsic air permeability by 8.7%, 8.8%, and 16.2%, respectively. After exposure to 800 °C, water absorption decreased from 32 to 28.5%, apparent porosity from 55 to 49%, and intrinsic air permeability from 0.86 × 10⁻⁹ to 0.67 × 10⁻⁹ m2/s, compared with the control FC.The microstructural results confirmed that NT refined the pore system and reduced heat-induced pore coarsening. At ambient temperature, the addition of 1.5% NT reduced the proportion of pores larger than 700 µm from 30 to 14%, while the large pore content and average pore size were also reduced. After exposure to 800 °C, the percentage of pores greater than 700 µm decreased from 60 to 28.5%, the large-pore fraction from 97 to 82%, and the average pore diameter from 820 to 620 µm. These findings indicate that NT effectively limits microstructural deterioration at high temperatures.Overall, the incorporation of NT, particularly at an optimum dosage of 1.5%, substantially improved the strength, durability-related transport properties, and thermal resistance of FC. The enhanced performance is mainly attributed to pore refinement, improved matrix compactness, and reduced microcrack development, as supported by the SEM and pore-structure analyses. Therefore, NT can be considered an effective nano-modifier for the development of high-performance, fire-resistant FC.
