Fracture behaviour and structural integrity of hemp fibre reinforced composites for low load-bearing aerospace applications
Authors: Santhosh Nagaraja, Praveena Bindiganavile Anand, Ramesha Kodandappa, Adisu Frinjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a research undertaking on the mechanical performance of hemp fibre reinforced epoxy composites with special attention to tensile, flexural and impact behavior. The hand lay-up technique was used to produce laminates that were composed of different proportions of hemp fibres within the range between 25 wt% and 45 wt% in an epoxy base. The findings showed that composites with 35 wt% hemp fibre had the most balanced mechanical performance. This structure was found to have tensile strength of 67.3 MPa, tensile modulus of 2.85 GPa and an elongation at break of 5.2%, which is the best combination of strength and ductility. Flexural properties were no different and similarly, the 35 wt% hemp fibre reinforced composite showed better performance viz., a flexural strength of 99.7 MPa and flexural modulus of 3.9 GPa. Impact resistance, measured under the charpy test, was 9.4 kJ/m2 for the same composition (35 wt% hemp fibre) indicating better energy absorption ability. The observed improvement in the mechanical behaviour at 35 wt% fibre loading is due to the enhancement in the fibre-matrix contact and efficient stress transfer of the composite structure. These results define an improved reinforcement level that yields further performance improvements because of the potential agglomeration of fibres or loss of matrix continuity. The novelty of this work lies in the determination of the optimum loading of hemp fibre, which maximizes the strength, stiffness, and toughness, resulting in a balanced performance profile, optimized to work in structurally-lightweight applications. Further, the findings demonstrate the possibility of hemp fibre as a viable green reinforcement in semi-structural uses of hemp fibre in automotive body panels, lightweight construction, and framework structures.

Introduction

The low density, renewable nature, biodegradability and good weight-to-strength ratio of natural fibre reinforced polymer composites (NFRPCs) have led to the new generation of sustainable structural material. Hemp fibre reinforced polymers are one of the promising types of eco-friendly materials, which provide the combination of sustainability and mechanical performance. The hemp fibres are produced using one of the fastest growing plants with superior tensile strength, flexibility and biodegradability1. The cellulose content helps them to have better load bearing capacity and their high rate of growth is what guarantees their availability in terms of raw material that is not only constant but also renewable2,3. When included into thermosetting materials like epoxy, polyester, or vinyl ester, these fibres are effective in structural performance in which load transfer, dimensional stability, resistance to corrosion and chemical degradation is also enhanced4–6. Hemp fibres may also have tensile strength exceeding 200 MPa under normal mechanical conditions depending on the species and treatment procedures7 and the reinforcement of a composite has been demonstrated to dramatically enhance the performance of the composite8. It is because of their high aspect ratio and natural toughness that hemp fibres can increase energy absorption and damage tolerance which is why hemp fibres are applicable in applications that require dynamic or bending loads9. Furthermore, hemp farming doesn’t need a significant level of pesticides, and it creates a beneficial impact on the soil and carbon capturing, which adds to the environmental advantages of hemp compared to synthetic fibres made of petroleum products10–14.

Over the past few years, the growing interest in environmentally friendly material systems has facilitated the development of studies in natural fibre-based composites as effective alternatives to traditional synthetic reinforcement, including glass and carbon fibres26–33. It has been shown that fibres like hemp, flax, jute, kenaf and sisal have the capacity to enhance damping of vibration, impact resistance, and energy uptake and have a major reduction in environmental footprint throughout their lifecycle34–38. The recent development has also increased the versatility of natural fibre-based composites by introducing nanoscale fillers, reinforcing hybrid approaches and surface engineering technology to enhance the fibre/matrix compatibility and durability39–42. The nano-engineered natural fibre composites have been demonstrated to exhibit increased stiffness, fracture resistance, and stability of long-term performance because of the enhanced mechanism of transfer of interfacial stresses43–45. These trends can be regarded as an increasing research trend towards optimizing the structural reliability and readiness to be used as opposed to merely maximizing mechanical strength46–49.

Although they have good qualities, earlier studies on hemp fibre composites have majorly aimed at obtaining maximum mechanical characteristics by using high fibre content, fibre orientation or optimum processing conditions in the laboratory. Although these methods can improve strength and stiffness levels, they tend to create some challenges including fibre agglomeration, void formation as well as inconsistent interfacial bonding that could undermine manufacturability and structural reliability in practical situations. Moreover, natural fibre composites are hygroscopic in nature because lignocellulosic fibres are natural fibres. Debonding and swelling between interfaces and progressive degradation of mechanical properties with time may occur due to absorption of moisture15. The differences in the morphology of fibres and surface properties, as well as, the absence of standardized processing procedures, may also be the causes of performance variability16–23. Other factors that inhibit the adoption of bio-composites in large scale structural applications are economic factors and weak regulation frameworks.

In order to overcome these inhibitions, recent studies on sustainable hybrid natural fibre composites have primarily focused on performance optimization through material hybridization and stacking sequence design24–30. For instance, hybrid hemp–pineapple–glass fibre reinforced epoxy systems have demonstrated that mechanical strength and impact resistance can be effectively tailored for lightweight structural applications through appropriate laminate configuration27. Similarly, investigations on jute/hemp bio-epoxy hybrid composites have reported that stacking sequence significantly influences tensile and flexural properties, fatigue resistance, and interfacial bonding behavior28. These findings reflect a growing research trend toward improving composite performance through structural architecture and hybrid reinforcement strategies. In contrast, the present study adopts a composition-driven approach by examining the influence of controlled fibre loading on fracture behaviour and structural integrity under practical fabrication conditions. This distinction provides complementary insight into performance optimization, emphasizing the role of material distribution rather than laminate configuration, and thereby contributes to a broader understanding of sustainable composite design for real-world applications.

The fabrication of such sustainable composite structures require a systematic knowledge of the effects of fibre loading on interfacial bonding, fracture mechanisms and structural integrity in general in realistic fabrication conditions. Against this backdrop, the current research looks into the mechanical behaviour and fracture behaviour of hemp fibre reinforced epoxy composites which have moderate amounts of fibre load between 25 Wt.% and 45 Wt.%. The study aims at establishing an optimum reinforcement level that will result in a balanced tensile, flexural, and impact performance without compromising manufacturability and structural reliability. The morphological analysis is done using SEM to determine the relationship between fibre contents, interfacial behaviour, and failure mechanisms applicable in low-load aerospace interior components (e.g. cabin panel, seat back, insulation structure) used in low-load aerospace applications. Therefore, the novelty of the current work lies in the fact that the structural integrity-oriented optimization of the hemp fibre composite under the conditions of real processing provides an application-oriented framework, which fills the gap between the laboratory-scale performance improvement of the materials and the real-world engineering practice.

Materials and methods

Materials

The materials to be used in this research comprise resin and fibre materials that are necessary in manufacturing of hemp fibre-reinforced polymer composites. The LY-556 epoxy resin was mixed with HY-951 hardener in a weight ratio of 10:1 to form the matrix phase of the composite. This epoxy system is a thermosetting synthetic polymer known for its excellent mechanical strength, chemical resistance, and thermal stability, making it suitable for structural composite applications. The use of epoxy ensures effective load transfer between the reinforcement and matrix due to its superior adhesion characteristics and durability. The epoxy resin and the hemp fibres used as reinforcement were procured from Go Green Products Ltd., Chennai, India. The technical information that was supplied by the supplier indicated that the fibres were obtained using mature stalks of hemp which were then processed mechanically to obtain individual fibres. Such processing entailed decortication, separation of fibres and cutting to a standard length of 10 mm to ensure homogenous dispersion and easy manipulation during composite fabrication. To improve interfacial bonding between the hemp fibres and the epoxy matrix, as well as to reduce undesirable hydrophilic behavior of the fibres, alkali treatment was used. The fibres were impregnated with a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, a technique that tends to enhance the surface roughness and to eliminate lignin and hemicellulose and thus enhance fibre-matrix compatibility. The visual morphology of hemp fibres before and after surface treatment is presented in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b). The untreated hemp fibres shown in Fig. 1(a) exhibit a rough, compact, and entangled structure with visible surface impurities such as waxes and other non-cellulosic materials, which restrict proper fibre separation. In contrast, the treated hemp fibres shown in Fig. 1(b) display a cleaner and more individualized fibrillar structure with reduced surface impurities and improved fibre separation. This noticeable change in appearance indicates the removal of amorphous constituents such as hemicellulose, lignin, and surface oils during the treatment process. The improved surface condition after treatment suggests enhanced surface activation, which is expected to promote better fibre–matrix adhesion and improve the overall performance of the developed composites.

Methods

The alkali treatment was used in order to increase the interfacial bonding between the hemp fibres and the epoxy matrix. A 5% solution of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was applied on the raw fibres and allowed to be impinged upon for 4 h at room temperature. This chemical treatment is used to remove the surface impurities like lignin, hemicellulose and waxes and hence enhance the surface roughness and makes it easier to mechanical interlock with the polymer matrix. The fibres were washed using the distilled water after the alkali treatment to get rid of all the alkali and neutralize the pH. The fibres were then air dried in the oven in 24 h to dry them and allow them to be fabricated into composites. The composites were made by use of a normal hand lay-up process and compression molding done to attain uniformity and compaction. In the fabrication, the chemically treated hemp fibres were evenly placed in the mold, then epoxy applied to be sure that the fibres were completely wet and impregnated. The effect of fibre content on the performance of composite, the various fractions of fibre weight viz. 25%, 30%, 35%, 40%, and 45% were investigated. In addition to fibre-reinforced specimens, a baseline control sample (S0) consisting of pristine epoxy (without fibre reinforcement) was also fabricated. This specimen served as a reference to evaluate the reinforcing effect of hemp fibres on composite performance. The selection of fibre weight fractions in the range of 25 wt% to 45 wt% was based on practical processing considerations and the need to balance effective reinforcement with matrix continuity. At lower fibre contents (< 25 wt%), the reinforcing effect is generally limited due to insufficient stress transfer and lack of fibre interaction within the matrix. Conversely, higher fibre loadings (> 45 wt%) often lead to poor resin impregnation, fibre agglomeration, increased void formation, and reduced wettability, particularly in hand lay-up fabrication methods commonly used for natural fibre composites. The chosen range therefore represents an optimal processing window that ensures adequate fibre wetting while minimizing defects associated with excessive reinforcement. Additionally, this range allows the study to capture the transition toward fibre network formation, where fibre–fibre interactions begin to influence load transfer and fracture behaviour, without compromising manufacturability and structural integrity. The laminate stacks were subjected to the vacuum bag moulding process and maintained under controlled vacuum pressure for 24 h to facilitate proper curing of the composite. This process was employed to ensure uniform laminate thickness, effective fibre dispersion, and improved fibre–matrix interfacial bonding throughout the composite layers. The application of vacuum helped in eliminating entrapped air, reducing void formation, and promoting better resin impregnation within the fibre network, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the laminate. Mechanical characterization of the developed composites was carried out using specimens precisely prepared in accordance with relevant ASTM standards to ensure dimensional accuracy and edge integrity. Water jet cutting was employed to minimize thermal and mechanical damage during specimen preparation. Tensile test specimens were fabricated as per ASTM D638 Type I geometry, with an overall length of 165 mm, gauge length of 50 mm, reduced section width of 13 mm, grip section width of 19 mm, and thickness of 3.2 mm. A standard dog-bone profile with a gauge radius of 76 mm was adopted to ensure uniform stress distribution during loading. Flexural specimens were prepared following ASTM D790 with dimensions of 127 mm × 12.7 mm × 3.2 mm50,51 and tested under three-point bending using a span length of 64 mm to maintain an appropriate span-to-depth ratio for accurate flexural response. Impact resistance was evaluated using notched specimens prepared in accordance with ASTM D256, with dimensions of 64 mm × 12.7 mm × 3.2 mm52. A centrally machined V-notch of 2.54 mm depth and 0.25 mm root radius at a 45° angle was introduced to facilitate controlled fracture initiation. Tensile and flexural tests were conducted using an Instron 3369 universal testing machine at the Advanced Materials Technology Centre, Peenya, Bangalore, while impact testing was performed using an Instron CEAST 9050 charpy impact tester at Raghavendra Testing Lab, Peenya, Bangalore.

To ensure repeatability and statistical reliability of the experimental results, a minimum of five specimens were tested for each composite configuration. The reported mechanical properties represent the mean values obtained, and the corresponding standard deviation was calculated to quantify experimental variability associated with natural fibre dispersion, interfacial characteristics, and processing-induced inconsistencies. The relatively low variation observed across test results confirms the consistency of the fabrication and testing procedures, thereby enhancing confidence in the measured mechanical performance and demonstrating that the reported trends are representative of the material system rather than isolated observations.

Results and discussions

The structural features of composites are evaluated using the evaluation of properties that are critical. These mechanical tests and water absorption investigations provide information on different fibre content and it can impact the general performance of the composite material. In this part, a detailed discussion of the experimental findings is made, their effect on the mechanical properties and sensitivity to moisture is discussed and their implications to the application in practice are discussed.

Tensile test the table 3 gives the results of tensile test

The pristine epoxy specimen (S0) exhibited a tensile strength of 52.4 MPa, tensile modulus of 2.05 GPa, and elongation at break of 8.3%, representing the inherent ductile but relatively low-strength behaviour of the unreinforced matrix. The incorporation of hemp fibres resulted in a consistent improvement in load-bearing capability and stiffness across all reinforced specimens (S1–S5) when compared to S0. At 25 wt% fibre loading (S1), tensile strength increased to 58.5 MPa (~ 12% improvement), indicating the initial reinforcing contribution of hemp fibres through partial stress transfer. With further increase in fibre content to 30 wt% (S2), tensile strength reached 61.2 MPa (~ 17% higher than S0), accompanied by an increase in modulus to 2.61 GPa, confirming enhanced stiffness due to fibre dominance in load sharing. The optimum performance was achieved at 35 wt% fibre loading (S3), where tensile strength increased to 67.3 MPa, representing an improvement of approximately 28% over pristine epoxy. Simultaneously, tensile modulus increased to 2.87 GPa, reflecting efficient fibre–matrix interaction and improved stress transfer efficiency. This behaviour indicates the formation of an effective reinforcing network within the matrix. However, beyond this optimal fibre content, a decline in performance was observed. At 40 wt% (S4) and 45 wt% (S5), tensile strength reduced to 62.7 MPa and 59.1 MPa, respectively. Although these values remained higher than the baseline S0, the reduction indicates the onset of fibre agglomeration, reduced resin wetting, and interfacial defects that hinder effective load transfer. In contrast to strength and stiffness, elongation at break decreased progressively from 8.3% (S0) to 5.2% (S3) and further to 4.5% (S5). This reduction confirms the transition from ductile matrix-dominated behaviour to fibre-dominated brittle behaviour, which is typical in natural fibre reinforced composites. Flexural test

The Table 4 gives the flexural test data. The pristine epoxy specimen (S0) exhibited a flexural strength of 72.5 MPa and flexural modulus of 2.84 GPa, representing the baseline bending performance of the unreinforced matrix. The incorporation of hemp fibres resulted in a consistent improvement in flexural properties across all reinforced specimens. At 35 wt% fibre loading (S3), flexural strength increased to 99.7 MPa, representing a significant enhancement over S0. Similarly, flexural modulus improved from 2.84 GPa (S0) to 3.90 GPa (S3), indicating increased stiffness and improved resistance to bending deformation due to effective fibre reinforcement. However, beyond this optimum loading, flexural strength decreased to 95.6 MPa (S4) and 89.4 MPa (S5), while modulus reduced to 3.53 GPa and 3.29 GPa, respectively. This decline is attributed to fibre clustering and reduced matrix continuity, which weaken stress transfer under bending loads. In contrast, deflection at break reduced from 5.2 mm (S0) to 4.0 mm (S3) and further to 3.7 mm (S5), indicating increased rigidity and reduced flexibility with fibre addition.

Mechanical characterizations and mapping mechanical findings with Aerospace applications

Mechanical property of bio-fiber reinforced polymer composites is highly reliant on fillers that are added55. As an example, flax and hemp can be discussed as fibers which can considerably enhance the strength and stiffness because their nature does provide them with some superior features. On the other hand, fibers like jute and sisal provide moderate reinforcement and are often chosen for their cost-effectiveness. Kenaf and coir fibers are usually used to increase impact resistance, even though they do not usually contribute to overall stiffness. Factors such as the quality of fiber- matrix bonding can also affect the performance of less conventional reinforcements like fruit peels, eggshells, Phormium tenax and Acacia caesia bark fibers which can be improved through a number of surface treatments to achieve better adhesion52–54,56–58. In addition, the paramount factors such as fiber length, alignment, and loading (%) have a great influence on the composite characteristics; longer and well-oriented fibers are more likely to provide excellent mechanical performance, whereas the concentration of fibers can be too high, leading to difficulty in manufacturing and loss of strength59. These findings show that adding hemp fibres to polymer composites have a tremendous impact resistance especially at 35 wt% fibre loading which is an optimal balance between the toughness of the matrix and the reinforcing fibre. Beyond this threshold, performance declines due to structural inconsistencies and weakened interfacial bonding. The results indicate that hemp fibre reinforced composites possess potential for lightweight applications, particularly in non-structural and semi-structural components. It is important to note that the use of natural fibre composites in aerospace is still in a nascent stage and is currently limited to exploratory research focused on interior and secondary applications rather than primary load-bearing structures. Recent studies have suggested that natural fibre composites may be suitable for aircraft interior elements such as cabin panels, seat components, tray tables, and insulation structures, where weight reduction and sustainability are key considerations41–45. Their low density contributes to potential fuel efficiency benefits, while their renewable origin aligns with evolving sustainability goals in the transportation sector. However, successful implementation requires careful optimization of fibre loading to ensure adequate impact resistance and structural integrity, particularly under dynamic or sudden loading conditions. Therefore, at the present stage, hemp fibre composites are more realistically applicable in automotive and other lightweight engineering domains, with aerospace interior applications representing a future developmental direction rather than an established deployment.

Morphological investigations and elemental mapping

The above analysis of the hemp fibre reinforced polymer composite using the scanning electron (SE) microscopy and energy-dispersive (ED) X-ray spectroscopy offers useful information on the fracture morphology and elemental composition of the composite. The SE micrograph (SEM) in Fig. 12 (a) shows clear morphological aspects that are related to mechanical failure. It is worth noting that fibre pull-out has been observed which means that the interfacial bond between the hemp fibres and the polymer matrix is moderate and thus fibres can disengage during tensile loading. There is also evident matrix shearing that is identified by long zones of deformation and coarse fracture surfaces indicating mechanisms of plastic deformation and energy dissipation. The exposed hemp fibres are relatively clean and this is also an indication that interfacial debonding and not fibre rupture is the failure mode of domination. To support the morphological study, the ED X-ray spectroscopy is conducted on area 1 shown in Fig. 12 (b). The elemental composition of the composite is confirmed by the ED Spectrum (EDS) displayed in Fig. 12 (c) obtained as a result of the spectroscopy. C and O are also observed to have prominent peaks at about 0.33 keV and 0.66 keV respectively. These factors are in line with the organicity of both the hemp fibres and the polymer matrix. The fibre cellulose and lignin components are responsible to the high carbon content and the presence of hydroxyl and ester functional groups to the peak of oxygen respectively. The fact that there are no major peaks of inorganic elements indicates that there is little contamination or filler amount in the composite.

Conclusions

The baseline pristine epoxy specimen (S0) showed a tensile strength of 52.4 MPa, flexural strength of 72.5 MPa, and impact strength of 5.4 kJ/m², serving as the reference for evaluating reinforcement efficiency.The introduction of hemp fibres significantly enhanced mechanical performance. The optimum properties were obtained at 35 wt% fibre loading (S3).Tensile strength increased from 52.4 MPa (S0) to 67.3 MPa (S3) (~ 28% improvement), while tensile modulus improved from 2.05 GPa to 2.87 GPa, indicating improved stiffness and load transfer capability.Flexural strength improved from 72.5 MPa (S0) to 99.7 MPa (S3) (~ 37% increase), and flexural modulus increased from 2.84 GPa to 3.90 GPa, demonstrating enhanced bending resistance.Impact strength increased from 5.4 kJ/m² (S0) to 9.4 kJ/m² (S3) (~ 74% improvement), confirming superior energy absorption due to fibre pull-out and crack bridging mechanisms.At higher fibre loading (> 35 wt%), mechanical performance declined (e.g., tensile strength reduced to 59.1 MPa at S5) due to fibre agglomeration, void formation, and reduced resin infiltration.Ductility decreased with fibre addition, as elongation reduced from 8.3% (S0) to 5.2% (S3), indicating a trade-off between strength and flexibility.SEM–EDS analysis confirmed fibre pull-out and matrix shearing along with lignocellulosic elemental presence (C, O), validating effective fibre–matrix interaction.From a technical competitiveness perspective, the optimized composite at 35 wt% hemp fibre exhibited significant improvements over pristine epoxy, including ~ 28% higher tensile strength, ~ 37% higher flexural strength, and ~ 74% greater impact resistance.The composite demonstrated enhanced stiffness, toughness, and strength-to-weight efficiency, making it a structurally superior alternative to the unreinforced matrix.
