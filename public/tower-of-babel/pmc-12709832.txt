Associations and predictive value of weight-adjusted waist index for cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes: evidence from the ACCORD study
Authors: Maojun Liu, Junyu Pei, Cheng Zeng, Ying Xin, Peiqi Tang, Xinqun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to investigate the association between the weight-adjusted waist circumference index (WWI) and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with T2DM.MethodsUsing Cox proportional hazards regression models to determine the impact of WWI on cardiovascular event. Nonlinear associations were explored through restricted cubic splines (RCS) and smooth curve fitting (SCF), and the integrity of these findings was supported by subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ResultsAn elevation in WWI was linked to a significant rise in the likelihood of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), composite cardiovascular outcomes MSD, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and any death, congestive heart failure (CHF), and total mortality (TM). An increase of per 1 standard deviations (SD) in WWI corresponded to a 7% heightened risk of MACEs (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.13), a 9% greater risk of MSD (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.13), a 20% greater risk of CHF (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10, 1.30), and a 11% increase in TM (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.17). RCS and SCF analysis revealed a nonlinear correlation between WWI and the risks of CHF and TM. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that WWI more accurately predicted CHF risk in patients whose duration of diabetes was under 10 years. Sensitivity analyses reinforced the reliability of these results.The integration of WWI into conventional predictive models improved the accuracy of these outcomes.ConclusionsWWI is closely associated with future cardiovascular outcomes and TM in patients with T2DM.WWI serves as an autonomous predictor of both future cardiovascular events and TM among patients with T2DM, outperforming traditional obesity indices in predictive ability.Clinical trial URLs https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT00000620 Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01251-0.Keywords: Weight-adjusted waist index, Cardiovascular outcomes, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Action to control cardiovascular risk in diabetes

Introduction

T2DM poses a considerable global public health issue, with its incidence increasing dramatically [1–3]. Reports from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) in 2021 indicate that approximately 500 million individuals globally were affected by T2DM, with projections suggesting an escalation to 783 million by 2024 [4]. T2DM significantly contributes to vascular complications and is a principal cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD), the predominant cause of mortality among those diagnosed with T2DM [5]. Such patients are at an increased risk for CVD, experiencing more severe prognoses and elevated rates of CVD-related morbidity and mortality [6]. It is noted that the incidence of cardiovascular events in T2DM individuals is more than double that of non-diabetic individuals, often occurring at younger ages [7]. Moreover, the rate of all-cause mortality is notably higher in diabetic individuals [8]. Given the severe morbidity and mortality linked with diabetes and CVD, it is vital to perform precise cardiovascular risk assessments and identify modifiable factors that can influence the clinical outcomes of T2DM patients to mitigate cardiovascular incidents and decrease mortality rates.

Obesity, a key pathophysiological contributor to T2DM, is acknowledged for its strong association with both the onset of T2DM and the development of CVD, and is consistently identified as a crucial risk factor for cardiovascular issues. This condition also significantly impacts the cardiovascular risk profile of individuals with T2DM [9, 10]. Historical research has employed various obesity metrics, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and the visceral adiposity index (VAI), to explore the connections between obesity, diabetes with its complications, and CVD. These metrics each possess unique benefits and drawbacks [11–13]. Although BMI is commonly utilized to gauge obesity, it fails to differentiate between fat distribution and muscle mass [14], which is problematic since abdominal fat is closely linked to cardiovascular risk, making fat distribution a more critical factor than overall weight [15, 16]. While WC is useful in assessing abdominal obesity, it is not typically recommended as a sole metric over BMI [17].

Introduced recently, the WWI integrates WC measurements with body weight adjustments by dividing the WC by the square root of the body weight. This index amalgamates the advantages of WC and overcomes the limitations associated with BMI, providing a more accurate evaluation of body fat and muscle mass [18]. Research has demonstrated that a heightened WWI is a significant predictor of various metabolic conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, and CVD [19–22]. However, most of the research has primarily focused on Asian populations. Recently, WWI has proven to be a robust and comprehensive obesity metric, with studies indicating its independent correlation with increased risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among the general U.S. population, including pre-diabetic and diabetic individuals [23–26]. However, the effectiveness of WWI in forecasting cardiovascular outcomes, including MACEs, MSD and CHF, in diabetic patients remains underexplored.

This retrospective cohort study is designed to investigate the correlation between WWI and cardiovascular outcomes within the participants of the Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD) cohort, aiming to generate new insights that may support clinical practices and provide valuable references for risk assessment and tailored management strategies for adverse cardiovascular outcomes in T2DM patients.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This analysis utilized a post hoc approach, employing data from the ACCORD/ACCORDION trial. The ACCORD study, a randomized, multicenter, double-blind, 2 × 2 factorial design clinical trial, has been thoroughly detailed and published in previous studies and is accessible at https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/accord/. The ACCORD/ACCORDION study involved 10,251 type 2 diabetes patients, aged 40 to 79, with a mean age of 62.2 years and a median HbA1c of 8.1%, who either had existing CVD or were at elevated risk for such conditions. Conducted across 77 sites in the United States and Canada from June 2001 to June 2009, the trial provided treatment and monitoring over an average span of about five years. Subsequent follow-ups from 2011 to 2014 in the ACCORDION trial involved an average of 3.5 years of additional data collection through clinic visits and telephonic updates. During each follow-up, comprehensive medical data were gathered and outcomes consistently evaluated by the dedicated Morbidity and Mortality Subcommittee.

Data collection and outcomes

This study gathered data on a variety of demographic and clinical parameters, such as age, sex, educational background, ethnicity, treatment modalities, medical histories, physical assessments, laboratory diagnostics, and previous medication use. Of the initial 10,251 study subjects, 103 were excluded due to the absence of baseline WWI data, leaving 10,148 participants for inclusion in the analysis (Fig. 1). We collected the baseline WWI, WC, and BMI values of the participants included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical assessments employed SPSS 26.0, R, and EmpowerStats. We reported baseline characteristics as means ± standard deviations (SD), frequencies, percentages, or medians with interquartile ranges, contingent on the distribution type of each variable. For comparing continuous variables, ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied, whereas categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square tests. To assess the cumulative risk of cardiovascular events and overall mortality, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were employed, with inter-group differences analyzed via the log-rank test.

The relationship between WWI and subsequent health outcomes was analyzed using a Cox proportional hazards regression model. Initially, a univariate analysis was conducted to pinpoint potential confounders by examining the correlations of all variables collected with the occurrence of future MACEs. Selection of covariates was grounded in both their theoretical underpinnings and clinical significance. In the multifactorial analysis, variables that attained statistical significance in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 1), as well as clinically pertinent variables with a strong association with MACEs or those exhibiting an effect size alteration of over 10% (regardless of a p-value greater than 0.05), were included. This analytical process incorporated an unadjusted model and three progressively adjusted models to mitigate confounding effects. Model 1 (minimally adjusted) accounted for sex, race, age, height, educational attainment, living alone, history of CVD, previous hypertension and hyperlipidemia, proteinuria, heart failure, smoking and depression. Model 2 (moderately adjusted) further included variables such as diabetes duration, systolic and diastolic blood pressures (SBP and DBP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), LDL-C, HDL-C, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) building upon Model 1. Model 3 (fully adjusted) added additional factors from Model 2, incorporating medication use, such as diuretics, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), beta-blockers, biguanides, meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, insulins, fibrates, cholesterol absorption inhibitors, and aspirin.

Collinearity tests were conducted for all adjustment variables included in the analysis. Typically, collinearity exists when the tolerance is less than 0.1 or the variance inflation factor (VIF) exceeds 5. The analysis was conducted separately for WWI, BMI, and WC models.The results showed that the VIF values for all variables were below 5, and the tolerance values were above 0.1 (Supplementary Tables 2–4), indicating no significant multicollinearity.

RCS and SCF analysis were utilized to investigate nonlinear relationships. A four-knot RCS model was adopted to ensure sufficient flexibility while maintaining model stability.Once nonlinearity was detected, we applied a two-piecewise Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate the threshold effect. The inflection point was determined in a data-driven manner using a recursive algorithm based on the maximum model likelihood. The hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each segment on either side of the threshold.Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to confirm the robustness and consistency of the results. Stratified analyses encompassed factors such as sex, age (< 60 years or older), race, CVD history, prior hyperlipidemia, hypertension, HbA1c levels (< 8.1% and ≥ 8.1%), duration of diabetes (< 10 years and ≥ 10 years), and BMI (< 25 kg/m2; ≥25 kg/m2 and < 30 kg/m2; and ≥ 30 kg/m2). Subgroup analyses were executed via Cox regression modeling. Nonsignificant interaction P-values suggested uniformity and reliability across strata, whereas significant interaction P-values indicated pronounced effects within specific subgroups. The added predictive value of WWI over conventional risk factors was assessed using area under curve (AUC), net reclassification index (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).

A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, multiple imputation was applied to handle missing data and reduce bias due to incomplete covariate information. Second, participants with a baseline history of CVD were excluded to minimize potential confounding from pre-existing conditions. Third, those with heart failure at baseline were removed, given its strong association with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Fourth, to reduce the risk of reverse causality and early mortality bias, individuals who experienced cardiovascular events or died within the first 3 years of follow-up were excluded. Finally, we also recognized that certain variables, such as SBP, DBP, HbA1c, and eGFR, may act as both confounders and potential mediators. Therefore, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding these variables from the fully adjusted model to evaluate the stability of the associations.

Results

Baseline characteristics were stratified by WWI quartile

The study profiled 10,148 participants, 61.46% of whom were male, with a mean age of 62.80 ± 6.65 years. The average WWI was 11.08 ± 0.77. Participants were distributed into four quartiles based on baseline WWI values: Quartile 1 (10.14 ± 0.36), Quartile 2 (10.82 ± 0.14), Quartile 3 (11.31 ± 0.15), and Quartile 4 (12.07 ± 0.45) (Table 1). Higher WWI quartiles were associated with older age, a higher proportion of female and white participants, lower educational attainment, reduced alcohol consumption, increased likelihood of living alone, and greater depression prevalence. High WWI participants also exhibited elevated rates of heart failure and CVD, along with increased BMI, HbA1c, and triglyceride levels but decreased height and LDL-C levels. More frequent use of diuretics, ARB/ACEI, CCBs, beta-blockers, insulins, and aspirin was noted among this group.

Association between WWI and cardiovascular outcomes in T2DM patients

The initial analysis of the relationship between WWI and cardiovascular outcomes revealed that higher WWI quartiles significantly correlated with increased risks of MACEs, MSD, CHF, and TM (Table 1). During a median follow-up of 8.82 years, 1,803 (17.77%) participants suffered MACE events and 2,837 (27.96%) participants suffered MSD events.Additionally, there were 1,927 total deaths (18.99%) and 690 CHF cases (6.80%), with incidence rates escalating markedly in higher WWI quartiles, culminating in the highest rate of 19.89% in the top quartile. Both TM and CHF risks escalated with increasing WWI quartiles (Table 1).

Kaplan-Meier curve visualizations were employed to assess the cumulative risks of MACEs, MSD, CHF, and TM. Results indicated that higher WWI significantly predicted increased future risks of these outcomes, with the highest WWI quartile showing markedly greater cardiovascular adverse outcomes than the lowest quartile (log-rank test, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

In the models, three progressively adjusted regression analyses were conducted to examine the impact of WWI as a continuous variable, fully adjusted for potential confounders (Table 2). A collinearity test was conducted for all the adjusted variables included in the analysis. Generally, the results indicate that there is no significant multicollinearity(Supplementary Table 2).An increase of per 1 SD in WWI corresponded to a 7% heightened risk of MACEs (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.13; p = 0.0077), a 9% greater risk of MSD (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.13; p < 0.0001), a 20% greater risk of CHF (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10, 1.30; p < 0.0001), and a 11% increase in TM (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.17; p < 0.0001).In the most comprehensively adjusted model (Model 3),the highest WWI quartile was associated with a 1.19-fold higher risk of MACEs, a 1.25-fold higher risk of MSD, a 1.43-fold higher risk of CHF, and a 1.31-fold higher risk of TM Furthermore, a significant trend was observed in the increasing risk of MACEs, MSD, CHF, and TM across WWI quartiles (p for trend < 0.05). However, compared to the lowest quartile, the traditional obesity indices body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) showed no significant associations with MACEs and MSD.

Detection of nonlinear relationships

To examine potential nonlinear associations, we analyzed the relationship between the WWI and various outcome events utilizing RCS and SCF. While a linear relationship was noted between WWI and MACEs and MSD, potential nonlinear associations were identified between WWI and both TM and CHF risk (Fig. 3), and confirmed through log-likelihood tests (p < 0.05).

Restricted cubic spline analysis and smooth curve fitting were used to assess the relationship between WWI and primary and secondary outcomes. The inflection points for TM and CHF were determined using a recursive method to be 10.00 and 10.92, respectively. A two-stage Cox proportional hazards model was applied to calculate the effect sizes and confidence intervals around these inflection points. Notably, every one-unit decrease in WWI beyond these points corresponded to a 19% decrease in the risk of TM (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.11,1.27) and a 45% decrease in the risk of future CHF events (HR = 1.45, 95%CI: 1.25,1.69) (Table 3). Overall, WWI is U-shaped related to TM in T2DM patients.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed to confirm the robustness of our findings. Multiple imputation techniques were used to address missing data, reducing potential impacts on the analysis. A multivariate Cox regression analysis of the imputed dataset yielded results consistent with those from the complete case method (Supplementary Table 5), supporting the reliability of our study conclusions. Following the elimination of subjects with a baseline heart failure history, the final study population included 9,658 individuals. The correlation of WWI with cardiovascular outcomes and total mortality remained largely consistent with the primary analysis after adjusting for the entire covariates (Supplementary Table 6). In addition, after excluding participants from the first 3-year follow-up period, totally 9,028 individuals were included, and the above correlation remained similar (Supplementary Table 7). Finally, the sensitivity analysis excluding SBP, DBP, HbA1c, and eGFR from the fully adjusted model yielded results concordant with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the observed associations(Supplementary Table 8).Collectively, these analytical results further confirmed that our results are robust.

Subgroup and interaction analysis

To ascertain the reliability of our results and delve deeper into the dynamics between the WWI and various health outcomes, we carried out comprehensive subgroup and interaction analyses. These analyses were segmented by variables such as sex, age (< 60 years or older), race, CVD history, prior instances of hyperlipidemia, hypertension, HbA1c levels (< 8.1% and ≥ 8.1%), duration of diabetes (< 10 years and ≥ 10 years), and BMI categories (< 25 kg/m2; ≥25 kg/m2 and < 30 kg/m2; and ≥ 30 kg/m2). Our investigation did not reveal significant interactions between WWI, MACEs, MSD, and TM across the stratified groups (P for interaction > 0.05). Nevertheless, the length of diabetes significantly influenced the correlation between WWI and CHF (P for interaction = 0.0070) (Fig. 4), suggesting that WWI serves as a more robust predictor of impending CHF risk, particularly in individuals whose diabetes duration is less than a decade. We used SCF to perform a stratified analysis of the relationship between WWI and CHF based on diabetes duration of less than 10 years and 10 years or more. It was observed that WWI is more strongly associated with the future risk of CHF in T2DM patients with a disease duration of less than 10 years (Fig. 5).No other stratification elements demonstrated noteworthy interactions between WWI and CHF (P for interaction > 0.05), pointing to the potential of WWI as an especially reliable prognostic tool for CHF in patients with shorter-term T2DM.

Discussion

This investigation within the ACCORD/ACCORDION cohort marks the first to establish a connection between WWI, MACEs, MSD, CHF and TM in T2DM patients. Our findings corroborate WWI as an independent predictor of future adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality. Adjustments for various confounders revealed that higher WWI levels are independently linked to increased risks of adverse cardiovascular events: patients in the highest WWI quartile faced an 19% higher risk of MACEs, a 25% higher risk of MSD, a 43% higher risk of CHF, and a 31% higher risk of TM than those in the lowest quartile. Particularly in patients with less than 10 years of diabetes, WWI emerged as a stronger predictor of CHF. However, the traditional obesity indices BMI and WC showed no significant associations with MACEs and MSD.Additionally, WWI displayed a significant linear relationship with MACEs, MSD and a nonlinear relationship with CHF and TM, offering new insights that may inform the prevention and management of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in T2DM patients and aid in shaping future cardiovascular risk prevention strategies.

Our conclusions are supported by broader literature, indicating that WWI acts as a risk factor for numerous adverse health outcomes. For instance, research from Korea posited that WWI could act as a dual predictor for the incidence and mortality of cardiometabolic diseases, serving as a viable alternative marker for obesity and associated health issues. Combined with BMI, WWI achieved optimal performance in forecasting cardiometabolic disease and mortality [27]. Furthermore, a Chinese study showed that elevated WWI levels correlate positively with all-cause mortality among the elderly, affirming WWI as an independent risk factor for mortality [28]. These results underscore WWI’s potential as a potent predictor of cardiovascular prognostic risk and death, reinforcing the validity of our findings.

BMI has traditionally served as a primary metric for assessing obesity and has been extensively studied. However, recent research highlights a paradox: while BMI generally serves as an obesity marker, it inconsistently predicts mortality risks across various demographics [29, 30]. Epidemiological data reveal that individuals categorized with a normal BMI may still possess significant abdominal obesity, thus challenging the comprehensive reliability of BMI in assessing health risks [31, 32]. Conversely, WC tends to be a more direct indicator of fat accumulation, more accurately reflecting the true extent of central obesity [33]. Despite this, relying solely on WC as an obesity measure is typically discouraged due to its limitations. The WWI has been developed as an innovative obesity index that amalgamates WC’s advantages while addressing BMI’s limitations. The mechanisms through which WWI potentially influences CVD risk include: its association with fundamental obesity-related risk factors for CVD, the capacity of adipose tissue to initiate inflammatory responses, escalate oxidative stress, and trigger neurohormonal activities, all of which contribute to increased cardiometabolic risk [34, 35]). Furthermore, excess adipose tissue may produce reactive oxygen species, diminishing nitric oxide levels and consequently leading to endothelial dysfunction, a key factor in CVD development [36]. Additionally, obesity’s direct impact on myocardial function may alter cardiac structure and functionality, potentially culminating in obese cardiomyopathy or sudden cardiac death [37, 38]. Recent investigations using the NHANES and UK Biobank databases have scrutinized the relationship between the WWI and both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Research by Zhang et al. revealed that in patients with T2DM, elevated WWI is linked to higher mortality rates from both cardiovascular causes and all causes [24]. In a parallel finding, Han et al. confirmed that in the general adult population of the U.S., WWI is a predictor of mortality due to cardiovascular reasons as well as other causes [23]. These results are consistent with our own, indicating the predictive capability of WWI for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Nonetheless, prior studies have not addressed the relationship between WWI and specific cardiovascular events such as MACEs, MSD and CHF, which our research now addresses.

This study marks the first to correlate WWI with prospective cardiovascular events and overall mortality among T2DM patients within the ACCORD study framework. Analyzing data from 10,148 subjects, we observed that an elevated baseline WWI significantly correlated with a heightened risk of negative cardiovascular outcomes and increased overall mortality. These correlations persisted as statistically significant even after controlling for major confounding factors. Specifically, the risk for MACEs and MSD was notably higher in the uppermost WWI quartile, 1.19 and 1.25 times respectively, compared to the lowest quartile, and for CHF and TM, the risk increased by 1.43 times and 1.31 times. We utilized RCS to investigate potential non-linear relationships, discovering a linear trend between WWI and MACEs and MSD, but a non-linear trend with overall mortality and CHF risks. The inflection points were determined at 10.0 for overall mortality and 10.92 for CHF. Employing a two-stage Cox regression model, we identified that on the higher side of these inflection points, each unit decrease in WWI corresponded to a 19% and 45% reduction in the likelihood of overall mortality and CHF occurrence, respectively. Moreover, we found that the relationship between WWI and TM in T2DM patients is U-shaped, with both very low and very high levels of WWI increasing the risk of TM. This evidence accentuates the clinical relevance and applicability of WWI, promoting improved clinical consultations, better patient self-management, and informing public health interventions to decrease cardiovascular risks and mortality in T2DM patients. Sustaining WWI below 10.00, but not excessively low, through diet and lifestyle changes markedly reduces mortality risks and bolsters cardiovascular health. Our sensitivity analysis corroborated the durability of these findings, with subsequent stratification and interaction analyses aligning closely with the primary outcomes. Importantly, WWI emerged as a more potent predictor of CHF in T2DM patients with a disease duration under ten years, indicating that early diabetes stages, primarily driven by central obesity and metabolic imbalances, are effectively signaled by WWI. Conversely, in patients with a longer duration of diabetes, the complexity of heart failure risk factors may diminish the predictive strength of WWI.

Our research underscores the utility of the novel obesity metric, WWI, as an effective predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes and overall mortality in T2DM patients. Maintaining WWI within a defined healthy range is associated with significant improvements in cardiovascular health and reductions in mortality. These observations highlight the importance of diligent monitoring of WWI for clinical management and prevention, particularly in diabetic patients. Accurate management of body composition may help mitigate cardiovascular risks and reduce mortality, emphasizing the need for a proactive approach throughout the diagnosis and ongoing management of these patients. Furthermore, incorporating WWI into traditional models with conventional risk factors significantly enhances the predictive performance for cardiovascular risk and TM in T2DM patients.Moreover, WWI demonstrates better predictive performance for MACEs and MSD compared to traditional obesity indicators like BMI and WC.The superior predictive value of WWI for cardiovascular outcomes in patients with T2DM may be attributed to its ability to more accurately reflect central fat accumulation and abnormal fat distribution. Obesity is highly prevalent among individuals with T2DM and plays a central role in the development of insulin resistance and cardiovascular complications.In contrast to BMI, which does not distinguish between fat and lean mass, and WC, which is influenced by body size, WWI adjusts for body weight and more effectively reflects visceral adiposity [18, 39].Visceral fat is metabolically active and contributes to insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are key mechanisms in the development of cardiovascular disease, particularly in diabetic populations [36, 40].Moreover, a high WWI may indicate sarcopenic obesity, a phenotype marked by increased central fat and decreased muscle mass, which is associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk and mortality [41–43].Furthermore, WWI’s consistent association with atherosclerotic burden and metabolic markers across diverse populations highlights its potential clinical utility [20, 44].

This study represents the first rigorous evaluation of the predictive capabilities of WWI for future cardiovascular incidents and mortality in T2DM patients. Following extensive adjustments for confounders, our extensive, long-term follow-up of a large T2DM cohort has conclusively validated the predictive utility of WWI. The findings underscore the profound clinical value and potential future applications of WWI.

However, this study is not without limitations. It primarily relies on post-hoc analyses from the ACCORD and ACCORDION trials, and despite comprehensive adjustments via multivariate Cox regression for potential confounders, including commonly used medications, the possibility of unaddressed or unknown confounding factors remains. Furthermore, the exclusion of subjects with incomplete baseline data could introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of our results. While multiple imputation techniques were employed to manage missing data, with consistent results across deletion analyses, subsequent research should aim to utilize more complete data sets to strengthen external validity. Moreover, the observational design of this study precludes establishing causative relationships between baseline WWI and future cardiovascular events in T2DM patients; therefore, meticulously designed prospective intervention studies are necessary to further investigate these correlations. Also, the ACCORD and ACCORDION trial participants were primarily high-risk cardiovascular T2DM patients from North America, possibly limiting the wider applicability of the findings. Caution should be exercised when generalizing these results to other demographics, necessitating additional studies across varied cohorts to corroborate these findings.Furthermore, since some participants had pre-existing chronic conditions at baseline, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of reverse causality, in which prior disease status may influence body composition metrics such as WWI, BMI, or WC. This should be considered when interpreting the associations reported in this study.In addition, although our predictive models showed statistically significant improvements in AUC, NRI, and IDI after incorporating WWI, the overall magnitude of improvement was modest. This reflects a limitation of the model’s discrimination ability, indicating a need for further refinement and external validation in future research.

In conclusion, our research confirms a significant correlation between WWI and both future adverse cardiovascular outcomes and TM in T2DM patients. WWI has proven to be a robust predictor of these outcomes, providing essential information for cardiovascular risk assessment that could inform public health initiatives aimed at improving targeted interventions and management for T2DM patients. Continuing to investigate WWI’s clinical efficacy and validating its significant role in cardiovascular risk assessment remains imperative for future studies.
