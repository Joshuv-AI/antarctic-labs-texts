Enhancing smartphone security with human centric bimodal fallback authentication leveraging sensors
Authors: Asma Ahmad Farhan, Amna Basharat, Nasser Allheeib, Summrina Kanwal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Smartphones store valuable personal information, necessitating robust authentication methods to protect user data. This research proposes a lightweight bi-model fallback authentication technique that combines dynamic security questions and finger pattern recognition using inertial measurement units. The dynamic security questions are generated based on the smartphone’s usage behavior, while the owner’s finger movements are captured using four different inertial sensors: accelerometer, gyroscope, gravity sensor, and magnetometer. By combining the answers to the questions and the owner’s finger movements, the user can be authenticated even if the primary authentication method fails. In this study, data was collected from 24 participants, including 12 primary phone users and 12 close adversaries, over a span of 28 days. The dynamic security questions, derived from call, SMS, battery charging events, application usage, location, and physical activity categories, achieved high accuracy rates, with call, SMS, and application usage surpassing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90\%$$\end{document}90%. Incorporating the inertial measurement units significantly improved the accuracy of all question types, increasing from a maximum of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$76\%$$\end{document}76% to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90.99\%$$\end{document}90.99%, while also enhancing the True Positive Rate from 0.79 to 0.99 compared to a previous study. This research presents a promising lightweight bi-model fallback authentication technique that leverages dynamic security questions and inertial measurement units data, demonstrating its effectiveness for enhancing smartphone security.

Introduction

The increasing use of smartphones for various daily tasks has made them an integral part of people’s lives. From social media and online shopping to banking applications, smartphones have become indispensable. With sensitive personal information stored on these devices, safeguarding data has become crucial1. To ensure security, users rely on primary authentication methods. However, there are instances when these methods fail, such as forgotten passwords or multiple unauthorized access attempts1. In such cases, fallback authentication methods are necessary1–4.

The current techniques for dynamic security questions expose the usage history of users, as all options are chosen from the usage history5. Moreover, attackers can potentially spy on users to learn about their usage patterns. To mitigate this risk, in this paper, we propose a novel fallback authentication approach that combines dynamic security questions with the user’s hand movement patterns. Our intuition is that the unique hand movement pattern, along with dynamic security questions, will result in a secure secondary authentication method.

Primary authentication methods should prioritize both security and speed to meet the demands of frequent smartphone usage2. Users utilize various authentication methods, including biometric options like fingerprint and facial recognition offered by modern devices1,2,4,6. PIN codes and passwords continue to be widely used, with PIN codes protecting the SIM card and passwords required for unlocking the smartphone2. However, despite the recommendation of using complex passwords, they can be compromised through shoulder surfing and smudge attacks1,7. Pattern locks, although popular, are also susceptible to similar attacks2. Moreover, direction locks are less commonly employed, while fingerprint scanners have gained popularity due to their convenience and enhanced security2,8. Additionally, facial and iris scanners serve as alternative biometric methods8,9.

Primary authentication methods can fail to recognize the user, prompting the need for a password entry. Failed attempts, such as entering incorrect passwords multiple times, can result in prolonged lockout periods. Adversaries, including close individuals like siblings or partners, may target personal data and conversations stored on the device, while other attackers aim for photos, videos, social media accounts, or banking details2,3. Physical access and incorrect inputs during the absence of the primary user can be used to guess passwords or patterns. Biometric authentication methods, like fingerprint scanners, may prompt password entry if the wrong finger is repeatedly used, and multiple incorrect password attempts can extend the lockout period1. Damage to specialized hardware used in biometric methods, such as water or drop damage, can also lead to authentication failures2,8,9. A 2007 study found that users typically have around 25 accounts, each requiring a password10. However, managing multiple accounts with different complex password policies poses challenges, leading to frequent password forgetfulness. This compromises security and increases vulnerability to attacks2,3,10.

When the primary authentication method fails, the backup authentication method, known as the “fallback” authentication method, is employed2,3. If the fallback method successfully recognizes the user, access is granted; otherwise, it access is denied. Fallback authentication methods are rarely used, as primary methods seldom fail, making it unnecessary for them to be as fast. Nonetheless, they should still prioritize security and user-friendliness when needed.

Literature review

Fallback authentication methods, as discussed in the literature1–4,11,12, encompass security questions, behavioral biometrics, and email-based methods. Security questions can be classified into two types: static security questions4,11and dynamic security questions1–3,12. Furthermore, static security questions can be subcategorized based on system-generated data11or user data4. We explore and synthesize notable research and advancements in the field of fallback authentication.

Security questions

Security questions serve as a means of verifying a user’s identity by presenting questions and authenticating their answers2. They are commonly employed by popular web services and email providers as a fallback authentication method for password resets. Two broad categories of security questions exist: static and dynamic. Static security questions can be further divided into user-based and system-generated questions.

Static security questions

Static security questions rely on users’ long-term memory and are used during setup and fallback authentication12. Users provide answers to these questions, and if the answers match during authentication, access is granted. However, static security questions are susceptible to guessability, as close acquaintances can guess the answers through shared knowledge or online searches2,12. Sharing personal information on social media platforms further compromises their security. Additionally, users often forget the questions and answers they selected, leading to potential access issues. Studies indicate that a 78–80% of users can recall their answers, and a notable portion of attacks involve guessing by close adversaries13,14. Furthermore, users tend to forget 20–22% of their security question answers within a 3 months.

In response to the challenges of static security questions, Hang proposed location-based security questions4. These questions incorporate map-based locations in the answers, utilizing the user’s life events and their association with specific places. For instance, questions like “When and where was your first plane travel?” are used, where the user selects a location on a map, and the answer is considered correct if it falls within a 30-meter radius. By employing map-based locations, the memorability for users increases, while the guessability for attackers decreases due to the difficulty of pinpointing a specific location on the map. Hang conducted a four-week study, with an additional six-month study for long-term results, achieving accuracy rates of 95% and 92% respectively. They provided a set of 22 predefined fixed questions and also allowed users to create their own guided questions based on provided guidelines. However, one limitation is that internet access is necessary to load and search locations on the map, making this method impractical in situations where users lack internet connectivity, such as when traveling to different cities or countries.

To address the limitations of traditional static security questions, another approach involves the use of system-generated data11,15. In this method, the system creates fictional characters and generates corresponding data such as names, pet names, and dates of birth. Users can then utilize this generated data in their security questions, making it more difficult for attackers to guess the answers by searching online profiles. However, a significant drawback of this approach is the lack of memorability for users16.

To address the memorability problem11, proposed a game design. They used a game “4 pics 1 word” for this purpose. They ask questions from the system generated data which user use in security questions. They encourage the user to play this game on regular basis so that the user can remember and recall system generated data. Because fallback authentication method is used infrequently3, it is not a good approach to ask user to play games on regular bases for that method user may use after a long time or may do not use even once.

Dynamic security questions

The other type of security questions i.e., dynamic security questions are generated by exploiting the usage behavior pattern of the user1–3,12. In the fallback authentication methods, dynamic security questions perform better than static security questions2,3,12. However, the main issue with these methods is that these compromise on the privacy of the users. Dynamic security questions are generally based on usage history of the user for example: call, SMS, location, and application usage etc. These questions are generally multi-choice type questions in which the user has to select the correct answer. Further, wrong options are also generated using the usage history of user.

In one of the works2, proposed a dynamic security question approach utilizing smartphone usage history. They used seven categories of smartphone data. Questions were divided into recall type (requiring users to remember specific information) and recognize type (requiring users to choose the correct option). In their 30-day study with 24 participants, they achieved high accuracy rates ranging from 94.1% to 98% for different question categories, considering three potential close adversaries3. conducted research on dynamic security questions for smartphones, focusing on user-friendly yet difficult-to-guess categories and questions. They conducted a pre-study and a final study, with participants and adversaries. In the pre-study, participants provided 74% correct answers, outperforming adversaries. The performance further improved in the final study. Moreover, in another study1, lockout scenarios and countermeasures for smartphones were explored. The authors proposed an alternative authentication concept using security questions related to installed and non-installed applications on the smartphone, achieving 95% accuracy by excluding system applications.

Behavioral biometrics

Behavioral biometrics, another fallback authentication method, utilize the user’s behavior and interaction patterns with a device for authentication. These methods leverage smartphone sensors to capture user actions such as device handling, movement, and touch. Buriro introduced a bi-model behavioral biometric authentication approach for smartphones, focusing on micro-movements and finger movements on the screen17. Users are prompted to sign their name on the smartphone’s screen, and readings from inertial measurement units (accelerometer, gravity sensor, and magnetometer) are recorded along with finger movement and velocity during the signature. The scheme does not include the actual image of the signature, making it difficult for attackers to reproduce the user’s movements and holding style. Through implementation and evaluation on commercially available smartphones, Buriro achieved a 95% True Acceptance Rate (TAR) with a 3.1% False Acceptance Rate (FAR). Moreover, several researchers have developed datasets suitable for authentication tasks, as demonstrated in studies such as18–20. Authentication can be achieved by analyzing behavioral patterns like hand movements and walking styles. To this end21–24, have proposed methods that utilize sensors to gather data on individuals’ walking styles and behavioral patterns for continuous authentication. Moreover25–28, have proposed approaches that combine biometric, touch, and behavioral patterns for smartphone authentication.

Despite the previous successes of dynamic authentication approaches, a significant drawback is their reliance on smartphone usage history, which can be exploited by attackers who spy on the user. In this work, we address this issue by proposing a fallback authentication model that incorporates both usage history and the user’s behavioral biometrics. We also redefine the security questions in a manner which does not expose any personal information of the user. The underlying idea is that while a spy might be able to answer security questions based on usage history, they would be unable to replicate the user’s unique behavioral biometrics. Furthermore, we collected a dataset to validate the performance of our proposed approach.

A smart approach for human centric bi-model fallback authentication

Our approach integrates both security question answering and behavioral biometrics in a bi-modal authentication technique, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Along with the user’s responses to security questions, we also capture data from inertial measurement units during the authentication process. This collected inertial measurement units data is then compared with previously stored data to verify and authenticate the user. Moreover, in this work, we focus on the immediate threat posed by insiders or close relatives. Our initial research in designing the experiments indicated that these individuals, who are familiar with the user’s habits and behaviors, often employ simple social engineering tactics that can be just as effective as advanced technical attacks. Moreover, these everyday threats are often underestimated compared to those posed by experts, and our study emphasizes their importance. However, we acknowledge the need to include advanced adversarial strategies in future assessments to ensure a thorough analysis. Considering both will help strengthen our security frameworks against both common and sophisticated threats. Category selection

The selection of categories plays a crucial role in generating dynamic security questions. An optimal question pool should include questions that are easy for users to answer but difficult for attackers to guess. Previous research has utilized categories such as call history, SMS records, location information, battery charging events, application usage, music preferences, physical activity, and captured images. However, in our study, we have excluded the music and captured images categories due to concerns related to memorability and privacy. Listening to music while multitasking makes it challenging for users to recall specific song-related information. Additionally, the display of user-captured images in questions raises privacy concerns and may cause discomfort for the primary user. Consequently, we have focused on the remaining six categories for our research.

Dynamic question generation

All questions are multiple choice type questions in which the user has to select the right answer from the available options. Only the data obtained during the past 24 hours is used to generate the questions to overcome the memorability issues. Generally, users can easily recall previous 24 hours activities as compared to 48 hours or one week2,3. Questions from all categories are shown in Table 2. Below we describe the details of the approach for question generation respective to each category.

Question generation based on call

Questions in this category requires the user to select one right option from the four available choices. After getting previous 24 hours data, calls which have 0 seconds duration or from unknown numbers are filtered because it is hard to remember the exact time for these2. After filtering the data, one random entry is selected from the data to generate the question. Contact name, call type, duration, and time is used for generating the question as shown in Table 2. For the available four options, two options are selected from the previous 24 hour data (including one right and one wrong answer) and the remaining two options are selected from the contact list (all four selected options are different, and one option cannot be selected more than once). By using this method, the question does not expose the call history of the user (as all options are not selected from the actual call history of the user). It also makes it harder to guess the right answer for the attacker.

Question generation based on SMS

Questions in this category are similar to the ones generated in call category. Table 2 shows the question in this category. The four available answer options are generated in a manner similar to the call category.

Question generation based on battery charging events

Only three options are available for this category because the available charging methods are only three (AC adapter (direct charging), USB (computer charging) and wireless). Due to this limitation, every question has all the three options, and the user has to choose one of these.

Question generation based on physical activity

Four types of activities which include walking, running, in-vehicle, and on-bicycle are used for generating questions in this category. The still activity is not used because this is the most common activity (especially when the user is sleeping or if the phone is placed on a flat surface) and also is easy to guess2. On-foot activity is also not used because this is the parent activity of walking and running. Minimum duration of 10 minute activities are used to increase memorability. Activity type, time and duration attributes are used to generate the question. All four possible activities are shown as options and the user has to choose the correct one.

Question generation based on application usage

System applications like home screen, launcher and settings are removed for generating questions of this category. Two options are selected from the actual used applications and the other two options are selected from the pre-defined library (predefined library contains those applications which may or may not have been installed on the device). The use of predefined library helps to not expose all applications installed on the device. Data used for this question includes application icon, time and duration. The generated question shows four icons as options, and the user has to choose the icon of the correct application. Questions generated are shown in Table 2.

Bio-metric data collection

Every user not only holds their phone differently, the user’s hand movements are also different while holding and using the smartphone. By using inertial sensors of the smartphone, we can detect the holding pattern of the user for the purpose of authentication. In order to authenticate the user, inertial sensors including gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer and gravity sensor are very helpful . All these sensors provide readings in three dimensions (x-axis, y-axis and z-axis). We employ all four of these sensors in our study. Results and discussion

Participants

Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from participants in accordance with guidelines set and approved by the Ethics Research Committee at the National University of Computer & Emerging Sciences, Islamabad, Pakistan. After data collection, following the guidelines provided by the Ethics Research Committee, the data was anonymized to remove any identifying information, .

Total 24 participants took part in this research study, out of which, 12 users of smartphone were chosen as smartphone owners and 12 were chosen to act as their close adversaries. Participants were required to own an Android smartphone with API level 23 or above, and have basic knowledge about smartphone usage. Participants were asked to fill a consent form to take part in the research work. Details about the data collection was described in the form. In addition, basic demographic information about the participants (i.e., name, gender, age, and occupation) was recorded. In this study, 8 users were male and 4 were female. Users’ age was between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$18-28$$\end{document}18-28 years with 24 years average. 15 users were students (7 from Engineering, 5 from Computer Science, 2 from Psychology and 1 was doing MBA), 3 were teachers and 2 house wives were also part of the study.

One close adversary was required per user to act as an attacker to get access of the smartphone. 7 users’ adversaries were their close friends, and 5 were siblings. 4 users played the role of an adversary for each other. They were either close friends or roommates.

Study design

There are two parts to this study. In the first part, data was collected from the users, and in the second part, data was collected from their adversaries. Two data collection applications were developed: (i) for smartphone usage data collection and (ii) for inertial sensors data collection. The users were required to answer security questions on a daily basis. The number of questions was based on the available data within the previous 24 hours, with a maximum of 10 questions per category. The data from adversaries was collected once a week. Adversaries were required to answer the security questions using the primary users’ phone. Since it was difficult for all adversaries to access their respective user’s phone on a daily basis, they were only required to answer the security questions once a week.

Thus, in order to authenticate the user, 10 questions can be asked. 10 points are assigned on the right answer and 30 points are deducted on a wrong answer. If the final score is positive, then the user gets the access otherwise the access is denied. According to this point based method, the users have to provide at least 8 right answers. A maximum of 2 wrong answers will be accepted out of 10. If the user provides more than two wrong answers, the final score will be a negative value (e.g., 7 x 10 = 70, 3 x -30 = -90, 70 - 90 = -20), and the access will be denied. Behavioral biometrics

For behavioral bio-metric, data was collected only when users and adversaries were answering the security questions with 0.05 milliseconds delay. Total 4 classifiers are considered that include: NB (Naive Bayes), K Nearest Neighbors (KNN), MLP (Multilayer Perceptron), and RF (Random Forest). All these classifiers are implemented using Weka33with default parameters. The frequency of data collection is very important for the results. If the frequency of data collection is very low then all classifiers perform worst and if the frequency of data collection is very high, then it gives garbage data and classifiers tend to over fit17,18. In this study, we found that the frequency of 0.05 milliseconds gives the best results.

For this study, True positive (TP) are instances which were actually from the user and classifiers also classify it as the user. True negative (TN) are instances which were actually from the adversary and classifiers also classify as the adversary. False positive (FP) are instances which were actually from the adversary but classifiers classify as the user. Lastly, False Negative (FN) are instances which were actually from the user but classifiers classify as the adversary. Discussion

The results of our study show that the questions from the battery charging events category performed worst with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$51.22\%$$\end{document}51.22% accuracy because guess-ability of this question was very high. The questions from the call category perform the best with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90.99\%$$\end{document}90.99% accuracy. All questions were multiple choice type questions with 4 available options. In order to reduce the random guess-ability of answers, minimum 7 questions are required to reduce the chances of guess-ability to as near as a 4 digit PIN. 10 questions are recommended, from which the user has to provide at least 8 correct answers in order to get authenticated. In this work, we comared the performance of NB, KNN, MLP, and RF (random forest). NB gives the minimum accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$91.20\%$$\end{document}91.20% amongst all others and MLP gives the best accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97.32\%$$\end{document}97.32%. However, NB also takes much less time as compared to all other classifiers, given that time taken is also every important for the users’ convenience.

Research limitations

Our experiments were limited to a small group of participants due to time constraints. However, for a smartphone authentication system designed for large-scale deployment, it is crucial to include a larger and more diverse participant pool from various countries to ensure the proposed approach’s effectiveness. Given that our solution is based on user responses, it offers inherent flexibility and can be repeatedly tested with different participant groups.

Conclusions & future work

Our research introduces a Bi-modal fallback authentication method for smartphone users, combining dynamic security questions and behavioral biometrics captured through inertial sensors. To protect participants’ privacy, we assign each one a random user ID for anonymity. Moreover, the sensitive data collected by the apps is encrypted before being stored on the phone and then securely transmitted to a server. In this way, the data available on the server is completely anonymized, ensuring the protection of users’ personal and sensitive information. The results highlight the varying accuracy of questions across different categories, with battery charging events performing the worst and call history performing the best. We recommend a minimum of seven questions to reduce guess-ability and suggest exploring alternative question formats. By employing classifiers such as Naive Bayes and Multilayer Perceptron, we achieved promising accuracy rates.Our proposed approach can be utilized by smartphone manufacturers as a service that operates seamlessly in the background. To enhance the memorability of security questions, the primary user can be prompted to answer them during device unlock events after every 24 to 48 hours.. Future work should focus on refining battery charging event questions and improving data collection using sensors. Our findings contribute to enhancing smartphone security and protecting personal information.
