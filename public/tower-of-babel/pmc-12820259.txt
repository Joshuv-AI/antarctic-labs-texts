Matrix-based pagerank control in hypergraphs for semantic text summaries
Authors: David Aleja, Regino Criado, Ángeles Criado-Alonso, Guillermo Vera de Salas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a novel description of random walks in a hypergraph using a labeled multidigraph. This is based on a special decomposition of the frequency matrix of relations. This decomposition enables intrinsic analysis and control of the random walker’s behavior at the matrix level, incorporating memory and modifying its behavior based on additional walk restrictions. As an aspect to highlight about this new vision, we will point out that it allows the non-back-tracking restriction to be generalized, facilitating the use of this tool in multiple applications. This article includes an application in the field of linguistics for documents summarization and the extraction of significant phrases from them.

Introduction

Over the last three decades, the introduction of various types and models of complex networks to represent the interactions between different parts of a complex system has become a widely recognized and popular phenomenon in almost all areas of knowledge 1–8. Choosing the right type of complex network is essential to simulate interactions between system components and analyze their properties. This has become a key research tool even in linguistics, which lies at the intersection of social sciences and humanities9–14.

Particularly, in the realm of computational linguistics, the exploration of novel network models has opened new avenues for understanding and summarizing textual data15–20. Multilayer network models and those based on high-order networks and hypergraphs have become increasingly significant in linguistic analysis due to their ability to represent complex relationships within language19,21,22. New models and tools based on high-order multilayer networks are introduced in21,22 together with their application to the field of linguistics. This novel framework allows for a deeper understanding of language dynamics and the interplay between different linguistic levels. This paper introduces a novel approach to get a semantic summarization of a text through the lexical layer of a multilayer model, as described in the recent work22 in which the concepts of line graph associated with a hypergraph and its derivative graph are applied.

Specifically, as an extension of22, where the most significant lexical words (units of vocabulary that have a specific and distinctive meaning within a particular field of specialization, mainly nouns, adjectives, and verbal nouns) are used and ordered to identify the most representative phrases of a specific text from the hypergraph associated with the analyzed corpus, in this work a different and complementary line of research is developed.

Thus, throughout the linguistic application of this work, we have in mind a “dual” vision of the considered hypergraph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {H}=(V,\varepsilon )$$\end{document} in which the vertices are the sentences and the hyperedges are the lexical words. By identifying the skeleton of a sentence as the set of lexical words it contains, we use these sets of lexical words to define the navigation modes of a random walker on this hypergraph, increasing or decreasing the number of lexical words that two phrases must share so that the walker can move from one node to another and using the defined structure in order to establish restrictions and conditions that allow us to refine the context of the sentences and the lexical words they share.

Therefore, in this new model, in which the sentences are the nodes and the hyperedges are the lexical words, we propose a method in which the movement from one sentence to another is governed by shared lexical words. The probability of transitioning between sentences increases with the number of shared lexical words, naturally highlighting the context in which these words appear (sets of lexical words common to these sentences).

This procedure allows us to obtain the most representative and significant phrases of a text (article or corpus) according to the real context of the lexical words it contains. In other words: the lexical words allow us to define the navigation modes of the random walkers considered in such a way as to guarantee that the use of a specific word shared by two sentences has a similar context and meaning in both. This approach facilitates not only the extraction of semantic summaries but also the identification of semantic subsets within the text.

In order to do all this, in this work we introduce a variant of the PageRank algorithm in hypergraphs that follows a logic similar to traditional PageRank23,24, but adapted to the structure of hypergraphs, considering an auxiliary labeled multidigraph that is described in Sect. 4.1. At this point it should be mentioned that the use of the PageRank algorithm and its applications in the field of hypergraphs is relatively recent. In25 two clustering algorithms based on personalized PageRank are developed using a methodology completely different from the one developed in this work, and the same happens with the version of PageRank for hypergraphs presented in26.

We conclude this introduction by highlighting among other mathematical achievements of the model presented its ability to intrinsically control and analyze the walks of a random walker on the hypergraph in such a way that the walker not only has memory (the next sentence in a walk depends on the sentence where the random walker is coming from), but the model also allows for the addition of other types of restrictions on the type of walks allowed with a view to obtaining a version of PageRank on the hypergraph that has these properties. This control of the random walker is achieved by defining an appropriate transition matrix on a newly constructed graph structure. Specifically, if the next step of the walker is not a random jump, the model will determine, in accordance with the established restrictions, where the random walker can travel depending on the vertex from which it comes. This is due to an appropriate notation introduced in this paper, which extends the relationship between the edge’s personalized PageRank and the nodes PageRank. This notation sheds light on this relationship, deepening and improving the result obtained in27, where a direct relationship between the PageRank of the nodes and the PageRank of the edges of a directed and weighted graph was shown. To accomplish this it is necessary to find a matrix decomposition of the relationship frequency matrix of the hypergraph that behaves well with the property of being stochastic (see Sect. 2). We prove that if the transition matrix P of a random walker can be decomposed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P=AB$$\end{document}, then the PageRank vector can be related to the PageRank vector of a random walker governed by the transition matrix BA (Theorem 3.2). This result provides a framework in which the new random walker, defined by BA, can be systematically controlled by modifying its transition matrix. Using this framework, we can subsequently recover the PageRank vector of the original random walker through the relation established in Theorem 3.3.

After this introduction, Sect. 2 introduces some preliminary concepts and technical results prior to the fundamental theorems. Section 3 is devoted to stablish a necessary theorem on random walkers in hypergraphs. In Sect. 4, we present two distinct settings in which such modifications of the transition matrix can be implemented. In particular, we introduce a decomposition based on the source and range matrices, giving rise to the line digraph, and another based on the head and tail matrices, giving rise to the subhyperedge digraph. In both structures, the non-backtracking property can be naturally incorporated. Furthermore, we also propose an additional modification based on the Jaccard index. Finally, Sect. 5 is dedicated to presenting an application of the model in the field of computational linguistics and in Sect. 6 we present the conclusions of this work. All the proofs can be found as Supplementary Note S3 online.

Our motivation arises as follows: random walkers on graphs are often modeled using row-stochastic matrices, such as the row-normalized adjacency matrix. Further steps in the walk are captured by higher powers of this matrix. However, when we attempt to impose natural constraints on the random walker, for instance, a non-backtracking condition, we quickly notice that such restrictions do not translate directly into matrix form. This is due to the fact that the standard model does not account for the node it came from.

Inspired by27, the authors in31 explored the connection between the PageRank vector of a graph and that of its line graph, using this relationship to compute the PageRank of a random walker with a non-backtracking restriction. This is achieved by modifying the adjacency matrix of the line graph. There is a clear structural connection between a graph and its line graph, but how do their adjacency matrices relate, especially in the context of modeling random walkers? This observation leads to the following insight. This raises an important question: If a random walker on a graph is modeled by the matrix AB (e.g., a row-stochastic adjacency matrix), is there a meaningful relationship with a random walker on the structure defined by BA? As we will show in Theorem 3.2, and in particular in Theorem 3.1, the answer is yes.

Now, a natural question arises: If P is the matrix that models a random walker on a graph, which factorizations of P are meaningful? The answer depends on the type of modifications we wish to impose on the walker’s behavior. In this work, we consider a random walker on a hypergraph, modeled by a row-stochastic adjacency matrix s(P). We present two distinct factorizations of the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s(P)=AB$$\end{document}, which enable us to control non-backtracking behavior by analyzing the modified structure defined by the reversed product BA.

More broadly, our aim is to study the general relationship between the PageRank vectors of the matrix products AB and BA, for any pair of matrices A and B. It is important to highlight that this philosophy of decomposition and twist, that is, decomposing the adjacency matrix into two factors and analyzing its “twisted” counterpart, has great potential. It opens new avenues for studying constrained random walkers on graph-based structures, thanks to the elegant algebraic connections between AB and BA.

The importance of the following theorem lies in the fact that a random walker is typically modeled by a row-stochastic matrix, which often originates from a matrix that is not row-stochastic. In practice, it is common to first analyze the behavior of a random walker on a given matrix and then work with its row-normalized version. Since our approach involves decomposing the adjacency matrix and then twisting the matrix product, it is crucial to ensure that the row-stochastic property is preserved after this transformation. Therefore, for our forthcoming decompositions, it will be essential to verify the hypotheses of this theorem to guarantee that the twisted matrix product remains row-stochastic, ensuring it continues to accurately model the behavior of the random walker.

Centrality measures are a way of quantifying the relative importance of a node in a complex network. One of the most important and widely used centrality measures is the centrality index known as PageRank33,34 and its variants, which form the fundamental basis on which the Google search engine relies to establish the rankings of web pages according to their importance. PageRank can be understood as the stable distribution of a random walker moving through a graph. Random walks and pagerank on graphs or digraphs

As previously motivated, if a random walker on a graph is modeled by a row-stochastic matrix AB, how can we relate this to a random walker on a new, potentially unknown graph, modeled instead by the row-stochastic matrix BA? The following theorem formalises the relationship between these two random walkers in the limit case, i.e., by describing the connection between their PageRank vectors.

Furthermore, we will also assume that a random walker on a hypergraph can jump, i.e., if with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-q)$$\end{document}, it can jump to any other vertex. The probability, knowing that is jumping, to arrive to any vertex is encoded in the personality vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vec {v}$$\end{document}, with all components positive and with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Vert \vec {v} \Vert _1 = 1$$\end{document}. Hence, the transition matrix of this random walker is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} qs(P) + (1-q)\vec {e}_N^{\top } \vec {v}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}We will refer as a step if the walker is using the matrix s(P) or a jump if is using the matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vec {e}_N^{\top } \vec {v}$$\end{document}.

Remark

We are identifying a random walk on a hypergraph with a random walk on its projected multigraph/weighted projected graph with adjacency matrix P.

We recall that our motivation is to modify the behavior of a random walker by identifying it with another random walker on a related graph structure, one where the desired features or constraints are more naturally expressed. In this context, our goal is to extend the non-backtracking restriction to hypergraphs. To achieve this, it is natural to reinterpret certain steps of the walk by regrouping consecutive steps occurring at the same node. This leads to the following identification: a walk of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n+1$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \ge 1$$\end{document}, on the original hypergraph corresponds to a walk of length n on a newly defined graph structure.

This new finite walk can be seen as a walk on the line digraph of a labeled multidigraph.

Modifications on the line digraph

A natural modification is to avoid walks where the same hyperedge is used consecutively, forcing the random walker to take a new hyperedge each time it moves. Therefore, avoiding these walks, means to cancel the directed edge connecting their respective vertices on the line digraph.

Heads and tails matrices

While these last modifications are theoretically well-founded, the computational cost can be substantial. The size of the edge set E may grow rapidly, since each hyperedge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h\in \varepsilon$$\end{document} generates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\left( {\begin{array}{c}|h|\\ 2\end{array}}\right) =|h|(|h|-1)$$\end{document} nodes in the line digraph. Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E| = \sum _{h\in \varepsilon }|h|(|h|-1)$$\end{document}, which can be large even for moderately sized hypergraphs. Application to computational linguistics: document summarization and extraction of the most significant phrases

The most common tasks related to computational linguistics consist of classifying texts, searching for answers to questions, summarizing texts and generating texts. For the development of this application, we have focused on summarizing texts and searching for and ordering the most representative phrases of a text (or a corpus) according to the context in which these phrases appear, regardless of the usefulness that the presented tool also has for text classification13,14,16,17. Based on the fact that the English language has four main classes of words39, and using a methodology close to supervised machine learning to extract and label lexical words (units of vocabulary with a specific meaning in a specialized language or a specific area of knowledge previously identified) we construct the hypergraph we are going to work on. In this hypergraph the nodes are the sentences and the hyperedges are the lexical words, since these words encapsulate concepts and terminologies belonging to a specific area of knowledge and are essential for understanding and communication in specialized contexts. Therefore, for the application developed in this work, computerized processing of a linguistic corpus has been carried out on a collection of texts compiled electronically as a representative sample of a subset of a language 40,41. Specifically, the corpus is made up of 86 abstracts and articles (volumes 1-6 of the International Journal of Complex Systems in Science (IJCSS), published between April 2011 and November 2016 (http://www.ij-css.org)), with a total of 147,637 words, of any type, and 25,210 sentences analyzed in this study. The unit of analysis considered in this work is the sentence, that is, the words between two points18,22. In addition, it is important to point out that commas and other punctuation marks within the sentence were eliminated for the analysis performed.

The meaning of a word often depends on its context, which is largely defined by the lexical words present in the same sentence. Therefore, it is reasonable to use a PageRank algorithm that incorporates contextual restrictions. In this model, the random walker moves between sentences only if they share a sufficient number of lexical words. This ensures that transitions reflect meaningful semantic relationships, especially in the specific field of summarizing texts written in a specialized language.

Thus, for the purposes of this article, we have in mind that a linguistic corpus can be mathematically modeled as a hypergraph maintaining a similar but dual approach to that used in22. Under this new perspective, the nodes represent the sentences in the corpus, and each sentence (understood as the set of lexical words between two points) is understood to belong to the hyperedges corresponding to the specific lexical words it contains. In the computer application developed in this section, we have focused on obtaining meaningful sentences and summaries of the corpus or part of it based on the computer and algorithmic processing of the context of these sentences, going far beyond the identification of the most frequent words or the lexical behavior of a set of these words, as is often the objective of many works of this type.

Conclusions

This work introduces an innovative methodology based on the matrix decomposition of the frequency matrix of relations in a hypergraph, providing a new perspective for describing random walks within the hypergraph using a labeled multidigraph and its associated line digraph. The labels in the multidigraph correspond to the origin node, the destination node, and the hyper-edge used to travel from the origin node to the destination node. The methodology allows for the correlation of walks through the hypergraph with walks through the line digraph, where desired restrictions can be established, and the movement of the random walker can be controlled using a matrix decomposition with previously demonstrated interesting properties. In fact, a step in a walk on the line digraph corresponds to two steps on the hypergraph since to determine the next step in a walk on the line digraph we need to know the total amount of sentences shared by two lexical words acting as the hyperedges involved in such step.

These properties enable intrinsic analysis and control of the random walker’s behavior at the matrix level, incorporating memory and modifying its behavior according to additional restrictions on the types of walks allowed.

A notable feature of this new perspective is its ability to generalize the non-backtracking restriction, thus enhancing the applicability of the tool in various domains. This article also presents an application that shows how to use the developed methodology to extract the most significant phrases from a text, once the desired level of common semantic context to relate one phrase to another has been established. This results in a ranking of phrases ordered by their importance and the level of shared context, determined by the number of lexical words they must share to be related.

The application of this methodology in the field of computational linguistics is not limited to document summarization or the extraction of the most significant phrases from a text. It also provides an additional tool for locating and classifying new linguistic characteristics intrinsic to the style of different authors, based on the specific use of lexical words according to different levels of semantic context.

This methodology offers new opportunities for linguistic research. It provides advanced tools for analyzing specialized texts, detecting authorship, studying stylistic patterns, and improving machine translation. The ability to identify semantic and stylistic patterns through complex networks provides a solid foundation for future research aimed at deepening the understanding of language dynamics and its evolution.

Using the concept of lexical word as defined in18,21,22, this approach enhances the precision and clarity of specialized corpus analysis. The integration of these concepts into the methodology allows for a more nuanced understanding of semantic relationships within texts, facilitating more accurate and context-rich summaries.

In conclusion, this work not only allows the analysis and control of the behavior of a random walker according to the types of walks allowed, but also provides valuable insights and tools for various linguistic applications, ultimately contributing to a deeper and more complete understanding of language and its intricate structures.
