Subspace gradient descent methods for linear tensor equations Thanks: Version of February 24, 2026.
Martina Iannacito, Lorenzo Piccinini ∗, Valeria Simoncini ∗

Abstract
The numerical solution of algebraic tensor equations is a largely open and challenging task. Assuming that the operator is symmetric and positive definite, we propose two new gradient-descent type methods for tensor equations that generalize the recently proposed Subspace Conjugate Gradient ( S s –cg ), D. Palitta et al, SIAM J. Matrix Analysis and Appl (2025). As our interest is mainly in a modest number of tensor modes, the Tucker format is used to efficiently represent low-rank tensors. Moreover, mixed-precision strategies are employed in certain subtasks to improve the memory usage, and different preconditioners are applied to enhance convergence. The potential of our strategies is illustrated by experimental results on tensor-oriented discretizations of three-dimensional partial differential equations with separable coefficients. Comparisons with the state-of-the-art Alternating Minimal Energy (AMEn) algorithm confirm the competitiveness of the proposed strategies.

Introduction

We are interested in solving the tensor equation

\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathdutchbcal{x})=\mathdutchbcal{c}

(1)

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is a sum of tensorized operators acting on

\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
, and is symmetric and positive definite, in
a sense that will be defined later; moreover,

\mathdutchbcal{x}
and
\mathdutchbcal{c}
are tensors of appropriate order and size.
As an example, in the case
d=3
, the Kronecker form of (
1
) reads

(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\bm{{C}}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{{B}}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{{A}}}_{i})\underline{\bm{x}}=\underline{\bm{c}},

where
{\bm{{A}}}_{i},{\bm{{B}}}_{i},{\bm{{C}}}_{i}
are matrices and
\underline{\bm{x}}
,

\underline{\bm{c}}

are vectors of conforming dimension.

Tensor equations naturally appear in several domains such as quantum
chemistry
[
27
,
28
]
, financial mathematics
[
19
,
39
]
,
imaging
[
22
]
,
deep neural networks
[
20
]
; we refer to
[
12
]
for additional
established applications.
Tensor equations also arise whenever certain discretizations of (parameterized)
partial differential equations
of dimension three or higher are employed, see, e.g.,

[
35
,
26
,
24
]
.

Typical solvers for tensor equations
use a low-rank format to represent the objects involved in the computations.
Among these, we recall the Tucker decomposition
[
37
]
, the Canonical
Polyadic Decomposition (CP)
[
17
,
23
]
, the Hierarchical Tucker
(HT)
[
15
,
13
]
, and the Tensor-Train
(TT)
[
40
,
29
]
.
Moreover, methods can be grouped in two main categories.
In the first class, we include all methods that formulate the equation as an optimization problem. The
solution is assumed to belong to a specific low-rank tensor set, whose specific constraints are used to design an optimization strategy. For instance, ALS-based methods optimize each factor of the chosen
low-rank representation of
\mathdutchbcal{x}
, while keeping the others constant.
Among widely used methods we recall
the Density Matrix Re-orthogonalization Group (DMRG) method
[
40
]
and its variants
such as MALS
[
18
]
, and Riemannian optimization techniques
such as
[
3
]
, whose low-rank sets are Riemannian manifolds.
The second family includes iterative methods adapted from classical vector iterations.
Examples of such algorithms are power methods combined with Canonical Polyadic
tensor decomposition
[
2
]
, Krylov based methods combined with different
low-rank tensor representations, for example CG and BiCG with
H-Tucker
[
24
,
36
]
, and GMRES with
TT
[
7
,
4
]
,
see
[
12
]
for a richer account.
In between these two classes, there is the Alternating Minimal Energy (AMEn)
algorithm
[
8
]
that merges the ALS scheme with an algebraic solver, all in
the TT format. This method is one of the most robust and efficient
solvers for tensor equations both in the symmetric and nonsymmetric cases.
Existing optimization-based solvers, such as AMEn, may experience lack of convergence
guarantees and the risk of swamping in local minima, whereas classical
iterative solvers usually offer a more transparent management of memory,
accuracy control and possibilities for parallelization.

In this work, we propose to combine the idea of subspace gradient-descent strategies
(such as in the matrix algorithm
s
s
–
cg

[
30
]
) with the Tucker format to
attack the symmetric tensor equation. The matrix subspace approach developed in
[
30
]

generates a gradient-descent type recurrence for the approximate solution, in which the
coefficient of the new direction is determined by subspace projection.
In our setting, the choice of the Tucker format is natural for two reasons. First, when
dealing with few modes,
the Tucker representation is quite competitive with respect to other formats that can handle massive
numbers of modes, such as Tensor Train. Second, this representation makes it easier to identify
the projection subspaces that are used to update the sequence iterates.
We derive two closely related approaches, which may be viewed as the subspace tensor oriented
versions of the steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods, from which we borrow the names.
Our methods are equipped with various features to improve performance, such as preconditioning,
mixed-precision computations and inexact inner solves.

Preliminary numerical experiments on various three-dimensional discretizations by finite
differences of elliptic self-adjoint partial differential equations with
separable coefficients are reported,
to illustrate the competitiveness of our newly designed solvers with respect to the
well established AMEn method.

1.1
Notation and preliminaries

Low-case, underlined bold letters denote vectors (
\underline{\bm{x}}
), while capital,
bold letters (
{\bm{{A}}}
) denote matrices.
Low-case, calligraphic, bold letters (
\mathdutchbcal{x}
) stand for order-
d
tensors, either in full or in compressed format, of size
({n}_{1},\dots,n_{d})
.
To shorten the notation, we say that order-
d
tensors belong to
\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
where
\underline{\bm{n}}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
. Upper-case, calligraphic, bold letters (
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
) stand for linear operators among tensor spaces. The identity matrix of dimension
n\times n
is
denoted
\mathbb{I}
, as long as the size is clear from the context.
In the following we will make great use of the Tucker format for tensors. In particular, a
d
-mode tensor
can be represented as
\mathdutchbcal{y}={\bm{{U}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{U}}}_{d}\mathdutchbcal{x}
where
\mathdutchbcal{x}
is the core
tensor
[
37
,
1
]
.
The mode-
k
tensor-times-matrix product between a matrix

{\bm{{U}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}\times n_{k}}
and a tensor
\mathdutchbcal{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
is a mode-wise
tensor-times-matrix (TTM) multiplication denoted as
\mathdutchbcal{y}=\mathdutchbcal{x}\times_{k}{\bm{{U}}}
,
where
\mathdutchbcal{y}(\underline{\bm{i}})=\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{n_{k}}{\bm{{U}}}(i_{k},j_{k})\mathdutchbcal{x}(i_{1},\dots,j_{k},\dots,i_{d})
.

Let
\mathdutchbcal{x}
be an order-
d
tensor of size
\underline{\bm{n}}
, and
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
a multilinear operator between tensor spaces of compatible order and size.
As a linear operator between two vector spaces (tensor space of order-
1
) can be represented by a matrix (tensor of order-
2
), a multilinear operator between tensor spaces of order
d
can be represented by a tensor of order
2d
.
Hence, the image of
\mathdutchbcal{x}
through
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
corresponds
to the contraction between the last
d
modes of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
and all the
d
modes of
\mathdutchbcal{x}
, that is

\left(\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathdutchbcal{x})\right)(\underline{\bm{i}})=(\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x})(\underline{\bm{i}})=\sum_{\underline{\bm{j}}=\bm{1}}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}})\mathdutchbcal{x}(\underline{\bm{j}}),

(2)

with
\underline{\bm{i}}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
and
\underline{\bm{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})
multilinear indices. This is the standard way to generalize the matrix-vector product to the tensor framework. Similarly we can define the composition of two multilinear operators
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
, that is
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\mathdutchbcal{x}))
as the contraction between the last
d
modes of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
and all the
d
modes of
\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\mathdutchbcal{x})
computed as in Equation (
2
), that is

\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\mathdutchbcal{x})\bigr)\bigr)(\underline{\bm{i}})=\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}\mathdutchbcal{x}\bigr)(\underline{\bm{i}})=\sum_{\underline{\bm{j}},\underline{\bm{k}}=1}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}})\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\underline{\bm{j}},\underline{\bm{k}})\mathdutchbcal{x}(\underline{\bm{k}}).

(3)

To simplify the exposition, we will write
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}
and
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}\mathdutchbcal{x}
, referring to the operations in (
2
) and (
3
). Additionally, we assume that
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is symmetric and positive definite, that is,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}})=\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\underline{\bm{j}},\underline{\bm{i}})
for whatever multilinear indices
\underline{\bm{i}}
and
\underline{\bm{j}}
, and
\langle\mathdutchbcal{x},\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}\rangle>0
, where the inner product between tensors is defined as

\langle\mathdutchbcal{x},\mathdutchbcal{y}\rangle:=\sum_{\underline{\bm{i}}=\bm{1}}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\mathdutchbcal{x}(\underline{\bm{i}})\mathdutchbcal{y}(\underline{\bm{i}}).

2
Subspace gradient descent methods for
multilinear system

With the operations formalized above,
we aim at finding the solution
\mathdutchbcal{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
to the multilinear system in (
1
)
using an iterative method.

We follow the classical derivation of gradient descent methods as a procedure to approximate the minimizer of a convex function.
Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as

\Phi(\mathdutchbcal{x})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathdutchbcal{x},\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}\rangle-\langle\mathdutchbcal{x},\mathdutchbcal{c}\rangle,

(4)

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}
is computed as in (
2
), and
consider the following minimization problem: Find
\mathdutchbcal{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
such that

\mathdutchbcal{x}^{*}={\rm arg}\min_{\mathdutchbcal{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}}\Phi(\mathdutchbcal{x}).

Starting with a zero initial guess
\mathdutchbcal{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
and the multilinear operator

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
,
we define the recurrence
\{\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

of approximate solutions by means of the following relation

\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k+1}=\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k},

(5)

where
\bm{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}
is computed as in (
2
).
We emphasize that
\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}
, the vector of dimensions of the last
d
modes of
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
, depends on the iteration index
k
and it may (and will) change as the iterations proceed, possibly growing
up to a certain maximum value, corresponding
to the maximum allowed rank of all iterates.

The corresponding recurrence for the residual is

\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}=\mathdutchbcal{c}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k+1}=\mathdutchbcal{c}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k})=\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k},

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}
is computed as in (
3
).
We require that the tensor operator
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}

satisfies a descent direction
condition completely conforming to the vector case, that is

\langle\nabla\Phi(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}),\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\rangle<0.

(6)

To determine
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
, we
let
\phi(\bm{\alpha})=\Phi(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha})
.
For given
\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k},\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
,
at the
k
th iteration we construct
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
so that

\phi(\bm{\alpha}_{k})=\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}}\phi(\bm{\alpha}).

(7)

The minimizer
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
can be explicitly determined by
solving a multilinear equation of reduced dimensions,
as
described in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

The minimizer
\bm{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}
of (
7
)
is the unique solution of

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k},

(8)

where for
\underline{\bm{\ell}},\underline{\bm{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}
and
\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
multilinear indices,

\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}\bigr)(\underline{\bm{\ell}})=\sum_{\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}},\underline{\bm{h}}=\bm{1}}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{\ell}})\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\underline{\bm{i}},\underline{\bm{j}})\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\underline{\bm{j}},\underline{\bm{h}})\bm{\alpha}(\underline{\bm{h}}).

(9)

Proof.

We start by explicitly writing the function
\phi
, that is

\phi(\bm{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha},\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha}\bigr)\rangle-\langle\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\alpha},\mathdutchbcal{c}\rangle.

To ease the derivation, we introduce the vectorization and matricization of tensors and the tensor operators, respectively.
More precisely, we let

\underline{\bm{c}}=\text{vec}(\mathdutchbcal{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}=\text{vec}(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for vectorizations, and

{\bm{{A}}}=\text{mat}(\bm{\mathcal{A}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{{P}}}_{k}=\text{mat}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s_{k}}

(10)

for matrices,
where
n=\prod_{h=1}^{d}n_{h}
and
s_{k}=\prod_{h=1}^{d}s_{k,h}
.
The function
\phi
transforms into

\hat{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\hat{\phi}(\underline{\bm{\alpha}})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}},\,{\bm{{A}}}(\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}})\rangle-\langle\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}},\underline{\bm{c}}\rangle.

To find the stationary points of
\hat{\phi}
, we compute the
partial derivatives of
\hat{\phi}
with respect to
\underline{\bm{\alpha}}
: this can
be done in vector compact form, see, e.g.,

[
31
]
.
We carry out this computation term by term, getting

\frac{\partial(\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k}+{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}))}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}=\frac{\partial(\underline{\bm{x}}_{{k}}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}})}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}=(\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k})^{{\text{T}}}={\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}^{{\text{T}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}={\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}

since
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is a symmetric. Additionally,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,\bigl(({\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}})^{\text{T}}{\bm{{A}}}\bigl(\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)\bigr)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,\bigl(\bigl({\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}\bigr)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}+\frac{\partial\bigl(\bigl({\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,\bigl(\underline{\bm{\alpha}}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}\bigr)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}+\frac{\partial\bigl(\underline{\bm{\alpha}}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}+2{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}.

Moreover, the derivative of

({\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}\underline{\bm{c}}
with respect to
\underline{\bm{\alpha}}
is given
by
{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\underline{\bm{c}}
, see
[
31
, Equations (69), (81)]
.
Letting
\underline{\bm{r}}_{k}=\underline{\bm{c}}-{\bm{{A}}}\underline{\bm{x}}_{k}=\text{vec}(\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1})
,
the final expression of the Jacobian of
\hat{\phi}
with respect to
\underline{\bm{\alpha}}
is thus

\frac{\partial\hat{\phi}(\underline{\bm{\alpha}})}{\partial\underline{\bm{\alpha}}}={\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{\alpha}}-{\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\underline{\bm{r}}_{k}.

The vectorized solution
\underline{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}
of (
8
) is a
stationary point of
\hat{\phi}
, and consequently a stationary point of
\phi
.
To ensure that
\underline{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}
is a minimizer, we show that the
Hessian of
\hat{\phi}
,

{\bm{{H}}}_{k}={\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}\times s_{k}}
, is positive definite.
Using the hypothesis on the operator
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
,
for any nonzero
\underline{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}}
it holds that

\underline{\bm{y}}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{H}}}_{k}\underline{\bm{y}}>0
, so that
{\bm{{H}}}_{k}
is positive definite.

Remark 2
.

The minimization problem in (
7
) can also be recast in terms of an orthogonality condition.
Indeed, solving (

\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}\perp\text{range}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k})

(11)

where

2.1
Conjugate gradients

We define the recurrence for the directions
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
as

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}=\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}

where
\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k+1}}
, and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}

are computed as in (
2
). The tensor
\bm{\beta}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}

is obtained by imposing that the new directions
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}
are
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
-orthogonal with respect to the previous ones, that is

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}\perp_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\text{range}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}),

or, equivalently,

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}=\bm{0}_{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}},

(12)

where
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}
is computed
as in (
9
). Using the vectorization, (
12
) is written as

\text{mat}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}})\text{vec}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1})={\bm{{P}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}{\bm{{P}}}_{k+1}\text{vec}(\bm{\gamma}_{k+1})=\underline{\bm{0}}_{s_{k}}

where
s_{k}=\prod{s}_{h,k}
,
{\bm{{A}}}
and
{\bm{{P}}}_{k}
are defined in Equation (
10
).

Inserting the expression for
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}
, (

) becomes

\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{{\text{T}}}_{k}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\beta})=\bm{0}_{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}
,
that is,

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}=\bm{0}_{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k}}

(13)

where
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k+1}
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}
are computed as in (
3
) and (
9
), respectively.
Equation (
13
) is a multilinear equation in the unknown
\bm{\beta}_{k}
, with
the same multilinear operator used to
compute
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
.
Once again,
\bm{\beta}_{k}
is obtained
by solving a multilinear equation of the same type as the original one, but
with smaller dimensions, by projecting the problem orthogonally onto
\text{range}(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k})
,
in a matrix sense.

Concerning the quality of the computed direction iterates,
the next proposition shows that the descent direction property (
6
)
is maintained.

Proposition 3
.

The tensor
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}
is a descent direction for
\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{s}}_{k+1}}
.

Proof.

To prove that
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}
is a descent direction,
we must show that

\langle\nabla{\Phi}(\mathdutchbcal{x}_{k+1}),\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}\rangle<0,

(14)

with
\Phi
defined in (
4
), and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}
computed as in (
2
). As in Proposition
1
, we consider the vectorized form
\hat{\Phi}
, setting
\underline{\bm{\gamma}}_{k+1}=\text{vec}(\bm{\gamma}_{k+1})
and
\underline{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=\text{vec}(\bm{\beta}_{k})
, both belonging to
\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}}

3
Implementation

The sound implementation of both algorithms requires the description of several technical details.
Every operations with tensors such as sums and products, increase the tensor rank. As previously mentioned,
a maximum tensor rank needs to be fixed, to avoid wild memory consumptions, leading to rank truncation
strategies. Moreover, the computation of the recurrence coefficients requires solving (small) tensor equations,
which all need be addressed. In the following we provide these details, together with the complete
algorithms.

3.1
The Tucker format and tensor operations

The Tucker decomposition
[
37
,
1
]

factorizes
\mathdutchbcal{x}
as the product of a smaller order-
d
tensor,
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bf r}
called
core tensor
, times
d
factor matrices,
{\bm{{U}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times r_{k}}
, that is

\mathdutchbcal{x}=\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}\times_{1}{\bm{{U}}}_{1}\times_{2}{\bm{{U}}}_{2}\cdots\times_{d}{\bm{{U}}}_{d},

with
{\bm{{U}}}_{j}
having orthonormal columns.
The index vector
\underline{\bm{r}}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{d})
is called
multilinear rank
. The Tucker factorization can be equivalently expressed as

\mathdutchbcal{x}={\bm{{U}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{U}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}},

see
[
1
, Proposition 3.22]
for further details. Several algorithms exist for computing the Tucker decomposition, among which the most widely used are High-Order SVD (HOSVD)
[
5
]
, the Sequentially Truncated HOSVD (ST-HOSVD)
[
38
]
, and the High-Order Orthogonal Iteration
[
25
,
21
,
6
]
.
Both tensor-oriented algorithms are designed with all tensors in Tucker format.
However, sums and products among tensors tend to be computationally expensive and memory consuming.
Thus, we employ two distinct operations to handle the sum of tensors and the
product between a tensor operator and a tensor in a cheaper way by truncation.

Sum of Tucker-tensors

Consider the sum of two tensors
\mathdutchbcal{x}
and
\mathdutchbcal{y}
,
both expressed in Tucker-format, that is

\mathdutchbcal{x}={\bm{{F}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{F}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathdutchbcal{y}={\bm{{G}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{G}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}},

where
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{r}}}
and
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{p}}}
. To
construct the Tucker factorization of the sum
\mathdutchbcal{z}=\mathdutchbcal{x}+\mathdutchbcal{y}
,
i.e.,
\mathdutchbcal{z}={\bm{{L}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{L}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}

we proceed as follows.
The tensor core
\mathdutchbcal{c}{z}
is an
\underline{\bm{r}}+\underline{\bm{p}}
block-diagonal tensor
computed by diagonally concatenating the core tensors of
\mathdutchbcal{x}
and
\mathdutchbcal{y}
,
that is
{\mathdutchbcal{c}}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}=\texttt{tenblkdiag}(\mathdutchbcal{c}{x},\mathdutchbcal{c}{y})
.
The factor matrices of
\mathdutchbcal{z}
are obtained by matrix superposition, that is

{{\bm{{L}}}}_{j}=[{\bm{{F}}}_{j},{\bm{{G}}}_{j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}\times(r_{j}+p_{j})}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
The storage costs of these quantities grows exponentially with
d
, hence
a size reduction becomes mandatory.
We then approximate
{\bm{{L}}}_{j}
with a truncated-SVD
by discarding the singular values which are less than a threshold, and the corresponding left and right singular
vectors. We thus store the retained left singular matrix

{\bm{{U}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}\times t_{j}}
,
and we multiply
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}
along each mode
by the corresponding singular value matrix and transposed right singular matrix.
These steps are repeated for every
j=1,\dots,d
.
The so-obtained core tensor
{\mathdutchbcal{c}}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}
is truncated by ST-HOSVD
at multilinear-rank equal to
maxrank
in all its components,
giving
{\mathdutchbcal{c}}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}={\bm{{M}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{M}}}_{d}\,{\mathdutchbcal{c}}^{\prime}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}
,
where
{\mathdutchbcal{c}}^{\prime}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{q}}}
, and

{\bm{{M}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{t_{j}\times q_{j}}
with
q_{j}\leq\texttt{maxrank}

for
j=1,\dots,d
. The final Tucker-approximation of
\mathdutchbcal{z}=\mathdutchbcal{x}+\mathdutchbcal{y}
is

{{\bm{{L}}}}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{{\bm{{L}}}}^{\prime}_{d}\,{\mathdutchbcal{c}^{\prime}}_{\mathdutchbcal{z}}

where
{{\bm{{L}}}}^{\prime}_{j}={{\bm{{U}}}}_{j}{\bm{{M}}}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
This rounding strategy is summarized in the appendix as
Algorithm
2
, and it will referred to as

Tucker-rounding-sum
; computational and memory costs are also discussed there.

Product of tensor operator times Tucker-tensor

A very similar rounding strategy has to be applied to compute the product between a tensor operator,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
, and a tensor in Tucker format,
\mathdutchbcal{x}={\bm{{F}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{F}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}
. Recalling that a tensor operator
\bm{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{m}}}
can be expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{h=1}^{\ell}{\bm{{A}}}_{1,h}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{A}}}_{d,h}

(15)

where
{\bm{{A}}}_{j,h}
is an
(m_{j}\times n_{j})
matrix,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is not minimal.
The product
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}
results in

\begin{split}\mathdutchbcal{y}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}&=\Bigl(\sum_{h=1}^{\ell}{\bm{{A}}}_{1,h}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{A}}}_{d,h}\Bigr){\bm{{F}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{F}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}.\end{split}

(16)

This tensor operator times Tucker-tensor product reduces to the sum of
\ell
tensors in Tucker format. Thus, an algorithm similar to the
Tucker-rounding-sum
can be designed to efficiently compute the
Tucker (approximate) factorization of
\mathdutchbcal{y}
in the form

{\bm{{G}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{G}}}_{d}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}

\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}
equal to
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}
, and we define the matrices
{\bm{{G}}}_{j,1}={\bm{{A}}}_{j,1}{\bm{{F}}}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
. Then, for
h=2

\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}
by diagonally concatenating it with
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}
, and we
form the matrix
{\bm{{G}}}_{j,h}=[{\bm{{G}}}_{j-1,h},{\bm{{A}}}_{j,h}{\bm{{F}}}_{j}]
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
As done with
{\bm{{L}}}_{j}
in the previous paragraph, we approximate
{\bm{{G}}}_{j,h}
by a truncated-SVD
with thresholding.
We store the retained left singular matrix
{\bm{{U}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}\times t_{j}}
,
and we multiply
\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}
along mode

We repeat these steps for every
j=1,\dots,d

\bm{\mathcal{A}}

{\mathdutchbcal{c}}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}={\bm{{L}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{{L}}}_{d}\,{\mathdutchbcal{c}^{\prime}}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}

{\mathdutchbcal{c}^{\prime}}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{q}}}

with
q_{j}\leq\texttt{maxrank}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
The final Tucker-approximation of
\mathdutchbcal{y}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}\mathdutchbcal{x}

{\mathdutchbcal{y}}={{\bm{{G}}}}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{{\bm{{G}}}}^{\prime}_{d}\,{\mathdutchbcal{c}^{\prime}}_{\mathdutchbcal{y}}

In the following we describe preconditioning strategies aimed at
accelerating the proposed algorithms.
Preconditioning is routinely used in vector
gradient descent methods such as CG, and it usually consists in
the construction of a matrix or operator to be applied to the original problem.
In multiterm equations building an effective preconditioner is particularly complex, because
of the number of operators to take care of. The presence of tensor addends just exacerbates the problem.
Modern literature has yet to establish an effective and reliable preconditioner for
generic tensor operators, hence the topic is still exploratory.
For the sake of generality, we thus consider as preconditioner
\bm{\mathcal{M}}

a tensor operator from
\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\underline{\bm{n}}}
. As it is usually done in the

that is, to the tensor computed in Line 7 of Algorithm

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}={\bm{z}}_{k}

following the updating formula

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k+1}{\bm{\gamma}}_{k+1}={\bm{z}}_{k}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}.

4.1
Matrix preconditioning

To simplify the exposition, in this section we will assume to work with order-
3
tensors. Whenever the problem tensor stems from the discretization of a multidimensional elliptic PDE,
a possibly successful preconditioner investigated in the literature
consists of explicitly approximating the inverse of the Laplace operator
[
14
]
.
More precisely, let
\bm{\Delta}_{1}=(n+1)^{2}{\rm tridiag}(-1,\underline{2},-1)
be the matrix discretization of the 1-dimensional Laplace operator on a limited closed interval over a grid of
n
.
In
[
14
]
,
the authors show that an approximate inverse of the discrete
3
-dimensional
(tensor) Laplace operator can be written as

\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{h=-q}^{q}c_{h}\,\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1}),

(18)

where
c_{h}=\eta t_{h}
,
t_{h}=\exp(h\eta)
,
\eta=\pi/\sqrt{q}
. We consider two possible strategies to apply the operator
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
as preconditioner.

Eigenvalue factorization

The first strategy explicitly forms the exponential matrices
appearing in Equation (
18
) in a generic manner.
Recalling that
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
is a symmetric, we let
\bm{\Delta}_{1}={\bm{{V}}}\bm{\Lambda}{\bm{{V}}}^{\top}

be its spectral decomposition, so that

\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})={\bm{{V}}}\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Lambda}){\bm{{V}}}^{\top}
.
The operator
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
can
be applied to a given tensor, by a sequence of tensor-tensor operations.
As the exponential matrices are equal in all modes, we have to form only
2q+1
exponential matrices, which
is costly also for moderate sizes of
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
. Hence,
q
should be kept low. In our experiments, we
used
q=1
.
The action of the operator over a tensor in Tucker format can be computed using the

tucker-rounding-product
routine, cf. Algorithm
3
.
We observe that this procedure does not exploit the structure of
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
, except for its symmetry,
thefore it could be applied for other symmetric matrices.
In the numerical experiments section below we will refer to this preconditioning strategy as

P
–
Eig
.

Fast Fourier Transforms

The second strategy corresponds to an operator-aware version
of the previous strategy. Indeed, the full eigendecomposition of
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
is analytically
available, and the exponential enjoys particularly strong structural properties when applied to Kronecker sums.
Hence, the whole procedure significantly simplifies. Let
{\bm{{V}}}\bm{\Lambda}{\bm{{V}}}^{-1}
be the eigenvalue decomposition of
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
. Then, setting
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}={\bm{{V}}}_{h}\otimes{\bm{{V}}}_{h}\otimes{\bm{{V}}}_{h}
,
it holds that (
[
7
,
16
]
)

\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\bigl(\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Lambda})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Lambda})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Lambda})\bigr)\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}^{-1}

from which, using

\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda}:=\bm{\Lambda}\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\bm{\Lambda}\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\bm{\Lambda}
,

\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})\otimes\exp(t_{h}\bm{\Delta}_{1})=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\exp\bigl(t_{h}(\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda})\bigr)\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}^{-1}.

Thanks to this construction, the operator defined in (
18
) can be expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{h=-q}^{q}c_{h}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\exp\bigl(t_{h}(\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda})\bigr)\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}^{-1}

Let
\mathdutchbcal{x}={\bm{{U}}}_{1}\otimes{\bm{{U}}}_{2}\otimes{\bm{{U}}}_{3}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}
, then by substitution,

\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}^{-1}\mathdutchbcal{x}={\bm{{V_{h}^{-1}}}}{\bm{{U}}}_{1}\otimes{\bm{{V_{h}^{-1}}}}{\bm{{U}}}_{2}\otimes{\bm{{V_{h}^{-1}}}}{\bm{{U}}}_{3}\,\mathdutchbcal{c}_{\mathdutchbcal{x}}
.
Recalling that the
{\bm{{V}}}_{h}
s are the eigenvectors of the 1-D Laplacian,
the matrices
\widehat{{\bm{{U}}}}_{j}={\bm{{V}}}_{h}^{-1}{\bm{{U}}}_{j}
correspond to the image
of
{\bm{{U}}}_{j}
by the Discrete Sine Transform of Type I (DST-I).
Similarly, the product between
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{h}
and a tensor in Tucker format can be computed using
the inverse of DST-I (iDST-I).
We refer to the appendix for more details on our implementation in the tensor setting.
We expect this preconditioner to be very fast in terms of application, and very effective in
reducing the number of iterations whenever the problem operator is close to the d-D Laplacian.

In our numerical experiments we shall use the term
P
–
FFT
to denote this implementation
of the preconditioner.

4.2
Inner-Outer preconditioning

A classical and easy way to construct a preconditioning operator consists of
approximating the action of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}
to a tensor by calling a few iterations,
usually a fixed number of them, of some iterative procedure. This strategy takes
the name of inner-outer procedure: at each iteration of an outer iterative methods,
another - or the same - iterative method is called for a few iterations.
We refer to
[
32
,
10
]
for examples in the nonsymmetric and symmetric
matrix cases, respectively, and to the
bibliography of
[
34
]
for a recollection of more references.
With this strategy, instead of applying the preconditioner,
we call a few iterations of either of the two new methods to solve the multilinear system

\bm{\mathcal{A}}{\bm{z}}_{k}=\mathdutchbcal{r}_{k}
.
Stopping the inner solver after a limited number of steps is key to
ensure that the overall procedure remains computationally competitive.
This strategy is useful for those applications where an explicit preconditioner is
unavailable or too expensive to construct.

In the experiments in Section
5
,
we choose
Tk
–
s
s
–
sd
as inner solver because is cheaper than
Tk
–
s
s
–
cg
.
We set the stopping tolerance to
10^{-1}
and the maximum number of iterations to
4
.
This strategy is referred to as
P
–
InnOut
.

5
Numerical experiments

This section is devoted to the illustration of the performance
of the proposed class of methods.
The experiments are performed in MATLAB 2025b on a machine with an
Intel Core i7-12700H CPU @ 2.30GHz and 16 GB of RAM.

We consider
the
Alternating Minimal Energy
algorithm
(AMEn)
1
1

1

Code available at

https://mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/46312-oseledets-tt-toolbox

[
8
]
for comparison purposes.
In AMEn data and working quantities are stored in Tensor-Train (TT) format, a
popular and effective way of representing high order tensors, as
it requires
\mathcal{O}(dnr^{2})
memory allocations
[
29
]
.
For large
d
, this compares favourably with the Tucker representation, which
uses
\mathcal{O}(dnr+r^{d})
storage.
AMEn alternates between two main phases: i) a local optimization phase, in which one
block of the TT-vector (a TT-core) is updated at a time by solving a
small projected linear system; ii) an expansion phase, where information
from the global residual is used to enrich the local search space before
proceeding to the next TT-core. The phases round-robin through
all cores; such a cycle is called
sweep
.
We should mention that in our experiments we will consider the discretization of
three-dimensional PDEs, yielding a moderate (
d=3
) order tensor, making
the Tucker format more appealing.
Nonetheless, an implementation of our algorithms can be naturally carried out
in the TT-format in case problems with high order tensors occur, without any further
changes in the algorithms.

All experiments below deal with algebraic problems stemming from the discretization of
separable elliptic operators. More precisely,
consider the discretization by finite differences
of three-dimensional
diffusion problems with separable coefficients, defined in a box with
zero boundary conditions. As usual, the one-dimensional Laplace operator is given
by

{\bm{{\Delta}}}_{1}:=\frac{1}{h^{2}}{\rm tridiag}(-1,\underline{2},-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)},

where
h
is the grid step size and
n+1

is the number of stencil points. The diffusion 1D operator with non-constant
coefficients can be discretized as

(a(x)u_{x})_{x}\approx{\bm{R}}{\bm{D}}{\bm{R}}^{T},\qquad{\bm{R}}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&&&\\
&1&-1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&-1\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+2)},

and
{\bm{D}}={\rm diag}(a_{\frac{1}{2}},a_{\frac{3}{2}},\ldots,a_{n+\frac{3}{2}})
, with

a_{j}=a(x_{j})
.
If the term does not involve a derivative, such as in
a(x)u(x,y,z)
, then the
matrix coefficient is given as

{\bm{D}}_{x}={\rm diag}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n+1})
.

d\cdot\texttt{maxrank}

6
Conclusions

We have presented a generalization to tensor equations of the recently introduced
subspace conjugate gradient
method, with symmetric and positive definite operators among tensor
spaces.
We have also extended the initial idea so as to include a new tensor-based version of the
classical steepest descent procedures, which
naturally adapts to this setting, while somewhat lowering the computational.
To enhance the algorithm performance, we
have introduced a mixed-precision strategy and we
have considered different preconditioning strategies. Our numerical experiments
illustrate that the new class of methods is computationally competitive with
respect to the well established AMEn.
