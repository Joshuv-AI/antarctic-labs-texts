Serum amyloid A kinetics predicts outcomes to PD-1 inhibitors in patients with recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Zhiting Sun, Dafeng Lin, Yingjun Liu, Suchen Li, Jiamin chen, Shiqing Nie, Yan Zhang, Shenwen Deng, Haoxiang Long, Li Yuan, Linquan Tang, Wenhui Chen, Jianfu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerum amyloid A (SAA) is an acute-phase responsive protein, and its kinetic changes may be related to the immune therapy response. Therefore, we assume that it can predict the outcomes of recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma (RM-NPC) patients receiving immunotherapy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study to analyze the clinical data of 186 patients with RM-NPC who received PD-1 inhibitor therapy. The patients were divided into three groups: the SAA flare-responder group (SAA increased by at least two times compared to baseline and then decreased below baseline), the SAA responder group (SAA decreased by ≥ 30% within three months without a prior flare), and the remaining patients were SAA non-responders. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). Secondary endpoints included the objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and overall survival (OS).ResultsThe median PFS (mPFS) of the entire cohort was 3.52 months, and the median OS (mOS) was 17.02 months. The ORR was 24.73% and DCR was 58.60%. Fourteen (7.53%) patients were classified as SAA flare-responders, 95 (51.08%) patients were defined as SAA responders, and 77 (41.40%) as SAA non-responders. The mPFS of the three SAA kinetic groups was 7.47, 3.97, and 1.87 months (p = 0.0008) and the mOS was 31.13, 26.93, and 15.87 months (p = 0.0283). The ORR of the three groups was 35.71%, 33.68%, and 11.69%, respectively (p = 0.0024). The DCR of the three groups was 85.71%, 71.58%, and 37.66%, respectively (p < 0.0001). It is worth noting that SAA kinetics have no predictive value for RM-NPC patients with low EBV DNA levels.ConclusionSAA kinetics can predict the prognosis of RM-NPC patients treated with PD-1 inhibitors, and can reliably differentiate RM-NPC patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, PD-1 inhibitors, Serum amyloid A, EBV DNA, Potential predictor

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor originating from the nasopharyngeal mucosa that has a distinct etiology and geographical distribution [1, 2]. Local recurrence and distant metastasis are the main causes of treatment failure in nasopharyngeal patients. Currently, the prognosis of patients with recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma (RM-NPC) remains unsatisfactory. With the advent of immunotherapy, RM-NPC patients have received new treatment options. PD-1 inhibitors combined with gemcitabine and cisplatin are currently used as first-line treatments for RM-NPC, however the median progression-free survival (mPFS) was only 9.2–21.4 months [3–5]. PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy has become a second-line or higher therapy for RM-NPC; however, its objective response rate (ORR) is limited, ranging from 17.7% to 34.0% [6–8]. Therefore, it is necessary to find effective predictors of outcomes in RM-NPC patients receiving PD-1 inhibitor therapy.

The main biomarkers for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy in RM-NPC include programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) levels [4]. However, based on the results of POLARIS-02, there was no significant correlation between PD-L1 expression and TMB, and the prognosis of RM-NPC patients receiving PD-1 treatment [6]. The detection of PD-L1 and TMB requires tumor tissues, which poses certain challenges for clinical applications. Our previous research indicated that high concentrations of EBV DNA are associated with NPC prognosis [9, 10], and EBV DNA titers and their kinetics have been demonstrated as biomarkers for predicting long-term outcomes and monitoring disease progression in single-immunotherapy NPC patients [11]. However, the clinical application of EBV DNA has been limited because of its lack of stability and standardization during testing [12]. Given the shortcomings of the above biomarkers, it is urgent to explore a stable and easy-to-implement biomarker.

Our previous studies have confirmed that serum amyloid A (SAA) is associated with the prognosis of patients with non-metastatic NPC [13]. SAA is an acute-phase reactive protein synthesized by hepatocytes and secreted into serum [14–16]. Previous studies have reported that SAA can serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for various cancers, and a few articles have also reported that SAA can be used to predict the outcomes of patients with tumors receiving immunotherapy [17–21]. Recently, studies have shown that kinetic changes in inflammatory markers may be more predictive of tumor response to immunotherapy [22, 23]. Anti-tumor immune activation is a dynamic process; therefore, inflammatory biomarkers during treatment have great potential to capture tumor immune interactions and can serve as accurate predictive tools [22]. However, there is currently no research on the correlation between SAA kinetic changes and the prognosis of patients with RM-NPC receiving PD-1 inhibitors.

Therefore, in this study, we investigated the correlation between the SAA kinetic model and the prognosis of RM-NPC patients treated with PD-1 inhibitor therapy.

Materials and methods

Patients and treatment procedures

We summarized the clinical data of patients with RM-NPC who received continuous PD-1 inhibitor treatment at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) between April 2016 and March 2022. Patients received anti-PD-1 monotherapy once every two weeks, and at doses of toripalimab (3 mg/kg), camrelizumab (200 mg), pembrolizumab (3 mg/kg), nivolumab (3 mg/kg or 240 mg), and spartalizumab (400 mg). According to RECIST v1.1, the tumor response was assessed every 8 weeks for 6 months and every 12 weeks thereafter. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Committee of the Sun Yat-Sen University Cancer Center (B2022–497). As this was a retrospective study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study endpoint

The primary endpoint of the study was progression-free survival (PFS), and the secondary endpoints were ORR, disease control rate (DCR), and overall survival (OS). PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of anti-PD-1 monotherapy to the first occurrence of disease progression or death from any other cause. According to RECIST v1.1, a complete response (CR) was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions, whereas a partial response (PR) was defined as a reduction of at least 30% in the sum of the diameters of target lesions. The ORR was the proportion of patients who achieved a CR or PR. Progressive disease (PD) was defined as at least a 20% increase in the sum of diameters of target lesions. Stable disease (SD) was described as a condition where the shrinkage was insufficient to qualify as PR, and the increase was insufficient to qualify as PD. The DCR was defined as the proportion of patients exhibiting a best overall response of CR, PR, or SD. The OS was defined as the time between the first day of treatment and death from any cause.

Data collection

Clinical variables included age, sex, ECOG score, BMI, history of smoking and drinking, family history of NPC, number of metastatic sites, SAA, EBV DNA, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), treatment lines of anti-PD-1 monotherapy and type of anti-PD-1.

Blood samples were allowed to clot and centrifuged at 1,500 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. SAA was measured with an immuno-turbidometric assay using LABOSPECT 008 AS analyzers (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) [8]. Neutrophils and lymphocytes were measured using an XN9000 analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Plasma EBV DNA levels were measured using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as previously described [8, 24].

Definition of SAA kinetics

Patients were classified based on the kinetic classification reported by Klümper et al. [22]. The patients were divided into three groups: the SAA flare-responder group (at least two-fold increase in baseline SAA within 30 days after PD-1 inhibitor treatment, followed by a decrease in serum SAA level below baseline within 3 months), the SAA responder group (decline in baseline SAA by ≥ 30% within three months without a prior flare), and the remaining patients as SAA non-responders.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 19.0), GraphPad Prism (version 7.0), and R (version 4.5.2). The optimal cut-off points for baseline SAA, NLR, and EBV DNA were determined using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on 12‑month PFS, with optimal cut-offs identified by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). The 12-month time point was selected because PFS curves in multiple phase 3 trials for RM-NPC did not separate until 6 months after treatment initiation, rendering 12-month PFS a more robust indicator that captures both early treatment response and long-term prognosis [3, 4, 25]. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare relationships between categorical variables. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of PFS and OS; variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into multivariable Cox regression models using backward stepwise selection (likelihood ratio). Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival function and log-rank test. The combined predictive value of EBV DNA and SAA kinetics for PFS was assessed using ROC curve analysis at 6 and 12 months post‑treatment. Binary logistic regression models were constructed for each time point to predict PFS events, with EBV DNA status, SAA kinetics, and their combination as predictors. The predictive performance of the models was quantified using the AUC. Statistical comparisons of AUC values among the models were performed using DeLong’s test, with Holm’s correction applied to account for multiple comparisons [26].

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 186 patients with RM-NPC who received PD-1 inhibitor treatment at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center were included in the study. Detailed patient information is presented in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 47 years (interquartile range [IQR], 39.75-54 years), and 160 (86.02%) were men. The median baseline SAA level was 28.7 mg/L (IQR, 8.2–119.13), the median EBV DNA level was 15,300 copies/mL (IQR, 2545–61750), and the median NLR was 3.78 (IQR, 2.566–5.801). Using time‑dependent ROC analysis with 12‑month PFS as the endpoint, the optimal cut-off points for baseline SAA, EBV DNA, and NLR were determined by maximizing the Youden index. The statistically derived cut-off points were 10.1 mg/L, 1490 copies/mL, and 4.56, respectively. To enhance clinical practicality, these values were set as 10.0 mg/L, 1500 copies/mL, and 4.6. The 10.0 mg/L cut-off point for SAA is the upper limit of our institution’s detection, making it an easily clinically applicable value [8], which is better than 10.1 mg/L. Our center typically uses cut-off points of 1500, 2000, and 4000 copies/mL for EBV DNA. Patients with levels between 1500 and 4000 copies/mL are seen as having a moderate to high risk of progression. Thus, setting the cut-off at 1500 copies/mL, rather than 1490, ensures better identification of patients with low progression risk and those who might benefit from the new treatment strategy [27]. Based on these clinically optimized cut‑off points, EBV DNA was divided into high and low groups, as well as the NLR. There were 145 (77.96%) patients in the high EBV DNA group and 67 (36.02%) in the high NLR group. The median PFS of the entire cohort was 3.52 months, and the median OS (mOS) was 17.02 months. The ORR was 24.73% and DCR was 58.60%. Patients were divided into three groups based on SAA kinetics: 14 (7.53%) patients were SAA flare-responders, 95 (51.08%) were classified as SAA responders, and 77 (41.40%) were SAA non-responders.

Survival analysis of different SAA kinetic groups

In addition, the mPFS of the SAA flare-responder group, SAA responder group, and SAA non-responder group were 7.47 (95%-CI: 4.08–10.85), 3.97 (95%-CI: 2.53–5.40), 1.87 months (95%-CI: 1.80–1.93), respectively, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0008) (Fig. 1b). The mOS of the three SAA kinetic groups was 31.13 (95%-CI: 0.41–61.86), 26.93 (95%-CI: 16.93–36.94), 15.87 months (95%-CI: 9.22–22.51), respectively (p = 0.0283) (Fig. 1c). The ORR of the three SAA kinetic groups was 35.71% (95%-CI: 7%-64.4%), 33.68% (95%-CI: 24%-43.4%), and 11.69% (95%-CI: 4.3%-19%), respectively, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0024) (Fig. 1d). A statistically significant difference was also observed in the DCR among the three groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1e).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis

Univariate analysis showed that No. of metastatic sites, baseline EBV DNA, NLR, ICI type and SAA kinetics were associated with PFS. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were then entered into multivariable Cox regression using backward stepwise selection (likelihood ratio). Multivariate analysis revealed that baseline EBV DNA, ICI type and SAA kinetics were significantly correlated with better PFS, as shown in Table 2. In the univariate and multivariate analyses, baseline EBV DNA, NLR and SAA kinetics were independent predictors of OS (Table 3). Of note, pembrolizumab was administered in only one patient, resulting in inadequate statistical power to assess its prognostic effect. In summary, our data indicate that SAA kinetics and baseline EBV DNA can predict the response of patients with RM-NPC to immunotherapy.

Survival analysis of different EBV DNA groups

Based on the cutoff point of baseline EBV DNA, the patients were divided into two groups. 41 (22.04%) cases were included in the low EBV DNA level group and 145 (77.96%) cases were included in the high EBV DNA level group. The mPFS of the low EBV DNA group was longer than the high EBV DNA group (7.47 months [95%CI = 5.08–9.85] vs. 2.43 months [95%CI = 1.36–3.51], p = 0.0014) (Fig. 2a). The mOS of the low EBV DNA group was not reached, and the mOS of the high EBV DNA group was 18.53 months (95%-CI: 13.25–23.82), and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.002) (Fig. 2b). In addition, the ORR of the low EBV DNA group and high EBV DNA group was 46.34% (95%-CI: 30.4%-62.3%) and 18.62% (95%-CI: 12.2%-25.0%), respectively (p = 0.0003) (Fig. 2c), and a significant difference was also observed in DCR between the two groups (p = 0.0013) (Fig. 2d).

Survival analysis of SAA kinetics in different EBV DNA groups

We further analyzed the impact of SAA kinetics on the prognosis of patients with RM-NPC by combining baseline EBV DNA. We found that in the high EBV DNA group, the mPFS of the SAA flare-responder group, SAA responder group, SAA non-responder group was 7.47 months (95%-CI: 4.19–10.75), 3.77 months (95%-CI: 3.31–4.23), and 1.83 months (95%-CI: 1.78–1.89), respectively, and the mOS was 45.9 months (95%-CI: 12.60–79.20), 21.03 months (95%-CI: 11.28–30.79) and 12.07 months (95%-CI: 6.48–17.66), respectively (Fig. 3a, b). In addition, in the high baseline EBV DNA groups, the ORR of SAA kinetics in the three groups was 33.33% (95%-CI: 2%-64.6%), 23.94% (95%-CI: 13.8%-34.1%), and 9.68% (95%-CI: 2.1%-17.2%), respectively (p = 0.0425) (Fig. 3c), with a significant difference also observed for DCR (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3d). However, the results showed no statistically significant difference in the PFS, OS, ORR, and DCR of SAA kinetics among the three groups in the low EBV DNA group (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To further evaluate the complementary predictive value of EBV DNA and SAA kinetics, ROC curve analysis for PFS was performed at 6 and 12 months post‑treatment. As shown in Fig. 4A-B, the combined model consistently achieved the highest predictive accuracy at both time points. At 6 months, the AUC for the combined model was 0.757 (95% CI: 0.689–0.825), which was significantly superior to that of EBV DNA alone (AUC = 0.641, 95% CI: 0.572–0.710; Holm-adjusted p = 5.34 × 10⁻⁵) and SAA kinetics alone (AUC = 0.663; 95% CI: 0.590–0.735; Holm-adjusted p = 0.007). At 12 months, the combined model maintained a higher AUC (0.699; 95% CI: 0.606–0.792) compared with EBV DNA alone (AUC = 0.627; 95% CI: 0.532–0.722; Holm-adjusted p = 0.042) and SAA kinetics alone (AUC = 0.590; 95% CI: 0.494–0.686; Holm-adjusted p = 0.051). Although the difference between the combined model and the SAA kinetics model at 12 months did not reach statistical significance, the p value was borderline (0.051), suggesting a strong trend toward improved prediction. These results indicate that the integration of EBV DNA and SAA kinetics provides complementary information, as EBV DNA reflects tumor burden while SAA kinetics captures systemic anti-tumor immune dynamics, thereby enhancing prognostic accuracy, particularly for short-term progression events. Therefore, combining these two biomarkers offers a more comprehensive assessment of clinical outcomes in RM-NPC patients treated with PD-1 inhibitors.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective study to confirm that kinetic changes in SAA can predict the prognosis of patients with RM-NPC receiving PD-1 monotherapy. It is worth noting that it can complement EBV DNA, and the SAA kinetic model can robustly predict the response of RM-NPC patients to PD-1 inhibitor treatment in the case of high EBV DNA. We also confirmed that SAA kinetics and EBV DNA were independent predictors of prognosis in patients with RM-NPC receiving PD-1 inhibitor treatment.

SAA is an acute-phase response protein synthesized by the liver and its concentration can increase significantly in disease states such as inflammation, infection, and tumors [28]. It has been studied as a prognostic biomarker for various malignant tumors [29–32]. SAA has been proven to promote immune cell infiltration, induce macrophage M2 polarization, and promote neutrophil phagocytosis, thereby exerting immunomodulatory effects in tumors [33, 34]. Therefore, SAA is currently receiving attention as a potential biomarker of immunotherapy efficacy. Previous studies have shown that serum SAA level is an important prognostic marker in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer treated with pembrolizumab [17].

Compared with baseline SAA levels, changes in SAA kinetics during treatment may be a better indicator for predicting tumor response to immunotherapy. Our findings confirmed that SAA kinetics could serve as a prognostic marker in patients with RM-NPC treated with PD-1 inhibitors. Clinical data suggest that immunotherapy may be more effective in treating “inflamed” tumors [35]. Ozawa et al. confirmed that the early increase in inflammatory cytokines caused by PD1-I can predict the efficacy of non-small cell lung cancer patients and believed that the increase in inflammatory cytokines in the early stage indicates early activation of the immune system [36]. Therefore, we believe that the elevation of early inflammatory markers promotes a response to immunotherapy. SAA kinetics might reflect the systemic dynamics of anti-tumor immune responses upon PD-1 inhibitor treatment. The significantly better prognosis in the SAA flare-responder group compared to the responder group further supports this hypothesis. We suggest that the initial SAA flare indicates strong and rapid systemic immune activation after successful PD-1 blockade. This is likely attributable to rapid tumor cell lysis and subsequent antigen release, which enhances T-cell responses and transforms the tumor microenvironment from cold to hot [37]. Conversely, patients in the responder cohort, who demonstrated a direct decline in SAA without an initial flare, may harbor tumors with lower immunogenicity or less vigorous immune priming. Although these tumors may still respond to therapy, the absence of an inflammatory surge indicates weaker immune engagement, potentially leading to less durable tumor control. As the effectiveness of immunotherapy increases, tumor control leads to a decrease in inflammation levels and inflammatory markers such as SAA.

Epstein-Barr virus infection is a major carcinogenic factor in NPC, and it plays an important role in its occurrence and development [38]. EBV can lurk in tumor cells, express a limited number of latent proteins, and encode microRNAs, leading to tumor immune escape [39–41]. Therefore, plasma EBV DNA is important for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy. EBV DNA has been shown to predict the prognosis of patients with RM-NPC receiving immunotherapy [42]. Our current and previous studies have confirmed this finding [8]. In this study, we found that the SAA kinetics had better predictive performance in the high EBV DNA group. This may be because NPC patients with high EBV DNA levels have more severe immune evasion [43–45]. If immunotherapy is effective, it may more effectively activate the anti-tumor immune response, thereby more significantly reversing tumor immune escape [46]. Therefore, in the high EBV DNA group, SAA kinetics could better distinguish the prognosis of patients receiving PD-1 monotherapy.

In this study, we confirmed that SAA kinetics and EBV DNA were independent predictors of prognosis in patients with RM-NPC treated with PD-1 monotherapy. We further analyzed the impact of SAA kinetics on the prognosis of patients with RM-NPC by combining baseline EBV DNA levels. The results showed that in the high EBV DNA group, SAA kinetics could better predictive performance. This method can non-invasively and cost-effectively distinguish nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients who are most likely to benefit from immunotherapy, providing patients with more precise and appropriate treatment. In contrast, the detection of EBV DNA lacks stability and standardization. Predictions of PD-L1 and TMB are expensive and not sufficiently accurate, and their detection requires tumor tissue. Overall, SAA kinetics have a higher clinical value than the existing mainstream biomarkers. Furthermore, we verified the synergistic predictive value of EBV DNA and SAA kinetics. ROC analysis showed that the combined model had better predictive performance for PFS than individual biomarkers at 6 and 12 months. Their combination thus enables a more comprehensive assessment of therapeutic response.

Our study had some limitations. First, because this study was a retrospective analysis, potential selection bias cannot be avoided. Second, our research data came from a single center and lack external validation cohorts to evaluate the results. Third, the SAA flare-responder subgroup comprised only 14 patients (7.53% of the cohort), and this small sample size may limit the statistical power of stratified analyses, especially when combined with EBV DNA. Therefore, further multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate the predictive value of SAA kinetics in RM-NPC patients treated with PD-1 inhibitors.
