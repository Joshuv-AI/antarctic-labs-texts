Elucidating response effects of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes for municipal wastewater using big data analysis and automated machine learning
Authors: Jia-Xin Lou, Jia-Shun Cao, Run-Ze Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApplications of anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) processes for treating municipal wastewater have promising benefits in reducing carbon emissions and have been heavily investigated recently. However, the practical operation of mainstream anammox processes still suffers from unclear external regulation strategies. Therefore, this study utilized the H2O automated machine learning (AutoML) algorithm and interpretable analysis to capture the internal relationships existing in the big dataset collected from anammox-based studies for treating municipal wastewater. The eXtreme Gradient Boosting and gradient boosting machine models automatically generated by the AutoML algorithm provided the most accurate prediction results (R2 = 0.814–0.993). Moreover, the optimal models showed good generalization ability (R2 = 0.725–0.945) for unseen data collected from this study. The appropriate ranges of operation conditions and influent characteristics response to the high removal efficiency of nitrogen pollutants and high nitrogen removal rate through the anammox reaction pathway were revealed by the one-dimensional, and two-dimensional partial dependence plots. This work would advance the understanding of how to improve the practical operation of mainstream anammox-based nitrogen removal processes for treating municipal wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57033-z.Keywords: Mainstream anammox, Automated machine learning, Interpretable analysis, Nitrogen removal efficiency, Meta-analysisSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Water resources

Introduction

The problem of water eutrophication caused by excessive nitrogen and phosphorus emissions has been a global concern for a long time1,2. The traditional activated sludge is the most commonly used biological process for removing nitrogen and phosphorus. However, eliminating nitrogen through the nitrification and denitrification process by the conventional activated sludge has been identified as an energy-intensive process3. Therefore, anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox), a new technology with low energy consumption, low sludge productivity, and a high nitrogen removal rate, has drawn increasing attention in recent years2,4–6.

Unlike traditional two-step nitrification and denitrification processes, the anammox process uses nitrite and ammonia as substrates and directly oxidizes them to N27. Anammox is one of the most promising processes for saving the operating costs of wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), reducing organic carbon requirements by 100%, aeration energy consumption by approximately 60%, and sludge production by approximately 90%8. However, the stable supply of nitrite to anammox remains an urgent problem. To provide nitrite steadily, several types of combined processes including simultaneous nitrification, anammox and denitrification9,10, partial nitrification coupling with anammox (PNA)11, partial denitrification coupling with anammox (PDA)12,13, and more complex processes combining PNA and PDA processes14,15 have been investigated extremely. Moreover, these anammox processes for mainstream application face many technical bottlenecks such as low nitrogen concentration of municipal wastewater, high organic matter concentration16, and low water temperature17 in winter. To solve these issues, numerous attempts have been made to mainstream anammox processes from multiple operational factors such as influent characteristics, inoculation mode, temperature, reactor type, and hydraulic retention time. A large amount of data has been generated during these experiments. However, the internal knowledge about anammox-based biological nitrogen removal processes remains to be explored from these big data.

Machine learning algorithms have drawn increasing attention due to their ability to identify the internal relationships in big datasets and create corresponding prediction models18,19. Explainable analysis and interpretable methods have been developed to further explain the trained machine learning models20–22. For instance, Xu et al. (2022b)23 analyzed response effects of anammox processes to different antibiotics through a universal machine learning model (R2 >0.9). Similarly, Yang et al. (2023)24 focused on the heavy metal stress on anammox processes based on machine learning models. Moreover, Liu et al. (2023a)25 explored the N2O emissions from anammox-based processes. They revealed the relationship between N2O emissions and operational factors as well as microbial communities through machine learning models and corresponding interpretable analysis. These studies have demonstrated that machine learning models have performed well in big data analysis for anammox-based processes23–25.

However, the response effects of nitrogen treatment performance in the different types of anammox-based processes to multiple operational factors are still unclear. Moreover, previous studies for big data analysis of anammox-based processes established machine learning models manually23–25, which suffers from several drawbacks. On one hand, the procedure of training and tuning these machine learning models had a significant time cost22. On the other hand, the accuracy of these trained models heavily depended on the experience of model designers, which had poor reproducibility and robustness. To overcome these drawbacks, automated machine learning (AutoML) algorithms have been proposed and drawn increasing attention22,26,27.

Therefore, in this study, the AutoML algorithm was applied to predict nitrogen treatment performance and interpret the response effects of anammox-based processes to multiple operational factors. Data from relevant anammox-based studies were first analyzed to select the potential input and output variables, which formed the integrated dataset for running the H2O AutoML algorithm. Then, the candidate models generated by the H2O AutoML algorithm for 7 output variables were compared. Moreover, lab-scale anammox-based experiments were carried out to test the prediction performance of candidate models. Based on the optimal models, interpretable analysis was conducted to clarify the response effects of output variables to the important input variables. These results highlight previously overlooked yet crucial relationships impacting the anammox-based treatment performance, providing a significant step towards the improvement of mainstream anammox-based processes.

Materials and methods

Data sources and analysis methods

To reveal internal relationships among factors in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes, this study first conducted a comprehensive literature search on the Web of Science using keywords such as “Anaerobic ammonia oxidation”, “mainstream anammox”, “municipal wastewater”, “partial nitrification”, and “partial denitrification”. The content of this literature was examined and 28 studies were finally selected because they had comprehensive and consistent variables related to the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes (Table S9). Liu et al. (2020)28 conducted a meta-analysis for anammox-based processes with data collected from 62 studies. However, they did not consider the operational parameters such as hydraulic retention time (HRT) and nitrogen loading rate (NLR) which were utilized as input variables in our study. In total, 2940 samples from different types of anammox-based processes in the selected study were collected by the Origin software, including 1927 PDA samples, 282 PNA samples, and 731 PDA combined with PNA samples.

The key variables involved in these anammox-based processes were organized, including operation condition (sequencing batch OR continuous flow), sludge morphology (floc OR aggregates), influent type (synthetic wastewater OR municipal wastewater), enrichment strategy (self-enrichment OR inoculation), anammox-based process type (PNA OR PDA OR PNA combined with PDA), operation time (d), HRT (h), carbon to nitrogen ratio (C/N), influent chemical oxygen demand (COD, mg/L), influent NH4+-N (mg/L), influent NO3−-N (mg/L), influent NO2−-N (mg/L), influent total inorganic nitrogen (TIN, mg/L), effluent NH4+-N (mg/L), effluent NO3−-N (mg/L), effluent NO2−-N (mg/L), effluent TIN (mg/L), and genera of dominant anammox bacteria (Table S1). Furthermore, several mathematic indices including NLR (kg/m3/d), NH4+-N removal efficiency (%), TIN removal efficiency (%), nitrification reaction rate by the ammonia oxidation bacteria (NiRR), nitrogen removal rate through the anammox reaction pathway (NARR), nitrogen removal rate (NRR) were calculated according to the equations in the Supporting information (Text S1)25. To preliminary analyze the relationships among these variables, box plots, exploratory data analysis (EDA), and Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (PPMC) were utilized according to our previous study22.

Model development and optimization

In total, 24 variables were collected from relevant literature to be selected as the input and output variables of regression machine learning models (Fig. 1). Among them, the operation condition, sludge morphology, influent type, enrichment strategy, anammox-based process type and genera of dominant anammox bacteria were represented as categorical objects in the dataset (Table S1). The optional 15 input variables included operation condition, sludge morphology, influent type, enrichment strategy, process type, dominant anammox bacteria, operation time, HRT, C/N, influent COD, influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, influent NO2−-N, influent TIN, and NLR. The 7 output variables including effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO3−-N, effluent NO2−-N, effluent TIN, NH4+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR were predicted separately to identify the relationships between input variables and the specific output variable. The selection of input variables was according to PPMC results.

Flowchart of the automated machine learning approach to analyze the big data collected from anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.

To reduce prediction errors and avoid non-convergence of data, 2940 samples were randomly selected and divided into the training dataset (70%), validation dataset (15%), and testing dataset (15%) (Fig. 1). As a result, the training sample-size to the feature-size ratio (SFR) reached 138 (2070 training samples divided by a total of 15 input variables), which presented higher confidence and good learning potential (SFR ≥ 100)19,29. Standardization (mean and standard deviation) for numeric columns was embedded in the H2O AutoML platform. To improve the model efficiency, correlation analysis was conducted to remove features that may cause potential multicollinearity, and retain features highly correlated with the output variable (Fig. S1)29.

We followed the procedures introduced in our previous work22 to establish models by the H2O AutoML platform (Text S2). Briefly, various machine learning algorithms including Distributed Random Forest (DRF), Extremely Randomized Trees (XRT), Generalized Linear Model with regularization, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), gradient boosting machines (GBM), and deep neural networks were automatedly optimized and compared to form the modeling leaderboard (Fig. 1). Moreover, the combination of fast random search and fivefold cross-validation was applied to accelerate the training speed and enhance the model performance22. The proper tuning of numerous kinds of machine learning models is notoriously difficult and time-consuming19. The H2O AutoML platform was selected because it has high efficiency in both automated model development and model explanation30. Due to the high efficiency of the H2O AutoML platform, the maximum number of models was set as 200 and the maximum run time was set as 900 s. AutoML run will stop as soon as it hits either of these limits. Five different seeds were utilized for different data splitting because the data splitting may result in a huge discrepancy in the final prediction performance19. The best training, validation, and test performance of these candidate models were summarized based on mean absolute error (MAE) and Coefficient of Determination (R2).

Experimental validation of candidate models

To confirm the high performance of candidate models, the candidate models including DRF, XRT, XGBoost, GBM, Generalized Linear Model, and deep neural networks trained by the same training dataset (2070 training samples) were all tested by the unseen experimental dataset (185 samples). A PDA up-flow anaerobic sludge bed (UASB) reactor with a 5.72 L working volume was established to investigate the PDA performance under different C/N. The PD flocculent sludge from a long-term operating glycerol-driven PD reactor and the mature anammox granular sludge from a UASB anammox reactor were mixed with the biomass ratio of 1:5 and inoculated into the PDA UASB reactor. Synthetic wastewater was prepared according to our previous study31. The reaction temperature was kept at 35 °C using the water bath, and the HRT was set at 4 h. The categorical variables of this experiment have been summarized in Table S9. The PDA UASB reactor operated for 185 d with 6 stages under different influent conditions (Table S10). The varying treatment performance of this experiment including effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO3−-N, effluent NO2−-N, effluent TIN, NH4+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR is illustrated in Fig. 5. These nitrogen concentrations of samples were measured according to the Standard Methods32.

Interpretable analysis

Interpretable methods including variable importance, one-dimensional (1D), and two-dimensional (2D) partial dependence plot (PDP) were utilized to analyze the optimal candidate models generated by the AutoML algorithm for each output variable. Variable importance is used to calculate the importance of sample features and quantitatively describe the contribution of features to classification or regression33. The PDP can show the functional relationship of one or two characteristic variables (1D PDP or 2D PDP) on the model’s prediction results34. In 2D PDP, the values of the two selected features are changed at the same time, while the values of the other features are fixed, and the prediction results are calculated to show the combined effect of the two features on the prediction results. The interpretable analysis was conducted on the Flow UI of H2O AutoML platform (https://h2o-release.s3.amazonaws.com/h2o/rel-3.46.0/6/docs-website/h2o-docs/flow.html).

Results and discussion

Statistical analysis of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes

To better understand the characteristics of data collected from the anammox-based processes, the box plots, EDA, and PPMC were utilized to analyze collected data (Fig. 2). There were 2940 samples collected from 28 studies, which investigated different types of anammox-based processes and applied different operation conditions. Box plots illustrated the distribution of data from different groups. Outliers in all groups indicated occasional significant deviations among different experiments. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the sequencing batch experiments (median = 78.18%) showed a higher TIN removal efficiency than the continuous flow experiments (median = 74.81%). Meanwhile, more stable NLR, NiRR, NARR, and NRR were observed in the sequencing batch experiments. Extremely high NLR (2.91–7.55 kg N/m3/d), NiRR (0.33–2.53 kg N/m3/d), NARR (0.02–3.92 kg N/m3/d) and NRR (2.63–6.55 kg N/m3/d) were found in Du et al. (2016)35 and Wang et al. (2024b)36 due to their high influent nitrogen concentration and short HRT in the UASB and expanded granular sludge bed reactor operating under continuous flow. This difference was also obtained between the floc experiments and aggregate experiments (Fig. 2(b)). The high NLR in continuous flow reactors operated by aggregates demonstrated that this combination had great potential to treat wastewater with high levels of nitrogen37,38.

For experiments using synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater, the effluent properties were similar (Fig. S1(a)). However, the experiments using municipal wastewater had more stable NLR, NiRR, NARR, and NRR (Fig. S1(b)), suggesting that influent properties of municipal were analogous across different studies39–42. Instead, the synthetic wastewater utilized in different studies had a larger range of influent COD, NH4+-N and NO3−-N concentrations35,36,43,44. To further analyze the differences in influent properties of synthetic and municipal wastewater, the distribution of influent properties was visualized by the EDA approach (Fig. 2(c) and (d)). Data points in larger regions were observed in the C/N versus influent NH4+-N, C/N versus influent TIN, influent COD versus influent NH4+-N, and influent COD versus influent TIN in Fig. 2(d), indicating that the influent variables of synthetic wastewater were more concentrated and had less flocculation than that of municipal wastewater. In reality, the concentrations of pollutants in real wastewater normally fluctuate greatly45,46. Therefore, the experiments using synthetic wastewater need to further take into account the real composition and fluctuation of the municipal wastewater.

Notably, the data from different anammox-based process types (i.e., PNA, PDA, and PNA combined with PDA) presented high differences (Fig. S2). The PNA and PNA combined with PDA experiments had higher TIN removal efficiency than the PDA experiments (Fig. S2(a)). In all three types of anammox-based processes, the effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO2−-N, and effluent TIN were positively correlated to the influent NH4+-N, influent TIN, and NLR, whereas the strongest positive correlation (0.55–0.95) was found in the PNA experiments (Fig. 2(e), (f) and (g)). Unlike PDA processes, the realization of PNA processes requires inhibition of nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB) activity and enhancement of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) activity47. The NOB and AOB activity have been found to be largely affected by the substrate concentrations (e.g., NO2−-N, NH4+-N, and COD)48,49. This may be the reason for the stronger correlation between influent and effluent variables in PNA experiments.

Moreover, the NiRR, NARR, and NRR had strong correlations with the variables involved in their calculations (Text S1). Meanwhile, the influent NH4+-N and influent TIN had very similar pairwise relationships with other variables because NH4+-N was extremely high in both synthetic and municipal wastewater. For real municipal wastewater, the NH4+-N normally accounts for more than 90% of influent TIN42,50.

The PPMC for all categorical and numerical variables in the entire dataset is illustrated in Fig. S3. Strong pairwise relationships were found between the influent type and dominant anammox bacteria, influent NO3−-N as well as influent TIN. This correlation further highlighted the differences between synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater. Except for influent NH4+-N and influent TIN, no other strong linear relationship was found between these potential input variables. Therefore, these variables could be utilized as the input variables for establishing machine learning models because they lacked potential multicollinearity19. The relationships among these variables involved in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes should be further explored via data-driven models and interpretable methods.

Predicting nitrogen removal performance in literature-based anammox processes

Based on the above pairwise correlation analysis (Fig. S3), all 15 variables including 6 categorical variables and 9 numerical variables were selected as the input variables for predicting the effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO3−-N, effluent NO2−-N, effluent TIN, NH4+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR, separately.

The best performance and optimized models for each output variable are summarized in Table 1 and the Supporting Information. GBM-like models (i.e., GBM and XGBoost) presented the advantages in predicting effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO3−-N, effluent NO2−-N, effluent TIN, NH4+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR. GBM has been recognized as an ensemble and powerful supervised machine learning algorithm22,51. GBM demonstrates remarkable performance advantages owing to its integration of regularization techniques that effectively prevent overfitting. It also features built-in handling of missing values, efficient tree-pruning algorithms, and parallel processing capabilities. Additionally, it employs advanced methods for addressing imbalanced data. Collectively, these features enhance the model’s efficiency, accuracy, and robustness when dealing with complex data scenarios, making it an excellent choice for a wide range of machine learning tasks52. For instance, GBM also presented high prediction performance in simulating total nitrogen in the influent of WWTPs53 and energy consumption of WWTPs54.

Testing candidate models by the experimental dataset

To confirm the high performance of candidate models generated by the H2O AutoML platform, the candidate models trained by the same training dataset (2070 training samples) were tested by the unseen experimental dataset (185 samples). The optimized models for predicting 7 output variables are summarized in Table 2. High prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.725–0.945) was achieved for effluent NH4+-N (Fig. S15), effluent NO3−-N (Fig. S16), effluent TIN (Fig. S18), NH4+-N removal efficiency (Fig. S19) and TIN removal efficiency (Fig. S20). Although the prediction accuracy of effluent NO2−-N and NARR was lower than other output variables, the predicted values of effluent NO2−-N (Fig. S17) and NARR (Fig. S21) still fulfilled the overall trends of real values in experiments (Fig. 5). The prediction MAE of effluent NO2−-N (1.045) by the GBM was still lower than the MAE of ensemble regression trees (3.428) created by Huang et al. (2023)55 based on the input variables including operating days, influent NH4+-N, influent NO2−-N, effluent pH, and effluent DO. The low prediction accuracy of the effluent NO2−-N and NARR may be attributed to the high flocculation of these values in the anammox-based experiments (Fig. 5(c) and (g)). Moreover, the NARR was calculated based on multiple variables (Text S1), which brought high uncertainty in it. On the other hand, some missing mechanistic variables in dataset, such as pH, DO and specific inhibitory organic substances, may be the reasons for the low accuracy of effluent NO2−-N and NARR.

Interpretable analysis of the optimized models for anammox-based nitrogen removal processes

To further understand the internal relationships between input variables and output variables, the variable importance of each input variable was analyzed for the optimized models (i.e., GBM and XGBoost mentioned above).

Effluent NH4+-N

For effluent NH4+-N and NH4+-N removal efficiency, different variable importance was observed. The variable importance for predicting effluent NH4+-N was found to follow the order of influent TIN > C/N > influent NH4+-N > operation time > dominant anammox bacteria > HRT > influent NO3−-N > sludge morphology > NLR > process type > influent COD > influent NO2−-N > enrichment strategy > operation condition > influent type (Fig. 3(a)). Instead, the influent NO3−-N had higher importance for predicting NH4+-N removal efficiency (Fig. 3(e)).

The variable importance of 15 input variables for 9 output variables. (a) effluent NH4+-N; (b) effluent NO3−-N; (c) effluent NO2−-N; (d) effluent TIN; (e) NH4+-N removal efficiency; (f) TIN removal efficiency; (g) NARR.

The variables (i.e., influent TIN, C/N, influent NH4+-N, and influent NO3−-N) related to the carbon and nitrogen composition of the influent have been identified as the most important variables for predicting NH4+-N variables, suggesting that the removal of NH4+-N in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes were strongly affected by the detailed carbon and nitrogen compositions in influent. As indispensable substrate for the anammox processes, the NH4+-N removal highly depends on the anammox activity, which is greatly influenced by the influent compositions56,57. Meanwhile, the influent type (synthetic wastewater OR municipal wastewater) was the least important feature for predicting NH4+-N removal. In most studies, the composition of municipal wastewater was also simplified as the basic pollutant concentrations (e.g., COD, NH4+-N and TIN). Therefore, the difference between synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater may be overlooked in the dataset, which may explain the least importance of influent type for predicting NH4+-N removal.

To further explore the numerical dependence of NH4+-N removal, the 1D PDP of important numerical input variables are illustrated (Fig. S4). The response of effluent NH4+-N showed a decrease trend with the operation time (Fig. S4(a)), which agreed with the common observation of the improving treatment performance at the later period of anammox-based experiments14,58,59. Meanwhile, the 1D PDP results revealed an appropriate range of C/N (2.72–6.32), in which lower effluent NH4+-N concentrations were achieved compared with C/N < 2.72 and C/N > 6.32 (Fig. S4(b)). Previous studies also confirmed that too low or too high C/N led to the deteriorated NH4+-N removal rates in the anammox processes60,61, which was agreed with the 1D PDP results (Fig. S4(b)). For instance, Miao et al. (2018)60 reported that the removal efficiency of NH4+-N in the anammox process increased progressively as the C/N ratio rose from 1.1 to 2.5.

The 1D PDP of influent NH4+-N and influent TIN has similar trend (Fig. S4(c) and (d)). A great increase of effluent NH4+-N was found when the influent NH4+-N and influent TIN reached 62.32 mg/L and 91.08 mg/L. These results suggested that municipal wastewater with nitrogen concentrations below these threshold values may be more suitable to be treated by the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes. Actually, the influent NH4+-N and influent TIN in real municipal wastewater are normally below these threshold values62,63, which ensured the high contribution of anammox process for municipal wastewater.

These input variables also have a mutual influence together on the effluent NH4+-N. Therefore, the 2D PDP is illustrated to examine the influence of a couple of important numerical input variables on the effluent NH4+-N as well as their mutual interaction (Fig. 4). Clear peaks and valleys were observed in the 2D PDP, implying that effluent NH4+-N had strong dependence on these numerical input variables (Fig. 4). The lowest response of effluent NH4+-N was achieved at the C/N of 2.75 and influent TIN from 18.85 mg/L to 87.76 mg/L (Fig. 4(a)). However, with the continuous increase of influent TIN, the response of effluent NH4+-N extremely increased from 4.23 mg/L to 17.0 mg/L. At the same C/N, the increase of influent TIN leads to the increase of influent COD, which will cause the proliferation of heterotrophic bacteria to occupy the living space of anammox bacteria and inhibit its growth56.

2D Partial dependence plot of two input variables interactions for predicting effluent NH4+-N. (a) influent TIN versus C/N; (b) influent TIN versus influent NH4+-N; (c) influent TIN versus operation time; (d) C/N versus influent NH4+-N; (e) C/N versus operation time; (f) operation time versus influent NH4+-N.

The 2D PDP of C/N versus influent NH4+-N showed a similar trend to the 2D PDP of C/N versus influent TIN (Fig. 4(d)). However, a wider range of C/N (2.36–9.62) and influent NH4+-N (13.83–213.84 mg/L) were observed with a low response of effluent NH4+-N (7.17–10.74 mg/L). This difference between influent NH4+-N and influent TIN may be due to the influent TIN composed of other types of nitrogen (e.g., NO3−-N, and NO2−-N). A notable increase response of effluent NH4+-N on the increase of influent NH4+-N and influent TIN was observed (Fig. 4(b)), which was agreed with the 1D PDP of influent NH4+-N (Fig. S4(c)) and influent TIN (Fig. S4(d)).

Moreover, the operation time had similar mutual influence with the influent TIN (Fig. 4(c)) and influent NH4+-N (Fig. 4(f)) on the effluent NH4+-N. A greatly decreasing response of effluent NH4+-N from 32.16 mg/L to 4.68 mg/L and from 20.46 mg/L to 4.12 mg/L was observed for operation time versus influent TIN and operation time versus influent NH4+-N, respectively. Notably, the 2D PDP of operation time versus C/N had the most complex peaks and valleys (Fig. 4(e)). The low response of effluent NH4+-N was found to be at the C/N of 2.75–6.48, which was consistent with the results of 1D PDP.

Besides, the operation time and genera of dominant anammox bacteria played second important roles in predicting NH4+-N removal, implying that the evolution of microbial groups and enrichment of anammox genera during the experiments were crucial for consuming NH4+-N in the anammox-based processes. Interestingly, the sludge morphology, process type, enrichment strategy, operation condition and influent type did not show strong importance in predicting NH4+-N removal (Fig. 3(a) and (e)). These results may be due to the similar nitrogen removal pathways in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes with different sludge morphology, process types, enrichment strategies and operation conditions. The influent type was the least important variable for predicting NH4+-N removal, suggesting that the difference of synthetic and municipal wastewater could be overlooked in the NH4+-N removal processes Fig. 5.

Effluent NO3−-N and NO2−-N

The operation time, influent NO3−-N, HRT, influent TIN, influent COD, and process type were the most important variables for predicting effluent NO3−-N (Fig. 3(b)), whereas the influent TIN, operation time, influent NO3−-N, influent NH4+-N, NLR and dominant anammox bacteria were the most important variables for predicting effluent NO2−-N (Fig. 3(c)).

The 1D PDP of effluent NO3−-N and effluent NO2−-N response to these important variables is shown in Fig. S5 and S7. The operation time showed different effects on the effluent NO3−-N and effluent NO2−-N. A clear peak was observed at the operation time of 30 d for the effluent NO2−-N (Fig. S7(a)), then the response of effluent NO2−-N to operation time was similar to that of effluent NO3−-N. These results demonstrated that the NO2−-N generation was more than the NO2−-N consumption in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes at the beginning of operation. In the mainstream anammox processes for municipal wastewater, NO2−-N is normally generated through PN or PD processes6,56. At the beginning of operation, the unmatured anammox bacteria cannot consume the NO2−-N generated by the PN or PD processes. For instance, Yang et al. (2024)39 reported that anammox only contributed 13.8% of nitrogen removal at the operation of 36–75 d, whereas its contribution increased to 67.1% at the operation of 216–258 d.

Interestingly, the response of effluent NO3−-N had a strong peak and valley at the HRT of 10.47–15.13 h (Fig. S5(b)). Yang et al. (2024)39 found that the HRT of 10 h did not improve the mainstream anammox processes, and further aggravated the issue of insufficient carbon sources. By increasing HRT to 17 h, the HRT of the anoxic zone increased from 5.67 h to 8.50 h. As a result, the average effluent NO3−-N decreased from 14.46 mg/L to 9.79 mg/L39. The prolongation of HRT affected the nitrite competition between denitrification and anammox processes, which had great effects on the effluent NO3−-N15. However, the effects of HRT should be considered combined with other factors such as influent COD, influent TIN, which decided the concentrations of substrates available to the denitrification and anammox processes.

The influent COD also strongly affected the effluent NO3−-N (Fig. S5(c)). On one hand, low COD concentration (< 189.87 mg/L) may limit the heterotrophic denitrification activities39, which causes the accumulation of NO3−-N and increase the response of effluent NO3−-N. On the other hand, high COD concentration (> 316.46 mg/L) will limit the anammox activities64, weakening the nitrogen removal efficiency in the anammox-based processes. Meanwhile, the response of effluent NO3−-N to influent NO3−-N and influent TIN had a different trend at the beginning of their increase (Fig. S5(d) and (e)). The low influent TIN (< 55.33 mg/L) led to a high response of effluent NO3−-N (6.85–7.82 mg/L). Then, the response of effluent NO3−-N gradually increased from 4.41 mg/L to 7.42 mg/L and from 4.45 mg/L to 5.81 mg/L according to the effects of influent NO3−-N and influent TIN, respectively. However, for the response of effluent NO2−-N, the influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN had similar increasing trends (Fig. S7(b), (c) and (d)). Notably, low NLR (< 0.95 kg/m3/d) resulted in a high response of effluent NO2−-N (Fig. S7(e)). With the increase of NLR, the response of effluent NO2−-N decreased from 4.57 mg/L to 2.51 mg/L and kept stable.

The 2D PDP further provided the response of effluent NO3−-N and NO2−-N to mutual effects from pairwise variables (Fig. S6 and S8). The highest response of effluent NO3−-N was observed at the beginning operation period with high influent NO3−-N (Fig. S6(a)). With the increase in operation time, the anammox-based system gradually enhanced the removal capacity of NO3−-N. The NO3−-N may be consumed by the nitrate reduction to ammonium pathway, where NO3−-N is reduced to NO2−-N and NH4+-N65. Then, the NO2−-N and NH4+-N are subsequently transformed to N2 via anammox process in these anammox-based systems.

A peak of effluent NO3−-N was found at the HRT of 10.47–10.98 h through all operation time (Fig. S6(b)), which was consistent with the 1D PDP of HRT for effluent NO3−-N (Fig. S5(b)). Moreover, low HRT (< 9.95 h) was more suitable for the removal of NO3−-N. For influent TIN and influent COD versus operation time, clear valleys of the response of effluent NO3−-N were observed with small ranges of influent TIN (59.38–87.76 mg/L) and influent COD (0–137.13 mg/L) (Fig. S6(c) and (d)). Compared with operation time, the mutual effects of HRT with influent NO3−-N, influent TIN, and influent COD were more complex (Fig. S6(e), (h), and (i)). Low HRT (< 9.95 h) was favorable to reduce the accumulation of NO3−-N with all ranges of influent NO3−-N (Fig. S6(e)), which was agreed with the 2D PDP of HRT versus operation time (Fig. S6(b)). The response of effluent NO3−-N was lower at the combined of high HRT (> 15.64 h) and high influent TIN, as well as the combined of low HRT (< 9.95 h) and low influent TIN, implying that high HRT may promote the accumulation of NO3−-N under low TIN loading (Fig. S6(h)). The influent COD versus HRT had the most complex effects on the response of effluent NO3−-N (Fig. S6(i)). Two response valleys were found in the range of influent COD (200.42–305.91 mg/L) and HRT (< 9.95 h, and 11.50–12.54 h).

Notably, the interactions of different influent nutrients (i.e., influent TIN versus influent NO3−-N, influent COD versus influent NO3−-N, and influent COD versus influent TIN) had a strong influence on the response of effluent NO3−-N (Fig. S6(f), (g), and (j)). Overall, the positive correlation between influent NO3−-N concentration and the response of effluent NO3−-N was confirmed (Fig. S6(f) and (g)), whereas the response of effluent NO3−-N was higher at low influent TIN with all range of influent COD (Fig. S6(j)). This difference may be caused by the high percentage of NH4+-N in the influent TIN. Combining influent TIN of 59.38–87.76 mg/L and influent COD of 200.42–305.91 mg/L reached the lowest response of effluent NO3−-N (1.39–1.64 mg/L).

The response of effluent NO2−-N had an identical trend in the 2D PDP of operation time versus influent NH4+-N (Fig. S8(a)), operation time versus influent NO3−-N (Fig. S8(b)), operation time versus influent TIN (Fig. S8(c)), and operation time versus NLR (Fig. S8(d)). Namely, the long-term operation time extremely reduced the response of effluent NO2−-N, suggesting that few NO2−-N remained in the effluent of successful anammox-based nitrogen removal processes. The 2D PDP of influent nitrogen and NLR also presented clear trends (Fig. S8(e)-(j)), implying that the effluent NO2−-N was strongly related to the nitrogen loading in the anammox systems. PD and PN processes were the main sources of NO2−-N in the anammox-based system, whereas the anammox process consumes most of NO2−-N56. The high influent nitrogen loadings may lead to the accumulation of NO2−-N in effluent due to the insufficient activity of the anammox process66.

Effluent TIN

For predicting effluent TIN and TIN removal efficiency, the influent TIN and operation time were the most important input variables. Besides, the input variables including influent NO3−-N, HRT, influent NH4+-N, and NLR played more important roles in predicting effluent TIN (Fig. 2(d)), whereas the COD-related input variables (i.e., influent COD and C/N), HRT and NLR were more important for predicting TIN removal efficiency (Fig. 2(f)).

In the 1D PDP of operation time, the decreasing response of effluent TIN (Fig. S9(a)) and the increasing response of TIN removal efficiency (Fig. S11(a)) indicated the maturity of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes. Moreover, the short HRT (< 6.84 h) and long HRT (> 24.45 h) presented better TIN removal performance (Fig. S9(b) and Fig. S11(e)). For influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN, identical response trends of the effluent TIN were achieved (Fig. S9(c), (d) and (e)). The response of the effluent TIN greatly increased at 181.18 mg/L, 50.07 mg/L, and 95.87 mg/L for influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN, respectively. However, the increasing NLR did not lead to the growing response of effluent TIN (Fig. S9(f)) because NLR was affected by the mutual interactions of influent TIN and HRT. In the 1D PDP of influent TIN (Fig. S11(b)), influent COD (Fig. S11(c)), C/N (Fig. S11(d)), and NLR (Fig. S11(f)), similar trends of the response of TIN removal efficiency were observed. The highest responses of TIN removal efficiency were achieved at 87.76 mg/L, 179.33 mg/L, 2.95, and 0.49 kg/m3/d for influent TIN, influent COD, C/N, and NLR, respectively.

For the response of effluent TIN, the 2D PDP of operation time versus HRT (Fig. S10(a)) and operation time versus NLR (Fig. S10(e)) had a decreasing trend with the decrease of operation time. Instead, increasing response of effluent TIN with the increase of influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN was identified in the 2D PDP of operation time versus influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN (Fig. S10(b), (c) and (d)). Moreover, special patterns in the 2D PDP of HRT versus influent NH4+-N, influent NO3−-N, and influent TIN were captured, where the low valleys of effluent TIN were achieved at the HRT of < 8.91 h, 11.50–12.54 h, 15.64–17.20 h, and > 24.45 h (Fig. S10(f), (g) and (h)). The 2D PDP of HRT versus NLR further confirmed that high HRT (> 24.45 h) and low HRT (< 2.18 h) led to the lowest effluent TIN at high NLR levels (Fig. S10(i)).

For TIN removal efficiency, the 2D PDP of operation time versus influent TIN (Fig. S12(a)), influent COD (Fig. S12(b)), C/N (Fig. S12(c)), HRT (Fig. S12(d)), and NLR (Fig. S12(e)) showed a clearly increasing response of TIN removal efficiency with the increase of operation time. Meanwhile, low influent TIN, low influent COD, low C/N, and low NLR led to the peaks of TIN removal efficiency. More complex patterns were found in the 2D PDP of influent TIN versus influent COD (Fig. S12(f)), HRT (Fig. S12(g)), and NLR (Fig. S12(h)). The highest response of TIN removal efficiency was observed at the influent TIN = 71.54–87.76 mg/L and influent COD = 200.42–221.52 mg/L (Fig. S12(f)). In the 2D PDP of influent TIN versus HRT, the highest response of TIN removal efficiency was found at the influent TIN = 71.54–87.76 mg/L and HRT = 0.63–6.84 h, as well as the influent TIN > 108.03 mg/L and HRT = 24.45–26 h (Fig. S12(g)). Similarly, the highest response of TIN removal efficiency in the 2D PDP of influent TIN versus NLR was identified at the influent TIN = 71.54–87.76 mg/L (Fig. S12(h)). In the 2D PDP of influent COD versus HRT, the highest TIN removal efficiency was also found at the HRT = 0.63–6.84 h and HRT = 24.45–26 h with influent COD = 168.78–200.42 mg/L (Fig. S12(i)). In the 2D PDP of influent COD versus NLR, the influent COD = 168.78–200.42 mg/L also provided the highest response of TIN removal efficiency (Fig. S12(j)). These optimized influent COD ranges fulfilled the normal COD concentrations in the municipal wastewater26,30, whereas higher TIN concentrations in influent than that in the municipal wastewater were required for the high efficiency of the anammox-based nitrogen processes67,68. These results also provided an ideal range for selecting the parameters (e.g., HRT and NLR) of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.

NARR

NARR, as an important calculation variable for anammox-based nitrogen removal rate, was highly affected by the input variables including HRT, NLR, influent TIN, influent NO2−-N, influent COD, and influent NH4+-N (Fig. 2(g)). The response of NARR had a decreasing trend in the 1D PDP of HRT (Fig. S13(a)) because the HRT was the denominator in the equation of NARR (Text S1). In contrast, for nitrogen loading variables (i.e., NLR, influent TIN, influent NO2−-N, influent NH4+-N), the response of NARR increased (Fig. S13(b), (c), (d), and (f)), suggesting that the contribution of anammox processes was enhanced with higher nitrogen loadings. Meanwhile, except for influent COD > 506.33 mg/L, no obvious variation was observed in the 1D PDP of the influent COD (Fig. S13 (e)). The 2D PDP of influent COD versus NLR (Fig. S14 (a)), influent TIN (Fig. S14 (b)), influent NO2−-N (Fig. S14 (c)), and influent NH4+-N (Fig. S14 (d)) further confirmed that a great increase of NARR response occurred at the influent COD > 506.33 mg/L. Moreover, an extreme increase in NARR response was found at NLR > 1.28 kg/m3/d, influent TIN > 176.89 mg/L, influent NO2−-N > 18.78 mg/L and influent NH4+-N > 152.61 mg/L. The combination of influent TIN > 176.94 mg/L and influent NO2−-N > 18.78 mg/L led to the highest response of NARR (Fig. S14(e)). For HRT versus influent nitrogen and carbon variables (Fig. S14(f), (g), (h), (i) and (j)), an increasing trend of NARR response was identified with the decreasing HRT (< 7.36 h). Furthermore, higher NARR was found at the high NLR (> 2.18 kg/m3/d) and high influent NH4+-N (> 148.53 kg/m3/d) (Fig. S14(f) and (j)). Overall, the high NARR was observed at the high nitrogen loading and low HRT, implying that the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes were capable of adapting to high processing pressure in modern wastewater treatment plants69.

Practical implications and limitations

Forecasting the stabilization and operational performance of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes is essential for engineering operations, especially for treating municipal wastewater by anammox-based processes, which were usually unstable in practice39,70. By integrating and comparing the big data collected from different anammox-based studies, this study revealed the difference in data characteristics between experiments using sequencing batch reactor and continuous flow reactor, using floc and aggregate, using synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater, as well as using different anammox-based process types. Results showed that the anammox-based continuous flow reactors operated by microbial aggregates had great potential to treat high levels of nitrogen pollutants. The big difference between the characteristics of synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater applied in current experiments extremely weakened the practical meaning of anammox-based experiments based on synthetic wastewater30. Meanwhile, the PNA experiments and PNA combined with PDA experiments showed higher TIN removal efficiency than the PDA experiments. Stronger positive correlations (0.55–0.95) between influent and effluent nitrogen concentrations were found in the PNA experiments, implying the higher sensitivity of PNA processes to the influent characteristics.

Importantly, this study provided a new insight: the fast implementation and high accuracy of the AutoML algorithm for these important output variables (i.e., effluent NH4+-N, effluent NO3−-N, effluent NO2−-N, effluent TIN, NH4+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR) makes it a useful tool to learn the information inside the anammox-based datasets, and predict the practical operation of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes. GBM and XGBoost models were identified as the most accurate machine learning models for predicting the effluent variables in the anammox-based processes (R2 = 0.814–0.993). The high accuracy of these models was not only achieved for data collected from literature but also confirmed by unseen data from our anammox-based experiment, which proved the excellent generalization ability in predicting the performance of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.

Moreover, the potential improvement strategies for anammox-based processes could be proposed based on the interpretable results of optimal models generated by the AutoML algorithm. The appropriate ranges of key characteristics such as C/N, influent NH4+-N, influent TIN, influent COD, HRT, and NLR were identified by the 1D and 2D PDP results. To reduce effluent NH4+-N, it is recommended to keep C/N at 2.72–6.32 and influent TIN at 18.85–87.76 mg/L. NO3−-N as the by-product of the anammox bacteria accounted for most of the effluent TIN in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes68. Therefore, the conditions for reducing effluent NO3−-N in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes deserve deep exploration. Specifically, this study found that the low effluent NO3−-N could be achieved at COD of 200.42–305.91 mg/L, HRT of < 9.95 h and 11.50–12.54 h, influent TIN of 59.38–87.76 mg/L, and NLR > 0.95 kg/m3/d. The effluent TIN and TIN removal efficiency had a similar but more special pattern compared with the effluent NO3−-N. The lower effluent TIN and higher TIN removal efficiency were found at the combination of lower influent TIN (71.54–87.76 mg/L) with lower HRT (0.63–6.84 h), and the combination of higher influent TIN (> 108.03 mg/L) with higher HRT (24.45–26 h). It means that a matching HRT and influent TIN concentration should be selected to improve the performance of the anammox-based processes. Meanwhile, a lower influent COD of 168.78–200.42 mg/L was responsible for the high TIN removal efficiency. NARR, as an important index for representing the anammox activity, showed a clear increasing trend for higher nitrogen loading and lower HRT. The high NARR was obtained at HRT < 7.36 h, NLR > 1.28 kg/m3/d, influent TIN > 176.89 mg/L, influent NO2−-N > 18.78 mg/L and influent NH4+-N > 152.61 mg/L. Although this high nitrogen loading was uncommon for municipal wastewater, an appropriate mix of municipal wastewater and industrial wastewater may be helpful to achieve high NARR in mainstream anammox-based processes71. Integrating these data characteristics inside the big dataset could largely deepen the understanding of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes, and effectively reduce the difficulty of managing and operating them.

There are also some limitations existing in this study. First, the prediction accuracy of optimal models generated by the AutoML algorithm suffered from a slight decrease in the prediction of effluent NO2−-N, effluent NO3−-N, and NARR. This decreasing generalization ability may be due to the different operating conditions and microbial compositions in experiments between the training dataset collected from the literature and the unseen dataset collected from our study. Importantly, it was found that effluent NO2−-N, effluent NO3−-N, and NARR, as output variables, were more sensitive to the different operating conditions and microbial compositions in the anammox-based processes. The generalization ability of candidate models generated by the AutoML algorithm may be improved by including more essential input variables related to these output variables in the training datasets55.

Secondly, various organics with complex chemical structures exist in municipal wastewater. Among them, some substituents (e.g., −CH3, −OH) in organics impact the substrate diffusion and membrane stability by changing the hydrophobicity. Moreover, some substituents (e.g., −Cl, −F) in organics contribute to the biotoxicity and will inhibit the anammox activity72. However, COD was set as an index for representing all these organics in most anammox-based experiments fed with municipal wastewater. The missing concentrations of these sensitive organics in the datasets will limit the understanding of machine learning models for the effects of different organics on the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.

On the other hand, although we included the dominant anammox genera in the dataset as a categorical variable, more variables related to microbes should be considered in future studies. Nowadays, the data size of microbes in these experiments is much smaller than the data size of influent and effluent variables. The appropriate ways to collect and utilize microbial data of anammox-based experiments should be further explored to overcome the insufficient data volume73. Employing machine learning algorithms to scrutinize these microbial datasets will not only facilitate a more profound comprehension of the microorganisms involved in anammox-based processes, but also offer valuable insights that enhance the nitrogen removal efficiency of practical anammox-based systems for treating municipal wastewater52. Future directions for developing multifactor optimization strategies for anammox treatment should increasingly explore the valuable information hidden in microbial information. Ultimately, the fusion of high-dimensional microbial community fingerprints with process variables will enable the development of biology-informed digital twins, shifting the paradigm of anammox optimization from empirical hydraulic control toward precision microbiome management.

Conclusions

This study explored the response effects of important effluent variables to operation conditions and influent characteristics involved in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes through the big data analysis and AutoML algorithm. Analysis of big data collected from the literature presented that the continuous flow reactors with anammox aggregates could address higher nitrogen loadings in municipal wastewater. High accuracy of the candidate models generated by the AutoML algorithm for these important output variables was achieved for data collected from both literature and unseen experiments. Moreover, the interpretable analysis of the optimal models clarified the 1D and 2D response effects between input and output variables. The appropriate ranges of key characteristics such as C/N, influent NH4+-N, influent TIN, influent COD, HRT, and NLR were identified. These findings would deepen the understanding and support the practical operation of anammox-based processes for treating municipal wastewater.
