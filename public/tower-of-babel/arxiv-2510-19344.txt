Module structure of Weyl algebras
Gwyn Bellamy

Abstract
. The seminal paper “J.T. Stafford, Module structure of Weyl algebras , J. London Math. Soc. (2) 18 (1978), no. 3, 429–442” was a major step forward in our understanding of Weyl algebras. Beginning with Serre’s Theorem on free summands of projective modules and Bass’ Stable Range Theorem in commutative algebra, we attempt to trace the origins of this work and explain how it led to Stafford’s construction of non-holonomic simple modules over Weyl algebras. We also describe Bernstein-Lunts’ geometric construction of infinite families of non-holonomic simple modules. We recall more recent developments related to Weyl algebras, especially that of parametrizing right ideals in the first Weyl algebra and its relation to Calogero-Moser spaces. Finally, we revisit Stafford’s results in the context of quantized symplectic singularities, where they lead naturally to open problems on the behaviour of simple modules.

Primary: 13N10, 16S32, 14F10, 01-02.

1.
Introduction

Weyl algebras were introduced in the early 20th century, in the context of quantum mechanics and differential operators. As the name suggests, the defining relations of these algebras were written down by Hermann Weyl
[
48
, Equation (36)]
in his foundational article
Gruppentheorie und Quantenmechanik
in 1927, later developed into the book of the same name. The
n
th Weyl algebra
A_{n}
is the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
and satisfying the defining relations

(1.1)

[x_{i},x_{j}]=[y_{i},y_{j}]=0,\quad[y_{i},x_{j}]=\delta_{i,j},\quad\textrm{for all $1\leq i,j\leq n$}.

Here
[A,B]:=AB-BA
is the commutator bracket.

In the context of quantum mechanics, letting
x_{i}
denote the position operator and
p_{i}
the momentum operator, the canonical commutation relation

[x_{i},p_{j}]=\sqrt{-1}\hbar\delta_{i,j}

underlying Heisenberg’s uncertainty principal becomes the relation
[y_{j},x_{i}]=\delta_{i,j}
if we set
y_{i}=\sqrt{-1}\hbar^{-1}p_{i}
. It is in this context that Weyl originally introduced the algebra bearing his name.

Alternatively, one can consider the algebra
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
of differential operators on
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. The ring
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is generated as a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}
, where
\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
, and the product rule for differentiation implies that
[\partial_{i},x_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}
. Thus
A_{n}{\;\stackrel{{{}_{\sim}}}{{\to}}\;}\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
via
y_{i}\mapsto\partial_{i}
.

These different realizations of the Weyl algebra mean that it can be studied from the point of view of
C^{*}
-algebras, systems of partial differential equations, (complex) symplectic geometry or non-commutative ring theory. The truly interesting mathematics arises from thinking of the Weyl algebra as a bridge between these four disparate fields. This bridge has been extensively exploited and developed in the 98 years since Weyl’s article was published.

This article surveys Stafford’s fundamental work in describing the ring-theoretic and module-theoretic properties of Weyl algebras during the decade 1976-1987. In particular, we focus on the paper
Module structure of Weyl algebras

[
45
]
published in the Journal of the London Mathematical Society in 1978 and the subsequent paper
Non-holonomic modules over Weyl algebras and enveloping algebras

[
46
]
published in Inventiones Mathematicae in 1985. These papers beautifully illustrate that Weyl algebras exhibit very surprising (and frankly bizarre) ring-theoretic behaviour confounding intuition coming from commutative ring theory. As we’ll see, this behaviour stems largely from the fact that Weyl algebras are
simple rings
- they contain no proper two-sided ideals.

Outline

The results in the papers we are surveying are ring-theoretic and their genesis can clearly be seen in the earlier papers
[
43
,
44
]
that came out of Stafford’s PhD thesis. Since these results provide motivation for the results in
[
45
]
, we begin in Section 2 with a summary of these earlier papers. We recall three fundamental results in commutative ring theory due to Serre and Bass. As we will see, Stafford showed that these results have natural counterparts for simple non-commutative rings and are closely related to the basic question of how many elements are required to generate a given module. Section 3 explains how these results were developed and greatly strengthened in the paper
Module structure of Weyl algebras

[
45
]
published in the Journal of the London Mathematical Society.

As is often the case in mathematics, part of the appeal and beauty of the results in this survey come not just from the fact that they are surprising and deep but that they can be translated into different results in a (seemingly tangentially) related field where they reveal truly surprising and revolutionary consequences. Therefore, after a short Section 4 recalling the definition of holonomic modules over Weyl algebras, we describe in Section 5 Stafford’s remarkable results around the existence of non-holonomic simple modules over Weyl algebras. These examples showed that simple modules can behave in very surprising ways and that intuition built from the properties of holonomic modules can be very misleading in general. We also explain how Stafford used these examples to describe the unusual behaviour of simple modules over the enveloping algebra of a semi-simple Lie algebra. Finally, we describe subsequent work of Bernstein-Lunts, where they give a geometric approach to constructing families of examples of non-holonomic simple modules for Weyl algebras.

In Section 6, we describe more recent developments in the study of Weyl algebras. This section is very subjective and we have decided to concentrate on the remarkable properties of right ideals in the first Weyl algebra. In the final Section 7 we highlight the fact that Stafford’s work, viewed in the context of quantized symplectic singularities, raises several interesting open problems.

2.
Prehistory - The stable Range Theorems

Our story begins not with the Weyl algebra but rather at a key moment in the establishment of modern commutative algebra. In the late 1950’s, J. P. Serre did seminal work in translating concepts in topology into the language and setting of algebra. A fundamental observation of Serre
[
42
]
along these lines is that the category of (algebraic) vector bundles on an affine variety is equivalent to that of finitely generated projective modules over the corresponding commutative Noetherian ring. He goes on to prove in the algebraic setting several results that were known previously in the geometric setting. The most important of these is
Serre’s Theorem
saying that projective modules have a free summand if they are sufficiently large.

Theorem 2.1

(Serre’s Theorem)
.

Let
R
be a commutative Noetherian domain with
\Kdim R=n
and

a finitely generated projective module of rank
>n

P\cong P^{\prime}\oplus R
for some (projective) submodule

.

Here
\Kdim R
denotes the Krull dimension of the ring
R

[
35
, Chapter 5]
and the rank of a finitely generated module
M
is defined to be
\dim_{K}(K\otimes_{R}M)
, where
K
is the field of fractions of the domain
R
. The above result is
[
42
, Théorème 1]
. As noted at the end of Serre’s paper, this result has applications to understanding the structure of the Grothendieck group of projective
R
-modules. Later Bass
[
2
]
undertook a far more systematic study of the Grothendieck group
K_{0}
, together with the higher
K
-group

. In this context, Bass establishes two other key results closely related to Serre’s Theorem. The first of these is
[

, Theorem 11.1]

Theorem 2.2

(Stable Range Theorem)
.

Let
R
be a commutative Noetherian ring with
\Kdim R=n
, and suppose
R=\sum_{i=1}^{n+2}a_{i}R

such that

Bass was motivated to prove this theorem in order to understand the properties of

Theorem 2.3

(Cancellation Theorem)

Let

be a commutative Noetherian domain with

>n

is another finitely generated projective module and

The cancellation theorem also appears to be due to Bass from his work on the stable range for

[
2
, Theorem 9.3]

One would imagine that all this is rather far removed from the paper
[
45
]
by Stafford on modules over Weyl algebras. However, a glance at the papers
[

44
]
resulting from his PhD thesis make the connection very clear. In these papers, Stafford explores the extent to which the above three results make sense for non-commutative simple (left or right) Noetherian rings. As we’ll see, much stronger versions of these results can be shown to hold when the simplicity hypothesis is assumed. It is surprising to the author that one would even consider these fundamental results in commutative algebra in the context of simple non-commutative rings given that the two classes are in a sense diametrically opposite.

Here

3.
Module structure of Weyl algebras

Krull dimension plays a crucial role in the proof of the results described in Section
2
. This is evident even from the statement of the results, which is encoded in terms of Krull dimension. However, Krull dimension does not appear in the statement of the results for the Weyl algebra (which is partly why they are so surprising) and neither is it used in the proof.

The key technical result is the following version of the Stable Range Theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
45
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
I=aA_{n}+bA_{n}+cA_{n}
be a right ideal of
A_{n}
and let
d_{1},d_{2}
be arbitrary non-zero elements of
A_{n}
. Then there exist
f
and

in
A_{n}
such that

I=(a+cfd_{1})A_{n}+(b+cgd_{2})A_{n}.

In particular, this implies that every right ideal in
A_{n}
can be generated by just two (!!) elements. One should compare this with Theorem
2.6
, noting that the Krull dimension of
A_{n}
is

[
36
, Theorem 6.6.15]
. In terms of PDEs with polynomial coefficients, Theorem
3.1
implies that if

then there exist

such that a function

if and only if
Pf=Qf=0

[

has developed an algorithm implemented in Macaulay2
[
24
]
to explicitly compute two generators of a given right ideal. The following analogues of Serre’s Theorem and the Cancellation Theorem are deduced, more or less directly, from Theorem

Theorem 3.2
.

[

Let

-module. Then

is a module of rank
\leq 1

Theorem 3.3
.

[

(a) Let

(b) Let

Notice that if we take

there exist

such that

[

that

Every finitely generated torsion

This conjecture is stated in a different (and slightly stronger) form as

Stafford’s proof of Theorem

is by induction on

4.
Holonomic
\mathscr{D}
-modules

We have seen that there is a clear path from commutative algebra and
K
-theory, through analogous results for simple Noetherian rings, to the generation and Stable Range theorems for Weyl algebras. An important aspect of the representation theory of Weyl algebras which has not yet made an appearance is the notion of holonomic modules. Though the notion appears to have its origin in the study of “maximally overdetermined systems of partial differential equations” (see
[
41
]
), it is more clearly motivated by asking the basic question: what is the “size” of simple modules over the Weyl algebra? Before making this precise, consider the following elementary fact. If
M
is a non-zero
A_{n}
-module then
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M=\infty
. Indeed, suppose that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M<\infty
. Then
x_{1},y_{1}
act as linear transformations
X_{1},Y_{1}
of
M
such that
[Y_{1},X_{1}]=\mathrm{Id}_{M}
. Taking trace of both sides of this equation gives
0=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
, a contradiction. Therefore one needs a way to measure size beyond naive dimension as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space.

The correct notion of size is Gelfand-Kirillov dimension. We will not recall the definition here. Suffice it to say, this is a measure of the rate of growth of finitely generated modules over an affine (i.e., finitely generated) algebra. A key result is Bernstein’s inequality
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
, which states that if
M
is a non-zero finitely generated module over the Weyl algebra
A_{n}
then

(4.1)

2\GKdim(M)\geq\GKdim(A_{n})=2n\quad\textrm{i.e.,}\quad\GKdim(M)\geq n.

The holonomic modules are precisely those of smallest Gelfand-Kirillov dimension i.e., the modules
M
with
\GKdim(M)=n
. In many ways, the (abelian) category of holonomic modules behaves like the category of finite-dimensional modules over a finite-dimensional algebra. For instance, holonomic modules have finite length and
\Hom_{A_{n}}(M,N)
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space when
M,N
are holonomic. Moreover, it has been shown that every holonomic module is cyclic, which to the author is a very surpring result; see e.g.,
[
16
, Corollary 10.2.6]
. This is relevant for Conjecture
3.4
since holonomic modules are torsion.

The standard example of a holonomic module is
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, with
A_{n}
acting as differential operators. It is easy to see that this module is both holonomic and simple. The ring itself gives an example of a module which is not holonomic. There is an abstract classification of simple holonomic modules for
A_{n}
in terms of minimal extensions of integrable connections; see e.g.,
[
25
, §3.4]
.

5.
Existence of non-holonomic simple modules

Though we have seen an example of a simple holonomic module and they are classifiable in a certain sense, it is much harder to write down a simple module that is not holonomic. In fact, the expectation in the 1970s was that all simple modules over
A_{n}
should be holonomic. In the literature, one can see this as a special case of a more general expectation of a relation between the Krull dimension and the GK-dimension of a module. Precise statements can be found in Problem 1 (Chapter 1, §9) of the book
[
12
]
by J.-E. Björk and Section 7 of
[
37
]
.

Therefore, it was very suprising when Stafford showed:

Theorem 5.1
.

[
46
, Theorem 1.1]

There exists a cyclic, maximal right ideal
aA_{n}
in
A_{n}
. In particular, the simple
A_{n}
-module
N=A_{n}/aA_{n}
has Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
2n-1
.

If you want to check that this contradicts the above mentioned expectations, note that a simple module has Krull dimension zero. Stafford goes on to show that these non-holonomic modules behave very differently to holonomic modules.

To illustrate this, let us first recall the conjecture by I. M. Gelfand that motived J. Bernstein to introduce, and study, holonomic modules over the Weyl algebra. Let
P
be a polynomial in
n
variables with real coefficients and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>0
. If
\Theta
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
on which
P
is positive then we can define the (generalized) function
P^{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
by
P^{\lambda}(x)=P(x)^{\lambda}
for
x\in\Theta
, and zero outside
\Theta
. In the International Congress of Mathematicians, Amsterdam, 1954, Gelfand conjectured in his lecture
[
22
]
that
P^{\lambda}
extends to a function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}
that is meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
. In fact, he made a strong conjecture on the meromorphic extension of certain distributions associated to
P
. A proof of this (more general) conjecture was given by S. I. Gelfand–J. Bernstein
[
9
]
and M. Atiayh
[
1
]
, both relying on Hironaka’s Theorem on the existence of resolutions of singularities.

In 1972, Bernstein used holonomic modules to give a pure algebraic proof
[
8
, Theorem 1]
of the conjecture, as stated above. This was done by proving the existence
[
8
, Theorem 1’]
of the “Bernstein-Sato” polynomial associated to
P
. The key step in the proof of the latter result is to show that if
M
is holonomic and
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
non-zero then the localization
M[f^{-1}]
is still holonomic. In particular, it is finitely generated. This is a central result in the theory of holonomic
\mathscr{D}
-modules since it allows one to prove that holonomicity is preserved under (derived) pull-back and push-forward for arbitrary morphisms. See
[
27
, Chapter 8]
for a detailed exposition of Bernstein’s proof of Gelfand’s conjecture.

Contrast Bernstein’s result on the localization of holonomic modules with the following corollary by Stafford of Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

[
46
, Corollary 1.2]

Let

and
M=A_{2}/\alpha A_{2}
. Then
M
is a simple

-module such that
M\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]}\mathbb{C}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}]

Another result already mentioned is that the space of homomorphisms between holonomic modules is finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{C}
. This holds more generally for all Ext-groups between holonomic modules. Therefore, if we take
\alpha=x_{1}+y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}+x_{2}+y_{2}\in A_{2}

.

[
46
, Corollary 1.3]

-vector space.

Perhaps even more strangely, V. Bavula gives in
[
3
, Theorem 2.2]
a simple holonomic module

such that

is infinite-dimensional as a complex vector space. Here

is the simple module introduced in Theorem

. This implies that there exist (uncountably many) indecomposable modules

of length two with submodule isomorphic to
S
and quotient
N
. This answered positively a question raise by T. Lenagan
[
28
]
. In the opposite direction, G. Perets
[
40

gave an example of an indecomposable module of length two with non-holonomic simple submodule
N
and simple holonomic quotient.

5.1.
Applications to enveloping algebras

Bernstein’s inequality is also known to hold
[
27
, Theorem 9.11]
for the enveloping algebra of a finite dimensional (complex) Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
; a result due to O. Gabber. However, the statement of the inequality must be modified to take into account the fact that the enveloping algebra is not a simple ring. Namely, Gabber showed that if
M
is a finitely generated
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module with annihilator
\ann_{U}(M)\lhd U(\mathfrak{g})
then:

(5.1)

2\GKdim(M)\geq\GKdim(U(\mathfrak{g})/\ann_{U}(M)).

A simple
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module
M
is
holonomic
if we have equality in (
5.1
). More generally, a module
M
is holonomic if it has finite length and all simple subquotients are holonomic.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a semi-simple Lie algebra then the Conze embedding gives an embedding of each primitive central quotient
U(\mathfrak{g})/P
of
U(\mathfrak{g})
into some Weyl algebra
A_{n}
. Stafford considers the Conze embedding of a primitive factor
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})/P
of
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
into
A_{2}
. He shows that one can choose
\beta\in A_{2}
(unfortunately one cannot use the element
\alpha
appearing in Corollary
5.2
for this) such that
A_{2}/\beta A_{2}
is simple and
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})/(\beta A_{2}\cap U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2}))
is a simple
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})/P
-module.

Theorem 5.4
.

[
46
, Theorem 2.7]

There exists
\beta\in A_{2}
such that
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})/(\beta A_{2}\cap U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2}))
is a non-holonomic simple
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-module.

Since
U(\mathfrak{g})
is a Hopf algebra, the tensor product
E\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}M
of any two
U(\mathfrak{g})
-modules
E
and
M
is again a
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module. The following is a truly remarkable result, which beautifully illustrates the strange behaviour of non-holonomic simple modules.

Theorem 5.5
.

[
46
, Theorem 4.1]

There exist simple right
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-modules
E
and
M
, with
E
finite-dimensional, such that
E\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}M
has infinite length.

Given the importance of projective functors
[
11
]
in the representation theory of simple Lie algebras, Theorem
5.5
has motivated a great deal of research into finding classes of simple modules
M
for which
E\otimes M
has finite length for all finite-dimensional representations
E
; see e.g.,
[
34
, §1.3]
. Stafford’s approach is very explicit, involving concrete computations in
U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
. It raises the question of whether Theorems
5.4
and
5.5
hold for any semi-simple Lie algebra. S. C. Coutinho shows in
[
17
]
that this is indeed the case, except possibly when
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

5.2.
The generalization by Bernstein-Lunts

A couple of years after Stafford’s work
[
46
]
showing the existence of non-holonomic modules for the Weyl algebra, J. Bernstein and V. Lunts
[
10
]
used a very different, geometric, method to constructing non-holonomic simple modules. They showed that any sufficiently generic
d\in A_{n}
gives rise to a simple module
A_{n}/dA_{n}
. In this section we describe more precisely their result.

The link to geometry is made via the characteristic variety of an
A_{n}
-module, allowing one to define holonomic modules via geometry. This approach requires some preparation but provides in return finer information about the module. The Weyl algebra can be endowed with the
Bernstein filtration

A_{n}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{i}A_{n}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{i}A_{n}
is the
\mathbb{C}
-linear span of all polynomials of degree at most
i
in the generators
x_{j},y_{k}
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}_{0}A_{n}=\mathbb{C}
,
\mathcal{B}_{1}A_{n}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
and
(\mathcal{B}_{i}A_{n})\cdot(\mathcal{B}_{j}A_{n})=\mathcal{B}_{i+j}A_{n}
. The associated graded algebra is commutative:

(5.2)

P:=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]{\;\stackrel{{{}_{\sim}}}{{\to}}\;}\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{B}}A_{n}:=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{i}A_{n}/\mathcal{B}_{i-1}A_{n},

where
\mathcal{B}_{-1}A_{n}:=\{0\}
and
\xi_{i}
maps to the class of
y_{i}
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}A_{n}/\mathcal{B}_{0}A_{n}
. This isomorphism allows one to use the commutator bracket
[-,-]
on
A_{n}
to define a Poisson bracket
\{-,-\}
on the polynomial ring
P
. The commutation relations (
1.1
) imply that

\{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\}=\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=0,\quad\{\xi_{i},x_{j}\}=\delta_{i,j},\quad\textrm{for all $1\leq i,j\leq n$},

so that, up to sign, one can view
P
with this Poisson bracket as the algebra of functions on the symplectic vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated left
A_{n}
-module. We say that a filtration
\mathcal{B}_{{\>\raisebox{2.0pt}{\text{\circle*{1.5}}}}}M
on
M
is a
good filtration
if

(\mathcal{B}_{i}A_{n})(\mathcal{B}_{j}M)\subset\mathcal{B}_{i+j}M,\quad\mathcal{B}_{i}M=\{0\}\textrm{ for $i\ll 0$},\quad M=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{B}_{i}M,

and, most importantly,

\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{B}}M:=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{B}_{i}M/\mathcal{B}_{i-1}M

is finitely generated over
P
. One can easily show that any finitely generated
A_{n}
-module can be equipped with a good filtration, though good filtrations are far from unique. The
characteristic variety

\mathrm{Ch}_{\mathcal{B}}(M)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
of
M
is the support of the finitely generated
P
-module
\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{B}}M
. As a (reduced) subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
,
\mathrm{Ch}_{\mathcal{B}}(M)
is independent of the choice of good filtration. We recall that an ideal
I
in the Poisson algebra
P
is
involutive
if
\{I,I\}\subset I
. The following deep result is central in the theory of
\mathscr{D}
-modules.

Theorem 5.6

(Gabber’s Theorem
[
21
]
)
.

The ideal
\sqrt{\ann\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{B}}M}
is involutive.

The importance of Gabber’s Theorem is that it implies that
\Ch_{\mathcal{B}}(M)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
is a coistropic subvariety. This implies that
2\dim\mathrm{Ch}_{\mathcal{B}}(M)\geq\dim\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. Since it is easily shown that
\dim\mathrm{Ch}_{\mathcal{B}}(M)=\GKdim(M)
, we see that Bernstein’s inequality (
4.1
) can deduced from Gabber’s Theorem
5.6
.

The isomorphism (
5.2
) is graded, where
P
is graded with
\deg x_{i}=\deg\xi_{j}=1
. We let
P_{k}
denote the finite-dimensional subspace of homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
.

Theorem 5.7
.

[
10
, Theorem A]

For
n=2
and
k\geq 4
, if
d\in\mathcal{B}_{k}A_{n}
is any element whose symbol
s=\sigma(d)\in P_{k}
is generic then
A_{n}/dA_{n}
is irreducible.

As in
[
10
, Remark 1]
, here “generic” means that the set
N\subset P_{k}
of polynomials
s
for which the conclusion of Theorem
5.7
holds satisfies the following condition: Its complement
P_{k}\setminus N
can be covered by a countable number of hypersurfaces.

Using the fact that
\mathrm{Ch}_{\mathcal{B}}(M)
is a homogeneous coisotropic subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, the above theorem is easily deduced from the corresponding geometric statement outlined in Theorem
5.8
below. Following Bernstein-Lunts, we say that a subvariety
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
is a
minimal homogeneous coistropic subvariety
if it is a homogeneous coistropic subvariety that does not properly contain any other (closed) homogeneous coistropic subvariety (they use the term “involutive” rather than coistropic). Using the non-existence of algebraic solutions to a certain generic first order partial differential equation
[
10
, Theorem 1]
, they show that:

Theorem 5.8
.

[
10
, Theorem A’]

For
n=2
and
k\geq 4
and generic
s\in P_{k}
, the irreducible subvariety
V(s)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
is a minimal homogeneous coistropic subvariety.

Again, “generic” means that the set
N\subset P_{k}
of polynomials
s
for which the conclusion of Theorem
5.8
holds has the property that its complement
P_{k}\setminus N
can be covered by a countable number of hypersurfaces. Bernstein-Lunts conjecture that the statement of Theorem
5.8
, and hence of Theorem
5.7
too, should hold for all
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 3
. This conjecture was soon confirmed by V. Lunts
[
33
]
for
n\geq 2,k\geq 4
. The case
n=2
and
k=3
was done by T. McCune
[
38
]
and the induction argument of
[
33
]
applied to this case also deals with
n\geq 2
and
k=3
.

Since
A_{n}
is a ring of differential operators it can be given the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{{\>\raisebox{2.0pt}{\text{\circle*{1.5}}}}}A_{n}
by order of differential operators. Here
\mathcal{F}_{i}A_{n}
is the subspace spanned by all differential operators of order at most
i
so that
\mathcal{F}_{0}A_{n}=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and
y_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}A_{n}
. One can ask what happens if we replaced the Bernstein filtration by the order filtration. The associated graded ring is again the polynomial ring
P
, though one should now think of
P
as the ring of functions on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathbb{C}^{n}
(the Poisson structure is the same, but the grading is different). Again, every finitely generated module
M
has a characteristic variety
\Ch_{\mathcal{F}}(M)\subset T^{*}\mathbb{C}^{n}
, coistropic by Gabber’s Theorem
5.6
. We note that under the obvious identification
T^{*}\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{2n}
,
\Ch_{\mathcal{B}}(M)\neq\Ch_{\mathcal{F}}(M)
though they have the same dimension.

Bernstein-Lunts consider polynomial vector fields
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(x)\partial_{i}
and say that a pair
(\xi,f)
, where
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, is very generic if it satisfies certain technical genericity conditions (these are conditions (*) and (**) of
[
10
, Proposition 6]
). If
(\xi,f)
is very generic then they show that
A_{n}/(f+\xi)A_{n}
is a simple module. The difficulty is to prove that there exist any very generic pairs
(\xi,f)
. They show that this is the case when
n=2
.

Theorem 5.9
.

[
10
, § 4.3]

If
n=2
then there exist very generic pairs
(\xi,f)
, where the coefficients
r_{i}(x)
of
\xi
have degree
\geq 2
.

Again, they conjecture that the statement of Theorem
5.8
holds for all
n\geq 2
. This conjecture was confirmed by J. Vitório Pereira
[
39
]
.

6.
Subsequent developments

As noted in the introduction, one can view the Weyl algebra as the ring of differential operators on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. From this point of view, the Weyl algebra is just a special case of rings
\mathscr{D}(X)
of differential operators on smooth affine varieties
X
. The study of modules over these rings (or rather modules over the corresponding sheaf of differential operators in the case of non-affine varieties) is a vast area - this is the theory of
\mathscr{D}
-modules. We won’t touch on this subject here. See, for instance, the books
[
16
,
25
]
for excellent introductions. However, one can ask to what extent Stafford’s results extend to the rings
\mathscr{D}(X)
. This question was considered by S. C. Couhtino and M. Holland
2
2

2

See Section 4 of
[
18
]
for a number of open conjectures in this direction.

[
18
]
. In particular, they showed that every right ideal in
\mathscr{D}(X)
can be generated by
3
elements; it is a question of J.-E. Björk
[
13
, Problem 2.10]
whether
2
elements suffice.

Somewhat tangential to the general theory of
\mathscr{D}
-modules, Stafford considers in
[
47
]
one-sided ideals in the first Weyl algebra. Since we are working over the complex numbers, the Weyl algebra
A_{1}
is hereditary, i.e., has (left and right) global dimension one, and hence every one-sided ideal is projective. If
0\neq I\subset A_{1}
is a right ideal then the fact that
A_{1}
is simple and hereditary implies that
A_{1}
is Morita equivalent to
E=\End_{A_{1}}(I)
; it is a generator because
I^{*}I
is a non-zero two-sided ideal of
A_{1}
and hence
I^{*}I=A_{1}
. Stafford shows
[
47
, Theorem A]
that
E\cong A_{1}
if and only if
I
is a cyclic right ideal. As a consequence:

Corollary 6.1
.

[
47
, Corollary B]

Let
P
and
Q
be right ideals of
A_{1}
. Then
\End_{A_{1}}(P)\cong\End_{A_{1}}(Q)
if and only if
P=t\sigma(Q)
for some
t\in D(A_{1})
, the division ring of fractions of
A_{1}
, and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{1})
, the group of
\mathbb{C}
-algebra automorphisms of
A_{1}
.

This remarkable result raises more questions than it answers. How many isomorphism classes of right ideals are there in the first Weyl algebra? What is
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{n})
?…

The first question has led to an explosion of work connecting the first Weyl algebra to integrable systems, rational Cherednik algebras and non-commutative projective geometry. We only touch on the beginnings of this story. Two right ideals
P,Q
of
A_{1}
are said to be isomorphic if there exist non-zero
s,t\in A_{1}
such that
sP=tQ
. Since
A_{1}
is a Noetherian domain, this is the same as saying that they are isomorphic as
A_{1}
-modules. R. Cummings and M. Holland
[
14
]
described a certain (infinite) set
\mathcal{C}
that parametrizes explicitly the isomorphism classes of right ideals. The group
G=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{1})
acts on the set
\mathcal{C}
. A key observation by Y. Berest and G. Wilson, which was the foundation of their seminal work
[
7
]
, is that this set is precisely the “adelic Grassmanian” appearing in integrable systems. They went on to show that

\mathcal{C}=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{C}_{n},

where each
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is a
G
-orbit which can also be identified with Wilson’s compactified Calogero-Moser space
[
49
]
. This connection between right ideals in the Weyl algebra and the Calogero-Moser space is very mysterious and several different constructions of the above bijection have appeared in the literature in order to try and demystify the connection. Since each
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is a
G
-orbit, Corollary
6.1
says that the ideals in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
all give rise to isomorphic endomorphism rings. Thus, right ideals in the Weyl algebra provide a countable family
E_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, of rings, all Morita equivalent but pairwise non-isomorphic. Stafford shows that
E_{m}
can be identified with the ring of differential operators on the non-normal rational curve
X_{m}
where
\mathcal{O}(X_{m})=\mathbb{C}\oplus x^{m+1}\mathbb{C}[x]
.

The group
G=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{1})
was first studied by Dixmier
[
19
]
, who gave an explicit set of generators. Setting
G_{m}=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(E_{m})
, Stafford asked
[
47
, p. 636]
:

(1)

Does there exist a description of
G_{m}
for
m>0
similar to Dixmier’s for
G
?

(2)

Is it true that
G_{m}\not\cong G
when
m>0
?

Berest–Eshmatov–Eshmatov
[
6
]
show that the answer to both questions is “yes”. For higher
n
, the group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{n})
is not so well-understood. Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich conjectured in
[
5
]
that
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{n})
should be equal to the group of Poisson
\mathbb{C}
-algebra automorphisms of the polynomial ring
P
. This conjecture has been proven in
[
26
]
, and a related (but slightly weaker) conjecture by Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich
[
5
, Conjecture 6]
was proven recently by C. Dodd
[
20
]
.

7.
Generalizations to quantized symplectic singularities

In this final section we wish to advertise the fact that Stafford’s results, and the subsequent ones by Bernstein-Lunts, should have natural generalizations to quantized symplectic singularities. These algebras are currently the subject of intense research within geometric representation theory.

A normal affine variety
X
is said to have symplectic singularities if its smooth locus has a holomorphic symplectic form compatible, in a strong sense, with all resolutions of singularities. See the original paper
[
4
]
by Beauville for details. We say that a variety
X
with symplectic singularities is
conic
if
\mathbb{C}[X]
is connected graded such that the symplectic form is homogeneous of (strictly) positive weight. Let
U=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{n}U
be a filtered
\mathbb{C}
-algebra such that
\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{F}}U
is a commutative ring. Then
\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{F}}U
is a Poisson algebra. If
\mathsf{gr}_{\mathcal{F}}U\cong\mathbb{C}[X]
as graded Poisson algebras, where
X
is a conic symplectic singularity, then
U
is said to be a quantization of a symplectic singularity.

The quantizations of a given conic symplectic singularity
X
have been classified by I. Losev
[
31
, Theorem 3.4]
. In particular, the Weyl algebra is the unique quantization of
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. The variety
X
has a finite stratification by symplectic leaves - these are smooth locally closed Poisson subvarieties where the Poisson structure is non-degenerate. If
M
is a finitely generated
U
-module then the characteristic variety
\Ch(M)
of
M
is coisotropic by Gabber’s Theorem. This means that
\Ch(M)\cap\mathcal{L}
is a coisotropic subvariety for all leaves
\mathcal{L}
. We say that
M
is
holonomic
if
\Ch(M)\cap\mathcal{L}
is a Lagrangian subvariety for every leaf
\mathcal{L}
.

If
M
is a simple
U
-module then, by definition, its annihilator is a primitive ideal
I\lhd U
. V. Ginzburg
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
showed that the associated variety
\Ch(U/I)\subset X
is the closure of a symplectic leaf. Notice that
\Ch(M)\subset\Ch(U/I)
. We ask:

Question 7.1
.

If
\Ch(U/I)
is the closure of the leaf
\mathcal{L}
then is
\mathcal{L}\cap\Ch(M)\neq\emptyset
?

If
\Ch(M)
intersects
\mathcal{L}
then
\mathcal{L}\cap\Ch(M)
is a coistropic subvariety of
\mathcal{L}
and hence
2\dim\Ch(M)\geq\dim\mathcal{L}
. This implies that
2\GKdim(M)\geq\GKdim(U/I)
i.e., the Bernstein inequality holds. If
\Ch(U/I)=\overline{\mathcal{L}}
then we say that
I
is a quantization of
\mathcal{L}
over
U
. For a given
U
, not all leaves of
X
admit a quantization. In fact, for a given leaf
\mathcal{L}
there might not be any quantization
U
of
X
such that
\mathcal{L}
can be quantized over
U
.

Question 7.2
.

Which leaves of
X
can be quantized over
U
?

Comparing Question
7.1
with the results on existence of non-holonomic simple modules for Weyl algebras, it is natural to ask:

Question 7.3
.

Assume that the leaf
\mathcal{L}
can be quantized over
U
. Is it the case that for all
0\leq i\leq(1/2)\dim\mathcal{L}
there exists a simple
U
-module
M_{i}
such that

\Ch(U/\ann_{U}M_{i})=\overline{\mathcal{L}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\GKdim(M_{i})=(1/2)\dim\mathcal{L}+i?

A related question concerns the behaviour of Ext-groups between simple modules.

Question 7.4
.

Let
M,N
be simple
U
-modules.

(1)

Is there a concise characterization of when the group
\Ext^{i}_{U}(M,N)
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space?

(2)

In particular, if
M,N
are holonomic is it the case that
\Ext^{i}_{U}(M,N)
is always a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space?

7.1.
Simplicity

We have seen throughout that a key property of the Weyl algebra is that it is a simple ring. Quantizations
U
are not always simple rings. However, in Losev’s classification
[
31
]
there is always a preferred quantization of
X
called the canonical quantization, see
[
32
, §5]
.

Conjecture 7.5
.

[
32
, Conjecture 5.5.1]

The canonical quantization is simple.

If
X
has
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminal singularities then there is a unique quantization
U
of
X
. In this case, the above conjecture says that
U
should be simple. This is a special case of an earlier conjecture by Losev
[
30
, Conjecture 3.1]
. The truth of Conjecture
7.5
would imply that every conic symplectic singularity has a simple quantization. If
U
is a simple quantization then it would be interesting to know if the results of
[
45
]
can be shown to hold for
U
.

7.2.
Automorphisms

In Section
6
, we saw the appearance of the automorphism group of the Weyl algebra in relation to right ideals in the first Weyl algebra. Therefore, it is natural to consider the group of
\mathbb{C}
-algebra automorphisms
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(U)
of a quantization
U
of a conic symplectic singularity
X
. Since Losev’s classification says that there is actually a family of quantizations, parametrized by a certain space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
where
W
is Namikawa’s Weyl group, it is natural to consider instead the groupoid
\mathsf{QIso}(X)
whose objects are points of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
and, for
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
,
\Hom_{\mathsf{QIso}(X)}(c,c^{\prime})
is the set of all isomorphisms
U_{c}{\;\stackrel{{{}_{\sim}}}{{\to}}\;}U_{c^{\prime}}
(not necessarily filtration preserving). As explained in
[
31
, §2]
, the same space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
also parametrizes all filtered Poisson deformations of
\mathbb{C}[X]
and one can similarly form the groupoid
\mathsf{PIso}(X)
whose objects are points of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
and
\Hom_{\mathsf{PIso}(X)}(c,c^{\prime})
is the set of all Poisson isomorphisms
A_{c}{\;\stackrel{{{}_{\sim}}}{{\to}}\;}A_{c^{\prime}}
between the filtered Poisson deformations
A_{c},A_{c^{\prime}}
(again, not necessarily filtration preserving). The following question was raised in
[
15
, Final Remarks]
.

Question 7.6
.

Are the groupoids
\mathsf{QIso}(X)
and
\mathsf{PIso}(X)
isomorphic over
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W
?

S. Castellan
[
15
]
shows that the answer is yes when
X
is a Kleinian singularity of type
A
or
D
and conjectures it should be the case for any Kleinian singularity. The equivalence
\mathsf{QIso}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})\simeq\mathsf{PIso}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
is precisely Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich’s conjecture since
\mathfrak{h}^{*}/W=\{0\}
in this case.
