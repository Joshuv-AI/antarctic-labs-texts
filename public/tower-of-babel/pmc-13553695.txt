Immunometabolic and RAAS determinants of circulating interferon gamma in an HIV and TB Zambian cohort
Authors: Dauyrinah Sidambi, David Chisompola, Propheria Lwindi, Arasidah Kaango, Benson M. Hamooya, Joreen P. Povia, Sepiso K. Masenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remain major global health challenges, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa where co-infection and chronic immune activation are highly prevalent. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) plays a central role in host defense against Mycobacterium tuberculosis, yet its determinants in real-world, high-burden populations especially in the context of metabolic and systemic inflammation are not fully understood. This study investigated clinical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors associated with circulating IFN-γ levels in adults in a high HIV/TB burden setting.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study of 227 adults attending medical clinic at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital, Zambia. Circulating IFN-γ and a panel of inflammatory, metabolic, and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) biomarkers were measured. Associations were assessed using simple and multivariable linear regression models, with log transformation applied to skewed variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.ResultsAmong participants (median age 50 years; 64.3% living with HIV), several variables were associated with IFN-γ in unadjusted analyses, including inflammatory cytokines, hs-CRP, angiotensin II, and fasting glucose. However, in multivariable analysis, only female sex (β = −0.440, p = 0.023), fasting glucose (β = 0.153, p = 0.042), angiotensin II (log-transformed) (β = 0.688, p = 0.022), and IL-17 A (log-transformed) (β = 0.332, p = 0.019) remained independently associated with IFN-γ levels. Notably, HIV status, prior tuberculosis infection, hs-CRP, and other inflammatory markers were not independently associated after adjustment.ConclusionIn this high HIV/TB burden population, circulating IFN-γ levels were independently associated with metabolic (fasting glucose), vascular-hormonal (angiotensin II), and immune (IL-17 A) factors, but not with HIV infection or prior TB after adjustment. These findings suggest that IFN-γ may reflect broader immunometabolic and inflammatory processes rather than infection-specific immune activation in treated populations. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the prognostic and clinical utility of IFN-γ in integrated chronic disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12982-026-02839-5.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality globally, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. HIV infection is the strongest known risk factor for progression from latent to active TB, and the convergence of these epidemics has created a persistent syndemic characterized by complex immune dysregulation and high transmission dynamics [2].

A central feature of host defense against Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the T-helper 1 immune response, in which interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) plays a critical role in macrophage activation and intracellular pathogen control [3, 4]. Consequently, IFN-γ has been widely used in immunodiagnostic assays and investigated as a biomarker of TB infection and disease activity [5–7]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that IFN-γ alone does not fully capture the complexity of host immune responses, particularly in HIV-infected individuals, where the IFN-γ signaling pathway is disrupted at multiple levels [8, 9]. Chronic HIV infection induces upregulation of negative regulators of the JAK–STAT signaling pathway, including SOCS1 (Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 1) and PIAS1 (Protein Inhibitor of Activated STAT 1), while progressive CD4 + Th1 cell depletion and functional exhaustion of CD8 + T cells result in quantitatively and qualitatively impaired IFN-γ production [10–12]. Importantly, these alterations persist despite ART, a phenomenon termed ‘residual immune activation’ driven by ongoing microbial translocation, T-cell senescence, TB co-infection, and ART-related immunomodulation [11, 13, 14]. Consequently, in treated populations, IFN-γ reflects a composite of residual viral responses, homeostatic proliferation, co-infections, and metabolic dysregulation rather than infection-specific immunity alone [15].

Recent research has shifted toward a broader systems-based understanding of TB and HIV pathogenesis, highlighting interactions between immune responses and metabolic, renal, and neurohormonal pathways. In particular, biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukins, and components of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) have been implicated in modulating inflammation and disease progression. Chronic immune activation in HIV has also been linked to metabolic dysfunction, including altered glucose homeostasis, which may further influence cytokine production and immune responses [16, 17]. HIV-driven inflammation promotes insulin resistance through multiple mechanisms, including TNF-α-mediated impairment of insulin signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and adipose tissue dysregulation [18, 19]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia enhances pro-inflammatory cytokine production via oxidative stress and advanced glycation end-products. This bidirectional relationship between immune activation and glucose metabolism creates a self-perpetuating cycle of immunometabolic dysfunction that may sustain IFN-γ production even in virologically suppressed individuals [20]. These findings suggest that IFN-γ regulation may reflect integrated immunometabolic and inflammatory processes rather than infection-specific immunity alone.

Despite these advances, important gaps remain. Most studies have examined IFN-γ or other cytokines in isolation or focused primarily on their diagnostic performance. Few studies have simultaneously evaluated inflammatory, metabolic, renal, and hormonal determinants of IFN-γ in real-world, high HIV/TB-burden populations, particularly among individuals receiving long-term antiretroviral therapy. Understanding these relationships is critical for interpreting IFN-γ as a biomarker in complex clinical settings where multiple comorbidities coexist.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the factors associated with circulating IFN-γ levels in adults attending a tertiary hospital in a high HIV/TB burden setting in Zambia, with a focus on integrating immunological, metabolic, and cardiometabolic biomarkers. We hypothesize that IFN-γ levels are driven more by systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation than by infection status alone in ART-treated populations.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Livingstone District at the University Teaching Hospital, located in Southern Zambia. The district has a high Human Immunodeficiency Virus burden recording the highest HIV prevalence rate in the country at 25.3% [21] .On approximation, the Hospital attends to about 3000 patients monthly. The study enrolled a total of 227 adults recruited from the outpatient medical and antiretroviral therapy clinics who were attending routine medical checkups between 01/04/2025 to 30/11/2025.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older, who provided written informed consent before enrolling on the study, living with HIV or without HIV. Those living with HIV were on stable antiretroviral therapy for at least 12 months and were virologically suppressed (viral load < 50 copies/mL). On the other hand, participants without HIV had a negative HIV test result. To minimize confounding effects on inflammatory markers, individuals with severe renal or hepatic disease, pregnancy, active opportunistic infections, or other serious co-morbidities were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

A Convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. This non-probability method was chosen to efficiently enroll as many participants as possible. A sample size calculation was performed for a multiple linear regression model. To detect a medium effect size (f² = 0.15) with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90% for a model testing up to 12 predictors, a sample size of n = 227 was used.

Sociodemographic and clinical data

Sociodemographic and clinical data were systematically collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform. Trained research personnel gathered information on age, sex, marital status, employment status, and medical history (including HIV, hypertension, and cardiovascular conditions) through standardized structured interviews in Tonga, Nyanja and English as appropriate and medical record reviews. HIV status and ART regimen details were confirmed from clinical records.

Specimen collection and biochemical measurements

Participants were recruited during routine outpatient clinic visits. Those who expressed interest and provided written informed consent were scheduled for a dedicated study visit. Participants were instructed to fast for a minimum of 8 h prior to the visit (from 20:00 the preceding evening), with only water permitted. They returned to the clinic the following morning between 08:00 and 09:00 h for blood sample collection. Venous blood was drawn with participants in a seated position. While fasting status and timing of sample collection were standardized, pre-sampling posture (e.g., duration of seated or supine rest) and dietary sodium intake were not strictly controlled. All samples were processed in accordance with standard laboratory procedures.

Following an overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected into separate commercially available tubes according to standard phlebotomy procedures. For plasma biochemical analyses (glucose, RAAS components, hs-CRP) and cytokine quantification (IL-17 A, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, sST2, and IFN-γ), blood was collected into lithium heparin tubes (BD Vacutainer, lithium heparin, 6.0 mL, Ref. 368886, separated and stored at -80 °C until analysis. For hematological parameters (white blood cell count), blood was collected into K₂EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer, K2EDTA, 3.0 mL, Ref. 367835). Tubes were collected in the recommended order of draw, with K2EDTA tubes drawn last to prevent cross-contamination of heparinized samples.

Cytokines including IL-17 A, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, Sst2 and IFN-γ were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) commercially available kits Elabscience (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, USA), following manufacturer protocols. The detection range for IFN-γ was 7.81–4000 pg/mL, with comparable ranges applied to other cytokines depending on the specific assay configuration. All cytokine concentrations were expressed in pg/mL. The intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were below 8%, while inter-assay CVs were below 10%, indicating acceptable assay precision consistent with established thresholds for biomarker studies (typically < 10%).

Fasting glucose, renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were quantified using standard automated clinical chemistry analyzers (Cobas c311, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) following manufacturer protocols. Total immunoglobulin E (IgE) was measured using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, USA; catalog no. E-EL-H6084), with a detection range of 31.25–2000 IU/mL and intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation < 8% and < 10%, respectively. All laboratory analyses were performed in a single accredited laboratory, with samples analyzed in batches to minimize inter-assay variability and ensure consistency of measurements.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable in this study was circulating interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), measured in pg/mL and analyzed as a continuous variable. IFN-γ concentrations were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges due to non-normal distribution, and appropriate transformations and regression techniques were used to account for skewness. All statistical analyses, including univariable and multivariable linear regression models, treated IFN-γ as a continuous dependent variable to preserve statistical power and accurately capture variability across the cohort. Independent variables considered in the analysis included demographic factors (age, sex), clinical characteristics (HIV status, history of tuberculosis, hypertension status, smoking, body mass index), hematological indices (white blood cell count), metabolic markers (fasting glucose, triglycerides), inflammatory and immunological biomarkers (IL-17 A, IL-6, IL-5, TNF-α, hs-CRP, IgE, sST2), and components of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (renin, aldosterone, and angiotensin II), selected based on biological plausibility and prior literature.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from the REDCap platform into a structured format and underwent initial cleaning in Microsoft Excel to ensure data integrity. All statistical analyses were performed using StatCrunch and GraphPad prism 10.3.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using graphical methods and the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most variables were not normally distributed, they are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages.

For the correlation analyses, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), a non-parametric measure of association that does not assume a normal distribution of the study variables was used. Spearman’s correlation was selected because most continuous variables in our dataset exhibited non-normal distributions, as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p < 0.05), and because it is robust to outliers, which were present in several biomarkers (supplementary file 1). Correlation strength was interpreted according to the following criteria based on the absolute value of Spearman’s ρ: negligible (0.00–0.09), weak (0.10–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.69), strong (0.70–0.89), and very strong (≥ 0.90) [22]. These criteria were applied consistently across all correlation analyses. Statistical significance for correlation coefficients was determined using a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

For inferential analysis, multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate independent associations between selected demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables and the study outcome. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on biological plausibility and prior literature.

To assess multicollinearity among independent variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated. Variables demonstrating significant multicollinearity were excluded from the final model; specifically, IL-5, IL-6, renin, and aldosterone were removed based on elevated VIF values. This approach was used to improve model stability and interpretability. To ensure the validity of the linear regression models, key assumptions were evaluated. Heteroscedasticity was assessed using the Breusch–Pagan test. Where evidence of heteroscedasticity was detected (p < 0.05), heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors were used to ensure valid statistical inference. Additionally, residual plots were visually inspected to confirm the presence or absence of non-constant variance.

Several skewed biomarkers (angiotensin II, hs-CRP, IL-17 A, IgE, and TNF-α) were log-transformed prior to analysis to approximate normal distributions. Regression results are reported as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 227 participants were recruited, of whom 146 (64.3%) were living with HIV and 81 (35.7%) were without HIV. Participants living with HIV were older than those without HIV (median age: 50 [IQR: 43–59] vs. 46 [29–59] years), and females predominated in both groups (71.2% vs. 63.0%). Most participants living with HIV were unmarried (60.3%) and unemployed (70.5%) (Table 1). Nearly all participants living with HIV (95.3%) were receiving an INSTI-based ART regimen, and 19.8% had a history of tuberculosis. Hypertension was similarly prevalent among participants living with and without HIV (30.1% vs. 27.2%), whereas hypertensive heart disease was more common among participants without HIV (17.6% vs. 7.3%). Smoking was reported only among participants living with HIV (7.1%). Body mass index (24.6 vs. 26.3 kg/m2) and fasting glucose (4.9 vs. 4.8 mmol/L) were comparable between groups (Table 1).

Compared with participants without HIV, those living with HIV exhibited substantially higher median concentrations of several inflammatory and immune biomarkers, including IL-6 (101.2 vs. 0.78 pg/mL), IL-17 A (123.9 vs. 19.9 pg/mL), hs-CRP (77,190 vs. 9,211.1 pg/mL), IFN-γ (2,110 vs. 22.0 pg/mL), IL-5 (1,315.5 vs. 12.3 pg/mL), soluble sT2 (24.8 vs. 0.21 pg/mL), aldosterone (4,816.4 vs. 36.9 pg/mL), renin (3,144.0 vs. 21.5 pg/mL), angiotensin II (4,337.1 vs. 36.9 pg/mL), and IgE (3,072.6 vs. 48.5 pg/mL). White blood cell counts were also modestly higher among participants living with HIV (3.7 vs. 3.1 × 109/L), whereas TNF-α concentrations were higher among HIV-negative participants (1,476.5 vs. 152.6 pg/mL) (Table 1).

IFN-γ was also positively correlated with hs-CRP, IgE, and components of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (renin, aldosterone, and angiotensin II). The observed significant correlations in this study were predominantly weak, with no moderate or stronger correlations identified (Fig. 1).

Unadjusted linear regression of factors associated with IFN-γ

In unadjusted linear regression analysis, several demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables showed independent associations with IFN-γ levels. HIV status (Living with HIV) (β = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.27 to 1.70, p < 0.001), a history of tuberculosis (β = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.98, p = 0.015), fasting glucose (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.27, p = 0.016), triglyceride (β = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.57, p = 0.028). and white blood cell count (β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.15, p = 0.018) were positively associated with IFN-γ (Table 2). Additionally, angiotensin II (log-transformed) (β = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.51 to 0.67, p < 0.001), IL-17 A (log-transformed) (β = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.54 to 0.84, p < 0.001), hs-CRP (log-transformed) (β = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.24, p = 0.010) and IgE (log-transformed) (β = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.26, p < 0.001) were associated with higher IFN-γ levels. In contrast, age, sex, body mass index, smoking status and TNF-α levels were not significantly associated with IFN-γ in the unadjusted analysis (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Multiple linear regression for factors associated with IFN-γ (adjusted)

In multivariable linear regression analysis, several factors showed independent associations with IFN-γ after adjustment for potential confounders (Table 3). Female sex was significantly associated with a lower outcome measure compared to males (β = −0.440, 95% CI: −0.817 to − 0.063, p = 0.023). Among metabolic parameters, higher fasting glucose levels were positively associated with the outcome (β = 0.153, 95% CI: 0.006 to 0.301, p = 0.042). Similarly, increasing levels of angiotensin II (log-transformed) showed a strong positive association (β = 0.688, 95% CI: 0.100 to 1.276, p = 0.022). Inflammatory marker IL-17 A (log-transformed) was also independently associated with higher outcome values (β = 0.332, 95% CI: 0.055 to 0.608, p = 0.019). Other variables including age, body mass index, HIV status, history of tuberculosis, smoking status, hs-CRP, IgE, TNF-α, white blood cell count, and triglyceride levels, were not associated with the IFN-γ in the adjusted model (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical, inflammatory, metabolic, and neurohormonal determinants of circulating interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) in a high HIV/TB burden population in Zambia. The principal finding is that IFN-γ levels were independently associated with IL-17 A, angiotensin II, fasting glucose, and female sex, while HIV status and prior tuberculosis were not independently associated after adjustment. These findings suggest that in this cohort, IFN-γ reflects integrated immunometabolic and inflammatory processes rather than infection-specific immune activation alone, particularly in individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART).

A key observation is the independent association between IFN-γ and IL-17 A, highlighting coordinated activation of the T-helper 1 (Th1) and T-helper 17 (Th17) immune pathways. This is biologically plausible, as IFN-γ and IL-17 A are often co-regulated during chronic inflammatory responses and contribute synergistically to host defense and tissue inflammation. Previous studies have demonstrated that Th1/Th17 interactions play a central role in tuberculosis and chronic immune activation, particularly in settings of persistent antigen exposure or immune dysregulation [23–25]. The persistence of this association after adjustment underscores the importance of network-level immune activation rather than isolated cytokine effects.

The association between IFN-γ and angiotensin II provides further evidence of the emerging link between the RAAS and immune regulation. Angiotensin II is increasingly recognized as a pro-inflammatory mediator that promotes cytokine production, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction. In populations living with HIV, RAAS activation has been linked to chronic inflammation, hypertension, and metabolic disturbances [26]. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that angiotensin II can directly stimulate immune cells and amplify cytokine signaling pathways, including those involving IFN-γ [27, 28]. The association observed in this study supports the concept that neurohormonal activation contributes to systemic immune activation, even in the absence of active infection.

The independent association between fasting glucose and IFN-γ further highlights the role of metabolic dysregulation in shaping immune responses. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance are known to promote pro-inflammatory cytokine production and immune activation through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress and altered cellular metabolism. Chronic inflammation in HIV-infected individuals has also been linked to metabolic abnormalities, even in virologically suppressed patients [29, 30]. In this cohort, 95.3% of people living with HIV on INSTI-based regimens, predominantly dolutegravir-containing TLD or TafED. While these regimens are highly effective, recent evidence has linked dolutegravir to weight gain, insulin resistance, and increased diabetes risk, effects that may be particularly pronounced in women and in sub-Saharan African populations [31–33]. The association between glucose and IFN-γ may therefore reflect a confluence of HIV-driven inflammation, ART-related metabolic effects, and background metabolic risk. This is consistent with the growing recognition that metabolic factors are important factors of immune activation even in virologically suppressed patients [29].

Notably, although HIV status and prior tuberculosis were associated with IFN-γ in unadjusted analyses, these associations did not persist in the multivariable model. This suggests that the observed unadjusted relationships may be confounded by underlying inflammatory, metabolic, or neurohormonal factors. In ART-treated populations, chronic immune activation persists despite viral suppression, and cytokine levels may reflect this broader inflammatory milieu rather than active infection alone [28, 29]. Similarly, prior tuberculosis may leave residual immune signatures, but these may be overshadowed by systemic processes when multiple pathways are considered simultaneously. These findings align with studies showing that IFN-γ, while central to TB immunity, has limited specificity as a standalone biomarker in complex clinical settings [16, 17].

An additional finding was the association between female sex and lower IFN-γ levels. Sex-based differences in immune responses are well documented, with hormonal, genetic, and immunological factors contributing to differential cytokine production. Estrogen, for example, has been shown to modulate immune responses in a dose- and context-dependent manner: at physiological concentrations, estrogen generally enhances humoral immunity and may suppress Th1-type responses including IFN-γ production through estrogen receptor-mediated inhibition of NF-κB and STAT1 signaling, while at higher concentrations (e.g., during pregnancy or hormone replacement therapy), it can paradoxically promote pro-inflammatory cytokine production [34, 35]. While the mechanisms underlying this observation were not directly assessed in this study, it highlights the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in immunological research.

Importantly, several inflammatory markers, including hs-CRP, TNF-α, and IgE, were associated with IFN-γ in univariable analyses but not after adjustment. This likely reflects shared variance and overlapping inflammatory pathways, where multiple biomarkers capture similar underlying processes. The attenuation of these associations in multivariable models emphasizes the need to interpret cytokine data within a multidimensional framework, rather than attributing independent significance to individual markers.

Collectively, these findings support a systems-based model of IFN-γ regulation, in which immune, metabolic, and neurohormonal pathways interact to shape cytokine profiles. In this context, IFN-γ may serve as a marker of chronic immunometabolic activation, rather than a specific indicator of TB or HIV-related immune responses in treated populations. This perspective has important implications for the interpretation of IFN-γ in both research and clinical settings, particularly in regions where infectious and non-communicable diseases coexist.

The findings suggest that IFN-γ should be interpreted cautiously as a biomarker in high-burden settings, as its levels may be influenced by metabolic and cardiovascular factors in addition to infection. The association with angiotensin II and glucose raises the possibility that IFN-γ could reflect cardiometabolic risk and vascular inflammation, which are increasingly recognized as important contributors to morbidity in people living with HIV. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings do not establish causality or predictive value. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether IFN-γ is associated with clinical outcomes such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic complications, or immune recovery.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the comprehensive assessment of inflammatory, metabolic, and RAAS-related biomarkers in a well-characterized cohort from a high HIV/TB burden setting. The use of multivariable modeling and appropriate statistical techniques enhances the robustness of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. The use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, and the findings may not be generalizable beyond similar clinical settings. Additionally, circulating IFN-γ levels may not reflect tissue-level immune responses, particularly in latent or prior tuberculosis. Measurement at a single time point also limits the ability to capture temporal variability in cytokine levels.

Conclusion

Circulating IFN-γ levels in this high HIV/TB burden population were independently associated with IL-17 A, angiotensin II, fasting glucose, and sex, but not with HIV status or prior tuberculosis after multivariable adjustment. These findings indicate that IFN-γ is influenced by integrated immunological, metabolic, and neurohormonal pathways rather than infection-specific immune responses alone. The association with IL-17 A highlights coordinated Th1/Th17 immune activation, while the link with angiotensin II and glucose underscores the role of RAAS and metabolic dysregulation in systemic inflammation. Together, these results suggest that IFN-γ may serve as a marker of chronic immunometabolic activation in treated populations. Further longitudinal studies are needed to determine its prognostic and clinical utility in high-burden settings.
