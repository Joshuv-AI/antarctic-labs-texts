Multimodal data-driven clustering analysis of heart failure patients identifies two distinct patterns of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction: Results from the PACIFIC-PRESERVED study
Authors: Jean-Sébastien Hulot, Pierre-Yves Hervé, Nicolas Girerd, Frédérique Chézalviel-Guilbert, Philip Janiak, Pierre Boutouyrie, Jean-Joseph Christophe, Ariel Cohen, Thibaud Damy, Liliane Berkani, Kevin Duarte, Caroline Fraslon, Hüseyin Firat, Olivier Hanon, Richard Isnard, Guillaume Jondeau, Elie Mousseaux, Mathieu Pernot, Andrea Ruiz-Velasco, Alfonso Sartorius, Benoit Tyl, Gilles Soulat, Philippe Boutinaud, Damien Logeart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for over half of heart failure cases but remains poorly defined due to its clinical heterogeneity. In the prospective PACIFIC-Preserved cohort (NCT04189029, n = 155), we applied multimodal deep phenotyping, including high-throughput proteomics, and unsupervised machine learning to identify biologically distinct HFpEF subgroups. Multiview clustering revealed two major HFpEF phenogroups: PEF1, characterized by inflammatory signaling, TNF receptor activation, cardio-skeletal injury, and greater renal dysfunction; and PEF2, marked by endothelial stress and milder perturbations. Conventional diagnostics alone could not reliably distinguish these subgroups, but a multimodal classifier integrating clinical, imaging, and proteomic variables achieved high predictive accuracy (F1-score 0.87). External validation in the MEDIA-DHF cohort (n = 456) confirmed that PEF1 was independently associated with adverse outcomes (adjusted HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.04–4.94). These findings establish a classifier-driven, biologically informed stratification of HFpEF, providing a framework for precision medicine in heart failure.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a major public health concern, associated with high mortality and frequent rehospitalization despite significant therapeutic advances1,2. While patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (HFrEF) benefit from well-established, evidence-based treatments, those with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), now representing over 50% of all HF cases, continue to experience poor clinical outcomes and have fewer effective therapeutic options2. This disparity stems from an incomplete understanding of HFpEF pathophysiology which is characterized by marked clinical and biological heterogeneity3,4.

In contrast to HFrEF, which is defined by impaired systolic function and has a relatively uniform myocardial substrate, HFpEF is currently diagnosed on the basis of heart failure symptoms, elevated natriuretic peptides levels (e.g., NT-proBNP), and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) that strikingly stays in the normal range (≥ 50%)2. Although new diagnostic algorithms, such as the HFA-PEFF5 and H(2)FPEF6 scores, have been proposed, they fall short in accounting for the diverse pathophysiological processes encompassed by HFpEF. As a result, HFpEF patients are still largely managed as a single clinical entity, despite clear evidence of distinct underlying mechanisms, clinical trajectories and prognoses3,4. HFpEF patients often present with multiple comorbidities, and the condition likely arises from complex interactions among systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, metabolic dysregulation, and aging4. These interrelated factors may give rise to biologically distinct subgroups within HFpEF, but this remains undetected by current clinical approaches, highlighting the urgent need for novel, multidimensional stratification strategies.

Recent advances in high-dimensional phenotyping (including proteomics, transcriptomics and imaging biomarkers) combined with unsupervised machine learning techniques, have enabled the discovery of hidden patterns within heterogeneous patient populations7,8. This data-driven methodology, known as phenomapping, is an appropriate approach to reclassify HFpEF into biologically and therapeutically meaningful subgroups. While prior retrospective or post-hoc studies have suggested that HFpEF comprises multiple sub-entities7,9,10, the resulting phenotypes often show substantial overlap and rely on limited discriminatory variables, reducing their clinical applicability8. Moreover, most of these studies have focused exclusively on a single category of heart failure (i.e., typically HFpEF as defined by a threshold on left ventricular ejection fraction) without considering the broader spectrum of heart failure presentations. Robust and transferable classification algorithms thus remain largely undeveloped. Lastly, the retrospective nature of most existing studies constrains their depth, as they are usually based on routine clinical datasets and lack the integration of more advanced molecular or imaging technologies.

The PACIFIC-Preserved study is a prospective, multidimensional phenotyping study specifically designed to address this knowledge gap11. By integrating clinical, imaging, proteomics, and functional data, the study aimed to identify mechanistically distinct subtypes of HF, and notably HFpEF. In this work, we establish a classifier-driven, biologically informed stratification of HFpEF, providing a framework for precision medicine in heart failure.

Results

Development cohort

A total of 167 patients were enrolled in the prospective multidimensional phenotyping study. Of these, 12 participants had missing data in at least one dimension, and the clustering analysis was performed in a development cohort of 155 patients (Fig. 1a). In line with the predefined 3:2:1 inclusion ratio, the development cohort included 78 patients with HFpEF (50%), 51 with HFrEF (33%) and 26 age- and sex-matched control subjects without known heart failure (17%). As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the clinical profile of HFpEF patients was consistent with expectations: they were slightly older, had a more balanced sex ratio than HFrEF, a higher prevalence of hypertension with elevated blood pressure, a slightly higher BMI, and more frequent atrial fibrillation at inclusion as compared to other groups. Despite the preserved LVEF, functional capacity was similarly impaired in HFpEF and HFrEF patients, as evidenced by comparable KCCQ (Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire) scores and 6 min walking distances (Supplementary Table 1). Routine laboratory assessments (Supplementary Table 2) showed that HFpEF patients had a significantly higher burden of kidney dysfunction, as indicated by a greater proportion in the high / very high KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) risk categories, elevated proteinuria and lower estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR).Fig. 1Clustering analysis of the PACIFIC-Preserved development cohort.a Flowchart summarizing the overall analysis pipeline from data integration to clustering and subgroup characterization. At the echographic level (Supplementary Table 2), HFpEF patients exhibited features of concentric hypertrophic remodeling with preservation of systolic contractile parameters, increased left atrial volume and tricuspid regurgitation velocity, consistent with a pathophysiological profile of diastolic dysfunction. Notably, 14 HFrEF patients showed an improvement in LVEF (> 40%) at the time of evaluation, despite having a prior documented LVEF < 40%. These individuals were accordingly classified as having heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF), but overall, the clinical profile of HFrEF patients was also consistent with expectations (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Clustering analysis

We applied multiview spectral clustering (MVSC) to integrate heterogeneous data types (here grouped into 5 domains: clinical factors, clinical scores and numerical variables, imaging, routine biology and high-throughput panels (Supplementary Table 3)) into a unified framework. The high-throughput panel encompasses the Olink® proteomic panel, which measured 5400 proteins on plasma samples (Explore HT panel). Unlike single-modality approaches, MVSC captures complementary information across the five predefined domains. The method was embedded within a consensus clustering pipeline incorporating row and column resampling and recursive feature elimination, enhancing stability and robustness (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Although the choice of an initial value of k = 3 clusters aligned with the pre-defined clinical categories (i.e., HFpEF, HFrEF and controls), the three-cluster solution proved suboptimal, offering limited distinction between HFpEF and HFrEF patients (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, the four-cluster solution (k = 4) yielded the most robust results across the different measures compared to the 3-, 5- and 6-cluster solutions, as determined by the lowest proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC), and the highest adjusted Rand index (ARI), indicating superior clustering stability and coherence with the preexisting classification (Fig. 1b–d).

These 4 groups were labeled PEF1, PEF2, REF, and Healthier (Fig. 1b). In this configuration, HFpEF patients were mainly divided into two distinct subgroups: PEF1 composed exclusively of HFpEF patients and representing approximately a third of HFpEF (n = 26/78, 33.3%), and PEF2, which included the majority of HFpEF cases (n = 43/78, 55.1%). Most of the HFrEF patients clustered separately in a distinct group (that we named REF), while the final cluster contained all control subjects along with several HF patients, primarily those with HFimpEF, suggesting a subgroup of comparatively “healthier” individuals. Interestingly, a minority of HFpEF patients (6/78; 7.6%) clustered with the Healthier group (Fig. 1e), suggesting phenotypic convergence with control subjects despite an initial clinical diagnosis of HFpEF. Of importance, a secondary clustering analysis restricted to the 78 HFpEF patients yielded a similar two-group structure, with the two clusters significantly aligned with the PEF1/PEF2 classification (Chi²(3) = 19.673, p = 0.0002), supporting the internal consistency of the clustering.

Signatures associated to clusters

As shown in Table 1, the clinical, functional, and therapeutic characteristics of the PEF1 and PEF2 phenogroups were largely similar, except for type 2 diabetes, which was more prevalent in PEF1 patients, and atrial fibrillation, which tended to be more frequent in PEF2 patients. In contrast, the Olink proteomics panel revealed marked differences between the PEF1 and the other phenogroups (Fig. 2a). Specifically, PEF1 patients exhibited a distinct proteomic signature characterized by the overexpression of multiple proteins compared to Healthier, REF and PEF2 phenogroups (Fig. 2a, b). Notably, 597 proteins were uniquely elevated in PEF1 (Fig. 2b), and these were predominantly associated with inflammatory markers (Supplementary Fig. 3). Compared to PEF2, the PEF1 phenogroup also demonstrated a pronounced overexpression of proteins (Fig. 2c). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 2d) highlighted a significant enrichment in pathways related to leukocyte activation, cytokine-receptor activation, immune regulation of lymphoid and myeloid cells, and neutrophil degranulation with a particular involvement of TNF receptor superfamily members (Fig. 2c, d). In addition, as one of the pathways (i.e., regulation of system process, top16) included cardiac and skeletal muscle contraction, we performed further protein-protein interaction enrichment analysis and found that the proteomic signature in the plasma of PEF1 patients was highly enriched in proteins associated with cardiac and skeletal muscle injury, (-log10(p) = 17.2, Fig. 2e). Concordantly, routine biological measurements (Table 2) indicated higher ultra-sensitive troponin levels in PEF1 patients. PEF1 patients also had a higher prevalence of renal dysfunction, as evidenced by lower eGFR, higher proteinuria levels, and a greater proportion of patients classified in the high / very high KDIGO risk categories compared to PEF2, as well as other phenogroups (Fig. 2f, g). Importantly, sensitivity clustering analyses excluding the Olink proteomic dataset resulted in a marked decline in clustering performance, particularly in the discrimination between PEF1 and PEF2 clusters. At the echocardiographic levels, intergroup differences were modest, with PEF1 patients exhibiting higher LV mass, and slightly increased LVEF and stroke volumes compared to PEF2 patients (Table 2). Collectively, this indicates that the proteomic layer provides critical biological resolution for distinguishing PEF1 from PEF2 patients, beyond what is captured by conventional clinical, imaging or routine biological markers, including standard indicators of renal function.

In contrast, PEF2 patients showed overexpression of a very limited number of proteins when compared to PEF1, with two proteins (UMOD -uromodulin- and NELL1 -neural EGFL like 1-), showing the highest fold changes and being associated with relatively preserved kidney function. The proteins unique to PEF2 were significantly associated with heart and muscle structure development on one hand, and with cytoskeletal organization, centrosome cycle and cell proliferation on the other hand (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, enrichment in some key endothelial signaling pathways, such as the VEGF signaling, suggested the presence of a more predominant endothelial stress and vascular remodeling in PEF2. This pattern differentiates PEF2 from the PEF1 phenogroup, while bringing it closer to HFrEF, as evidenced by the very limited proteomic differences between PEF2 and HFrEF (Fig. 2a).

Supervised learning and classification in the development and validation cohort

We next trained XG-Boost classifiers to identify key variables discriminating either between the four phenogroups identified through unsupervised clustering (i.e., PEF1, PEF2, REF, Healthier) or specifically between the two major HFpEF subgroups (i.e., PEF1 and PEF2). Feature selection was performed using a recursive feature elimination (RFE) loop (Fig. 3a), resulting in a final set of 36 clinical, echocardiographic and biological variables (Fig. 3b) that maximized prediction performance. The resulting multi-class classifier attained an overall F1-score of 0.90 for prediction of the four phenogroups (Supplementary Fig. 5a). When input features were restricted to Olink proteomic markers alone, classification performance was modestly reduced (F1-score = 0.81) but remained effective for discriminating PEF1 and PEF2 while classification in the REF group was more imprecise (Fig. 3a, c and Supplementary Fig. 5b). In contrast, performance for discriminating between PEF1 and PEF2 declined further when Olink proteomics data were excluded from the model (Supplementary Fig. 5c), underscoring the added discriminatory value of proteomics beyond conventional clinical, biological, and imaging markers. Consistent with findings from the GSEA results, top-ranked features in the proteomics-only classifier included several inflammatory markers, notably members of the TNF receptor superfamily (Fig. 3c).

Finally, a binary classifier trained to distinguish between the PEF1 and PEF2 phenogroups achieved even higher predictive performance, with a final F1-score of 0.99 (Fig. 3a, d, e). Although this model was initially trained on the full PACIFIC dataset (> 6200 variables), the final classifier retained only 8 features, all of which were Olink proteins (i.e., THBD, TNFRSF21, NECAB2, BSG, TGFBR2, SCARB2, TGOLN2, IGFBP6). Notably, markers of renal function (e.g., eGFR, proteinuria) were included in the initial feature pool but were not selected during the RFE process, indicating limited incremental value for phenogroup discrimination. In contrast, the final set of proteomic features provided robust discriminatory power, suggesting that the distinction between these two HFpEF subtypes is best captured by a minimal yet highly informative panel of proteomic markers.

Application of the PACIFIC cluster membership prediction model to the MEDIA-DHF Cohort

To expand and validate our findings in an independent population, we derived a custom classifier from the PACIFIC-Preserved cohort to be applied to the MEDIA-DHF (Metabolic road to diastolic heart failure) validation cohort12. MEDIA-DHF included 456 HFpEF patients meeting PACIFIC inclusion criteria with available Olink proteomic data. Comparison of the two datasets identified 499 shared variables, encompassing most of the key proteomic markers highlighted in our discovery analysis. The classifier was then trained using this harmonized dataset of 499 variables and achieved high predictive performance, with a peak F1-score of 0.87 after 61 RFE iterations, yielding an optimized final model based on only 12 variables, including 6 Olink biomarkers (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

The optimized classifier was applied to the MEDIA-DHF patients and showed that 148 (32.4%) were classified as PEF1 subjects, 242 (53.1%) as PEF2, 49 (10.7%) as Healthier and 17 (3.7%) as REF, consistent with the proportions observed in the PACIFIC-Preserved cohort. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, the profile of PEF1 subjects in MEDIA-DHF was comparable to the one found in the original PACIFIC-Preserved analysis, with more prevalent impaired kidney function as estimated by CKD-EPI, more prevalent diabetes, and higher NT-proBNP levels, even though these latter variables were not part of the set of 12 variables used by the classifier. Interestingly, the 17 REF patients had the lowest left-ventricular ejection fractions and blood pressures of all 4 groups, while the Healthier patients had the lowest levels of NT-proBNP.

Prognostic value of the phenogroups in the development and validation cohorts

Cardiovascular outcomes (defined as death, cardiovascular or HF-related hospitalizations) were evaluated in both the PACIFIC development cohort and the MEDIA-DHF validation cohort. In both cohorts, patients assigned to the PEF1 phenogroup exhibited a significantly worse prognosis compared to other HF phenogroups (Fig. 4a–f and Supplementary Table 5). This prognostic impact was particularly evident in the MEDIA-DHF validation cohort, which provided greater statistical power to detect outcome differences (Fig. 4b–f). In this validation cohort, cox proportional hazard models demonstrated that patients in the PEF1 phenogroup had a significantly higher risk of death or heart failure hospitalization as compared to those in the PEF2 cluster (as a reference). Specifically, the hazard ratio (HR) adjusted on age and sex for the composite endpoint was 3.26 (95% CI: 1.57 − 6.77, p = 0.002). Importantly, this association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex and the NT-proBNP levels, with an adjusted HR of 2.27 (95% CI: 1.04 − 4.94, p = 0.039). Discussion

In this prospective, deeply phenotyped cohort of patients with HFpEF, we applied unsupervised machine learning to multimodal clinical, imaging, and proteomic data to uncover novel biologically distinct disease subtypes. This approach revealed two major HFpEF phenogroups (PEF1 and PEF2) that could not be readily differentiated using conventional clinical and echocardiographic parameters, yet exhibited striking differences in proteomic signatures, pathophysiological profiles, and clinical outcomes. From a translational perspective, the PACIFIC algorithm enables robust phenogroup prediction using a relatively limited set of proteomic biomarkers, while maintaining prognostic discrimination in an independent European validation cohort (MEDIA-DHF)12.

While traditional classification based on LVEF and clinical symptoms portrays HFpEF as a single entity, our study shows that it encompasses at least two biologically distinct disease processes, which challenges the view of HFpEF as a unified clinical syndrome. These hidden layers of disease biology were best revealed through molecular profiling, underscoring the value of high-dimensional proteomics in resolving HFpEF heterogeneity.

The PEF1 subgroup comprised approximately one-third of HFpEF patients and was distinguished by a systemic inflammatory state, TNF receptor activation, and biomarkers indicative of cardio-skeletal muscle injury and renal dysfunction. Interestingly, findings from the BIOSTAT study, which compared 92 circulating biomarkers across HFrEF and HFpEF populations, demonstrated that inflammatory and extracellular matrix–related biomarkers were more specific to HFpEF, whereas markers of cellular proliferation and metabolism predominated in HFrEF13. Our analysis builds upon this by delineating a distinct HFpEF subtype (PEF1) particularly enriched in inflammatory and cardio-renal signatures. Notably, PEF1 patients exhibited a higher prevalence of renal dysfunction and experienced significantly worse outcomes, including a 2.27-fold increased risk of death or heart failure hospitalization compared to PEF2, even after adjusting for established prognostic markers such as NT-proBNP. However, this difference was attenuated after adjustment for renal function, a well-established predictor of outcomes in HFpEF14. While moderate reductions in eGFR likely contribute to the elevated risk and proteomic changes in PEF1, the proteomic signature extends beyond what can be attributed to renal dysfunction alone. Only a minority of the PEF1-associated proteins overlap with previously reported CKD-related inflammatory profiles15,16, and classical renal function markers were not retained in supervised models discriminating PEF1 from PEF2. Moreover, removal of proteomic variables markedly reduced clustering performance for PEF1 versus PEF2, underscoring the added biological resolution afforded by the proteomic data in discriminating HFpEF subgroups. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that PEF1 phenotype integrates renal dysfunction within a broader, systemic cardio-inflammatory process, rather than being solely driven by kidney impairment. Further studies directly comparing CKD- and HFpEF-associated proteomic signatures will be essential to delineate shared versus disease-specific mechanisms.

In contrast, PEF2 encompassed over half of HFpEF patients and represented a biologically distinct HFpEF subtype, with proteomic evidence of endothelial stress, relatively preserved bio-clinical parameters, and a pathophysiological profile more closely resembling that of HFrEF patients. While appearing more heterogeneous, our current sample size limited the ability to resolve finer substructure within the PEF2 phenogroup, an important question for future investigation.

These phenogroups diverge substantially from those identified in previous phenomapping studies7,8,10, likely due to the prospective deep phenotyping design of the PACIFIC-Preserved study, which enabled a more comprehensive and systematic evaluation of patients. Prior studies, often based on retrospective datasets or medico-administrative records, have reported phenotypic clustering in HFpEF, largely driven by clinical features such as age, obesity or sex7,8,10. A more recent analysis proposed the existence of an inflammatory HFpEF subgroup, but its identification was limited to high-sensitivity CRP measurements9. In contrast, a central insight of our study is that the apparent clinical uniformity of HFpEF conceals a profound biological divergence. Approximately 600 proteins were uniquely overexpressed in PEF1, while conventional clinical or imaging parameters offered limited discriminatory value. This finding highlights the limitations of current HFpEF classifications that rely mainly on clinical factors and supports a shift toward biologically informed subtyping. The robustness of our clustering and classification was first ensured at the methodological level. To mitigate the inherent sensitivity of clustering to variable selection and modeling choices, we implemented a multi-step, consensus-based framework incorporating extensive resampling and recursive feature elimination. This strategy was specifically designed to ensure that the final phenogroups reflected robust and reproducible biological signals, rather than artifacts of specific variable sets or clustering parameters. In addition, our results were replicated in an independent external validation cohort (MEDIA-DHF)12, where a simplified classifier (driven predominantly by six proteomic markers) also achieved strong predictive performance.

These results underscore the feasibility of future HFpEF stratification approaches that combine conventional clinical and imaging data, enriched by a targeted panel of key proteins associated with the high-risk PEF1 phenotype. Indeed, the classifier developed here relies on a limited set of proteins, making implementation via a custom Olink panel a feasible next step. Similar target panels have been successfully applied in other disorders16,17. In addition, the code used in our study will be openly shared, facilitating external validation and potential integration into other HFpEF cohorts and clinical studies.

Our results extend prior observations on HFpEF heterogeneity by demonstrating that a biologically defined inflammatory and cardio-renal phenotype exists within the broader syndrome18,19. This has important therapeutic implications. First, PEF1 may represent a subgroup that benefits from anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory interventions, strategies that have thus far yielded neutral results in unselected HFpEF populations20. Second, proteomic-informed classifiers such as the one developed here may enable better patient stratification in future clinical trials, improving power and therapeutic targeting. Whether the clinical benefit of current HFpEF therapies (i.e., SGLT2 inhibitors21, MRAs22 or GLP1 receptor agonists23,24) is modulated by underlying biological subtypes like PEF1 and PEF2 remains an open and critical question. Prospective studies and post hoc trial analyses incorporating molecular phenotyping will be essential to clarify this point. It is worth emphasizing that while differences in outcomes between phenogroups offer supportive evidence of clinical relevance, they should not be viewed as the primary validation criterion for this classification. Our objective was to identify biologically distinct HFpEF subtypes, which can serve as a foundation for future precision medicine strategies. In a syndrome as heterogeneous and slowly progressive as HFpEF, biologically meaningful subtypes may exist even in the absence of immediate prognostic divergence. The ultimate value of our classification lies in its potential to inform treatment responsiveness, an important hypothesis for further investigation.

Our study has limitations. While the depth of proteomic profiling and phenotypic characterization were clear strength, the overall cohort size was moderate. At the time of study design, no standard guidelines were available for estimating sample size in clustering analyses. However, recent work suggests that subgroups of 20–30 participants with a strong effect size, such as those observed in our study, are sufficient25. Nonetheless, the limited sample size may have constrained our ability to identify finer substructure within the broader HFpEF population or to delineate cluster boundaries more precisely. In addition, our study focused on stable outpatients recruited in Western Europe. Geographical and demographic variations in HFpEF phenotypes are well recognized26, and external validation in larger and more diverse populations is warranted. Moreover, causality cannot be inferred from the observed associations. While the PEF1 phenogroup exhibited a very distinct profile marked by cardiac, skeletal muscle and kidney injury alongside a pro-inflammatory proteomic signature, the upstream triggers of these abnormalities remain undefined. Further mechanistic investigations in experimental models are warranted to clarify these pathophysiological links. As our analysis focused on clinical, imaging, routine laboratory data and proteomics, other omics approaches (such as metabolomics) were not available for this cohort at the time of analysis. Whether integrating additional omics data could further enhance HFpEF phenotypic classification remains an important question for future investigation. In addition, the Olink platform provides only relative quantification of protein abundance. The development of specific proteomic measurements (e.g., by LC-MS or targeted assays) will now be needed to directly confirm protein abundance changes and strengthen biological interpretation and clinical translation of the identified phenogroups.

In conclusion, proteomic profiling combined with unsupervised machine learning revealed two biologically and prognostically divergent subtypes within HFpEF, a syndrome that appears clinically unified but molecularly heterogeneous. The inflammatory, cardio-renal PEF1 phenotype represents a high-risk group that may benefit from targeted therapies. Our developed algorithm will be shared with others as an online application in order to support the use of biologically informed patient stratification to guide personalized approaches in HFpEF management and clinical trial design.

Methods

Ethics statement

The PACIFIC-PRESERVED clinical study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT04189029) and has been approved by the Ethics Committee (CPP Rouen 19.01178.044829) and the Agence nationale de sécurité du médicament et des produits de santé (ANSM; 2019-A01795-52). All patients provided written informed consent.

The MEDIA-DHF study protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02446327) complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committees (CPP Est III 12-03-03) and the Agence nationale de sécurité du médicament et des produits de santé (ANSM; 2012-A00097-36). All patients provided written informed consent. The analyses reported in the present study were conducted within the scope of the original ethics committee approvals, and participant informed consent obtained for the parent cohort. Accordingly, no additional ethics approval was required for this study. Both study protocols can be accessed upon request.

Study design and population

Development cohort

Details of the rationale and design of the PACIFIC-Preserved prospective study were previously published11. Briefly, PACIFIC-Preserved is a prospective, multicenter observational cohort study designed to perform multidimensional deep phenotyping of patients with HFpEF (defined by LVEF ≥ 50%), patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (LVEF ≤ 40%) and subjects without overt heart failure. The study was conducted in 6 different hospitals in the Paris area in France. Subjects without HF were age- (in the decade) and sex-matched to patients with HFpEF, with the objective of a final ratio of 3 HFpEF:2 HFrEF:1 without HF. HFpEF was defined according to the 2016 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for heart failure27, including a diagnosis of symptomatic congestive HF ≥ 3 months and a hospitalization for HF in the last 30 months (with hospital discharge for at least 2 months), and BNP ≥ 100 pg/mL or NT-proBNP ≥ 300 pg/mL. Patients with a history of systolic dysfunction with proven LVEF reduction (≤ 40%) and LVEF improvement (≥ 50%) known before inclusion were not allowed to participate. However, some HFrEF patients showed improvement of LVEF at the time of evaluation and were classified as HFimpEF, in line with current guidelines. Subjects without HF were allowed to present hypertension grade 1 (treated or not), and/or dyslipidemia.

Patient enrollment began on December 9, 2019, upon receipt of all regulatory approvals and ended on December 31, 2023. At the time of study design, no validated tools were available to estimate sample size requirements for clustering analyses. The sample size was therefore determined empirically, based on recruitment feasibility and the objective to identify clusters comprising at least 20–30 participants, an approach that has since been supported as adequate in the presence of strong effect sizes25. The study protocol was approved to include up to 300 HFpEF patients, although final enrollment was limited by external factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic.

The study employed an extensive multimodal data collection strategy during a 1-day hospital stay, integrating clinical, functional, biological, and imaging domains. Participants underwent comprehensive phenotyping, including physical performance tests (e.g., 6 min walk test, hand grip), cardiac imaging (echocardiography, cardiac MRI, CT-scan), and patient-reported outcomes (e.g., KCCQ-12, SF-36). Blood sampling was enriched to perform routine biomarkers, and proteomics based on the Olink® Explore-HT (Olink proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) panel, which is a high-throughput, antibody-based proteomics technology that employs proximity extension assay (PEA) combined with next-generation sequencing (NGS) readout. The Explore-HT panel enables the simultaneous relative quantification of ~ 5400 proteins from a minimal plasma volume (2 µL). Protein expression values are reported as normalized protein expression on a log2 scale, which reflects relative abundance across samples. Internal and external controls are included to ensure assay performance and quality according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, myocardial shear wave elastography, non-invasive measurement of arterial stiffness were performed in a subset of included patients and were not considered for the primary analysis reported here. A specific ontology framework was developed to foster interoperability of these multimodal cardiac phenomapping datasets28.

Validation cohort

The MEDIA-DHF (Metabolic road to diastolic heart failure) cohort is a multicenter multinational (10 centers in France, Netherlands, UK, Belgium, Spain, Italy, Ireland, Hungary), observational study that enrolled 626 patients between 2012 and 201412. Patients were included following a diagnosis of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction using the 2007 European Society of Cardiology consensus recommendations. In the present analysis, only patients with available Olink biomarkers (N = 456, performed on target 96 panels CVDII, CVDIII, Inflammation, and organ damage) were included, corresponding to patients recently discharged after admission for an acute HF episode (< 60 days), or ambulatory patients with chronic disease (no patients with acute decompensation). Key inclusion criteria were (1) signs and symptoms of HF with a preserved (ie, ≥ 50%) left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction and an LV end-diastolic volume index < 97 mL/m2 and (2) elevated serum concentration of brain natriuretic peptide > 100 pg/mL or NT- proBNP (N-terminal pro- B-type natriuretic peptide) > 300 pg/mL. The primary outcome was time to the composite of cardiovascular death (CVD) or first HF hospitalization. Follow-up assessments were performed at 3, 6, and 12 months after inclusion.

Clustering analysis

Missing values were imputed with the missForest algorithm29, separately within the 3 inclusion groups. We then assembled the 5 views, with preestablished variable sets for numerical clinical data, clinical factors, biology, echography and CT-scan and a subset of the Olink panel. To select Olink features of interest, we used linear models with age as a covariate to discard 18 proteins showing a strong main effect of sex. We then retained proteins showing a significant association, after false-discovery-rate correction, with at least one of the following clinically relevant factors: inclusion group (q < 0.01), grade of chronic kidney disease (q < 0.001), type of diabetes (q < 0.01), or high blood pressure (q < 0.05). Because the strength of proteomic associations differed substantially across these factors, particularly for chronic kidney disease, which can exert a strong influence on the circulating proteome, different significance thresholds were applied to avoid overrepresentation of proteins associated with a single clinical condition and to preserve a balanced representation of proteins across major axes of clinical heterogeneity. This procedure yielded 637 proteins.

Continuous variables were trimmed for outliers (replacing extreme values by the next value within the acceptable range, defined with boxplot statistics as 3 times the inter-quartile range from the median), adjusted linearly for age and sex and rescaled, with either a standardization or a min-max transform. Categorical variables from all modalities were binarized and grouped in a single view. Treatments were included as categorical features during both the clustering step and the supervised training step. For the clustering analysis, we relied on a multiview spectral clustering algorithm (https://dev.b2s.club/b2slab/multiview), as implemented in the multiview R package30.

In order to evaluate the stability of the clustering solutions at different numbers of classes, from K = 3 to K = 6, we used a meta-consensus clustering procedure, where we combined automated variable selection (including both continuous and categorical variables) with repeated resampling on both the samples and the columns of each view. We performed 1,000 resamplings per iteration and with a sampling rate of 0.8 to reduce the influence of any single variable or subject. Resampling columns as part of the Monti algorithm31 also enabled us to implement an automatic selection of the features that improve the stability of the clustering, as measured by the Proportion of Ambiguous Clusterings (PAC)32. At each iteration, we computed the consensus matrices in the presence or absence of each feature (20% of the clusterings) and computed the differences in PAC scores to measure their influence on the quality of the clustering. Within each view, after ranking the features, we set the sampling probability of the 5% worst-performing features of the view to zero, excluding them from the analysis, before starting a new iteration. We applied 21 iterations of this Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) loop, with 1024 different samples being drawn at each step. We then obtained a final partition by hierarchical clustering (average linkage) of the consensus matrix at the RFE iteration, which yielded the lowest PAC. Because of the random resampling, however, many different feature elimination paths are possible, resulting in a variability of the clustering solutions around a similar pattern, depending on differences in terms of the retained variables. Besides, the degree of improvement of the PAC achieved by the RFE also varies when running the procedure several times, with some runs performing better than others. To compute a representative final clustering, for each K, we ran the procedure 200 times and averaged the best 41 runs through a hierarchical clustering of the obtained “metaconsensus matrix” (average linkage). We also computed the PAC scores of the meta-consensus matrices as well as adjusted Rand indices, symmetric and asymmetric, with the initial 3-group classification. The Rand index measures the degree of agreement between two partitions, whereas the asymmetric Rand index measures the degree of inclusion of a partition into a preexisting partition with a lower number of classes. Its value gets closer to 1.0 when a class is split into two new classes at a higher value of K, which is our hypothesis for the HFpEF group.

Supervised learning

To find relevant features that discriminate between the 4 classes from the unsupervised classification analysis, we implemented an iterative feature reduction pipeline by the XGBoost algorithm and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to quantify feature importance.

We entered all available PACIFIC-Preserved variables (6000+ variables) in a recursive feature elimination (RFE) loop. A hyperparameter search for XGBoost parameters was done using the hyperopt software library (1,000 trials) and using the Tree-structured Parzen estimator. Hyperparameters are: colsample_bylevel, colsample_bynode, colsample_bytree, gamma, learning_rate, max_depth, n_estimators, reg_alpha, reg_lambda, smote_num.

When the set of classifiers for an RFE iteration is done, we compute the average F1-score, average the importances given by the SHAP values for each variable. We then keep either the top 90% variables or the ones with an importance ranking over the random variable, or remove the least important variable. The SMOTENC algorithm was used to address class imbalance between the inclusion groups (i.e., HFrEF, HFpEF, no HF). Prediction performances were made with LeaveOneOut cross- validation, precluding dispersion estimates.

We let the procedure exhaust all variables and then selected the iteration that provided the best F1-score with the fewest variables. Note that re-optimizing the hyperparameters of the classifier ensemble on the reduced set of variables offered a slight increase in performance (F1-score of 0.93 compared with 0.90 using the initial parameters, which were optimized using all the variables).

We used a similar approach to design a custom classifier for the validation cohort, albeit starting with only those 499 variables that MEDIA-DHF and PACIFIC-Preserved had in common. This included the overlap between the different Olink proteomic panels that were used in the two studies. We trained the MEDIA-DHF classifier ensemble on the PACIFIC-Preserved participants, for whom a class label was available. Because of the semiquantitative nature of Olink biomarkers, the relative positions of the distributions of the biomarkers in PACIFIC and MEDIA were different. To correct this bias, we rescaled each of the selected Olink biomarkers using a linear model (quantile-quantile regression), effectively aligning the Olink biomarker distributions of MEDIA-DHF on those of only the HFpEF subpopulation of PACIFIC-Preserved, since there were no control participants or HFrEF patients in MEDIA-DHF.

Statistical analysis

To interpret the data-driven patient classification scheme, we computed all pairwise comparisons between the 4 clusters for key clinical variables. We log-transformed variables with a skewness above 1.5. For numerical variables, we used linear models with age and sex as covariates, after trimming outliers (4 standard-deviations away from the mean). For categorical variables, we relied on Fisher exact tests for 2-level factors, and Chi-squared tests for factors with more levels. For count data (number of hospitalizations and rounded weekly number of alcohol units), we used a negative-binomial Generalized Linear Model with sex and age as covariates. For these latter analyses, we did not use missing data imputation procedures and did not adjust p-values for multiple comparisons, as the PEF1 versus PEF2 contrast clearly stood out as the comparison of interest.

For Olink differential expression analysis, we computed Wilcoxon tests between all pairs of clusters, for all 5416 proteins, with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons (q < 0.05). We also computed the log2 fold-changes, using the medians of each group, and produced Volcano plots for each of the 6 contrasts. To identify uniquely differentially expressed proteins in each of the 3 patient groups, we computed set differences and intersections between significant-protein names found in PEF1, PEF2 and REF, using the Healthier group as reference. Only the proteins that were differentially expressed in each particular group were entered for functional enrichment analysis in the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) procedure (Metascape), which consisted of statistical overrepresentation tests for all the Gene Ontology, KEGG and Reactome gene sets, among others, with Benjamini-Hochberg p-value correction.

For the survival analyses in the PACIFIC and MEDIA-DHF cohorts, we used Cox proportional hazards models to test the effect of cluster membership on survival to different event combinations (death or cardiology ward admission, death or hospital admission for heart failure, death), with different sets of confounding covariates (age and sex, with or without log-transformed NT-proBNP levels). We used PEF2 as the reference level of the cluster factor, to which the 3 other clusters, including PEF1, were contrasted.
