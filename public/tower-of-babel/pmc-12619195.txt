Quality of life in older adults (aged 60–80 years) after lung cancer surgery: a longitudinal observational study
Authors: Rongjia Lin, Genmiao Yu, Chunmei Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAccurate health-related quality of life (HRQOL) data after video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) are lacking for older patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We aimed to characterize the trajectories of symptom burden and functional interference and identify predictors of delayed recovery.MethodsIn this longitudinal study, older adults (aged 60–80 years) with NSCLC undergoing VATS were enrolled. The MD Anderson Symptom Inventory for Lung Cancer (MDASI-LC) was used to assess symptom burden and functional interference at preoperative, discharge, and 7 days, 1, 2, 3, and 6 months after discharge.ResultsComplete prospective data were available for 250 of 254 consecutive patients (98.4% completion rate). The cohort had a median age of 68 years (IQR: 64–71) and comprised 40.9% men. The four most prevalent symptom items were fatigue, pain, shortness of breath, and coughing, while the four most prevalent interference items were general activity, work (including housework), walking, and enjoyment of life. By 6 months after discharge, 68.8% of patients overall returned to preoperative levels (symptoms: 66.9–83.5%; interference: 62.6–66.5%). Multivariable analysis identified the extent of VATS resection (wedge → segmentectomy → lobectomy)as an independent predictor of delayed recovery (ORs per increment:1.868–2.109, 95% CIs:1.260–3.157).ConclusionThe majority of older adults’ symptom burden and functional interference returned to preoperative levels 6 months after discharge. The extent of VATS resection was independently associated with delayed recovery. These findings advocate for the preoperative integration of recovery patterns into surgical strategy selection, particularly when weighing oncological radicality against geriatric patients’ physiological reserves.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02448-x.

Introduction

The number of older lung cancer patients (aged 60–80 years) is rising rapidly, yet the optimal surgical approaches and their outcomes remain inadequately investigated [1, 2]. For older patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the goal of surgery should be not only to treat malignancy but also to preserve health-related quality of life (HRQOL).

Surgeons have increasingly recognized that some traditional oncologic outcomes (progression-free survival, disease-free survival, etc.) have a limited value for older patients whose primary target is to survive the surgery and enjoy a satisfactory postoperative HRQOL [3].

This clinical focus on HRQOL is underscored by compelling demographic and physiological realities. Globally, the population aged 60 years and older is expanding rapidly, driving a concomitant increase in the number of older adults diagnosed with lung cancer [2, 4]. Furthermore, age-related respiratory decline, which features loss of pulmonary function, decreased chest wall compliance, respiratory muscle atrophy, and chronic low-grade inflammation, predisposes older adults to postoperative complications [5–8]. HRQOL assessments have been used as an outcome measure in surgical oncology for many years and play a valuable role in clinical decision making [9, 10]. However, surgery has a considerable detrimental effect on HRQOL, clinical characterization and postoperative recovery in older patients with NSCLC have been poorly documented.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether HRQOL in older patients with NSCLC returns to preoperative (baseline) levels following video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). We also sought to identify risk factors associated with delayed recovery of postoperative HRQOL. The MD Anderson Symptom Inventory for Lung Cancer (MDASI-LC) was selected as the instrument for assessment and was administered over a 6-month follow-up period [11, 12].

Methods

Subjects

This longitudinal study was performed between October 2022 and February 2024 at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Fujian Provincial Hospital.

Patient eligibility criteria included: (1) age 60–80 years, (2) underwent VATS, and (3) postoperative pathological confirmation of NSCLC with TNM stage I–IIIa. Exclusion criteria were: (1) bilateral pulmonary surgery or pneumonectomy, (2) conversion to open thoracotomy or blood loss > 1000 mL, (3) planned intensive care unit (ICU) admission post-surgery, (4) preoperative chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy, or (5) refusal to participate or study withdrawal.

Staging followed the eighth edition the Union for International Cancer Control /American Joint Committee on Cancer lung cancer classification guidelines [13]. Postoperative complications were documented using the Clavien-Dindo classification (CD) [14].

Surgical procedures

Single utility port VATS was performed and the appropriate N1 and N2 lymph node stations were sampled or dissected [15, 16]. Lymph node dissection included N1 and N2 nodes with a minimum of three N2 stations sampled or completely dissected [17]. Patients with adenocarcinoma in situ or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma did not need lymph node dissection or sampling [18, 19].

Outcome assessment

The assessment was based on a psychometrically validated assessment. The MDASI-LC includes 16 symptom burden items (13 core symptoms and 3 symptoms specific to lung cancer) and 6 functional interference items [11, 12]. Sixteen symptom items are assigned a severity grade. Each symptom item is rated on an 11-point scale, with 0 = “not present” and 10 = “as bad as you can imagine” in the previous 24 h. Six interference items that measure symptom interference with daily life. Each interference item is rated on an 11-point scale, with 0 = “did not interfere” and 10 = “interfered completely” in the previous 24 h. Patients completed the MDASI-LC at study entry (preoperative), at discharge and at multiple timepoints during the 6 months after discharge (Fig. 1).

Definition of recovery to preoperative (baseline) level, recovered and delayed recovery

According to the MDASI-LC, we defined “recovery to preoperative (baseline) level” as either (1) a report of MDASI-LC symptoms or interference scores returning to or below the preoperative levels at 2 consecutive measurements (excluding patients with preoperative scores ≥ 3); or (2) for patients with preoperative scores ≥ 3, achievement of scores < 3 at 2 consecutive measurements [20, 21]. Median and mean recovery time and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis.

In addition, “recovered” was defined as achieving recovery to preoperative levels for all four most-severe symptoms and four most-severe interference items. Delayed recovery was operationally defined as failure to meet the criteria for “recovered” status during the 6-month follow-up after discharge.

Data collection and management

The MDASI-LC was administered electronically using a network database for management (jinshuju, a data collection and management platform developed by MININGLAMP TECHNOLOGY, Beijing, China, https://jinshuju.net/). During hospitalization, research nurses interviewed patients and entered responses directly into the electronic MDASI-LC form on the jinshuju platform. Follow-up assessments at 7 days and 1, 2, 3, and 6 months after discharge were conducted via telephone or WeChat interviews using the same electronic data capture method.

Baseline data extracted from the hospital information system (HIS) were stored locally in Excel files accessible only to authorized researchers. De-identified data (linking hospital admission IDs to MDASI-LC responses) were processed on the jinshuju platform without collecting personal identifiers. Data integrity was ensured by automated range checks (0–10) during data entry, physician review of MDASI-LC data to flag outliers, and independent cross-checking of HIS data by one nurse and one physician.

Statistical analysis

All values represent the mean (standard deviation, SD), median (interquartile range, IQR) or number (percentage), unless stated otherwise. Multilevel mixed-effects regression models were utilized to analyze longitudinal changes in MDASI-LC scores. Time was modeled as a categorical variable with seven distinct levels: preoperative, at discharge, and 7 days, 1, 2, 3 and 6 months after discharge. Least-squares means with 95% CIs were computed via marginal predictions to estimate adjusted mean differences in symptom and interference scores across time points. Pairwise contrasts between all postoperative intervals and preoperative levels were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted p values to control the family-wise error rate. All models incorporated subject-specific random intercepts to account for individual heterogeneity and were fitted with restricted maximum likelihood estimation.

Variables were first compared between the recovered and delayed recovery groups using between-group comparisons: χ2 tests for categorical variables, t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and the Cochran-Armitage trend test for ordinal variables. Variables with p < 0.20 in these analyses were selected as candidates for inclusion in the multivariable model. The multivariable logistic regression was then performed using the forced-entry (enter) method to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses addressed missing data (patients who were lost to follow-up) under missing not at random (MNAR) assumptions: all patients lost to follow-up to opposing outcome groups (recovered or delayed recovery groups) under two extreme scenarios. The ranges of ORs and 95% CIs across these scenarios were reported to evaluate the robustness of the observed associations.

All comparisons were 2-sided, and differences with p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were carried out using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorpLP, College Station, TX).

Results

Characteristics of patients

Among the 271 patients who consented to participate in the study, 254 (254/271, 93.7%) met the criteria for study entry, and 250 (250/254, 98.4%) patients completed the MDASI-LC or reached postoperative recovery for the 6-month study; 4 patients withdrew during the follow-up period (2 at 1 month, 1 at 2 months and 1 at 3 months), because they refused to answer the survey (n = 4). 3 patients were hospitalized for serious complications (1 for massive pleural effusion at 1 month, 1 for bronchopleural fistula at 2 months and 1 for haemoptysis at 6 months), 1 patient underwent repeated bronchoscopic treatment for bronchial stenosis during 2 months after discharge. Additionally, only 1 patient died at 1 month due to pyothorax with stroke (Fig. 1). Therefore, there 5 patients were classified as failing to reach postoperative recovery (delayed recovery).

There were 104 men (40.9%), and median age was 68 years (IQR: 64–71 years). Most patients (94.5%, 240/254) were early-stage NSCLC (TNM stage I). All procedures were performed using VATS, with lobectomy performed in 45.3% (115/254) of patients. The clinical characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1.

Prevalence and severity of symptom burden and functional interference on day 7 after discharge

The four most symptom burden items and four most functional interference items were identified by highest prevalence (score ≥ 1) on day 7 after discharge (Table S1). For symptom burden, the four most prevalent and severe were pain (prevalence: 98.0%; severity: mean scores ± SD, 2.89 ± 1.16), fatigue (91.7%; 2.24 ± 1.38), shortness of breath (93.3%; 2.37 ± 1.61), and coughing (92.5%; 2.69 ± 1.68). Similarly, functional interference revealed aligned hierarchies, the most four prevalence and severe were activity (96.9%; 2.60 ± 1.15), work (including housework) (86.2%; 2.76 ± 1.34), walking (97.6%; 2.55 ± 1.24), and enjoyment of life (90.2%; 2.72 ± 1.56).

Longitudinal profiles of major symptom burden and functional interference items according to MDASI-LC

Three months after discharge, 54.5% (50.4–73.2% of four symptom items and 48.0–50.4% of four interference items) of patients returned to preoperative levels. Six months after discharge, 68.8% (66.9–83.5% of four symptom items and 62.6–66.5% of four interference items) of patients returned to preoperative levels (Fig. 2A). Data showed that 6 months after discharge, the majority of patients reported symptom burden and functional interference were improved according to MDASI-LC compared with their postoperative levels (evaluation at discharge) (Fig. 2B-C) (Table S2 and S3).

Linear mixed-effect model, taking account of variability for individual patients, showed an effect of time over MDASI-LC scores (P value all < 0.0001). Marginal predictions to estimate adjusted mean differences in symptom and interference scores across time points, on the basis of the of the MDASI-LC, showed an improvement over time. Figure 2 (B-C), Table S2 and S3 showed that mean MDASI-LC symptom and interference scores increased rapidly after surgery, peaking at discharge, and then returned to preoperative levels over time.

The mean scores of the four symptom items ranged from 0.05 to 0.14 at preoperative and 3.08 to 3.93 at discharge, with mean scores 0.44 to 1.19 at 3 months (p values all < 0.001 for post hoc comparison among preoperative vs. 3 months). The mean scores of the four symptom items at 6 months ranged from 0.22 to 0.65. Pain and fatigue showed a significant difference compared to preoperative levels (p < 0.001 and p = 0.024 for post hoc), while all other symptoms were non-significant (p = 1.000 for post hoc).

Similar trajectories were recorded when analyzing the four interference items of MDASI-LC. Mean scores of the four interference items ranged from 0.16 to 0.19 at preoperative and 3.25 to 3.78 at discharge, versus 0.92 to 1.25 and 0.60 to 0.77 at 3 and 6 months (p values all < 0.001 for post hoc comparison among preoperative vs. 3 months and 6 months).

Risk factor analysis for delayed recovery of postoperative symptom burden and functional interference

In the analysis of 250 patients with complete follow-up data (4 lost to follow-up), we performed sensitivity analyses to address potential attrition bias by reclassifying these patients under two extreme scenarios: assuming all 4 patients had either recovered (Scenario 1) or experienced delayed recovery (Scenario 2).

Intergroup analyses revealed differential predictors of delayed recovery: age (p = 0.018–0.023) and anesthesia duration (p = 0.013–0.051) showed statistical significance, whereas body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.127–0.203) and diabetes mellitus (p = 0.061–0.100) approached but did not reach significance. Notably, the extent of VATS resection (wedge → segmentectomy → lobectomy) demonstrated a robust ordinal association via Cochran-Armitage trend test (p < 0.0001–0.0006), with escalating resection magnitude conferring progressive risk elevation (Table 3). Variables meeting the prescreening threshold (p < 0.2) were entered into multivariable analysis, which revealed that the extent of surgery (VATS) (ORs 1.868–2.109, 95% CIs 1.260–3.157 per resection grade increase) was independently associated with delayed recovery (Table 4).

Discussion

This longitudinal study, to our knowledge for the first time, prospective data on symptom burden and functional interference in older adults (aged 60–80 years) after lung cancer surgery. This study also suggests which metrics are likely to be associated with delayed recovery of postoperative symptom burden and functional interference.

Published data show that older patients aim for alternative outcomes, that is, activity, work, walking and HRQOL in addition to oncologic results [22]. Among all MDASI-LC items (16 symptoms and 6 interference), we identified the four most prevalent symptoms (pain, fatigue, coughing, and shortness of breath) and four most prevalent interference items (activity, work, walking, and enjoyment of life) based on their highest occurrence rates on day 7 after discharge, representing a profile of the most significant symptom burden and functional interference for this study. A cluster of symptom and interference scores peaked immediately after surgery, which were highly prevalent in the first week after discharge, representing a combined effect from surgical insult and the perioperative care (Table S1) [20]. Most symptom and interference scores (except for interference with work) returned to preoperative baseline levels within approximately 3 months after discharge. Previous reports have stated that factors influencing returning to work include socio-demographic factors (aged ≥ 57 years, female), disease-specific variables (site, stage, adjuvant therapy treatment, comorbidity), psychiatric impairment, and social-environmental elements [23–25]. Notably, 59.1% of participants in our study were female (n = 150), and median age was 68 years (IQR: 64–71 years) (Table 1). Therefore, interference with work (including housework) demonstrated a distinct recovery pattern compared with other interference items, exhibiting a slower recovery (Table 2).

Our study also shows that VATS in older patients is effective in maintaining HRQOL (symptom burden and functional interference) in addition to benefits of cancer cure and symptoms relief. More than 60% of the study population reported postoperative recovery of symptom burden and functional interference according to the MDASI-LC. Although no standardized increment level was previously validated among geriatric patients undergoing VATS, other researchers reported an increment of 1.95 in the MDASCI as the minimal clinically important difference (Δ mean scores: 2.95 to 3.88, postoperative vs. preoperative levels, all P value < 0.0001) (Table S2 and S3) [26].

Additionally, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that extend of surgery are the key predictors of delayed recovery of symptom burden and functional interference (Table 4). Critically, the extent of VATS resection demonstrated a significant ordinal association with delayed recovery (via Cochran-Armitage trend test), wherein each incremental resection grade (wedge → segmentectomy → lobectomy) was associated with a near doubling of risk (ORs: 1.868–2.109, 95% CIs: 1.260–3.157). VATS sublobectomy, a parenchymal-sparing procedure recommended for early-stage NSCLC, demonstrates superior postoperative recovery compared to VATS lobectomy, particularly in elderly patients [27–29]. Although the exact mechanism linking resection extent to postoperative recovery remains to be elucidated, some speculative mechanisms might have explanatory value. Regarding shortness of breath and two interference (activity and walking), it is well-known that sublobectomy’s superior pulmonary function preservation and less lung tissue damage is associated with reduced shortness of breath and two interference (activity and walking) [30–33]. We have previously reported that VATS sublobectomy had generally better HRQOL and faster recovery of postoperative cough than VATS lobectomy [34]. Worse pain may be due to postoperative coughing, which exacerbates postoperative pain and thus slows recovery from postoperative pain [35]. Our finding that larger resection extent correlated with delayed recovery aligns with previous studies demonstrating that patients undergoing multi-lobe resections experienced more severe postoperative symptoms (pain, shortness of breath, fatigue) and prolonged functional impairment (activity and walking) [36]. These recovery challenges extend beyond clinical outcomes to social domain: return-to-work has emerged as a priority in cancer control, with robust evidence identifying fatigue as the dominant barrier (supported by pain and distress) to occupational rehabilitation [37, 38]. Patients who underwent larger resection extent that interference with activity, work and walking appear to suffer deterioration in physical function, rather than deterioration in cognitive function. Although patients experienced less enjoyment of life and slower recovery, these findings require cautious interpretation given the preliminary 6-month follow-up data.

This study has several limitations. First, the outcome measurement used for older patients were not designed uniquely for the senior population. However, the MDASI-LC has been rigorously validated in multinational population-based studies and has been shown to be a valid, reliable and sensitive instrument for assessing symptom burden and functional interference [11, 12, 39, 40]. Otherwise, older patients may minimize symptoms and interference, caregivers might be completing MDASI-LC sheets on the patient’s behalf, and cognitive impairment and other geriatric syndromes might weaken the accrual and accuracy [41]. Second, we usually administer MDASI-LC on the day of discharge and do not collect data on postoperative days 1–3, and follow-up after discharge should be performed monthly for at least 12 months instead of during the 6-month follow-up. The abbreviated observation period likely compromised detection of clinically meaningful changes in postoperative HRQOL(symptom burden and functional interference) and recovery trajectories. In addition to this, the definition of recovery was based on a composite criterion rather than established metrics like minimal clinically important difference (MCID) or percent improvement, which otherwise could have provided a more standardized benchmark. Third, our sample predominantly comprised early-stage lung cancer patients (240/254, 94.5%). Furthermore, all patients underwent surgery via a single utility port VATS approach, which, despite its increasing adoption in China, may limit the generalizability of our findings to centers employing other techniques such as multi-port VATS, robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or open thoracotomy [42–44]. Future studies with more diversity (including advanced stage patients, surgical approaches and surgical extent) are warranted to identify the most important symptoms and interference and to define the trajectory of postoperative recovery with greater generalizability [45, 46].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study establishes that VATS effectively achieves oncological control while preserving HRQOL (symptom burden and functional interference) in older NSCLC patients. The extent of VATS resection demonstrated a clinically meaningful association with delayed recovery during 6-month follow-up. These findings advocate for preoperative integration of functional restoration patterns into surgical strategy selection, particularly when weighing oncological radicality against geriatric patients’ physiological reserves in clinical decision-making.
