Frequency, clinical characteristics and cataract surgery outcomes of pseudoexfoliation syndrome in patients with senile cataracts: a review of hospital-based data
Authors: Alper Can Yilmaz, Hayati Yilmaz, Bagim Aycin Cakir ince
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the frequency of pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PEXS), its ocular and systemic clinical features in patients scheduled for cataract surgery, and to evaluate surgical complications and outcomes. Data from 585 eyes that underwent cataract surgery were included in this retrospective study. Data retrieved from patient files and our hospital’s electronic medical records were reviewed, including the presence of PEXS, pupil dilation, cataract type, nuclear hardness according to Lens Opacities Classification System III, and glaucoma. Additionally, data on clinical characteristics of pre-existing systemic diseases, surgical outcomes, and intraoperative and postoperative complications were also noted. The data were analyzed between two groups: eyes with PEXS and eyes without PEXS. The frequency of PEXS was 10.7%. The most common type of cataracts in the PEXS group was nuclear cataracts. Nuclear cataracts were harder in eyes with PEXS than in eyes without PEXS. Mean intraocular pressure was significantly higher in eyes with PEXS than in eyes without PEXS (p = 0.001). The prevalence of glaucoma was significantly higher in the PEXS group (p = 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of the frequency of intraoperative and postoperative complications. The non-PEXS group showed significantly better best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) compared to the PEXS group at the first week postoperatively (p = 0.001). However, BCVA was comparable between groups at the one-month postoperative visit. The frequency of PEXS was relatively high in our study. Awareness of the potential risks associated with PEXS in cataract surgery is essential, and appropriate preparations, including necessary surgical tools and techniques, should be made to ensure optimal outcomes. Although no differences were observed in the rates of intraoperative complications between the groups, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered as evidence of equivalent surgical risk.

Introduction

Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PEXS) is an age-related disorder of the extracellular matrix characterized by the accumulation of an abnormal extracellular fibrillar material in ocular and extraocular tissues, including the skin, blood vessels and various visceral organs1,2. This fibrillar material is produced by intraocular epithelial cells, such as the iris pigment epithelium and non-pigmented ciliary epithelium, and endothelial cells, such as the trabecular endothelium3. Pseudoexfoliative material causes sphincter atrophy in the iris stroma and restricts pupil dilation. It accumulates on the anterior surface of the lens and at the pupil border, making the anterior lens capsule fragile. The deposition of pseudoexfoliative material on the ciliary processes and zonules results in zonular weakness and phacodonesis4. The accumulation of pseudoexfoliative material in the iridocorneal angle and fluctuations in intraocular pressure (IOP) have been identified as important risk factors for pseudoexfoliation glaucoma5. Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma, which follows a more aggressive clinical course than primary open-angle glaucoma, is the most common cause of secondary open-angle glaucoma6,7. PEXS is associated with numerous complications during and after cataract surgery due to the accumulation of the pseudoexfoliative material in the anterior segment structures of the eye8,9. Intraoperative complications include zonular dialysis, anterior capsular tear, posterior capsular rupture (PCR), vitreous loss, and nucleus or nuclear segment drop. Postoperative complications that are relatively more common in patients with PEXS include prolonged anterior chamber reaction, anterior chamber fibrinoid syndrome, anterior capsular contraction syndrome, intraocular lens (IOL) decentration, posterior capsule opacification, increased IOP, and cystoid macular edema8–10.

The accumulation of pseudoexfoliative material in the skin and various visceral organs has led to PEXS being considered a systemic disease. PEXS is known to be associated with many systemic conditions, particularly cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases11,12. PEXS holds significant clinical importance for ophthalmologists, and its systemic manifestations and demographic variations should be considered3. Epidemiological studies in the literature report varying prevalence rates of PEXS13–16. The different results may be attributed to differences in study designs and demographic characteristics of the populations studied, such as age, sex, race, geographic location, or ocular and systemic comorbidities3. Studies evaluating PEXS in specific patient groups selected based on certain demographic and clinical characteristics are typically frequency studies, distinct from prevalence studies14. Frequency studies may not fully reflect the true prevalence in the general population. However, focusing on patients with senile cataracts is valuable for understanding epidemiological trends, given the significant association of PEXS with advanced age, cataract development, and cataract surgery outcomes.

This retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted to obtain epidemiological data on the frequency of PEXS in patients with senile cataracts treated at our hospital. Secondary outcomes evaluated in this study included ocular manifestations of PEXS, its systemic correlates, as well as cataract surgery outcomes, and complication rates.

Materials and methods

In this retrospective, cross-sectional, observational study, the data of 585 eyes that underwent phacoemulsification at Gulhane School of Medicine, Department of Ophthalmology between January 2023 and June 2023 were reviewed. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (approval number: 2025-73).

The data were retrieved from patient files, which included the findings of comprehensive ophthalmological and systemic examinations performed before the surgery, as well as from the electronic medical records of our hospital. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was assessed using the logMAR (Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution) chart after autorefraction measurements (Tonoref III, Nidec Co. Ltd, Aichi, Japan). Pupil dilation was measured under the operating microscope using a caliper and categorized into three groups based on the degree of dilation: grade 1 (< 5 mm), grade 2 (5–7 mm), and grade 3 (> 7 mm). After pupil dilation was achieved using tropicamide (Tropamid, 0.5% tropicamide, 5 ml eye drops, Bilim Pharmaceuticals, Turkey), slit lamp biomicroscopy and retina and optic nerve head examinations were performed using a + 90 D lens (Volk Optical, OH, USA). B-scan ocular ultrasonography was conducted for patients who could not undergo retinal examination due to mature cataracts, dense posterior subcapsular cataracts, or grade 5–6 nuclear sclerosis. Goldmann applanation tonometer was used to measure IOP. Patients with suspected or previously diagnosed glaucoma underwent iridocorneal angle examination using a Goldmann 3-mirror lens. Additionally, preoperative systemic evaluations were carried out by anesthesiologists, including complete blood count, electrocardiography, and chest radiography.

For this study, data on age, sex, BCVA, pupil dilation, cataract characteristics, IOP, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) were noted. Additionally, data on systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and coronary artery disease, as well as the ocular or systemic medications used by the patients were collected. Cataracts were classified as nuclear, cortical, posterior subcapsular, mixed, or mature. Nuclear hardness was graded from 1 to 6 using the Lens Opacities Classification System III (LOCS III)17. Furthermore, the presence of pseudoexfoliative material, central to our study and crucial for cataract surgery, was identified at the pupillary margin, anterior lens capsule, and iridocorneal angle, leading to the clinical diagnosis of PEXS. All patients were evaluated preoperatively using spectral domain optical coherence tomography (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) for macular morphology and retinal nerve fiber layer measurement. Patients suspected of having glaucoma underwent visual field testing (Humphrey field analyzer II, Humphrey-Zeiss Instruments). Patients with suspected glaucoma (e.g., known history of glaucoma, IOP > 21 mm Hg, and/or glaucomatous optic nerve head abnormalities) were screened for glaucoma. Patients with visual field defects suggestive of glaucoma were diagnosed with glaucoma. For patients without reliable visual field results due to cataracts, glaucoma was evaluated based on optic nerve head abnormalities (such as cup enlargement, asymmetry of cupping, thinning of the neuroretinal rim, or loss of nerve fibre layer) and nerve fiber loss on optical coherence tomography. In patients with mature cataract, where optic nerve head evaluation and visual field testing were not possible, criteria such as previous glaucoma surgery and/or elevated IOP were used for diagnosis.

Since our study aimed to obtain epidemiological data on the frequency of PEXS among patients with senile cataracts, congenital, traumatic, uveitic cataracts and individuals under 50 years of age were excluded from the study. Other exclusion criteria included a history of intraocular surgery, long-term use of topical or systemic corticosteroids, use of miotic drugs, central nervous system pathologies affecting the pupil reflex, and a history of radiotherapy. When comparing BCVA results after cataract surgery, the eyes with conditions that could affect postoperative visual acuity, such as nystagmus, corneal opacity, glaucoma, AMD, diabetic retinopathy, and retinal pathologies detected postoperatively in mature cataracts were not included in the evaluation.

Preoperative preparation and surgical technique

Before cataract surgery, patients were provided with detailed information about the procedure and its associated risks, and their written consent was obtained. IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany) was used to calculate IOL power, SRK/T formula was preferred as standard, and Hoffer Q formula was preferred in eyes with axial length shorter than 22 mm. Pupil dilation was achieved using 1% cyclopentolate, 1% tropicamide, and 2.5% phenylephrine prior to surgery. All surgical procedures were performed by two experienced surgeons.

All procedures were performed under topical anesthesia using 0.5% proparacaine eye drops and an intracameral injection of 2% lidocaine. Infiniti or Centurion Phacoemulsification systems (Alcon Laboratories, Inc.) was used. After creating temporal and nasal corneal incisions, the anterior chamber was filled with dispersive viscoelastic material. A superior clear corneal incision was then created. Mechanical stretching or iris hooks were used in patients with insufficient pupil dilation. Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis was performed with a 27 G capsulorhexis cystotome needle. Following hydrodissection and hydrodelineation, the nucleus was phacoemulsified using stop-and-chop and divide-and-conquer techniques. The cortex was removed using a bimanual irrigation/aspiration handpiece. The capsular bag was filled with cohesive viscoelastic material, and a one-piece hydrophobic acrylic posterior chamber foldable IOL was implanted into the capsular bag. In cases of PCR, a three-piece foldable IOL was implanted into the ciliary sulcus following anterior vitrectomy. A capsular tension ring (CTR) was used in cases of zonular dialysis (if less than or equal to 4 clock hours). Viscoelastic material was removed from the anterior chamber, capsular bag, and behind the IOL. After hydration of the clear corneal incision wounds, intracameral cefuroxime injection was administered, and the surgery was completed.

Intraoperative complications included anterior capsule tear, PCR, vitreous loss, zonular dialysis, Descemet membrane detachment, corneal wound burn, suprachoroidal hemorrhage, dropped lens fragments, and iris prolapse. Postoperative complications included corneal edema, capsular contraction, posterior capsule opacification, IOL subluxation, cystoid macular edema, wound leakage, hypotony, choroidal effusion, and delayed-onset endophthalmitis.

Postoperatively, each patient was prescribed a topical antibiotic/steroid formulation containing 5 mg moxifloxacin and 1 mg dexamethasone for use every hour on the first day and every two hours on the second and third days. The medication was continued for two weeks by gradually reducing the dosage frequency. Additionally, 0.1% nepafenac was prescribed for use 4 times a day for one month. Follow-up examinations were conducted on the first day, first week, and first month after surgery. Patients who underwent complicated surgeries were scheduled for more frequent follow-up visits. During follow-up, patients underwent refraction measurements, BCVA, slit lamp examination, retina and optic disc examination, and IOP measurements.

Statistical analysis

In calculating the sample size for this study, the power of the test for each variable was determined to be at least 80%, with a type-1 error of 5%. Kolmogorov-Smirnov (n > 50) and Skewness-Kurtosis tests were used to check whether the continuous variables followed a normal distribution. Since the measurements were not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were applied. Descriptive statistics for the study variables were expressed as mean, standard deviation, median, range, number (n), and percentage (%), as appropiate. Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between the groups. Friedman test was employed to compare the differences between measurement periods separately within groups. Following the Friedman test, a post-hoc multiple comparison test with Bonferroni correction was conducted to identify the specific measurement times contributing to the differences. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the effect of certain independent variables on dependent categorical variables. Chi-square test was used to examine relationships between categorical variables. To assess the effect of sample size on statistical power, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). Sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the reliability of our findings. The statistical significance level (α) was set at 5% for all calculations, and the SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analyses.

Results

Data from 599 patients were initially collected for analysis. After excluding nine patients under the age of 50, one patient with a history of radiotherapy, two patients with traumatic cataract, and two patients lost to follow-up, data from 585 patients with senile cataracts were included in the study. The frequency of PEXS was 10.7% (63 eyes). When the frequency of PEXS was analyzed by sex, no significant difference was observed between males and females. There were 30 (47.6%) females and 33 (52.3%) males in the PEXS group. The mean age of the patients in the PEXS group was significantly higher than that of non-PEXS patients (73.5 ± 7.1 years vs. 70.6 ± 7.8 years, respectively, p = 0.004). A significant difference was found in the frequency of PEXS among age groups (p = 0.010). Patients aged over 70 years constituted 71.4% of the PEXS group, compared to 55.3% in the non-PEXS group.

Nuclear cataract was the most common type in the PEXS group was, affecting 41.2% of the patients (26 eyes), followed by mixed type cataracts at 38.0%. The mean IOP was significantly higher in eyes with PEXS than in eyes without PEXS (17.2 ± 1.9 mmHg vs. 13.8 ± 1.8 mmHg, respectively; p = 0.001). The prevalence of glaucoma was also significantly higher in the PEXS group (19.0% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.001). Hypertension was more common in the non-PEXS group (57.2% vs. 42.8%, p = 0.029). The non-PEXS group showed significantly better BCVA compared to the PEXS group at the first week postoperatively (p = 0.001). However, by the first postoperative month, the difference in BCVA between the groups was no longer statistically significant. Nuclear cataract hardness was compared between the two groups using the LOCS III (Fig. 2). Nuclear cataracts were significantly harder in eyes with PEXS than in eyes without PEXS (p = 0.047). Grade 5 and 6 nuclear sclerosis was observed in 23% of eyes in the PEXS group, compared to 11.5% of eyes in the non-PEXS group. Subgroup analyses showed that the significant association between PEXS and nuclear hardness persisted only in patients over 70 years of age (p < 0.001). Results are shown in Table 3. Pupil size was significantly smaller in eyes with PEXS compared to eyes without PEXS (5.31 ± 0.45 mm vs. 7.06 ± 0.11 mm, respectively; p = 0.001). In subgroup analyses, this significant difference persisted in all age groups (p = 0.026 in the 50–60 age group and p = 0.001 in all other age groups). Results are shown in Table 4. Grade 3 pupil dilation was found in 17 (26.9%) eyes with PEXS and 474 (90.8%) eyes without PEXS (Fig. 3). Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to predict the presence of glaucoma using four predictors: age, sex, presence of DM, and laterality of PEXS. Laterality of PEXS was found to be significantly associated with glaucoma in eyes with PEXS (p = 0.040). Eyes with clinically bilateral PEXS were 2.8 times more likely to develop glaucoma (odds ratio [OR] 2.895) than eyes with clinically unilateral PEXS. During the study, various intraoperative and postoperative complications were observed in both the PEXS and non-PEXS groups. While Descemet membrane detachment was not observed in the PEXS group, it was present in two patients in the non-PEXS group. This condition improved in both patients after air injection into the anterior chamber. Intraoperative iris prolapse occurred in 3 patients in the PEXS group and 5 patients in the non-PEXS group. Among the patients with iris prolapse, benign prostatic hyperplasia was present in three patients in the PEXS group and two patients in the non-PEXS group. PCR was observed in 3 eyes in the PEXS group and 6 eyes in the non-PEXS group. Vitreous loss with PCR was detected in 3 eyes in the PEXS group and 4 eyes in the non-PEXS group, requiring anterior vitrectomy. A three-piece IOL was implanted in the ciliary sulcus in 2 of these three patients in the PEXS group and 3 of the six patients in the non-PEXS group. One patient in the PEXS group and two patients in the non-PEXS group underwent scleral fixation IOL implantation with a second surgery. Nuclear segment drop occurred in one patient in the non-PEXS group. Zonular dialysis was identified in 2 eyes in the PEXS group and 4 eyes in the non-PEXS group. The dialysis angle was less than 60° in all eyes, and an IOL was implanted into the capsular bag following the placement of a CTR. One patient in the non-PEXS group developed suprachoroidal hemorrhage after IOL implantation during surgery. As the hemorrhage began to liquefy, suprachoroidal hemorrhage drainage, pars plana vitrectomy, and silicone oil injection were performed. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of the incidence of intraoperative complications listed in Table 6 (p > 0.05; X2 test).

Discussion

The frequency and prevalence of PEXS have been investigated across various geographic locations and ethnic groups worldwide, with notable variations reported in the literature18–21. Although PEXS primarily affects Scandinavian and Eastern European populations, it can occur in almost all ethnic groups22. Gunes et al.23 conducted a study on 352 eyes with senile cataracts in Turkey, reporting a PEXS frequency of 11%. Govetto et al.14 observed a higher frequency of 21.6% in 1763 eyes scheduled for cataract surgery in Spain. Teshome et al.15 reported an even higher frequency of 39.3% in 229 eyes of Ethiopian patients with senile cataracts. Obuchowska et al.24 found a PEXS frequency of 8.2% in 1000 patients scheduled for cataract surgery in Poland. In our study, we observed a PEXS frequency of 10.7% in patients undergoing cataract surgery for senile cataracts. Several factors may contribute to the observed variations in PEXS frequency across studies, including study design (population-based vs. hospital-based, prevalence studies vs. frequency studies), geographic location, ethnic background, genetic makeup, and use of different inclusion criteria. Frequency values derived from hospital-based patient groups selected according to specific criteria may not represent the true prevalence rates observed in population-based studies with randomly selected individuals. In two previous studies conducted in Turkey, the reported frequencies of PEXS were 11.2% and 12.2%, respectively25,26.

In our study, the frequency of PEXS was 6.0% in the 50–60 age group, 11.9% in the 71–80 age group and 22.9% in the 80 + age group. A study from the United States reported PEXS frequency rates of 0.6% for individuals aged 52–65 and 5.24% for those aged 75–8522. Joshi et al.27 observed a PEXS frequency of 22.1% in 1022 patients scheduled for cataract surgery, with 35.8% of the cases occurring in patients over 80 years of age. Our findings support the existing literature showing that PEXS is a strongly age-related disorder and frequently occurs after the age of 5028–31. In our study, most of the patients with PEXS were between the ages of 71–80 (53.9%), and patients in the PEXS group were significantly older than those in the non-PEXS group. Erkayhan et al.32 reported that the mean age in the PEXS group was 74.64 ± 6.8 years, compared to 68.95 ± 7.5 years in the control group, showing that the patients in the PEXS group were significantly older than controls. Andaloussi et al.30 reported a PEXS frequency of 9.8% in 837 patients scheduled for cataract surgery, with a mean age of 69.3 years in the PEXS group and noted that the frequency of PEXS increased with age. Our results are in line with the literature showing that among patients with cataracts, those with PEXS tend to be older than those without PEXS33,34. As our study was conducted in a population with senile cataracts, none of the patients were under the age of 50. There were 4 patients (6.3%) with PEXS in the youngest age group of 50–60 years. Previous studies have also reported PEXS in individuals under the age of 503,15. One study reported that a prevalence rate of 6.4% in the 30–39 age group35. These studies highlight that PEXS may occur at younger ages, particularly in populations living at lower latitudes.

In our study, the PEXS group included a higher proportion of males (52.3%) than females (47.6%), but the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant. While some studies report a predominance of females and others indicate a male predominance, more recent literature suggests no gender preference for PEXS36–42. The parameters that contribute to the observed differences in frequency and prevalence remain a topic of investigation. Population-based studies would provide more accurate and comprehensive insights into this subject.

Different rates of laterality have been reported in the literature. Joshi et al.27 found that PEXS was bilateral in 38.1% of eyes. In a study by Jammal et al.31 evaluating 19,753 patients aged 50 or older, 44% of the PEXS cases were bilateral. A high bilateral involvement rate of 66.7% was also reported in a study by Gelaw and Tibebu43. In the current study, bilateral involvement was observed in 39.6% of the PEXS cases, with equal right and left eye involvement in the remaining cases. It is generally accepted that PEXS exhibits bilateral but asymmetric involvement. Studies have shown that ultrastructural and biomicroscopic changes occur in eyes without clinically detectable PEXS44. The variability in the reported rates of unilateral or bilateral involvement may be attributed to factors such as the examining physician’s experience in identifying early cases and the challenges in detecting PEXS in patients with poor pupil dilation. Since the disease often starts in one eye and progresses to bilateral involvement over time, bilateral cases are more commonly reported in most studies45–47. Additionally, geographic and racial factors may also account for higher prevalence of bilateral involvement.

PEXS has often been associated with poor pupil dilation, attributed to the accumulation of exfoliative material in the stromal and muscle tissue of the iris, along with ocular ischemia1. Poor pupil dilation can hinder the creation of a capsulorhexis with an optimal size and shape, which can complicate nucleus phacoemulsification. It may also increase the risk of capsular contraction postoperatively. Poor pupil dilation is associated with an increased risk of complications during cataract surgery. Govetto et al.14 reported that the pupil size was significantly smaller in the PEXS group than in the non-PEXS group, with pupil diameters exceeding 7 mm in 25.5% of the eyes with PEXS compared to 74.9% in the non-PEXS eyes. Philip et al.48 reported that pupil dilation was ≤ 6 mm in 96.7% of eyes with PEXS. Similarly, Akinci et al.49 observed a significantly smaller mean pupil diameter in the PEXS group, with a pupil dilation of 6 mm or more in 62% of eyes with PEXS compared to 94% of non-PEXS eyes. Consistently, in the present study, we observed that the mean pupil diameter was significantly smaller in the PEXS group than in the non-PEXS group. While the pupil diameter exceeded 7 mm in 26.9% of the eyes with PEXS, it was 90.8% in the non-PEXS group. These findings support the existing literature, clearly illustrating the relationship between PEXS and pupil dilation.

The association between PEXS and glaucoma is well known50. PEXS is an important risk factor for open-angle glaucoma, which clinically progresses more rapidly, responds less effectively to medical treatment, and requires surgical intervention more often51,52. While studies investigating the prevalence of glaucoma in eyes with PEXS report mixed results, they consistently support the close relationship between PEXS and glaucoma. Reported prevalence rates include 4.2% in India, 7% in the USA, 45% in Saudi Arabia, 26% in Turkey, 25.4% in Jordan, 36.7% in Spain and 34.1% in Poland24,31,42,53–56. In our study, the prevalence of glaucoma in eyes with PEXS was 19.0%. The variations in reported rates across studies may be attributed to differences in study design, patient inclusion criteria, diagnostic criteria for glaucoma, as well as racial differences. Additionally, the progression from PEXS to glaucoma occurs over time. Therefore, studies with long-term follow-up, rather than cross-sectional studies are better suited to determine the true prevalence of glaucoma in patients with PEXS. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that PEXS laterality is significantly associated with glaucoma development, with clinically bilateral involvement carrying approximately 2.8 times higher risk compared to unilateral cases. This finding can be explained by the fact that bilateral PEXS likely reflects a more advanced and widespread stage of the disease. In bilateral cases, a larger cumulative pseudoexfoliative material load can lead to more pronounced trabecular dysfunction, facilitating glaucoma development1. PEXS often presents as a unilateral condition and tends to become bilateral over time, suggesting that bilateral involvement may represent a longer disease duration and cumulative exposure to pathogenic mechanisms1. Taken together, these findings support the idea that bilateral PEXS is not only a laterality determinant but also an indicator of increased disease severity, which may explain its stronger association with glaucoma. The lack of a statistically significant relationship between age and glaucoma in patients with PEXS suggests that, although age is a known risk factor for the development of PEXS, it may not independently trigger glaucomatous transformation after the syndrome has developed.

In our study, the mean IOP in eyes without glaucoma was significantly higher in the PEXS group than in the non-PEXS group. IOP was above 20 mmHg in 9.5% of the eyes with PEXS, compared to 3.8% in the non-PEXS group. Thakur et al.13 reported similar findings, with IOP was above 20 mmHg in 10.3% of eyes with PEXS versus 5.9% in the non-PEXS group. Kovac et al.33 also observed significantly higher IOP in eyes with PEXS (17.8 ± 3.2 mmHg) compared to those without PEXS (15.8 ± 2.8 mmHg). These findings align with numerous studies demonstrating a correlation between elevated IOP and PEXS57,58.

The accumulation of pseudoexfoliative material in the skin, blood vessels, and some internal organs, in addition to ocular structures, support the concept of PEXS as a systemic disorder1,59. Studies have linked PEXS to systemic conditions, particularly cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as rheumatoid arthritis, hypothyroidism, and benign prostatic hyperplasia11,12,60,61. Wood et al.62 reported a significantly lower prevalence of PEXS in diabetic patients compared to age-matched nondiabetic patients. Psilas et al.63 found PEXS in 11% of diabetic patients compared to 23.7% in non-diabetic individuals. Thakur et al.13 stated that both PEXS and DM are systemic, multifactorial, metabolic conditions and that there may be a possible link between their genetically influenced mechanisms. They suggested that PEXS is less common in patients with uncontrolled DM who use insulin and that the presence of DM may protect against the development of PEXS. However, other studies have found no significant association between PEXS and DM29,40. Conflicting results have also been reported regarding the relationship between PEXS and hypertension, with some studies demonstrating a significant association between PEXS and hypertension, while others suggesting a potential protective effect of PEXS against hypertension9,64,65. Sarda et al.64 reported a significantly higher prevalence of ischemic heart disease in the PEXS group (28.8%) compared to the non-PEXS group (9.6%). Andrikopoulos et al.29 also found an increased risk of coronary ischemia in the PEXS group than in the control group.

There is no consensus in the literature regarding the relationship between PEXS and DM, hypertension, and coronary artery disease. Selecting patients with PEXS who are scheduled for cataract surgery may introduce bias, as individuals with conditions preventing elective cataract surgery, as well as cataract patients without surgical indications, would be excluded from this group. It is important to consider that in most studies, the number of patients in the PEXS group was smaller than in the non-PEXS group. Comparisons involving more balanced patient numbers would provide more meaningful insights. In our study, the prevalence of DM in the PEXS group (33.3%) was slightly higher than in the non-PEXS group (32.3%). Coronary artery disease was more common in the non-PEXS group (12.4%) compared to the PEXS group (6.3%). However, these differences were not statistically significant. Hypertension, on the other hand, was significantly less common in the PEXS group (42.8%) than in the non-PEXS group (57.2%). The differences reported in the literature on this subject may be due to possible confounding factors such as patients’ characteristics included in the study (such as mean age) and access to healthcare services.

It has been underscored that PEXS may negatively affect lens metabolism and accelerate cataract development by disrupting ocular blood flow and oxidation-antioxidation balance1. While some studies show that the prevalence of PEXS increases in eyes with cataracts, others have found no significant association between them66,67. Studies investigating the relationship between PEXS and cataract types reported differential results regarding which subtype is more common. In different studies, mixed cataracts were identified as the most common type of cataract in eyes with PEXS, with reported rates of 51.2%, 55%, and 42.3%13,23,64. Some studies have also noted a significant association between hypermature cataracts and PEXS15,27,30. In studies reporting a high prevalence of hypermature cataracts, late presentation for cataract surgery in rural areas was cited as the major cause. In addition to these findings, there are many studies highlighting the association between PEXS and nuclear cataracts, and our results also support these observations68–70. Furthermore, the relationship between PEXS and nuclear hardness has also been examined. Some studies reporting significantly greater nuclear hardness in eyes with PEXS, while others found no difference between the PEXS and non-PEXS groups56,71. Using LOCS III classification, Joshi et al.27 reported that the prevalence of grade VI cataract was 41.2% in eyes with PEXS, whereas in our study, it was 11.5%. While the association between PEXS and nuclear hardness is evident in older individuals, as seen in our study, this relationship is weaker in younger age groups. Therefore, age should be considered when evaluating nuclear hardness. In our study, the most common type of cataracts in the PEXS group was nuclear cataracts (41.2%), followed by mixed cataracts (38%), and mature cataracts (%3.1) was the least common. Nuclear cataracts were more prevalent in eyes with PEXS (%41.2) compared to eyes without PEXS (%31.6). Additionally, nuclear cataracts in the PEXS group were significantly harder than those in the non-PEXS group.

Cataract surgery in eyes with PEXS presents several challenges, and it is known that the complication rates are higher in these eyes8,9. Poor pupil dilation, nuclear hardness, zonular weakness, phacodonesis, elevated IOP, and increased frequency of glaucoma have been cited as factors contributing to surgical challenges in PEXS cases. Drolsum et al.72, while reviewing the results of phacoemulsification in patients with PEXS, reported the overall prevalence of intraoperative complications (capsular tear, zonular dialysis or vitreous loss) as 9.6% in eyes with PEXS and 3.7% in eyes without PEXS. They also reported that the percentage of patients achieving visual acuity of 6/12 or better on the Snellen chart at 4 months postoperatively was 86.4% in the PEXS group compared to 92.5% in the non-PEXS group. Lumme et al.73 reported that the rates of zonular dialysis (14.8%), PCR (10.2%), and vitreous loss (7.4%) during cataract surgery were significantly higher in eyes with PEXS. Similarly, Scrolli et al.74 reported higher rates of zonular dialysis, PCR, and vitreous loss in patients with PEXS than in patients without PEXS. In a study evaluating cataract surgery outcomes in 200 eyes (122 with PEXS), Jawad et al.75 identified vitreous prolapse (10.5%) and PCR (9%) as the most frequent complications in the PEXS group. Shingleton et al.9 reported a significantly higher incidence of intraoperative complications in the PEXS group, with vitreous loss occurring in 2.4% of cases compared to 0% in the non-PEXS group. Zonular weakness was observed in 3.4% of the PEXS group. However, they found no difference between the groups in terms of postoperative complications. At the end of the one-year follow-up, BCVA was comparable between the two groups.

In a study by Akinci et al.49 reviewing the phacoemulsification outcomes of 800 eyes with PEXS and 800 eyes without PEXS, no significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of intraoperative and early postoperative complications. They also reported similar BCVA improvements in both groups. Sufi et al.76 reported comparable intraoperative complication rates between PEXS and non-PEXS groups. They showed that visual acuity was better in the non-PEX group in the early postoperative period, but there was no significant difference between the two groups at six months. Singh et al.77 reported the cataract surgery outcomes of 914 patients with PEXS. Their findings included a PCR (with or without vitreous loss) prevalence of 4%, zonular dialysis of 4.2%, nucleus drop of 0.2%, and IOL drop of 0.1%. They also observed iridodialysis in 0.6%, Descemet membrane detachment in 0.5%, anterior capsule tear in 0.3%, and tunnel-related complications in 0.8% of cases.

Small pupil diameter can affect the size of the capsulorhexis, cause anterior and posterior capsular tears, and increase the risk of vitreous loss. In our study, anterior capsular tear was observed during surgery in one eye with a small pupil in the PEXS group. PCR was observed in 3 (4.7%) patients in the PEXS group, compared to 6 (1.1%) patients in the non-PEXS group. Vitreous loss was seen in 3 (4.7%) patients in the PEXS group and 4 (0.07%) patients in the non-PEXS group. Zonular dialysis occurred in 2 (3.1%) patients in the PEXS group versus 4 (0.07%) patients in the non-PEXS group. Posterior capsule opacification was observed in 2 (3.1%) patients in the PEXS group, compared to 4 (0.07%) patients in the non-PEXS group. Anterior capsular phimosis was seen in 2 (3.1%) patients in the PEXS group and 1 (0.01%) patient in the non-PEXS group. Our results showed that there were no significant differences in intraoperative and postoperative complications between the two groups.

PEXS increases the risk of complications during and after cataract surgery due to predisposing factors, such as small pupil size, hard nucleus, and phacodonesis. In eyes wth PEXS, these factors heighten the likelihood of complications even in the hands of experienced surgeons. Consequently, cataract surgery outcomes of eyes with and without PEXS have been compared in the literature. As a matter of fact, there have been reports of higher complication rates in eyes with PEXS. Therefore, it is crucial to be well-prepared for these known challenges in patients with PEXS, both in terms of surgical instruments that may be required and surgical experience. In our study, we observed that small pupil size and nuclear hardening were associated with an increased risk of intraoperative complications in patients with PEXS. Small pupils can make it difficult to detect PEXS and zonular dialysis and may reduce the chances of achieving optimal capsulorhexis. In eyes with small pupils, intraoperative mydriatic agents, mechanical stretching or pupil expansion devices will be useful. During capsulorhexis, signs such as phacodonesis, variable anterior chamber depth, and the appearance of wrinkles in the anterior capsule should alert the surgeon to potential zonular dialysis. Maneuvers that increase zonular stress during surgery increase the risk of intraoperative zonular dialysis and vitreous loss. Therefore, the capsular bag must be stabilized with capsular tension rings or retractors. Turalba et al.65 reported that pupil expansion devices (25.2%) and CTRs (4.9%) are used more frequently in PEXS cases. In our study, pupil expansion devices (11.2%) and CTRs (4.2%) were used more frequently in the PEXS group, but there was no significant difference between the two groups. In the late postoperative period, anterior capsule contraction and zonular weakness may lead to lens decentralization. In cases with severe zonular weakness, lenses with a larger optical diameter can be used to reduce the risk of lens decentration. In our study, capsular bag contraction was observed in 2 patients in the PEXS group and 1 patient in the non-PEXS group. Additionally, lens decentration was observed in one patient in the PEXS group. Postoperatively, corneal edema occurred in 2 patients in the PEXS group and 3 eyes in the non-PEXS group, with no significant difference between the two groups. These findings indicate that the risks of intraoperative and postoperative complications may be reduced if standard phacoemulsification techniques are used appropriately in eyes with PEXS.

In our study, while BCVA was better in the non-PEXS group at the first postoperative week, there was no significant difference in vision improvement in both groups at the first month. IOP fluctuations, corneal edema, posterior capsule opacification, and a relatively longer inflammatory response may cause poorer visual acuity in the early postoperative period after phacoemulsification in patients with PEXS. However, with appropriate and timely treatments, there is often no significant difference in long-term vision outcomes.

Although this study did not show a statistically significant difference in intraoperative complication rates between eyes with PEXS and the control group, this finding should be approached with caution. A significant body of knowledge in the literature consistently shows that PEXS is associated with an increased risk of surgical complications primarily due to zonular instability, inadequate pupillary dilation, and capsule fragility. In contrast, the lack of difference in the present study may reflect the effect of optimized surgical conditions. Detailed preoperative assessment allowed for the prediction of potential challenges in eyes with PEXS. The availability of necessary equipment, including pupillary dilation devices and CTRs, and the fact that all procedures were performed by experienced surgeons may have effectively reduced intraoperative risks. The underrepresentation of eyes at high risk of complications, such as those with advanced zonular damage, may have led to selection bias. In light of this information, the study results should be interpreted cautiously, not as a counter-argument to the known increased surgical risk in eyes with PEXS, but rather as an indication that these results can be achieved with appropriate preparation and surgical expertise.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and the relatively small sample size of the PEXS group. The selection of patients from a single center and over a relatively short time period limited the representation of the larger population of patients with senile cataract and PEXS, which may have led to selection bias. This limitation may have caused the PEXS frequency in the senile cataract group and the complication rates associated with cataract surgery to be determined lower in this study. To address this concern, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted. With a sample size of PEXS (n = 63) and non-PEXS (n = 522), assuming an effect size of 0.5 (moderate effect), the achieved statistical post-hoc power was calculated as 96% for independent t-tests (α = 0.05). This indicates a statistically adequate sample size for detecting moderate group differences. Nevertheless, we recognise that the limited number of PEXS cases may reduce the power to detect rare complications or subtle differences. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the robustness of the findings. In this analysis, the number of PEXS patients was hypothetically increased, and its effect on the confidence intervals and effect estimates for key surgical outcomes was evaluated. The direction and magnitude of the associations remained largely stable across these scenarios, suggesting that the study’s findings were not overly sensitive to small changes in sample size. In summary, while the sample size of the PEXS group was limited, both the post-hoc power analysis and sensitivity checks indicate that the main conclusions remained statistically robust.

Although the aim was to reveal the frequency of PEXS in the patient group with senile cataracts, the exclusion of patients under the age of 50 and those with congenital, traumatic, and uveitic cataracts may have affected the generalizability of the results. However, only 14 patients (none of them had PEXS) were excluded from the study due to these criteria and it was seen that it would not have a significant effect on the results. Additionally, the possibility of misclassification for glaucoma in patients with unreliable visual field results due to cataracts may have led to an overestimation of glaucoma prevalence in both groups. Since this is a hospital-based study, the technical equipment and surgical experience available in the hospital could be a potential cause of bias. The surgeries were performed by two experienced surgeons using the same surgical equipment. This is a contributing factor to our similar complication rates in the two groups. The study found that early visual outcomes (in the first month) were similar in the two groups. The exclusion of patients with additional pathologies that could affect BCVA (nystagmus, corneal opacity, glaucoma, etc.) necessitates a cautious interpretation of this result, as it may have led to an overly optimistic approach regarding visual potential in PEXS cases. To reduce confounding in postoperative BCVA assessment, eyes with concomitant glaucoma were specifically excluded from the analysis. The aim was to identify the impact of PEXS-specific intraoperative challenges (e.g., decreased pupil dilation, increased nuclear hardness, and zonular instability) on surgical complications and early postoperative visual outcomes. However, this methodology may have introduced a selection bias for mild disease phenotypes and should be considered a significant limitation. However, potential complications (capsular contraction, IOL dislocation, etc.) that may occur in the late postoperative period in patients with PEXS may negatively affect the final visual outcomes. Long-term outcomes were not evaluated in this study. This limitation should be taken into consideration. The fact that the study was conducted on a homogeneous Turkish population should be considered as a limiting factor on the generalizability of the results. The strength of this study lies in its holistic approach to evaluating patients with PEXS and presenting the most up-to-date and comprehensive data specific to the geographic region in which the study was conducted.

In conclusion, a relatively high frequency (10.7%) of PEXS was found in senile cataract patients at a tertiary teaching hospital, and this was shown to be associated with age, small pupils, increased IOP, and glaucoma. A significant association was shown between eyes with PEXS and harder nuclear cataracts. In addition, binary logistic regression probability estimates were proposed to identify factors predicting the presence of glaucoma, and cataract surgery complications in cataract patients with PEXS. However, this results should be interpreted with caution. The possibility of confounding factors that could affect the results should not be ignored. Larger population-based epidemiological studies are needed to reduce bias and obtain more objective results.
