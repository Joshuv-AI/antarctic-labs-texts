Biosynthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles and evaluation of anticancer activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cell line via phyto-synthesised nanoparticles
Authors: Shruti N. Tanwar, Yatish R. Parauha, Yogesh There, Anil M. Pethe, S. J. Dhoble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present research work aims to biosynthesize ZnO nanoparticles using plant-mediated extracts (A. marmleos, A. indica and a mixture of both extracts) and to evaluate their physiochemical properties and associated biological activities. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the majority of ZnO nanoparticles were spherical, uniformly distributed, and exhibited an average size within the nanometer scale. Photosynthesised ZnO NPs were characterized with particular emphasis on zeta potential, a key parameter influencing nanoparticle stability and biological interactions and its correlation with cytotoxic efficiency. Anticancer potential was assessed in vitro against the MCF-7 human breast cancer cell line, revealing dose-dependent inhibition of cancer cell proliferation with IC50 values closely associative reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, mitochondrial membrane disruption, and induction of apoptosis. The nanoparticle demonstrated significantly antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and indicated potential antioxidant properties through the scavenging of DPPH and H2O2, attributed to bioactive plant-derived surface capping agents. The finding indicates a significant correlation between the surface characteristics of nanoparticles and their biological efficiency. This indicates that ZnO NPs synthesised from plant extracts may have potential applications in various biomedical applications.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosed in women, and it continues to be one of the leading causes of death globally. It was estimated that, breast cancer reaches up to 23 million in 2020, accounting for about 11.7% of all new cancers. In 2020, breast cancer became the most leading diagnosed cancer globally, surpassing lung cancer1. Based on WHO report most prevent type of cancers occurred in women are breast, lung and colorectal cancers2. In China, lung cancer remained the most common type, but breast cancer became the most frequent among women. Gastrointestinal cancers contributed to the 23 burdens, with breast cancer being the most common form in women. The American Cancer Society anticipates that 608,570 cancer-related deaths and 1,898,160 new cancer cases in the United States in 20213. Despite advances in early detection and treatment, breast cancer continues to pose a major public health challenge.

Conventional cancer treatments such as radiation and chemotherapy are often limited by non-specific toxicity, severe side effects, and drug resistance. This has fuelled the hunt for substitute treatment modalities that are less damaging to healthy cells and equally effective. An established in vitro model for assessing the cytotoxic and apoptotic effects of new anticancer drugs is the MCF-7 cell line, which is derived from human breast adenocarcinoma. Therapeutics based on nanoparticles offer a promising approach since they can be designed for controlled release, targeted delivery, and improved interaction with the membranes of cancer cells4.

Nanotechnology is a emerged as one of the most rapidly advancing interdisciplinary fields, with wide range of applications spanning from medicine, agriculture to electronics and environmental remediation. Among various nanomaterials, metal oxide nanoparticles have garnered significant attention due to their distinctive physiochemical, optical and biological properties. Zinc oxide nanoparticles are particularly having noteworthy attention because of their chemical stability, low-cost synthesis, high exciton binding energy and biocompatibility. These properties make zinc oxide a promising candidate for various applications such as biosensing, photocatalysis, drug delivery, antimicrobial coating and anticancer therapeutics5,6. Plant mediated nanoparticle have emerged as a sustainable alternative against conventional methods. Plant extracts containing phytochemicals such as flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolic acids, alkaloids and proteins facilitates bio-reduction of metal ions acting as reducing agent converting metal ions into stable nanoparticle under mild conditions7–9. This green synthesis method offers several advantages including eco-friendliness, cost-effectiveness, scalability and elimination of toxic by-products. Furthermore, the coating of bioactive compounds present in the extracts enhances therapeutic potential of nanoparticle in addition to improving their anticancer efficacy10–12.

Several studies have demonstrated the biological potential of plant mediated ZnO NPs. ZnO NPs synthesized using Aegle marmelos extracts have shown cytotoxic effects against MCF-7 cells, along with notable antioxidant and antimicrobial activities, while Azadirachta indica-mediated ZnO NPs have exhibited similar anticancer, antibacterial, and antioxidant properties. Moreover, study using other plant extract combination, such as Catharanthus roseus and Morinda Citrifolia, indicate thar nanoparticle size, morphology and surface chemistry are critical determinants of biological performance13. Most studies primarily examine single plant-mediated synthesis, with limited information on combined application of A. marmelos, and A. indica extracts. There is a lack of research regarding their effects on the physiochemical properties of nanoparticles, including zeta potential, and particle size, as well as the relationship of these properties to biological activities such as cytotoxicity and ROS generation. To address this gap, the present study aims to biosynthesize ZnO NPs using three different extracts; A. marmleos, A. indica and mixture of both extracts. The combined extracts of A. marmelos, A. indica for biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles has not been previously documented in the context of ZnO nanoparticle fabrication and anticancer evaluation. In the present work, we systematically characterize their physicochemical properties including morphology, particle size, and zeta potential, and evaluate their biological activities, focusing on cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells, ROS generation, antioxidant potential, and antimicrobial efficacy. We hypothesize that the combined plant extract-mediated ZnO nanoparticles will exhibit enhanced stability and synergistic biological activity compared to nanoparticles prepared from individual extracts, and that a clear relationship exists between nanoparticle surface characteristics and anticancer efficacy, providing insight into their structure–activity relationship.

Materials and synthesis method

Materials

Leaves of A. marmleos and A. indica were collected from the campus of Rashtrasant Tukadoji Maharaj Nagpur University (RTMNU). The plant materials were taxonomically authenticated by experts Dr. Nitin Dongarwar, Professor at the Department of Botany, Rashtrasant Tukadoji Maharaj Nagpur University, Nagpur. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the departmental herbarium for public access and future reference under voucher numbers 10,565 (Aegle marmelos (L.) Corrêa, family Rutaceae) and 10,566 (Azadirachta indica A. Juss). Precursor used in the study zinc nitrate and ethanol was of analytical grade and has been procured from Loba chemicals. Moreover, nutrient broth and Hi-sensitivity agar were obtained from Hi-media (India). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), trypsin, tryptophan, fetal bovine serum, and MTT dye were also been obtained from Hi-media.

Preparation of plant extract

After the collection of leaves, leaves were washed completed followed by rinse with distilled water and dried for several days. Dried leaves were grinded to powder for extract preparation. Around 10 g of fine powder of leaves was blended in 100 ml distilled water in a beaker. The mixture was stirred continuously at 70 °C for 1 h. Resultant mixture was filtered through Whatman no. 1 filter paper and subsequently stored at 4 °C for further use (Fig. 1).

Synthesis method for zinc oxide nanoparticles.

Synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles

0.1 M zinc nitrate was weighed and mixed with 10 ml distilled water along with 10 ml plant extract (A. marmelos and A. indica). The resultant solution was allowed to stir at constant speed for 20–30 min to obtain a homogenous solution. Solution was placed for combustion at 350 °C until the sample turned to powder. Final black to grey coloured powder is termed as nanoparticles and stored for further characterization.

The formation of zinc oxide nanoparticle is clearly been noticeable with the change in color of the solution. The introduction of plant extract to zinc nitrate solution triggers the reduction of zinc ions, leading to the formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles. This reduction accompanied color shifting from dark brown to light brown color ensures the formation of nanoparticles. Further, with the combustion synthesis in open air at 350 °C solution turns to black colored powder when synthesised with neem extract precursor and grey colored powder when treated with beal extract. For A. marmelos presence of various bioactive compounds at high level such as carotenoids, phenolic, alkaloids, pectins, tannins, coumarins, flavonoids and terpenoids act as reducing and capping agents14,15. The Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry GCMS analysis of A. marmelos revealed presence of total 51 compounds matched with phytoconstituent in the NIST GC/MS librabry along with galic acid and rutin analysed by HPLC analysis16. Likewise, alkaloids, saponons, tannins, glycosides, flavonoids and reducing sugar were known to found in A. indica17,18. GCMS analysis also reveals the presence of multiple phytoconstituent in A. indica leaves extract19–21. The bio-inspired zinc oxide nanoparticles zinc ion possesses Zn2+ ions which eventually reduced to ZnO when treated with plant extracts. Presence of phytochemicals including phenols, flavonoids and terpenoids donates electron in redox reaction, initiating nucleation and subsequent growth of zinc oxide nanoparticles.

Characterization

The synthesized ZnO NPs were characterized by various techniques to evaluate their structural, morphological, optical and surface properties. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded using Rigaku Miniflex D600 X-ray diffractometer over a 2θ range of 10o to 90o with a scanning rate 0.02o per second to confirm their phase purity. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) images were obtained using JEM-ARM200F NEOARM at accelerating voltage 200 kV to examine particle morphology and size distribution. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer was performed on Brucker Alpha FT-IR spectrometer to identify functional groups. UV-Visible absorption spectra were recorded using PC based double beam spectrometer (Systronics-20702) to study optical property. The zeta potential measurement was performed using Horiba Scientific SZ-100 instrument to access surface charge and stability of samples.

Antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles

The antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles using A. marmelos, A. indica were assessed using well diffusion method. Autoclaved sterile cotton swab used to spread microbial population (S. aureus) over Hi-sensitivity agar plate. The plates were drilled with borer and 100 µL of 1 mg/mL stock solution have been loaded to the wells separately in sterile condition; whereas vancomycin 5 mg have been used a positive control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the zone of inhibition was observed and noted in mm.

Antioxidant assay

2, 2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay

DPPH radical scavenging activity is widely used assay to evaluate antioxidant efficacy of nanoparticles or extracts to those of the standard antioxidant Vitamin C. The basic principle for the measurement of antioxidant potential is based on the ability of a compound to donate hydrogen atom or electron to neutralize DPPH free radical. 1 mL different concentration of synthesised nanoparticles was added to 1 mL DPPH reagent. The solution was kept in dark for 30 min to carry out the reaction. Resultant changes deep violet color to yellow, which is quantified spectrophotometrically at 517 nm.

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging activity

The hydrogen peroxide scavenging activity assay based on the principle to neutralize hydrogen peroxide, one of the main components for the release of reactive oxygen species that can cause oxidative damage to cells. The compounds acting as antioxidant neutralises H2O2 to water. For the analysis, 40 mM of hydrogen peroxide was prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution. 0.6 mL of this solution added to 3.4 mL of phosphate buffer which is used as control. Different concentration of nanoparticles (100–1000 µg/mL) were added to above final solution. The test tubes were allowed to incubate for 5 min at room temperature. After the incubation the test tubes were quantified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at λ = 230 nm.

Anticancer assay

Cells

MCF-7 breast cancer cell line were precured from NCCS, Pune and were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Cells were grown in tissue culture flasks with growth medium at 37 °C in atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 90% relative humidity in a CO2 incubator.

MTT assay

Based on mitochondrial enzyme degradation of tetrazolium MTT assay helps to determine and verify cell viability under the influence of different factors. The cells were plated in 96 well plates with the density of 50,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h in a humidified CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After the completion of incubation period, cells were treated with different concentration of nanoparticles. The plates were then incubated for 24 h in humidified CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After the complete treatment with nanoparticles the wells were treated with 5 mg/ml concentration of MTT solution. 10 µL solution of MTT dye has been added to each cell for the identification of viable and dead cells. Plate was incubated in dark for 3–4 h till the purple formazan was visible, then 100 µl of Dimethyl Sulfoxide was added in each well to dissolve purple formazan crystals followed by 10–20 min incubation period. Thereafter, reduction of organic solvent was determined spectrophotometrically at λ = 490 nm.

Result and discussion

UV visible spectroscopy

The absorption of nanoparticles was observed in the range of 350–800 nm. Synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles in the influence of A. indica shows maximum absorption at 427 nm. Whereas, A. marmleos mediated zinc oxide nanoparticles show maximum absorption of 500 nm with secondary peak at 654 nm. The mixed solution of both the plant extract was also been used for the synthesis of nanoparticles which shows slight increase in absorption at same wavelength. Typically, absorption of zinc oxide nanoparticles is seen in the range of 350–380 nm, herein the prior synthesis absorption wavelength shows lesser absorption with lesser broad band. Increase in the wavelength typically due to the larger particle size and surface defects observed on the surface of nanoparticles. Similarly, increase in the absorption band is may be due to the influence of plant extract and biomolecules present in the extracts (Fig. 2).

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR analysis confirmed the successful synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using phytochemical enriched plant extracts. the detected peaks were placed in between the range of 4000 –400 cm− 1, the range in between 400 and 600 cm− 1 is well documented for the Zn-O bond vibrations22–24. Small stretch at range 2359.26 cm− 1 indicates presence of C= C bond23, while bond in the range 1392.87 cm− 1 corresponds to C–O–H bending mainly from plant extract26. The sharp peak present at 1115.73 cm− 1 asymmetric stretching shows the presence of C–N or C–O bond due to the presence of plant residual biomolecules27. Zinc oxide nanoparticles synthesised via A. indica (ZnON) The peak around 417. 86 cm− 1 confirms the successful formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles and corresponds to Zn–O vibrational stretching28.

In contrast to ZnON, A. marmelos mediated ZnO shows complete absence of O–H bond, whereas amide bonds or COO− symmetric stretching are shown around 1415.85 cm−129,30. ZnOB nanoparticles shows lesser organic compounds as compare to ZnON nanoparticles.

Bond appear at 875. 93 cm− 1 is formed due to presence of carbonated group or residual plant organics. XRD analysis

Figure 4 shows the XRD pattern of crystalline nature of ZnO NPs synthesized by using A. Marmleos and A. Indica extracts. The diffraction peaks of ZnO NPs individually prepared with A. marmelos and A. indica closely resembled the standard reference pattern for hexagonal zincite ZnO, indicating the successful development of crystalline ZnO. However, when both extracts were combined, minor changes and shifts in intensity peaks were noticed, indicative of variations in crystallite size, strain, or defect structures because of the synergistic interaction among phytochemicals from both extracts. Such variations could be indicative of a variation in the rate of growth and nucleation of ZnO crystals depending on the different bioactive compounds as reducing as well as stabilizing agents. This outcome suggests that the selection and combination of plant sources in green synthesis have great influences on the structural attributes of the resulting nanomaterials, which in turn could affect their functional performance in applications.

TEM analysis

Figures 5a, 6a and 7a shows TEM images of biosynthesized ZnON, ZnOB and ZnONB NPs, respectively. The TEM images represent morphological behaviour of prepared samples by green synthesis route. The TEM images of ZnO NPs reveal spherical shape of particles with homogeneous distribution. Figures 5b, 6b and 7b shows SAED pattern of prepared ZnON, ZnOB and ZnONB NPs, respectively. The SAED pattern displays concentric rings that correspond to the (100), (002), and (101) planes, confirming the ZnO NPs crystalline structure. The particle size distribution curve confirms that ZnON particles have a primarily spherical morphology, a relatively uniform dispersion, and an average particle size of 9.48 ± 0.11 nm (Fig. 5c). Their crystalline nature is demonstrated by the well-defined concentric rings with distinct bright spots in the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern. With an average particle size of 9.96 ± 0.22 nm, ZnONB nanoparticles (Fig. 7c) also exhibit a spherical shape, albeit with slightly larger dimensions. ZnONB’s SAED pattern supports polycrystalline properties that align with the TEM’s observed particle boundaries (Fig. 7b). On the other hand, ZnOB nanoparticles exhibit a much wider range of sizes, sometimes surpassing 100 nm, with an average of 25.08 ± 0.50 nm (Fig. 6c). Crystallinity is further confirmed by the SAED pattern’s sharp diffraction spots and rings and the TEM image’s aggregated clusters. In contrast to the ZnOB, which forms larger and more agglomerated structures, on comparative analysis with ZnON and ZnONB shows smaller, more uniformly distributed nanoparticles. It is anticipated that these differences in size and shape will affect surface area and, in turn, their biological and physicochemical properties.

Zeta potential

Zeta potential of nanoparticle is the appropriate for characterizing surface charge of biosynthesised nanoparticles. Higher surface to volume ratio makes it more essential to understand the toxicity of nanoparticle than their microscopic studies. It is important to avoid the toxicity of upon nanoparticles changes through different routes and improve biocompatibility when designed for various applications such as biomedicine. The charge on material directly relates to the interaction of particle with cell. Cationic nanoparticles are stated to be more toxic due to higher cell uptake and their damaging effect on cell and lysosomal members43,44.

The stability and colloidal properties of nanomaterials are important aspects for toxicological criteria, as the unstable nanoparticles may produce unwanted redox reactions and metal ion to the surrounding. Zeta values between ± 30 mV are considered to be more stable and less toxic due to their lower surface mass ratio and agglomerate formation. It also helps to determine the surface coating, biomolecule-cell and nanoparticle-cell/tissue interaction which are major factors to improve the material for biocompatibility45. According to the study, the size and electronic structure of nanoparticles have a major impact on zeta, with smaller particles showing a higher surface charge because of increased surface ionization. Metal ion release, cellular interactions, and nanoparticle aggregation are all significantly impacted by zeta potential. Low absolute zeta values cause nanoparticles to clump together, changing their toxicity and bioavailability46–48.

Figure 9 shows about charge on the surface ZnO nanoparticles. ZnON shows zeta potential value of − 37.9 mV, whereas, ZnOB and ZnONB have zeta potential value of -21.8 mV and − 23.3 mV respectively. The negative charge on zinc oxide nanoparticles helps easy electrostatic interaction with the Gram-positive bacteria, causing inhibition of bacterial colony. Based on the results of zeta potential, ZnON shows good dispersity and stable nature with high surface area available for bacterial interaction. Shah and Rather reports higher negative value of synthesised TiO2 nanoparticles (− 30.8 to − 37.5 mV) with great electrostatic interaction and colloidal property. Particle size and surface charge, as reflected by zeta potential highly influence nanoparticle behaviour in biological fluids and bioavailability49. Similarly, Salayova et al. have also sates variation in the zeta potential value widely affects antibacterial efficiency of green synthesised silver nanoparticles against different bacterial strains. Lower the zeta potential value showed greater antibacterial effect50. Our finding suggests ZnON enhanced ROS protection leading to good antibacterial property against S. aureus than ZnONB and ZnOB. This finding attributes to better ROS generation and surface interaction due to higher zeta potential and smaller particle size. The biological efficiency of synthesised nanoparticles exhibits strong dependence on their physiochemical characteristics. Decreased in particle size increases surface area which ultimately improved cellular interaction and enhanced antimicrobial activity. similarly higher the zeta potential of the nanoparticle, higher would be the electrostatic interaction with microbial membrane thereby enhancing biological performance. These synergistic influence of nanoparticle with reduced particle size and increased zeta potential ultimately governed the overall biological efficiency51–53. As a result, zeta is a significant predictor of biological reactions and interactions between nanoparticles and cells. However, not all studies gives relation between zeta potential of nanoparticles and cytotoxicity54,55.

The production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a crucial mechanism that underlies metal oxide nanoparticles’ anticancer activity because too much ROS upsets the cellular redox balance and causes oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis. Higher surface reactivity and appropriate zeta potential nanoparticles encourage improved cellular internalization and interaction, which facilitates the generation of intracellular ROS. DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction are made possible by the effective interaction of negatively or moderately charged nanoparticles with cancer cell membranes56. Zeta potential also affects protein corona formation and colloidal stability, which further control nanoparticle uptake and ROS-induced cytotoxicity. Through oxidative stress-driven cell death pathways, surface charge and ROS generation interact to control the anticancer efficacy of nanoparticles. Negatively charged or moderately charged nanoparticles interact efficiently with cancer cell membrane enabling mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage44. Additionally, the colloidal stability and protein corona formation modulates nanoparticle uptake and ROS induced cytotoxicity. Excessive production of ROS disrupts cellular redox balance, leading to oxidative stress mediated apoptosis. Several reports indicate that surface charge facilitates electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial and cancer cell membranes, promoting membrane disruption, intracellular penetration, and ROS generation. Study suggests change in zeta potential widely affects the ROS generation and their biological activities57–59. MTT assay

MTT assay was evaluated to explore cytotoxic effect of all three ZnON, ZnOB and ZnONB nanoparticles against breast cancer cell line MCF-7. These breast cancer cell lines were treated with different concentration of nanoparticles ranging from 0 to 1000 µg/ml. The MTT reacts to the undissolved formazan crystals through cells which are metabolically active. These crystals turn purple when react to viable cells, darker the purple color, more viable cells are present in well. the strength of the color formed were analysed spectrophotometrically.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles have been reported to show anticancer activity against various human cancer cell lines such as MCF-7 (Breast), LN-18 (Glioblastoma–Brain), A-549 (Lung) and SHSY-5Y (Neuroblastoma–Bone)61. In present study, plant mediated zinc oxide nanoparticles have been studied against breast cancer cell line. Figure 12 shows dose-dependent response of MCF-7 cell line and the IC50 results of each nanoparticle. IC50 values describe half maximal inhibitory concentration to kill 50% of cells. even though the metal precursor is same for synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles, when the plant extract changes the cytotoxic activity also changes. the IC50 values for different nanoparticles have been calculated by using MATLAB. The graph was plotted concentration of nanoparticle against percent viability. The obtained non-linear regression sigmoidal graph gives IC50 values. Findings show that, the IC50 value for ZnON is 63.07 µg/mL, whereas IC50 for ZnOB and ZnONB is 105.94 µg/mL and 66.10 µg/mL respectively. ZnON have lowest IC50 value which suggest to be potent cellular uptake leading to the most effect nanoparticle for inhibition of cancer cells. While, ZnOB shows higher IC50 value which has lowest anticancer activity.

It is not yet confirmed that how precisely ZnO nanoparticle exert their cytotoxicity. In general zinc oxide nanoparticles are attributed to show higher ROS induction and accumulation within cancer cells. Intracellular release of Zn2+ ion followed by ROS generation is stated to be the primary mechanism behind the cytotoxicity mechanism of ZnO nanoparticles62. Reactive oxygen species accumulation leads to oxidative stress which interrupts with cellular process, leading to DNA damage and ultimately death. Moreover, ZnO damages the cell to induce apoptosis, triggering signalling pathways promoting cell death in cancer cells. Release of Zn2+ ion from nanoparticles induce mitochondrial damage and disrupt disrupts homeostasis of cell, further amplify oxidative stress within the cancer cells63. Green synthesised nanoparticles in comparison with chemically synthesised zinc oxide exhibit stronger physiochemical and biological performance. Biologically synthesised ZnO nanoparticles shows higher cytotoxicity towards MCF-7 cell line (IC50 = 81.75 µg/mL) along with enhanced antimicrobial activity across Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and fungal strains, lower α-amylase IC50 (70.08 µg/mL), whereas chemically synthesised ZnO shows higher α-amylase IC50 74.08 µg/mL64. Similarly, Ghotekar et al. have demonstrated antioxidant and anticancer efficiency of Azadirechta indica leaves extract mediated CeVO4 nanoparticles. Nanoparticles exhibited moderate colloidal stability with zeta potential of -20.4 mV. The dose dependant cytotoxicity against HeLa cells with IC50 of 89.15 µg mL−1, accompanied by marked morphological disruption, while antioxidant assays demonstrated substantial free-radical scavenging 71.03% DPPH highlights their redox-modulated anticancer and antioxidant functionality65 (Table 6).

Conclusion

This study successfully synthesized ZnO NPs using A. marmelos, A. indica and combined leaf extracts as reducing and capping agents. TEM investigation confirmed the nanoscale dimensions and morphology, while FT-IR analysis demonstrated the presence of organic functional groups and metal-oxygen linkages. A comparative analysis indicated that ZnO NPs produced using A. indica (ZnON) exhibited the most substantial negative zeta potential (–37.9 mV), signifying improved colloidal stability. ZnON exhibited the strongest in vitro anticancer activity against MCF-7 cells, with an IC50 of 63.07 µg/mL, and had higher antibacterial efficacy compared to ZnO NPs produced from A. marmelos. These results indicate that plant-mediated ZnO nanoparticles, particularly ZnON, have considerable in vitro biological activity, necessitating detailed research for biomedical applications.
