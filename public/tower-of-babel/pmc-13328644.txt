Mechanical properties and slope stability of a copper mine waste dump in China
Authors: Zhenying Zhang, Zheheng Ma, Bo Huang, Hui Zhu, Xiuyun Zhao, Guohua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of mines creates substantial amounts of soil-rock mixture (S-RM) waste that is accumulated as waste dumps. These dumps pose significant safety hazards that require management. In this study, S-RM was sampled from a waste dump at a copper mine in Jiangxi Province, China. Consolidated-undrained triaxial tests were conducted on four gradations of S-RM specimens. Numerical simulations of dump slope stability were performed by considering factors such as groundwater level, rainfall intensity, and root reinforcement depth. The findings are as follows: (1) cohesion and friction angle are sensitive to rock content; (2) the power-law model outperforms the Duncan-Chang model in fitting triaxial data; (3) the stability of the dump deteriorates as the groundwater level gets shallower; (4) the factors of safety decrease with increasing rainfall duration and intensity with an approximately exponential decay pattern; (5) vegetation root reinforcement improves slope stability; however, extreme rainfall can trigger perched water accumulation, which can compromise stability. These findings provide valuable indicators that can assist in managing the safety hazard posed by such waste dumps.Keywords: Soil-rock mixture, Shear strength, Rainfall effects, Root reinforcement, Slope stabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

During the development and utilization of mines, substantial amounts of soil-rock mixture (S-RM) waste are generated, which accumulate to form waste dumps. These dumps pose significant safety hazards, as extreme conditions may trigger geological events, such as landslides and debris flows, which may severely impact mine production, infrastructure, and worker safety, thereby causing substantial economic losses. Consequently, research into the mechanical properties and slope stability of mine waste dumps is crucial for effective waste management.

Field in situ tests can reveal the mechanical behavior of S-RMs under natural conditions. Li et al.1 and Coli et al.2 studied the failure mechanisms and mechanical characteristics of waste dumps by conducting in situ horizontal shear tests. However, because of their high cost and limited repeatability, researchers have increasingly moved to laboratory-based experiments. Several previous studies3–7 used large-scale direct-shear apparatuses to investigate the effects of particle size distribution, rock content, and moisture content on shear strength and deformation. Despite these efforts, direct-shear tests suffer from stress concentrations and predetermined failure planes. In contrast, triaxial tests provide a more comprehensive characterization of the mechanical and deformation properties. Ling et al.8 performed large-scale triaxial tests on mixtures with varying fine-grained content and reported reduced peak strength under low confining pressure, but increased strength under high confining pressure as the fine content increased. Xu et al.9 analyzed the influence of porosity through large-scale static triaxial tests. Li et al.10 established a damage constitutive model validated by triaxial tests under wet-dry cycling. Liu et al.11 and Wu et al.12 studied gradation evolution during triaxial tests and revealed correlations between porosity, stress paths, and particle breakage. Zhang et al.13 developed a nonlinear strength criterion based on a generalized Hoek-Brown model that was validated using global large-scale triaxial data. Complementing these experiments, Xu and Zhang14 and Zhao and Liu15 used 3D modeling and discrete element simulations to analyze the deformation and shear strength.

Given the direct impact of dump stability on mine safety and operation, extensive research has used diverse methodologies. Engineering geological analysis identifies landslide patterns via analogy, thereby supporting quantitative stability studies. Beyond this, the limit equilibrium method refined by Sarma16 remains widely applicable. Computational advances have accelerated numerical stability analyses. Rotaru et al.17 and Chen et al.18 used finite element models (FEMs) to evaluate the effects of slip surfaces on seepage and stability, noting the failure surface dependence on the slope geometry and material. Sloan19 combined the limit theory with FEM to study slopes with weak interlayers and demonstrated how these layers trigger connected plastic zones, leading to failure. Moussa et al.20 applied the discrete element method and simplified Bishop’s method to analyze stability under confining pressures of 400, 800, and 1600 kPa, identifying critical slip surfaces near dump surfaces and consolidation-weak zones. Rangarajan et al.21 integrated the GEOtop hydrological model with 3D slope stability analysis under extreme rainfall conditions, revealing a lower factor of safety (FS) in combined assessments. Wen et al.22 investigated rainfall-induced landslide mechanisms through case studies, while Yin et al.23 modeled multi-stage stability evolution at Hong’ao Dump under filling and water-level changes. He et al.24 linked groundwater saturation and rainfall infiltration to landslide initiation, and Cho and Song25 applied Bishop’s method to assess the FS in natural versus rainfall scenarios. Wang et al.26 demonstrated the progressive deformation mechanism of rainfall-triggered landslides and predicted the failure process through numerical simulations. Chen et al.27 proposed a dual-wedge limit equilibrium method for weak-base slopes aligned with strength reduction results.

Current research focuses on road embankments and dam fillers, with limited attention paid to mine waste dumps. Most studies have relied on artificially graded laboratory rather than naturally graded specimens, which limits their practical applicability. Additionally, root-reinforced slope stability analysis remains underexplored. To address these gaps, this study uses naturally graded S-RM from mine waste dumps for geotechnical tests to analyze the shear strength and slope stability. The results expand the dump material property database, refine the theoretical frameworks, and provide data-driven insights into engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

A copper mine in Jiangxi Province, China, which was designed in the 1970 s, was officially commissioned in 1985. The total mining area covers 13.44 km² with a mining scale of up to 10,000 t/d. By 2015, the cumulative ore output of this mine reached 90.491 million tons. Waste dumps are formed during the open-pit mining process with the waste mass and slope exposed. This waste dump has a final stacking height of 65 m and comprises three benches with heights of 40, 15, and 10 m, respectively. The slope of the waste dump and soil sampling points are shown in Fig. 1. Sampling point Q1 is located at the toe of the slope and points Q2, Q3, and Q4 are at the top of the first, second, and third benches, 40, 55, and 65 m above the base, respectively. During sample collection, the surface of the waste dump was excavated to a depth of approximately 30 cm, and the operation was carried out using a custom-made cutting ring of 300 mm in diameter and 200 mm in height.

The “Code for investigation of geotechnical engineering: GB 50021 − 2001 (2009)”29 specifies a threshold particle size of d = 2 mm to distinguish between soil and rock fragments. Methods

Four scaled soil specimens with different gradations (Q1x, Q2x, Q3x, and Q4x) were prepared and subjected to consolidated-undrained triaxial tests using a fully automated strain-controlled triaxial apparatus. The tests were conducted at confining pressures of 100, 200, 300, and 400 kPa.

Specimen preparation and testing procedures were as follows. For each test group, 600 g of oven-dried soil was mixed with 100 g of water. The mixture was then divided into four equal portions. Each portion of soil and water was thoroughly mixed, placed in a plastic bag, and sealed for 24 h to allow the moisture to reach a state of equilibrium. The moist soil was then compacted into a mold in layers. Each layer received an equal soil mass and was compacted to the required height with the same active effort. The surface of each compacted layer was scarified before the next layer was placed to ensure good interlayer bonding. This process was continued until the mold was filled. The prepared specimens were sealed and cured for one week before being removed from the molds for testing. The specimens had a diameter of 61.8 mm, height of 125 mm, moisture content of 16.5% (for uniform specimen preparation), density of 1.60 g/cm³ (the average density of the specimens was used as the target compaction density for preparing the triaxial test specimens), specific gravity of solids of 2.772 (average specific gravity of the specimens), and void ratio of 0.733.

The testing sequence comprised placing the specimen in a pressure chamber and saturating it using water head saturation followed by backpressure saturation. A specimen is considered to have reached a saturated state when the B-value (the ratio of the pore water pressure increment to the confining pressure increment) ≥ 0.95. A confining pressure of 100 kPa was applied and the drainage valve was opened to allow the specimen to consolidate. The consolidation process lasted approximately 44 h. After the consolidation was complete, the drainage valve was closed, and the confining pressure was maintained. An axial deviator stress was then applied at a constant strain rate of 0.625 mm/min until the axial strain reached 20%, at which point data acquisition was stopped. Throughout the shearing stage, pore water pressure, axial load, and axial displacement data were recorded, and the deformation characteristics of the specimens were observed. After completing the test under a confining pressure of 100 kPa, the failed specimens were removed. An identical specimen was then tested following the same procedure under confining pressures of 200, 300, and 400 kPa until all tests were completed, and all relevant data were recorded.

In the initial stage (axial strain < 2%), the difference in the deviator stress among specimens with different gradations is insignificant. At higher axial strains the differences become increasingly significant, indicating that the influence of gradation on the shear strength of the soil becomes more pronounced as the deformation develops. Furthermore, the difference in the deviator stress among specimens with different gradations is small under a low confining pressure; as the confining pressure increases, the differences in the deviator stress gradually increase. The deviator stress increases with the rock content in the order Q3x, Q2x, Q1x, and Q4x. This is because the specimens with a higher rock content contain coarser particles. The interlocking effect between these coarse particles generates a significant interlocking resistance, and the friction between the particles increases, thereby enhancing the deviator stress of the specimen.

Power-Law model

The Power-Law model was fitted using the Levenberg-Marquardt optimization algorithm. The parameters of the power-law model for S-RM are listed in Table 4, which shows that the goodness-of-fit for the power-law model is consistently > 0.96 (as reflected by R2), indicating a high level of accuracy. By contrast, the goodness-of-fit values for the Duncan-Chang model in Table 3 are all < 0.96. The power-law model therefore provides a better fit for the S-RM than that provided by the Duncan-Chang model. For S-RM, their complex mechanical response makes it difficult for the Duncan-Chang model to accurately capture the curvature variation of the stress-strain curve, especially during the initial compression stage, where the discrepancy between the model and experimental data is particularly pronounced. Mathematically, the hyperbolic form of the Duncan-Chang model imposes an asymptote on the stress, which restricts its curvature at large strains. In contrast, the power-law formulation offers greater nonlinear fitting capability and curvature flexibility, enabling it to better capture the extended strain-hardening behavior typical of granular mixtures containing rock fragments.

Limitations

This study investigated the mechanical properties of S-RM with different gradations through triaxial tests, although the experimental testing has certain limitations. The shape, angularity, surface roughness, and spatial distribution of the rock fragments in their natural state are random. However, to specifically investigate the influence of gradation, the test aimed for a uniform distribution of particles of certain sizes, which reduced this randomness. The scaling method employed in this study is the parallel gradation method. Its primary advantage lies in maintaining geometric similarity of the particle size distribution. However, this method leads to a relative increase in fine particle content, which can affect the density and engineering properties of the specimens. Compared to the original materials, the scaled specimens may exhibit issues such as a reduced particle breakage rate and an enhanced degree of shear contraction. Nevertheless, the variation patterns of shear strength parameters among different gradations for the scaled specimens remain similar to those of their corresponding original gradations, indicating that the scaled specimens retain a certain degree of representativeness. Furthermore, constrained by the instrument’s specifications, the maximum confining pressure in the tests was set at 400 kPa, which may be insufficient for adequately simulating the high-stress environments encountered in practical engineering scenarios, such as deep fills or high waste dumps.

While the experimental testing has limitations in terms of variable control, it clearly revealed the profound influence of rock content on the strength mechanisms of S-RMs. It also validated the superiority of the power-law model in simulating their mechanical behavior. The study provides an important parametric basis and theoretical reference for related engineering design.

Numerical simulation of dump slope stability

Groundwater level

Groundwater was detected at a depth of approximately 5 m in boreholes drilled at the base of the waste dump. Therefore, the groundwater table in the experimental model was set 5 m below the surface at the base of the dump. To analyze the influence of the internal groundwater level on the slope stability in the simulation, the water table was set at depths of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 35 m below the surface. In accordance with the “Code for waste dump design of metal mine GB51119-GB52015”30, the FS for the waste dump was determined for each water table level setting.

Figure 8 shows that as the groundwater level sinks further below the surface the FS increases. The FS stability threshold required is 1.30 (indicated by the broken red line in the figure). Below this threshold, the waste dump is in an unstable state. It is crucial that the water table level be more than 10 m below the surface. Therefore, the warning water level was set 10 m below the surface to maintain stability. The critical slip surface does not pass through the water table at depths > 20 m below the surface so the FS below 20 m remains almost constant.

FS variation with groundwater level in waste dumps.

Rainfall

The influence of rainfall intensity on the waste dump was analyzed using the SEEP/W module in Geo-Studio. A unit flux boundary was applied to the surface of the waste dump to simulate rainfall. The saturated permeability coefficient of the dump soil was set to 1.65 × 10− 4 cm/s (data from laboratory permeability tests), and the shear strength parameters from the previous section were adopted. A steady-state seepage analysis was first established by setting the left and right water head boundaries of the dump at 60 and 15 m, respectively. The software automatically calculates the internal groundwater flow based on these boundary conditions. An element independence verification was performed for a simulation scenario with a rainfall intensity of 50 mm/d over a duration of 5 days. Element sizes of 5 m, 3 m, 2 m, 1 m, and 0.5 m were respectively evaluated, and the results are presented in Fig. 9. As the element size decreased, the FS increased, with the rate of increase gradually slowing down. The difference in the FS between an element size of 1 m and 0.5 m was within 1%. Therefore, to balance computational efficiency, a global element size of 1 m was defined for the entire dump slope, resulting in a computational mesh comprising 14,417 elements and 14,722 nodes. A transient seepage analysis was subsequently built upon the steady-state analysis, with the slope crest and face defined as the free infiltration boundaries. To investigate the impact of continuous rainfall on dump stability, three rainfall intensity scenarios (50, 100, and 200 mm/d) were simulated over a 15-day period. The transient infiltration analysis was set for a duration of 15 days with 15 time steps. The time step size for the stability analysis was set identical to that of the transient infiltration analysis. The FS for the dump slope was determined at different times during each rainfall event. The critical slip surface of the waste dump after 5 days of rainfall at an intensity of 200 mm/d is shown in Fig. 10.

Root reinforcement

Studies have indicated that the enhancement of soil shear strength by plant roots is primarily manifested as an increase in additional cohesion, with a relatively minor effect on the internal friction angle31. In the revegetated area of the waste dump, the additional cohesion provided by the vegetation ranges from 10 to 20 kPa32,33, and the soil permeability coefficient in this area is 5.03 × 10− 4 cm/s (data were obtained from laboratory tests on soil specimens collected from the revegetated waste dump). Herbaceous plants typically have root lengths ranging from several tens of centimeters to 2 m, while trees can have root lengths reaching 3–5 m. To determine the time required for root growth, a maximum slope-reinforcement depth of 3 m was selected for the simulation. For the stability analysis of the experimental waste dump, which was planted with vegetation like that in the revegetated area, the parameters used were: cohesion increment = 10 kPa; permeability coefficient = 5.03 × 10− 4 cm/s; and rainfall interception rate = 10%34. Simulations were conducted for root reinforcement depths of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 m under rainfall scenarios of 50, 100, and 200 mm/d. Figure 13 shows the relationship between the FS of the waste dump slope and rainfall duration for the four reinforcement depths.

FS at different depths variation with rainfall duration for different rainfall intensities: (a) 50 mm/d; (b) 100 mm/d; (c) 200 mm/d.

Figure 13 shows that there is an increase in the FS with reinforcement depth. Furthermore, rainfall intensities of 50 and 100 mm/d reflect similar variations with rainfall duration after root reinforcement.

Under no-rain conditions, the FS increases by 0.33%, 0.44%, 0.67%, and 0.95% for soil reinforcement depths of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 m, respectively, compared to those for unreinforced slopes. After 15 d of rainfall at 50 mm/d, these improvements increase to 1.12%, 1.50%, 2.25%, and 2.83%, respectively, confirming that rainfall enhances the slope resistance due to root reinforcement.

At 200 mm/d rainfall, the variation in the FS for the first 9 d is like that under lower rainfall intensities. However, after 9 d there is a decrease in the FS for all root reinforcement conditions below those of the unreinforced state, at a rate that increases with reinforcement depth. For example, with a 3 m reinforcement depth, a perched water table develops in the upper slope layer after 9 d of rainfall. After 11 d, this saturated zone fully covers the interface between the reinforced and unreinforced soil areas, because the root-soil composite zone that forms after root reinforcement has a higher permeability coefficient than the unreinforced zone. Under extremely heavy rainfall, infiltrating rainwater is impeded at the interface where the permeability is less and fails to drain promptly, thus leading to the formation of a perched water table. When this happens, the stability of the upper slope layer decreases, causing a rapid decline in the FS. Nevertheless, before the formation of a perched water table, the root-reinforced slope exhibits greater resistance to heavy rainfall than the unreinforced slope.

In this simulation scenario, even without the formation of a perched water table, the FS of the waste dump slope drops below 1.25 after 6 d, indicating that it is entering a critically unstable state. At this point, urgent attention should be paid to the risks associated with the precarious conditions of the slope.

Limitations

While a systematic numerical simulation of the dump slope stability was conducted in this study, certain limitations remain. The soil parameters were directly adopted from laboratory test results without considering the spatial variability or uncertainty of in situ soils, which may increase the discrepancy between the simulation and the actual conditions. FS was calculated solely using the Morgenstern-Price method, without a comparative analysis of the results from other mainstream methods. Given the limited size of the dataset, the fitting results in “Rainfall” section lack cross-validation. Given the complex mechanisms of root reinforcement, its effect on slope stability was simplified as a uniform additional cohesion and a fixed permeability coefficient increment, without an in-depth investigation into the influence of root distribution within the soil. Furthermore, the general applicability of the perched water phenomenon under extreme rainfall conditions requires further validation.

Despite these limitations inherent in the numerical simulation, the results reveal the influencing range of the groundwater level on the FS, as well as the relationships between rainfall, root reinforcement, and the FS. This provides a theoretical reference for the prevention and control of dump instability.

Conclusions

In this study, S-RM specimens were collected from a waste dump in a copper mine in Jiangxi Province. Laboratory triaxial tests were conducted on 16 groups of specimens considering four different gradations (Q1x, Q2x, Q3x, and Q4x) and four confining pressures (100, 200, 300, and 400 kPa) to investigate the strength characteristics of the S-RMs. Numerical modeling software was also used to establish a geometric model of the waste dump, and the effects of several different influencing factors such as groundwater level, rainfall intensity and duration, and root reinforcement on the slope stability (FS), were studied. The key findings are summarized as follows.

The deviator stress increases with the axial strain. There is no distinct peak in the deviator stress up to an axial strain of 20%, after which it remains constant indicating strain-hardening behavior. For axial strains < 2%, the influence of the gradation on the deviator stress is minimal; however, for axial strains > 4%, this influence becomes significant. High confining pressures result in large differences in the deviator stress among specimens with different gradations, and high rock content specimens exhibit high deviator stress.

The deviator stress increases with the axial strain. There is no distinct peak in the deviator stress up to an axial strain of 20%, after which it remains constant indicating strain-hardening behavior. For axial strains < 2%, the influence of the gradation on the deviator stress is minimal; however, for axial strains > 4%, this influence becomes significant. High confining pressures result in large differences in the deviator stress among specimens with different gradations, and high rock content specimens exhibit high deviator stress.

The cohesion is 0.75–25.87 kPa, and the internal friction angle is 22.00–30.35°. The effective cohesion is 1.53–28.45 kPa, and the effective internal friction angle is 27.61–39.53°. Both the cohesion and effective cohesion decrease, and both the internal and effective internal friction angles increase as the axial strain increases from 10 to 20%, indicating an overall enhancement of the shear strength.

The influence of vegetation root reinforcement on the stability of the waste dump slope exhibits dual characteristics. Root system stabilization can significantly enhance the stability of waste dump slopes under average rainfall conditions (50–100 mm/d), where the FS increases with greater reinforcement depth. However, under extremely heavy rainfall conditions (> 200 mm/d), a permeability contrast between the root and soil composite zone and the underlying native soil can lead to the formation of a perched water table at the interface. This phenomenon may result in a decrease in the slope stability.
