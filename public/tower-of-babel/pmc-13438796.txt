Hypoxemia with atelectasis during clazosentan therapy after subarachnoid hemorrhage: a potential effect of suppressed hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
Authors: Daisuke Yamazaki, Asuka Nakamura, Mana Wakabayashi, Yota Suzuki, Kohei Kanaya, Tetsuyoshi Horiuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClazosentan, a selective endothelin-A (ET-A) receptor antagonist, is used to prevent cerebral vasospasm after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Although pulmonary edema and fluid retention are recognized adverse effects, hypoxemia associated with atelectasis has not been described. Endothelin-1 contributes to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV), which preserves ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) matching by diverting blood flow from poorly ventilated regions; pharmacologic ET-A blockade may attenuate HPV, increasing intrapulmonary shunt and impairing oxygenation. We report a man in his 60s with aneurysmal SAH (World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies [WFNS] I, Fisher 3) who underwent clipping surgery and postoperative clazosentan infusion (10 mg/h, standard Japanese dosage). On day 7 of infusion, hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90% room air) developed; on day 8, arterial blood gas showed PaO2 62.5 mmHg with a PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio of 109. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated right lower-lobe atelectasis without pulmonary edema or pleural effusion, and echocardiography showed normal cardiac function. Clazosentan was discontinued and corticosteroid therapy initiated, after which oxygenation improved and the atelectasis resolved. The patient was extubated on day 18 and discharged with good neurological recovery. This case illustrates a plausible mechanism whereby ET-A receptor blockade suppresses HPV, exacerbating V/Q mismatch in the presence of segmental atelectasis. Improvement after drug withdrawal supports this association. Clinicians should recognize that hypoxemia during clazosentan therapy may occur independently of fluid overload and warrants early respiratory evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Clazosentan, a selective endothelin-A (ET-A) receptor antagonist, was developed to prevent cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia following aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Large randomized clinical trials have shown that clazosentan significantly reduces the incidence of angiographic vasospasm, although its impact on clinical outcomes remains modest.1 Despite this benefit, systemic adverse reactions—including pulmonary edema, pleural effusion, and fluid retention—are frequently reported and are generally attributed to increased endothelial permeability and altered fluid dynamics.2

Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is not only a potent vasoconstrictor but also a key regulator of pulmonary vascular tone and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV).3,4 In the lungs, ET-1 contributes to HPV, an essential mechanism that maintains ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) matching by diverting blood flow away from poorly ventilated regions.4,5 Pharmacologic inhibition of ET-A receptors may blunt HPV, leading to increased perfusion of unventilated alveoli, intrapulmonary shunting, and hypoxemia.6

We report a patient with aneurysmal SAH who developed acute hypoxemia associated with segmental atelectasis during clazosentan therapy. This case raises the possibility of drug-induced hypoxemia through pharmacologic suppression of HPV and highlights the importance of close respiratory monitoring during endothelin antagonist therapy.

CASE PRESENTATION

A man in his 60s presented with a sudden headache and was diagnosed with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (Fig. 1A) caused by rupture of a left internal carotid artery aneurysm (World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies [WFNS] grade I, Fisher group 3). He had hypertension but no history of pulmonary or cardiac disease. Institutional review board approval at Shinshu University School of Medicine was not required for a single case report according to institutional policy. Informed consent for the use of clinical information was obtained from the patient in accordance with institutional policy. Emergency craniotomy and aneurysm clipping were performed on the day of admission without complications. Postoperatively, clazosentan infusion was started for vasospasm prevention at the approved dosage in Japan (300 mg in 500 mL saline, 17 mL/h; 10 mg/h). The patient’s hemodynamics remained stable, with neutral fluid balance maintained by intermittent diuretics.

On day 7 of infusion, hypoxemia developed (SpO2 < 90% on room air). On day 8, arterial blood gas analysis showed PaO2 62.5 mmHg, PaCO2 32.6 mmHg, and a PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio of 109. There was no fever, pulmonary crackles, or peripheral edema. Laboratory results showed C-reactive protein (CRP) 1.43 mg/dL and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) 11.8 pg/mL. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) on day 8 demonstrated right lower-lobe atelectasis without pulmonary edema or pleural effusion (Fig. 1B). Echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular function and no signs of heart failure. The atelectasis was considered to be related to postoperative hypoventilation and secretion retention. Sedation and analgesia were minimized, there was no clinical or radiologic evidence of aspiration, and no focal neurological deficit suggestive of diaphragmatic or phrenic nerve dysfunction was observed. Bronchoscopic evidence of mucus plugging was not obtained; however, no copious or tenacious secretions were noted.

The patient was intubated and mechanically ventilated for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure in the setting of right lower lobe atelectasis, and clazosentan was discontinued immediately. During the period of most severe hypoxemia (days 8–9), the patient was ventilated in synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) mode with a tidal volume of approximately 6.6 mL/kg, FiO2 0.4–0.6, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 13 cmH2O, with a pressure support of 8 cmH2O. Under these conditions, the calculated alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient was approximately 310 mmHg, indicating a substantial shunt component despite lung-protective ventilatory settings. Following discontinuation, oxygenation gradually improved and markedly recovered after corticosteroid therapy (methylprednisolone 125 mg/day for 8 days) combined with physiotherapy and postural drainage. Follow-up CT demonstrated re-expansion of the right lower lobe (Fig. 1C). He was extubated on day 18 with no recurrence of hypoxemia thereafter. The Naranjo adverse reaction score was 7 (“probable”). The patient completed 14 days of vasospasm prophylaxis without recurrence and was discharged with good neurological recovery (modified Rankin Scale = 1).

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates a reversible hypoxemic episode that developed during clazosentan therapy in the absence of pulmonary edema. A plausible explanation is pharmacologic inhibition of HPV through ET-A receptor blockade. Although the P/F ratio met the threshold for mild acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), imaging findings did not fulfill the Berlin criteria (bilateral opacities), and the condition was best characterized as acute hypoxemic respiratory failure with segmental atelectasis. Importantly, during the nadir of hypoxemia the P/F ratio fell to 109 despite moderate PEEP and lung-protective ventilatory settings, and the calculated alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient was markedly elevated. These physiological findings support the presence of a significant shunt component rather than isolated hypoventilation.

ET-1 is a key regulator of pulmonary vascular tone and contributes to HPV.4,5 Under physiological conditions, HPV redistributes pulmonary blood flow away from hypoventilated or atelectatic regions, maintaining V/Q matching and systemic oxygenation.5 The proposed mechanism linking ET-A receptor blockade to impaired HPV and intrapulmonary shunting is illustrated in Fig. 3. When ET-A receptors are inhibited, this compensatory vasoconstriction is blunted, allowing continued perfusion of non-ventilated alveoli and promoting right-to-left shunting.6,7

In the present case, right lower-lobe atelectasis created a localized hypoventilated region. During clazosentan administration, suppression of HPV likely enhanced perfusion to this region, leading to severe hypoxemia despite the limited extent of atelectasis. The subsequent improvement in oxygenation following clazosentan withdrawal, together with adjunctive therapies, is consistent with this mechanism. Corticosteroid therapy may have contributed to recovery through reduction of inflammatory changes and secondary improvement of lung compliance, but the temporal association between clazosentan withdrawal and early oxygenation improvement suggests a potential role of ET-A receptor blockade.

Previous clinical trials have described pulmonary edema and effusion as major adverse events of clazosentan,1,2 but hypoxemia secondary to altered pulmonary vascular regulation has not been reported. To address the possibility of fluid overload, we examined multiple indicators beyond the absence of pulmonary edema on CT. The patient maintained a near-neutral daily fluid balance (range, –916 to +1082 mL/day) with a cumulative balance of approximately –900 mL by the time hypoxemia developed. Body weight remained stable throughout hospitalization without any upward trend, arguing against progressive volume accumulation. BNP was low at admission (11.8 pg/mL) and showed no clinical signs of cardiac strain. Serial chest radiographs revealed no interstitial markings or pleural effusion. Taken together, these findings make a hydrostatic mechanism unlikely and support a non–fluid overload explanation for the patient’s hypoxemia. Even mild atelectasis can markedly impair oxygenation when HPV is pharmacologically inhibited because of the nonlinear relationship between shunt fraction and arterial oxygen tension.

We acknowledge that high-dose corticosteroid therapy, physiotherapy, and postural drainage were initiated around the same time as clazosentan discontinuation and likely contributed to the resolution of atelectasis. Therefore, the relative contribution of clazosentan withdrawal versus these co-interventions cannot be clearly separated, and our causal inference remains hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability score was 7 (“probable”), based on a temporal association with drug administration and withdrawal (+2), improvement after discontinuation (+1), lack of more likely alternative explanations (+2), and consistency with the known pharmacologic effects of ET-A receptor antagonism on pulmonary vascular tone (+2).

Clinicians should recognize that clazosentan may induce hypoxemia not only via fluid retention but also through disruption of pulmonary vascular homeostasis. Careful monitoring of oxygenation and prompt evaluation of pulmonary findings are warranted when unexplained respiratory failure occurs during endothelin receptor antagonist therapy.

This report has limitations. As a single observational case, the proposed mechanism of hypoxemia due to suppression of HPV cannot be confirmed. Direct hemodynamic or perfusion imaging data were not available to quantify V/Q mismatch. Nevertheless, the consistent temporal relationship between clazosentan exposure and reversible hypoxemia supports a probable causal association.

CONCLUSION

Clazosentan can induce hypoxemia through suppression of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, leading to intrapulmonary shunt in the presence of atelectasis. Awareness of this mechanism is important for early diagnosis and management of respiratory complications during vasospasm prophylaxis after subarachnoid hemorrhage.
