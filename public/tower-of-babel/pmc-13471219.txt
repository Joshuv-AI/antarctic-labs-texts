Interpreting homologous recombination deficiency in rare gynecological cancers: A case of primary mucinous endometrial adenocarcinoma of gastrointestinal type
Authors: Farhia Kabeer, Dawn Cochrane, Cam Grisdale, Branden J. Lynch, Melissa K. McConechy, Juliana Sobral de Barros, Lindsay Zibrik, Janine Senz, Amy Jamieson, Jessica N. McAlpine, Blake Gilks, Marco A. Marra, Janessa Laskin, Yvette Drew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucinous endometrial carcinoma of gastric type (MCG) is a rare and aggressive malignancy with no established standard of care. Biomarkers of homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), including BRCA1/2 alterations and genomic signatures, are increasingly used to predict sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi), but their utility in rare gynecologic cancers remains unclear.Case presentationWe report a 52-year-old female with metastatic TP53-abnormal endometrial MCG. Whole-genome and transcriptome analysis identified a pathogenic germline BRCA1 mutation with somatic loss of heterozygosity (LOH), resulting in biallelic BRCA1 loss. A high HRDetect score (0.96) further supported an HRD phenotype. The patient achieved an initial partial response to frontline platinum-based chemotherapy. However, despite genomic evidence predicting PARPi sensitivity, she experienced rapid disease progression within three months of completion of platinum and starting maintenance niraparib. Retrospective copy-number signature analysis classified the tumor as a non-HRD signature characterized by high ploidy rather than classic BRCA-associated genomic scarring. To further investigate treatment response, a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model was established. The model faithfully recapitulated the clinical phenotype, showing response to cisplatin but intrinsic resistance to the PARP inhibition.ConclusionThis case demonstrates a discordance between genomic HRD biomarkers and therapeutic response. Despite germline BRCA1 mutation, somatic LOH, and a high HRDetect score, platinum response was not durable and PARPi sensitivity was not observed. These findings highlight the limitations of current HRD biomarkers in rare gynecologic cancers and support integrating functional and genomic approaches to guide precision oncology.

Introduction

Deficiencies in the homologous recombination DNA repair (HRR) pathway result in accumulation of unrepaired DNA double-strand breaks and genomic instability (Li et al., 2025). Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is a characteristic of several cancers, most notably high-grade serous ovarian cancer, where ∼50% are HRD. The most common cause of HRD is biallelic inactivation of BRCA or other HRR genes (e.g., RAD51C, PALB2, ATM) or the epigenetic silencing of such genes (Li et al., 2025). Deleterious BRCA1/2 mutations and the associated HRD create a vulnerability such that they are considered actionable therapeutic targets in ovary, breast and prostate cancers. In these cancers, HRD can be exploited by PARP inhibitors (PARPi) through the principle of synthetic lethality and are now integrated into standard of care with significant survival benefits (Wang et al., 2020). The presence of pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations appear to be reliable predictive biomarkers for PARPi response in cancers traditionally associated with the hereditary cancer BRCA syndrome (i.e. breast, ovary, pancreas, prostate cancers). However, the use of BRCA1/2 m as predictive biomarkers of HRD and subsequent PARPi response in other cancers including rare cancers like MCG remains unclear. Jonsson et al (Jonsson et al., 2019) performed a pan-cancer analysis of over 800 patients across multiple tumor types harboring germline or somatic loss-of-function BRCA1/2 mutations to investigate lineage-dependent effects on HRD and potential PARP inhibitor sensitivity. Clinical benefit from PARP inhibition, assessed as time on treatment, was significantly greater in patients with cancers traditionally associated with BRCA1/2 mutations (breast, ovarian, pancreatic, and prostate; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.46–0.73; log-rank P = 3.7 × 10−6), whereas patients with other cancer types, including rarer tumors, showed no significant benefit (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.60–1.70; P = 0.984). These findings suggest that the dependency on BRCA1/2 mutations for tumorigenesis varies by tumor lineage, and in some non-canonical cancers, BRCA1/2 mutations may represent incidental events unrelated to pathogenesis and less likely to predict therapeutic response. In addition, BRCA1/2 mutations are not the only way a cancer can be HRD, HRD can occur in the absence of BRCA1/2 mutations, suggesting that a broader test of HRR function might be a more inclusive and reliable way to predict PARPi response in these cancers.

Current HRD testing methodologies fall into four categories: 1) single or panel HRR gene mutation testing, including germline and somatic BRCA1/2 and non-BRCA HRR genes such as RAD51C, RAD51D, and BRIP1; 2) genomic instability scarring assays using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array based algorithms to assess large-scale transitions (LST), loss of heterozygosity (LOH), and sub-chromosomal regions with telomeric allelic imbalance (NtA) (Miller et al., 2020); 3) copy-number signature tests and 4) functional assays such as γH2AX/RAD51 immunofluorescence (IF) testing.

In high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), HRD companion diagnostic tests, including the FDA-approved MyChoice CDx assay, are incorporated into routine clinical guidelines to identify patients most likely to benefit from first-line maintenance PARP inhibitors. This guidance is based on pivotal phase 3 trials demonstrating significant survival benefit in stage III/IV HGSOC, including both BRCAm/HRD and non-BRCAm/HRD subtypes (SOLO1/PAOLA-1/PRIMA (Tew et al., 2022). These HRD genomic scarring assays aim to predict current or historical HRD, but no test is perfect, and there is no consensus on which is best or whether results apply across cancer types. HRD scores vary across cancers due to differences in genomic instability and mutational processes. Even within HGSOC, assays can be discordant, and combining methodologies may improve predictive power.

In endometrial cancer (EC), there is no validated HRD test and the current literature on the frequency of HRD is discordant, ranging from 15 to 46%, using multiple HRD testing methods. Using a functional RAD51 IF assessment in a small cohort of p53 abnormal EC, de Jonge et al., reported a rate of 46% (de Jonge et al., 2023). Whole-exome sequencing of the EC cohort of the Cancer Genome Atlas revealed only 15% of cases could be considered HRD using CN high mutational signatures(Ashley et al., 2019). What is clear in EC is that HRD is enriched in specific histo-molecular EC subtypes, with the highest frequencies in TP53-abnormal (p53abn) serous cancers and carcinosarcomas.

We present a rare case of metastatic mucinous, p53-abnormal endometrial carcinoma (p53abn EC) with a germline BRCA1 mutation in which genomic testing suggested homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), yet the tumor was intrinsically resistant to PARP inhibitor therapy. This case demonstrates that BRCA mutations or high HRD scores alone may be insufficient to predict PARPi sensitivity in rare cancers, highlighting challenges for molecular tumor boards (MTB) and limitations of histology-agnostic, HRD-based treatment recommendations in non-traditional settings.

Primary endometrial mucinous carcinoma of gastric (gastrointestinal) type (MCG) is a rare, aggressive cancer, with ∼30 cases reported in the literature (Fadare et al., 2025). In 2020, the WHO recognized it as a distinct endometrial cancer subtype (WHO Classification of Tumors Editorial Board, 2026). Case reports suggest that the most frequent aberrations in MCGs are in the TP53, KRAS and PIK3CA genes (Fadare et al., 2025). Data to guide management remain limited.

Case report

A 52-year-old, pre-menopausal female presented with heavy intermenstrual bleeding, increasing fatigue and shortness of breath. She had no comorbidities and no family history of cancer.

Subsequent investigations (endometrial biopsy and CT scan) demonstrated a high-grade endometrial adenocarcinoma with mucinous morphology with multiple lung, lymph node (including para-aortic and mediastinal) and liver metastases. The molecular subtype was identified on the diagnostic endometrial biopsy as p53abn. CA125, CEA and CA19–9 tumor markers were elevated at 170, 51 and 74 respectively. Due to the heavy bleeding, the patient underwent palliative debulking surgery which included: hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and omentectomy.

Surgical pathology indicated a primary mucinous endometrial adenocarcinoma of gastric (gastrointestinal) type. FIGO stage 4b, TP53 abnormal molecular subtype, ER negative, HER2 negative, mismatch repair proficient.

Given the rarity of this tumor type and limited data to guide therapy, the patient was enrolled in the Personalized OncoGenomics (POG) program (NCT02155621) at BC Cancer Centre, Vancouver, and consented to tissue banking for patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model development through the BC Gynecological Tissue Bank (REB#: H22–03493). POG is a single-arm prospective study performing whole genome and transcriptome analysis (WGTA) in high-risk of recurrence or metastatic cancer patients. The program provides clinically actionable findings via WGTA reports and weekly multi-disciplinary molecular tumor board (MTB) discussions. WGTA results are reviewed at MTB, and clinicians may choose to treat patients using WGTA-guided options. Detailed tissue collection, sequencing, and analysis are described in (Pleasance et al., 2022).

Whilst awaiting the results of the WGTA analysis, the patient commenced 1st line palliative standard of care systemic chemotherapy for endometrial cancer with carboplatin and paclitaxel (combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors was not available at this time).

The WGTA analysis was performed on the primary hysterectomy FFPE (formalin fixed paraffin embedded) specimen and a fresh frozen endometrial biopsy taken at the of surgery. WGTA findings

The most clinically actionable findings, based on the FFPE sample data, was determined to be the germline pathogenic loss of function BRCA1 mutation (NM_007294.4(BRCA1):c.4372C > T (p.Gln1458Ter)), and tumor loss-of-heterozygosity (LOH), resulting in biallelic loss of BRCA1. The germline BRCA1 variant is deemed pathogenic in clinvar (Variation ID: 55183) and previously reviewed by cancer molecular geneticists as a pathogenic variant, therefore the patient and her family were referred to the hereditary cancer genetics program. At the time of the MTB case discussion, the patient had already completed three cycles of the platinum doublet with a partial response with 57.8% reduction in measurable target lesions (lung, liver and multi-site lymph node metastases) and normalization of tumor markers (Fig. 1B). Two HRD scores were predicted from whole genome mutation data as part of standard analysis. The calculated HRD score was 35, an unweighted sum of large-scale alterations including LOH, TAI (telomeric allelic imbalance), and LST (large-scale state transitions), below the ≥42 threshold for HRD. A second tool, HRDetect, which incorporates the HRD score along with complex SNV and rearrangement-based mutation signatures, predicted an HRD tumor with a score of 0.96, consistent with BRCA1 loss of function. The patient's case and the POG findings were discussed at Molecular Tumor Boards. It was recognized that MCG are rare cancers with limited evidence to guide treatment. However, the genomic findings and the patient's initial radiological, biochemical and clinical response to platinum-based chemotherapy, could be indicative of an HRD phenotype. Accepting the lack of data to support this approach in MCG and the uncertainty of any response, the role of PARP inhibitors was discussed as a potential therapeutic option, recommendation evidence level IPR-C (Li et al., 2017).

The patient subsequently completed 6 cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel with ongoing response and commenced maintenance PARP inhibitor therapy with niraparib through a compassionate drug access program. However, within three months, her cancer progressed with rising tumor markers and increasing thoracic metastases. The patient experienced marked functional decline, was deemed unfit for further therapy, and transitioned to palliative care, dying shortly after discontinuing niraparib.

Copy number signature suggests non-HRD tumor

The genomic analyses including the germline BRCA1m and a high HRDetect score suggested this mucinous endometrial adenocarcinoma of gastric (gastrointestinal) type was likely to be HRD and might respond to PARP inhibition. In response to the clinical picture of this cancer's inherent PARPi resistance, copy number signature (CN) analysis was performed retrospectively on the FFPE tumor material and compared with an existing database of p53abn EC CN signatures, denoted VS1-VS5. Our previous work using shallow whole genome sequencing (sWGS) in 187 p53abn ECs identified five distinct CN signatures, with signature VS5 being associated with BRCA1/2 CN loss and features similar to HGSOC HRD signature 5 (Jamieson et al., 2024). The complex copy number profile, with marked genomic instability (Fig. 2A) and TP53 mutation status (Table 1) of this tumor are in line with the TCGA CN-high group (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network et al., 2013) and the analogous p53abn molecular subtype (Jamieson et al., 2023). Despite the HRD status suggested by the germline BRCA1 loss-of-function mutation and high HRDetect score, this cancer was classified as signature VS4, not VS5, the putative HRD signature. Patient derived xenograft model

In addition to the WGTA and CN signature analysis, treatment naive, patient-derived tumor tissue, taken from surgery were implanted subcutaneously into NRG (NOD-Rag1−/− IL2rg−/−) immunodeficient mice. This work was performed in retrospect after the patient's death. Once tumors reached an appropriate size, mice were randomized into three treatment arms: 1. Cisplatin at 2 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally twice per week over a 21-day period. 2. PARPi Olaparib at 100 mg/kg, administered once daily via oral gavage for 21 days (olaparib was used instead of niraparib as the mice protocol was already approved at our institute) and 3. Vehicle Control Group. Tumor volumes were monitored regularly throughout the treatment period using caliper measurements. At the end of the treatment cycle, tumor growth trajectories were compared across the three groups to evaluate therapeutic efficacy. The results demonstrate overall resistance to the PARPi olaparib with tumor volumes rising over time in contrast to the cisplatin-treated PDX, in which sustained tumor growth suppression was seen; these findings mirror the patient's clinical response to treatment (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

Primary mucinous endometrial adenocarcinoma of gastric (gastrointestinal) type is a rare, aggressive cancer with limited data supporting precision medicine approaches to therapy.

Genomic analysis of this MCG highlighted a potential therapeutic target, with a BRCA1m and a high HRDetect score indicating HRD. However, the patient did not have a clinical response to PARPi or a durable response to platinum despite the initial sensitivity. Retrospective assessment using CN signatures identified the cancer as non-HRD, which was discordant with the patient having a pathogenic germline BRCA variant, a high HRDetect score. Additionally, the HRD score (LOH + TAI + LST) was below clinical ovarian HRD threshold, also indicating non-HRD. Both LSTs and the features defining CN signatures are accessible using sWGS, so these features may represent essentially equivalent readouts of the HRD status. While it remains possible that a reversion mutation restored the function of BRCA1 during treatment, accounting for the tumor's resistance to PARPi treatment, this would not explain the lack of response to PARPi observed in the cisplatin/PARPi naive, untreated PDX model.

This case highlights the challenges MTBs face when interpreting the clinical significance of BRCA1/2 m and HRD scores in non-traditionally associated BRCAm and rare gynecological cancers. Where possible the integration of genomic data with functional assessments such as PDX modeling may better validate personalized strategies. We acknowledge that this approach presents logistical challenges in real-world clinical practice. Nevertheless, this case underscores the importance of collaboration to establish local networks for biobanking and global comprehensive databases of multi-omic aberrations, linked with annotated clinical responses to specific therapies in rare gynecological cancers. Such resources would provide valuable evidence to support future clinical decision-making.

These findings underscore the limitations of histology-agnostic HRD-based treatment recommendations and emphasize precision oncology approaches that account for the unique genomic and phenotypic landscape of rare tumor subtypes.
