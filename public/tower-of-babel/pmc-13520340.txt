GPNMB-ECD drives acquired resistance to osimertinib in NSCLC via inducing tumor cell cytoskeletal reorganization
Authors: Min Zhang, Jia-Qi Wang, Xiao Yang, Wen-Yi Liu, Yu-Tang Huang, Chun-Jie Wen, Dan Chen, Lan-Xiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcquired resistance constitutes a major obstacle to the efficacy of osimertinib therapy in nonsmall-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. While the soluble extracellular domain of GPNMB (GPNMB-ECD) is recognized as a driver of tumor progression, its involvement in acquired resistance to osimertinib remains unknown.PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the role of GPNMB-ECD in acquired osimertinib resistance in NSCLC.MethodsThe expression dynamics of GPNMB in osimertinib-sensitive (OS) and osimertinib-resistant (OR) NSCLC tissues and cell lines were profiled using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA-seq, respectively. Full-length GPNMB and GPNMB-ECD levels in tumor tissues and patient plasma were assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In vitro, the impact of GPNMB-ECD on osimertinib sensitivity was evaluated using CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, and transwell assays. Correspondingly, in vivo effects were investigated in nude mouse models of subcutaneous and pulmonary metastasis. The underlying mechanisms were explored through co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), immunofluorescence, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Finally, the therapeutic efficacy of the anti-GPNMB-ECD antibody was evaluated in humanized patient-derived xenograft (huPDX) models.ResultsTumor cells exhibited elevated GPNMB expression in OR tissues compared with OS controls and emerged as the primary source of GPNMB-ECD within the OR TME. Plasma levels of GPNMB-ECD were substantially higher in patients with OR NSCLC compared with patients with OS NSCLC, suggesting this marker as a potential indicator of acquired resistance and poor prognosis. Functional studies confirmed that GPNMB-ECD promotes osimertinib resistance both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, GPNMB-ECD engages syndecan-4 (SDC4) on tumor cells, triggering SDC4 phosphorylation, F-actin reorganization, and Yes-associated protein (YAP) nuclear translocation, thereby upregulating pro-tumorigenic genes and reducing osimertinib sensitivity. Crucially, anti-GPNMB-ECD antibodies restored osimertinib sensitivity in huPDX models established from human NSCLC tumors.ConclusionsOur findings define GPNMB-ECD-driven resistance as a novel paradigm in NSCLC and identify a viable precision therapeutic strategy to overcome it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00965-1.

Background

According to GLOBOCAN 2022, lung cancer was the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. Nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents nearly 85% of all cases. Despite significant advancements in therapeutics, the 5-year survival rate for patients with NSCLC remains dismally poor [2]. Fortunately, identifying oncogenic driver mutations in NSCLC has unlocked targeted therapies, offering a promising path to better outcomes for carefully selected patients. Among the most critical oncogenic drivers are mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene [3]. Consequently, the development and clinical implementation of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) have yielded substantial benefits for patients with tumors harboring activating EGFR mutations [4].

Osimertinib, an oral third-generation EGFR-TKI exhibiting selective and irreversible activity against both activating EGFR mutations and the T790M resistance mutation, is currently a standard first-line therapy for treatment-naïve patients and second-line therapy for those who have progressed on earlier-generation EGFR-TKIs [5]. Unfortunately, despite initial responses, most patients inevitably develop acquired resistance within 9–15 months, leading to disease progression [6]. While established mechanisms encompass EGFR-dependent pathways (e.g., secondary mutations) and EGFR-independent bypass signaling [7], the underlying basis for resistance remains undetermined in 40–50% of first-line and 30–40% of second-line cases [8], underscoring an urgent need for comprehensive mechanistic investigations and novel therapeutic strategies to counteract this challenge.

Glycoprotein nonmelanoma protein B (GPNMB), alternatively termed osteoactivin (OA), dendritic cell-heparin integrin ligand (DC-HIL), or hematopoietic growth factor inducible neurokinin-1 type (HGFIN), is a glycosylated type I transmembrane protein with broad tissue distribution that plays pivotal roles in physiological processes including bone homeostasis, neural maintenance, melanogenesis, and immune regulation [9]. GPNMB is often highly expressed on the cell surface of diverse cancer types, including triple-negative breast cancer, melanoma, gliomas, and prostate cancer, where it undergoes A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase 10 (ADAM10)-mediated ectodomain shedding to generate soluble extracellular domain (ECD) fragments. These GPNMB-ECD fragments interact with multiple cell surface receptors, including CD44, Syndecan-4 (SDC4), Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (NKA), EGFR, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), and integrins, thereby activating autocrine and paracrine signaling cascades that drive tumor cell proliferation and metastatic dissemination [10]. Although these advances have considerably elucidated the pleiotropic oncogenic functions of GPNMB-ECD, its potential role in mediating osimertinib resistance remains poorly characterized.

This study demonstrates, for the first time, that tumor cell-derived GPNMB-ECD drives acquired osimertinib resistance in NSCLC by engaging the SDC4/α-Actinin axis to orchestrate actin cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby promoting Yes-associated protein (YAP) nuclear translocation and the subsequent expression of pro-tumorigenic genes. Targeting GPNMB-ECD effectively reverses acquired osimertinib resistance in humanized patient-derived xenograft (huPDX) models. These findings reveal a pivotal role for GPNMB-ECD in osimertinib resistance, elucidate its underlying mechanism, and identify a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with NSCLC who develop acquired resistance to osimertinib.

Methods

Patient cohort and sample collection

Tumor specimens and plasma samples were collected from patients with NSCLC with adenocarcinoma at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. This study included two cohorts: (i) a discovery cohort (n = 60), comprising 40 osimertinib-sensitive (OS) and 20 osimertinib-resistant (OR) cases and (ii) a comparative cohort (n = 16), including 5 pairs of erlotinib-sensitive/resistant cases and 3 pairs of dacomitinib-sensitive/resistant cases. All patients harbored EGFR-sensitizing mutations (exon 19 deletion or L858R). Sensitive patients were defined by progression-free survival (PFS) exceeding 9 months on EGFR‑TKI treatment; resistant patients were defined by progressive disease (PD) after achieving complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD) lasting 3 months or longer following EGFR‑TKI treatment, confirmed by RECIST 1.1 criteria [11]. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics, including EGFR mutation profiles and treatment responses, are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants under ethics committee approval (2022-K432), and the study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200066004).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

For fresh tumor tissue, scRNA-seq was performed on specimens after enzymatic digestion using the Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, China). After confirming > 85% cell viability (Countess® II FL), single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10 × Genomics Chromium Controller (v3.1 chemistry). Sequencing libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10 × Genomics PN-1000268) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired-end; target depth 20,000 reads/cell). Raw data were processed with the Cell Ranger pipeline (v7.1.0) and aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using Seurat (v5.0.1). Cells were included for downstream analysis only if they expressed between 500 and 5000 genes and maintained mitochondrial content below 20%. Data integration and batch correction were performed using Harmony (v1.0), with subsequent dimensionality reduction via UMAP and graph-based clustering at a resolution of 0.8. To distinguish malignant tumor cells from normal epithelial cells, epithelial cells were first extracted and subjected to subclustering analysis. The gene expression matrix of epithelial cells was then merged with that of mast cells, which served as the diploid reference, and CNV analysis was performed using inferCNV (v1.18.1). Epithelial cells exhibiting significantly elevated CNV scores compared with mast cells were inferred as malignant tumor cells. Cell type annotation was performed by cross-referencing expression profiles against the ImmGen database supplemented with canonical markers. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the MAST algorithm with significance thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC|> 0.25. For public databases [12], scRNA-seq data were re-analyzed using the same analytical pipeline, including cell type annotation and CNV-based tumor cell inference with mast cells as a diploid reference.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

For cultured cells, total RNA was extracted from cell lines using the mirVana™ RNA Isolation Kit (Ambion, USA). RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), retaining only samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7. Libraries were prepared with the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit with Ribo-Zero Gold and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (paired-end 150 bp). Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For public databases, bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE121634 [13] and GSE168043 [14]) were analyzed.

Cell culture

Human NSCLC cell lines HCC827 (CVCL_2063) and H1975 (CVCL_1511) were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Beijing, China) and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. OR cell lines (H1975/OR and HCC827/OR) were established in our laboratory [15]. Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and then treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using 1 μg RNA with PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). qPCR was conducted in triplicate using TB Green Supermix (Takara, Japan) on a Bio-Rad CFX96™ system. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Membrane proteins were extracted using the Mem-PER PLUS Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separated using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China). For secreted protein detection, conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatants were mixed with an equal volume of 2 × sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer and boiled for 10 min. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were loaded per lane, separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), and transferred to 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using Immobilon enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Millipore, USA), captured on a ChemiDoc MP System (Bio-Rad, USA). Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0). Target protein expression levels were normalized to their respective loading controls (GAPDH for total protein, ATP1A1 for membrane protein, H3 for nuclear protein, and Ponceau S staining for secreted protein). All quantifications were performed from at least three independent experiments. Detailed antibody information is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Recombinant GPNMB-ECD (rGPNMB-ECD) generation and anti-GPNMB-ECD neutralizing antibody construction

HEK293T cells were transfected with pCMV14-Flag-GPNMB-ECD using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Then, 6 h post-transfection, cells were incubated in FreeStyle™ serum-free medium (Gibco, USA) for 48 h. Conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 min to pellet debris, then concentrated using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore, USA). rGPNMB-ECD protein was purified with Anti-Flag M2 Affinity Gel (Merck, USA), eluted with 3 × Flag peptide, sterile-filtered through 0.22 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and stored at −80 °C.

To generate monoclonal antibodies against human GPNMB-ECD, 6-week-old female BALB/c mice were immunized intraperitoneally with 50 μg of rGPNMB-ECD emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA; Sigma-Aldrich). This was followed by three weekly booster injections of the same antigen dose emulsified in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA; Sigma-Aldrich). Hybridoma generation was performed by fusing splenocytes from immunized mice with SP2/0-Ag14 myeloma cells. Positive clones were selected in HAT medium by ELISA screening for GPNMB-ECD binding. Subsequent subcloning yielded one stable hybridoma cell line producing the desired antibody. The specificity and neutralizing activity of the anti-GPNMB-ECD antibody were validated by western blot, ELISA, and competitive binding assays, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

GPNMB-ECD levels in patient plasma and cell culture supernatants were quantified using a human GPNMB ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meibiao Biological Technology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To assess the neutralizing activity of the anti-GPNMB-ECD antibody, a competitive ELISA was performed. For binding assays, increasing concentrations of rGPNMB-ECD were immobilized onto ELISA plates, followed by incubation with recombinant SDC4 (ab243162, Abcam, UK). Binding was detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-SDC4 antibody. For competition assays, rGPNMB-ECD was immobilized and pre-incubated with increasing concentrations of anti-GPNMB-ECD antibody or an isotype control immunoglobulin G (IgG) prior to the addition of SDC4. All samples were measured in duplicate to calculate mean optical density (OD) values, and each experiment was performed three times independently.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence

For IHC, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned at 5 μm, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, then stained with an EnVision System (Dako, USA). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. For each tissue sample, at least five randomly selected nonoverlapping fields were captured. Following color deconvolution (H-DAB), the integrated optical density (IOD) of positive staining in each field was calculated as the sum of [(pixel intensity − background intensity) × pixel area]. The IOD values from all five fields were then summed to obtain the total IOD per sample. For graphical presentation, the total IOD values were expressed in units of 103 and denoted as Sum IOD × 103. For immunofluorescence, cells grown on coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and quenched with 0.1 M NH4Cl. Coverslips were blocked with 5% normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies or phalloidin. Following washes, coverslips were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence quantification was performed on confocal images by measuring mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) within at least five randomly selected regions of interest (ROIs) of consistent size per experiment. Background fluorescence intensity was subtracted from each measurement, and the average MFI value from all ROIs was used for statistical analysis. Nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio: nuclear translocation of target proteins was quantified as: nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio = (MFInucleus − MFIbackground) / (MFIcytoplasm − MFIbackground), with nuclear and cytoplasmic regions delineated on the basis of DAPI staining. For each cell, at least 20 cells per sample were analyzed, and the average ratio was calculated. All evaluations were conducted independently by two blinded pathologists, with antibody details presented in Supplementary Table 3.

DNA constructs and transfection

The coding sequences of full-length GPNMB and SDC4 were PCR-amplified from NSCLC cell cDNA and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 plasmid. The wild-type GPNMB-ECD and its domain-deletion mutants (GPNMB-ECD ΔKRFR and GPNMB-ECD ΔRGD) were cloned into the pCMV14 vector. These plasmids were generated by GeneCreate Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). For knockdown experiments, cells were transfected with gene-specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting GPNMB (5′-GAAGAACGATCGAAATTCA-3′) or SDC4 (5′-GGCAGGAATCTGATGACTT-3′). All constructs and siRNAs were delivered into NSCLC cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vitro drug susceptibility test

For cell viability assessment, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well. Following 24 h incubation, cells were treated with graded concentrations of osimertinib (TargetMol, China) for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corp., Japan). For colony formation assays, cells were seeded at 1 × 103 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 weeks. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (15 min). For apoptosis assessment, cells were harvested and stained with an Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry. For cell migration and invasion assays, 0.5 × 105 cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper compartments of Transwell chambers (BD Biosciences, Germany), with or without Matrigel precoating. Medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, the cells on the lower side were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis

Membrane protein lysates were precleared with Protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz, USA) at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rocking. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the clarified supernatant was incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h with gentle rocking at 4 °C. Next, 35 µL of fresh Protein A/G beads was added to each immunoprecipitation mixture, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rocking. The next day, complexes were washed five times with cold 1 × Co-IP buffer (Beyotime, China). Bound proteins were eluted by denaturation in 2 × SDS sample buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by western blot analysis. Complete antibody information is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells cultured on Polysine™ Microscope Adhesion Slides (Solarbio, China) were washed with PBS and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Subsequent processing, performed by Chengdu Lilai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China), included post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide followed by dehydration through a graded acetone series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% with three changes in absolute acetone). Samples were then infiltrated sequentially with acetone-Epon 812 mixtures at increasing resin concentrations (3:1, 1:1, and 1:3) before final embedding in pure Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections (60–90 nm thickness) were prepared using an ultramicrotome and collected on copper grids. Sections were stained with uranyl acetate for 10 min and lead citrate for 1 min at room temperature. Imaging was performed using a JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Experimental Committee of Chongqing Medical University and performed following the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Six-week-old BALB/c-nude mice and humanized huHSC-NCG-M mice (GemPharmatech, USA) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Experiment Center of Chongqing Medical University and randomized for experiments.

For the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model, BALB/c-nude mice were subcutaneously injected with 3 × 106 tumor cells. Once the tumor volume reached 150 mm3, mice were randomly allocated to different experimental groups and treated with osimertinib (p.o. 10 mg/kg), rGPNMB-ECD (i.t. 5 μg in 100 μL), or anti-GPNMB-ECD (i.p. 10 mg/kg), three times weekly for 3 weeks. Tumor volumes were measured three times weekly by blinded operators using vernier calipers (calculated as L × W2/2) until reaching ethical endpoints (1500 mm3 or ulceration). For the metastatic model, tumor cells were injected into the lateral tail veins at a dose of 3 × 106 cells/mouse. After experiments were completed, the animals were euthanized, and the subcutaneous or metastatic tumors were collected for further research.

For the huPDX model evaluation, three osimertinib-sensitive PDX lines were first established from three distinct patients with NSCLC harboring EGFR-activating mutants. After expansion in immunodeficient mice, tumor fragments (each measuring 3 × 3 × 3 mm) from each PDX line were subcutaneously implanted into three huHSC-NCG-M mice, yielding a total of nine mice (three per patient-derived tumor). When the tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, huPDX mice were randomly divided into three treatment groups and treated with control vehicle/IgG, osimertinib, or osimertinib plus anti-GPNMB-ECD, three times weekly. Each group consisted of three mice, with each mouse carrying tissue from a distinct patient. Tumors were measured twice a week until the volume of three consecutive measurements increased by ≥ 10% relative to the previous measurement, at which point drug resistance induction was considered successful.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software after rigorous statistical validation, including assessments of normality and variance homogeneity. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. Tumor growth curves were analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate diagnostic value, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated. Correlations were assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. For scRNA-seq data, differential expression was analyzed using the MAST algorithm with FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 0.25. Statistical significance thresholds were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 for all two-sided tests, with appropriate multiplicity adjustments applied to post hoc comparisons.

Results

GPNMB-ECD is a potential biomarker for acquired osimertinib resistance and poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC

To characterize the changes in GPNMB expression in NSCLC following acquired resistance to osimertinib, we conducted scRNA-seq on tumor tissues from five osimertinib-sensitive (OS) and five osimertinib-resistant (OR) patients. After quality control, 10,370 cells were available for analysis, each expressing an average of 500–7,500 genes and 50,000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis identified six distinct cell populations: endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, mast cells, and myeloid cells (Fig. 1, A and B). Tumor cells were subsequently distinguished from the nontumor epithelial cells via copy number variation (CNV) profiling (Fig. 1C). Notably, cell type proportions differed substantially between OS and OR tissues. OR tissues showed higher proportions of tumor cells, lymphocytes, and myeloid cells than OS tissues. By contrast, proportions of other cell types were reduced (Fig. 1D). For the seven annotated cell types, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OR tissues compared with OS tissues using thresholds of |log₂FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. The top five upregulated and downregulated genes for each cell type are shown in Fig. 1E. Significantly elevated GPNMB mRNA levels were specifically detected in tumor cells and not in any other cell types. To further investigate the potential role of GPNMB in mediating acquired osimertinib resistance in NSCLC, we conducted bulk RNA-seq on paired NSCLC cell line models comprising parental OS cells (HCC827OS and H1975OS) and their derivative resistant counterparts (HCC827OR and H1975OR), which had been established previously [15]. Comparative transcriptomic profiling revealed 134 DEGs in HCC827OR versus HCC827OS cells (98 upregulated and 36 downregulated), while 492 mRNAs were differentially expressed between H1975OR and H1975OS cells (195 upregulated and 297 downregulated). Intersection analysis identified 10 consistently dysregulated mRNA transcripts shared between both pairs, a finding subsequently confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 2A–C). Remarkably, GPNMB emerged as the most prominently upregulated gene among these candidates. Compared with sensitive controls, resistant cells exhibited increased GPNMB mRNA levels (Fig. 2D), elevated GPNMB protein levels in cell lysates, and enhanced GPNMB-ECD secretion into culture supernatants (Fig. 2E and F). We next quantified full-length GPNMB (by selectively analyzing IHC staining in tumor cell regions) and its ECD levels in tumor tissues and plasma from 40 OS and 20 OR patients. While full-length GPNMB expression increased moderately (2.54-fold) in OR versus OS tissues, its soluble derivative, GPNMB-ECD, showed a dramatic 11.28-fold elevation in OR patient plasma (Fig. 2G and H). Notably, the increase in secreted GPNMB-ECD was markedly greater than that of the full-length GPNMB protein. ROC curve analysis confirmed the strong diagnostic potential of plasma GPNMB-ECD for identifying osimertinib resistance, with theAUC of 0.888 (95% CI, 0.797–0.979) (Fig. 2I). Moreover, elevated plasma GPNMB-ECD levels were associated with worse overall survival (log-rank P = 0.004; Fig. 2J), indicating a poorer prognosis in this patient cohort. To determine whether GPNMB upregulation is specific to osimertinib resistance, we examined GPNMB expression in samples sensitive or resistant to first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs (erlotinib and dacomitinib). IHC of patient tissues and analysis of public bulk RNA-seq datasets revealed no significant differences in GPNMB protein or mRNA levels between resistant and sensitive cases (Fig. S2, B and C). Re-analysis of a public scRNA-seq cohort [12] showed no significant difference in GPNMB expression between erlotinib-sensitive and -resistant malignant tumor cells (Fig. S2, D–I).

These findings identify tumor cell-secreted GPNMB-ECD as a potential circulating biomarker specifically associated with acquired osimertinib resistance and poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC.

GPNMB-ECD facilitates acquired osimertinib resistance in vitro and in vivo

To elucidate the functional impact of GPNMB-ECD on osimertinib response in NSCLC, we employed complementary gain- and loss-of-function approaches in vitro. Specifically, OS cells were subjected to GPNMB overexpression or treated with rGPNMB-ECD, while OR cells were subjected to GPNMB knockdown or treated with anti-GPNMB-ECD neutralizing antibodies. Results derived from CCK-8 viability assays, colony-formation assays, flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis, and transwell migration/invasion assays collectively demonstrated that under osimertinib exposure, both GPNMB overexpression and rGPNMB-ECD treatment substantially augmented the malignant behaviors of sensitive cells, manifesting as enhanced cell viability, elevated colony-forming potential, profound inhibition of apoptosis, and accelerated migratory and invasive capabilities. SDC4, a ubiquitously expressed transmembrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan, has been implicated in the progression of multiple malignancies, including NSCLC [17–19]. However, its involvement in acquired osimertinib resistance remains undefined. Our investigation revealed significantly elevated SDC4 expression in OR cells and clinical specimens compared with sensitive controls (Fig. 5C–E). Notably, a significant positive correlation was observed between tissue SDC4 protein levels and plasma GPNMB-ECD concentrations in patients with NSCLC, indicating a biologically and clinically relevant association (Fig. 5F). Previous studies have established that the RGD (64–66) and KRFR (23–26) domains within GPNMB-ECD are critical for its interactions with heparin and heparan sulfate [20–22]. Therefore, we investigated whether these motifs are also required for GPNMB-ECD-SDC4 interaction. We generated Flag-tagged rGPNMB-ECD truncation mutants lacking either the RGD (ΔRGD) or the KRFR (ΔKRFR) motif. Co-IP assays in H1975OR cells demonstrated that deletion of either the RGD or KRFR motif drastically reduced the rGPNMB-ECD-SDC4 interaction, indicating that both motifs are essential for this binding (Fig. 5G). To assess the dependency of GPNMB-ECD-mediated osimertinib resistance on SDC4, we conducted rescue experiments in OS cells. As expected, SDC4 knockdown markedly abrogated the effects of rGPNMB-ECD on cellular proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion following osimertinib treatment (Fig. S3). These findings demonstrate that GPNMB-ECD confers osimertinib resistance in NSCLC cells primarily through its functional interaction with SDC4.

α-Actinin, a ubiquitous cytoskeletal protein and member of the F-actin cross-linking superfamily, not only stabilizes the cytoskeleton by cross-linking actin filaments but also serves as a critical scaffold. It connects diverse transmembrane receptors to the cytoskeleton, thereby transducing extracellular mechanical and biochemical cues and regulating receptor activity [26]. Prior studies have demonstrated that direct SDC4-α-Actinin interaction drives microfilament bundling in fibroblasts [22]. Consistent with these findings, our data revealed that rGPNMB-ECD treatment markedly enhanced F-actin bundle formation in OS cells, an effect abrogated by SDC4 knockdown. Conversely, anti-GPNMB-ECD profoundly disrupted F-actin reorganization in OR cells, whereas SDC4 overexpression restored cytoskeletal integrity (Fig. 6C).

Cellular nuclear morphology displays dynamic plasticity regulated by cytoplasmic and nucleoplasmic factors, including actin cytoskeletal reorganization. The physical forces exerted by the dynamic actin cytoskeleton can deform the nucleus, leading to nuclear elongation and dilation of the nuclear pores. This process further facilitates nuclear import of Yes-associated protein (YAP), a mechanosensitive transcription factor, thereby activating its downstream target genes [27–29]. Aligned with these mechanisms, rGPNMB-ECD treatment induced pronounced nuclear deformation in OS cells, characterized by increased nuclear dimensions (length and height) and dilated nuclear pores. These morphological changes were accompanied by enhanced nuclear accumulation of YAP and upregulation of targeted pro-oncogenic transcripts, including forkhead box M1 (FOXM1), matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), and myeloid cell leukemia 1 (MCL-1). Conversely, anti-GPNMB-ECD triggered nuclear condensation, accompanied by reduced YAP nuclear localization and reduced expression of its target oncogenes in resistant cells. Notably, SDC4 knockdown reversed the rGPNMB-ECD-induced phenotype in sensitive cells, while SDC4 overexpression rescued the anti-GPNMB-ECD effects in resistant cells (Fig. 6D–H).

These results reveal that GPNMB-ECD binding to SDC4 induces Ser179 phosphorylation, enhances SDC4-α-Actinin interaction and triggers F-actin reorganization, nuclear deformation and YAP nuclear translocation, culminating in the upregulation of pro-tumorigenic genes, and decreased osimertinib sensitivity in NSCLC cells.

Discussion

The functional significance of GPNMB-ECD in anticancer therapy has yet to be fully elucidated. Our study provides the first evidence that GPNMB expression is elevated in NSCLC cells exhibiting acquired resistance to osimertinib, and its ECD fragment drives this resistant phenotype by engaging the SDC4/α-Actinin axis to induce cytoskeletal reorganization, thereby promoting YAP nuclear translocation and decreasing osimertinib sensitivity (Fig. 8). SDC4, a member of the syndecan (SDC) family, transduces both chemical and mechanical signals across the plasma membrane. Unlike other SDCs, SDC4 is ubiquitously expressed in nearly all cell types. Through heparan sulfate glycosaminoglycan (HS-GAG) chains on its extracellular domain, SDC4 binds diverse ligands, including growth factors, chemokines, morphogens, and extracellular matrix components. These interactions modulate its engagement with cytoplasmic proteins, establishing SDC4 as a central regulator of cell proliferation, migration, and adhesion [31]. For example, the cytoplasmic domain of SDC4 binds phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), thereby activating protein kinase Cα (PKCα), which subsequently activates downstream effectors such as transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) channels and Ras homolog family member A (RhoA). Notably, phosphorylation at Ser179 of SDC4 induced by ligand binding leads to a conformational change in SDC4 and weakens its interaction with PIP2 [31]. However, the effect of Ser179 phosphorylation on SDC4’s affinity for cytoplasmic partners seems partner-specific. While it disrupts PIP2 binding, this modification is required for SDC4 to interact with Tiam1, thereby promoting Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1) activity [25]. Here, we further demonstrate that GPNMB-ECD engagement with SDC4 elicits Ser179 phosphorylation, a modification that subsequently strengthens its interaction with α-Actinin and drives F-actin cytoskeletal reorganization.

The actin cytoskeleton plays a central role in reshaping nuclear architecture during extracellular signal transduction. As established previously, stressed F-actin fibers or F-actin-bundling proteins specifically engage nesprin and other nuclear envelope (NE) proteins to form Linkers of the Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton (LINC) complexes [28, 32]. These complexes bridge cytoplasmic cytoskeletal elements with the nucleoplasmic lamina, creating a continuous mechanical pathway for force transmission from the cytoskeleton directly to the nucleus[33]. Consequently, this force transmission induces nuclear deformation and flattening while concurrently expanding nuclear pore diameters. This structural rearrangement increases pore exposure to the cytoplasmic compartment relative to the nucleoplasmic side, thereby allowing the import of unfolded proteins from the cytoplasm into the nucleus [34]. Central to this mechanoresponsive program is YAP, a master transcriptional regulator implicated in oncogenesis whose nucleocytoplasmic shuttling is dual-regulated by biochemical and mechanical inputs. Biochemically, YAP is regulated primarily by phosphorylation through the Hippo signaling pathway. Mechanically, YAP responds to cues such as extracellular matrix rigidity, which is mediated by cytoskeletal integrity. Owing to its intrinsically disordered structure, YAP exhibits low mechanical stability. This structural flexibility promotes partial unfolding, facilitating nuclear import [35]. Furthermore, this process activates mitochondrial motility toward the nucleus, generating ATP to fuel YAP translocation and reinforce F-actin polymerization [29, 36]. Upon nuclear entry, YAP drives transcriptional activation of pro-oncogenic targets, executing its oncogenic functions. Supporting this mechanism, we demonstrate that GPNMB-ECD-SDC4-mediated cytoskeletal reorganization recapitulates nuclear architectural reprogramming, triggers YAP nuclear translocation, and induces expression of pro-tumorigenic genes, including FOXM1, MMP9, and MCL-1, collectively conferring resistance to osimertinib in NSCLC.

While this study has elucidated the downstream mechanisms by which GPNMB-ECD drives osimertinib resistance, the upstream regulators responsible for its elevated expression in resistant samples were not directly investigated here. GPNMB has been reported to be upregulated through diverse mechanisms. Transcriptional regulation by members of the microphthalmia (MiT/TFE) family, including MITF, TFEB, and TFEC, plays a central role in driving GPNMB expression [37–39]. In addition, various cellular and environmental stimuli have been shown to induce GPNMB upregulation, such as lysosomal stress, soluble “find-me” signals released from apoptotic cells, high-fat or high-fructose diets, and homocysteine treatment [39–42]. In various cancer types, GPNMB overexpression has been consistently associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis, including in triple-negative breast cancer, glioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, and gastric adenocarcinoma [9, 43–47]. In NSCLC, GPNMB expression has been shown to correlate with EGFR mutation status and promote cancer metastasis [48]. Additionally, GPNMB-ECD shedding has been demonstrated to facilitate NSCLC cell migration, invasion, and in vivo tumor growth [49]. Despite these advances, the specific mechanisms underlying GPNMB upregulation in the context of acquired osimertinib resistance in NSCLC have not been previously reported. In our group’s previous work (unpublished), Zhao et al. demonstrated that the long noncoding RNA Lnc-TMEM132D-AS1 acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to sponge has-miR-766-5p in osimertinib-resistant NSCLC cells, thereby relieving miRNA-mediated repression and leading to increased GPNMB mRNA and protein levels. This regulatory axis may contribute to the upregulation of GPNMB and the subsequent release of its soluble ECD fragment observed in the present study.

Importantly, we observed that the increase in secreted GPNMB-ECD was markedly greater than that of full-length GPNMB in osimertinib-resistant models, suggesting that, upon acquiring resistance, GPNMB is not only upregulated at the transcriptional level but also undergoes enhanced shedding. This indicates that GPNMB-ECD may be a more sensitive indicator of acquired osimertinib resistance than full-length GPNMB. Furthermore, detection of GPNMB-ECD requires only a peripheral blood draw, offering a noninvasive and repeatable approach for monitoring resistance development, whereas assessment of full-length GPNMB necessitates invasive tumor tissue biopsy. These features position plasma GPNMB-ECD as a clinically accessible biomarker with potential utility in guiding treatment decisions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the deeper mechanisms underlying GPNMB upregulation and its increased cleavage upon resistance development remain to be further elucidated. Second, the patient cohorts used for biomarker analysis were relatively small and retrospective, which may introduce selection bias. The prognostic value of plasma GPNMB-ECD and the association between tissue GPNMB expression and patient survival observed in these cohorts need to be validated in larger, prospective, multicenter studies. Furthermore, while our in vivo huPDX model more closely recapitulates the human clinical setting, it was limited by the small number of patient-derived samples (n = 3). Expanding the huPDX cohort to include more patient-derived samples will be necessary to confirm the therapeutic efficacy of anti-GPNMB-ECD. Additionally, our experiments employed the H1975 and HCC827 cell lines. H1975 carries the L858R mutation together with T790M, while HCC827 harbors an exon 19 deletion. Among these, exon 19 deletion and L858R are the two predominant EGFR-sensitizing mutation subtypes in NSCLC, accounting for approximately 85% of EGFR mutations [50]. T790M is the main acquired resistance mutation following first- and second-generation EGFR-TKI therapy, occurring in about 50–60% of cases [51]. Given that osimertinib inhibits both sensitizing mutations and T790M, these cell lines provide a clinically relevant system to investigate acquired resistance to osimertinib. Nevertheless, the applicability of our findings to uncommon EGFR variants (e.g., exon 18 G719X, exon 21 L861Q, etc., collectively ~ 10–15% of cases [52]) remains to be established. Future studies using osimertinib-resistant models with diverse EGFR mutations will be needed to confirm the broader role of GPNMB-ECD as a driver of resistance and a therapeutic target.

Conclusions

Our study provides a comprehensive mechanistic dissection of GPNMB-ECD-mediated osimertinib resistance in NSCLC. This resistance stems from decreased drug sensitivity due to actin cytoskeletal remodeling via the SDC4/α-Actinin axis, which triggers YAP nuclear translocation and subsequent upregulation of pro-tumorigenic genes. These findings identify GPNMB-ECD as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming acquired resistance to osimertinib. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the dynamic evolution of the tumor microenvironment, particularly intercellular communication networks, cytokine/chemokine signaling cascades, and stroma-associated matrix remodeling, to uncover novel combination therapies that enhance the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in NSCLC.
