On generating functions and automata associated to reflections in Coxeter systems Thanks: Hohlweg was partially supported by the NSERC grant Combinatorics of infinite Coxeter groups held by Hohlweg, and Biagioli by the PRIN 2022 Project 2022S8SSW2 Algebraic and geometric aspects of Lie theory .
Riccardo Biagioli, Christophe Hohlweg, Elisa Sasso

Abstract
. In this article, we study two combinatorial problems concerning the set of reflections of a Coxeter system. The first problem asks whether the language of palindromic reduced words for reflections is regular, and the second is about finding formulas for the Poincaré series of reflections, namely the generating function of reflection lengths. These two problems were inspired by a conjecture of Stembridge stating that the Poincaré series of reflections is rational and by the solution provided by de Man. To address the first problem, we introduce reflection-prefixes, arising naturally from palindromic reduced words. We study their connections with the root poset, dominance order on roots, and dihedral reflection subgroups. Using m m -canonical automata associated with m m -Shi arrangements, we prove that the language of reduced words for reflection-prefixes is regular. For the second problem, we focus on affine Coxeter groups. In this case, we derive a simple formula for the Poincaré series using symmetries of the Hasse diagram of the root poset.

1.
Introduction

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system, with
S
finite. We denote by
S^{*}
the free monoid on the alphabet
S
. To distinguish between a word and its corresponding group element, we denote a word in
S^{*}
using bold letters,
\mathbb{w}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
, while the resulting product in
W
is denoted by
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
. Let
w\in W
, a word
\mathbb{w}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\in S^{*}
is a
reduced word for
w
if
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
and
k
is minimal for this property; in this case the
length
of
w
is
\ell(w)=k
. The identity
e\in W
is represented by the empty word, and
\ell(e)=0
. We denote the set of all reduced words of
w\in W
by
{\operatorname{Red}}(w)
, and the set of all reduced words in
W
by
{\operatorname{Red}}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}{\operatorname{Red}}(w)
, where
\sqcup
denotes the disjoint union.
Finally, for any subset
A\subseteq W
, the
Poincaré series of
A
is the formal power series

A(q):=\sum_{w\in A}q^{\ell(w)}.

In this article, we study two combinatorial problems regarding the
set of reflections

T:=\bigcup_{w\in W}wSw^{-1}
of a Coxeter group
W
:

(1)

Is the language of
palindromic reduced words (for the reflections)
regular?

(2)

Are there explicit and elegant formulas for the Poincaré series of the set
T
of reflections ?

Our main contributions to those questions are the following:

•

We introduce the notion of
reflection-prefixes
, a class of elements in
W
arising naturally from palindromic reduced words of reflections, and study their properties in relation to the
root poset
, the
dominance order on roots
and
dihedral reflection subgroups
.

•

For any Coxeter system, we show that the
language of reduced words for reflection-prefixes
,
\operatorname{Pref}_{T}
, is regular. This is achieved using the family of
m
-canonical automata associated with
m
-Shi arrangements (see
[
15
, §3.4]
and
[
6
]
), which provide a family of finite deterministic automata recognizing
\operatorname{Pref}_{T}
. As a consequence, we show that the generating function of palindromic reduced words is rational.

•

In the case of affine Coxeter groups, we derive a simple expression for the Poincaré series of the set of reflections in terms of rational fractions. This formula is obtained from some symmetries of the Hasse diagram of the root poset.

We discuss now some history and motivations that lead to this article.
If
W
is finite,
A(q)
is clearly a polynomial for any
A\subseteq W
. A natural question in combinatorics of infinite Coxeter systems is to classify for which subsets
A
the Poincaré series
A(q)
is
rational
, that is, can be written as a ratio of two polynomials in
q
. It is well-known that
W(q)
is rational, see for instance
[
1
, Corollary 7.1.8]
. Furthermore, an explicit recursive formula for
W(q)
in term of standard parabolic subgroups is provided in
[
1
, Proposition 7.1.7]
.

A further natural direction is the study of generating functions of words in
S^{*}
in relation to the Coxeter system
(W,S)
. More precisely, given the canonical projection
\pi:S^{*}\rightarrow W
sending a word
\mathbb{w}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
to the element
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
,
it is natural to consider the
“lifted” Poincaré series
of a subset
B\subseteq S^{*}
relative to
(W,S)
:

B(q)=\sum_{\mathbb{w}\in B}q^{\ell(w)},

where the notation
{\ell(w)}
is understood to be
\ell(\pi({\bf w}))
.

A longstanding open problem in combinatorics of Coxeter groups is the enumeration of
|{\operatorname{Red}}(w)|
; for a detailed discussion see
[
1
, p.123]
, and for various partial results, see
[
12
,
14
]
. However, if we consider the set of all elements of a given length, we know that the numbers
r_{k}
of reduced words of length
k\in\mathbb{N}
is enumerated by the following Poincaré series

{\operatorname{Red}}(q)=\sum_{\mathbb{w}\in{\operatorname{Red}}}q^{\ell(w)}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}r_{k}q^{k},

which is known to be rational. The proof follows from the existence of a finite deterministic automaton that recognizes the language
{\operatorname{Red}}
; see for instance
[
1
, Theorem 4.9.1]
for more details.

As reported by Brenti
[
3
]
, Stembridge proposed the following problem during an open problem session at the Mathematical Sciences Research Institute at Berkeley in 1997: Is it true that the Poincaré series

T(q)=\sum_{t\in T}q^{\ell(t)}\quad\textrm{is rational?}

Since reflections are known to admit palindromic reduced words, a natural approach to this question is to construct a finite automaton that recognizes exactly one palindromic reduced word for each reflection. The existence of such a regular language would imply that the associated generating function is rational. However, the property of being regular is not generally preserved under the palindromic constraint. If a language is regular, it is well-known that the sublanguage of its palindromic words is context-free but not necessarily regular; for instance if
S=\{a,b\}
then
S^{*}
is regular but a standard application of the
pumping lemma
shows that the sublanguage of its palindroms is not. Indeed, the regularity of
\operatorname{Pal}
,
the language of all palindromic reduced words
, which are necessarily reduced words for reflections, remains an open question. In a recent article, Milićević
[
17
]
provides palindromic reduced words for all reflections in finite Weyl groups, but highlights the challenge of finding a general algorithm applicable to any Coxeter group. To overcome these challenges, we shift to the language of reflection-prefixes which we define below.

The language of reflection-prefixes

We recall that the
(right) weak order

(W,\leq_{R})
is the poset defined by
u\leq_{R}w
if there exists a reduced word for
u\in W
that is a prefix for a reduced word for
w\in W
. Equivalently,
u\leq_{R}w
if and only if
\ell(w)=\ell(u^{-1}w)+\ell(u)
.

It is well-known that every reflection has an odd length. Let
t\in T
be a reflection of length
\ell(t)=2k+1
, we define a
t
-prefix
to be any element
p_{t}\in W
such that
p_{t}\leq_{R}t
and
\ell(p_{t})=k+1
.
It turns out that
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}s_{k}\cdots s_{1}
is a palindromic reduced word for
t
if and only if
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}
is a reduced word for a
t
-prefix; see §
3.1
.

Reflection-prefixes turn out to be a useful combinatorial framework to study the root poset (Proposition
3.10
), the dominance order (Proposition
3.14
) and to find the canonical generators of dihedral reflection subgroups (Theorem
3.19
). Our main results concerning reflection-prefixes and their associated languages are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system.

(1)

The language
\operatorname{Pref}_{T}
of reduced words for reflection-prefixes is regular.

(2)

The language of palindromic reduced words is:

\operatorname{Pal}=\{s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}s_{k}\cdots s_{1}\in S^{*}\mid s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}\in\operatorname{Pref}_{T}\}.

(3)

The following Poincaré series is rational:

\operatorname{Pref}_{T}(q)=\sum_{\mathbb{w}\in\operatorname{Pref}_{T}}q^{\ell(w)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\operatorname{pal}_{k}q^{k},

where
\operatorname{pal}_{k}
is the number of palindromic reduced words of length
k
.

(4)

The Poincaré series
\operatorname{Pal}(q)=q\operatorname{Pref}_{T}(q^{2})
is rational.

The first assertion, the regularity of
\operatorname{Pref}_{T}
, is established in Corollary
3.26
. The proof relies on a construction of automata derived from the family of Garside shadow automata associated with
m
-Shi arrangements
(m\in\mathbb{N})
; for further details, see
[
15
,
6
]
. The second assertion follows from Proposition
3.1
, while the third and fourth are consequence of the first two.

Closed formulas for
T(q)
in affine Coxeter systems

Remarkably, a solution to Stembridge’s problem was provided in 1999 by de Man
[
5
]
. However, the author seemed unaware of Stembridge’s question and the combinatorics community was not aware of the solution. This oversight may be partly due to the title of de Man’s article
“The generating function for the number of roots of a Coxeter group”
, which only address counting reflections by length at the end of the article in
[
5
, §5.2]
. His solution is based on the Brink–Howlett automaton
[
4
]
that recognizes the language of
lexicographically ordered reduced word
, that is, each element
w
has a unique lexicographically reduced word in
{\operatorname{Red}}(w)
.

More precisely, in
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
, de Man shows that the generating function
\Phi^{+}(q)
of the depth of positive roots is a sum of rational functions that are obtained from a subset of states of the Brink-Howlett automaton and from the enumeration of the vertices of a family of graphs parameterized by
T
. So the generating function
\Phi^{+}(q)
is rational. Since
T(q)=q\,\Phi^{+}(q^{2})
, the Poincaré series
T(q)
is rational as well. However, the Brink–Howlett automaton contains a lot of states, most of them “dead states”, which make this automaton difficult to use in practice. For instance, in affine type
\widetilde{A}_{2}
, the number of states of the Brink–Howlett automaton is bounded by
2^{6}
; de Man provides some example of
T(q)
using a computer to handle his formula.

In this article, we give, in the case of affine Coxeter systems, an alternative solution to Stembridge’s problem by providing a simple formula for
T(q)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(W,S)
be an affine Coxeter system. Then

T(q):=\sum_{t\in T}q^{\ell(t)}=q\frac{P(q^{2})}{1-q^{2M}},

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
P(q)
is a palindromic polynomial.

The proof of the above theorem relies on two fundamental symmetries of the root poset on
\Phi^{+}
(Theorems
4.4
and
4.6
). These properties yield an explicit formula for
M
and
P(q)
(Theorem
4.11
)), from which the above result follows.

Plan of the article

The article is organized as follows. In §
2
, we recall some needed facts about the combinatorics of words and roots and, in particular, about two partial orders on the root system of a Coxeter system: the root poset and the dominance order, which were introduced by Brink and Howlett
[
4
]
in their work to show that Coxeter systems are automatic. In §

, we discuss reflection-prefix, palindromic reduced words for reflections and automata and, in §

2.1.
Combinatorics of words in Coxeter systems

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. As recalled in the introduction, if the word
{\bf w}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
in
S^{*}
is a reduced word for
w\in W
, we simply say that the product
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
is a reduced word for
w\in W
. Similarly, for
u,v,w\in W
, we say that
w=uv
is
a reduced product
if
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+\ell(v)
. In this case, we say that
u
is a
prefix
of
w
and
v
is a
suffix
of
w
. In other words,
u
is a prefix (resp. suffix) of
w
if and only if there exists a reduced word for
u
that is a prefix (resp. suffix) of some reduced word for
w
.

Reduced words of
w\in W
are in bijections with geodesics starting at
e
and ending at
w
in the (right) Cayley graph of
(W,S)
. Recall from the introduction that the
(right) weak order

(W,\leq_{R})
is the poset defined by:
u\leq_{R}w
if
u
is a prefix of
w
. The (right) weak order has for Hasse diagram the (right) Cayley graph for which the edges
\{w,ws\}
are oriented from
w
to
ws
if
\ell(w)<\ell(ws)
.

The
right descent set of
w\in W
is

D_{R}(w)=\{s\in S\mid\ell(ws)<\ell(w)\}=\{s\in S\mid ws\leq_{R}w\}.

Similarly, the
left descent set of
w\in W
is

D_{L}(w)=\{s\in S\mid\ell(sw)<\ell(w)\}=\{sw\in S\mid s\leq_{R}w\}.

For
I\subseteq S
, we consider
standard parabolic subgroup
W_{I}=\langle I\rangle
. The set of minimal coset representatives of
W/W_{I}
is

X_{I}=\{x\in W\mid x\leq_{R}xs,\ \forall s\in I\}.

In particular, any element
w
has a unique reduced product
w=uv
with
u\in X_{I}
and
v\in W_{I}
. Moreover, any product
uv
in
W
where
u\in X_{I}
and
v\in W_{I}
is a reduced product; see for instance
[
1
, Proposition 2.4.4]
.
The following statement is well-known; we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
w\in W
and
I\subseteq D_{R}(w)
. Then
W_{I}
is finite and
w=uw_{\circ,I}
is a reduced product, where
u\in X_{I}
and
w_{\circ,I}
is the longest element in
W_{I}
.

Proof.

We consider the reduced product
w=uv
, where
u\in X_{I}
and
v\in W_{I}
. Since
I\subseteq D_{R}(w)
and by definition of reduced product, we have for all
s\in I
:

\ell(w)-1=\ell(ws)=\ell(u(vs))=\ell(u)+\ell(vs).

So
\ell(vs)<\ell(v)
since
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+\ell(v)
. In other words,
D_{R}(v)=I
, since
v\in W_{I}
. Therefore, by
[
1
, Proposition 2.3.1 (ii)]
applied to
W_{I}
, we obtain that
W_{I}
is finite and
v=w_{\circ,I}
.
∎

Björner showed that
(W,\leq_{R})
is a meet-semilattice, meaning that any nonempty subset
X\subseteq W
admits a
meet
, that is a greatest lower bound denoted by
\bigwedge_{R}X
; see
[
1
, §3.2]
. A subset

is
bounded
in

if there exists
g\in W
such that

for any

. Therefore, any bounded subset

admits a least upper bound

called the

of

\bigvee_{R}X=\bigwedge_{R}\{g\in W\mid x\leq_{R}g,\,\forall x\in X\}\in W.

2.2.
Garside shadows automata

A
Garside shadow for
(W,S)
is a subset
G\subseteq W
such that
S\subseteq G
and

(i)

is closed under join in the right weak order, i.e. if

;

(ii)

is closed under suffixes, i.e. if

Associated with any Garside shadow

defined as:

\displaystyle\pi_{G}:W

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\bigvee_{R}\{g\in G\mid g\leq_{R}w\}.

Garside shadows were introduced by Dyer and the second author to show the existence of a finite Garside family in Artin-Tits monoids, see
[
8
]
. In particular, they show that finite Garside shadows exist whenever

In
[

]
, the second author together with Nadeau and Williams defined for any Garside shadow a deterministic automaton that recognized

(W,S)
. Let

be a Garside shadow for
(W,S)
. The

\mathcal{A}_{G}
is the deterministic automaton over the alphabet

defined as follows:

•

the set of
states
of
\mathcal{A}_{G}
is
G
;

•

the
final states
are the elements in
G
;

•

the
initial state
is the identity

;

•

the
transitions
are:
w\in W\xrightarrow{s}\pi_{G}(sw)\in G
, whenever

.

The automaton
\mathcal{A}_{G}
is represented by a directed edge-labeled graph on the vertex set

(

)

As a consequence, the authors recover that

2.3.
Root systems and inversion sets

We recall some basic facts about root systems and inversion sets; for more details, see
[
8
, §2.3]
.

Recall that a quadratic space
(V,B)
is the data of a finite dimensional real vector space
V
endowed with a symmetric bilinear form
B
. We denote by
O_{B}(V)
the group of linear maps of
V
preserving
B
and by
Q=\{v\in V\mid B(v,v)=0\}
the
isotropic cone
of
(V,B)
.

Any Coxeter system
(W,S)
admits a
geometric representation
, that is, a faithful representation of
W
as a discrete reflection subgroup of
O_{B}(V)
for some quadratic space
(V,B)
with
simple system

\Delta=\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S\}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\Delta
is
positively linearly independent
: if
\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}a_{\alpha}\alpha=0
with
a_{\alpha}\geq 0
, then all
a_{\alpha}=0

(2)

if the order
m_{st}
of the product
st
is finite, then
B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{m_{st}}\right)
; while
B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})\leq-1
if and only
m_{st}
is infinite.

(3)

for all
s\in S
,
B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})=1.

Then
s\in S
acts on
V
as the reflection defined by
s(v)=v-2B(\alpha_{s},v)\alpha_{s}
. Denote by
\Phi:=W(\Delta)
the associated
root system
, which is the disjoint union of the sets of
positive roots

\Phi^{+}=\operatorname{cone}(\Delta)\cap\Phi
and
negative roots

\Phi^{-}:=-\Phi^{+}
.
The pair
(\Phi,\Delta)
is called a
based root system
, and its
rank
is
|\Delta|=|S|
. The
classical geometric representation
is obtained by assuming that
\Delta
is a basis of
V
and that
B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=-1
whenever the order of
st
in
W
is infinite. For further details on the classical geometric representation, we refer the reader to [
[
1
, §4.1]
.

It is well-known that
\Phi^{+}
is in bijection with
T
, see for instance
[
1
, Proposition 4.4.5]
:

\Phi^{+}=\{\alpha_{t}\mid t\in T\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad T=\{s_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\},

where
\alpha_{t}
is the root associated to the reflection
s_{\alpha_{t}}=t
.

The
inversion set of
w\in W
is:

\Phi(w)=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\mid w^{-1}(\alpha)\in\Phi^{-}\}.

It is well-known that if
w=uv
is a reduced product in
W
, then

\Phi(w)=\Phi(u)\sqcup u(\Phi(v)),

see for instance
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
. It follows in particular that:

•

u\leq_{R}w
in the weak order if and only if
\Phi(u)\subseteq\Phi(w)
;

•

\Phi(w)=\{\alpha_{s_{1}},s_{1}(\alpha_{s_{2}}),\dots,s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}(\alpha_{s_{k}})\}
if
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
is a reduced word for
w
.

2.4.
The root poset

The
depth function
\operatorname{dp}:\Phi^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by

\operatorname{dp}(\beta):=\min\{\ell(w)\mid w\in W,w(\beta)\in\Delta\}.

The next result is
[
1
, Lemma 4.6.2]
and describes how the depth of a root changes under the action of a simple reflection
s\in S
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
s\in S
and
\beta\in\Phi^{+}\setminus\{\alpha_{s}\}
. Then
\operatorname{dp}(\alpha_{s})=0
and

(1)

\operatorname{dp}(s(\beta))=\begin{cases}\operatorname{dp}(\beta)-1,&\mbox{ if }B(\beta,\alpha_{s})>0;\\
\operatorname{dp}(\beta),&\mbox{ if }B(\beta,\alpha_{s})=0;\\
\operatorname{dp}(\beta)+1,&\mbox{ if }B(\beta,\alpha_{s})<0.\end{cases}

Remark 2.5
.

The definition for the depth of a positive root
\beta
provided above is analogous to the one originally introduced in
[
4
, Definition 1.5]
(see also
[
1
, Definition 4.6.1]
), which is given by:

\operatorname{dp}^{\prime}(\beta):=\min\{\ell(w)\mid w\in W,w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}.

This is equivalent to
\operatorname{dp}^{\prime}(\beta)=\min\{\ell(w)\mid w\in W,w(\beta)\in-\Delta\}
. Indeed, if
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
is such that
w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}
, then there exists an index
1\leq i\leq k
such that
s_{i}\cdots s_{k}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
while
s_{i-1}\cdots s_{k}(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}
. Since
s_{i-1}
permutes
\Phi^{+}\setminus\{\alpha_{s_{i-1}}\}
, it follows that
s_{i}\cdots s_{k}(\beta)=\alpha_{s_{i-1}}\in\Delta
. Hence, the two definitions are related by
\operatorname{dp}^{\prime}(\beta)=\operatorname{dp}(\beta)+1
.

Recall from
[
1
, §4.6]
that
\Phi^{+}
is equipped with a partial order
\leq
called the
root poset
.

Definition 2.6
.

The
root poset

(\Phi^{+},\leq)
is defined as follows:
\alpha\leq\beta
if and only if there exists
w\in W
such that
w(\alpha)=\beta
and
\ell(w)=\operatorname{dp}(\beta)-\operatorname{dp}(\alpha)
.

Here we state well-known properties of the root poset, which can be found within
[
1
, §4.6 & §4.7]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The root poset
(\Phi^{+},\leq)
satisfies:

(i)

(\Phi^{+},\leq)
is a graded poset with rank function
\operatorname{dp}:\Phi^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

(ii)

For
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
,
\operatorname{dp}(\alpha)=0
if and only if
\alpha\in\Delta
.

(iii)

The cover relations are given by
\alpha\lhd s(\alpha)
with
s\in S

2.6.
The dominance order

We present yet another partial order

on the positive roots of

[

. Let

\alpha\leq_{d}\beta

\beta\in\Phi(w)

{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\mid\alpha\leq_{d}\beta\}.

The
dominance-depth (or infinite-depth)
of a positive root
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
is defined as follows:

\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta):=|\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\setminus\{\beta\}\mid\alpha\leq_{d}\beta\}|=|{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)|-1.

We have an analogous result of Lemma
2.4

, Proposition 3.3]

Proposition 2.13

Let

\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\alpha_{s})=0

\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(s(\beta))=\begin{cases}\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta),&\mbox{ if }|B(\beta,\alpha_{s})|<1;\\
\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta)+1,&\mbox{ if }B(\beta,\alpha_{s})\leq-1;\\
\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta)-1,&\mbox{ if }B(\beta,\alpha_{s})\geq 1.\end{cases}

Note that if

\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(s(\alpha))=\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\alpha)+1

The following characterization of the dominance order, which relates the dominance-depth to the depth of a positive root, is
[

Proposition 2.14

Let

\alpha\leq_{d}\beta

It follows that if

3.
Reflection-prefixes and palindromic reduced words of reflections

In this section, we first define and state properties of
reflection-prefixes
in relation to the root poset, to the dominance order and to the canonical generators of maximal dihedral subgroups. Then we produce a family of automata built from the finite Garside shadows
L_{m}
(
m\in\mathbb{N})
that recognize the language of reflection-prefixes.

3.1.
Reflection-prefixes

Let
t\in T
be a reflection of
(W,S)
. It is well known that
\ell(t)=2k+1
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and that if
t=s_{1}\dots s_{k}s_{k+1}s_{k+2}\dots s_{2k+1}
is a reduced word for
t
, the word
s_{1}\dots s_{k}s_{k+1}s_{k}\dots s_{1}
is a
palindromic reduced word
for
t
; see for instance
[
6
, Proposition 2.3]
.

The following proposition shows in particular that
t
is uniquely determined by the prefix
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}
of any of its palindromic reduced words.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
t\in T
with corresponding positive root
\alpha_{t}
. Let
w\in W
and assume there is
r\in D_{R}(w)
such that
t=wrw^{-1}
and
\ell(t)=2\ell(w)-1
. Then
D_{R}(w)=\{r\}
. Moreover:

(1)

if
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}
is a reduced word for
w
, then
s_{k+1}=r
and
t=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}rs_{k}\cdots s_{1}
is a palindromic reduced word for
t
;

(2)

\alpha_{t}=w(\alpha_{r})=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r})
and
\operatorname{dp}(\alpha_{t})=k=\ell(w)-1
.

Proof.

Let
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}r
be a reduced word. We show that
D_{R}(w)=\{r\}
. By contradiction, assume that
|D_{R}(w)|\geq 2
. So there exists a
s\in D_{R}(w)
with
s\not=r
; set
I=\{s,r\}\subseteq D_{R}(w)
. By Lemma
2.1
, we know that
W_{I}
is finite and there is
u\in X_{I}
such that
w=uw_{\circ,I}
is a reduced product. So
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+m
, where
m=m_{sr}\geq 2
. Now, observe that
w_{\circ,I}rw_{\circ,I}\in I
, since the longest element of a finite Coxeter system acts by conjugation on the set of simple reflections. Therefore

t=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}s_{k+1}s_{k}\cdots s_{1}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}rs_{k}\cdots s_{1}=wrw^{-1}=uw_{\circ,I}rw_{\circ,I}u^{-1},

implying that
\ell(t)\leq 2\ell(u)+1=2\ell(w)-2m+1<2\ell(w)-1=\ell(t)
, a contradiction. The remaining claims follow from the uniqueness of
r
, the definition of the depth of roots and from the equalities
\ell(t)=2\operatorname{dp}(\alpha_{t})+1=2\ell(w)-1
.
∎

Definition 3.2

(Reflection-prefixes)
.

A

such that for any reduced word

as a
t
-prefix

Thanks to Proposition
3.1
, the reflection

associated with a reflection-prefix

-prefix is

such that
u=s_{i}\cdots s_{k}r
. So
-u(\alpha_{r})=s_{i}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r})\leq s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r})=-p_{t}(\alpha_{r})=\alpha_{t}
by Proposition
3.10
.
∎

The following theorem provides a characterization of reflection-prefixes in term of inversion sets.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
w\in W
and
r\in D_{R}(w)
. Let
t\in T
such that
\alpha_{t}=-w(\alpha_{r})\in\Phi(w)
. Then
w
is a
t
-prefix if and only if
B(\alpha,\alpha_{t})>0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi(w)
.

Proof.

Assume first that
w=p_{t}
is a
t
-prefix. By Proposition
3.10
, we have

\alpha_{r}\lhd s_{k}(\alpha_{r})\lhd\dots\lhd s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}s_{k}(\alpha_{r})=\alpha_{t}

a saturated chain associated to the reduced word
p_{t}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}r
. By the characterization of covers in the root poset, see Proposition
2.7
, we have:

B(s_{i+1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),\alpha_{s_{i}})<0,\quad\textrm{for all}\ 1\leq i\leq k-1.

Since
p_{t}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}r
is a reduced word, we know that the inversion set of
p_{t}
is:

\Phi(w)=\{\alpha_{s_{1}},s_{1}(\alpha_{s_{2}}),\dots,s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}(\alpha_{s_{k}}),s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r})=\alpha_{t}\}

Let
\alpha\in\Phi(p_{t})
. If
\alpha=\alpha_{t}
then
B(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t})>0
. If
\alpha=\alpha_{s_{1}}
, then

B(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s_{1}})=B(s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),\alpha_{s_{1}})=-B(s_{2}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),\alpha_{s_{1}})>0.

If
\alpha\notin\{\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s_{1}}\}
, then there is
2\leq i\leq k-1
such that
\alpha=s_{1}\cdots s_{i-1}(\alpha_{s_{i}})
, then

B(\alpha_{t},\alpha)=B(s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),s_{1}\cdots s_{i-1}(\alpha_{s_{i}}))=-B(s_{i+1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),\alpha_{s_{i}})>0.

Therefore
B(\alpha,\alpha_{t})>0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi(p_{t})
.

Conversely, assume that
B(\alpha,\alpha_{t})>0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi(w)
. Consider a reduced word
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}r
, so that
\alpha_{t}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r})
. To show that
w
is a
t
-prefix, it suffices to prove that:

B(s_{i+1}\cdots s_{k}(\alpha_{r}),\alpha_{s_{i}})<0,\quad\textrm{for all}\ 1\leq i\leq k-1.

This follows directly from the same computations done in the first part of the proof.
∎

3.3.
Reflection-prefixes and the dominance order

Reflection-prefixes allow an efficient way to compute the dominance set of
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
:

{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\mid\alpha\leq_{d}\beta\}

and its infinite-depth
\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta)
; see §
2.6
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
and let
t=s_{\beta}
be the corresponding reflection. For any
t
-prefix
p_{t}
we have:

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\alpha\in\Phi(p_{t})\ \mid\ B(\alpha,\beta)\geq 1\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\beta\}\sqcup\{\alpha_{s}\in\Phi(p_{t})\ \mid\ |\langle s,t\rangle|=\infty\}.

In particular,
\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta)=|\{\alpha_{s}\in\Phi(p_{t})\ \mid\ |\langle s,t\rangle|=\infty\}|
.

Proof.

The second equality follows from the well-known fact that a dihedral subgroup containing
s,t
is infinite if and only if
|B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})|\geq 1
and
s\not=t
, see for instance
[
1
, Proposition 4.5.4]
.

We show the first equality. Set
A=\{\alpha\in\Phi(p_{t})\ \mid\ B(\alpha,\beta)\geq 1\}
. Let
\alpha\in A
, then
\operatorname{dp}(\alpha)\leq\operatorname{dp}(\alpha_{t})=\operatorname{dp}(\beta)
by Proposition
3.9
. By Proposition
2.14
, we have therefore
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)
. Let
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Dom}}(\beta)
, then
B(\alpha,\beta)\geq 1
by Proposition
2.14
again. Now,
\beta\in\Phi(p_{t})
by Corollary
3.8
. So
p_{t}^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}
. Since
\alpha\leq_{d}\beta
, we have by definition of the dominance order that
p_{t}^{-1}(\alpha)\in\Phi^{-}
. In other words
\alpha\in\Phi(p_{t})
, which completes the proof.
∎

be the automaton such that:

;

4.
Poincare series of affine Coxeter groups

In this section we provide a direct solution to Stembridge’s problem by providing an explicit description of the Poincaré series for affine Coxeter groups. This formula is based on a detailed description of the Hasse diagram of the associated root poset.

4.1.
Crystallographic root systems

When one works with an affine Coxeter system
(W,S)
, with underlying finite Weyl group
(W_{0},S_{0})
, it is customary to use its associated crystallographic root system in a positive semi-definite quadratic space
(V,B)
. In this case, the classical geometric representation (see §
2.3
) is the unique geometric representation, and the simple system
\Delta
is a basis of
V
. The crystallographic condition allows us to consider root systems with non-necessarily unitary roots. We briefly recall this construction below.

Let
\Phi_{0}
be a reduced, irreducible, crystallographic root system for the finite
Weyl group
W_{0}

(V_{0},B_{0})
with corresponding set of simple roots
\Delta_{0}=\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S_{0}\}
and positive roots

(see for instance
[
2
,
16

, we have:

B_{0}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=\begin{cases}-\|\alpha_{s}\|\|\alpha_{t}\|\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{m_{st}}\right)&\text{if }m_{st}<\infty,\\
-1&\text{if }m_{st}=\infty.\end{cases}

The quadratic space
(V,B)
is obtained as follows:
V=V_{0}\oplus\mathbb{R}\delta

B
is the extension of

by setting the radical of

to be equal to

. Let
\omega
denote the highest root of
\Phi_{0}
; the crystallographic affine simple system is

with root system

\Phi=\{\alpha+k\delta\mid\alpha\in\Phi_{0},\;k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

The set of positive roots is:

\Phi^{+}=\{\,k\delta+\alpha,\;(k+1)\delta-\alpha\mid\alpha\in\Phi_{0}^{+},\;k\in\mathbb{N}\,\}=(\mathbb{N}\delta+\Phi^{+}_{0})\sqcup(\mathbb{N}^{*}\delta-\Phi^{+}_{0}).

It follows that

crystallographic (infinite) root system
for the affine Weyl group
(W,S)

4.2.
The root poset and dominance order in the affine case

Structural results for unitary root systems, specifically those determined by the sign of the bilinear form
B

[

From the crystallographic root system,
\Phi

unitary
root system in the sense of §
2.3
by normalizing each root via the map
\alpha\mapsto\frac{\alpha}{\|\alpha\|}

B\left(\frac{\alpha}{\|\alpha\|},\frac{\beta}{\|\beta\|}\right)=\frac{B(\alpha,\beta)}{\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|}.

Therefore, the condition
B(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\leq-1
for unitary roots
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}

2.8

2.13

B(\alpha,\beta)\leq-\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|.

For this reason Definition

Definition 4.1

A covering

in the root poset

if

The Hasse diagram of the root poset is studied in more details in the subsequent section.

In the affine case, the Hasse diagram of the dominance order is the union of
2|\Phi_{0}^{+}|
total ordered chains. Indeed, the only dominance relations in

are of the form

where

in
\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\Phi_{0}^{+}
; see
[

, Example 3.9]
.

An interesting consequence of these dominance relations is that for

and

\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(k\delta+\alpha)=k\quad\textrm{and}\quad\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}((k+1)\delta-\alpha)=k,

-small roots (see Section

Symmetry at the level of small roots

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a
W_{0}
-orbit.
The map
\phi:\mathcal{O}^{+}\to\delta-\mathcal{O}^{+}
defined by
\phi(\alpha)=\delta-\alpha
is an isomorphism of posets between
(\mathcal{O}^{+},\leq)
and the opposite poset
(\delta-\mathcal{O}^{+},\leq)^{op}
: for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{O}^{+}
, we have
\alpha\leq\beta
if and only of
\delta-\beta\leq\delta-\alpha
. Moreover all covering relations in
(\mathcal{O}^{+},\leq)
and in
(\delta-\mathcal{O}^{+},\leq)
are short.

Proof.

Since a poset isomorphism is uniquely determined by the preservation (or reversal) of covering relations, it suffices to show that
\alpha\lhd\beta
in
\mathcal{O}^{+}
if and only if
\delta-\beta\lhd\delta-\alpha
in
\delta-\mathcal{O}^{+}
. Recall that for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{+}
, a covering
\alpha\lhd\beta
exists if and only if there is a simple reflection
s\in S
such that
\beta=s(\alpha)
and
B(\alpha,\alpha_{s})<0
. We examine the two types of simple reflections in the affine Weyl group
W
.

(i)

Let
s\in S_{0}
be a finite reflection. For a finite simple root
\alpha_{s}
, we have:

B(\delta-\alpha,\alpha_{s})=B(\delta,\alpha_{s})-B(\alpha,\alpha_{s})=-B(\alpha,\alpha_{s}).

It follows that
B(\alpha,\alpha_{s})<0
if and only if
B(\delta-\alpha,\alpha_{s})>0
. Thus,
\alpha\lhd s(\alpha)
if and only if
s(\delta-\alpha)=\delta-s(\alpha)\lhd\delta-\alpha
.

(ii)

Let
s_{\delta-\omega}
be the affine reflection. Similarly, for the affine simple root
\delta-\omega
, the property of the radical implies:

B(\delta-\alpha,\delta-\omega)=B(\delta,\delta-\omega)-B(\alpha,\delta-\omega)=-B(\alpha,\delta-\omega).

Again, the sign of the bilinear form is reversed:
B(\alpha,\delta-\omega)<0
if and only if
B(\delta-\alpha,\delta-\omega)>0
. This ensures that
\alpha\lhd s_{\delta-\omega}(\alpha)
if and only if
s_{\delta-\omega}(\delta-\alpha)=\delta-s_{\delta-\omega}(\alpha)\lhd\delta-\alpha
.

Now, let
\alpha\lhd s(\alpha)
be a covering in
\mathcal{O}^{+}
for some
s\in S
. The type of this cover depends on the value
B(\alpha,\alpha_{s})
. Under the map
\phi
, this corresponds to the cover
\delta-s(\alpha)\lhd\delta-\alpha
. By the properties of the reflection
s
and of the radical, we have:

B(\delta-s(\alpha),\alpha_{s})=-B(s(\alpha),\alpha_{s})=B(\alpha,\alpha_{s}).

Since
B(\cdot,\alpha_{s})
is identical in both case, the condition for being short or long in Definition
2.8
is preserved. Since
\mathcal{O}^{+}
and
\delta-\mathcal{O}^{+}
decomposes the set of small roots
\Sigma
, so they are all short.
∎

4.4.
An explicit formula for the generating function of depth

We are ready now to provide a closed formula for the generating function of the depth of positive roots in
\Phi
, using the restriction of the root poset to the sets
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}
introduced in the previous section.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\Phi_{0}
be
W_{0}
-orbit. Define

M_{\mathcal{O}}=\max\{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)\mid\beta\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O}}\}.

We show that the depth of a positive root depends only on
M_{\mathcal{O}}
and on
the depth of a root in
\mathcal{O}^{+}
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}^{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then :

(1)

\operatorname{dp}(k\delta+\alpha)=\operatorname{dp}(\alpha)+k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)
;

(2)

\operatorname{dp}((k+1)\delta-\alpha)=\operatorname{dp}(\delta-\alpha)+k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.6
, the lowest depth in
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k}
is
k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)
.
It suffices to show that the only roots in
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k}
that have depth equal to
k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)
are

\{k\delta+\alpha_{s},(k+1)\delta-\omega\mid s\in S\}.

Note that
B(k\delta+\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})=B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})>0
, and
s(k\delta+\alpha_{s})=k\delta-\alpha_{s}\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k-1}
. So we have the covering
k\delta-\alpha_{s}\lhd k\delta+\alpha_{s}
. Analogously, we also have the covering
k\delta-\omega\lhd(k+1)\delta-\omega
, where
k\delta-\omega\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k-1}
. Hence
\{k\delta+\alpha_{s},(k+1)\delta-\omega\mid s\in S\}
are the roots with lowest depth in
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k}
and there are no more roots with this property.
∎

We denote the generating functions of the depth over the whole set
\Phi^{+}
, and its restriction to
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}
respectively, by:

(5)

\Phi^{+}(q)=\sum_{\beta\in\Phi^{+}}q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)},\quad P_{\mathcal{O}}(q):=\sum_{\beta\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O}}}q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)},\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}(q):=\sum_{\beta\in\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}}q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}.

Proposition 4.10
.

The polynomial
P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)
is palindromic of degree
M_{\mathcal{O}}
, and the generating function for the depth of roots in
\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}
is given by:

(6)

\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}(q)=\frac{P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)}{1-q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}+1}}.

Proof.

First of all we observe that by the definitions of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{O}}
and
M_{\mathcal{O}}
, the maximum degree of the terms in the sum defining
P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)
is exactly
M_{\mathcal{O}}
. More precisely, we have

P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{O}^{+}}(q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}+q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}-\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}).

Since

\displaystyle q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}}P_{\mathcal{O}}(q^{-1})

\displaystyle=q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{O}^{+}}(q^{-\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}+q^{-(M_{\mathcal{O}}-\operatorname{dp}(\beta))})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{O}^{+}}(q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}-\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}+q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)})=P_{\mathcal{O}}(q),

the polynomial
P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)
is palindromic.

By Corollary
4.9
, the depth of each root in the
k
-th slice is shifted by
k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)
, namely

\sum_{\beta\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k}}q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}=q^{k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)}P_{\mathcal{O}}(q).

Summing these contributions yields the geometric series:

\Phi_{\mathcal{O}}(q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\beta\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{O},k}}q^{\operatorname{dp}(\beta)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1)}P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)=\frac{P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)}{1-q^{M_{\mathcal{O}}+1}},

and this completes the proof.
∎

By combining the polynomials associated to each orbit, we obtain our final result.

Theorem 4.11
.

The generating function of the depth of positive roots is of the form

\Phi^{+}(q)=\frac{P(q)}{1-q^{M}}

where
M={\operatorname{lcm}}(M_{\mathcal{O}}+1\mid\mathcal{O}\ \mbox{ $W_{0}$-orbit})
and
P
is palindromic.

Proof.

By construction,
\Phi^{+}(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{h}\Phi_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}(q)
, where
h=1,2
is the number of
W_{0}
-orbits. For simplicity, set
P_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}=P_{i}
and
M_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}=M_{i}
for all
i
, and let
M={\operatorname{lcm}}(M_{i}+1)_{i=1}^{h}
. Then we may rewrite
\Phi^{+}(q)
as

\Phi^{+}(q)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{M}}{1-q^{M_{i}+1}}P_{i}(q)}{1-q^{M}}=\frac{P(q)}{1-q^{M}}.

Now we want to show that
P(q)
is palindromic. Denote by
D_{i}(q)=\frac{1-q^{M}}{1-q^{M_{i}+1}}
for every
i
. It is not difficult to see that
D_{i}(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{M/(M_{i}+1)}q^{M-j(M_{i}+1)}
is a palindromic polynomial of
\deg(D_{i}(q))=M-M_{i}-1
. In particular,
\deg(D_{i}(q)P_{i}(q))=M-1
for all
i
, so
\deg(P(q))=M-1
. We have

\displaystyle q^{M-1}P(q^{-1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}q^{M-1}D_{i}(q^{-1})P_{i}(q^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}q^{M-M_{i}-1}D_{i}(q^{-1})q^{M_{i}}P_{i}(q^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}(q)P_{i}(q)=P(q)

where the third equality holds since
D_{i}(q)
and
P_{i}(q)
are palindromic for all
i
(Proposition
4.10
). Therefore,
P(q)
is palindromic.
∎

Example of classical types

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.

Example 4.15
.

In type
\widetilde{A}_{n}
, since there is only one orbit, we obtain by direct computation that
M=n-1
, which is the height of the highest root minus
1
, and

P_{\mathcal{O}}(q)=P(q)=(n+1)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}q^{i}=(n+1)\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}.

Therefore:

\Phi^{+}(q)=\frac{n+1}{1-q}.

Example 4.16
.

Consider
(W,S)
of type
\widetilde{B}_{n}
. One shows that the polynomials corresponding to the two orbits are:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}q^{i}\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-1-i)\left(q^{2i}+q^{2i+1}\right).

Therefore
M_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}=n-1
and
M_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}=2n-3
. So from Proposition
4.10
we obtain:

\Phi_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}=\frac{1}{1-q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}=\frac{P_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}}{1-q^{2n-2}}.

Example 4.17
.

Consider
(W,S)
of type
\widetilde{C}_{n}
. One shows that the polynomials corresponding to the two orbits are:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}(q)=2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}q^{i}\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}(q)=(n-1)q^{2n-2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-1)q^{2i}+nq^{2i+1}.

Therefore
M_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}=n-1
and
M_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}=2n-2
. So from Proposition
4.10
we obtain:

\Phi_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}=\frac{2}{1-q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}=\frac{P_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}}{1-q^{2n-1}}.

5.
Open problems

We propose here some natural problems arising from this article.

Problem 1
.

Is the language of palindromic reduced word (or lexicographically ordered palindromic reduced word) regular?

Problem 2
.

Characterize which Grassmannian elements (elements with only one right descent) are reflection prefixes (see Proposition
3.1
). (Note that Grassmannian elements are also minimal representatives in right weak order for Nathan Reading’s shards
[
20
]
.)

Problem 3
.

For indefinite Coxeter systems, i.e., not affine or finite, find closed formulas for the generating function
\Phi^{+}(q)
and
T(q)=q\Phi^{+}(q^{2})
.

Problem 4

In the case of affine types, we provide a combinatorial description of the Hasse diagram of the root poset and recall that the dominance order is just the union of total orders. In the case of indefinite Coxeter systems, Dyer and the second authors initiated the study of
weak orders on roots
, which are a generalization of both orders, see
[
8
, §5]
. A first fundamental difference between both orders is that the dominance order is not graded by the infinite-depth (whereas the root poset is graded by the depth). That led us to propose the following questions in the case of indefinite Coxeter systems.

(1)

Study the Hasse diagram of the root poset and its connected compoments obtained by removing the long edges.

(2)

Study the formal power series that distinguished short and long covers in the root poset:
\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k,l}q^{k}t^{l}
, where
a_{k,l}
is the number of saturated chains in the root poset with
k
short edges and
l
long edges.

(3)

Study the dominance order.

(4)

Study the generating function of infinite depth
\sum_{\beta\in\Phi^{+}}q^{\operatorname{dp}_{\infty}(\beta)}
.

(5)

Study the formal power series
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}b_{k}q^{k}
, where
b_{k}
is the number of saturated chains in the dominance order of length
k
. (This problem is related to
[
6
, Question 5]
).
