Fusing LLM Capabilities with Routing Data
Tao Feng, Haozhen Zhang, Zijie Lei, Pengrui Han, Mostofa Patwary, Mohammad Shoeybi, Bryan Catanzaro, Jiaxuan You

Abstract
The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has fostered a vibrant ecosystem of diverse architectures, each exhibiting unique strengths across different tasks due to variations in model design, training data, and optimization objectives. Despite this diversity, most LLM applications rely on a single backend model, limiting their ability to cover a wide range of capabilities and often resulting in inefficiencies in both performance and token costs when tackling complex tasks. We point out that modern LLM hosting platforms typically serve a variety of models in response to diverse queries, generating a rich and underutilized resource: LLM routing data, which offers insights into the comparative strengths of different models across tasks. We identify a critical gap in current LLM ecosystems, the underexploitation of large-scale LLM routing data, which holds significant potential for optimizing multi-LLM integration and effectively harnessing their complementary capabilities. To tackle this question, we propose FusionBench , a comprehensive routing benchmark that includes diverse response types from multiple LLMs, summarizes reusable thought templates from top models, and covers 14 tasks across five domains using 20 open-source LLMs ranging from 8B to 671B parameters, consisting of 103M tokens. Based on FusionBench , we propose FusionFactory , a systematic LLM fusion framework with three representative levels: (1) query-level fusion, which constructs tailored LLM routers for each query while considering both direct response patterns and reasoning-augmented outputs; (2) thought-level fusion, which enhances response quality by leveraging abstract thought templates derived from top-performing LLMs’ answers to similar past queries; and (3) model-level fusion, which transfers capabilities between models through distillation methods, selecting top-performing responses or those with highest judge scores as training data. We systematically evaluate FusionFactory on FusionBench and find that fusing LLMs with FusionFactory yields consistent benchmark performance improvement over the best individual LLM across 14 popular LLM benchmarks, where the optimal FusionFactory configuration varies across different benchmarks. Our code is released at https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/FusionFactory .

To support research on LLM capability fusion, we propose a large-scale routing benchmark,
FusionBench
, to support comprehensive studies on fusing LLM capabilities. As shown in Table

offers the following main advantages: (1)

(1) query-level fusion:

(2) thought-level fusion:
It aims to enhance the response quality of new queries by leveraging the answers from different LLMs to previous queries as demonstrations, thereby fusing their capabilities through few-shot prompting
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34

. However, prior studies have shown that directly using the detailed responses of past queries can negatively impact the response quality for new queries

Furthermore, we enable new queries to retrieve the most relevant thought templates by computing embedding similarity to past queries, which are then used to guide the construction of the new query’s response.
(3) model-level fusion:
This level of fusion is primarily approached from the perspective of model distillation, which means transferring the capabilities of other LLMs to the base model through distillation methods
[
40

41

43

We systematically evaluate
FusionFactory
on

, where the optimal

Specifically, we find that: (1)

model-level fusion performs the worst among the three levels

, achieving decent results while requiring minimal computational overhead; (4)
gains from fusing World Knowledge and Math domains are moderate
, where these domains demand high factual accuracy or strict logical consistency, which are difficult to maintain through multi-LLM fusion.

FusionFactory
: LLM Capabilities Fusion from Different Levels

Figure 4
:
Overview of

:

. The three levels query level, thought level, and model level correspond to early fusion, mid fusion, and late fusion, respectively, in

For the thought level, we employ an LLM as a thought summarizer to extract a generalized thought template from the top-k responses selected based on performance or LLM judgment. As shown in Figure
4
, this section mainly introduces LLM capabilities fusion with
FusionFactory
from three representative levels: query-level, thought-level, and model-level, representing different stages of LLM fusions. These three levels correspond to early fusion, mid fusion, and late fusion respectively in the FusionFactory framework, each targeting distinct temporal phases of the reasoning process.

Query-level fusion (Early Fusion)
in section
4
operates at the problem interpretation stage, where a router analyzes the incoming query and task characteristics to select the most suitable LLM configuration. This approach considers multiple factors including performance metrics, computational cost, and LLM-based evaluations to make routing decisions before the actual reasoning begins. The selected model then processes the query independently, ensuring computational efficiency while leveraging specialized capabilities for specific task types.

Thought-level fusion (Mid Fusion)
in section
5
integrates multiple LLM capabilities during the reasoning process itself. The system employs an LLM as a thought summarizer to extract generalized thought templates from top-k responses selected based on performance or LLM judgment. For new queries, the framework retrieves the most similar queries via similarity search and utilizes their corresponding thought templates to enable few-shot prompting, allowing dynamic collaboration between different reasoning approaches throughout the problem-solving process.

Model-level fusion (Late Fusion)
in section
6
performs integration at the final evaluation stage, where multiple models generate complete responses independently before a meta-evaluation process selects the optimal solution. This approach involves supervised fine-tuning of the base LLM using training data derived from top-k responses, which are selected based on performance metrics or LLM judgment associated with each query. The fusion occurs through post-hoc evaluation and selection rather than collaborative reasoning during the problem-solving process.

4.1
Methodology: Query-level Fusion

The insight of query-level fusion is to assign the most appropriate LLM for each user query to fuse different LLMs’ capabilities. A router is designed for the assignment process based on the query, its associated task information, and LLM features, considering factors such as performance, cost, and LLM judge. Specifically, as shown in the first part on the right side of Figure
4
, for a routing task, we follow
[
15
]
and use the
Reward
to model the task optimization objective:

\text{Reward}=\alpha\cdot\text{Performance}-\beta\cdot\text{Cost}+\gamma\cdot\text{LLM-judge},

(1)

where the weights
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
control the relative importance of performance, cost, and the LLM judge, respectively.
Base on this, for a query
q
, its corresponding task
t
, and LLM features
m
, our goal is to learn a router model
f_{\phi}
such as a neural network that maximizes the
Reward
:

\phi^{*}=\arg\max_{\phi}\;\mathbb{E}_{(q,t)\sim\mathcal{D},m\sim\mathcal{M}},\left[\text{Reward}\left(f_{\phi}(q,t,m)\right)\right],

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}
denotes the joint distribution over query-task pairs
(q,t)
and
\mathcal{M}
denotes the categories of different LLM features. After obtaining
\phi^{*}
,
f_{\phi^{*}}
can select the appropriate LLM to respond to different queries and tasks.

5.3
Result Analysis: Thought-level Fusion

As shown in Figure
6
, our thought-level fusion approach consistently improves model performance across all scales. Averaging across the six task domains, the small, medium, and large model improved by 12.7%, 4.8%, and 8.3% respectively comparing zero-shot to the best Thought Template paradigm performance.

Thought fusion excels on tasks requiring complex reasoning.

Thought fusion yields the largest gains on reasoning-intensive tasks like
Math
and
Code
, with hybrid fusion improving math accuracy by 21.3% (small) and 15.8% (medium). The large model
Palmyra Creative (122b))
sees a 57.6% boost in coding accuracy (from 0.33 to 0.52), despite being designed for creative tasks. General reasoning tasks also benefit, with commonsense and reading comprehension improving by 14.9% and 16.9% for small models. In contrast, performance drops in world knowledge tasks (–7.9% for small models) and is mixed for popular knowledge, likely because such tasks require factual recall rather than strategic reasoning, making thought templates less helpful or even distracting.

Large models can also benefit from thought fusion.

Thought-level fusion provides substantial gains even for larger models, with the largest model improving by 57.6% in code and 6.8% in math over zero-shot. Medium models also see notable boosts: 11.9% in code and 15.8% in math. These results show that thought fusion helps models at all scales by effectively distilling diverse, high-quality reasoning from multiple sources.

Hybrid selection strategy provides optimal balance.

The hybrid approach delivers the most ideal improvement. This strategy effectively balances accuracy with response quality. For small models, it improves accuracy by 12.7%, outperforming performance-based (3.6%) and LLMScore-based (7.3%) methods. This suggests hybrid selection effectively balances raw performance with reasoning quality, leading to more coherent and helpful thought templates.

6.1
Methodology: Model-level Fusion

The model-level aims to leverage different LLMs’ high-quality responses as training data to fuse LLM capabilities. Specifically, as shown in the last part on the right side of Figure
4
, we obtain the top-k responses
R_{i}
of query
q_{i}
selected according to performance or LLM judge as equation (
3
). Based on this, we can obtain a training data containing
n
samples
Z=\{q_{i},R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Finally, we leverage
Z
to train an LLM
f_{\theta}
based on supervised fine-tuning:

\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}\left(f_{\theta}(q_{i}),R_{i}\right)

(5)

Once
\theta^{*}
is obtained, the fine-tuned
f_{\theta^{*}}
can generate responses for different queries.

6.2
Experimental Settings: Model-level Fusion

Model and Implementation
. To investigate the effectiveness of model-level fusion, we select the best-performing small-scale LLM (LLaMA-3.1 (8b)) and fine-tune it on
FusionBench
.
For each query, we rank the LLM-generated responses for fine-tuning based on two criteria introduced in Section
2.1
: actual performance metrics and LLM judge scores. Accordingly, we define three sorting strategies:
(1)
Task Performance
selects the top-
K
responses based solely on performance metrics;
(2)
LLM Judge
selects the top-
K
responses based on LLM judge scores;
(3)
Hybrid
first ranks responses by performance metrics, and then re-ranks those with same performance using LLM judge scores to select the final top-
K
responses. The selected responses from each strategy are used as training data.

Inspired by prior work in knowledge distillation
[
47
,
48
]
, we augment the training set by including the original ground truth for each query alongside the top-
K
responses generated by different LLMs. We refer to this approach as
Top-
K
Router SFT
. To evaluate the effectiveness of this method, we introduce two baseline approaches for comparison:
Zero-Shot
, which directly employs
LLaMA-3.1 (8B)
to generate responses for each task without any fine-tuning; and
Top-K Label Only SFT
, in which the training data consists solely of ground truth answers. To ensure fairness in training data volume, we replicate each ground truth label
K+1
times in this baseline, aligning the total number of training samples with that of the Top-
K
Router SFT. These comparisons enable us to investigate whether model-level fusion can leverage the diverse outputs from multiple LLMs in a manner analogous to knowledge distillation.

6.3
Result Analysis: Model-level Fusion

We show our results in Table
5
, and we can obtain several conclusions.

Model-level fusion brings modest average improvements across tasks, with gains in 4 out of 6 domains
. While model-level fusion does not guarantee improvements across all tasks, it achieves performance gains in 4 out of 6 domains compared to the zero-shot baseline. On average, fusion models outperform their corresponding baselines under both performance-metric– and LLM-judge–based sampling strategies.
This suggests that fine-tuning on responses distilled from multiple LLMs helps the model leverage complementary insights, leading to more robust task-general capabilities.

Fusion gains are not uniform, with performance drops observed in code tasks due to domain mismatch and data scarcity
. Despite the general gains, model-level fusion can incur negative transfer in certain domains.
Notably, on code tasks, we observe a decline in performance for all fusion variants, which may stem from (1) the inherently higher difficulty and lower sample volume of the code data and (2) the domain mismatch between code generation and the primarily QA-style outputs of the other tasks.
Consequently, during multi-task training, the model struggles to reconcile these heterogeneous formats, leading to suboptimal learning in the coding domain.

LLM Judge–based selection yields the best results by offering nuanced and continuous quality assessment over rigid rule-based metrics.
. We compare three sampling strategies: sorting by actual performance metrics, by LLM Judge scores, and by a hybrid combination of the two.
While all fusion methods benefit from ranking, the LLM Judge–based selection achieves the best overall results (Table
5
).
We attribute this to two factors: (1) rule-based metrics such as exact match are binary and may misclassify correct yet variationally phrased answers, whereas an LLM Judge can assign a more granular, continuous quality score; (2) the LLM Judge’s nuanced assessment better identifies the most informative responses, thereby improving the precision of the top-K selection.

Comparisons Across Three
FusionFactory
Fusion Levels

In this section, we compare the three
FusionFactory
levels on the full testing set of 14 tasks, as shown in Table
6
. We select the best-performing setting (averaged across all domains) for each fusion level to conduct comparisons in this section. For the query level, we use GraphRouter with task performance as the routing criterion. For the thought level, we adopt the hybrid strategy to generate thoughts for guiding the response to each query. For the model level, we use top
-
K
responses selected by the LLM Judge to obtain the fine-tuned LLM.

Specifically, due to the constraint that our model-level backbone LLM is LLaMA-3.1 (8B), we adopt LLaMA-3.1 (8B) as backbone LLM for the thought-level fusion and restrict the query-level LLMs used in GraphRouter to small-sized models, ensuring a fair and consistent comparison across fusion levels. Specifically, we analyze the variance across different levels based on the following factors: Fusion LLM Size, which considers whether responses are fused from the full set or only from small-sized LLMs and Fusion Criteria, which refers to the approach used to select and fuse responses from different LLMs. For example, the fourth row in Table
6
represents a setting at the thought level, where responses from small-sized LLMs are selected using the hybrid strategy. These responses are then summarized into thoughts for each query using LLaMA-3.1 (8B) as the summarizer to assist in answering the test queries. From the results comparison in Table
6
, we can observe that:

The thought-level approaches achieve the best fusion performances
. Specifically, Thought-Full-Hybrid-70B achieves the best performance across all tasks, demonstrating the effectiveness of thought-level fusion in enabling deep reasoning over the capabilities of different LLMs. Moreover, by comparing different thought-level strategies in terms of Fusion LLM Size and Fusion Criteria, we observe a diminishing return effect: increasing the size of the summarizer or the number of responses does not necessarily lead to significant performance gains, but instead results in higher computational cost.

The model-level approaches yield the lowest performance among the three fusion levels
. The model-level approaches outperform zero-shot on only three tasks and even underperform zero-shot in terms of average performance. This may be due to the fact that training samples with multiple responses per query can lead to overfitting in model-level fusion, thereby reducing generalization to test queries. Furthermore, since model-level fusion uses a unified LLM for multiple tasks during SFT, the current results suggest that this approach struggles to simultaneously abstract the heterogeneous task requirements and varying capabilities of different LLMs.

The query-level fusion demonstrates moderate performance but is the most cost-efficient method
. For query-level fusion, it only requires fine-tuning a lightweight GraphRouter model with a few hundred million parameters to achieve relatively good performance, making it highly practical for real-world applications.

Across different domains, we observe that compared to the zero-shot setting, fusing LLM capabilities is most challenging in the World Knowledge and Math domains
. World Knowledge fusion fails because factual retrieval requires precise, deterministic answers, where collaborative deliberation introduces noise rather than insight, while Math fusion degrades performance because mathematical reasoning demands uninterrupted logical chains, where multi-model collaboration disrupts the sequential precision required for accurate computation.

Additional Related Work

LLM fusion
. Recent efforts in LLM fusion aim to leverage multiple language models or reasoning traces to improve performance, robustness, and generalization. Query-level fusion methods, such as LLMRouter
[
49
]
and STaR
[
50
]
, dynamically select the most suitable model based on input queries, treating model routing as a policy learning problem. Thought-level fusion approaches like Thought Propagation
[
51
]
and Tree of Thoughts
[
29
]
aggregate multiple reasoning paths to enhance answer consistency and quality. At the model-output level, techniques such as FrugalGPT
[
52
]
and Self-Consistency
[
53
]
combine outputs from multiple models or multiple samples to approximate stronger reasoning abilities while controlling cost. Additionally, LLM-as-judge methods, including LLM-as-Judge
[
54
]
and RAG Fusion
[
55
]
, evaluate and select responses using a secondary LLM, often achieving stronger results through comparison-based fusion. However, these studies typically focus on LLM capabilities fusion from only a single level of analysis. Building upon this prior work, we propose a large-scale benchmark,
FusionBench
, specifically designed for LLM capabilities fusion. Based on this benchmark, we conduct a comprehensive comparison and analysis across three levels, leading to more holistic and integrated conclusions.

LLM router
. Recent advances in LLM routing aim to improve inference efficiency and response quality by directing queries to the most suitable model. Early efforts such as RouteLLM
[
56
]
explore preference-driven routing strategies, training policies to select LLMs that align with user preferences. RouterBench
[
26
]
provides a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating routing systems, but it mainly focuses on evaluating routing decisions rather than leveraging routing logs for capability fusion. Similarly, TO-Router
[
57
]
and Expert Router
[
58
]
emphasize efficiency through parallel model selection and dispatching, while Glider
[
59
]
integrates both global semantics and local instruction cues for improved expert selection. In contrast, our work shifts the focus toward capability-oriented analysis, leveraging large-scale routing data not just for model selection but as a form of weak supervision to systematically study and understand LLM capabilities across three levels: query-level routing behavior, thought-level reasoning patterns, and model-level performance signals.

9
Conclusion

This paper explores how to leverage routing data for fusing the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). We propose
FusionBench
, a large-scale benchmark that captures diverse response patterns, reusable thought templates, and broad task coverage across multiple LLMs. Based on
FusionBench
, we systematically study LLM fusion from three levels with
FusionFactory
—query, thought, and model—demonstrating that routing data can serve not only for model selection, but also as a valuable supervision source for understanding and integrating LLM capabilities.

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Work

While our work focuses on evaluating real-world task performance - particularly in domains like math and coding - future research should also investigate aspects of trustworthiness
[
60
,
61
]
and justice
[
62
,
63
]
in the fusion process. The act of fusing multiple responses may inadvertently introduce social biases, inaccuracies, or conflicting reasoning, which could impact the reliability and ethical soundness of the LLMs. Exploring safeguards or calibration techniques to ensure more robust and equitable fusion outcomes presents a valuable direction for further study.
