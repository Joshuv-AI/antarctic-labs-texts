Examining Input Modalities and Visual Feedback Designs in Mobile Expressive Writing CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in HCI CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in ubiquitous and mobile computing CCS: Applied computing Health informatics
Shunpei Norihama, Shixian Geng, Kakeru Miyazaki, Arissa J.Sato, Mari Hirano, Simo Hosio, Koji Yatani

Abstract
. Expressive writing is an established approach for stress management, and recent practices include information technology. Although mobile interfaces have the potential to support daily stress management practices, interface designs for such mobile expressive writing and their effects on stress relief still lack empirical understanding. To fill the gap, we examined the interface design of mobile expressive writing by investigating the influence of input modalities and visual feedback designs on usability and perceived cathartic effects through in-the-wild studies. While our studies confirmed the stress relief effects of mobile expressive writing, our results offer important insights in interface design. We found keyboard-based text entry more user-friendly and preferred over voice messages due to its privacy friendliness and reflection process. Participants expressed different reasons for preferring different post-writing visual feedback depending on the cause and type of stress. This paper also discusses future research opportunities in interface designs for mobile expressive writing.



1.
Introduction

Excessive mental stress has a negative impact on many aspects of life, including mental and physical well-being
(
Veit and Ware 1983
;
Hammen 2005
;
Mushtaq et al. 2018
)
.
Due to its high global prevalence, several approaches to lower stress have been developed, primarily in the field of psychology.
One established method is
expressive writing
, referring to the disclosure of distressing experiences and their related emotions typically by journaling
(
Pennebaker 1997
;
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
.
Expressive writing can provide people with a sense of stress relief
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
, known as a
cathartic effect

(
Breuer and Freud 1955
)
.
Expressive writing started with pen and paper
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
, but the practice has expanded to the information technology
(
Hardey 2002
)
.
More recently, mobile apps
(
Wang et al. 2018
;
Wu and Bailey 2018
;
MacIsaac et al. 2022
)
and chatbots
(
Howe et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2021
)
have also been investigated to support expressive writing.
For example, smartphone apps or chatbots ask users to type their negative emotions
(
Howe et al. 2022
;
Abd-Alrazaq et al. 2021
;
Ahmed et al. 2022
)
.
Smartphones, in particular, are now highly ubiquitous, which creates an opportunity to investigate and distribute affordable, effective, and easily accessible stress-lowering tools to those suffering from stress.

Due to recent technological advances, speech input is now a viable input option on smartphones.
Previous HCI work has found that speech input has an advantage in speed
(
Ruan et al. 2018
)
, workload
(
Rzepka et al. 2022
)
, and content richness
(
Luo 2022
)
over keyboard input.
Further, studies about expressive writing without smartphones or chatbots have found that verbal expression had a more positive effect than written expression (e.g.
(
Esterling et al. 1994
;
Murray and Segal 1994
)
).
Speech-enabled expressive writing with smartphones is currently underexplored, as most existing expressive writing support systems rely on keyboard text entry (see e.g.
(
Howe et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2021
)
).
This warrants further studies into the suitability of speech input in expressive writing.

Another powerful aspect of smartphones is their high programmability, allowing developers to build experiences that mimic those outside the digital world.
Clinical psychology has shown that deleting objects tied to emotion helps to resolve negative moods
(
Briñol et al. 2013
;
Lee and Schwarz 2011
;
Soesilo et al. 2021
)
.
People may write a note with their negative emotion and put it into a shredder or simply tear it up by hand
(
Nussbaum 1995
;
Soesilo et al. 2021
)
.
This consequent action is a widely recognized practice to obtain an explicit feeling of removing or destroying the negative experience
(
Briñol et al. 2013
;
Lee and Schwarz 2011
)
.
Similar features can be implemented as digital mobile experiences through different visual feedback solutions, but the potential of these solutions has not well examined.

This paper aims to better understand the cathartic effect of expressive writing on mobile devices.
We first examined the impact of speech input versus typed input on user experience and the cathartic effect within a mobile expressive writing application.
We also adapted key findings from the first investigation and improved the mobile solution.
We then compared different
visual feedback
designs that are based on prior work and were provided immediately after the users engaged in expressive writing.
Specifically, these studies answer two research questions:

RQ1.

How do speech and text-based input in mobile expressive writing influence the stress-relieving effect, the content of expression, and the perceived usability and preference?

RQ2.

How do different visual feedback designs in mobile expressive writing influence stress-relieving effect, and the perceived usability and preference?

This work conducts two in-the-wild studies (15 and 23 days long with 28 and 36 participants, respectively) using a mobile chatbot application and offers the following contributions:

•

Investigation of the effects of input modalities and visual feedback designs provided after emotional disclosure in mobile expressive writing.

•

Design and research implications based on our two studies, highlighting future research directions in technology-supported expressive writing and stress coping.

2.
Related Work

Our work is contextualized within the intersection of digital tools for
expressive writing

(
Pennebaker 1997
;
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
and related HCI aspects.

2.1.
Expressive Writing

The therapeutic use of writing is called
expressive writing

(
Pennebaker 1997
;
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
(also called
written emotional disclosure

(
Frisina et al. 2004
)
or
therapeutic writing

(
Wright and Chung 2001
)
).
In expressive writing, people write freely about their traumatic or stressful events with their emotions in detail.
Undergoing this expressive writing session is known for its various benefits in the long term, ranging from physical and mental health to social and behavioral aspects
(
Pennebaker 1997
;
Reinhold et al. 2018
)
.
Expressive writing can bring increased distress and negative mood right after writing as short-term effects
(
Esterling et al. 1999
)
while prior research also found a decrease in depressive symptoms
(
Reinhold et al. 2018
)
.
To unfold these complex and contradictory outcomes, there has been a large discussion on the process underlying expressive writing.

The mechanism of how expressive writing works has not been fully understood, but there are three common theories to explain:
inhibition theory
,
exposure theory
, and
cognitive processing

(
Reinhold et al. 2018
)
.
Early work on expressive writing followed
inhibition theory
, assuming that inhibiting thoughts or emotions caused a high mental workload, and therefore, expressing thoughts and emotions led to physical and mental health
(
Pennebaker and Beall 1986
)
.
Another explanation,
exposure theory
, proposed that expressive writing might work as an imaginative exposure to negative emotional events and let people inured to stimuli.
During exposure, the response to the stress was unlearned or replaced by other responses,
and this process might relate to the outcome of expressive writing
(
Sloan and Marx 2004
)
.

Cognitive processing
spotlighted the content of expressive writing and claimed that the process of constructing a coherent story helped people organize their experiences and thoughts and change perspectives
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
.
Considering these theories, expressive writing lets us confront and unleash our negative experiences and emotions, which we tend to inhibit or avoid.
Although it may temporarily provoke negative moods, it still helps people restructure their cognition toward stressful events and brings various benefits.

Along with psychology revealing its effects and mechanisms, existing research has explored the applications of information technology to support expressive writing
(
Sauter 2014
;
Hardey 2002
)
.
Experiments with
EmotionDiary
showed how the tool was most effective for people with past experience on emotional disclosure on social networking services
(
Lee et al. 2016
)
.
Another group designed chatbot interactions for expressive writing, and the responsive chatbot enhanced emotional disclosure than writing on a document
(
Park et al. 2021
)
.
Other applications support journaling
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
, reflection
(
McDuff et al. 2012
)
or recollection
(
Peesapati et al. 2010
)
of everyday emotional experiences as well as trauma and stress experiences.

One previous study explored chatbot designs to mitigate workplace stress by comparing three types of interventions: taking a break, supporting mindfulness, and emotional expression
(
Howe et al. 2022
)
.
While the emotional expression intervention reduced stress most efficiently, participants rated it the lowest because of two drawbacks: it required the highest effort, and they felt more stressed while reflecting their stress and the associated negative emotion.
Technology-supported expressive writing, like conventional longhand writing, helped disclose emotions and reduce stress, while short-term deterioration of emotions was observed as well, and its burden became problematic.
Therefore, there is a need to explore usability aspects of interface designs for mobile expressive writing for cathartic effect.

2.2.
The Effect of Input Modalities in Expressive Writing

Previous work found that verbal expressions (e.g., speaking into a tape recorder) could improve cognitive change and enhance immune function more
(
Esterling et al. 1994
)
than written expressions (e.g., writing on a piece of paper) while both verbal and written expressions led to increase in negative emotion
(
Murray and Segal 1994
)
.
For the degree of emotional expression, there are two conflicting results; one reported greater expression in written mode
(
Esterling et al. 1994
)
while the other in a verbal condition
(
Murray and Segal 1994
)
.
Prior work has also examined the effects of verbal and written forms only in self-disclosure but also in disclosure to others.
Iacovelli et al.
(
Iacovelli and Johnson 2012
)
found that face-to-face disclosure showed stronger physiological improvements than remote communication via text messaging.
Other work has compared therapy interviews (in verbal form with therapists) with expressive writing (in written form by oneself) and found that therapy interviews helped a shift to positive affect
(
Donnelly and Murray 1991
;
Murray et al. 1989
)
and cognitive change
(
Murray et al. 1989
)
.
Combining these findings, verbal expression, which is the most natural and prevailing modality for therapy, may have a greater effect on post-expression mood and stress reduction than written expression.

Many of the existing expressive writing technologies and studies have mainly employed text entry using keyboards (either physical or virtual)
(
Abd-Alrazaq et al. 2019
;
Park et al. 2021
;
Howe et al. 2022
)
.
Brewin and Lennard’s study on comparing typing against writing for a long term revealed that typing resulted in less negative effects but less self-rated disclosure
(
Brewin and Lennard 1999
)
.
This result motivates more extensive explorations on the effect of input modalities in technology-enabled expressive writing.
Speech-based input is in particular a promising text entry approach commonly available in various smartphones and mobile devices
(
Enge 2020
)
.
Speech-based input has been shown as superior over keyboard-based methods for entering short messages on touchscreen mobile devices
(
Ruan et al. 2018
)
.
It also has demonstrated benefits in information search with conversational agents
(
Rzepka et al. 2022
)
and food journaling
(
Luo 2022
)
.
However, its effect on mobile expresstive writing is still underexplored.
Comparative studies in input modalities thus contribute to deepening the understanding of the effect of technology-enabled expressive writing.

2.3.
Feedback Effects in Expressive Writing

Prior work in psychology has shown that additional actions on the expressed negative emotion, such as physically throwing it away, can enhance the sense of stress relief
(
Briñol et al. 2013
;
Lee and Schwarz 2011
;
Soesilo et al. 2021
)
.
These studies show that the physical clearing of materialized emotions leads to the psychological clearing of emotions.
Washing or sanitizing hands can also diminish the extent and impact of previous negative sensations
(
Xu et al. 2012
)
or feelings of guilt
(
Zhong and Liljenquist 2006
)
.
These psychological effects depend on the intensity of the physical action or the degree of embodiment.
Soesilo et al’s experiment found that shredding the paper with scissors reduced envy more than tearing by hand
(
Soesilo et al. 2021
)
.
Briñol et al.
(
Briñol et al. 2013
)
also tested the effect of interaction on a computer, and revealed that moving files with negative emotions to the trash with a mouse had a greater effect than merely imagining them.
As our cognition is based on sensory organs, the mental state is grounded in the physical state
(
Barsalou 2008
;
Lee and Schwarz 2021
)
.
Therefore, the embodiment and agency of the elimination action are important, but the effects when others perform the elimination movements are not fully clarified
(
Lee and Schwarz 2021
)
.

Digital technology offers great flexibility on the consequent actions on expresstive writing.
Grieger et al.
(
Grieger et al. 2021
)
demonstrated that in virtual reality, punching objects displaying stressful messages promoted relaxation and encouraged self-reflection.
Björling et al.
(
Björling et al. 2019
)
developed a virtual reality system to reduce stress in teenagers, which allowed users to burn their negative thoughts with leaves in a fire.
While previous studies in virtual reality explored the effects of eliminating emotional objects, most studies and applications on mobile devices have implemented no feedback or only text feedback
(
Park et al. 2021
;
Howe et al. 2022
)
.
Yet, the effects of the visual feedback after emotional disclosure on mobile devices remain relatively underexplored.

3.
Study 1: Examination of Input Modalities

We first conducted a study to explore the influence of using different input methods to answer RQ1.
The following study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the first author’s institution.

3.1.
System Implementation

We developed a chatbot system that allows users to perform expressive writing on their smartphones (Figure
1
).
We adopted chatbot interaction as it can enhance emotional disclosure rather than writing on a document
(
Park et al. 2021
)
.
We chose LINE as the underlying platform because it is one of the most widely used messaging apps in Japan, the country where we conducted our studies.
In our implementation, participants could express their stressful experience that happened that day and how they felt then, using a given input method (Figure
1
b).
The instructions were based on the standard wording
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
, but translated into more casual, conversational words in reference to the study that designed a chatbot for expressive writing
(
Park et al. 2021
)
.
After the system received stress-related emotional expression content, it asked participants to rate the perceived stress changes at that moment (Figure
1
e).
The system could also send a message to remind participants and encourage them to share their experiences.
We prepared fixed messages for notifications and responses.
We prepared fixed notification and response messages for the chatbot, which also deliberately included emojis as prior work had shown the effect of making a chatbot more credible
(
Beattie et al. 2020
)
or enjoyable
(
Fadhil et al. 2018
)
.

3.2.
Input Modality Conditions

We asked the participants to type text with the soft keyboard in
keyboard-based
condition and send a voice message in
voice
condition.
In Japan, where this study was conducted, there are different keyboard designs people commonly use besides the QWERTY layout (e.g., flick input).
We allowed our participants to use any keyboard they liked to maintain the realistic setting of their technology use.
For the voice message input modality, we used the voice message functionality already available in LINE, with which our study participants audio-recorded their utterances.
We prohibited our participants from using speech recognition functionalities for text input as the LINE platform did not offer a way to differentiate between text generated by speech recognition or text entered with a software keyboard.

3.3.
Experiment Design

3.3.1.
Independent Variables

We prepared two conditions for this part of the study:
keyboard-based
and
voice
conditions.
We chose these two methods as they represent major text entry approaches commonly available in computers and smart devices
(
Yu et al. 2019
)
.
In using smartphones, soft keyboards are the most common text entry method while voice input is increasing its share
(
Enge 2020
)
.
They also represent two common communication modalities in real-world therapy (i.e., writing down stressful experiences vs. talking to a therapist).

3.3.2.
Dependent Variables for Stress-relieving Effect and Content

We captured the following four quantitative measures.
For the text-related measures, we used Whisper
1
1

1

We decided to use Whisper because it exhibited a high level of recognition accuracy (approximately 94%) for Japanese at the time of our analysis.

(
Radford et al. 2022
)
to transcribe the collected voice data.

Immediate Stress Change

To capture how the participants perceived the cathartic effect of each expressive writing, we asked them to respond to the question of “Comparing your responses before and after you described a stressful event, how did you feel your stress level has changed?” (Figure
1
e).
This question was based on previous work that captured stress level change through stress coping interventions
(
Tong et al. 2023
)
.
The response was in a five-point Likert scale, ranging from [-2, 2] (2: greatly increased, 1: increased, 0: no difference, -1: decreased, and -2: greatly decreased).
We asked participants to respond to this question immediately after every expressive writing.

Post-experiment Cathartic Effect

To capture the post-experiment perceptions of the cathartic effect, we asked “{Keyboard-based/Voice} input was helpful for reducing my stress,” based on Howe’s work
(
Howe et al. 2022
)
.
The response was a five-point Likert scale (1: strongly disagree to 5: strongly agree).
We collected this data in the post-experiment questionnaire.

Length

We counted the length of each expressive writing.
The length was calculated as the character length of the original Japanese text, and one English word experimentally corresponds to 2–2.5 characters in Japanese in terms of word/character counts.

Emotional Words

To capture how much the participants disclosed their emotions in the expressive writing, we collected the use of words related to negative emotion.
The usage of emotional words is known to relate to cathartic effect
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
.
We analyzed the text with the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count program (LIWC)
(
Pennebaker et al. 2001
;
Igarashi et al. 2022
)
.
For each participant/input modality combination, we separated the descriptions into words using MeCab
(
Kudo 2005
)
(a widely used segmentation and morphological analysis tool for Japanese) and then fed them into LIWC.
We used the score of positive/negative emotion words from the results of LIWC analysis, which is the percentage of words registered in the library as positive/negative emotion words out of the number of words that appeared in the text.

3.3.3.
Dependent Variables for Usability and Preference

We collected the following three quantitative data measures in the post-experiment questionnaire to understand how the participants perceived the usability of each input modality and which modality they preferred.

Ease of Use

To capture perceptions of the ease of use, we asked a question of “It was easy to enter stress by {Keyboard/Voice},” based on previous work compared the usability of digital coping methods
(
Howe et al. 2022
)
.
The response was a five-point Likert scale (1: “It was difficult to do” to 5: “It was easy to do.”).

Mental Workload

To capture the perceived mental workload associated with each input modality condition, we used NASA-TLX
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
.
NASA-TLX’s score ranges from 0 to 100, calculated as the weighted sum of the six subjective subscales.

Preference

To capture the preference for input modalities, we asked a question of “Which input method would you prefer to use?” with two answering options: “keyboard-based input” and “voice input.”

3.4.
Procedure

On the first day, we asked the participants to sign the consent form, fill in a pre-experiment questionnaire (see Appendix
A
), and ensure that they were able to use our system.
The pre-experiment questionnaire included the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)
(
Cohen et al. 1994
)
to gauge the stress levels of our participants prior to the experiment.
We explained that the main objective of this study was to collect their stress-related emotion through our system instead of investigating the effect of the two input modalities.
In this manner, we avoided giving participants potential biases.
After the pre-experiment questionnaire, we shared the link and QR code for the installation of our chatbot.
We split participants into two groups with considering the balance of gender, age, and occupations to counter-balance the order of the presentations of the two input modality conditions.

From Day 2 to Day 14, our system asked participants to share their stressful experiences and related emotions given one of the input modalities.
We sent the question between 6 p.m. and 7 p.m. daily, and reminded the incomplete participants between 9 p.m. and 10 p.m.
Additionally, since stress has been noted to have correlations with weekdays
(
Brantley et al. 1988
)
, we aimed to minimize this effect by having participants experience two different conditions on a weekly basis.
On Day 8, we flipped the input modality conditions.
We set the condition lengths to 7 days and 6 days so that the first and final day, which required a relatively longer time to answer the questionnaire, occurred on weekends and made participation easier.

On the last day, we conducted a debriefing where we disclosed the true objectives of this study.
We explicitly explained that they would be fully compensated even if they decided to withdraw at this point, and explicitly offered an opportunity to withdraw.
None of the participants withdrew from the study.
We then asked the participants to fill out a post-experiment questionnaire (Table
3
in Appendix
A
), which included questions about
ease of use
,
mental workload
and
post-experiment cathartic effect
of each input modality and the
preference
on input modalities, and the questions for PSS.
We also collected qualitative data with the open-ended questions, asking participants for their thoughts or opinions about each input condition and their reasons for their preferences.
We offered all participants approximately 19 USD in the local currency for the completion of the study.

3.5.
Participant recruitment

We recruited participants through Crowdworks, one of the most widely used crowdsourcing platforms in Japan.
Majima et al.
(
Majima et al. 2017
)
confirmed that the subjects available through Crowdworks are sufficiently reliable for behavioral research.
We recruited 28 participants (14 female and 14 male; PA1 – PA28) in their 20s to 60s (Table

).

3.6.
Data Analysis

To compare quantitative data collected in each condition or at pre- and post-experiment, we used non-parametric statistical tests.
For analyzing qualitative data, two of the authors performed a thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
.
The first author read through the free-text responses from the post-experiment questionnaire and developed a codebook with respect to factors that determined participants’ preferences.
Then, the first author and another author reviewed the codes and discussed until a consensus was reached.
Finally, we categorized the codes into themes.

Preference

Twenty-one participants preferred keyboard text entry to voice messages while seven preferred voice messages (Figure
2
).
Our chi-square test revealed that participants preferred the keyboard text entry significantly more compared to the voice message condition (
\chi^{2}(1)
= 7.0,
p
< .01).

We further examined comments we obtained from our participants in the post-experiment questionnaire.
We identified three major factors that highlighted the two important factors in the keyboard text entry method:
privacy friendliness
, and
reflection through typing
.

Our participants expressed that the voice message input was often inappropriate, because emotional disclosure tends to include private or sensitive information.
Stressful events often occur on the job or in relationships with others, and expressive writing may include negative emotions toward others.
Therefore, our participants hesitated to record voice messages in public spaces as PA1 mentioned: “
I found it very challenging to use voice input because I didn’t want others to hear me, so I had to wait for moments when there were no people around. This was especially difficult when I was at work in the company.
”

On the contrary, typing allowed participants to refine their thoughts and feelings because they could review and modify the content.
As they typed on the keyboard, the text they entered appeared on their screen.
They reviewed the content, making revisions as needed, which helped them review their writing objectively and organize their thoughts and emotions as PA12 commented “
I was able to write about the events of the day while recalling them in detail, which allowed me to organize my emotions.
”

In contrast, some participants said organizing the content while speaking was challenging, and they sometimes found themselves not knowing what to say in the middle of recording.
PA1 commented: “
I felt that I didn’t know what to say once I started voice input, and I couldn’t properly convey my thoughts.
”
With the keyboards, our participants were relaxed and could enter calmly: “
Also, when it came to speech, I was too nervous to say what I was thinking, so texting was easier for me since I could slowly look back and express my thoughts.
” (PA2).

4.3.
Summary

Our first study confirmed that the keyboard-based text entry provided better usability and was preferred more because of its privacy friendliness and reviewing process.
On the other hand, the two input modalities did not show clear differences in immediate stress relief, and pre- and post-experiment stress levels showed no significant difference.
We, therefore, kept working on improving the interface design for mobile expressive writing.
We moved our attention from input to output and examined how visual feedback could impact perceived cathartic effects.

5.
Study 2: Examination of Effects of Visual Feedback

Our next exploration is to examine the visual feedback provided after expressive writing, related to RQ2.
We obtained a separate approval for the study protocol described in this section form the Institutional Review Board of the first author’s institution.

5.1.
Experiment Design

We used the same system architecture with additions of visual feedback for the expressed emotional writing.
We decided to use the a keyboard-based text entry method based on our findings in the first study.
We also made a minor modification to allow participants to share positive episodes when they did not have any stressful moments though we did not use such data for our later analysis.

Passive
Feedback Condition

This condition offered participants unembodied, non-interactive visual feedback.
When participants submitted their stress-related emotional expression text, the system provided a short video where a note with the submitted text exploded.
As we introduced in section
2.3
, materializing negative emotions and giving feedback that the object extinguished also dissolve its tied emotions
(
Briñol et al. 2013
)
.
In the context of mobile stress management, users may rather prefer low-burden interactions
(
Howe et al. 2022
)
, and physically-demanding actions may not be suitable.
This condition also represents a situation where users delegate deleting actions to others, which has not been fully explored yet
(
Lee and Schwarz 2021
)
.
Figure
4
shows a walkthrough of this condition.
We adopted an explosion visual effect similar to feedback used in the previous VR studies
(
Grieger et al. 2021
;
Björling et al. 2019
)
.

Interactive
Feedback Condition

This condition offers participants embodied, interactive visual feedback.
After submitting their stress-related emotional expression text, the system provides an interface where they can proactively explode a note with the submitted text by tapping it.
This design was also based on studies on destroying materialized emotions.
Figure
5
shows the walkthrough of this condition.
This condition thus would require additional actions from users, but may help them build the sense of control, potentially contributing to cathartic effect positively.

We also obtained the following quantitative data for our analysis.

Long-term Stress Change

To gauge how the participants’ stress levels changed over a longer period, we used Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)
(
Cohen et al. 1994
)

This assessment tool consists of 14 questions about how often each certain situation occurred in the past week on a five-point Likert scale ranging from (0: never – 4: very often).
After collecting the score every week, we subtracted the after score from the before score and used it as the
long-term stress change
for the condition they experienced that week.

5.2.

The study lasted for 23 days (three full weeks for data collection and two days for the onboarding and post-experimental stages) to accommodate the three conditions (

The process in the onboarding and post-experimental sessions was the same as in Study 1.
In this study, we explained our participants the objective of our study to examine the effects of three feedback designs at this time.
The pre-experiment questionnaire included the questions from PSS for long-term stress change.
The onboarding required approximately 30 minutes to complete on the first day.
We split participants into six groups with considering the balance of gender, age, and occupations to fully counter-balance the order of the presentations of the three feedback conditions.

Participants are under each of the visual feedback conditions for each week of the experiment (Day 2–8, Day 9–15, and Day 16–22).
On the mornings of days 9 and 16, we asked participants to fill out PSS to track their long-term stress level trends.

We also asked when they would like to use each condition and what other feedback designs they would like to have.
The post-experiment questionnaires took around 30 minutes to complete on the final day.
We offered all participants approximately 21 USD in the local currency for the completion of the study.

We performed data analysis in the same way as we did in our first study.

6.2.1.

Our participants mentioned the cathartic effects as the advantages of

Passive

They felt they were

However, our participants saw the value of this condition outside the cathartic effect.

Qualitative Results for Preferences

6.4.1.
Scenario-dependent Use of Feedback Designs

Our participants shared their views on different use scenarios for each design.
They commented that the
None
condition
could be useful when they simply wanted someone else to hear their complaints.
Another notable use scenario was for reflecting on their feelings through writing or leaving records for future reflections: “
I want to use it (the None condition) when I can’t talk to others about something, but it’s hard for me to keep it to myself, and I want to be heard, even if it’s by a robot.
” (PB29)

With respect to the
Passive
condition
, our participants expressed that they would use it when the stress originated from others.
Other participants also explicitly mentioned that they would rather avoid using the
Passive
design when the cause of their stress was associated with themselves: “
I want to use it (the Passive condition) when I feel stressed about others. I would not use it for stress within myself because I don’t feel it is very effective.
” (PB13)

The
Interactive
condition
was deemed useful when participants felt intense stress.
However, our participants felt that they would not use it when they were exhausted: “
I wanted to use the Interactive condition when I was feeling a bit strongly stressed. There are quite a few times when expressions alone are not enough to completely relieve stress, so I felt that this condition would help me relieve even the intense stress.
” (PB33)
This impression is in line with our quantitative results on the perceived effort, where the
Interactive
condition was rated as significantly more effortful than the
Passive
condition (Section
6.3
).

In summary, our qualitative results highlight different possible use scenarios of the three visual conditions that the quantitative results did not uncover.
Our participants considered that the
None
condition would be rather useful for seeking mental cool-down.
Their comments also clarified the similarities and differences between the
Passive
and
Interactive
conditions.
Both feedback designs were considered better suited for stress stemming from others, while the
Interactive
design can be effective for supporting the relief of intense stress.

6.4.2.
Further Explorations of Visual Feedback Designs

Our participants offered various ideas on other possible visual feedback designs.
They are categorized into three design philosophies: designs that offer immediate destruction; designs that offer gradual disappearance; and designs that offer detachment.
Designs to show immediate destruction included braking blocks and letting virtual characters eat.

Designs to show gradual disappearance are visual effects where expressed negative experience would slowly disappear, such as a fade-away effect of the written text.
Our participants commented that such effect may offer more calm feeling for stress relief: “
I felt a sense of calm with a video that had a fading text effect, and I would like to continue using it. I thought the explosion effect was too exciting for stress relief and not very effective.
” (PB30)

Designs to show detachment intend to allow users to simply leave their negative experience outside themselves.
This design philosophy matches with the
None
condition, which may promote reflection.
Our participants suggested a calm visual feedback that is not directly related to the written experience, such as flowers blooming up and animals playing around: “
I think something soothing or relaxing, like animals, would be good.
” (PB8)

7.
Discussion

7.1.
RQ1: Advantages of Keyboard-based Text Entry for Stress-related Emotional Expression

Consistent with previous research in psychological literature
(
Murray and Segal 1994
)
, our results from the first study confirmed that both speech and text input produce similar immediate cathartic effects.
This result helps to understand the advantage of therapy interviews to expressive writing
(
Donnelly and Murray 1991
;
Murray et al. 1989
)
, which may lie in not the mode of expression (speech vs. written) but the presence of the listener.

Our results also showed that Keyboard-based text entry had an advantage in usability and preference when compared to Voice message input.
This contradicts the previous studies
(
Kim et al. 2018
;
Luo 2022
;
Rzepka et al. 2022
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018
)
indicating the superiority of speech input to keyboard input in usability and preference in other use contexts.
Our qualitative results suggest two possible explanations: the
confidentiality
and the
affordances
of the keyboard-based entry are a better fit for expressive writing that can involve private matters.

Stress-related expression can contain highly-private content.
In recording voice messages, our participants spoke their thoughts aloud in various settings, including in public spaces.
This privacy threat has been a major concern of voice assistants and depends on the type of information and the location
(
Easwara Moorthy and Vu 2015
)
.
However, these concerns may not be particularly serious in case where users utter non-private content (e.g., commands
(
Kim et al. 2018
)
or short journaling
(
Luo 2022
)
) in private situations.
As discussed in Section
4.1
, our participant recognized the value of keyboard-based input even in private settings as it allowed participants to relax, write at their own pace, and avoid additional strain or stress.
Our findings thus suggest that effects by input modality differences can form important application-dependent effect, stimulating further application-focused research

Our results also suggested that keyboard-based text entry may have helped the cognitive restructuring with its affordances.
The keyboard-based condition was preferred because of its persistence and editability.
These two affordances
(
Treem and Leonardi 2013
)
may have enhanced the effective process of expressive writing:
exposure
and
cognitive processing
.
Typing can make the explanation of one’s stress visible and reviewable persistently to oneself
(
Clark and Brennan 1991
)
.
This may have worked as exposure to their past stressful events, a significant process in expressive writing
(
Sloan and Marx 2004
)
.
Editability offers a capability to recraft and establish an ideal explanation
(
Walther 1993
)
.
Editing the text during expressive writing may encourage people to reconsider our thoughts and reconstruct perceptions, known as
cognitive processing, another important process to change our perspectives positively
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
.
Keyboard-based text entry was thus able to successfully offer desirable features for mobile expressive writing.

7.2.
RQ2: Visual Design Preferences Affected by Perceived Cathartic Effects, Sense of Agency, Required Effort, and Sense of Self-Denial

Our results in the second study unveiled different benefits and possible use scenarios of the three visual feedback conditions.
This result is not fully aligned with prior work showing the advantages of performing post-writing tearing that create a feeling of destruction
(
Briñol et al. 2013
;
Grieger et al. 2021
)
.
One possible explanation for this contradiction is that stress relates to diverse conditions and emotions.
According to our results, the cause of stress, particularly whether it comes from users themselves or others, was one of the determining factors to the perceived appropriateness of feedback designs.
Previous studies only examined emotion about specific topics, such as one’s body or Mediterranean diet
(
Briñol et al. 2013
)
or envious experience
(
Soesilo et al. 2021
)
.
Previous literature also reported the influence by the source of emotion.
Briñol et al. found that negative emotions related to participants’ bodies persisted even after tearing up their written thoughts, while negative emotions about the Mediterranean diet disappeared
(
Briñol et al. 2013
)
.
Grief therapy research has examined the relationship between the type of emotion and the way of disposal
(
Sas et al. 2016
)
.
Dynamic disposal (e.g., burning) is suitable for anger, but open or covert disposal (e.g., drifting or burying) is rather appropriate for sadness or regret.
Our results suggest that future systems should combine different feedback designs to accommodate people’s various types of stress and emotions, offering an important direction for future research.

Participants also found that the
None
design was still useful for calmly reflecting on their emotions.
This might be another explanation for our quantitative results not showing differences in the perceived cathartic effect.
The reply to the expressive writing may have functioned as active listening.
Active listening is one of the key skills in counseling
(
Hutchby 2005
)
and helps speakers be more emotionally mature and open to their experiences
(
Rogers and Farson 1957
)
.
Our results suggest that future research on stress management systems should consider purposeful absence of visual feedback to encourage users’ focus on their narrative and promote cognitive restructuring.

7.3.
Research Opportunities in Mobile Expressive Writing

The affordances of modern smartphones are different from those of pen and paper — the original tools of expressive writing.
Prior work has shown that expressive writing helps release repressed emotions, reorganize feelings and calm down
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2007
)
.
Yet, most of past work has naturally been conducted with tools other than the relatively modern ones, such as smartphones.
Our results with digital smartphone-powered expressive writing thus contribute to new empirical understanding of how interface factors of mobile technology can influence expressive writing effect and experience.

The qualitative findings in the second study suggest that different visual designs offer different experiences for the users.
People felt a different level of agency over relieving their stress; participants felt as if they are observers in the
Passive
condition while they felt like they are actively destroying their stress in the
Interactive
condition.
Existing literature suggests that personality (e.g., extrovert vs. introvert) could influence preferences and use scenarios of different visual feedback
(
Carver and Connor-Smith 2010
)
.
A more extensive study considering these and other similar factors will lead into a deeper understanding of appropriate interface designs for different users and use cases.

The latest speech-based mobile input technologies can perform real-time recognition and transcription
(
Li et al. 2022
)
.
Engaging in a sensitive task, such as expressive writing, calls for exploring optimal input methods.
Our results between text and speech based input showed clear differences, as explored in Section
4.1
.
Related to this, Luo demonstrated a combination of keyboard-based and speech-based text entry for food journaling
(
Luo 2022
)
, and such mixture may obtain a more optimal experience with respect to usability and stimulation of self-reflection.
Examining the division of input tasks between keyboard-based and speech-based text entry for mobile expressive writing is yet another an interesting future research direction.

Overall, our results suggest that smartphones can facilitate effective expressive writing.
Given their unique features, they can facilitate many types of rich interactions that are not available with other writing platforms.
Auditory or haptic feedback
(
Takami et al. 2022
)
may be a possible direction to explore with expressive writing solutions
For instance, Costa et al.
(
Costa et al. 2016
)
showed how haptic feedback of heart rate could help users regulate their anxiety.
Future systems could include deep breathing and mindfulness activities with emotional expression and examine how they could enhance cathartic effects and overall stress relief in conjunction with expressive writing tasks.
Such multimodal, multi-approach experiences are fairly trivial to program with modern smartphones coupled with off-the-shelf wearable sensors, such as heart rate monitors.
Multimodal interface and feedback designs for stress relief may thus lead to a new interdisciplinary research opportunity for HCI and psychology.

7.4.
Limitations

We acknowledge limitations in our work.
We conducted our studies in Japan, and they required proficient Japanese communication skills.
Accordingly, all participants were Japanese, constituting a homogeneous sample pool with respect to cultural backgrounds.
This cultural specificity may have influenced the effectiveness of speech input as talking does not strongly relate to thinking in some Asian cultures, unlike in Western contexts
(
Kim 2002
)
.
Future studies should examine how speech input could offer different effects and user experience in other cultural contexts.
This work would serve as a reference for such studies to uncover the influence of cultural differences on mobile expressive writing.

Another homogeneity in our participants was that none of our participants regularly used speech input with their smartphones, which might have affected the preference results.
However, our qualitative results uncover reasons of preferences that go beyond the established familiarity to software keyboards, and thus confirm the advantage of keyboard-based text entry for mobile expressive writing.
Our results encourage researchers to further explore input modality effects in different cultural backgrounds and user populations.

The studies reported in this article are also rather short, compared to some of the seminal work in expressive writing in the field of psychology.
Yet, during the time span our participants used the developed solutions, we were able to collect sufficiently data for quantitative and qualitative account of how the input modalities and visual designs differed.
We argue these studies adequately contribute to the HCI literature on exploring smartphones as an expressive writing tool for cathartic effect, even if not so much on the already well-established method of expressive writing itself.

8.
Conclusion

Expressing negative emotions is known to be an effective stress-coping mechanism, but challenges with respect to interface designs emerge when the practice is transitioned to technology-supported mental well-being systems.
This work investigated how input modalities for emotional expression and visual feedback designs for post-expression interventions could affect the perceived cathartic effects and user experience.
Our study highlighted the benefits and stronger preference for keyboard-based text entry over input methods using voice messages, and also revealed how different visual feedback designs can benefit users to accommodate different scenarios of stress-related emotional expression.
Our findings also suggest important design considerations and future research directions in mobile expressive writing, and further encourage interdisciplinary research between HCI and self-help approach in psychology.
