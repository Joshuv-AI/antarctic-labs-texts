Information-Theoretic Bayesian Optimization for Bilevel Optimization Problems
Takuya Kanayama, Yuki Ito, Tomoyuki Tamura, Masayuki Karasuyama

Abstract
A bilevel optimization problem consists of two optimization problems nested as an upper- and a lower-level problem, in which the optimality of the lower-level problem defines a constraint for the upper-level problem. This paper considers Bayesian optimization (BO) for the case that both the upper- and lower-levels involve expensive black-box functions. Because of its nested structure, bilevel optimization has a complex problem definition and, compared with other standard extensions of BO such as multi-objective or constraint settings, it has not been widely studied. We propose an information-theoretic approach that considers the information gain of both the upper- and lower-optimal solutions and values. This enables us to define a unified criterion that measures the benefit for both level problems, simultaneously. Further, we also show a practical lower bound based approach to evaluating the information gain. We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method through several benchmark datasets.



1
Introduction

The bilevel optimization is a standard formulation for a decision making problem that has a hierarchical structure.
It consists of two optimization problems nested as an upper- and a lower-level problem, in which the optimality of the lower-level problem defines a constraint for the upper-level problem.
For example, in the computational materials design, a target property should be optimized under the constraint of the energy minimization.
Bilevel optimization techniques is applicable to hierarchical decision makings in a variety of contexts such as inverse optimal control
(
Suryan et al., 2016
)
, chemical reaction optimization
(
Abbassi et al., 2021
)
, and shape optimization
(
Herskovits et al., 2000
)
.

We particularly focus on the case both level problems are defined by expensive black-box functions.
Most of BO studies for bilevel optimization consider applying BO only to the upper-level problem
(
Kieffer et al., 2017
;
Dogan and Prestwich, 2023
, e.g.,)
as pointed out by
(
Chew et al., 2025
)
.
Typically, under a selected query for the upper-level problem, repeated queries to the lower-level problem is required, and further, the gradient of the lower-level problem is often assumed
(
Fu et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Islam et al., 2018
and
Wang et al., 2021
consider BO in both levels, but repeated queries on lower-level is still required.
These approaches are not fully suitable when both levels are expensive black-boxes in which the gradient is not available.
On the other hand, recently a few methods without those limitations have also been studied.

Ekmekcioglu et al., 2024
combine the Thompson sampling on the upper-level query and a knowledge gradient-based extension of multi-task BO on the lower-level, but the theoretical justification for the combination of these two different criteria has not been revealed.
Further,
Chew et al., 2025
propose the well-known GP upper confidence bound (UCB) based approach to bilevel BO, called BILBO.
Although BILBO has a theoretical regret guarantee, in general, the performance of GP-UCB based methods depend on the selection of the balancing parameter of the exploitation and exploration, because the theoretically recommended value often does not provide the best performance
(
Srinivas et al., 2010
)
.

We propose an information-theoretic approach that considers the simultaneous information gain for both the upper- and lower-optimal solutions and values, which we call bilevel information gain.
This enables us to define a unified criterion that measures the benefit for both level problems simultaneously, which is not necessarily common in the case of bilevel methods as mentioned in the previous paragraph.
Although the effectiveness of information-theoretic BO has been shown in several different contexts
(
Hennig and Schuler, 2012
;
Hernández-Lobato et al., 2014
;
Hoffman and Ghahramani, 2015
;
Wang and Jegelka, 2017
;
Hernández-Lobato et al., 2015
;
Hernandez-Lobato et al., 2016
;
Suzuki et al., 2020
;
Takeno et al., 2022a
;
Takeno et al., 2022b
;
Hvarfner et al., 2022
;
Tu et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
, it has not been combined with bilevel optimization, to our knowledge.
We first define bilevel information gain by extending the idea of the joint entropy search
(
Hvarfner et al., 2022
;
Tu et al., 2022
)
.
Unfortunately, the original definition of bilevel information gain is computationally intractable, and we show that a natural extension of the truncation based approximation, which has been widely employed in information-theoretic BO
(
Wang and Jegelka, 2017
, e.g.,)
, can be derived.
By combining the truncation based approximation and a variational lower bound
(
Takeno et al., 2022b
)
, we obtain our criterion called Bilevel optimization via Lower-bound based Joint Entropy Search (BLJES).
Further, while we mainly consider ‘coupled setting’ in which upper- and lower-level observations are obtained simultaneously, ‘decoupled setting’, in which a separate observation for each level is available, is also discussed.
For example, in the case that the objective function values are outputs of some simulators (e.g., physical simulation), if a common simulator provides both level observations simultaneously, coupled setting is suitable, while if the upper- and the lower-level observations are from different simulators, decoupled setting can be more appropriate.
We further propose an extension for the case that each level problem has inequality constraints (i.e., each level problem is a constraint problem).

Our contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We show an information-theoretic formulation of bilevel BO, which has never been explored, to our knowledge.
Bilevel information gain is defined to measure the benefit for both level problems.

•

We derive a lower bound based approximation of bilevel information gain.
We extend the standard truncation based approach in the single-level information-theoretic BO to the bilevel problem.

•

We further propose extensions for decoupled setting and constraint problems.
We show that our framework can handle these settings by a natural extension of bilevel information gain.

We demonstrate effectiveness of BLJES through functions generated from Gaussian processes and several benchmark problems.

2
Preliminaries

Bilevel optimization.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
denote the upper- and the lower-level objective functions, respectively, both of which are assumed to be costly black-box functions.
The upper- and the lower-level variables are denoted by
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, respectively, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
.
The bilevel optimization problem is formulated as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}&\ f(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}))\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\ \bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x})=\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}{\arg\max}\ g(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\end{split}

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x})
represents the optimal solution of the lower-level problem for a given upper-level variable
\bm{x}
.
For simplicity, we assume the lower-level optimum
\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x})
is uniquely determined for each
\bm{x}
(called optimistic setting
(
Sinha et al., 2020
)
).
The bilevel optimal solution is denoted by
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*})
, while the lower-level optimum corresponding to a given
\bm{x}
is written as
(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}))
, noting that
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}^{*})
.
The upper- and the lower-level optimal values are denoted by

f^{*}\coloneqq f(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*})

and

g^{*}\coloneqq g(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*})
, respectively.
Figure
1
shows an illustration.
Observations of the objective functions contain additive Gaussian noise

y_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}^{f}\coloneqq f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\epsilon^{f},\ \epsilon^{f}\sim{\cal N}(0,\{\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{f}\}^{2})
,
and

y_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}^{g}\coloneqq g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\epsilon^{g},\ \epsilon^{g}\sim{\cal N}(0,\{\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{g}\}^{2})
,
where

\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{f}

and

\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{g}

are the noise standard deviations, respectively.
Let

{\cal D}_{t}\coloneqq\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},y_{i}^{f},y_{i}^{g})\}_{i=1}^{n}

be the dataset that we have at the
t
-th iteration of BO, where

y_{i}^{f}\coloneqq y_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}^{f}

and

y_{i}^{g}\coloneqq y_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}^{g}
.
The number of observed points
n
is
t+n_{0}
, where
n_{0}
is the number of the initial observations.

Figure 1:

Example of Bilevel optimization (
d_{\cal X}=1,d_{\Theta}=1
) and its optimal solution.
For each upper-level variable
x
, the feasible solution is defined by

\theta^{*}(x)=\arg\max_{\theta}g(x,\theta)
,
shown as the orange line.
The optimal solution
(x^{*},\theta^{*})
is the maximizer of
f
on the orange line.

Gaussian process.

The upper- and the lower-level objective functions are each modeled by independent Gaussian processes (GPs) with kernel functions

k^{f}((\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))

and

k^{g}((\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))
,
respectively.
Given the dataset
{\cal D}_{t}
, the predictive distribution of an objective function
h\in\{f,g\}
at a point
(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
is expressed as:

\displaystyle h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid{\cal D}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{t}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),\{\sigma_{t}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}),

(2)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{k}^{h\top}(\boldsymbol{K}^{h}+\{\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{h}\}^{2}\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}^{h},

\displaystyle\{\sigma_{t}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}

\displaystyle=k^{h}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))-\boldsymbol{k}^{h\top}(\boldsymbol{K}^{h}+\{\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{h}\}^{2}\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}^{h},

where

\boldsymbol{y}^{h}=\left(y_{1}^{h},\dots,y_{n}^{h}\right)^{\top}
,

\boldsymbol{k}^{h}=\left(k^{h}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})),\dots,k^{h}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}))\right)^{\top}
,
and

\boldsymbol{K}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

is the kernel matrix with an entry
k^{h}((\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}))
at a position
(i,j)
.
Here,
\boldsymbol{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the identity matrix.

Bayesian optimization.

We consider BO for the bilevel optimization problem (
1
).
Bayesian optimization is a method for efficiently optimizing black-box functions with a limited number of samples.
At step
t
, GPs are fitted to the dataset
{\cal D}_{t}
, and the next query point is determined as

{\arg\max}_{\bm{x},\bm{\theta}}\ \alpha_{t}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
,
where

\alpha_{t}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})

denotes the acquisition function.
After sampling the query point, the newly obtained data are added to the dataset, and the GPs are refitted.

3
Bilevel Optimization via Lower-Bound based Joint Entropy Search

We consider bilevel BO based on the information gain for the optimal solutions and values (
\bm{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*},f^{*}
, and
g^{*}
) achieved by next observations

y^{f}_{(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})}

and

y^{g}_{(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})}
,
which we call bilevel information gain.
Note that we regard the optimal
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}^{*},f^{*},g^{*})
as random variables defined by the predictive distributions of the objective functions
f(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
and
g(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
.
Our approach combines the concept of entropy search
(
Hennig and Schuler, 2012
)
, in particular, joint entropy search
(
Hvarfner et al., 2022
;
Tu et al., 2022
)
, and a variational lower bound based approximation of mutual information (MI).
We refer to our proposed method as
Bilevel optimization via Lower-bound based Joint Entropy Search
(BLJES).

3.1
Lower Bound of Mutual Information

Bilevel information gain is represented as
the MI between the candidate observations

(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})})

and the set of the optimal solutions and their upper- and lower-objective values

\{f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t}).

This criterion naturally allows simultaneous consideration of both the upper- and the lower-objectives. Since the direct evaluation of this MI is difficult, we employ a lower bound based approximation.
Let

\Omega\coloneqq\{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\}
.
Our lower bound of the MI is derived by a technique that is often used in the context of the variational approximation
(
Poole et al., 2019
, e.g.,)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t}}\biggl[\log\frac{q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathrm{KL}\left(p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\parallel q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]\eqqcolon\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(3)

where

\mathrm{KL}

is Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and

q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})

is a variational distribution (
q
can be any density function as far as the KL divergence can be defined).
The inequality of the last line can be taken because the KL divergence is non-negative (the equality holds when

p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})=q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})
).
Similar lower bounds of the MI have been used in information-theoretic multi-objective and constraint BO
(
Ishikura and Karasuyama, 2025
;
Takeno et al., 2022b
)
.

The variational distribution
q
is an approximation of

p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})

for which an exact analytical representation is difficult to know.
The difficulty is in the conditioning by the optimal solutions and values, for which the most widely accepted approach in information-theoretic BO is to use truncated distributions
(
Wang and Jegelka, 2017
;
Suzuki et al., 2020
;
Tu et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
.
For example, in the case of well-known max-value entropy search (MES), proposed by
(
Wang and Jegelka, 2017
)
for the standard single-level problem

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})
,
the predictive distribution conditioning on the max-value

f_{\rm unc}^{*}\coloneqq\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})

is approximated by the truncated normal distribution, i.e.,

p(f(\boldsymbol{x})\mid f_{\rm unc}^{*})\approx p(f(\boldsymbol{x})\mid f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f_{\rm unc}^{*})
.
When

f_{\rm unc}^{*}

is given,

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq f_{\rm unc}^{*}

should hold for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
(and there should exist at least one
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
such that
f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=f_{\rm unc}^{*}
), while MES simplifies this condition so that

f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f_{\rm unc}^{*}

holds only for the current
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Similar simplifications have been employed by most of information-theoretic BO algorithms and shown superior performance.

We extend the truncation based approach to our bilevel problem as follows.

\displaystyle q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})

\displaystyle\coloneqq p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*},g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+}),

(4)

where

{\cal D}_{t}^{+}={\cal D}_{t}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},f^{*},g^{*})\}

is the dataset augmented by the optimal point

(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},f^{*},g^{*})
.
The right hand side has the three conditions, each of which can be interpreted as follows.

•

When
f^{*}
is given,

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))\leq f^{*}

should hold for

\forall\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
.
However, this condition is computationally intractable as mentioned for the case of MES.
Based on a similar idea of MES, the condition

f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*}

is only imposed on the current
\boldsymbol{x}
.

•

When
g^{*}
is given,

g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\leq g^{*}

should hold for

\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
.
We replace it with

g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*}

in which the inequality is only imposed on the current

\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

•

In the right hand side of (
4
),
{\cal D}_{t}
is replaced
{\cal D}_{t}^{+}
.
This condition can impose that the GPs satisfy

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})=f^{*}

and

g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})=g^{*}

by adding

(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},f^{*},g^{*})

into the training data.

By substituting (
4
) into (
3
) and using the conditional independence of

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

and

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

in the right hand side of (
4
), we see

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*},g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}+\log\frac{p(y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right].

(5)

The inside of the expectation (
5
) can be analytically derived.
For both the first and second terms, the denominators are the predictive distribution of the GPs, whose density can be obtained from (
2
).
Next, we consider the numerator of the first term of (
5
).
Although the truncation is imposed on

f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))
,
the distribution is for

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

that has different input point

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

from the truncated point, unlike the case of MES.
The following theorem shows that the analytical representation can still be derived even with this difference:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

(m_{1}^{f},s_{1}^{f})
,

(m_{2}^{f},s_{2}^{f})
,
and

(m_{3}^{f},s_{3}^{f})

be the mean and standard deviation of

p(f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mid y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})
,

p(f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})
,
and

p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})
,
respectively.
Then,

\displaystyle p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Phi\left(\frac{f^{*}-m_{1}^{f}}{s_{1}^{f}}\right)\phi\left(\frac{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-m_{3}^{f}}{s_{3}^{f}}\right)/\left\{\Phi\left(\frac{f^{*}-m_{2}^{f}}{s_{2}^{f}}\right)s_{3}^{f}\right\}&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\\
\phi\left(\frac{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-m_{3}^{f}}{s_{3}^{f}}\right)/s_{3}^{f}&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

(6)

where

\phi

and

\Phi

are the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative density function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution, respectively.

The proof is in Appendix
A.1
.
Note that all of

\{(m_{i}^{f},s_{i}^{f})\}_{i=1}^{3}

can be analytically calculated from the GP posterior of
f
for which details are also show in Appendix
A.1
.
For the numerator in the second term of (
5
) can be reduced to the similar analytical form, which is shown in Appendix
A.2
.
As a result, we obtain an analytical form of the inside of the expectation (
5
).

3.2
Computations

The expectation of
(
5
)
is approximated by the Monte-Carlo method by sampling

\Omega\coloneqq\{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\}
.
The sample of all the elements of

\Omega

can be obtained through the sample of the objective functions
f
and
g
.
We use random Fourier feature (RFF)
(
Rahimi and Recht, 2008
)
, by which the GP posterior can be approximated by the Bayesian linear model.
For a
D
-dimensional RFF vector

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
the linear model

\boldsymbol{w}^{h\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

can be constructed, where

\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

is a parameter vector and

h\in\{f,g\}

represents one of objective functions.
By sampling

\boldsymbol{w}^{h}

from the posterior, approximate sample paths of

f

and

g

are obtained, which denoted as

\tilde{f}

and

\tilde{g}
,
respectively.
Then, the sample of

(f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})

is obtained through

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\text{ s.t. }\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
which can be seen as a white-box bilevel optimization problem.
Since both

\tilde{f}

and

\tilde{g}

are represented by the Bayesian liner model, they are differentiable.
Then, the gradient

\partial\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))/\partial\boldsymbol{x}

can be obtained through the implicit function theorem (see Appendix
B
for detail), by which standard gradient based optimization methods can be applied.
The samples of

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

and

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

can be obtained by adding the random noise from

{\cal N}(0,\{\sigma^{f}_{\rm noise}\}^{2})

and

{\cal N}(0,\{\sigma^{g}_{\rm noise}\}^{2})

to the sampled

f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

and

g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
respectively.

Let

K

be the number of samplings of

\Omega
.
In a variety of contexts of information-theoretic BO
(
Wang and Jegelka, 2017
, e.g.,)
, the superior performance has been repeatedly shown with small
K
settings (e.g.,
10
).
After obtaining
K
samples of
\Omega
, the Monte-Carlo approximation of (
5
) can be calculated for any
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
(Note that these
K
samples are reused during the acquisition function optimization without regenerating for each candidate
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
).
Since (
5
) contains
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})
, the maximization of the approximate (
5
) is also bilevel optimization, for which gradient-based methods can also be applied through the implicit function theorem (details are also in Appendix
B
).

4
Extensions

We here describe two extensions of BLJES, which are for decoupled setting and constraint problems.

4.1
Decoupled Setting

We mainly consider the setting in which

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

and

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

are observed simultaneously, which we call ‘coupled’ setting.
On the other hand, only one of

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

or

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

can be separately observed in some scenarios.
In this paper, this setting is called ‘decoupled’ setting, inspired by the similar setting in multi-objective BO
(
Hernandez-Lobato et al., 2016
)
.

A natural criterion for decoupled setting is information gain obtained by only one of

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

or

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}
,
for which the lower bounds can be derived by the almost same way as (
3
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right],

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right].

(8)

The derivation is in Appendix
C
.
For both the inside of the expectation of (
7
) and (
8
), the analytical calculations shown in section
3.1
can be used.
The expectation is approximated by Monte-Carlo sampling of
\Omega
, which is also same as coupled setting.
As a result, the decision making not only for selecting
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, but also selecting the upper- or the lower-observation (i.e.,

y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

or

y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}

) can be performed.

4.2
Incorporating Constraint Problems

In a more general formulation of bilevel optimization, constraints are imposed on both of the upper- and the lower-level problems.
When we have
N
and
M
inequality constraints for the upper- and the lower-level problems, respectively, the bilevel optimization problem is written as

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\

\displaystyle c^{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\geq 0,n=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}{\arg\max}\{g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid c^{L}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0,m=1,\dots,M\},

where

c^{U}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{{\cal X}}+d_{\Theta}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

and

c^{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{{\cal X}}+d_{\Theta}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

are constraint functions.
We assume that

c^{U}

and

c^{L}

are also expensive black-box functions and modeled by the independent GPs.

For constraint BO,
Takeno et al., 2022b
show an information-theoretic approach based on a lower bound with a truncated variational distribution.
By combining the truncation shown by
(
Takeno et al., 2022b
)
and our bilevel information gain, we can extend BLJES to the bilevel constraint problem.
In particular, the conditioning on the predictive distributions by the optimal points are required to extend.
For example, if
f^{*}
is given, the inequality

f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*}

is imposed only when the constraints

c^{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\geq 0,n=1,\dots,N

hold (if the constraints are not satisfied,
f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))
is not truncated).
Details are in Appendix
D
.

Experiments

We evaluated the performance of BLJES by using sample path functions from the GP prior and several benchmark functions.
For baselines, we employed Random selection and BILBO.
The initial number of observations was set
n_{0}=5
random points.
The both level observations contain an additive noise whose mean is
0
and standard deviation is
10^{-3}
.
Each experiment was performed
10
times with different initial points.
We used the Gaussian kernel for both the GPs of
f
and
g
, in which the prior mean, the kernel length-scale, the output scale, and the noise variance are optimized by the marginal likelihood at every iteration.
In BLJES, the number of Mont-Carlo samples was set as
K=30
.
We here employed the pool setting (query candidates are finite grid points defined later) because BILBO is proposed for the finite domain setting.

For the metric at the
t
-th iteration, we used the following criterion, denoted as bilevel simple regret:

\displaystyle\min_{i\in[n0+t]}\max_{h\in\{f,g\}}r_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),

(9)

where

\displaystyle r_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\max(0,f^{*}-f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}))/(f^{*}-\min_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})),

\displaystyle r_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=(g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))-g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}))/(g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))-\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})).

Our metric (
9
) takes the larger value between

r_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
,
which represents the regret of the upper-level problem, and

r_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
,
which represents those of the lower-level problem.
Since
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
can be larger than
f^{*}
, the ‘
\max
’ operation is taken to guarantee

r_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\geq 0
,
while the numerator of
r_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
is non-negative without ‘
\max
’ from the definition of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.
The denominators of

r_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})

and

r_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})

are for absorbing the scale difference of two objectives.
In (
9
), we employed the best value obtained during the entire search procedure by taking the minimum with respect to observed points.

6
Conclusion

We propose an information-theoretic approach to bilevel Bayesian optimization, called Bilevel optimization via Lower-bound based Joint Entropy Search (BLJES).
BLJES considers information gain of optimal points and values of both the upper- and lower- level problems simultaneously, by which we can define a unified criterion that measures the benefit for both the problems.
We derive a lower bound based approximation of bilevel information gain, which can be seen as a natural extension of the single level information-theoretic Bayesian optimization.
Further, we also propose extensions for decoupled setting and constraint problems.
The effectiveness of BLJES is demonstrated through sample path functions from Gaussian processes and benchmark functions.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

From Bayes theorem,

\displaystyle p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})=\frac{p(f{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*}\mid y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(f{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}.

•

The probability

m_{1}^{f}

and variance

\displaystyle=\ \mu^{f}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\\
\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}))\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\{\sigma_{t}^{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}+\{\sigma^{f}_{\rm noise}\}^{2}&\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}))\\

\displaystyle\{s_{1}^{f}\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\ \{\sigma^{f}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\}^{2}

\displaystyle-\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\\
\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}))\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\{\sigma_{t}^{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}+\{\sigma^{f}_{\rm noise}\}^{2}&\mathrm{Cov}^{f}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}))\\

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
,
and variables in
\Omega
is replaced by a sample, denoted with ‘
\tilde{~}
’.
Note that

\tilde{D}_{t}^{+}={\cal D}_{t}\cup(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*},\tilde{\theta}^{*},\tilde{f}^{*},\tilde{g}^{*})
.
Then, the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
can be written as

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}}=\left.{\frac{\partial\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})}+\left\{{\frac{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}}}\right\}^{\top}\left.{\frac{\partial\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})}

The gradient with respect
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be obtained through the usual derivative.

Appendix C
Lower Bound of Decoupled Setting

The lower bound of the information gain for the upper-level observation is

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t}}\biggl[\log\frac{q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathrm{KL}\left(p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\parallel q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]\eqqcolon\mathrm{LB}^{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}).

By setting the variational distribution as

\displaystyle q(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})

\displaystyle\coloneqq p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\leq f^{*},g(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq g^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=p(y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq f^{*},{\cal D}_{t}^{+}),

we obtain the lower bound (
7
).

D.1
Lower Bound

The MI and its lower bound can be derived by the same approach as (
3
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ ;\ f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t}}\biggl[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathrm{KL}\left(p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\parallel q(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]\eqqcolon\mathrm{LB}_{\rm c}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where here

{\cal D}_{t}^{+}\coloneqq{\cal D}_{t}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},f^{*},g^{*})\}
.

To define the variational distribution
q
, we follow the same approach as information-theoretic constraint BO proposed by
(
Takeno et al., 2022b
)
.
Let

{\cal A}^{f}=\{(c_{0},\boldsymbol{c})\mid c_{0}\geq f^{*},\boldsymbol{c}\geq 0,c_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}

and

{\cal A}^{g}=\{(c_{0},\boldsymbol{c})\mid c_{0}\geq g^{*},\boldsymbol{c}\geq 0,c_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
.
When
f^{*}
is given,
{\cal A}^{f}
is the region that

\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})}^{f}

cannot exist for
\forall\boldsymbol{x}
.
When
g^{*}
is given,
{\cal A}^{g}
is the region that

\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}

cannot exist for
\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Based on the same simplification of the conditioning discussed in section
3.1
, we define the variational distribution as

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid f^{*},g^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},{\cal D}_{t})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}},\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+}),

where

\bar{{\cal A}^{f}}

and

\bar{{\cal A}^{g}}

are the complement sets of

{\cal A}^{f}

and

{\cal A}^{g}
,
respectively.
As a result, we see

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}_{c}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}},\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\left[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}+\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t})}\right].

D.2
Analytical Representation of Variational Distribution

From Bayes theorem,

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}.

(14)

The density

p(\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})
is an
(N+1)
-dimensional independent Gaussian distribution, for which the first dimension is

{\cal N}(m^{f}_{1},\{s_{1}^{f}\}^{2})

shown in Appendix
A.1
and from the second to the
(N+1)
-th dimension is

{\cal N}(m^{c^{U}_{n}},\{s^{c^{U}_{n}}\}^{2})

where

\displaystyle m^{c^{U}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{c^{U}_{n}}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))}{\left\{\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}^{2}}(y^{c_{n}^{U}}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-\mu_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})),

\displaystyle\{s^{c^{U}_{n}}\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\frac{\left\{\mathrm{Cov}_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}((\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right\}^{2}}{\left\{\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}^{2}}.

As a result, we can derive

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=1-(1-\Phi(\frac{f^{*}-m_{1}^{f}}{s_{1}^{f}}))\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1-\Phi(\frac{0-m^{c^{U}_{n}}}{s^{c^{U}_{n}}})),

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{h}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{f}}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=1-(1-\Phi(\frac{f^{*}-m_{2}^{f}}{s_{2}^{f}}))\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1-\Phi(\frac{0-\mu_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))}{\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))})),

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=\phi(\frac{y^{f}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-m_{3}^{f}}{s_{3}^{f}})\prod_{n=1}^{N}\phi(\frac{y^{c^{U}_{n}}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-\mu_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})})/(s_{3}^{f}\sigma_{t}^{c^{U}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})).

Similarly, for the lower-level density, Bayes theorem transforms

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})p(\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}{p(\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})}

Here again, the density of the first dimension of

p(\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})
is

{\cal N}(m_{1}^{g},\{s_{1}^{g}\}^{2})

shown in Appendix
A.1
and from the second to the
(M+1)
-th dimension is

{\cal N}(m^{c^{L}_{m}},\{s^{c^{L}_{m}}\}^{2})

where

\displaystyle m^{c^{L}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{c^{L}_{m}}_{t}((\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))}{\left\{\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}^{2}}(y^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mu_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})),

\displaystyle\{s^{c^{L}_{m}}\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\left\{\mathrm{Cov}_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}((\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right\}^{2}}{\left\{\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}^{2}}.

As a result

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})},{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{g^{*}-m_{1}^{g}}{s_{1}^{g}}\right)\right)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{0-m^{c^{L}_{m}}}{s^{c^{L}_{m}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{h}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\in\bar{{\cal A}^{g}}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{g^{*}-m_{2}^{g}}{s_{2}^{g}}\right)\right)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{0-\mu_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mid{\cal D}_{t}^{+})

\displaystyle=\phi\left(\frac{y^{g}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-m_{3}^{g}}{s_{3}^{g}}\right)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\phi\left(\frac{y^{c^{L}_{m}}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}-\mu_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right)/(s_{3}^{g}\sigma_{t}^{c^{L}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))
