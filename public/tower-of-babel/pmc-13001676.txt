Transoral Approach for Balloon Dilation of the Eustachian Tube

Introduction

Obstructive Eustachian tube dysfunction (ETD) refers to the inability of the Eustachian tube (ET) to properly equalize middle ear pressure with ambient pressure. Mucosal inflammation is a common cause of obstructive ETD [1]. Typical symptoms include aural fullness, otalgia, tinnitus, and hearing loss [2]. Long‐term sequelae include middle ear atelectasis, tympanic membrane perforation, chronic otitis media, and cholesteatoma [3]. Medical management is generally directed toward reducing nasopharyngeal mucosal inflammation by addressing its underlying causes, such as treating allergies, reflux, or smoking cessation.

In patients with symptoms that are unresponsive to medical treatment, obstructive ETD was historically treated indirectly with tympanostomy tube placement or, more recently, with balloon dilation of the Eustachian tube (BDET) [3]. The use of balloons to dilate the ET under endoscopic guidance was first reported in 2010 as a minimally invasive approach to widen the ET valve to relieve symptoms related to ETD [1]. In 2016, the FDA approved the marketing of an endoscopically directed balloon device to treat persistent obstructive ETD [3].

BDET is typically performed by advancing a balloon through the nasal cavity into the ET orifice using a guide catheter. In most cases, BDET can be performed without difficulty through the nasal cavity. However, in patients with challenging nasal or Eustachian tube anatomy, this transnasal approach may be unsuccessful. Here, we present a case in which transnasal BDET was not possible due to challenging anatomy. We describe a transoral technique for BDET that bypasses obstructive nasal anatomy and offers greater flexibility in catheter angulation. This approach may serve as a useful alternative in select cases where standard access is not feasible.

Methods

Written consent was obtained from the patient for the use of photos and video. Subsequently, a literature review of current operative techniques for BDET was conducted.

Case

A 63‐year‐old female with bilateral OETD was referred from a community otolaryngologist who had previously attempted a Eustachian tube dilation. The prior attempt could not be completed due to difficult sinonasal anatomy. The patient had experienced symptoms of persistent aural fullness for 5 years, which were exacerbated on flights and during upper respiratory infections. During these exacerbations, she often had otalgia and received oral steroids. She experienced no benefit from nasal steroids or decongestants. Notably, the patient had an extensive history of scuba diving, which was limited by her symptoms. An audiogram revealed mild high‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss, and tympanometry was normal in the clinic. A prior computed tomography scan of the sinus showed a left septal spur and large right concha bullosa (Figure 1). On physical examination, moderate attic retraction of the pars flaccida was seen on the left side. Flexible nasopharyngoscopy identified hypertrophy of the right inferior turbinate and a significant left‐sided septal spur indenting the inferior turbinate. Surgical correction of the patient's nasal anatomy was discussed but ultimately decided against due to lack of significant nasal symptoms.

Technique

The patient was brought to the operating room, where general anesthesia was induced. Oxymetazoline nasal spray was applied to both nares. After draping, both nasal passages were examined with a 0‐degree rigid nasal endoscope. The bulky right inferior turbinate was lateralized with a Freer elevator, allowing passage of both the guide catheter and the endoscope. However, the left septal spur prohibited passage of the guide catheter and endoscope in the same nasal passage. Dilation of the right ET was attempted first. A 45‐degree rigid nasal endoscope was used for visualization in the right nasal passage while the guide catheter was introduced along the right nasal floor. The ET lumen was well visualized, but all attempts to advance the balloon into the cartilaginous portion of the ET were unsuccessful; the balloon would bend and deflect into the nasopharynx (Figure 2). Similarly, a transnasal approach was attempted using the guide catheter through the left nasal passage and the endoscope in the right nasal passage, but the angle of the catheter was insufficient to access the ET lumen.

Attempts to advance the balloon transnasally were unsuccessful, resulting in bending and deflection against the torus tubarius.

We suspected the angle of insertion was at issue and that this could be addressed via a transoral approach by angling the guide catheter around the soft palate. A Crowe‐Davis mouth retractor was placed. Then, a red‐rubber catheter was placed through the left nasal passage to retract the soft palate (Figure 3). With the 45‐degree endoscope providing visualization through the right nasal passage, the guide catheter was advanced into the oral cavity and around the soft palate. Subsequently, the balloon was easily advanced into the ET until resistance was encountered (Figure 4). After the balloon was inflated, deflated, and removed, the procedure was repeated transorally on the left side, with the red‐rubber catheter providing retraction through the right nasal passage and the 45‐degree endoscope providing visualization through the left nasal passage. The balloon was advanced with similar ease and successfully inflated (Video 1).

A Crowe‐Davis mouth retractor and a red‐rubber catheter were used to gain access to the nasopharynx via the oral cavity.

Results

During her four‐month follow‐up appointment, the patient reported intermittent aural fullness in the left ear but was otherwise doing well. She was able to ventilate the ears without difficulty and experienced minimal pain. There was no drainage, breath, or voice autophony. She also flew on an airplane without experiencing otalgia, otorrhea, or autophony. The patient has made plans to go scuba diving again soon.

Discussion

We present a transoral approach for BDET in an adult patient in whom the standard transnasal technique was unsuccessful due to challenging nasal and Eustachian tube anatomy. When advancing the balloon catheter and endoscope through the ipsilateral nasal passage is not feasible, the typical next step is to introduce the balloon catheter through the ipsilateral passage, while endoscopically visualizing the catheter through the contralateral nasal passage using a 45‐degree endoscope. In this case, however, that approach also failed, presumably due to the unfavorable angle of the Eustachian tube, leaving no established alternatives. The transoral approach offers key advantages in difficult cases: it bypasses obstructive nasal anatomy on both sides and adds flexibility in the angle of insertion while also avoiding additional surgery to correct the nasal abnormalities. Although this technique has previously been described in select pediatric patients [4], those reports also involved cases where the guide catheter could not be advanced due to anatomical limitations, paralleling the challenge faced in this adult case.

The only other alternative approach discussed in the literature is the transtympanic route, which involves elevating a tympanomeatal flap and carries inherent risk to middle ear structures [5]. Devices for this approach are not yet available and will have to ensure that the bony isthmus is not dilated, risking injury to the petrous carotid artery.

This case also highlights the importance of having contingency plans during surgical planning. For this patient who had failed medical management, BDET was a minimally invasive option. However, since current literature only describes transnasal access in adults, surgeons more often consider nasal corrective surgery before attempting dilation. The addition of a transoral route for BDET as a backup technique offers a minimally invasive alternative and may reduce the need for more extensive interventions.
