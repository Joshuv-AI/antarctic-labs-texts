Arthroscopic Autologous Minced Cartilage Implantation for Subchondral Insufficiency Fracture of the Knee
Authors: Eugene Pak‐Lin Ng, Richard Hin‐Lun Lee, Keith Hay‐Man Wan, Christine Yuen‐Shan Lai, Kevin Kwun‐Hung Wong, Isaac Tin‐Sui Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubchondral insufficiency fracture of the knee, previously known as spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee, commonly affects the medial femoral condyle and may progress to subchondral collapse and eventually osteoarthritis. Joint‐preserving options for focal medial femoral condyle lesions remain limited, particularly in middle‐aged patients with concomitant meniscal pathology. We describe an all‐arthroscopic technique using autologous minced cartilage implantation to treat a focal chondral defect overlying a medial femoral condyle subchondral insufficiency fracture of the knee. After diagnostic arthroscopy and meniscal repair, unstable cartilage is debrided to stable margins; the debrided cartilage is harvested and mixed with platelet‐rich plasma to form a composite. The defect is filled with the composite, which is then stabilized using fibrin sealant. The procedure provides a cost‐effective, minimally invasive solution with no donor site morbidity to treat subchondral insufficiency fracture of the knee with the aim of joint preservation.

Subchondral insufficiency fracture of the knee (SIFK) is now widely regarded as the underlying pathologic process for many cases previously labeled as spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee, with “spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee” often representing a more advanced stage in the disease spectrum rather than a primary osteonecrotic pathology. 1 Meniscal pathology such as medial meniscus posterior root tears and posterior horn radial tears is linked to the development and rapid progression of medial compartment overload and SIFK. 2 When symptoms persist or collapse occurs, surgical options range from joint‐preservation methods such as debridement, microfracture, mosaicplasty, and high tibial osteotomy to unicompartmental or total knee arthroplasty, depending on lesion stage, size, and overall joint status. 3

Arthroscopic solutions such as mosaicplasty may be attractive in selected middle‐aged patients who are not ideal arthroplasty candidates; however, they are limited by donor site availability and morbidity. 4 Subchondral‐targeted procedures such as subchondroplasty have been proposed to mechanically support insufficiency fracture regions with encouraging short‐ to midterm results, but they do not directly restore the chondral surface. 5 Autologous minced cartilage implantation targets the chondral surface and is a safe and effective single‐stage solution with low donor morbidity for treatment of cartilage defects. 6 , 7 We previously described an arthroscopic, single‐stage technique using autologous minced cartilage harvested from nonweight‐bearing areas of the knee to restore degenerative medial femoral condyle cartilage lesions. 8 In this technical note, we describe an arthroscopic single‐stage procedure using autologous minced cartilage harvested from the loose chondral fragment overlying the SIFK, augmented with platelet‐rich plasma and reimplanted into the chondral defect, performed concomitantly with repair of a medial meniscus posterior horn radial tear (Video 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications

Indications include symptomatic medial femoral condyle SIFK with a focal high‐grade unstable chondral defect (Figure 1). Contraindications include bicompartmental or tricompartmental advanced osteoarthritis, uncontained defects, concurrent ligamentous instability, and unaddressed malalignment.

Preoperative coronal and sagittal T2 MRI sequences of the left knee. (A) Coronal image shows medial femoral condyle SIFK with overlying focal chondral defect and bone marrow edema (white arrow). (B) Sagittal image shows unstable chondral flap with underlying T2 fluid signal beneath the cartilage (white arrow). (MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; SIFK, subchondral insufficiency fracture of the knee.)

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine with a lateral post and foot support to allow valgus stress and full range of motion. A thigh tourniquet is placed.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

Diagnostic arthroscopy is performed using standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals. The medial compartment is carefully evaluated for the chondral defect overlying the medial femoral condyle SIFK region. The lesion size, containment, and cartilage stability are examined (Figure 2A). The lateral compartment, patellofemoral joint, and ligaments are examined to rule out concurrent pathology.

Intraoperative arthroscopic photograph of the left knee centering on the medial femoral condyle viewing from the anterolateral portal. (A) The medial femoral condyle defect with unstable cartilage flap prior to debridement (black arrow). (B) The defect after debridement of the cartilage flap.

Meniscus Repair

The medial meniscus is examined for posterior horn radial or root tears and treated accordingly. The posterior horn radial tear in this case is repaired using an all‐inside device (Novostitch Pro, Smith & Nephew, UK).

Autologous Cartilage Harvest

Loose cartilage flaps and chondral fragments are systematically debrided using an arthroscopic shaver, working from peripheral margins toward the center of the lesion until a stable rim is achieved (Figure 2B). The debrided autologous cartilage (harvested from the unstable flap and debrided rim) is collected in a sterile manner. This is accomplished using a tissue collection basket connected to the shaver system (GraftNet, Arthrex, Naples, FL).

Minced Cartilage Preparation

The harvested cartilage is transferred to a sterile back‐table workspace and inspected to ensure it is of appropriate size and volume (Figure 3A,B). It is then mixed with autologous platelet‐rich plasma (Autologous Conditioned Plasma, Arthrex) to produce a slurry‐like composite (Figure 3C). The composite is loaded into a syringe connected to a cannulated arthroscopic spatula (Mixing and Delivery System, Arthrex).

Intraoperative photograph of the back‐table showing the preparation of the minced cartilage and platelet‐rich plasma composite. (A) Debrided cartilage is collected by a collection basket connected to the shaver (white arrow). (B) The cartilage is retrieved and inspected to ensure adequate size and volume. (C) The cartilage is emptied into the mixing funnel, and platelet‐rich plasma is added to create a slurry‐like composite.

Minced Cartilage Implantation

Arthroscopic inflow is stopped, and the defect region is carefully dried using suction and sterile applicators. The arthroscopic spatula is introduced into the joint, and the composite is carefully extruded into the defect (Figure 4A), advancing from the deepest portion and packing progressively toward the periphery until there is slight overfilling to account for settling (Figure 4B).

Intraoperative arthroscopic photograph of the left knee centering on the medial femoral condyle, viewing from the anterolateral portal. (A) Minced cartilage is extruded from the arthroscopic spatula into the defect (white arrow). The normal cartilage is depicted by the black arrow. (B) The minced cartilage is used to fill the defect (white arrow) and gently packed using the spatula. (C) Fibrin glue is applied over the implanted cartilage, sealing the surface and the perimeter (white arrow).

A fibrin sealant (Tisseel, Baxter, Austria) is then applied over the minced cartilage to seal and stabilize the construct (Figure 4C). The fibrin sealant is applied in layers: first, a thin layer directly over the graft surface and then additional layers around the lesion perimeter to create a stable scaffold that secures the implanted material and prevents displacement during early healing phases. Once the sealant has set, gentle passive range of motion is performed visualizing the graft to confirm stable graft position and intact sealant integrity (Figure 5). The portal wounds are closed in standard fashion.

Intraoperative arthroscopic photograph of the medial femoral condyle of the left knee viewing from the anterolateral portal showing the final implanted cartilage graft (black arrow), displaying complete coverage and sealed construct.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Patients are postoperatively kept nonweight‐bearing for 4 weeks and then transitioned into toe‐touch‐down for 4 weeks before finally full‐weight‐bearing at 8 weeks. Knee flexion is limited to less than 90° in the initial 8 weeks and then progresses to 0° to 120° or greater afterward. At 6 months postoperative, magnetic resonance imaging showed satisfactory regeneration of the cartilage (Figure 6).

Follow‐up MRI of the left knee taken 6 months postoperatively. (A) T2‐weighted coronal image showing regeneration of the cartilage layer over the medial femoral condyle (white arrow). (B) T2‐weighted sagittal image showing regeneration of the cartilage layer over the medial femoral condyle. (MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.)

DISCUSSION

This technical note describes an all‐arthroscopic joint‐preservation procedure for subchondral insufficiency fracture of the medial femoral condyle. We performed autologous minced cartilage implantation and concomitant repair of a medial meniscus posterior horn radial tear. Meniscal deficiency increases contact pressures and accelerates cartilage/subchondral breakdown; therefore, repair of radial or root tears is increasingly considered an important component of joint preservation in appropriately selected patients. 2 In cases where there is joint malalignment, combination with an alignment correction procedure such as high tibial osteotomy is warranted to offload pressure and protect the repair. 9 , 10

Classical joint‐preserving surgery such as arthroscopic debridement and marrow stimulation techniques such as microfracture or drilling are limited by the biomechanics and durability of the regenerated cartilage, particularly in the setting of persistent compartment overload and subchondral structural compromise. Osteochondral autograft transplantation or mosaicplasty is an established modality with favorable long‐term survivorship when appropriate mechanical alignment is achieved or corrected. 11 However, mosaicplasty is limited by osteochondral availability and donor site morbidity. Alternatively, minimally invasive subchondral augmentation such as calcium phosphate subchondroplasty has shown benefits including pain reduction and functional improvement in selected cohorts, and the technique directly targets the mechanically compromised subchondral region. 5 However, it does not directly address the chondral surface defect nor the underlying meniscal insufficiency that results in medial compartment overload. In that context, the current technique can be viewed as a procedure aiming for combined chondral surface restoration and meniscal restoration, which may be complementary to subchondral augmentation or alignment correction procedures.

The current technique offers several theoretical and practical advantages. By recycling cartilage obtained during debridement, the technique avoids donor‐site morbidity inherent to osteochondral autograft transfer. Second, the single‐stage, all‐arthroscopic approach is cost‐effective and does not require cell‐expansion facilities. Third, by addressing both cartilage and meniscal pathology simultaneously, the procedure targets 2 key contributors to SIFK progression, aligning with current biomechanical and pathoanatomic understanding. Finally, biologic enhancement with platelet‐rich plasma allows growth factors and cytokines to promote chondrogenic differentiation and healing with preliminary evidence in cartilage repair suggesting potential synergistic benefit. 12

There are several limitations inherent to this technique. First, SIFK prognosis varies substantially with lesion size, degree of subchondral compromise, and cartilage grade. The present case may not be representative of all SIFK presentations, limiting generalizability. Second, although 6‐month magnetic resonance imaging shows cartilage fill, imaging appearance does not establish long‐term durability, biomechanical function, or prevention of osteoarthritis progression. Longer follow‐up with imaging, patient‐reported outcome measures, and survivorship is necessary. Third, this technique only restores the cartilage surface but not the subchondral changes in SIFK; combination with subchondroplasty may be warranted in certain advanced collapse cases. Finally, the technique is most applicable to contained lesions; uncontained or bipolar lesions may not be adequately addressed using this technique. Specific risks of this technique include incomplete filling and minced cartilage displacement. In general, a greater amount of minced cartilage should be prepared to account for lesion packing and dead space within the delivery system. Furthermore, careful application of fibrin glue, first on the cartilage graft and then around the perimeter, and allowing sufficient time for setting should be done to ensure maximal stability and prevent early displacement. In conclusion, arthroscopic autologous minced cartilage implantation combined with repair of a medial meniscus posterior horn radial tear is a minimally invasive joint‐preserving option for carefully selected patients with a medial femoral condyle subchondral insufficiency fracture and a focal, contained chondral defect. Early postoperative imaging suggests cartilage restoration is possible. Prospective clinical studies with validated outcomes and longer follow‐up are needed to clarify the durability, clinical benefit, and appropriate role of this technique within the broader SIFK management algorithm.
