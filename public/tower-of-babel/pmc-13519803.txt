First Report of a Carbapenem-Resistant Comamonas resistens Strain Co-Carrying blaIMP-8 and Two Putative Rare β-Lactamase Genes, blaCAE-1 and blaPAU-1, in China
Authors: Yizhang Wang, Meifang Zeng, Yunfeng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenems are among the last-line antibiotics for treating severe Gram-negative infections. Comamonas spp. are increasingly recognized for their clinical pathogenicity; however, carbapenem-resistant isolates have rarely been documented.MethodsSpecies identification was confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS and whole-genome sequencing. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was determined via Vitek 2 Compact. Genomic features and genetic environments of resistance genes were analyzed using comparative genomics, with iTOL, Easyfig, and BRIG for visualization.ResultsIn this study, we identified and characterized a carbapenem-resistant C. resistens strain, CRSM1, from a pooled clinical fecal sample in China. AST revealed a multidrug-resistant phenotype, with the strain resistant to most β-lactams (including imipenem and meropenem), aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones, while remaining susceptible only to tigecycline and minocycline. Whole genome analysis identified a novel 42.8 kb plasmid, pCRSM1, which carries 12 resistance genes, including carbapenemase blaIMP-8 and two putative rare β-lactamases, blaCAE-1 and blaPAU-1. Genetic environment analysis showed that blaIMP-8 lies within a class 1 integron carrying a gene cassette array consisting of aac(6′)-Ib, a hypothetical protein gene, and blaIMP-8. blaCAE-1 is associated with a conserved IScaq2-ampR-blaCAE-1-ΔIScaq2-IScaq1 structure, and blaPAU-1 forms a conserved backbone with ampR adjacent to an IS6 element. Despite the absence of conjugative genes, these mobilization elements suggest the potential for horizontal transfer.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first report of a carbapenem-resistant C. resistens strain co-harboring blaIMP-8, blaCAE-1, and blaPAU-1 in China, underscoring the need to monitor Comamonas spp. as reservoirs of clinically significant resistance genes.

Introduction

Comamonas spp. are non-fermenting, oxidase-positive, gram-negative rods that belong to the family Comamonadaceae within the phylum Proteobacteria.1 The genus was first described in 1894, and 32 validly published species have been characterized to date.2 Comamonas spp. are well known for their functions in wastewater bioaugmentation and bioremediation,3 and can be found in a wide range of environments, such as wastewater, soil, wetlands, and hospital settings.4 Although often considered of low virulence,4 Comamonas spp. have been increasingly linked to serious human infections—including bacteremia,5 urinary tract infections,6 pneumonia,7 intra-abdominal infections,8 and meningitis9—with at least 55 outbreaks reported.10 Therefore, their clinical relevance may have been underestimated.

β-Lactams are still among the most commonly used antibiotics for treating bacterial infections because of their safety, effectiveness, and broad-spectrum activity.11 However, their clinical value has been steadily undermined by the spread of β-lactamases. These enzymes fall into four molecular classes (A–D) based on their amino acid sequence similarities. Classes A, C, and D rely on an active-site serine to hydrolyze the β-lactam rings, whereas class B enzymes such as metallo-β-lactamases use one or two zinc ions for the same reaction. β-lactamases are frequently encoded by genes that are carried on mobile genetic elements, such as plasmids, transposons, integrons, or insertion sequences. This allows the horizontal transfer of resistance characteristics between the bacteria.12 No controlled trials have been published to guide antimicrobial therapy, specifically for Comamonas spp. infections. Ceftazidime and meropenem appear to be the most consistently active agents against these organisms.13 Comamonas spp., however, have been reported to be resistant to several kinds of antibiotics, including β-lactams (eg, penicillins and cephalosporins), and can gain resistance to carbapenems. For example, a novel broad-spectrum β-lactamase blaCAE-1 was described in C. aquatica.14 Resistance to carbapenems in Comamonas has been associated with several carbapenemase genes, such as blaVIM-4 in C. jiangduensis from Algeria,15 blaAFM-1 in C. aquatica from China,16 blaIMP-1 in C. thiooxydans from Japan,17 and blaIMP-8 in C. thiooxydans from China.18

In 2023, C. resistens was identified for the first time in the activated sludge of a pharmaceutical factory in China.19 In 2025, a carbapenem-resistant C. resistens strain that co-produces blaGES-5 and blaOXA-17 was detected in urban wastewater in Brazil.20 To date, all reported C. resistens strains have been recovered from environmental sources. In this study, we identified and characterized an IMP-8-producing C. resistens strain co-carrying two putative β-lactamases (blaCAE-1 and blaPAU-1) isolated from a pooled human fecal sample in China. Given that Comamonas spp. have increasingly been implicated in nosocomial infections and that plasmid-borne carbapenemase genes are readily transferable to other Gram-negative pathogens, the detection of such a strain warrants an in-depth analysis of its genomic structure, particularly the genetic environments of the carbapenemase.

Materials and Methods

Strain Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Strain CRSM1 was isolated in October 2025 from a pooled fecal sample of patients at Sanmen People’s Hospital in Zhejiang, China. Species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; bioMérieux, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the VITEK2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) supplemented by the broth microdilution and Kirby–Bauer (K-B) methods. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were interpreted according to CLSI 2025 standards for other non-Enterobacterales bacteria (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute, CLSI document M100-35th Edition), whereas the K-B method results were interpreted using CLSI 2025 breakpoints for P. aeruginosa. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were used as controls.

Whole Genome Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using a Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., CA, United States) with a paired-end 2×150 bp library (insert size: 400 bp) and the Oxford Nanopore PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) in the standard sequencing mode. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v.0.23.1) with default parameters to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Hybrid assembly of short Illumina reads and long Nanopore reads was performed using Unicycler (v.0.5.0), which internally incorporates SPAdes for de Bruijn graph construction. The assembly was further polished with Pilon (v.1.24) via the Unicycler --pilon option. To assess the completeness and potential contamination of the assembled genomes, CheckM was used to analyze the assembly results. The average nucleotide identity (ANI) was calculated using the OrthoANIu algorithm available on the EzBioCloud platform (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani/). Digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were calculated using the GGDC 3.0 server (http://ggdc.dsmz.de/distcalc2.php) by genome sequence comparison. Assembled genome sequences were annotated using Prokka with additional BLASTP searches against the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot database for functional annotation. Specific genomic features were further predicted using dedicated tools: rRNA genes were predicted using Barrnap (v.0.9), tRNA genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE (v.2.0.10), ncRNAs using the Rfam database (v.12.2), CRISPRs using CRISPRCasFinder (v. 4.2.20), prophages using PhiSpy (v.4.2.21), and genomic islands using IslandPick (v.0.2). Default parameters were applied to all tools. ARGs were predicted using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; v.4.0.1) and the ResFinder database (v.4.7.2; https://github.com/ genomicepidemiology/resfinder). Plasmid types were identified using the PlasmidFinder database available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://www.genomic epidemiology.org). Insertion sequences (ISs) were identified using the ISfinder updated in 2026 (https://www-is.biotoul.fr). Transposons were predicted using TnCentral’s standalone tool (https://tncentral.ncc.unesp.br/). Integrons were predicted using Integron_Finder (v.2.0.2) (https://github.com/gem-pasteur/Integron_Finder). Virulence factors were analyzed using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB v. 6.0; http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/). Plasmid maps were generated using BRIG (v. 0.95; https://sourceforge.net/projects/brig/), and resistance gene background comparisons were performed using Easyfig (v. 2.2.5; https://github.com/mjsull/Easyfig).

Phylogenetic Analysis

We retrieved all publicly available genomes carrying blaCAE-1 or blaPAU-1 from the NCBI Genome database (as of April 2026; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Among these, P. aeruginosa accounted for the largest share; 11 strains harbored blaPAU-1, and six strains harbored blaCAE-1. Core genome-based phylogenetic trees were separately constructed to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships among P. aeruginosa strains harboring each resistance gene. The taxonomic classification of CRSM1 within the genus Comamonas was determined using reference genomes of various Comamonas species obtained from GenBank. All the available C. resistens genome sequences were retrieved to examine the global distribution of this species. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka (v. 1.15.6). The resulting GFF3 files were then fed into Roary (v. 3.13.0) to generate core genome alignment. For SNP detection, we used Snippy (v. 4.6.0) by mapping the sequencing reads against the reference genome. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed using IQ-TREE (v. 2.3.6) under the GTR + G model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The final tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Conjugation Assay

C. resistens CRSM1 was used as the donor, and E. coli J53 was used as the recipient. The donor was grown on Mueller–Hinton (MH) agar with 64 μg/mL ceftazidime, and the recipient was grown on MH agar with 25 μg/mL sodium azide. As a control, both strains were simultaneously inoculated on MH agar containing both antibiotics. Single colonies from single-antibiotic plates were inoculated into 2 mL LB broth and incubated at 37°C for 16 h. For mating, 200 µL of donor and 200 µL of recipient cultures were transferred into 2 mL and 4 mL LB broth, respectively, and shaken at 37°C to mid-log phase (2–3 h). Next, 1 mL of donor culture was added to 4 mL of recipient culture and incubated statically at 37°C for 24 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 200 µL saline, diluted 100- or 1,000-fold, and 100 µL was spread onto MH agar with 64 μg/mL ceftazidime and 150 μg/mL sodium azide. Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C for 16–18 h. Putative transconjugants were verified using E. coli chromogenic medium. The entire conjugation experiment was performed in three independent biological replicates. For each replicate, the number of colonies growing on double-antibiotic plates (MH agar with 64 μg/mL ceftazidime and 150 μg/mL sodium azide) was counted as transconjugants (T), while the number of colonies growing on single-antibiotic plates (MH agar with 150 μg/mL sodium azide) was counted as recipients (R). Colony counts between 30 and 300 CFU per plate were considered valid. The conjugation frequency was calculated as the ratio of transconjugants to recipients (T/R) for each replicate and expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Whole-Genome Sequencing and Taxonomic Identification of Strain CRSM1

Whole-genome sequencing of strain CRSM1 revealed a circular chromosome of approximately 5.14 Mb (GC content: 61.07%; 4,799 ORFs) and a novel circular plasmid of 42,813 bp, designated as pCRSM1 (GC content: 59.7%; 54 ORFs). CheckM analysis indicated that the genome was 99.69% complete with less than 1% contamination. The chromosome encodes two antimicrobial resistance genes (sul1 and blaOXA-21) and 44 virulence factors across eight functional classes: effector delivery system (3), immune modulation (12), nutritional/metabolic factors (7), stress survival (5), exoenzymes (1), biofilm (2), adherence (7), and motility (7). The plasmid pCRSM1 harbors 12 resistance genes (12/54 ORFs, 22.2%), including blaIMP-8, blaPAU-1, blaCAE-1, aac(6′)-Ib, aac(6′)-Ib3, aph(3″)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, catB3, dfrA17, sul1, and tet(G) (Figure 1), but no virulence genes. To determine the taxonomic position of strain CRSM1, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on the whole-genome sequences of related Comamonas species (Figure 2). Initial identification using MALDI-TOF MS suggested that CRSM1 was most closely related to C. testosteroni (99.9% confidence). However, phylogenetic analysis revealed that CRSM1 (highlighted in red) clustered within a distinct monophyletic group that contained several Comamonas spp. Among these, CRSM1 showed the highest genetic similarity to C. resistens (GCF_030064165.1), with which it formed a well-supported sub-clade. Other closely related species include C. suwonensis, C. testosteroni, and C. thiooxydans. ANI analysis confirmed the identification of CRSM1 as C. resistens with 97.68% identity to the reference genome GCF_030064165.1. The dDDH value was 80.1%, which further supports this classification. Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The CRSM1 isolate exhibited a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype. Most β-lactams failed: ticarcillin-clavulanic acid (MIC ≥128 μg/mL), piperacillin-tazobactam (MIC ≥128 μg/mL), cefoperazone-sulbactam (MIC ≥64 μg/mL), ceftazidime-avibactam (6 mm), ceftazidime (MIC ≥64 μg/mL), cefepime (MIC ≥32 μg/mL), and aztreonam (MIC ≥ 16 μg/mL). Carbapenem susceptibility results varied depending on the testing method used. The VITEK 2 system showed susceptibility to imipenem (MIC 2 μg/mL) and intermediate resistance to meropenem (MIC 8 μg/mL). In contrast, both the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion and broth microdilution methods consistently indicated resistance to imipenem (12 mm, 16 μg/mL) and meropenem (6 mm, 16 μg/mL). In addition to β-lactams, the strain also resisted several other antibiotic classes. It was resistant to the aminoglycosides gentamicin, tobramycin, and amikacin (MICs all ≥ 64 μg/mL). The fluoroquinolones ciprofloxacin (MIC ≥4 μg/mL) and levofloxacin (MIC ≥8 μg/mL) also failed. The same was true for doxycycline (MIC ≥16 μg/mL) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (MIC ≥320 μg/mL). Nevertheless, the strain remained susceptible to minocycline (MIC ≤1 μg/mL) and tigecycline (MIC ≤0.5 μg/mL) (Table 1). Worldwide Distribution and Resistance Gene Repertoire of C. Resistens

Only three C. resistens genomes with confirmed species identification were available in the GenBank database and were retrieved for comparison with strain CRSM1 (as of April 2026) (Table 2). Phylogenetic analysis showed that CRSM1 was highly divergent from all other strains (Figure 3). SNP distance analysis revealed that CRSM1 differed from the Chinese strains ZM22 and Y6 by 60,098 and 60,061 SNPs, respectively, and from Brazilian strain M13 by 59,325 SNPs. M13 differed from ZM22 and Y6 by 12,453 and 12,452 SNPs, respectively. In contrast, ZM22 and Y6, both of which were isolated from activated sludge, differed by only 3 SNPs, indicating a close genetic relationship. A comparative analysis of the ARGs revealed distinct resistance profiles among the four strains. CRSM1 harbors the most diverse ARG repertoire, which includes a carbapenemase gene (blaIMP-8) and several β-lactamases (blaOXA-21, blaPAU-1, and blaCAE-1). M13 carried carbapenemase and ESBL genes (blaGES-5 and blaOXA-17) as well as aminoglycoside resistance genes (aac(6′)-Ib and aph(3′)-XV) and sul1. ZM22 and Y6 shared a core ARG profile (blaOXA-129, catB3, mph(E), msr(E), and tet(A)), with minor differences: ZM22 additionally carried aac(6′)-Ib variants, dfrA16, and floR, whereas Y6 harbored aph(3′)-Ia (Figure 3).

Genomic Distribution and Genetic Context of blaCAE-1 and blaIMP-8

Ten CAE-1-positive genomes were identified, including six P. aeruginosa strains isolated from clinical samples in China and Thailand, mainly from sputum and urine (ZY94, ZY36, ZY1710, ZY156, PA56381, and PA99), and four Comamonas strains from both environmental and clinical sources. Comamonas isolates included C. aquatica SCLZS63 from sewage (Luzhou, China), C. thiooxydans ZDHYF418 from urine (Zhengzhou, China), C. aquatica BB1454 from hospital wastewater (Tamale, Ghana), and C. aquatica NY8661 from urine (Beijing, China). In all six P. aeruginosa isolates, blaCAE-1 was located on the chromosome of all six P. aeruginosa isolates. Among the four Comamonas isolates, blaCAE-1 was located on a plasmid in C. aquatica SCLZS63, whereas it was located chromosomally in the remaining three strains (ZDHYF418, BB1454, and NY8661). Within the genus Comamonas, blaIMP-8 was detected in two clinical isolates, C. aquatica NY8661 and C. thiooxydans ZDHYF418, and it was located on the chromosome in both strains (Supplementary Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis of the six CAE-1-positive P. aeruginosa isolates grouped them into two distinct clusters (Figure 4A). Based on Easyfig alignment, one representative strain from each cluster (ZY1710 and PA99) was selected for comparative genome alignment (Figure 4B). EasyFig-based analysis of representative P. aeruginosa and Comamonas isolates identified a conserved syntenic block containing ISCaq2-ampR-blaCAE-1-ΔISCaq2-ISCaq1 across both genera (Figure 5). The relative genomic positions of blaIMP-8 and blaCAE-1 differed between the two IMP-8-positive Comamonas isolates. In C. thiooxydans ZDHYF418, the two genes are far apart and separated by a large genomic region. In contrast, C. aquatica NY8661 and CRSM1 were located close together, with only five ORFs apart. In all three strains, blaIMP-8 lay inside a class 1 integron. The integron had a typical structure: a 5′-conserved segment (5′-CS) carrying the intI1 integrase gene, the Pc promoter, and the attI1 recombination site, together with a 3′-conserved segment (3′-CS). Inside the integron, we found a gene cassette array containing aac(6′)-Ib, a hypothetical protein gene, and blaIMP-8 (Figure 5). Global Distribution, Phylogenetic Diversity, and Sequencing Characteristics of blaPAU-1

Between 2008 and 2024, blaPAU-1 was identified across multiple regions including Asia (China, India, and Japan), North America (USA), South America (Chile), and Europe (Denmark). The gene was mostly found in P. aeruginosa isolated from different clinical samples, such as sputum, blood, urine, eye and ear secretions, and endotracheal aspirates. It was also identified in non-Pseudomonas and environmental species, including Limnohabitans spp. in aquatic habitats and Burkholderia spp. in clinical settings (Supplementary Table S2).

Core genome phylogenetic analysis of PAU-1-positive P. aeruginosa isolates revealed three clades (Clades I–III) (Figure 6A). We selected PA219 (GCA_009727125.1), P-469 (GCA_014489175.1), and LNF337 (GCA_053320655.1) as representatives of Clades I–III, respectively, and performed linear alignments against plasmid PA1280 (MH053445.1) using Easyfig. Comparative genomic analysis revealed a conserved genetic context surrounding blaPAU-1 in P. aeruginosa, characterized by a lysR–blaPAU-1–parE–toxin/antitoxin arrangement (Figure 6B). In contrast, only the lysR–blaPAU-1 segment was conserved in Limnohabitans (Figure 6C), Burkholderia, and Comamonas strains CRSM1 (Figure 6D). In CRSM1, this region is flanked downstream by a transposase gene (tnp) and upstream by accessory genes encoding a secreted protein, a DUF2845 domain-containing protein, and hypothetical proteins. Discussion

Comamonas spp. are ubiquitous in the environment and are globally distributed. To the best of our knowledge, only three C. resistens genomes (M13, ZM22T, and Y6) have been reported to date, all of which were isolated from environmental sources, with M13 carrying the carbapenemase gene blaGES-5. In this study, the C. resistens strain CRSM1 isolated from a pooled fecal sample harbored the carbapenemase gene blaIMP-8. Unexpectedly, phylogenetic analysis revealed approximately 60,000 SNPs between CRSM1 and the three aforementioned strains, which may reflect adaptive responses to distinct environmental niches, such as antibiotic pressure and immune selection within the human intestinal tract.

Clinically, Comamonas spp. are frequently associated with intra-abdominal infections, such as peritonitis and appendicitis, and bacterial translocation from the digestive tract is considered the predominant cause of infection.22 The most commonly reported species was C. testosteroni, followed by C. kerstersii. Due to the limited recognition of the pathogenic potential of intestinal Comamonas, only a few studies have reported the isolation of Comamonas spp. from stool samples.22–24 Comamonas spp., as opportunistic pathogens, may translocate across a disrupted intestinal mucosal barrier, particularly in immunocompromised patients or those with underlying comorbidities such as liver cirrhosis and diabetes mellitus, thereby leading to infection.10 Genomic analysis revealed that the MDR C. resistens strain CRSM1 carried 44 virulence genes and 14 resistance genes. Given that the human intestinal tract is a critical reservoir for MDR bacteria,25 which complicates clinical management and limits therapeutic options, this genomic case report of the gut-derived strain CRSM1 may, to some extent, provide reference data for understanding resistance mechanisms in this genus.

IMP-type metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) are a class of carbapenemases that constitute a global public health challenge.26 These enzymes were first described in Japan in 1991 when blaIMP-1 was discovered in a clinical strain of P. aeruginosa.27 To date, 91 variants of IMP-type MBLs have been identified, of which blaIMP-1 is the most prevalent. MBLs are widely distributed worldwide, particularly in Asia, and are most commonly associated with P. aeruginosa.28 blaIMP-8, a blaIMP-2 variation with four nucleotide differences, was initially reported in a Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate in Taiwan, China, in 2001.29 Since then, blaIMP-8 has been identified in several non-Comamonas species, such as E. coli in Portugal,30 Citrobacter freundii in Germany,31 Serratia spp. in China,32 P. aeruginosa in Spain,33 and Acinetobacter spp. in China.34 Within the genus Comamonas, three IMP-8-positive Comamonas isolates were identified: C. aquatica NY8661, C. thiooxydans ZDHYF418, and C. resistens CRSM1 (this study), all of which also carried blaCAE-1. Among these, C. thiooxydans ZDHYF418 is the only species previously documented to cause urinary tract infection in China.18

In this study, a notable discrepancy in carbapenem susceptibility profiles for CRSM1 was observed between the automated VITEK-2 system and the manual methods (disk diffusion and broth dilution). The VITEK 2 system reported imipenem and meropenem MICs of 2 (susceptible) and 8 μg/mL (intermediate), respectively. In contrast, both disk diffusion and broth dilution indicated a resistant phenotype (imipenem: zone diameter = 12 mm, MIC = 16 μg/mL; meropenem: zone diameter = 6 mm, MIC = 16 μg/mL), in line with a previous study on C. testosteroni that reported 100% agreement between disk diffusion and broth dilution methods for imipenem and meropenem.35 To date, no published study has evaluated the performance of the VITEK 2 system in Comamonas spp. However, several studies have questioned the accuracy of carbapenem susceptibility testing, especially for imipenem. For example, Joyanes et al reported only 87.0% essential agreement for imipenem against P. aeruginosa for the VITEK 2 system, which is below the usually accepted 90%.36 Similarly, the lowest categorical agreement (83%) of the VITEK 2 Compact system for imipenem compared to the broth microdilution method was reported in a multicenter study by Scandinavia.37 The discrepancy in carbapenem susceptibility profiles may be due to the slow growth of C. resistens, which causes attenuated VITEK 2 signals and compromises carbapenem MIC readings. Since broth microdilution is generally considered the reference method, the carbapenem susceptibility results from the VITEK 2 system should be verified by broth microdilution.

Class 1 integrons are key genetic elements that capture and express mobile gene cassettes via site-specific recombination.38 It has been previously reported that blaIMP-8 is located within class 1 integrons.34,39–41 We identified a class 1 complete integron carrying the aac(6′)-Ib-hypothetical protein(hp)-blaIMP-8 array in all IMP-8-harboring Comamonas. Notably, this integron was plasmid-borne only in strain CRSM1, whereas in strains NY8661 and ZDHYF418, it was embedded within a Tn402 transposon (tniA-tniB-tniQ-tniR-aac(6ʹ)-Ib-hp-blaIMP-8-intI1) located on the chromosome. Tn402-like transposons are spread through TniABQ-mediated cointegration and TniR-catalyzed resolution.42,43 In plasmid pCRSM1, however, the Tn402 structure was truncated, and the tniABQ cluster was missing. This deletion may have resulted from an abortive transposition or resolution error. One possible explanation is that such deletions reduce the fitness cost of plasmid carriage; by eliminating the metabolic burden of a fully functional transposition system, the host may gain a competitive advantage.

Recently, a rare broad-spectrum class A serine β-lactamase, designated blaCAE-1, was identified in a plasmid from a strain of C. aquatica isolated from sewage. A cloning assay in E. coli demonstrated that blaCAE-1 conferred resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins, but not to carbapenems.14 However, another study suggested that blaCAE-1 is a carbapenemase that contributes to reduced carbapenem susceptibility in combination with porin loss in clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa. Comparative genomic analysis further suggested that blaCAE-1 could be horizontally transferred through integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) or plasmids and is already disseminated among clinical P. aeruginosa isolates.44 Using BLASTn against sequences in the GenBank database, we identified a rare resistance gene blaCAE-1 in P. aeruginosa and Comamonas spp. from environmental and clinical samples in China, Thailand, and Ghana. P. aeruginosa is widely recognized as a major opportunistic pathogen, whereas Comamonas species are increasingly being recognized as emerging opportunistic pathogens in clinical settings. The presence of blaCAE-1 in both genera may implicate non-fermentative Gram-negative bacteria as potential reservoirs.

In all six P. aeruginosa isolates, blaCAE-1 was chromosomally located, whereas in Comamonas, it was found on a non-transmissible plasmid in C. aquatica SCLZS63 or C. resistens CRSM1 and on the chromosome in the remaining three strains. Furthermore, a conserved resistance region (ISCaq2-ampR-blaCAE-1-ΔISCaq2-ISCaq1) was identified as a syntenic block shared between the genera. Within this syntenic block, the ampR-blaCAE-1 element was flanked by an intact upstream ISCaq2 and a truncated downstream ΔISCaq2 (disrupted by ISCaq1). Both ISCaq1 and ISCaq2 belong to the IS5 family (the IS903 group). The flanking IScaq2 elements were in opposite orientations, which rules out the formation of a typical IScaq2-based composite transposon. The mechanism by which blaCAE-1 is mobilized remains unclear. However, its presence across different genera—mostly on chromosomes or non-transmissible plasmids—raises the possibility that this syntenic block may act as a mobile unit, potentially via unconventional transposition or site-specific recombination, although this hypothesis requires experimental validation.

The genomic locations of blaIMP-8 and blaCAE-1 varied significantly among CRSM1, NY8661, and ZDHYF418. In ZDHYF418, the two genes are located far apart on the chromosome. NY8661 (chromosome) and CRSM1 (plasmid) were located in close proximity and were separated by only five ORFs. It is speculated that the two resistance genes were acquired through independent horizontal transfer or recombination events, rather than being inherited as a fixed genomic pattern shared across isolates.

blaPAU-1 is a class A Bush–Jacoby group 2be β-lactamase (293 amino acids) that shares 74% identity with a putative class A β-lactamase from Rhodoferax saidenbachensis.45 To date, reports of blaPAU-1 remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one study by Wang et al documented a Tn3 family transposon (tnpA–res–hp–relE–blaPAU-1–lysR) inside a transferable plasmid from a clinical P. aeruginosa strain (PA1280).45 Based on BLASTn analysis, we identified blaPAU-1 in isolates from Asia, North America, South America, and Europe between 2008 and 2024. Although predominantly identified in P. aeruginosa from various clinical specimens, blaPAU-1 was also detected in environmental Limnohabitans spp. and in non-Pseudomonas clinical genera, such as Burkholderia. This study reports the first identification of blaPAU-1 in Comamonas (strain CRSM1). Comparative genomic analysis revealed a conserved lysR–blaPAU-1 core structure cross-species and cross-ecological distribution, without a conserved or fixed mobile genetic element. It is embedded within chromosomal or plasmid backbones and exhibits diverse genetic contexts. This structural variability may reflect multiple horizontal transfer mechanisms, which warrant further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, because the isolate was recovered from a pooled fecal specimen without individual traceability, its pathogenicity remains undetermined. Second, the discrepancy between the VITEK 2 and manual AST methods suggests that automated systems may underestimate carbapenem resistance in this genus, necessitating further validation with larger cohorts. Third, the functional roles of blaCAE-1 and blaPAU-1 remain unverified, as no cloning, heterologous expression, susceptibility testing of transformants, or enzymatic characterization was performed in this study. Finally, while the cross-genus distribution of blaIMP-8, blaCAE-1, and blaPAU-1 indicates horizontal gene transfer, the exact mobilization mechanisms and evolutionary drivers of their diverse genetic contexts require additional functional molecular studies to be fully elucidated.

Conclusions

In summary, we characterized an MDR C. resistens strain, CRSM1, harboring blaIMP-8 and two putative rare β-lactamase genes (blaCAE-1 and blaPAU-1) on a novel non-conjugative plasmid. These resistance genes were located on defective or truncated transposons or adjacent to insertion sequences, suggesting a possible horizontal transfer or clonal spread. Although the clinical significance of this strain remains to be determined, our findings highlight the role of Comamonas spp. as reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance genes, and underscore the need for confirmatory carbapenem susceptibility testing using broth microdilution, continued genomic surveillance, and enhanced clinical monitoring.
