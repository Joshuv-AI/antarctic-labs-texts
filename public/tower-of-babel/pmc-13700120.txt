Incidence and predictors of diabetic ketoacidosis among patients with diabetes mellitus receiving follow-up care in public health centers of Lideta Sub-City, Addis Ababa: a retrospective cohort study, 2023
Authors: Getasew Kassaw, Asressie Molla, Binalfew Tsehay, Mellese Tsehay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe most dangerous acute consequence of diabetes mellitus is diabetic ketoacidosis, which has become more prevalent in diabetic individuals. Although diabetic ketoacidosis is becoming more common in people with diabetes mellitus, little is known about its incidence and predictors. In Ethiopia, this is particularly true.ObjectiveTo assess the incidence of diabetic ketoacidosis and its predictors among patients with diabetes mellitus attending follow-up care in public health centers of Lideta Sub-City, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodA retrospective follow-up study was conducted among diabetic mellitus patients who were registered from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, in Lideta Sub-City public health centers, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Sampling with probability proportional to size and the simple random sampling technique were used to select patients. Data were collected from the patient charts by using a pretested checklist. The data were entered into EPI data version 3.1 and analyzed using STATA version 14.0. The Cox proportional hazard regression model was fitted to identify the independent predictors of diabetic ketoacidosis.ResultsOut of the 419 diabetic patients included in the study, 114 (27.2%) developed Diabetic Ketoacidosis in the entire follow-up period. Hence, the cumulative incidence was 0.742 per 100-person-month of observation. Type 1 diabetes mellitus (AHR = 1.88, 95%CI: 1.12, 3.15), history of medication noncompliance (AHR = 2.7, 95% CI: 1.52, 4.8), recent six month poor glycemic control (AHR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.07, 3.4), acute recent illness (AHR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.02, 3.01) and talking insulin medication (AHR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.39, 3.8) were significant predictors of DKA.ConclusionThe incidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was high. Uncontrolled recent six-month glycemic levels, taking insulin medication rather than OHGA, having type 1 diabetes mellitus, has a history of medication noncompliance, and acute illness were the predictors. Diabetic care should prioritize these predictors to mitigate the public health and economic impacts of diabetic ketoacidosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Diabetes is a disease characterized by high blood glucose resulting from abnormal insulin production, function, or both [1]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) has become much more common worldwide, accounting for over 5 million deaths over the past ten years [2]. DM can be classified as type 1 or type 2 (T1DM or T2DM) [1].

A state of acute metabolic stress known as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is brought on by an absolute or relative lack of insulin for the body’s glucose metabolism [3]. It is mainly explained by hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and acidemia that can be associated with the arising counter-regulatory hormones, including cortisol, glucagon, and epinephrine [2].

Acidosis, ketosis, and generally hyperglycemia are the hallmarks of DKA, a significant acute metabolic consequence of T1DM [4]. DKA commonly occurs in T1DM; however, it can occur irrespective of DM type [1]. It is becoming more frequently reported in T2DM patients, and in some cases, it may even be their first symptom [5]. The symptoms of uncontrolled diabetes that may lead to the development of DKA are typically of short duration and include weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain, fatigue, polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia [6].

The primary cause of hospitalization for DM patients is DKA, and treating it has a significant financial impact on health care expenses, both directly and indirectly, from the person to the national economy [7]. After high blood pressure and tobacco use, high blood glucose levels from DM is the third greatest risk factor for early death [8]. The predominant cause of diabetic-related morbidity, mortality, and medical expenses for DM patients is DKA [8].

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is one of the most serious acute complications of diabetes mellitus and remains a major cause of morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to comprehensive diabetes care is often limited. Although advances in diabetes management have reduced the occurrence of DKA in many high-income countries, it continues to be a common reason for hospital admission in sub-Saharan Africa. Factors such as delayed diagnosis, poor glycaemic control, inadequate treatment adherence, infections, and limited access to insulin and diabetes self-management support contribute to its continued burden [9–11].

Developing countries like Sub-Saharan countries, including Ethiopia, mostly focus on communicable diseases; consequently, DM complications, particularly DKA continues to be the leading cause of premature mortality [12].

It is challenging to attain high glycaemic control, and previous studies have identified a number of factors that contribute to poor control in patients, including age, gender, education level, weight, smoking status, length of diabetes, medication use, and many other factors [12]. The incidence of poor glycaemic control is crucial, but it has proven challenging to determine which of these characteristics are most closely linked to poor glycaemic control [12, 13].

In Ethiopia, DKA plays a significant role in both drug use and bed occupancy, as well as the cost of inpatient diabetes care and medical admissions for both adults and children. According to research done in some regions of our nation, DKA accounts for around 71.1% of diabetic admissions, with a 5.8% death rate [1].

In Ethiopia, most available studies have focused on the prevalence, treatment outcomes, or precipitating factors of DKA among patients admitted to referral or tertiary hospitals [3, 14–16]. However, there is limited evidence on the incidence of DKA after the diagnosis of diabetes and the factors associated with its development among patients receiving routine follow-up care at public primary healthcare facilities. Because primary healthcare centers provide ongoing diabetes care for a large proportion of patients in Ethiopia, understanding the occurrence of DKA in these settings is essential for improving patient management and preventing serious complications. The lack of evidence from primary healthcare facilities makes it difficult for clinicians and health planners to identify patients at increased risk and to develop targeted strategies for DKA prevention.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the incidence of diabetic ketoacidosis and identify its predictors among patients with diabetes mellitus receiving follow-up care at public health centers in Lideta Sub-City, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Lideta Sub-City public health centers, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, on DM clients who had follow-up between January1, 2018, and December 31, 2022. Lideta Sub-City is one of the Sub-Cities of the Addis Ababa city administration, located in the central part of the city. It is bordered by Arada Sub-City to the north, Kolfe Keranio to the west, Kerkos to the east, and Nefas Silk Lafto Sub-City to the south. The Sub-City has 10 woredas. According to the 2009 E.C. census projection, the Sub-City has a total population of 265,187 (127,290 male and 137,897 female). Currently, in the Sub-City, there were eight public health centers, and the public health centers have 2,155 DM patients [17].

Study design

An institution-based retrospective cohort.

Source population

All individuals diagnosed with DM, and having follow up in Lideta Sub-City public health centers.

Study population

All individuals diagnosed with DM and who had DM follow-up from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, in Lideta Sub-City public health centers.

Inclusion criteria

All individuals who were diagnosed with DM and have DM follow-up from January1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, in Lideta Sub-City public health centers.

Exclusion criteria

Patients diagnosed with DM and DKA at the same time.

The 11 medical records excluded from the analysis had missing information on one or more key variables required for the study. Specifically, incomplete data were identified for history of medication compliance, which prevented determination of the study outcome and/or inclusion of these records in the regression analysis.

To evaluate the potential for selection bias, we compared the excluded records with those included in the analysis using the limited information available, such as [e.g., age, sex, admission year, or facility]. No notable differences were observed between the two groups, suggesting that the excluded records were unlikely to differ systematically from those analyzed.

Sampling technique and procedure

All eight public health centers (Abinet Health Center, Beletshachew Health Center, Dadim Hidassie Health Center, Hidassiefre Health Center, Jejagama Kello Health Center, Lideta Health Center, Teklehaymanot Health Center, and Woreda 4 Health Center) were considered. Then, to select the study participants from each Health Center, probability proportional to size (PPS) was used based on caseloads. After that, medical records of individuals diagnosed with DM were identified from diabetic follow-up logbooks from January1, 2018, to December 30 2022. From the identified medical charts in each Health Center, the computer-generated simple random sampling technique was applied to select the study participants (Fig. 1).

Dependent variable

Time to develop diabetic ketoacidosis.

Independent variable

Socio-demographic Variables: Age, sex, and residence.

Clinical variables: Recent acute illness, types of DM, glycemic control, DM duration, presence of comorbidity, and presence of chronic diabetic complications.

Treatment factors: Follow-up frequency, type of drug used, and medication non-compliance.

Operational definitions

Event

occurrence of DKA from diagnosis to the end of the study.

Censored

individuals diagnosed with DM and who do not develop DKA during the follow-up study (transfers out, died, lost to follow-up, or still have not developed DKA).

Survival

Time measured between the date of DM diagnosis and the date of developing DKA or date censored.

Diabetic ketoacidosis

was determined based on the clinical diagnosis of DKA made by a clinician or the health care provider.

Classification of diabetes type

was determined based on the clinical diagnosis made by the physician or the health care provider (T1DM and T2DM).

Co-morbidities

an individual with DM and having other chronic medical or psychiatric conditions.

Psychiatric disorders

diagnosed with depression, anxiety Schizophrenia, substance abuse behaviors, dementia, epilepsy, and eating disorders.

Chronic respiratory disorders

chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and pulmonary hypertension.

Chronic liver diseases

chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, alcoholic liver disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

Incomplete records

medical charts with incomplete information for the variables of date of DM diagnosis and date of the event occurred or last observation.

Recent acute illness

diagnosis of (acute febrile illness, respiratory infection, myocardial infarction, urinary tract infection, and gastrointestinal infection) before two weeks of developing DKA or the last follow-up observation, which is recorded in the patients’ medical records card.

Controlled blood glucose

average three-month FBS level ranges from 70 to 130 mg/dl or HgA1c < 7%, referred to as controlled [19].

Uncontrolled blood glucose

average three-month FBS > 130 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 7% [19].

Recent six-month glycemic control

history of recorded glycemic control (poor or good glycemic control) in the near six-month follow-up periods before developing DKA or being censored.

Medication noncompliance

history of medication noncompliance recorded in patients ‘medical chart.

Data collection tool and procedure

The data extraction checklist was developed by the principal investigator after reviewing the medical chart and different relevant literature [1, 7, 18, 20]. Data were collected from the patient’s chart by using a pretested checklist. Charts were retrieved based on their medical registration number in each Health Center. The data-collecting team consisted of chart finders.

Three BSc Nurses, were data collectors, and the supervisor was a BSC Health Officer. The extracted data was coded to avoid duplication. The data was collected from January1, 2023, to March 30, 2023.

Data quality assurance

Before data collection, the checklist was evaluated by experts and advisors. The pre-test was done in Lideta Sub-City health centers on 5% of the sample size from diabetic patients’ charts that were registered before one month of January 1, 2018. After that, the necessary modifications were done on the checklist. One day, training was given to data collectors and a supervisor by the principal investigator before actual data collection. The training was focused on introducing the purpose of the study, data collection tools, the initial and end of the data collection period, correct completion of the checklist, and ethical considerations to standardize the data collection. The completeness of the collected data was checked on the site daily basis on the site during data collection with feedback by the supervisor and the principal investigator.

Data processing and analysis

The data were coded, cleaned, and checked for consistency and completeness, and then entered into EPI INFO version 3.1 and exported into STATA version 14.0 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed to determine the frequencies and summary statistics. The Cox proportional hazard model assumption was checked using the Schoenfeld residuals test, and Cox Snell residual test. Variables having a p-value less than 0.25 in bivariable analysis were candidates for multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis [21]. Variables having a p-value less than or equal to 0.05 in the multivariable model was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Among 430 diabetic medical charts from the 8 public health centers, 419 of them were included in the study and analyzed. The 11 (2.5%) were accounted for as incomplete charts and had no history of follow up after their DM diagnosis. Out of 430 DM patients, more than half, 232 (55.37%) were females (Fig. 2), and all were dwellers. The mean age of the patients at the time of diabetes diagnosis was 40 years, with 95% CI (39–41).

Of all patients, 329 (78.52%) were T2DM patients and 297 (70.88%) used oral hypoglycemic agents. Out of the total diabetic patients, 115 (27.45%) had chronic comorbidity. Hypertension, chronic respiratory disorders, cardiovascular disorders, and HIV/AIDS were found to be the most common comorbid diseases. About 104 (24.82%) of patients had poor recent six-month glycemic control (Tables 2 and 3).

Incidence of DKA among diabetic patients

Totally, 419 adult diabetic patients were followed for different periods for five years with 15,350 person months’ observations. The overall mean follow-up length was 36.6 months (95% CI: 35.4, 37.87). From the total diabetic patients included in the analysis, about 114 (27.2%, 95% CI: 23.1, 31.7) developed DKA, and 305 (72.8%) were censored in the entire follow-up period. Out of the total DKA, 63 (55.3%) were females (Table 4).

The overall DKA incidence in the cohort was 0.74 per 100 person-month (8.9 per 100 person-years) observation (95% CI, 0.62, 0.89). The incidence of DKA was 2.41 per 100 person-month T1DM and 0.42 per 100 person-month T2DM (Table 4).

Overall survival function

The overall Kaplan-Meier estimate showed that the probability of DKA free survival of diabetic patients was high during 1 to 15 months of observation. The probability of DKA free survival relatively falls as the follow-up month increases (Fig. 3).

The Kaplan-Meier estimator survival curve shows the estimate of DKA free survival among different groups of variables to make comparisons. Separate graphs of the estimates of the Kaplan-Meier survivor functions were constructed for categorical variables as described below (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). In general, the pattern that one survivorship function lies above another means the group defined by the upper curve has better DKA free survival than the group defined by the lower curve.

Bivariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis

In the bivariable Cox regression model, eight variables, namely DM type, type of DM treatment, presence of acute illness, comorbidities, medication noncompliance, presence of other acute DM complications, recent six-month glycemic control, and follow-up frequency were selected for multivariable analysis by bi-variable Cox proportional hazard regression with a cut-off point of P ≤ 0.25 (Table 5).

In the multivariable Cox regression model, five variables were statistically significant risk factors for the development of diabetic ketoacidosis. DM type, insulin medication, presence of acute illness, medication noncompliance, and recent six-month glycemic control were significantly associated with the incidence and time to develop DKA (Table 5).

The assumption of proportional hazard was checked using the overall Schoenfeld global test of the full model, graphically, and time varying covariate.

Discussion

This retrospective follow-up study aimed to determine the incidence and predictors of DKA among diabetic patients who have a diabetic follow-up in Lideta Sub-City public health centers.

Among the total 419 followed diabetic patients in this study, the proportion of DKA during the study period was 27.21% (95% CI: 23.1, 31.7). This finding was lower than a study conducted in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia,32.25% [1], West and East Gojjam Referral Hospitals,58.5% [3], and Hawassa, 40% [15]. A number of factors may account for these differences, including variations in the proportion of patients with type 1 diabetes, disease severity at baseline, duration of follow-up, and the accessibility and quality of diabetes care. Together, these factors are likely to influence glycaemic control and, consequently, the risk of DKA [22].

Additionally, this result was lower than an institution-based retrospective study done in Nigeria, 40% [4], Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, 34% [23], Iran, 55.5% [24], and Hangzhou, China, 50.1% [6], and another retrospective study conducted in Australia and New Zealand, 41.5% [25].

On the other hand, this finding is greater than the study in Jimma medical center, Jimma, Ethiopia, 19.5% [1], the study conducted in the tertiary hospital of Northeast Ethiopia, 19.9% [26], and in Sao Paulo, Brazil, 0.87% [27]. However, the reasons for these differences are likely to vary across settings. The higher proportion reported in Hangzhou, China (50.1%), may be explained by differences in the study population, including a greater proportion of patients with type 1 diabetes or the inclusion of patients receiving care at a specialized tertiary diabetes center, where more severe or complex cases are commonly managed. Variations in study design, follow-up duration, and case ascertainment may also have contributed to the higher estimate. In contrast, the much lower proportion reported in Brazil (0.87%) may reflect differences in healthcare delivery, including more comprehensive diabetes management, better access to diabetes education, insulin therapy, and glucose-monitoring technologies, as well as earlier identification and management of poor glycaemic control. Additionally, differences in healthcare systems, socioeconomic conditions, patient adherence to treatment, and support for diabetes self-management may have further contributed to the variation observed between studies.

The incidence rate of DKA was 0.742 per 100 person month (8.9 PY) (95% CI, 0.62, 0.89) observation. The incidence of DKA was 2.41 per 100 person-month (28.8 per 100 person-year (T1DM) and 0.42 per 100 person-month (5.04 per 100 person-year) (T2DM)). This is in line with a systematic study conducted in developed countries (0.263 per 1000 PY of DKA incidence) [7] and the study conducted in Korea [28]. On the other hand, this is lower than the study in Bahir Dar (1.04 per 100 person-month) [3]. The discrepancy in this study might be explained by the accessibility of health care facilities.

In this study, patients with a history of acute recent illness were 1.75 times hazardous to DKA as compared to those without a history of acute recent illness. This is in line with studies in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia [1] and the study conducted in West and East Gojjam Referral Hospitals [3]. The release of cytokines and counter-regulatory hormones during acute sickness may be the cause of this hypermetabolic condition. This may increase the production of glucose in the liver and cause insulin resistance, which could lead to DKA [8].

Diabetic patients with poor six-month glycemic control had 1.9 times higher risk of DKA as compared to patients with controlled glycemic control and this is comparable with studies in Jimma [1], USA [29], and Australia [25]. The explanation could be that a broad spectrum of hyperglycaemic emergencies can result from both lower and higher blood sugar levels from the normal range due to increased counter-regulatory hormones, altered glucose synthesis, and lipolysis [30].

The findings of this study also revealed that type T1DM patients had 1.88 times more hazards to DKA than those patients with T2DM. This is supported by different studies conducted at tertiary hospitals in North East Ethiopia [3], Debre Markos referral hospital [29], North Wollo and Waghimra zone [18], and Jima Medical Center [1]. This is because individuals with T1DM lack insulin, which leads to lipid breakdown and DKA, while those with T2DM have endogenous insulin, which inhibits lipolysis and can prevent DKA.

The risk of DKA among patients who had a history of medication noncompliance was 2.7 times more hazards relative to those who were without a history of medication noncompliance. The research conducted in North Wollo and Waghimra, Northern Ethiopia, showed a similar result [29, 18]. This is because not taking or not following the prescribed course of anti-diabetic medication treatment could increase the blood glucose level, which is the major cause of DKA [18], and the lower magnitude of risk of DKA in our study could be the result of under documentation, as our study was secondary data.

A diabetes patient who took an insulin drug had 2.3 times more likely to reflect underlying disease severity and the predominance of type 1 diabetes rather than being a direct cause of DKA. This finding is in line with the community-based study conducted in Australia [31]. By reducing tissue glucose uptake, increasing glucose absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, and increasing gluconeogenesis and glycogenosis-which leads to lipolysis and DKA-insulin therapy can raise blood glucose levels. Another explanation could be that patients who take insulin require ongoing education on how to properly manage their diabetes, and there is a difference in the clinical course of the disease between those who use insulin and those who use OHGA.

Strength and limitations

To the extent of our knowledge, it was the first study to establish the incidence and predictors of DKA among diabetic patients in the study areas in Ethiopia. This study was conducted in multi-center facilities of health centers. The study was done with a long follow-up period, five years.

Since the data were collected from secondary sources, some important variables such as nutritional history, economic factors, behavioral characteristics (use of alcohol, cigarettes, and chat abuse), and demographic characteristics (educational status, religion, and marital status) were missed, which may have a significant prediction with the incidence of DKA. Also, this finding was based on patient data documented in the medical charts of patients. Sometimes, it might have missed documentation.

Excluding simultaneous DM/DKA diagnoses could systematically remove a clinically important subset of T1DM patients from the cohort, which would tend to bias the T1DM incidence estimate downward relative to the “true” risk from diagnosis onward.

The DKA was identified based on the treating clinician’s documented diagnosis in the medical records, as the biochemical parameters required for standard diagnostic confirmation were not consistently available in this retrospective study.

Conclusion

The incidence of DKA among diabetes patients was found to be high. More than a quarter of DM patients developed DKA within 36.6 months of follow-up. Uncontrolled recent six-month glycemic level, taking insulin medication, than OHGA, a patient with T1DM, has a history of medication noncompliance, and acute illness were factors independently associated with DKA. However, controlled recent six-month glycemic levels and patients with T2DM was reduced risk for the occurrence of DKA.
