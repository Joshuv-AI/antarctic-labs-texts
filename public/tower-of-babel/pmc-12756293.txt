Antepartum prediction of shoulder dystocia using machine learning
Authors: Lior Heresco, Noa Levy, Omer Todress, Hadar Gluska, Tal Biron-Shental, Omer Weitzner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeShoulder dystocia (SD) is a serious delivery complication, often occurring without identifiable risk factors. Accurate risk assessment is essential for educated decisions regarding the mode of delivery. This study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning-based model for SD prediction.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of term singleton vaginal deliveries at a single academic hospital over 10 years. Exclusion criteria included cesarean deliveries and multiple gestations. Maternal and fetal characteristics were compared between SD and non-SD cases. Eleven features were selected for model development. Missing values in normally distributed variables were imputed using mean values. To address class imbalance, repeated random sampling of non-SD cases was performed. Data were split into training and test sets (70:30) and standardized. Multiple machine learning models—including logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, support vector machine, XGBoost, and CatBoost—were evaluated using cross-validation and area under the ROC curve (AUC).ResultsAmong 51,628 deliveries, 94 (0.18%) involved SD. SD was associated with higher BMI, shorter stature, and increased diabetes rates (all p < 0.05). Mean birthweight was significantly higher in the SD group (3751 g vs 3287 g, p < 0.01). The CatBoost model achieved the highest performance (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI 0.77–0.89). Key predictive features were sonographic estimated fetal weight (EFW, 55.6% of the model’s decision-making weight), maternal BMI (20.1%), and clinical EFW (7.9%).ConclusionOur machine learning-based model predicted SD with an AUC of 0.83 and may support clinicians in delivery planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-025-08271-5.

Introduction

Shoulder dystocia (SD) is an obstetric emergency that occurs in 0.2–3% of vaginal deliveries, when one or both of the fetal shoulders are obstructed at the level of the pelvic inlet, typically by the symphysis pubis, after the expulsion of the fetal head. SD results from a size discrepancy between the fetal shoulders and the pelvic inlet [1].

The definition of SD is different in various sources, but a commonly accepted definition is the inability to deliver the fetal shoulders with gentle downward pressure on the fetal head, necessitating additional obstetric maneuvers to facilitate the delivery. Alternatively, a more precise definition involves the duration of more than 60 s between the delivery of the fetal head and the rest of the body [2].

SD is associated with both increased maternal complications, including postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), third- and fourth-degree tears, and cervical lacerations [3], and increased neonatal morbidity such as low Apgar scores, brachial plexus injuries, clavicular and brachial fractures, acidosis, and hypoxic neurologic damage [4–6].

Although some risk factors for the occurrence of SD, including maternal diabetes mellitus (DM), maternal obesity, and fetal macrosomia, have been previously identified [7, 8], SD remains difficult to predict [9–11]. Previous studies have shown that only 25% of shoulder dystocia cases are associated with at least one significant risk factor. Additionally, most of these cases occur in neonates weighing less than 4000 g [12].

While cesarean delivery can be performed as a preventive measure against shoulder dystocia, the potential rise in unnecessary cesarean deliveries prompts a need to explore additional independent risk factors that could improve SD prediction.

Machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for developing predictive models across diverse areas of medicine. In the field of obstetrics and gynecology, numerous studies have demonstrated its utility in forecasting a wide range of clinical outcomes, including implantation success, neonatal outcomes, preterm delivery, fetal weight estimation, postpartum complications, and the likelihood of successful vaginal delivery [13–16].

Consequently, the study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning-based model for predicting shoulder dystocia. This model would integrate both maternal data and fetal growth parameters obtained through ultrasound examination conducted near delivery, thus enabling the estimation of SD risk prior to the onset of labor. The model could serve as an additional tool to aid clinicians in their decision-making process concerning counseling for delivery mode and in estimating the risk of shoulder dystocia.

Materials and methods

In this retrospective study, data were collected from medical records of patients delivered at Meir Medical center between 2014 and 2023. Included in the study singleton pregnancies of patients delivered at term (≥ 37 + 0 weeks of gestational age), in a successful spontaneous or instrumental vaginal delivery. Exclusion criteria were cesarean deliveries and multiple gestations. Maternal characteristics included maternal age, body mass index (BMI), gestational weight gain, obstetric history, and the presence of diabetes. Fetal data included gestational age at delivery, estimated fetal weight (EFW) based on both sonographic and clinical assessments performed within 2 weeks prior to delivery, fetal sex, and birthweight. Ultrasound EFW included the standard fetal biometry measurements of AC, FL, BPD, and HC. Calculation by the ultrasound machine is based on Hadlock’s formula.

Maternal and fetal parameters were compared between two groups: deliveries complicated by shoulder dystocia and those without shoulder dystocia.

Statistical and computational analysis

Categorical variables were compared between shoulder dystocia and control cases using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables between groups were compared using t test. A probability value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

All additional analyses were conducted in Python using Pandas, Scikit-learn, and NumPy libraries.

The dataset included both continuous and categorical variables.

Data preprocessing steps included:

Initial preprocessing of the SD dataset involved identifying and handling missing values. Several variables had no missing data, while others required imputation. Variables such as Clinical EFW, which were not normally distributed, were imputed using the median. Conversely, for normally distributed variables such as sonographic EFW US weight and BMI, missing values were filled using the mean. Although the sonographic EFW had a high proportion of missing values (41.4% in the SD group), its distribution was normal, and therefore, the missing data were imputed and the column was retained in the analysis. Two features—current weight and weight gain during pregnancy—had excessive missing data (48% and 58% respectively), and thus were excluded from further analysis.

For the non-SD cohort, given the significant class imbalance, a representative subset was selected to match the SD group. Only variables retained in the SD dataset were included. Records containing missing or outlier values, likely due to data entry or measurement errors, were excluded. To reduce the imbalance between 94 shoulder dystocia (SD) cases and over 50,000 non-SD deliveries, we used repeated random undersampling. In each round, 200 non-SD cases with complete data were randomly chosen and combined with all SD cases to create a balanced training set. The process was repeated several times to include different non-SD samples and to limit bias toward the majority group. After filtering, 6,637 complete and clean records without SD remained, forming a high-quality and balanced dataset for model development.

The final dataset included 11 variables: maternal age, diabetes mellitus status, number of abortions (AB), number of previous cesarean deliveries (CD), number of prior vaginal deliveries (P), number of successful vaginal births after cesarean (VBAC), sonographic and clinical estimated fetal weight (EFW), BMI, gestational age at delivery, and fetal sex.

Machine learning model development

We evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, and CatBoost. The dataset was randomly split into 70% training and 30% testing sets. Cross-validation was performed to optimize hyperparameters, and model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent, as the data were fully retrospective and anonymized.

Results

Model evaluation and performance metrics

After data preprocessing and filtering, a total of 6731 patients were included in the dataset used for model development, comprising 94 SD cases and 6637 non-SD cases. CatBoost demonstrated the highest predictive performance, outperforming all other models. Figure 1 displays the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the Catboost model, a common tool for evaluating the performance of a binary classification model. Catboost model achieved a mean area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.83 (95% CI 0.77–0.89), suggesting that the model correctly ranks a randomly chosen positive instance higher than a randomly chosen negative instance approximately 83% of the time. The CatBoost model identified the sonographic EFW as the most influential predictor of SD, contributing 55.56% of the model’s decision-making weight. This was followed by maternal BMI at 20.08%, and clinical EFW with 7.87%.

Together, the top five features account for over 91% of the model’s predictive power.

To translate our predictive model into a clinically applicable tool, we developed a user-friendly application using the Python Tkinter library, based on the CatBoost algorithm. The application functions as a risk calculator: clinicians can input patient-specific parameters, and the tool provides an individualized prediction regarding the likelihood of shoulder dystocia. A demonstration of the application is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Video 1).

Discussion

Shoulder dystocia (SD) remains a challenging obstetric emergency due to its unpredictable occurrence, even among neonates with average birth weights. Notably, the mean birth weight in our SD cohort was 3751 g, emphasizing that significant SD events occur frequently at weights below the commonly cited threshold of macrosomia (4000 g) [17]. This underscores the limitation of relying solely on fetal weight to predict SD, reinforcing the critical need for more accurate and comprehensive predictive models.

Machine learning methodologies have emerged as highly effective predictive tools across various medical disciplines, including obstetrics and gynecology. Previous studies have successfully utilized machine learning models to predict numerous obstetric outcomes, including preterm birth, postpartum complications, and fetal weight estimation [18–20]. The adoption of machine learning allows for nuanced analyses of large, complex datasets, enhancing clinical decision-making by providing robust predictive capabilities beyond traditional statistical models. Given the complex nature of obstetric complications like SD, machine learning offers substantial potential for improving risk stratification and clinical outcomes [2]. The proposed calculator (Supplementary Video 1) could be implemented prior to delivery as a decision-support tool to estimate the individualized risk for shoulder dystocia. In cases identified as high risk, the model may assist clinicians in counseling patients regarding the potential benefits and risks of elective cesarean delivery, or in providing more informed explanations to facilitate shared and informed decision-making. Importantly, this tool is intended to complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment, and should serve as an adjunct to the clinician’s comprehensive assessment.

One notable advantage of our predictive model is its reliance exclusively on basic antenatal parameters available prior to labor onset—primarily demographic details, maternal BMI, and fetal weight estimations. The simplicity and accessibility of these predictors ensure that the model is broadly applicable, facilitating easy integration into standard clinical practice [21, 22]. This simplicity could significantly enhance prenatal counseling and decision-making regarding delivery mode, especially in low-resource settings or clinical environments where extensive data collection may not be feasible.

Our model identifies 44.8% of true SD cases, it maintains a very high specificity of 92.9% with a low false-positive rate of only 7.1%. This performance is particularly important in the context of a rare outcome such as shoulder dystocia. The model reliably distinguishes low-risk patients and minimizes unnecessary alerts, thereby reducing the likelihood of overestimating risk or prompting unwarranted interventions. Even with moderate sensitivity, the ability to limit false positives makes the model clinically valuable by providing meaningful and practical risk stratification to support decision-making.

However, our study is subject to several limitations inherent to its retrospective nature. Retrospective data collection often results in incomplete medical records, necessitating imputation of missing data, which could potentially affect the accuracy and generalizability of our findings [23]. The use of mean-value imputation to address missing data, while statistically acceptable, may introduce bias and diminish the reliability of predictions [24]. Additionally, the relative rarity of SD events limited our ability to analyze large number of cases; nevertheless, our cohort size remains substantial relative to existing literature, lending credibility to the study despite these constraints [25]. The model was developed using data from a single center, which may limit its generalizability. However, Meir Medical Centre serves a diverse population of women referred from different geographic and socioeconomic regions across Israel, which partially mitigates this limitation. Nevertheless, future multicenter studies are warranted to externally validate the model across different clinical settings and to increase the number of shoulder dystocia cases, thereby improving the model’s robustness and generalizability. In addition, prospective studies are needed to validate the model’s predictive performance in real-world clinical settings and to continuously refine its algorithm as additional data become available, thereby enhancing its clinical reliability and applicability.

In conclusion, our machine learning-based model demonstrated significant predictive accuracy for antenatal shoulder dystocia, capitalizing on basic demographic and obstetric parameters easily accessible before delivery. Further prospective validation and refinement of such predictive models could markedly improve clinical management strategies and outcomes associated with shoulder dystocia [26].
