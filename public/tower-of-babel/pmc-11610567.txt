DNA damage repair factor Rad18 controls virulence partially via transcriptional suppression of genes HWP1 and ECE1 in Candida albicans
Authors: Runlu Chen, Yuting Feng, Huaxin Cai, Shaling Yang, Xiaoyin She, Jinrong Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDNA damage repair is a crucial cellular mechanism for rectifying DNA lesions arising during growth and development. Among the various repair pathways, postreplication repair (PRR) plays a pivotal role in resolving single-stranded gaps induced by DNA damage. However, the contribution of PRR to virulence remains elusive in the fungal pathogen Candida albicans (C. albicans). In this study, we investigated the role of Rad18, a critical component of PRR, in DNA damage response and virulence in C. albicans. We observed that deletion of RAD18 in C. albicans resulted in heightened sensitivity to DNA damage stress. Through deletion of specific internal domains coupled with spot assay analysis, we show that the internal RING and SAP domains play essential roles in DNA damage response, whereas the ZNF domain was less important. Surprisingly, the lack of Rad18 in C. albicans resulted in heightened intracellular survival within macrophages and elevated virulence in the Galleria mellonella model. RNAseq analysis revealed that loss of Rad18 upregulated the transcription of genes encoding transporters and oxidoreductases, as well as virulence genes, including HWP1 and ECE1. Suppression of the transcription of these virulence genes in the RAD18 deletion strain by a dCas9-mediated CRISPRi system reversed this increased virulence. Taken together, these data demonstrate that Rad18 plays a significant role in virulence partially through transcriptional suppression of virulence genes HWP1 and ECE1 in C. albicans. Our findings provide valuable insights into the intricate relationship between DNA damage response and virulence in C. albicans.KEYWORDS: DNA damage response, postreplication repair, Rad18, virulence, Candida albicans

Introduction

Candida albicans remains one of the most prevalent fungal pathogens in humans, causing both mucosal and systemic infections, and is responsible for approximately 70% of fungal infections worldwide [1]. The pathogenicity of this fungus is intricately associated with its capacity to switch between different morphologies, to adhere to and invade host cells, to adapt metabolically and acquire nutrients, as well as to mask cell wall components for immune evasion [2]. For instance, adhesion/invasion proteins encoded by ALS1, ALS3, and HWP1, as well as critical transcription factors Efg1 and Cph1, play crucial roles in virulence [3]. Additionally, fungal resistance to DNA damage stress induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by host macrophages or neutrophils is also associated with its ability to infect the host [4]. Several studies have demonstrated that deletion of RTT109 and RAD23, which are involved in DNA damage repair in C. albicans, leads to a reduction in pathogenicity in host models [5,6], emphasizing the significance of DNA damage repair genes in the virulence of this fungal pathogen.

The accurate copying of genomic DNA is crucial for cells to faithfully transfer genetic information from mother cells to daughter cells [7]. However, various intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including cell metabolism, radiation, and viral infection, can induce modifications in genomic DNA and give rise to an array of DNA lesions within the genome, resulting in detrimental effects on cells [8,9]. Thus, critical DNA damage repair mechanisms such as homologous recombination (HR), non−homologous end-joining (NHEJ), base excision repair (BER), nucleotide excision repair (NER), DNA postreplication repair (PRR), and DNA mismatch repair (MMR), are employed to rectify damaged DNA [10]. For instance, DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are typically repaired through HR or NHEJ [11], while UV-induced DNA lesions are usually fixed by NER [12].

DNA PRR is a mechanism that fills single-stranded gaps induced by DNA damage without removing replication-blocking lesions [13,14]. PRR consists of two coordinated repair pathways [13]: the translesion synthesis (TLS) process, which bypasses DNA lesions without excising mutated DNA using low-fidelity DNA polymerase, and the template switch process, which utilizes information from the newly synthesized sister chromatid. The E3 ubiquitin ligase Rad18 plays an essential role in coordinating PRR by interacting with the Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA). During DNA replication, PCNA loads onto chromatin but dissociates upon the completion of replication, serving as a platform for proteins involved in replication and damage repair [15]. Upon DNA damage, Rad18 is recruited to DNA lesions by RPA-coated ssDNA and mono-ubiquitinates PCNA on K164 with the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme Rad6 [16,17]. Subsequently, mono-ubiquitinated PCNA recruits the TLS polymerase to initiate PRR [18]. Additionally, Rad18 also mediates the poly-ubiquitination of PCNA by recruiting Rad5, an E3 ubiquitin-ligase, and redirects the PRR mode towards template switching (TS) [19]. Rad18 has additional functions independent of PRR [20]. It transmits DNA damage signals to initiate homologous recombination repair [21], relying on its recruitment to DNA break sites for promoting homologous recombination. In addition, Rad18 acts as an adaptor by directly interacting with the recombinase Rad51C to facilitate homologous recombination [21]. Moreover, Rad18 suppresses the toxic effect of NHEJ on HR during DNA replication and promotes Rad51 foci at sites of radiotherapy-induced double-strand breaks (DSBs) [22,23]. In chicken DT40 cells, rad18-deficient cells fully restored HR-mediated DSB repair, suggesting that vertebrate Rad18 regulates the balance between NHEJ and HR [23].

DNA postreplication repair has been extensively studied in the model eukaryotic organism Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and the classical components, including Pol30, Rad6, Rad18, and Rad5, have been well characterized. However, our understanding of PRR in the pathogenic fungus C. albicans is limited, except for the primary PRR factor Rad6 [24]. In C. albicans, Rad6 is indispensable, similar to its ortholog in S. cerevisiae, and plays a critical role in conferring resistance against UV stress [25]. Interestingly, Rad6 negatively affects hyphal growth in an Efg1-dependent manner, suggesting a correlation to virulence [24]. Our previous study has demonstrated that in C. albicans, deletion of RAD18 increases sensitivity to the DNA damage-inducing agent methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) [26], but its detailed function in DNA damage response and potential association with morphogenesis and virulence remains unclear. In this study, we elucidated the function of Rad18 in DNA damage response and discovered its negative impact on antioxidation processes and virulence; this impact is associated with the downregulation of genes involved in antioxidation processes and virulence, such as ECE1 and HWP1. Our findings establish a connection between the DNA damage repair factor Rad18 and virulence in C. albicans.

Materials and methods

Strains, media, and reagents

C. albicans strains were cultured in liquid YPD media supplemented with 50 mg uridine per litre as previously described [26]. Details of the strains and primers used in this study can be found in Table S1 and S2, respectively. The ScRAD18-deletion strain was generously provided by Dr. Linghuo Jiang from the Guangxi Academy of Sciences. Methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) was purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). Hydroxyurea (HU) and other reagents or amino acids for media preparation were obtained from Sangon (Shanghai, China). 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) was purchased from ACMEC (Shanghai, China). Solid media contained 2% agar.

Gene deletion, rescue, and epitope tagging of proteins

The RAD18-deletion strain was obtained from a previous study [26]. To construct a complemented strain for the RAD18-deletion strain, a DNA fragment containing the ORF, promoter, and terminator of the RAD18 gene was amplified from the genome and cloned into the Kpn I site in the CIP10 plasmid using T4 ligase (TransGen, China), generating the CIP10-RAD18 plasmid. The Stu I site sequence within the RAD18 ORF was mutated to a synonymous codon by PCR using the CIP10-RAD18 plasmid as a template. Subsequently, the PCR products were digested with the Dpn I enzyme before being transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Vazyme, China). The transformants were confirmed by sequencing to validate the presence of the mutated region in the RAD18 gene. Following this, the mutated CIP10-RAD18 plasmid was linearized with Stu I and transformed into the RAD18-deletion strain using the PEG/LiAc method. The integration of the linearized plasmid into the genomic RP10 locus was confirmed by PCR.

To overexpress the RAD18 gene, a DNA fragment containing the ORF and the terminator of the RAD18 gene was amplified from the genome and integrated into the Xho I and Kpn I sites in the CIP10-ADH1p plasmid [4] using a One Step Cloning Kit (Yeasen, China), generating the CIP10-ADH1p-RAD18 plasmid. Additionally, the Stu I site in the RAD18 ORF was also mutated by PCR, as described above. The mutated CIP10-ADH1p-RAD18 plasmid was digested with Stu I and then transformed into a wild-type SN148 strain using the PEG/LiAc method. The transcription of the RAD18 gene in the wild-type strain carrying the ADH1p-RAD18 cassette was validated through qRT-PCR.

The DNA sequences encoding internal RING, SAP, and ZNF domains within the Rad18 protein were excised using an integrated CRISPR/Cas9 system [27]. To introduce the sgRNA sequence into the pV1093 plasmid, a pair of primers encompassing the sgRNA sequence targeting the RING, SAP, or ZNF domains were employed for PCR amplification with the pV1093 plasmid serving as a template. Subsequently, the PCR products were digested with the Dpn I enzyme before transformation into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Vazyme, China). The plasmid from the transformants was confirmed through EcoR I digestion. Meanwhile, the donor DNA for replacing the deletion region was generated through primer annealing, wherein a pair of primers containing 60 ~ 80 bps homology to the upstream and downstream sequences of the specific region were utilized, along with ~ 20bp complementary sequences. Subsequently, the pV1093 plasmid containing the specific sgRNA sequence was linearized with Sac I and Kpn I, and transformed into the wild-type strain SN148 along with its specific donor DNA, using the PEG/LiAc method. Transformants were selected on YPD supplemented with 200 μg/mL nourseothricin. The domain deletion strains were validated through PCR and DNA sequencing.

To suppress the transcription of ECE1 and HWP1 in the RAD18-deletion strain, a dCas9-mediated CRISPRi repression system was employed [22]. In brief, the interference plasmid containing the sgRNA sequence targeting specific promoter regions of the virulence genes was generated by PCR using the pRS159 plasmid as a template. The PCR products were digested with the Dpn I enzyme before transformation into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Vazyme, China). The plasmids from the transformants were confirmed through Xho I digestion. Subsequently, the interference plasmid carrying sgRNA was linearized by Pac I and transformed into the RAD18 deletion strain. Transformants were selected on YPD supplemented with 200 μg/mL nourseothricin. The transcription of ECE1 and HWP1 in these transformants was validated by qRT-PCR.

Real time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The samples of the wild-type strain and the RAD18-deletion strain were collected as described previously, with or without treatment of 0.015% MMS for 90 minutes. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (R701, Vazyme, China), and cDNA was synthesized using the 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R312, Vazyme, China) containing DNase for removing residual DNA in the template. The qRT-PCRs were performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q311, Vazyme, China) according to the protocol from the manufacturer. The transcription of GAPDH was used as a control. Data for each gene was averaged from at least three independent experiments.

Genome stability assay

Genome stability was assessed as previously described [26]. Wild-type and RAD18-deletion strains were transformed with a Stu I-linearized CIP10 plasmid integrated at the RP10 locus. The strains carrying a heterozygous URA3 marker were cultured in YPD medium overnight at 30°C. The following day, cells were collected and washed twice with distilled water, and 10-fold dilutions were made before spotting. For each strain, overnight cells (15 μL) were spotted on YPD plus 5-FOA (10 mg/mL) plates, while 10−4 dilutions of cells (15 μL) were spotted onto YPD plates to determine cell numbers. After a 2-day incubation period, the colonies on YPD plus 5-FOA plates and YPD plates were counted. The genome stability was evaluated by determining the ratio of colonies on the YPD plus 5-FOA plates to every 100,000 colonies on the YPD plates.

Hyphal formation assay

The hyphal formation of C. albicans cells in liquid medium was evaluated by inoculating overnight cells at a dilution ratio of 1/20 into YPD medium plus 20% foetal bovine serum (FBS), or into Spider medium, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. After centrifugation, the cells from 1.5 mL cultures were collected and resuspended in 100 μL medium. Subsequently, a 5 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was mounted and visualized using a Leica DM5000B microscope equipped with a 40× objective.

Hyphal formation on solid media was examined using YPD plates supplemented with 20% FBS and Spider plates. Overnight cultures were diluted by 10−4, and 3 μL of the dilution was spread onto each plate. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 3 to 5 days before being imaged.

Biofilm assay

Biofilm assays were conducted according to a previously described protocol [6]. Single colonies of the experimental strains were inoculated into 3 mL of SC medium and incubated overnight at 30°C with shaking. The overnight culture was then diluted to an OD600 of 1.0, added into a 24-well polystyrene plate (NEST, China), and incubated for 48 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the SC medium was decanted, and the remaining cells were washed three times with 700 μL PBS. After air drying for 45 minutes, samples were stained with 385 μL of 0.4% (wt/vol) aqueous crystal violet for a further period of 45 minutes. Wells were subsequently washed four times with sterile distilled water (1 mL each time). Each culture was destained by the addition of 700 μL of 95% ethanol. Following a room temperature incubation for another 45 minutes, the destaining solutions were diluted 5-fold in 95% ethanol. Finally, absorbance readings at OD595 were measured and averaged across triplicate experiments for each strain.

Survival of C. albicans cells within macrophages

RAW264.7 mouse macrophages (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) were seeded into 24-well plates (5 × 104/well) and challenged with C. albicans at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:1. Following a co-incubation period of 2 or 5 h at 37°C with 5% CO2, non-engulfed fungal cells were removed by washing plates with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To assess the viability of C. albicans cells, 500 μL of 0.01% SDS was applied to each well of the plates, followed by cultivation the released cells on YPD agar plates using 10-fold serial dilutions [6].

Virulence studies

To assess the essentiality of Rad18 in virulence, a Galleria mellonella model was employed as previously described [28]. A 10 μL inoculum of C. albicans cells at a concentration of 5 × 107/mL was injected through the left proleg into larvae weighing approximately 300 mg. The infected larvae were incubated at either 37°C or 30°C and monitored daily for survival. Data from each strain were combined, and survival curves were generated according to the Kaplan – Meier method using the PRISM program (GraphPad Software 8.0.1).

To assess fungal burden, 200 μL of 0.7 × 107/mL wild-type and RAD18 deletion cells were injected intravenously into the tail vein of male ICR mice (6 weeks of age, from Animal Center of Nantong University) as described previously [6]. After 48 h of infection, the right kidneys of the infected mice were collected and ground in 1 mL of PBS. The resulting kidney homogenates were then spread onto YPD agar plates with dilutions spanning from 10 to 100-fold. The CFUs in the kidneys from the RAD18 deletion group and the WT group were counted and compared using GraphPad Software 8.0.1. Mouse studies were carried out under the guidelines established by the Ethics Committee of Nantong University, China (S20240715–050). All animal experiments in this study were in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and checklist.

RNA preparation and RNAseq assay

Two individual colonies of the C. albicans wild-type strain and the RAD18-deletion strain were inoculated into 3 mL liquid YPD and incubated overnight at 30°C on a shaker rotating at 200 rpm. The overnight cultures were then diluted to OD600 of 0.1 in 10 mL YPD media and grown to OD600 of 0.6–0.8 at 30°C with shaking. The cells were subsequently exposed to 0.015% MMS for 90 minutes before being harvested for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the standard method by Suzhou Panomix Biomedical Tech Co. [29]. The mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. To preferentially select cDNA fragments of 370 ~ 420 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system. Then, PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers and Index (X) Primer. Finally, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system), and library quality was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Following library preparation, sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated (Suzhou Panomix Biomedical Tech Co.). C. albicans SC5314 was used as a reference genome, and was downloaded from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/21?genome_assembly_id=294796). Raw and processed data have been deposited in the NGDC GSA database (CRA014168).

Transcripts exhibiting adjusted P-values ≤0.05 as determined by DESeq2 analysis were considered indicative of differential expression. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes was implemented by the cluster Profiler R package, which accounted for gene length bias correction. GO terms displaying corrected P-values less than or equal to 0.05 were deemed significantly enriched among differentially expressed genes.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

The pGADT7 and pHIS2 plasmids were employed for the yeast one-hybrid assay [30]. The ORF of the CaRAD18 gene was amplified from the genome and integrated into the EcoR I site of the pGADT7 plasmid using a One Step Cloning Kit as described above (Yeasen, China). Similarly, the promoters of HWP1, AMO1, ALD6, and IFF4 were amplified by PCR and integrated into the EcoR I site of the pHIS2 plasmid. Yeast strain Y187 was transformed with different pairs of pGADT7-CaRad18 plasmids and a series of pHIS2 plasmids using the PEG/LiAc method and selected on SC medium lacking tryptophan and leucine (SC−Trp−Leu). Subsequently, the transformants were picked and spotted onto SC medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, and histidine (SC−Trp−Leu−His) containing 50 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT), followed by incubation at 30°C for 2–3 days.

Results

Rad18 contributes to resistance to genotoxic stress in C. albicans

The DNA damage response in C. albicans is intricately linked to the organism’s virulence [14]; however, the precise role of PRR in DNA damage response and virulence remains unclear. Rad18 is a core subunit involved in PRR in various model organisms. To elucidate the role of RAD18 in DNA damage response in C. albicans, we examined its transcriptional response to genotoxic stresses. Upon exposure to MMS stress, the transcription of RAD18 increased by approximately 3.1-fold compared to the control group without stress; by contrast, no evident change was observed in RAD18 transcription during HU-induced replication stress (Figure 1a). Consistent with its close relationship with RAD18, the transcription of RAD6 was also significantly induced by MMS stress in C. albicans, while HU stress slightly suppressed its transcription (Figure 1a).

Previous studies have identified the involvement of Rad18 in conferring resistance to MMS in C. albicans [26]. In this study, we further investigated its phenotype under other genotoxic stresses and found that its deletion led to pronounced sensitivity towards MMS, which could be rescued by introducing an ectopic RAD18 gene (Figure 1b). In addition, deletion of the RAD18 gene resulted in heightened sensitivity towards UV light, but no apparent sensitivity towards 40 mm HU or fluconazole (Figure 1b). Therefore, our findings suggest that while playing a vital role in the response to DNA damage stress, Rad18 does not appear to contribute significantly to DNA replication stress responses.

In S. cerevisiae, Rad18 is identified as a PRR factor contributing to DNA damage repair [31]. We examined the phenotype of the ScRAD18-deletion under genotoxic stress; deleting the ScRAD18 gene in S. cerevisiae generated strong sensitivity to MMS and UV light, but minimal sensitivity towards HU (Figure 1c). This result indicates that Rad18 functions similarly in response to DNA damage stress across different species, including C. albicans.

The TLS and TS pathways, which are the two predominant PRR pathways, represent tightly regulated signalling cascades that integrate DNA synthesis with the overall DNA damage response, thereby playing a crucial role in maintaining genome stability [13]. Given the critical role of Rad18 in PRR, we investigated genomic stability following the deletion of the RAD18 gene in C. albicans. While the wild-type strain exhibited a relatively low frequency of spontaneous URA3 marker loss (0.86 × 10−5), both two independent RAD18-deletion strains displayed an elevated frequency of URA3 marker loss (4.3 × 10−5 and 3.8 × 10−5, respectively), highlighting a crucial role for Rad18 in maintaining genomic stability (Figure 1d).

Overexpression of RAD18 leads to increased resistance against genotoxic stress in C. albicans

In order to further elucidate the function of Rad18, we overexpressed the RAD18 gene and assessed its effect on the cell’s phenotype under various stresses. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the ADH1 promoter effectively enhanced the transcription of the RAD18 gene, resulting in a 36-fold upregulation compared to the wild-type strain (Figure 2a). Overexpression of the RAD18 gene in a wild-type strain conferred a slight resistance to MMS and UV light, suggesting a role in DNA damage response (Figure 2b). Strikingly, although the RAD18-deletion strain exhibited no clear sensitivity to HU, overexpression of RAD18 significantly increased resistance to HU (Figure 2b), indicating its involvement in response to DNA replication stress.

(b) Phenotypic assay of the RAD18-overexpression strain. Wild-type SN148 strain carrying a linearized CIP10 plasmid and the RAD18-overexpression strain were used.

Functional assay of domains within Rad18

The Rad18 protein encompasses multiple functional domains and contributes to diverse cellular processes in various model organisms. A comparison of the amino acid sequence between CaRad18 and ScRad18 revealed a shared sequence identity of 23.3%. Through analysis of the CaRad18 amino acid sequence using the SMART online service (http://smart.embl.de/), we identified three distinct internal domains within the CaRad18 protein: a RING domain (29 aa to 66 aa), a ZNF domain (181 aa to 205 aa), and an SAP domain (287 aa to 321 aa) (Figure 3a) [32]. These domains in CaRad18 are conserved in SpRad18 from the model fungus Schizosaccharomyces pombe but are different from those present in ScRad18; notably, ScRad18 lacks a ZNF domain (Figure 3a).

Functional assay of the RING, ZNF, and SAP domains in Rad18 in C. albicans. (a) Comparison of protein secondary structure of CaRad18 to the potential orthologs from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ScRad18) and schizosaccharomyces pombe (SpRad18). (b) Phenotypic assay of the strain lacking either the RING, ZNF, or SAP domain of Rad18. Wild-type SN148 strain, the RAD18-deletion strain, and the strain lacking RING, ZNF, or SAP domains of Rad18 were utilized.

To elucidate the function of Rad18, we deleted the internal RING, ZNF, and SAP domains and assessed the resulting phenotypes. RING domain-containing proteins typically serve as scaffolds that facilitate direct ubiquitin transfer from E2 conjugating enzymes to substrates, influencing various cellular processes such as development, oncogenesis, apoptosis, and viral replication [33,34]. Disrupting Rad18’s internal RING domain yielded a result similar to complete RAD18-deletion; an increase in the sensitivity to DNA damage induced by MMS and UV light (Figure 3b). The SAP motif is involved in sequence- or structure-specific DNA binding and participates in coupling transcription with splicing and repair processes as well as apoptotic degradation of chromatin [35]. In our current study, deletion of the SAP domain in Rad18 resulted in a similar outcome to that of complete RAD18-deletion, causing decreased resistance to MMS and UV stresses (Figure 3b). ZNFs interact with DNA or RNA and are commonly involved in transcriptional regulation, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, signal transduction, DNA repair, and other cellular processes [36]. Deletion of the ZNF domain of Rad18 conferred increased sensitivity to MMS, while causing a lower sensitivity compared to complete deletion of RAD18 (Figure 3b). Moreover, the ZNF domain deletion also exhibited nearly equivalent resistance to UV stress as observed in wild-type strain, suggesting that ZNF may play a relatively less significant role in DNA damage response.

Deletion of RAD18 increases the virulence of C. albicans cells

In C. albicans, Rad6, a potential ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2) for Rad18, has been reported to negatively regulate hyphal formation independent of Efg1, suggesting a possible involvement in virulence [24]. To investigate the role of Rad18 in hyphal formation, we examined its essentiality using both FBS and Spider media. The RAD18-deletion and overexpression strains exhibited normal hyphal formation, similar to that observed in the wild-type (WT) strain, in either FBS or Spider media (Figure 4a). Moreover, deletion or overexpression of RAD18 both resulted in wrinkled colonies on solid FBS or Spider media similar to those of the wild-type strain (Figure 4a). Additionally, we assessed biofilm formation but observed no discernible differences upon the deletion or overexpression of RAD18 (Data not shown). Our findings indicate that, unlike Rad6, Rad18 does not directly contribute to hyphal formation.

To assess the impact of RAD18 deletion on the pathogenicity of C. albicans, we evaluated the survival within macrophages. Following a 2-hour incubation period, the wild-type (WT) group exhibited a colony-forming unit (CFU) count of 3.3 × 104/well; remarkably, the CFU count for the RAD18 deletion group increased to 7.7 × 104/well, while the RAD18 complemented strain mirrored the CFU levels observed in the wild-type strain (Figure 4b). Likewise, after a 5-hour incubation period, the RAD18 deletion group continued to exhibit a higher CFU count than the wild-type group.

Consistently, deletion of the RAD18 gene significantly increased virulence in the G. mellonella model. When infected larvae were kept at 37°C, the wild-type group showed an average survival of 6 days, whereas the RAD18-deletion group displayed an average survival of 3 days, demonstrating a significant difference from the WT group (Figure 4c). Overexpression of RAD18 generated similar pathogenicity as seen with the WT strain (Data not shown). Consistently, under incubation conditions at 30°C, deletion of the RAD18 gene substantially increased the virulence of C. albicans cells (Figure 4c), indicating an inhibitory role played by Rad18 in virulence.

The suppressive role of Rad18 in virulence was validated through a fungal burden assay conducted in mice. Despite the injection of an equal number of cells into the mice, the wild-type group exhibited an average fungal burden of 1.1 × 105 CFUs after 48 hours, whereas the RAD18 deletion group displayed a significantly higher fungal burden, reaching 3.1 × 106 CFUs (Figure 4d).

Transcriptional profiling of the RAD18-deletion strain

According to these findings, Rad18 exerts a positive role in DNA damage response while having a negative impact on virulence. To gain deeper insights into the role of Rad18 in DNA damage response and virulence, we performed RNAseq analysis to uncover the transcriptional changes resulting from RAD18 deletion under MMS stress. In general, 6,154 transcripts were detected (Figure 5a), with 185 genes exhibiting significant alterations using a log2 fold cut-off of 1.0 (p < 0.05, Table S3). Among these differed genes, 135 genes were upregulated, including several transporter-encoding genes (HGT17, HGT2, HGT12, and OPT4), and a hyphal cell wall protein-encoding gene (HWP1) (Figure 5b). In contrast to the upregulated genes, only 50 genes showed downregulation; noteworthy among the highly downregulated transcripts were those associated with the nuclear protein-encoding gene KAR5, the cell wall protein-encoding gene CWP419, and various noncoding RNAs (Figure 5b). Taken together, deletion of the RAD18 gene leads to primary upregulation of its potential targets.

To further classify the cellular roles of Rad18, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) analysis based on the differentially expressed genes following RAD18 deletion. In terms of molecular function, 3 GO terms were significantly enriched: transporter activity (GO:0005215), transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022857), and oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491) (Figure 5c). Among these, 15 genes were assigned to the transporter activity category (Figure 5d). Notably, HGT17, encoding a glucose transporter, JEN2, encoding a dicarboxylic acid transporter, as well as HGT2, encoding an MFS glucose transporter, all displayed significant upregulation. Importantly, all 15 identified transporter genes demonstrated substantial upregulation collectively, suggesting a suppressive role of Rad18 in cellular transportation. The transcription of transporter-encoding genes was examined by qRT-PCRs. The expression of JEN2 was significantly upregulated by 3.3-fold, while HGT17 showed a moderate upregulation of 1.8-fold compared to the untreated group, which is consistent with the RNAseq data (Figure 5d).

Rad18 negatively regulates antioxidation in C. albicans

According to GO analysis, 17 genes were assigned to the antioxidation process. Among these, 15 genes exhibited upregulation, including ALD6, encoding aldehyde dehydrogenase, and AMO1, encoding a peroxisomal copper amine oxidase; only SMM1, encoding a dihydrouridine synthase and GCV2, encoding a glycine decarboxylase P subunit, showed slight downregulation (Figure 6a). The transcription of antioxidation genes was examined by qRT-PCRs. AMO1 exhibited a robust upregulation of 4.4-fold compared to the untreated group. Moreover, under MMS stress, ALD6 and HPD1 also exhibited evident upregulation upon RAD18 deletion. Importantly, CAP1, encoding a well-established transcription factor involved in antioxidation processes, was similarly observed to be upregulated (Figure 6b).

The upregulation of antioxidation genes suggests a role of Rad18 in repressing antioxidation. To assess the impact of Rad18 on antioxidation, we examined the phenotype of the RAD18-deletion strain under oxidative stress induced by H2O2 and ZnCl2. As expected, deletion of RAD18 conferred increased resistance to both H2O2 and ZnCl2 (Figure 6c). Consistently, loss of the RING, SAP, and ZNF domains of Rad18 all resulted in increased resistance to H2O2, while only the RING domain deletion generated a similar resistance to ZnCl2 as the RAD18 complete deletion (Figure 6c). We also investigated the phenotype of the RAD18-overexpression strain, but did not find any effect on response to H2O2 (Data not shown).

The mutation of ScRAD18 in S. cerevisiae is reported to compromise survival under the stress of 3 mm H2O2, as demonstrated by a comprehensive quantitative sensitivity assay [37]. However, our spot assay results revealed that the ScRAD18 mutation did not confer an apparent sensitivity or resistance to H2O2 stress, indicating a functional distinction between ScRad18 and CaRad18 (Figure 6d). In general, Rad18 negatively regulates the antioxidation stress, probably through transcriptional repression.

RAD18 negatively regulates the transcription of virulence genes in C. albicans

Based on the transcriptional profile of the RAD18-deletion strain, we noticed differential transcription of several virulence genes upon deleting the RAD18 gene in C. albicans. Specifically, HWP1, ECE1, ALS4, PHO84, and IFF4 were upregulated, while PLB1 was downregulated. Notably, HWP1, encoding a hyphal cell wall protein which is a critical virulence factor, was upregulated (Figure 7a). Additionally, the transcription of ECE1, encoding candidalysin, was also enhanced, suggesting potential targets of Rad18 in virulence.

The transcription of virulence genes was examined by qRT-PCRs. Notably, HWP1 exhibited a robust upregulation of 2.3-fold compared to the untreated group (Figure 7a). Moreover, IFF4, encoding an adhesin-like cell surface protein, displayed a significant increase of 1.8-fold relative to the untreated group. ECE1 also demonstrated upregulation; however, it only showed a modest 1.4-fold change compared to the untreated group (Figure 7a). Overall, deletion of the RAD18 gene resulted in enhanced transcription of virulence genes.

To further classify the impact of virulence genes on pathogenicity, we employed a dCas9-mediated CRISPRi system to attenuate the transcription of HWP1 and ECE1 in the RAD18-deletion strain. According to qRT-PCR data, the dCas9-mediated CRISPRi system significantly downregulated the transcription of HWP1 and ECE1 to 44.5% and 56%, respectively, compared to the RAD18-deletion strain. In the G. mellonella model, we observed that larvae infected with the RAD18-deletion mutants showed an average survival of 1.5 days, whereas suppression of the ECE1 gene dramatically extended the survival to 6 days (Figure 7b). Similarly, suppression of the HWP1 gene in the RAD18-deletion strain exhibited a potent effect in reversing its virulence (Figure 7b). Therefore, ECE1 and HWP1 could serve as functional targets for Rad18 in modulating pathogenicity.

Rad18 is considered a potential transcription factor with a zinc finger DNA-binding motif; therefore, we investigated its interaction with promoters of HWP1, AMO1, ALD6, and IFF4 by employing a yeast one-hybrid assay. The yeast Y187 strain harbouring pGADT7-CaRAD18 and pHIS2 plasmids with promoters exhibited robust growth on SC−Trp−Leu−His medium, but all failed to grow on SC−Trp−Leu−His with 50 mm 3-AT (Figure 7C), suggesting that Rad18 indirectly contributes to the transcriptional repression of target genes.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the function of the RAD18 gene in DNA damage response and virulence in C. albicans, and observed that Rad18 May regulate the oxidative stress response and virulence via transcriptional suppression.

Postreplication repair is one of the DNA damage repair pathways employed by cells to address gaps in DNA that arise during replication [13,14]. Our findings indicate that both ScRad18 and CaRad18 likely function similarly in response to genotoxic stress; deletion of both genes resulted in heightened sensitivity to MMS and UV light. However, the ScRAD18 mutant exhibited slight sensitivity to 40 mm HU, while the CaRAD18 mutant showed no apparent sensitivity to HU. The phenotype resulting from the deletion of the CaRAD18 gene most closely resembles that observed upon loss of the SpRHP18 gene (the ortholog of RAD18 in S. pombe), both disruptions leading to heightened sensitivity towards MMS and UV radiation, but allowing normal growth on plates containing HU [38]. Furthermore, overexpression of CaRAD18 caused increased resistance to MMS and HU, consistent with the role of Rad18 in response to genotoxic stress. We tried to introduce the CaRAD18 into the mutant of ScRAD18 but did not find it could rescue the sensitive phenotype caused by the loss of ScRAD18. This result could be attributed to low sequence identity and differential internal domains between these two orthologs. However, in general, our current phenotypic results suggest a role of Rad18 in PRR in C. albicans, similar to its orthologs in S. cerevisiae and S. pombe.

Given the various internal domains within Rad18, we investigated its function in detail. Deleting the RING or SAP domains significantly impaired the function of Rad18 in response to DNA damage stress caused by MMS and UV. The N-terminal RING domain of human Rad18 plays a crucial role in interacting with Rad6, which is essential for DNA damage response [39]. The SAP motif participates in DNA interaction and facilitates coupling between transcription, splicing, and repair processes, as well as apoptotic degradation of chromatin [35]. Therefore, it is plausible that the conservation of the SAP and RING domains in CaRad18 reflects functional similarity with their counterparts in humans, enabling them to respond effectively to diverse DNA damage stresses. However, it should be noted that the loss of the ZNF domain caused heightened sensitivity towards MMS but not UV. MMS induces alkylating damage to DNA, resulting in single-strand breaks (SSBs), and DSBs, whereas UV light predominantly causes pyrimidine dimers and pyrimidine-pyrimidone (6–4) photoproducts [4]. Consistent with this notion, several studies have reported that ZNF domain proteins recognize DSBs or participate in DSB repair [40]. The relatively lower importance of the ZNF domain under UV stress suggests a primary role for the ZNF domain of Rad18 during MMS-mediated DNA damage response.

In addition to its role in DNA damage response, Rad18 also plays a critical role in antioxidation. Pooling the transcriptional data caused by deleting the RAD18 gene revealed its role in transcriptional repression; RAD18-deletion enhanced global transcription significantly, particularly with genes involved in transport and antioxidation. Furthermore, we observed upregulation of several antioxidant genes upon RAD18 deletion using qRT-PCR, implying a primarily repressive effect of Rad18 on transcription regulation. Deletion of the RAD18 gene and its internal domains did confer resistance to H2O2 and other oxidative agents. Given the conserved internal domains, deletion of SpRHP18 (the ortholog of CaRAD18 in S. pombe) resulted in enhanced resistance to MgCl2 and SDS, suggesting a suppressive role of Rhp18. The regulatory mechanism by which Rad18 modulates the transcription of antioxidation genes captures our attention. We notice that the SAP and ZNF domains of Rad18 are implicated in DNA interaction, indicating a direct role of Rad18 in transcriptional regulation. Moreover, the RING domain potentially contributes to transcriptional repression. For example, Mdm2 can be recruited to gene promoters and repress their transcription through its RING domain in humans, suggesting that its E3 activity contributes to repression [41]. Furthermore, TRIM22 with an internal RING domain exhibits anti-HBV activity by acting as a transcriptional suppressor [42]. In Oryza sativa, the RING finger protein 1 (OsDHSRP1) acts as a negative regulator in regulating various abiotic stress responses via an ABA-independent pathway [43]. Therefore, Rad18 exerts a negative role in antioxidation, potentially through a role in transcriptional repression.

The transition from yeast form to hyphae form is crucial for the invasion of C. albicans cells into host tissues and in the formation of biofilms; both behaviours are closely related to the virulence of C. albicans [44]. Here, the RAD18-deletion strain showed no apparent change in hyphal or biofilm formation, although Rad6, its partner in mediating ubiquitination, plays a negative role in hyphal formation in C. albicans [24]. Furthermore, DNA damage response is crucial for cells to cope with various forms of DNA damage stress, including the ROS generated by host immune cells. Genes that are implicated in DNA damage response, like RTT109, RAD52, or RAD23 in C. albicans, are also required for virulence [14]. Despite displaying heightened sensitivity to DNA damage, the RAD18 mutant surprisingly exhibited increased survival within macrophages and kidney tissues of infected mice, along with heightened virulence in the G. mellonella model, without any apparent alterations in hyphal growth. Furthermore, a survival analysis demonstrated a reduced lifespan in mice infected with the RAD18 deletion strain (4 days compared to 6 days in the WT group, data not shown). While no statistically significant difference (p = 0.0809) was identified, potentially due to the limited sample size (n = 10), these findings substantiate the increased virulence resulting from RAD18 deletion in C. albicans. To date, a large number of DDR genes have been reported to play key roles in virulence, but only a limited number of DDR gene mutants have exhibited increased virulence [45]. Deletion of PPH3 and PSY2, encoding a catalytic and regulatory subunit, respectively, conferred heightened sensitivity to MMS and HU treatment; however, their respective deletions paradoxically led to increased virulence when assessed using a murine model system [46]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the compensatory effect exerted by elevated hyphal formation resulting from PPH3 and PSY2 deletions, which masks the underlying defects associated with resistance against DNA damage stress and ROS generation. In contrast, RAD18-deletion did not exhibit any discernible change regarding hyphal formation; however, it is plausible that other factors influencing virulence might undergo alterations counteracting the defect associated with resisting ROS generated by host cells. Taken together, the impact of DNA damage repair genes on virulence in C. albicans may not be deterministic; instead, it is a consequence of the interplay between host immune responses and pathogen antagonism.

We find that Rad18 negatively regulates the transcription of virulence genes. As was seen with antioxidation genes, deletion of the RAD18 gene resulted in the upregulation of various virulence genes. Subsequent qRT-PCR assays confirmed the upregulation of HWP1, ECE1, and IFF4 caused by the loss of Rad18, consistent with Rad18 also playing a role in the regulation of virulence-related genes. Our virulence assay further confirmed that transcriptional suppression of virulence genes significantly reduced the pathogenicity of the RAD18-deletion strain. Thus, the observed enhanced virulence upon deletion of the RAD18 gene in C. albicans could be attributed to its transcriptional suppression role. However, the mechanism by which Rad18 represses the transcription of target genes remains unclear. Our yeast one-hybrid assay revealed that Rad18 does not directly bind to the promoters of target genes, suggesting an indirect regulation of their transcription. Previously, several E3 ubiquitin ligases, including BRCA1 and RNF31, have been reported to play regulatory roles in transcriptional repression [47,48]. Given that Rad18 is a ubiquitin ligase, protein degradation of transcription factors or regulators mediated by Rad18 May be a potential mechanism for transcriptional repression. Further investigation into the protein binding partners and DNA targets of Rad18 May enhance our understanding of Rad18-mediated DNA damage repair and virulence in C. albicans.

In general, our study identifies a novel negative role of Rad18 in antioxidation and virulence in C. albicans, providing valuable insights into the correlation between the DNA damage response and virulence. These findings further improve our comprehension of the pathogenicity of C. albicans and other fungal pathogens.
