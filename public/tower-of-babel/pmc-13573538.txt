Five New Records of Endophytic Fungi Isolated from Sargassum spp. in Korea
Authors: Yu-Na Choi, Ji-Won Kim, Seong-Eun Kim, Ahn-Heum Eom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFive endophytic fungal species were isolated from the brown macroalgae Sargassum muticum and Sargassum thunbergii collected from coastal regions of Korea. The isolates were identified based on morphological characteristics and phylogenetic analyses using combinations of internal transcribed spacer (ITS), large subunit ribosomal RNA (LSU), β-tubulin (TUB), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF), calmodulin (CaM), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) gene sequences. The identified species were Hypoxylon cinnabarinum, Neopestalotiopsis asiatica, Allophoma hayatii, Sclerostagonospora cycadis, and Aspergillus occultus. Phylogenetic analyses based on multilocus sequence data supported the taxonomic placement of all isolates, and their morphological characteristics were consistent with those of previously described species. This study represents the first report of H. cinnabarinum, N. asiatica, A. hayatii, S. cycadis, and A. occultus in Korea. These findings expand the known diversity of endophytic fungi associated with Sargassum species and provide baseline taxonomic information for future studies of seaweed-associated fungal communities in Korean coastal ecosystems.Keywords: Endophytic fungi, Sargassum, marine fungi, fungal systematics, phylogeny

Introduction

Species of the genus Sargassum (Sargassaceae, Fucales) are among the dominant brown macroalgae in temperate and subtropical coastal ecosystems. Their high biomass and productivity contribute substantially to coastal primary production, while their dense canopies provide habitat and refuge for diverse marine organisms, thereby supporting the structure and functioning of coastal ecosystems [1,2].

Among them, Sargassum thunbergii (Mertens ex Roth) Kuntze and Sargassum muticum (Yendo) Fensholt are representative brown macroalgal species distributed along the coasts of East Asia. S. thunbergii forms extensive populations on rocky intertidal shores and serves as an important ecological component of coastal ecosystems because of its high biomass and productivity [3–5]. In addition, this species has been reported to contribute to coastal bioremediation through the accumulation of heavy metals and metalloids, as well as the uptake of excess nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus in polluted and eutrophic coastal waters [6]. S. muticum is native to the northwestern Pacific, including the coastal waters of Japan, Korea, and China, but has become one of the most successful invasive seaweeds in many regions outside its native range [7]. Despite its invasive status, dense stands of S. muticum provide habitat and refuge for diverse marine invertebrates and contribute to the maintenance of coastal ecosystem structure and function [8,9].

Endophytic fungi are microorganisms that inhabit internal host tissues without causing apparent disease symptoms and are widely distributed across terrestrial and marine ecosystems. In marine environments, macroalgal-associated endophytic fungi are increasingly recognized as important components of the algal holobiont and may contribute to host survival and adaptation under fluctuating environmental condition [10]. These fungi produce diverse secondary metabolites that can protect their hosts from pathogens, herbivores, ultraviolet radiation, and other environmental stressors [11]. Furthermore, bioactive compounds produced by marine endophytic fungi exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties, indicating their potential as sources of novel natural products and bioactive compounds [12].

Despite their ecological and biotechnological importance, marine macroalgal-associated endophytic fungi remain considerably less studied than their terrestrial counterparts, and knowledge of their diversity, distribution, and taxonomy is still limited. Although numerous fungal taxa have been reported from marine macroalgae, the diversity of algal-associated endophytic fungal communities remains insufficiently characterized. Continued investigations of marine macroalgal endophytes are therefore necessary to improve our understanding of fungal diversity in marine ecosystems.

In the present study, endophytic fungi were isolated from S. thunbergii and S. muticum collected from coastal regions of Korea. Based on morphological characteristics and multilocus phylogenetic analyses, five fungal species previously unrecorded in Korea, namely Hypoxylon cinnabarinum, Neopestalotiopsis asiatica, Allophoma hayatii, Sclerostagonospora cycadis, and Aspergillus occultus, were identified and taxonomically characterized.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation. From August 2024 to April 2025, samples of Sargassum spp. were collected from intertidal zones along the coasts of Jeju-do and Jeollabuk-do, Korea. Sargassum muticum was collected from Jeju-si, Jeju-do, in August 2024, whereas Sargassum thunbergii was collected from Jeju-si, Jeju-do, in April 2025 and from Buan-gun, Jeollabuk-do, in March 2025. Only healthy thalli without visible lesions or disease symptoms were selected. Samples were placed in clean plastic bags filled with ambient seawater and transported to the laboratory within 12 h.

Surface sterilization and fungal isolation. Thalli of Sargassum spp. were thoroughly rinsed with running tap water to remove surface-associated debris and epiphytic contaminants and subsequently excised into segments approximately 1.5 × 0.5 cm in size. Surface sterilization was performed by immersing the tissue segments in 70% ethanol for 15 s, followed by rinsing with sterile artificial seawater [13]. The sterilized segments were aseptically dried on sterile Kimtech wipes and inoculated onto DRBC (MBcell, Seoul, South Korea) [14] and chloramphenicol-amended PDA (Difco Lab., Detroit, MI) prepared with or without seawater [13]. All inoculated plates were incubated at 25 °C under dark conditions, and fungal hyphae emerging from the internal tissues were subsequently transferred onto fresh PDA plates to obtain pure cultures.

Culturing and morphological characterization. Pure isolates were cultured on malt extract agar (MEA; Kisan Bio, Seoul, South Korea) and PDA at 25 °C in darkness. Colony morphology, including pigmentation, texture, and margin characteristics, was recorded after 7 d. Morphological characteristics were examined using a stereomicroscope (SZX7, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a light microscope (Axio Imager A2, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh mycelia using the HiGene Genomic DNA Prep Kit (Biofact, Daejeon, South Korea). The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, large subunit ribosomal RNA (LSU) region, partial β-tubulin (TUB), partial translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF), calmodulin (CaM), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) genes were amplified using the primer pairs ITS1F/ITS4 [15], LR0R/LR5 [16,17], T1/Bt-2b or Bt-2a/Bt-2b [18,19], EF1-728F/EF2 [20,21], CMD5/CMD6 [22], and fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR [23], respectively. PCR products were sequenced by Solgent Co., Ltd. (Daejeon, South Korea). The resulting sequences were compared with those available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST).

Phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using combined datasets of ITS, LSU, TUB, TEF, CaM, and RPB2 sequences, depending on marker availability for each isolate. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum-likelihood method in MEGA11 software [24]. The TN93 + G model was used for the phylogenetic analysis of strain KNUE25S135, whereas the K2 + G model was used for the remaining strains (KNUE25S217, KNUE25S222, KNUE25S032, and KNUE24S520). Node support was assessed by bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates to evaluate the robustness of the tree topology. The isolated strains were deposited in the National Institute of Biological Resources (NIBR), and the corresponding sequences were registered in the NCBI database under the assigned accession numbers. The GenBank accession numbers of all strains included in the phylogenetic analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Results and discussion

Phylogenetic analysis

The identities of the five isolates were determined by maximum-likelihood analyses of combined multilocus datasets, using the marker combinations available for each taxon (Figures 1–5); the closest BLAST matches for every isolate and locus, together with query coverage and sequence identity, are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Strain KNUE24S520 formed a well-supported monophyletic clade with the ex-type strain of H. cinnabarinum in the combined ITS and TUB tree (Figure 1). Strain KNUE25S032 clustered with the ex-type strain of N. asiatica in the combined ITS, TUB, and TEF tree (Figure 2); although its ITS sequence was identical to those of several closely related species, β-tubulin and tef1 clearly distinguished it from these taxa. Strain KNUE25S135 grouped with the ex-type strains of A. hayatii in the combined ITS, LSU, TUB, and RPB2 tree, distinct from the closely related Al. labilis (Figure 3). Strain KNUE25S217 was placed with the ex-type strain of S. cycadis in the combined ITS and LSU tree (Figure 4), and strain KNUE25S222 with the ex-type strain of A. occultus in the combined ITS, TUB, and CaM tree (Figure 5).

Scale bars: C = 500 μm, D = 10 μm, E = 20 μm.

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUE24S520: Morphological characteristics were observed after incubation on PDA and MEA at 25 °C for seven days. On PDA, the colony surface exhibited a black pigmentation at the margin, while the central region was densely covered with greenish-white, cottony mycelia. The colony margin was irregular, with well-developed filamentous hyphae. The surface showed partially aggregated hyphae forming a three-dimensional, tufted colony structure. The reverse side was entirely black due to pigmentation. Colony diameter ranged from 19.0 to 22.0 mm. On MEA, the colony surface was dark brown at the margin, with a central region densely covered by white to yellowish cottony mycelia. The colony margin was irregular with well-developed filamentous hyphae, and the surface formed a partially aggregated, three-dimensional colony structure. The reverse side was entirely dark brown. Colony diameter ranged from 17.0 to 22.0 mm. Conidiogenous structure with virgariella-like branching patterns. Conidia were produced on aerial hyphae, were subglobose to broadly ellipsoidal in shape, and measured (4.8–)5.6(–6.1) × (3.2–)3.7(–4.0) μm (n = 20).

Specimen examined: Jeju-si, Jeju-do, Korea (33°27′36.87″N, 126°56′05.15″E), August 1 2024, H. cinnabarinum, isolated from Sargassum muticum, strain KNUE24S520, NIBRFGC000514880, GenBank No. PZ477857 (ITS) and PZ488185 (TUB).

Notes: No sexual structures were observed in strain KNUE24S520 under the culture conditions examined. The isolate produced subglobose to broadly ellipsoidal conidia on aerial hyphae and formed a monophyletic clade with H. cinnabarinum based on combined ITS and TUB sequences. Therefore, strain KNUE24S520 was identified as H. cinnabarinum based on the combined evidence from phylogenetic analyses and morphological observations.

Neopestalotiopsis asiatica (Maharachch. & K.D. Hyde) Maharachch., K.D. Hyde & Crous, Stud. Mycol. 79: 136 (2014) [MB#809761] (Figure 7).

Morphology of Neopestalotiopsis asiatica KNUE25S032. (A, B) Colonies after seven days of growth at 25 °C, showing the obverse (left) and reverse (right) sides on malt extract agar (A) and potato dextrose agar (B); (C) Conidiophores; (D) Conidia. Scale bars: C, D = 20 μm.

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUE25S032: After seven days of incubation at 25 °C, colonies on PDA reached a diameter of 55.0–63.0 mm. The colony surface was white, nearly circular in shape, with a crenate margin and dense cottony texture. The reverse side showed a deep yellow center becoming light yellow toward the margin. On MEA, colonies reached a diameter of 59.0–66.0 mm. The colony surface was yellowish white and irregular in shape, with a sparse cottony texture and uneven surface. The reverse side showed a brilliant yellow center becoming yellowish white toward the margin. Conidiophores were indistinct. Conidia were fusiform, 4-septate, and measured (17.1–)23.5(–30.5) × (4.8–)6.7(–8.6) μm (n = 20). The three median cells were dark brown, and the apical cell possessed 2–4 appendages, most commonly three.

Specimen examined: Buan-gun, Jeollabuk-do, South Korea (35°38′12.91″N, 126°27′33.96″E), March 21 2025, N. asiatica, isolated from S. thunbergii, strain KNUE25S032, NIBRFGC000514882, GenBank No. PZ438125 (ITS), PZ443235 (TUB) and PZ443236 (TEF).

Notes: Neopestalotiopsis asiatica was originally described from an endophytic isolate obtained from living leaves of an unidentified host tree collected in Hunan Province, China [25]. The species was later transferred to Neopestalotiopsis following the establishment of the genus based on morphological and multigene phylogenetic evidence [26].

Allophoma hayatii Babaahmadi & M. Mehrabi, Mycol. Progr. 17 (3): 370 (2017) [MB#820519] (Figure 8).

Morphology of Allophoma hayatii KNUE25S135. (A, B) Colonies after seven days of growth at 25 °C, showing the obverse (left) and reverse (right) sides on malt extract agar (A) and potato dextrose agar (B); (C) Conidiomata on medium; (D) Conidia. Scale bars: C = 300 μm, D = 20 μm.

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUE25S135: Morphological characteristics were observed after incubation on PDA and MEA at 25 °C for seven days. On PDA, the colony surface was overall dark yellowish-brown, with a pale yellowish-white margin. The colony was circular in shape, with an even surface and a cottony texture. The reverse side showed a lighter yellowish-brown pigmentation in the central region, gradually transitioning to brown and pale yellow toward the margin. Colony diameter ranged from 52.0 to 54.0 mm. On MEA, the colony surface was dark grayish-brown in the central region, becoming yellowish-brown toward the margin, with a white outer edge. Colony morphology was consistent with that observed on PDA, showing a circular form with an even surface and cottony texture. The reverse side exhibited the darkest brown pigmentation in the center, transitioning to yellow in the middle region and pale yellowish-white toward the margin. Colony diameter ranged from 63.8 to 64.4 mm. Conidia were hyaline, smooth-walled, aseptate, and ellipsoid to oblong in shape, with obtuse to rounded ends. Conidia measured (2.0–)2.4(–2.9) × (5.8–)6.9(–8.8) μm (n = 20).

Specimen examined: Jeju-si, Jeju-do, Korea (33°31′13.10″N, 126°29′36.58″E), April 3 2025, Al. hayatii, isolated from S. thunbergii, strain KNUE25S135, NIBRFGC000515217, GenBank No. PZ477856 (ITS), PZ477872 (LSU), PZ488184 (TUB), and PZ488186 (RPB2).

Notes: Allophoma hayatii was originally described from dead branches of Lantana camara in Iran and is characterized by pycnidial conidiomata and hyaline to pale brown, guttulate conidia that are described as 0–1-septate, with predominantly 1-septate [27]. KNUE25S135 formed a supported clade with the ex-type strain of A. hayatii CBS 142859 in the multigene phylogenetic analysis. Morphologically, the isolate produced hyaline, smooth-walled, ellipsoid to oblong conidia that were comparable in shape and size to those reported for A. hayatii. Although septa were not observed in the examined conidia, the original description of A. hayatii also included aseptate conidia, indicating that conidial septation is variable within the species. The isolate also showed morphological similarity to the closely related species Allophoma labilis (Supplementary Table S2); however, the combined multilocus phylogenetic analysis supported its placement within the A. hayatii clade.

(A, B) Colonies after seven days of growth at 25 °C, showing the obverse (left) and reverse (right) sides on malt extract agar (A) and potato dextrose agar (B); (C) Conidia; (D) Conidiogenous cells; (E) Conidiomata. Scale bars: C = 10 μm, D = 20 μm, and E = 400 μm.

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUE25S217: After seven days of incubation at 25 °C, colonies on PDA reached a diameter of 32.0–34.2 mm. The colony surface was white with a yellowish center, circular in shape, and had an even surface with a cottony texture. The reverse side was honey-colored with scattered dark brown spots. On MEA, colonies reached a diameter of 31.2–32.8 mm. The colony surface was white at the margin and yellow at the center, circular in shape, with an even surface and cottony texture, similar to that observed on PDA. The reverse side was overall yellow. Conidiomata were pycnidial, globose, and brown. Conidia were ellipsoidal to subcylindrical, 0–3-septate, hyaline to pale brown, and measured (7.2–)8.5(–10.5) × (2.8–)3.6(–4.3) μm (n = 20).

Specimen examined: Jeju-si, Jeju-do, South Korea (33°18′32.28″N, 126°09′51.36″E), April 3 2025, Sc. cycadis, isolated from S. thunbergii, strain KNUE25S217, NIBRFGC000515214, GenBank No. PZ439031 (ITS) and PZ476902 (LSU).

Notes: Sclerostagonospora cycadis was originally isolated from living leaves of Cycas revoluta collected in Tokyo Bay, Japan, and the specific epithet cycadis was derived from the host genus Cycas [28]. The genus Sclerostagonospora is characterized by pycnidial conidiomata and septate conidia that become pale brown at maturity [29]. The original description reported conidia measuring (6–)7–10(–13) × 3–4(–4.5) μm, which is generally consistent with the morphological characteristics and conidial dimensions observed in the present study [28]. In addition, the isolate formed a monophyletic clade with Sc. cycadis CBS 123538 based on combined ITS and LSU sequence analyses. These morphological and molecular characteristics support the identification of strain KNUE25S217 as S. cycadis.

Aspergillus occultus Visagie, Seifert, Frisvad & Samson, Stud. Mycol. 78: 32 (2014) [MB#809198] (Figure 10).

Morphology of Aspergillus occultus KNUE25S222. (A, B) Colonies after seven days of growth at 25 °C, showing the obverse (left) and reverse (right) sides on malt extract agar (A) and potato dextrose agar (B); (C) Conidiophores; (D) Metulae and Phialides; (E) Conidia. Scale bars: C, D = 20 μm, and E = 10 μm.

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUE25S222: After seven days of incubation at 25 °C, colonies on PDA reached a diameter of 33.0–35.2 mm. The colony surface was white, with brown areas formed by abundant sporulation. Colonies were generally circular with slightly wrinkled margins, and had an even surface with a cottony texture. The reverse side was pale yellow. On MEA, colonies reached a diameter of 30.8–34.0 mm. The colony surface was white and showed morphology similar to that observed on PDA. Vesicles were globose, and metulae covered 100% of the head. Phialides were ampulliform. Conidia were globose to subglobose and measured (2.8–)3.2(–3.6) × (2.2–)2.6(–3.0) μm (n = 20).

Specimen examined: Jeju-si, Jeju-do, South Korea (33°18′32.28″N, 126°09′51.36″E), April 3 2025, As. occultus, isolated from S. thunbergii, strain KNUE25S222, NIBRFGC000515215, GenBank No. PZ439022 (ITS), PZ443238 (TUB) and PZ443237 (CaM).

Notes: Aspergillus occultus was originally described by Visagie et al. as a member of Aspergillus section Circumdati [30]. The species is characterized by biseriate conidial heads, globose vesicles completely covered by metulae, ampulliform phialides, and globose to subglobose conidia measuring 2.5–3 μm in diameter [30]. Strain KNUE25S222 exhibited morphological characteristics consistent with the original description, including globose vesicles, metulae covering the entire vesicle surface, ampulliform phialides, and globose to subglobose conidia measuring (2.8–)3.2(–3.6) × (2.2–)2.6(–3.0) μm. Phylogenetic analysis based on combined ITS, TUB, and CaM sequences placed KNUE25S222 in a monophyletic clade with As. occultus CBS 137330. These morphological and molecular characteristics support the identification of strain KNUE25S222 as As. occultus.

In this study, five endophytic fungi, H. cinnabarinum, N. asiatica, A. hayatii, S. cycadis, and A. occultus, were isolated from Sargassum spp. collected along the Korean coast and identified based on morphological characteristics and multilocus phylogenetic analyses. To our knowledge, this is the first report of these species from Sargassum spp. in Korea.

Most of these species were originally described from terrestrial plant hosts or other non-marine substrates. All five isolates were nevertheless recovered from the internal thallus tissues of Sargassum after surface sterilization, and are therefore regarded as endophytes of these macroalgae. Because Sargassum forms dense stands in intertidal zones that are periodically exposed to air, its thalli are subject to frequent contact with airborne and terrestrially derived fungal propagules, which may facilitate colonization of the internal tissues by taxa of predominantly terrestrial origin [31,32]. The recovery of such taxa as endophytes of Sargassum is consistent with previous reports that many marine macroalgal endophytes are phylogenetically affiliated with terrestrial lineages and are able to tolerate the physicochemical conditions of the marine environment [31].

Most species of Allophoma and Sclerostagonospora have been reported from terrestrial plants, whereas members of Aspergillus and Hypoxylon have been isolated as endophytic fungi from marine macroalgae in different geographic regions [11]. For example, Aspergillus species have frequently been reported from brown, red, and green macroalgae. Marine-derived Aspergillus species are recognized as prolific producers of diverse secondary metabolites, including polyketides, alkaloids, terpenoids, and diketopiperazines, exhibiting antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic activities [33–36]. Likewise, species of Hypoxylon are known to produce a variety of bioactive secondary metabolites with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties [37]. In contrast, to our knowledge, Allophoma and Sclerostagonospora have not previously been reported from marine macroalgae, suggesting that these genera may represent uncommon members of marine algal endophytic communities.

Marine endophytic fungi associated with macroalgae are considered potential sources of biologically active secondary metabolites [11–13]. Although this study focused on taxonomic characterization, the isolates obtained here provide useful material for future studies on fungal diversity and bioactive compounds. Because the present study was based on culture-dependent isolation from a limited number of sampling sites and collection periods, additional studies using culture-independent approaches together with broader geographic and seasonal sampling will improve our understanding of the diversity and ecological roles of Sargassum-associated endophytic fungi.
