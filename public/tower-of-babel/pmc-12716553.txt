Diminishing activities of daily living as severity of chronic breathlessness worsens: a national, cross-sectional survey
Authors: Slavica Kochovska, Sungwon Chang, Diana Ferreira, Vanessa Brunelli, Jessica Macdonald, Hiu San, Max Olsson, Jacob Sandberg, Tim Luckett, Miriam J Johnson, Magnus Ekström, David Currow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEffective management of chronic breathlessness requires understanding people’s activity limitations. This study evaluated the extent to which chronic breathlessness limits people’s self-reported everyday activities.MethodsA web-based, cross-sectional survey (adults ≥18 years). Recruitment was through a marketing research company, stratified to the 2016 Australian Census for key demographics (age, sex, state/territory of residence, rurality). Self-reported measures included demographics, breathlessness limiting exertion (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) breathlessness scale) and breathlessness impact (yes/no question; three most important activities affected). Impact was categorised as performing with difficulty/reduced/ceased.Results7300 respondents were included (mean age 46.5 (SD 18.6); men 50.8%; mMRC ≥1 290.0%). 30.6% (648/2119) with mMRC ≥1 versus 2.6% (136/5181; p<0.001) with mMRC 0 reported activities affected; the proportions increased for each mMRC level.2342 activities were nominated with the most frequent being: high intensity sport, household chores and mobility. The order changed for mMRC 2 (household chores>high intensity sports>mobility) and mMRC 3–4 (mobility>household chores>high intensity sports). Breathlessness increased the likelihood of activities being reduced or ceased.In a logistic regression model exploring the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule, controlling for baseline factors, most affected domains were getting along (OR 2.5 (95% CI 1.5 to 4.2)), life activities (OR 1.8 (95% CI 1.2 to 2.7)) and participation (OR 6.4 (95% CI 4.2 to 9.9)).ConclusionChronic breathlessness of every intensity above mMRC 0 affects people’s ability to perform a range of everyday activities. The progressive loss of these activities is a key coping mechanism to avoid precipitating breathlessness and is mostly invisible to other people.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Our findings indicate that chronic breathlessness affects people’s ability to perform a range of everyday activities in a nuanced manner, from continuing to perform them with difficulty to reducing and ceasing tasks. The likelihood of activities being affected in the first place and the frequency with which individuals undertake those activities increases with the symptom’s severity.

Introduction

Chronic breathlessness is a disabling symptom, prevalent in people with chronic conditions. The symptom adversely affects a person’s life with impaired function, increased anxiety and depression, reduced ability to do everyday activities, poorer sexual well-being and poorer quality of life overall.14 Withdrawal from social activities is common, potentially leading to increasing social isolation, while reduced functioning in daily life leads to greater dependence on others.5 Such changes can lead to ripple effects in the immediate and broader environment for caregivers, family and social networks. Exacerbations of chronic breathlessness are often the cause of emergency department presentations and hospital admissions, with significant cost to the health system.68 Consequently, the personal, societal and embedded economic costs of chronic breathlessness are substantial.9

People often limit their exertion to cope with their breathlessness, triggering a downward spiral of progressive deconditioning which, in turn, exacerbates the symptom.10 Over time, doing less becomes the person’s new normal and a new baseline against which they consider, measure and report the presence (and impacts) of their symptom. This modified lifestyle evolves so insidiously that patients do not notice these changes. Likewise, clinicians often ask if a person is breathless without checking what they are not doing in order to avoid the symptom. This can result in suboptimal clinical response and missed opportunities to identify unmet needs.1113 Limitations in activities can also have implications for caregivers’ burden.5 14

Effective intervention and, where possible, mitigating people’s progressive deconditioning proactively, necessitate a more detailed understanding of the activity limitations experienced by people living with chronic breathlessness.1517 Such insights can increase clinicians’ awareness about what life looks like for their patients and help them use available, evidence-based symptom management strategies to make a meaningful difference.18 19 Leveraging online survey methods enables delineation of such impacts at the population level independent of health service contact, with sample stratification facilitating socio-demographic representation. The methodology has provided insightful understanding of epidemiological and public health issues in the context of breathlessness and more broadly.2022

This study evaluated the extent to which chronic breathlessness limits individuals’ everyday activities, where ‘extent’ is defined as the self-reported level of frequency and difficulty in undertaking everyday activities that have been affected by a person’s breathlessness.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional survey of Australian adults using a web-based questionnaire (Qualtrics, Provo, Utah, USA). All adults (aged ≥18 years) were eligible to participate; no exclusion criteria applied.

Recruitment was through a marketing research company (Qualtrics) using demographic quotas to ensure the study sample was representative of the 2016 Census population in terms of age, sex, state/territory of residence and rurality. Data were collected from 21 July to 2 August 2021. The first 10 000 eligible responses formed the cohort for analyses.

Recruitment was through the market research company’s blended panels (>800 000 members) to minimise bias in sampling. Panel members receive rewards for their time and participation in surveys (eg, vouchers, airline miles), with an estimated value equivalent to $A1.50.

Potential participants were invited by email containing an embedded survey link and were presented with a participant information sheet with study details before survey entry. To minimise selection bias, the survey was introduced as being about health and breathlessness was not mentioned in the participant information sheet (with permission of the Human Research Ethics Committee).

Participants provided consent to participate in research when they joined the market research company’s panels. Additionally, consent was obtained from all participants before starting the survey (by ticking a ‘consent’ box); continued engagement also implied consent.

The survey questions were devised entirely by the research team. The survey was piloted with members of the Consumer Advisory Committee at the IMPACCT (Improving Palliative, Aged and Chronic Care through Clinical Research and Translation) research group at the University of Technology Sydney, and then with 110 panel members of the market research company. The initial review resulted in minor wording and survey design changes which improved its readability and interpretation; no further changes were made following the second review. The final questionnaire is in the public domain at https://osf.io/zwgfc/.

The reporting follows the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies guidelines.23

Assessments

All assessments relied on self-report. Demographics collected included: age, sex, postcode (to derive state/territory of residence, rurality and socio-economic status), smoking status (people who smoke, people who have quit smoking, people who have never smoked, prefer not to say) and height and weight (to derive body mass index (BMI)). Rurality was calculated using the Accessibility and Remoteness Index of Australia24; socio-economic status was calculated using the Socio-Economic Index for Areas (SEIFA).25 BMI categories followed the WHO classification: underweight (BMI <18.5), normal weight (BMI 18.5–25.0), overweight (BMI >25–30) and obese/morbidly obese (BMI >30).26

The impact of breathlessness on everyday activities was assessed using the question ‘Have your everyday activities been affected by your breathlessness (e.g. work, sports or hobbies, shopping, caregiving responsibilities, household chores, sexual activity, gardening, driving)?’2 Responses included: ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘prefer not to say’ and ‘not applicable’. Those responding ‘yes’ were asked to nominate (by free text) the three most important everyday activities that had been affected because of their breathlessness (collectively referred to as ‘activities affected’). For each activity, respondents selected that they (1) continued to perform it but with difficulty, (2) reduced it or (3) ceased it. Only one option per activity was available.

The presence and severity of breathlessness was assessed using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) breathlessness scale.27 The mMRC 5-point ordinal scale (from 0 ‘I only get breathlessness with strenuous exercise’ to 4 ‘I am too breathless to leave the house or breathlessness when dressing or undressing’) indicates breathlessness that limits exertion, with higher scores indicating greater impairment due to breathlessness. Respondents were asked about the breathlessness’ duration (months; years) and the self-perceived primary cause to which they attributed their symptom (multiple-choice question, with free-text option).

Included in the current analyses were complete data on the impact of breathlessness on everyday activities and mMRC levels (for mMRC ≥1 this included duration of breathlessness (>8 weeks; ‘chronic breathlessness’28)) (online supplemental figure 1).

Statistical analysis

Socio-demographic characteristics were described. Activities affected and the extent of impact (ie, self-reported level of frequency in undertaking activities) were categorised and compared by mMRC levels. Nominated activities affected were classified using a coding frame from previous analyses, augmented to reflect the activities nominated in this survey.2 For the regression analyses, the coded activities were further classified using the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS) V.2.029 (online supplemental table 1). The extent of impact was described using the metric ‘continue to perform but with difficulty’, ‘reduced’ or ‘ceased’, applied to activities affected. Due to small numbers, mMRC level 3 and 4 were combined to enable meaningful analysis. No missing data were imputed.

A multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the extent to which chronic breathlessness affects individuals’ everyday activities. The dependent variable was binary, categorised as ‘0=No everyday activities affected’ and ‘1=Everyday activities affected’. Independent variables included demographic factors (age, sex, rurality), SEIFA, mMRC and self-reported smoking status. These variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance and prior evidence of associations with activity limitations in individuals experiencing breathlessness.

Only those covariates that were significantly associated with the outcome (p<0.05) were retained. This approach aimed to ensure that the final model remained parsimonious while accounting for potential confounders. The strength of associations was quantified using adjusted OR (aOR) with 95% CIs. Model diagnostics, including checks for multicollinearity and goodness-of-fit, were performed to ensure the robustness of the findings.

A logistic generalised estimating equations (GEE) model was used to evaluate the association between chronic breathlessness and the likelihood of ceasing everyday activities, controlling for statistically significant covariates in the extended model: age, sex, smoking status, mMRC and five of the six domains of activities affected using WHODAS V.2.0. The parsimonious model excluded age, sex and smoking status. The GEE approach accounted for the repeated nature of activity nominations within individuals, with an exchangeable working correlation structure assumed. aORs with 95% CIs were reported to quantify the associations. All analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (V.28.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA; 2016), with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

Results

Respondents’ characteristics

Of the 10 033 survey respondents, 7300 (72%) provided complete data on mMRC and activities affected, forming the cohort for analysis (online supplemental figure 1). Their mean age was 46.5 (SD 18.6), 50.8% were men, 49.0% reported being overweight/obese and 50.2% reported a history of smoking (table 1). Chronic breathlessness of any intensity (mMRC ≥1) was reported by 29.0% of respondents, the primary underlying condition being respiratory (43.9%).

Compared with those not reporting activities affected by breathlessness (89.3%, 6516/7300), those who identified such impact (10.7%, 748/7300) had higher rates of being overweight/obese (59.9% vs 48.5%; p<0.001), history of smoking (68.7% vs 48.4%; p<0.001) and chronic breathlessness of any intensity (82.7% vs 22.6%; p<0.001; table 1). Lung diseases remained the most prevalent underlying cause for both groups compared with all other diseases (p<0.001).

Overall, 30.6% (648/2119) of people with mMRC ≥1 versus 2.6% (136/5181) of those with mMRC 0 indicated that their everyday activities had been affected by breathlessness (p<0.001; table 1). Within the former, the proportion of people reporting such impact increased for each mMRC level (mMRC 1 23.0%, 346/1505; mMRC 2 45.1%, 197/437; mMRC 3–4 59.3%, 105/177).

Activities affected

A total of 2342 activities were nominated as being affected by breathlessness (figure 1). The top three activities affected overall were high intensity sports (85.2%), household chores (44.2%) and mobility (25.7%). They remained the top three nominated activities for each level of mMRC. The order of the top three activities for mMRC 0 and 1 was the same as in the overall ranking. For mMRC 2, the most frequently nominated activity was household chores (21.6%), followed by high intensity sports (17.0%) and mobility (13.9%). For mMRC 3–4, the most frequently nominated activity was mobility (17.1%), followed by household chores (16.2%) and high intensity sports (12.1%).

Cessation of activities increased with breathlessness severity (mMRC 1 16.8%; mMRC 2 24.7%; and mMRC 3–4 38.1%) (figure 2; online supplemental figure 2). The proportion of people continuing to perform activities with difficulty was lower for severe breathlessness compared with moderate and mild breathlessness (mMRC 3–4 19.4% vs mMRC 2 30.1% and mMRC 1 29.6%, respectively).

Regression analysis

Compared with mMRC 0, the odds of reporting activities affected increased with breathlessness intensity (mMRC 1 OR 11.06 (95% CI 8.95 to 13.68); mMRC 2 OR 30.79 (95% CI 23.75 to 39.92) and mMRC 3–4 OR 51.45 (95% CI 36.21 to 73.09)) (online supplemental table 2). Older people were more likely to report such impact compared with 18–24 years old (55–64 years old OR 1.57 (95% CI 1.14 to 2.15); 65–74 years old OR 1.32 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.82) and >75 years old OR 1.59 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.32)), as were people who have quit smoking compared with people who smoke (OR 0.93; 95% CI 0.75 to 1.17). The direction and magnitude of outcomes remained the same for all factors in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses (online supplemental table 3).

Breathlessness was also strongly associated with the likelihood of ceasing everyday activities using the WHODAS domains (table 3). Individuals with higher mMRC scores were significantly more likely to cease activities, even after adjusting for activity type. Compared with those with no breathlessness (mMRC 0), individuals with moderate breathlessness (mMRC 2) had 1.8 times higher odds of ceasing activities (aOR=1.9, 95% CI 1.3 to 2.8), while those with severe breathlessness (mMRC 3–4) had more than four times higher odds (aOR=4.0, 95% CI 2.6 to 6.2). Activity cessation varied by WHODAS domain: getting along (OR 2.5 (95% CI 1.5 to 4.2)); life activities (OR 1.8 (95% CI 1.2 to 2.7)); and participation (OR 6.4 (95% CI 4.2 to 9.9)) were most affected.

Discussion

This study found that chronic breathlessness of any intensity is linked to a more detailed understanding of a person’s ability to undertake everyday activities, reflected in the frequency with which they perform various tasks. In addition to ceasing, findings indicate that people also reduce or continue to perform tasks with difficulty, suggesting the impact goes beyond a dichotomised choice of person’s ability or inability to do something. The likelihood of activities being affected in the first place and the degree of impact is linked to the symptom’s severity. While all levels of mMRC impose a restricted lifestyle evident in the range of activities affected, severe breathlessness (mMRC 3–4) leads to an almost complete cessation of those activities, while mild-to-moderate breathlessness (mMRC 1–2) leads to a more nuanced diminished ability.

Our findings reaffirm that living with chronic breathlessness is detrimental to the most basic functions in everyday life exemplified by ‘household chores’ and ‘mobility’ being some of the most common activities affected. Combined with a compromised performance of activities that require higher exertion (eg, sports, gardening) or unimpaired mobility (eg, shopping), or promote social connectedness (eg, socialising), independence (eg, driving) or other basic needs (eg, work, sexual activities, personal care),30 the picture that emerges is of a diminishing world where people labour, despite their breathlessness, to maintain normality and personal agency. This reality aligns with people’s experiences of a disintegrating or unpredictable body when living with an advanced illness, including disabling breathlessness, which restricts people’s abilities to participate in everyday activities, social or otherwise, and prompts adaptation to progressive functional decline.31

Although this was a cross-sectional study, the findings reflect the chronic, long-term impacts as people move through increasing levels of breathlessness, suggesting a cumulative effect of impact over time. Given the insidiousness of the symptom, the lifestyle changes may be unnoticed by the person or secondary to their primary objective of minimising exertion so as to avoid breathlessness where possible, thus making the symptom largely invisible to patients and others, including clinicians.111332 A greater emphasis on these accumulative functional restrictions may support better symptom recognition and response in clinical practice.

The insights on gradation in activity limitations add to the evidence base that breathlessness leads to disability,33 impairs quality of life,3 sexual function4 and daily activities including for older people,2 34 and confines individuals to a smaller geographical space.35 Our study showed that older people and those with a history of smoking are more likely to experience impact on everyday activities, which aligns with current evidence that older age and smoking are associated with lower physical activity and poorer health outcomes.3639 Although counterintuitive, some people with no breathlessness (mMRC 0; 4%) reported impact, suggesting an awareness of interference before breathlessness becomes an issue and/or indicating ‘a change from the normal’. The fact that one in three people with breathlessness (30.6%) in our study experience impact in their everyday life has very substantial implications for the economy and society in general.

Strengths and limitations

This was a large, community-based survey, representative of the Australian adult population. The web-based delivery and recruitment outside of health services may have enabled participation of individuals with compromised mobility, including due to breathlessness, while potentially attracting participants with higher internet literacy. While the study adjusted for key demographic factors, by its nature, people with poor digital literacy are likely under-represented. Although health literacy has a strong socio-economic gradient, the questions in this survey did not require high levels of health literacy and likely included people with poor health literacy. Future studies will consider formally measuring health literacy. Although self-selection to participate might have been influenced by the reward panel members receive for their time, the quota system implemented for recruitment ensured the sample was demographically representative, minimising any self-selection bias; remuneration amounts were also low and consistent with market research industry standards.

Although free text, the question eliciting impact on everyday activities included examples of such activities. These might have biased some of the responses, while also helping respondents to think about what might constitute a meaningful activity for them at that point in time. Cessation of activities might have been under-reported as acknowledging lifestyle changes can sometimes be difficult, especially if that activity is part of one’s identity (eg, a sports person giving up sport). As noted, some people with mMRC 0 reported impact of breathlessness on their everyday activities. Given the design of the study (and its reliance on self-report), it is not possible to know whether this was driven by any underlying conditions. The Australian socio-economic classification used in the analysis should be adapted to the setting in future studies.

Breathlessness intensity was defined by mMRC levels. Though widely used in population and clinical research, recent studies suggest that the scale’s descriptors may overlap for some people, with differences in interpretation of their content.40 41 Its use here enabled comparison between studies.

Implications for research and practice

The extent of activity limitations reported in this study pertained to a single time point and should be investigated longitudinally to provide a more accurate picture of people’s changing function across the symptom’s trajectory. Future clinical studies might also consider incorporating such assessment as a non-primary outcome to help delineate the impact of interventions on meaningful outcomes for their participants. Potentially, the observed gradation in activity limitations might be indicative of symptom severity, and that relationship should be explored further.

Routine clinical assessment of chronic breathlessness should include enquiries about its impact and, if present, its extent. This, in turn, can help inform subsequent symptom treatment including non-pharmacological (eg, pulmonary rehabilitation, hand-held fan) and pharmacological (eg, regular, low-dose opioids) therapies within a personalised care plan.18 Understanding the availability and roles (current and future) of caregivers is also required as patients navigate these impacts. High caregiver burden is common in this population, so any clinical discussion should also enquire about the implications for caregivers as they deal with changing roles in their partnerships and increasing responsibilities.4245 Given the wide-ranging effects of chronic breathlessness on people’s lives, a multidisciplinary approach that includes physiotherapists, occupational therapists and social workers will be needed to help to optimise a person’s function and independence for as long as possible.

Conclusion

Chronic breathlessness affects people’s ability to perform a range of everyday activities in a nuanced manner, from continuing to perform them with difficulty to reducing and ceasing tasks. The likelihood of activities being affected and the frequency with which individuals undertake those activities increases with the symptom’s severity. Clinical recognition should extend beyond a dichotomised assessment of a person’s ability or inability to do something.
