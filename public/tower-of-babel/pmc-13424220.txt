“It Doesn’t Matter Where OCD Comes From,” or Does It?
Authors: Caitlin M Pinciotti, Ryan J McCarty, Gabriella T Ponzini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewThis review critically evaluates the claim that etiology is not relevant to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Research on the clinical presentation and treatment outcomes associated with traumatic/stressor and biological etiologies is presented.Recent FindingsThe extant literature demonstrates varying clinical utility across the reviewed etiologies. Traumatic/stressor etiologies show the most consistent and robust evidence of clinical utility, demonstrating compelling associations with clinical presentation and treatment outcomes, while also being amenable to existing evidence-based approaches. Medical conditions (e.g., Pediatric Acute onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome) and neurobiological factors also demonstrate considerable associations with OCD presentation, yet effective treatments are more variable. Genetics research is still emerging, with unclear clinical utility based on presently available treatments.SummaryEtiology has variable clinical utility for OCD. Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), characterized by unwanted and intrusive thoughts/images/urges (obsessions) and repetitive avoidance behaviors intended to reduce anxiety and prevent feared outcomes (compulsions), is a complex and often chronic psychiatric condition. OCD is theorized to be maintained through cycles of negative reinforcement, wherein compulsions provide temporary relief from the distress produced by obsessions, but ultimately sustain and intensify symptoms over time [1].

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention (ERP), the front-line behavioral treatment for OCD, targets symptom maintenance cycles through the systematic approach of feared and avoided stimuli (Exposure) while abstaining from compulsions (Response Prevention). This approach is theorized to facilitate fear extinction and corrective learning [2]. Unlike earlier psychodynamic approaches which attempted (unsuccessfully) to incorporate etiology into treatment [3] – and are now considered potentially harmful for OCD [4] – ERP targets the mechanisms believed to maintain OCD symptoms in the present rather than explore the etiology that led to symptom development in the first place [4]. The absence of a definitive causal model within CBT/ERP is therefore not seen as problematic because symptoms can be reduced by preventing their reinforcement [5]. Yet, the success of CBT/ERP in treating OCD symptoms has resulted in the widespread adoption of a much simpler, specious conclusion: “It doesn’t matter where OCD comes from.” Indeed, the premise that etiology is wholly irrelevant to OCD treatment was deeply embedded in our earliest training experiences in CBT/ERP and continues to be largely accepted without question.

While CBT/ERP has garnered substantial empirical support [1], OCD remains a chronic condition with high risk for relapse [6, 7]. Although there is no known singular cause of OCD, there are complex and interacting biological, psychological, and environmental factors that contribute not only to its development but also to its maintenance [8, 9]. In this paper, we argue against the claim that etiology does not matter in the conceptualization and treatment of OCD, pointing to literature suggesting that the reductive nature of this claim misrepresents etiological mechanisms that subsequently become maintenance factors, making them potentially clinically relevant within the CBT/ERP framework. To illustrate this point most clearly, we draw from literature on the impact of traumatic/stressor and biological etiologies on the course, severity, and presentation of OCD symptoms, and their respective impacts on CBT/ERP treatment outcomes.

Traumatic and Stressor Etiology

Background

There is growing recognition of the etiological role of traumatic and stressful life events in the pathogenesis of OCD [10]. While the experience of these events is not unique to OCD, research repeatedly finds elevated rates of these experiences among individuals with OCD [11, 12], including prior to the onset of OCD [11]. In one study, 61.2% of adults with OCD experienced an adverse event in the year leading up to OCD onset, and over one-third (34.9%) experienced an event within the month prior [13]. Trauma exposure is associated with a nearly two-fold increase in cross-sectional OCD risk, increasing with subsequent exposure [14].

While extant cross-sectional studies imply a likely association between traumatic events and OCD risk, methodological limitations have, for a long time, precluded conclusions about causality. A recent Swedish cohort registry study of nearly 3.5 million individuals represents the first prospective evaluation of trauma-related OCD risk, offering the first compelling evidence for a potential causal pathway [15]. In this study, interpersonal violence was associated with a 73% increased risk of subsequent OCD onset. Dovetailing earlier cross-sectional research, risk for OCD onset was highest within the first year after exposure (131%), with decreasing yet still elevated risk thereafter (67%), and sustained risk beyond ten years after exposure. Within the year after exposure, risk for OCD onset was highest between the second and third months, where risk was nearly 250% higher for exposed compared to unexposed individuals. Crucially, this association persisted in discordant full sibling comparisons, suggesting unique environmental factors above genetic factors.

It is therefore not surprising that individuals with OCD often attribute a traumatic or stressful etiology to their OCD. Of residential inpatients asked an open-ended question regarding their causal attributions, 44.4% attributed their OCD to an adverse event, including 13.3% who clearly described a traumatic event aligned with the DSM-5 [16]. When causal options are provided, this rate increases drastically. In a recent study, 61.9% of adults with OCD agreed that experiencing a traumatic event was “to some extent” or “definitely a reason” for their OCD [17].

Impact on Clinical Presentation

Beyond contributing to OCD pathogenesis, traumatic and stressful life events, particularly interpersonal childhood trauma, have shown a consistent impact on the severity, chronicity, and presentation of OCD. Childhood trauma is associated with more severe and chronic OCD symptoms among adults cross-sectionally [18] and over four- and six-year follow-ups [19, 20], showing a doubled odds of chronic illness course [6]. Number of negative life events in the past year also showed a 37% increase in risk for chronic OCD, and childhood trauma exposure increased risk for maintaining chronic OCD by 20% – although the latter association was rendered nonsignificant in the presence of the stronger effects of illness duration and OCD severity. Notably, the associations between OCD severity and trauma exposure persist even when genetic factors, shared environmental factors, and co-occurring depression are controlled, suggesting a unique contributor to OCD severity [21].

Traumatic and stressful life experiences also impact clinical presentation. The aforementioned residential inpatients who attributed their OCD to adverse events reported markedly more obsessions and compulsions, particularly those related to repeating, symmetry, and ordering [16]. More broadly, recent research has found that individuals who perceive their OCD and trauma to overlap – ostensibly the majority of cases with this co-occurring presentation (88.5%) – endorse more severe OCD and posttraumatic stress symptoms, including more severe obsessions, compulsions, and trauma-related intrusions [22].

Traumatic and stressful life events also impact patterns of psychiatric comorbidities, including elevated rates of mood disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), trichotillomania, and personality disorders, particularly when these events preceded OCD [18, 23, 24]. Broadly, childhood trauma confers a four-fold increased risk for additional psychiatric comorbidities with OCD [25]. Comorbid OCD and PTSD is likewise associated with a greater number of comorbidities, including substance use, mood, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and related, feeding/eating, somatoform, impulse control, psychotic, and personality disorders [26–29]. As with traumatic and stressful life event exposure, PTSD onset prior to OCD – sometimes referred to as “posttraumatic OCD” – is associated with a more severe clinical presentation than OCD onset prior to PTSD (“pretraumatic OCD”), including more severe obsessions, compulsions, and suicidality, and greater prevalence of other comorbidities, likely reflecting the traumatic impact on overlapping symptoms and subsequent severity [26].

Indeed, while symptom overlap is generally not measured directly, unique associations between trauma type and OCD presentation suggest trauma-related intersections present in the phenomenology of OCD symptoms. For example, interpersonal traumas are associated with greater endorsement of responsibility for harm and unacceptable thoughts symptoms, whereas non-interpersonal traumas are associated with greater endorsement of hoarding, symmetry, and contamination symptoms [30–33]. Disaster survivors appear to show the most unique symptom profile, reporting elevations in contamination and responsibility for harm symptoms [31]. Unwanted sexual experiences ranging from imagined events to experienced sexual assault have demonstrated consistent associations with mental contamination symptoms in clinical and non-clinical samples [34–37]. Traumatic and stressful events related to aggression/violence (e.g., witnessing murder, accidentally hurting someone with a sharp object), sexual harm, disrespect of religious beliefs, or immorality (e.g., sexually victimized, accused of lying or cheating), contamination (e.g., contact with people with serious illnesses, contact with bodily fluids), and disorganization (e.g., made a mistake resulting in a serious consequence, upbringing in disorderly or untidy house) are associated with elevated obsessive-compulsive symptoms, even when controlling for family history of obsessive-compulsive and related disorders [38]. Taken together, these findings suggest that certain obsessions and compulsions may be common, maladaptive responses to various traumatic and stressful experiences.

At the granular level, trauma is associated not only with thematic presentation but also the presence and severity of compulsions [16, 39], highlighting the theorized impact of trauma exposure on engagement in compulsions serving to cope with past trauma and prevent future trauma [10]. Trauma-exposed patients in intensive treatment were more likely to engage in reassurance-seeking, rumination, and hair pulling compulsions, and less likely to engage in self-assurance compulsions, compared to those without trauma [18]. These compulsions also evidenced unique clustering patterns based on trauma exposure, perhaps reflecting differences in underlying mechanisms and functions [37]. More specifically, body-focused compulsions in the broader OCD sample included only repetitive behaviors (i.e., picking and pulling), but among the trauma-exposed patients, these behaviors additionally clustered with symmetry/just right behaviors (i.e., somatic movements, touch/tap/rub), perhaps reflecting the unique association between trauma exposure and just right symptoms theorized to distract from or otherwise cope with trauma-related responses [33]. In addition, compulsions intended to absolve guilt, obligation, or punishment clustered together only in the trauma-exposed sample, perhaps reflecting the use of these compulsions to assuage trauma- and OCD-related guilt and shame [10, 37].

Impact on Treatment Outcomes

“Treatment-resistant” OCD, or OCD that persists despite adequate first-line treatment trials, is characterized by more extensive traumatic and stressful life event exposure compared to OCD that is responsive to treatment [40–43], however the impact of traumatic and stressful life events on treatment outcomes is complex and dependent on a range of methodological factors. Although trauma, when operationalized dichotomously, does not appear to broadly impact treatment outcomes [18], more nuanced methodologies have found trauma-related influences on treatment outcomes. For example, the combination of certain trauma types and compulsions (e.g., sexual abuse with cleaning rituals) negatively impacts treatment outcomes [37]. Dissociation, rather than trauma exposure directly, may also be a more salient predictor of treatment resistance [40]. Nonetheless, it has been proposed that even in cases where trauma does not adversely affect treatment outcomes within an episode of care, it may be more strongly associated with symptom relapse and treatment reinitiation given its association with chronicity over time [18]. Comorbid PTSD has further shown to interfere with the effectiveness of CBT/ERP [44, 45], highlighting the need to address these co-occurring symptoms in OCD treatment.

Biological Etiology

Background

Etiologies of a “biological” nature consist of multiple contributors to the development and maintenance of OCD. We briefly review three categories, which are not mutually exclusive, nor do they represent all possible biological factors. The first to be discussed are genetic factors. Broad-sense heritability (i.e., total genetic contribution) estimates of OCD from family and twin-based studies are around 50% [46, 47]. Importantly, shared environmental influences appear to add little risk beyond genetic transmission [48, 49]. Recently, the largest genome-wide association study (GWAS) of OCD identified the first 30 genome-wide risk loci [50], though their findings suggest that much of the genetic risk for OCD is likely shaped by many small variations across thousands of genes. At the same time, there is also evidence that rarer gene variants, including low frequency variants, mutations and copy number variants, may contribute to risk in a subset of cases [51–54].

The second category concerns neurobiological components. Meta-analytic work indicates that individuals with OCD appear to differ from healthy controls in various aspects of brain structure, connectivity, and activity [55–58] although importantly some findings may be confounded by medication status [58, 59] and developmental stage [58]. In particular, a substantial body of literature implicates dysfunction of the cortico–striato–thalamic–cortical circuit (CTSC) in OCD [60, 61], potentially reflecting an imbalance between the direct (excitatory) and indirect (inhibitory) pathways that impairs inhibition of behavioral responses, contributing to compulsions [60]. More recent work suggests that dysfunction within large-brain networks, including the default mode, salience, and frontoparietal networks, may also contribute to OCD pathophysiology via cognitive inflexibility [62, 63]. At the neurotransmitter level, growing evidence implicates abnormalities in the glutamatergic system [60, 64, 65], consistent with circuit-based models, whereas findings regarding the serotonergic system are more mixed [60, 64, 66]. Despite these advances, the prevalence of such abnormalities among individuals with OCD remains unclear; moreover, effect sizes are often modest [55], and structural and functional findings show considerable heterogeneity across studies [56, 61, 63]. Indeed, larger scale multisite machine learning investigations using neuroimaging findings to predict cases of OCD have to date only achieved low-to-moderate classification accuracy [55, 57].

A third category of biological etiology involves specific medical conditions. Secondary obsessive-compulsive syndromes have been linked to brain lesions, genetic syndromes, head trauma, tumors, neurodegenerative disorders, seizures, and autoimmune-inflammatory processes, among others [67]. Increasing attention has focused on immunological mechanisms, including proposals for an autoimmune OCD subtype [68]. The most discussed examples of this are Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorder Associated with Streptococcal infection (PANDAS) and Pediatric Acute onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome (PANS), which are presentations of rapid onset psychiatric and neurological symptoms – including obsessive-compulsive symptoms – often following recent infection [69]. Although these diagnoses remain controversial due to difficulty establishing casual evidence of the proposed etiology [70], emerging evidence suggests a possible neuroimmune pathway, with antibodies from affected children shown to bind and alter striatal cholinergic interneurons [71, 72]. However, the proportion of OCD cases attributable to such acquired conditions remains uncertain. Earlier research estimated that approximately 1–3% of pediatric OCD cases may meet criteria for PANS or PANDAS [73], whereas more recent population-based medical record studies report prevalence and incidence rates ranging from 0.001% to 0.008% [74, 75].

Despite its complexity, many individuals with OCD attribute their condition to biological causes. When open-endedly asked to describe the cause of their OCD, 33–47% cited biological explanations (e.g., genetics, brain “problem”), with an additional 11–26% describing an interaction between biology and other factors like the environment [76, 77]. Similarly, biogenetic causal attributions are rated as “very believable” on average [78]. When provided possible casual options, a recent large study of nearly three thousand individuals with OCD found that 68.4% endorsed their brain’s “wiring” as at least partly causal, compared to 15.8% who rejected this explanation [17]. In this same study, 12.9% endorsed illness-related causes such as PANS/PANDAS, though 77.3% denied this possibility entirely [17].

Impact on Clinical Presentation

Multiple large-scale studies indicate that genetic risk for OCD substantially overlaps with anxiety, depression, anorexia nervosa, and Tourette syndrome [50, 79, 80]. At the same time, emerging evidence suggests that different comorbidity patterns may reflect distinct underlying genetic architectures. For example, an earlier GWAS found that the OCD polygenic signal was significantly attenuated when cases with co-occurring tics were included, implying that tic-related OCD may involve partially distinct genetic susceptibility [81]. Similarly, the strength and pattern of polygenic associations across multiple traits vary by comorbidity-defined subgroups – particularly those involving major depressive disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and autism spectrum disorder [82]. Copy number variants and damaging mutations may also be associated with higher risk of comorbidity (e.g., autism) and obsessing and checking scores [52, 54]. In parallel, research examining individual genetic variants has identified preliminary associations between specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and clinical features such as symptom severity, age at onset, and symptom dimensions (e.g., contamination/washing, checking, ordering), with some effects differing by biological sex [83].

Symptom severity in OCD has been linked to brain structural [84, 85], connectivity [61], and neurotransmitter [65] features, although other studies report no significant associations among the constructs examined [58]. Comorbidity patterns, such as co-occurring depression, may also relate to structural brain differences [58, 84]. There is some evidence for some neurobiological associations with age of onset and illness duration [58, 61, 86, 87], potentially signaling differences in neurodevelopment, though findings are again inconsistent [84]. Smaller neuroimaging studies have also reported symptom-subtype-related differences in brain structure [84, 88], resting-state connectivity [89], and activation during symptom provocation [90]. Despite heterogeneity, these findings suggest the possibility of presentation-specific neural deviations within broader OCD-related networks.

A key characteristic of OCD associated with a PANS/PANDAS etiology is its abrupt and rapid onset, and an episodic, remitting course [69, 91]. Although the existing research base is limited by small sample sizes and variable methodological quality, some studies suggest that individuals with suspected PANS/PANDAS etiologies may more frequently exhibit symptoms such as neurological soft signs, separation anxiety, irritability, emotional lability, tics, and sensory issues compared to those without such etiologies [91, 92]. Symptoms like dysgraphia, behavioral regression, academic decline, urinary symptoms, psychotic symptoms, and food restriction are also commonly observed in PANS/PANDAS cases [91]. In contrast, the frequency of OCD symptom content, beyond these concomitant features, appears to generally resemble that of non-PANS/PANDAS cases [91].

Impact on Treatment Outcomes

An emerging area of research examines genetic and epigenetic markers of response to CBT/ERP. In considering candidate genes, certain SNPs and DNA methylation patterns have been associated with poorer OCD treatment response [93, 94]. Based on the genes implicated, these findings suggest alterations in fear extinction learning and goal directed behaviors may be mediating CBT efficacy [93], though these studies are limited by smaller sample sizes and methodological limitations. In contrast, larger, recent Scandinavian studies yield more mixed results. One found that only one of nine polygenic risk scores (PRS; schizophrenia) - a metric of overall genetic liability - weakly predicted symptom changes following CBT, while others – including an OCD PRS based on the latest GWAS [50] – did not [95]. Another study of CBT response across diagnoses (including OCD) could not identify any significant genetic risk variants, though estimated heritability of symptom change was at ~ 22% [96]. Overall, this literature remains in its infancy; larger, prospective studies that are not focused on candidate genes are needed to establish more definitive evidence and clinical guidance, but current evidence suggests genetic and epigenetic variation may contribute at least partly to treatment response.

Current research is also increasingly focused on identifying neurobiological predictors of treatment response. Across treatment modalities, including behavioral, pharmacological, surgical, and device-based, clinical improvement is generally associated with normalization of hyperactivity in CTSC circuits, though via different mechanisms [60, 97, 98]. Consistent with this, candidate predictors of CBT/ERP treatment response within CSTC and large-scale brain networks included degree of functional connectivity [99, 100], task-based activation [101, 102], glutamate levels [103, 104], and structural brain abnormalities [105]. A smaller body of work also suggests that abnormal electroencephalographic event-related potentials may normalize with treatment in some cases [106]. However, efforts to develop predictive algorithms using neurobiological markers have yielded inconsistent results. While one study found that multilevel resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging indices could predict CBT response with high accuracy [107], another reported low accuracy using a neuroimaging-based machine learning approach to predict remission [108]. As with genetic markers, further research is needed to improve the reliability and clinical utility of these predictors.

For OCD linked to autoimmune or infection-related conditions (e.g., PANS/PANDAS), expert recommendations typically combine psychotherapy and psychotropic medication with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, or immunomodulatory treatments to address the proposed mechanisms [68]. However, the evidence base is limited: a systematic review found “very low” certainty for the benefits of these adjunctive approaches in PANS-like presentations [109]. Research on CBT/ERP in this context is also sparse and inconclusive, due to small sample sizes and low study quality [110], though a recent small, retrospective study linked CBT with symptom improvement in PANDAS/PANS [111]. Additionally, case-based literature indicates conventional treatments like CBT may still be effective in some other secondary OCD presentations, while casual therapies also provided benefit for about one third of cases [67].

When considering the impacts of patients’ casual attributions on treatment, a concern is prognostic pessimism – the expectation of poor health and treatment outcomes. Biological attributions are linked with greater prognostic pessimism both among those with OCD [78] and those rating a hypothetical person with OCD [112]. Consistent with this, several investigations have also identified that endorsing brain- and genetics-based etiologies is at least bivariately correlated with worse obsessive-compulsive symptom severity and perceptions of stigma [17, 113]. However, their impact on treatment response is less clear. One residential treatment study found that endorsing biological/genetic causes predicted greater odds of response (≥ 25% symptom reduction), even after controlling for length of stay and comorbidity, though this effect disappeared with stricter response criteria or when adjusting for baseline severity [77]. Similarly, another study found no effect of pre-treatment biological attributions on treatment response after controlling for baseline symptoms but did observe that reductions in biological attributions during treatment were associated with greater residualized change in OCD symptom severity [113].

Biological casual attributions are also linked to treatment history and preferences. Stronger endorsement of biological causes is associated with prior psychotropic medication use and greater belief in medication efficacy [76, 78]. However, in a recent study, parents of children with self-reported PANDAS rated standard OCD treatments (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs], CBT/ERP, deep brain stimulation, transcranial magnetic stimulation) as largely inappropriate, favoring infection/inflammation-focused and lifestyle interventions instead [114]. Similarly, adults with OCD who endorsed acquired illness explanations reported lower likelihood of prior therapy or medication use [17].

Conclusions

Does it matter where OCD comes from? The sampling of emerging evidence presented here suggests a more conditional answer: etiology is neither universally informative nor universally irrelevant (see Table 1 for summary of the current evidence).

Regarding traumatic/stressor factors, etiology seems consistently relevant for the conceptualization and treatment of OCD, as the literature is clear that traumatic and stressful life events serve as both triggering and aggravating factors in OCD [115] and these experiences are theorized to directly impact the form and function of symptoms [10]. These adverse experiences are associated with greater symptom severity, chronicity, distinct symptom presentations, and comorbidities, and, importantly, often remain integral to the phenomenology of OCD long after onset. While trauma exposure alone does not consistently impact CBT/ERP treatment response, specific trauma-symptom pairings, trauma-related dissociation, and comorbid PTSD can interfere with standard OCD treatment. Fortunately, there exist evidence-based treatment approaches for trauma/PTSD (e.g., prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy) that can be readily incorporated into OCD treatment to target these overlapping maintenance mechanisms [116]. Emerging evidence suggests that trauma-focused treatments can be feasibly and effectively integrated into OCD treatment [117, 118].

Regarding biological factors, etiology is certainly relevant to conceptualization, but its clinical utility is still emerging. Biological etiologies seem important for understanding heterogeneity in clinical presentation, illness course, and (less consistently) treatment response. Genetic, neurobiological, and neurochemical variations contribute to differences in symptom severity, presentation, and comorbidities, and may partially account for variability in CBT/ERP outcomes. However, the clinical utility of these findings remains limited. Although biological markers show promise as predictors of treatment response, they have not yet translated into readily actionable treatment modifications. Exceptions may exist for secondary OCD presentations, such as those associated with autoimmune or infectious processes (e.g., PANS/PANDAS), where adjunctive medical interventions are sometimes considered, albeit with inconsistent and limited empirical support. Thus, while biological etiology clearly matters for understanding what OCD looks like and how it may respond to CBT/ERP treatment, it currently offers less guidance for how to intervene in most cases.

Caveats and Future Directions

The etiological mechanisms described here are not mutually exclusive and often interact. Within epigenetics research, hypermethylation of the oxytocin receptor gene – associated with worsened CBT treatment response – is greater among those with OCD reporting higher levels of stressful life events within the last year [119]. In another study, compared to healthy controls, patients without stressful life events before OCD onset showed increased gray matter volume regions implicated in habit formation and action selection, whereas those with preceding stressful life events showed increases in regions involved in integrating cognitive and affective processes [120]. Direct comparisons suggest relatively greater cerebellar involvement following stressful life events and greater striatal/brainstem involvement without these experiences, though both patterns are present across groups. Taken together, these findings suggest that OCD without prior stressors may be more strongly driven by habit-based processes, whereas OCD following stress exposure may reflect greater dysregulation in systems responsible for adapting to environmental demands.

We cannot confirm the etiology of OCD, with absolute certainty, in any case. There are many interacting causal pathways through which OCD is believed to develop. Although we may infer to a reasonable degree the relevant influences (e.g., when OCD develops immediately following trauma exposure or infection), there are many cases where etiology cannot even be reliably assumed. We chose to focus only on traumatic/stressor and biological factors because they currently offer the clearest and most clinically informative evidence, but they represent only part of a broader landscape of etiological influence. There is a growing literature, for example, regarding biomarker predictors of response to SSRIs that is likewise relevant but beyond the scope of our paper’s focus on CBT/ERP. Finally, our interpretation of extant literature is inherently biased by our training as clinical psychologists with backgrounds in CBT/ERP, and experts may disagree with our categorization of relevance and clinical utility. Continued research is needed to clarify when these pathways meaningfully impact case conceptualization, treatment response, and relapse risk, and when they do not.

Summary

Effective CBT/ERP is not contingent on a complete account of OCD origin. Yet etiology is not inert. In some cases, an understanding of etiology may only slightly inform conceptualization, whereas in others, it may be deeply relevant to its maintenance and prognosis, pointing to necessary adaptations in treatment approach. Perhaps in the latter such cases, greater acknowledgement and direct targeting of these etiological-turned-maintenance factors could reduce the chronicity and relapse risk of OCD. The field has benefited significantly from prioritizing tractable maintenance mechanisms in CBT/ERP, but the accompanying shorthand that etiology is does not matter has simply outpaced and oversimplified the evidence. We therefore reject the notion that “it doesn’t matter where OCD comes from,” and call for more nuanced conceptual models and treatment approaches in which etiology is considered variably consequential as aligned with the present body of research.
