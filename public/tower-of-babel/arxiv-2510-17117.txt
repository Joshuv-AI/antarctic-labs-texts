Digitization Can Stall Swarm Transport: Commensurability Locking in Quantized‑Sensing Chains
Caroline N. Cappetto, Penelope Messinger, Kaitlyn S. Yasumura, Miro Rothman, Tuan K. Do, Gao Wang, Liyu Liu, Robert H. Austin, Shengkai Li, Trung V. Phan

Abstract
We present a minimal model for autonomous robotic swarms in both one-dimensional and higher-dimensional spaces, where identical, field-driven agents interact pairwise to self-organize spacing and independently follow local gradients sensed through quantized digital sensors. We show that the collective response of a multi-agent train amplifies sensitivity to weak gradients beyond what is achievable by a single agent. We discover a fractional transport phenomenon in which, under a uniform gradient, collective motion freezes abruptly whenever the ratio of intra‑agent sensor separation to inter‑agent spacing satisfies a number‑theoretic commensurability condition. This commensurability locking persists even as the number of agents tends to infinity. We find that this condition is exactly solvable on the rationals – a dense subset of real numbers – providing analytic, testable predictions for when transport stalls. Our findings establish a surprising bridge between number theory and emergent transport in swarm robotics, informing design principles with implications for collective migration, analog computation, and even the exploration of number-theoretic structure via physical experimentation.

Introduction

Inspired by decentralized swarm behavior in nature
Duan et al. 2023
;
Tan 2013
;
Cavagna et al. 2017
, robotic swarms offer scalable automation that continues working reliably even when some robots fail
Schranz et al. 2020
;
Winfield and Nembrini 2006
;
Bjerknes and Winfield 2013
. What makes robot swarms effective for engineering purposes is not only distributed decision-making
Vigelius et al. 2014
;
Valentini et al. 2015
but also their ability to self-organize movement
Zhou et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2018
– sensing weak signals, aligning directions, and migrating as a coherent collective despite noise, failures, and limited communication – hallmarks of natural swarms
Wadhams and Armitage 2004
;
Viscido et al. 2004
;
Cavagna and Giardina 2014
. Because coordinated locomotion under minimal sensing – often via quantized digital sensors
Iskakov et al. 2007
;
Pu et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
, unlike natural sensing – and simple spacing control
Brambilla et al. 2013
;
Endsley 2017
is a core primitive for nearly every application, understanding when and how swarms move – or stall – in response to environment cues is a fundamental question of first importance in robophysics.

Here, we investigate the collective response of identical, field-driven agents – arranged as one-dimensional chains
Apoorva et al. 2018
;
Sinaasappel et al. 2025
or as two-dimensional structures
Hernández-López et al. 2024
– to a constant external drive, e.g. a uniform gradient. We do so through both
in-silico
experiments and theoretical analysis. These active, autonomous agents interact pairwise via a damping constraint that enforces equal spacing, and each independently senses the local field using multiple digital sensors with quantized resolution to decide its future movement
Phan et al. 2021
. We see “more is different”
Anderson 1972
: once the agents are coupled, their collective response could outperform any individual, boosting sensitivity to weak gradients. Curiously, we find that the collective response can abruptly collapse to near-zero across parameter space, e.g. gradient strength and inter-agent spacing, revealing a fractal pattern which persists even for infinitely many agents. We explain this emergent transport phenomenon by mapping it to a number-theoretic commensurability problem
Ostlund 1981
, which we provide a complete solution on the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
(a dense subset of the reals
\mathbb{R}
) consistent with our simulations.

We introduce the mathematical model of the robot swarm in Section
II
, report simulation observations in one-dimensional spaces in Section
III
, and present a theoretical explanation in Section
IV
. In Section
V
, we showcase some interesting findings in higher-dimensional spaces. Our results link number theory with robotic swarm behavior and identify concrete levers for reliable operation, while enabling physical probes of arithmetic structure. Commensurability locking phenomena have been observed and studied in condensed matter physics, with many potential for application
Petit-Watelot et al. 2012
;
Reichhardt and Reichhardt 2010
;
Kasper et al. 2020
. In principle, this could open a path to swarm-based analog computation, where collective flow stands in for electron currents – but with greater controllability.

II
Robotic Swarm Model

We start by formulating the mathematical model used for the robotic swarm in our study. For ease of exposition, we focus on the one-dimensional case, noting that the ideas extend naturally to higher dimensions. With
X_{j}(t)
the center position of the
j
-th robot at time
t
and
\Delta t
the discrete time between consecutive time steps, we generate all robot trajectories via:

X_{j}(t+\Delta t)-X_{j}(t)=\lambda_{\text{rand}}+\lambda_{\text{sens}}+\lambda_{\text{int}}\ .

(1)

Let us explain, separately, the three contributions (i.e.
\lambda_{\text{rand}}
,
\lambda_{\text{sens}}
, and
\lambda_{\text{int}}
) to the total displacement:

•

Each robot moves with some stochasticity given by a Wiener increment:

\lambda_{\text{rand}}=(2D\Delta t)^{1/2}\eta_{j}(t)\ ,

(2)

where
D
is the effective diffusivity that quantifies its motion randomness. The factor
\eta_{j}(t)
is an independent draw from a standard normal distribution,
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, with independence across robots
j
and time steps
t
.

•

Every robot has a physical size
R
, with sensors mounted at its two ends, i.e. located at
X_{j}(t)\pm 1
. Each sensor returns a quantized reading of the local signal, so that if the analog input is
S(x)
, where
x
is the position of the sensor, then the perceived signal is digitized into the floor value
\lfloor S(x)\rfloor
(see Fig.
1
A). Based on the perceived signals, the robot compares the two and biases its motion toward the higher value:

\lambda_{\text{sens}}=\kappa\frac{\left\lfloor S\left(X_{j}(t)+R\right)\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor S\left(X_{j}(t)-R\right)\right\rfloor}{2R}\Delta t\ ,

(3)

where
\kappa
controls the magnitude of the directed drift induced by sensing (i.e. field-drive). After a standard spacetime rescaling, we nondimensionalize the model so that
R=1
and
\kappa=1

Manteca Jr et al. 2012
;
Phan et al. 2024
.

•

Robots interact pair-wise with fixed neighbors to maintain the swarm formation. The interaction pulls when separations exceed a distance
L
and pushes when they fall below
L
(see Fig.
1
B), thus stabilizing the spacing at
L
via a damping interaction:

\lambda_{\text{int}}=\mu\sum^{\text{neighbors of }j}_{k}\left(\left|\delta_{kj}(t)\right|-L\right)\frac{\delta_{kj}(t)}{\left|\delta_{kj}(t)\right|}\Delta t\ ,

(4)

where
\delta_{kj}(t)=X_{k}(t)-X_{j}(t)
and
\mu
is the damping coefficient that sets the rate at which pairwise spacing deviations (compared to
L
) are corrected. For a one-dimensional chain formation, the robots are labeled in order
j=1,2,3,...,N
, and interactions occur only between consecutive indices, i.e. the interaction graph is given by
\{(j,j+1)\}^{N-1}_{j=1}
.

We can use Eq. (
2
), Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
4
) to calculate position update with Eq. (
1
), allowing us to simulate robotic swarm behavior. In this work, we only consider motion in a uniform gradient, i.e.
S(x)=\alpha x
. Together, the four constants

\alpha\ ,\ L\ ,\ \mu\ ,\ \text{and}\ D

parameterize the model. To keep neighboring robots separated beyond their physical size, we require
L\geq 2
. Moreover, by reflection symmetry
x\rightarrow-x
,
\alpha
and
-\alpha
are equivalent behavior (but in opposite directions), hence it suffices to consider
\alpha\geq 0
.

It can already be seen from Eq. (
3
) that when the environmental gradient is sufficiently weak – the signal difference
2R\alpha
between two sensors is below the quantized resolution
1
, thus

\alpha<1/2R=0.5

(5)

– there exist positions where the two sensors on the same robot perceive identical signals, and therefore no motion bias arises, i.e.
\lambda_{\text{sens}}=0
(see Fig.
1
A). In a robot swarm, some robots may occupy positions where their sensors perceive a difference; these agents are field-driven up-gradient, and their interactions – intended to maintain the swarm’s formation – can mediate this information and bias the collective motion (see Fig.
1
B).

Fractional Stalling in One-Dimensional Space

Using

The MathWorks 2025
, we implement the model in Section

N
, damping
\mu
, and effective diffusivity
D
, and scan the parameter grid
(L,\alpha)\in[2,20]\texttimes[0,0.6]
with discretizations
\Delta L=0.2
and
\Delta\alpha=0.1
. In all panels of Fig.

\Delta t=0.1

). The transport response is estimated from a time-averaging statistics as

\langle V\rangle/\alpha\ \ \text{with}\ \ \langle V\rangle=\frac{\langle X(t=T)\rangle-\langle X(t=0)\rangle}{T}\ ,\

where
\langle X(t)\rangle
is the center of mass location (space-average) of all robots at time
t
. The transport response equals
1
in the ideal limit –robots driven solely by the bias in Eq. (
3
), with analog (undigitized) sensing that reports the true signal. Interpreting the robots as responsive particles driven by a uniform field (the constant gradient), the emergent transport response serves as a conductivity-like coefficient (e.g. drift mobility).

We begin, in Fig.
2
A, with a baseline swarm of
N=100
robots (to approximate an infinite-long chain), with inter-robot damping coefficient
\mu=0.1

D=0.01

N=1

2

2

IV
A Number-Theoretic Explanation

The following is our conjecture to explain the stalling phenomena reported in Section
III
. The collective transport of the robot swarm can stall because, for certain values of parameters
\alpha
and
L
, there exists a spatial distribution of agents, equally spaced by
L
, in which none of the agents detects any difference between the digitized values perceived by its sensors – even in the limit of infinitely-many agents
N\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, the set
\mathcal{S}
of all
(\alpha,L)
for which this situation may occurs can be described as a number-theoretic commensurability problem:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{S}=\Big\{(\alpha,L):\alpha\in[0,0.5),L\in[2,+\infty)\Big|\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \exists x\in\mathbb{R},\forall(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:x+\frac{m}{\alpha}+nL\notin[-1,+1]\Big\}\end{split}

(6)

– see Appendix
B
for a full derivation. While we are still not able to completely solve this problem, we can if we focus on its rational subset:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathcal{S}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{2}\ ,

(7)

which is dense in
\mathcal{S}
– meaning any
(\alpha,L)
in
\mathcal{S}
can be approximated arbitrarily closely by rational pairs in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Intuitively, restricting to rational parameters loses no generality for approximation, i.e. results proved on
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}
should extend to all of
\mathcal{S}
by continuity.

Let us write
\alpha=a_{1}/b_{1}
and
L=a_{2}/b_{2}
as irreducible fractions, which means
(a_{i},b_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and gcd
(a_{i},b_{i})=1
with
i=1,2
. As a corollary of the Euclid algorithm
Lehmer 1938
, we have the following set-equality:

\{ma+nb:(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}=\{k\times\text{ gcd}(a,b):k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(8)

By using this equality on the numerator of

\frac{m}{\alpha}+nL=\frac{mb_{1}}{a_{1}}+\frac{na_{2}}{b_{2}}=\frac{mb_{1}b_{2}+na_{1}a_{2}}{a_{1}b_{2}}\ ,

(9)

we see that:

\begin{split}\left\{\frac{m}{\alpha}+nL:(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}=\left\{k\times\frac{\text{ gcd}(b_{1}b_{2},a_{1}a_{2})}{a_{1}b_{2}}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\ .\end{split}

(10)

Denote gcd
(b_{1}b_{2},a_{1}a_{2})/(a_{1}b_{2})=c
, then:

•

If
c>2
then any value in the interval
(1,c-1)
satisfies the condition in
|...\}
of Eq. (
6
), thus
(\alpha,L)=(a_{1}/b_{1},a_{2}/b_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

•

If
c\leq 2
then because the length of the interval
[-1,1]
equals to
2
, such
(\alpha,L)
will not land on
\mathcal{S}
.

Fig.
2
A shows a reasonable agreement between the values of
(\alpha,L)
that exhibit collective stalling and points in our solution set:

S_{\mathbb{Q}}=\left\{(\alpha,L)=\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}},\frac{a_{2}}{b_{2}}\right):\frac{\text{ gcd}(b_{1}b_{2},a_{1}a_{2})}{a_{1}b_{2}}>2\right\}\ ,

(11)

which can be generated numerically by scanning through integer values of
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
.

There are many features of Fig.
2
that our simple number-theoretic theory has yet to explain, such as the distribution of peaks and dips in the transport response. We also observe rich behavior at large slopes (
\alpha\geq 0.5
) – we report our observations in Section
C
.

V
Some Observations in Higher-Dimensional Spaces

This section explores phenomena that emerge in higher-dimensional generalizations of the robotic swarm, using the same model as in Section
II
for simulations. The gradient always points along the
x
-direction, and the signal is otherwise uniform transversely – the
y
-direction in two-dimensional space and the
xy
-plane in three-dimensional space. For simplicity, we assume the robots are always oriented with their sensors aligned along the

x
-direction. We use the same parameters across all simulations and dimensions, i.e.
\alpha=0.1
,
L=3
,
\mu=0.1
, and
D=0.01
; the only change is the swarm formation (number of robots and topology).

In two-dimensional space, we consider different triangle formations: a solid triangle (see Fig.
3
A), a Sierpinski fractal triangle (see Fig.
3
B), and an empty triangle (see Fig.
3
C). In the robophysics literature, the first and last structures are known as
active solids

Hernández-López et al. 2024
and
active polymers

Sinaasappel et al. 2025
; here we also consider
active fractals
– the middle structures. Active solids are very rigid; active fractals are more deformable; active polymers are the easiest to deform. We observed from our simulations that the collective transport response of the solid triangle swarm is the highest and that of the empty triangle swarm is the lowest (see Fig.
3
A-C1). In the center of mass frame, over a total time
T=5\times 10^{4}

(with time step
\Delta t=0.1
), the solid triangle swarm exhibits little rotation (see Fig.
3
A2). By contrast, the Sierpinski fractal triangle swarm reorients and its transport response decreases (see Fig.
3
B2). Our interpretation is that the reorientation establishes persistent internal constraints, diverting power into maintaining internal order rather than coherent up-gradient motion, so the emergent collective transport slows down. The hollow triangle collapses, flattening in the transverse direction then shrinking (see Fig.
3
C2). All these behaviors are shown in Supplementary Movies
smovie02.mp4
,
smovie03.mp4
, and
smovie04.mp4
. Similar collapsing behavior can also be seen in three dimensions for an
active “fuzzy sphere”

;
Vo et al. 2025
(arranged in a buckyball C
60
formation): the structure first “pancakifies” onto the transverse plane and then contracts (see Supplementary Movies
smovie05.mp4
). The collapsed buckyball also exhibits a very clear
crawling motion
– appearing to expand and contract in the center of mass frame along the
x
-direction – as it climbs up the gradient (see Appendix
D
).

VI
Discussion

We have found that sensing quantization can make an infinite chain of field-driven robots stall in a one-dimensional constant drive, when the distance between sensors and robot spacing with this swarm satisfy the commensurability locking condition Eq. (
11
). In two- and three-dimensional spaces, depending on the swarm topology, the structure can reorient, collapse, and alter its collective transport properties. These physical properties are fundamental to the transport of robotic swarms using digital sensing, in the limit where individual capabilities become ineffective and collective effects dominate.

It is important to note that here we have only scratched the surface of a potentially deep topic. There are many interesting features of the data that remain unexplained, such as the local peaks and dips in the transport response beside commensurability locking (as shown in Fig.
2
and Fig.
6

There are many further directions to explore, such as considering different swarm formation structures, performance robustness to failed or deactivated robots, collective response to time-varying drives or adaptive spacing to deliberately break commensurability, and replacing fixed connections to active ones in which direct interacting relations evolve dynamically via Thiessen/Dirichlet–Voronoi tessellations
Burrough et al. 2015
(which preliminary findings have been reported in
Van Phan 2021
). The randomness of robot motion can be made more biologically relevant by using an adaptive random walk
Nguyen et al. 2024
instead of a simple random walk as described in Eq. (
2
); this feature could have future implications for bioengineering. Physical experiments are underway and will be reported in future work (see Fig.

).
