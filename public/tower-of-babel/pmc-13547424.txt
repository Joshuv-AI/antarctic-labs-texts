Prior PrEP use and discontinuation in individuals newly diagnosed with HIV
Authors: Rohan Saini, Sarah Fidler, Marta Boffito, Vicky Tittle, Nicolo Girometti, Gary Whitlock, the Dean Street Collaborative Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn settings with high uptake of HIV pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), an increasing proportion of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV report prior PrEP exposure. Understanding patterns of PrEP use, discontinuation and adherence in such settings is essential to inform future HIV prevention strategies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case‐note review of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV at 56 Dean Street, London, UK, between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2024. Data collected included demographics, prior PrEP use, adherence and reasons for discontinuation and baseline blood results. Individuals with prior PrEP use were compared with those who had never used PrEP.ResultsAmong 1080 individuals newly diagnosed with HIV, 210 (19.4%) reported prior PrEP use. The annual proportion with prior PrEP use increased from 4.3% in 2017 to 60.5% in 2024. Compared with never‐PrEP users, those with prior PrEP use were more likely to have recently acquired HIV (85.7% vs. 58.2%, p < 0.00001), to have previously attended the clinic (70.0% vs. 37.7%, p < 0.00001) and to have undergone more frequent HIV testing. Among prior PrEP users, 36.2% reported PrEP discontinuation, and in those who had not discontinued PrEP at diagnosis, 73.5% reported poor adherence. The most common reasons for discontinuation were lack of PrEP supply, monogamy and gastrointestinal side effects. The only major resistance mutation that showed a significant difference between the two groups was M184I/V: 27 (19.6%) in PrEP‐exposed versus 7 (0.8%) in not‐PrEP‐exposed.ConclusionsIn this high‐PrEP‐uptake setting, a growing proportion of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV report prior PrEP use, most commonly characterized by discontinuation or suboptimal adherence. These findings highlight an ongoing unmet need for interventions that support PrEP persistence and continuity, including improved access pathways and alternative PrEP formulations.Keywords: GBMSM, HIV prevention, PrEP

INTRODUCTION

Sexual health services play a central role in the United Kingdom's HIV prevention and 56 Dean Street (56DS) is the largest publicly funded combined sexual health and HIV service nationally [1]. Between 2015 and 2017, the service experienced an 80% reduction in new HIV diagnoses among gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM), a decline attributed to the implementation of a comprehensive model of combination prevention characterized by increased testing frequency, earlier diagnosis, rapid anti‐retroviral therapy (ART) initiation and high PrEP uptake [2, 3]. HIV diagnoses at this service have continued to decline year‐on‐year since then [2, 4].

The number of PrEP users at 56DS is among the highest reported globally [5], with almost half of all PrEP use in England prescribed at 56DS in 2021 [2]. By the end of 2024, 56DS had 24 487 PrEP users [2]. This is primarily due to nurse‐led PrEP delivery using a legal framework, known as Patient Group Directions (PGD), that facilitates nurses and allied health professionals to prescribe medication such as oral PrEP and hence provide PrEP initiation and delivery [6]. In 2021, 90.8% of all PrEP consultations at 56DS were nurse‐led. In addition, to facilitate PrEP use, 56DS allows individuals to walk‐in without a booked appointment slot to start PrEP or for an emergency PrEP supply [6].

Recent analyses have demonstrated both the effectiveness of HIV prevention strategies in London and persistent gaps in protection, even in settings with high service engagement and PrEP availability [1, 7]. These gaps may include inconsistent PrEP use, discontinuation and barriers to sustained adherence, all of which reduce the population‐level effectiveness of PrEP‐based prevention [1, 2].

Despite increasing PrEP coverage, there are relatively few published studies describing individuals newly diagnosed with HIV in high‐PrEP settings. Surveillance‐based analyses from New York City have shown that only a minority of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV report prior PrEP use, with limited data available on patterns of use or reasons for discontinuation [8]. European cohort studies to date remain small and have provided limited insight into adherence and persistence [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. As PrEP use increases, understanding why HIV acquisition occurs among individuals with prior PrEP exposure is increasingly important. In particular, describing the patterns of PrEP use, discontinuation and adherence in such settings is essential to inform future HIV prevention strategies.

We therefore undertook a retrospective case‐based review of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV at 56DS between 2017 and 2024. The objectives of this study were to describe the prevalence and patterns of prior PrEP use and discontinuation and to compare clinical and testing characteristics between individuals with and without prior PrEP exposure.

We also looked at baseline resistance mutations in individuals according to PrEP exposure. Previous studies have shown an increased chance of harbouring the M184I/V mutation in those with prior PrEP use [9, 10, 11, 13]. Therefore, knowledge around baseline resistance is important as it has consequences for the choice of initial ART and subsequent virological suppression.

METHODS

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective review of clinical records for all individuals newly diagnosed with HIV at 56DS between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2024.

Data collection

Data extracted from electronic clinical records included age, gender identity, sexual orientation, ethnicity, country of birth, CD4 cell count and HIV viral load at diagnosis, prior HIV testing history, markers of recent HIV acquisition, prior attendance at 56DS and PrEP use history.

Prior PrEP use is defined by individuals self‐reporting previously taking at least one PrEP tablet. For individuals reporting prior PrEP use, additional data were collected on PrEP formulation and self‐reporting of prior PrEP discontinuation where applicable and reasons for PrEP discontinuation.

Individuals self‐reported previous poor adherence defined as less than four PrEP tablets per week (for daily PrEP use) or incorrect event‐based dosing.

Suboptimal use was defined as PrEP discontinuation or, for those still taking PrEP at diagnosis, poor PrEP adherence.

Recent HIV acquisition was defined as either a documented negative HIV test within the preceding 12 months or laboratory evidence of recent infection based on avidity testing where available [1, 2].

Resistance mutations

For all individuals newly diagnosed with HIV at 56DS, it is normal practise to order a ‘standard’ baseline genotypic resistance test for protease inhibitor (PI), nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) and non‐nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI). From 1st January 2023, integrase inhibitor (INSTI) resistance testing was also introduced at baseline. Before that date, baseline INSTI resistance testing could be ordered at the physician's discretion.

Major resistance‐associated mutations according to the four drug classes (PI, NRTI, NNRTI, INSTI) were determined using the Stanford algorithm [14]. A full list of all the major baseline resistance mutations is presented in the Supplementary Table S1.

In order to investigate the effect of previous PrEP exposure on baseline resistance mutations, we performed a sub‐analysis of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV who had been exposed to PrEP since their last negative HIV test (PrEP‐exposed). We compared them with individuals who had never used PrEP and those who had used PrEP previously but had tested negative for HIV since their last PrEP use (Not‐PrEP‐exposed). In this sub‐analysis, we excluded individuals for whom it was unknown if they had tested negative for HIV since their last PrEP use.

Comparison was made between individuals with prior PrEP use versus those who had never used PrEP for all baseline categories relevant for the analysis: We did not include gender or year of diagnosis in the analysis. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi‐square tests, and continuous variables using Mann–Whitney U tests.

For resistance mutations, where there were no mutations in any sub‐category, no analysis was done. Due to small numbers, we used Fisher's exact test for other mutations.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethics

This real‐world cohort consists of routinely collected clinical data from electronic health records at a single National Health Service (NHS) service. The project was registered and approved as a service evaluation by the clinical governance team. All collected data were anonymized in line with Caldicott Principles, and the Health Research Authority assessed that ethics board approval was not required. Reporting of data and outcomes is aggregated to prevent potential identifiability.

Individuals with prior PrEP use were predominantly cisgender GBMSM and did not differ significantly from never‐PrEP users in terms of age, ethnicity or country of birth. Median CD4 cell count at diagnosis was higher among those with prior PrEP use compared with never‐PrEP users (570 vs. 450 cells/mm3, p < 0.00001). There was no difference in baseline median HIV viral load.

Of all individuals newly diagnosed with HIV, the proportion who have previously used PrEP according to their gender and sexual orientation was 200/1002 (20.0%) in cisgender GBMSM, 9/26 (34.6%) transgender women, 1/28 (3.6%) cisgender heterosexual men and 0/24 (0%) cisgender women.

Trends in prior PrEP use

The annual proportion of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV who reported prior PrEP use increased over time, from 4.3% (9/209) in 2017 to 60.5% (49/81) in 2024. (Figure 1).

Testing history and recency of infection

As presented in Table 1, individuals with prior PrEP use were more likely to have recently acquired HIV compared with never‐PrEP users (85.7% vs. 58.2%, p < 0.00001). They had shorter intervals between HIV tests and were significantly more likely to have previously attended 56DS (70.0% vs. 37.7%, p < 0.00001).

Never‐PrEP users were significantly more likely to have never (or never known) tested for HIV previously (15.3% vs. 1.4%, p < 0.00001).

Nearly half of individuals reported daily PrEP use (98, 46.7%), and a similar proportion reported event‐based dosing (96, 45.7%). The last PrEP dose was taken within three months of HIV diagnosis in 119 (56.7%) individuals, including 29 (13.8%) who reported PrEP use on the day of diagnosis.

Of the 75 who self‐reported poor PrEP adherence, 56 (75%) reported using event‐based dosing, 18 (24%) daily and 1 (1%) dosing unknown.

The most frequently reported reasons for PrEP discontinuation were lack of PrEP supply (34, 44.7%), monogamy (18, 23.7%), gastrointestinal side effects (7, 9.2%) and periods of abstinence (4, 5.3%).

DISCUSSION

In this large retrospective cohort from a high‐PrEP‐uptake sexual health service, we observed a substantial and increasing proportion of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV who reported prior PrEP use. By 2024, over 60% of new HIV diagnoses occurred in individuals with prior PrEP exposure, reflecting the changing epidemiology of HIV acquisition in settings with widespread PrEP availability. In health care systems where PrEP use is expanding, such as the Netherlands and Italy, prior PrEP use in individuals newly diagnosed with HIV is also increasing with time [10, 11].

Individuals with prior PrEP use demonstrated high engagement with sexual health services such as 56DS, frequent HIV testing and a high likelihood of recent infection at diagnosis. For instance, from previous analysis, the characteristics of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV at 56DS differed substantially depending on whether they had previously attended our service compared with those who had never done so: Prior attenders were more often diagnosed early, with recent HIV infection, three times higher prior PrEP use and the median time since their last HIV test was 187 days [1]. We feel that these findings suggest that HIV acquisition in those with prior PrEP use is less likely to reflect disengagement from care and more likely to be related to gaps in effective prevention, particularly PrEP persistence and adherence.

The majority of prior PrEP users reported suboptimal use, most commonly due to discontinuation or poor adherence. A recent global systemic review of oral PrEP showed that 41% of PrEP users discontinued PrEP within 6 months and suboptimal adherence was 38% over the same period [15].

At 56DS, structural factors such as running out of PrEP were prominent, highlighting ongoing access barriers even in well‐resourced urban services. As stated in the Introduction, 56DS offers a walk‐in service for emergency PrEP supply. It is unclear if PrEP users are fully aware of this service and/or if supply issues occur in individuals when they are outside London and unable to access PrEP elsewhere. In addition to disseminating information about the walk‐in clinic for emergency supplies, other measures are needed to optimize PrEP care at all UK services including 56DS. It would be useful to ask those who cite lack of PrEP supply as a barrier to PrEP use what measures could be put in place to address this.

Behavioural factors, including changes in relationship status and perceived HIV risk, also contributed to discontinuation. These findings align with recent analyses of HIV prevention outcomes and gaps in London, which have emphasized the need to better understand who remains unprotected despite high overall PrEP coverage [1, 2].

Our findings have important implications for future HIV prevention strategies. Interventions that support PrEP persistence, reduce interruptions in supply and offer alternative formulations and PrEP provision such as online PrEP and/or home delivery may be necessary to sustain declines in HIV incidence. For instance, at 56DS we have introduced a new service led by the health adviser team to offer support to individuals who report poor PrEP adherence as well as PrEPbox, an online service to provide PrEP by home delivery [16].

Long‐acting PrEP formulations may be particularly beneficial for individuals who experience challenges with daily or event‐based oral dosing. While these options may be beneficial by increasing choice, they may also introduce new challenges, such as the need for more precise appointment scheduling and the effect this may have on clinic capacity, potential local reactions at the site of administration where injections are used, and possible resistance issues involving drug classes beyond NRTIs. Subsequent real‐world data for long‐acting PrEP, such as is presented here for oral PrEP, will be required to understand their benefits and limitations.

In terms of baseline resistance tests, our analysis reinforces previous global findings: In those with prior PrEP use, baseline genotypic resistance tests are less likely to amplify DNA and where they do, M184I/V is more frequent with no significant increases in the frequency of other major resistance mutations [9, 10, 11, 13]. This has consequences for the choice of first‐line ART in these individuals.

Table 4 is useful information for a clinician's choice of ART in individuals newly diagnosed with HIV in the absence of a baseline resistance test result: for around a quarter who took PrEP within 1 month of their confirmed diagnosis, the resistance test did not amplify, and for those where it did, around a third had M184I/V.

Strengths of this study include the large cohort size over 8 years and detailed characterization of PrEP use patterns.

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and single‐centre design, which may reduce generalizability to other settings. Additionally, reasons for PrEP discontinuation initiation were determined through clinician review of case notes, which may be subject to interpretation bias as well as reliance on self‐reported adherence and its documentation, and missing data for some variables.

The analysis presented does not include long‐term outcomes of these individuals once their HIV diagnosis is confirmed, such as data regarding timing and choice of ART and time to viral suppression or retention in care: We intend to perform this analysis in future.

In a setting with exceptionally high PrEP uptake, a growing proportion of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV report prior PrEP use, most often characterized by discontinuation or suboptimal adherence. Addressing barriers to PrEP persistence and continuity remains critical to achieving further reductions in HIV transmission.
