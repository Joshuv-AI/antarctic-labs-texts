Accuracy of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring in predicting postoperative neurological decline in intradural extramedullary spinal tumor surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lukasz Antkowiak, Marta Antkowiak, Anna Kasperczuk, Wojciech Kaspera, Marek Mandera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The role of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) in intradural extramedullary (IDEM) tumor surgery remains unestablished. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to systematically review and meta-analyze the recent literature on the accuracy of IONM in the resection of IDEM tumors.The study was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were searched for English-language papers published from inception until February 19, 2025. The study was found eligible if it reported the complete data on the accuracy of motor evoked potentials (MEPs), somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), D-wave monitoring, or multimodal IONM (mIONM – defined as a combination of more than one of the abovementioned modalities) in patients with IDEM tumors. Each modality’s accuracy was evaluated using the Hierarchical Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic (HSROC) curve for predicting short-term (immediate postoperative) and long-term postoperative deficits (minimum of 3 months after discharge).From a total of 3220 papers, 16 studies met the eligibility criteria. Additionally, one study was found based on references analysis, resulting in the inclusion of 17 papers. MEPs significantly predicted the occurrence of both short-term (AUROC = 0.72; p < 0.0001) and long-term (AUROC = 0.91; p = 0.006) new motor deficits. Conversely, D-wave failed to accurately predict motor deficits (AUROC = 0.83; p = 0.1094, and AUROC = 0 in the short- and long-term observation, respectively). Similarly, SSEPs showed low accuracy in predicting short-term (AUROC = 0.55; p = 0.7461) and long-term (AUROC = 0.53; p = 0.3953) sensory deficits. Conversely, mIONM was found to accurately predict short-term (AUROC = 0.75; p = 0.001) and long-term (AUROC = 0.70; p = 0.005) new neurological deficits.MEPs and mIONM constitute the sole modalities that can accurately predict motor and neurological deficits, respectively. Contrarily, the low sensitivity of SSEPs for predicting sensory deficits underlines its efficacy in IDEM tumor surgery. Although D-wave did not achieve satisfactory accuracy, its application seems reasonable to prevent permanent motor deficits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10143-025-03926-y.

Introduction

Intradural extramedullary (IDEM) tumors represent the second most common group of spinal neoplasms, accounting for approximately 70–80% of purely intradural tumors [30, 36]. Considering the benign character of most IDEM tumors, a gross-total resection (GTR) remains a primary goal of treatment [4, 32]. Given the high eloquence of the spinal cord, surgery for both intramedullary spinal cord tumors (IMSCTs) and IDEM tumors is considered a high-risk surgery [9, 18, 45]. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) has been implemented into daily practice to maximize the extent of resection (EOR) without compromising patient neurological status during surgical IMSCT removal. Various modalities, including motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs), and D-wave monitoring, have been found feasible in accurately predicting the occurrence of either motor or sensory iatrogenic deficits [3, 16, 34]. In that way, IONM became a standard of care in IMSCT surgery [14, 24, 25, 35].

Unlike in IMSCTs, where the spinal cord is at direct risk of iatrogenic injury, IDEM tumors rather compress the spinal cord from the outside. Nevertheless, demanding anterior or anterolateral tumor location, its tight adhesion to the spinal cord pia mater, or high vasculature may increase the risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury, either by traction, compression, or vascular pauperization [12]. Therefore, the trend towards (although still center-dependent) implementation of IONM to maximize the safety of IDEM tumor surgery has become more apparent in recent years. However, the scarcity of high-quality literature on that topic precluded the formulation of any recommendations so far. In 2019, Ishida et al. [17] performed a meta-analysis encompassing the data from four studies and the authors' own single-institutional experience. The study highlighted the high accuracy of IONM in predicting neurological deficits, with a sensitivity of 82.4% and specificity of 90.7%. In that way, the authors shed light on the possible prognostic efficacy of IONM in that setting. Since the publication of the study by Ishida et al., numerous high-quality papers have been published on that topic [2, 7, 11, 13, 19, 26, 27, 33, 37, 38, 41–43, 46]. Therefore, throughout this study, we aimed to update the recent literature on the accuracy of IONM in the resection of IDEM tumors.

Methods

Study guidance

The review was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [31]. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database under the identification number CRD42024533295.

Search strategy and criteria

The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were independently searched by two authors (L.A. and M.A.) for English-language full-text papers published from inception until February 19, 2025. The advanced strategy of the electronic database searching process is presented in Supplementary Data 1.

The entire database searching results have been extracted into the covidence.org software and automatically scanned for duplicates. After duplicate removal, the title and abstract of each study were screened independently by two authors (L.A. and M.A.). Inclusion criteria comprised clinical studies and case series evaluating the accuracy of IONM in patients undergoing surgery for IDEM tumors. Contrarily, publications reporting on mixed (IDEM tumors with IMSCTs or extradural tumors) or unspecified spinal tumor groups, papers focusing on purely pediatric patients, as well as conference papers, review articles, meta-analyses, commentaries, letters to the editor, and case reports were excluded. Additionally, the reference lists in all preselected articles were screened for further relevant papers. Notably, the automation tool (covidence.org) was used solely for duplicate removal, leaving all the following steps to be performed manually by two authors (L.A. and M.A.).

Eligibility criteria

The study was found eligible if it clearly reported the complete data on the accuracy [true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) events] of MEPs, SSEPs, D-wave monitoring, or multimodal IONM (mIONM – defined as a combination of more than one of the abovementioned modalities)[10] in the group of patients with IDEM tumors. Any discrepancies appearing in the paper (unclear number of IONM alarms, unclear neurological evaluation point) that precluded the precise and undoubtful calculation of accuracy values (TP, TN, FP, FN) resulted in the paper's exclusion from the systematic review. Moreover, studies that presented accuracy values only by reporting on the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) without details on the number of TP, FP, TN, and FN cases were excluded from analyses.

Data extraction

From the included studies, the following data were extracted: first author, publication year, study design, study period, the presence of a non-monitored control group, baseline patient characteristics, used IONM modalities, tumor pathology, tumor axial localization, tumor sagittal localization, tumor EOR, modality for motor, sensory or neurological status determination, and preventive measures taken after IONM potentials decline.

Additionally, the following data were extracted for each IONM modality: (1) amplitude/latency decline alarm criteria; (2) baseline monitorability; (3) number of alarm cases; (4) number of rescue cases; (5) number of permanent potentials amplitude/latency decline; (6) number of TP, TN, FP, FN cases; (7) number of related short- and long-term motor, sensory or neurological deficits.

Strategy for alarm classification

The intraoperative decline of IONM potentials below the predefined amplitude/latency alarm threshold was defined as an alarm case. Following the undertaken preventive measures (such as halting surgical maneuvers, irrigation with warm saline, elevating blood pressure and body temperature, etc.), IONM alarm cases were stratified into either rescue cases (when the potentials recovered above the predefined amplitude/latency threshold before the end of the surgery), or permanent IONM decline cases (when the IONM potential values did not improve above the predefined amplitude/latency threshold before the end of surgery). Only permanent IONM decline cases were considered positive events and were further stratified into TP or FP, depending on the patient's postoperative motor/sensory/neurological status. Contrarily, both rescue cases and cases that did not exceed the modality predefined amplitude/latency threshold were considered negative events, either TN or FN, depending on the patient's postoperative motor/sensory/neurological status.

Quality assessment

The quality of all studies included in this systematic review was evaluated by two authors (L.A. and M.A.) independently using the second version of the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool [44]. All discrepancies between authors' judgments were discussed to reach a reliable consensus.

The accuracy of each modality was evaluated for predicting short-term (immediate postoperative) and long-term postoperative deficits (at least 3 months after hospital discharge).

In addition, Hierarchical Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic (HSROC) curves were generated using a non-linear mixed-effects regression model, accounting for heterogeneity between studies and providing a pooled estimation of diagnostic performance. The Chi-square test for categorical data assessed intergroup differences in diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV). For continuous variables, comparisons were made using t-tests (if data were normally distributed) or Mann–Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data). Forest plots were generated for each diagnostic metric to visualize individual study estimates and pooled values. 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed using the Wilson score method. The binomial exact test was used to evaluate whether AUROC values significantly differed from a random classifier (AUROC = 0.5).

Results

Through the advanced literature searching process, 3220 papers were identified, including 951 from PubMed, 1352 from Embase, and 917 from Cochrane. After removing 874 duplicates, 2346 studies were further evaluated based on title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 2197 papers did not meet the inclusion criteria, leaving 149 studies for full-text review. Among them, 5 papers did not have full text to be retrieved. Accuracy of MEPs for predicting motor deficits

Data for the calculation of the accuracy of MEPs for prediction of motor deficits were present in 12 studies [2, 11–13, 17, 19, 26, 27, 33, 38, 41, 42]. Mean monitorability rate was 98.44%. Alarm cases were present in 19.7% of the monitored population. After preventive measures, MEPs in 35.68% of patients improved above the alarm threshold, further considered rescue cases, while 14.35% experienced permanent MEPs decline at the end of surgery. As a result, 6.56% experienced motor decline immediately after surgery, with a 1.97% rate of permanent new motor deficits (evaluated at least 3 months after discharge). The detailed characteristics are presented in Supplementary Data 4.

The mean calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of MEPs were 70.12%, 93.86%, 65.04%, and 96.92% for predicting short-term (immediate postoperative) motor deficits, respectively. In comparison, these values reached 90.55%, 91.87%, 24.53%, and 99.6% for predicting long-term motor deficits, respectively (Supplementary Data 5). Analysis of AUROC values indicated that MEPs significantly predicted the occurrence of both short-term (AUROC = 0.72; p < 0.0001) and long-term (AUROC = 0.91; p = 0.006) new motor deficits (Fig. 2).

Accuracy of mIONM for predicting neurological deficits

Data for the calculation of the accuracy of mIONM for the prediction of neurological deficits were present in 10 studies [2, 7, 12, 13, 17, 26, 37, 38, 43, 46]. Mean monitorability rate was 92.43%. Alarm cases were present in 13.97% of the monitored population. After preventive measures were undertaken, mIONM in 48.04% of patients improved above the alarm threshold, further considered rescue cases, while 9.41% of patients experienced permanent mIONM decline at the end of surgery. As a result, 7.38% experienced neurological decline immediately after surgery, with a 4.65% rate of permanent new neurological deficits (evaluated at least 3 months after discharge). The detailed characteristics are presented in Supplementary Data 10.

The mean calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of mIONM were 56.19%, 99.41%, 91.67%, and 95.21% for predicting short-term (immediate postoperative) neurological deficits, respectively. In comparison, these values reached 75.03%, 92.6%, 29.92%, and 98.64% for predicting long-term neurological deficits, respectively (Supplementary Data 11). Risk factors of postoperative motor deficits

Additionally, to define whether any tumor-related factors influence the occurrence of meaningful intraoperative alerts, the individual patient data were extracted from 7 studies with sufficiently detailed data [12, 26, 27, 33, 38, 41, 42]. Due to the limited data, merely MEPs alerts and their association with short-term motor deficits could have been evaluated, leaving further SSEPs, D-wave, and mIONM alerts to be studied in the future. To determine the differences in deficit impending TP alerts, patients were grouped into the TP MEPs alert group and the non-TP MEPs alert group. There were no intergroup differences in alert rate in terms of tumor level (cervical, thoracic, thoraco-lumbar, lumbar, and sacral), tumor axial location (anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and posterior), and pathology (schwannoma and meningioma). No further tumor pathology subtypes (such as ependymoma, neurofibroma, paraganglioma, metastases, or dermoid cyst) could have been incorporated into the analyses due to the scarcity of the data.

Quality assessment of included studies

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the role of MEPs, SSEPs, D-wave, and mIONM in predicting both short-term and long-term postoperative motor, sensory, or neurological decline in patients undergoing IONM-guided IDEM tumor surgery. Immediate postoperative evaluation point was chosen as short-term observation, while long-term neurological assessment was performed at least 3 months after discharge. Several included studies presented multiple neurological evaluation points with a meaningful number of patients improving between immediate postoperative time and the moment of discharge. While our study aimed to show the accuracy of IONM in predicting any neurological deterioration, we intended to capture all deficits that appeared after IONM-guided surgery. Therefore, delaying the evaluation point would have falsely underestimated the ability of IONM to indicate a new neurological decline. Concurrently, our strategy still left some space for underestimation. Without a wake-up test, it is impossible to define subtle deficits that can result even from transient IONM potential decline [6].

While some reports excluded rescue cases from accuracy analyses [11, 19, 27, 41, 45], we included them either as TN or FN. In fact, even transient IONM potential changes should either be classified as TN (when potentials recovered above the alarm threshold, leaving patient neurologically intact) or FN (when despite IONM potential recovery, postoperative motor/sensory/neurological deficit was observed). Omitting rescue cases in accuracy analysis may not reflect the true ability of one modality to predict postoperative deficits. Similarly, the term “rescue rate” applied in our paper gathered patients that presented IONM potential recovery above the predefined alarm threshold, divided by the total number of patients with IONM potential decline (permanent and transient). That definition differs from that reported in previously published papers, which encompassed patients with IONM potential recovery divided by TP (permanent IONM decline with subsequent neurological decline) and patients with transient IONM changes [11, 41]. In our opinion, the lack of FP cases in the calculation denominator falsely overestimates the rescue rate. The authors reckon that the rescue rate should indicate the number of patients with IONM potential decline who can benefit from preventive measures, giving feedback on their efficacy in preventing neurological sequelae. According to our analysis, preventive measures (such as temporal suspension of surgical maneuvers, irrigation with warm saline, blood pressure elevation, body temperature elevation, changes in anesthesia depth, shifting surgical focus, reduction of traction, and steroids [11, 19, 41, 46] administration) led to IONM potential improvement in 20.69%−50%, depending on the applied IONM modality. Although we cannot unequivocally correlate the application of preventive measures with neurological decline prevention, no prospective studies could be performed to define their efficacy since that strategy would be ethically questionable. Notably, Yoshida et al. [45] reported that 30.8% of IONM declines in IDEM tumors were unrelated to surgical maneuvers. While some of these events can be linked to anesthesiologic factors, strict cooperation between neurophysiologists, neurosurgeons, and neuroanesthesiologists is recommended.

Our analysis demonstrated that MEPs and mIONM represent the sole modalities in IDEM tumor surgery that achieved statistically significant accuracy for predicting motor and neurological deficits, respectively, in both short-term and long-term observation. Contrarily, SSEPs showed merely 64.29% and 47.50% sensitivity in the short- and long-term observation, respectively, for predicting sensory deficits, rendering this modality highly inaccurate in IDEM tumor surgery. Notably, we advocate caution in interpreting the SSEPs accuracy values in predicting sensory deficits. Considerable methodological drawbacks present in the analyzed literature, such as the lack of precise determination of the type of sensory deficits preclude reliable calculation of the actual accuracy of that modality. Induction of SSEPs reflects the integrity of the dorsal somatosensory system, which is clinically responsible for conveying information on discriminative touch, vibration, and proprioception [24]. Conversely, crude touch, pain, and temperature sensations are conveyed by the anterolateral somatosensory system, whose integrity is not reflected by SSEPs. The observed low sensitivity values of SSEPs for predicting sensory deficits reflect the high number of FN events, which may be misinterpreted without a thorough neurological examination (including proprioception and vibration determination). Moreover, clinicians should be aware of the possibility of position-related sensory deficits that should be differentiated from those anatomically related to the operated spinal level. Therefore, we strongly encourage researchers to define their strategy precisely in correlating SSEPs alarms with the type of sensory deficits to optimize diagnostic accuracy.

D-wave monitoring has an established role in IMSCT surgery. Its efficacy, in concordance with MEPs monitoring, has been extensively studied, making D-wave monitoring an indispensable tool in preventing long-term motor deficits after IMSCT removal [1, 8, 21, 22, 29, 40]. Encouraged by the efficacy of D-wave monitoring in IMSCTs, some reports on the implementation of that technique in IDEM tumors appeared [7, 8, 12, 13, 20]. In 2018, Ghadirpour et al. [13] reported 100% sensitivity, 98.4% specificity, 66.67% PPV, and 100% NPV of D-wave monitoring in IDEM tumor surgery, with significantly higher accuracy of this modality in predicting postoperative motor deficits compared to both SSEPs and MEPs [13].

Despite the promising evidence on the clinical efficacy of D-wave monitoring in IDEM tumor surgery, our meta-analysis revealed that this modality did not appear sufficiently accurate to reflect short-term (AUROC = 0.83, p = 0.1094) and long-term (AUROC = 0) motor deficits.

When D-wave and MEPs monitoring are combined, the presence of MEPs amplitude decline or abolishment, with concurrent D-wave stability, does not indicate permanent motor decline [7, 8, 12, 13, 20, 28, 29]. The role of D-wave is to guide surgical resection until its amplitude decrease exceeds 50% compared to baseline potential voltage [28, 29]. Considering the ability of the D-wave to be continuously monitored, the surgery should be stopped before the D-wave amplitude exceeds > 50% drop. Therefore, while TP events would be valuable from the statistical point of view (to define sensitivity and PPV), the clinical perspective should opt for preventing TP alerts while protecting the patient from permanent motor deficits.

Concurrently, NPVs of 98.96% and 99.6% for predicting short-term and long-term motor deficits, respectively, indicate high reliability of maintained D-wave amplitude (above 50% of the baseline level) in preventing long-term motor impairment. Although D-wave monitoring failed to achieve significant accuracy values for predicting both short- and long-term motor deficits, the authors strongly encourage clinicians to support their IDEM tumor surgery with mIONM consisting of both MEPs and D-wave monitoring. However, considering the limited number of studies included in the analysis, the data should be analyzed cautiously.

Additionally, we aimed to define the risk factors for TP IONM alerts. Due to the highly limited individual patient data, no extensive analyses could have been performed. The data were extracted from 7 studies with sufficiently detailed data [12, 26, 27, 33, 38, 41, 42]. Moreover, solely MEPs alerts could have been analyzed to seek potential intraoperative event risk factors. Consequently, we found that neither specific tumor level, tumor axial location, nor tumor histology increased the risk of TP intraoperative MEPs alerts. Some previous reports indicated that thoracic tumor level and anterolateral tumor location are at risk of significant IONM alarms [13, 19, 27], which, however, has not been proven in our study. Considering the very limited data that were considered when calculating risk factors, particular caution is needed in transferring these data to clinical practice. We strongly believe that more data is required to address that topic thoroughly.

Limitations

Despite providing evidence on the efficacy of IONM in IDEM tumor surgery, the present study has several shortcomings, such as the retrospective character of most included studies. Given that the implementation of IONM in IDEM tumor surgery remains center-dependent, with no guidelines or recommendations supporting its routine application, the patient allocation to IONM-supported surgery might have caused a selection bias. Additionally, most tumors in the analyzed cohort were located posteriorly to the spinal cord. Conversely, anteriorly or anterolaterally located neoplasms are at greater risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury (most commonly by its compression that is being made to get access to the tumor); therefore, the accuracy values of IONM modalities of the analyzed cohort might have been underestimated. On the other hand, the predominance of thoracic tumor location (50%) might have overestimated the alarm cases in IDEM tumor surgery since the thoracic spinal cord is known for low blood supply, being at significant risk of iatrogenic ischemia [45]. Notably, while the potential alarm threshold remains relatively established for both D-wave and SSEPs monitoring, there is no consensus on the optimal threshold of MEPs amplitude decline that may indicate potential motor deficits. Among 12 studies [2, 11–13, 17, 19, 26, 27, 33, 38, 41, 42] included in MEPs accuracy determination, 4 used > 50% MEPs amplitude decline [17, 26, 33, 42], 4 used > 70% MEPs amplitude decline [11, 19, 27, 41], one used > 80% MEPs amplitude decline [38], and 3 used permanent MEPs amplitude decline [2, 12, 13]. Considering the complexity of our results, additional determination of the most accurate MEPs alarm threshold exceeded the scope of the current study. Therefore, the accuracy of MEPs presented in our research is based on varying MEPs alarm thresholds, representing the overall accuracy of MEPs towards predicting motor deficits. However, to precisely determine the actual accuracy of MEPs, further study focused on comparing MEPs alarm thresholds remains vital.

Furthermore, a wide range of evaluation time points was observed, with varying long-term follow-up, ranging from 3 months [7, 20, 41] to almost 14.8 months [38]. It has already been indicated that the most prominent recovery of neurological function occurs within 3–6 months postoperatively, while significant neurological status improvement is unlikely to happen after that time [5, 39].

Moreover, the lack of reported clinician blinding to the intraoperatively observed changes in IONM could have induced judgment bias, potentially overestimating the modalities’ accuracy values. Notably, the absence of a control group in all studies besides the one designed by Cofano et al. [7] precluded any quantitative comparison of the motor/sensory/neurological deficits rate between patients treated with and without IONM.

Conclusions

MEPs and mIONM represent the sole modalities with sufficient accuracy to predict motor and neurological deficits, respectively. Contrarily, the low sensitivity of SSEPs for predicting sensory deficits undermines its efficacy in IDEM tumor surgery. Although D-wave failed to achieve enough accuracy to predict motor deficits reliably, its role in preventing permanent motor deficits advocates for its application in the clinical setting. A high rescue rate after the administration of preventive measures supports strict cooperation between neurosurgeons, neurophysiologists, and neuroanesthesiologists to maximalize the preventive IONM effect. Although the predictive value of IONM has been determined, the lack of a control group in most studies included in the meta-analysis precludes forming any conclusions on the therapeutical benefit of IONM.
