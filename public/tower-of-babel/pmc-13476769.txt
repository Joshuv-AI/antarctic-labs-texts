Association between adiposity indices and lower urinary tract symptoms suggestive of benign prostatic hyperplasia in middle-aged and older Chinese men: a cross-sectional analysis of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Yanping Zhu, Xiaohui Wang, Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear editor,

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and its associated lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) impose a substantial and growing burden on ageing men in China, with a reported prevalence of 10.66% among men aged ≥50 years [1]. Despite this epidemiological burden, the contribution of modifiable metabolic and anthropometric risk factors to LUTS/BPH in Chinese men remains incompletely characterised. Recent investigations in this journal have highlighted the importance of adiposity indices for health outcomes in older adults, including the body roundness index for cardiometabolic multimorbidity [2] and the weight-adjusted waist index for frailty [3]. We therefore examined associations between adiposity indices and LUTS/BPH using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [4].

CHARLS is a nationally representative, biennial longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged ≥45 years, employing a four-stage stratified cluster sampling design [4]. We analysed male participants from the 2015 wave, excluding those with missing data on the outcome, anthropometric measurements, or blood pressure. The primary outcome was self-reported physician diagnosis of prostate illness (variable da029), coded as a binary variable, consistent with prior CHARLS-based research [1]. Exposures included waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), all measured by trained fieldworkers using standardised protocols. Central obesity was defined as WC > 90 cm per Chinese-specific International Diabetes Federation criteria. Three logistic regression models were constructed for each exposure: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (age-adjusted), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for age, BMI, systolic blood pressure, current smoking, and alcohol consumption). Continuous exposures were standardised. Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 (statsmodels). The study was exempt from additional ethical review as CHARLS data are publicly available and de-identified.

The analytical sample comprised 6,730 men (mean age 61.9 ± 10.2 years), of whom 830 (12.3%) reported LUTS/BPH. Men with LUTS/BPH were significantly older (64.8 ± 9.7 vs 60.3 ± 9.5 years; P < 0.001), had greater WC (88.5 ± 10.1 vs 86.2 ± 10.5 cm; P < 0.001), higher BMI (23.9 ± 3.5 vs 23.4 ± 3.6 kg/m²; P < 0.001), and higher prevalence of central obesity (44.8% vs 35.1%; P < 0.001) and overweight/obesity (48.2% vs 40.2%; P < 0.001). Current smoking (47.3% vs 60.4%; P < 0.001) and alcohol consumption (38.7% vs 48.6%; P < 0.001) were lower in the LUTS/BPH group. Systolic blood pressure and hypertension did not differ significantly.

In fully adjusted models, central obesity was independently associated with LUTS/BPH (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.15–1.73; P = 0.001), as was overweight/obesity (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.04–1.66; P = 0.024), whereas general obesity (BMI ≥ 28) was not (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.60–1.10; P = 0.175), suggesting that the overweight range rather than frank obesity may drive the association in this population. Each 1-SD increment in WC (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.20–1.39), BMI (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.16–1.33), and WHtR (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.15–1.33) was associated with higher odds of LUTS/BPH (all P < 0.001). Dose-response analysis by WC quartile showed a stepwise increase in prevalence from 9.2% (Q1, ≤79 cm) to 15.1% (Q4, >94 cm), with ORs of 1.66 (95% CI 1.22–2.27; P = 0.001) and 1.57 (95% CI 1.07–2.30; P = 0.020) for Q3 and Q4 versus Q1 (P for trend < 0.001). Age-stratified analyses showed consistent positive associations in men aged 45–59 (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.11–2.32; P = 0.011) and ≥70 years (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.08–2.31; P = 0.019), with a non-significant association in the 60–69 group (P for interaction = 0.581). The finding that abdominal adiposity is independently associated with LUTS/BPH is biologically plausible. Visceral adipose tissue promotes chronic low-grade inflammation via elevated circulating interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor-α, and C-reactive protein, which may stimulate prostatic stromal proliferation [5]. Hyperinsulinaemia secondary to visceral obesity activates the sympathetic nervous system and increases adrenergic tone in the bladder neck and prostatic smooth muscle, contributing to both storage and voiding symptoms [5]. Additionally, adipose-derived oestrogen excess alters the androgen-to-oestrogen ratio permissive to prostatic growth [6]. The similar performance of WHtR and WC, both showing stronger associations than BMI ≥ 28, may suggest that central fat distribution is more relevant to prostate-related metabolic processes than total body adiposity. This observation aligns with recent findings in this journal that waist-based indices may better capture metabolically relevant adiposity than BMI alone [2,3].

The present analysis has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. LUTS/BPH was ascertained by self-report rather than urodynamic evaluation, which may introduce misclassification. The 2015 CHARLS release did not include blood-based biomarkers, precluding metabolic syndrome scoring. Residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as physical activity and medication use cannot be excluded.

In conclusion, central adiposity—measured by WC and WHtR—is independently and dose-dependently associated with LUTS/BPH in Chinese men aged ≥45 years. These findings suggest that waist circumference may be a useful marker for identifying men at higher odds of LUTS/BPH. However, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether weight management targeting abdominal obesity could reduce LUTS/BPH burden.
