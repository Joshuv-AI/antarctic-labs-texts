Successful Surgical Tricuspid Valve Repair of Severe Recurrent Tricuspid Regurgitation After Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Andreas Winkler, Katja Gansterer, Elke Boxhammer, Matthias Neuner, Michael Kirnbauer, Thomas Wuppinger, Christian Dinges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter edge-to-edge repair has emerged as a minimally invasive option for patients at high surgical risk. Residual/recurrent tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is associated with high morbidity and mortality.Case SummaryA 79-year-old woman developed recurrent severe TR after initially successful TriClip implantation. Symptoms progressed despite optimal medical therapy. As redo tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) was not feasible, the patient underwent surgical intervention. Following careful clip removal, surgical tricuspid valve (TV) repair was successfully performed. At 7-month follow-up, echocardiography confirmed a stable repair with mild TR, and the patient reported substantial symptomatic improvement.DiscussionThis case shows that surgical TV repair can be a safe and effective treatment option for recurrent TR after T-TEER. With the increasing use of transcatheter therapies, awareness of surgical bailout options will become increasingly important.Take-Home MessageSurgical TV repair may represent a safe and effective strategy in selected patients with recurrent TR after T-TEER.Key words: recurrent regurgitation, surgical tricuspid valve repair, TriClip, tricuspid regurgitation, T-TEER

History of Presentation

The 77-year-old woman underwent tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) for symptomatic severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR). Echocardiography showed a myxomatous tricuspid valve (TV) with probable prolapse of the posterior leaflet and annular dilation (43 mm). The right ventricle (RV) function was reduced. (Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). The TriScore was 3 (Supplemental Figure 1). Retrospective assessment using established prediction models for T-TEER success yielded a EuroTR Risk Score of 2.7 (Supplemental Figure 2) and a GLIDE score of 2. At her request, the patient sought interventional repair at an external specialized center. T-TEER (TriClip, XTW) was performed without complications and resulted in a reduction to moderate TR. According to the operative report, 1 clip was positioned anteroseptally and 2 clips anteroposteriorly. Over the following months, the patient’s symptoms improved.

Ten months later, the patient presented with a renewed decline in exercise capacity, reporting that her functional status had returned to the preintervention level. Furthermore, she reported frequent episodes of dizziness. On Holter monitoring, bradycardic episodes were documented in a patient with a known history of permanent atrial fibrillation. Echocardiography (Figure 1, Video 4, Video 5, Video 11, Video 12) revealed severe recurrent TR with presumably single leaflet device attachment. The clip positions were visualized as follows: 1 clip was located posteroseptally (Video 6), 1 anteroseptally (Video 7), and a third below the annular plane in the anteroposterior region (Video 8). Either an anterior-posterior clip was intentionally placed here, or an additional posteroseptal clip has detached from the septal leaflet. The exact cause of this unusual clip position could not be reproduced post hoc. The right ventricle was mildly dilated with impaired function. Holosystolic flow reversal in the hepatic veins was observed. A repeat procedure was, in our view, not feasible because of the inability to position an additional clip given the 3 clips in situ, the artifact-limited imaging, and the lack of a clear edge-to-edge target. Therefore, a conservative therapy was decided. The patient herself was very reluctant regarding a repeat procedure. A leadless pacemaker was implanted for bradycardic episodes with successful right ventricular deployment under fluoroscopic guidance.

The symptoms did not improve over the following year. Diuretic agent therapy with spironolactone was maintained, and furosemide was added.

Despite optimized medical therapy, the patient developed progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class III) with markedly reduced exercise tolerance and occasional mild peripheral edema. Diuretic agent therapy was reduced because of hypotension and orthostatic symptoms.

Past Medical History

Of relevance in the patient's medical history are permanent atrial fibrillation, a persistent foramen ovale, mild pulmonary hypertension, and a chronic pericardial effusion.

Differential Diagnosis

Since the recurrent TR was initially borderline severe, the comorbidities (bradycardia, pericardial effusion, and constriction) were evaluated for their contribution to the symptoms.

Investigations

Echocardiography demonstrated severe TR (annular diameter of 44 mm) with unchanged clip position compared with the previous description. Additional findings included a mildly dilated RV with reduced function, preserved left ventricular function, and mild mitral regurgitation due to myxomatous bileaflet prolapse. The persistent foramen ovale (left-to-right shunt, Qp/Qs 1.33) and the pericardial effusion without hemodynamic significance appeared unchanged compared with the previous examinations. The inferior vena cava was dilated without respiratory collapse.

Right heart catheterization showed mild pulmonary hypertension (systolic pulmonary artery pressure 43 mm Hg), normal cardiac index (2.76 L/min/m2), a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 16 mm Hg, and an elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (3.9 WU).

Because of progressive symptoms, limited suitability for redo T-TEER, and limited medical treatment options, the now 79-year-old patient was evaluated for surgical TV intervention.

Considering the patient's stable condition and low-to-intermediate surgical risk (Tricuspid Regurgitation In-Hospital Mortality Score 4 (Supplemental Figure 3), European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II 1.7), the interdisciplinary heart team, in agreement with the patient, decided to proceed with surgery.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Because of the impaired right ventricular function, levosimendan was administered (12.5 mg over 42.5 hours) before surgery.

The patient underwent surgery via median sternotomy (aortic cross-clamp time: 53 minutes, cardiopulmonary bypass time: 81 minutes, total surgical time: 179 minutes). Median sternotomy was chosen to ensure optimal exposure given the uncertain extent of clip adhesion and the presence and extent of leaflet damage. After right atriotomy, the TV was inspected. One clip was securely attached to the anterior and septal leaflet, and 1 clip to the posterior and septal leaflet. The third clip was attached to the posterior leaflet and grasped the subvalvular apparatus, specifically the chordae tendineae in the anteroposterior region (Figure 2). We could not say with certainty whether this clip was intentionally or rather inadvertently placed there, or if the clip detached from the septal leaflet, as we found something that might have been a tear in the septal leaflet.

Intraoperative Picture of the Tricuspid Valve (Surgical View)

One anteroseptal and 1 posteroseptal clip are securely attached to the leaflet tissue. A third clip was attached to the posterior leaflet and grasped the subvalvular apparatus in the anteroposterior region. The clip may have detached from the septal leaflet, as we found a finding suggestive of a tear in the septal leaflet (arrow).

All clips were removed carefully with particular emphasis on not damaging the leaflet tissue and the subvalvular apparatus (Figure 3, Videos 13 and 14). Therefore, we used forceps and a small vascular clamp to open the clips, and we carefully blunt-dissected the valve tissue off the clips. A ring annuloplasty with an incomplete ring (Medtronic Contour 3-dimensional, 32 mm) was performed. Because of a residual insufficiency at the anteroseptal commissure with prolapsing of the anterior leaflet, we performed a commissuroplasty. Since the patient already had an implanted pacemaker (ventricular pacing >95%), we had no concerns regarding the proximity to the conduction system. Intraoperative valve testing confirmed satisfactory valve competence, obviating the need for additional repair techniques. Additionally, a persistent foramen ovale closure was performed using direct suturing along with a left atrial appendage closure using an AtriClip. A pericardial window was created in the pleural cavity bilaterally. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed an excellent result of TV repair with trivial residual regurgitation (Figure 4, Video 9).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was extubated and weaned off catecholamines on the day of surgery, transferred to the ward on postoperative day 2, and discharged on postoperative day 10 in good condition. Predischarge echocardiography demonstrated mild TR.

At 3-month follow-up, the patient reported substantial symptomatic improvement (NYHA functional class II) and improved functional capacity without rehospitalization. Echocardiography demonstrated persistent mild TR and stable valve repair (Figure 5, Video 10). These findings remained stable at 7 months with no more than trace-to-mild TR and further clinical improvement to NYHA functional class I-II.

Discussion

Functional TR remains a therapeutic challenge, particularly in patients with advanced right-sided heart failure. Current guidelines recommend surgical intervention primarily in patients undergoing left-sided valve surgery or in selected patients with isolated severe TR who are symptomatic or have right ventricular dilatation/function deterioration but without severe left ventricle/RV dysfunction or severe pulmonary hypertension.1 Patients are frequently referred for surgery at a late stage, when advanced RV dysfunction and/or additional organ failure have already developed. Thus, isolated TV surgery is often regarded as a high-risk procedure. Transcatheter therapies like T-TEER have emerged as a minimally invasive option for patients at high surgical risk. This procedure was found to be feasible, safe, and effective in reducing TR severity in patients with symptomatic moderate or severe TR, with favorable short- and mid-term outcomes including improvement in functional status and quality of life up to 3 years after the procedure.2, 3, 4, 5 Longterm results are lacking.

Residual or recurrent TR remains a relevant clinical challenge and has received increasing attention as it is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates.6 Although recurrent regurgitation after T-TEER is not uncommon, experience with repeat interventions is limited and primarily reported in case studies including transcatheter TV replacement.7 As redo T-TEER was not feasible in our case, medical therapy was initially chosen as the treatment approach. As the patient’s symptoms progressed despite receiving optimal pharmacological treatment, the case was discussed in the heart team, and the patient was found to be eligible for cardiac surgery. Given the patient's acceptable operative risk and the feasibility of surgical valve repair despite prior T-TEER, alternative transcatheter treatment options were not pursued. Valve reconstruction was the intended primary approach. In case the valve had been excessively damaged with insufficient remaining leaflet tissue, we would have replaced the valve using a self-modeled extracellular matrix cylinder. The surgical procedure was successful, and the TV was repaired with an excellent result. An incomplete annuloplasty ring was implanted, consistent with our institutional standard for TV repair. Although future transcatheter valve-in-ring therapies are an increasingly important consideration in lifetime management and complete annuloplasty rings may facilitate certain future interventions, the primary objective in this case was to achieve a durable native valve repair rather than to optimize for potential future transcatheter intervention.

It is certainly a matter for discussion whether the patient should have been offered cardiac surgery at the initial stage instead of T-TEER since the patient would have been suitable for surgery at that time.

Conclusions

Surgical TV repair may represent a feasible and safe treatment option for patients with severe recurrent TR after T-TEER and can achieve excellent early clinical and echocardiographic outcomes. Comprehensive anatomical evaluation, multidisciplinary decision-making and thorough patient selection are essential to guide individualized therapy and to increase the likelihood of therapeutic success.

With the increasing use of transcatheter therapies, awareness of surgical bailout options will become increasingly important.
