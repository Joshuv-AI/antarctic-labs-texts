Sharp estimates for the Fourier transform of surface-carried measures and maximal operators associated with hypersurfaces in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} with vanishing Gaussian curvature.
Isroil A. Ikromov, Gayrat Toshpulatov

Abstract
In this paper, we study problems related to harmonic analysis on hypersurfaces in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} with zero Gaussian curvature and given as graphs of polynomial functions. We derive sharp uniform estimates with respect to the direction of frequencies for the Fourier transform of measures supported on such hypersurfaces. Additionally, we study the L p L^{p} -boundedness problem of maximal operators associated with hypersurfaces. We determine the exact value of the boundedness exponent in terms of the heights of these hypersurfaces.



Keywords:
Fourier transform
of surface carried measure, maximal operator, hypersurface, oscillatory integral, Newton polyhedron, adapted coordinate system.

42B25, 42B10.

Introduction

Let
S
be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and let
\rho d\sigma
be a surface-carried measure on
S,
where
d\sigma
denotes the surface measure on
S
and
\rho\geq 0
is a smooth density with a compact support. The Fourier transform of
\rho d\sigma
is defined by

\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{S}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\rho(x)d\sigma(x),\,\,\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

One of the fundamental problems in harmonic analysis is to determine the exact asymptotic behavior of
\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)
as
|\xi|
tends to infinity. The applications range from number theory to partial differential equations, see
[
35
,
36
]
.

Using a partition of unity argument, we can assume that
\rho
is concentrated in a sufficiently small neighborhood of a fixed point
x^{0}\in S.

It is known that the asymptotic behavior of
\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)
is
invariant under translations and rotations of the ambient space. Hence, we may replace
the surface
S
by any suitable image under a Euclidean motion of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.
We may thus assume
x^{0}=(0,...,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and that
S
is the graph

S=\{(x_{1},...,x_{n},\phi(x_{1},...,x_{n}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\,x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in U\}

of a smooth function
\phi
defined in a sufficiently small neighborhood
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the origin such that

\phi(0)=0,\,\,\,\,\nabla\phi(0)=0.

This let us write
\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)
as an oscillatory integral

\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\xi_{n+1}\phi(x)+\xi_{1}x_{1}+...+\xi_{n}x_{n})}\eta(x)dx,

(1)

where

\eta(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\rho(x_{1},...,x_{n},\phi(x))\sqrt{1+|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(U).

If
\xi_{1}=...=\xi_{n}=0
and
\xi_{n+1}=:\lambda,
then (
1
) can be written in the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\lambda\phi(x)}\eta(x)dx.

(2)

In one dimension
n=1
, the asymptotic behavior of the integral (
2
) as the parameter
\lambda
tends to infinity is determined by the maximum order of vanishing of the function
\phi
at its critical points, see
[
35
]
.
In higher dimensions, this problem is substantially difficult. This is connected with the multiplicity and complexity of the critical points of
\phi
that occur when the dimension exceeds one. It is well-known that the asymptotic behavior of the oscillatory integral (
1
) does not change under a smooth change of variables. Arnold conjectured in
[
1
]
that the asymptotic behavior of the oscillatory integral (
1
) is completely determined by the Newton polyhedron of
\phi
in a so-called
adapted
coordinate system (see the next section for the definition of the Newton polyhedron and others). More precisely, with
\phi

we can associate the so-called
height

h(\phi)

defined in terms of the Newton polyhedron of
\phi
when represented in a smooth coordinate
system near the origin. An important property of this height
h(\phi)
is that
it is invariant under local smooth changes of coordinates fixing the origin.
Arnold expected that the leading asymptotic behavior or the main part of the asymptotic development would be determined by the height
h(\phi)
of
\phi
.
Later, when
\phi
is real-analytic, Varchenko
[
38
]
proved that Arnold’s conjecture holds true for (
2
) in two dimensions
n=2.
In particular, Varchenko gave sufficient conditions for the adaptedness of a given coordinate system, proved the existence of an adapted coordinate system for analytic functions
\phi
without multiple components, and showed that the leading term of the asymptotic expansion of (
2
) can determined by the height
h(\phi)
and the dimension of the principal face in an adapted coordinate system. Karpushkin
[
25
]
proved that
Varchenko’s estimate is stable under sufficiently small analytic perturbations of
\phi.
Varchenko’s result on the existence of an adapted coordinate system has been extended by Ikromov and Müller
[
18
]
to arbitrary real-analytic functions and arbitrary smooth functions of finite type in two dimensions, see also
[
13
]
. Their approach is inspired by the work of Phong, Stein, and Strum
[
31
]
on the Newton polyhedron and oscillatory integrals; see
[
30
]
for applications in the context of oscillatory integral operators.
Ikromov and Müller
[
19
]
also proved Arnold’s conjecture for smooth functions of finite type in two dimensions. In this study, the asymptotic decay rates are sharp.

However, in three dimensions and higher, Arnold’s conjecture does not hold in general. The reason is that Varchenko
[
38
]
constructed an example in three dimensions, for which the leading asymptotics differ from the predicted. Arnold’s conjecture is currently known to hold in any dimension for smooth convex functions of finite line type, see
[
32
,
21
]
.
For analytic functions, the asymptotic behavior of oscillatory integrals can be studied based on Hironaka’s theorem on the resolution of singularities
[
10
,
3
]
, for recent results on this topic, see
[
8
]
.

In three dimensions and higher, the simple description of the asymptotic behavior of integral (
1
) remains unknown for general hypersurfaces. In this paper, we aim to shed light on the three-dimensional case
n=3
. We investigate the asymptotic behavior of the Fourier transform
\widehat{\rho d\sigma}
for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{4}.
Specifically, we focus on hypersurfaces
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
with zero Gaussian curvature and given as graphs of polynomial functions. This means that we consider hypersurfaces of the form

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\,x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in U\},

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a neighborhood of the origin. We assume that
\phi
is a polynomial function satisfying

\phi(0)=0,\,\,\,\nabla\phi(0)=0,

(3)

and

\det(D^{2}\phi(x))=0,\,\,\,\,\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(4)

The condition (
4
) means that the Gaussian curvature of
S
is identically zero. Our results concerning the oscillatory integral (
1
) are the following:

•

In Theorem
3.2
, we show that there is an adapted coordinate system for any polynomial function
\phi
satisfying (
3
) and (
4
).

•

In Theorem
4.1
, we prove Arnold’s conjecture. More precisely, we show that the leading term of the asymptotic expansion of (
1
) is determined by the height
h(\phi)
of
\phi
and the dimension of the principal face of the Newton polyhedron in this adapted coordinate system. Our asymptotic decay rates are sharp.

•

In Corollary
4.2
, we show that our asymptotic estimate on (
1
) is stable under sufficiently small analytic perturbation of
\phi.
This result is analogous to Karpushkin’s result
[
25
]
in two dimensions.

The problem of finding the exact decay rate of the Fourier transform of surface-carried measures is closely related to the
L^{p}
-boundedness problem of maximal operators. As before, let
d\sigma
denote the surface measure on a hypersurface
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and let
\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(S)
be non-negative. We define the averaging operator

\mathcal{A}_{t}f(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{S}f(y-tx)\rho(x)d\sigma(x),\,\,\,\,t>0,\,\,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}).

The maximal operator associated with the hypersurface
S
is given by

\mathcal{M}f(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup_{t>0}|\mathcal{A}_{t}f(y)|,\,\,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}).

We say that the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
if there exists a positive constant
C_{p}
such that

||\mathcal{M}f||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\leq C_{p}||f||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})},\,\,\,\,\forall\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}).

The fundamental question is for which
p\geq 1
the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
.
Since
\rho
has a compact support,
\mathcal{M}
is always bounded on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
. Hence, the question is to determine

p(S)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\{p\geq 1:\mathcal{M}\,\,\text{is bounded on}\,\,L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\text{ for all }0\leq\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(S)\}.

The number
p(S)
is called the
boundedness exponent
of the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
associated with the hypersurface
S
.

Note that, unlike linear transformations, translations do not commute with
dilations, which is why Euclidean motions are not admissible coordinate changes for the
study of the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
. Hence, as it is done in
[
17
,
6
,
7
]
, we require the transversality assumption on
S,
i.e.,
for every
x\in S,
the affine tangent plane
x+T_{x}S
to
S
through
x
does not pass the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.
This transversality assumption is natural in this context. Indeed, if this condition is not met, then the behavior of the maximal operator may change significantly.
For studies without the transversality condition, we refer to
[
40
]
for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
[
26
]
for the multidimensional case.

Using a partition of unity argument, we can assume that
\rho
is concentrated in a sufficiently small neighborhood of a fixed point
x^{0}\in S.
The transversality assumption allows
us to find a linear change of coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
so that in the new coordinates

S
can locally be represented as the graph of a smooth function. The norm of

\mathcal{M}
when acting on
L^{p}
is invariant under such a linear change of coordinates.
More precisely, after applying a suitable linear change of coordinates to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1},
we may
assume that
x^{0}=(0,...,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},
and
S
is given as the
graph

S=\{(x,1+\phi(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\,x\in U\},

(5)

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a small neighborhood of the origin and
\phi
is a smooth function satisfying
\phi(0)=0,

\nabla\phi(0)=0.

Then the maximal operator can be written as

\mathcal{M}f(y)=\sup_{t>0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y_{1}-tx_{1},...,y_{n}-tx_{n},y_{n+1}-t(1+\phi(x)))\eta(x)dx\right|,\,\,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}),

where
\eta(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\rho(x_{1},...,x_{n},1+\phi(x))\sqrt{1+|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(U).

The first fundamental result on the
L^{p}
-boundedness of the maximal operator was due to Stein
[
37
]
, who proved that if
S
is the unit sphere centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
n\geq 2
, then the corresponding spherical maximal operator is bounded on
L^{p}
for every
p>1+{1}/{n}
.
A two-dimensional version of this result was proven by Bourgain
[
5
]
.
The non-vanishing Gaussian curvature of the unit sphere plays a crucial role in these articles. A more general result
[
12
]
is that, if
S
has non-zero
k\geq 2
principal curvatures at each point of
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
, then the corresponding maximal operator is bounded on
L^{p}
for every
p>1+1/k.
Later, a similar result was obtained for the more subtle case
k=1
by Sogge in
[
33
]
. Sogge and Stein
[
34
]
showed that, if the Gaussian curvature of
S
does not vanish to infinite order at any point of
S,
then
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}
for a sufficiently large
p
. Moreover, if
S
is a smooth finite type hypersurface, then the boundedness exponent
p(S)
is finite, see
[
21
]
. However, the exact value of
p(S)
is not determined in these articles, except for the case of
n=1
. Iosevich
[
22
]
studied the maximal operator associated with finite type curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and determined the exact value of
p(S)
.
Iosevich and Sawyer
[
23
]
showed that, if the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p},
then

d(x,H)^{-1/p}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(S),

where
H
is any hyperplane not passing through the origin and
d(x,H)
denotes the distance from
x\in S
to
H
. It was conjectured in
[
23
]
that for
p>2
the above condition is necessary and sufficient for the boundedness of the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
on
L^{p}.

An essentially complete answer to the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
\mathcal{M}
has been given by Ikromov, Müller et al.
[
17
,
6
,
7
]
for hypersurfaces
S
of finite type in
\mathbb{R}^{3}.
In these studies, the boundedness exponent
p(S)
is determined by the height
h(\phi)
of the function
\phi
when
S
has the form in (
5
).
In higher dimensions, there are studies for convex hypersurfaces: Using Schulz’s decomposition of convex functions in
[
32
]
, Iosevich and Sawyer
[
24
]
proved sharp
L^{p}
-estimates for smooth convex hypersurfaces
S
of finite line type for
p>2.
This result has been extended in
[
21
]
for analytic convex hypersurfaces
S
of finite type, which are not necessarily finite line type. However, in dimensions
n\geq 3,
the
L^{p}
-boundedness
of maximal operators associated with non-convex hypersurfaces
S
whose
Gaussian curvature vanishes at some points is still open. In this paper, we aim to contribute the three-dimensional case
n=3
.

As we mentioned before, asymptotic behavior of the Fourier transform of surface-carried measures is intimately connected with the
L^{p}
-boundedness of maximal operators: Greenleaf
[
12
]
proved that, if

|\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{-q}

(6)

for some
C>0
and
q>1/2,
then the maximal operator is bounded on
L^{p}
whenever
p>1+1/(2q).
However, this result is not optimal in general. Stein, for
q=1/2,
and later Iosevich and Sawyer
[
24
]
, for
0<q<1/2,
conjectured that if (
6
) holds for some
0<q\leq 1/2,
then
\mathcal{M}
is bounded for
p>1/q.
This conjecture was proven for curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
[
22
]
, for hypersurfaces of fine type in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
[
17
]
, and for convex hypersurfaces in any dimension in
[
21
]
. This conjecture with
q=1/2
has been recently proven by Oh
[
28
]
for all smooth hypersurfaces.

In this paper, we also study the
L^{p}
-boundedness of the maximal operators associated with hypersurfaces
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
whose Gaussian curvature is identically zero and given as graphs of polynomial functions.
This means that we consider hypersurfaces
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
given as the
graph

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},1+\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\,(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in U\},

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a neighborhood of the origin and
\phi
is a polynomial function satisfying (
3
) and (
4
).
Our main results concerning the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
are the following:

•

In Theorem
5.1
, we prove that the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
for
p>\max\{h(\phi),2\},
where
h(\phi)
is the height of
\phi.

•

In Proposition
5.2
, based on
[
23
]
we prove a necessary condition: if the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
with
\rho(x^{0})>0
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
for
p>1
, then we necessarily have
p>h(\phi)
. In the case
h(\phi)\geq 2,
Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
5.2
yields (see Corollary
5.3
) that the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
with
\rho(x^{0})>0
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
if and only if
p>h(\phi)
, and so
p(S)=h(\phi).

In addition, we obtain confirmation of the Iosevich-Sawyer and Stein-Iosevich-Sawyer conjectures.

•

In Theorem
5.5
, we consider the case
h(\phi)<2
: by assuming that all the principal curvatures of
S
are zero at
x^{0}=(0,0,0,1)\in S
(i.e.,
D^{2}\phi(0)=0
), we prove that the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
with
\rho(x^{0})>0
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
if and only if
p>h(\phi)
, and so
p(S)=h(\phi)
. When two principal curvatures of
S
are non-zero at
x^{0}=(0,0,0,1)\in S
, i.e.,
\mathrm{rank}(D^{2}\phi(0))=2
, using
[
12
]
we show that the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
for
p>3/2.
The case where only one principal curvature is non-zero at
x^{0}=(0,0,0,1)\in S
remains unsolved, and the exact value of the boundedness exponent
p(S)
is unknown, even for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3},
see
[
7
]
for an appropriate geometric conjecture
for this case.

In analogy with
Arnold’s notion of the
singularity index

[
1
,
2
]
, one can define the so-called
(uniform) oscillation index
and
(uniform) contact index
at any point
x^{0}
of a smooth hypersurface
S
(see Section 6 for their definitions). These indexes are related to the Fourier transform of surface-carried measures and the maximal operator associated with
S
. In this paper, we also consider these indexes for a hypersurface
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with zero Gaussian curvature and given as a graph of a polynomial function
\phi
.
Our result concerning these indexes is given in Theorem

, where we show that all of these indexes equal to

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we define the Newton polyhedron of smooth functions and related notions such as adapted coordinate system and height. In Section 3, we study some properties of polynomial functions with vanishing Hessian determinant and prove that there are adapted coordinate systems for them. Our results concerning oscillatory integrals and their proofs are given in Section 4. Section 5 is devoted to the
L^{p}
-boundedness of maximal operators. In Section 6, we define (uniform) oscillation and (uniform) contact indexes of hypersurfaces and determine their exact value. Finally, we discuss possible extensions of our results in Section 7.

2
The Newton polyhedron

Let
\phi
be a smooth real-valued function defined on an open neighborhood
U
of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0.
We consider the associated Taylor series at the origin

\phi(x)\approx\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},

where
\mathbb{N}_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
c_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!...\alpha_{n}!}\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}\phi(0)}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}...\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}
for the multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
|\alpha|\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{1}+...+\alpha_{n}
. The set

\mathcal{T}(\phi)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:\,c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}

is called the
Taylor support
of
\phi
at the origin. We assume
\mathcal{T}(\phi)
is not empty, i.e., the function
\phi
is
finite type
at the origin. The
Newton polyhedron

\mathcal{N}(\phi)
of
\phi
at the origin is defined to be the convex hull of the union

\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}(\phi)}(\alpha+\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),

where
\mathbb{R}_{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\,\,x\geq 0\}.
The
Newton diagram

\mathcal{N}_{d}(\phi)
in the sense of Varchenko
[
38
]
is the union of all compact faces of the Newton polyhedron. Let
(d,...,d)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the coordinate of the intersection of the line
x_{1}=...=x_{n}
and the boundary of the Newton polyhedron. The number
d=d(\phi)
is called the
distance
between the Newton polyhedron and the origin.

The principal face
\pi(\phi)
of the Newton polyhedron of
\phi
is the face of the minimal dimension containing the point
(d,...,d)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
We call the series

\phi_{\text{pr}}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{\alpha\in\pi(\phi)}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}

the
principal part
of
\phi.
In case
\pi(\phi)
is compact,
\phi_{\text{pr}}(x)
is a polynomial; otherwise, we shall consider
\phi_{\text{pr}}(x)
as a formal power series.

We note that the distance between the Newton polyhedron and the origin depends on the chosen
local coordinate system
at the origin. Here by a local coordinate system we mean any smooth diffeomorphic map of a neighborhood of the origin into itself such that the origin is a fixed point. The
height
of the smooth function
\phi
is defined by

h(\phi)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup\{d_{x}\},

(7)

where the supremum is taken over all local smooth coordinates systems
x
at the origin and
d_{x}
is the distance between the Newton polyhedron and the origin in the coordinate system
x
. We say a given smooth local coordinate system is
adapted
to
\phi
if
h(\phi)=d_{x}.

If the supremum in (
7
) is attained by a linear transformation of variables, then the coordinate system obtained by applying this linear transformation of variables is said to be linearly adapted to
\phi
.

In two dimensions, Varchenko
[
38
]
gave sufficient conditions for the adaptness of a given coordinate system and proved the existence of an adapted coordinate system for analytic functions without multiple components. This result is extended in
[
18
]
for arbitrary real-analytic functions and arbitrary smooth functions of finite type. In three dimensions, in general, no analogs of such coordinate
systems exist because an counterexample was constructed by Varchenko
[
38
]
. Adapted coordinate systems exist for smooth convex functions in any dimension and it is obtained by applying linear change of variables to the original orthogonal coordinate system, see
[
32
,
21
]
.

In the following we state some results of
[
18
]
concerning the adaptness of a given coordinate system for a smooth function in two dimensions. If the principal face of the Newton polyhedron
\mathcal{N}(\phi)
is a compact edge in two dimensions, then it lies on a unique line
\kappa_{1}t_{1}+\kappa_{2}t_{2}=1
with
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0.
The principal part
\phi_{\text{pr}}
of
\phi
is
\kappa-
homogeneous of degree one, i.e.,

\phi_{\text{pr}}(t^{\kappa_{1}}x_{1},t^{\kappa_{2}}x_{2})=t\phi_{\text{pr}}(x_{1},x_{2}),\,\,\,\,\forall\,t>0,\,\,\,\forall\,x\in U.

\kappa\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
is called the principal weight and
d_{h}(\phi_{\text{pr}})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1/(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})
is called the homogeneous distance. We denote by
m(\phi_{\text{pr}})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\text{ord}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\phi_{\text{pr}}

the maximal order of vanishing of
\phi_{\text{pr}}
along the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
centered at the origin.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\phi
be a smooth real-valued function defined on an open neighborhood
U
of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0.
The given coordinate system
x
is adapted to
\phi
if and only if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

The principal face
\pi(\phi)
of the Newton polyhedron is a compact edge, and
m(\phi_{\mathrm{pr}})\leq d(\phi).

(ii)

\pi(\phi)
is a vertex.

(iii)

\pi(\phi)
is an unbounded edge.

Moreover, in case of
(i)
we have
h(\phi)=h(\phi_{\mathrm{pr}})=d_{h}(\phi_{\mathrm{pr}}).

The proof of Proposition
2.1
can be found in
[
18
]
.

3
Adapted coordinate system

In this section we prove that there is an adapted coordinate system for any polynomial function whose Hessian determinant is identically zero.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial function. Let
\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}
denote the Hessian determinant

\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{vmatrix}\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{1}}\phi(x)&...&\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{n}}\phi(x)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\partial^{2}_{x_{n}x_{1}}\phi(x)&...&\partial^{2}_{x_{n}x_{n}}\phi(x)\end{vmatrix},\,\,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

When
\phi
is a homogeneous polynomial, Hesse
[
15
,
16
]
claimed in 1851 that the equality

\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}=0

holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if, after a suitable linear transformation of variables,
\phi
depends on at most
n-1
variables. The
if
part in this claim is trivial and the
only if
part is not obvious. One can easily show that this claim holds true for quadratic forms. It is also true for ternary and quaternary cubic forms, see
[
29
]
. However, in 1876, Gordan and Noether
[
11
,
27
,
39
]
showed that Hesse’s proof in
[
15
,
16
]
is not correct in general. More precisely, they showed that Hesse’s claim holds for
n\leq 4
and constructed counterexamples for
n\geq 5.
For non-homogeneous polynomials the following result has been obtained in
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial such that
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0.
Assume

\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}=0

holds for all
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then
then there exists an invertible matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
such that
\phi(Ax)
either depends on at most two variable
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
or has the form

\phi(Ax)=Q_{1}(x_{1})+Q_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+Q_{3}(x_{1})x_{3},

(8)

where
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomial functions.

We use this algebraic result to prove the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-trivial polynomial function such that
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0.
Assume

\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}=0

holds for all
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then there exists an adapted coordinate system to
\phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}.
Moreover, let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be the matrix given in Theorem
3.1
. If
\phi(Ax)
only depends on one variable, then the given coordinate system
x
is adapted. If it depends on two variables, then an adapted coordinate system can be found by either
y_{1}=x_{1}
,
y_{2}=x_{2}-\psi(x_{1})
or
y_{1}=x_{2}
,
y_{2}=x_{1}-\psi(x_{2})
, where
\psi
represents a real-valued analytic function. If
\phi(Ax)
has a form as in (
8
), then an adapted coordinate system can be obtained by applying a linear transformation of variables.

Proof.

We divide the proof into two parts.

Part 1:
Assume
\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

(a)
Let
\phi(Ax)
depend on only one variable
x_{1}.
Then we can write

\phi(Ax)=x_{1}^{\nu}Q(x_{1}),

where
Q
is a polynomial function with
Q(0)\neq 0
and
\nu\geq 2
is a positive integer number. Then the Newton polyhedron of
\phi(Ax)
is

\{(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}:\,t_{1}\geq\nu\}

and the distance
d_{x}=\nu.

We claim that the given coordinate system
x
is adapted. To prove that we consider any smooth local coordinate system
y
at the origin given by

\displaystyle x_{1}=\varphi_{1}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}),

\displaystyle x_{2}=\varphi_{2}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}),

\displaystyle x_{3}=\varphi_{3}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}).

(9)

Let
d_{y}
denote the distance between the Newton polyhedron and the origin in the coordinate system
y.
By definition, the coordinate system

is adapted if
d_{y}\leq d_{x}
for any smooth local coordinate system
y.
We will prove that this inequality holds.
Since the Jacobian of
\varphi
must be non-zero at the origin, by flipping coordinates
y_{1},

y_{2},
and
y_{3},
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
\partial_{y_{i}}\varphi_{i}(0,0,0)\neq 0
for all

In this new coordinate system
\phi(Ax)
equals

\Phi(y)=\varphi_{1}^{\nu}(y)Q(\varphi_{1}^{\nu}(y)).

Since
\partial_{y_{1}}\varphi_{1}(0)\neq 0
and
Q(0)\neq 0,
by product rule,
(\nu,0,0)
belongs to the Newton polyhedron of
\Phi(y).
This means the distance
d_{y}
can not be greater than
d_{x}=\nu.
Therefore, the given coordinate system
x
is adapted.

(b)
Let
\phi(Ax)
depend on two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}.
Then the problem of finding an adapted coordinate system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
reduces to find an adapted coordinate system in
\mathbb{R}^{2}.
If the given coordinate system
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is not adapted, then
[
18
, Theorem 4.2]
shows that an adapted coordinate system in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be obtained by either

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{1},

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{2}-\psi(x_{1})

or

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{2},

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{1}-\psi(x_{2}),

where
\psi
is a real analytic function.

Part 2:
Assume
\phi(Ax)
has the form

\phi(Ax)=Q_{1}(x_{1})+Q_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+Q_{3}(x_{1})x_{3},

where
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomial functions.
Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
denote the multiplicities of
x_{1}=0
as the root of
Q_{1}(x_{1}),

Q_{2}(x_{1}),
and
Q_{3}(x_{1}),
respectively. Since
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0,
we have
\nu_{1}\geq 2,

\nu_{2}\geq 1,
and
\nu_{3}\geq 1.

If
Q_{2}(x_{1})
or
Q_{3}(x_{1})
is identically zero, then
\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables and so Part 1 implies the proof. Hence, we assume that both
Q_{2}(x_{1})
and
Q_{3}(x_{1})
are not identically zero.
Q_{1}(x_{1})
can be identically zero. In this case, we formally put
\nu_{1}=\infty
.

We can write

Q_{i}(x_{1})=x_{1}^{\nu_{j}}\tilde{Q}_{i}(x_{1}),\,\,\,i\in\{1,2,3\}

where
\tilde{Q}_{i}
are polynomial functions. According to our assumption we have
\tilde{Q}_{i}(0)\neq 0
for

and
\tilde{Q}_{1}(0)\neq 0
if

is not identically zero.
The Newton polyhedron of
\phi(Ax)
is the convex hull of

\{(\nu_{2},1,0)+\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}\}\cup\{(\nu_{1},0,0)+\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}\}\cup\{(\nu_{3},0,1)+\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}\}.

Without loss of generality we can assume
\nu_{3}\geq\nu_{2}.
Then
\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}\}
can be
\nu_{1}
or
\nu_{2}.

Hence, we consider two different cases:

Case 1:
Let
\nu_{1}=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}\}.

In this case the Newton polyhedron of
\phi(Ax)
is

\{(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}:\,t_{1}\geq\nu_{1}\}

and the distance
d_{x}=\nu_{1}.
By definition, the coordinate system
x
is adapted if
d_{y}\leq d_{x}
for any smooth local coordinate system
y.
We will prove that this inequality holds.
We consider any smooth local coordinate system
y
as in (
3
). In these new coordinates

equals

\displaystyle\Phi(y)=\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{1}}(y)\tilde{Q}_{1}(\varphi_{1}(y))+\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{2}}(y)\varphi_{2}(y)\tilde{Q}_{2}(\varphi_{1}(y))+\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{3}}(y)\varphi_{3}(y)\tilde{Q}_{3}(\varphi_{1}(y)).

Using
\partial_{y_{1}}\varphi_{1}(0)\neq 0
and the product rule, one can show that

belongs to the Newton polyhedron of

Hence, the distance

can not be greater than

Case 2:
Let
\nu_{2}=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}\}.
If
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2},
then Case 1 implies the proof. Hence, we assume

We introduce the following notations

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{cases}\tilde{Q}_{1}(0)\quad&\mbox{if}\quad\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}+1\\

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\tilde{Q}_{2}(0)\neq 0,

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{cases}\tilde{Q}_{3}(0)\quad&\mbox{if}\quad\nu_{3}=\nu_{2}\\

With these notations we can write

\displaystyle z_{1}=x_{1},

\displaystyle z_{2}=c_{1}x_{1}+c_{2}x_{2}+c_{3}x_{3}

\displaystyle z_{3}=x_{3}.

Let

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-c_{1}/c_{2}&1/c_{2}&-c_{3}/c_{2}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

After changing the variables we get

\displaystyle\phi(Bz)=

\displaystyle z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}z_{2}+z_{1}^{\nu_{1}}(\tilde{Q}_{1}(z_{1})-c_{1})-\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}+1}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}z_{2}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})+\left(z_{1}^{\nu_{3}}(\tilde{Q}_{3}(z_{1})-c_{3})-\frac{c_{3}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})\right)z_{3}.

Since

we have

We claim that the coordinate system

y
as in (
3
). In this new coordinates,

equals

\displaystyle\Phi(y)=

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{2}}(y)\varphi_{2}(y)+\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{1}}(y)(\tilde{Q}_{1}(\varphi_{1}(y))-c_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{2}+1}(y)(\tilde{Q}_{2}(\varphi_{1}(y))-c_{2})+\frac{\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{2}}(y)\varphi_{2}(y)}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(\varphi_{1}(y))-c_{2})

\displaystyle+\left(\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{3}}(y)(\tilde{Q}_{3}(\varphi_{1}(y))-c_{3})-\frac{c_{3}\varphi_{1}^{\nu_{2}}(y)}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(\varphi_{1}(y))-c_{2})\right)\varphi_{3}(y).

Note that every monomial of

\displaystyle z_{1}^{\nu_{1}}(\tilde{Q}_{1}(z_{1})-c_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}+1}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})+\frac{z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}z_{2}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})

\displaystyle+\left(z_{1}^{\nu_{3}}(\tilde{Q}_{3}(z_{1})-c_{3})-\frac{c_{3}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})\right)z_{3}

Therefore,

and the product rule, one can check that

belongs to the Newton polyhedron of

Hence,

can not be greater than

∎

\displaystyle y_{3}

\displaystyle=x_{3}.

4
Estimates for oscillatory integrals

In this section we study the asymptotic behavior of the oscillatory integral (
1
).

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be a polynomial function and an invertible matrix as in Theorem
3.1
, respectively. Then
\phi(Ax)
either depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
or has the form as in (
8

\nu(\phi)\in\{0,1\}:
When

\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2},
we have shown in Theorem
3.2
that there is an adapted coordinate system
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}.
If the principal face

when expressed by the function

in the adapted coordinate system

is a vertex, and if

\phi(Ax)
has the form as in (

.

[

. When

[

With this definition of
\nu(\phi)
we can now state the main results of this section:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial function such that
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0.
Assume

\det{(D^{2}\phi(x))}=0

holds for all

. Let

be the height of
\phi
defined in (

\nu=\nu(\phi)\in\{0,1\}

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
of the origin and a constant
C>0
such that, for every
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(U)
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{4},
the following estimate holds:

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(\xi_{4}\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+\xi_{1}x_{1}+\xi_{2}x_{2}+x_{3}\xi_{3})}\eta(x)dx\right|\leq C||\eta||_{C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}(\log(2+|\xi|))^{\nu}(1+|\xi|)^{-1/h}.

(10)

Moreover, if we restrict ourselves to the direction where
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=\xi_{3}=0,
then the estimate (
10
) is sharp, i.e., for any smooth function
\eta
with
\eta(0)\neq 0
and a support in a sufficiently small neighborhood of the origin, the following limit

\lim_{\xi_{4}\to+\infty}\frac{\xi_{4}^{1/h}}{(\log{\xi_{4}})^{\nu}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i\xi_{4}\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})}\eta(x)dx=c

(11)

exists, where

is a non-zero constant depending on

\eta
.

A corollary of Theorem
4.1
and Corollary

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}

. Let

be the height of
\phi

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(\xi_{4}(\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))+\xi_{1}x_{1}+\xi_{2}x_{2}+\xi_{3}x_{3}}\eta(x)dx\right|\leq C||\eta||_{C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}(\log(2+|\xi|))^{\nu}(1+|\xi|)^{-1/h}.

(12)

Indeed, if the polynomial function
\phi

, then, due to Corollary
3.4
, it can be reduced to a polynomial function that depends on two variables by applying a local analytic change of variables. We can then use the stability result proved by Karpushkin

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We denote

J(\xi,\phi)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(\xi_{4}\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+\xi_{1}x_{1}+\xi_{2}x_{2}+x_{3}\xi_{3})}\eta(x)dx.

Let
|\xi_{1}|+|\xi_{2}|+|\xi_{3}|>\delta|\xi_{4}|

then the estimate (
10

[
35

5
Estimates for maximal operators

In this section, we study the
L^{p}
-boundedness of maximal operators associated with hypersurfaces
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Let
d\sigma
denote the surface measure on
S
and let

\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(S)

\mathcal{A}_{t}f(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{S}f(y-tx)\rho(x)d\sigma(x),\,\,\,\,t>0,\,\,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})

\mathcal{M}f(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup_{t>0}|\mathcal{A}_{t}f(y)|,\,\,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4}).

As mentioned in the introduction, using a partition of unity argument, we can assume that
\rho
is supported in a sufficiently small neighborhood of a fixed point
x^{0}\in S.
The transversality assumption let us find a linear change of coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
so that in the new coordinates
S
can be locally represented as a graph

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},1+\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\,(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in U\},

(14)

where

is a sufficiently small neighborhood of the origin.
We assume
\phi
is a polynomial function and satisfies (
3
) and (
4
).
Then the maximal operator can be written as

\mathcal{M}f(y)=\sup_{t>0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(y_{1}-tx_{1},y_{2}-tx_{2},y_{3}-tx_{3},y_{4}-t(1+\phi(x)))\eta(x)dx\right|,\,\,f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4}),

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}

\phi(0)=0

\nabla\phi(0)=0.

\det(D^{2}\phi(x))=0

\phi

\mathcal{M}
in (
15
) is bounded
on

for

Iosevich and Sawyer
[

proved that if the maximal operator
\mathcal{M}
with
\eta(0)>0
is bounded on

, then

denotes the distance from

to

. As shown in the proof of Theorem 2 in
[

]
, for a hypersurface

given in (

(17)

We check for which

Proposition 5.2

(i)

(ii)

As an immediate consequence of Theorem

\displaystyle=\det(A)\int_{\tilde{U}}|\phi(Ax)|^{-\frac{1}{p}}dx=\det(A)\int_{\tilde{U}}\frac{dx}{|x_{1}|^{\frac{\nu_{1}}{p}}|\tilde{Q}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})|^{\frac{1}{p}}},

where
\tilde{U}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{Ax:\,x\in U\}
. If
U
is sufficiently small, then
\tilde{Q}
is non-zero in
U
. Hence, there is a constant
C>0
and an interval
(a,b)
containing
0
such that

\int_{U}|\phi(x)|^{-\frac{1}{p}}dx\geq C\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{|x_{1}|^{\frac{\nu_{1}}{p}}}dx_{1}.

Obviously, the integral on the right hand side of this inequality diverges if
p\leq\nu_{1}.

Case 2:
Let
\nu_{2}=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}\}.
If
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2},
then the proof follows from Case 1. Hence, we assume
\nu_{1}>\nu_{2}.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
shows that the expression
\phi
in an adapted coordinate system is

\displaystyle\phi(Bz)=

\displaystyle z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}z_{2}+z_{1}^{\nu_{1}}(\tilde{Q}_{1}(z_{1})-c_{1})-\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}+1}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}z_{2}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})+\left(z_{1}^{\nu_{3}}(\tilde{Q}_{3}(z_{1})-c_{3})-\frac{c_{3}z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}}{c_{2}}(\tilde{Q}_{2}(z_{1})-c_{2})\right)z_{3}.

Then this function can be written as
\phi(Bz)=z_{1}^{\nu_{2}}\tilde{Q}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}),
where
\tilde{Q}
is a polynomial function satisfying

\tilde{Q}(0,0,0)=0
and
\partial_{z_{2}}\tilde{Q}(0,0,0)\neq 0
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\phi(x)|^{-\frac{1}{p}}dx

\displaystyle=\det(B)\int_{\tilde{U}}|\phi(Ax)|^{-\frac{1}{p}}dx=\det(B)\int_{\tilde{U}}\frac{1}{|x_{1}|^{\frac{\nu_{2}}{p}}|\tilde{Q}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})|^{\frac{1}{p}}}dx

where
\tilde{U}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{Bx:\,x\in U\}
.
There exist an interval
(a,b)
containing
0
and an neighborhood
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the origin such that

\int_{U}|\phi(x)|^{-\frac{1}{p}}dx=\det(B)\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dx_{1}}{|x_{1}|^{\frac{\nu_{2}}{p}}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{dx_{2}dx_{3}}{|\tilde{Q}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})|^{\frac{1}{p}}}.

Obviously, the last integral diverges if
p\leq\nu_{2}
and converges whenever
p>\nu_{2}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

When the rank of
D^{2}\phi(0)
is two, the claimed result follows from
[
12
, Theorem 2]
.

We now prove the case
D^{2}\phi(0)=0.

By Theorem
3.1
there exists an invertible matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
such that

\phi(Ax)
either depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
or has the form in (
8
).
In the later case, one can check that, if
D^{2}\phi(0)=0
, then the height
h(\phi)
can not be strictly less than 2. Hence, it remains to prove the case where
\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}.

Assume
\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. By following the proof Theorem
5.1
, we can present the averaging operator as

\mathcal{A}_{t}f(y)=\int_{a}^{b}T_{\theta}R^{\theta}{A}_{t}^{\theta}R^{-\theta}T_{\theta}^{-1}f(y)d\theta.

We remind the operator
{A}_{t}^{\theta}

{A}_{t}^{\theta}f(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(y_{1}-tx_{1},y_{2}-tx_{2},y_{3},y_{4}-t(1+\phi(x_{1},x_{2})))\tilde{\eta}(x_{1},x_{2},\theta)dx_{1}dx_{2}.

By considering
y_{3}
and
\theta
as parameters, we can apply
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
if
U
is sufficiently small, and obtain that, for
p>h(\phi),

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(\sup_{t>0}|A^{\theta}_{t}f(y)|\big)^{p}dy_{1}dy_{2}dy_{4}\leq C_{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})|^{p}dy_{1}dy_{2}dy_{4},

where
C_{p}
is independent of
\theta
and
y_{3}.

Integrating this equation with respect to
y_{3},
we obtain that the operator
\sup_{t>0}|{A}_{t}^{\theta}f|
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
for
p>h(\phi).
Using this fact and repeating the same arguments in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we obtain that
\mathcal{M}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
for
p>h(\phi).
The sharpness of the exponent

follows from Corollary
5.3
.

∎

6
Oscillation and contact indexes

In analogy with
Arnold’s notion of the
singularity index

[
1
,
2
]
, one can define (see
[
17
]
) the
uniform oscillation
index

\beta_{u}(x^{0},S)
of the hypersurface
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
at the point
x^{0}\in S
as follows: Let
\mathcal{B}_{u}(x^{0},S)
denote the set of all
\beta\geq 0
for which there exists an open neighborhood

U_{\beta}
of
x^{0}
in
S
such that estimate

|\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\xi)|\leq\frac{C_{\rho,\beta}}{(1+|\xi|)^{\beta}}

(19)

holds true for every function
\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U_{\beta})
. Then the uniform oscillation
index
\beta_{u}(x^{0},S)
is defined by

\displaystyle\beta_{u}(x^{0},S)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup\{\beta:\beta\in\mathcal{B}_{u}(x^{0},S)\}.

If we restrict our attention to the normal direction to
S
at
x^{0}
only, then we can define
analogously the notion of
oscillation index
of the hypersurface
S
at the point
x^{0}\in S
.
More precisely, if
n(x^{0})
is a unit normal to
S
at
x^{0}
, then we let
\mathcal{B}(x^{0},S)
denote the set of all
\beta\geq 0
for which there exists an open neighborhood

U_{\beta}
of
x^{0}
in
S
such that estimate (
19
) holds true along the line
\mathbb{R}n(x^{0})
for every function
\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U_{\rho})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|\widehat{\rho d\sigma}(\lambda n(x^{0}))|\leq\frac{C_{\rho,\beta}}{(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}}.

Then the oscillation index
\beta(x^{0},S)
is defined by

\displaystyle\beta(x^{0},S)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup\{\beta:\beta\in\mathcal{B}(x^{0},S)\}.

We also define the
uniform contact index

\gamma_{u}(x^{0},S)
of the hypersurface
S
at the
point
x^{0}\in S
as follows: Let
\mathcal{C}_{u}(x^{0},S)
denote the set of all

\gamma>
0 for which there exists an
open neighborhood
U_{\gamma}

of
x^{0}
in
S
such that the estimate

\int_{U_{\gamma}}d_{H}(x)^{-\gamma}d\sigma(x)<\infty

holds true for every affine hyperplane
H
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then we put

\displaystyle\gamma_{u}(x^{0},S):=\sup\{\gamma:\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{u}(x^{0},S)\}.

Similarly, we let
\mathcal{C}(x^{0},S)
denote the set of all

\gamma>0
for which there exists an open neighborhood

U_{\gamma}
of
x^{0}
in
S
such that

\int_{U_{\gamma}}d_{T_{x^{0}}}(x)^{-\gamma}d\sigma(x)<\infty,

where
T_{x^{0}}
affine tangent hyperplane at the point
x^{0}

and call

\displaystyle\gamma(x^{0},S):=\sup\{\gamma:\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x^{0},S)\}.

the contact index

\gamma(x^{0},S)
of the hypersurface
S
at the point
x^{0}\in S
. Then clearly

\displaystyle\beta_{u}(x^{0},S)\leq\beta(x^{0},S)\quad\mbox{ and}\quad\gamma_{u}(x^{0},S)\leq\gamma(x^{0},S).

The main finding of this paper related to estimates for the Fourier transform of surface-carried measures, can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
S
be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
x^{0}\in S.
After applying a suitable Euclidean motion of
\mathbb{R}^{4},
let us assume that
x^{0}:=(0,0,0,0)\in S
and in a neighborhood of
x^{0}
we may view
S
as the graph of a polynomial function
\phi
satisfying
\phi(0)=0,\nabla\phi(0)=0
. Assume the Gaussian curvature of
S
is identically zero in this neighborhood of
x^{0}\in S
. Let
h(x^{0},S)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}h(\phi)
be the height of
\phi
defined in (
7
). Then the following relations

\beta_{u}(x^{0},S)=\beta(x^{0},S)=\gamma_{u}(x^{0},S)=\gamma(x^{0},S)=\frac{1}{h(x^{0},S)}.

(20)

Proof.

The proof follows by using Theorem
4.1
, Theorem
5.1
, and Proposition
5.2
and by repeating the same arguments in the proof of
[

.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

6.1
is analogous to Theorem 1.14 in

. It should be noted that the examples of Varchenko in

7
Extensions

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function of finite type at the origin (see Section 2) with
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0
. Assume that there is an invertible matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
such that
\phi(Ax)
either depends on at most two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
or has the form

\phi(Ax)=\phi_{1}(x_{1})+\phi_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+\phi_{3}(x_{1})x_{3},

where
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\,i\in\{1,2,3\}
are smooth functions. For such functions, one can check that

\det(D^{2}\phi(x))=0,\,\,\,\,\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Theorem
3.1
shows that all polynomials with vanishing Hessian determinant have this property.

All of our results can be extended for all smooth functions
\phi
satisfying the above condition.
We comment on how to construct an adapted coordinate system: Let
\phi(Ax)
depends on at most two variables, then, as in Part 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.2
, the existence of an adapted coordinate system follows from
[
18
]
.
Let
\phi(Ax)=\phi_{1}(x_{1})+\phi_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+\phi_{3}(x_{1})x_{3},
then the conditions
\phi(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0
imply
\phi_{1}(0)=\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)=0
and
\phi_{2}(0)=\phi_{3}(0)=0.

Since we assume
\phi
is finite type, at least one of
\phi_{1},

\phi_{2}
, and
\phi_{3}
is not flat at the origin. Hence, there exists a finite natural number
m\geq 1
such that
\partial_{x_{1}}^{k}\phi_{1}(0)=0
for all
k\in\{0,...,m\}
,
\partial_{x_{1}}^{j}\phi_{2}(0)=\partial_{x_{1}}^{j}\phi_{3}(0)=0
for all
j\in\{0,...,m-1\}
, and at least one of
\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0),\,\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0),
and
\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)
is non-zero.
Then we can write

\displaystyle\phi(Ax)=x_{1}^{m}\left(\frac{\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)}{(m+1)!}x_{1}+\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0)}{m!}x_{2}+\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)}{m!}x_{3}\right)+\tilde{\phi}_{1}(x_{1})+\tilde{\phi}_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+\tilde{\phi}_{3}(x_{1})x_{3},

where
\tilde{\phi}_{1}(x_{1})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\phi}_{1}(x_{1})-\frac{\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)}{(m+1)!}x_{1}^{m+1}
,
\tilde{\phi}_{2}(x_{1})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\phi}_{2}(x_{1})-\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0)}{m!}x_{1}^{m},
and
\tilde{\phi}_{3}(x_{1})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\phi}_{3}(x_{1})-\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)}{m!}x_{1}^{m}.

If
\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)\neq 0
and
\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0)=\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)=0,
then

\phi(Ax)=\frac{\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)}{(m+1)!}x_{1}^{m+1}+\tilde{\phi}_{1}(x_{1})+\tilde{\phi}_{2}(x_{1})x_{2}+\tilde{\phi}_{3}(x_{1})x_{3}

and the given coordinate system
x
is adapted. The distance is
d_{x}=m+1
. If at least one of
\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0)
and
\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)
is non-zero, then an adapted coordinate system can be obtained by

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1},

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{m+1}_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}(0)}{(m+1)!}x_{1}+\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{2}(0)}{m!}x_{2}+\frac{\partial^{m}_{x_{1}}\phi_{3}(0)}{m!}x_{3},

\displaystyle z_{3}

\displaystyle=x_{3}.

In this new coordinate system
z
,
\phi
has the form

z_{1}^{m}z_{2}+\psi_{1}(z_{1})+\psi_{2}(z_{1})z_{2}+\psi_{3}(z_{1})z_{3}

for some smooth function
\psi_{1}(z_{1}),

\psi_{2}(z_{1}),
and
\psi_{3}(z_{1}),
which satisfy
\partial_{x_{1}}^{k}\psi_{1}(0)=0
for all
k\in\{0,...,m+1\}
and
\partial_{x_{1}}^{j}\psi_{2}(0)=\partial_{x_{1}}^{j}\psi_{3}(0)=0
for all
j\in\{0,...,m\}
.
The distance in the coordinate system
z
is
d_{z}=m.
The adaptness of these coordinate systems can be proven by considering any smooth local coordinate system
y
as in (
3
) and using the arguments in Part 2 of the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Also, our results concerning oscillatory integrals in Theorem
4.1
and maximal operators in Theorem
5.1
can be obtained for such functions by using the arguments in the proofs of these theorems.

Getting rid of the above
assumption on
\phi
would of course be interesting, but since our results are, to the best of our knowledge, the first sharp results in three dimensions, we believe that they have their own interest.

As an extension of the present work, one could study smooth functions whose Hessian determinant is identically zero. Yet, this extension is not within reach so far and will be a matter of further study.

In three dimensions and higher, sharp estimates for asymptotic behavior of oscillatory integrals and maximal operators on
L^{p}
remain unknown for general hypersurfaces. This paper contributes to the three-dimensional case. We expect that the techniques used in this paper can
be useful for future studies.
