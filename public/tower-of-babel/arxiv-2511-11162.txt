OT-ALD: Aligning Latent Distributions with Optimal Transport for Accelerated Image-to-Image Translation
Zhanpeng Wang, Shuting Cao, Yuhang Lu, Yuhan Li, Na Lei, Zhongxuan Luo

Abstract
The Dual Diffusion Implicit Bridge (DDIB) is an emerging image-to-image (I2I) translation method that preserves cycle consistency while achieving strong flexibility. It links two independently trained diffusion models (DMs) in the source and target domains by first adding noise to a source image to obtain a latent code, then denoising it in the target domain to generate the translated image. However, this method faces two key challenges: (1) low translation efficiency, and (2) translation trajectory deviations caused by mismatched latent distributions. To address these issues, we propose a novel I2I translation framework, OT-ALD, grounded in optimal transport (OT) theory, which retains the strengths of DDIB-based approach. Specifically, we compute an OT map from the latent distribution of the source domain to that of the target domain, and use the mapped distribution as the starting point for the reverse diffusion process in the target domain. Our error analysis confirms that OT-ALD eliminates latent distribution mismatches. Moreover, OT-ALD effectively balances faster image translation with improved image quality. Experiments on four translation tasks across three high-resolution datasets show that OT-ALD improves sampling efficiency by 20.29% and reduces the FID score by 2.6 on average compared to the top-performing baseline models.

Image synthesis through the DMs

The DMs include forward and reverse processes. Given the drift coefficient
\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},t\right):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\left[0,T\right]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the diffusion term
g\left(t\right):\left[0,T\right]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

(
79

d\bm{x}=\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},t\right)dt+g\left(t\right)d\bm{W}_{t},\bm{x}\left(0\right)\sim p_{0}\left(\bm{x}\right),

(1)

where
p_{0}\left(\bm{x}\right)
is the image distribution and

is the
n
-dimensional standard Wiener process. The marginal distribution of the solution to (
1

(

\frac{\partial p_{t}\left(\bm{x}\right)}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},t\right)p_{t}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right)-\frac{g\left(t\right)^{2}}{2}\Delta p_{t}\left(\bm{x}\right)=0.

(2)

According to (
2

(
79
)
, which shares the same marginal probability density
p_{t}
as (
1
)

d\bm{x}=(\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},t\right)-\frac{g\left(t\right)^{2}}{2}\nabla\log p_{t}\left(\bm{x}\right))dt,\bm{x}\left(0\right)\sim p_{0}\left(\bm{x}\right).

Similarly, the reverse process can be expressed as reverse-time SDE
(
2
)
with

(3)

where

and
\bar{\bm{W}}_{t}
is the

In practice,

is usually approximated by a score network

with the weighted mean square error (MSE) loss function

\mathcal{J}_{SM}\left(\bm{\theta},\phi,T\right):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\left(t\right)\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}[\left\|\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x},t\right)-\nabla\log p_{t}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}]dt,

is obtained from

. The solution of DMs can be formalized as

\begin{split}&Solver_{\eta}(\bm{x}_{t_{0}},\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}},t_{0},t_{1})\\

(4)

where

Optimal transport theory

We begin by introducing the classical geometric variational approach to semi-discrete OT. As the number of target samples increases, the discrete solution progressively converges to its continuous counterpart. Suppose the source measure
\mu
is defined on a convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the target domain is a discrete set
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The target measure is a Dirac measure
\nu=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\nu_{i}\delta(\bm{y}-\bm{y}_{i})
, with the total mass matched as
\mu(\Omega)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\nu_{i}
. Under a transport map
M:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, the domain
\Omega
is partitioned into cells
W_{i}
such that each point
\bm{x}\in W_{i}
is mapped to
\bm{y}_{i}
, i.e.,
M(\bm{x})=\bm{y}_{i}
. The map
M
is measure-preserving (denoted
M_{\#}\mu=\nu
) if each cell satisfies
\mu(W_{i})=\nu_{i}
. Let
c:\Omega\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the cost function, where
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})
denotes the cost of transporting unit mass from
\bm{x}
to
\bm{y}
. The total transport cost of the map
M
is given by

\int_{\Omega}c(\bm{x},M(\bm{x}))d\mu(\bm{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{W_{i}}c\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}\right)d\mu(\bm{x}).

(5)

The OT map
M_{ot}
is a measure-preserving map that minimizes the total cost in (
5
),

M_{ot}:=\arg\min_{M_{\#}\mu=\nu}\int_{\Omega}c(\bm{x},M(\bm{x}))d\mu(\bm{x}).

(6)

Specifically, when the cost function is
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}
, there exists a convex function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that its gradient
\nabla u
uniquely solves (
6
)
(
9
)
, i.e.,
M_{ot}=\nabla u
. This shows that the OT map is the gradient of Brenier’s potential. As noted in
(
48
;
1
)
,
u
can be represented as the upper envelope of hyperplanes

\pi_{\bm{h},i}(\bm{x})=\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}\rangle+h_{i}
,
and is uniquely parameterized (up to an additive constant) by a height vector
\bm{h}=(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{|\mathcal{I}|})^{T}
. The resulting potential, denoted
u_{\bm{h}}
, is given by:

u_{\bm{h}}(\bm{x})=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\{\pi_{\bm{h},i}(\bm{x})\},u_{\bm{h}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7)

Given the target measure
\nu
, there exists a Brenier potential
u_{\bm{h}}
(as in (
7
)) such that the projected volume under each support plane matches the prescribed mass
\nu_{i}
. To obtain
u_{\bm{h}}
, it suffices to optimize the height vector
\bm{h}
by minimizing the following convex energy function:

E(\bm{h})=\int_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}w_{i}(\gamma)d\gamma_{i}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}h_{i}\nu_{i},

(8)

where
\omega_{i}(\gamma)
is the
\mu
-volume of
W_{i}(\gamma)
. For more theoretical details, we refer to Reference
(
24
)

\bm{x}_{T}^{\prime A}=M_{ot,T}^{B\to A}(\bm{x}_{T}^{\prime B}),\bm{x}_{0}^{\prime A}=Solver_{\eta}(\bm{x}_{T}^{\prime A},\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A},T,0),\end{matrix}

where
M_{ot,T}^{B\to A}=(M_{ot,T}^{A\to B})^{-1}
, then
\|\bm{x}_{0}^{A}-\bm{x}_{0}^{\prime A}\|_{2}=0
holds. See Appendix D.3 for the proof detail.

Theorem 4

(Distributional cycle consistency)
.

Under the same setting as Theorem
3
, we apply the proposed method to translate the source distribution
p_{0}^{A}
into the target distribution. Subsequently, the approximate source distribution
p_{0}^{\prime A}
derived from the reverse translation action on the target distribution. Then we have
\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{0}^{A},p_{0}^{\prime A})=0
for
\forall\eta\in[0,1]
. See Appendix D.4 for the proof detail.

Sample consistency forms the basis of distribution consistency—when all individuals satisfy cycle consistency, the distribution naturally aligns. However, distribution consistency does not require strict reversibility of individual samples, making it more flexible and tolerant of individual differences to improve overall distribution alignment.

Conclusion

This paper provides a theoretical analysis of the limitations of classical DDIB-based I2I translation methods, namely: (1) low efficiency during the translation process and (2) translation trajectory deviations due to mismatched latent distributions. To address these issues, we propose OT-ALD, which effectively eliminates latent distribution mismatch, and offers theoretical guarantees for key properties such as cycle consistency. In practice, OT-ALD enables fast, high-quality image translation with flexible control over transformation objectives, allowing users to balance generation speed and synthesis quality according to their specific needs.

Assumption 5
.

Both the drift term
\bm{f}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)
and score network
\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)
are Lipschitz continuous with respect to variable
\bm{x}^{A}
, namely, there exist two scale continuous functions
L_{\bm{f}}^{A}\left(t\right),L_{\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}}^{A}\left(t\right):\left[0,T\right]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\|\bm{f}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)-\bm{f}^{A}(\bm{y}^{A},t)\|_{2}\leq L_{\bm{f}}^{A}\left(t\right)\|\bm{x}^{A}-\bm{y}^{A}\|_{2}
and
\|\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)-\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{y}^{A},t)\|_{2}\leq L_{\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}}^{A}(t)\|\bm{x}^{A}-\bm{y}^{A}\|_{2}
for any
\bm{x}^{A},\bm{y}^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. In addition, there exists constant
C_{g}>0
for all
t\in\left[0,T\right]
such that
\frac{1}{C_{g}^{A}}<g^{A}\left(t\right)<C_{g}
.

.

There exists constant
C_{p}>0
that satisfies
\{\|\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)\|_{2},\|\nabla\log p_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A})\|_{2}\}\leq C_{p}(1+\|\bm{x}^{A}\|_{2})
for any
\bm{x}^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\left[0,T\right]
.

.

The score network
\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)
and probability density

satisfy conditions
\{\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}^{A}}[\|\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A},t)\|_{2}^{2}]dt,\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}^{A}}[\|\nabla\log p_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}^{A})\|_{2}^{2}]dt\}<+\infty
.

C. Geometric variational principle of OT map

Supposing
\mathcal{Y}^{B}=\{\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}p_{T}^{B}
is the set of discrete points, here we introduce geometric variational methods
(
24
)
for calculating the OT map from
p_{T}^{A}
to
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\delta(\bm{y}^{B}-\bm{y}_{i}^{B})
. The dual of Monge problem (8) in main text is

\begin{split}&\max_{\psi}E(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi^{c}(\bm{x}^{B})p_{T}^{A}(\bm{x}^{B})d\bm{x}^{B}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\psi_{i}\\
&=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{W_{i}(\psi)}(\langle\bm{y}_{i}^{B},\bm{x}^{A}\rangle-\psi_{i})d\bm{x}^{A}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\psi_{i},\end{split}

(9)

where
\psi_{i}=\psi(\bm{y}_{i}^{B})
,
\psi^{c}(\bm{x}^{A})=\min\limits_{i}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}^{A}-\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\|_{2}^{2}-\psi_{i}
and
W_{i}(\psi)=\{\bm{x}^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\langle\bm{y}_{i}^{B}-\bm{y}_{j}^{B},\bm{x}^{A}\rangle\geq\psi_{i}-\psi_{j},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}\}
implies nearest power Voronoi diagram. Each target point
\bm{y}_{i}^{B}
corresponds to a support plane
\pi_{i}=\langle\bm{x}^{A},\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\rangle+\psi_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\|_{2}^{2}
, therefore the Brenier potential energy can be constructed as
u_{\bm{h}}(\bm{x}^{A})=\max\limits_{i}\langle\bm{x}^{A},\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\rangle+h_{i}
, here
h_{i}=\psi_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}_{i}^{B}\|_{2}^{2}
. We optimize the energy function
E(\psi)
in (
9
) through the Monte Carlo algorithm
(
1
)
to obtain Brenier potential
u_{\bm{h}}
. Referring to this geometric property, the image of
\nabla u_{\bm{h}}
falls precisely within the data set
\mathcal{Y}
, namely
\nabla u_{\bm{h}}(\bm{x}^{A})\in\mathcal{Y}
for any
\bm{x}^{A}\sim p_{T}^{A}
. We denote the OT map from
p_{T}^{A}
to
p_{T}^{B}
as
u_{T}^{A\to B}
, so there is
u_{\bm{h}}\to u_{T}^{A\to B}
when
|\mathcal{I}|\to+\infty
.

D. Proofs

In this section, we give a detailed derivation of the main theories proposed in this paper.

D.1 Proof of Theorem 1

Proof.

It can be directly proved based on the contraction property of Wasserstein distance
(
11
)

\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{T}^{B},q_{T}^{B})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}L_{f}^{B}\left(t\right)dt\right)\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{0}^{B},q_{0}^{B})

(10)

and Theorem 1 in
(
44
)
. The scale function
L_{f}^{B}(t)
in (
10
) satisfies
\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}L_{f}^{B}\left(t\right)dt=\infty
.
∎

D.2 Proof of Theorem 2

Proof.

On the basis of Theorem 1 in
(
44
)
, we have

\begin{split}&\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{0}^{B},q_{0}^{B})\\
\leq&\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{B}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}^{B}}[\|\nabla\log p_{t}^{B}(\bm{x}_{t}^{B})-\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{B}(\bm{x}_{t}^{B},t)\|_{2}^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt\\
+&I(T)\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{T}^{B},q_{T}^{B})\\
\overset{\star}{\leq}&\sqrt{2T}\mathcal{J}_{SM}^{B}(\bm{\theta}^{B},\phi^{B},T)^{\frac{1}{2}}+I(T)\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{T}^{B},q_{T}^{B})\\
=&\sqrt{2T}(\mathcal{J}_{SM}^{B})^{\frac{1}{2}}+I(T)\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}(p_{T}^{A}),\nabla u_{\bm{h}}(p_{T}^{A})),\end{split}

(11)

where
\!I^{B}\left(T\right)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}(L_{f}^{B}\left(t\right)+\frac{g^{B}\left(t\right)^{2}}{2}L_{\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}}^{B}\left(t\right))dt\right)\!
,
\star
is derived from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and
\!\phi^{B}=(g^{B})^{4}(I^{B})^{2}\!
.
Referring to Proposition 2.1 of
(
62
)
and Theorem 22 in
(
13
)
, we concern with the last term of the above inequality in the following form

\begin{split}&\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}(p_{T}^{A}),\nabla u_{\bm{h}}(p_{T}^{A}))\\
\leq&\|\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}-\nabla u_{\bm{h}}\|_{L_{2}(p_{T}^{A})}\\
=&\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}(\bm{x}^{A})-\nabla u_{\bm{h}}(\bm{x}^{A})\|_{2}^{2}p_{T}(\bm{x}^{A})d\bm{x}^{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\leq&\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}(\bm{x}^{A})-\nabla u_{\bm{h}}(\bm{x}^{A})\|_{2}^{2}d\bm{x}^{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\leq&2K_{1}\|u_{T}^{A\to B}-u_{\bm{h}}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\|\nabla u_{T}^{A\to B}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left\|\nabla u_{\bm{h}}\right\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}})\\
\leq&4K_{1}K_{2}\|u_{T}^{A\to B}-u_{\bm{h}}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

(12)

where
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are constants, respectively. Then we can prove the below formula

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{0}^{B},q_{0}^{B})\leq&\sqrt{2T}(\mathcal{J}_{SM}^{B})^{\frac{1}{2}}+KI(T)\|u_{T}^{A\to B}-u_{\bm{h}}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

(13)

by taking
K=4K_{1}K_{2}
and applying (
12
) to (
11
).
∎

D.3 Proof of Theorem 3

Proof.

All diffusion process can be described by a deterministic ODE with matching marginal distributions, known as the PF-ODE
(
79
)
, which uniquely encodes the data. For the forward SDE (1) in main text, the corresponding PF-ODE is shown as (3) in main text. In the reverse process, we replace
\nabla\log p_{t}^{A/B}(\bm{x}^{A/B})
with the trained, parameterized score network
\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A/B}(\bm{x}^{A/B},t)
to obtain an approximate PF-ODE

d\bm{x}^{A/B}=(\bm{f}^{A/B}(\bm{x}^{A/B},t)-\frac{g^{A/B}(t)^{2}}{2}\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A/B}(\bm{x}^{A/B},t))dt.

(14)

When
\eta=0
, the mapping from
\bm{x}_{t_{0}}^{A/B}
to
\bm{x}_{t_{1}}^{A/B}
then can be formulated as

\begin{split}&Solver_{\eta}(\bm{x}_{t_{0}}^{A/B},\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A/B},t_{0},t_{1})\\
=&\bm{x}_{0}^{A/B}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(\bm{f}^{A/B}(\bm{x}_{t}^{A/B},t)\\
-&\frac{g^{A/B}(t)^{2}}{2}\bm{S}_{\bm{\theta}}^{A/B}(\bm{x}_{t}^{A/B},t))dt\end{split}

(15)

Under the premise that Assumption 5 is satisfied and the discretization error is ignored,
Solver_{\eta}
completely follows PF-ODE (
14
). Further, when the calculation error of OT is not taken into account, then we have
M_{ot,T}^{A\to B}\circ M_{ot,T}^{B\to A}(\bm{x}^{A/B})=\bm{x}^{A/B}
for any
\bm{x}^{A/B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Based on the above analysis, OT-ALD satisfies the sample-level cycle consistency.
∎

D.4 Proof of Theorem 4

Proof.

Even if noise is introduced, namely,
\eta>0
, the marginal distribution
p_{t}^{A/B}
always satisfies FPE (2) in main text. Under the premise that Assumption 5 is satisfied, and the discretization error and calculation error of OT are ignored, we have
q_{T}^{B}=M_{ot,T}^{A\to B}(p_{T}^{A})=p_{T}^{B}
and
q_{T}^{A}=M_{ot,T}^{B\to A}(p_{T}^{B})=p_{T}^{A}
, which ensures that the forward FPE and approximate reverse FPE are equivalent
(
44
)
. Therefore,
q_{0}^{B}=p_{0}^{B}
, and further we have
p_{0}^{A}=p_{0}^{\prime A}
is satisfied. Based on the above analysis, OT-ALD satisfies the distributional-level cycle consistency.
∎

E.1 More experimental details

Hardware and Setup.
The experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 cluster, utilizing CUDA 12.1 acceleration and implemented using the PyTorch framework.

Codebase.
Our model is implemented based on the official GitHub repository from OpenAI (
https://github.com/openai/guided-diffusion
). All baseline models are reproduced using their respective official implementations: EGSDE is implemented using
https://github.com/ML-GSAI/EGSDE
with
T=1000
; CUT and CycleGAN use
https://github.com/taesungp/contrastive-unpaired-translation
; ILVR is based on
https://github.com/jychoi118/ilvr˙adm
with
T=1000
; SDEdit uses
https://github.com/ermongroup/SDEdit
with
T=500
and
K=2
repetitions; and DDIB is implemented with
https://github.com/suxuann/ddib
. All hyperparameters follow the settings reported in the original papers.

Implementation Parameters.
The OT-ALD introduced in this study is compared strictly in the same DDIM
(
77
)
backbone, employing a linear noisy schedule defined by the parameters (
\beta_{1}=1\times 10^{-4}
,
\beta_{T}=0.02
,
T=1000
) for the AFHQ, CelebA-HQ and summer2winter dataset. More detailed hyperparameters are shown in Tab.
4
. To construct the OT-ALD with truncation parameter
T_{trun}
, the initial values for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{T}
must first be established, followed by the cessation of the diffusion process at the
T_{trun}
-th step. To solve the Brenier potential energy

as delineated in (9) (main text), we employ the geometric variational principle, as referenced in Section
C. Geometric variational principle of OT map
and
(
1
)
. The number of Monte Carlo samples is established at
N_{mc}=10\times|\mathcal{I}|
. The learning rates for the datasets AFHQ, CelebA-HQ and summer2winter are set to
lr=1\times 10^{-5}
,
lr=1\times 10^{-5}
, and
lr=2\times 10^{-4}
, respectively. To enhance convergence, we double the number of samples
N_{mc}
and reduce the learning rate
lr
by a factor of 0.8 if the energy function
E(\bm{h})
in (10) (main text) does not show a decrease over 50 iterations. Furthermore, a termination threshold
\tau
is set to
8\times 10^{-4}
. The optimization procedure for
\bm{h}
will cease when
E(\bm{h})<\tau
or when the total number of iterations surpasses 10000.
