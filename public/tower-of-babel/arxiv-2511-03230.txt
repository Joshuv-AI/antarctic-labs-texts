Chiral symmetry breaking in accelerating and rotating frames
Zhi-Bin Zhu, Hao-Lei Chen, Xu-Guang Huang

Abstract
We study chiral symmetry breaking and restoration in accelerating and rotating frames using low-energy effective models. By analyzing the chiral condensate in Rindler coordinates, we show that different renormalization schemes lead to distinct conclusions in accelerating frame: the scheme with subtracting divergences in Rindler vacuum supports an acceleration-independent critical temperatures, while the other scheme with subtracting divergences in Minkowski vacuum suggests enhanced critical temperature. We further investigate system with both rotation and acceleration. We find that the critical acceleration (see definition in Section V) for chiral symmetry restoration decreases with angular velocity, indicating cooperative effects from acceleration-induced thermalization and rotation-induced effective chemical potential.



I
Introduction

Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) is the fundamental theory of the strong interaction governing the dynamics of quarks and gluons. Extensive research on the QCD phase diagram aims to clarify the nature of strongly interacting quark-gluon matter under extreme conditions, including high temperature, high density, and strong external fields. In the low-temperature and low-baryon-density regime, quarks remain confined in hadrons (baryons and mesons), which corresponds to ordinary nuclear matter. Above a pseudo-critical temperature of approximately 150 MeV (at zero baryon density), quarks and gluons become deconfined and the (approximate) chiral symmetry is restored, resulting in the formation of a quark-gluon plasma (QGP) in which quarks and gluons can move over extended distances.

II
Thermal equilibrium under acceleration and rotation

Before we going into detailed discussion about how the acceleration and rotation affect the chiral symmetry breaking, it is important to notify that a many-body system could maintain thermal equilibrium under acceleration and rotation. We briefly discuss such a thermal equilibrium in this section; see Refs.
Becattini 2012
;
Becattini 2018
;
Buzzegoli 2021
for more discussions. The so-called local equilibrium density operator
\rho_{\rm LE}
is the density operator that maximizes the entropy functional

S[\rho]=-{\rm Tr}\left(\rho\ln\rho\right),

(1)

under the constraints that the local energy-momentum and angular momentum fluxes across a given space-like hypersurface
\Xi_{\mu}
in the Minkowski spacetime is fixed to their true physical values,

\displaystyle n_{\mu}(x){\rm Tr}\left[\rho T^{\mu\nu}(x)\right]=n_{\mu}(x)T_{\rm true}^{\mu\nu}(x),

(2)

\displaystyle n_{\mu}(x){\rm Tr}\left[\rho J^{\mu\rho\sigma}(x)\right]=n_{\mu}(x)J_{\rm true}^{\mu\rho\sigma}(x),

(3)

where
n_{\mu}
is the normal direction of
\Xi_{\mu}
,
T^{\mu\nu}
is the energy-momentum tensor,
J^{\mu\rho\sigma}=T^{\mu\sigma}x^{\rho}-T^{\mu\rho}x^{\sigma}+\Sigma^{\mu\rho\sigma}
is the angular momentum tensor with
\Sigma^{\mu\rho\sigma}
the spin tensor. The resultant
\rho_{\rm LE}
has the following form:

\rho_{\rm LE}=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm LE}}\exp\left[-\int d\Xi_{\mu}\left(T^{\mu\nu}\beta_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}J^{\mu\rho\sigma}\varpi_{\rho\sigma}\right)\right],

(4)

where
\beta_{\nu}
and
\varpi_{\rho\sigma}(=-\varpi_{\sigma\rho})
are two local Lagrange multipliers which have the physical meaning of thermal flow and “chemical” potential for angular momentum and
Z_{\rm Le}
is the partition function. When the system reaches global thermal equilibrium, the density operator must be time independent, or more precisely, independent of the choice of the space-like hypersurface
\Xi_{\mu}
. This is achieved when
\beta^{\mu}
and
\varpi^{\mu\nu}
are constants as can be easily checked by noticing that
\partial_{\mu}\left(T^{\mu\nu}\beta_{\nu}-J^{\mu\rho\sigma}\varpi_{\rho\sigma}/2\right)=0
owing to Gauss’ theorem and the conservation laws for energy-momentum and angular momentum. Therefore, we have

\rho_{\rm eq}=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm eq}}\exp\left\{-\int d\Xi_{\mu}\left[T^{\mu\nu}\left(\beta_{\nu}+\varpi_{\nu\lambda}x^{\lambda}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\mu\rho\sigma}\right]\right\}.

(5)

Let us choose
n_{\mu}
be along the direction of
\beta_{\mu}
(which is time-like) and without loss of generality, we set
\beta_{\mu}
to the coordinate time direction,
\beta^{\mu}=(\beta,\bm{0})
, so that

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm eq}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\rm eq}}\exp\left[-\beta H+\varpi_{0i}K^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{ij}J^{ij}\right],

(6)

where
H=\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}T^{00}
is the Hamiltonian,
K^{i}=\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\left(T^{0i}t-T^{00}x^{i}+\Sigma^{00i}\right)
is the boost operator, and
J^{ij}=\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\left(T^{0j}x^{i}-T^{0i}x^{j}+\Sigma^{0ij}\right)
is the angular momentum operator. If we write
\varpi_{0i}=\beta a^{i}
and
\varpi_{ij}=\beta\epsilon^{ijk}\Omega^{k}
, it becomes clear that
\bm{a}
is the linear acceleration and
\bm{\Omega}
is the angular velocity. Note that though
K^{i}
explicitly depends on
t
, it is actually time independent as it is conserved (which can be directly checked using Poincare algebra,
dK^{i}/dt=0
)
Becattini 2018
. This allows us to set
t=0
in
\rho_{\rm eq}
. Choosing both the linear acceleration and rotation along
z
direction, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm eq}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\rm eq}}\exp\left[-\beta\left(H-aK^{z}-\Omega J^{z}\right)\right]

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\rm eq}}\exp\left[-\beta\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\left((1+az)T^{00}-a\Sigma^{003}-\Omega(T^{02}x-T^{01}y+\Sigma^{012})\right)\right]\bigg|_{t=0},

where
J^{z}=J^{12}
.

We now apply the above formalism to the single-flavor Nambu–Jona-Lasinio (NJL) model, whose Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}=\bar{\psi}\left[i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m_{0}\right]\psi+\frac{G_{\pi}}{2}\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi\right)^{2}\right],

(8)

where
\psi
denotes the Dirac spinor field,
m_{0}
is the fermion mass, and
G_{\pi}
is the coupling constant. Applying the Noether theorem, we obtain the canonical energy-momentum tensor and spin tensor as

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bar{\psi}}i\gamma^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\psi-g^{\mu\nu}{\cal L},

(9)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\mu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\bar{\psi}}\gamma^{\mu}\sigma^{\rho\sigma}\psi,

(10)

with
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=(i/2)[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}]
so that
\Sigma^{003}=(i/2){\bar{\psi}}\gamma^{3}\psi
and
\Sigma^{012}=(1/2)\psi^{\dagger}\sigma^{z}\psi
. Then we have

\displaystyle H-aK^{z}-\Omega J^{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\Big\{(1+az)\bar{\psi}\left[-i\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+m_{0}\right]\psi

(11)

\displaystyle-(1+az)\frac{G_{\pi}}{2}\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}i\gamma_{5}\psi\right)^{2}\right]-a\frac{i}{2}{\bar{\psi}}\gamma^{3}\psi-\Omega\psi^{\dagger}\left[-ix\partial_{y}+iy\partial_{x}+\frac{\sigma^{z}}{2}\right]\psi\Big\}.

The partition function can then be written in a path-integral form as

\displaystyle Z_{\rm eq}={\rm Tr}\;e^{-\beta(H-aK^{z}-\Omega J^{z})}=\int[d\psi][d\bar{\psi}]e^{-S_{E}[\psi,\bar{\psi}]},

(12)

where
S_{E}
is the Euclidean action which is obtained from the Minkowski action
S=\int d^{4}x{\bar{\psi}}i\gamma^{0}\partial_{t}\psi-\int dt(H-aK^{z}-\Omega J^{z})

\psi
and
{\bar{\psi}}

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}(1+az)^{2}-\Omega^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})&\Omega y&-\Omega x&0\\

(13)

Therefore, we can study the accelerating and rotating thermal equilibrium by formulating the theory in the accelerating and rotating coordinates so that the techniques developed for field theory in curved spacetime can be used. This will be our strategy in the following sections and it will be shown more convenient to shift

III

It is well known that the definitions of energy-momentum tensor and spin tensor are not unique. They suffer from the so-called pseudo-gauge ambiguity
Becattini et al. 2019

Huang 2025

\Phi^{\lambda\mu\nu}(=-\Phi^{\lambda\nu\mu})

and
\Sigma^{\prime\mu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle T^{\prime\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\lambda}\left(\Phi^{\lambda\mu\nu}+\Phi^{\nu\mu\lambda}+\Phi^{\mu\nu\lambda}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime\mu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\mu\rho\sigma}-\Phi^{\mu\rho\sigma},

(15)

are also well-defined energy-momentum and spin tensors in the sense that the conservation laws and the total four-momentum and total angular momentum are unchanged by this transformation. Therefore, the global thermal equilibrium density operator
\rho_{\rm eq}
and partition function

are invariant under pseudo-gauge transformation (though the local equilibrium density operator and partition function are not) and we thus have a freedom to choose a suitable pseudo-gauge to simply the action
S

-a\Sigma^{003}=-ia{\bar{\psi}}\gamma^{3}\psi/2

\Phi^{\lambda\mu\nu}=\frac{i}{2}\bar{\psi}\left(\eta^{\lambda\mu}\gamma^{\nu}-\eta^{\lambda\nu}\gamma^{\mu}\right)\psi.

(16)

This transforms Eqs. (
9
)-(
10

\displaystyle T^{\prime\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}[\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\psi-\bar{\psi}\overleftarrow{\partial}^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}\psi]-g^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}^{\prime},

(17)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime\mu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}{\bar{\psi}}\{\gamma^{\mu},\sigma^{\rho\sigma}\}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\rho\sigma\nu}{\bar{\psi}}\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{5}\psi,

(18)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is a new Lagrangian,

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}={\bar{\psi}}\left[\frac{i}{2}(\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu})-m_{0}\right]\psi+\frac{G_{\pi}}{2}\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi\right)^{2}\right].

(19)

The difference is that
\Sigma^{\prime 003}=0

\displaystyle H^{\prime}-aK^{\prime z}-\Omega J^{\prime z}

(20)

\displaystyle-(1+az)\frac{G_{\pi}}{2}\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}i\gamma_{5}\psi\right)^{2}\right]-\Omega\psi^{\dagger}\left[-ix\partial_{y}+iy\partial_{x}+\frac{\sigma^{z}}{2}\right]\psi\Big\}.

III
Rindler coordinates

The spacetime geometry in Rindler coordinates is characterized by the metric (see Appendix
A
for a derivation)

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\xi^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}-\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}-\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}-\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}.

(21)

A particle at rest in Rindler coordinates corresponds to the one undergoing constant proper acceleration in Minkowski spacetime. For a particle under proper acceleration
a>0
, the worldline is given by
t=a\,\tau
,
\xi=1/a
, where
\tau
denotes the proper time. To describe finite-temperature systems, a Euclidean formulation can be obtained via analytic continuation
t_{E}=it
. A system with proper acceleration
a
and temperature
T
is described by the Euclidean metric:

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\mathrm{d}s_{E}^{2}=-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\mathrm{d}t_{E}-\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}-\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}-\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}.

(22)

where
\nu=2\pi T/a
. The Euclidean time
t_{E}
satisfies
0<t_{E}<2\pi
. This geometry represents a two-dimensional conical manifold with angular deficit
2\pi(1-\nu^{-1})

Prokhorov et al. 2023
. To avoid a negative angular deficit, we impose
\nu\geq 1
in this work, which leads to the condition
T\geq T_{U}
. When
T<T_{U}
, the angular deficit becomes negative and the geometric meaning of manifolds becomes unclear. Previous work has shown that negative energy manifests at temperatures
T<T_{U}
, indicating that it is an unstable state
Prokhorov et al. 2019b
. When
T=T_{U}
, the parameter
\nu=1
eliminates the angular deficit, resulting in a flat Euclidean manifold—an outcome consistent with the Unruh effect. A rigorous derivation can be found in Ref.
Unruh and Weiss 1984
.
Let us clarify the roles of observer and observed systems. In Unruh’s scenario, an accelerating observer detects the Minkowski vacuum as a thermal bath at
T=T_{U}
.
In this paper, we consider such an accelerating observer, while the observed system is stationary in the oberver’s frame with temperature
T\geq T_{U}
.

Renormalization schemes are essential in this analysis, as different approaches may yield distinct physical outcomes
Salluce et al. 2024
. We evaluate two choices of renormalization schemes corresponding two viewpoints about the subtracted vacuum.
One viewpoint suggests that, for accelerating observers, physical measurements should be referenced to their
perceived vacuum state (the state containing no detectable particles), which leads to the renormalization scheme
subtracting the Rindler vacuum expectation value
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{R}
:

\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{ren}=\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\beta}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{R}.

(23)

Here
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\beta}
denotes the thermal expectation value of an arbitrary operator
\hat{O}
at temperature
T=1/\beta
.
Note that the vacuum term (
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{R}
) in this scheme is observer-dependent, varying with acceleration. An alternative viewpoint is that the vacuum should be frame-independent and measurements should be referenced to the Minkowski vacuum
Becattini 2018
, leading to the second scheme, subtracting the Minkowski vacuum expectation value

\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{M}
:

\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{ren}=\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\beta}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{M}.

(24)

The first renormalization scheme extends flat-space renormalization intuitively—accelerating observers naturally references their local vacuum rather than the Minkowski vacuum which they perceive as thermal. We therefore tentatively adopt it in subsequent sections. Nevertheless, the second scheme is compelling, preserving frame-independent observables and satisfying the Unruh-Weiss condition where thermal corrections vanish
Unruh and Weiss 1984
. For comparison, we give the results based on this scheme in Sec.
IV.3
.

IV
Chiral condensate under acceleration

IV.1
NL
\sigma
M analysis

We first employ the nonlinear sigma model (NL
\sigma
M) to investigate the effect of acceleration on the chiral condensate. The NL
\sigma
M consists of
N
pion fields
\pi^{a}
(
a=1,\dots,N
) and a single
\sigma
field. The corresponding Lagrangian in a general curved spacetime takes the form:

\mathcal{L}_{NL\sigma M}=-\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{T}\Box\Phi-M^{2}_{\pi}f_{\pi}\sigma,

(25)

where
f_{\pi}
is the pion decay constant, and
\Phi^{T}=(\pi^{a},\sigma)
is a multiplet constrained by the nonlinear condition
\Phi^{T}\Phi=f_{\pi}^{2}
. The operator
\Box
denotes the covariant d’Alembertian acting on scalar fields, defined as:

\Box\phi\equiv g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left[g^{\mu\nu}\sqrt{-g}\partial_{\nu}\phi\right],

(26)

where
\nabla_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative and
g\equiv\det(g_{\mu\nu})
.

To impose the nonlinear constraint within the path integral formalism, we insert a delta functional:

Z=\int\mathcal{D}[\Phi]\delta[\Phi^{T}\Phi-f_{\pi}^{2}]\exp\left(i\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}_{NL\sigma M}\right).

(27)

The constraint is implemented by introducing an auxiliary field
\lambda(x)
. After integrating out the pion fields and taking the large-
N
limit, we arrive at the following effective action:

\Gamma[\sigma,\lambda]=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma\Box\sigma+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\sigma^{2}-f_{\pi}^{2})+\frac{N}{2}\ln\frac{-\Box+\lambda}{-\Box}-M^{2}_{\pi}f_{\pi}\sigma\right).

(28)

The gap equations are obtained by minimizing the effective action with respect to the fields
\sigma
and
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\sigma}=-\Box\sigma+\lambda\sigma-M^{2}_{\pi}f_{\pi}=0,

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\lambda}=\frac{\sigma^{2}-f^{2}_{\pi}}{2}+\frac{N}{2}G(x,x;\lambda)=0,

where the propagator
G(x,x^{\prime};\lambda)
satisfies

(-\Box+\lambda)_{x}G(x,x^{\prime};\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\delta^{4}(x-x^{\prime}).

(30)

In this work, we consider the chiral limit
M_{\pi}=0
, under which the gap equations naturally yield two distinct phases: the chiral restoration phase with
\sigma=0
and the spontaneously broken phase with
\sigma\neq 0
. This framework establishes a direct correspondence between the chiral condensate
\langle\bar{\psi}\psi\rangle
in a fermionic theory and the expectation value
\langle\sigma\rangle=\sigma
solved from gap equations (
29
).

To obtain the propagator, we first consider the solution of Klein-Gordon equation. For the Rindler metric given in Eq. (
21
), the Klein-Gordon equation takes the explicit form:

\left(-\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\xi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{2}^{2}}-m^{2}\right)\phi=0,

(31)

with
m
the mass parameter. Solving this equation yields the eigenfunctions of d’Alembertian:

\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2\omega\sinh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}}K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t+i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}},

(32)

where
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})
is the transverse coordinate, and we define the transverse mass squared as
m_{\perp}^{2}=k^{2}+m^{2}
and
K_{\nu}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Using the integral identity for modified Bessel functions
Candelas and Deutsch 1978
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}xK_{\mu}(\alpha x)K_{\nu}(\alpha x)x^{l}

(33)

\displaystyle=2^{l-2}\alpha^{-l-1}\Gamma(l+1)^{-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{l+\mu+\nu+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{l+\mu-\nu+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{l-\mu+\nu+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{l-\mu-\nu+1}{2}\right),

(valid for
\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)>0
and
\mathrm{Re}(l+\mu+\nu)>-1
), and following the Klein-Gordon inner product in Rindler coordinates
Candelas and Raine 1976
;
Crispino et al. 2008
, we find that the eigenfunctions obey the orthonormality condition
Candelas and Raine 1976
:

(\phi_{i},\phi_{j})_{\text{KG}}=2\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{x}}\sqrt{-g}g^{00}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}=\delta(i,j),

(34)

where
i,j=(\omega,k_{1},k_{2})
labels the eigenmodes of the field
\phi
.
Upon performing the secondary quantization, the scalar field
\phi
becomes an operator:

\hat{\phi}=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\sinh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}}K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi)(\hat{a}_{\omega,k}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t+i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}+\hat{a}_{\omega,k}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{i\omega t-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}),

(35)

where
\hat{a}_{\omega,k}
and
\hat{a}_{\omega,k}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators, respectively. Particles and antiparticles in Rindler coordinates are defined with respect to the Killing vector
\partial/\partial t
through their frequency
\omega
: modes with positive (negative) frequency correspond to particles (antiparticles).
It follows directly that the Euclidean propagator can be written as:

\begin{split}G_{E}(x_{E},x_{E}^{\prime})&=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sinh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi)K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi^{\prime})e^{-\omega|t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}|+i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})},\end{split}

(36)

where
t_{E}
is the Euclidean Rindler time. We emphasize that the above expression for
G_{E}
corresponds to the Rindler vacuum at zero temperature, serving as the baseline for the construction of the thermal propagator and the renormalization scheme discussed later.

In previous studies, the temperature has typically been fixed at
T=T_{U}
, a natural choice from the Unruh effect (see, e.g., Ref.
Casado-Turrión and Dobado 2019
). In this work, we extend the analysis by developing a generalized formalism valid for arbitrary temperatures satisfying
T\geq T_{U}
.

It is important to note that Eq. (
36
) describes the Euclidean propagator without boundary condition, corresponds to a system in the Rindler vacuum at zero temperature, from which we can construct a propagator at any temperature by imposing periodic boundary condition in Euclidean time.
Specifically, the finite-temperature propagator
G_{\nu}
should satisfy the condition
G_{\nu}(t_{E},t_{E}^{\prime})=G_{\nu}(t_{E}+\beta_{R},t_{E}^{\prime})
where
\beta_{R}=1/T_{R}=a/T
and
\nu=2\pi T/a
as defined in Eq. (
22
).

Based on the periodic boundary condition in Euclidean time, the finite-temperature propagator
G_{\nu}
can be constructed from the zero-temperature propagator
G_{E}
in Eq. (
36
) via

\begin{split}G_{\nu}(x_{E},x^{\prime}_{E})&=\sum_{n}G_{E}(t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}+\beta_{R}n)\\
&=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\cosh\omega(|t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}|-\beta_{R}/2)}{\sinh(\beta_{R}\omega/2)}\frac{\sinh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi)K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})}.\\
\end{split}

(37)

Alternatively, this result can be obtained by directly solving the thermal Green’s function equation in Euclidean Rindler space with periodic boundary condition in
t_{E}
:

\left(\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{E}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\xi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{E1}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{E2}^{2}}-m^{2}\right)G(x_{E},x^{\prime}_{E})=-\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x_{E}-x^{\prime}_{E})}{\xi},

(38)

whose solution is

G_{\nu}(x_{E},x^{\prime}_{E})=\sum_{n}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\beta}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i\omega_{n}(t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime})+i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2})}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\omega^{2}}\frac{2\omega\sinh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi)K_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi^{\prime}),

(39)

where
\omega_{n}=2\pi n/\beta_{R}
is the Matsubara frequency of bosons. Performing the frequency summation in Eq. (
39
) yields Eq. (
37
).

For our purpose of studying chiral phase transition, the key quantity is the diagonal part of the propagator:

\begin{split}G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E})&=\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\coth{(\omega\beta_{R}/2)}\,\sinh(\pi\omega)K^{2}_{i\omega}(m_{\perp}\xi).\end{split}

(40)

According to the first gap equation in Eq. (
29
), the order parameter
\sigma
satisfies

-\Box\sigma+\lambda\sigma=0

in the chiral limit (
M_{\pi}\to 0
). Although both
\lambda
and
\sigma
generally depend on position due to the inhomogeneous nature of acceleration, we focus on phase transitions evaluated at the observer’s instantaneous location (with a fixed
\xi
). Under the slow-variation approximation (
\Box\sigma\approx 0
), the gap equation simplifies to
\lambda=0
and
\sigma^{2}=f_{\pi}^{2}-NG_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)
, and the phase structure can be determined by evaluating the diagonal part of the massless scalar propagator at the observer’s location. Applying the integral identity,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}xK_{i\mu}(\alpha x)K_{i\mu}(\alpha x)x=\frac{\pi\mu}{2\alpha^{2}\sinh(\pi\mu)},

(41)

valid for
-1<\mathrm{Im}(\mu)<1
and
\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)>0
, we can derive the diagonal part of the massless propagator as:

\displaystyle G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)}\coth{(\omega\beta_{R}/2)}\frac{\pi\omega}{2\xi^{2}}

(42)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\frac{\omega}{4\pi^{2}a^{2}\xi^{2}}+\int^{\infty}_{0}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\frac{\omega}{4\pi^{2}a^{2}\xi^{2}}\frac{2}{e^{\omega\beta}-1}.

In the last line, we have applied a change of variables
\omega\to\omega/a
, which facilitates ultraviolet regularization via an
a
independent energy cutoff
\Lambda
.
To obtain a finite result, we subtract the divergent, temperature-independent part of
G_{\nu}
(the first term in the second line of Eq. (
42
)), corresponding to the vacuum contribution. This accounts to the a subtraction
G_{\nu}^{ren}=G_{\nu}-G_{\nu=0}
. A more detailed discussion of the renormalization scheme can be found in Sec.
IV.3
.
Accordingly, the renormalized diagonal part of the propagator takes the form:

G^{ren}_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=\frac{\nu^{2}}{48\pi^{2}\xi^{2}}.

(43)

Note that this subtraction procedure can also be viewed as a renormalization procedure to the phenomenological constant,
f^{2}_{\pi}\to f^{2}_{\pi}+\frac{N\Lambda^{2}}{8\pi^{2}a^{2}\xi^{2}}
. Substituting this into the gap equation (
29
), we obtain the expression for the order parameter
\sigma
:

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}=f_{\pi}^{2}-\frac{T^{2}}{a^{2}\xi^{2}}\frac{N}{12}.\end{split}

(44)

This shows that, as the temperature increases, the chiral condensate
\sigma
decreases and eventually vanishes.
We define the critical temperature at which
\sigma
reaches
0
as

\begin{split}T_{c}(\xi)=a\xi\sqrt{\frac{12f_{\pi}^{2}}{N}}.\end{split}

(45)

This result is consistent with earlier study
Casado-Turrión and Dobado 2019
.

As emphasized previously, our analysis focuses on the observer’s instantaneous local position. The worldline of an observer of constant proper acceleration
a
is given by
t=a\tau
and
\xi=1/a
, as discussed in Sec.
III
.
Thus we find that the critical temperature observed by this observer becomes independent of its proper acceleration:
T_{c}(\xi=1/a)=\sqrt{12f_{\pi}^{2}/N}\equiv T_{c0}
, indicating that the phase transition depends solely on the local temperature experienced by the accelerated observer, not on the proper acceleration of the observer itself. Note that thus Eq. (
45
) can be rewritten as
T_{c}(\xi)/a\xi=T_{c0}
with the left-hand side representing the Tolman-Ehrenfest critical temperature. Therefore the chiral phase transition happens at the same Tolman-Ehrenfest temperature showing that local observers at different
\xi
agree with the critical temperature for chiral phase transition. We will revisit and further interpret this result after evaluating the fermionic case in the next subsection.

IV.2
NJL model analysis

We next turn to the quark degree of freedom to investigate how acceleration affects fermions.
In contrast to the scalar case, a key distinction lies in the coupling between acceleration and fermion spin for some choices of pseudo-gauge, analogous to the spin-rotation coupling in a rotating system.
Here we start with the single-flavor NJL model, whose dynamics in a general spacetime are governed by the curved-spacetime version of Lagrangian (
8
):

\mathcal{L}_{NJL}=\bar{\psi}\left[i\gamma^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}-m_{0}\right]\psi+\frac{G_{\pi}}{2}\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi\right)^{2}\right].

(46)

The covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}
, which encodes the effects of curved spacetime on fermions, will be defined explicitly in the following discussion.
From the Lagrangian in Eq. (
46
), the corresponding generating functional or partition function is given by:

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[\bar{\psi},\psi]\exp\left(i\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NJL}}\right)

(47)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[\bar{\psi},\psi,\sigma,\pi]\exp\left\{i\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\bar{\psi}\left(i\gamma^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}-m-i\gamma^{5}\pi\right)\psi-\frac{\sigma^{2}+\pi^{2}}{2G_{\pi}}\right]\right\},

where the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation introduces auxiliary fields
\sigma\equiv-G_{\pi}\bar{\psi}\psi
and
\pi\equiv-G_{\pi}\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi
, with the constituent quark mass defined as
m=m_{0}+\sigma
.
Within the mean-field approximation, we derive the spacetime-dependent effective potential density, which is given by:

V_{eff}(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}+\pi^{2}}{2G_{\pi}}-\frac{1}{i}\operatorname{Tr}\ln\left(i\gamma^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}-m-i\gamma^{5}\pi\right).

(48)

Minimizing the effective potential density leads to the gap equations:

\frac{m-m_{0}}{G_{\pi}}=i\operatorname{Tr}(S),\quad\frac{\pi}{G_{\pi}}=i\operatorname{Tr}(i\gamma^{5}S),

(49)

where the fermion propagator
S
satisfies:

\left(i\gamma^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}-m-i\gamma^{5}\pi\right)_{x}S(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\delta^{4}(x,x^{\prime}).

(50)

Without loss of generality, here we can set
\pi=0
due to the
U(1)
chiral symmetry and focus solely on
\sigma
.
Following general formalism of quantum field theory in curved spacetime,
\gamma^{\mu}=e^{\mu}_{\hat{m}}\gamma^{\hat{m}}
represent the spacetime-dependent gamma matrices and
\gamma^{\hat{m}}
represent the gamma matrices in Minkowski spacetime. And we choose the vierbein
e^{\mu}_{\hat{m}}
as

e^{0}_{\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{\xi}\,,\,e^{i}_{\hat{0}}=0,e^{0}_{\hat{j}}=0,e^{i}_{\hat{j}}=\delta^{i}_{j}.

(51)

where
i,j=1,2,3
with
3
for
\xi
coordinate. The covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}
and spin connection
\Gamma_{\mu}
satisfy:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+\Gamma_{\mu},\quad\Gamma_{\mu}=-\frac{i}{4}\omega_{\mu\hat{\nu}\hat{\rho}}\sigma^{\hat{\nu}\hat{\rho}},\quad\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\frac{i}{2}[\gamma^{\hat{\mu}},\gamma^{\hat{\nu}}],\quad\omega_{\mu\hat{\kappa}\hat{\eta}}=g_{\nu\rho}e^{\nu}_{\hat{\kappa}}\nabla_{\mu}e^{\rho}_{\hat{\eta}}.

(52)

For the metric specified in Eq. (
21
), the corresponding gamma matrices take the form:

\begin{gathered}\gamma^{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\xi}{\gamma^{\hat{0}}},\quad\gamma^{i}(x)=\gamma^{\hat{i}},\end{gathered}

(53)

with the non-zero spin connection component:

\Gamma_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}.

(54)

Note that the spin connection introduces a spin-dependent term in the partition function [Eq. (
47
)] and in the propagator [see Eq. (
50
) and also Eq. (
55
) below]. Such a term does not appear in
Becattini 2018
;
Prokhorov et al. 2019c
, where accelerating fermions are treated via the density operator. In fact, this extra contribution can be removed through a pseudo-gauge transformation, as we have shown in Sec.
II
. The resultant theory is equivalent to replacing
\mathcal{L}_{NJL}
in Eq. (
46
) by
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{NJL}=(\mathcal{L}_{NJL}+\mathcal{L}_{NJL}^{\dagger})/2
. Note that the equation of motion is unchanged by the pseudo-gauge transformation, and therefore both
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{NJL}
and
\mathcal{L}_{NJL}
yield the same propagator. We will hance focus solely on
\mathcal{L}_{NJL}
in the following.

We now return to the discussion of the propagator. Based on the above setup, the explicit expression of Eq. (
50
) is:

{\left[\frac{\gamma^{\hat{0}}}{\xi}\left(i\partial_{0}+\frac{i}{2}\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}\right)+i\gamma^{\hat{i}}\partial_{i}-m\right]S(x,x^{\prime})\equiv(\hat{D}-m)S(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\delta^{4}(x,x^{\prime}).}

(55)

Since both
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{NJL}
and
\mathcal{L}_{NJL}
yield the same equations of motion,
S(x,x^{\prime})
is also the propagator for
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{NJL}
.
To solve this equation, we adopt the ansatz that the propagator can be expressed as

S(x,x^{\prime})=(\hat{D}+m)G(x,x^{\prime}),

(56)

with
G(x,x^{\prime})
an auxiliary propagator which satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\hat{D}^{2}-m^{2}\right)G(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\delta(x,x^{\prime}),

(57)

where the squared Dirac operator
\hat{D}^{2}
explicitly takes the form:

\hat{D}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\left(i(i\partial_{0})-\frac{\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}}{2}\right)^{2}+\partial_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{\xi}\partial_{3}+\partial_{1}^{2}+\partial_{2}^{2}.

(58)

In comparison to the scalar case, a only distinguishing feature is the presence of the
\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}
term, which reflects the contribution from spin. Therefore, the propagator can be constructed by the eigenmodes of Klein-Gordon operator with
i\omega\rightarrow i\omega-{\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}}/{2}
:

e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\mathbb{k}\cdot\mathbb{x}}K_{i\omega-{\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}}/{2}}(m_{\perp}\xi).

(59)

For later use, it is useful to notice that, for a function containing
\gamma^{0}\gamma^{3}
, it can always be decomposed as

f(\gamma^{0}\gamma^{3})=P^{+}f(1)+P^{-}f(-1),

(60)

where
P^{\pm}=(1\pm\gamma^{0}\gamma^{3})/2
are two projections. Therefore, the auxiliary propagator in Euclidean Rindler spacetime is directly obtained from Eq. (
36
). The result is

G_{E}(x_{E},x_{E}^{\prime})=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sinh\pi(\omega+i\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2)}{\pi^{2}}K_{i\omega-\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2}(m_{\perp}\xi)K_{i\omega-\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2}(m_{\perp}\xi^{\prime})e^{-\omega|t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}|+i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})}.

(61)

By applying the fermionic anti-periodic boundary condition
G_{\nu}(t_{E},t_{E}^{\prime})=-G_{\nu}(t_{E}+\beta_{R},t_{E}^{\prime})
, the thermal propagator
G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E}^{\prime})
can be constructed in a manner analogous to the scalar case:

\begin{split}G_{\nu}&=\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}G_{E}(t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}+\beta_{R}n)\\
&=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sinh(\beta_{R}\omega/2-\omega|t_{E}-t_{E}^{\prime}|)}{\cosh(\beta_{R}\omega/2)}\frac{\sinh\pi(\omega+i\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2)}{\pi^{2}}K_{i\omega-\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2}(m_{\perp}\xi)K_{i\omega-\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}}/2}(m_{\perp}\xi^{\prime})e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})}.\end{split}

(62)

The diagonal part of the propagator becomes:

G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E})=\sum_{s=\pm}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sinh({\beta_{R}}\omega/2)}{\cosh({\beta_{R}}\omega/2)}\frac{is\cosh\pi\omega}{\pi^{2}}K^{2}_{i\omega-s/2}(m_{\perp}\xi).

(63)

As before, we consider the chiral limit and focus on the phase boundary where
m=0
,

\displaystyle G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\frac{\omega}{\pi^{2}\xi^{2}}\tanh({\beta_{R}}\omega/2)

(64)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\omega\frac{\omega}{\pi^{2}a^{2}\xi^{2}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\frac{\omega}{\pi^{2}a^{2}\xi^{2}}\frac{2}{e^{\beta\omega}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6a^{2}\xi^{2}}\left(\frac{3\Lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-T^{2}\right).

In the second line, we have performed a variable substitution
\omega\to\omega/a
and introduced an acceleration-independent cutoff
\Lambda
to regularize the divergent term (the first term), which corresponds to the Rindler vacuum contribution,
G_{\nu=0}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)
.
Substituting this result into the gap equation Eq. (
49
) and using the properties that the trace of odd-number gamma matrices vanishes, we have

\frac{1}{G_{\pi}}=\frac{1}{6a^{2}\xi^{2}}\left(\frac{3\Lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-T^{2}\right)

(65)

Therefore, we get the critical temperature:

T_{c}(\xi)=a\xi\sqrt{\frac{3\Lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-\frac{6}{G_{\pi}}}.

(66)

This expression agrees with the result in Ref.
Ohsaku 2004
.
Furthermore, by substituting the accelerated observer’s worldline condition
\xi=1/a
, one finds that the critical temperature is independent of the acceleration
a
,
T_{c}(\xi=1/a)=\sqrt{{3\Lambda^{2}}/{\pi^{2}}-{6}/{G_{\pi}}}\equiv T_{c0}
. As discussed in Section
III
, renormalization schemes are not unique. In Sec.
IV.3
, we present a supplemental alternative scheme that yields results different from those in this subsection and in Sec.
IV.1
.

Figure
1
shows the phase diagram calculated from Eqs. (
45
) and (
66
).
The blue region corresponds to the phase with spontaneously broken chiral symmetry, the white region indicates restored symmetry, and the grey region marks the unphysical domain where
T<T_{U}
.
Although it may be intuitive to expect acceleration-induced thermal effects to lower the critical temperature, our results reveal that the critical temperature is, in fact, independent of acceleration, as along as
T_{c0}>T_{U}
.
As shown in Eqs. (
45
) and (
66
), the phase transition is governed solely by the temperature perceived by an accelerated observer with respect to the Rindler vacuum. Acceleration manifests its influence on phase transitions only indirectly via the temperature dependence. For instance, if one assumes the accelerated observer’s temperature to be fixed to the Unruh relation
T=T_{U}=a/2\pi
, as has been considered in Refs.
Ohsaku 2004
;
Ebert and Zhukovsky 2007
;
Castorina and Finocchiaro 2012
;
Casado-Turrión and Dobado 2019
;
Basu et al. 2023
, one can determine a critical acceleration
a_{c}=2\pi T_{c}
, above which(
a>a_{c}
) chiral symmetry is restored.

In Secs.

24
)].

We consider the bosonic case first. In Sec.
IV.1
, we regularize
G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)
by subtracting its Rindler vacuum counterpart
G_{\nu=0}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=\langle 0_{R}|\phi^{2}(x_{E};m=0)|0_{R}\rangle

G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)

G_{\nu=1}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=\langle 0_{M}|\phi^{2}(x_{E};m=0)|0_{M}\rangle
with
|0_{M}\rangle
the Minkowski vacuum state. The result is

G^{ren}_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=G_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)-G_{\nu=1}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=\frac{\nu^{2}-1}{\xi^{2}}\frac{1}{48\pi^{2}}.

(67)

Under this renormalization scheme, Eq. (
44
) is modified into

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=f^{2}_{\pi}-\frac{1}{(a\xi)^{2}}\frac{N}{12}\left(T^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\right).

(68)

Substituting the accelerating observer’s world line
\xi=1/a
, it shows that the acceleration would enhance the chiral symmetry breaking
Chernodub 2025
. Accordingly, the critical temperature is

\displaystyle T_{c}(\xi=1/a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{12f^{2}_{\pi}}{N}+(\frac{a}{2\pi})^{2}}

(69)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T_{c0}^{2}+(\frac{a}{2\pi})^{2}}.

This result is consistent with Ref.
Chernodub 2025
.

Similarly, we can also apply the same renormalization scheme to the fermionic case. It is easily to verified that, the Eq. (
64
) is modified to

G^{ren}_{\nu}(x_{E},x_{E};m=0)=\frac{1}{6a^{2}\xi^{2}}\left(\frac{3\Lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-T^{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\right).

(70)

And the critical temperature for the fermion case is

\displaystyle T_{c}(\xi=1/a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{3\Lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-\frac{6}{G}+\frac{a^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}}

(71)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T_{c0}^{2}+(\frac{a}{2\pi})^{2}},

which coincide with Eq. (

). The corresponding phase diagram calculated from Eq. (

) and Eq. (

) is shown in Fig.

V
NJL model under acceleration and rotation

In this section, we investigate NJL model under both acceleration and rotation. For simplicity, we assume that the direction of acceleration is aligned with the rotation axis.
The spacetime metric describing such an accelerating and rotating frame is given by:

\displaystyle g_{00}

\displaystyle=\xi^{2}-(\bm{\bar{\Omega}}\times\bm{x})^{2},

(72)

\displaystyle g_{0i}

\displaystyle=g_{i0}=-(\bm{\bar{\Omega}}\times\bm{x})^{i},

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\delta_{ij},

where
i,j=1,2,3
, with dimensionless angular velocity
\bm{\bar{\Omega}}=(0,0,\bar{\Omega})
aligned with the acceleration axis and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\xi)
. The dimensionless angular velocity obeys
\bar{\Omega}={\Omega}/a
, where
\Omega
is the usual energy-dimension angular velocity.
The vierbein
e^{\mu}_{\hat{m}}
for acceleration and rotation are

e^{0}_{\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{\xi}\,,\,e^{i}_{\hat{0}}=-\frac{(\bm{\bar{\Omega}}\times\bm{x})^{i}}{\xi},e^{0}_{\hat{j}}=0,e^{i}_{\hat{j}}=\delta^{i}_{j}.

(73)

Accordingly, the spacetime-dependent gamma matrices are

\begin{gathered}\gamma^{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\xi}{\gamma^{\hat{0}}},\quad\gamma^{i}(x)=\frac{(\bm{\bar{\Omega}}\times\bm{x})^{i}}{\xi}{\gamma^{\hat{0}}}+\gamma^{\hat{i}},\end{gathered}

(74)

and the only nonzero spin connection is

\Gamma_{0}=-\frac{i}{2}\bm{\bar{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\hat{0}}\gamma^{\hat{3}},

(75)

where
\bm{\sigma}
denotes

VI
summary

In this study, we investigate chiral symmetry breaking behavior for accelerating and rotating systems using effective models in non-inertial frames. We solve the Klein-Gordon and Dirac equations in an accelerating frame and construct the phase diagram in the
T
–
a
plane.
Our analysis confirms that the choice of renormalization scheme can affect the physical implications of acceleration on chiral symmetry breaking. Using the renormalization scheme in Eq. (
23
), we find that phase transitions depend solely on the temperature
T
as measured by the observer as long as
T_{U}<T
.
The role of acceleration is encoded through its relation to
T
. In particular, when the Unruh relation
T=a/2\pi
is imposed, acceleration induces chiral symmetry restoration via effective thermalization. On the other hand, using the renormalization scheme as given in Eq. (
24
), similar to Ref.
Chernodub 2025
, an acceleration induced enhancement of chiral symmetry breaking is obtained.

We further extend our analysis to frames with both rotation and acceleration. Two restoration mechanisms are identified: one from the thermal effect induced by acceleration (at fixed temperature
T=T_{U}
), and the other from the rotation-induced modification of the “chemical potential”, consistent with previous findings (e.g., Ref.
Chen et al. 2021a
for a review). We further define the critical acceleration
a_{c}
and derive its dependence on the angular velocity
\Omega
. Analytical solutions to the gap equation reveal that increasing
\Omega
reduces
a_{c}
, and this suppression becomes more pronounced at larger radial distances.

Several open issues remain unresolved. While our calculations clearly demonstrate that different renormalization schemes—whether defined with respect to the Minkowski vacuum or the Rindler vacuum—can lead to dramatically different physical conclusions, the physical interpretation of these results and their interrelationships remain obscure. This ambiguity underscores the need for further investigation. We leave the resolution of these fundamental issues, along with the exploration of deconfinement transitions under acceleration, to future study.
