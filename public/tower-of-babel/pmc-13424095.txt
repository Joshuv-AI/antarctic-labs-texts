Ambivalent self-concept and feared self mediates the relationship between negative early life experiences and obsessive-compulsive symptoms
Authors: Isabella Gomez, Michaela Peck, Mathew D Marques, Maja Nedeljkovic, Jessica R Grisham, Ritika Sukhani, Shiu Fung Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe feared self, the self that a person fears they might be or become, has been increasingly shown to drive the development and maintenance of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). However, there is little empirical research on how this feared self develops. Aardema and Wong’s cognitive-behavioural theory of OCD proposes that negative early life experiences (e.g., childhood trauma) can disrupt healthy self-concept development, leading to an ambivalent self-concept and the feared self, and consequently OCD symptoms. We investigated these hypothesised relationships using a cross-sectional design and online self-report with non-clinical participants (N = 242). Data was analysed using path analysis which indicated multiple significant indirect effects. Self-reported experiences of authoritarian parenting and negative perceptions of pubertal timing significantly predicted greater OCD symptom severity through self-ambivalence and the corrupted feared self. These findings suggest that certain negative early life experiences could be associated with OCD symptoms through its effects on self-concepts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54202-y.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Feared self, Self-ambivalence, Negative early life experiencesSubject terms: Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental disorder characterised by obsessions and/or compulsions1. Obsessions are recurring and intrusive thoughts, images, or impulses. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to the specific obsessional threat and associated distress1. Content of obsessions are highly idiosyncratic but commonly grouped under four themes: order/symmetry, contamination, repugnant/obsessions, and harm to self or others1. OCD is associated with significantly reduced quality of life2, necessitating the development of effective treatments3. These treatments should be based on sound psychological theories outlining potential causal and maintaining mechanisms4.

Aardema and Wong5 proposed a novel cognitive-behavioural theory of OCD which emphasises the causal role of the feared self, which is a person’s fear of who they might be or become (e.g., ‘I might be a dangerous person’). The feared self is proposed to result in hypervigilance towards obsessional triggers (e.g., pedestrians on the sidewalk) and drives the idiosyncratic theme of obsessions (e.g., intrusive image of driving into a pedestrian). The individual misappraises the obsessions as personally significant, such that their occurrence validates their feared self (e.g., “Only a dangerous person would have these thoughts”). Therefore, the feared self is what ultimately drives emotional distress (e.g., fear, shame) and the performance of compulsions (e.g., avoiding driving cars, checking for bodies on the street). There is growing empirical evidence for this model and the role of the feared self in OCD. It is a significant and unique predictor of OCD symptoms in clinical and non-clinical populations6–17, with experimental studies showing this relationship to be causal in nature10,18.

Three domains of the feared self have been identified to be relevant to obsessive-compulsive and related disorders: the corrupted feared self, the culpable feared self, and the malformed feared self12. These are currently assessed using the Fear-of-Self Questionnaire—Multidimensional Version12, with studies including this self-report measure showing that the corrupted feared self (e.g., dangerous, tainted, insane) is the strongest predictor of all subtypes of OCD symptoms, followed by the culpable feared self (e.g., negligent, untrustworthy, inattentive), and the malformed feared self as the weakest predictor [e.g., unappealing, ugly, hideous; 6, 8, 12]. Despite the burgeoning empirical evidence for the significant role of the feared self in OCD, there is none on how this self-concept develops in the first place. According to Aardema and Wong5, the feared self develops from an ambivalent self-concept (or self-ambivalence) resulting from a disruption of the normative process of self-concept formation during critical periods, such as early childhood or early adulthood5.

Briefly, self-ambivalence is characterised as competing and contradicting self-perceptions [e.g., “I am a moral and immoral person”19–21. When measured using the Self-Ambivalence Measure21, self-ambivalence has been found to be greater in OCD populations19,21; is a positive predictor of OCD symptom severity19,21,22; reductions in self-ambivalence predict reductions in OCD symptom severity23; and is significantly associated with the feared self7,11.

Early life environment, biology, family dynamics, and comparison to peers have all been proposed to influence self-concept development24,25. Specifically, self-concept formation is proposed to be disrupted by negative early life experiences such as adverse childhood experiences26; negative parenting styles27–30; negative perceptions of pubertal timing31–33; and loneliness34–36.

Adverse childhood experiences refer specifically to childhood trauma and risky family environments37,38, which are associated with OCD phenomenology including higher prevalence39, greater symptom severity40,41, earlier onset42, and increased treatment resistance41. Importantly, it has been suggested that adverse childhood experiences such as trauma and neglect may cause fragmentation of one’s self-concept26,29,35 and have been associated with more self-reported conflicts in self-perception [i.e., self-ambivalence43.

Negative parenting styles have been associated with mental health problems, including OCD27,44–48. The authoritarian parenting style is highly demanding, strict, controlling, non-responsive, and lacking in warmth28,49,50, whereas the permissive parenting style is less demanding, strict, and controlling28,49,50. Unsurprisingly, there are reported differences between the effect of mother’s and father’s parenting styles on OCD symptoms44,51. Specifically, the most common parenting styles experienced by people with OCD is the authoritarian father and permissive mother45. Although both authoritarian and permissive parenting styles are associated with issues with self-concept in the general population28,30,52–54, it is currently unknown whether this applies to the self-concept of OCD individuals. We propose that children, who integrate their parent’s positive and negative feedback into their self-concept55, may develop OCD when consistently told they are “bad” or “immoral” by their authoritarian parent(s). On the other hand, inconsistencies in control and warmth may confuse a child’s self-concept27,28,30,56. Indeed, Guidano and Liotti20 proposed that children who have parents who communicate contradictory messages about their self-concept (e.g., loved and unloved) develop compulsions as an attempt to stabilise their ambivalent self-concept.

A developmental phase that is key to self-concept is puberty. Puberty is a time of dramatic physical, cognitive, and emotional change, and one’s self-concept during this period is largely influenced by their evaluations of these changes. One such evaluation is whether their pubertal development is occurring at the same pace or earlier/later than their peers31,57. Indeed, perceptions of not starting puberty on-time is associated with poorer self-concept31–33,58–61.

Feelings of loneliness, arising from a perceived disparity between a person’s desired and actual social interactions62, may discourage socialisation with others which limits one’s opportunities for external validation of and feedback on developing self-concepts in childhood34,36. Indeed, higher levels of adult loneliness are associated with issues with self-concept34–36,63–67 and poor mental health outcomes like OCD35,68–70. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no research on retrospective accounts of early experiences of loneliness and its relation to mental disorders and self-concept.

The aim of the current study was to investigate the relationships between negative early life experiences (childhood trauma, risky families, authoritarian and permissive parenting styles, early and late pubertal timing, and loneliness), self-ambivalence, feared self, and OCD symptom severity. Negative early life experiences may be a potential risk factor for OCD through ambivalent and feared self. If so, prevention and early intervention strategies should address the effects of these experiences on OCD by addressing these self-concepts. It was first hypothesised that these variables would be significantly positively correlated with each other. We then hypothesised that the relationship between negative early life experiences and OCD symptom severity would be significantly mediated by self-ambivalence and a feared self, particularly the corrupted feared self. It was further hypothesised that these indirect effects would remain significant after controlling for key cognitive constructs emphasised by traditional aetiological theories of OCD (i.e., obsessive beliefs).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the relationship between negative early life experiences, self-ambivalence, feared self, and OCD symptom severity. Results largely supported our hypotheses, and by extension, is consistent with the feared self model of OCD5. First, childhood trauma, risky family environment, authoritarian parenting, and perceived pubertal timing were all significantly associated with self-ambivalence, the corrupted and culpable feared self, and OCD symptom severity. This replicated previous findings in the literature28,31,33,46,61,71,72. Second, authoritarian parenting and negative perceptions of pubertal timing were significantly associated with greater OCD symptom severity through greater self-ambivalence and the corrupted feared self.

Despite mixed findings in the literature, our findings are consistent with the link between authoritarian parenting style and OCD46,73, and between authoritarian parenting style and ambivalent self-concept28,52,53. Authoritarian parenting is characterised by a lack of warmth and warmth is an influential factor in self-concept development and, more specifically, self-ambivalence20,24,74,75. In other words, the lack of affection from a parent could be internalised into a child’s self-concept (e.g., “I am not loved”) as well as contribute to an environment where it is not safe for them to explore and consolidate their self-concept, leading to self-ambivalence. Authoritarian parenting is also characterised by control, which also manifests in OCD compulsions44,76. Stable self-concept development is, in part, achieved by allowing children to feel competent and autonomous77. As such, too much or too little control interferes with this and can lead to ambivalence in the self-concept74.

The effect of negative perceptions of pubertal timing effect on poor self-concept and OCD symptom severity could be attributed to the period of development at which it occurs, since adolescence is a critical development period for self-concept78–80. Specifically, an individual who perceives their physical development as being different to their peers may struggle in developing a coherent sense of self, which leaves them vulnerable to later developing a feared self-concept (e.g., from “There might be something wrong with me” to “I am a monster”).

Contrary to predictions, childhood trauma and risky family environment did not significantly predict OCD symptom severity through self-ambivalence and the feared self. This is inconsistent with the finding that global self-concept mediates the relationship between childhood trauma and mental health outcomes81. Differences in the measured self-concept constructs may explain this discrepancy. Indeed, self-concept clarity is associated with adverse childhood experiences and mediates the relationship between these experiences and OCD symptoms82. This differs from the measurement of vulnerable self-concept in this study (i.e., measurement of self-ambivalence). In addition, as clinical samples report having had more of these adverse childhood experiences71,83,84, the use of a non-clinical sample in this study may have affected the ability to detect significant indirect effects. Also contrary to predictions, the culpable feared self did not act as a significant mediator for any of the indirect effects. However, our finding is consistent with studies suggesting that the corrupted feared self is a stronger predictor of OCD symptoms8,12.

Regarding our other exogenous variables, loneliness was not significantly associated with OCD symptom severity, self-ambivalence, or feared self. This is contrary to research showing an association between loneliness and OCD69, and between loneliness and poor self-concept35,68. This is perhaps due to loneliness being a consequence of issues with self-concept and OCD symptoms, rather than a predictor35,68–70. This may also be due to measurement of retrospective loneliness as a child, rather than current state of loneliness as is the case in most previous empirical studies35,68–70. Permissive parenting was also not significantly associated with OCD symptom severity, self-ambivalence, or feared self. While contrary to findings in other studies28,85, an explanation may be the use of a non-clinical sample, as the link between permissive parenting and OCD symptoms occurs primarily in contexts when clinically significant compulsions are accommodated and even encouraged by significant others85.

The findings from the current study suggest that children exposed to an authoritarian parenting style and/or hold negative perceptions of their pubertal timing may be at risk of developing OCD symptoms because of the effects of these experiences on their self-concept, particularly self-ambivalence and a corrupted feared self. Knowing this could help in the early identification of at-risk children and to direct prevention strategies. For instance, group parenting education programs have been shown to effectively and efficiently change child behaviour and anxiety86–88, and so one could be designed to: (1) educate parents about the effects of an authoritarian parenting style on their child’s self-concept and the subsequent development of OCD symptoms; (2) emphasise the importance of warmth, such as through authoritative parenting styles; and (3) to use this parenting style to facilitate the successful navigation of challenges to developing a coherent self-concept during critical periods like puberty (e.g., negative perceptions of pubertal timing). From an intervention perspective, our findings suggest that effective treatment could include the explicit targeting of self-ambivalence and the corrupted feared self such as through cognitive restructuring. Our findings further suggest that a way of restructuring these might be by addressing the memories of events that first preceded these problematic self-concepts. Indeed, Cooper and colleagues89 have developed a short imagery rescripting protocol to address the memories of early life events that informed the development of the feared self. While anxiety and urge to neutralise distressing imagery were reduced more in the neutral imagery condition compared to the rescripting condition, the findings suggest that it does not currently effectively address the feared self.

Despite the promising findings, multiple limitations warrant consideration. First, although non-clinical samples are appropriate for researching OCD-related phenomena90, this study may have yielded different results with a clinical sample. For example, effects related to childhood trauma may have emerged since these experiences are highly prevalent in OCD populations39. As such, future replications of the current study should attempt to include individuals diagnosed with OCD to increase the variability of scores on these measures. Second, the use of a cross-sectional study design prevents us from drawing conclusions around causality and limits generalisability. In other words, our results should be interpreted in terms of direct and indirect correlational relationships and not causal relationships. Despite this, the associations found in this study provides a compelling rationale to conduct a longitudinal or experimental study to investigate these associations further and to establish causality. Random-intercept cross-lagged panel modelling is one statistical method that could be applied to draw stronger evidence about causal relationships from longitudinal data91.

The current study’s sample size was also considerably small relative to the number of participants required to effectively test the large number of variables and pathways, thus impacting our ability to discover significant paths. With a larger sample, more significant patterns and larger effects may emerge. Our current indirect effects are also limited in their generalisability due to being small.

Finally, participants’ scores on the OCD self-report measure were considerably low. Indeed, this restriction in variance may have reduced the strength of correlations and the direct and indirect paths. However, analogue samples (who will have much lower OCD scores) are well-established as appropriate in OCD research as OCD symptoms are sufficiently prevalent in non-clinical samples, though not as severe as those with OCD90. As such, these relationships may in fact be stronger, rather than different, in OCD populations.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the precursors of self-ambivalence, feared self, and OCD symptoms. We have provided further clarity to existing cognitive-behavioural models of OCD by showing that authoritarian parenting and negative perceptions of pubertal timing were significantly associated with OCD symptom severity via self-ambivalence and a corrupted feared self. If these relationships are determined to be causal using longitudinal or experimental designs, then it may be important to consider these precursors in children and individuals with OCD as to direct appropriate resources toward prevention and intervention, respectively. In addition, these prevention and intervention strategies should ideally address self-ambivalence and the feared self.

Measures and materials

Childhood trauma questionnaire [CTQ92. The CTQ is a 25-item self-report measure of early life trauma. The types of trauma measured include emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, and physical and emotional neglect92. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (never true) to 5 (always true). Items are summed together, with higher scores indicating a higher incidence of trauma. In the current study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.74).

Parental authority questionnaire [PAQ50. The PAQ is a 30-item self-report measure of permissive, authoritarian, and authoritative parenting50. The PAQ was completed twice by each participant, once in reference to their mother and once for their father. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores on each subscale indicates a higher prevalence of that parenting style. In the current study, for both mothers and fathers, internal consistency was high on each subscale: Mother’s Permissiveness (Cronbach’s α = 0.84), Authoritarianism (Cronbach’s α = 0.90), and Authoritativeness (Cronbach’s α = 0.91); Father’s Permissiveness (Cronbach’s α = 0.84), Authoritarianism (Cronbach’s α = 0.93), and Authoritativeness (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Pubertal development reference [PDR93. The PDR is an 8-item self-report measure of an individual’s perception of their pubertal development rate relative to their peers93. The PDR was modified so that participants could answer items retrospectively. Items are rated on a 3-point scale, with 1 being ‘ahead of most other kids’, 2 being ‘about the same as other kids’, and 3 being ‘behind most other kids’. As research indicates that both early and late pubertal timing are predictors of negative outcomes such as low self-esteem and mental health problems [e.g., 31, 59], responses of 1 (‘ahead’) and 3 (‘behind’) were both recoded to 1, while 2 (‘about the same’) was recoded to 0. Items were then summed together, with higher total scores indicating that a person perceived their pubertal timing as different than their peers. The PDR had acceptable internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.73).

Peer network and dyadic loneliness scale [PNDLS94. The PNDLS is a 16-item retrospective self-report measure of childhood loneliness94. For each item, individuals first indicate which of the two descriptors of a ‘less lonely’ child or a ‘more lonely’ child they were most like (respectively assigned 1 or 2 points), before rating on a 2-point scale whether that descriptor is ‘sort of true’ (1 point) or ‘really true’ (2 points) for them. As such, the PNDLS becomes a 4-point scale, where a higher score indicates more loneliness experienced as a child. The PNDLS has two loneliness subscales: the peer network subscale and the peer dyadic subscale. The peer network subscale measures a person’s social network, and the peer dyadic subscales measures a person’s friendships with another person. In the current study, the PNDLS has acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.65).

Risky families questionnaire [RFQ95. The RFQ is an 11-item self-report measure of an individual’s perception of whether they grew up in a dysfunctional home between the ages of 5 and 1595,96. The RFQ evaluates dysfunctionality as presence of conflict, aggression, unsupportive relationships, lack of warmth, and neglect. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very often). Items are summed together, with higher scores indicate a higher prevalence of dysfunctional environment. In the current study, the RFQ has acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

Self-ambivalence measure [SAM21. The SAM is a 21-item self-report measure of a person’s ambivalence toward different dimensions of themselves or their self-concept21. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (agree totally). A higher score indicates more self-ambivalence. Items are summed together, with higher scores indicating greater self-ambivalence. In the current study, the SAM had excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Fear of self questionnaire—multidimensional version [FSQ-MV12. The FSQ-MV is a 27-item self-report measure of how much a person fears being or becoming someone who is corrupted, culpable, and/or malformed. Items are rated on a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Items are summed, with higher scores indicate a greater levels of the feared self. In the current study, the malformed feared self was not analysed as it is theoretically linked to symptoms of body dysmorphic disorder whereas the corrupted and culpable feared self are linked to OCD symptoms. In the current study, the FSQ-MV had excellent internal consistency for its total score (Cronbach’s α = 0.97) and for each used subscale: the Corrupted Feared Self (Cronbach’s α = 0.94), and the Culpable Feared Self (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Obsessive beliefs questionnaire—9 [OBQ-997. The OBQ-9 is a 9-item self-report measure of obsessive beliefs, such as responsibility and threat overestimation97. Items are rated on a 7-point scale, ranging from 1 (disagree very much) to 7 (agree very much). Items are summed together, with higher scores indicating elevated levels of obsessive beliefs. In the current study, the OBQ-9 has acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.76).

Obsessive compulsive inventory—12 [OCI-1298. The OCI-12 is a 12-item self-report measure of OCD symptom severity experienced in the past month across four symptom domains: checking, washing, ordering, and obsessing98. Items are rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). A higher score indicates greater OCD symptom severity. In the current study, the OCI-12 had high internal consistency for the overall scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) and for each subscale: washing (Cronbach’s α = 0.75); checking (Cronbach’s α = 0.82); ordering (Cronbach’s α = 0.87); and obsessing (Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Procedure

Two-hundred and seventy-five participants were recruited via the Prolific platform to complete a 90-min survey hosted online on QuestionPro. Eligibility requirements were fluency in English, being over 18 years old, and not currently diagnosed with a mental illness. Participants registered on Prolific could view and sign up for the study, which then redirected them to QuestionPro. After participants provided informed consent, they completed a demographics survey and all the listed measures. Excluding the demographics survey, the presentation order of measures was randomised. After completion of the final measure, participants were debriefed, thanked, and compensated $16.50 AUD for their participation.

Data analytic plan

Participants who only completed the demographics survey were excluded (n = 16), participants who completed the study twice had the less complete response removed (n = 5), and participants who did not respond correctly to the attention checks (e.g., ‘please select ‘Much’ as your answer’) were removed (n = 8). Data was screened for multivariate outliers using Mahalanobis distance99 after which four outliers were identified and removed. The final analyses included 242 participants.

Jamovi was used to test statistical assumptions and conduct correlational and path analyses [SEMLj module100–102. Inspection of histograms showed that all measures, except the OBQ-9 and the PAQ Mother’s Permissive subscale, had skewed distributions and therefore violated assumptions of normality. As most of the measures were not normally distributed, a maximum likelihood estimator was used in the path analysis to address this103. Assumptions of homoscedasticity and linearity were met and assessed through visual inspection of scatterplots. The assumption of multicollinearity was met, as the tolerance value was not less than 0.10 and the VIF value was below the cut-off of 10104.

The endogenous variables of the path analysis were self-ambivalence, the corrupted feared self, culpable feared self, and OCD symptom severity. The exogenous variables were obsessive beliefs, childhood trauma, risky family environment, mother and father’s permissive and authoritarian parenting styles, perceived pubertal timing, and loneliness. The following direct paths were defined: childhood trauma, risky family environment, father’s authoritarian parenting style, mother’s authoritarian parenting style, and negative perceptions of pubertal timing each predicted OCD symptoms. As loneliness, mother’s permissive parenting style, and father’s permissive parenting style were not significantly associated with any endogenous variable (Table 2), they were not included in the final path analysis model. The following indirect paths were defined: childhood trauma, risky family environment, father’s authoritarian parenting style, mother’s authoritarian parenting style, and negative perceptions of pubertal timing each predicted self-ambivalence; self-ambivalence predicted the corrupted feared self, and the culpable feared self; and the corrupted feared self and the culpable feared self each predicted OCD symptoms. To function as a covariate, obsessive beliefs predicted OCD symptom severity. A path model was tested controlling for obsessive beliefs and not. Figures 1 and 2 show the final models controlling and not controlling for obsessive beliefs, respectively, as well as the paths tested.

Correlations were interpreted according to Cohen’s105 interpretation parameters. Acceptable path analysis model fit was determined by 0.92 or higher on the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI); and 0.08 or lower on the Root Moon Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR106–109. Alternative path models were tested to try to improve model fit, but we decided on the models presented in Figs. 1 and 2 because these were derived from the theory. The supplementary materials file includes the fit statistics of an overfitted alternative model.

To establish the robustness of direct and indirect effects, 5000 bootstrapped samples with 99% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CI) were conducted110. Stringent 99% CIs were used to account for an inflated Type 1 error rate due to testing many indirect effects. Direct and indirect effects were determined to be statistically significant if their CIs did not contain 0.
