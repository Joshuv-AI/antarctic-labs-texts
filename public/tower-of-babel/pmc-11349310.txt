Patient reported outcomes measures are infrequently used in clinical studies of heart transplant recipients
Authors: Melissa A Austin, Sarah Chuzi, Thomas M Cascino, Amanda R Vest, Nosheen Reza
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical trials in heart transplantation (HT) recipients have largely focused on objective outcomes such as survival; however, there is a paucity of data regarding the use of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in these studies. We aimed to characterize the use of PROMs in registered clinical studies of HT recipients.MethodsAll clinical studies of adult HT recipients were queried from ClinicalTrials.gov and stratified by inclusion of PROMs. Studies reporting PROMs were identified via specific search terms using the “outcomes measures” field. Summary statistics compared characteristics of studies with and without PROMs.ResultsBetween November 1999 and August 2022, 227 studies of HT recipients were registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. PROMs were included in 11% (n = 24/227) of studies. Studies reporting PROMs were more likely to be conducted outside of the United States (91.7% vs 54.2%, p < 0.001) and report a greater number of primary/secondary outcomes (PROMs: median 7 [interquartile ranges (IQR): 4, 9] vs no PROMs: median 3 [IQR: 2, 6]; p < 0.001). The majority of studies reporting PROMs (58.3%) were initiated after 2020. Twenty-one distinct PROM tools/domains were reported as outcome measures, with the Short Form Health Survey 36 being most frequently used (n = 10/24). Thirteen of the 21 PROMs included questions assessing mental health, whereas only 3 PROM tools were cardiac-specific.ConclusionsAbout 1 in 10 registered clinical trials of HT recipients includes PROMs, and mental health is the most commonly assessed PROM domain. Development and validation of PROM tools is needed to fully assess health-related quality of life in HT recipients.KEYWORDS: heart transplantation, quality of life, clinical trials, patient-reported outcomes

Heart transplantation (HT) is the definitive treatment for end stage heart failure (HF) that currently provides the greatest long-term survival.1 Over 30,000 people in the United States have received heart transplants in the past decade, and the number of transplantations performed each year continues to rise.2 While HT research has historically focused on objective outcomes, such as survival, incidence of rejection, and measures of cardiac function, improved surgical techniques and advancements in post-transplant medical management have begun to shift the focus of post-transplant outcomes research. With a median survival of over 12 years,1 HT recipients and cardiologists are now afforded the time and opportunity to focus on quality of life as opposed to just quantity of life.

There is increasing interest in the inclusion of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in cardiovascular disease research as a method to assess quality of life. PROMs are tools used to gain insight into the most important aspects of health from the patient perspective. They aim to quantify concepts such as well-being, health-related quality of life, and symptomatology in relation to a health condition and/or its therapies, and they are being increasingly recognized in HF outcomes research as integral markers of disease severity and trajectory.3 In HF, examining PROMs has been shown to be feasible and can provide evidence regarding the efficacy and effectiveness of interventions from the patient perspective. The utility and validity of PROMs in monitoring HF severity and progression have been extensively studied, with the most notable emphasis on the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) and Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ).4, 5, 6 Notably, the 2022 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA)/Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA) Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure included a 2A recommendation to include patient-reported health status in the standard assessment of patients with HF.7 While studies have demonstrated improvement in KCCQ and/or MLHFQ scores following advanced HF interventions, such as continuous flow left ventricular assist devices8 and cardiac resynchronization therapy,9 similar studies do not exist for HT recipients. In fact, the 2022 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation guidelines for the care of heart transplant recipients do not address or even mention the use of PROMs.10 This is likely because no PROM tool is currently validated for monitoring patients post-HT, which effectively prohibits the ability to do serial assessments of health-related quality of life over time and limits our full understanding of patient health status trajectory and wellbeing following HT. Moreover, there is a paucity of data regarding the use of existing cardiac and noncardiac PROMs in HT research.

In this context, we aimed to describe the incidence and characteristics of PROM use in registered clinical studies of HT recipients. We hypothesized that the majority of studies would not include PROMs given the historical emphasis on objective outcomes in HT research and current lack of PROM tools validated in HT recipients.

Materials and methods

Registered studies of HT recipients were queried from ClinicalTrials.gov on January 21, 2023. Studies of interest were identified via entering the search terms “heart transplant” OR “cardiac transplant” in the “Conditions or disease” field. The search was limited to adult patients (age > 18 years), no time frame filters were applied, and all studies were included regardless of current status (i.e., completed, active, withdrawn). A subcohort of studies which reported PROMs was identified by entering 61 unique PROM terms or abbreviations in the “Outcome measure” field (Supplemental Material). The PROMs utilized in the search were identified via a literature search of review articles and studies investigating patient-reported outcomes (both cardiology specific as well as more generalized clinical reviews). These search terms spanned from general phrases/words (e.g., “quality of life,” “questionnaire”) to specific, validated tools (e.g., “PHQ-9,” “Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire”).4, 11, 12 Study names and characteristics were exported from the ClinicalTrials.gov website. During the review of each queried study record, translational studies and clinical studies which did not specifically investigate cardiac transplant recipients were excluded. Additionally, study records in the PROM subcohort were reviewed to identify if PROMs were reported as a primary or secondary outcomes. When available, associated publications were reviewed to identify any discrepancies between the intended outcome measures at time of study registration and the PROMs reported at time of publication.

Statistical methods

Study characteristics were summarized and compared between registered studies that did and did not report PROMs using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact/chi square tests for categorical variables. Variables summarized included study-level (type, design, intervention, phase, funding source, sites, year) and patient-level (number and sex of participants) characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as percentages, and continuous data were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The frequencies of individual PROM use in the registered studies were collected and summarized in both table and bar graph form and examined by year. RStudio Version 2022.02.2 Build 485 (Boston, MA) was used for all data analysis and visualization. Publicly available data were used for the analysis; thus, institutional review board approval was not required.

Results

Between November 4, 1999, and August 5, 2022, a total of 274 studies of HT were registered on ClinicalTrials.org. After excluding duplicates, translational studies, and clinical studies not focused on HT recipients, 227 unique studies were included in the analysis (Table 1). Only one study included exclusively male subjects. An average of 9.5 studies were registered each year between 1999 and 2022, with 50% of the studies registered in 2015 or later (Figure 1A).

PROMs were included as primary or secondary outcomes in 24 (10.6%) of the 227 total queried studies. While the overall cohort of studies reported a median of 4 outcomes (IQR: 2, 7), studies including PROMs reported significantly more outcomes compared with those without PROMs (median 7 [IQR: 4, 9] vs median 3 [IQR: 2, 6], p < 0.001). The majority (160/227) of overall studies were interventional in nature, with a similar distribution for studies with and without PROMs (75.5% and 70.0%, respectively). However, there was a significantly different distribution in the type of intervention between studies with and without PROMs (p < 0.001); studies with PROMs included 25% drug and 25% behavioral interventions whereas studies without PROMs included 42% drug and 3% behavioral interventions. Studies including PROMs were more likely to be randomized (58.3% vs 38.4%, p = 0.049) and start after the year 2020 (58.3% vs 13.3%, p < 0.001, Figure 1B). There was no significant difference in the number of enrolled subjects between cohorts (with PROMs: median 62 [IQR: 37, 111] vs without PROMs: median 63 [27,162]; p = 0.882).

While there was no difference in the number of study sites between cohorts (with PROMs: median 1 [IQR: 1.0, 1.2] vs without PROMs: median 1 [1,2]; p = 0.379), studies including PROMs were more likely to be conducted outside of the United States (with PROMs: 91.7% vs without PROMs: 54.2%; p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in the funding body between studies with and without PROMs. The National Institutes of Health cofunded one study with PROMs but was not the sole funding body of any studies including PROMs.

Twenty-one distinct PROM tools/domains were reported as outcome measures, including cardiac-specific questionnaires, general health-related questionnaires, and patient-reported symptoms (Table 2). The most frequently used PROMs were the Short Form Health Survey 36 (SF-36) and EuroQOL-5D instrument (used in 41.7% (10/24) and 25.0% (6/24) of studies, respectively). The major domains assessed by the PROM tools included mental health, physical health, cardiac health, and general quality of life/satisfaction (Table 2, Figure 2). The most frequently assessed domain was mental health, with 13 of the 21 PROMs including questions addressing mental health (i.e., Patient Health Questionnaire 9, Beck Anxiety Inventory). Eight studies included questions about physical (noncardiac) health and 7 PROMs assessed general quality of life or satisfaction. Only 3 PROM tools specifically addressed cardiac health, including the MLHFQ, KCCQ, and Seattle Angina Questionnaire. Three (12.5%) studies with PROMs included a PROM score as a primary outcome, one of which intended to use the MLHFQ to measure the impact of stress management training after HT (currently unknown study status), another which intended to use various PROMs to measure fatigue and mental health following HT (completed, never published), and a third which aimed to demonstrate reliability for the use of several PROM tools/domains in a small cohort of HT recipients.13 The remainder of studies included PROMs as secondary outcomes. Nine of the 24 studies registered as reporting PROMs had published results at the time of the ClinicalTrials.gov search. Of those 9 studies, 5 included PROMs in the associated publication.

Discussion

This study is the first to describe the use of PROMs specifically in registered clinical studies of adult HT recipients. Our search of the ClinicalTrials.gov database resulted in 227 studies of HT recipients; however, only approximately 10% of these studies included PROMs despite the emerging importance of measuring patient-reported health status in chronic cardiovascular disease14 and in research and clinical practice more generally. Our analysis provides insight into the historical and current implementation of PROMs in both clinical practice and research in HT.

First, we found that studies which report a greater total number of outcomes are more likely to report PROMs, and even when PROMs are included, they are usually used as secondary outcomes. This highlights that PROMs have not historically been considered as essential or standalone study outcomes in the evolution of HT research. As our knowledge in other relevant domains such as immunosuppression and organ preservation have matured, patient-centered outcomes research should be a next dimension of investigation in HT to inform drug development, clinical trial design, and ultimately improved patient care. Importantly, we noted that a significantly larger proportion of HT studies reporting PROMs began or will begin after the year 2020. This signifies the growing understanding of PROMs as valid, reproducible, and sensitive tools to assess the patient experience. Moreover, we showed that compared with studies not reporting PROMs, a greater proportion of those with PROMs were randomized. This demonstrates the potential to further expand the design of prospective, randomized trials in HT to include PROM tools, both as pre-HT baseline and at serial follow-up, and promote patient agency.

Next, we observed that clinical studies of HT recipients which include PROMs were more likely to be conducted outside of the United States (U.S.). Notably, the U.S. is responsible for slightly fewer than half of the heart transplants performed world-wide each year,15 which is reflected in the distribution of study site locations in our analysis. However, less than 10% of studies with PROMs included U.S. sites. A potential explanation for this finding might be related to the time frame in which academic and regulatory bodies in other countries recognized the importance and utility of PROMs as compared to the academic and regulatory bodies in the U.S. In 2005, the European Medicines Agency released guidance for using health-related quality of life measures (a specific type of PROM) in drug evaluation, and shortly after, in 2009, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released general guidance on the use of PROMs for supporting medical product labeling claims.16, 17 However, the National Health Services of England was the first to mandate collection of PROMs for common, acute services (i.e., total hip arthroplasty) in 2009.18 The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services only just adopted this same mandate (referred to as a patient-reported outcome-based performance measure) for hip and knee arthroplasty, with mandatory reporting not going into effect until 2027.19 In terms of academic and professional organizations, the European Society of Cardiology formally issued a call to action regarding the comprehensive integration of PROMs in cardiovascular clinical trials and registries in 2014.20 By comparison, cardiology academic bodies in the United States did not publish a formal recommendation for the routine collection of PROMs until the recent 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline for the management of HF (though PROMs were included as a quality measure in the 2020 ACC/AHA Clinical Performance and Quality Measures for Adults with Heart Failure task force report).7, 21 Currently, both the MLHFQ and KCCQ are recognized by the FDA as “qualified medical device development tools” for clinical outcome assessments in heart failure,22 and in 2020 the KCCQ was also recognized as a “qualified drug development tool.”23 Notably, there is currently no recommendation for the collection of PROMs post heart transplantation. While the U.S. has had a historically delayed adoption of PROMs into regular clinical and research practice, recent progress has certainly been made. Thus, it is reasonable to anticipate a rising number of U.S.-based, clinical studies of HT that incorporate PROMs in the coming years.

A third major observation from our study is that the PROMs utilized by registered HT studies were quite variable in both format and domains assessed. We identified 21 different tools or symptom categories used to assess PROMs, each with variable inclusion of cardiac health, physical health, mental health, and quality of life parameters. The most frequently used individual PROM tool in HT studies was the SF-36, which was employed in 10 of the 24 studies including PROMs. Created in 1992 as part of the Medical Outcomes Study, the SF-36 is a multipurpose, generic measure of health that produces 2 summary measures (mental component and physical component).24, 25 The SF-36 has been studied for validity and reliability for assessing general health-related quality of life in ischemic heart disease and HF,26, 27, 28 and has even been used in the validation process for new, cardiac-specific PROMs (i.e., KCCQ and the recent Chronic Heart Failure health-related Quality of Life Questionnaire).29, 30 However, the SF-36 has yet to be validated in HT recipients.

In fact, there are currently no validated or widely preferred patient-reported outcome instruments/questionnaires in the HT recipient population.12, 31 The use of PROMs has, however, been investigated in the context of other procedural interventions for advanced HF. For example, several studies have demonstrated significantly improved MLWHF and/or KCCQ scores in patients who received continuous flow left ventricular assist devices or cardiac resynchronization therapy compared to preprocedural baselines.8, 9 Similar studies do not currently exist in HT recipients, demonstrating a clear gap in the literature and need for more attention and focus to be directed toward ensuring that HTs are improving the aspects of living that patients prioritize quality of life, mental health, etc. Additionally, despite the similarities between symptoms of progressive HF and HT graft failure, there are several experiences, lifestyle changes, and psychological stressors unique to HT recipients which limit the ability to extrapolate PROM tools used in HF to the heart transplant population (i.e., physical/mental trauma of surgery, graft rejection, and chronic immunosuppression). Moreover, it has been shown that the validation processes for the majority of PROMs for cardiovascular disease in general do not address all the psychometric properties recommended in the FDA’s framework for development and validation of PROMs.32 This further complicates the process of extrapolating PROMs validated in other cardiovascular diseases to the HT recipient population even before accounting for the additional complexities of transplant medicine. Thus, there is a clear need to develop and validate PROMs specifically for HT recipients. In addition to the obvious patient-centered benefit of assessing PROMs in HT research, quantifying the magnitude of benefit or decline in health status and/or health-related quality of life after HT will enable a more comprehensive comparison between HT, LVAD and future emerging device therapies for advanced HF. This may encourage the field to move beyond the current framework of treatment selection primarily based upon survival statistics.

Lastly, we observed that mental health was the most commonly assessed domain across the PROMs included in HT studies. There has been extensive research demonstrating the associations between negative psychological factors, such as stress, anxiety, depression, and loneliness, with worse cardiovascular outcomes.33, 34, 35 The American Heart Association released a scientific statement in 2021 recognizing the importance of the “mind-heart-body connection.”36 Specifically in HT recipients, small scale studies have identified an association between post-transplant depression and worse survival, longer hospital stays and increased incidence of acute rejection.37, 38 Additionally, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation guidelines for the care of HT patients include a Class IB recommendation to screen for depressive symptoms before transplant and regularly during long-term follow-up.10 Given the significant implications of poor mental health following HT, implementation and validation of PROMs that include items assessing mental health in routine post-transplant monitoring and evaluation is a logical and vital future step and can enable identification of HT recipients who may benefit from mental health services.

Limitations of this study include potential omission of relevant clinical studies given this is a single database analysis. We intentionally kept the search strategy broad to minimize omission of relevant studies within the ClinicalTrials.gov database. Only a minority of registered studies reporting PROMs had associated publications, limiting assessment of how PROMs were actually incorporated into study design and results.

Overall, we describe the current use of PROMs in registered clinical studies of HT recipients. Only about 1 in 10 clinical studies of HT recipients registered on ClinicalTrials.gov include PROMs, and mental health was the most commonly assessed domain when a PROM was included. There are currently no validated PROMs specifically for monitoring clinical status following HT. Given the scarcity of research regarding the use of PROMs in this population, future studies are vital to develop and validate PROM tools which can measure, prognosticate, and monitor the clinical status and trajectories of HT recipients over time.
