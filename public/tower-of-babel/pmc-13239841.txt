Treatment Outcomes of Presacral Pilonidal Sinus Disease Using Crystallised Phenol: A Single‐Centre Clinical Series
Authors: Berkan Acar, Ali Muhtaroğlu, Murat Akalin, Elif Yilmaz, Mehmet Yildiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Objective: To evaluate short‐term treatment outcomes and treatment burden in patients with presacral pilonidal sinus disease managed with crystallised phenol in a single‐centre clinical series, with particular emphasis on the relationship between pit burden and session requirement. Methods: Consecutive patients treated with crystallised phenol were analysed (n = 40). Demographic and clinical characteristics, symptom duration, pit count, number of phenol sessions (2 vs. 3), inter‐session intervals, and total treatment duration were recorded. All patients completed scheduled clinical reassessment at 5 weeks and 3 months. Treatment‐related abscess and recurrence during follow‐up were documented. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum). Comparisons between the two‐session and three‐session groups were performed with the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Results: The cohort was predominantly male (75%), with a mean age of 29.23 ± 9.85 years and a mean body mass index of 25.81 ± 3.85 kg/m2; 62.5% were smokers. Symptom duration was 6.80 ± 7.20 months, and mean pit count was 1.90 ± 0.93. Two sessions were sufficient in 42.5% (17/40), whereas 57.5% (23/40) required three sessions. The interval between the first and second sessions was 11.32 ± 3.30 days, and the interval between the second and third sessions was 11.96 ± 3.23 days in patients who underwent three sessions; overall treatment duration was 18.2 ± 7.58 days. No patient was lost to follow‐up. Pit burden was the strongest determinant of requiring a third session: pit count was significantly higher in the three‐session group than in the two‐session group (2.39 ± 0.89 vs. 1.24 ± 0.44, p < 0.001), and all patients with ≥ 3 pits required three sessions. Smoking status (p = 1.000) and body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.208) were not associated with session requirement. Treatment‐related abscess developed in 12.5% (5/40), and recurrence during the 3‐month follow‐up was observed in 12.5% (5/40); notably, all such events occurred in the three‐session group (0/17 vs. 5/23, p = 0.061 for both comparisons). Conclusion: In this single‐centre series, crystallised phenol provided acceptable short‐term outcomes and a relatively short treatment course in presacral pilonidal sinus disease. Pit count emerged as the main determinant of requiring a third session, whereas smoking status and BMI were not associated with session requirement. Larger studies with longer follow‐up are needed to clarify predictors of recurrence and to determine whether higher session requirements simply reflect disease complexity or also influence outcome.Keywords: crystallised phenol, pilonidal sinus disease, pit count, presacral, recurrence

Introduction

Pilonidal disease (PD) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the natal cleft that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults. Although benign, it can be disproportionately disabling because of pain, intermittent discharge, abscess formation, repeated clinic visits, prolonged wound care and time away from work or study. Contemporary guidelines emphasise that no single operation suits every patient; outcomes are shaped by disease phenotype, local anatomy and modifiable patient factors [1].

Current understanding supports an acquired rather than congenital origin. Disease appears to begin with follicular occlusion and local inflammation, followed by the ingress of loose hair and debris into vulnerable skin within the natal cleft. This sustains a foreign‐body reaction and promotes sinus formation. In modern surgical literature, this concept is generally discussed through the mechanistic models of Bascom and Karydakis. Bascom described the primary event as follicular disease with secondary hair penetration, whereas Karydakis emphasised the combined effect of loose hair, local friction, negative pressure within the cleft and the susceptibility of natal cleft skin [2]. These mechanisms also explain recurrence: unless pits are eradicated and the local conditions that favour re‐entry—deep cleft, moisture, friction and hair load—are addressed, disease can readily re‐establish itself [3].

Risk factors repeatedly described in contemporary surgical series include male sex, young age, obesity or increased hair density, a deep natal cleft, prolonged sitting and family history. These factors probably interact by increasing hair load, friction and the local forces that favour hair penetration into the skin and perpetuate chronic inflammation. In presacral and sacrococcygeal disease, where shear and moisture are particularly pronounced, these effects may be amplified [4].

The clinical spectrum ranges from asymptomatic midline pits to persistent drainage and acute abscess formation. Patients commonly present with pain or discomfort, often aggravated by sitting, together with a sensation of moisture, malodorous discharge, local swelling and episodes of infection. Recurrent or previously treated disease may show more complex lateral extensions and scarring, which in turn can influence both treatment burden and the risk of recurrence [5].

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and rests on careful examination of the natal cleft for midline pits, secondary openings and signs of active inflammation. In presacral or peri‐anal adjacent presentations, assessment of the perianal region is important, and mapping disease extent may help both procedural planning and differential diagnosis. According to current European Society of Coloproctology (ESCP) guidance, serum tests and imaging are not routinely required; imaging is reserved for selected acute presentations or when another anorectal pathology, such as fistula‐in‐ano, is suspected [1].

The aims of treatment are straightforward: eliminate pits and tracts, minimise wound‐related morbidity, shorten time away from normal activity and reduce recurrence. Acute abscess is usually managed with lateral incision and drainage, with definitive treatment deferred until inflammation has settled [1, 6]. For chronic disease, options range from conservative care in asymptomatic patients to minimally invasive procedures and excisional surgery. When excision is required, current evidence favours off‐midline closure over primary midline closure because healing is faster and wound‐related complications are fewer [1, 7].

Current minimally invasive approaches include pit‐picking, endoscopic techniques such as EPSiT, phenolisation and laser‐based methods. These strategies aim to preserve tissue while clearing hair and debris from the sinus cavity and reducing the burden of postoperative wound care. According to the ESCP guideline, such options may be appropriate in carefully selected patients, particularly when the disease is limited to midline pits with minimal lateral extension. A 2023 systematic review of non‐excisional techniques, including pit‐picking, un‐roofing, endoscopic procedures and adjunctive methods such as phenol and laser therapies, reported generally favourable postoperative recovery profiles but also highlighted substantial variation in recurrence. That heterogeneity is clinically important, as it suggests that outcome depends not only on the technique itself but also on disease extent, case selection and the consistency of follow‐up [8].

Phenolisation remains one of the best‐established outpatient chemical treatments. By ablating the epithelialised tract lining and inflammatory granulation tissue, phenol encourages cavity collapse and secondary healing. A 2024 meta‐analysis comparing phenol with surgical excision found no significant difference in recurrence, while phenol was associated with fewer wound complications, shorter operative time, earlier return to work and faster complete healing [9]. Crystallised phenol has attracted particular interest as a practical and repeatable variant of this technique, and recent series continue to report encouraging results when patient selection and local hair control are considered carefully [10]. In parallel, adjunctive laser hair epilation has shown promise in reducing recurrence, with a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomised trials demonstrating a significant benefit at a minimum follow‐up of 1 year [11].

Taken together, contemporary evidence supports an individualised approach to PD, in which disease extent, pit burden and local anatomical features guide the choice between minimally invasive treatment and excisional or off‐midline reconstruction. Within that framework, evaluating the outcomes of crystallised phenol, particularly when multiple sessions and clinically tailored inter‐session intervals are used, remains relevant to understanding real treatment burden and early outcomes in presacral‐localised disease.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective, single‐centre observational clinical series evaluated outcomes after crystallised phenol application in patients with presacral pilonidal sinus disease. All eligible patients treated in the General Surgery Outpatient Clinic of Giresun University Training and Research Hospital between July 2023 and January 2026 were included. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No: BAEK‐512, 24.12.2025/03). In keeping with local regulations, written informed consent was obtained from all patients for treatment and for the use of anonymised clinical data for research purposes.

Patients were included if they had a clinical diagnosis of pilonidal sinus disease localised to the presacral region, had undergone treatment with crystallised phenol in our unit, and had completed the scheduled early follow‐up. Patients were excluded if an alternative diagnosis was suspected, such as fistula‐in‐ano or another anorectal disorder, if key treatment variables were missing, or if follow‐up was shorter than 3 months. None of the patients in this series had undergone previous surgery for PD.

Diagnosis was established clinically by documenting the number and location of midline pits, the presence of any secondary openings, signs of acute inflammation and previous abscess episodes. Baseline variables extracted from the medical record included age, sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), smoking status (current smoker vs. non‐smoker), employment status, comorbidity, prior abscess history and local hair‐removal practises, including epilation or waxing where applicable. Symptom duration was recorded in months and defined as the interval between patient‐reported symptom onset and the first phenol session. Each visible pit was gently dilated, and the sinus cavity was curetted to remove hair, debris and granulation tissue. The surrounding skin was protected with a barrier ointment, typically petroleum jelly, to minimise chemical irritation. Crystallised phenol was then introduced into the sinus tract until adequate contact with the cavity wall had been achieved. After a contact period of approximately 2–3 min, residual phenol was aspirated, and the area was dressed with absorbent gauze. Patients were discharged on the same day with advice regarding wound care, hygiene and local hair control. A second session was scheduled when persistent drainage, incomplete cavity closure or ongoing tenderness suggested residual disease. A third session was reserved for patients with persistent clinical findings after the second application. The interval between the first and second sessions, and where applicable between the second and third sessions, was determined by the patient's clinical course and recorded in days. Total treatment duration was defined as the interval from the first to the final phenol session.

All patients were reviewed routinely at 5 weeks and again at 3 months after the initial treatment. These visits were scheduled for every patient irrespective of apparent earlier healing, and no patient was discharged from follow‐up before the 3‐month assessment. During each review, the wound was examined for cessation of discharge, epithelialisation or closure of the pits, local tenderness and any sign of infection.

Outcome measures were defined a priori. A complication during treatment was defined as the development of an abscess or other infectious event requiring additional intervention, such as antibiotics or drainage. Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of clinical disease after initial healing, manifested by new drainage, pain associated with the reopening of a pit, or reformation of a sinus or abscess during scheduled follow‐up. For comparative analysis, patients were categorised according to the total number of phenol sessions received, namely two or three sessions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages). For comparisons based on session requirement (two vs. three sessions), continuous variables were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non‐normality and/or small sample size. Categorical variables were compared with Fisher's exact test where appropriate. Analyses were performed on a complete‐case basis for each variable.

Results

A total of 40 patients treated with crystallised phenol were included. The cohort was predominantly male (30/40, 75.0%), with 10 women (25.0%). Most patients were 18–39 years of age (35/40, 87.5%), while five patients (12.5%) were 40–59 years of age. BMI was most commonly in the overweight range (25–29.9 kg/m2) (21/40, 52.5%), followed by normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) in 16 patients (40.0%) and BMI 30–34.9 kg/m2 in three patients (7.5%). In terms of occupation, patients were most often workers (13/40, 32.5%) or students (10/40, 25.0%). Smoking was present in 25 patients (62.5%). Mean age was 29.23 ± 9.85 years (median 26, range 18–57) and mean BMI was 25.81 ± 3.85 kg/m2 (median 25.95, range 17.90–35.50) (Table 1).

Discussion

The present series addresses a practical question that arises whenever crystallised phenol is used in routine practise: What does it mean when a patient needs another application? In our cohort, the number of sessions did not simply reflect surgeon preference. It appeared to mirror the early behaviour of the disease. Patients who completed treatment in two sessions generally followed a smoother course, whereas those who required a third session formed a subgroup with greater disease burden and less favourable early outcomes, accounting for all recorded abscesses and all early recurrences.

One point warrants emphasis at the outset. Follow‐up in this study was complete but deliberately short. No patients were lost to follow‐up, and all 40 patients were examined at 5 weeks and 3 months, including those whose wounds appeared to have healed before the final visit. This strengthens the internal consistency of the early outcome data. At the same time, a 3‐month horizon can only capture early failure and early recurrence; it cannot be interpreted as a durable long‐term recurrence assessment, which remains an important limitation of the present series [12].

The clearest signal in our data was the association between pit burden and treatment intensity. Pit count was significantly higher in the three‐session group, and every patient with three or more pits required a third application. This is clinically plausible. A larger number of pits likely indicates a broader epithelialised disease field, a greater volume of retained hair and debris, and a higher likelihood of small side tracts persisting after the initial procedure. Recent work has similarly identified pit number and disease extent as predictors of recurrence after phenol‐based treatment [13, 14]. In practical terms, pit count seems to function as a simple bedside marker of complexity.

The reviewer's observation that no patient in this series healed after a single application is well taken. We believe this should be interpreted in light of our treatment protocol rather than as evidence that one session is never sufficient. In our outpatient practise, the threshold for repeating treatment was intentionally low, particularly in presacral disease. Any persistent drainage, incomplete closure or residual tenderness at review prompted a further application. The mean interval between the first and second sessions was just over 11 days, and it is entirely possible that this relatively short interval limited the time allowed for the full effect of the first application to become clinically apparent in some patients. For that reason, the absence of a one‐session subgroup should be read as a feature of protocol and case selection, not as a universal property of crystallised phenol therapy.

The clustering of abscess formation and recurrence in the three‐session group should be understood within the same framework. These patients were probably not merely slower to heal; they were more likely to have extensive residual epithelial lining or retained debris after the first stages of treatment. The p‐values were borderline, which is unsurprising given the small number of adverse events, but the clinical pattern remains coherent. Requiring a third session should therefore not be regarded as procedural failure in itself. Rather, it identifies a subgroup in whom closer surveillance and more meticulous adjunctive care are warranted.

A related question is the specific additional value of phenol beyond curettage alone. In our view, the mechanical component of the procedure removes hair, debris and granulation tissue, whereas phenol adds chemical ablation of the epithelialised tract lining and residual inflammatory tissue. The intention is to promote the collapse of the cavity and reduce the likelihood that a partially treated sinus persists after debridement. That said, our study was not designed to compare curettage alone with curettage plus phenol, and we cannot claim, based on these data, that the same results would not have been obtained without phenol. The rationale for its use rests on the biological rationale for tract ablation and on the broader literature showing that phenol‐based treatment can achieve acceptable recurrence rates with relatively low wound morbidity [9].

The choice of crystallised rather than liquid phenol also deserves comment. Liquid phenol has the theoretical advantage of easier penetration into very small sinus branches. Crystallised phenol, however, liquefies at body temperature and, in our experience, allows a more controlled local application, with less concern about uncontrolled spread or spillage to surrounding skin in the natal cleft. Recent comparative data suggest that both liquid and crystallised forms can be effective, without clear evidence that one is universally superior [10]. We therefore regard the choice of form as a matter of protocol, familiarity and local practicality rather than established superiority.

We did not find a significant association between session requirement and either smoking status or BMI. This negative finding should be interpreted with caution. It does not imply that smoking or adiposity are irrelevant to wound healing or recurrence. More likely, in this cohort, local disease complexity—as reflected by pit burden—had a stronger influence on short‐term treatment course than systemic variables, and the limited sample size further reduced our ability to detect smaller effects.

When placed alongside contemporary real‐world data, these findings support the continued relevance of outpatient phenol treatment. Large cohort studies have shown that pilonidal surgery in everyday practise often carries a meaningful burden in terms of healing time, wound care and recurrence [15, 16]. Against that background, crystallised phenol remains a reasonable and defensible option for carefully selected patients, particularly when minimising wound burden is a priority. The practical message for clinicians is straightforward: document pit count carefully at the first visit, counsel patients with a higher pit burden that additional sessions may be needed, and interpret repeated application as a staged strategy rather than an automatic sign of treatment failure.

If outcomes are to improve further in the higher‐burden subgroup, several areas deserve attention. Repeat‐application protocols should be standardised more clearly, the interval between sessions may merit reappraisal, and adjunctive measures—particularly hair control and cleft hygiene—should be treated as part of the treatment pathway rather than an afterthought. Randomised evidence now supports laser epilation as an adjunct to reduce recurrence [17], and future prospective studies should examine whether improved cavity mapping, more standardised retreatment criteria, and longer follow‐up can improve the durability of phenol‐based treatment [18, 19].

Study Limitations

This study carries the usual limitations of a single‐centre observational series. The modest sample size and small number of adverse events limited the power of subgroup analyses. Follow‐up, although complete with no losses, was limited to 3 months and therefore reflects early outcomes rather than definitive recurrence. In addition, because all patients in this protocol underwent at least two applications, the study cannot answer whether a one‐session strategy would have been sufficient in a subset of cases. Nevertheless, the internal consistency of the dataset, particularly the strong relation between pit burden and session requirement, provides a useful clinical signal.

Conclusion

In this single‐centre clinical series examining presacral pilonidal sinus disease, crystallised phenol appeared to be a practical, outpatient, tissue‐preserving treatment with acceptable short‐term safety and early clinical effectiveness. Most patients completed treatment in two or three sessions over a relatively brief course. The need for a third application was not incidental; it seemed to reflect greater baseline disease burden, most clearly captured by pit count, and it identified a subgroup in whom abscess formation and early recurrence clustered. These findings suggest a simple clinical message: careful baseline assessment of pit burden should inform counselling and follow‐up planning, and repeated phenol applications should be viewed as a staged strategy in more complex disease rather than as treatment failure. Longer follow‐up and larger prospective studies are needed to establish durable recurrence rates and to determine whether refinements in timing, cavity clearance and adjunctive hair control can further improve outcomes.
