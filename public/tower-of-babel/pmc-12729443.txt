Psychometric evaluation of the Chinese version of the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) and analysis of associated factors
Authors: Chunyan Zhang, Huijuan Tong, Yanhong Liu, Chuanqi Wen, Kaiyan Xu, Mingyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the accelerating aging population in China, there is increasing attention to the quality of life and mental health issues among older adults. Elevated burden not only deteriorates psychological and physical health but also leads to social isolation and decreased quality of life. However, there is currently a lack of specific assessment tools for measuring burden among older adults in China. This study aims to translate the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) into Chinese, assess its validity and reliability within the general older adult population, and explore the factors influencing GFBS scores.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted involving 500 Chinese older adults. Using the Brislin translation technique, the original scale was translated into Chinese employing a forward-backward translation method. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were used to test the structural validity of the scale. In contrast, content validity was evaluated using the Content Validity Index (CVI). The scale’s internal consistency was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, McDonald’s Omega coefficient, corrected item-total correlations, and test-retest reliability. Additionally, the relationship between older adults’ feelings of being a burden and their general demographic characteristics was explored.ResultsThe EFA identified a three-factor structure, and the CFA indicated satisfactory model fit indices, all within acceptable thresholds. The CVI of the scale was 0.914, while Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients were 0.965 and 0.966, respectively. The corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.607 ~ 0.825. The test-retest reliability was 0.763. Statistically significant differences in the level of burdensomeness were observed among older adults based on gender, marital status, chronic diseases, health status, and economic status (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe GFBS is a reliable and effective tool for assessing the level of burden among older adults in China. It consists of three dimensions: economic and social burden, personal emotional burden, and social interaction burden. Among older adults, females, widowed individuals, those with chronic illnesses, those lacking a fixed income, and those in poor health experience a higher level of feelings of being a burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06743-6.

Introduction

Global population aging is becoming increasingly severe, with the number of people aged 60 and above projected to reach nearly 2.1 billion by 2025 [1]. The issue presents a significant challenge for China. According to the seventh national census results, the country has approximately 264.02 million people aged 60 and above, accounting for 18.70% of the total population [2]. Compared to younger individuals, older adults are more likely to experience negative life events such as retirement, bereavement, living alone, and chronic illnesses [3, 4]. As individuals age, age-related issues such as health problems, limited physical mobility, and declining memory become more prevalent. When older adults increasingly require care from family members and other social networks, the combination of objective need for assistance and personal perception may lead to an increased sense of burdensomeness. This self-perception can result in distressing effects, including symptoms of depression, anxiety, loneliness, hopelessness, and suicidal tendencies [5, 6]. Life satisfaction, self-care, self-control, and help-seeking behaviors may also decrease [7, 8]. Therefore, the mental health of older adults is crucial. In the context of an aging population, it is necessary to conduct more extensive research on perceived burdensomeness among this high-risk group of older adults.

Currently, most research on perceived burdensomeness focuses on specific contexts. For instance, studies often evaluate the burdensomeness experienced by family caregivers, particularly those caring for individuals with chronic and geriatric illnesses [9]. The assessment tools used are tailored to very specific burden characteristics and do not generally address the older adult population as a whole. The Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS) is the most widely used questionnaire in this regard [10]. However, it primarily targets patients with chronic or major illnesses who may feel they are a burden to others. It is more suited for clinical assessment of self-perceived burden among patients with chronic diseases, cancer patients, and other individuals requiring long-term care. The Psychological Burden Scale (PBS) [11] is also utilized to measure patients’ psychological burden in terms of anxiety, depression, and stress during illness [11]. However, it does not specifically address the aging issues of the general older adult population. The Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ), commonly used internationally, focuses on perceived burdensomeness with an emphasis on suicide risk [12]. The items in the INQ place greater focus on suicide/death (e.g., feeling as if others would be better off if one were dead), resulting in a higher proportion of the most severe perceived burdensomeness (suicide risk) being represented in the scale. Furthermore, the sense of burdensomeness among older adults differs from that of younger populations, and the INQ was not specifically developed for older adults. According to the Interpersonal-Psychological Theory of Suicide (IPTS), one of the three factors contributing to suicide risk is perceived burdensomeness, defined as the individual’s belief that they are a burden to their family and friends [13]. Research indicates that the suicide rate among older adults is as high as 48.7/100,000 [14], making this demographic the group with the highest risk of suicide-related deaths worldwide [15]. However, perceived burdensomeness may be a significant proximal cause of suicidal ideation and is directly related to the desire for death [16]. There is currently no scale in China specifically designed to assess perceived burdensomeness among older adults. Therefore, it is essential to develop and validate a tool tailored to the general older adult population. Such a tool should be capable of detecting various types of burdensomeness, including emotional, physiological, psychological, economic, time-related, or burdens causing harm to others. This will enable timely, accurate, and comprehensive assessment of perceived burdensomeness among older adults.

The Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) was initially developed in English by the team led by Lutz in the United States as a tool for measuring perceived burdensomeness among older adults [17]. The original English version of the scale has been proven to be reliable and effective (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.97, average interitem correlation = 0.58, 0.05/9 correlations between GFBS and validity measures = 0.006). Additionally, the scale has been validated in Turkey [18], demonstrating similarly high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.953). The scale covers multiple dimensions of burden, including physical, emotional, economic, and social burdens, providing a comprehensive assessment of the burden experienced by older adults in both familial and societal contexts. Measuring this broader sense of burden is crucial, as the perceived burden is associated with various psychological and behavioral phenomena. For example, economic pressure is a strong predictor of mental health [19], further exacerbating the burden on older adults. People with chronic illnesses report increased distress when they need more assistance due to the challenges and treatment burdens associated with their chronic conditions [20]. Julie Lutz’s research has highlighted that even well-educated older adults in the United States experience significant personal burdens [17]. Older adults with lower levels of education may experience even greater feelings of being a burden than their well-educated counterpart [18]. However, there is limited research on the impact of educational level on perceived burden among older adults in China. Further exploration of how educational variability influences the sense of burden is one of the objectives of this study. Another study indicates that some women delay treatment and seek help due to concerns about the energy and time required, as well as the fear of becoming a burden to their families [21]. These studies further underscore the importance of assessing perceived burden.

However, the applicability of the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) in the cultural and social context of China has not been validated. Therefore, this study aims to translate the GFBS into Chinese and assess its validity and reliability within the general older adult population. The GFBS can be used to assess the burden of older adults in various caregiving settings, such as home-based care, community care, and institutional care. This study will help identify high-risk populations and inform interventions to enhance their quality of life and mental well-being.

Materials and methods

Translation and cultural adaptation

Before translating and validating the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale, permission was obtained from Julie Lutz, PhD. The Brislin translation method was systematically followed for this process [22]. Two graduate students majoring in English independently translated the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale into Chinese. The translated Chinese version of the scale was then compared and discussed with the research team to produce a draft version. Two English experts, who had no prior exposure to the scale, were engaged to translate the draft Chinese version back into English using Brislin’s back-translation method. Finally, a psychologist from Liaoning Province and four nursing experts reviewed the original questionnaire, the initial Chinese version, and the retranslated English version. The content of the scale was ultimately determined to be more suitable for the Chinese context.

Design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted from October 2023 to September 2024 in communities and hospitals in Shenyang and Jinzhou cities, Liaoning Province. A total of 500 elderly individuals (aged > 60 years) participated in the study. Additionally, 30 participants were asked to provide contact information. Four weeks later, we recruited these 30 participants for a retest to assess the reliability of the measure. All participants were native Mandarin speakers, and the sample included a diverse range of educational backgrounds, encompassing both highly educated and less educated older adults. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study. The research procedures followed the ethical standards of the Jinzhou Medical University Ethics Committee (JZMULL2021009) and adhered to the principles outlined in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments.

Materials

The research questionnaire included a general demographic questionnaire and the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS).

The general demographic questionnaire was developed based on a literature review and expert guidance. It included questions on gender, age, education level, place of residence, number of children, economic status, health status, marital status (with ‘single’ referring to lifelong unmarried and ‘widowed’ referring to currently having a deceased spouse), and the presence of chronic illnesses (Including diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease). The detailed content of the general demographic questionnaire is available in the supplementary files.

The Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) was developed by Julie Lutz, PhD, and her team [17]. It measures older adults’ subjective feelings of being a burden to others. The scale aims to capture the broad concept of burdensomeness associated with various important psychological variables. The GFBS consists of 25 items, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.97. Responses range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores on this scale range from 25 to 125, with higher scores indicating greater feelings of burdensomeness. The Cronbach’s alpha for this study was 0.965.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 26.0 (IBM Corporation) software. Differences in GFBS scores among sociodemographic variables were assessed using independent samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA. Bonferroni correction was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons to adjust for significance, maintaining a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Item analysis

Considering that item validity is the foundation of developing psychological measurement instruments, this study conducted item analysis before validity and reliability analyses [23–25]. The total scores of the Chinese revised version of the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) were ranked from high to low, with the top 27% categorized as the high group and the bottom 27% as the low group. Independent sample t-tests were then used to analyze the scores of each item between the two groups. Discriminant validity was considered satisfactory if the scores of each item in both groups reached significance (P < 0.05) in both tails.

Validity analysis

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated to assess the relevance and representativeness of the scale items to the target construct. The Chinese version of the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) underwent a rigorous expert validation process involving a panel of seven carefully selected experts [26]. The panel was composed of three licensed clinical psychologists specializing in geriatric mental health, each with over ten years of relevant experience, and four senior bilingual physicians specializing in geriatrics. This composition ensured both clinical relevance and linguistic accuracy during the evaluation [27]. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was employed to quantitatively assess the results. Each expert independently rated all 25 items on a 4-point relevance scale. The Item-level CVI (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a rating of 3 or 4 to each item, while the Scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was computed as the mean of all I-CVI values. The analysis demonstrated excellent content validity, with all I-CVI scores ranging from 0.86 to 1.00 and the S-CVI/Ave reaching 0.95, thereby exceeding the recommended thresholds of 0.78 and 0.90, respectively [28, 29].The complete dataset of expert ratings and calculations is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Structural validity

We assessed the structural validity of the Chinese version of the GFBS using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The 500 participants were randomly divided into two groups. The responses of 250 participants were analyzed using exploratory factor analysis, while the responses of the remaining 250 participants were analyzed using confirmatory factor analysis. To ensure the adequacy of our sample size, we referred to relevant literature. Maccallum et al. (1999) indicated that when the level of communality is high, a sample size of approximately 200 is sufficient for factor analysis [30]. Additionally, Kline (2016) and Brown (2015) recommended that in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), each free parameter should be represented by at least 5–10 samples [31, 32]. Thus, our sample size of 250 is both sufficient and appropriate.

In the first group (n = 250), principal component analysis (PCA) was used to evaluate the internal structure of the translated Chinese version of the GFBS scale. The suitability for factor analysis is determined using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test [33] and Bartlett’s test of sphericity [34]. When the KMO value is greater than 0.60 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity is significant (P < 0.05), the data are considered suitable for factor analysis. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were subjected to varimax orthogonal rotation. Items with loadings of 0.50 or higher were considered for inclusion in separate factors [35]. Factor extraction was guided by eigenvalues, total variance explained, and scree plot.

In the second group (n = 250), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to validate the results of EFA or further evaluate the measurement model [36]. Based on the established factor structure, CFA helps assess the fit of the model. The goodness of fit of the model is evaluated using several indicators: χ2 and degrees of freedom (df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI). Acceptable model fit is defined by χ2/df < 5, RMSEA < 0.08, and IFI, TLI, and CFI > 0.90 [37]. The average variance extracted (AVE) value greater than 0.5 indicates good convergent validity, while a composite reliability (CR) value greater than 0.7 indicates good reliability [38–40]. Finally, the discriminant validity of the model is assessed using the correlation coefficients and the square root of AVE from AMOS 26.0 [41]. If the square root of AVE is greater than the correlation coefficients between the items, good discriminant validity is indicated [42, 43].

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency of the scale was evaluated using various methodologies, including Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, the Omega coefficient, corrected item-total correlations, and test-retest reliabilit [44]. Values of 0.70 or higher for Cronbach’s alpha and the Omega coefficient are generally considered acceptable [45]. The threshold for corrected item-total correlations is set at 0.4, indicating the correlation of each item with the sum of the remaining items on the scale. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is used to assess test-retest reliability, measuring the scale’s stability over time [46]. To evaluate test-retest reliability, 30 participants who had completed the test four weeks prior were re-assessed [47–49]. The correlation between their initial and subsequent test scores was examined using Spearman’s correlation. A correlation coefficient of 0.7 or higher was considered the cutoff for acceptable reliability [50].

Two-thirds of the respondents were married (315/500, 63.0%), while a smaller proportion were widowed or divorced. The majority had an educational level of elementary school or less (202/500, 40.4%). A minority of older adults had no children (73/500, 14.6%), while 33.0% had more than three children. Validity analysis

Content validity

The content validity of the Chinese version of the GFBS was assessed by a panel of seven experts [51]. Detailed results of the content validity analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.857 to 1.00, with an overall scale-level content validity index average (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.914. The experts generally agreed that the items and overall content of the scale were well-aligned with its measurement objectives.

Structural validity

Exploratory factor analysis

The exploratory factor analysis (EFA) indicated that Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ2 = 5636.172, P < 0.001), and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index was 0.956, indicating that the dataset was appropriate for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation revealed three common factors, accounting for 71.044% of the variance. The contribution rates of the three extracted factors were 28.463%, 25.248%, and 17.333%, respectively. This finding is aligned with the original three-factor structure of the scale. The EFA results showed that the factor loadings of the items ranged from 0.541 to 0.867 (see Table 3). The scree plot (Fig. 1) also supported the three-factor structure, displaying a noticeable drop-off after the third point.

Reliability analysis

The reliability analysis results indicate that the Chinese version of the GFBS (25 items) demonstrates good internal consistency. Both Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and McDonald’s Omega coefficient are 0.965 and 0.966, respectively, which are slightly lower than Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.97 reported for the original scale The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the three dimensions are 0.948, 0.953, and 0.879, respectively. There is a significant positive correlation between item scores and total scores (r = 0.607 ~ 0.825, P < 0.001), suggesting a moderate correlation between individual items and the overall scale. Table 6 shows that deleting any single item would lead to a slight decrease in the overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, confirming the reliability of all items, which can be retained. The split-half reliability coefficient of the scale is 0.932, indicating high internal consistency. Additionally, the test-retest reliability, assessed with 30 participants after four weeks, is 0.763, exceeding 0.7, suggesting good long-term stability of the Chinese version of the GFBS.

Analysis of factors affecting the level of burden on older persons

The analysis of 500 datasets indicated that the Chinese version of the GFBS demonstrated no statistically significant differences with respect to education level, place of residence, or number of children. These non-significant findings could stem from either insufficient contrast between groups or limited statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes. Notably, the presence of a significant gender difference confirms that the scale is robust enough to detect meaningful effects. Specifically, among the older adult population, females reported higher feelings of being a burden than males (P < 0.05). Regarding marital status, widowed older adults exhibit a significantly higher burden compared to single older adults. Significant disparities exist between older adult individuals with chronic diseases and those without (P < 0.05). Additionally, significant differences are found in health and economic status (P < 0.05), with statistical differences among the three health categories: poor, fair, and good. Older adults without a fixed income often feel like a burden, with significant differences between those who have a fixed income and those who have no income at all. (P = 0.006 < 0.05). Discussion

This study is the first to introduce the Geriatric Feelings of Burdensomeness Scale (GFBS) in China to assess the levels of being a burden of older adults within the general population. The scale covers a broad range of item content, thereby capturing a more comprehensive concept of burden. Through meticulous cultural adaptation and translation into Chinese, the GFBS was thoroughly validated for its psychometric properties among older adults in China. The validity of the scale was confirmed through rigorous benchmarks, including item analysis, content validity, structural validity, criterion validity, and reliability analysis. The findings underscore the robust reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the GFBS. Based on our study results, the mean score for feelings of burdensomeness among older adults was 62.16 ± 21.60. This score is comparable to the average burden score among older adults in the Turkish community (61.67 ± 20.97) [18]. However, compared to older adults in the United States (48.11 ± 20.90), Chinese older adults exhibited higher levels of burdensomeness [17]. This disparity may be attributed to the greater challenges faced by Chinese elderly individuals concerning healthcare coverage [52]. In traditional Chinese society, the emphasis on family values and filial piety often results in increased responsibilities and expectations placed on older adults within the family [53], which may consequently heighten their feelings of burdensomeness.

The original scale, through exploratory factor analysis, identified three dimensions based on combinations with eigenvalues greater than 1, with a cumulative contribution rate of 69.96%. However, due to several items loading highly on multiple factors, the original authors opted to use the total score rather than individual factor scores, thus refraining from naming the three factors. In our study, we retained the same number of items as the original scale but further refined and supplemented the attribution of item factors. Given the diversity of cultural backgrounds and populations, further investigation into the potential dimensions of the scale is warranted. In our research, the first dimension was labeled as “Economic and Social Burden” (comprising items 1, 6, 8, 9, 14, 15, 20, 23, 24), the second dimension as “Personal Emotional Burden” (comprising items 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 18, 19, 21), and the third dimension as “Social Interaction Burden” (comprising items 16, 17, 22, 25). These dimensional labels were not solely based on theoretical considerations but also stemmed from a thorough analysis and reflection on the actual life circumstances of older adults. Firstly, labeling “Economic and Social Burden” integrates core concepts from ecological systems theory and social support theory [54, 55], highlighting the various challenges that older adults face at the economic and social levels. These challenges include financial difficulties, medical expenses burdens, and access to social welfare, accurately capturing the core issues affecting the quality of life of older adults. Furthermore, the labeling of “Personal Emotional Burden” emphasizes the importance of emotions and mental well-being in happiness and quality of life, aligning with the focus of social support theory [56]. It highlights the psychological burden that older adults bear when coping with life stressors. Lastly, the labeling of “Social Interaction Burden” highlights the challenges older adults face in social interaction and interpersonal relationships, reflecting the perspective of ecosystem theory on individual-environment interactions [57]. It profoundly illustrates the difficulties and challenges individuals encounter in social interactions. The current three-factor structure can be attributed to two logical reasons. Firstly, the original structure may have been influenced by cross-cultural changes during the translation process, potentially altering the correspondence between certain items and their expressions in Chinese. Secondly, differences in policies regarding healthy aging for older adults between domestic and international contexts may further contribute to variations in burden levels. The three factors of the Chinese version of GFBS explain a cumulative contribution rate of 71.044%, with each item loading on a factor of at least 0.50, which is considered ideal [58]. The study found that the translated scale is easy to understand, and its structure is reasonable, indicating that the three-dimensional nature of the scale is more suitable for the Chinese population.

The feeling of being a burden among older adults is a relatively new area of research, with relatively few studies on levels of burden in the general older adult population. In this study, differences in burden levels were observed between genders, with females exhibiting a higher average GFBS score than males, indicating a greater sense of burden among females. Sugiyama notes that women often prioritize others over themselves in various situations [59]. Even in contemporary society, women are still significantly influenced by traditional values, commonly believing that they have to provide care for their families [60]. Increasing evidence also suggests that older women experience higher levels of depression and anxiety due to declining estrogen and melatonin levels, as well as deprivation of deep REM sleep [61, 62]. Research has confirmed that levels of depression and anxiety are positively correlated with a sense of burden [63]. Therefore, this may lead to an overall higher sense of burden among older women.

Regarding marital status, a significant difference was found between widowed older adults and those who are single (P < 0.05). This may be attributed to the fact that widowed elderly individuals have lost their long-term partners, resulting in a substantial impact on their emotional and psychological support. Research indicates that the incidence of depression and anxiety is higher among individuals who have lost their spouse, showing a negative correlation between widowhood and mental health in the elderly [64]. In contrast, single elderly individuals may have adapted to living alone and established supportive social networks, which provide resilience [65].In terms of economic status, older adults without a fixed income experience a higher sense of burden compared to those with a stable income. Additionally, older adults with no income have a lower sense of burden than those without a fixed income, indicating that those without a fixed income experience the greatest burden. This may align with the findings indicated by Wilkinson’s research, where increased economic uncertainty significantly contributes to the psychological stress and burden experienced by older adults [19], serving as a major factor predisposing them to depression and anxiety. On the contrary, older adults with no income often have easier access to social welfare and economic assistance [66]. They are recognized as a group in greater economic need, thus receiving more support. Additionally, older adults with no income may have a certain level of psychological adaptation and acceptance of their situation, resulting in a lower sense of burden [67]. Based on the results of this study, it is suggested that more attention should be directed towards the psychological well-being of older adults who are widowed or have no fixed income.

The limitations section of the original scale article mentioned a lack of participants with lower levels of education. This study aimed to address this gap by including older adults with varying levels of education [17]. However, the study results indicated no statistically significant differences between different levels of education (P < 0.05), as detailed in Table 7. Therefore, the relationship between education and the sense of burden remains inconclusive and warrants further investigation in larger and more diverse samples to clarify whether a true association exists. Similarly, it is unrelated to their place of residence or the number of children they have. The inclusion of variables such as urban and rural residence aimed to investigate whether the living environment significantly influences the sense of burden among older adults. However, the living environment and the number of children did not significantly impact this study. The sample predominantly consists of older adults aged 60 and above with lower educational levels. Despite utilizing paper-based questionnaires and providing standardized explanations to participants when necessary, this characteristic may introduce comprehension difficulties, potentially affecting the accuracy and generalizability of the burden scores. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating the findings to populations with varying educational backgrounds in China. Future research should aim to include a more diverse and representative sample. Additionally, this study utilized a standardized self-report burden scale, and some participants may have underestimated their burden due to social desirability bias, leading to potential bias. In the research design, we minimized the impact of this bias by ensuring anonymity and emphasizing that the data would be used solely for research purposes. Future studies could further mitigate the influence of social desirability bias by incorporating external informants or lie detection measures.

In terms of chronic illness, consistent with previous studies [68], older adults with chronic diseases tend to experience a heightened sense of burden. Significant differences in burden levels were observed among older adults with varying health conditions (P < 0.05). This could be attributed to the fact that older adults with chronic diseases and poorer physical health require long-term medication, regular check-ups, and treatment, which significantly increase their medical expenses and, consequently, their economic burden. The research indicates that healthcare costs for individuals with chronic diseases are several times higher than those for healthy older adults [69]. Additionally, managing chronic diseases entails ongoing health management and self-monitoring, imposing additional time and energy burdens on older adults [70]. From a psychological stress perspective, individuals with chronic diseases are more likely to experience anxiety and depression. They require more care and support while facing inadequate social support, thereby increasing their burden [70]. In contrast, healthy older adults can carry out daily activities independently, resulting in a lower sense of burden [71]. Research indicates that the quality of life and psychological well-being of healthy older adults are significantly higher than those with poor or fair health conditions. Therefore, chronic diseases appear to be a major source of stress contributing to the burden felt by older adults. Focusing on addressing chronic diseases among older adult can have a positive impact on alleviating their sense of burden.

In this study, we ranked the total scores of the GFBS from high to low, with the top 27% categorized as the high-score group and the bottom 27% as the low-score group. The cut-off scores were 76.00 and 47.00, respectively. The analysis showed that these cut-off scores could significantly distinguish between different levels of perceived burden (P < 0.05). Therefore, the GFBS scale provides crucial guidance and theoretical support for healthcare professionals, helping them understand and address the burden experienced by older adults. The scale assesses burden levels among older adults, enhances the understanding of their needs, and provides a basis for cross-cultural comparisons and intervention strategies. In practical applications, the GFBS scale assists in formulating personalized support plans and allocating resources efficiently. It allows healthcare professionals to tailor interventions for individuals with high perceived burdens, ensuring that these individuals receive more focused care and support, ultimately improving their physical and mental well-being. Although the current results support the validity of these cut-off scores, further studies are needed to confirm their applicability in different samples and settings to ensure the scale’s broad utility and reliability.

However, this study has limitations that should be considered. The external validity of the findings may be constrained by the geographically restricted sample, which was drawn exclusively from certain areas of Northeast China. Given the substantial regional socioeconomic and cultural variations across China, the generalizability of these results to the broader older adult population in other regions—particularly in Southern China—requires further investigation. Future studies should adopt multi-regional or nationwide sampling approaches to strengthen the external validity and general robustness of the findings. Secondly, due to the lack of literature on the sense of burden among the older adult population in general, comparing these results with other studies is challenging. Future research should focus more on the psychological well-being of the older adult population in the context of population aging.

Conclusion

Therefore, we conclude that the Chinese version of the GFBS demonstrates strong reliability and validity. The GFBS supports a three-factor structure with 25 items and is effective for assessing the sense of burden among older adults in the general Chinese population. This measurement encompasses various situations in which older adults may perceive themselves as burdens, encompassing not only issues related to suicide or chronic illness but also other critical aspects of late life. The scale is concise, convenient, and suitable for rapid screening of the sense of burden across large-scale older adult populations. This study offers further insights into potential correlates of burden identified through the GFBS. Additionally, the preliminary screening and identification of older adult populations with higher levels of burden, including females, widowed individuals, those without a fixed income, individuals with poor health status, and those with chronic diseases, can assist in pinpointing high-risk populations. Such identification is crucial for developing targeted public health intervention strategies. This is crucial for promoting healthy aging.
