1.
Introduction

A subset
A
of the integers
\mathbb{N}
is said to have
positive upper density
if

(1.1)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\lvert A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}\rvert}{N}>0.

And,
A
has positive lower density if, replacing the limsup above by a liminf, the same inequality holds.

Recall that an
IP-set
is built out of an infinite set of
integers
\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots\}
which need not be distinct.
And then the IP-set is all distinct finite sums
\sum_{n\in F}r_{n}
,
where
F\subset\mathbb{N}
is finite.
This is an instance of the deep work of Furstenberg and Katznelson on the Szemeredi Theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be set of positive upper density, and
\{n_{k}\}
an IP-set, and
t
an integer. Then for some
k
, there is
a\in A
so that

(1.3)

a,a+n_{k},\ldots,a+(t-1)n_{k}\in A.

That is,
A
contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions whose step size is given by a member of the IP-set.

We show here that for Cantor sets of integers, that the statement above can be upgraded to a density statement.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{b,D}
denote a
classical integer Cantor set
defined by a base
b\geq 3
,
a choice of residues
\mathbb{R}_{b}
forming a complete collection of residues for
\mathbb{Z}_{b}
, and a collection of digits

D\subset\mathbb{R}_{b}
that satisfies
2\leq\lvert D\rvert<b
,
That is,

(1.4)

\mathcal{C}_{b,D}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}d_{j}b^{j}\colon k\in\mathbb{N},\ d_{j}\in D\Bigr\}

The most classical version would be the middle third Cantor set
\mathcal{C}_{3,\{0,2\}}
, that is, base
b=3
, the choice of residues is
\mathbb{R}_{3}=\{0,1,2\}
,
and allowed digits
D=\{0,2\}
.
This is also an IP set. Indeed, for any base
b\geq 2
, and two allowed digits, with one being zero, forms a

rational spectra
IP set, as defined by Kra and Shalom
[
7
]
. For such sequences, they have shown that the multiple recurrence IP averages above have the same characteristic factors as the full linear averages.
We give a partial extension of this result to general Cantor sets, with
0
as an allowed digit.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{b,D}
be a Cantor set with base
b
,
digits
D\subset[b]
, with
0\in D
, and
\lvert D\rvert\geq 2
. Then, for
for all integers
\ell
, and all subsets
A\subset\mathbb{N}
of positive lower density, we have

(1.6)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\Expectation_{x\in[N]}\Expectation_{n\in[N]}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x-jk_{n})>0.

Above,
\{0=k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots\}
is the increasing enumeration of
\mathcal{K}
.

It is seems very plausible that many of the results of Kra and Shalom
[
7
]

can be extended to the Cantor sets.

Our proof will follow the outline of the Furstenberg proof
[
6
]
of Szemerédi’s Theorem.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{b,D}
be a Cantor set with base
b
,
digits
D\subset[b]
, with
0\in D
, and
\lvert D\rvert\geq 2
. Then, for
for all integers
\ell
, and all measure preserving systems
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)

and non-negative bounded
f\neq 0
, we have

(1.8)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\Expectation_{X}\Expectation_{N}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}f(T^{jk_{n}})>0.

Above,
\{0=k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots\}
is the increasing enumeration of
\mathcal{K}
.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We use standard Vinogradov notation, thus
A\ll B
means that
\lvert A\rvert\leq C\lvert B\rvert
,
for an absolute constant
C
. By
A=B+O(\delta)
we mean that
A-B\ll\delta
.

For integers
N
, we set
[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
For a finite set
N
and
f\colon N\to\mathbb{C}
, we write

(2.1)

\Expectation_{N}f=\frac{1}{\lvert N\rvert}\sum_{n\in N}f(n).

And we will frequently write
\Expectation_{[N]}=\Expectation_{N}
.

2.2.
Measure Preserving Systems

We recall standard notions around measure preserving systems.
In particular,
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu,T)

is a
measure preserving system
if
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a probability space, and
T\colon X\to X
preserves
m
measure, in that
\mu(T^{-1}A)=\mu(A)
for all measurable
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
By
\Expectation_{X}f
we mean
\int_{X}f\;d\mu
.

There are standard reductions that we impose throughout.

(1)

All transformations
T
are invertible.

(2)

(X,\mathcal{A},m)
is a Borel probability space.

(3)

(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
is an ergodic measure preserving system, that is, the sets
A
that are invariant under
T
have measure either
0
or
1
.

These reductions follow as in
[
3
]
*§7.2.1,2,3.

In different settings, the underling space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
remains the same, while we change the measure preserving operator from
T
, to some power
T^{k}
.
In this case, we just indicate the change in the operator.
In other cases, we have
T
invariant sigma algebras,
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{A}
.
In that case, we may refer to the system by its sigma algebra.

The system is called
weak mixing
if for all
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, one has

(2.2)

\lim_{N}\Expectation_{N}\lvert\mu(A\cap T^{-n}B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)\rvert=0.

This condition for sets extends to functions. For
f\in L^{2}(X)
with mean zero,

(2.3)

\lim_{N}\Expectation_{N}\lvert\langle f,T^{n}g\rangle\rvert=0,\qquad g\in L^{2}(X).

Weak mixing has several well known equivalences, of which we mention these.

Proposition 2.4
.

For a system
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
, these conditions are equivalent.

(1)

(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
is weak mixing.

(2)

X\times X
is ergodic.

(3)

For any other ergodic system
Y
,

X\times Y
is ergodic.

The concept of weakly mixing has several extensions. We will use this one.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
be weakly mixing. Then, for all integers
t
,
the system
T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{t}
is weakly mixing.

Proof.

We check the condition (
2.3
)
for functions
f
on
L^{\infty}(X^{t})
which are tensor products in the coordinates. The equality then holds for linear combinations of such functions, which are dense.

Thus
f=f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{t}
, with
f_{s_{0}}
having integral zero for some
1\leq s_{0}\leq t
.
Likewise let
g=g_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes g_{t}
.
Then,

(2.6)

\langle(T\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{t})^{n}f,g\rangle_{X^{t}}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\langle T^{sn}f_{s},g_{s}\rangle\ll\lvert\langle T^{s_{0}n}f_{s_{0}},g_{s}\rangle\rvert.

And,
T^{s_{0}}
is weakly mixing, so the claim follows.
∎

A function
f\in L^{2}(X)
is called
compact
if the set
\overline{\{T^{n}f\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}}
is a compact subset of
L^{2}(X)
. The system is
compact
if every
f\in L^{2}(X)
is compact. In this case, the operator
T
only has rational spectra.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{A}
be a
T
invariant
sigma algebra. The system

(X,\mathcal{D},m,T)
is said to be a
factor
of
the original system
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
.
(Here, there is a slight abuse of notation, in that we use the same notation for the set function
m
restricted to the collection
\mathcal{D}
.)
Factors are then naturally identified with their sigma algebras.
And, given
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{A}
,
we refer to
\mathcal{E}
as an extension of
\mathcal{D}
.

With
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{A}
, we define a conditional norm

(2.7)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{E}_{m}(f\overline{g}\mid\mathcal{D})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),\qquad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E}).

This conditional inner product defines conditional
L^{2}
norms by

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}
.
A conditional Cauchy-Schwartz and triangle inequality hold for this norm.

Definition 2.8
.

The Hilbert module
L^{2}(\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D})

are all those
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
for which the conditional norm
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
.

Definition 2.9
.

A function
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
is said to be
conditionally weakly mixing
if

(2.10)

\Expectation_{N}\lvert\langle T^{n}f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}\rvert=0,\quad\textup{in $L^{2}(\mathcal{D})$}.

The extension
\mathcal{E}
of
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
weakly mixing
if every
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
with
\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{D})=0
is conditionally weakly mixing.

Definition 2.11
.

A function
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
is said to be
conditionally compact
if for every
\epsilon>0
there
are a finite number of functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
so that

(2.12)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\min_{1\leq q\leq r}\bigl\lVert\lVert T^{n}f-g_{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}\bigr\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}<\epsilon.

The extension
\mathcal{E}
of
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
compact
if conditionally compact functions are dense in
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.

We defined the Cantor sets of interest in the introduction. Here, we collect some properties needed to complete the main theorem.

The first property is established in

*Prop. 2.2.
The detailed structure it provides is important to their analysis.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(k_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be an integer Cantor set in base
b
, with admissible digits
D=\{d_{0}<...<d_{|D|-1}\}\subset\{0,...,b-1\}
. Then, the following are true:

(i)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, written in base-
|D|
as

with
n_{c}\in\{0,...,|D|-1\}

k_{n}=\sum_{c=0}^{t}d_{n_{c}}b^{c}
. And
s_{b}(k_{n})=\sum_{c=0}^{t}d_{n_{c}}
. Note: we do not allow leading zeroes in the base-
|D|
representation of
n
unless
0\not\in D
.

(ii)

For any
i\geq 0
,
n\geq 0
, and
0\leq j<|D|^{i}

k_{|D|^{i}n+j}=b^{i}k_{n}+k_{j}
,
and
s_{b}(k_{|D|^{i}n+j})=s_{b}(k_{n})+s_{b}(k_{j})
.

(iii)

Fix
h\geq 1
and
k,s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Define

(2.14)

\Delta_{h}^{*}(k,s):=\{n\geq 0:k_{n+h}-k_{n}=k,\ s_{b}(k_{n+h})-s_{b}(k_{n})=s\}.

Then, if
\Delta_{h}^{*}(k,s)
is non-empty, it is the disjoint union of arithmetic progressions
\Delta_{h}^{*}(k,s)=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}A_{i}
, with the step size of each
A_{i}
being a power of
|D|
.

The equidistribution properties of Cantor sets have been investigated in several papers, including

8
]
.
For us, we need the following properties, which were established in our first paper

Lemma 2.15

Let

And, we have the equidistribution result, for

Lemma 2.17

Let

Then, for all irrational

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Expectation e^{2\pi\alpha k_{n}}=0,\quad\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Q}.

2.4.
Outline of the Proof

The method of proof follows the lines of ergodic proofs of Szemerédi’s Theorem. To summarize this approach, we say that a measure preserving system
(X,A,m,T)
is
Cantor-Szemerédi
, or
CSZ
,
if this holds:
For all measurable
A\subset X
, with
\mu(A)>0
, all integers
t\geq 1
, and all Cantor sets
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{b,D}
, with base
b
,
and digit set
D\subset[b]
, and

, there holds

(2.25)

\liminf_{N}\Expectation\int_{A}\prod_{s=1}^{t}T^{sk_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\;dm>0.

Here,
\mathcal{K}=\{k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots\}
.
We prove

Theorem 2.26
.

Every ergodic separable and invertible measure preserving system is Cantor-Szemerédi.

By the Furstenberg Correspondence principle
[
6
]
*Thm 1.1, this proves Theorem
1.7
.
The proof of Theorem
2.26

proceeds by factors. For a fixed system
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
, the factors of

are indexed by complete

\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathbf{K}
denote the collection of factors that are CSZ. They are ordered by inclusion.
Following
[
5
]
, if there holds

•

The set
\mathbf{K}
contains a maximal factor.

•

No

factor is maximal.

Then, the
\mathcal{A}
is the maximal factor. And our main theorem is proved.

The first point is seen by the line of reasoning of
[
5
]
*Proposition 7.26.
The second point is the main novelty of this paper. It is proved in the following sections of this paper.

Lemma 2.27
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},m,T)
be a measure preserving system, with factor

, and extension

. Then,

(2)

(3)

Let

Either

And, when we address the weakly mixing cases, we will use this standard fact.

Let

3.
Weak Mixing Systems and Extensions

We show that our ergodic theorem holds for weakly mixing systems.
And that it holds for weakly mixing extensions of
CSZ
systems.
Throughout, let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{b,D}
be a classical Cantor set, with
0\in D
. Let
\{k_{n}\colon n\geq 1\}
be its elements written in increasing order.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any weakly mixing system
(X,m,T)
,
and functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\in L^{\infty}(X)
, we have

(3.2)

\lim_{N}\Expectation_{N}\prod_{s=1}^{t}T^{sk_{n}}f_{s}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\int f_{s}\;d\mu.

The limit holds in
L^{2}(X)
norm.

This establishes the first point of Lemma
2.27
.
The weakly mixing extension will be a refinement of this proof, making every step relative to the extension.
And, the
\epsilon
management of the proof is a little complicated. So our intention is to clearly address each step of the proof.

Proof.

The proof of the Theorem is by induction on
t
and all weakly mixing systems. The case of
t=1
follows from the
L^{2}
ergodic theorem already proved for Cantor sets.
More precisely, the
L^{2}
ergodic theorem, see Theorem
2.19
, shows that the averages of
f
along the Cantor set converge to
a limit which is a linear combination of the rational spectra of
f
.
Yet, as
T
is weakly mixing, its only rational spectra is at the origin. But, then we have

(3.3)

\lim_{N}\Expectation_{N}T^{sk_{n}}f=\int_{X}f\;d\mu.

We move to the inductive step of the proof. We assume that the statement is true for
t-1\geq 1
, and Cantor sets
\mathcal{K}
, and prove it for
t
.
Fix
f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\in L^{\infty}(X)
bounded by
1
.
Expanding each
f_{s}

as its mean plus mean zero term gives us
2^{t}
products. One term is trivial, as it only consists of constants. The induction hypothesis applies to all the other terms which have at least one constant term, as then the product is only over at most
t-1
non-constant terms.
That leaves the last term in which every
f_{s}
has mean zero, and bounded by
1
in
L^{\infty}
norm.
We show that the limit of the averages tends to zero in
L^{2}(X)
norm.

To clarify the proof, we fix
0<\epsilon<1
,
and the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}
.
Let

(3.4)

\mathsf{X}^{t}=(X^{t},\mathcal{A}^{t},m^{t},S)

be the product system, where
S=T\otimes T^{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{t}
.
It is well known that
\mathsf{X}^{t}
is also weakly mixing (Proposition
2.5
).

We invoke the uniform ergodic Theorem
2.22
.
Fix
H>1/\epsilon
so large that for function
F=f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{t}
on the system
\mathsf{X}^{t}
, we have

(3.5)

\Bigl\lVert\Expectation_{M\leq n\leq N}S^{k_{n}}F\Big\rVert_{L^{2}(X^{t})}<\epsilon,\qquad N-M>H.

We turn to the structure of the Cantor set, as described in Lemma
2.13
.
For
1\leq h<H
, recall that the differences
k_{n+h}-k_{n}

take infinitely many values. Set
\Delta^{\ast}(h)
to be those values.
For each
\Delta\in\Delta^{\ast}
,
the set of integers

(3.6)

\mathbb{N}_{\Delta}\coloneqq\{n\colon k_{n+h}-k_{n}=\Delta\}

is a union of progressions in
n
of step size a power of
\delta=\lvert D\rvert
. In particular, the set is of positive density.
Considering any progression of step size

r=\delta^{i}
, note that for
0\leq c<r
and integer

,

(3.7)

k_{ar+c}=b^{i}k_{a}+k_{c}.

In particular, our induction hypothesis applies when restricted to such a progression,
as we just need to apply the induction hypothesis to the system given by
T^{b^{i}}
.

Fix

4.
Van der Waerden’s Theorem

We let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{C}(b,D)
be an integer Cantor set in base
b\geq 2
and digit set
D\subset\{0,\cdots,b-1\}
. We require that
0\in D
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{F},T)
be a topological dynamical system, and
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
be an open cover of
X
. Then for every
n\geq 1
, there exists an open set
U_{\alpha}
which contains an arithmetic progression

x,T^{r}x,T^{2r}x,\cdots,T^{(n-1)r}x
for some
x\in X
and
r\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}
.

This result is a corollary to work of Bergelson and Leibman
[
1
]
*Theorem 0.13. We include a proof here for the sake of completeness.
Indeed, we follow the familiar lines of the proof of the van der Waerden’s Theorem.

The important special case is when the system
(X,\mathcal{F},T)
is
minimal
, that is if
F\subset X
is an invariant set, namely
TF\subset F
, then
F=X
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{F},T)
be a minimal topological dynamical system,
U\subset X
a nonempty open set, and
n\geq 1
. Then
U
contains an arithmetic progression
y,T^{r}y,\cdots,T^{(n-1)r}y
for some
y\in X
and
r\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof that Theorem
4.2
implies Theorem
4.1
.

This is a standard argument.
Given
(X,\mathcal{F},T)
, a topological dynamical system, let
\mathbf{F}

to the collection of closed
F\subset X
, which are
T
invariant, thus
TF\subset F
.
This set is ordered by inclusion, hence by Zorn’s Lemma, must have a minimal element.
If the only minimal element is the empty set, then the system is minimal, and we have nothing to do. Otherwise, we fix nonempty and minimal
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then, for any open cover
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
of
X
, there is an
\alpha
so that
U_{\alpha}\cap F
is non-empty. And relatively open.
Moreover,
(F,\mathcal{F}\cap F,T|_{F})
is a minimal system. Hence

U_{\alpha}\cap F
must contain an arithmetic progression of the desired type, so
Theorem
4.1
follows.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We do this by induction on the length
k
of the arithmetic progression. The case
k=1
is trivial, so we take
k\geq 2
and assume that the Theorem has been proved for
k-1
. Note that this also implies that Theorem
4.2
holds for
k-1
.

Fix a minimal system
(X,\mathcal{F},T)
and an open cover
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
, which we can take to be finite. We must show that one of the
U_{\alpha}
contains an arithmetic progression
x,T^{r^{\prime}}x,\cdots,T^{(\ell-1)r^{\prime}}x
of length
\ell
, with

.

We now require the following Lemma, sometimes referred to as the ‘color focusing’ Lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let the notation and assumptions be as above. Then for any
J\geq 0
there exists a sequence
x_{0},\cdots,x_{J}
of points in
X
, a sequence
U_{\alpha_{0}},\cdots,U_{\alpha_{J}}
of sets in the open cover (not necessarily distinct), and a sequence
k_{1},\cdots,k_{J}
of elements of
\mathcal{K}
such that

(1)

k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}
for all
0\leq j\leq j^{\prime}\leq J
, and

(2)

For all
0\leq j\leq j^{\prime}\leq J
and
1\leq i\leq\ell-1

\displaystyle T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{j^{\prime}})}x_{j^{\prime}}\in U_{\alpha_{j}}

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We induct on
J
. The case
J=0
is trivial. Now suppose inductively that
J\geq 1
, and that we have already constructed points
x_{0},\cdots,x_{J-1}
, and sets
U_{\alpha_{0}},\cdots,U_{\alpha_{J-1}}
, and integers
k_{1},\cdots,k_{J-1}
with the requisite properties. Choose
h
sufficiently large such that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{J-1}<b^{h}
. Now, let
V
be a suitably small neighborhood of
x_{J-1}
(depending on all this data), to be determined later. By Theorem
4.2
for
k-1
,

contains an arithmetic progression
y,T^{r}y,\cdots,T^{(k-2)r}y
for some
y\in X
and

. We may write

\displaystyle r=r_{0}+b^{h}r_{1}

for some

with

.
(Note here that we require

in the case that

is zero.)
If one sets

and

to be an arbitrary member of the open cover that contains

, then we observe that

\displaystyle T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{J-1}+b^{h}r_{1})}x_{J}

\displaystyle=T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{J-1})}(T^{(i-1)b^{h}r_{1}}y)

\displaystyle=T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{J-1})}(T^{(i-1)(r-r_{0})}y)

\displaystyle=T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{J-1})}T^{-r_{0}(i-1)}(T^{(i-1)r}y)

for all

and

. The important point is that we now have control over the number of transformations, because

. By continuity of

, we then can choose our neighborhood

to be sufficiently small to ensure that

\displaystyle T^{i(k_{j+1}+k_{j+2}+\cdots+k_{J-1}+b^{h}r_{1})}x_{J}\in U_{\alpha_{J}},\quad\quad\forall\ 0\leq j<J,\ 1\leq i\leq k-1.

By choosing

∎

We then apply the above lemma with

equal to the number of sets in the open cover. By the pigeonhole principle, we can then find some

such that

. If we set

and

then we complete the induction step for

∎

5.
Compact Systems and Extensions

We address the compact cases of Lemma

Throughout, let

Theorem 5.1

Let

Then, for non-negative

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\int\prod_{s=0}^{t}T^{sk_{n}}f-\prod_{s=0}^{t}h^{t+1}\;dm\Bigr\rvert

We now address compact extensions.

Theorem 5.6

If

is an ergodic factor

The proof follows the lines of Furstenberg’s original proof, using the van der Waerden Theorem.
This line of reasoning is available to us as we have established the Cantor set version of van der Waerden’s Theorem.
However, once that step is done, we appeal to the Szemerédi Theorem.

We need this known Lemma.

Lemma 5.8

Let
\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}

We can proceed under the assumption that

For if not, fix

Then, let

It clearly suffices to prove the theorem for

We proceed with a choice of

The function
f
is conditionally compact, so we can a finite collection of functions

For

Then, we can select color
