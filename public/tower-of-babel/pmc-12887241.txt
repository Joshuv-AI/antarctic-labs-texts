Extreme Ambient Air Pressure and Its Drop Between Neighboring Days Are Associated With an Increased Risk of Acute Myocardial Infarction Onset: A Case‐Crossover Study
Authors: Qinghui Zeng, Piaopiao Hu, Jie Chang, Moning Guo, Feng Lu, Yue Qi, Zhao Yang, Yongchen Hao, Qiuju Deng, Jing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between air pressure and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) has not been assessed at individual level, and the effect of air pressure change between neighboring days (APCN) on AMI onset is unknown. This study aimed to examine the associations of daily air pressure and APCN with the onset of AMI.MethodsA time‐stratified case‐crossover study was conducted using data from 473 599 patients with AMI recorded in the Beijing Cardiovascular Disease Surveillance System between 2007 and 2021. The associations between daily air pressure and APCN with AMI onset were assessed using conditional logistic regression and distributed lag‐nonlinear models.ResultsBoth low and high air pressure were significantly associated with an increased risk of AMI, with odds ratios (ORs) of 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.12) and 1.16 (95% CI, 1.11–1.20), respectively. Patients with comorbidities of heart failure and hypertension exhibited increased vulnerability to extremely high air pressure. Moreover, the effects of both extremely low and high air pressure were amplified on days with higher levels of particulate matter <2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter and temperature. A negative APCN was associated with an elevated risk of AMI, with an OR of 1.19 (95% CI, 1.12–1.25) for an extremely negative APCN (−9.74 hPa) compared with no change in air pressure. Negative APCN risk was notably higher in older adults, women, and individuals with hypertension or diabetes.ConclusionsExtreme air pressure and its acute drop between neighboring days are associated with increased AMI onset risk, highlighting the need for enhanced attention in predicting or preventing environment‐attributed cardiovascular risk.

The global burden of cardiovascular diseases is primarily driven by ischemic heart disease. 1 , 2 Among the most severe outcomes of ischemic heart disease is acute myocardial infarction (AMI), which accounts for >30% of ischemic heart disease‐related mortality. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 Although environmental factors such as extreme temperatures and air pollution have been recognized as triggers for the acute onset of AMI, 5 , 6 , 7 the influence of other meteorological factors remains less thoroughly investigated.

Air pressure is defined as the force exerted per unit area by the weight of the overlying atmosphere. 7 Although previous studies have linked air pressure with the risk of cardiovascular death, these investigations often employed time‐series designs. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Only a few studies have adopted an individual‐level design, yet these relied on exposure estimates derived from monitoring stations. 13 To date, there is a lack of individual‐level studies using high‐resolution, finer‐gridded environmental data that can more accurately reflect the exposure‐response relationship. 14 Moreover, these studies often used AMI hospitalizations as outcomes, which may not fully reflect the risk of AMI, given that a considerable proportion of AMI deaths occur before hospital admission. 9 , 11 , 12 , 15 Notably, the potential role of air pressure changes between neighboring days (APCN) in the onset of AMI has yet to be investigated. Additionally, there is limited understanding of the susceptible subgroups within the population affected by extreme air pressure and its acute changes, as well as the interactions between air pressure and other environmental factors. Addressing these inquiries could significantly enhance our understanding and inform preventive strategies for AMI.

In response to these gaps, we conducted a time‐stratified case‐crossover study using a large sample of over 470 000 AMI cases from the BCDSS (Beijing Cardiovascular Disease Surveillance System) in Beijing, China. This study aimed to quantitatively assess the association between ambient air pressure and APCN with the onset of AMI and to identify specific vulnerable populations. Additionally, we examined the potential effect modification by levels of air pollutants and temperature within the residential environment.

METHODS

The data used in this study were obtained from the Beijing Municipal Health Big Data and Policy Research Center and cannot be shared publicly, given institutional regulations and data confidentiality agreements. These data may be requested by researchers from the data holder authorities for research purposes. The analytic methods can be reproduced based on the details provided in this article, and the statistical analysis code is available upon request. The corresponding author (J.L.) has full access to all of the data used in the study and takes full responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis. The study was approved by the ethics review committee at Beijing An Zhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, and the requirement for informed consent was waived (2021139X). Our study aims to examine the relationship between air pressure and AMI. It does not involve an analysis of racial or ethnic health disparities.

Study Setting

Beijing, the capital of China, is located in the Northern China Plain (39°56′ N and 116°20′ E), covering an area of 16 410 km2. The city features a typical continental monsoon climate. 16 , 17 The topography of Beijing is primarily flat, with an average elevation of 43.5 meters.

Study Design

A time‐stratified case‐crossover study design was employed to assess the association between air pressure and the onset of AMI. Each case period was matched with 3 to 4 control periods from the same days of the week within the same calendar month and year, serving as self‐controls. This design effectively accounts for potential confounders that are either constant (eg, individual characteristics) or change slowly over time (eg, lifestyle habits) throughout the study period. 18

Study Population

The AMI events were obtained from the BCDSS, 19 which monitors residents of all age groups with Beijing household registration. The BCDSS integrates data from the Beijing Hospital Discharge Information System and Beijing Vital Registration Monitoring System using personal identification information. The Hospital Discharge Information System, managed by the Beijing Municipal Health Big Data and Policy Research Center, comprises discharge information submitted by all secondary and tertiary nonmilitary hospitals in Beijing. Vital Registration Monitoring System, managed by Beijing Center for Disease Control and Prevention, encompasses information derived from the death certificates of the entire population in Beijing. In summary, this city‐level monitoring system has achieved near‐complete coverage of the entire population.

Between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2021, a total of 529 903 AMI events among permanent residents of Beijing were identified in BCDSS. To ensure accurate identification of AMI events, multiple steps were implemented. Events were considered a continuous course of care and merged if a patient was rehospitalized or transferred on the day following discharge (n=9632). Events were excluded if the patient was hospitalized for <1 day and discharged alive without subsequent rehospitalization or death on the same day, indicating they were unlikely to be true AMI cases (n=4460). In accordance with the World Health Organization MONICA (Multinational Monitoring of Trends in Cardiovascular Disease) protocols, events were considered as a single AMI event if there was any record of subsequent rehospitalization or death within 28 days (n=8349); in such instances, the onset time of the first event and the outcome within 28 days were retained. Onset date of AMI was defined as the admission date in the BCDSS. We evaluated the accuracy of AMI onset dates recorded in the BCDSS in a sample of 4220 patients who were selected using a stratified sampling strategy by hospital level (secondary or tertiary) and geographical location (urban, suburban, or extraurban). The agreement between onset dates documented in the BCDSS and original admission date obtained from hospital medical records was found in 4209 cases (99.7%).

The current addresses of patients with AMI were then geocoded using automated geocoding services via AMAP and Tencent Map, supplemented by manual verification. 20 Patients were excluded if their current address was not within Beijing (n=9385). To reduce the impact of outliers, barometric pressures <2.5th percentile and >97.5th percentile of the barometric pressure distribution were excluded (n=24 478) in accordance with previous studies. 21 Ultimately, 473 599 AMI events were included in the final analysis.

Further characteristics of the AMI events, including age, sex, marital status, onset time, residential address, and comorbidities (heart failure, arrhythmia, hypertension, and diabetes), were extracted. AMI types were classified based on the principal discharge diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes: I21.0 to I21.3, I22.0, I22.1, and I22.8 for ST‐segment–elevation MI (STEMI), and I21.4 for non‐STEMI (NSTEMI). Comorbidities were identified based on other discharge diagnoses, employing the following ICD‐10 codes: heart failure (I50), arrhythmia (I46–I49), hypertension (I10–I15), and diabetes (E10–E14). 22

Environmental Data

All exposure data are sourced from publicly available high‐resolution satellite‐retrieved data. Meteorological factor data were obtained from the ERA5‐land datas et, encompassing air pressure (hPa), temperature (°C), and dew point temperature (°C) with a spatial resolution of 9 km and a temporal resolution of 1 hour. 23 Daily averages were calculated as the mean of 24‐hour measurements. Air pressure variation was assessed by calculating the daily change between neighboring days (APCN). Particulate matter <2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM2.5) concentrations (μg/m3) were sourced from the Tracking Air Pollution in China database (http://tapdata.org.cn/), which provides a spatial resolution of 1 km and a temporal resolution of 1 day. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Individual‐level environmental exposures for both case and control days were assigned based on the patients’ residential addresses.

Statistical Analysis

The AMI events were stratified by patient characteristics. Meteorological variables and air pollutant levels were summarized using the mean±SD, minimum, maximum, and selected percentiles. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify the relationships among air pressure, APCN, temperature, dew point temperature, and PM2.5.

A conditional logistic regression model combined with a distributed lag nonlinear model was employed to assess the association between exposure (air pressure or APCN) and AMI onset. 28 This modeling approach allows for a flexible representation of exposure–response relationships over time, accounting for both immediate and delayed (“lagged”) effects at (single‐lag effects), and over cumulative periods spanning multiple days post exposure (cumulative‐lag effects). 29 There is a strong correlation between temperature and dew point temperature (Spearman correlation coefficient >0.9, Table S1). Given that dew point temperature could better represent the combined effects of heat and humidity stress, only dew point temperature was included in the primary analysis to mitigate multicollinearity issues. 30 The model used is specified as follows: logitPcase=1in stratumiexposurecovariate=α+stratumi+βExposuret,l+Dewpoint temperaturet+PM2.5t, where Pcase=1in stratumiexposurecovariate denotes the conditional probability of AMI occurrence in the i th stratum given the exposure (air pressure or APCN), dew point temperature, and PM2.5 concentrations. Exposuret,l is the cross‐basis matrix of air pressure or APCN, and 2 natural cubic spline functions with 3 degrees of freedom to describe the nonlinear effects across both exposure and lag dimensions. Here, l represents the maximum number of lag days, and t denotes the day t in the lag period. Given that 3 degrees of freedom have been extensively used in prior studies employing distributed lag nonlinear model studies, 31 we applied the same parameterization in our model. Multicollinearity was formally assessed using generalized variance inflation factors. All generalized variance inflation factors were below the conventional threshold of 5 (Table S2), confirming no significant multicollinearity among predictors.

To determine the duration of the effect, lag structure plots were generated, illustrating the association with AMI onset along with lag days for air pressure at extremely low (P2.5, 971.83 hPa) and high (P97.5, 1024.95 hPa) levels, as well as APCN values, defined as extreme negative APCN (P1, −9.74 hPa), extreme positive APCN (P99, 11.30 hPa), negative APCN (P10, −5.19 hPa) and positive APCN (P90, 5.18 hPa). We first considered a maximum lag of 21 days before AMI onset (lag 0–21 days) to comprehensively examine the lag‐response pattern and then examined the single‐day lag‐response curves and found that the risk approached a null effect at distinct lag days for each exposure: at lag 2 days for extremely low air pressure, at lag 18 days for extremely high air pressure, and at lag 8 days for APCN. Accordingly, we subsequently restricted the analysis of cumulative‐lag effects to these windows. The 21‐day lag period was sufficient to capture lag effects, as effects beyond these lags were no longer statistically significant. 28 Cumulative‐lag effects for extreme air pressure were quantified as odds ratios (ORs), comparing the risk associated with low (P2.5, 971.83 hPa), and high (P97.5, 1024.95 hPa) air pressure to the median value (P50, 1005 hPa). Similarly, cumulative‐lag effects of APCN were assessed by calculating ORs for extreme negative APCN (P1, −9.74 hPa) and negative (P10, −5.19 hPa) APCN values relative to the reference (0 hPa). We also isolated the independent effects of APCN by incorporating both the daily mean air pressure and APCN simultaneously into the model. The annual number of AMI cases attributable to extreme air pressure events was estimated using a formula detailed in Supplemental Material S1. Subgroup analyses were conducted in hospitalized patients to identify vulnerable populations based on sex, age group (<65 and ≥65 years), AMI type (STEMI versus NSTEMI), and comorbidities (heart failure, arrhythmia, hypertension, and diabetes). Effect modification by temperature and PM2.5 was evaluated to determine their potential influence on the relationship between extreme air pressure or APCN and AMI onset. Temperature and PM2.5 were dichotomized based on their medians (12.18 °C and 57.55 μg/m3, respectively). A Z‐test was conducted to compare the differences in effect estimates across subgroups, and P values for interaction were reported to assess the statistical significance of effect modifications. 32

Sensitivity Analyses

To ensure the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. We considered sensitivity analyses by adding the daily mean temperature into the model to adjust for possible confounding by temperature. In addition, we further controlled for ozone levels using data from 2013 to 2021 to account for potential confounders since ozone data were first available in 2013. To address the potential influence of the COVID‐19 pandemic on the results, another analysis was restricted to the period between 2007 and 2019. Additionally, to evaluate the robustness of the model specification, sensitivity analyses were performed using 2 and 4 degrees of freedom for both the exposure‐response and lag‐response dimensions.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (www.r‐project.org), specifically using the “survival” and “dlnm” packages. Statistical tests were two‐tailed, with a significance threshold set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Descriptive Statistics

Between 2007 and 2021, a total of 473 599 AMI cases were identified. The mean age of these patients was 72.5 years (SD: 13.6), with 71.7% (n=339 449) being >65 years of age. The majority of patients with AMI were male, accounting for 60.1% (n=285 097) (Table S3). During the study period, the median daily air pressure was 1005.71 hPa (interquartile range [IQR], 17.89 hPa), with a range from 949.93 to 1027.53 hPa (Table 1). Notable regional variations in air pressure were observed in Beijing, with higher values predominantly in the southeastern region and lower values in the northwestern region (Figure S1). The median APCN was −0.27 hPa (IQR, 5.12 hPa), with values ranging from −16.42 to 19.38 hPa. Additionally, the median values for temperature, dew point temperature, and PM2.5 concentrations were 12.18 °C (IQR, 22.01 °C), 0.29 °C (IQR, 22.42 °C), and 57.55 μg/m3 (IQR, 66.35 μg/m3), respectively (Table 1). The Spearman correlation matrix for all variables was provided in Table S1. As shown, aside from the strong correlation between temperature and dew point temperature (r>0.6), all other variable pairs demonstrated relatively low correlation coefficients below 0.54. Specifically, the correlation between air pressure and APCN was −0.12, and between air pressure and PM2.5 was 0.19.

Association Between Air Pressure and the Onset of AMI

The single‐day lag patterns of the associations between extremely low air pressure (P2.5, 971.83 hPa) and extremely high air pressure (P97.5, 1024.95 hPa) with the onset of AMI were analyzed (Figures S2 and S3). A significant risk associated with extremely low air pressure was observed on the concurrent day, with the effect diminishing by lag day 2 and becoming statistically insignificant thereafter. Therefore, the cumulative risks associated with extremely low air pressure over a lag period of 0 to 2 days were considered in subsequent analyses. In contrast, the lag‐response associations for extremely high air pressure (P97.5, 1024.95 hPa), persisted for up to 18 days (Figure S3). Consequently, cumulative risks associated with extremely high air pressure were examined over a lag period of 0 to 18 days.

Both extremely low and high air pressure were associated with an elevated cumulative risk of AMI onset (Figure 1, Figures S4 and S5). Specifically, the OR for AMI onset associated with extremely low air pressure (2.5th percentile versus 50th percentile) was 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.12). For extremely high air pressure (97.5th percentile versus 50th percentile), the OR was 1.16 (95% CI, 1.11–1.20) (Figure 1). In addition, we estimated roughly 1915 excess cases per year associated with extremely low air pressure, 4666 for extremely high air pressure.

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the cumulative risks of AMI onset associated with extremely low and high air pressure, stratified by patient characteristics (Figure 2). A more pronounced association between extremely low air pressure and AMI was observed among unmarried individuals (P for interaction=0.04). Additionally, the OR for AMI onset associated with extremely high air pressure was significantly elevated among patients with comorbidities of heart failure (P for interaction <0.01) and hypertension (P for interaction=0.01). Further effect modification analysis revealed that the effects of extremely low air pressure were higher on days with elevated levels of PM2.5 compared with days with lower levels (P for interaction=0.02). Similarly, the effect of extremely high air pressure was modified by temperature, with a more substantial effect observed on days with higher temperatures (P for interaction <0.01) (Table 2).

Association Between APCN and the Onset of AMI

The lag‐response analysis for the association between APCN and AMI onset demonstrated a significant effect of APCN from the concurrent day (lag 0) up to 8 days (Figures S6 and S7). Consequently, cumulative risks associated with APCN over a lag period of 0 to 8 days were reported. The exposure–response relationship between APCN and the risk of AMI onset, cumulated over this lag period, is illustrated in Figure 3. Notably, an inverse relationship was observed, with negative APCN correlating with an elevated risk of AMI onset. Specifically, compared with no change in air pressure from the previous day, the adjusted ORs for AMI onset associated with negative APCN (10th percentile) and extremely negative APCN (first percentile) were 1.08 (95% CI, 1.06–1.11) and 1.19 (95% CI, 1.12–1.25), respectively. (Figure 4). Notably, the exposure–response curves for APCN did not change substantially after adjusting for air pressure. Furthermore, we estimated approximately 5401 excess cases per year associated with extreme 1‐day air pressure drops.

Subgroup analyses further assessed the cumulative risk of AMI onset in relation to negative APCN, stratified by patient characteristics (Figure 4). A significantly higher risk of AMI onset was observed in patients aged >65, female, unmarried, diagnosed with NSTEMI, or those with comorbidities such as hypertension or diabetes. Sensitivity Analyses

The results of the sensitivity analyses demonstrated general consistency with the primary findings. The exposure–response curves for air pressure remained largely stable after adjustment for temperature (Figures S11 through S13). Associations remained positive and statistically significant between extreme air pressure and negative APCN and the risk of AMI when O3 was further adjusted (Figures S14 and S15). Additionally, the exposure‐response curves for both air pressure (Figure S16) and APCN (Figure S17) from the period 2007 to 2019 produced comparable results to those obtained from the 2007 to 2021 data set. The exposure‐response curves for air pressure and APCN were largely consistent across models with 2, 3, and 4 degrees of freedom (Figures S18 through S23).

DISCUSSION

Principal Findings

This time‐stratified case‐crossover study, based on a large sample of citywide registry data, identified significant associations between extremely low and high air pressure and the onset of AMI at the individual level. Notably, this investigation highlights negative APCN as a noteworthy environmental factor contributing to AMI occurrence. Subgroup analyses revealed that older individuals, women, unmarried individuals, patients diagnosed with NSTEMI, and those with a history of hypertension or diabetes demonstrated heightened susceptibility to fluctuations in atmospheric pressure or its rapid decline. Furthermore, significant effect modification by temperature and PM2.5 was observed regarding the association between air pressure and AMI onset.

Comparison With Other Studies

Previous studies primarily used AMI hospitalizations as outcomes, which may not fully reflect the risk of AMI, given that a considerable proportion of AMI deaths occur before hospital admission. 8 , 9 , 12 , 33 Only 1 study that examined AMI onset as the outcome identified a V‐shaped relationship between the citywide average air pressure level and AMI. 11 Our study confirmed this relationship and further extended these findings by employing high‐resolution (9 km) environmental data, thereby more precisely assessing the relationship between air pressure and AMI. 14 Besides, we compared the ORs for extreme air pressure events—calculated at specific percentiles relative to the minimum‐risk reference value—including 1.06 at the 2.5th percentile (low air pressure) and 1.16 at the 97.5th percentile (high air pressure), with the ORs reported for extreme temperature in a previous study also conducted in Beijing: 1.094 at the 97.5th percentile and 1.471 at the 2.5th percentile, relative to the minimum‐risk temperature. 34 This comparison demonstrates that the modest associations we observed for air pressure are of similar magnitude to those for temperature. Although the individual‐level risk increase under extreme air pressure events was modest, the large population size of Beijing could still result in a considerable absolute number of excess AMI cases. Furthermore, although previous studies have adjusted for environmental factors such as temperature and air pollution as confounders, 8 , 9 , 11 our study advanced these by systematically examine effect modification (rather than just adjustment) by these factors. Our study fills this gap by examining how established environmental risk factors, such as temperature and air pollution, modulate the association between extreme air pressure and AMI onset. This analysis is particularly relevant for public health interventions, as it highlights the interplay between extreme air pressure, temperature, and PM2.5.

An important finding of this study is the identification of a negative APCN, a metric representing a drop in air pressure from previous day, as a risk factor for increased AMI onset. Although no prior studies have directly explored the association between APCN and AMI onset, 2 studies examining diurnal barometric pressure differences found that sharp declines in barometric pressure over the course of a day were associated with increased AMI risk, which partially supports our findings. 10 , 13 Additionally, we observed that individuals over the age of 65, women, those diagnosed with NSTEMI, or those with a history of hypertension or diabetes exhibited a higher AMI risk associated with negative APCN. These factors have been infrequently reported in earlier studies, highlighting the novel contributions of our research.

Although the precise biological mechanisms underlying the link between atmospheric pressure and AMI onset remain to be fully elucidated, several plausible hypotheses have been proposed. Atmospheric pressure may influence vascular function by affecting vessel walls, triggering endogenous inflammatory processes, and impairing endothelial function, thereby promoting cardiovascular disease. 35 Specifically, low atmospheric pressure could induce hypoxia, increasing the risk of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events. 36 Conversely, elevated air pressure may exacerbate vascular disease by influencing vasoconstriction. 37 , 38 Previous studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between barometric pressure and blood oxygen saturation, indicating that decreases in atmospheric pressure are associated with declines in blood oxygenation and increases in pulse rate. 39 , 40 In summary, exposure to extreme barometric pressure and acute decreases in atmospheric pressure may compromise vascular integrity and increase cardiac workload, thus heightening the risk of cardiovascular events. Further experimental studies are necessary to elucidate these underlying mechanisms.

Implications for Public Health

The findings of this study have significant implications for policymakers, individuals, and the scientific community. Although air pressure is a trigger rather than a modifiable risk factor, it can serve as a valuable early warning indicator. It is reasonable to integrate extreme air pressure and acute drop into existing meteorological warning systems which usually provide alerts for extreme weather such as heat waves and cold spells. These systems could incorporate measures to promptly inform residents about air pressure fluctuations, including sending text message alerts or notifications to enhance awareness. Upon receiving these notifications, vulnerable individuals (eg, women, older adults, unmarried individuals, patients with NSTEMI, and those with hypertension or diabetes) are advised to monitor cardiovascular symptoms such as chest discomfort or shortness of breath, avoid vigorous outdoor activities and physical exertion. Patients with hypertension or diabetes should monitor blood pressure and blood glucose change and ensure adherence to prescribed medications. For health care systems, emergency physicians should recognize the cardiovascular risks associated with air pressure, while emergency medical services should prepare contingency plans for potential surges in AMI events. Moreover, the observed association between air pressure and AMI under conditions of elevated temperature and pollution highlights the necessity for targeted interventions addressing extreme weather conditions and severe pollution, such as improving air quality or minimizing outdoor exposure during high‐risk periods, thereby mitigating risk of combined exposure. Lastly, for researchers, this study underscores the importance of considering air pressure as a critical environmental factor in future investigations of AMI onset. 41

Strengths

This study presents several notable strengths. First, this is the first study to explore the health effects of APCN on the onset of AMI and analyze the effect modification of meteorological factors and air pollution on the impact of air pressure. Furthermore, the use of a case‐crossover design provides robust control of time‐invariant covariates at the individual level, such as personal characteristics, as well as slowly varying covariates, including lifestyle habits. 28 A further strength lies in the extensive sample size derived from a citywide registry, enabling the examination of the effects of extreme air pressure across various subgroups. The study also incorporates finer‐gridded environmental data, significantly improving the precision of individual‐level exposure assessments. Additionally, the direct use of AMI onset as an outcome measure, encompassing both hospitalized cases and deaths, facilitates a comprehensive analysis of the association between air pressure and AMI onset. 19 Finally, the diagnostic data have been validated according to the World Health Organization MONICA criteria via chart reviews, ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the data set. 42

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration in this study. First, we used outdoor ambient air pressure at residential addresses as a proxy for exposure, without accounting for indoor environmental conditions. Nonetheless, previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between indoor and outdoor air pressure, supporting the validity of outdoor air pressure as an appropriate surrogate for personal exposure. 13 Second, all exposure measurements were confined to individual residential addresses, requiring the assumption that patients remained at or near their homes on the day of, or in the days preceding, AMI onset during control days within the same month, which might introduce some exposure measurement errors. Third, although the time‐stratified case‐crossover design adjusted for potential confounding factors, some unmeasured time‐varying variables—such as dietary habits, physical activity, and medication use—may partially influence the observed associations. However, it is reasonable to assume that these factors would not vary significantly within the 1‐month period considered. Fourth, despite the large sample size exceeding 470 000 participants, the study was limited to a single city, which constrains the generalizability of the findings. Further research in diverse geographic regions—particularly those with distinct climate conditions, varying altitudes, or different baseline air pressure levels—is necessary to validate and extend these results. Fifth, as this study is inherently observational and lacks the ability to demonstrate causal relationships, the results should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, although data for comorbidity (eg, diabetes and hypertension) were systematically recorded for all hospitalized patients, it was unavailable for out‐of‐hospital deaths. Thus, caution is advised when extending these findings to broader populations. Finally, although the lag period was derived using the widely used distributed lag nonlinear model approach, further mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the underlying processes.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that extreme air pressure and acute drops in air pressure are significantly associated with increased incident risk of AMI, with these effects further modified by air pollution and temperature. Vulnerable groups, including women, older adults, unmarried individuals, patients with NSTEMI, and those with hypertension or diabetes, are particularly at risk. To address these findings, we propose integrating air pressure alerts into meteorological warning systems and adopting targeted preventive measures, such as key health indicators monitoring and actionable strategies to mitigate multienvironmental exposures for high‐risk populations.
