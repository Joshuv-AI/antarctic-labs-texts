Abstract
. We investigate real eigenvalues of real elliptic Ginibre matrices of size n n , indexed by the parameter of asymmetry τ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \tau\in[0,1] . In both the strongly and weakly non-Hermitian regimes, where τ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \tau\in[0,1) is fixed or 1 − τ = O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) 1-\tau=O(1/n) , respectively, we derive the asymptotic expansion of the probability p n , n − 2 ​ l p_{n,n-2l} that all but a finite number 2 ​ l 2l of eigenvalues are real. In particular, we show that the expansion is of the form log ⁡ p n , n − 2 ​ l = { a 1 ​ n 2 + a 2 ​ n + a 3 ​ log ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( 1 ) at strong non-Hermiticity , b 1 ​ n + b 2 ​ log ⁡ n + b 3 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) at weak non-Hermiticity , \displaystyle\log p_{n,n-2l}=\begin{cases}a_{1}n^{2}+a_{2}n+a_{3}\log n+O(1)&\textup{at strong non-Hermiticity},\\ b_{1}n+b_{2}\log n+b_{3}+o(1)&\textup{at weak non-Hermiticity},\end{cases} and we determine all coefficients explicitly. In the latter case, we utilise the skew-orthogonal polynomial formalism and the asymptotic behaviour of the Hermite polynomials.

Introduction and Main results

In the study of point processes, one of the most natural observables of interest is their counting or number statistics, which addresses fundamental questions about how many particles can be observed within a given domain.
From a probabilistic perspective, the natural questions that arise can be explored step by step as follows:

•

What is the typical number of particles?

This involves determining both the expectation and variance of the observable, which are central to the law of large numbers.

•

What are the fluctuations around the typical number of particles?

This question provides a more refined statistical description and relates to the central limit theorem.

•

What is the probability of rare events?

Here, one typically investigates the probability of observing an extremely small or large number of particles compared to the typical number. This falls within the scope of large deviation theory.

These types of questions have been extensively studied in the literature and serve as central topics in the field.

In this work, we address a question in this direction for a point process arising from non-Hermitian random matrix theory. Specifically, we focus on asymmetric random matrices with real entries, with the real Ginibre matrix (denoted by GinOE) serving as a prototypical example
[
11
]
.
For asymmetric random matrices, an interesting domain of study in the number statistics is the real axis. In other words, we examine the number statistics of real eigenvalues.
The presence of real eigenvalues with non-zero probability is particularly characteristic of the orthogonal symmetry class, distinguishing it from the unitary and symplectic symmetry classes (see e.g.

We first introduce our model of study, the elliptic GinOE. The GinOE is an
n\times n
matrix
G
with independent and identically distributed standard normal entries. Its elliptic extension is then defined as

(1.1)

X:=\frac{\sqrt{1+\tau}}{2}(G+G^{T})+\frac{\sqrt{1-\tau}}{2}(G-G^{T}),

where
\tau\in[0,1)
is the asymmetry parameter. This parameter governs the extent to which the matrix deviates from being symmetric, allowing the elliptic GinOE to interpolate between the GinOE (
\tau=0
) and the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) in the limit as
\tau\to 1
.

We investigate the asymptotic properties of the real eigenvalues of the elliptic GinOE as
n\to\infty
. When examining the asymptotic behaviour of the elliptic GinOE, two distinct regimes of interest emerge:

•

Strong Non-Hermiticity
: This is the case where
\tau\in[0,1)
is fixed as
n\to\infty
.

•

Weak Non-Hermiticity
: This corresponds to the case where
\tau\to 1
at a specific rate. In our study, we consider
\tau=1-\alpha^{2}/n
, where
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
is a fixed parameter. In this regime, one can observe critical transitions between asymmetric and symmetric matrices. This regime was introduced in the papers
[
36
,
37
,
38

.

For the readers’ convenience, we provide a brief summary of the development of the study of real eigenvalues of the elliptic GinOE, both at strong and weak non-Hermiticity.

The first natural question concerns the law of large numbers. To address this, let
\mathcal{N}\equiv\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
denote the (random) number of real eigenvalues of the elliptic GinOE. In order to establish the law of large numbers for the random variable
\mathcal{N}
(viewed as a sequence indexed by
N
), one needs to determine the asymptotic behaviour of its mean and variance. It was established in
[
33
,
12
]
(cf. see
[
21
]
for an earlier study on the GinOE case) that

(1.2)

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{b}(\tau)\sqrt{n}+O(1),&\textup{for }\tau\in[0,1)\text{ fixed},\\
\mathfrak{c}(\alpha)\,n+O(1),&\textup{for }\tau=1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n},\end{cases}

where the expectation is taken with respect to the elliptic GinOE, and

(1.3)

\mathfrak{b}(\tau):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1+\tau}{1-\tau}},\qquad\mathfrak{c}(\alpha):=e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}\Big(I_{0}\big(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\big)+I_{1}\big(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\big)\Big).

Here,
I_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind given by

(1.4)

I_{\nu}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z/2)^{2k+\nu}}{k!\,\Gamma(\nu+k+1)},

see e.g.
[
50
, Chapter 10]
. We mention that the coefficients (
1.2
) can be realised as normalisation constants in the limiting macroscopic densities
[
22
,
12
,
13
]
.

It is evident from (
1.2
) that a non-trivial proportion of eigenvalues is expected to be real in weak non-Hermiticity, whereas only a subdominant
O(\sqrt{n})
number of eigenvalues are real in strong non-Hermiticity. This observation aligns with the intuition that the number of real eigenvalues increases as the ensemble becomes more symmetric.
In addition to the mean asymptotic of
\mathcal{N}
, its variance has been studied in
[
33
,
12
]
as well, which reads

(1.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\textup{Var}\,\mathcal{N}}{\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}}=\begin{cases}2-\sqrt{2},&\textup{for }\tau\in[0,1)\text{ fixed},\\
2-2\dfrac{\mathfrak{c}(\sqrt{2}\alpha)}{\mathfrak{c}(\alpha)},&\textup{for }\tau=1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}.\end{cases}

Combining (
1.2
) and (
1.5
), one can immediately observe the law of large numbers for
\mathcal{N}
in probability.

The final question of interest concerns the probability of rare events. In our context, one again needs to distinguish further between two cases:

•

Left tail event
: This corresponds to the case where there is an exceptionally small number of real eigenvalues compared to the typical number.

•

Right tail event
: This is the opposite to the first one, namely, the situation where there is an exceptionally large number of real eigenvalues.

See Figure
1

To describe such rare events, let
p_{n,m}
denote the probability that the elliptic GinOE of size
n
has exactly
m
real eigenvalues.
The left tail event was addressed in
[
14
]

m

m=O(\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}_{n}/\log n)
–the following holds:

(1.7)

\log p_{n,m}\begin{cases}=\mathfrak{d}(\tau)\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n}),&\textup{for }\tau\in[0,1)\text{ fixed},\\
\leq\mathfrak{f}(\alpha)\,n+o(n),&\textup{for }\tau=1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n},\end{cases}

where

(1.8)

\mathfrak{d}(\tau):=-\sqrt{\frac{1+\tau}{1-\tau}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\zeta(\tfrac{3}{2}),\qquad\mathfrak{f}(\alpha):=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(1-e^{-\alpha^{2}s^{2}}\Big)\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\,ds.

Here,
\zeta(s)

[

In contrast to the left tail case, the right tail event remains less understood. In this work, we aim to contribute to this direction.
In the extreme case
m=n
, where all eigenvalues are real, a closed-form expression for the probability exists
[

(1.9)

\log p_{n,n}=-\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big)n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big)n.

This formula is derived from the ratio between the partition functions of the elliptic GinOE and the GOE.
The only other known case is for the GinOE, where the spectrum contains only a single complex conjugate pair. To be more precise, it was established in
[

(1.10)

\log p_{n,n-2}\Big|_{\tau=0}=-\frac{\log 2}{4}n^{2}+\Big(\frac{\log 2}{4}+\log 3\Big)n-\frac{1}{2}\log n+\log\frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)

as
n\to\infty
.

Beyond the mentioned results (

In the sequel, we assume that

Our first main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(
The probability of having a finite number
l
of complex eigenvalue pairs
)
.

Let
n
be an even integer and
l
be a fixed nonnegative integer. Then as
n\to\infty
, we have the following.

(i)

(Strong non-Hermiticity)

For fixed
\tau\in[0,1)
, we have

(1.11)

\log p_{n,n-2l}=a_{1}n^{2}+a_{2}n+a_{3}\log n+O(1),

where

(1.12)

a_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big),\qquad a_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big)+l\log\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big),\qquad a_{3}=-\frac{l^{2}}{2}.

(ii)

(Weak non-Hermiticity)

For fixed
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
and
\tau=1-\alpha^{2}/n
, we have

(1.13)

\log p_{n,n-2l}=b_{1}n+b_{2}\log n+b_{3}+O(n^{-\epsilon})

for some sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, where

(1.14)

b_{1}=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8},\qquad b_{2}=l,\qquad b_{3}=l\log\Big(\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}))-1}{2}\Big)-\log(l!)+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}-\frac{\alpha^{4}}{32}.

An equivalent way of writing (
1.11
) is by taking the ratio with the closed formula (
1.9
) as follows

(1.16)

\log\Big(\frac{p_{n,n-2l}}{p_{n,n}}\Big)=l\log\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)n-\frac{l^{2}}{2}\log n+O(1).

On the other hand, by comparing with the case
l=1
, one can also rewrite (
1.16
) as

(1.17)

\frac{p_{n,n-2l}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{1}{n^{l(l-1)/2}}\Big(\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}\Big)^{l}O(1).

Similarly, for weak non-Hermiticity, the expansion (
1.13
) can be rewritten as

(1.18)

\log\Big(\frac{p_{n,n-2l}}{p_{n,n}}\Big)=l\log n+l\log\Big(\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}))-1}{2}\Big)-\log(l!)+O(n^{-\epsilon})

and

(1.19)

\frac{p_{n,n-2l}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{1}{l!}\Big(\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}\Big)^{l}(1+O(n^{-\epsilon})).

Observe that the leading order in the expansions (
1.11
) and (
1.13
) does not depend on
l
. Such behaviour can be intuitively understood from an electrostatic perspective. More precisely,
\log p_{n,n-2l}
can be interpreted as the logarithm of the ratio between the partition function of the elliptic GinOE and that of the elliptic GinOE conditioned on having
n-2l
real eigenvalues. Under this conditional event, the leading-order macroscopic behaviour remains unaffected by the finite parameter
l
. However, this phenomenon no longer holds when
l=O(n)
, as the leading order does depend on the magnitude of
l
. We refer to
[
49
]
for further discussion.

Beyond the leading-order coefficients, the subleading-order coefficients can also be understood from an electrostatic perspective. For example, consider two random
n
-point configurations on the complex plane associated with the Gibbs measure defined by the electrostatic Hamiltonian. One configuration is conditioned to have all
n
points on the real line, while the other is conditioned to have
n-2l
points on the real line and
l

Then, the term
l\log(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau})
in (
1.16
) arises from the typical electrostatic energy difference between these two
n
-point configurations.
See also Lemma
4.3

Our next result focuses on the specific case where
l=1
. In this case, we are able to derive the full order asymptotic expansion.
For this purpose, we first define

(1.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{r}(\tau)&=\frac{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{4\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}(1-\tau)}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{4}\Big)^{r+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\frac{2}{1-\tau}\Big)^{j+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}\\
&\qquad\times\bigg[\Gamma(r+\tfrac{1}{2})+\sum_{q=1}^{r-j}\sum_{k=1}^{q}\Big(\frac{4}{3-\tau}\Big)^{q}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(r+k+\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(k+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k}=q,\\
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{v=1}^{k}\frac{1}{u_{v}+1}\bigg].\end{split}

This will be used in the expansion at strong non-Hermiticity.
Notice that by definition, the first few terms of (
1.20
) are given by

(1.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}_{0}(\tau)=\frac{(3-\tau)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}(1-\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{1}(\tau)=\frac{(3-\tau)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}(9-4\tau+\tau^{2})}{64\sqrt{\pi}(1-\tau)^{\frac{5}{2}}},\\
&\mathcal{A}_{2}(\tau)=\frac{(3-\tau)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(3\tau^{4}-18\tau^{3}+44\tau^{2}-82\tau+143\right)}{512\sqrt{\pi}(1-\tau)^{\frac{7}{2}}}.\end{split}

To describe the expansion for weak non-Hermiticity, we define

(1.22)

\mathcal{B}_{k}(\alpha)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{\tfrac{1}{2}}{j}\Big(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big)^{j}\mathfrak{s}_{k-j}(\alpha),

where

(1.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{s}_{k}(\alpha)&=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(2k-(2k-1)\alpha^{2})\Gamma(k-\tfrac{1}{2})}{2^{k+1}\pi\alpha^{2}\Gamma(k+1)}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathfrak{u}_{k,j}\frac{\Gamma(j-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha^{2}\Gamma(j)}{}_{1}{F}_{1}(j-\tfrac{1}{2},j,\alpha^{2})\\
&\quad-\sum_{q=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-q}\frac{\mathfrak{u}_{k-q,j}}{2^{q}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(q-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\pi\alpha^{2}\Gamma(j+q)}{}_{1}{F}_{1}(j+\tfrac{1}{2},j+q,\alpha^{2}).\end{split}

Here,
{}_{1}{F}_{1}
is Kummer confluent hypergeometric function
[
50

(1.24)

{}_{1}{F}_{1}(a,b,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+k)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b+k)}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

and
\mathfrak{u}_{j,s}
are defined by the coefficients of the polynomial

(1.25)

\sum_{s=0}^{j}\mathfrak{u}_{j,s}t^{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\alpha^{2k}}{2^{j+k}k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j,\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1-(1-2t)^{j_{s}+1}}{j_{s}+1}.

The first few terms are given by

(1.26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{B}_{0}(\alpha)=e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}\Big(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})\Big)-1,\qquad\mathcal{B}_{1}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-\alpha^{2}-1\Big),\\
&\mathcal{B}_{2}(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{24}e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}\Big(\alpha^{2}I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})+(\alpha^{2}-1)I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})\Big),\end{split}

where we expanded

in terms of the modified Bessel functions of the first kind using (

Theorem 1.2

(
The probability of having one complex conjugate pair of eigenvalues, i.e.
l=1
)
.

Let
M
be a fixed positive integer. Then as
n\to\infty
, we have the following.

(i)

(Strong non-Hermiticity)

For fixed
\tau\in[0,1)
, we have

(1.27)

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathcal{A}_{k}(\tau)n^{-k}+O(n^{-M})\Big),

where
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is given by (
1.20
). In particular, we have

(1.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log p_{n,n-2}&=-\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big)n^{2}+\bigg[\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{2}{1+\tau}\Big)+\log\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)\bigg]n\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\log n+\log\Big(\frac{(3-\tau)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}(1-\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Big)+O(n^{-1}).\end{split}

(ii)

(Weak non-Hermiticity)
For fixed
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
and
\tau=1-\alpha^{2}/n
, we have

(1.29)

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{n}{2}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathcal{B}_{k}(\alpha)n^{-k}+O(n^{-M})\Big),

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is given by (
1.22
). In particular, we have

(1.30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log p_{n,n-2}&=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}n+\log n+\log\Big(\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}))-1}{2}\Big)+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}-\frac{\alpha^{4}}{32}\\
&\quad+\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-\alpha^{2}-1}{e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})-I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}))-1}+\frac{\alpha^{4}}{32}-\frac{\alpha^{6}}{96}\bigg]n^{-1}+O(n^{-2}).\end{split}

Notice in particular that (
1.28
) recovers the previous finding (
1.10
) for the extremal case
\tau=0.

We mention that Theorem
1.2
is one of the very few cases where the full order expansion is tractable in an explicit way.

We emphasise that in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, the strong and weak non-Hermiticity regimes should be treated separately, as no uniform asymptotic behaviour holds over the entire range
\tau\in[0,1]
. Nonetheless, the leading-order behaviours can be formally connected—as
\tau\to 1
from the strong non-Hermiticity side and
\alpha\to\infty
from the weak non-Hermiticity side—in a manner analogous to the discussion in
[
12
, Section 1.1]
concerning the mean and variance of the number of real eigenvalues.

To be more precise, assume
\alpha=n^{\epsilon}
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Then, one can verify that the right-hand side of (
1.27
) exhibits the asymptotic behaviour

\frac{n}{2\sqrt{\pi}\alpha^{3}}e^{\alpha^{2}}\Big(1+O(n^{-4\epsilon})\Big),

On the other hand, using the asymptotic expansions of the modified Bessel functions of the first kind,

I_{0}(x)\sim\frac{e^{x}}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{8x}+\frac{9}{128x^{2}}\Big),\qquad I_{1}(x)\sim\frac{e^{x}}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\Big(1-\frac{3}{8x}-\frac{15}{128x^{2}}\Big),\qquad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty,

it follows that the right-hand side of (
1.29
) satisfies the asymptotic behaviour

\frac{n}{2\sqrt{\pi}\alpha^{3}}e^{\alpha^{2}}\Big(1+O(n^{-2\epsilon})\Big).

These expressions show that the two asymptotic behaviours agree at least at the leading order.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. In Section

, we provide some preliminaries for our analysis, including the Pfaffian integration formula for
p_{n,m}
. Section
3
focuses on the special case when there is only one complex conjugate pair, and we prove Theorem
1.2
. In Section
4
, we establish Theorem
1.1
(i) for the strongly non-Hermitian regime using a mean-field approximation and potential-theoretic approach. In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.1
(ii) for the weakly non-Hermitian regime through asymptotic analysis involving skew-orthogonal polynomials.

2.
Integrable structure and Pfaffian formula of the elliptic GinOE

In this section, we recall the Pfaffian formula for the probability
p_{n,m}
.
The formula was derived in
[
4
]
for
\tau=0
using
the Pfaffian integration Theorem
, see also
[
7
]
.
With minor modifications, the derivation of the formula for general
\tau\in[0,1)
is similar to that for
\tau=0
.
We give an idea of the derivation in this section, and refer to
[
4
, Sections 3 and 6]
for details.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{l})\in\mathbb{H}^{l}
, where
\mathbb{H}
is the upper-half plane.
Then, the partial joint probability density function of the elliptic GinOE, with a number
m
of
real eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
l
of complex eigenvalue pairs
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
, can be found in
[
33
]
.
For the latter purpose, it is often convenient to rescale eigenvalues

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\tau}{n}}\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad\boldsymbol{z}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\tau}{n}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}.

To describe the joint probability distribution, it is convenient to introduce the following three Hamiltonian functions that capture pairwise interactions among the
n
eigenvalues and the influence of an external potential:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{2(1+\tau)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq l}\log\frac{1}{|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}|z_{j}-\bar{z}_{k}|^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}Q_{\tau,n}(z_{j}),

where

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{\tau,n}(z)&=-\frac{1}{n}\log\Big[|z-\bar{z}|\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{n}(z-\overline{z})}{i\sqrt{2{(1-\tau^{2})}}}\Big)\Big]+\frac{z^{2}+\overline{z}^{2}}{2(1+\tau)}.\end{split}

Then the joint distribution is given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{m,l}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{z})&=\frac{1}{Z_{m,l}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z})+\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z}))},\end{split}

where

(2.6)

Z_{m,l}=2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}-l}n^{-\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}m!\,l!\,(1+\tau)^{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(j/2).

By definition, the probability
p_{n,m}
to have
m
real eigenvalues and
l
complex conjugate pairs is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle p_{n,m}=\frac{1}{Z_{m,l}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{l}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z})+\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}.

The formalism of skew-orthogonal polynomials is crucial for the derivation of the Pfaffian formula, see
[
4
, Section 3]
.
For this, recall that the GOE skew product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is defined by

(2.8)

\langle f,g\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\xi\,e^{-\xi^{2}/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\eta\,e^{-\eta^{2}/2}\operatorname{sgn}(\eta-\xi)f(\xi)g(\eta).

We define polynomials

(2.9)

q_{2j}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2^{2j}}H_{2j}(\xi),\qquad q_{2j+1}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2^{2j}}(H_{2j}(\xi)-4jH_{2j-1}(\xi)),

where

H_{k}(\xi)=(-1)^{k}e^{\xi^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{d\xi^{k}}e^{-\xi^{2}}

is the Hermite polynomial.
They form skew-orthogonal polynomials with respect to the GOE skew product, i.e.

(2.10)

\displaystyle\langle q_{2j},q_{2j}\rangle=\langle q_{2j+1},q_{2j+1}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle q_{2j},q_{2k+1}\rangle=-\langle q_{2j+1},q_{2k}\rangle=h_{j}\delta_{jk},\qquad h_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(2j)!}{2^{2j}}.

These are building blocks to define prekernels

(2.11)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-(\zeta^{2}+\eta^{2})/2}\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1}\frac{q_{2j+1}(\zeta)q_{2j}(\eta)-q_{2j}(\zeta)q_{2j+1}(\eta)}{h_{j}}.

A key idea of the derivation for the Pfaffian formula is identifying the integrand in (
2.7
) depending on
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as a product of characteristic polynomials of an
m\times m
GOE.
The average of a product of GOE characteristic polynomials can be expressed in terms of Pfaffian of the prekernels in (
2.11
)
[
8
]
.
Then we have

(2.12)

p_{n,m}=\frac{p_{n,n}}{l!}\Big(\frac{2}{i}\Big)^{l}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{l}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\,{\textup{Pf}}\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{n}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{k})&\kappa_{n}(\zeta_{j},\overline{\zeta}_{k})\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\kappa_{n}(\overline{\zeta}_{j},\zeta_{k})&\kappa_{n}(\overline{\zeta}_{j},\overline{\zeta}_{k})\end{bmatrix}_{j,k=1}^{l}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{j}}{i\sqrt{2{(1-\tau)}}}\Big).

The integral representation (
2.12
) can be further evaluated using
Pfaffian integration theorem

[
4
,
7
]
. To illustrate the result, we recall the generalised Laguerre polynomials and the zonal polynomials.
For
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the generalised Laguerre polynomials
L_{k}^{a}(\xi)
are defined by

(2.13)

L_{k}^{a}(\xi)=\frac{1}{k!}\xi^{-a}e^{\xi}\frac{d^{k}}{d\xi^{k}}(\xi^{k+a}e^{-\xi}).

The zonal polynomials are defined for nonnegative integers
k
, and can be written in terms of a sum over all partitions of
k

[
48
]
. Let us denote by
\sigma=(1^{\sigma_{1}},\ldots,k^{\sigma_{k}})
a partition of
k
. That is
|\sigma|=k
, where
|\sigma|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\sigma_{j}
. Then the zonal polynomial associated to
(1^{k})
is defined by

(2.14)

Z_{(1^{k})}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})=(-1)^{k}k!\sum_{|\sigma|=k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}!}\Big(-\frac{\xi_{j}}{j}\Big)^{\sigma_{j}}.

In particular, when
k=1
, we have
Z_{(1^{1})}(\xi)=\xi
.
We define an
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{\hat{\rho}}_{n}
whose
(j,k)
entries
[\mathbf{\hat{\rho}}_{n}]_{j,k}
are given as

(2.15)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{\hat{\rho}}_{n}]_{j,k}=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{2(k-j)-1}e^{y^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(y\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\tau}}\Big)\bigg[(2j-1)L_{2j-1}^{2(k-j)-1}(-2y^{2})+2y^{2}L_{2j-3}^{2(k-j)+1}(-2y^{2})\bigg]\,dy.

Then, it follows from the computations in
[
4
, Appendices 3 and 4]
that the integral (
2.12
) is evaluated as follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
n=m+2l
. Then we have

(2.16)

p_{n,m}=\frac{p_{n,n}}{l!}Z_{(1^{l})}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{\hat{\rho}}_{n}^{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{\hat{\rho}}_{n}^{l})\Big).

In particular, for
n=m+2
, i.e.
l=1
, the formula further reduces to

(2.17)

p_{n,n-2}=p_{n,n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,2y\,e^{y^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(y\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\tau}}\Big)L_{n-2}^{2}(-2y^{2}).

This lemma plays an important role in the asymptotic analysis in the sequel.
In particular, (
2.17
) can be applied to show Theorem
1.2
, which will be the focus of the next section.

3.
Special case: one complex conjugate pair

In this section, we consider the probability
p_{n,n-2}
that there exists exactly one pair of complex eigenvalues, and provide the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The integral representation (
2.17
) is closely related to Gauss’ hypergeometric function defined by

(3.1)

{}_{2}{F}_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+k)\Gamma(b+k)}{\Gamma(c+k)k!}z^{k}

in
|z|<1
, and by analytic continuation elsewhere.
We begin by changing (
2.17
) into a more suitable expression for asymptotic analysis.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

(3.2)

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi(1-\tau)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-s)^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{1-\tfrac{1-\tau}{2}s}\bigg[\Big(1+\tfrac{2(1-\tau)}{1+\tau}(1-s)\Big)^{n}-n\tfrac{2(1-\tau)}{1+\tau}(1-s)-1\bigg]\,ds.

Proof.

Recall that the Laguerre polynomial can be explicitly written as

L_{n}^{a}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+a}{n-k}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

Using this and (
2.17
), we have

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{y^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(y\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\tau}}\Big)\,2^{k+1}\binom{n}{k+2}\frac{y^{2k+1}}{k!}\,dy.

We use an integral formula of the complementary error function
[
41
, Section 4.3 Eq.(9)]

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{erfc}(ax)e^{b^{2}x^{2}}x^{p}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}p+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}(p+1)a^{p+1}}{}_{2}{F}_{1}(\tfrac{p+1}{2},\tfrac{p+2}{2};\tfrac{p+3}{2};\tfrac{b^{2}}{a^{2}}),

valid for
\operatorname{Re}b^{2}<\operatorname{Re}a^{2},\ \operatorname{Re}p>-1
. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n}{k+2}\frac{\Gamma(k+\tfrac{3}{2})}{(k+1)!}(1-\tau)^{k+1}{}_{2}{F}_{1}(k+1,k+\tfrac{3}{2};k+2;\tfrac{1-\tau}{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi(1+\tau)}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n}{k+2}\frac{\Gamma(k+\tfrac{3}{2})}{(k+1)!}\Big(\frac{2(1-\tau)}{1+\tau}\Big)^{k}{}_{2}{F}_{1}(1,\tfrac{1}{2};k+2;\tfrac{1-\tau}{2}),

where we used a transformation of the hypergeometric function
[
50
, Eq.(15.8.1)]
for the second equality.
Furthermore, by using the well-known Euler integral formula (see e.g.
[
50
, Eq.(15.6.1)]
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\tau}{\pi\sqrt{2(1+\tau)}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-\tfrac{1-\tau}{2}s}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n}{k+2}\Big(\frac{2(1-\tau)(1-s)}{1+\tau}\Big)^{k}\,ds.

The summation can be further simplified using the binomial theorem, which gives the desired result.
∎

We shall use the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M
be a nonnegative integer. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have the following.

•

For given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(3.3)

\Big(1-\frac{x}{N}\Big)^{N}=e^{-x}\bigg[\sum_{q=0}^{M}\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)\Big(\frac{x}{N}\Big)^{q}+O(N^{-M-1})\bigg],

where
\mathcal{F}_{0}(x)=1
and

(3.4)

\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{(-x)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=q,\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(j_{s}+1)},\qquad\textup{for }q\geq 1.

•

For given
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(3.5)

\Big(1+\frac{2\alpha^{2}t}{2N-\alpha^{2}}\Big)^{N}=e^{\alpha^{2}t}\bigg[\sum_{q=0}^{M}\mathcal{G}_{q}(t)\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N}\Big)^{q}+O(N^{-M-1})\bigg],

where
\mathcal{G}_{0}(t)=1
and

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{q}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha^{2k}}{2^{q+k}k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=q,\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1-(1-2t)^{j_{s}+1}}{j_{s}+1},\qquad\textup{for }q\geq 1.

Proof.

Note that by expanding the logarithm, we have

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{x}{N}\Big)^{N}

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-x-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{x}{j+1}\Big(\frac{x}{N}\Big)^{j}\Big).

Then the expansion (
3.3
) follows from

(3.7)

\exp\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}x^{j}\Big)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j}x^{j},\qquad b_{q}=\sum_{k=0}^{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=q,\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{s=1}^{k}a_{j_{s}}.

The second assertion is proved similarly. We use series expansions of
1/(2N-\alpha^{2})
and logarithmic function to obtain

\displaystyle\log\Big(1+\frac{2\alpha^{2}t}{2N-\alpha^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\log\Big(1+\sum_{j\geq 1}\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2N}\Big)^{j}\frac{\alpha^{2}t}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k-1}\frac{1}{k}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2N}\Big)^{j}\frac{\alpha^{2}t}{N}\Big)^{k}=\sum_{q\geq 1}t\,\mathfrak{G}_{q}(t)\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N}\Big)^{q}

with

\mathfrak{G}_{j}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{j-k-1}}\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{j-1}{k}t^{k}=\frac{1-(1-2t)^{j}}{2^{j}jt}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Big(1+\frac{2\alpha^{2}t}{2N-\alpha^{2}}\Big)^{N}

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\alpha^{2}t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{2}t\,\mathfrak{G}_{j+1}(t)\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N}\Big)^{j}\Big).

Then the desired asymptotic expansion (
3.5
) follows from (
3.7
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We first consider the strongly non-Hermitian regime.
Notice that for given
\tau\in[0,1)
, the integrand in (
3.2
) is exponentially large near
s=0
.
Thus for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi(1-\tau)}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\frac{s^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-s)^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{1-\tfrac{1-\tau}{2}s}\Big(1+\tfrac{2(1-\tau)}{1+\tau}(1-s)\Big)^{n}ds\,\Big(1+O(e^{-\delta n})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi(1-\tau)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)^{n}\int_{0}^{\epsilon n}\frac{s^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-\tfrac{s}{n})^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{1-\tfrac{1-\tau}{2n}s}\Big(1-\frac{2(1-\tau)}{3-\tau}\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{n}ds\,\Big(1+O(e^{-\delta n})\Big).

By using the binomial series expansion and (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\tfrac{s}{n})^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{1-\tfrac{1-\tau}{2n}s}\Big(1-\frac{2(1-\tau)}{3-\tau}\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\Big)^{k}\Big(\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}\Big(\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{j}\sum_{q\geq 0}e^{-a_{\tau}s}\mathcal{F}_{q}(a_{\tau}s)\Big(\frac{a_{\tau}s}{n}\Big)^{q}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-a_{\tau}s}\sum_{r\geq 0}A_{r}(s)\Big(\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{r},

where

a_{\tau}=\frac{2(1-\tau)}{3-\tau},\qquad A_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\sum_{q=0}^{r-j}\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\Big)^{r-j-q}\frac{\Gamma(j+\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}\mathcal{F}_{q}(a_{\tau}s)a_{\tau}^{q}.

Since
A_{r}(s)
is a polynomial in
s
, we can change the range of integration from
[0,\epsilon n]
to
[0,\infty)
by introducing an exponentially small error.
Therefore, for some
\delta^{\prime}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)^{n}\sum_{r\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{r}(\tau)\frac{1}{n^{r}}\Big(1+O(e^{-\delta^{\prime}n})\Big),

where

(3.8)

\mathcal{A}_{r}(\tau):=\frac{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{4\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}(1-\tau)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{r-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-a_{\tau}s}A_{r}(s)\,ds.

Then, straightforward computations show that it can be evaluated as (
1.20
), which completes the first part of the theorem.

Next, we consider the weakly non-Hermitian regime. Substituting
\tau=1-\alpha^{2}/n
and changing variable
s\mapsto 1-s
, we rewrite (
3.2
) as

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{n}{2\pi\alpha^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}s^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{1+\frac{\alpha^{2}s}{2n-\alpha^{2}}}\bigg[\Big(1+\frac{2\alpha^{2}s}{2n-\alpha^{2}}\Big)^{n}-n\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{2n-\alpha^{2}}s-1\bigg]\,ds.

Using (
3.5
) and the series expansions

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\frac{\alpha^{2}s}{2n-\alpha^{2}}}=1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s(1-s)^{k}\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n}\Big)^{k+1},\qquad\frac{\alpha^{2}s}{1-\frac{\alpha^{2}s}{2n}}=\alpha^{2}s\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n}\Big)^{k},

we obtain

\frac{p_{n,n-2}}{p_{n,n}}=\frac{n}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathfrak{s}_{k}(\alpha)\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)^{k},

where

(3.9)

\mathfrak{s}_{0}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{\pi\alpha^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}t^{-\frac{3}{2}}(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(e^{\alpha^{2}t}-\alpha^{2}t-1)\,dt=e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}(I_{0}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2})+I_{1}(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}))-1.

and

(3.10)

\mathfrak{s}_{k}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{\pi\alpha^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}t^{-\frac{3}{2}}(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(e^{\alpha^{2}t}\Big[\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)-\sum_{r=1}^{k}\mathcal{G}_{k-r}(t)\frac{t(1-t)^{r-1}}{2^{r}}\Big]-\frac{\alpha^{2}(1-t)-1}{2^{k}}t(1-t)^{k-1}\bigg)\,dt

for
k\geq 1
. Then, by expanding
(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2n})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, we conclude (
1.29
).
∎

4.
General case: strong non-Hermiticity

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
(i). As previously mentioned, we shall apply a potential theoretic approach.
For this purpose, we define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{\rm sc}(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}Q_{\rm sc}(z_{k}),\qquad Q_{\rm sc}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\frac{1}{|z-t|^{2}}\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(t),\qquad\rho_{\rm sc}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\tau)-t^{2}}}{\pi(1+\tau)}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{t^{2}\leq 2(1+\tau)\}}.

Here, the subscript “
\rm sc
” stands for “semicircle”.
The overall strategy of our proof can be summarised as follows.

•

We construct elementary bounds for
p_{n,m}
in Lemma
4.1
, and approximate them using the
strong Szegő theorem
as demonstrated in Lemma
4.2
. The approximated bounds can be expressed in terms of the potential
Q_{n}^{(r)}
in (
4.20
).

•

Next, we analyse the approximated potential
Q^{(r)}_{n}
, and show that it attains its global minimum in
\mathbb{H}
for all
n
(Lemma
4.3
). Furthermore, we compute the precise location and corresponding value with
O(n^{-1})
accuracy.

•

We analyse the integral with exponential weights in Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
. Using these results, we show that the bounds for
p_{n,m}
obtained in the previous step converge to the same value up to a multiplicative bounded constant. This establishes Theorem
1.1
for the case of strong non-Hermiticity.

4.1.
Mean field approximation of the real eigenvalue distribution

It is convenient to introduce the following notation: for a constant
r\geq 0
and
\boldsymbol{z}=(x_{1}+iy_{1},\ldots,x_{l}+iy_{l})\in\mathbb{H}^{l}
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{l}_{r}=\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)}:\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{H}^{l}\},\qquad\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)}=(x_{1}+i\max\{y_{1},r\},\ldots,x_{l}+i\max\{y_{l},r\}).

In Lemma
4.1
below, we derive bounds for the probability
p_{n,m}
by truncating the domains of the integrals into subdomains where the integrand remains regular for our analysis. Similar results can be found in the literature, see e.g.
[
51
, Eq.(2.1)]
,
[
43
, Eq.(4.26)]
, and
[
10
, Lemma 1]
.

We proceed our analysis based on a statement given in
[
10
, Lemma 1]
which is formulated as follow. Suppose a function
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded below, and assume that there exist constants
\epsilon>0
and
s>2e
such that

V(\lambda)-\max_{\lambda^{\prime}\in[-e,e]}V(\lambda^{\prime})\geq(2+\epsilon)\log|\lambda|

for any
|\lambda|>s
.
In terms of the Hamiltonian

(4.3)

\mathsf{H}_{N}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(\lambda_{j}),

we define the probability density function

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N,s}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,s}}e^{-N\mathsf{H}_{N}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{[-s,s]}(\lambda_{j}),

where
Z_{N,s}
is the partition function. In particular, we write
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\mathcal{P}_{N,\infty}
for the ensemble without constraint and
Z_{N}
for the associated partition function.
Denoting by
R_{k}
and
R_{k,s}
the
k
-point functions associated to
\mathcal{P}_{N}
and
\mathcal{P}_{N,s}
, respectively, we have

(4.5)

|R_{k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})-R_{k,s}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})|<R_{k,s}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})e^{-CN}

for
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\in[-s,s]

and

.
Integrating both handed side of (

\bigg|\frac{Z_{N,s}}{Z_{N}}-1\bigg|=\bigg|\int_{[-s,s]^{m}}\mathcal{P}_{N}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N}-1\bigg|<e^{-CN}.

Recall that the Hamiltonians
\mathsf{H}_{11}
,
\mathsf{H}_{12}
and

are given by (

We then have the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
n,m,l
, we have

(4.7)

q_{n,m}^{\rm(low)}\leq p_{n,m}\leq q_{n,m}^{\rm(upp)},

where

(4.8)

\displaystyle q_{n,m}^{\rm(low)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{m,l}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{l}_{r}}\int_{[-s,s]^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z})+\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{z},

(4.9)

\displaystyle q_{n,m}^{\rm(upp)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{m,l}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{l}}\int_{[-s,s]^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)})+\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\,(1+e^{-cn})

for a sufficiently large constant

, and a constant

that only depends on

and

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious.
For the upper bound, it follows from

\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{k}-z_{j}|^{2}}\,\geq\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{k}-z^{(r)}_{j}|^{2}}=\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)})

that

\displaystyle p_{n,m}\leq\frac{1}{Z_{m,l}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{l}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)})+\mathsf{H}_{22}(\boldsymbol{z}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}.

Note that the sum of Hamiltonians

\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{2(1+\tau)}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-z_{k}^{(r)}|}\Big)

can be interpreted as the Hamiltonian of the eigenvalues of the GOE with finite point charge insertions at
z_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,z_{l}^{(r)}
.
Especially, the points

are bounded away from the real line which provides sufficient regularity to apply (
4.6
) for sufficiently large constant
s
.
Therefore, there exists a constant
c>0
depending only on
s
and
r
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\leq\int_{[-s,s]^{m}}e^{-n(\mathsf{H}_{11}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\mathsf{H}_{12}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{z}^{(r)}))}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}(1+e^{-cn})

for any

.
Putting them together, we obtain the desired result for the upper bound.
∎

Let

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=(h_{\boldsymbol{z}},\nu)+(A_{S}h_{\boldsymbol{z}},h_{\boldsymbol{z}})

Lemma 4.2

Let

4.2.
Analysis on the approximated Hamiltonian

Building on the previous subsection, we define

(4.20)

\displaystyle Q^{(r)}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}Q_{\tau,n}(z)+Q_{\rm sc}(z^{(r)})

for a constant
r\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.3

(
Global minimum of the approximated potential
)
.

For a sufficiently small
r\geq 0
, the potential
Q^{(r)}_{n}(z)
has a unique global minimum
z_{n}^{\star}
in
\mathbb{H}
which satisfies

(4.21)

z_{n}^{\star}=z^{\star}+O(n^{-1}),\qquad z^{\star}:=i\sqrt{\frac{2(1-\tau)^{2}}{3-\tau}},

as
n\to\infty
.
Moreover, we have

(4.22)

Q_{n}^{(r)}(z_{n}^{\star})-Q_{n}^{(r)}(0)=\log\Big(\frac{3-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)+O(n^{-1})

and

(4.23)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}Q_{n}^{(r)}(z_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1+O(n^{-1})&0\\
0&\frac{1}{1-\tau}+O(n^{-1})\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We begin by establishing the uniqueness and existence of the global minimum.
Throughout the proof, we write
z=x+iy
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, allowing us to express

(4.24)

Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)=-\frac{1}{n}\log\Big[4y^{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{2n}{1-\tau^{2}}}y\Big)\Big]+\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{1+\tau}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\Big((x-t)^{2}+y^{2}\Big)\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(t).

We claim that
Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)
attains its minimum at
x=0
for a given
y>0
.
Since
Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)
is an even function in
x=\operatorname{Re}z
, it is enough to show
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)>0
for
x>0
.
By definition, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)=\frac{2x}{1+\tau}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2(x-t)}{(x-t)^{2}+y^{2}}\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(t).

Observe that as a function of the variable
x
, the integral in the right-hand side of this equation decreases for
x>\sqrt{2(1+\tau)}
.
Therefore, it is enough to show
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)>0
for
x\in[0,\sqrt{2(1+\tau)}]
.
Note also that
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)
strictly decreases as
y
decreases to
0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)>\frac{2x}{1+\tau}-\operatorname{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2}{x-t}\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(t)=0,\end{split}

where the equality follows from the variational condition (Euler-Lagrange equation) for the semicircle law.
This proves the claim.

We now show that there exists a global minimum on the positive imaginary axis.
It follows from straightforward computations that

(4.25)

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)=-\frac{2}{ny}+\frac{2c_{n}}{n}\frac{e^{-c_{n}^{2}y^{2}}}{\operatorname{erfc}(c_{n}y)}-\frac{2\sqrt{2(1+\tau)+y^{2}}}{1+\tau},\qquad c_{n}:=\sqrt{\frac{2n}{1-\tau^{2}}}.

We can easily see that
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)<0
for sufficiently small

.
Differentiating once more, we have

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)=\frac{2}{ny^{2}}+\frac{8(1-\sqrt{\pi}c_{n}ye^{-c_{n}^{2}y^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(c_{n}y))}{(1-\tau^{2})\pi e^{2c_{n}^{2}y^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(c_{n}y)^{2}}-\frac{2y}{(1+\tau)\sqrt{2(1+\tau)+y^{2}}}.

We use inequalities for the complementary error function
[
50

\displaystyle\sqrt{\pi}e^{t^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\leq\frac{2}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4/\pi}},\qquad\sqrt{\pi}te^{t^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(t)<\frac{2t^{2}+2}{2t^{2}+3},

which holds for any
t\geq 0
. Substituting

, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{8(1-\sqrt{\pi}te^{t^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(t))}{(1-\tau^{2})\pi e^{2t^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(t)^{2}}&>\frac{8}{(2t^{2}+3)(1-\tau^{2})}\frac{1}{\pi e^{2t^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(t)^{2}}>\frac{2}{1-\tau^{2}}\frac{(t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4/\pi})^{2}}{2t^{2}+3}.\end{split}

Notice also that

\frac{2y}{(1+\tau)\sqrt{2(1+\tau)+y^{2}}}=\frac{2}{1+\tau}\frac{t}{\sqrt{\frac{4n}{1-\tau}+t^{2}}}.

Combining all of the above, redand since
n\geq 1
and
\tau\in[0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)

\displaystyle>\frac{2}{n}\frac{1}{y^{2}}+\frac{2}{1-\tau^{2}}\frac{(t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4/\pi})^{2}}{2t^{2}+3}-\frac{2}{1+\tau}\frac{t}{\sqrt{\frac{4n}{1-\tau}+t^{2}}}

\displaystyle>\frac{2}{n}\frac{1}{y^{2}}+\frac{2}{1-\tau^{2}}\frac{(t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4/\pi})^{2}}{2t^{2}+3}-\frac{2}{1+\tau}\frac{t}{\sqrt{4+t^{2}}}>\frac{2}{n}\frac{1}{y^{2}}>0.

Hence, the function
Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)
is convex for
y>0
.
Therefore, there exists a global minimum
z_{n}^{\star}=iy_{n}^{\star}
of
Q_{n}^{(0)}(z)
.

Suppose
c_{n}y\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Recall that the error function has the power series expansion (see e.g.
[
50

)

(4.26)

\operatorname{erfc}(z)=\frac{e^{-z^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}z}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{(1/2)_{j}}{z^{2j}},

which holds for
\arg(z)\leq\frac{3}{4}\pi-\delta
, and the error term has the same sign as the first neglected term if

is real positive.
Then, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)=\frac{2y}{1-\tau^{2}}-\frac{2\sqrt{2(1+\tau)+y^{2}}}{1+\tau}+O\Big(\frac{1}{ny}\Big).

Since
Q_{n}^{(0)}(iy)
is convex, we conclude that

y_{n}^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{2(1-\tau)^{2}}{3-\tau}}+O(n^{-1}).

Note also that the integrals from (
4.24

[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\log x\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{8}(2\log 2+1),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\log(1+ax^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\Big(\log\Big(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+a}}{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1-\sqrt{1+a}}{1+\sqrt{1+a}}\Big),\qquad a>0.

Then, (
4.22
) and (

) follow from straightforward computations. The proof for

remains valid for sufficiently small

4.3.
Asymptotic analysis around the global minimum

The main contribution of the integral (

z^{\star}
.
This allows us to focus our attention on the neighbourhood.
We formulate this statement in a more general context.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose a non-negative function
p:\mathbb{C}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}
has a zero set of measure zero, and a continuous function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
attains a unique global minimum at
z_{*}
, with
f(z)\to\infty
as
|z|\to\infty
, and

(4.27)

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}<\infty.

Then, for any neighbourhood
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{L}
of
(z_{*},\ldots,z_{*})
, there exists an
\epsilon=\epsilon(K)>0
such that

(4.28)

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=\int_{K^{L}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\ \Big(1+O(e^{-\epsilon N})\Big),

as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
f(z_{*})=0
.
For a constant
\epsilon>0
, define

A_{k}=\textstyle\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}:k\epsilon\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})<(k+1)\epsilon\},\qquad(k=0,1).

Given a neighbourhood
K
of
z_{*}
, we choose a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, so that
A_{0}\cup A_{1}\subset K
.
Since
\sum_{j}f(z_{j})-2\epsilon\geq 0
for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1})}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N\sum_{j}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}&=e^{-2\epsilon N}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1})}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N(\sum_{j}f(z_{j})-2\epsilon)}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\\
&\leq e^{-2\epsilon N}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1})}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-(\sum_{j}f(z_{j})-2\epsilon)}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=O(e^{-2\epsilon N}).\end{split}

Similar arguments show that

\int_{A_{1}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N\sum_{j}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=O(e^{-\epsilon N}).

On the other hand, since
\sum_{j}f(z_{j})-\epsilon\leq 0
for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in A_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{A_{0}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N\sum_{j}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}&=e^{-\epsilon N}\int_{A_{0}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-N(\sum_{j}f(z_{j})-\epsilon)}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\\
&\geq e^{-\epsilon N}\int_{A_{0}}p(\boldsymbol{z})e^{-\sum_{j}(f(z_{j})-\epsilon)}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\geq C_{\epsilon}e^{-\epsilon N}\end{split}

for some constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
, which implies the desired result.
∎

We investigate the asymptotic effect of the Vandermonde determinant on the integral.
While we only need the case
\gamma=2
in the following lemma for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we present the general statement for
\gamma>0
, as the same proof applies without further effort.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact, and a smooth function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
has a unique global minimum
z_{*}
in the interior of
K
with the non-degenerate Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(z_{*})=2\operatorname{diag}(a^{2},b^{2})
.
Assume that there exists a function
g(N)
such that

(4.29)

\int_{K}e^{-Nf(z)}\,d^{2}z=g(N)e^{O(1)},

as
N\to\infty
. Then, for any
\gamma>0
and fixed
L\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(4.30)

\int_{K^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=\frac{g(N)^{L}}{N^{\gamma L(L-1)/4}}e^{O(1)},

as
N\to\infty
.

For the special case when
\gamma=2
, the result immediately follows from the well-known evaluation of the partition function in terms of the orthogonal polynomial norms, see e.g.
[
11
, Section 5.3]
. For instance, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{2}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}|z_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=N^{-\frac{L(L+1)}{2}}\pi^{L}\prod_{j=1}^{L}j!,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}|z_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=N^{-L}\pi^{L},

from which it is evident that the absolute square of the Vandermonde determinant contributes a factor of
N^{-L(L-1)/2}
to the value of the integral, up to a constant.

The following proof is suggested by the anonymous referee whom we greatly appreciate.
An alternative, but longer proof can be found in the first version of arXiv preprint.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

We may assume that
z_{*}=0
and
f(z_{*})=0
, otherwise consider
f(z+z_{*})-f(z_{*})
and
e^{-Nf(z_{*})}g(N)
instead of
f(z)
and
g(N)
, respectively.
Suppose the assertion is true for
a=b=1
, and consider general
a,b>0
. For a linear map
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{z})=\sqrt{a}\operatorname{Re}\boldsymbol{z}+i\sqrt{b}\operatorname{Im}\boldsymbol{z}
, we observe that
\Delta(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})e^{O(1)}
, as
N\to\infty
. Then, the conclusion directly follows by change of variables.
Thus, for the rest of the proof, we assume
a=b=1
, which implies
f(z)=|z|^{2}+O(|z|^{3})
for sufficiently small
|z|
.

Following the proof of Lemma
4.4
, we have that for any constant
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, there exists a constant
c_{\epsilon}>0
such that

\int_{K^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=\int_{B_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\ \Big(1+O(e^{-c_{\epsilon}N^{-\epsilon}})\Big),

as
N\to\infty
, where

B_{N}:=\{z\in K:|z|<N^{-\epsilon}\}.

Then, there is a constant
c>0
such that
|z|^{2}(1-cN^{-\epsilon})\leq f(z)\leq|z|^{2}(1+cN^{-\epsilon})
for all
z\in B_{N}
.
This implies that we have upper and lower bounds

(4.31)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{B}_{\rm low}\leq\int_{B_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}\leq\mathfrak{B}_{\rm upp},\end{split}

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\rm low}:=\int_{B_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N(1+cN^{-\epsilon})\sum_{j=1}^{L}|z_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z},\qquad\mathfrak{B}_{\rm upp}:=\int_{B_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N(1-cN^{-\epsilon})\sum_{j=1}^{L}|z_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}.

By change of variables, we rewrite the upper bound in (
4.31
) as

\mathfrak{B}_{\rm upp}=\frac{1}{(N(1-cN^{-\epsilon}))^{L+\gamma L(L-1)/4}}\int_{\widetilde{B}_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\mathbf{w})|^{\gamma}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}|w_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\mathbf{w},

where

\widetilde{B}_{N}:=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w|<\sqrt{N(1-cN^{-\epsilon})}N^{-\epsilon}\}.

Notice that
\widetilde{B}_{N}\nearrow\mathbb{C}
as
N\to\infty
.
Thus, the integral converges to

\int_{\widetilde{B}_{N}^{L}}|\Delta(\mathbf{w})|^{\gamma}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}|w_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\mathbf{w}\to\int_{\mathbb{C}^{L}}|\Delta(\mathbf{w})|^{\gamma}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}|w_{j}|^{2}}d^{2}\mathbf{w},

as
N\to\infty
.
This implies that

\mathfrak{B}_{\rm upp}\leq\frac{C_{L}}{N^{L+\gamma L(L-1)/4}}

for some constant
C_{L}>0
.
Similarly, the lower bound has a bound

\frac{c_{L}}{N^{L+\gamma L(L-1)/4}}\leq\mathfrak{B}_{\rm low}

for some constant
c_{L}>0
. Substituting both bounds into (
4.31
), we obtain

\int_{K^{L}}|\Delta(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\gamma}e^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(z_{j})}d^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=\frac{1}{N^{L+\gamma L(L-1)/4}}e^{O(1)},

In this section, we examine the asymptotic behaviour of the probability

5.1.
Alternative representations of the prekernel

To analyse the prekernel (

Lemma 5.1

(
Alternative representations of the GOE prekernel
)
.

For a positive even integer

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\sqrt{2}}&\Bigg[\frac{\Psi_{n,1}(\zeta,\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}-\frac{\Psi_{n,2}(\zeta,\eta)}{(\zeta-\eta)^{2}}\Bigg],\end{split}

(5.3)

Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle=(s^{2}-1)\psi_{n}(s)-2\sqrt{2n}s\psi_{n-1}(s)+2\sqrt{n(n-1)}\psi_{n-2}(s).

Proof.

Recall that the GOE skew-orthogonal polynomials

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1}\frac{q_{2j+1}(\zeta)q_{2j}(\eta)-q_{2j}(\zeta)q_{2j+1}(\eta)}{h_{j}}\\
&=-e^{\frac{\zeta^{2}+\eta^{2}}{2}}\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n-1}}\Bigg[\frac{d}{d\zeta}\Big(\frac{\psi_{n}(\zeta)\psi_{n-1}(\eta)-\psi_{n-1}(\zeta)\psi_{n}(\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}\Big)+\psi_{n}(\zeta)\psi_{n-1}(\eta)\Bigg],\end{split}

see e.g.

and references therein.
Then, the rational expression (

\displaystyle\psi_{N}^{\prime}(\zeta)=\sqrt{2N}\psi_{N-1}(\zeta)-\zeta\psi_{N}(\zeta)

for any positive

To show (

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{n}(\zeta)\psi_{n-1}(\eta)-\psi_{n-1}(\zeta)\psi_{n}(\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{n}(\zeta)-\psi_{n}(\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}\psi_{n-1}(\eta)-\frac{\psi_{n-1}(\zeta)-\psi_{n-1}(\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}\psi_{n}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\psi_{n-1}(\eta)\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{n}^{\prime}(t\zeta+(1-t)\eta)\,dt-\psi_{n}(\eta)\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{n-1}^{\prime}(t\zeta+(1-t)\eta)\,dt,

which leads to

\displaystyle=\psi_{n-1}(\eta)\int_{0}^{1}t\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t\zeta+(1-t)\eta)\,dt-\psi_{n}(\eta)\int_{0}^{1}t\psi_{n-1}^{\prime\prime}(t\zeta+(1-t)\eta)\,dt.

We provide a heuristic explanation for why the pairing
\sigma=(2j-1,2j)_{j=1}^{l}
contributes to the leading asymptotics in (
5.53
).
As in the strong non-Hermiticity regime, one can compute the approximated potential
Q_{n}^{(r)}(z)
defined in (
4.20
).
However, since
\tau=1-\alpha^{2}/n
, the difference
Q_{n}^{(r)}(z_{n}^{\star})-Q_{n}^{(r)}(0)
in (
4.21
) vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
Instead,
Q_{n}^{(r)}(z)
forms a potential well in the upper half-plane near the real axis, see Figure
3
for an illustration.
As a result, the complex eigenvalues are typically distributed within this potential well, which forms an elongated neighbourhood along
[-2,2]\subset\mathbb{C}
.
This heuristically suggests that the typical distance between complex eigenvalues in the upper half-plane is of order
1
, implying that the interaction between distinct complex-conjugate pairs may be neglected.
In terms of the Pfaffian in (
5.53
), this suggests that the block-diagonal contribution corresponding to
\sigma=(2j-1,2j)_{j=1}^{l}
dominates in the computation.
