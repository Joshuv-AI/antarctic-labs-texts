Tropical Donagi theorem
Felix Röhrle, Thomas Saillez

Abstract
The tropical n n -gonal construction was introduced in recent work by the first author and D. Zakharov and structural results for n = 2 , 3 n=2,3 were established. In this article we explore the construction for n = 4 n=4 and prove a tropical analogue of Donagi’s theorem which states that the tetragonal construction is a triality which preserves Prym varieties. This confirms the speculations in previous work and establishes new results on the non-injectivity of the tropical Prym-Torelli morphism. Finally, we demonstrate that the tropical n n -gonal construction is poorly behaved under edge contractions, thus preventing any immediate moduli-theoretic perspective.

1
Introduction

Tropical geometry is a branch of mathematics which replaces the role of polynomial functions in algebraic geometry with piecewise linear functions. This yields a theory of geometry which is in many ways surprisingly similar to algebraic geometry.

This paper contributes to the development of the theory of tropical Prym varieties, which are an analogue of the algebro-geometric concept of Prym varieties. These are named after the work of Prym
[
Pry66
]
and were reintroduced a century later by Mumford
[
Mum74
]
using modern algebro geometric language. Starting with an étale double cover of smooth algebraic curves
\pi:\widetilde{C}\to C
, the Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{C}/C)
is the abelian variety given by the kernel of the norm map, i.e. the kernel of the point-wise application of
\pi
to divisor classes of
\widetilde{C}
.

In
[
Don81
]
and
[
Don92
]
, Donagi introduced the
n
-gonal construction on pairs of maps of curves. The construction looks at the space of sections of a pair of covers
\widetilde{C}\to C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
where the first map is an étale double cover and the second one is a ramified
n
-tuple cover and produces a new curve
D
with structural map
D\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
2^{n}
.

Some special cases of the
n
-gonal construction have lead to structural results.
The bigonal construction (
n=2
) naturally appeared when studying the integrability of geodesic flows on
SO(n)
in works such as
[
Pan86
]
and
[
Hai83
]
. The Recillas’ construction is inverse to the trigonal construction (
n=3
) and appeared in the work of Recillas
[
Rec74
]
, connecting Prym varieties of trigonal curves and Jacobians of tetragonal curves. The tetragonal construction (
n=4
) was used in
[
Don92
]
to discover a triality of tetragonal curves having isomorphic Prym varieties, giving a better understanding of the non-injectivity of the Prym-Torelli map, with the goal of solving the Schottky problem.

On the tropical geometry side, the notions of divisor and Jacobian were introduced in the work of Mikhalkin and Zharkov
[
MZ08
]
. In this context, a tropical curve is a metric graph and a divisor is a formal linear combination of the points of this metric graph. A suitable notion of linear equivalence of divisors is obtained with piecewise linear functions. In this language, the Jacobian of a metric graph is the group of degree 0 divisors modulo linear equivalence. The tropical Prym variety was introduced in
[
JL18
]
as the kernel of the push forward of the Jacobian in a double cover of tropical curves. This definition is in pure analogy to that of the algebraic Prym variety. The Prym variety was then showed, using dilated double covers, to be discontinuous under edge contraction in
[
GZ24
]
. In
[
RZ23
]
the authors used matroids to analyse Prym varieties and provided some initial results on the non-injectivity of the tropical Prym-Torelli morphism. The undilated (i.e.
“free”
) case was studied further in
[
LU21
,
LZ22
]
.

The first author and D. Zakharov introduced a tropical analogue of the
n
-gonal construction in
[
RZ25
]
. The definition is a combinatorial version of the algebraic construction, where the authors replaced the projective line (which has trivial algebraic Jacobian) with metric trees (which are the metric graphs with trivial tropical Jacobian).
The authors proved the tropical analogues of the main theorem for the bigonal and trigonal construction using cohomological techniques. The tetragonal case was left open as Donagi’s original proof relied on cohomological considerations that are not yet available in tropical geometry (see also
[
BL04
, Section 12.8]
for a more recent treatment of the theorem). We still believe that the language of tropical (co)homology introduced by
[
IKMZ19
]
and then further developed in
[
GS23
]
is suitable for reproducing the original proof in tropical language, however, this would require establishing the tropical analogues of various cohomological formulae, which lies beyond the scope of a single paper.

In the present article, we follow the much more combinatorial proof of Donagi’s theorem developed by Izadi and Lange in
[
IL12
]
. It turns out that this proof can be adapted to the tropical setting to establish the tropical Donagi theorem. Izadi and Lange also extended Donagi’s theorem to situations where the base curve is not
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and we show that the tropicalization of the proof falls flat when the base tropical curve is not a tree.

Roughly speaking, the tropical tetragonal construction associates to a
“tower”

\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma\to K
consisting of a double cover
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
followed by a harmonic map
\Gamma\to K
of degree 4 a harmonic map
\widetilde{\Delta}\to K
of degree 16. If the tower is
orientable
(
Definition

3.3
), this data decomposes as a disjoint union of two towers
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}\to K
for
i=1,2
with harmonic maps of degree 2 and 4 as in the initial data. In this setting the main theorem of our paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1

(tropical Donagi theorem)
.

Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma\to K
be an orientable tower consisting of a free double cover
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
and a harmonic map
\Gamma\to K
of degree 4 with dilation profile nowhere
(2,2)
or
(4)
(see
Definition

3.2
). Then the tetragonal construction produces two more towers
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}\to K
for
i=1,2
of the same type. In this situation we have the following.

1.

The construction is a triality, meaning that tetragonal construction applied to
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}\to K
gives
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma\to K
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{3-i}\to\Lambda_{3-i}\to K
as its output.

2.

If additionally the curves
\widetilde{\Gamma}
,
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{1}
, and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{2}
are all connected and
K
is a tree, then there are isomorphisms of principally polarized tropical abelian varieties

\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{1}/\Lambda_{1})\cong\Prym(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{2}/\Lambda_{2}).

A large class of orientable towers is given by
Lemma

3.5
and
Proposition

3.10
:
if
K
is a tree, or more generally, when
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a fiberwise quotient of the trivial octuple cover of
K
, then the tower
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma\to K
is orientable. In those cases, the connectedness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
implies the connectedness of both
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{2}
.

Theorem

1.1
is similar to the tropical Recillas’ theorem in
[
RZ25
]
in the sense that both tropical theorems substitute
“étale”
and
“ramification”
in the algebraic theorem by
“free”
and
“dilation”
in the tropical version. This means that the tropical theorems are really analogues of the algebraic theorems and not tropicalizations: indeed, the tropicalization of an étale double cover will in general have dilation.

Finally, in
Section

6
we study the interaction of the tetragonal construction and the Prym-Torelli morphism. It turns out that the tropical
n
-gonal construction is not continuous under edge contractions and that the tetragonal construction cannot even be modified to extend
Theorem

1.1
by continuity. This shows poor behavior of the construction in families and that the assumptions on the dilation profiles in
Theorem

1.1
cannot easily be lifted.

Preliminaries

2.1
Graphs, harmonic morphisms, and tropical curves

We consider a
graph

G
to be a set of vertices
V(G)
, a set of half-edges
H(G)
, a root map
r:H(G)\to V(G)
and a fixed-point-free involution
H(G)\to H(G),\penalty\ h\mapsto\bar{h}.

A
point
of
G
is an element of
H(G)\cup V(G)
, and by abuse of notation we will usually denote the set of points by
G
as well.
An
edge
is a set
\left\{e,\bar{e}\right\}
and the set of edges is denoted
E(G)
.
We assume our set of points to always be
finite
. For a vertex
v\in V(G)
we define the
tangent space

T_{v}(G)=r^{-1}(v)
and the valence of a vertex is the cardinality of its tangent space.
For a connected graph
G
, we denote its
genus
by
g(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1
.

A
morphism
of graphs from
\widetilde{G}
to
G
is a function
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
such that vertices are mapped to vertices and half-edges to half-edges and which commutes with both the root map and the involution. In particular, no edge is contracted.

A
harmonic morphism
from
\widetilde{G}
to
G
is a pair consisting of a morphism
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
and a local degree function
d_{f}:\widetilde{G}\to\mathbb{N}^{*}
which is constant on every edge and which satisfy that for every vertex
v\in V(\widetilde{G})
and half-edge
h\in T_{f(v)}(G)
we have

d_{f}(v)=\sum_{h^{\prime}\in T_{v}\widetilde{G}\cap f^{-1}(h)}d_{f}(h^{\prime}).

If
G
is connected, it follows that the quantity
\sum_{y\in f^{-1}(x)}d_{f}(y)
does not depend on the choice of
x\in G
. In that case, we call this quantity the
degree
of
f
and we denote it
\text{deg}(f)
.
A harmonic morphism
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
such that the corresponding function
d_{f}
is the constant 1 is called
free
.

A
tropical curve
is a pair

\Gamma=(G,\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0})
consisting of a graph

), but we will not use the implied distance function. We will generally abuse notation and refer to the metric space of

the lengths of

A

\iota:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{\Gamma}

The edges/ vertices in

undilated

2.2
Divisors and linear equivalence

We now recall basic definitions of divisors. More details can be found in
[
Len24
]
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected metric graph. We denote
\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma):=\bigoplus_{p\in\Gamma}\mathbb{Z}
the group of divisors of
\Gamma
. A divisor
D
is said to be
effective
if for all
p\in\Gamma
we have
D(p)\geq 0
.

Let
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous piecewise linear function and let
p\in\Gamma
. A small enough neighborhood of
p
decomposes as a finite union of disjoint open intervals and
\left\{p\right\}
. We fix an orientation on the intervals such that they are pointing away from
p
, and this fixes the sign of the slope
f^{\prime}
on those intervals. We define

\operatorname{div}(f)(p)=\sum_{h\in r^{-1}(p)}f^{\prime}(h),

where
r^{-1}(p)
is taken as the tangent space of
p
for a model of
\Gamma
where
f^{\prime}
is constant on edges.
In a more concrete way, we associate to a point
p
the sum of the outgoing slopes of
f
at
p
.

A piecewise linear function
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is called
rational
if all slopes are integers. A divisor
D
is said to be
principal
if there exists a rational function
f
such that
D=\operatorname{div}(f)
. Principal divisors form a subgroup
\text{Prin}(\Gamma)\subseteq\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma)
.
The
\emph{degree}
of a divisor
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma)
is
\deg(D)=\sum_{p\in\Gamma}D(p)
. This sum is well-defined because
D
has finite support. A classical result is that all divisors arising from functions have degree
0
. Denoting the set of degree
0
divisors by
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\Gamma)
, we define the
Picard group

\Pic^{0}(\Gamma)=\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\Gamma)/\Prin(\Gamma)
.

Moreover, any degree 0 divisor can be written as the divisor of a (not necessarily rational) function and this function is unique up to the addition of a constant function. For any degree 0 divisor
D
we define the
Euclidean norm

||D||^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}(f^{\prime})^{2}

(2.1)

where
f
satisfies
\operatorname{div}(f)=D
and the integral is taken with respect to the Lebesgue measure. This norm allows to define a scalar product on
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\Gamma)
which we call the
polarization
.

If
f:\Gamma\to\Theta
is a continuous map of metric graphs and
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma)
, the
pushforward
of
D
is the divisor
f_{\ast}D\in\operatorname{Div}(\Theta)
where for
p\in\Theta
we have
(f_{\ast}D)(p)=\sum_{q\in f^{-1}(p)}D(q)
.

2.3
Tropical Abelian varieties

Tropical abelian varieties can be introduced in different ways. In
[
RZ25
]
the following language was developed with a view towards non-principally polarized varieties such as Prym varieties of dilated double covers. We recall these notions quickly and then explain how this can be reconciled with the divisorial perspective for free double covers (and hence naturally principally polarized Prym variety).

A
tropical abelian variety
, abbreviated
tav
, is a triple
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
, where
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are lattices of the same rank and
[\cdot,\cdot]:\Lambda\times\Lambda^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-degenerate pairing.
The
dimension
of the tropical abelian variety is the rank of the lattices
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
.
A
polarization
is an injective group homomorphism
\xi:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\Lambda
such that
[\xi(\cdot),\cdot]
is a symmetric positive definite bilinear form.
A polarization is called
principal
if it is an isomorphism.
A tav together with a polarization is called
polarized
tropical abelian variety.

A
morphism
between tavs
(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda^{\prime}_{i},[\cdot,\cdot]_{i})
for
i=1,2
consists of two
\mathbb{Z}
-linear maps
f^{\#}:\Lambda_{2}\to\Lambda_{1}
and
f_{\#}:\Lambda^{\prime}_{1}\to\Lambda^{\prime}_{2}
such that

[f^{\#}(\cdot),\cdot]_{1}=[\cdot,f_{\#}(\cdot)]_{2}

on all of
\Lambda_{2}\times\Lambda^{\prime}_{1}
. The pair
(f^{\#},f_{\#})
is an
isomorphism
if and only if both
f^{\#}
and
f_{\#}
are isomorphisms. If the tavs
(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda^{\prime}_{i},[\cdot,\cdot]_{i})
are additionally equipped with polarizations
\xi_{i}:\Lambda^{\prime}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}
, then
(f^{\#},f_{\#})
is an
isomorphism
of polarized tavs if it is an isomorphism of tavs which respects the polarizations in the sense that
\xi_{1}=f^{\#}\circ\xi_{2}\circ f_{\#}
.

Remark 2.1
.

We emphasize that up to isomorphism, a principally polarized tav is entirely determined by the positive definite bilinear form
[\xi(\cdot),\cdot]:\Lambda^{\prime}\times\Lambda^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
, which in turn is again equivalent to giving the quadratic form
\gamma\mapsto[\xi(\gamma),\gamma]
. This statement does not hold for non-principally polarized tavs.

2.4
Jacobians

Let
\Gamma
be a tropical curve. Choose an orientation for the edges of the underlying graph. Then there are two canonically associated lattices to
\Gamma
, namely harmonic 1-forms with integer coefficients

\Omega^{1}(\Gamma)=\Big\{\sum_{e}a_{e}de\mathrel{\Big|}\text{for every vertex }v:\sum_{e\text{ entering }v}a_{e}-\sum_{e\text{ leaving }v}a_{e}=0\Big\}

and the first homology group

H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=\bigg\{\sum_{e}b_{e}e\mathrel{\bigg|}\begin{minipage}[176.407pt]$\partial(\sum b_{e}e)=0$, where $\partial$ is generated by $\partial(e)=\text{ end vertex }-\text{ start vertex of }e$
\end{minipage}\bigg\}.

These two lattices define the
(tropical) Jacobian
of
\Gamma
as follows

\Jac(\Gamma)=\big(\Omega^{1}(\Gamma),H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),[\cdot,\cdot]\big),

where

[\cdot,\cdot]:\Omega^{1}(\Gamma)\times H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the integration pairing generated by

[de,e^{\prime}]=\int_{e^{\prime}}de=\begin{cases}\ell(e)&\text{if $e=e^{\prime}$ and the orientations match},\\
-\ell(e)&\text{if $e=e^{\prime}$ with opposing orientation},\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The Jacobian is a tropical abelian variety of dimension
g(\Gamma)
and it carries a natural principal polarization, which is given by the isomorphism
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\to\Omega^{1}(\Gamma)
induced by
e\mapsto de
.
The tropical Abel-Jacobi theorem states that
\Jac(\Gamma)\cong\Pic^{0}(\Gamma)
as principally polarized tropical abelian varieties and the idea behind this isomorphism is as follows:
let
D\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\Gamma)
be a divisor and choose a model of
\Gamma
such that
D
is supported on the vertices of
\Gamma
. Then there exists a (not necessarily rational) function
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
which is piecewise linear, unique up to addition of a constant and such that
\operatorname{div}(f)=D
. This function has constant slopes on the edges of
\Gamma
, so the data of
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
modulo the constant is equivalent to the slopes of
f
on the edges, i.e.
f
can be summarized as
\sum_{e\in E}f^{\prime}(e)\cdot e\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
. This computations turns degree 0 divisors into formal sums of edges and it preserves the polarization in the sense that

||D||^{2}=\Big[\zeta\Big(\sum_{e\in E}f^{\prime}(e)\cdot e\Big),\sum_{e\in E}f^{\prime}(e)\cdot e\Big]

where
[\zeta(\cdot),\cdot]
is extended to real coefficients by linearity. More details about this isomorphism are the subject of
[
BF11
, Sections 2 and 3]
.

2.5
Pryms

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of tropical curves. It induces the
norm homomorphism

\Nm=(\pi^{\ast},\pi_{\ast}):\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})\longrightarrow\Jac(\Gamma).

We define the
(divisorial) Prym variety
as the kernel of the norm homomorphism, i.e.

\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)=(\Ker\Nm)_{0}=\big((\Coker\pi^{\ast})^{\mathrm{tf}},\Ker\pi_{\ast},[\cdot,\cdot]\big),

where
()^{\mathrm{tf}}
denotes the torsion free quotient and the pairing
[\cdot,\cdot]
is simply the restriction of the integration pairing on
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})
.
The Prym variety is a tropical abelian variety of dimension
g(\widetilde{\Gamma})-g(\Gamma)
.
We endow
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
with an induced polarization by pulling back the principal polarization of
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})
, i.e.

\xi:\Ker\pi_{\ast}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma}){\mathrel{\to}}(\Coker\pi^{\ast})^{\mathrm{tf}}.

This induced polarization on Prym is in general not principal. For free double covers
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
, which is the setting of our main theorem, the induced polarization is always twice a principal polarization
\zeta=\xi/2
and thus
Remark

2.1
applies.
In general,
[
RZ25
]
provides the construction of the
continuous Prym variety
, which is a finite cover of
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and which naturally carries an induced polarization which is twice a principal polarization.

In view of our example computations in
Section

5
we will now describe the bilinear form
[\zeta(\cdot),\cdot]
for
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of a free double cover in more details.
For this we use the following convention. If
e\in E(\Gamma)
is an undilated edge, endowed with a an orientation, then we denote the two preimages of
e
by
\widetilde{e}^{+}
and
\widetilde{e}^{-}
in some order and endow them with the orientation compatible with that of
e
, meaning such that
\pi_{\ast}(\widetilde{e}^{\pm})=e
.
In
[
RZ25
]
we presented an explicit basis for
\Ker\pi_{\ast}
consisting of elements of the form

\alpha=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma)}a_{e}(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})

where
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma)
is the set of undilated edges of
\Gamma
. We obtain the following formula:

\bigg[\zeta\Big(\sum_{e}a_{e}(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})\Big),\sum_{e}b_{e}(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})\bigg]=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma)}a_{e}b_{e}\ell(e).

(2.2)

For the proof strategy of
Theorem

1.1
, however, the divisorial view on Prym is more beneficial. To make this precise, note that the (divisorial) Prym variety embeds into
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})
simply by restricting the pairing.
Hence the isomorphism
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})\cong\Pic^{0}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
restricts to an isomorphism of principally polarized tavs between
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and the subvariety of
antisymmetric divisors
in
\Pic^{0}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
, i.e. divisors of the form
D-\iota_{\ast}D
, where
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
can be taken effective.

3
The tropical tetragonal construction

In
[
RZ25
, Section 2]
the authors defined the tropical
n
-gonal construction for general
n
.
Although we will want to apply the construction to metric graphs in order to state and proof our main theorem, the construction itself is purely combinatorial and hence this section will be phrased in terms of (unmetrized) graphs.
We will recall the
n
-gonal construction in full generality before we specialize to
n=4
. It turns out that the construction is in general very poorly behaved from a moduli theoretic point of view because it is not compatible with edge contractions in general. We will explore these short comings in
Section

6
and demonstrate that no continuation of the
n
-gonal construction through edge contractions can be found.

3.1
The tropical
n
-gonal construction

We may define the following notions of pullback and pushforward: let
f:G\to K
and
\pi:\widetilde{G}\to G

is connected. Using
\widetilde{G}

G

\displaystyle f^{*}:\mathbb{Z}^{K}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{G},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad

\displaystyle\pi_{*}:\mathbb{Z}^{\widetilde{G}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{G},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{y\in f^{-1}(x)}d_{f}(y)y,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto\pi(x).

The following is the tropical Donagi construction as it was defined in
[
RZ25
, Section 2.2]
.

Definition 3.1

(tropical Donagi construction)
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover and
f:G\to K
a harmonic map of degree
n
. We define the set

\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}=\left\{D\in\mathbb{Z}^{\widetilde{G}}\hskip 1.0pt\middle|\hskip 1.0ptD\geq 0\text{ and }\exists x\in K\text{ such that }\pi_{*}(D)=f^{*}(x)\right\}.

Note that the
x
in the definition of
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
is necessarily unique. We say that
D\in\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
is a vertex (resp. half-edge) if and only if
x
is a vertex (resp. half-edge). Define the root and involution maps of
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
by

r\Big(\sum a_{h}h\Big)=\sum a_{h}r(h)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\sum a_{h}h}=\sum a_{h}\overline{h}.

This gives
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
the structure of a graph.
We associate the map
\widetilde{p}:\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}\to K
mapping
D
to
x
if
\pi_{*}(D)=f^{*}(x)
.
Furthermore, we define the local degree function

d_{\widetilde{p}}(D)=\prod_{y\in f^{-1}(x)\text{ free}}\binom{d_{f}(y)}{a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}}\prod_{y\in f^{-1}(x)\text{ dilated}}2^{d_{f}(y)}.

By

3.2
Orientability and connectedness

In
[
RZ25
, Section 2.2]
, the authors factorize

\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}\longrightarrow\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}/\iota\longrightarrow\widetilde{K}\longrightarrow K,

(3.1)

where the respective degrees of these harmonic maps are 2, 4, and 2, respectively.
We will now explain this factorization.

First, we abuse notation and use
\iota
to indicate the involution defining the double cover
\widetilde{G}\to G
as well as the involution on
\widetilde{\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{G}}
induced by it via

\iota\Big(\sum a_{h}h\Big)=\sum a_{h}\iota(h).

The first map in
Equation

3.1
is then the quotient of
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
by
\iota
.

Next,
\widetilde{K}\to K
is the orientation double cover, which is constructed by a fiberwise equivalence relation. In the case of a free double cover in the original tower, the relation is defined as follows. Fix
x\in K
and let
D=\sum_{y\in f^{-1}(x)}a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}\widetilde{y}^{+}+a_{\widetilde{y}^{-}}\widetilde{y}^{-}
and

be points in
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
.
Now

and

are defined as equivalent if and only if
\sum_{y}a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}-b_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}
is even. Applying
\iota
to

swaps all the coefficients
a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}
with the coefficients
a_{\widetilde{y}^{-}}
. Remembering that
\sum a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}+a_{\widetilde{y}^{-}}=4
we see that
\sum a_{\widetilde{y}^{+}}
and
\sum a_{\widetilde{y}^{-}}
always have the same parity. This explains the rest of

is always free.

Definition 3.3

A tower
\widetilde{G}\to G\to K
is

if its orientation double cover

is trivial.

If a tower is orientable, then the tetragonal construction decomposes as a disjoint union of two towers with degrees 2 and 4 and each of these two towers is defined over the connected components of
\widetilde{K}=K\sqcup K
, i.e. over
K
.
More explicitly, when

is an orientable tower, the tetragonal construction provides

, that we can split into to covers
\widetilde{C}_{i}\to K
for

. Since 4 is even, applying the involution

of

on the orientation double cover is the identity, therefore

restricts to both

and
\widetilde{C}_{2}
, allowing to produce two new towers
\widetilde{C}_{i}\to C_{i}\to K
for

.

Whenever

When a tower is orientable, we know that
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
is not connected. However it might have more than two connected components, producing new towers where the top graph is not connected. The next proposition provides connectedness of the
\widetilde{C}_{i}
for a large set of examples.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\widetilde{G}\to G\to K
be a generic tower (in particular let
\widetilde{G}\to G
be free) with connected
\widetilde{G}
such that
\widetilde{G}\to K
is a fiberwise quotient of the trivial octuple cover of
K
. Then the tower is orientable and the output curves
\widetilde{C}_{1}
and
\widetilde{C}_{2}
are connected.

Note that, whenever
K
is a tree, the map
\widetilde{G}\to K
is automatically a quotient of the trivial octuple cover of
K
.

Proof.

We define
N=\left\{1,2,3,4,-1,-2,-3,-4\right\}
. We name the projection
p:\tilde{G}\to K
and we let
q:\coprod_{N}K\to\widetilde{G}
be the (non-harmonic) fiberwise quotient map. We define the group
WD_{4}
to be the subgroup of
S_{N}
generated by

(1,2)(-1,-2),\quad(1,3)(-1,-3),\quad(1,4)(-1,-4),\quad\text{and}\quad(1,-1)(2,-2).

A direct computation shows that
|WD_{4}|=192
. This group is the even signed permutation group and already appears in
[
Don92
, Remark 2.6]
.

Now let
x
be a point of
K
, we associate to it the subgroup of
WD_{4}
of all the permutations (in
WD_{4}
) that act as identity on
p^{-1}(x)
. In more rigorous terms,
WD_{4}
acts on
\coprod_{N}K
as a subgroup of
S_{N}
. We associate to
x
the subgroup of
WD_{4}
of all permutations
s
such that the restriction
\bar{q}:\coprod_{N}\left\{x\right\}\to\tilde{G}
satisfies
\bar{q}\circ s=\bar{q}
. We name this subgroup
K_{x}
.

The cover
\widetilde{G}
is connected if and only if the subgroup generated by all
K_{x}
acts transitively on
N
. To see this, let us remark that a path inside
\widetilde{G}
can be lifted to a union of paths inside
\coprod_{N}K
, such that paths begin and end at points that are the same under the quotient map

. An explicit computation fiber type per fiber type shows that all

are always generated by bitranspositions. A direct computation in Magma shows that the only subgroup of
WD_{4}
that acts transitively on

and is generated by bitranspositions is
WD_{4}
itself. Therefore, the subgroup generated by the union of all

is

.

Now we can define

to be set of 4 element subsets of

such that the map

is bijective for all

. The action of

on

are exactly the quadruplets of divisors defined in the tetragonal construction, and the action of

on

in the same way as the action of

on

\widetilde{G}
.
∎

G_{i}\to K
is a double cover for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

If
\widetilde{G}
is disconnected, then either
G
is connected or it is not.

If
G
is connected, then
\widetilde{G}\to G
is the trivial double cover and we can label the sheets as
\widetilde{G}=G^{+}\coprod G^{-}
with
G^{+}\cong G^{-}\cong G
. One can define a map
\text{deg}_{+}:\widetilde{G}\to\mathbb{Z}
mapping
x
to 1 if
x\in G^{+}
and 0 elsewhere. This map can be extended to a map
\text{deg}_{+}:\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}\to\mathbb{Z}
. The latter is locally constant and has image
\left\{0,1,2,3,4\right\}
. The points mapping to 0, 2 and 4 come from the component
\widetilde{C}_{1}
hence this component is disconnected and the points mapping to 1 and 3 come from the component
\widetilde{C}_{2}
hence that component is also disconnected and we are done.

In the case where
G
itself is disconnected as well, we can either decompose
G
into two connected components as in the theorem or we can decompose it as
G=G^{\prime}\coprod K
where the map
G^{\prime}\to K
is degree 3 (with
G^{\prime}
not necessarily connected). We then can decompose
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{G}^{\prime}\coprod K\coprod K
. Then any point
D\in\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
decomposes as
D^{\prime}+y
where
y
is one point in the two copies of
K
and
D^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}}
. Then
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
is made of two copies of the trigonal construction
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}}
that are exchanged via the involution, hence four copies of a degree 4 cover of
K
. This implies that both towers of
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{G}}
are disconnected.

Therefore, if
\widetilde{G}
is disconnected and
\widetilde{C}_{i}
is connected for at least one
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
then
G
is disconnected and cannot be decomposed as
G=G^{\prime}\coprod K
where
G^{\prime}\to K
is a degree 3 cover. Hence,
G
has can only be decomposed into two connected components
G=G_{1}\coprod G_{2}
where
G_{1}\to K
and
G_{2}\to K
are both double covers.
∎

Lastly, let us show an example of a triality of towers where two towers are connected and one tower is disconnected.

Example 3.12
.

We start with the following tower which is
not
a quotient of the trivial octuple cover. The involution of the double cover is represented via an horizontal symmetry. This tower is orientable since computing the tetragonal construction leads to two towers.

One of the two output of the tetragonal construction is the following tower, which is connected.

The other output of the tetragonal construction is the following tower which is not connected.

Definition 3.13
.

A tower
\widetilde{G}\to G\to K
is
good
if it is generic,
\widetilde{G}
is connected, the tower is orientable and the tetragonal output is two connected towers. In particular, in any good tower
\widetilde{G}\to G
is free.

By
Lemma

3.5
and
Proposition

3.10
, we know that a class of examples of good towers is the class of generic towers such that the map
\widetilde{G}\to K
is a quotient of the trivial octuple cover and
\widetilde{G}
is connected.

Note that, for each fiber of generic type, the tetragonal construction gives two copies of the same type. This does not mean that the tetragonal construction is trivial since the global structure of the tower may change.
However, these towers keep the same properties when the tetragonal construction: if a tower is a connected quotient of the trivial octuple cover, then it is orientable and the output towers of the tetragonal construction are also connected and quotients of the trivial octuple cover.
This is and extension of
[
RZ25
, Proposition 2.14]
to a larger class of towers where
K
is not necessarily a tree.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\widetilde{G}\to G\to K
be a good tower and
\widetilde{C}_{i}\to C_{i}\to K
be the two output towers of the tetragonal construction. Then the dimensions of the Prym varieties agree, i.e.

g(\widetilde{G})-g(G)=g(\widetilde{C}_{1})-g(C_{1})=g(\widetilde{C}_{2})-g(C_{2}).

Proof.

By definition,
\widetilde{G}
,
G
,
\widetilde{C}
, and
C_{i}
are all connected.
For a connected graph
H
, the genus is
g(H)=|E(H)|-|V(H)|+1
. But restricted to any individual fiber, the tetragonal construction does not change its type. Therefore,
|E(\widetilde{G})|=|E(\widetilde{C}_{1})|=|E(\widetilde{C}_{2})|
and
|E(G)|=|E(C_{1})|=|E(C_{2})|
and similar for the number of vertices. The claim follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 4.2
