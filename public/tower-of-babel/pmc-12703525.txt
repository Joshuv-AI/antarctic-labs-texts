Exploratory Study: Survey of Digital and Physical Threats and Assaults by Patients on American Psychiatry Resident Doctors
Authors: Angela Ho, Jeanie Kim, Joselyn Gonzalez, Jo Everett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorkplace violence in health care, particularly against psychiatry residents, remains a substantial issue, with high rates of both physical assaults and emerging digital threats. This study aimed to explore the prevalence and nature of these risks to inform potential solutions.MethodsParticipants in this study were psychiatry residents from accredited US residency programs, with data collected via a 20-item anonymous self-report questionnaire distributed through program coordinators. The Google form survey, administered over 3 months (February through April 2024), assessed residents' experiences with various forms of workplace violence. Demographic data and details on safety measures, including training and online privacy protection, were also collected.ResultsOf the respondents, 78.9% of psychiatry residents experienced at least one form of assault: verbal threats (76.4%), physical assaults (15.0%), and sexual assault (2.4%). These incidents often occurred in inpatient settings with high-risk patients and limited security. A digital threat was reported by 0.8% of respondents. Meanwhile, 90.6% reported to have received safety training, while only a single participant reported having access to a subscription program provided by their institution to protect their personal information online.DiscussionAlthough some psychiatry programs provide safety training, specific guidance on physical and digital safety remains inconsistent and varies by institution. Digital threats have recently emerged as a new concern, with increased online exposure potentially extending risk into the personal lives of residents.ConclusionThese findings highlight the safety gaps and need for standardized, evidence-based protocols to protect psychiatry residents in light of the potential impact of physical and digital threats.

Background

Workplace violence in health care settings is a critical issue, with substantial implications for practitioner safety and well-being. 1–4 Health care practitioners face disproportionately high rates of violence compared with other industries, most commonly from patients or their families. 5,6 Psychiatry residents are particularly vulnerable given their interactions with behaviorally dysregulated patients. 7,8 Despite increasing recognition of these risks, there are no systematic solutions or standardized safety protocols in place across psychiatry training sites to reduce violence, physical or virtual, against psychiatry residents. 9,10

Psychiatry residents report the highest prevalence, with 25% to 64% experiencing patient-initiated assaults compared with 5% to 9% in pediatrics and 16% to 40% in internal medicine. 11 Beyond physical assaults, physicians face digital threats such as doxxing, which is the unauthorized release of personal information (ie, home address, phone number) online with the intent to harass, intimidate, or cause harm. 12 Unlike physical assaults, digital threats intrude into residents’ personal lives, increasing stress and diminishing their sense of safety. 13–16 However, few resources or formal training address these emerging risks. 17

With the high rates of reported assault toward residents, one may expect residents to receive training on how to manage violent patients. Only 21% to 79% of psychiatry residents and 30% of residents in other specialties report receiving any training, and no formal curricula exist for managing patient violence or its aftermath. 11,18 When provided, instruction is often perceived as insufficient. 19 The variability in training and the absence of nationally applied safety measures leave many psychiatry residents unprepared to address both physical and digital threats effectively. 7 To address this gap, the authors explored the data from psychiatry residency programs across the United States to characterize the prevalence and nature of these risks.

Methods

STUDY PARTICIPANTS, QUESTIONNAIRE AND RECRUITMENT

Participants were current psychiatry residents enrolled in accredited residency programs across the United States. Program coordinator contact information was obtained from FREIDA™, and 323 program coordinators’ email addresses were compiled into an Excel sheet. They were then used to send a mass email message, inviting distribution of the survey. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with electronic informed consent obtained prior to survey initiation. Recruitment occurred over a 3-month period (February 2024 through April 2024). Data were collected using a 20-item multiple choice, anonymous self-report questionnaire developed by the investigators and administered via Google Forms. Key definitions were provided to ensure consistent interpretation, including:

Verbal threat: oral, written, or electronic statements indicating intent to cause harm

Unwanted advances: unwelcome romantic or sexual expressions

Sexual assault: nonconsensual sexual contact or behavior (eg, rape, unwanted touching)

The authors collected data on demographics and experiences with workplace violence across different settings. This included inpatient (ie, emergency department, medical hospital, psychiatric hospital), outpatient (ie, in-person clinic), telehealth (ie, interactions via telephone or video platforms), social media, and information on safety measures provided by programs (Figure 1). Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using a combination of descriptive and association statistical methods. Means were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical and ordinal data. Correlations and analysis of variance were applied to examine relationships and differences in the prevalence of incidents across demographic groups. Cross-tabulations were performed to explore associations between categorical variables. All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide 8.2 (SAS Institute Inc), with statistical significance set at P < .05.

The study was reviewed and approved by the Kaiser Permanente Institutional Review Board (approval number: kppsych01 #20240117) and was exempted due to its minimal risk. Data were anonymized, and all responses were securely stored.

Results

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE STUDY POPULATION

As seen in the Table, a total of 127 psychiatry residents from programs across the United States participated in the survey. Among the respondents, 72 (56.7%) identified as female, 51 (40.2%) as male, and 4 (3.1%) as nonbinary or unknown. Most participants were aged ≤ 30 years (n = 71, 55.9%), followed by those aged 31–35 years (n = 40, 31.5%) and ≥ 36 years (n = 16, 12.6%). Most participants identified as White (n = 78, 61.4%), followed by Asian/Pacific Islander (n = 33, 26.0%), multiple races and ethnicities (n = 9, 7.1%), and Black (n = 7, 5.5%). Regarding residency program type, 72 (56.7%) participants were enrolled in university-based programs, while 55 (43.3%) were in community-based programs. Geographically, the largest proportion of residents were in the west (n = 52, 40.9%), followed by the south (n = 28, 22.0%), Mid-Atlantic (n = 27, 21.2%), midwest (n = 14, 11.0%), and New England (n = 6, 4.7%).

Prevalence of Verbal and Physical Threats, Physical Intimidation, Unwanted Advances, Sexual Harassment, and Digital Threats

Among the 127 participants, verbal threats were the most frequently reported form of threats and assaults, experienced by 97 residents (76.4%), followed by physical intimidation (n = 93, 73.2%), unwanted advances (n = 62, 48.8%), physical assault (n = 19, 15.0%), and sexual assault (n = 3, 2.4%). One participant reported receiving a verbal threat through social media (n = 1, 0.8%). The inpatient setting was the most common location for these incidents: 96 of the 97 reported verbal threats (99.0%), 88 of the 93 reported instances of physical intimidation (94.6%), 58 of the 62 reported unwanted advances (93.5%), and all 3 reports of sexual assault (100%) occurred in inpatient settings. All 3 reported sexual assault cases involved 1 White woman, 1 Asian woman, and 1 Asian man, all between the ages of 26 and 30.

Current Safety Measures in Psychiatry Residency Programs

Most residents (n = 115, 90.6%) reported receiving safety training through their residency programs, including de-escalation training (n = 105, 92.1%) followed by self-defense (n = 84, 73.7%) and sexual assault prevention (n = 51, 44.7%). However, only 1 participant (0.8%) indicated their program provided access to subscription-based tools for protecting personal information online. Regarding safety measures within training facilities, most programs reported the presence of security personnel on medical floors (n = 95, 74.8%), followed by the availability of emergency alarm buttons in the interview room (n= 55, 43.3%).

Association Between Demographics, Program Characteristics, and Threats and Assaults

No significant associations were observed between gender and incidents of threats and assaults. However, a trend showed higher rates of unwanted advances among residents who identify as women (55.6%) compared to residents who identify as men (37.3%) (P = .08). Figure 2 illustrates that race and ethnicity was significantly associated with the prevalence of unwanted advances (P = .04), with Black residents reporting the highest rates (71.4%, n = 5), followed by White residents (53.8%, n = 42) and Asian/Pacific Islander residents (42.4%, n = 14). Participants identifying as multiple races and ethnicities reported the lowest rates (11.1%, n = 1). However, the small sample size for Black residents (n = 7) may have limited the generalizability of these findings. A significant relationship was observed between residency program type and the prevalence of verbal threats (P = .03) (Figure 3). Residents in university-based programs reported a higher prevalence of verbal threats (83.3%, n = 60) than those in community-based programs (67.3%, n = 37) (Figure 3). No significant associations were found between geographic region or safety training and any type of threats and assaults.

Social Media and Prevalence of Digital Threats

Among respondents, 87 psychiatry residents reported having public social media accounts (ie, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, LinkedIn), while 116 indicated that they maintained their accounts with private settings. Most psychiatry residents did not report verbal threats via social media (99.2%, n = 126), and only one respondent experienced a verbal threat who had a private social media account. All respondents denied any unwanted advances by a patient or family member via social media (100%, n = 127).

Discussion

Workplace violence remains a substantial challenge in health care, with psychiatry residents experiencing distinct risks due to the nature of their specialty. This study built upon prior research by offering the most up-to-date assessment of workplace violence in this population, with a particular emphasis on experiences of digital threats via social media platforms. Previous studies have highlighted high rates of verbal threats and physical intimidation among psychiatry residents. These findings aligned with those trends, showing 76.4% and 73.2% reporting such experiences, respectively. These rates are consistent with or exceed previous reports, emphasizing the unique vulnerability of psychiatry residents compared to other specialties. In line with earlier research, inpatient settings were identified as the primary site for these incidents, reflecting the acuity often associated with psychiatric crises. The authors observed that de-escalation training was widespread, with 92.1% of respondents having received such training. This supported findings from previous studies that showed its positive impact on confidence and safety in simulated scenarios. 20,21 However, the effectiveness of de-escalation training in reducing actual episodes of violence remains mixed, as some systematic reviews point out it may be due to the lack of rigorous research in the area. 20,22,23

Although safety and de-escalation training may improve resident knowledge and confidence, its impact is greatest when integrated into a broader institutional safety culture with ongoing organizational support. 24 Such a culture should also recognize and address identity-based risks, as race and ethnicity were significantly associated in this study with the likelihood of experiencing unwanted advances (P = .04). Still, future research with larger samples is necessary to generalize the results. The authors acknowledge that there is a disproportionately lower number of active psychiatry residents in the United States who identify as Black (n = 611, 9.6%) who participated in this study. 25 Although limited, the results highlighted a complex relationship between race and workplace violence in medical training, suggesting that patient perceptions and behaviors may differ based on a physician’s racial or ethnic background. Contrary to previous studies, gender did not substantially predict unwanted advances in this study, suggesting possible variations in experiences across studies. 19,26,27

Interestingly, the authors observed a higher prevalence of verbal threats reported in university-based residency programs (83.3%) compared to community-based programs (67.3%). This finding contrasted with prior research, which identified higher verbal aggression in community settings. 28 These differences may be explained by variations in patient demographics or institutional factors such as culture and training practices.

Although this study did not find significant differences in geographic location, age group, or safety/de-escalation training, it highlighted the need for psychiatry programs to reassess their safety training approaches. As the regulatory bodies for program requirements, the American Psychiatry Association and the American Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) should establish standardized protocols for US psychiatry residency programs. These may include de-escalation training programs (ie, Nonviolent Crisis Intervention® Training, Management of Aggressive Behavior, Violence Education and Reduction Action De-escalation Training), self-defense training (ie, institution-led workshops on behavioral awareness, safety, and intervention), and simulation-based and interdisciplinary approaches (ie, patient simulations, team-based interdisciplinary workshops).

Digital Threats via Social Media

This study also added novel insights into the role of digital threats, which, although growing in significance, were less prevalent in this sample compared to other studies. Unlike a 2023 study that found higher rates of social media harassment among physicians (70%), only one participant reported verbal threats via social media, and no one reported unwanted advances. 12

These findings contrasted with growing concerns in the medical community about online threats and suggested that psychiatry residents may adopt more cautious practices, such as limiting public visibility of their accounts. 12 Although this study did not capture the rising digital threats and doxxing via social media, protecting health care practitioners' personal information online remains essential. Although there is no single universal program for this, a combination of institutional policies, third-party services, and legal protection may be utilized. Most training programs have standardized IT security and digital hygiene training; however, third-party privacy protected services are not often provided to psychiatry residents. 29 With an accelerating rise in unauthorized access to personal information online, it may be beneficial to provide subscriptions to private protected services that can scan and remove personal information (addresses, phone numbers) across multiple data broker websites that can be monitored and reported on a regular basis. ACGME may also advise psychiatry programs to include professional social media guidelines to help psychiatry residents maintain professional boundaries and avoid oversharing. The authors acknowledge this may also be a barrier, as approximately 70% to 90% of physicians use social media in some capacity, for purposes including but not limited to professional engagement, networking, medical education, collaboration, and personal or institutional brand building. 30 This includes some resident physicians who may seek to establish an online presence during training to support future clinical practice.

Study Limitations

This study provided a timely update on workplace violence among psychiatry residents, including new considerations such as the role of social media. Although prior studies have explored assaults and microaggressions, this study included new considerations, notably digital threats. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size of 127 participants may have limited the generalizability of these findings, particularly for subanalyses stratified by race or program type. For example, the small number of Black residents in this sample reduced the reliability of conclusions about racial disparities in unwanted advances. Additionally, this study relied on self-reported data. This method is subject to recall bias and potential underreporting of incidents due to stigma or fear of repercussions. The authors were unable to ascertain whether program coordinators disseminated the survey, and they also could not determine how many residents ultimately received and responded to it. The authors also recognize the need for follow-up questions on the frequency of exposure to the current safety measures and training. This may provide a clearer understanding of the support available. Additionally, collecting data on the diagnoses, demographics, and substance use statuses of the patients involved in workplace violence could have helped identify residents at greater risk.

Implications and Future Work

Although no significant findings emerged regarding digital threats, their inclusion reflected an important step toward understanding challenges in an increasingly digital landscape. This study captured how recent workplace violence is evolving alongside technological shifts, offering valuable comparisons to earlier research. Future research should investigate the role of social media in threats more broadly across medical trainees, particularly given the increasing digital presence of physicians. Such studies should also differentiate between different digital media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and/or LinkedIn, to identify which platforms warrant greater digital safety measures.

These findings reveal potential racial disparities in experiences of unwanted advances. However, given the limited participation of psychiatry residents who are underrepesented in medicine, further research is needed. As only about 16% of psychiatry residents in the United States identify as underrepresented in medicine, 31 future studies should prioritize culturally sensitive interventions and policies that support those residents who identify as underrepresented in medicine. Differences in verbal threat prevalence between university-based and community-based programs also suggest that institutional factors may influence resident safety.

Overall, these results have highlighted the need for standardized interventions: de-escalation training, robust security measures, self-defense courses, scenario-based experiences, and cybersecurity protocols. Currently, no standardized guidelines for safety training or cyberprotection exist from the ACGME, which leaves responsibilities to individual program leadership and institutions despite emphasis on resident wellness in the ACGME requirements. Residency programs should also prioritize cultural competence and implicit bias training beyond online lectures and quizzes, incorporating more interactive and experiential learning methods. Future research should use larger, more diverse samples to examine how repeated exposure to physical and digital threats can impact resident burnout, psychiatric disorders (ie, posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety), leave of absence, career satisfaction, and long-term well-being. Further investigation into the impact of social media platforms on threats is also warranted. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to assess the long-term impact of workplace violence and harassment assault on psychiatry residents’ well-being and career trajectories.

Conclusion

This study reaffirmed that psychiatry residents continue to face substantial risks related to workplace violence. Although limited, the presence of racial disparities and program-type differences highlights the need for a more standardized approach to safety training. Although only a single participant in this sample reported receiving a digital threat, this should not be dismissed. With the growing use of social media and digital platforms, psychiatry residents should receive appropriate education on digital threats and strategies for online safety. Notably, although most participants received some form of safety training, only one reported access to resources addressing digital safety. This finding shows that current safety protocols are inconsistent, are inadequate, and do not address evolving threats in clinical and virtual settings . Digital threats remain underrecognized and underaddressed, leaving residents unprepared and susceptible to vulnerabilities. To protect the well-being and professional development of psychiatry trainees, residency programs and accrediting bodies must prioritize the creation and implementation of comprehensive, standardized safety protocols that encompass both physical and digital domains. Addressing these risks would ensure that future psychiatrists would be able to train and thrive in safe and supportive environments.
