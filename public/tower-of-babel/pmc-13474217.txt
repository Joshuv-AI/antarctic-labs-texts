An overview of immunohistochemistry: Evidence and personal experience
Authors: Suleyman Aydin, Suna Aydin, Kader Ugur, İbrahim Sahin, Elif Emre, Sevval Yagmur Gencer, Aziz Aksoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunohistochemistry is a tissue-based technique that enables in situ visualization of specific molecules, most commonly proteins or peptides, within preserved cellular and tissue architecture through antigen–antibody specificity. By using labeled antibodies, immunohistochemistry allows spatially resolved and cell type–specific detection of target molecules and maintains the morphological context, thereby providing both localization and relative expression information at the light or electron microscopy level. Owing to this integrative capacity, immunohistochemistry has become an essential tool in biological research and diagnostic pathology for characterizing molecular distribution and tissue organization across animal and plant systems. The detection techniques based on fluorescence and enzymes (chromogenic) are compared in terms of their repeatability, multiplexing capability, sensitivity, and specificity. With a focus on protocol standardization and methodological rigor, important procedural processes are covered, including fixation, antigen extraction, antibody optimization, signal detection, and quality control. Additionally, the immunohistochemistry technique remains essential for locating and identifying immunogenic substances in a variety of biological settings. Therefore, drawing on more than two decades of laboratory experience, this narrative review summarizes the history of the immunohistochemistry assay, its underlying principles and procedures, common immunohistochemistry protocol, methodological process, essential reagents, infrastructure, and best-practice principles required for the establishment and optimization of a high-quality immunohistochemistry laboratory.

Background

Understanding biological processes in both plant and animal systems depends critically on the identification of immunogenic substances. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) are two of the most popular techniques for detecting them. Both methods have comparable foundations in terms of specificity and sensitivity because they are predicated on the idea of antigen–antibody specificity and frequently use chromogenic substrates and enzyme-conjugated antibodies to produce observable signals. However, the type of information they offer, varies considerably.1,2 Although ELISA is especially well suited for high-throughput analysis and allows quantitative assessment of antigens or antibodies in liquid samples, it does not provide spatial information about tissue localization. In contrast, IHC integrates molecular detection with the morphological context by maintaining tissue architecture and enabling the visualization of the cellular and regional distribution of target molecules.1,3

IHC is a histological technique that enables the in situ detection and localization of specific proteins within animal and plant tissues, allowing assessment of their spatial distribution and relative expression at the microscopic level.4,5 The method is based on the highly specific interaction between antigens and antibodies, in which antibodies serve as affinity probes to identify target molecules within preserved tissue architecture. Upon binding to their corresponding antigens, antibodies form stable immune complexes that permit the visualization of the target structures in their histological context. Structurally, antibodies are Y-shaped immunoglobulins composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, with antigen recognition mediated by the variable regions located at the tips of the Fab domains. In practice, antibodies used in IHC are broadly categorized as monoclonal or polyclonal, differing in epitope specificity and binding characteristics, which makes each type suitable for distinct experimental and diagnostic applications.

IHC plays a central role in clinical pathology for disease diagnosis and classification, tumor origin identification, and evaluation of prognostic and predictive biomarkers. In addition, it significantly contributes to basic research areas such as immunology, neuroscience, and developmental biology by enabling cell type–specific protein localization and improving the understanding of complex biological processes. 5 In plant biology, IHC is increasingly used to investigate the tissue-specific distribution of defense-related proteins, hormones, enzymes, and cell wall components, thereby providing important molecular-level insights into plant–pathogen interactions, stress responses, and developmental regulation. Despite technical challenges associated with the rigid plant cell wall, optimized fixation, permeabilization, and antigen retrieval protocols have substantially improved the reliability and reproducibility of IHC results in plant tissues.6,7

The present review is based on the premise that the reliability and interpretability of IHC are largely determined by the cumulative variability arising during the preanalytical, analytical, and postanalytical stages. Although the literature on IHC is extensive and rapidly expanding, it remains fragmented across methodological, clinical, and cross-kingdom applications, with limited integration into a unified conceptual framework. This fragmentation creates an important knowledge gap regarding how methodological variability influences staining quality, reproducibility, and diagnostic interpretation. Accordingly, the main objective of this review was to perform an integrated synthesis of the existing evidence on IHC by organizing it around variability-related limitations and methodological challenges. In addition to summarizing the existing knowledge, this work adds value by integrating findings from both animal and plant IHC systems with practical laboratory experience, thereby offering a structured perspective on common technical pitfalls, troubleshooting strategies, and reproducibility issues relevant to both research and diagnostic applications.

Literature search strategy

This article was designed as a narrative review with an applied methodological perspective rather than a systematic review. The primary objective was to provide a comprehensive overview of IHC by integrating published evidence with practical laboratory experience and technical considerations encountered during routine diagnostic and research applications.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. To ensure broad and structured coverage of the literature, both medical subject headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords were employed. The principal search terms included “immunohistochemistry,” “IHC,” “immunohistochemical staining,” “IHC history,” “animal immunohistochemistry,” “plant immunohistochemistry,” “types of IHC,” “antigen retrieval,” “epitope retrieval,” “tissue fixation,” “antibody optimization,” “chromogenic detection,” “fluorescence immunohistochemistry,” “multiplex IHC,” “biomarkers,” “technical challenges,” “analytical errors,” “laboratory standardization,” and “quality control.”

The literature search primarily focused on studies published between 1990 and 2025; however, seminal publications predating this period were also included when considered essential for understanding the historical development and methodological foundations of IHC. Publications written in English were prioritized. Articles were selected based on their scientific relevance; methodological importance; citation impact; and contribution to the understanding of the principles, applications, limitations, troubleshooting approaches, and quality assurance aspects of IHC.

The topics considered eligible for inclusion encompassed the historical evolution of IHC, methodological developments, diagnostic and research applications, technical limitations, troubleshooting strategies, quality control measures, and laboratory standardization practices. Because this work was intended as a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review, formal study selection procedures, risk-of-bias assessments, meta-analysis, and quantitative evidence synthesis were not performed.

To enhance the transparency, structure, and reporting quality of the review, the manuscript was prepared in accordance with the recommendations of the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). Rather than serving as a formal quality-assessment instrument for individual studies, SANRA was used as a guiding framework to improve the methodological rigor and reporting standards of this narrative review. 8

Review articles were selectively included when they provided comprehensive historical context, methodological overviews, or critical discussions relevant to the objectives of this review. Preference was given to publications that substantially contributed to the understanding of immunohistochemical principles, applications, standardization, and troubleshooting. Original research articles were additionally used to support specific methodological developments, technical recommendations, and emerging applications. Consequently, the literature was synthesized according to thematic relevance rather than through a systematic inclusion process.

History of IHC and important figures

The theoretical underpinnings of IHC date back to the early 1900s when Emil Adolf von Behring established the foundation for antibody-based immunological techniques by defining the notion of serum-based immunity and winning the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901. This breakthrough gave rise to the fundamental idea of IHC, which is that antibodies can be used to identify certain proteins or antigens inside tissue sections.9,10 The use of fluorescently labeled antibodies was described by Coons et al. in 1941, marking the first documented practical application of immunohistochemical techniques. 11 They were able to locate pneumococcal antigens in tissue slices by conjugating antibodies to the fluorescent chemical fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). Several people consider this groundbreaking study to be the source of contemporary immunofluorescence and IHC techniques.10,12

The sensitivity of immunostaining was greatly increased in the 1960s when Nakane and Pierce developed enzyme-based labeling systems using conjugates of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and alkaline phosphatase (AP). HRP is a 40-kDa enzyme isolated from the roots of horseradish (Armoracia rusticana). It catalyzes the reaction of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), forming an enzyme–substrate complex and subsequently decomposing H2O2 into water and reactive oxygen species, thereby generating the signal used in immunohistochemical detection systems. AP is a 100-kDa enzyme commonly isolated from bovine intestinal tissue. It catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphate groups from organic phosphate esters, thereby generating reaction products that can be visualized in immunohistochemical detection systems. This development encouraged the widespread utilization of chromogenic IHC using light microscopy, especially in clinical diagnostic labs. 13

The creation of monoclonal antibody technology using hybridoma methods by Köhler and Milstein in 1975 was another significant turning point in the 1970s. 14 The developers were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1984 for this discovery, which made it possible to produce antibodies with previously unheard-of specificity and reproducibility, significantly increasing the sensitivity of antigen detection in tissues.10,15

Dr Taylor developed IHC applications in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue slices during his time, which facilitated the integration of IHC into standard diagnostic pathology. The invention of antigen retrieval methods was another significant breakthrough. By reversing the masking effects of formalin fixation, Shan Rong Shi and associates discovered heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) in 1991, which restored antigen accessibility in FFPE tissues. This invention made IHC a standardized process and considerable improved its diagnostic accuracy and dependability.10,16

The quantitative, repeatable, and uniform use of IHC in research and diagnostic contexts was improved in the 2000s by additional antigen retrieval process optimization and integration with molecular morphology. IHC has developed over the years from straightforward fluorescent antibody techniques to include complex methods that use monoclonal antibodies, signal amplification techniques, and extremely sensitive enzyme- or fluorescence-based detection systems. 10 Together, the contributions of experts in immunology, histology, and biochemistry have made IHC a vital tool for basic science as well as clinical pathology. Principle of IHC and the chromogens used in it

The principle of IHC is based on the specific binding between an antibody and its corresponding antigen within a tissue or cellular context. A labeled secondary antibody or a direct conjugate is then used to detect the antigen–antibody combination after a primary antibody identifies and binds to a target protein (antigen). The target molecule's spatial distribution and localization can be visualized while maintaining tissue morphology using either fluorescent tags (immunofluorescence) or enzymatic reactions that give rise to a colored precipitate (chromogenic detection).5,17

Characteristics of common IHC chromogens

In enzyme-based IHC, chromogens are colored substrates that are utilized to view antigen–antibody interactions, especially in systems that use AP or HRP. The hue, stability, contrast, and sensitivity of various chromogens differ. The following is a list and descriptions of the most widely used chromogens. 18

3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC)

In enzyme-based IHC, especially in HRP-mediated detection systems, AEC is a frequently employed chromogen. It offers several benefits, such as the production of distinct, unambiguous signals that are easy to observe under light microscopy and strong contrast with hematoxylin counterstaining. However, AEC has certain limitations, including the potential for color fading during storage and the fact that it is alcohol soluble and hence not permanent in paraffin-embedded sections. 19

3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB)

A chromogen based on HRP, DAB gives off a brown hue. Its benefits include being recognized as the standard chromogen for HRP-based detection systems, possessing great stability and permanence, and being simple to observe under bright-field light microscopy. However, DAB has certain limitations, including the potential for toxicity and health risks as well as the fact that its single hue limits its application in multiplex antigen studies. 20

Fast Red

Fast Red is an AP-based chromogen that produces a red hue. It is sometimes referred to as Permanent Red. Its benefits include the production of bright, high-contrast signals; compatibility with AP-based detection systems; and the ability to be used in conjunction with DAB for multiplex staining. However, Fast Red has certain limitations, including a greater propensity for color fading over time and a potential for less-than-ideal contrast in some light microscopy scenarios. 18

Vector VIP/Vector Slate Gray (SG)

The HRP-based chromogens Vector VIP and Vector SG result in purple (VIP) and gray-purple (SG) coloration, respectively. They have the advantage of being appropriate for multiplex antigen detection employing various contrast combinations and providing a variety of color options for immunostaining. However, these chromogens have certain limitations, such as lesser color stability than DAB and the potential for decreased signal intensity in particular tissue types. 21

DAB is perfect for single-antigen research since it is the most widely used and stable chromogen. Because of their red coloring, AEC and Fast Red offer contrast; however, they fade easily. VIP and SG provide several color options and are appropriate for multiplex antigen tests. In IHC, chromogens are essential because they convert antigen–antibody interactions into observable visual signals. Without chromogens, antigen–antibody complexes would not be observable, making it impossible to pinpoint the exact location and identify target proteins in tissue sections. This would reduce the reliability of both qualitative and quantitative studies, compromise spatial resolution, and significantly impair the capacity to distinguish binding from nonspecific background. Furthermore, chromogenic differentiation is necessary for the simultaneous detection of several antigens in a single tissue segment, which limits the use of multiplex IHC. Thus, chromogens are crucial for the precise interpretation and analytical rigor of immunohistochemical investigations in addition to their visibility (Table 1).18–23

IHC types and their variations

Depending on the imaging technique and antibody labeling strategy used, IHC applications can be divided into several categories. The primary antibody binds directly to the target antigen in the direct IHC method, and a label, such as an enzyme or fluorophore, is used to visualize localization. The methodology for this approach is quick and straightforward methodology; however, its sensitivity is constrained. In contrast, by attaching a tagged secondary antibody to the primary antibody, the indirect IHC technique enhances the signal, thereby the increasing sensitivity and protocol reproducibility. 17

IHC can be further divided into enzyme-based chromogenic IHC and fluorescence-based IHC (immunofluorescence (IF)) based on imaging modalities. An epifluorescence microscope is used to study antibodies that have been coupled with fluorescent dyes in IF. This method requires specialized microscopy and is prone to photobleaching; however, it offers high-resolution, multicolor imaging that is especially well suited for the investigation of numerous antigens. Enzymes such as HRP and AP are used to mark antibodies in enzyme-based IHC. Substrate conversion is used to achieve colorimetric detection, which enables visibility under a typical light microscope. This approach is routinely employed in clinical laboratories.5,24

Multiplex IHC applications, which allow the simultaneous detection of numerous antigens inside a single tissue segment, have recently been established. Although this method increases protocol complexity and raises the possibility of signal overlap, it is an efficient tool for researching protein–protein interactions and intercellular linkages. Traditional IHC in animal tissues

The avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex (ABC) immunohistochemical method developed by Guesdon et al., and Hsu et al. remains widely used in histology and pathology laboratories because of its reliability and reproducibility.25,26 Animal tissues are fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, routinely processed, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5–6 μm) are mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated using graded ethanol solutions, and rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval is commonly performed by heating sections in citrate buffer using microwave treatment, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing Tween-20. Endogenous peroxidase activity is blocked using H2O2, and nonspecific binding is minimized using a protein-blocking solution. For intracellular antigens, mild permeabilization with detergents such as Triton X-100 may be applied. Sections are then incubated with the primary antibody, followed by a biotinylated secondary antibody and a streptavidin–peroxidase detection system. Chromogenic substrates such as AEC are used to visualize antigen localization. Counterstaining is typically performed using Mayer's hematoxylin before mounting and microscopic examination. IHC in plant tissues

Plant IHC requires methodological modifications because of rigid cell walls, high concentrations of phenolic compounds, and intrinsic tissue autofluorescence. Plant samples are typically fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS under vacuum infiltration to facilitate penetration through the cell wall matrix. Following fixation, tissues are dehydrated, embedded in paraffin or hydrophilic resins such as LR White, sectioned at 5–10 μm, and mounted on adhesive-coated slides. 4

After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval may be achieved using HIER in citrate buffer or via controlled enzymatic digestion using cellulase or pectinase to improve antibody accessibility. Endogenous peroxidase activity is suppressed by H2O2, whereas nonspecific binding is reduced using blocking solutions containing bovine serum albumin (BSA), casein, or normal serum. Additional treatments may be required to minimize autofluorescence caused by lignin, chlorophyll, and phenolic compounds.

For intracellular targets, permeabilization with mild detergents such as Triton X-100 may be performed. Sections are incubated with primary antibodies, followed by enzyme-conjugated or fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal detection can be achieved either chromogenically, commonly using DAB, or using fluorescence-based methods. Some plant tissues contain endogenous biotin; therefore, biotin-blocking procedures are recommended when streptavidin–biotin detection systems are employed.

Fluorescently labeled sections are examined using fluorescence or confocal microscopy with carefully selected filter sets to distinguish specific signals from tissue autofluorescence. To ensure specificity and reproducibility, negative controls, isotype controls, and positive control tissues should be included in every experiment. Despite limitations such as restricted antibody penetration, autofluorescence, epitope masking, and the limited availability of plant-specific antibodies, IHC remains a powerful technique for investigating protein localization and signaling pathways in plant tissues.4,7

Major differences between plant and animal IHC methods

Plant tissues are structurally complicated, requiring substantial technique adjustment due to factors, including cell walls, phenolic content, and autofluorescence, even though the fundamental principles of IHC are comparable in animal and plant species. Improving antibody penetration, using the right antigen retrieval techniques, and reducing background signal are essential for successful plant IHC. In plant IHC research, these methodological considerations require meticulous experimental design and the use of strict controls. The primary distinctions are explained below.28–30

Tissue structural features

Since animal tissues do not have a strong cell wall, antibodies can usually penetrate them more easily. In contrast, plant tissues have thick cell walls made of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. This barrier frequently requires further permeabilization or regulated enzymatic breakdown (e.g. using pectinase or cellulase) and can limit antibody access to target epitopes. Additionally, the uniformity of antibody distribution and section quality may be impacted by the strong cell wall matrix and substantial vacuolar volume found in plant cells.

Fixation and antigen preservation

Neutral-buffered formalin (10%) is commonly employed in animal tissues and is adequate for most antigens. However, fixative penetration in plant tissues is slowed by the cell wall, necessitating vacuum infiltration. Furthermore, secondary metabolites and phenolic chemicals in plant tissues can mask epitopes and promote protein cross-linking, making antigen retrieval processes more crucial than in animal systems.

Endogenous activity and background staining

The main causes of background staining in animal tissues are endogenous peroxidase or biotin, which are typically lessened by using standard blocking techniques. However, there are other difficulties in plant tissues, such as (a) increased endogenous peroxidase activity; (b) phenolic compounds; and (c) intrinsic autofluorescence from lignin, chlorophyll, and other secondary metabolites. All these elements work together to significantly increase background signal, and autofluorescence in particular makes it difficult to accurately interpret the results of fluorescent IHC.

Antibody specificity and validation

For several animal proteins, commercially confirmed antibodies are easily accessible. However, numerous antibodies are produced in plant systems against animal epitopes, and they frequently show little or erratic cross-reactivity with plant proteins. To guarantee the accuracy and interpretability of IHC results, it is crucial to thoroughly validate antibody specificity in plant tissues using methods such as Western blot analysis, use of genetic mutant controls, or peptide competition experiments.

Sample preparation and sectioning

Animal IHC frequently uses paraffin embedding and cryosectioning, although plant procedures frequently include hydrophilic resins (such LR White) in addition to paraffin. Cell wall integrity and antigenicity are better preserved by resin embedding. To investigate protein localization in intact tissues, whole-mount IHC techniques are also increasingly being applied in plants, particularly in model species such as Arabidopsis thaliana.

Quantitative interpretation

In both plant and animal systems, IHC is a method that is intrinsically semi-quantitative. However, several intrinsic factors, such as (a) heterogeneous antibody penetration due to the presence of rigid cell walls; (b) variation in cell wall thickness among various tissue types; and (c) differential autofluorescence resulting from chlorophyll, lignin, and other phenolic compounds, further complicate quantitative interpretation in plant tissues. Taken together, these elements pose significant obstacles to the precise measurement of IHC signals in plant tissues compared with that in animal systems. Practical recommendations, common challenges, and lessons learned from laboratory experience

Although the fundamental principles of IHC are well established, significant variability in staining quality and interpretation persists among laboratories. Such variability often arises from differences in tissue processing, fixation procedures, antigen retrieval methods, antibody selection, and quality control practices. Drawing on both current literature and our laboratory experience, several practical considerations and common technical challenges deserve particular attention. The reagents and procedural steps employed must be rigorously controlled to ensure the reliability and validity of the technique. In the absence of appropriate controls, staining evaluation may become subjective, thereby compromising reproducibility and leading to scientifically questionable conclusions. Rigorous validation of IHC reagents is essential for ensuring analytical accuracy, reproducibility, and interpretive reliability. Both manufacturers and end users share responsibility for verifying antibody performance, including the specificity of primary and secondary antibodies for their intended target antigens as well as determining optimal experimental conditions such as antibody dilution, incubation duration, and incubation temperature. It is equally important to standardize the procedural workflows to minimize inter-assay and inter-operator variability, thereby enhancing the consistency of staining outcomes across laboratories and experimental settings.

Quality assurance in IHC is typically achieved through two complementary validation strategies. The first, referred to as the reagent substitution test, involves replacing the antibody of interest with an alternative reagent or an appropriate negative control to evaluate staining specificity and identify potential nonspecific background reactions. The second, known as the tissue substitution test, assesses antibody performance by applying the primary antibody to tissues with well-characterized antigen expression patterns, thereby confirming both specificity and sensitivity under experimental conditions.

The implementation of these control measures is fundamental to the generation of robust and reproducible immunohistochemical data. Failure to adequately validate reagents and procedural parameters may compromise staining interpretation, increase observer-dependent variability, and ultimately lead to scientifically questionable conclusions. Consequently, rigorous quality control remains a cornerstone of contemporary immunohistochemical practice and a prerequisite for the reliable interpretation of tissue-based protein expression.

Additionally, tissue fixation represents one of the most critical determinants of successful immunohistochemical staining. Both under-fixation and over-fixation can alter antigen accessibility, leading to reduced staining intensity, loss of antigenicity, or inconsistent results. Therefore, standardized fixation protocols and controlled fixation times are essential for maintaining reproducibility.2,5,31

Another crucial factor is antigen retrieval. Formalin fixation may mask antigenic epitopes, resulting in weak or absent staining. The selection and optimization of antigen retrieval protocols—including buffer composition, pH, temperature, and retrieval duration—should be performed individually for each antibody, as these parameters significantly influence staining sensitivity and specificity.2,5,31

Nonspecific staining and excessive background signal remain some of the most common technical challenges in IHC. These artifacts may arise from excessive antibody concentrations, inadequate blocking, insufficient washing, antibody cross-reactivity, or nonspecific interactions between antibodies and tissue components. Such effects can obscure true antigen localization, reduce signal-to-noise ratios, and potentially lead to false-positive interpretations. Therefore, careful antibody titration, optimized incubation conditions, rigorous washing procedures, and the inclusion of appropriate positive and negative controls are essential for obtaining reliable and reproducible results.2,5,31

A major contributor to background staining is nonspecific hydrophobic binding, which originates from intrinsic hydrophobic domains within proteins or from hydrophobic properties induced during tissue processing, particularly fixation. Connective, epithelial, and adipose tissues are especially susceptible to this phenomenon because of their relatively high hydrophobicity. Several approaches have been developed to reduce hydrophobic interactions. The use of low ionic-strength buffers can weaken hydrophobic forces, whereas optimization of buffer composition through appropriate ionic constituents may further improve staining specificity. In addition, mild detergents incorporated into antibody diluents and adjustments in buffer pH can decrease nonspecific antibody adsorption.2,5,31

Among all available strategies, protein-blocking procedures remain the most effective and widely adopted method for controlling hydrophobicity-related background staining. Blocking agents such as BSA, normal serum, casein, and commercially available blocking reagents saturate nonspecific binding sites, thereby preventing unwanted antibody–tissue interactions. Therefore, effective blocking significantly enhances staining specificity, improves signal discrimination, and facilitates accurate interpretation of immunohistochemical findings.2,5,31

Another important cause of background staining in IHC is nonspecific ionic binding. Ionic interactions occur when oppositely charged molecules come into proximity. Most immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies possess isoelectric points ranging approximately from 5.8 to 7.3; therefore, they generally carry a net negative charge in commonly used antibody diluents and buffer systems with a pH of 7.0–7.8. Consequently, when tissue antigens or other cellular components exhibit a net positive charge under these conditions, electrostatic attraction may occur between the antibody and tissue structures, resulting in nonspecific staining.

Unlike hydrophobic interactions, nonspecific ionic binding is primarily influenced by the ionic strength of the incubation buffer. Increasing the ionic strength reduces electrostatic attraction by shielding charged groups and limiting antibody–tissue interactions unrelated to specific antigen recognition. Thus, the addition of salts such as sodium chloride (NaCl) to antibody diluents is commonly employed as a strategy to reduce ionic background staining. Concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 M NaCl are frequently effective in suppressing nonspecific electrostatic interactions without substantially affecting specific antigen–antibody binding. Optimization of buffer composition, pH, and salt concentration is therefore critical for maximizing staining specificity and minimizing background artifacts in immunohistochemical applications.2,5,31

In addition to antibody-related factors, widespread nonspecific staining may arise from preanalytical and tissue-processing variables that compromise tissue integrity before immunohistochemical evaluation. Mechanical trauma during tissue collection, handling, trimming, sectioning, or slide preparation can disrupt cellular architecture, expose intracellular constituents, and create artificial binding sites, thereby increasing nonspecific antibody deposition. Likewise, delays in fixation or tissue desiccation prior to fixation may induce protein denaturation, alter antigen distribution, and contribute to diffuse background staining.

Suboptimal fixation represents another major source of staining artifacts. Inadequate penetration of fixatives, particularly in large, thick, or densely organized specimens, may result in uneven tissue preservation and incomplete stabilization of cellular proteins. Such deficiencies can adversely affect antigen accessibility, increase nonspecific antibody interactions, and generate heterogeneous staining patterns that complicate interpretation. Furthermore, variations in fixation duration, fixative composition, and tissue-to-fixative ratios may influence both antigen preservation and staining quality.

Therefore, meticulous attention to preanalytical variables, including prompt tissue fixation, appropriate specimen dimensions, adequate fixative volume, and standardized fixation protocols, is essential for maintaining tissue morphology, preserving antigenicity, and ensuring reliable immunohistochemical results. Careful control of these parameters substantially reduces background artifacts and enhances the accuracy, reproducibility, and interpretive value of immunohistochemical analyses.

Endogenous enzyme activity represents another important source of background staining in IHC and must be effectively suppressed to distinguish true immunoreactivity from enzyme-mediated artifacts generated during the detection process. Failure to adequately block endogenous enzymes may result in nonspecific signal development and erroneous interpretation of staining patterns. Among endogenous enzymes, peroxidase activity is particularly problematic in tissues rich in erythrocytes, granulocytes, bone marrow elements, liver, spleen, and certain epithelial tissues. Several immunohistochemical detection systems utilize HRP as the reporter enzyme; therefore, residual endogenous peroxidase activity can produce chromogenic reactions that are indistinguishable from specific antigen-related signals. The most widely used and practical method for eliminating endogenous peroxidase activity is preincubation of tissue sections with H2O2. Typically, sections are treated with 3% H2O2 for 5–10 min before antibody application. This treatment irreversibly inactivates endogenous peroxidase enzymes and substantially reduces background staining without significantly affecting tissue morphology or antigenicity. Depending on the tissue type and staining protocol, H2O2 may be prepared in distilled water, PBS, or methanol. Therefore, effective endogenous enzyme blocking is a critical step in ensuring staining specificity, improving signal-to-noise ratios, and enhancing the overall reliability of immunohistochemical analyses.

Technical limitations may also arise during signal detection. In chromogenic assays, excessive or insufficient substrate development can affect staining interpretation, whereas fluorescence-based techniques are susceptible to photobleaching and signal fading. These challenges can be mitigated through optimization of incubation conditions, protection of fluorescent samples from light exposure, and the use of appropriate mounting media.31,32

Sample preparation represents an additional source of variability. Inconsistent section thickness, tissue folds, incomplete paraffin removal, or inadequate rehydration may adversely affect antibody penetration and staining uniformity. Standardization of sectioning procedures, careful tissue handling, and complete deparaffinization are therefore essential quality control measures. Finally, interpretation of immunohistochemical staining requires awareness of potential artifacts, background staining patterns, and tissue-specific expression characteristics. Histopathological correlation remains indispensable for accurate assessment. Moreover, implementation of standardized quality control procedures and participation in external quality-assessment programs can substantially improve reproducibility and reduce interlaboratory variation.

Based on both published evidence and routine laboratory practice, attention to these critical preanalytical, analytical, and postanalytical factors can significantly enhance the reliability, reproducibility, and diagnostic value of immunohistochemical analyses in both clinical and research settings (Table 4).33,34

Immunohistochemical characterization of metabolic regulators in animal tissues

IHC plays a pivotal role in tumor classification, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic decision making in both human and veterinary oncology. Among the most widely used proliferation markers, Ki-67 demonstrates nuclear expression in actively dividing cells and has been associated with increased tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and poorer clinical outcomes.35,36 The tumor suppressor protein p53 is another important biomarker, with abnormal nuclear accumulation frequently reflecting mutation-associated dysfunction, genomic instability, and therapy resistance.37,38

In canine mammary carcinomas, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2/neu) overexpression has been identified in a subset of tumors, supporting the use of comparative oncology models for investigating HER2-positive breast cancer biology and therapeutic responses. 39 In addition, KIT (KIT proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase; c-KIT) expression patterns are routinely evaluated in canine mast cell tumors where aberrant cytoplasmic localization is associated with higher tumor grade and less favorable prognosis.40,41 Collectively, markers such as Ki-67, tumor protein p53 (p53), HER2/neu, and KIT illustrate the value of IHC in characterizing tumor biology, refining prognostic stratification, and supporting individualized therapeutic approaches in veterinary oncology.35–41

Immunohistochemical evaluation of inflammatory and stress-related molecules in animal and comparative pathology

IHC is widely used to evaluate molecular pathways involved in inflammation, cellular stress, and neuroendocrine regulation. Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), first described by Baltimore and colleagues in 1986, is a key regulator of inflammatory and immune responses. Immunohistochemical detection of nuclear NF-κB localization serves as a marker of active inflammatory signaling and is frequently observed in autoimmune disorders, chronic inflammatory conditions, and tumor microenvironments.42–44

Heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70), originally identified by Ritossa, is a highly conserved molecular chaperone involved in protein folding and cellular protection against stress. Increased Hsp70 immunoreactivity is commonly associated with ischemia, oxidative stress, toxic injury, and neoplastic processes, reflecting cellular adaptive responses to tissue damage.45,46 IHC is also valuable for localizing neuroendocrine peptides, including serotonin and somatostatin. Serotonin immunostaining identifies enterochromaffin cells, serotonergic neurons, and neuroendocrine tumors, whereas somatostatin immunoreactivity is observed in pancreatic islets, gastrointestinal D cells, and hypothalamic nuclei. These markers contribute to the evaluation of neuroendocrine function, gastrointestinal disorders, and endocrine pathology in both experimental and clinical settings.47–49

Immunohistochemical insights into central and peripheral regulators of appetite

IHC has played a pivotal role in mapping the anatomical distribution of peptides and hormones involved in energy homeostasis, providing important insights into the neural and peripheral networks that regulate appetite.50,51 These regulatory molecules are broadly classified as orexigenic factors that stimulate food intake and anorexigenic factors that suppress feeding and promote energy balance. Among the principal orexigenic regulators, neuropeptide Y (NPY), agouti-related peptide (AgRP), ghrelin, orexin, galanin, growth hormone–releasing hormone (GHRH), and melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH) have been extensively characterized by IHC. NPY and AgRP are co-expressed in neurons of the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus and show increased immunoreactivity during fasting, highlighting their central role in adaptive feeding responses and long-term energy regulation.52–56 Ghrelin, primarily localized to X/A-like cells of the gastric fundus and specific hypothalamic nuclei, exhibits enhanced expression during negative energy balance and functions as a major peripheral orexigenic hormone.57–59 Orexin, MCH, galanin, and GHRH are distributed within hypothalamic feeding circuits where they contribute to appetite stimulation, metabolic regulation, and behavioral adaptation. 60

IHC has also been instrumental in identifying anorexigenic pathways. Leptin, initially localized to the adipose tissue and later detected in several peripheral tissues, represents a key component of adipose–brain communication and satiety signaling.61–63 Within the hypothalamus, pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)–derived α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) and cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART) exert potent inhibitory effects on food intake, with CART immunoreactivity decreasing during fasting.64–66 Cholecystokinin (CCK), localized to intestinal I-cells and vagal afferents, contributes to meal termination and gastrointestinal feedback mechanisms.66,67 Additional anorexigenic mediators identified using IHC include amylin, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), glucagon, nesfatin-1, neuromedin U (NMU), oxyntomodulin, pancreatic polypeptide (PP), peptide YY (PYY), and urocortin. These molecules are distributed across hypothalamic nuclei, pancreatic islets, gastrointestinal enteroendocrine cells, and brainstem regions where they collectively regulate satiety, energy expenditure, glucose metabolism, and stress-related feeding responses.68–75

Together, these findings demonstrate the unique value of IHC in elucidating the spatial organization of appetite-regulating networks and in advancing our understanding of the complex neuroendocrine mechanisms governing energy balance.50–75

Immunohistochemical insights into myokines and skeletal muscle endocrine signaling

The concept of myokines—cytokines and peptide mediators released by the skeletal muscle in response to contraction—was introduced by Pedersen in 2003, establishing skeletal muscle as an endocrine organ. 76 IHC has been fundamental in characterizing the localization and biological functions of these molecules. Among the best-characterized myokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6) is localized within skeletal muscle fibers and is upregulated following exercise. Muscle-derived IL-6 contributes to systemic energy homeostasis by regulating glucose metabolism, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and stimulating lipolysis. 77 Interleukin-15 (IL-15), predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle fibers and satellite cells, has been associated with muscle hypertrophy and reduced adiposity. 78

Myostatin (growth differentiation factor 8 (GDF-8)), identified in skeletal muscle fibers and extracellular matrix, functions as a potent negative regulator of muscle growth and remodeling. 79 In contrast, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), detected in exercised muscle tissue, contributes to neuromuscular plasticity, fatty acid oxidation, and metabolic adaptation. 80 More recently, meteorin-like (Metrnl) and myonectin C1q/tumor necrosis factor–related protein 15 (CTRP15) have emerged as important mediators of muscle-to-organ communication. Metrnl promotes adipose tissue browning and anti-inflammatory responses, whereas myonectin regulates lipid metabolism by enhancing fatty acid uptake in the liver and adipose tissue.81–83 Collectively, these findings highlight the value of IHC in elucidating the endocrine functions of skeletal muscle and the role of myokines in systemic metabolic regulation.

Decoding adipokine biology through IHC

Adipokines are bioactive peptides and proteins mainly produced by adipose tissue that regulate inflammation, metabolism, and energy homeostasis. IHC has been crucial in defining their tissue-specific distribution beyond adipose depots, including skeletal muscle, liver, pancreas, vasculature, and salivary glands. Leptin, identified in 1994, was first localized to white and brown adipocytes, with receptor expression demonstrated in hypothalamic neurons, establishing its role in appetite and energy regulation. Later studies detected leptin in salivary gland acinar and ductal epithelial cells, suggesting additional functions in oral mucosal protection and metabolic communication.61,84

Adiponectin, first identified between 1995 and 1996, is an anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing adipokine. It was initially localized to adipocytes and subsequently identified in skeletal muscle, cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelium, and salivary glands, supporting its broader endocrine and protective roles.85,86 Resistin, first detected in adipocytes and macrophages, was later found in stromal immune cells and salivary gland epithelium, linking metabolic inflammation to local immune regulation.87,88

Visfatin (nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT)) immunoreactivity has been demonstrated in visceral adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, pancreatic islets, hepatocytes, and salivary glands, implicating it in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) metabolism and cellular homeostasis. 89 Chemerin expression has been observed in adipocytes, immune cells, liver tissue, and inflamed salivary glands, suggesting its roles in inflammatory regulation and immune surveillance. 90 Omentin, initially localized to visceral adipose stromal cells and later to salivary gland acinar cells, contributes to metabolic regulation and tissue protection. 91

Immunohistochemical localization of emerging metabolic regulators

The immunohistochemical characterization of recently discovered metabolic peptides and regulatory proteins has significantly advanced comparative metabolic biology by enabling tissue-specific localization and functional interpretation within integrated endocrine networks. Fibronectin type III domain–containing protein 5 (FNDC5)/irisin, identified in 2012, was first localized to skeletal muscle fibers, confirming muscle as its principal source. Subsequent studies demonstrated irisin expression in the adipose tissue, heart, brain, and salivary glands. Functionally, irisin promotes white adipose tissue browning, thermogenesis, and insulin sensitivity.92,93 Asprosin, discovered in 2016, was localized to the adipocytes, hepatocytes, pancreatic islets, and hypothalamic nuclei, supporting its role in stimulating hepatic glucose release and appetite regulation during fasting.94,95

Dermcidin, initially identified in eccrine sweat glands, was later detected in the placenta, liver, skeletal muscle, and malignant tissues, indicating functions extending beyond antimicrobial defense to metabolic and stress-related signaling.96,97 Salusin-α and salusin-β were localized to the gastrointestinal, renal, vascular, and hypothalamic tissues, highlighting their roles in cardiovascular and metabolic regulation.98,99 Apelin, the endogenous ligand of the AP receptor, has been demonstrated in the adipocytes, cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelium, gastrointestinal epithelium, and hypothalamus, where it contributes to angiogenesis, glucose metabolism, and cardiovascular homeostasis.100–102 Renalase, predominantly localized to the renal tubular epithelial cells but also detected in cardiovascular, hepatic, pancreatic, muscular, and neural tissues, is involved in blood pressure regulation, metabolic balance, and cytoprotective responses.103,104 Studies, including those by Ayden et al., have further demonstrated the localization of ghrelin, leptin, irisin, and apelin in salivary gland epithelial cells, supporting the concept that salivary glands participate in systemic metabolic communication and may serve as noninvasive metabolic biomarkers.63,105,106 Comprehensive tissue localization of metabolic peptides and regulators is summarized in Table 5.52–71,77–81,84–104,107–110

Immunohistochemical landscape of plant metabolic regulators

Recent advances in plant molecular biology have expanded the application of IHC beyond structural macromolecules to the in situ localization of bioactive peptides, peptide-like compounds, and plant hormones. Traditionally, plant IHC focused on cell wall glycoproteins, photosynthesis-related proteins, and stress-responsive molecules. More recently, immunohistochemical and related immunochemical approaches have enabled the detection of hormone-associated molecules and signaling peptides at cellular and subcellular levels, providing new insights into plant signaling networks.111–113

In addition to endogenous plant peptides, IHC studies have demonstrated unexpected cross-kingdom immunoreactivity with antibodies raised against animal peptides. Ghrelin-like immunoreactive substances have been detected in the parenchymal tissues of Prunus domestica and Morus alba, whereas obestatin-like immunoreactivity has been reported in immature Camellia sinensis shoots. Furthermore, immunoreactivity against antibodies to alarin, asprosin, glucagon, elabela, and nesfatin-1 has been observed in Allium sativum and Allium tuncelianum. Although these findings likely reflect structural homology or shared epitopes rather than the presence of authentic animal peptides, they highlight the sensitivity of IHC and the molecular complexity of plant peptide repertoires.114–116

Plants also possess diverse endogenous peptide hormones, including systemin, phytosulfokine, stomagen, and (CLAVATA3/embryo surrounding region–related (CLE)) peptides, which regulate defense responses, vascular differentiation, meristem maintenance, and stomatal development. Antibody-based localization studies have proven valuable for mapping their spatial distribution.117–120 IHC has also been widely used to localize classical plant hormones such as auxin (indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)) in meristematic tissues, vascular bundles, root tips, and developing embryos, revealing spatial gradients critical for organogenesis, morphogenesis, and polar auxin transport.121,122 Studies by Ayden et al. and Calapoglu et al. have further demonstrated immunoreactivity for ghrelin-, obestatin-, nesfatin-1-, asprosin-, and glucagon-like molecules in plants, suggesting conserved epitope motifs across biological kingdoms.114–116

The findings of this study highlight the expanding role of IHC in plant sciences. In addition to enabling high-resolution localization of endogenous plant hormones and peptide regulators, IHC facilitates the detection of peptide-like compounds that share structural similarities with animal adipokines and metabolic peptides. This integrative approach contributes to a deeper understanding of peptide signaling, intercellular communication, and molecular regulatory mechanisms in plant tissues. IHC remains a cornerstone technique in pathology, cell biology, translational research, and plant sciences, with applications ranging from routine diagnostic evaluation to advanced biomarker discovery and therapeutic decision making. Despite its long-standing utility, the field continues to evolve through the integration of emerging technologies such as multiplex IHC, digital pathology, spatial omics approaches, spatial proteomics, and artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted image analysis. These advancements significantly expand the analytical depth of IHC by enabling simultaneous detection of multiple targets, high-throughput image analysis, and spatially resolved molecular profiling, thereby providing biological insights that are not achievable with conventional single-marker techniques.

However, several critical challenges remain unresolved. Variability in preanalytical and analytical procedures, including tissue fixation, antigen retrieval, antibody validation, staining protocols, and interpretation criteria, continues to limit reproducibility across laboratories. In addition, the lack of full standardization in multiplex platforms, differences in digital image analysis pipelines, and need for validated AI-based diagnostic tools represent important gaps that must be addressed. 123

Future research should focus on improving methodological standardization, developing universally accepted quality control frameworks, and validating emerging technologies in multicenter studies. Emphasis should be placed on harmonizing digital pathology workflows, optimizing multiplex assay reproducibility, and integrating spatial omics data with conventional IHC findings to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of tissue biology.

Thus, although IHC remains indispensable in modern biomedical science, its future advancement will depend on rigorous standardization, technological integration, and collaborative efforts aimed at improving reproducibility, interpretability, and clinical translatability across research and diagnostic setting.
