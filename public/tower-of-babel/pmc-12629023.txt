Early alternative splicing signatures and RBP networks in MASLD progression to cirrhosis
Authors: Li Chen, Wei Ma, Yaoqing Hu, Yao Qin, Shenghui Zhao, Jiayao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (MASLD) affects approximately 32.4% of adults globally, progressing from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (MASH), cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Alternative splicing (AS) is crucial for gene expression regulation, and its dysregulation is linked to disease progression. To identify early molecular signatures indicative of cirrhosis in MASLD, we analyzed RNA sequencing data from liver tissues of healthy controls (n = 10), MASLD patients (n = 9), and cirrhosis patients (n = 10) obtained from the SRA database (accession ERP146053). Utilizing SUVA for splicing event detection and k-means clustering, we examined dynamic changes in alternative splicing events and constructed regulatory networks of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Integration with immunological profiles enabled us to explore the interplay between splicing variants and immune responses during disease progression. We identified 671 differential alternative splicing events. Clustering revealed dynamic splicing patterns across disease stages, with two key patterns associated with DNA damage response (DDR) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes, showing early progressive alterations correlating with disease severity. Twelve immune-related splicing events were significantly associated with changes in immune cell populations, highlighting their role in immune regulation during Liver disease progression. Additionally, 11 RBPs, including CELF5 and PCBP1, showed expression changes that correlate with splicing events associated with MASLD progression. Identifying early alternative splicing signatures and understanding RBP networks enhance our ability to predict disease progression and present potential therapeutic targets for early intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02233-1.Keywords: Alternative splicing, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), Cirrhosis progression, RNA-Binding proteins (RBPs), Immune microenvironment

Introduction

MASLD is one of the most prevalent chronic Liver diseases worldwide, affecting an estimated 32.4% of adults. It demonstrates higher prevalence rates in men compared to women, particularly among the obese [1]. While most individuals with simple steatosis remain stable, approximately 20%−30% of those with MASLD may develop non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (MASH), and 10%−15% of these cases can progress to cirrhosis [2]. Key molecular mechanisms driving the progression of MASLD to more severe liver conditions include metabolic reprogramming of hepatocytes [3], EMT [4], DNA damage [5], and immune dysregulation [6]. Consequently, MASLD has emerged as a major contributor to end-stage liver disease. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the variability in MASLD progression, particularly the early development of cirrhosis, remain poorly understood. This lack of understanding limits our ability to predict which patients will progress to severe cirrhosis or HCC. Therefore, identifying early molecular signatures of cirrhosis within the MASLD spectrum and elucidating the regulatory mechanisms involved are crucial for enabling early interventions and developing targeted therapeutic strategies to prevent advanced liver disease.

Alternative splicing plays a critical role in the study of MASLD and other liver diseases by generating different mRNA isoforms that influence key cellular processes such as metabolism [7], proliferation [8], and immunity [9]. Several studies have demonstrated that dysregulation of alternative splicing can drive the progression of MASLD to more severe conditions like MASH and cirrhosis. For example, SRSF6 regulates the splicing of mitochondrial function-related genes, and its inhibition by DRAK2 promotes the progression of MASLD to MASH by disrupting mitochondrial function [10]. Additionally, ESRP2 suppression-mediated alternative splicing reprograms adult hepatocytes into immature cells in severe alcoholic hepatitis, exacerbating liver damage and promoting liver failure [11]. Notably, certain splicing patterns associated with cirrhosis are already evident in MASLD, suggesting an “early alternative splicing signature” that may forecast disease advancement. Comparable bioinformatics-based transcriptome analysis has clarified splicing landscapes in other liver disorders [12, 13]. Taken together, these findings underscore the critical role of alternative splicing in the pathogenesis of MASLD and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for early intervention and treatment of liver diseases.

Eukaryotic gene expression is not dictated by transcription alone; cells therefore rely on RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) that recognize sequence/structure motifs to couple cellular signals to the post-transcriptional fate of RNAs. RBPs are crucial regulators of RNA metabolism, playing vital roles in post-transcriptional processes such as RNA splicing, transport, polyadenylation, localization, translation, and degradation [14]. By binding to specific RNA sequences, RBPs help maintain proper gene expression. Recent studies have shown that RBPs are heavily involved in MASLD progression, with SRSF3 regulating splicing and HUR, TTP, and QKI controlling mRNA stability and inflammation—both essential in the disease’s development [15–18]. When these RBPs are dysregulated, it leads to abnormal RNA processing, contributing to liver dysfunction, inflammation, and fibrosis, which are hallmarks of MASLD and its progression to more severe stages like cirrhosis. Given the central role of RBPs in these processes, we hypothesize that specific RBPs regulate alternative splicing events that drive the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis. This study aims to investigate genome-wide RBP-mediated splicing regulation to identify potential early molecular markers of cirrhosis and therapeutic targets for intervention.

In this study, we investigate the roles of alternative splicing and RBPs in the progression of MASLD and its transition to cirrhosis. We first identify alternative splicing events across liver tissues from healthy controls, MASLD patients, and cirrhosis patients, highlighting dynamic changes in splicing patterns as the disease progresses. Notably, we observe splicing alterations in MASLD that mirror those found in cirrhosis, suggesting early molecular changes that may predict disease advancement. Additionally, we examine immune-related splicing events that could impact the immune microenvironment and drive inflammation during MASLD progression. Lastly, we assess the regulatory roles of key RBPs, identifying their potential as therapeutic targets to halt or reverse disease progression. By integrating splicing, immune, and regulatory data, this study provides a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying MASLD and offers novel strategies for early diagnosis and intervention to prevent the development of cirrhosis.

Results

Identification of differential alternative splicing events in liver tissues

To investigate regulatory differences in alternative splicing from healthy liver tissue to MASLD and cirrhosis, we analyzed the public transcriptome dataset ERP146053, comprising samples from 10 healthy individuals, 9 MASLD patients, and 10 cirrhosis patients (totaling 29 samples). Figure 1A presents the workflow used for identifying disease-associated regulated alternative splicing (RAS) events and the integration of RBPs in disease progression (Fig. 1A). In this study, we utilized the alternative splicing analysis software SUVA to identify RAS, as it redefines splicing event formulas to effectively address complex splicing scenarios [19] (fig. S1 A). SUVA analysis indicated that alternative 5’ splice site (alt5p) events are the predominant types of alternative splicing observed (Fig. 1B). As the disease advanced from healthy to MASLD and then to cirrhosis, there was a significant increase in aberrant splicing events, suggesting a close association between alternative splicing regulation and disease progression.

Additionally, by mapping the splicing events identified by SUVA to classical splicing types, we found that cassette exon and A5SS were the most frequently altered splicing types during disease progression. The greatest number of differential splicing events was observed in the comparison between the cirrhosis and healthy groups. Interestingly, there was a notable increase in MXE events in this comparison, which may represent a splicing signature unique to cirrhosis (Fig. 1C). Approximately 75% of splicing events were complex, highlighting the intricate nature of splicing regulation in MASLD and cirrhosis (fig. S1 B).

To focus on RAS events that predominantly affect host genes, we used the proportion of splicing events (pSAR) as a screening criterion, excluding events with a pSAR below 50% (see Methods). A total of 671 events with a pSAR of 50% or higher were selected for in-depth analysis (Fig. 1D, Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the splicing ratios of these events revealed significant differences between the healthy and MASLD groups. The cirrhosis group was clearly separated from the other two groups along the first principal component, suggesting greater splicing pattern heterogeneity during disease progression. (Fig. 1E). In SUVA, an alternative splicing events library was first constructed, with each AS event assigned a unique ID. We compared RAS identified in pairwise comparisons (MASLD vs. healthy, cirrhosis vs. healthy, and cirrhosis vs. MASLD) (Fig. 1F). The limited overlap observed implies that splicing events do not evolve progressively and uniformly across all stages of disease progression but are instead highly stage-specific, potentially reflecting distinct regulatory mechanisms operating at different points in MASLD progression.

Dynamic changes in alternative splicing patterns during disease progression

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of alternative splicing during the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis, we conducted an in-depth analysis focusing on major splicing events with pSAR ≥ 50%. We applied k-means clustering to categorize these events into four distinct clusters (C1 to C4), revealing different splicing patterns across healthy, MASLD, and cirrhosis groups (Fig. 2A and B). The transition from a healthy state to MASLD and cirrhosis was marked by irreversible changes in splicing patterns. Notably, in clusters C1 and C2, the splicing changes in MASLD closely resembled those observed in cirrhosis, suggesting that significant splicing regulatory abnormalities may arise early in MASLD, potentially driving disease progression.

In cluster C2, splicing ratio values decreased during the transition from health to disease. Genes associated with these splicing events were enriched in biological pathways positively regulating EMT (Fig. 2A). EMT plays a central role in the progression from MASLD to cirrhosis by promoting liver fibrosis, affecting liver regeneration, and participating in local inflammatory responses [20]. Box plots illustrated changes in alternative splicing events of key genes (e.g., CTNNB1, TNXB, PTK2, TGFB1I1) involved in EMT-related pathways, showing a decrease in splicing ratios as the disease progressed (Fig. 2C). For instance, the splicing event in the PTK2 gene demonstrated a trend toward exon skipping with disease advancement (Fig. 2D). The reads distribution plot for TGFB1I1 shows a gradual decrease in the proportion of intron retention splicing events as the disease progresses (fig. S2 A).

In cluster C1, splicing ratio values showed an upward trend. Genes associated with these splicing events were enriched in pathways related to the DNA damage response (DDR). Heatmaps indicated that as the disease progressed, the splicing ratios of events in DDR pathway genes increased (Fig. 2E and S2B). For example, the KAT5 gene showed an increased retention of middle exons as the disease advanced (fig. S2 C). These findings reveal significant stage-related changes in alternative splicing patterns during MASLD progression to cirrhosis, providing new perspectives for understanding the molecular mechanisms of disease development and generating testable biomarker hypotheses for early diagnosis and treatment.

Association of immune-Related splicing events with immune microenvironment alterations

To assess the significant role of the immune microenvironment in MASLD progression to cirrhosis, we used CIBERSORT deconvolution to calculate immune cell proportions and analyzed their interplay with alternative splicing regulation. Significant alterations in the relative proportions of immune cells were observed as the disease progressed (Fig. 3A and S3A). Specifically, there was an increase in B and T cells, a rise in M0 and M1 macrophages, a gradual decrease in M2 macrophages, and a reduction in mast cells. These changes indicate enhanced immune activity and accumulation of cellular stroma as the disease advanced (Fig. 3B).

We focused on the alternative splicing events of immune-related genes and displayed their splicing ratios using a heatmap (Fig. 3C). These splicing events showed distinct pattern changes corresponding to disease progression. Correlation analysis identified 12 alternative splicing events that were significantly associated with alterations in immune cell proportions (Fig. 3D, Table S2). Notably, a substantial number of these splicing events were strongly associated with macrophages, particularly M0 and M2 subtypes. These results suggest a significant role of splicing in macrophage activation and polarization during disease progression. For instance, previous studies have demonstrated that PTK2 is linked to infiltration of M2 polarized macrophages [21]. Additionally, regulatory T cells (Tregs) also exhibit strong correlations with specific splicing events. In conclusion, this analysis suggests that alternative splicing may have an impact on modulating the immune microenvironment during the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis, indicating potential molecular targets for future research into early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

Construction of a Co-Regulatory network of RBPs and splicing events

PCA based on all expressed genes showed that the normal and MASLD groups were distinct yet similar in gene expression levels, while the cirrhosis group exhibited significant differences and greater heterogeneity (fig. S4A). DEG analysis indicated that MASLD had fewer differential genes compared to healthy individuals, whereas cirrhosis patients displayed dramatic transcriptional differences (fig. S4B). As the disease progressed, genes related to extracellular matrix pathways were upregulated, while those involved in metabolic pathways were downregulated, aligning with existing literature (fig. S4C).

Given that RBPs play crucial roles in liver disease development and are key regulators of alternative splicing, we analyzed their expression patterns. Heatmap analysis revealed a sharp upregulation of numerous RBP genes at the cirrhosis stage, while expression changes between MASLD and normal conditions were less pronounced. However, a subset of RBPs in MASLD exhibited transcriptional changes similar to those in cirrhosis. Specifically, five RBPs (MOV10L1, SPTBN5, MKI67, NPM2, ZMAT3) were consistently upregulated or downregulated in both MASLD and cirrhosis compared to healthy controls (fig. S4D-E). Additionally, some MASLD patients exhibited significant transcriptional changes, which may suggest variability in disease progression or distinct subtypes of MASLD that are more closely aligned with the gene expression profiles seen in cirrhosis (Fig. 4A).

We constructed an RBP-RAS interaction network through Pearson correlation analysis, correlating the expression levels of differentially expressed RBPs with the splicing ratios of RAS events (Fig. 4B, Table S3). The RBP-RAS pairs in the network are supported by binding evidence in the RNAinter database, suggesting a potential association with alternative splicing. Notably, several RBP genes related to EMT and immunity, such as PCBP1, CELF5, ESRP1, U2AF1, VIM, IGF2BP2, and CELF2, were significantly upregulated during MASLD and cirrhosis progression (Fig. 4C). The upregulation of these RBPs may be associated with splicing events related to immunity and EMT. This co-regulatory network highlights potential mechanisms by which RBPs influence MASLD progression to cirrhosis through alternative splicing regulation, offering new insights into the molecular underpinnings of liver disease and potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to address the critical gap in understanding how alternative splicing and RBPs contribute to the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis. Our hypothesis was that dysregulated splicing events, driven by altered RBP activity, play a crucial role in modulating key pathways accelerating disease progression. One of the most significant findings from our study is the identification of distinct alternative splicing patterns already present in MASLD but becoming more pronounced in cirrhosis. These early splicing changes, particularly those linked to EMT, DDR, macrophage polarization, and Treg cell function, may prove useful as candidate progression markers, pending validation in larger cohorts. Additionally, the observed upregulation of RBPs at the cirrhosis stage suggests that these RBPs could be potential modulators of these splicing events. We have created an illustration of the main findings of this study (Fig. 5). Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MASLD progression and highlight the potential of targeting splicing pathways, including EMT- and DDR-related splicing, for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention, offering a promising direction for future liver disease research.

Schematic Representation of Aberrant Alternative Splicing and Its Impact on Liver Disease Progression. AThe transition from healthy liver to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (MASLD) and cirrhosis is accompanied by significant changes in RNA splicing. Aberrant alternative splicing events are regulated by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), leading to alterations in key biological pathways. These include the regulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), the DNA damage response, and the immune microenvironment, all of which contribute to disease progression

The early onset of splicing dysregulation observed in MASLD, with splicing patterns closely mirroring those in cirrhosis, suggests that splicing alterations may occur earlier in the disease trajectory than previously recognized. This finding corroborates previous studies documenting transcriptomic shifts during MASLD progression [22, 23]. Notably, key splicing events in CTNNB1 and PTK2, which are critical for EMT, reinforce the hypothesis that aberrant splicing contributes to fibrosis and impaired liver regeneration [24, 25]. The pronounced increase in DDR-related splicing events further implies a role in promoting genomic instability, consistent with studies linking DDR dysfunction to liver disease [26, 27]. The limited overlap in AS events across disease stages suggests dynamic reprogramming of the splicing machinery as MASLD advances, likely driven by alterations in the activity or expression of RBPs [28]. From a clinical perspective, identifying these early splicing signatures could enable risk stratification of MASLD patients and facilitate timely interventions before the onset of advanced cirrhosis. By monitoring specific alternative splicing events, clinicians may be able to pinpoint individuals at higher risk for progressive fibrosis and develop strategies to either slow or halt the disease course. In particular, splicing events that mirror cirrhotic changes at the MASLD stage may serve as early warning signals for more severe disease. Likewise, the discovery of RBP networks implicated in regulating these splicing alterations underscores their therapeutic potential.

Our analysis of immune-related AS events revealed significant changes coinciding with alterations in the immune microenvironment during the progression from MASLD to cirrhosis. The observed increase in immune cell populations, such as macrophages, alongside corresponding splicing events in genes like PTK2, suggests that splicing plays a critical role in modulating immune responses, thereby contributing to inflammation and fibrosis. Previous studies have demonstrated PTK2’s involvement in immune cell infiltration and activation [29, 30], further supporting our observation that splicing regulation in immune-related genes is pivotal to disease progression. Notably, the correlation between specific splicing events and the proportions of M1 and M2 macrophages indicates that alternative splicing may drive immune cell plasticity within the liver. This finding aligns with recent research suggesting that immune cell reprogramming through splicing represents a novel mechanism in chronic liver diseases [24, 31]. These results highlight the potential of targeting immune-related splicing events as a therapeutic strategy, especially as immune modulation becomes an increasingly prominent focus in MASLD treatment.

The construction of a co-regulatory network between RBPs and AS events provides valuable insights into the post-transcriptional regulation of MASLD progression. The upregulation of RBPs such as CELF5 and U2AF1 in both MASLD and cirrhosis suggests that these proteins play critical roles in maintaining or disrupting splicing homeostasis during disease development. This observation aligns with previous research underscoring the pivotal role of RBPs in regulating splicing dynamics in liver diseases [25, 28]. Our results indicate that RBP dysregulation may directly contribute to the increased heterogeneity in splicing events observed in cirrhosis. Specifically, RBPs linked to EMT and immune modulation, such as IGF2BP2, CELF5, and ESRP1, are likely influencing key pathways involved in fibrosis and inflammation [32, 33]. Interestingly, we observed that certain RBPs, typically associated with the later stages of liver disease, exhibited altered expression even in the MASLD stage, suggesting their potential as early markers for disease progression.

This work has several limitations. First, analyses were performed on a modest, cross-sectional cohort (n = 29) without external or experimental validation, so conclusions are exploratory. Second, per-sample histology and full phenotypic annotation were incomplete (e.g., steatosis, inflammation, ballooning, fibrosis stage; sex/age/BMI/diabetes), precluding covariate-adjusted models and limiting stage-specific interpretation. Third, the cirrhosis group differs in aetiology (predominantly alcoholic liver disease/treated HBV), so MASLD–cirrhosis contrasts should not be read as a single MASLD trajectory. Fourth, immune composition was inferred from bulk RNA-seq with CIBERSORT, which conflates tissue-resident and infiltrating cells; accordingly, RBP–RAS links are correlative and cell-type–specific regulation remains untested. Finally, we did not perform wet-lab confirmation of splicing events or RBP binding. Future studies will replicate these findings in MASLD-only cohorts with sample-level histology and standardized metabolic profiling, apply covariate-aware models, and incorporate orthogonal validation—immunohistochemistry/flow cytometry, single-cell or spatial transcriptomics, CLIP/eCLIP, RBP perturbation and minigene assays—to establish mechanism and clinical relevance.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the significant role of alternative splicing and RBPs in the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis. These splicing shifts, particularly in DDR- and EMT-associated transcripts, may reflect early molecular alterations in MASLD and therefore warrant further investigation as candidate progression markers. The identification of immune-related splicing events and their correlation with changes in immune cell populations suggest new avenues for therapeutic intervention. By elucidating the molecular underpinnings of MASLD at the post-transcriptional level, this study provides a foundation for the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies targeting splicing regulators to mitigate disease progression.

Materials and methods

Data retrieval

RNA-sequencing data were retrieved from the SRA (accession ERP146053) originating from Albrechtsen et al. [34] and comprise 29 liver biopsies: healthy controls (n = 10), NAFLD (now termed MASLD) (n = 9), and cirrhosis with hepatic steatosis (n = 10). Clinical annotations from the primary report [35] indicate: healthy individuals had normal liver histology with < 5% fat and no fibrosis (mean age 25 ± 1 y; BMI 23 ± 0 kg/m²); the MASLD group had mainly no or mild fibrosis with one patient at stage F3 (median fibrosis stage 0, IQR 0–3; median NAS 2, IQR 1–4; mean age 50 ± 5 y; BMI 35 ± 2 kg/m²; 7/9 obese; no T2D); and the cirrhosis group had etiologies alcoholic liver disease (n = 9) or hepatitis B (n = 1, on entecavir with undetectable viral load), with Child–Pugh A/B = 6/4, mean HVPG 11 ± 2 mmHg (4 with HVPG > 10 mmHg), mean age 62 ± 3 y, BMI 32 ± 2 kg/m² (6/10 obese). The SRA Run files were converted to FASTQ format using the NCBI SRA Toolkit’s fastq-dump utility.

RNA-seq data processing

FastQC showed the reads were already adapter-trimmed, so we ran fastp (v0. 23.4) [36] with adapter trimming off, removing only low-quality/N-rich reads. After quality filtering, the cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner (version 2.7.10b) [37]. Uniquely mapped reads were used to calculate the read counts and normalized expression levels, measured as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM) for each gene.

Alternative splicing analysis with SUVA

We identified and quantified alternative splicing events (including regulated alternative splicing events, RAS) across healthy controls, MASLD, and cirrhosis samples using the SUVA pipeline, following the protocol described in [19]. When running SUVA (version 2.0), we made minor modifications to the default settings to minimize false positives. For differential splicing, between-group splicing ratio values were compared with a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Specifically, a p-value threshold of 0.01 was applied to determine significantly different splicing events, and we employed the following filtering criteria to reduce false positive alternative splicing events:

SUVA identifies five primary alternative splicing event types—alt5p, alt3p, contain, olp, and ir. For each SUVA-identified AS event, two principal splicing junctions are typically involved, except in the case of ir events, which involves one junction and two boundary regions. We calculated the splicing ratio by dividing the read count of the “short” splicing junction by the total reads spanning both junctions. In ir events, the “short” junction corresponds to the intron-retaining boundaries, whereas the other junction reflects exon-exon splicing.

For each AS event, SUVA computes both the difference in splicing ratios between conditions and the proportion of splice site usage (pSAR). The pSAR is calculated as the ratio of reads supporting the specific alternative splicing event to the total reads spanning the splice site, which includes all splicing junctions and boundary reads. This metric reflects the dominance of the AS event at a given splice site. To ensure sufficient read support and prioritize biologically significant events, we retained RAS events with pSAR ≥ 50% for subsequent analyses. Each SUVA-identified event can be mapped to one or more “classical splicing types,” including mutually exclusive 3’ UTRs (3pMXE), mutually exclusive 5’ UTRs (5pMXE), cassette exon (cassetteExon), exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exon (MXE), A5SS&ES, A3SS&ES, and intron retention (IR).

K-means clustering analysis

To explore dynamic changes in splicing patterns, we focused on major RAS with pSAR ≥ 50% and compiled their splicing ratios across all samples. Each RAS event was thus represented by a vector of splicing ratio values. We then performed k-means clustering (using the kmeans function in R) with k = 4 clusters, chosen based on the “elbow” method that indicated four as an optimal balance between cluster resolution and interpretability. The initialization step was repeated 100 iterations to ensure stable and reproducible cluster assignments. The Euclidean distance metric was employed to measure dissimilarities among events, and the algorithm aimed to minimize the total within-cluster sum of squares. Ultimately, the resulting four clusters (C1–C4) enabled us to identify distinct splicing patterns and assess how these patterns progressed from healthy controls through MASLD to cirrhosis.

Immune cell quantification

Immune-cell proportions were de-convoluted with CIBERSORT algorithm (version 1.03) [38] and the LM22 signature matrix. FPKM expression values were supplied as input, quantile normalization was disabled (the recommended setting for RNA-seq data), and 1,000 permutations were run to derive P-values. The output comprised relative abundances for 22 immune phenotypes, encompassing seven T cell subsets (CD8 T cells, naïve CD4 T cells, memory CD4 resting T cells, memory CD4 activated T cells, T follicular helper cells, and regulatory T cells [Tregs]), naïve and memory B cells, plasma cells, resting and activated NK cells, monocytes, macrophages M0, M1, and M2, resting and activated dendritic cells, resting and activated mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils.

Co-expression analysis of immune cell fractions and immune-related RAS splicing ratios

We retrieved 1,793 immune-related genes from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.org/shared/genelists). Alternative splicing events located within these immune-related genes (immune-RAS) were identified and analyzed. A co-disturbed network was constructed to explore the relationship between immune cell fractions and the splicing ratios of immune-RAS, retaining correlations with |Pearson’s r| ≥ 0.6 and p-value ≤ 0.05.

Identification of differentially expressed RNA-binding proteins (RBPs)

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [39] on raw count data to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). We applied the default Wald test in DESeq2 for differential gene expression analysis, which includes a built - in Benjamini - Hochberg (BH) adjustment to control the false discovery rate. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05. From the List of DEGs, differentially expressed RBPs were isolated based on a catalog of 2,141 mouse RNA-binding proteins retrieved from prior studies [40–43].

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional categorization of genes was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.6.2) [44], which facilitated the identification of enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways.

Co-expression analysis of RAS splicing ratios and DE RBP expression

We constructed a co-disturbed network to assess the relationship between the expression levels of DE RBPs and the splicing ratios of RAS events, retaining interactions with |Pearson’s r| ≥ 0.85 and p-value ≤ 0.01.

Additional analyses

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the factoextra package in R (https://cloud.r-project.org/package=factoextra) to visualize sample clustering based on the first two principal components. After normalizing read counts using TPM (Tags Per Million) for each gene, custom scripts were employed for visualization of next-generation sequencing data and genomic annotations. Clustering based on Euclidean distance was performed using the pheatmap package in R (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html).
