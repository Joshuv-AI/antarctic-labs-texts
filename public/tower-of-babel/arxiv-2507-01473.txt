Nonparametric learning of heterogeneous graphical model on network-linked data
Yuwen Wang, Changyu Liu, Xin He, Junhui Wang, [10pt] Department of StatisticsChinese University of Hong Kong

Abstract
Graphical models have been popularly used for capturing conditional independence structure in multivariate data, which are often built upon independent and identically distributed observations, limiting their applicability to complex datasets such as network-linked data. This paper proposes a nonparametric graphical model that addresses these limitations by accommodating heterogeneous graph structures without imposing any specific distributional assumptions. The proposed estimation method effectively integrates network embedding with nonparametric graphical model estimation. It further transforms the graph learning task into solving a finite-dimensional linear equation system by leveraging the properties of vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Moreover, theoretical guarantees are established for the proposed method in terms of the estimation consistency and exact recovery of the heterogeneous graph structures. Its effectiveness is also demonstrated through a variety of simulated examples and a real application to the statistician coauthorship dataset.



Keywords:
Conditional independence, Gaussian graphical model, heterogeneous data, network model, score matching

1
Introduction

Graphical models provide a powerful framework for modeling conditional independence structures in multivariate data and have attracted increasing attention in both academia and industry
[
Lauritzen 1996
;
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
;
Yuan and Lin 2007
;
Friedman et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2009
;
Li et al. 2020
]
. Although success has been widely reported, most existing graphical models heavily rely on the assumption of independent and identically distributed observations, and thus are not applicable to analyze datasets with heterogeneous distribution or dependent samples, such as network-linked data.

Network-linked data has been commonly encountered in modern real-world applications, where the network connects observations that may exhibit dependence structure and heterogeneous distributions. To address the non-tensor structure in such datasets, various statistical models for network-linked data have been developed under specific scenarios, such as classification
[
Tang et al. 2013
]
or regression
[
Li et al. 2019
;
Le and Li 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
]
. In particular,
Li et al. 2020
introduced a network-linked Gaussian graphical model that accommodates heterogeneous mean vectors while assuming a common graph structure across all nodes. Another related line of research is the time-varying Gaussian graphical model
[
Zhou et al. 2010
;
Kolar and Xing 2011
;
Lu et al. 2018
]
, where the timeline can be regarded as a simple chain network and the graph structures are allowed to evolve over each time node. It is important to note that most of the aforementioned graphical models are built upon the Gaussian assumption, and the graph structure is then fully captured by its precision matrix. Although simple in estimation, verifying the Gaussian assumption can be challenging in many medium-scale real applications.

This paper proposes an efficient method to learn a nonparametric graphical model that accommodates heterogeneous graph structures over each node without imposing any restrictive distributional assumptions. The proposed method estimates the node-wise heterogeneous graph models as functions of the embedding vectors of the newtork in a nonparametric fashion, where the nonparametric graphical model is motivated by the equivalence between conditional independence and the zero pattern of the second-order derivative of the log density. We then estimate the derivative function via score matching
[
Hyvärinen 2005
;
Zhou et al. 2020
]
in a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS;
Kimeldorf and Wahba 1971
) induced by a new decomposable kernel function. By leveraging the representer theorem and the derivative reproducing property, the proposed method leads to a closed-form solution, where the representer coefficients are derived by solving a linear equation system, enabling efficient estimation of large-scale heterogeneous graphical models.

We also conduct rigorous theoretical analysis that establishes the consistency of the proposed graphical model in exactly recovering the heterogeneous graph structures without imposing any explicit distributional assumptions. The analysis involves a careful study of the vector-valued RKHS and requires some novel theoretical treatments on the Hilbert-Schmidt operators. We also provide some theoretical examples, to showcase that all required assumptions are verified and the non-asymptotic convergence rate of the proposed method can be established. The effectiveness of the proposed method is further supported by numerical comparison against some existing competitors in several simulated examples as well as a real application to the statistician coauthorship dataset.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the nonparametric heterogeneous graphical model on network-linked data. Section
3
introduces an efficient two-step learning algorithm. Theoretical guarantees, including estimation consistency and exact graph recovery for the proposed graphical model, are presented in Section
4
. Section
5
presents simulation studies and a real application to the statistician coauthorship dataset. Section
6
gives some final concluding remarks. Proofs of the main theoretical results are included in the Appendix, and other additional technical details, including auxiliary Lemmas S1–S11, are available in the online Supplementary Material.

2
Network-linked heterogeneous graphical model

Suppose a training sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is observed, each with a different probability density function
p_{0}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
on a compact subset
\mathcal{X}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The conditional independence structure among the variables in
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{id})^{\top}
can be depicted as
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{(i)})
, where
\mathcal{V}=[d]
represents the common node set and
\mathcal{E}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
denotes edge set that may vary from node to node. Specifically, for any given node
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle(j,l)\notin{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ X_{ij}\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{il}\ |\ \mathbf{X}_{i,-\{j,l\}},

(1)

where
\mathbf{X}_{i,-\{j,l\}}=\big\{X_{ik}:k\in{\cal V}\backslash\{j,l\}\big\}
consists of all the random variables in
\mathbf{X}_{i}
except for
X_{ij}
and
X_{il}
.

Suppose further that
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linked via an undirected network with an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}=\big(A_{ij}\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, where
\mathbf{P}=\big(p_{ij}\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}\in(0,1)^{n\times n}
with
p_{ij}=P\left(A_{ij}=1\right)
. To leverage the network structure, we introduce a latent network embedding space, where each node
i
is embedded with a latent vector
\bm{\beta}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
\mathcal{X}_{m}
being a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We consider an integrated model,

\sigma(p_{ij})=g(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})\;\text{ and }\;p_{0}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})=p_{0}\left(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i}\right),

(2)

for any
i,j\in[n]
, where
\sigma(\cdot)
is a link function, and
g:\mathcal{X}_{m}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

This model renders both the latent structure of
p_{ij}
and the smoothness of
p_{0}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
via the latent network embedding vectors
\mathbf{B}=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{n})^{\top}
. The structure information about each node is essentially captured in the
\bm{\beta}_{i}
’s, and the smoothness of
p_{0}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
over the discrete network can be transformed into the well-defined smoothness over the continuous
\bm{\beta}_{i}
’s. Furthermore, many network models can be adopted in (
2
), including the latent space model
[
Hoff et al. 2002
]
with
\sigma(x)=\operatorname{logit}(x)
and
g(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})=\|\bm{\beta}_{i}-\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
, the network embedding model
[
Zhang et al. 2022
]
with
\sigma(x)=\operatorname{logit}(x)
and
g(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})=\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{j}
, the random dot product graph model
[
Young and Scheinerman 2007
]
with
\sigma(x)=x
and
g(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})=\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{j}
, and the kernel-based latent space model
[
Tang et al. 2013
]
with
\sigma(x)=x
and
g(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})=k(\bm{\beta}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{j})
for some kernel function
k
.

Note that the conditional independence in (
1
) can be fully captured by the second-order partial derivative of
\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
with respect to
\mathbf{x}

[
Dawid 1979
]
.
More specifically,
(j,l)\notin\mathcal{E}^{(i)}
if and only if
\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i})=0
almost surely, where
\partial_{j}
denotes the partial derivative with respect to the
j
-th coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
. Therefore, to estimate the heterogeneous graph structure
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
for each node
i
, it suffices to recover the zeros patterns of
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}=\{\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}_{jl}\}_{j,l=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with

\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}_{jl}=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{d}}\Big(\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i})\Big)^{2}p_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x},

(3)

where
p_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{k})
is the integrated marginal density of
\mathbf{x}
. Denote the Stein’s score function of
p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
as
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})=\big(\partial_{1}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}),\ldots,\partial_{d}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})\big)^{\top}
and thus
\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})=\partial_{j}\{\mathbf{e}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})\}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{l}
denotes the
l
-th natural basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It then suffices to estimate
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
and its partial derivatives.

3
Estimation

Estimation of
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
involves three steps, first the estimated network embedding vectors
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}=(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n})^{\top}
, then the estimated score function
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
in a vector-valued RKHS, and finally the estimated heterogeneity edge sets
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}
.

3.1
Vector-valued RKHS

We consider a specified vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
induced by the following kernel function,

K\big((\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta}),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\big)=k_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\mathbf{I}_{d},

(4)

where
k_{1}:\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
k_{2}:\mathcal{X}_{m}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote two universal scalar kernels, and
\mathbf{I}_{d}
is a
d
-dimensional identity matrix. Clearly,
\mathcal{H}_{K}
consists of functions mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and can be decomposed into a tensor product of two separated RKHSs for
\mathbf{x}
and
\bm{\beta}
, and thus allows us to separate their effects in estimating the node-wise heterogeneous graphical model.

Lemma
1
lays out some properties of this specific
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Other details about the general vector-valued RKHS are deferred to the Supplementary Material.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}
denote the RKHS induced by the kernel function
K
in (
4
), then it satisfies that

(a)

\mathcal{H}_{K}=(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})\times(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})\times\dots\times(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are the RKHSs induced by
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, respectively,
\times
denotes the Cartesian product, and
\otimes
denotes the tensor product.

(b)

\mathcal{H}_{K}
is dense in the set consisting of all continuous functions mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma
1
(a) characterizes the structure of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
as a Cartesian product of two separate RKHSs, one for latent vector
\bm{\beta}
and the other for
\mathbf{x}
. This structure implies that for any fixed
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}
, a function
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
is mapped to an element
\mathbf{s}(\cdot;\bm{\beta})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}
is an RKHS induced by the kernel
k_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathbf{I}_{d}
.
A detailed verification of this claim is provided in Lemma S5.
As a result, the distance between any two functions
\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
, evaluated at any specific
\bm{\beta}_{1}
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}
, can be measured by
\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(\cdot;\bm{\beta}_{1})-\mathbf{s}_{2}(\cdot;\bm{\beta}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}}
is the associated RKHS norm in
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}
.
Lemma
1
(b) further establishes the universality of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, asserting that any continuous function from
\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be arbitrarily well approximated by
\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

3.2
Efficient algorithm

We now develop an efficient algorithm for estimating the heterogeneous
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
’s in the vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. The first step of
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
can be obtained by employing any consistent network estimation method, such as
Hoff et al. 2002
,
Ma et al. 2020
, and
Zhang et al. 2022
. Next,
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
can be estimated by minimizing the integrated Fisher divergence that

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\mathbf{s}\in\cal\mathcal{H}_{K}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}_{i}}\big\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X};\bm{\beta}_{i})-\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{X};\bm{\beta}_{i})\big\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}_{i}}
denotes the expectation with respect to
\mathbf{X}
under
p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i})
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})\rightarrow 0
as
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
for any
\bm{\beta}
, (
5
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}J(\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}_{i}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X};\bm{\beta}_{i})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X};\bm{\beta}_{i})\big)\Big].

Lemma
2
shows that the minimization task in (
5
) enjoys the same property of standard score matching
[
Hyvärinen 2005
;
Zhou et al. 2020
]
, where the unknown
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
can be integrated out in the objective function without compromising its global minimizer. With
J(\mathbf{s})
replaced by its empirical counterpart,
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
can be estimated by

\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})\big)\Big)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{s}\|_{K}^{2},

(6)

where
\|\cdot\|_{K}
is the associated RKHS norm in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. For any
(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})\in\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
j\in[d]
, let
K_{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})}^{*}:\mathcal{H}_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the operator satisfying
K_{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})}^{*}\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
, let
K_{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})}
denote its adjoint operator, and let
\partial_{j}K_{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{K}
denote a bounded linear operator that
(\partial_{j}K_{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{e})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})=\partial_{x_{j}}K((\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}),(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta}))\mathbf{e}
for any
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The properties of vector-valued RKHS ensure the existence of these operators; see Section S3 in the Supplementary Material. The optimization task in (
6
) can be substantially simplified by leveraging the representer theorem
[
Kimeldorf and Wahba 1971
]
.

Lemma 3
.

The solution to (
6
) must satisfy that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{(i-1)d+j}K_{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})}\mathbf{e}_{j}-\frac{1}{\lambda n}\partial_{j}K_{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})}\mathbf{e}_{j},

where
\widehat{\bm{\bm{\beta}}}=\{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{(i-1)d+j}\}_{i,j=1}^{n,d}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
denotes the estimated representer coefficients. Moreover,
\widehat{\bm{\bm{\beta}}}
must satisfy the following linear equation system

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{F}+n\lambda{\mathbf{I}}_{nd}\big){\widehat{\bm{\bm{\beta}}}}=\frac{\mathbf{h}}{\lambda},

(7)

where
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
with
F_{(i-1)d+j,\ (i^{\prime}-1)d+j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}K\big((\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i}),(\mathbf{x}_{i^{\prime}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i^{\prime}})\big)\mathbf{e}_{j^{\prime}}
for
i,i^{\prime}=1,\dots,n
,
j,j^{\prime}=1,\dots,d
, and
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
with
h_{(i-1)d+j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j^{\prime}=1}^{d}\partial_{j^{\prime}}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}K\big((\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i}),(\mathbf{x}_{i^{\prime}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{i^{\prime}})\big)\mathbf{e}_{j^{\prime}}
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
j=1,\dots,d
.

Lemma
3
adapts the representer theorem for obtaining
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
in the vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, whose representer coefficients are obtained by solving the linear equation system in (
7
). This greatly facilitates efficient estimation of
\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
even for large-scale heterogeneity graphs. When the size of (
7
) is too large, stochastic gradient descent can also be adopted to alleviate the computational burden further.

Once
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}
is obtained, estimation of
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}_{jl}
can be directly computed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}_{jl}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\partial_{j}\{\mathbf{e}_{l}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k};\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})\}\right)^{2},

and the estimated edge set becomes
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}=\{(j,l):\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}_{jl}\geq\delta_{n}\}

with some pre-specified thresholding value
\delta_{n}
.

4
Theoretical guarantees

This section quantifies the asymptotic behavior of the proposed method in estimating the heterogeneous
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
’s, assuring that the true
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
’s can be exactly recovered with high probability.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
is a consistent estimate of
\mathbf{B}
in that
\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2,\infty}=o_{p}(1)
.

Assumption
1
assures that every latent network embedding vector in
\mathbf{B}
is well estimated, which is satisfied by many existing network estimation methods, such as
Lyzinski et al. 2014
and
Li et al. 2023
.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose
k_{1}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{d}
, and
k_{2}
is fourth-order continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{X}_{m}\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
. Moreover, there exist some positive constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},\kappa_{4},\kappa_{5},\kappa_{6}
such that
\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}{\sup}\ k_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\leq\kappa_{1}
,
\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}}{\sup}k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta})\leq\kappa_{2}
,
\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}{\sup}\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{{x}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}}k_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{3}
, for all
j\in[d]
,
\underset{\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}}{\sup}\partial_{\beta_{l}}\partial_{\beta_{l}^{\prime}}k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{4}
,
\underset{\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}}{\sup}\partial_{\beta_{r}}\partial_{\beta_{l}}\partial_{\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\partial_{\beta_{l}^{\prime}}k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{5}
, and
\underset{\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{m}}{\sup}\partial_{\beta_{l}}k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{6}
for all
l,r\in[m]
.

The differentiability conditions on
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
guarantee that all functions in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
are at least twice differentiable with respect to
\mathbf{x}
and fourth-order differentiable with respect to
\bm{\beta}
, which is commonly assumed in the literature
[
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
;
Sriperumbudur et al. 2017
]
. A stricter smoothness condition on
k_{2}
is necessary to control the effect of estimation error in
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
on the estimation accuracy of
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}
.
Assumption
2
also requires that the kernel functions in (
4
) and their corresponding gradients are bounded, a condition commonly adopted in the kernel methods literature
[
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
]
. Assumption
2
is satisfied by many commonly used kernels, such as the Gaussian and Laplacian kernels.

Assumption 3
.

Let
C_{\mathbf{B}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}_{i}}\big[K_{(\mathbf{X},\bm{\beta}_{i})}K^{*}_{(\mathbf{X},\bm{\beta}_{i})}\big]
, then
C_{\mathbf{B}}
converges in operator norm to a compact, self-adjoint, and positive semi-definite operator
C
as
n
diverges. Furthermore,
P_{\mathbf{B}}(I-P)\xrightarrow{s}0
, where
P_{\mathbf{B}}
and
P
are projections to
\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(C_{\mathbf{B}})}
and
\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(C)}
, respectively, and
\xrightarrow{s}
denotes the convergence of strong operator topology.

The operator
C_{\mathbf{B}}
can be regarded as a covariance-type operator in RKHS, whose convergence is then analogous to the widely adopted convergence assumption on the sample covariance matrices in literature
[
Zhao and Yu 2006
;
Zou 2006
;
Li et al. 2023
]
. Strong convergence of the projection
P_{\mathbf{B}}
is required to establish
\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim\sup}\|P_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}\leq\|P\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}
, which controls the performance of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
on
\mathbf{B}
and is considerably weaker than some commonly used assumptions, such as
\mathbf{s}_{0}\in\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(C)}

[
Sriperumbudur et al. 2017
]
. More importantly, Assumption
3
is satisfied by many existing network models, including the stochastic block model (SBM) and the random dot product graph model (RDPG).

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
3
are met, then there exists a sequence
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
, such that

\sup_{i\in[n]}\big\|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda_{n}}(\cdot;\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})-\mathbf{s}_{0}(\cdot;\bm{\beta}_{i})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}}=o_{p}(1).

Theorem
1
establishes the uniform consistency of
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda_{n}}
in estimating
\mathbf{s}_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}
across all latent positions. A global convergence rate of
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}
in terms of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda}-\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}
can also be established under some additional smoothness assumptions on
\bm{\beta}
, as illustrated in the theoretical example in Section S2.2 of the Supplementary Material.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose all the assumptions in Theorem
1
are met. Then, it holds true that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\big\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}\big\|_{\max}=o_{p}(1).

Theorem
2
further establishes the strong consistency in estimating
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}
across all nodes, which plays a crucial role in establishing the exact recovery of the heterogeneous graph structure. The strong consistency is established in terms of the matrix max norm, which is in contrast to the Frobenius norm or the spectral norm in most existing results
[
Zhou et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2020
]
. Moreover, Theorem
2
immediately implies that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{i\in[n]}\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}\|_{\max}\leq b_{n}\Big)\geq 1-a_{n},

for some non-negative diminishing sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, which may vary with the smoothness and convergence properties in Assumption
3
. A detailed verification is provided in Lemma S10.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose all the assumptions in Theorem
1
are met, and
\underset{(j,l)\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}{\inf}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}_{jl}>2b_{n}
for all
i\in[n]
. With
\delta_{n}=b_{n}
, it holds true that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}=\mathcal{E}^{(i)}\text{ for all }i\in[n]\Big)\geq 1-2a_{n}.

Theorem
3
ensures that the heterogeneous graph structure can be exactly recovered uniformly over all nodes. It is also important to remark that Theorem
3
holds true without requiring any explicit distributional assumptions, which is in sharp contrast to most existing distribution-based methods
[
Li et al. 2020
]
. In fact, non-asymptotic versions of Theorems
1
–
3
can also be established under some relaxed conditions. This point is further illustrated through two examples, the SBM model in Section
4.1
and the RDPG model in Section S2.2 of the Supplementary Material.

4.1
A theoretical example

We now present a theoretical example to illustrate the established results in Theorems 1-3. Suppose
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are observed with a network structure
\mathbf{A}
that follows an SBM model with
m
communities, parameterized as
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
is a membership matrix satisfying
\mathbf{Z}{\bf 1}_{m}={\bf 1}_{n}
and
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is symmetric and nonsingular. Here,
{\bf 1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector with all elements equal to
1
. Assume that the
m
communities are asymptotically balanced, and thus the proportion of nodes in the
j
-th community converges to a constant
\pi_{j}^{*}
for any
j\in[m]
, with
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}^{*}=1
. Define
\Delta=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\pi_{1}^{*}},\dots,\sqrt{\pi_{m}^{*}})
, and assume
\Delta\mathbf{D}\Delta
is positive-definite with distinct eigenvalues. Let
\mathbf{B}=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
satisfy
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}
is diagonal, and the elements of
\bm{\beta}_{1}
are all positive. Furthermore, assume each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are generated from a probability density
p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i})
, whose heterogeneous graph structure is determined by the zero pattern of the corresponding
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}
as defined in (
3
).

We estimate
\mathbf{B}
using the adjacency spectral embedding
[
Athreya et al. 2018
]
, which conducts a singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
and keeps the first
m
scaled leading singular eigenvectors. The resulting estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
satisfies
\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2,\infty}=o_{p}(1)
, as detailed in Section S2.1 of the Supplementary Material, thereby verifying Assumption
1
.

Next, we estimate the score function
\mathbf{s}_{0}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{0}
in a vector-valued RKHS induced by
K=k_{1}k_{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}
, as defined in (
4
), where
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are Gaussian kernels. This ensures Assumption
2
is satisfied, while verifying Assumption
3
requires further analysis. Specifically, there exists an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{n}
such that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{n}
, leading to
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{Q}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{n}
, which is diagonal and denoted as
\bm{\Lambda}_{n}
. By the fact that

n^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{n}{\bm{\Lambda}}_{n}\mathbf{Q}_{n}^{\top}=n^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\rightarrow\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\Delta^{2}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}
and Lemma S9,
\mathbf{Q}_{n}
converges to an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}
composed of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\Delta^{2}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}
. Let
\bm{\beta}_{j}^{*}
denote the
j
-th row of
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}
and define
C=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{*}}[K_{(\mathbf{X},\bm{\beta}_{j}^{*})}K^{*}_{(\mathbf{X},\bm{\beta}_{j}^{*})}]
.
It follows from the fact that
n^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{n}-\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\leq n^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\|_{F}\|\mathbf{Q}_{n}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{Q}_{n}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0
and Lemma S8 that
C_{\mathbf{B}}
converges to
C
. Furthermore,
P_{\mathbf{B}}(I-P)\xrightarrow{s}0
is verified in
Section S2.1 of the Supplementary Material. Putting together, all assumptions are verified, and it then follows from Theorem
3
that the heterogeneous graph structure in
p_{0}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{i})
can be exactly recovered with probability tending to 1.

It is important to remark that Theorems
1
–
3
can be extended to their non-asymptotic counterparts. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\mathbf{B},0}
denote the projection of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
onto
\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(C_{\mathbf{B}})}
, and assume
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\mathbf{B},0}=C_{\mathbf{B}}g_{\mathbf{B}}
for some
g_{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
such that
\|g_{\mathbf{B}}\|_{K}\leq L
, where
L
is a constant independent of
\mathbf{B}
. For sufficiently large
n
and any constant
\tau_{1}>0
, by taking
\lambda_{n}=\max\big\{\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2,\infty},n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2}\big\}^{1/2}
, with probability at least
1-n^{-\tau_{1}}
, we have

\begin{split}\sup_{i\in[n]}\big\|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\lambda_{n}}(\cdot;\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{i})-\mathbf{s}_{0}(\cdot;\bm{\beta}_{i})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{d}}\leq\tau_{2}\max\big\{\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2,\infty},n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2}\big\}^{1/2},\end{split}

where
\tau_{2}=2\sqrt{m\kappa_{6}}(1+\|\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K})+(4\max\{1,\sqrt{\tau_{1}}\}+2L)\max\{c_{3},1\}\max\{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2},1\}\tau_{3}
, with

\tau_{3}=\max\{\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}c_{3}(1+\|\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}),4d\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}(\sqrt{\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}}+\|\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}),4d\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\}

and
c_{3}=\max\{d\sqrt{\kappa_{3}\kappa_{4}m},2\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\kappa_{2}\kappa_{4}m}\}
.
Set
b_{n}=\tau_{4}(\tau_{2}+1)\lambda_{n}
, where

\tau_{4}=\max\{8\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}\|\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K}^{2},\kappa_{3}(1+8\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\|\mathbf{s}_{0}\|_{K})\}
,
and assume that
\underset{(j,l)\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}{\inf}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(i)}_{jl}>2b_{n},
for all
i\in[n]
. With
\delta_{n}=b_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}=\mathcal{E}^{(i)},\text{ for all }i\in[n]\Big)\geq 1-2(d^{2}n^{-1}+n^{-\tau_{1}}).

More details and proof are provided in Section S2.1 of the Supplementary Material.

5
Numerical experiments

This section investigates the numerical evaluation of the proposed network-linked graphical model, denoted as NGM, and compares it against some existing competitors, including the Graphical lasso [Glasso;
Friedman et al. 2008
], the Gaussian graphical model with network cohesion [GNC;
Li et al. 2020
], and the Dynamic graphical model [DGM;
Yang and Peng 2020
]. We implemented NGM in Python, Glasso using the Python package
scikit-learn
, and DGM with the R package
loggle
. The code for GNC is available online. For NGM, we set
k_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\exp\{-\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\}\mathbf{I}_{d}
and
k_{2}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})=\exp\{-\sigma^{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\}\mathbf{I}_{m}
with
\sigma
set as the inverse of the median of all pairwise distances, and
\delta_{n}
and
\lambda
are tuned via 5-fold cross validation. For Glasso, NGM, and DGM, all the tuning parameters or thresholding values are selected via 5-fold cross validation.

The numerical evaluation of all the methods is assessed via multiple evaluation metrics, including the false positive rate (FPR), true positive rate (TPR), F1-score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and structural Hamming distance (SHD). Specifically, we denote a correct prediction of the presence or absence of an edge as true positive (TP) or true negative (TN), and a false prediction of the presence or absence of an edge as false positive (FP) or false negative (FN), respectively. Then, the evaluation metrics are computed as

\text{FPR}=\frac{\operatorname{FP}}{\operatorname{FP}+\operatorname{TN}},\text{TPR}=\frac{\operatorname{TP}}{\operatorname{TP}+\operatorname{FN}},

\text{F1-score}=\frac{2\operatorname{TP}}{2\operatorname{TP}+\operatorname{FP}+\operatorname{FN}}
, and

\operatorname{MCC}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}\times\mathrm{TN}-\mathrm{FP}\times\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}.

Moreover, SHD counts the number of edge insertions, deletions, or flips to transform one graph to the other, which is also divided by the total number of possible edges to be normalized to within
[0,1]
. Note that a good estimation is indicated with small values of FPR and SHD, or large values of TPR, F1-score, and MCC.

5.1
Simulated examples

Three simulated examples are considered, each with a different graph structure over a different network structure. The key components of the data generating scheme for each example are provided below, and more details are deferred to Section S1 in the Supplementary Material.

Example 1. (RDPG with Gaussian)
The data generating scheme follows that in
Li et al. 2020
, where the graph structure remains unchanged over all nodes. Particularly,
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{id})^{\top}
are sampled independently from
N(\bm{\mu}_{i},\bm{\Sigma})
for
i\in[n]
, with
\bm{\mu}_{i}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
to be generated. First,
\bm{\mu}_{i}
’s are generated over a random dot product graph
\mathbf{A}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}=a\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
. Here the elements of
\mathbf{B}
are drawn from a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, and
a
is a scaling factor ensuring
\|\mathbf{P}\|_{\max}\leq 1
. Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, whose columns are sampled randomly from the first five leading eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
, and let
\bm{\mu}_{i}
be the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{M}
. Next, we generate an Erdős-Rényi network
\mathbf{S}
with
d
nodes and a homogeneous connection probability
0.01
, and define
\bm{\Sigma}
such that
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}=b\big(0.3\mathbf{S}+(0.3|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S})|+0.1)I_{d}\big)
, where
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S})
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{S}
, and the scalar
b
is chosen so that all diagonal elements of
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
equal to
1
.

Example 2. (Dynamic graph with Butterfly)
The data generating scheme follows a dynamic graphical model
[
Zhou et al. 2010
;
Yang and Peng 2020
]
evolving over the time interval
[0,1]
. Particularly,
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{id})^{\top}
at the time point
t_{i}=\frac{i-1}{n}
is sampled from a dynamic butterfly process
[
Baptista et al. 2024
]
, where each pair
\big(X_{i,2j-1},X_{i,2j}\big)
is independently generated from
X_{i,2j-1}\sim N(0,1)
and
X_{i,2j}=X_{i,2j-1}*\text{Bernoulli}\big(p_{j}(t_{i})\big)+\big(1-\text{Bernoulli}(p_{j}(t_{i}))\big)*N(0,1)
. It can be verified that
\big(X_{i,2j-1},X_{i,2j}\big)
are conditionally dependent given all other variables if and only if
p_{j}(t_{i})\neq 0
, whereas all other pairs of variables are conditionally independent. This property leads to a dynamic bipartite graph, where the edge set at
t_{i}
is
\big\{(2j-1,2j):p_{j}(t_{i})\neq 0\mbox{ for any }j=1,\dots,d/2\big\}
, and then the dynamic graph structure can be determined by examining the zero pattern of
\mathbf{p}(t)=\big(p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{d/2}(t)\big)^{\top}
. We further divide the time interval
[0,1]
into five equal sub-intervals, and generate
\mathbf{p}(t)
such that its zero pattern remains unchanged within each sub-interval. At each sub-interval,
60\%
element of
\mathbf{p}(t)
is set to be non-zero, and the adjacent sub-interval shares
40\%
common elements. Note that this time-dependent sample can be structured as a chain network structure
\mathbf{A}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{P}=(p_{ij})
,
p_{ij}={1}_{\{|i-j|=1\}}
, and
\mathbf{B}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})^{\top}
.

Example 3. (Permutation graph with Laplacian)
In this example, we generate
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{id})^{\top}
from a chain Laplacian distribution associated with a network of permutations. Each node
i
in the network corresponds to a permutation
\bm{\sigma}_{i}=(\sigma_{i}(1),\dots,\sigma_{i}(d))^{\top}
. The expected value of adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, denoted as
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{P}
, has its
ij
-th entry
p_{ij}
representing the scaled Kendall’s Tau distance between
\bm{\sigma}_{i}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{j}
. For each node
i
,
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is generated as follows:
X_{i,\sigma_{i}(1)}
is sampled from a Laplace distribution with parameters
(0,1)
, and for
j=2,\dots,d
,
X_{i,\sigma_{i}(j)}
is sampled from a Laplace distribution with parameters
(X_{i,\sigma_{i}(j-1)},1)
. This results in a chain-like graph, with an edge set
\{(\sigma_{i}(j),\sigma_{i}(j+1)):j=1,\dots,d-1\}.
The permutations
\{\bm{\sigma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated sequentially:
\bm{\sigma}_{1}
is uniformly generated from all permutations of
[d]
, and each subsequent
\bm{\sigma}_{i+1}
is obtained by swapping randomly two adjacent elements of
\bm{\sigma}_{i}
.

5.2
Statistician coauthorship dataset

We now apply NGM to analyze the dataset of research papers published in four leading statistical journals from 2003 to 2012, as described in
Ji and Jin 2016
. The dataset is publicly available at
https://github.com/ZhengTracyKe/MADStat
. It includes two coauthorship networks and one citation network among 3607 statisticians. Following
Li et al. 2020
, we focus on the second coauthorship network and remove statisticians with one published article, resulting in a network with 635 statisticians. For each statistician, the average frequencies of 300 popular statistical terms are calculated for all their published papers, so that each statistician is represented via a 300-dimensional vector. Furthermore, as analyzed in
Ji and Jin 2016
, these authors can be classified into three scientific communities as “Bayesian”, “Biostatistics” and “High-dimensional statistics”.

We first apply the adjacency spectral embedding method in
Athreya et al. 2018
to estimate the latent embedding vectors
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
. The proposed NGM is then applied to construct the heterogeneous graphical model based on the strength of
\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{jl}^{(i)}
for each author. Figure
1
displays the signal strengths of three selected statistical term pairs for the estimated latent embeddings of all statisticians. It is clear that different statistical term pairs exhibit distinct signal strength surfaces. For instance, in the left penal of Figure
1
, the “high dimension” community exhibits significantly stronger signal strength of “dimension - reduction” than those of the other two communities, which is reasonable as dimension reduction has been central to most high-dimensional statistics papers. Similar pattern can be observed in the other two panels, where “Bayesian” community exhibits the highest signal strength of “Dirichlet-process”, and “biostatistics” community exhibits the strongest signal of “gene-expression”. These observations demonstrate that statisticians in different communities possess highly heterogeneous graphical models showing their habits of statistical term usage.

6
Summary

This paper proposed a nonparametric framework for learning heterogeneous graphical models on network-linked data, without requiring any specific distributional assumptions. To achieve this, we introduced an efficient two-step estimation algorithm that integrates network embedding with nonparametric graphical model estimation, leveraging vector-valued RKHS for scalable computation. By the representer theorem in RKHS, our nonparametric graphical model estimation leads to a closed-form solution, ensuring both computational efficiency and practical applicability. The proposed method is supported by rigorous theoretical guarantees, ensuring both estimation consistency and exact recovery of the heterogeneous graph structures under mild conditions, which are illustrated through some specific theoretical examples. Numerical experiments on various simulation settings and real data demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms existing competitors in accuracy and scalability.
