The Self-Projecting Grassmannian
Alheydis Geiger, Francesca Zaffalon

Abstract
We introduce the self-projecting Grassmannian, an irreducible subvariety of the Grassmannian parametrizing linear subspaces that satisfy a generalized self-duality condition. We study its relation to classical moduli spaces, such as the moduli spaces of pointed curves of genus g g , as well as to other natural subvarieties of the Grassmannian. We further translate the self-projectivity condition in the combinatorial language of matroids, introducing self-projecting matroids, and we computationally investigate their realization spaces inside the self-projecting Grassmannian.

Introduction

The Grassmannian is the point of entry to moduli spaces. It parameterizes
k
-dimensional vector subspaces of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
, or configurations of
n
points in
\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
, modulo
\PGL
-action.
Subvarieties of the Grassmannian encode additional constraints on such point configurations. One important example is self-duality, where the set of points satisfies Gale duality.
These were studied, among others, by Dolgachev–Ortland, Coble, Petrakiev and Eisenbud–Popescu
12
;
7
;
28
;
13

15

Formally, a point
V\in\Gr(k,2k)
is said to be self-dual if
V
is an element of the
(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{2k}
torus orbit of
V^{\perp}
.

In this paper, we generalize self-dual point configurations to
self-projecting point configurations
. These describe points
V\in\Gr(k,n)
such that
V
is
contained
in an element of the
(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
torus orbit of
V^{\perp}
.
We introduce the subvariety of the Grassmannian parameterizing such point configurations: the
self-projecting Grassmannian
. It is defined as the Zariski closure of the space of
V\in\Gr(k,n)
, represented by a
k\times n
matrix
X
, such that

\exists\text{ diagonal full-rank matrix }\Lambda\text{ such that }X\cdot\Lambda\cdot X^{t}=0.

(1)

Theorem
2.10
.

If

is irreducible of dimension

For

we have

\SGr(k,n)=\Gr(k,n)

In particular, each self-projecting point configuration in

lies in some orthogonal Grassmannian

Therefore, the self-projecting Grassmannian encodes in a single object all vector subspaces which are isotropic with respect to some non-degenerate diagonal bilinear form; see

Orthogonal Grassmannians, and their totally positive parts, play an important role in applications, where they appear, among others, in the study of scattering amplitudes in ABJM theory

17

The self-projectivity condition on a space

2
The self-projecting Grassmannian

In this section we introduce the central object of study, the self-projecting Grassmannian, and investigate its relation to other parameter spaces of point configurations.

The Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
is the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces of an
n
-dimensional vector space over a field
\mathbb{K}
.
It can be embedded into projective space via the
dual Plücker embedding
, mapping a
k
-dimensional vector space
V
to the vector of maximal minors
(q_{I}(V))_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}
of a
k\times n
matrix
M
with
V=\rowspan(M)
. By abuse of notation, we denote the matrix
M
also by
V
.

2.1
Definitions

The self-dual Grassmannian, as introduced in
15
, is the subvariety of the Grassmannian
\Gr(k,2k)
defined as the Zariski closure of the set
\SGr^{\bullet}(k,2k)
of all vector spaces
V\in\Gr(k,2k)
such that

q_{[n]\setminus I}(V)=\lambda_{I}\sign(I)q_{I}(V),\quad\text{ for all }I\in\tbinom{[2k]}{k},

(2)

where
\lambda_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
.
An element
V\in\SGr^{\bullet}(k,2k)
is said to be
self-dual
. Note that for such an element
V
and the corresponding choice of
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{2k}
, we have that
V^{\perp}=\lambda\cdot V
or, equivalently,
V\cdot\diag(\lambda)\cdot V^{t}=0
.

Definition 2.1
.

A subspace
V\in\Gr(k,n)
is
self-projecting
if there exists
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
such that
V\cdot\diag(\lambda)\cdot V^{t}=0
.
Denote by
\SGr^{\bullet}(k,n)
the set of self-projecting points in
\Gr(k,n)
.
The
self-projecting Grassmannian

\SGr(k,n)
is the Zariski closure of
\SGr^{\bullet}(k,n)
inside the Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
.

Since the set of self-projecting points is not empty if and only if
n\geq 2k
, from now on we assume
n\geq 2k
. Geometrically, a subspace
V
is self-projecting if it is contained in some element of the orbit of the
(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
torus action on its orthogonal complement
V^{\perp}
.

Equivalently, a space is self-projecting, if there exists some
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
satisfying a system of linear equations as follows.
Let
\nu:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{\binom{\ell+1}{2}}
be the second Veronese embedding.
Given an
\ell\times m
matrix
M
, we denote by

\nu(M)=\begin{pmatrix}\nu(v_{1})\cdots\nu(v_{m})\end{pmatrix}

the
\binom{\ell+1}{2}\times m
matrix obtained by applying the Veronese embedding to each column of
M
. We refer to this as the
multi-Veronese matrix
of
M
. Given an element
V\in\Gr(k,n)
represented by a
k\times n
matrix
M_{V}
, we can consider the
\binom{k+1}{2}\times n
matrix
\nu(M_{V})
. Then
V
is self-projecting if and only if there exists a solution
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
to

\nu(M_{V})\cdot\lambda=0.

The above construction is independent of the choice of representative of the point
V
. Indeed, suppose
M_{W}
is another
k\times n
matrix representing
V
. Then there exists some
G\in\GL(k)
such that
M_{W}=G\cdot M_{V}
. Consider the second symmetric power
G^{[2]}
of
G
.
By construction,
\nu(M_{W})=G^{[2]}\cdot\nu(M_{V})
. The matrix
G^{[2]}
is invertible, and its inverse is
(G^{-1})^{[2]}
.
Hence, the system of equations defined by
\nu(M_{V})
does not depend on the choice of the representative for
V
. Therefore, we will write
\nu(V)
for
\nu(M_{V})
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The self-projecting Grassmannian can be defined in dual Stiefel coordinates as the variety
\mathcal{V}(I^{\sd}_{k,n})
with

I^{\sd}_{k,n}=\left(\langle\nu(X)\cdot\lambda\rangle:\langle\lambda_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{n}\rangle^{\infty}\right)\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{(1,1)},\ldots,x_{(k,n)}],

(3)

where
X
denotes the matrix with entries
X_{ij}=x_{(i,j)}
.

In order to introduce the ideal of the self-projecting Grassmannian in dual Plücker coordinates, we define an auxiliary matrix.

Definition 2.3
.

The
cocircuit matrix

D_{k,n}
is the
\binom{n}{k-1}\times n
matrix with

(D_{k,n})_{I,j}=\begin{cases}\sign(I,j)q_{I\cup j}\quad&\text{ if }j\not\in I\\
0&\text{ else}\end{cases}\qquad\text{for }I\in\tbinom{[n]}{k-1},j\in[n].

It was shown in
(
22
, Remark 2.3)
that given a diagonal bilinear form represented by a matrix
\Lambda
, a point
V\in\Gr(k,n)
is isotropic with respect to that bilinear form if and only if
D_{k,n}(V)\cdot\Lambda\cdot D_{k,n}(V)^{t}=0
. The matrix
D_{k,n}
has rank
k
,
(
11
, Remark 2.2)
.
Hence, a point
V\in\Gr(k,n)
is self-projecting if and only if there is a solution in the algebraic torus
(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
to the system of linear equations

\nu(D_{k,n}(V))\cdot\lambda=0.

Corollary 2.4
.

In dual Plücker coordinates, the self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(k,n)
is defined as the variety of the ideal

\displaystyle J^{\sd}_{k,n}:=\left(\left\langle\nu(D_{k,n})\cdot\lambda\right\rangle:\langle\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}\rangle^{\infty}\right)\cap\mathbb{K}\left[q_{I}\mid I\in\tbinom{[n]}{k}\right]+I_{k,n},

where
I_{k,n}
is the ideal of Plücker relations defining the Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
.

For any self-projecting point
V\in\SGr(k,n)
the matrix
\nu(V)
has rank at most
n-1
. As shown in examples in
5
, the ideal generated by the
n
-minors of
\nu(X)
inside
\mathbb{K}[x_{i,j}]
might have many irreducible components. However, by
Theorem

2.10
the self-projecting Grassmannian is an irreducible variety.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
2k\leq n\leq\binom{k+1}{2}
. The self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(k,n)
is equal to the top-dimensional irreducible component of the vanishing locus of the ideal
I_{n}
of
n
-minors of
\nu(X)
in dual Stiefel coordinates.

In dual Plücker coordinates it is equal to the top-dimensional irreducible component of the vanishing locus of the ideal
J_{n}
of
n
-minors of
\nu(D_{k,n}|_{[k]})
, where
D_{k,n}|_{[k]}
denotes the submatrix of
D_{k,n}
given by the first
k
rows.

Proof.

Consider the variety
\mathcal{V}(I_{n,n-1})
, where
I_{n,n-1}
is the ideal of
n
-minors of
\nu(X)
, saturated by the ideals of
(n-1)
-minors of
\nu(X)|_{S}
, for all
S\in\binom{[n]}{n-1}
. A point
V
is such that
\nu(V)
has an element of the right kernel in the torus if and only if
\nu(V)
is rank deficient and all submatrices obtained by deleting a column have full rank equal to
n-1
. Therefore
\mathcal{V}(I_{n,n-1})=\SGr(k,n)
. Since
\SGr(k,n)
is irreducible by
Theorem

2.10
, and the vanishing locus of the ideal of
(n-1)
-minors of
(\nu(X)|_{S})_{S}
has dimension strictly smaller than
\dim(\SGr(k,n))
, the first claim follows.

As above,
\SGr(k,n)
is equal to the variety of
V\in\Gr(k,n)
in Plücker coordinates such that
\nu(D_{k,n}(V))
is rank deficient but all
(n-1)
submatrices obtained by deleting one column have full rank equal to
n-1
. If all
(n-1)
-minors of
\nu(D_{k,n})
are non-vanishing,
q_{[k]}\neq 0
. Then the submatrix of
D_{k,n}
given by the first
k
rows, i.e. by the rows labeled by subsets in
\binom{[k]}{k-1}
, is full-rank. Hence, the second statement follows.
∎

2.2
The orthogonal Grassmannian

The self-projecting Grassmannian is closely related to another class of highly interesting subvarieties of the Grassmannian, the orthogonal Grassmannians
22
.
Given a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
\omega
, the corresponding orthogonal Grassmannian encodes the spaces which are isotropic with respect to
\omega
. The self-projecting Grassmannian is the Zariski closure of the union over all orthogonal Grassmannians defined by a
diagonal
bilinear form.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\omega
be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form, then the orthogonal Grassmannian
\OGr^{\omega}(k,n)
is the variety of
V\in\Gr(k,n)
with
\omega(u,v)=0\text{ for all }u,v\in V
.

In the following we consider orthogonal Grassmannians with respect to diagonal bilinear forms. Given
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
, denote by
\eta_{\lambda}
the bilinear form

\eta_{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda_{1}x_{1}y_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}y_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}x_{n}y_{n},

corresponding to the matrix
\diag(\lambda)
. We write
\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
for
\OGr^{\eta_{\lambda}}(k,n)
. By definition, we have set-theoretic equality

\SGr^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)=\bigcup_{\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}}\OGr_{\mathbb{K}}^{\lambda}(k,n).

(4)

For the study of the orthogonal Grassmannian over
\mathbb{C}
the choice of the bilinear form does not make a difference. However, over
\mathbb{R}
different choices of bilinear forms can behave very differently; see
Section

5
for more details. In the literature, the bilinear form is usually fixed to be either the diagonal form given by
\alt(1)=((-1)^{i-1})_{i=1,\ldots,n}\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
or the form
\omega
with matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&\Id_{k}\\
\Id_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}
.

We generalize
(
14
, Proposition 5.1)
for the
n=2k
case for arbitrary nondegenerate diagonal bilinear forms.

Proposition 2.7
.

For
V\in\Gr(k,2k)
the following are equivalent.

(i)

V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,2k)
;

(ii)

q_{[2k]\setminus I}(V)=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda_{[2k]}}}\sign(I,[2k]\setminus I)\lambda_{I}q_{I}(V)
, where
\pm
is the same for all
I\in\binom{[2k]}{k}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
.
We have
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,2k)
if and only if
\langle u,\lambda\cdot v\rangle=0
for all
u,v\in V
, or equivalently
V^{\perp}=\lambda\cdot V
. Hence, there exists
c\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
with

\displaystyle q_{I}(V)=c\cdot q_{[2k]\setminus I}(\lambda\cdot V)=c\cdot\sign([2k]\setminus I,I)\lambda_{[2k]\setminus I}q_{[2k]\setminus I}(V),

where
\lambda_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
. Applying the equality twice, it follows that
c^{2}\lambda_{[2k]}=1
, hence the statement follows.
∎

In particular, it follows that
\OGr^{\lambda}(k,2k)
has two connected components:
\OGr^{\lambda}_{+}(k,2k)
of points
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,2k)
such that the equality
(ii)
holds with a plus and
\OGr^{\lambda}_{-}(k,2k)
where
(ii)
holds with a minus.

Remark 2.8
.

One can ask if it is possible to always find a
\lambda^{\prime}
such that
\OGr^{\lambda}_{-}(k,2k)
is equal to
\OGr_{+}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(k,2k)
.
If
k
is odd, one can take
\lambda^{\prime}=-\lambda
. This works for any base field.
If
k
is even and the base field is algebraically closed, we can choose a
\lambda^{\prime}=i\lambda
, where
i
is a square root of
-1
.
Over
\mathbb{R}
there is no way to achieve the equality
\OGr^{\lambda}_{-}(k,2k)=\OGr_{+}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(k,2k)
if
k
is even.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, then
\SGr_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
is the Zariski closure of the image of
\OGr_{\mathbb{K}}^{\mathbf{1}}(k,n)
under the
(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
torus action.

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, then
\SGr_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
is the Zariski closure of the image of
\bigcup_{\mathbf{u}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\OGr_{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{u}}(k,n)
under the
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{n}
action.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be algebraically closed. We show that the image of the torus action on
\OGr^{\mathbf{1}}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
is equal to the set-theoretic union of all orthogonal Grassmannians with respect to some diagonal bilinear form. Let
V\in\OGr^{\mathbf{1}}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
, and
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
. Choose
\mu\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
with
\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}
. Then, the space
\lambda\cdot V
lies in
\OGr^{\mu}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
.
Conversely, given
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
and
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
, choose
\mu\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
such that
\mu_{i}^{2}=\lambda_{i}
.
Then,
\mu\cdot V
lies in
\OGr^{\mathbf{1}}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
V\in\SGr^{\bullet}(k,n)
and
\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
such that
V\diag(\lambda)V^{t}=0
. There exists a unique
\mathbf{u}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
\sqrt{|\lambda|}\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{n}
, with
\lambda_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot(\sqrt{|\lambda|})_{i}^{2}
. Then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}\cdot V\in\OGr^{\mathbf{u}}(k,n)
.
Conversely, let
V\in\OGr^{\mathbf{u}}(k,n)
for
\mathbf{u}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and let
\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{n}
. Then
\lambda V
is self-projecting with respect to
\mu_{i}=u_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}
.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

The variety
\SGr(k,n)

is absolutely irreducible.

Proof.

Taking the image under the torus action as in
Lemma

2.9
preserves irreducibility, so the statement follows for
n>2k
from the irreducibility of the orthogonal Grassmannian. For
n=2k,
we consider that in
Lemma

2.9
we can use
\OGr_{+}(k,2k)
instead of
\OGr(k,2k)
, since the torus action maps
\OGr_{+}(k,2k)
to
\OGr_{-}(k,2k)
as described in
Remark

2.8
. As
\OGr_{+}(k,2k)
is irreducible, the claim follows.
∎

We can compute the dimension of the self-projecting Grassmannian using the known dimensions of the orthogonal Grassmannian.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be algebraically closed or
\mathbb{R}
. For
n>\binom{k+1}{2}
,
\SGr(k,n)=\Gr(k,n)
. For
2k\leq n\leq\binom{k+1}{2}
,

\dim(\SGr_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n))=k(n-k)-\tbinom{k+1}{2}+n-1.

Proof.

The statement follows from
(
22
, Proposition 2.4)
and
Lemma

2.9
. If the base field is
\mathbb{R}
, these results can be applied, since the quadratic form used in the proof of
(
22
, Proposition 2.4)
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\OGr^{\alt(1)}(k,n)
has real dimension equal to its complex dimension.
∎

2.3
Point configurations
X(k,n)

Geometrically, self-projecting vector spaces
V\in\Gr(k,n)^{\circ}
are configurations of
n
points in
\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
such that their dual configuration in
\mathbb{P}^{n-k-1}
can be projected to the original one.

Definition 2.12
.

The
self-projecting configuration space

X_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\sd}
is the very affine variety
\SGr_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\circ}/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
inside
X_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)=\Gr_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\circ}/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
.

The self-projecting configuration space and the orthogonal Grassmannian are related as follows.

Proposition 2.13
.

For
\mathbb{K}
algebraically closed, there is a dominant finite map between open subsets of the very affine varieties
\OGr^{\mathbf{1}}(k,n)\,\text{and}\,X_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\sd}
.

Proof.

Consider the open subset
U_{S}
of points
V\in\SGr^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)
such that
\ker\nu(V)=\langle\lambda\rangle
for some
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
. Note that this set is closed under torus action and we can consider the quotient
U_{X}=U_{S}/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
as an open subset of
X_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\sd}
. Similarly, consider the open subset
U_{O}
of points
V\in\OGr^{\mathbf{1}}_{\mathbb{K}}(k,n)^{\circ}
such that
\ker(\nu(V))=\langle\mathbf{1}\rangle
. Consider the map
\varphi:U_{O}\to U_{X}
, sending a space
V\in U_{O}
to its equivalence class up to torus action inside
U_{X}
. That the map is surjective and each fiber contains
2^{n-1}
points follows similarly to
Lemma

2.9
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

The birational parametrization of
X(k,2k)^{\sd}
via the special orthogonal group
\SO(k)
as stated in
(
15
, Theorem 2.4)
is not correct. Indeed,
\SO(k)
is birational to one of the connected components of the orthogonal Grassmannian
\OGr(k,2k)
. Therefore, the map from
\SO(k)
to
X(k,2k)^{\sd}
defined by sending an orthogonal matrix
R
to
(I\mid R)
is only finite to one, but not injective.
However, the main consequence of the Theoreom,
(
15
, Corollary 2.5)
, was proven in
(
12
, Theorem 4, p.51)
and remains correct.

Proposition 2.15
.

There is a dominant finite map between open subsets of the very affine varieties
\bigcup_{\mathbf{u}\in\{1\}\times\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}}\OGr^{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)^{\circ}
and
X_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)^{\sd}
.

Proof.

Consider the same open subsets
U_{S},U_{X}
as in the proof of
Proposition

2.13
. Let
U_{O}
be the union of the open subsets of points
V\in\OGr^{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)^{\circ}
such that
\ker(\nu(V))=\langle\mathbf{u}\rangle
, for all
\mathbf{u}\in\{1\}\times\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}
. As before, we can define a map
\varphi:U_{O}\to U_{X}
by mapping a point
V\in U_{O}
to its equivalence class up to the action of
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{n}
. That
\varphi
is surjective and each fiber contains
2^{n-1}
points follows similarly to
Lemma

2.9
.
∎

2.4
Associated point configurations

The study of (self-)dual point configurations has a rich history. Introduced in 1922 by Coble
7
, they were studied among others by Dolgachev–Ortland, Petrakiev, Kapranov
12
;
28
;
19
.

Recall that an
associated
point configuration is a pair
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n-k-1})^{n}
, such that
(\phi(x_{i},y_{i}))_{i\in[n]}\in(\mathbb{P}^{k(n-k)-1})^{n}
is a minimal projectively dependent set, where
\phi:\mathbb{P}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-k-1}\to\mathbb{P}^{k(n-k)-1}
is the Segre embedding.

Proposition 2.16
.

A self-projecting point configuration
X\in X(k,n)^{\sd}
defines associated points
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n-k-1})^{n}
, with
\pi(y_{i})=x_{i}
, for all
i\in[n]
, such that the equivalence class of
x
in
X(k,n)^{\sd}
is equal to
X
.
Here
\pi:\mathbb{P}^{n-k-1}\to\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
is the projection to the first
k
coordinates.

Proof.

The claim follows from the following observation. Let
X,Y
be matrices with representatives of the projective points
x_{i},y_{i}
in the columns, respectively. Then
x,y
are associated if and only if there exists a diagonal matrix
\Lambda
with non-zero entries such that
X\Lambda Y^{t}=0
. Equivalently,
Y=\Lambda\cdot\ker(X)
.
For
X\in\SGr^{\bullet}(k,n)
with
X\Lambda X^{t}=0
, we have
X\subseteq\Lambda\ker(X)
. Take
Y=\ker(X)
represented by a matrix containing in the first
k
rows a basis of
X
.
∎

The equivalent definition of associated point configurations via the existence of a full-rank diagonal matrix
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda Y=\ker(X)
, motivated our definition of self-projectivity.
In particular,
X
is self-associated if and only if
X
is self-dual. The main difference between self-dual and self-associated points is the ambient space where they are considered: the first is considered in
X(k,2k)
, while the second is considered in
(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{2k}
. We can move between these via the
Gelfand-MacPherson correspondence

16
;
19

\displaystyle\varphi:(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}\dashrightarrow\Gr(k,n)/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1},\quad(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\mapsto[\rowspan(p_{1}\,\ldots\,p_{n})]

where
(p_{1}\,\ldots\,p_{n})
is the
k\times n
matrix with columns
p_{i}
. The map is defined whenever the
n
points do not lie on a vector space of dimension smaller than
k
. The map is surjective, but it is injective only up to the diagonal action of
\PGL(k)
on
(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}
. That is, there is a birational map

\overline{\varphi}:\;\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$\PGL(k)$}\backslash(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}\dashrightarrow\Gr(k,n)/\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1}$}.

By Gelfand and MacPherson, there is a natural diffeomorphism
(
16
, Proposition 2.2.2)
, between the generic configurations in
(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}
and
X(k,n)
.
This descends to a diffeomorphism between the self-projecting configuration space
X(k,n)^{\sd}
and the generic configurations of points in the parameter space
X_{k-1,2^{m},n}
, where
m=\binom{k+1}{2}-n+1
, as defined in
Section

2.5
.

A priori, the quotient
\Gr(k,n)/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1}
is well-defined only in the open part
\Gr(k,n)^{\circ}
.
There are many possible choices to define the quotient on the non-closed orbits.
The solution we use is Kapranov’s Chow quotient
19
, where
\Gr(k,n)\mathbin{/\mkern-6.0mu/}(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1}
can be thought of as the space of limits of closures of generic orbits. For a detailed definition, see
19
.
We can restrict the Chow quotient
\Gr(k,n)\mathbin{/\mkern-6.0mu/}(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1}
to the self-projecting Grassmannian.
This defines a compactification of the self-projecting configuration space
X(k,n)^{\sd}
. Keel and Tevelev
20
showed that for the self-dual case this is equal to the tropical compactification, as in
(
23
, Section 6.4)
.
By
(
19
, Theorem 2.2.4)
the Gelfand–MacPherson correspondence extends to an isomorphism of the Chow quotients

\overline{\varphi}:\;\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$\PGL(k)$}\mathbin{\setminus\mkern-6.0mu\setminus}(\mathbb{P}^{k-1})^{n}\rightarrow\Gr(k,n)\mathbin{/\mkern-6.0mu/}\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n-1}$}.

2.5
Point configurations on hypersurfaces
X_{r,d^{m},n}
and beyond

Point configurations contained in certain varieties, like hypersurfaces, also appear often in the literature
1
;
5
;
6
;
31
. The self-dual configuration space is strictly related to the moduli space
X_{r,d^{m},n}\subset(\mathbb{P}^{r})^{n}
describing configurations of
n
points in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
lying on
m
hypersurfaces of degree
d
. This space was introduced by Caminata, Moon and Schaffler in
5
motivated by the study of the geometry of data sets.

Setting
r=k-1
,
d=2
, and
m=\binom{k+1}{2}-n+1
, by
(
5
, Prop 2.4)
,
X_{k-1,2^{m},n}
is cut out by the
n
-minors of
\nu(X)
. Thus, it consists of projective points
V
such that
\nu(V)
has a non-trivial right kernel. Recall by
Proposition

2.5
that
\SGr(k,n)
is the generic irreducible component of the vanishing locus of the
n
-minors of
\nu(V)
. After quotienting by the torus action, we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.17
.

Under the Gelfand-MacPherson correspondence, the self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(k,n)
corresponds to the generic irreducible component of
X_{k-1,2^{m},n}
, with
m=\binom{k+1}{2}-n+1
, where generic means that the hypersurfaces are not degenerate, irreducible and intersect in the expected way.

In particular, we obtain an isomorphism between the quotients

\SGr\left(k,\tbinom{k+1}{2}\right)\mathbin{/\mkern-6.0mu/}\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{\tbinom{k+1}{2}-1}$}\to\raisebox{-5.69046pt}{$\PGL(k)$}\mathbin{\setminus\mkern-6.0mu\setminus}X_{k-1,2^{1},\tbinom{k+1}{2}}.

Proof.

For a point
X\in\SGr(k,n)
, the
n
-minors of second multi-Veronese matrix
\nu(X)
all vanish.
Therefore, when restricting the Gelfand-MacPherson correspondence to the open self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(k,n)^{\circ}
, the image is contained in
\raisebox{-2.84544pt}{$\PGL(k)$}\backslash X_{k-1,2^{m},n}.
As the Gelfand-MacPherson correspondence is an isomorphism and
\SGr(k,n)
is irreducible, it follows that the image is an irreducible component of
\raisebox{-2.84544pt}{$\PGL(k)$}\backslash X_{k-1,2^{m},n},
where the quadratic hypersurfaces are non-degenerate, irreducible, and intersect in the expected way. This extends to the level of the Chow quotients, when we take the closure.
If
n=\binom{k+1}{2}
, we have
m=1
and
X_{k-1,2^{m},n}
is irreducible by
(
5
, Theorem A)
.
∎

From
(
5
, Theorem A)
, we know that
X_{r,d^{1},n}
is a geometrically irreducible Cohen–Macaulay normal variety in
(\mathbb{P}^{r})^{n}
. When this is isomorphic to the self-projecting configuration space, we obtain the same properties for
\SGr(k,n)
.

Corollary 2.18
.

For
n=\binom{k+1}{2}
, we have that
\SGr(k,n)
is reduced, irreducible, and Cohen-Macaulay in
\Gr(k,n)
.

Proof.

This statement follows directly from
5
and can be found in detail in
(
1
, Theorem 3.5)
.
In
1
, the variety
Z_{2}(k,n)
is introduced, as the variety of points
\rowspan(M)\in\Gr(k,n)
where the second multi-Veronese matrix of
M
is not of full rank. This coincides with
\SGr(k,n)
whenever
\SGr(k,n)
is of codimension 1, i.e. whenever
n=\binom{k+1}{2}
.
From
(
1
, Theorem 3.5)
, we know that
Z_{2}(k,n)
is reduced, irreducible and Cohen-Macaulay.
∎

For higher codimension of
\SGr(k,n)
in
\Gr(k,n)
the situation is very intricate. As described in
(
5
, Section 2)
, the structure of
X_{r,d^{m},n}
for
m>1
is very complicated. For example, it can have multiple top-dimensional irreducible components.
Therefore, it is unclear whether properties like normal or Cohen-Macaulay still hold and, via Gelfand-MacPherson, could be applied to
\SGr(k,n)
. As the techniques used in
5
;
1
cannot be generalized for
m>2
, one would need much more heavy machinery to investigate this question.

Remark 2.19
.

Special instances of the self-projecting Grassmannian appear hidden in the literature. One example is the ABCT variety
V(3,n)
for
n=6
, which coincides with
\SGr(3,6)
.
The ABCT variety
V(3,n)
was first introduced by Arkani-Hamed, Bourjaily, Cachazo and Trnka
2
in the context of the spinor-helicity formalism in physics. In

(
1
, Section 4)
the authors study the homology class and degree of
V(3,6)=\SGr(3,6)
, while
(
1
, Section 5)
discusses the difficulties in defining a positive geometry structure.

3
Geometric interpretations

In this section, we study in detail some self-projecting Grassmannians and give a geometric interpretation for them.
We set
\mathbb{K}
to be algebraically closed and omit it from the notation.

3.1
9 self-projecting points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

The first non-trivial example of the self-projecting Grassmannian, which differs from the self-dual case, is
\SGr(4,9)
. An element of
X(4,9)
is a generic configuration of
9
points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
up to projective equivalence.
Given
9
points in general position in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
there is a unique quadric surface passing through the points. If the point configuration is self-projecting, there exists a
1
-dimensional family of quadric surfaces through the points.
The intersection of two linearly independent quadric surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
defines a quartic curve. For general quadric surfaces, this curve is irreducible and of genus 1, i.e., it is an elliptic curve. Hence, a generic
V\in X(4,9)^{\sd}
defines an elliptic curve with
9
marked points. That is, we obtain a map

\varphi:X(4,9)^{\sd}\to\mathcal{M}_{1,9}.

However, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1,9}
of elliptic curves with
9
marked points is a
9
-dimensional variety, while
X(4,9)^{\sd}
is
10
-dimensional. Informally, the extra dimension of the self-projective configuration space corresponds to the choice of an embedding for the elliptic curve. Formally, we have the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

The self-projecting configuration space
X(4,9)^{\sd}
and the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
are birationally equivalent.

Proof.

Consider the open subset
U
of
X(4,9)^{\sd}
of points
V\in X(4,9)^{\sd}
on an irreducible curve
C_{V}
of genus 1.
Different representations of
V\in U
define isomorphic elliptic curves in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Consider the hyperplane
H
through
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and denote by
p_{10}
the fourth intersection point of
H
with
C_{V}
. Since the
9
points are in general position, no four of them lie on a hyperplane. Thus,
p_{10}\neq p_{i}
for all
i\in[9]
. Therefore, we can define a map
\psi:U\to\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
.

The map
\psi
is injective on
U
. For any two configurations
V,W\in X(4,9)^{\sd}
, where

V=(p_{1}\ldots p_{9})
and
W=(q_{1}\ldots q_{9})
, and
\psi(V)=\psi(W)
, then the curves
C_{V}
and
C_{W}
and the linear systems
|p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}+p_{10}|
and
|q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}+q_{10}|
are isomorphic. Hence, there exists a map
A\in\PGL(4)
realizing this isomorphism. Since the curves are isomorphic as marked curves in
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
, this implies that
A
maps each
p_{i}
to
q_{i}
. It follows that
V
and
W
are equivalent up to
\PGL(4)
-action. In particular, they describe the same point in
X(4,9)^{\sd}
.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
is irreducible and has the same dimension as
X(4,9)^{\sd}
, it follows that
\psi
is a birational map between the two varieties.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

There is a birational map between
X(3,9)
and
X(4,9)^{\sd}
obtained by lifting the elliptic curve from
\mathbb{P}^{2}
to
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

3.1
, it is enough to show that
X(3,9)
is also birational to
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
. Consider the open subset
U
of
X(3,9)
given by points
V
such that the
10\times 9
multi-Veronese matrix obtained by applying the third Veronese embedding to each column of
V
has full rank equal to
9
. Then, given
V\in U
, the
9
points
(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{9})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
corresponding to the columns of
V
lie on a unique elliptic curve
C_{V}
. Define the
10
th point
p_{10}
to be the third point of intersection of
C
with the line through
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Note that
p_{10}\neq p_{i}
for all
i
, since we are assuming that the
9
points are in general position. Then we have a well-defined map
\psi:U\to\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
sending a point
V
to
(C_{V},p_{1},\ldots,p_{10})
as described above.

The map
\psi
is injective. If
V=(p_{1}\ldots p_{9}),W=(q_{1}\ldots q_{9})\in X(3,9)
are mapped to the same point in
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
, then, similarly to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
,
C_{V}
and
C_{W}
and the linear systems
|p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{10}|
and
|q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{10}|
are isomorphic. Hence, there exists a map
B\in\PGL(3)
realizing that isomorphism, sending
V
to
W
. Thus,
V
and
W
represent the same point in
X(3,9)
. As both varieties are irreducible and of the same dimension, the statement follows.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It would be interesting to explore whether and how the birational map between
\mathcal{M}_{1,10}
and
X(4,9)^{\sd}
translates to a correspondence between the respective tropical varieties. Tropical moduli spaces of curves have a rich combinatorial structure relating to graphs; see
6
. We expect the tropicalization of
X(4,9)^{\sd}
to be governed by matroid subdivisions of self-projecting valuated matroids, see
Definition

4.5
. Note that an explicit computation of the tropicalization of
X(4,9)^{\sd}
is currently out of reach. Similarly, it would be interesting to study whether the birational map
\mathcal{M}_{5,13}\dashrightarrow X(5,13)^{\sd}
as in
Theorem

3.5
can be tropicalized.

3.2
10 self-projecting points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

The self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(4,10)
is a codimension
1
variety inside
\Gr(4,10)
given by
V\in\Gr(4,10)
such that
\det(\nu(V))
vanishes.
In other words,
\SGr(4,10)
corresponds to the variety of
10
points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
lying on a quadric surface. The following can be checked computationally, but it was proved first by White
32
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The self-projecting Grassmannian
\SGr(4,10)\subset\mathbb{P}^{\binom{10}{4}-1}
is defined in Plücker coordinates by a degree
5
polynomial with
138
terms.

The problem of giving a synthetic construction determining when
10
points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
lie on a quadric surface is the content of the “Bruxelles’ Problem” posed in 1825 and solved by Will Traves in
31
.

3.3
13 self-projecting points in
\mathbb{P}^{4}

If a point
V\in\Gr(5,13)
lies in the self-projecting Grassmannian, then the
15\times 13
matrix
\nu(V)
has rank strictly smaller than
13
. Hence, the columns of
V
define points lying in the intersection of three distinct quadric hypersurfaces. Since a generic genus
5
curve can be written as the intersection of three quadric hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
via its canonical model, we obtain a connection to the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{5,13}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

The configuration space
X(5,13)^{\sd}
is birationally equivalent to the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{5,13}
of genus
5
curves with
13
distinct marked points.

Proof.

Consider the open subset
U
of
X(5,13)^{\sd}
of points
V
such that
\nu(V)
has rank equal to
12
. Then, the columns of
V
lie on a unique genus
5
curve, given by the complete intersection of
3
linearly independent quadrics in the left kernel of
\nu(V)
. Hence, there is a well-defined map
\psi:U\to\mathcal{M}_{5,13}
. The map is injective because the columns of
V
correspond to the canonical embedding of the marked points on the genus
5
curve, whenever this is non-trigonal and non-hyperelliptic. Since
\mathcal{M}_{5,13}
is irreducible and the varieties have the same dimension, the claim follows.
∎

4
Self-projecting matroids and their realization spaces

In this section, we study the combinatorial properties of self-projecting point configurations. We introduce self-projecting matroids and computationally study their realization spaces inside the self-projecting Grassmannian.

Recall from
15
that a self-dual point
V\in\Gr(k,2k)
defines an
(identically) self-dual matroid
, i.e. a matroid equal to its dual. In this sense, self-dual matroids encode how Plücker coordinates can vanish without breaking Equation (
2
). Realization spaces of self-dual matroids inside the self-dual Grassmannian
\SGr(k,2k)
where studied in
(
15
, Section 3)
. In particular, there exist realizable self-dual matroids that cannot be realized by a self-dual point configuration.
Here we show how this extends beyond the case
n=2k
.

4.1
Self-projecting matroids

We start by noticing that self-duality has many equivalent definitions. Recall that a matroid
\mathcal{M}
has the
disjoint basis property
if for every basis
B
of
\mathcal{M}
there exists a basis
C
of
\mathcal{M}
such that
B\cap C=\emptyset
. Moreover, we introduce the following.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a matroid
\mathcal{M}
of rank
k
on
[n]
, an element
e\in[n]
is said to be a
half-coloop
if there exist two rank
k-1
flats
F_{1},F_{2}
such that
F_{1}\cup F_{2}=[n]\setminus\{e\}
.

The choice of the name comes from the fact that
e\in[n]
is a coloop of
[n]
whenever
[n]\setminus\{e\}
is a flat of rank
k-1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For a rank
k
matroid
\mathcal{M}
on
[2k]
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{M}
is self-dual;

(ii)

\mathcal{M}
satisfies the disjoint basis property;

(iii)

\mathcal{M}
has no half-coloop.

Proof.

The equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
is trivial. It is easy to see that
(i)
implies
(iii)
. Conversely, let
\mathcal{M}
be a matroid with no half-coloop and let
B\in\binom{[2k]}{k}
be a basis of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose by contradiction that
[2k]\setminus B
is not a basis and let
F_{2}
be any rank
k-1
flat containing
[2k]\setminus B
. Then any
e\in B\setminus F_{1}
is a half-coloop, contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore the claim follows.
∎

Whenever
n>2k
, property
(i)
is not defined, and the non-existence of a half-coloop, property
(iii)
, is stronger than the disjoint basis property
(ii)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a matroid with no half-coloop. Then
\mathcal{M}
has the disjoint basis property.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a matroid of rank
k
on
n
elements with no half-coloop and fix
B
a basis of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose by contradiction that
\rank_{\mathcal{M}}([n]\setminus B)<k
. Then there exists some flat
F_{1}
of rank
k-1
containing
[n]\setminus B
. Let
F_{2}
be any
k-1
subset of
B
containing
F_{1}\cap B
. Then
F_{1},F_{2}
are two rank
k-1
flats with
|F_{1}\cup F_{2}|=n-1
, against the assumption.
∎

A self-projecting point satisfies the property of not having a half-coloop.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
V\in\SGr^{\bullet}(k,n)
, the matroid
\mathcal{M}_{V}
has no half-coloop.

Proof.

Let
S,T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
. The entry-wise product of the rows of the cocircuit matrix
D_{k,n}(V)
labeled by
S
and
T
give rise to the equation:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sign(S,i)\sign(T,i)q_{S\cup i}(V)q_{T\cup i}(V)=0.

(5)

Suppose by contradiction that
e\in[n]
is a half-coloop of
\mathcal{M}_{V}
. Then there exist independent
S,T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
, such that the union of the flats containing them is equal to
[n]\setminus\{e\}
. Hence,
e\in[n]
is the only element
i
such that
S\cup\{i\}
and
T\cup\{i\}
are bases of
\mathcal{M}_{V}
. For this choice of
S,T
, the equation above becomes
\lambda_{e}q_{S\cup e}(V)q_{T\cup e}(V)=0
, which implies
\lambda_{e}=0.
This is a contradiction.
∎

A matroid
\mathcal{M}

4.2
Self-projecting matroid realization spaces

As seen above, self-projecting point configurations give rise to self-projecting matroids. In the following we study the reverse question, that is, when and how are self-projecting matroids realized by self-projecting point configurations.
We show computationally that most small realizable self-projecting matroids can be realized (and are generically realized) by a self-projecting point. This provides strong evidence that our definition of self-projecting matroid is the right generalization of self-duality. Note that we cannot expect all realizable self-projecting matroids to be realized by a self-projecting point configuration, as this fails even for self-dual matroids
15
. Formally, we study the following objects.

Definition 4.8
.

A
k\times n
matrix
X
with entries in
\mathbb{K}
is a
realization
of a matroid
\mathcal{M}
if the columns of
X
indexed by
I
form a basis of
\mathbb{K}^{k}
if and only if
I
is a basis of
\mathcal{M}
.
The
realization space

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathcal{M})
of
\mathcal{M}
is the quotient of the matroid cell
\Gr(\mathcal{M})^{\circ}/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
generated by all realizations of
\mathcal{M}
over
\mathbb{K}
. The matroid
\mathcal{M}
is
realizable over
\mathbb{K}
if
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
.

A realization
X
of
\mathcal{M}
is a
self-projecting realization
of
\mathcal{M}
if there exists
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
such that
X\diag(\lambda)X^{t}=0
. The
self-projecting realization space

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathcal{M})
of
\mathcal{M}
is the quotient of the matroid cell
\SGr(\mathcal{M})^{\circ}/(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
generated by all self-projecting realizations of
\mathcal{M}
over
\mathbb{K}
.

Note the self-projecting realization space of a matroid might be strictly smaller than the intersection of its realization space with the self-projecting Grassmannian. In particular, whenever
\mathcal{M}
is a non self-projecting matroid of rank
k
on
n
elements, and
k
and
n
are such that
\SGr(k,n)=\Gr(k,n)
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathcal{M})=\emptyset
, while the latter will be equal to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathcal{M})
.

We study self-projecting realization spaces computationally for
\mathbb{K}=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, which will be dropped from the notation. In the following we report on the results, and discuss the computational methods used in
Section

4.3
.

Self-projecting realization spaces in rank
2

Matroids of rank
2

n

of

\textrm{rk }(P_{i})=1

\textrm{rk }(P_{i}\cup a)=2
for every

With this notation it is easy to check that a rank

Theorem 4.9
.

For any self-projecting matroid
\mathcal{M}
of rank
2

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})

Proof.

Note that we can assume

for each

. First assume

. Let

be any realization of
\mathcal{M}
and consider the restriction

to columns labeled by

\mathcal{U}_{2,r}
,

will be generically a self-projecting point with respect to some
\lambda^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{r}

and suppose that

for all

. Then

is self-projecting with respect to
\lambda\in(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n}
with
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{a_{i,j}|P_{i}|}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
for
j\in P_{i}
.

If
r=2
, then
\mathcal{M}
can be restricted to the matroid
\mathcal{M}_{1}
on four elements with two classes of two parallel elements each. If
r=3
, then
\mathcal{M}
can be restricted to the matroid
\mathcal{M}_{2}
on six elements with three classes of two parallel elements each. One can show that
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M}_{i})=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i})
for

. As above, realizations of
\mathcal{M}
are generically self-projecting.
∎

4.3
The computations, the code and the database

The proofs of the above theorems are computational. The usage of computational methods and their resulting data in mathematics has been developing quickly within the last decades. In particular, the emphasis on making these data FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) and on making computations reproducible and transparent increased
4
. We believe that our approach addresses the paramount questions and challenges on FAIR data and confirmable workflows.

We first describe the methods and the set-up used in the computations, thus providing the proof for the theorems. Then we describe how the code and output of these computations is made available to the research community, and how future research can profit from the created infrastructure.

Proof and Discussion of
Theorem

4.10
and
Theorem

4.11
.

We extracted all self-projecting matroids from all rank
k
matroids on
n
elements stored in the database
polyDB

27
by computationally checking each matroid for the existence of half-coloops.
For the computation of the (self-projecting) realization spaces, we used the methods described in the proofs of
(
15
, Proposition 3.3 and Theorem 3.7)
, and adapted the code available on GitHub
1
1

1

https://github.com/sachihashimoto/self-dual
to our purposes.

The main idea is to use Gröbner basis computations in
Magma
. For each matroid in the list, we chose an isomorphic matroid for which
[k]
is a basis. Note that in this setting the matroid is realizable if and only if it can be realized by a
k\times n
matrix of the form
X=(\Id_{k}|x_{ij})
, where
\Id_{k}
is the identity matrix.
Since we consider the realizations over characteristic zero, the underlying polynomial ring is over
\mathbb{Q}.

Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
describe the procedure.

Algorithm 1

Computing the realization space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})

1:

A self-projecting matroid
\mathcal{M}
with
[k]
one of the bases

2:

Gröbner bases of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})
, polynomials on which to localize

3:

X=(\Id_{k}|x_{ij})
, where

(x_{ij})_{i\in[k],j\in[n-k]}\in\text{Mat}(k,n-k)

4:

I^{\prime}=\langle\det((x_{ij})_{i\in[k],j\in I})|I\text{ nonbasis of }\mathcal{M}\rangle\subset\mathbb{Q}[x_{ij}:i\in[k],j\in[n-k]]

\rhd

This sets some of the entries
x_{ij}
to zero

5:

Consider the remaining nonzero entries
x_{kl}
in the
k\times(n-k)
matrix of unknowns, and put
x_{kl}-1
into the ideal
I^{\prime}
for the first such nonzero entry in each column and row.

6:

I_{\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})}=(I^{\prime}:\prod_{I\text{ basis of }\mathcal{M}}\det(X_{ij})_{i\in[k],j\in I})^{\infty}

7:

return
Gröbner basis of
I_{\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})}
in the localization of the polynomial ring at
\{\det(x_{ij})_{i\in[k],j\in I}|I\text{ basis of }\mathcal{M}\}

Computing the self-projecting realization space
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})

\mathcal{M}

5
Real and Positive

Real and positive versions of Grassmannians and their subvarieties lie at the heart of applications in the sciences. In this section we formulate some properties and questions regarding the real and positive self-projecting Grassmannian. We fix
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and omit it from the notation.

5.1
Real and positive self-projecting Grassmannian

In the recent years, totally non-negative orthogonal Grassmannians appear in applications in particle physics and cosmology
14
;
22
;
21
. However, as noted in
(
22
, Section 5)
, the structure and topological properties of these semialgebraic sets are not necessarily well-behaved whenever
n>2k
. Even in the
n=2k
case, the totally positive part of the self-dual Grassmannian does not inherit the good behaviour of the orthogonal case, as noted for
\SGr(3,6)
in the context of ABCT varieties
(
1
, Section 5)
.
Note that since
\SGr(k,n)
is not a homogeneous variety, we cannot consider its positive part in the sense of Lusztig. Therefore, we do not expect the totally non-negative self-projecting Grassmannian to behave nicely.

Recall that the real self-projecting Grassmannian is given by

\SGr^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)=\bigcup_{\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}}\OGr_{\mathbb{R}}^{\lambda}(k,n).

(6)

The behavior of
\OGr_{\mathbb{R}}^{\lambda}(k,n)
depends on the choice of
\lambda
. We write
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
for the set of
\lambda\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
for which
\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)\neq\emptyset
. From Witt’s theory, we have the following the following characterization of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
. The real orthogonal Grassmannian
\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
is non-empty if and only if
s(\lambda)\geq k
, where
s(\lambda)
is the signature of
\lambda
, i.e. the minimum between the number of positive and negative entries in
\lambda
.

Recall that the totally non-negative (resp. totally positive) Grassmannian
\Gr_{\geq 0}(k,n)
(resp.
\Gr_{>0}(k,n)
) is the semialgebraic set defined as the intersection of
\Gr(k,n)
in the dual Plücker embedding with the non-negative (resp. positive) orthant of
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}
. Not all non-empty real orthogonal Grassmannians intersect the totally positive part.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose
V\in\Gr_{>0}(k,n)
with
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
. Then there is
S\in\binom{[n]}{2k}
, such that
\lambda|_{S}=\pm((-1)^{i})_{i\in[2k]}
.

Proof.

Let
V\in\Gr_{>0}(k,n)
with
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
. Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
be such that
\lambda
changes sign at positions
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
. Assume by contradiction that
r<2k-1
. If
r<2k-2
define
i_{r+1},\ldots,i_{2k-2}
to be
equal to the last
2k-2-r
elements of
[n]\setminus\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
. Let
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}\}
and
T=\{i_{k},\ldots,i_{2k-2}\}
, we claim that in Equation (
5
) corresponding to this choice of
S,T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
, all the non-zero coefficients have the same sign. Since the Plücker coordinates of
V
are positive by assumption, this implies that Equation (
5
) does not vanish. This contradicts
V\in\OGr^{\lambda}(k,n)
.

Let
i\in[n]\setminus(S\cup T)
. If
i<i_{1}
, then
\lambda_{i}\cdot\sign(S,i)\cdot\sign(T,i)=1
. Suppose
i_{1}<i<i_{2}
, then both
\lambda_{i}
and
\sign(S,i)
will change sign. Therefore, the product remains equal to
1
. Proceeding this way it is possible to show that
\lambda_{i}\cdot\sign(S,i)\cdot\sign(T,i)=1
for all
i\in[n]\setminus(S\cup T)
.
∎

It is possible to restrict the self-projecting Grassmannian to the totally non-negative Grassmannian and obtain a notion of total non-negativity in this case. At the same time, by using Equation (
6
) one can consider the union of all
\OGr^{\lambda}_{\geq 0}(k,n)
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
.

Question 5.3
.

What is the best way to define the totally non-negative self-projecting Grassmannian and how do they relate? Can one show that

\SGr_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)\cap\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}_{\geq 0}=\overline{\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)}\OGr^{\lambda}_{\geq 0}(k,n)}?

Given the current interest in the possibility of defining a positive orthogonal Grassmannian according to Lusztig, we leave it to future research to determine whether it is possible to construct a positive self-projecting Grassmannian from the union of these.

5.2
Self-projecting positroids

Recall that a positroid is a matroid that can be realized by a point in the totally non-negative Grassmannian
\Gr_{\geq 0}(k,n)
. Self-dual positroids where studied in
14
and correspond to fixed point-free involutions. A more general study of positroid cells inside the orthogonal Grassmannian was initiated in
22
. In particular, we recall and generalize their notion of orthopositroids as follows.

Definition 5.4
.

Given a positroid
\mathcal{M}
on
[n]
of rank
k
, with bases
\mathcal{B}
, and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
, consider for all
S,T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
the sets

A^{\pm,\lambda}_{S,T}=\{\ell\in[n]\mid S\cup\ell,T\cup\ell\in\mathcal{B}\text{ and }\lambda_{\ell}\sign(S,\ell)\sign(T,\ell)=\pm 1\}.

We say that
\mathcal{M}
is an
orthopositroid with respect to
\lambda
if for every
S,T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
we have
A^{+,\lambda}_{S,T}=\emptyset
if and only if
A^{-,\lambda}_{S,T}=\emptyset
.

Clearly, in order for any positroid to be realizable by an isotropic totally non-negative space it has to be an orthopositroid. Self-projecting positroids are in general a larger class than orthopositroids with respect to some
\lambda
. The number of isomorphism classes of positroids, self-projecting positroids and orthopositroids with respect to some
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(k,n)
is recorded in
Table

5
. The numbers were computed in
sagemath
and the code is available at the GitHub repository
2
.

Remark 5.5
.

For
(k,n)=(4,9)
, there exists one self-projecting positroid which is not an orthopositroid with respect to any choice of
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}(4,9)
. The positroid
\mathcal{M}
has non-bases
\{1,2,3,4\},\{4,5,6,7\},\{1,7,8,9\}
. In particular this implies that there is no totally non-negative self-projecting realization of this positroid. That
\mathcal{M}
is not an orthopositroid follows from the fact that orthopositroids of type
(k,2k+1)
have a
2k
-subset
S
of the ground set such that the restriction
\mathcal{M}_{S}
is a self-dual positroid. The computation for the realization space of this positroid did not terminate, but it is possible to find self-projecting vector spaces representing the positroid, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}}\neq\emptyset
.

It is interesting to notice that for
k=4
and
n=10
, we recover the equality between self-projecting positroids and orthopositroids.
