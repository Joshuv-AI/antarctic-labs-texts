Abstract
. We characterize the function φ \varphi of minimal L 1 L^{1} norm among all functions f f of exponential type at most π \pi for which f ⁡ ( 0 ) = 1 f(0)=1 . This function, studied by Hörmander and Bernhardsson in 1993, has only real zeros ± τ n \pm\tau_{n} , n = 1 , 2 , … n=1,2,\ldots . Starting from the fact that n + 1 2 − τ n n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n} is an ℓ 2 \ell^{2} sequence, established in an earlier paper of ours, we identify φ \varphi in the following way. We factor φ ⁡ ( z ) \varphi(z) as Φ ⁡ ( z ) ​ Φ ​ ( − z ) \Phi(z)\Phi(-z) , where Φ ⁡ ( z ) = ∏ n = 1 ∞ ( 1 + ( − 1 ) n ​ z τ n ) \Phi(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{\tau_{n}}) and show that Φ \Phi satisfies a certain second order linear differential equation along with a functional equation either of which characterizes Φ \Phi . We use these facts to establish an odd power series expansion of n + 1 2 − τ n n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n} in terms of ( n + 1 2 ) − 1 (n+\frac{1}{2})^{-1} and a power series expansion of the Fourier transform of φ \varphi , as suggested by the numerical work of Hörmander and Bernhardsson. The dual characterization of Φ \Phi arises from a commutation relation that holds more generally for a two-parameter family of differential operators, a fact that is used to perform high precision numerical computations.

1.
Introduction

The Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal function is the unique solution
\varphi
to the extremal problem

(1.1)

\frac{1}{\mathscr{C}}=\inf_{f\in PW^{1}}\left\{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\,:\,f(0)=1\right\},

where

\mathscr{C}
is the smallest positive constant
C
such that the inequality

(1.2)

|f(0)|\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

holds for every
f
in the Paley–Wiener space
PW^{1}
, i.e., the subspace of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
consisting of entire functions of exponential type at most
\pi
. The problem of computing

\mathscr{C}
and identifying
\varphi
has attracted the attention of workers in operator theory
[
3
]
, partial differential equations
[
11
]
, approximation theory
[
1
,
9
]
, orthogonal polynomials
[
14
,
16
]
, and number theory
[
6
]
, and it appears to be of some basic interest in Fourier analysis.

In
[
11
]
, Hörmander and Bernhardsson obtained the impressive numerical approximation

(1.3)

0.5409288219\leq\mathscr{C}\leq 0.5409288220,

but added that, unfortunately, they had not been able to identify the extremal function using
the numerical data, as originally hoped for. Building on our preliminary work performed in
[
4
]
, we will in the present paper remedy this situation and give a comprehensive description of
\varphi
which, as we will see, stands out as a rather remarkable special function.

We start from the fact that

\varphi(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{\tau_{n}^{2}}\right),

with
\tau\coloneqq(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a strictly increasing sequence of positive numbers
(see
[
11
,
14
,
5
]
) and
n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}
belonging to
\ell^{2}
(see

[
4
]
). The main characters in what follows will be the constant
\mathscr{C}
, which can
be expressed as

(1.4)

\mathscr{C}=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n(n+1)}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{\mathscr{C}}=2+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}\Big),

the constant

L_{\tau}(1)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}},

and the entire function

\Phi(z)\coloneqq\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{\tau_{n}}\right).

Note that
\varphi
factors as
\varphi(z)=\Phi(z)\Phi(-z)
and also that
\Phi^{\prime}(0)=L_{\tau}(1)
.

Our main result can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

The function
\Phi
is a solution to the differential equation

(1.5)

z^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(z)+\Big(2z-\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}\Big)f^{\prime}(z)+\Big(\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}z^{2}+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2\mathscr{C}}\Big)f(z)=0

and the functional equation

(1.6)

F(z)e^{\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}}=\frac{e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}+i\frac{\pi}{2}z}F\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)+e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}-i\frac{\pi}{2}z}F\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{2\sqrt{\pi\mathscr{C}}z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}.

Conversely, an entire function solving either (
1.5
) or (
1.6
) is a complex scalar times
\Phi
.

This dual characterization of the function
\Phi
is rather unexpected. Compatibility
of (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) owes itself to the fact that the differential operator from (
1.5
) commutes with an action of the Klein four-group on holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{*}:=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}
. More precisely, the differential operator in question commutes with the two involutions sending
f(z)
to
e^{-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}\pm i\frac{\pi}{2}z}f(\pm\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z})
. These involutions define a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-action on holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and, because of the commutation relation, the equations (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) end up sharing a nonzero solution.

It is worth pointing out a curious similarity with another instance of time-frequency localization, namely the classical Landau–Pollak–Slepian theory of time-and-band limiting and the associated prolate spheroidal wave functions (see
[
18
,
12
,
13
,
17
]
). Of central importance in that subject is the surprising existence of a differential operator—the prolate spheriodal operator—that commutes with the integral operator of time-and-band limiting. We refer to Grünbaum’s commentary
[
10
]
on Connes and Moscovici’s remarkable evolution of this theory
[
7
]
for an overview of the substantial research efforts made to understand and extend the Landau–Pollak–Slepian theory. As in that story, the commutation relation at hand, though easily verified, appears at first glance rather coincidental. While it is crucial to our analysis, we lack for the moment a conceptual explanation for why the commutation occurs in our case.

The differential and functional equations complement each other by giving different kinds of information about

\Phi
and its zeros. The differential equation (
1.5
) shows for example that
all coefficients of the Taylor expansion of
\varphi
are polynomials with rational
coefficients in
\pi^{2}
,
\mathscr{C}
, and
L_{\tau}(1)
(see formula (
3.5
) below).
On the other hand, we use (
1.6
) to obtain a series
expansion for the zeros
\tau_{n}
and to establish strong regularity of the
Fourier transform
\widehat{\varphi}
. The former result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

The numbers
\tau_{n}
can be expanded as

(1.7)

\tau_{n}=n+1/2-\rho\left(\frac{1}{n+1/2}\right),

where
\rho(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m}
is an odd power series whose domain of convergence is
|z|\leq 2
, with
a_{m}\geq 0
for all
m\geq 1
and
\rho(2)=\frac{1}{2}
.

The curious point that
\rho(2)=\frac{1}{2}
suggests the existence of an “invisible” zero at
0
, which is consistent with the appearance of the point
0
in the orthogonality relations used in
[
5
, Lem. 3.3 and Sec. 8.2]
. The coefficients
a_{n}
are also rational functions of
\pi^{2}
,
\mathscr{C}
, and
L_{\tau}(1)
(see formulas (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) below), as can be seen to follow from the differential equation.

To state our result concerning the regularity of
\widehat{\varphi}
, we begin by defining the Fourier transform of an
L^{1}
function
f
as

\widehat{f}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-i\xi x}dx.

With this convention, functions in
PW^{1}
have Fourier transforms supported on
(-\pi,\pi)
.
To place our result in context, we give a brief description of Hörmander and Bernhardsson’s numerical work. The core of their approach
was to use the multivariate version of Newton’s method to solve the original extremal problem in the 4-dimensional subspace of
PW^{1}
consisting of
f
such that on
[-1,1]
,
\widehat{f}(\pi\xi)
is a polynomial in
1-\xi^{2}
of degree at most
4
. The Fourier transform of the corresponding extremal function
f_{\text{HB}}
was thus of the form

\widehat{f}_{\text{HB}}(\pi\xi)=c_{1}(1-\xi^{2})+c_{2}(1-\xi^{2})^{2}+c_{3}(1-\xi^{2})^{3}+c_{4}(1-\xi^{2})^{4},\quad\xi\in[-1,1].

Hörmander and Bernhardsson went even further and computed similar approximations for polynomials in
1-\xi^{2}
of degree at most
6
, but noted that the first 14 decimals of the approximation of
\mathscr{C}
would not change. In spite of the success of their approach, they could not conclude that
f_{\text{HB}}
is a good approximation to
\varphi
. Indeed, Hörmander and Bernhardsson remarked that they had no proof that
\xi\mapsto\widehat{\varphi}(\pi\xi)

is infinitely differentiable in
[-1,1]
, although the fast convergence of the
numerical approximations even suggested analyticity
[
11
, p. 92]
. The following result confirms what Hörmander and Bernhardsson were hinting at.

Theorem 1.3
.

We have

\widehat{\varphi}(\pi\xi)=h(\xi)
for
\xi
in
[-1,1]
, where
h
is the entire function of order
\frac{1}{2}
defined by the power series

(1.8)

h(z)\coloneqq\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\Phi^{2})^{(n-1)}(0)}{(n-1)!n!}\Big(\frac{1-z}{2\mathscr{C}}\Big)^{n}.

Since
h
is an even function, it has an alternate power series representation of the form

h(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}(1-z^{2})^{n}.

In retrospect, we see that Hörmander and Bernhardsson’s work yields approximations to the first four coefficients in this expansion. We may now verify that the decay of the coefficients
c_{n}
is indeed very fast, as it can be quantified by using that
w\mapsto\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}w^{n}
is an entire function of order
\frac{1}{4}
and finite type.

Outline of the paper

In the next section, we deduce from the familiar description of
\varphi
in terms of
L^{1}
orthogonality several alternate characterizations, one of which reveals that

A(z)\coloneqq e^{\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}}\Phi(z)
satisfies the quadratic differential equation

A^{\prime}(z)A(-z)+A^{\prime}(-z)A(z)=-\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}z^{2}}.

This relation opens up the path to the linear differential equation (
1.5
) which is presented in §
3
. The proof of the part of Theorem
1.1
pertaining to that equation is then completed in this section.

The salient features of the differential equation (
1.5
) are best understood by taking the point of view that
-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2\mathscr{C}}
is an eigenvalue and
\Phi
an associated eigenfunction of the second order linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
which acts on
f
by the rule

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(f)(z)\coloneqq z^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(z)+(2z-a)f^{\prime}(z)+b^{2}z^{2}f(z)\,,

with
a=\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}
and
b=\frac{\pi}{2}
. In §
4
, we take a closer look at this family of operators when
ab\neq 0
and establish the important fact that
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
commutes with the two operators
U_{\pm}
defined as follows:

U_{\pm}f(z)=z^{-1}e^{\mp ibz-\frac{a}{2z}}f\Big(\pm\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big).

We identify the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
, in part as preparation for high precision numerical computation of
\mathscr{C}
and
L_{\tau}(1)
.

In §
5
, we turn to the second part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The fact that
\Phi
solves (
1.6
) is an easy consequence of the work done in §
4
. We then apply a fixed point argument to deal with the remaining difficulty of showing that all solutions are multiples of
\Phi
. The next two sections §
6
and §
7
use the functional equation to establish Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, respectively.

In §
8
, we return to one of the consequences of the description of
\varphi
in terms of
L^{1}
orthogonality, namely the summation formula

-\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{2\mathscr{C}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}(f(\tau_{n})-f(-\tau_{n})),

valid for all
f
in
PW^{1}
. We show that we get a similar formula associated with the zeros of each of the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{a,\frac{\pi}{2}}
, and so we obtain an abundance of such formulas.

We describe in §

an algorithm for computing
\mathscr{C}
and

L_{+}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}^{s}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad L_{-}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}^{s}},

the latter of which extends to an entire function and the former to a meromorphic function in
\mathbb{C}
. We have verified numerically to very high precision that
L_{+}(-2k)=(2\pi i\mathscr{C})^{-2k}L_{+}(2k)
for all integers
k
, and the challenge is to prove this relation. The second problem is to verify an integrality phenomenon, namely that the Taylor coefficients of
\varphi
appear to lie in
\mathbb{Z}[\pi,L_{\tau}(1),\mathscr{C}]
. These problems suggest that there may be additional structures related to the Hörmander–Bernhardsson function yet to be disclosed.

2.
Characterizations of the sequence
\tau

We will say that a strictly increasing sequence
t=(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an admissible sequence of positive numbers (or simply an admissible sequence) if all the
t_{n}
are positive and
(n+\frac{1}{2}-t_{n})
is in
\ell^{2}
.
To any admissible sequence
t=(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we associate the two functions

(2.1)

\psi_{t}(z)\coloneqq\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta_{t}(z)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}+\frac{z}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{t_{n}-z}+\frac{1}{t_{n}+z}\Big).

We will now deduce the following characterization of the sequence
\tau
and hence of the extremal function
\varphi=\psi_{\tau}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
t
be an admissible sequence. Then
t=\tau
if and only if

(2.2)

\psi_{t}(z)\Theta_{t}(z)=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}.

The main conclusion of this section, of crucial importance in the sequel, is that Theorem
2.1
can be rephrased as saying that the function

A_{t}(z)\coloneqq\exp\Big(\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}\Big)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{t_{n}}\Big)

satisfies a first order quadratic differential equation when
t=\tau
. Indeed, observing that

\Theta_{t}(z)=\frac{z}{2}\left(\frac{A_{t}^{\prime}(z)}{A_{t}(z)}+\frac{A_{t}^{\prime}(-z)}{A_{t}(-z)}\right)

and using the fact that
\psi_{t}(z)=A_{t}(z)A_{t}(-z)
, we see that Theorem
2.1
can be recast as the following assertion.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
t
be an admissible sequence. Then
t=\tau
if and only if

(2.3)

A_{t}^{\prime}(z)A_{t}(-z)+A_{t}^{\prime}(-z)A_{t}(z)=-\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}z^{2}}.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
will rely on two equivalent characterizations of the sequence
\tau
, the first of which is tied more directly to our original extremal problem (
1.1
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
t
be an admissible sequence. Then
t=\tau
if and only if

(2.4)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\sin(t_{n}\xi)=-\frac{\xi}{4\mathscr{C}}

for
-\pi<\xi<\pi
in the sense of tempered distributions.

Proof.

We start from the fact that

(2.5)

\frac{f(0)}{\mathscr{C}}=\int_{-t_{1}}^{t_{1}}f(x)dx+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(f(x)+f(-x))dx

holds for every
f
in
PW^{1}
if and only if
t=\tau
(see
[
5
, Thm. 3.6]
).
Using that

f(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}dx

and the fact
\mathcal{S}\cap PW^{1}
is dense in
PW^{1}
,
we see by Plancherel’s identity that (
2.5
) holds for all
f
in
PW^{1}
if and only if

(2.6)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\sin t_{n}\xi}{\xi}=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}},\quad-\pi<\xi<\pi,

holds in the sense of tempered distributions. It is clear that (
2.6
) implies (
2.4
). On the other hand, since the distributional Fourier transform of
\mathrm{sgn}\cos(\pi x)

vanishes on
(-\pi,\pi)
(see
[
11
, p. 188]
), we have

(2.7)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\sin\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi}{\xi}=0,\quad-\pi<\xi<\pi.

Hence (
2.4
) implies that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\big(\sin t_{n}\xi-\sin\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi\big)=\sin\frac{\xi}{2}-\frac{\xi}{4\mathscr{C}},\quad-\pi<\xi<\pi.

Setting
d_{n}\coloneqq n+\frac{1}{2}-t_{n}
, we may write the left-hand side of this identity as

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\big(\big(\cos d_{n}\xi-1\big)\sin\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi-\sin d_{n}\xi\cos\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi\big)

from which we see that

\frac{1}{\xi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\big(\sin t_{n}\xi-\sin\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi\big)

is an
L^{2}
function by the assumption that
(d_{n})
is in
\ell^{2}
. This means that (
2.4
) yields

(2.8)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\Big(\frac{\sin t_{n}\xi}{\xi}-\frac{\sin\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)\xi}{\xi}\Big)=\frac{\sin\frac{\xi}{2}}{\xi}-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}},\quad-\pi<\xi<\pi.

Using again (
2.7
), we finally arrive at (
2.6
).
∎

Our second characterization of
\tau
shows that the identity of the preceding lemma can be recast as a reproducing formula for even functions in
PW^{1}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
t
be an admissible sequence. Then
t=\tau
if and only if

(2.9)

f(z)\Theta_{t}(z)=-\frac{f(0)}{4\mathscr{C}z}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{t_{n}z}{(t_{n}^{2}-z^{2})}f(t_{n})

for every even function
f
in
PW^{1}
.

Proof.

We set

L_{t}(2m-1)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t_{n}^{2m-1}}

for
m\geq 1
and
L_{t}(-1)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}}
so that

\Theta_{t}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}L_{t}(2m-1)z^{2m-1}

when
0<|z|<t_{1}
.

We begin by assuming that (
2.4
) holds. We observe that this identity can be written as

L_{t}(1)-\frac{L_{t}(-1)}{2}\xi^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t_{n}}\cos(t_{n}\xi),\quad-\pi<\xi<\pi,

which now holds pointwise, by comparison with Fourier series in terms of
\cos(n+\frac{1}{2})\xi
and
\sin(n+\frac{1}{2})\xi
.
Anti-differentiating iteratively, we get the formula

L_{t}(2m-1)-\frac{L_{t}(2m-3)}{2}\xi^{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{m}\frac{L_{t}(-1)}{(2m)!}\xi^{2m}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t_{n}^{2m-1}}\cos(t_{n}\xi),

which is valid for
-\pi<\xi<\pi
.
Integrating both sides of this identity against an even function
\widehat{f}
in
\mathcal{S}
supported on
[-\pi,\pi]
, we see that

(2.10)

L_{t}(2m-1)f(0)+\frac{L_{t}(2m-3)}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)+\cdots+\frac{L_{t}(-1)}{(2m)!}f^{(2m)}(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t_{n}^{2m-1}}f(t_{n}).

The precaution that
\widehat{f}
in
\mathcal{S}
is only needed when
m=1
, but in any case, this formula holds for all even functions
f
in
PW^{1}
by the Plancherel–Pólya inequality and the fact that
\mathcal{S}\cap PW^{1}
is dense in
PW^{1}
.
We observe that the left-hand side of (
2.10
) is the
(2m-1)
th coefficient of the Laurent expansion of
\Theta_{t}(z)f(z)
about
0
. Since

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t_{n}^{2m-1}}f(t_{n})z^{2m-1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{t_{n}z}{(t_{n}^{2}-z^{2})}f(t_{n}),

we arrive at (
2.9
).

We finally observe that (
2.9
) implies (
2.4
), simply because all steps in the above deduction can be reversed.
∎

Before turning to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we record an approximation result based on standard arguments from the theory of Paley–Wiener spaces. We associate with every admissible sequence
t
a function

\Psi_{t}(z)\coloneqq\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{t_{n}}\Big).

We will let
t^{\ast}
denote the symmetric sequence
(\pm t_{n})
(the zero set of
\psi_{t}
) and
t^{\circ}=((-1)^{n+1}t_{n})
be the zero set of
\Psi_{t}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
t=(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an admissible sequence, then

(2.11)

\Psi_{t}(z)\asymp\frac{e^{\frac{\pi}{2}|y|}}{(1+|z|)}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(z,t^{\circ})}{(1+\operatorname{dist}(z,t^{\circ}))},\quad z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, if
f
is in
PW^{1}
and vanishes on
t^{\ast}
, then
f
is a constant multiple of
\psi_{t}
.

Proof.

We set
d_{n}\coloneqq n+\frac{1}{2}-t_{n}
and express
\Psi_{t}
in the following way:

(2.12)

\Psi_{t}(z)=C\frac{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(z+\frac{1}{2})}{\frac{\pi}{2}(z+\frac{1}{2})}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{d_{n}}{n+\frac{1}{2}+(-1)^{n}z}\right),

where

(2.13)

C\coloneqq\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)}{t_{n}}.

Since
(d_{n})
is in
\ell^{2}
, we see by an application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that the infinite product in (
2.12
) is
\asymp 1
when the distance from
z
to the two sequences
t^{\circ}
and
((-1)^{n+1}(n+\frac{1}{2}))
exceeds, say,
\frac{1}{4}
. Hence the two-sided bound (
2.11
) holds for such

4.
A family of differential operators and a commutation relation

For
a,b
in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, consider the family of linear differential equations

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}f=\lambda f
, where

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(f)(z):=z^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(z)+(2z-a)f^{\prime}(z)+b^{2}z^{2}f(z)\,.

This differential equation has irregular singularities at
0
and at
\infty
and no
other singularities in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. If
R_{k}
denotes the rescaling
(R_{k}f)(z)\coloneqq f(kz)
, then we have
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}R_{k}=R_{k}\mathcal{L}_{ka,k^{-1}b}
, so up to conjugation
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
only depends on the product
ab
. We will also need the computation

(4.2)

e^{\frac{a}{2z}}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(e^{-\frac{a}{2z}}g)=z^{2}g^{\prime\prime}(z)+2zg^{\prime}(z)+\Big(b^{2}z^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{4z^{2}}\Big)g(z)

analogous to (
3.4
).

We will call
\lambda
in
\mathbb{C}
an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
if

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}f=\lambda f
admits a nonzero solution

holomorphic in
a neighborhood of
0
, and we will call a corresponding solution with
f(0)=1
its (normalized)
\lambda
-eigenfunction. We denote by
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{a,b})
the set of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
. The coefficients of the expansion
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\alpha_{n}z^{n}
of any eigenfunction
f

are uniquely determined by the recursion

(4.3)

a(n+1)\alpha_{n+1}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)\alpha_{n}+b^{2}\alpha_{n-2}\,,\qquad n\geq 0\,,

together with the initial condition
\alpha_{0}=1
(we also set
\alpha_{-1}=\alpha_{-2}=0
).
Therefore,
\lambda
is in
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{a,b})
if and only if the sequence

\alpha_{n}
grows at most exponentially. Moreover, a solution that is holomorphic around zero automatically extends to an entire function since
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
has no singularities in

\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

4.1.
Commuting operators and a functional equation

A key property of the differential operators
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
is the following commutation relation.

Lemma 4.1
.

The operators
U_{\pm}=U_{\pm,a,b}
, defined by

U_{\pm}f(z)\coloneqq z^{-1}e^{\mp ibz-\frac{a}{2z}}f\Big(\pm\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big)

commute with
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
.

Proof.

In terms of
g(z)\coloneqq e^{\frac{a}{2z}}f(z)
, the claim is equivalent to the assertion that

g\mapsto z^{2}g^{\prime\prime}+2zg^{\prime}+\Big(b^{2}z^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{4z^{2}}\Big)g

commutes with
g\mapsto z^{-1}g(\pm\frac{ia}{2bz})
. It is easy to check that
g\mapsto z^{2}g^{\prime\prime}+2zg^{\prime}
commutes with any rescaling
R_{k}
as well as with
g\mapsto z^{-1}g(\frac{1}{z})
, so it commutes with
g\mapsto z^{-1}g(\pm\frac{ia}{2bz})
. On the other hand, the mapping
g\mapsto(b^{2}z^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{4z^{2}})g
commutes with
g\mapsto z^{-1}g(\frac{k}{z})
if and only if
a^{2}+4k^{2}b^{2}=0
. Combining these facts, we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Any nonzero eigenfunction
f
of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
satisfies the functional equation

(4.4)

ze^{\frac{a}{2z}}f(z)=\kappa_{+}e^{-ibz}f\Big(\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big)+\kappa_{-}e^{ibz}f\Big(-\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big)

for some nonzero constants
\kappa_{\pm}
with
\kappa_{+}^{2}-\kappa_{-}^{2}=\frac{ia}{2b}
.

Proof.

The three functions
f
,
U_{+}f
, and
U_{-}f
are nonzero solutions of a second order differential equation and thus linearly dependent. It is easy to see that
U_{+}f
and
U_{-}f
cannot be proportional, so the relation can be written in the form (
4.4
) for some constants
\kappa_{\pm}
. Next, if we had
\kappa_{-}=0
, then we would have

e^{ibz}f(z)=\kappa_{+}z^{-1}e^{-\frac{a}{2z}}f\Big(\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big)\,,

and this would imply that
z\mapsto e^{ibz}f(z)
is nonzero, entire, and goes to
0
as
|z|\to\infty
, in conflict with Liouville’s theorem. So
\kappa_{-}\neq 0
, and similarly
\kappa_{+}\neq 0
by the same argument.

Writing
g(z)\coloneqq e^{\frac{a}{2z}}f(z)
, we see that the equation takes the form

zg(z)=\kappa_{+}g\Big(\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big)+\kappa_{-}g\Big(-\frac{ia}{2bz}\Big).

Considering Laurent expansions of both sides, we get for all
n
in
\mathbb{Z}

a_{n-1}=a_{-n}\Big(\frac{ia}{2b}\Big)^{-n}(\kappa_{+}+\kappa_{-}(-1)^{n}),

where
g(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{n}
. Comparing this with the equation for
n\mapsto-(n-1)
and using the fact that not all coefficients

vanish, we get

(4.5)

\kappa_{+}^{2}-\kappa_{-}^{2}=\frac{ia}{2b}\,.\qed

4.2.
Spectrum of

Up to this point we have only seen one eigenfunction of

for

b=\frac{\pi}{2}

We consider
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
as an unbounded operator acting on

. Since
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}

, we may assume that

n(n+1)

L_{a}g(\xi)\coloneqq L_{0}g(\xi)-ia\xi g(\xi),

1
1

1

We remark that

We note that by Bonnet’s recursion formula,

For any

Proof.

Without loss of generality we set

We require

We fix

Since the radii of the Gershgorin discs are
\leq 1

Lemma 4.4

We first note that if

T_{N}
such that

|\lambda_{k}-\lambda|\leq\kappa(V_{N})\frac{\|r_{N}\|}{\|v_{N}\|},

where
V_{N}
is the matrix of eigenvectors of
T_{N}
and
\kappa(V_{N})\coloneqq\|V_{N}\|\|V_{N}^{-1}\|
. The norm of the matrix
V_{N}
grows polynomially in
N

(uniformly for
a
in
[0,3/2-\varepsilon]
and
\varepsilon>0
), and similarly the norm of
V_{N}^{-1}
grows polynomially, which can be obtained
from the left eigenvectors of
T_{N}
. Thus altogether we have exponential
convergence (uniformly in
a
) of the eigenvalue
\mu_{N}(a)

towards
\lambda(a)
, and therefore also continuous dependence on
a
.

As for the corresponding eigenvector, we will use that by the Gershgorin
bounds obtained above, the eigenvalues of
T_{N}
are well separated, i.e.,
there is a
\delta>0
, independent of
N
, such that
|\lambda-\mu|\geq\delta

for any two different eigenvalues of
T_{N}
and any
a
in
[0,3/2-\varepsilon]
.

To approximate eigenvectors, we make the expansion
v_{N}=\sum_{j}c_{j}u_{j}
, where
u_{j}
are the eigenvectors of
T_{N}
and get thus
T_{N}v_{N}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\lambda_{j}u_{j}
. We may assume that
\lambda
is close to some
\lambda_{k}
. Since
T_{N}v_{N}=\lambda v_{N}+r_{N}
, we have
\sum c_{j}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)u_{j}=r_{N}
. For
k\neq j
we have
that
|c_{j}|=\frac{|\langle r_{N},w_{j}\rangle|}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda|}
, where

w_{j}
are the biorthogonal vectors associated to the right eigenvectors, i.e.
the left eigenvectors of
T_{N}
. Finally,
|c_{j}|
decays (uniformly) exponentially in
N
for

k\neq j
.
∎

We close this section by recording a puzzling property of the tridiagonal matrix appearing in the proof of Theorem
4.3
. Arguing as in the proof of Corollary
3.2
, we see that for a
\lambda
-eigenfunction
f
of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
, the function
F(z)=f(z)f(-z)
satisfies the third order differential equation

(z^{2}F)^{\prime\prime\prime}+\Big(4b^{2}z^{2}-4\lambda-\frac{a^{2}}{z^{2}}\Big)F^{\prime}+\Big(8b^{2}z-\frac{4\lambda}{z}\Big)F=0\,.

Writing
F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}z^{2n}
, we get the recursion

u_{n}(2n+2)(2n+1)(2n)+8nb^{2}u_{n-1}-a^{2}(2n+2)u_{n+1}=4\lambda u_{n}(1+2n),

which can be rewritten as

\lambda u_{n}=n(n+1)u_{n}+2b^{2}\frac{n}{2n+1}u_{n-1}-\frac{a^{2}}{2}\frac{n+1}{2n+1}u_{n+1}.

Surprisingly, we recognize on the right-hand side the transpose of the tridiagonal matrix
T
. In particular, setting
b=1
and
u_{n}=\frac{\xi_{n}(-2/a)^{n}}{2n+1}
, we get

\lambda\xi_{n}=n(n+1)\xi_{n}-a\big(\frac{n}{2n-1}\xi_{n-1}-\frac{n+1}{2n+3}\xi_{n+1}\big),

which is exactly (
4.6
). Thus we see that
\widehat{f}(\xi)=c\sum_{n\geq 0}(2n+1)(-ia/2)^{n}u_{n}P_{n}(\xi)
,
\xi\in(-1,1)
, for some constant
c
(in fact,
c=\pi^{-1}
, since
\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}(\xi)d\xi=2\delta_{n,0}
).

5.
The functional equation of Theorem
1.1

It remains to establish the second part of Theorem
1.1
, namely the claim that the solutions of the functional equation (
1.6
) are complex scalars times
\Phi
. We split the proof into two parts.

Proof that
\Phi
satisfies (
1.6
).

By (
1.5
) that we already proved in §
3
, we see that
\Phi
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
for
a=\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}
,
b=\frac{\pi}{2}
, with eigenvalue
\lambda=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2\mathscr{C}}
. Therefore
\Phi
satisfies (
4.4
) for some values
\kappa_{+}
and
\kappa_{-}
. Since
\Phi
is real-valued on
\mathbb{R}
, we see that
\kappa_{-}=\overline{\kappa_{+}}
by taking complex conjugation on both sides of (
4.4
). Setting
z=\tau_{2n-1}
, we find that

\kappa_{+}e^{-i\pi\tau_{2n-1}/2}+\kappa_{-}e^{i\pi\tau_{2n-1}/2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,
and since
\tau_{2n-1}=2n-1/2+o(1)
, this is equivalent to

\kappa_{+}e^{i\pi/4}+\kappa_{-}e^{-i\pi/4}=0.

Next, since
\Phi(0)=1
and positive zeros of
\Phi
are
\{\tau_{2n-1}\}_{n\geq 1}
, the number
\Phi(\tau_{2n})
has sign
(-1)^{n}
for large
n
. Setting
z=\tau_{2n}
in (
4.4
) and taking limits, we get

\kappa_{+}e^{-i\pi/4}+\kappa_{-}e^{i\pi/4}>0,

so that
\kappa_{\pm}=e^{\pm i\pi/4}\kappa
for some
\kappa>0
. Plugging these values into (
4.5
), we get
\kappa^{2}=\frac{a}{4b}
, and so
\kappa=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathscr{C}}}
. With these computations, we obtain precisely (
1.6
) by dividing (
4.4
) by
z
. ∎

Proof that all solutions to (
1.6
) are constant multiples of
\Phi
.

We assume to the contrary that
F
is a nontrivial entire function satisfying

F(z)e^{\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}}=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(z-\frac{1}{2})}F\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)+e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(z-\frac{1}{2})}F\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{2\sqrt{\pi\mathscr{C}}z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast},

with
F
not being a multiple of
\Phi
. We will show that this assumption leads to a contradiction.

We may assume that
F
is a real entire function satisfying
F(0)=0
. This follows from the observation that also
F-F(0)\Phi
and
F+F^{\ast}
(with
F^{\ast}(z)\coloneqq\overline{F(\overline{z})}
) satisfy the functional equation.
We start from the fact that the function

G_{\varepsilon}(z)\coloneqq\Phi(z)+\varepsilon F(z)

also satisfies the functional equation for every complex
\varepsilon
. The functional equation entails that
F
must be of exponential type
\frac{\pi}{2}
and that

(5.1)

F(z)=O\Big(\frac{e^{\frac{\pi}{2}|y|}}{|z|^{2}}\Big),\quad z\to\infty.

It follows from Rouché’s theorem and (
5.1
) that we may write

G_{\varepsilon}(z)=(1+\varepsilon F(0))\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{z}{t_{n}(\varepsilon)}\Big),

with
t_{n}(\varepsilon)\to\tau_{n}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
, uniformly in
n
. We will henceforth assume that
\varepsilon
is real and small enough, so that
(t_{n}(\varepsilon))
is a sequence of real numbers.

Now the functional equation yields the system of equations

e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(-1)^{n}d_{n}(\varepsilon)}F\big((-1)^{n+1}\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}t_{n}(\varepsilon)}\big)-e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(-1)^{n}d_{n}(\varepsilon)}F\big((-1)^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}t_{n}(\varepsilon)}\big)=0,

where
d_{n}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq n+\frac{1}{2}-t_{n}(\varepsilon)
.
Hence

i\pi(-1)^{n}d_{n}(\varepsilon)=\log F\big((-1)^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}t_{n}(\varepsilon)}\big)-\log F\big((-1)^{n+1}\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}t_{n}(\varepsilon)}\big).

It follows that for
\varepsilon
small enough,
(d_{n}(\varepsilon))
is a fixed point of the mapping
K
defined by the formula

\displaystyle(K\xi)_{n}\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\pi i}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\Big(

\displaystyle\log\Big(1+\frac{(-1)^{n+m}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(n+\frac{1}{2}-\xi_{n})(m+\frac{1}{2}-\xi_{m})}\Big)

\displaystyle-\log\Big(1+\frac{(-1)^{n+m+1}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(n+\frac{1}{2}-\xi_{n})(m+\frac{1}{2}-\xi_{m})}\Big)\Big).

This means that we have

(5.2)

Kd(\varepsilon)-K\delta=d(\varepsilon)-\delta,

where
\delta_{n}\coloneqq d_{n}(0)=n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}
. On the other hand, by the mean value theorem,

(5.3)

(Kd(\varepsilon))_{n}-(K\delta)_{n}=\nabla(K\xi)_{n}\big|_{\xi=c\delta+(1-c)d(\varepsilon)}\cdot\big(d(\varepsilon)-\delta\big)

for some
0<c<1
, where the dot signifies inner product between real sequences. Since
d(\varepsilon)\neq\delta
for all small
\varepsilon
, (
5.3
) will contradict (
5.2
) if we can show that the
\ell^{1}
norms of the gradients in (
5.3
) are uniformly
<1
when
\varepsilon
is small enough.

Since
t_{n}(\varepsilon)\to\tau_{n}
uniformly in
n
, it suffices to estimate the gradients at
\varepsilon=0
. We compute and get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(K\xi)_{n}}{\partial\xi_{n}}\big|_{\xi=\delta}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\Bigg(\frac{4\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}^{2}+1}-\frac{4\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}^{2}-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m\neq n}\Big(\frac{2\mathscr{C}\tau_{m}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}\tau_{m}+(-1)^{n+m}}-\frac{2\mathscr{C}\tau_{m}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}\tau_{m}-(-1)^{n+m}}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=4\mathscr{C}(-1)^{n+1}\Bigg(\frac{(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}}{4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}\tau_{n}^{4}+1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}\tau_{m}}{4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}\tau_{n}^{2}\tau_{m}^{2}+1}\Bigg).

By a similar computation, we find that when
m\neq n
,

\Big|\frac{\partial(K\xi)_{n}}{\partial\xi_{m}}\big|_{\xi=\delta}\Big|=\frac{8\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}}{4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}\tau_{n}^{2}\tau_{m}^{2}+1}.

Hence

\big\|\nabla(K\xi)_{n}\big|_{\xi=\delta}\big\|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\frac{16\mathscr{C}\tau_{1}}{4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}\tau_{1}^{4}+1}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{8\mathscr{C}\tau_{1}}{4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}\tau_{m}^{2}+1},

which is
<0.5
by a crude estimation. We have thus obtained the desired conflict between (
5.3
) and (
5.2
).
∎

The preceding proof shows that the linear space of
entire functions that satisfy (
4.4
) is one-dimensional in the special case when
a=\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}
,
b=\frac{\pi}{2}
, and
\kappa_{\pm}=\frac{e^{\pm i\pi/4}}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathscr{C}}}
.
It would be desirable to find an alternate proof that would allow us to conclude that this linear space is at most one-dimensional for general
a
,
b
, and
\kappa_{\pm}
satisfying the admissibility condition
\kappa_{+}^{2}-\kappa_{-}^{2}=\frac{ia}{2b}
. Since this solution space is invariant under
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
by the commutation relation of Lemma
4.1
, we would then be able to infer that all entire solutions of (
4.4
) would also be eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
.

6.
Power series expansion of
\tau_{n}

We will now use the functional equation (
1.6
) to establish the power series expansion of
\tau_{n}
proclaimed in Theorem
1.2
.
We begin by observing that (
1.6
) along with the fact that

\arg\Phi(iy)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\arctan\frac{y}{\tau_{n}},

implies that

(6.1)

n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m+1}\arctan\frac{1}{2\pi\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}\tau_{m}},\quad n\geq 1.

Now using the expansion

\arctan z=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m+1}\frac{z^{2m-1}}{2m-1},

we may rewrite (
6.1
) as

n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{L_{\tau}(2m-1)}{(2m-1)(2\pi\mathscr{C}\tau_{n})^{2m-1}},\quad n\geq 1.

Introducing next the auxiliary series

g(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi i}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{2L_{\tau}(2m-1)z^{2m-1}}{2m-1}\,,\qquad 0<|z|<\tau_{1}\,,

we see that the latter identity can be written as

g\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}}\Big)=n+\frac{1}{2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots\,.

We define a power series
\rho(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m}
by the implicit equation

(6.2)

g\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(1/z-\rho(z))}\Big)=\frac{1}{z}\,,\qquad|z|<\varepsilon.

Indeed, this equation simply asserts that
z\mapsto 1/g(z)
is inverse to
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(1/z-\rho(z))}
in a small neighborhood of the origin, so
\rho
is well-defined and is an odd function. In what follows, given a function
f
analytic in a small punctured neighborhood of the origin, we denote by
[z^{n}]f(z)
the coefficient of
z^{n}
in the Laurent series of
f
at
0
.

Theorem 6.1
.

We have
\rho(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m}
, where

a_{m}=[z]\frac{g(z)^{m}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}m}\quad\text{and}\quad a_{m}\geq 0,\quad m\geq 1.

This power series converges if and only if
|z|\leq 2
.

It is immediate from this theorem and the preceding discussion that
we now have the power series representation

\tau_{n}=n+1/2-\rho\Big(\frac{1}{n+1/2}\Big)=n+1/2-\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{2m-1}(n+1/2)^{1-2m}

for all
n\geq 1
, and so we obtain Theorem
1.2
as a corollary.
Note that
\rho
is an odd function, so
a_{m}
vanish for even
m
. The first few nontrivial relations between
a_{m}
and
L_{\tau}(2m-1)
are

(6.3)

\pi^{2}a_{1}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{\mathscr{C}}\,,\ \ \pi^{4}a_{3}=\frac{L_{\tau}(1)^{2}}{\mathscr{C}^{2}}+\frac{L_{\tau}(3)}{12\mathscr{C}^{3}}\,,\ \ \pi^{6}a_{5}=-2\frac{L_{\tau}(1)^{3}}{\mathscr{C}^{3}}-\frac{L_{\tau}(3)L_{\tau}(1)}{3\mathscr{C}^{4}}-\frac{L_{\tau}(5)}{80\mathscr{C}^{5}}\,.

From (
2.2
) it follows that
L_{\tau}(2k-1)
is an element in
\mathbb{Q}[\mathscr{C},L_{\tau}(1),\pi]
. For instance,

\displaystyle L_{\tau}(3)

\displaystyle=24L_{\tau}(1)\mathscr{C}^{2}+(\pi^{2}/2+2L_{\tau}(1)^{2})\mathscr{C}\,,

\displaystyle L_{\tau}(5)

\displaystyle=1920L_{\tau}(1)\mathscr{C}^{4}+(40\pi^{2}+448L_{\tau}(1)^{2})\mathscr{C}^{3}+(2L_{\tau}(1)\pi^{2}+8L_{\tau}(1)^{3})\mathscr{C}^{2}.

Therefore, the coefficients
a_{m}
can also be written in this way:

(6.4)

\displaystyle\pi^{4}a_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{48L_{\tau}(1)\mathscr{C}+\pi^{2}+28L_{\tau}(1)^{2}}{24\mathscr{C}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\pi^{6}a_{5}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2880L_{\tau}(1)\mathscr{C}^{2}+(60\pi^{2}+1632L_{\tau}(1)^{2})\mathscr{C}+(23L_{\tau}(1)\pi^{2}+332L_{\tau}(1)^{3})}{120\mathscr{C}^{3}}\,,

and so on.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

The first claim is simply a form of Lagrange’s inversion theorem, and can be seen directly from the following residue calculation:

a_{m}=[z^{m}]\rho(z)=-\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\frac{1/z-\rho(z)}{z^{m+1}}dz=-\mathrm{res}_{w=0}\frac{g(w)-\rho(1/g(w))}{g(w)^{-m-1}}d(1/g(w))\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}m}\mathrm{res}_{w=0}w^{-1}d(g(w)^{m})=\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}m}\mathrm{res}_{w=0}w^{-2}g(w)^{m}dw=[z]\frac{g(z)^{m}}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}m}\,.

As an immediate consequence, we see that
\rho(u)
has the representation

(6.5)

\rho(u)=-\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}\mathscr{C}}\oint_{\gamma}\log(1-ug(z))\frac{dz}{z^{2}}\,

for small values of
u
, where
\gamma
is any path around zero on which
|g(z)|
is bounded from above. In view of (
6.5
), it suffices to find a path
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
around
0
along which
|g(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
to prove that the radius of convergence
R
of
\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m}
is at least
2
. By symmetry, it will be enough to prescribe this path as we move in the first quadrant, from some point on the positive real axis to a point on the positive imaginary axis. We will use the identity

(6.6)

\exp(\pi ig(z))=\frac{A(z)}{A(-z)}\,,\qquad 0<|z|<\tau_{1}\,,

which defines
g(z)
in the punctured strip

\{x+iy:\ -\tau_{1}<x<\tau_{1}\}\setminus\{0\}
. By (
1.6
), we have

\frac{A(z)}{A(-z)}=-i\left(\frac{1-iw}{1+iw}\right),

where

(6.7)

w(z)\coloneqq e^{i\pi z}\frac{\Phi\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{\Phi\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}.

We see that
|w(z)|<1
when
z=x+iy
satisfies
x\geq 0
and
y>0
, whence
\frac{1-iw(z)}{1+iw(z)}
lies in the right half-plane. Since
g(x)
takes imaginary values for real
x
, we find from (
6.6
) that

\operatorname{Im}\pi ig(z)
lies in the lower half-plane.

We now prove that there exists a curve
\gamma^{+}
in the first quadrant along which
\operatorname{Re}w(z)<0
and
\operatorname{Im}w(z)=0
, with a parameterization of the form

\gamma^{+}(y)=\Xi(y)+iy,\quad\frac{1}{2}<\Xi(y)<1,\ y\geq 0.

To this end, we begin by noting that for
\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq 1
and
y\geq 0
, the sequence

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\coloneqq\arg

\displaystyle\left(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}2\pi i\mathscr{C}(x+iy)}\right)-\arg\left(1-(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}2\pi i\mathscr{C}(x+iy)}\right)

\displaystyle=\arg\left(1-(-1)^{n}\frac{y+ix}{\tau_{n}2\pi\mathscr{C}(x^{2}+y^{2})}\right)-\arg\left(1+(-1)^{n}\frac{y+ix}{\tau_{n}2\pi\mathscr{C}(x^{2}+y^{2})}\right)

is alternating with
0<\kappa_{1}<\frac{\pi}{2}
and
|\kappa_{n}|\searrow 0
. Hence

0<\arg\frac{A(x+iy)}{A(-x-iy)}<\frac{\pi}{2},\quad\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq 1,\ y\geq 0.

In view of (
6.7
), this means that for every
y\geq 0
, there exists a number
\Xi(y)
between
\frac{1}{2}
and
1
such that
\arg w\big(\Xi(y)+iy\big)=\pi
. We have thus verified the existence of the curve
\gamma^{+}
.

We see that
g(z)
is real for
z
in
\gamma^{+}
and in fact

(6.8)

-\frac{1}{2}\leq g(z)<0,\quad z\in\gamma^{+}.

To finish the proof that
R\geq 2
, we choose a sufficiently large
Y
such that
|g(x+iY)|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
for
x+iY
with
0\leq x\leq\Xi(Y)
, and we let
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
intersected with the first quadrant be the curve parametrized as
\Xi(y)+iy
for
0\leq y<Y
and
x+iY
for
0\leq x\leq x(Y)
.

To prove that
R=2
, we assume that
R>2
and show that this leads to a contradiction. First, since
g(-iy)\to\frac{1}{2}
when
y\to\infty
, we have that
\rho(x)\to\frac{1}{2}
when
x\to 2
. Then by our assumption that
R>2
,

\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{\rho(x)-\frac{1}{2}}{x-2}

exists and takes a finite value, and this is equivalent to the assertion that

\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{\rho(x)-\frac{1}{x}}{x-2}

exists and takes a finite value. But since
z\mapsto\frac{1}{g(z)}
is inverse to
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(1/z-\rho(z))}
, we have

\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{\rho(x)-\frac{1}{x}}{x-2}=\lim_{y\to\infty}\frac{y}{2\pi\mathscr{C}(\frac{1}{g(-iy)}-2)}.

Here the limit to the right cannot be finite because
g(-iy)=\frac{1}{2}+O(e^{-\pi y})
, and so our assumption that
R>2
cannot be true.

The preceding argument shows that

(6.9)

\rho(x)-\frac{1}{2}=O\Big(\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{2-x}}\Big)

when
x\to 2^{-}
, and so
\rho(2)=\frac{1}{2}
. Absolute convergence of the power series on its radius of convergence will therefore follow once we have shown that
a_{m}\geq 0
for all
m\geq 1
.
To this end, we note that it suffices to consider the case when
m
is odd, since
\rho
is an odd function. We start from the formula

a_{m}=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\mathscr{C}m}\oint_{\gamma}\,[g(z)]^{m}\frac{dz}{z^{2}},

where
\gamma
is again a suitable path around zero. We let
\gamma^{+}
be as above and find that, by symmetry,

a_{m}=-\frac{4}{(2\pi)^{2}\mathscr{C}m}\operatorname{Re}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\,[g(z)]^{m}\frac{dz}{z^{2}},

which can be expressed as

a_{m}=-\frac{4}{(2\pi)^{2}\mathscr{C}m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,[g(\Xi(y)+iy)]^{m}\big(2y\Xi(y)+\Xi^{\prime}(y)(\Xi^{2}(y)-y^{2})\big)\frac{dy}{|\Xi(y)+iy|^{4}}.

Since
[g(\Xi(y)+iy)]^{m}<0
by (
6.8
), we are done if we can show that

(6.10)

\frac{\Xi^{\prime}(y)}{\Xi(y)}(y^{2}-\Xi^{2}(y))\leq 2y

holds for all
y>0
. To prove (
6.10
), we start from the equation

\arg w(\Xi(y)+iy)=\pi,

which by implicit differentiation and (
6.7
) yields

\pi\Xi^{\prime}(y)=\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{\Phi\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{\Phi\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}\right)\frac{(\Xi^{\prime}(y)+i)}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}}.

Using the definition of
\Phi
, we find that

\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{\Phi\big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}{\Phi\big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\big)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(x+iy)}+(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}}+\frac{1}{\frac{-1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}(x+iy)}+(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}}\Big),

and so

\pi\Xi^{\prime}(y)=-\operatorname{Im}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4\pi i\mathscr{C}(-1)^{n}(\Xi^{\prime}(y)+i)}{(\tau_{n}^{2}4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}+1)}.

Hence

\frac{\Xi^{\prime}(y)}{\Xi(y)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{32(-1)^{n+1}\tau_{n}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{3}y}{|\tau_{n}^{2}4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}+1|^{2}}\cdot\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4(-1)^{n}\mathscr{C}\big(4\tau_{n}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi^{2}(y)-y^{2})+1\big)}{|\tau_{n}^{2}4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}+1|^{2}}\right)^{-1}.

We see from this expression that
\Xi^{\prime}(y)>0
for all
y>0
, and so (
6.10
) holds trivially when
0<y\leq\Xi(y)
. Furthermore, when
y\geq\Xi(y)
, the same expression yields

\displaystyle\frac{\Xi^{\prime}(y)}{\Xi(y)}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{32(-1)^{n+1}\tau_{n}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{3}y}{|\tau_{n}^{2}4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}+1|^{2}}\leq\frac{32\tau_{1}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{3}y}{|\tau_{1}^{2}4\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\Xi(y)+iy)^{2}+1|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\tau_{1}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}\Xi^{2}(y)y}\leq\frac{2}{\tau_{1}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}y},

where we in the last step used that
\Xi(y)>\frac{1}{2}
. Since clearly
\tau_{1}^{2}\pi^{2}\mathscr{C}\geq 1
(
\tau_{1}>1
by the main result of
[
4
]
and
\mathscr{C}>1/2
by (
1.3
)), we conclude that (
6.10
) holds for all
y>0
.
∎

7.
Behavior of the Fourier transform of
\varphi

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
We start with a lemma that will allow us to compute the Fourier transform of eigenfunctions of the differential operators
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
from §
4
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
b>0
and
f,g,h
be three entire functions satisfying

zf(z)=e^{ibz}g(1/z)+e^{-ibz}h(1/z).

Then
\widehat{f}
is supported in
[-b,b]
, and on
[-b,b]
it is an analytic function whose Taylor expansions at the endpoints are given by

\widehat{f}(\xi)=2\pi i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(n)}(0)}{n!^{2}}(i(b-\xi))^{n}=-2\pi i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{h^{(n)}(0)}{n!^{2}}(-i(b+\xi))^{n}\,.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{+}
be a path in
\mathbb{C}
going from
-\infty
to
-1
, then from
-1
to
1
along a semi-circle in the upper half-plane, and finally from
1
to
\infty
, and let
\gamma_{-}
be a similar path going along a semi-circle in the lower half-plane instead. A standard contour integral calculation shows that for any entire function
F
with
F(0)=0
we have

\int_{\gamma_{\pm}}e^{i\lambda z}F(1/z)dz=\begin{cases}0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\pm\lambda>0,\\
\mp 2\pi i\,\mathrm{res}_{z=0}e^{i\lambda z}F(1/z),\qquad\pm\lambda<0.\end{cases}

(If
F^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, then the integral on the left should be taken as a limit over symmetric truncations of
\gamma
.)
Since
\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\gamma_{\pm}}f(x)e^{-i\xi x}dx=\int_{\gamma_{\pm}}(e^{ibx}g(1/x)+e^{-ibx}h(1/x))e^{-i\xi x}\frac{dx}{x}
, applying the above observation shows that
\widehat{f}
is supported in
[-b,b]
, and for
\xi
in
(-b,b)
we have

\widehat{f}(\xi)=-2\pi i\,\mathrm{res}_{z=0}e^{-i(b+\xi)z}z^{-1}h(1/z)=2\pi i\,\mathrm{res}_{z=0}e^{i(b-\xi)z}z^{-1}g(1/z),

which implies the claim upon computing the residues.
∎

Applying this lemma to (
4.4
), we obtain the following.

Corollary 7.2
.

For any
b>0
and any eigenfunction
f
of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
, the Fourier transform of
f
is supported in
[-b,b]
, and is equal to a restriction of an analytic function. Moreover, the numbers
\kappa_{\pm}
can be computed from
\kappa_{\pm}=\pm 2\pi i\widehat{f}(\mp b)
.

We also get the desired regularity of the Fourier transform of the Hörmander–Bernhardsson function.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Multiplying (
1.6
) by the same identity after substitution

z\mapsto-z
and using the fact that
\varphi(z)=\Phi(z)\Phi(-z)
, we get

(7.1)

-4\pi\mathscr{C}z^{2}\varphi(z)=e^{\pi iz}\Phi^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\Big)+e^{-\pi iz}\Phi^{2}\Big(-\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}z}\Big)\,.

By Lemma
7.1
,

-\widehat{\varphi}(\pi\xi)=2\pi i\,\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\frac{e^{\pi i(1-\xi)z}}{4\pi\mathscr{C}z^{2}}\Phi^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi i\mathscr{C}x}\Big),

which after a short calculation implies (
1.8
).
∎

8.
Summation formulas

We now return to
(
2.4
)
which we reformulate as a summation
formula valid for all
f
in
PW^{1}
in terms of the values at the zeros

\pm\tau_{n}
of
\varphi
:

-\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{2\mathscr{C}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}(f(\tau_{n})-f(-\tau_{n})).

The observation to be made in this section is that any eigenfunction of the differential operator

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
defined in (
4.1
) yields an
analogous summation formula. We set for convenience
b=\pi/2
so that all formulas pertain to functions of exponential type at most
\pi
. For an entire function
g
, we let
\mathcal{Z}(g)
denote its zero set, with multiplicites accounted for in the usual way.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
a
be a nonzero complex number and
g
an eigenfunction of

\mathcal{L}_{a,\frac{\pi}{2}}
. Then for every

f
in
PW^{1}
we have

(8.1)

af^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{Z}(g)}\big(f(\mu)-f(-\mu)\big).

We may take note of the following subtlety at this point. If
a
were positive and

g(z)g(-z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\right)

with
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots
, we may deduce from (
8.1
) a counterpart to (
2.5
) of the form

(8.2)

2af(0)+c\widehat{f}(0)=\int_{-t_{1}}^{t_{1}}f(x)dx+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(f(x)+f(-x))dx

for some real constant
c
. We will then have
c\neq 0
if and only if
a=\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}
and
g=\Phi
. Indeed, if
c=0
, (
8.2
) would imply that
g=\Phi
by the characterization of

\varphi
in terms of
L^{1}
orthogonality (see
[
5
, Thm. 3.6]
). The fortuitous fact used in the proof of Lemma
2.3
to show that (
8.1
) would imply
c=0
for admissible sequences
(t_{n})
, is that such sequences are at finite
\ell^{2}
distance from the sign changes of
\mathrm{sgn}\cos(\pi x)
which in the terminology of
[
15
]
is a real extremal signature for
PW^{1}
, i.e., its Fourier transform vanishes on
(-\pi,\pi)
. We may conclude that for no other eigenfunction
g
with
a>0
and real simple zeros, is it possible to find a real extremal signature for
PW^{1}
with
sign changes similarly close to
\pm\mathcal{Z}(g)
.

The following observation allows us to connect eigenfunctions of

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
with the kind of summation formulas appearing in §
2
.

Lemma 8.2
.

If
g(z)
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
, normalized by

g(0)=1
, then the function

A(z)=e^{\frac{a}{2z}}g(z)
satisfies

(8.3)

A^{\prime}(z)A(-z)+A^{\prime}(-z)A(z)=-\frac{a}{z^{2}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

Proof.

By (
4.2
), the functions
A(z)
and
A(-z)
are linearly independent and both satisfy the differential equation

z^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(z)+2zh^{\prime}(z)+\Big(b^{2}z^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{4z^{2}}\Big)h(z)=\lambda h(z)

Therefore their Wronskian is a constant multiple of
z^{-2}
. The value of
the constant can be determined by a direct calculation using the fact that

g(0)=1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

We adopt the notation of §
2
and write

\Theta(z)\coloneqq\frac{z}{2}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}(z)}{A(z)}+\frac{A^{\prime}(-z)}{A(-z)}\right)

and

\psi(z)\coloneqq A(z)A(-z)=\prod_{\mu\in\mathcal{Z}(g)}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right).

Then (
8.3
) implies that

\psi(z)\Theta(z)=-\frac{a}{2z}\,,

and it follows that

(8.4)

f(z)\Theta(z)=-\frac{af(0)}{z}+\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{Z}(g)}\frac{\mu z}{(\mu^{2}-z^{2})}f(\mu)

for every
f
in
PW^{1}
. Indeed, we set

F(z):=f(z)\Theta(z)+\frac{af(0)}{z}-\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{Z}(g)}\frac{\mu z}{(\mu^{2}-z^{2})}f(\mu)

and see exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
that
\psi F
is in
PW^{1}
and then that
F\equiv 0
.

We finally observe that all the arguments in the proof of Lemma
2.4
can be
reversed, and from (
8.4
) we obtain

\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{Z}(g)}\sin(\mu\xi)=\frac{a\xi}{2}

in the distributional sense for all
-\pi<\xi<\pi
. Using again that
\mathcal{S}\cap PW^{1}
is dense in
PW^{1}
, we
arrive at (
8.1
) by Plancherel’s identity.
∎

9.
Numerics

Although the differential equation (
1.5
) completely determines
\Phi
(and hence
\varphi
), one needs to know the values
\mathscr{C}
and
L_{\tau}(1)
to start computing with it. Moreover, the recursion (
4.3
) is not numerically stable and to compute coefficients
[z^{n}]\Phi(z)
for very large values of
n
, one needs to know the initial parameters
\mathscr{C}
and
L_{\tau}(1)
to high precision. From this datum, together with (
1.6
), (
1.7
), (
7.1
), and (
1.8
), one can then compute any reasonable quantities related to
\varphi
and
\Phi
.

We now outline how one may compute
\mathscr{C}
and
L_{\tau}(1)
to any desired precision, exemplified by the 100 digit precision exhibited in (
1.9
).
We begin by giving a characterization of
\mathscr{C}
and
L_{\tau}(1)
in terms of the family of differential equations
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
a,b>0
, assume that
f
is a
\lambda
-eigenfunction
f
of
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
whose zeros are real and simple, and let
\kappa_{\pm}
associated with
f
be defined as in (
4.4
). Then the condition

(9.1)

\kappa_{+}e^{i\pi/4}+\kappa_{-}e^{-i\pi/4}=0

is equivalent to
ab=\frac{\pi}{4\mathscr{C}}
, in which case
\lambda=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2\mathscr{C}}
.

Proof.

The conditions are invariant under rescaling
(a,b)\mapsto(a/k,bk)
, so we may assume that
b=\pi/2
. Then as in the proof of (
1.6
), by (
4.4
) the condition (
9.1
) is equivalent to the set of zeros of
f(z)
being close to
2n-1/2+o(1)
,
n\to\pm\infty
. Then the summation formula from Theorem
8.1
involves an admissible sequence, and so we obtain (
8.2
) with
c=0
. As already noted in our discussion of (
8.2
), this implies that
f=\Phi
.
∎

It will be convenient to fix
b=1
and use the same notation as in §
4.2
.
By Corollary
7.2
, we have
\kappa_{\pm}=\pm 2\pi i\widehat{f}(\mp 1)
, so (
9.1
) is equivalent to

(9.2)

\widehat{f}(1)=i\widehat{f}(-1)

and since Legendre polynomials satisfy
P_{n}(\pm 1)=(\pm 1)^{n}
, and
\widehat{f}=\sum_{n\geq 0}i^{n}\xi_{n}P_{n}
, for
\xi
in
(-1,1)
, we may recast (
9.2
) as

\sum_{n\geq 0}i^{n}\xi_{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}i^{1-n}\xi_{n},

or equivalently, as

(9.3)

\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\xi_{n}=0.

Here we normalize
f
by setting
\xi_{0}=1
. Therefore, to compute
\mathscr{C}
(and
L_{\tau}(1)/(2\mathscr{C})
), it suffices to find
a
and
\lambda
for which an eigenvector
\xi
of the tridiagonal matrix
T
with entries (
4.7
) in addition satisfies (
9.3
). The computation is now as follows: for each truncation
T_{N}
of
T
we find the value of
0<a<3/2
for which the smallest eigenvector satisfies
\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\xi_{n}=0
, and then take the limit as
N\to\infty
. Lemma
4.4
then guarantees exponentially fast convergence of
a
to
\frac{\pi}{4\mathscr{C}}
, and
\lambda
to
-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2\mathscr{C}}
. Indeed, we get existence of an intermediate value by verifying that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\xi_{n}
changes sign when
a
goes from
1.44
to
1.46
. Moreover, it is clear that Lemma
9.1
applies for
a
in this range, since
f
and hence the zeros of
f
depend continuously on
a
, and the zeros of
\Phi
are well separated.

10.
Final remarks

10.1.
Two formulas for
\mathscr{C}

We now establish the two identities in (
1.4
). We begin by noting that the second formula

\frac{1}{\mathscr{C}}=2+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}\Big)

follows at once if we set
\xi=0
in (
2.8
). To prove the product formula in (
1.4
), we start from
Theorem
2.1
which shows that

\varphi^{\prime}(\tau_{n})=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}^{2}},

and so

\prod_{m\neq n}\left|1-\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{\tau_{m}^{2}}\right|=\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}\tau_{n}}.

On the other hand, setting

D\coloneqq\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{\tau_{n}^{2}},

we see that

\prod_{m\neq n}\left|1-\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{\tau_{m}^{2}}\right|=\frac{D\tau_{n}^{2}}{(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}\left|1-\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}\right|^{-1}(4\tau_{n}^{2}-1)^{-1}|\cos(\pi\tau_{n})|\prod_{m\neq n}\left|\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}}{(m+\frac{1}{2})^{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}}\right|,

so that

\frac{1}{4D\mathscr{C}}=\frac{\tau_{n}^{3}}{(\tau_{n}+n+\frac{1}{2})(4\tau_{n}^{2}-1)}\frac{|\cos(\pi\tau_{n})|}{|n+\frac{1}{2}-\tau_{n}|}\prod_{m\neq n}\left|\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}}{(m+\frac{1}{2})^{2}-\tau_{n}^{2}}\right|.

Letting
n
tend to
\infty
, we arrive at the formula

\frac{2}{\pi\mathscr{C}}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{\tau_{n}^{2}}.

Using finally the Wallis product, we obtain the desired identity

\mathscr{C}=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n(n+1)}.

10.2.
Associated Dirichlet series

We record several observations about the Dirichlet series

L_{+}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}^{s}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad L_{-}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}^{s}},

some of whose properties are reminiscent of the more familiar classical
L
-functions.
Note that
L_{-}(s)
was previously denoted by
L_{\tau}(s)
, when we needed to distinguish
\tau
from a generic admissible sequence
t
.
Defined initially for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
, both series extend meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
. Explicitly, we use expansion (
1.7
) of
\tau_{n}
to write

L_{\pm}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(\pm 1)^{n}}{(n+1/2)^{s}}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(\pm 1)^{n}sa_{1}}{(n+1/2)^{s+2}}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(\pm 1)^{n}(2a_{3}s+a_{1}^{2}s(s+1))}{2(n+1/2)^{s+4}}+\dots\,,

and then use
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{(n+1/2)^{s}}=2^{s}(\zeta(s)(1-2^{-s})-1)
, and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1/2)^{s}}=2^{s}(L(s,\chi_{4})-1)
, where
\chi_{4}
is the nontrivial primitive character modulo
4
. From this calculation it follows that
L_{+}(s)
is meromorphic, with simple poles at
1-2k,k\geq 0
, whereas
L_{-}(s)
is entire.

The special values of
L_{\pm}
at positive integers have already figured in §
2
, namely,

\displaystyle\Theta_{\tau}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}L_{-}(2m-1)z^{2m-1}\,,

\displaystyle\psi_{\tau}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}L_{+}(2k)\frac{z^{2k}}{k}\Big)\,.

The identity

\psi_{\tau}(z)\Theta_{\tau}(z)=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}z}

allows one to write
L_{+}(2k)
via
L_{-}(2m-1)
and vice versa (showing, in particular, that all these numbers lie in
\mathbb{Q}[\mathscr{C},\pi,L_{\tau}(1)]
). For example, the identity
L_{+}(2)=-4\mathscr{C}L_{-}(1)
, written out as

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}^{2}}=-4\mathscr{C}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}}

can be viewed as an analogue of the formula

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{(n-1/2)^{2}}=-\pi\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n-1/2)}

relating Euler’s solution of the Basel problem and Leibniz’s formula for
\pi
.

Going further to values of
L_{\pm}
at negative integers, we obtain another identity connecting the special values of
L_{+}
and
L_{-}
:

\mathrm{res}_{s=1-2k}L_{+}(s)=\frac{2i}{\pi}\frac{L_{-}(2k-1)}{(2\pi i\mathscr{C})^{2k-1}}\,.

This is not hard to prove using (
1.7
) together with Theorem
6.1
.

Next, for the values of
L_{-}
at negative integers, we claim that (
2.4
) implies that

(10.1)

L_{-}(-1)=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}},\qquad L_{-}(-1-2m)=0,\qquad m=1,2,\dots\,,

which is consistent with our declaration in §
2
that
L_{\tau}(-1)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}}
.
We may prove (
10.1
) in a familiar way using the Mellin transformation. Indeed, setting

S(t)\coloneqq 2\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}e^{-\tau_{n}^{2}t}\,

we find that

\int_{0}^{\infty}xS(x^{2})x^{s-1}dx=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}x\tau_{n}e^{-x^{2}\tau_{n}^{2}}x^{2(s/2-1)}dx^{2}=\Gamma(\tfrac{s+1}{2})L_{-}(s)\,.

Since
S(t)
is a pairing between a tempered distribution
\Psi\coloneqq\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\tau_{n}(\delta_{\tau_{n}}+\delta_{-\tau_{n}})
and the Gaussian
x\mapsto e^{-tx^{2}}
, and since
\widehat{\Psi}+\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}}
vanishes in
(-\pi,\pi)
by Lemma
2.3
, we get that
S(1/t)=-\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}+O(e^{-ct})
for any
c<\pi^{2}/4
when
t\to\infty
. It follows that

\Gamma(\tfrac{s+1}{2})L_{-}(s)=-\frac{1}{4\mathscr{C}(s+1)}+\text{an entire function},

and this implies (
10.1
).

Finally, for the values of
L_{+}
at negative even integers, we conjecture the following symmetry, verified numerically to very high precision.

Conjecture 1
.

The values
L_{+}(2k)
satisfy the following symmetry:

L_{+}(-2k)=\frac{L_{+}(2k)}{(2\pi i\mathscr{C})^{2k}}\,,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

10.3.
An integrality phenomenon

Looking at the Taylor series of
\varphi
and
\Phi
, we may note that whereas
[z^{n}]\Phi(z)
lies in
\mathbb{Q}[\pi,L_{\tau}(1),\mathscr{C}]
and has coefficients with large denominators as
n\to\infty
, the coefficients
[z^{n}]\varphi(z)
appear to lie in
\mathbb{Z}[\pi,L_{\tau}(1),\mathscr{C}]
. This observation can be made more precise if we consider the family of differential operators from §
4
, leading to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
u_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[b,\lambda]
be a sequence defined by the recursion

u_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}u_{n}(n(n+1)-\lambda)+b^{2}\frac{4n}{n+1}u_{n-1},\qquad n\geq 0,

where
u_{-1}=0
and
u_{0}=1
. Then
u_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[b,\lambda]
for
n\geq 0
.

The above recursion is satisfied by the coefficients of
f(z)f(-z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}z^{2n}
, where
f
is any eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{1,b}
. We may compare the above integrality property with Apéry’s proof of irrationality of
\zeta(3)
, where a similar phenomenon plays an important role (see
[
19
]
for an analysis of Apéry-like recurrences with integrality properties). Note that in Apéry-like recursions the associated differential equation is a period equation for a family of algebraic varieties, so it has regular singularities, whereas in Conjecture
2
the differential equation has irregular singularities. It would be interesting to know what explains the integrality in this case.
