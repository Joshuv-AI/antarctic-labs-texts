Tailoring Plasmonic Waveguide-Coupled Cavities for CO2 Sensing
Authors: Ghulam Abbas Khan, Shabana Parveen, Muhammad Faizan, Waseem Abbas, Hua Zhu, Shuting Fan, Zhengfang Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasmonic devices can guide and control light on the subwavelength scale, but their optical behavior is sensitive to minute changes in geometrical/material parameters; however, it requires a rigorous theoretical framework to obtain a high level of structural control and optimize their performance. This study investigates the coupling of a metal-dielectric-metal (MDM) waveguide with a dielectric resonator, yielding a transmittance spectrum with three distinct resonance modes. The physical mechanisms, governing transmission behavior, and the effects of geometrical parameters on mode characteristics are thoroughly analyzed. The dielectric resonator is replaced with polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) functional material, whose refractive index (RI) changes upon exposure to CO2 gas. Comprehensive simulations and analysis reveal that multiple modes can be effectively utilized for CO2 sensing application, demonstrating high sensitivities: mode 1 at 126.9 pm/ppm, mode 2 at 133.58 pm/ppm, and mode 3 at 202.2 pm/ppm. All modes maintain a higher sensitivity and outperform previously reported CO2 sensors. Consequently, hybrid plasmonic integration emerges as a promising strategy for next-generation sensors, outperforming conventional single-mode designs through an improved accuracy, multifunctionality, and enhanced measurement reliability.

Introduction

The field of surface plasmon polariton (SPP)-based nanophotonics has witnessed remarkable growth over the past two decades, driven by unique ability of SPPs to confine and control light at the nanoscale. − These electromagnetic waves, which propagate along metal-dielectric interfaces, circumvent the conventional diffraction limit that restricts dielectric waveguides to a critical dimension of approximately λ/2n. , This capacity for subwavelength light confinement has enabled significant advances in highly integrated photonic circuits, nanoscale sensing, and high-resolution imaging. A pivotal structure in this realm is the MDM waveguide, which harnesses the evanescent waves of SPPs for precise nanoscale propagation and control. − This architecture, featuring a dielectric core encased by metallic layers, significantly enhances light-matter interactions, making it exceptionally well-suited for applications like ultrasensitive RI sensing. , The versatility of MDM waveguides is further amplified by coupling them with adjacent dielectric resonators. This strategic interplay allows for precise tailoring of SPP behavior, influencing propagation characteristics, spectral response, and environmental sensitivity. − Consequently, a wide range of plasmonic devices based on this configuration have been demonstrated, including filters, , splitters, , and sensors, , with the latter attracting particular attention due to their high sensitivity and compact design. , It is noteworthy that optical sensors offer a highly sensitive, noncontact platform with exceptional resolution. These advantages have driven extensive research aimed at developing diverse applications such as point-of-care diagnostics, molecular fingerprint detection in the mid-infrared region, and portable sensing devices for various environments.

Global warming and enhanced climate challenges caused by rapid increase of atmospheric CO2 necessitate the development of sensors for the precise monitoring of its levels and for assessing the impacts of human-generated greenhouse gas emissions. The infrared photonic sensor capable of measuring CO2 concentration down to 5000 ppm is reported, based on an evanescent field absorption mechanism. Such higher concentrations cause significant health risks and can lead to serious symptoms such as sleepiness, nausea, headache, and an increased heartbeat. The other promising candidate for gas sensing is RI sensors; a grating slot waveguide-based CO2 gas sensor was numerically proposed having a sensitivity of 12.9 pm/ppm. A functional polymer, polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB), embedded in a metasurface has been employed for perfect absorption and CO2 sensing, achieving a detection range of 0 to 524 ppm. CO2 detection up to 500 ppm was also demonstrated by incorporating PHMB with a silicon ring resonator. Furthermore, an array of silicon ring resonators integrated with functional polymers was developed for the real-time, simultaneous detection of both H2 and CO2. Fabry–Perot interferometric optical fiber sensors functionalized with PHMB were also presented for CO2 detection, exhibiting a sensitivity of 12.2 pm/ppm. The performance of these MDM-resonator sensors is primarily gauged by their sensitivity and figure of merit (FoM). Prior studies have investigated various configurations, such as concentric ring resonators and optimized dielectric gaps, to enhance these metrics. − Achieving high performance is critically dependent on material selection and geometric parameters. For instance, silver is often the preferred metal due to its superior plasmonic response in the visible spectrum, while the dielectric resonator must be designed to support specific resonance modes. The MDM structure itself intensifies light-matter interactions by confining optical energy within its narrow gap, ensuring that even slight environmental changes induce substantial resonance shifts. However, a key challenge that impacts the sensor’s precision is the inherent ohmic loss in the metal, which broadens the resonance line width and remains a critical factor in design optimization. ,

To address the challenge of resonance broadening due to inherent ohmic losses of metals, a hybrid dielectric resonator coupled to an MDM waveguide is proposed. The structure incorporates a rectangular resonator divided by two vertical dielectric connectors, each embedded with a silver nanorod to enhance coupling efficiency and sensitivity. Additionally, two baffles and three silver cavities are integrated within the dielectric resonator to strengthen light–matter interactions. Finite element method (FEM) analysis reveals three distinct resonance modes with unique efficiencies. Initially, air is used as dielectric material in our simulation to maintain generality. Then, air is replaced with PHMB polymer which reacted with the CO2 gas and change its RI with different concentrations. The simulation results confirm that the design significantly improves sensing performance. These multiple resonance modes enable a robust multimode RI sensor capable of collecting comprehensive information and allowing cross-verification between modes, thereby enhancing measurement reliability. The simulated results demonstrate outstanding performance: Mode 1 achieves a sensitivity of 126.9 pm/ppm, Mode 2 reaches 133.58 pm/ppm, and Mode 3 exhibits 202.2 pm/ppm. All three modes maintain high figures of merit (FoM) and quality (Q) factors, indicating that the proposed design effectively overcomes the conventional trade-off between sensitivity and signal precision.

Design and Methodology

The proposed sensor design, shown in Figure a, consists of an MDM waveguide coupled to a rectangular dielectric resonator. To achieve efficient coupling, the resonator is positioned at a distance from the waveguide. The resonator is divided into three equal cavities by vertical dielectric connectors, a configuration that enhances the plasmonic interaction and light confinement. A transverse magnetic (TM) plane wave is excited at the input port (A), and the transmitted signal is collected at the output port (B). Silver is chosen for the metallic components due to its superior near-field enhancement and higher sensitivity to RI variations within the operational wavelength range. The permittivity of silver employed in this study was obtained from experimentally measured optical data reported by Babar et al. Since the adopted dielectric function is derived from experimentally characterized silver films, the corresponding optical constants inherently incorporate intrinsic Ohmic damping as well as practical material nonidealities, including surface roughness, grain-boundary scattering, and fabrication-induced imperfections. Consequently, these realistic dissipative loss mechanisms are implicitly incorporated into the numerical simulations through the complex-frequency-dependent permittivity of silver.

The simulations are done in an FEM-based commercially available tool COMSOL Multiphysics. An ultrafine triangular mesh was employed throughout the computational domain to ensure numerical accuracy and solution convergence. Port boundary conditions were applied at the input and output terminals of the MDM waveguide to evaluate the transmittance characteristics of the proposed structures. In addition, scattering boundary conditions were imposed on all external boundaries to absorb outgoing electromagnetic waves and suppress artificial reflections from the computational domain. The numerical reliability of the model was further verified through mesh refinement, where negligible variations in the resonant response and transmittance spectra were observed with successive mesh refinements.

The performance of plasmonic RI sensors is evaluated on several key metrics such as sensitivity, FoM, Q-factor, and limit of detection (LOD). These parameters are used to assess the sensing capability of the device, and here we describe their definitions and calculation method for the proposed sensor. Sensitivity is a key design and performance parameter for RI sensors. The sensitivity of proposed multimode sensor can be evaluated through this expression S=Δλresonant(pm)ΔConc.(ppm) . Here Δλresonant (pm) represents the shift in resonance wavelength and ΔConc.(ppm) denotes the variation in gas concentration. The sensitivity strongly depends on the geometrical configuration of the sensor and can be enhanced through structural optimization. The figure of merit (FoM) is another important performance metric used to evaluate sensing performance. It is defined as the ratio of sensitivity (S) to the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the resonance dip, FoM = S/FWHM. A higher FoM indicates improved sensing performance. The quality factor (Q-factor) of the RI sensor describes the sharpness of the resonance peak and is defined as Q = λ resonant /FWHM. A higher Q-factor corresponds to a narrower resonance line width and enhanced sensing performance. The limit of detection (LoD) represents the smallest change in RI that the sensor can reliably detect. It is calculated as LOD=ΔλminS , where S is the sensitivity and Δλmin is the minimum detectable wavelength shift. In this work, Δλ min is taken as the resolution of the optical spectrum analyzer. The commercially available resolution in the operating wavelength range is approximately 0.1 nm.

Results and Discussion

The mechanism of light coupling from the waveguide into the resonator cavity, along with the resulting resonance modes, was analyzed. The design parameters are given in Table , and the dielectric material is assumed to be air. Figure b left panel shows that a single dominant resonance emerged at 1818.50 nm with baseline structure (without silver baffles and nanorods). Lorentzian fitting (dotted curve) of the transmission spectrum shows excellent agreement with the simulated response, yielding a FWHM of 43.84 nm and a quality factor of 41.49. The high fitting accuracy (R 2 = 0.9987) confirms the single-mode resonant behavior of the baseline cavity. The optimized structure (with silver baffles and nanrorods) exhibits three distinct resonant modes (λ1 = 1475 nm, λ2 = 1710 nm, and λ3 = 2265 nm). A three-resonance Lorentzian model accurately reproduces the transmission response (R 2 = 0.9958), yielding FWHM of 47.45, 39.58, and 33.57 nm and corresponding quality factors of 31.15, 43.25, and 67.53, respectively. These results quantitatively demonstrate that the introduction of baffles and nanorods transforms the single-mode baseline cavity into a spectrally resolved multimode resonant system. The coupling efficiency (ηc) was estimated from the transmittance minima at the resonance wavelengths using ηc= 1 – Tres . The calculated coupling efficiencies are 95.3%, 88.5%, and 72.1% for Modes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The high coupling efficiencies confirm efficient energy transfer from the MDM bus waveguide to the side-coupled resonator, with Mode 1 exhibiting near-critical coupling characterized by a transmission minimum below 5%. The dispersion diagram shown in Figure c exhibits three distinct modes located below the light line, confirming their bound nature and indicating that they originate from SPP excitations.

To further verify the physical origin of the observed resonances, complex eigenfrequency analysis was performed for the optimized structure under open boundary conditions. Three localized eigenmodes were identified with complex eigenfrequencies of 203.4 + 0.922i THz, 176.05 + 1.187i THz, and 132.37 + 0.565i THz for Modes 1–3, respectively. The real part represents the resonance frequency, whereas the imaginary part describes the modal damping associated with intrinsic losses and radiation leakage of each mode. The corresponding eigenwavelengths are 1473.46, 1702.46, and 2264.43 nm, which agree closely with the transmission resonance minima at 1475, 1710, and 2265 nm. The small deviations between the eigenwavelengths and transmission minima arise from the difference between intrinsic cavity eigenstates and externally excited scattering resonances, where waveguide coupling, radiation leakage, and interference effects modify the spectral response. These results confirm that the baffles alter the electromagnetic coupling between bus waveguide and cavity regions, leading to the formation of three resonant states rather than spurious spectral features.

Rigorously elucidating the origin of each mode in relation to the complex geometrical components, as well as its field confinement characteristics and electromagnetic field enhancement, we present a detailed analysis. To quantitatively describe the modal energy distribution within the coupled resonator, the fill factor (or confinement factor) is evaluated by computing the fraction of modal power flux confined within three distinct regions: (i) the vertical connector incorporating metallic nanorods, (ii) the lower lobe, and (iii) the upper lobe, where the latter two regions are separated by side baffles, denoted as a and b. The fill factor is defined as FillFactor=∫RP(s).ds∫TotalP(s).ds×100 , where P(s) is power flux and R represents the region of interest such as vertical connectors, lower and upper lobe. The fill-factor contributions of the three resonant modes are presented in Figure d. The calculated confinement factors clearly indicate that all resonances exhibit hybrid characteristics, with the modal power flux distributed among multiple geometrical regions rather than being localized within a single structural component. For the first mode (λ1), 31.2% of the modal power is confined within the vertical connectors, while 27.1% and 33.2% are distributed in the lower and upper lobes, respectively. For the second mode (λ2), the confinement factors are 12.17% in the vertical connector, 42.36% in the lower lobe, and 36.68% in the upper lobe. Similarly, for the third mode (λ3), 13.72% of the power is confined within the vertical connectors, whereas 42.72% and 35.24% are localized in the lower and upper lobes, respectively. These results reveal that the first mode is characterized by a comparatively stronger contribution from the vertical connectors, whereas the third mode is predominantly associated with the lower lobe. The substantial power distribution across all regions for each resonance confirms the hybridized nature of the modes and highlights the strong electromagnetic coupling among the constituent geometrical elements of the resonator. Such a distributed confinement profiles indicate modal interaction and energy exchange between the connectors and lobe regions, leading to the formation of distinct hybrid modes.

The resonator length (L) is a critical parameter governing the spectral positions of the resonant modes. To evaluate its impact, L was systematically varied from 400 to 520 nm, while all other design parameters were kept constant, as specified in Table . As shown in Figure a–b, increasing the resonator length induces a consistent redshift in all three resonance wavelengths. For instance, Mode 1 shifts from 1313 to 1565 nm, Mode 2 from 1619 to 1784 nm, and Mode 3 from 2107 to 2365 nm as L is varied from 400 to 520 nm. The observed redshift of all resonance modes can be attributed to the modal confinement characteristics revealed by the fill-factor analysis. Since the modal energy of all three modes is predominantly distributed within the vertical connectors, upper and lower lobes, variations in the L exert the strongest influence on the resonance wavelengths. As L increases, the effective optical path length and the corresponding fill factor within these regions are enhanced, leading to a systematic shift of all resonance modes toward longer wavelengths. This behavior is consistent with the direct proportionality between the resonant wavelength (λres) and the resonator’s effective length, as described by eq . A longer resonator increases the optical path, enhancing phase accumulation and energy absorption within the dielectric material, which collectively shifts the resonance to longer wavelengths. The FWHM variations reflect changes in the total loss rate, governed by both radiative coupling to the waveguide and intrinsic material absorption. For Modes 1 and 3, the increasing FWHM with resonator length indicates enhanced radiative leakage due to stronger mode overlap with the waveguide continuum, leading to a more overcoupled response. In contrast, Mode 2 shows a decreasing FWHM, suggesting improved field confinement and reduced radiation loss, shifting it toward a more under-coupled regime. Overall, these opposite trends highlight a geometry-driven redistribution of loss channels, which is crucial for tailoring resonance sharpness and device performance.

CO2 Gas Sensing

The carbon dioxide CO2 is a colorless, odorless, and nonflammable greenhouse gas that is generated through the combustion of carbon-based materials and natural metabolic processes in animals. Global warming and emerging climate challenges are linked to the rising concentration of atmospheric CO2 and its rapid growth rate. Accurate monitoring of CO2 levels is essential for understanding climate change and assessing the environmental impact of human-generated greenhouse gas emissions. The generally accepted safe threshold for atmospheric CO2 concentration is approximately 350 ppm, yet current levels have already exceeded 400 ppm. The increasing demand for compact, highly sensitive sensors has accelerated the development of technologies for the accurate monitoring and tracking of greenhouse gas variations.

The PHMB polymer layer exhibits a linear refractive-index response to CO2 concentration, enabling precise detection under ambient conditions. As a guanidine-based polymer with amide functionalities, PHMB combines high sensitivity with exceptional selectivity to low CO2 levels, without requiring water vapor for adsorption or desorption. Experimental evidence and literature reports confirm that common gases such as H2 and N2 do not perturb the polymer’s optical response, ensuring specific CO2 detection. Mechanistically, the refractive-index modulation arises from electron-density redistribution within the polymer backbone induced by CO2 binding, altering its polarizability and producing a predictable, mode-dependent resonance shift. This intrinsic property positions PHMB as an ideal functional layer for high-performance optical CO2 sensors.

The dielectric resonator is replaced with PHMB functional polymer whose RI for different CO2 concentration can be calculated by this relationship nPHMB=1.55−1.36×10−4×C reported in the literature. C represents the concentration of CO2 absorbed by PHMB in ppm, 1.55 is RI of PHMB without CO2 absorption, and 1.36×10−4 is a fitting constant. The RI of PHMB polymer at different concentrations of absorbed CO2 is listed in Table , along with corresponding resonance dips in the spectrum. The PHMB refractive index variation with CO2 concentration is adopted from reported literature and implemented in the simulations. The model is assumed to be approximately linear within the considered concentration range, which is sufficient for evaluating the proposed sensor. In practical applications, deviations from linearity may occur due to saturation effects, as well as possible hysteresis arising from adsorption–desorption dynamics. Additionally, environmental factors such as temperature and humidity may introduce cross-sensitivity. Despite these limitations, the adopted model provides a reasonable approximation for performance analysis of the sensing structure.

This enhancement stems from the unique geometry of the proposed sensor. Specifically, the silver nanorods significantly enhance the local electromagnetic field, thereby strengthening the light–PHMB interaction. In addition, the incorporated baffles increase the effective optical path length and promote greater evanescent-field overlap with the PHMB layer. Together, these features result in stronger field confinement and provide more promising conditions for light–matter interaction, leading to enhanced sensitivity. The square-ring cavity in ref. lacks these nanostructured features, explaining its lower sensitivity. The calculated LOD are 0.788, 0.748, and 0.495 ppm for mode 1, mode 2 and mode 3 respectively. All of these modes can be utilized to detect different CO2 concentrations across a broad spectral range, providing richer information from the sample.

To quantify the benefit of multimode sensing, an error-propagation analysis was performed. For a single resonance mode, the concentration uncertainty is given by σC=σλS , where S is the sensitivity and σλ is the wavelength measurement uncertainty. By combining the three resonance modes through a weighted least-squares estimator, the effective sensitivity becomes Seff=S12+S22+S32=272.4pm/ppm . Assuming identical σ λ = 0.1nm for all resonances, σC is inversely proportional to sensitivity σC∝1S . Therefore, Mode 3 exhibits the lowest uncertainty (0.495 ppm), followed by Mode 2 (0.749 ppm) and Mode 1 (0.788 ppm). When all three resonance modes are utilized simultaneously, σC is further reduced to 0.37 ppm. These results quantitatively demonstrate that the multimode sensing strategy improves measurement reliability by reducing uncertainty while providing intrinsic cross-validation of the sensing response. Furthermore, the availability of multiple resonance modes enhances robustness against spectral noise, peak-fitting errors, and other measurement fluctuations.

The corresponding maximum Q factors are 40, 56, and 48 for Modes 1,2 and 3 respectively. The strong performance metrics such as high sensitivity, FoM and Q factor, demonstrates the structure’s promise as an efficient and high-performance sensing platform. Conclusion

We designed and optimized a PHMB-functionalized MDM waveguide coupled to a dielectric resonator for multimode CO2 sensing. Systematic geometric optimization enabled stable resonance formation and control of spectral tuning. The optimized structure supports three resonance modes with sensitivities of 126.9 pm/ppm, 133.58 pm/ppm, and 202.2 pm/ppm for modes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The corresponding limits of detection are 0.788, 0.748, and 0.495 ppm, confirming high-resolution CO2 detection under controlled or near-atmospheric conditions. Mode 3 delivers the best single-mode performance with the highest sensitivity and lowest uncertainty. When all three modes are used simultaneously, the combined uncertainty decreases to 0.37 ppm, improving precision by 53.0%, 50.6%, and 25.3% compared with modes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. These results show that multimode operation improves the measurement precision and reliability, making the proposed sensor a promising platform for compact optical CO2 detection.
