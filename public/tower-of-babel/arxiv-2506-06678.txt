Learning quantum phase transition in parametrized quantum circuits with an attention mechanism
Li Xin, Zhang-Qi Yin

Abstract
Learning many-body quantum states and quantum phase transitions remains a major challenge in quantum many-body physics. Classical machine learning methods offer certain advantages in addressing these difficulties. In this work, we propose a novel framework that bypasses the need to measure physical observables by directly learning the parameters of parameterized quantum circuits. By integrating the attention mechanism from large language models (LLMs) with a variational autoencoder (VAE), we efficiently capture hidden correlations within the circuit parameters. These correlations allow us to extract information about quantum phase transitions in an unsupervised manner. Moreover, our VAE acts as a classical representation of parameterized quantum circuits and the corresponding many-body quantum states, enabling the efficient generation of quantum states associated with specific phases. We apply our framework to a variety of quantum systems and demonstrate its broad applicability, with particularly strong performance in identifying topological quantum phase transitions.



I
Introduction

Quantum phase transitions (QPTs) refer to transitions between distinct ground states of quantum many-body systems driven by quantum fluctuations, typically at zero temperature. Unlike classical phase transitions that occur due to thermal fluctuations, QPTs are governed by changes in the system’s Hamiltonian parameters and are manifested in the qualitative changes of quantum correlations and entanglement structure in the ground state
Sachdev 2011
. Such transitions play a pivotal role in elucidating emergent phenomena in condensed matter systems, including magnetism, superconductivity, and topological order
Sachdev 2023
. The detection and characterization of quantum phase transitions (QPTs) remain significant challenges due to the difficulty of reaching the ground state in many-body system with strong interactions. Even if one can reach the ground state, for quantum phase transitions that go beyond the traditional Landau-Ginzburg paradigm—such as topological phase transitions—it remains necessary to measure global physical quantities derived from the full density matrix such as nonlocal order parameters or entanglement entropy, which are often highly model-specific and experimentally challenging to access.

The advent of Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices offers new tools for quantum simulation and control of many-body systems
Preskill 2018
. Techniques like the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) provide a promising approach for approximating ground states of specific Hamiltonians
Tilly et al. 2022
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
McClean et al. 2016
;
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Kandala et al. 2017
;
Moll et al. 2018
. In principle, this opens a path to study QPTs by directly simulating their ground state physics. However, several difficulties persist: VQE results may suffer from local minimum
Anschuetz and Kiani 2022
;
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Apte et al. 2022
or barren plateaus
McClean et al. 2018
;
Holmes et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Ortiz Marrero et al. 2021
, preventing accurate ground state preparation. Even when an approximate ground state is obtained, identifying appropriate observables to signal a quantum phase transition remains highly nontrivial. For a given family of Hamiltonians and their associated phase transitions, one must often possess prior knowledge—such as symmetry properties, stabilizer structures, or theoretical insight—to construct meaningful order parameters. This may involve designing nonlocal operators based on symmetry considerations or exhaustively measuring a large number of
k
-local observables (for varying
k
) and selecting suitable indicators through additional post-processing or learning. Such dependence on measurement-specific prior knowledge introduces a significant practical burden, especially in the discovery of a new quantum phase or phase transition, it is often unclear what feature or order parameter one
should extract from the data. For example, a number of studies have proposed methods that aim to learn nonlocal order parameters, and other relevant quantities directly from VQE-prepared ground states
Lively et al. 2024
;
Angelides et al. 2025
;
Okada et al. 2023
;
Grossi et al. 2023
. However, these approaches typically assume prior knowledge of the observables of interest and often require explicit reconstruction of functions over exponentially large density matrices. This leads to increased computational complexity and limits the scalability of such methods, posing further difficulties for practical deployment on NISQ devices. Although techniques like classical shadow tomography offer a scalable way to estimating physical quantities derived from the full density matrix
Huang et al. 2020
. and have been demonstrated to be effective in quantum many-body tasks including learning QPTs
Elben et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2022
;
Lewis et al. 2024
, their application to QPTs detection still relies on prior knowledge of relevant observables. Moreover, in practice, additional classical post-processing—such as regression or classification using machine learning techniques—is often required to extract meaningful phase-related information from randomized measurement outcomes. This dependence on observable-specific knowledge and classical inference adds further complexity to the QPT detection pipeline.
In addition, quantum algorithms—exemplified by quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs)
Cong et al. 2019
—have been employed to regress quantum phases, offering a programmable and scalable framework for phase recognition from quantum data
Cong et al. 2019
;
Herrmann et al. 2022
;
Monaco et al. 2023
;
Sander et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025b
. However, these methods typically assume that the VQE-prepared states correspond to true ground states, i.e., global minimum. As system size increases and quantum circuits become deeper, reliably preparing such high-fidelity states becomes increasingly challenging. This, in turn, hinders the scalability and practical applicability of QCNN-based phase classification in larger or more complex quantum systems.
Moreover, several machine learning approaches have been applied to analyze measurement data and identify phases in real quantum devices or numerical simulation
Dong et al. 2019
;
Rem et al. 2019
;
Lidiak and Gong 2020
;
Kasatkin et al. 2024
;
Broecker et al. 2017
;
Canabarro et al. 2019
;
Rahaman et al. 2023
;
Bloch et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2011
.
However, these methods often assume that the true ground state (i.e., the global minimum) can be accurately prepared on a quantum device, which is not always feasible in practice. More recently, some studies have shown that even when the system is trapped in a local minimum rather than reaching the true ground state, it may still be possible to extract meaningful signals of phase transitions
Cao et al. 2024
;
Crognaletti et al. 2024
. Nevertheless, these approaches usually involve complex many-body measurements and nontrivial post-processing of observables, posing additional practical and computational challenges.

Circuit parameters corresponding to VQE-optimized minimum are themselves distributed across a complex landscape, raising a natural question: can we directly learn from the distribution of VQE circuit parameters, thereby circumventing the need for extensive measurement and selection of observables? While conceptually appealing, this approach is hard in practice. Unlike order parameters, entanglement entropy, or other density-matrix-based quantities, variational parameters lack explicit physical interpretability and do not exhibit easily learnable low-dimensional features. Meanwhile, the rapid progress of artificial intelligence—particularly the emergence of large language models (LLMs) and generative models such as, transformers for natural language processing (NLP) and image processing
Vaswani et al. 2023
;
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
;
Peebles and Xie 2023
, diffusion models for image generate
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Rombach et al. 2022
have demonstrated a strong capacity to model and generate complex data. Motivated by these advances, recent works have begun exploring their application in quantum many-body physics and quantum computing. Notable examples include the use of transformer-based models and long short-term memory(LSTM)
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
-based models for quantum error correction
Cao et al. 2023
;
Bausch et al. 2024
, transformer-enhanced classical shadow tomography
Yao and You 2024
, and diffusion-based model of quantum circuit generation
Fürrutter et al. 2024
;
Shen 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
. The success of these approaches often stems from the models’ ability to capture deep correlations and extract relevant features from complex data—especially through mechanisms such as attention.

Inspired by these developments, in this work we propose a novel quantum–classical hybrid framework for learning and identifying quantum phase transitions. Our approach leverages NISQ devices to obtain the minimum (whether local or global) of the target Hamiltonian via the VQE, Instead of measuring specific physical observables, we directly encode the optimized quantum circuit parameters into a classical attention-based model. This enables automatic discovery of phase-related structures without requiring prior knowledge of measurable quantities. Moreover, our framework operates effectively without access to global minimum, relying solely on readily accessible local minimum, thereby significantly reducing both experimental and computational overhead.

The central idea of our framework is to fully exploit the information encoded in approximate quantum states generated by variational algorithms, and to allow a attention-based deep learning model to automatically extract signatures of phase transitions without relying on predefined order parameters or prior physical knowledge. The incorporation of attention mechanisms enables the model to focus on the most discriminative regions of the input states, enhancing both the accuracy and generalizability of phase detection. Notably, our framework is highly scalable and can be adapted to various Hamiltonian forms, making it broadly applicable. To evaluate the effectiveness of our approach, we conduct systematic experiments on several representative quantum many-body models, including those exhibiting topological and symmetry-breaking phase transitions.

The results demonstrate that our method can reliably identify phases in an unsupervised manner even in the presence of practical constraints such as VQE local minimum and limited quantum resources. It outperforms conventional order parameter–based analyses and existing learning-based techniques in both robustness and accuracy. Moreover, our framework can be seamlessly integrated with emerging generative models, enabling the generation of many-body ground states corresponding to specific phases conditioned on user-defined prompts. This further highlights the framework’s ability to efficiently learn phase information and its potential for quantum state generation tasks. Finally, our framework establishes a new paradigm for directly learning physical information from the distribution of parameters in parameterized quantum circuits. This opens up novel avenues for applying VQE-based parameterized circuits to problems in quantum many-body physics.

II
Preliminaries

In variational quantum algorithms (VQAs), the optimization landscape is often highly non-convex due to the expressibility constraints of quantum circuits and the inherent complexity of parameterized quantum states. As a result, the optimization process tends to converge to local minimum. Although traditionally regarded as an obstacle in reaching the ground state, recent studies have shown that local minimum can still encode meaningful physical information.
Previous theoretical work demonstrated that local minimum are more readily accessible on current NISQ devices through unitary perturbation-based optimizers
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Li 2025
, and these solutions can still reflect essential properties of the underlying many-body Hamiltonian, including signatures of quantum phase transitions. Additionally, it has been shown that the energy landscapes of VQE are typically filled with local minimum, making convergence to global minimum even more challenging
Anschuetz and Kiani 2022
.

Drawing an analogy to thermodynamic phase transitions—where low-lying excitations near the ground state govern the behavior of quasi-particles—we argue that the local minimum found by VQE often preserving critical information relevant to quantum phase transitions. Previous studies have exploited such local minimum to explore phase transitions, yet these approaches still rely on measuring observables and performing post-processing on the output quantum states
Cao et al. 2024
.
In contrast, our method directly encodes phase transition signatures from the circuit parameters themselves, eliminating the need for observable reconstruction and offering additional capabilities such as generation and generalization.

In recent years, generative AI models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in learning and generalizing complex representations of quantum systems. Notable examples include qecGPT
Cao et al. 2023
, where large language models are used to improve quantum error correction strategies, and ShadowGPT
Yao and You 2024
, which enhances many-body property reconstruction through classical shadow tomography. Other efforts involve leveraging Generative models to generate quantum circuits for explore optimal quantum circuit architectures
Fürrutter et al. 2024
, VQE ansatz reasearch
Nakaji et al. 2024
, offering novel tools for quantum circuit generation.

Inspired by these developments, we propose a central hypothesis: for a given family of Hamiltonians, even when starting from a fixed initial quantum state, executing VQE under varying physical parameters (i.e., Hamiltonian coefficients) leads to distinct optimizer trajectories, each converging to a different local or global minimum. Due to the expressibility and high dimensionality of parameterized quantum circuits, the resulting terminal circuit parameters form a complex distribution that does not directly reveal the underlying phase structure.

To uncover this hidden structure, we employ an attention-based neural network to model the final optimized parameters. This model learns deep representations embedded in the endpoint circuit parameters and successfully clusters them according to different quantum phases. Crucially, this enables quantum phase identification without explicit measurements of physical observables, bridging quantum optimization dynamics with classical pattern recognition.

III
Method

We first introduce our framework. We consider a quantum phase transition system controlled by a smooth parameter
\bm{x}
, whose Hamiltonian is given by
H(\bm{x})
.
For a series of different Hamiltonian parameters
\{\bm{x}_{l}\}
, we construct a parameterized quantum circuit ansatz
U(\bm{\theta})

where
\bm{\theta}
represents the set of tunable parameters in the circuit. Under the same initial optimization conditions, we employ the VQE algorithm to iteratively optimize and obtain the optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}_{l})

for each
\bm{x}_{l}
.

Subsequently, we utilize a classical neural network to compress and encode the features of these optimal parameters
\{\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}_{l})\}
. To achieve this, we design a neural network with an attention mechanism that effectively learns the mapping between the Hamiltonian parameters and the corresponding encoded feature vectors:

\bm{x}_{l}\rightarrow\sigma_{\text{encode}}(\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{x}_{l})).

(1)

Through this mapping, we accurately capture the phase transition information of the system, and in the subsequent analysis, these encoded features will be further utilized to develop an optimization strategy.

III.1
Variational Auto-Encoder with Self-Attention

Our classical neural network adopts a highly customized Variational Autoencoder (VAE)
Kingma and Welling 2022
with an attention mechanism
Vaswani et al. 2023
, the schematic of our framework in
1
(a). The VAE consists of two components: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder maps the input data into a latent space, while the decoder reconstructs the original data from the latent representation. We begin by sorting the optimized parameters obtained from the VQE algorithm,
\{\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\star}\}
, into a one-dimensional vector. This vector is then processed by three one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D CNNs), where each CNN progressively increases the number of feature channels.

Formally, let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the input vector, where
d
is the total number of optimized parameters. The first CNN applies a set of convolutional filters to extract local features while expanding the channel dimension, producing an output of shape
(d,c_{1})
. This process continues through the subsequent CNN layers, increasing the feature dimension to
(d,c_{2})
and then
(d,c_{3})
. In this work, we set
c_{1}=8
,
c_{2}=16
, and
c_{3}=32
. The use of CNNs serves two purposes: (i) they effectively capture local correlations within the parameter space by applying localized receptive fields, and (ii) the expansion of feature channels allows for a richer representation at each position, which facilitates subsequent self-attention mechanisms by providing a more expressive feature space.

Following the CNN layers, the feature map is passed through a multi-head self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to capture long-range dependencies across different positions in the input sequence
Vaswani et al. 2023
. The self-attention mechanism operates by computing weighted dependencies between all elements in the sequence, formally defined as follows:

\text{Attention}(\bm{Q},\bm{K},\bm{V})=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{QK}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)\bm{V},

(2)

where
\bm{Q}
,
\bm{K}
, and
\bm{V}
are the query, key, and value matrices derived from the input feature representation through learned linear projections. The term
\sqrt{d_{k}}
is a scaling factor that prevents the attention scores from growing excessively large, thereby stabilizing the softmax function.

Unlike single-head attention, which aggregates all information through a single transformation, multi-head attention employs multiple parallel attention heads, each learning different representations of the input:

\text{MultiHead}(\bm{X})=\text{Concat}(\text{head}_{1},\dots,\text{head}_{h})\bm{W}^{O},

(3)

where each attention head is computed independently as

\text{head}_{i}=\text{Attention}(\bm{X}\bm{W}^{Q}_{i},\bm{X}\bm{W}^{K}_{i},\bm{X}\bm{W}^{V}_{i}).

(4)

Here,
\bm{W}^{Q}_{i}
,
\bm{W}^{K}_{i}
, and
\bm{W}^{V}_{i}
are learned projection matrices specific to the
i
-th head, and
\bm{W}^{O}
is a linear transformation applied to the concatenated outputs of all heads.

The advantage of multi-head attention lies in its ability to capture diverse aspects of the input representation. Each attention head can focus on different regions of the input, enabling the model to simultaneously learn local and global dependencies. This is particularly beneficial in our context, where capturing complex correlations in quantum system parameters is crucial. Multi-head attention also improves the model’s expressivity and enables better generalization compared to single-head attention, as different heads can learn complementary features that enhance the robustness of the learned representations.

Next, we apply a residual block (ResNet block), which consists of a sequence of transformations:

\bm{X}_{\text{out}}=\bm{X}_{\text{in}}+f(\bm{X}_{\text{in}},\bm{W}),

(5)

where
f(\cdot)
represents a series of convolutional or fully connected layers followed by normalization and activation functions. Residual connections help mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, stabilize training, and enable deeper networks to retain information from earlier layers more effectively.

The output of the ResNet block is subsequently passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), which projects the high-dimensional feature representation into a lower-dimensional latent space. To achieve this, we employ a reparameterization trick. Specifically, we model the latent variable
\bm{z}
as a Gaussian distribution:

\bm{z}=\bm{\mu}+\bm{\sigma}\odot\bm{\epsilon},\quad\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),

(6)

where
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\sigma}
are the outputs of the MLP representing the mean and standard deviation of the latent distribution. This trick enables gradient-based optimization by allowing the sampling process to be differentiable.

To reconstruct the original data from the latent representation, we employ an inverse CNN architecture that decodes the compressed latent variables back into the original parameter space. This symmetric structure ensures that the learned latent representation captures essential generative features of the input, facilitating effective reconstruction.

By selecting an appropriate latent space dimension
d_{\text{latent}}
, we obtain the latent vector
\bm{z}
. Applying further classical processing to
\bm{z}
, such as Kernel Principal Component Analysis (Kernel PCA)
Schölkopf et al. 1998
, Gaussian mixture model(GMM)
Permuter et al. 2003
, etc, allows us to intuitively reveal quantum phase information and even directly infer phase transition points without supervision. Moreover, leveraging the generative properties of the VAE, we can not only directly sample quantum states from the latent space but also incorporate more advanced generative models, such as diffusion models, to generate quantum states with specific characteristics.

The loss function of a VAE consists of two terms: the reconstruction loss and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}=D_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\|p(\mathbf{z})\right)

(7)

where
q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
is the posterior distribution of the latent variables, and
p(\mathbf{z})
is the prior distribution. In our model, we use the mean squared error (MSE) as the reconstruction loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{i}\right\|^{2},

(8)

where
N
is the number of data features,
\mathbf{x}
is the input data,
\mathbf{\hat{x}}
is the reconstruct data,
\mathbf{z}
is the latent encode.
Thus, the total loss function of the VAE is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}

(9)

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter that controls the contribution of the KL divergence term during optimization.

The KL divergence plays a crucial role in regularizing the latent space distribution, ensuring that it closely aligns with a predefined prior (typically a standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
). This improves the generalization ability and generation quality of the model. Without the KL divergence term, the encoder might map input data to highly discrete regions in the latent space, making it difficult for the decoder to generate smooth and coherent samples. Moreover, KL divergence helps prevent mode collapse, ensuring that different regions of the latent space effectively represent the data distribution.

Unlike conventional VAEs, we introduce a multi-head self-attention mechanism in the encoder. Attention mechanisms were originally designed to model dependencies and contextual relationships in sequential data. In our case, we leverage attention to capture complex correlations between different spatial parameters across various quantum phases.

III.2
Conditional VAE and Diffusion Model to generate quantum state

While the VAE framework already possesses the ability to generate quantum states, it lacks explicit control over the phase to which the generated states belong. To enable controlled generation of quantum states at different phases, we introduce two generative strategies.

The first is to extend our VAE framework into a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). The overall architecture remains consistent with the previously described VAE—both the encoder and decoder retain their original structures (see
1
(b)). The key difference lies in the inclusion of explicit conditioning information, such as phase labels or physical control parameters, during the decoding stage. This conditioning enables the model to generate quantum states that are not only faithful reconstructions but also specific to the desired phase, making it particularly suitable for exploring phase-resolved characteristics in systems with subtle topological or symmetry-breaking features.
Specifically, after sampling the latent variable
z\sim q_{\phi}(z\mid x)
, we concatenate it with a condition vector
c
, which represents, for instance, the phase label associated with the input. The combined vector
[z,c]
is then passed to the decoder to reconstruct the original input
x
, enabling the model to generate states conditioned on specified quantum phases.
Formally, the decoder now models
p_{\theta}(x\mid z,c)
, and the training objective follows the standard VAE formulation, augmented with conditioning:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CVAE}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(z\mid x)}\left[\log p_{\theta}(x\mid z,c)\right]-D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\right),

(10)

where the reconstruction loss encourages accurate recovery of the input conditioned on the specified phase, and the KL divergence regularizes the latent distribution. Notably, the condition
c
is introduced only at the decoder level; the encoder remains unchanged from the original VAE, preserving its general structure and training pipeline. This conditional design allows for controllable and phase-specific generation of quantum states.

As the second generative strategy, we introduce a Conditional Diffusion Model (CDM) designed to operate within the latent space learned by the pretrained VAE (see Fig.
1
(c)). Inspired by the recent success of latent diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion
Rombach et al. 2022
, we observe that performing diffusion in a pretrained latent space can significantly reduce modeling complexity while retaining generative fidelity and controllability. In our proposed VAE-based framework for quantum phase learning, this strategy is particularly natural: the VAE encoder has already learned a compact and phase-sensitive representation of physical states, making conditional generation in latent space both feasible and physically meaningful. During training, a latent vector
z_{0}
, obtained by encoding a physical state, is progressively perturbed via a fixed forward diffusion process:

z_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}z_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(11)

where
\alpha_{t}\in(0,1)
follows a predefined noise schedule. The corresponding reverse process is learned using a neural network (implemented as a simple MLP) that predicts the noise given the condition
c
and time step
t
:

\epsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},c,t)\approx\epsilon.

(12)

The network is trained by minimizing the denoising loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}=\mathbb{E}_{z_{0},c,t,\epsilon}\left[\left\|\epsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},c,t)-\epsilon\right\|^{2}\right].

(13)

During generation, we sample
z_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and perform iterative denoising conditioned on
c
, ultimately recovering a latent vector
z_{0}
that is then passed through the pretrained VAE decoder to generate a quantum state corresponding to the specified phase.
This latent diffusion approach leverages both the structural advantages of the VAE latent space and the flexibility of conditional generative modeling, leading to diverse and controllable quantum state generation across different phases.

The second example we consider is a 15-qubit one-dimensional cluster-Ising model:

H=-\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}Z_{i}X_{i+1}Z_{i+2}-h_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}-h_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}X_{i}X_{i+1}

(15)

This model exhibits three quantum phases:a PM phase, a spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) phase and a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry-proted topological(SPT) phase. The SPT phase is driven by interaction terms of the form
\sum_{i}Z_{i}X_{i+1}Z_{i+2}
. The ground state in this phase is commonly referred to as a cluster state or a graph state. It plays a crucial role in measurement-based quantum computation and quantum error correction.

In this example, we demonstrate that even when the optimization process converges only to a local minimum, where the final circuit parameters fail to reach the physical ground state, it remains possible to reliably extract phase transition information. To systematically explore this behavior, we deliberately selected a parameterized circuit with limited expressibility as shown in Fig.
3
(a) and adopted a worse choice of initial parameters: all parameters were initialized to 1. Specifically, we uniformly sampled
h_{1}
within the range
[0.2,1.2]
with a step size of 0.1, and for each
h_{1}
, we scanned
h_{2}
over the range
[-2.3,1.6]
with a resolution of 0.001. Under this VQE setup, most of the converged parameters fall into local minimum (see Supplementary Material).

Similar to the procedure used for the Ising model, for each selected value of
h_{1}
, we collect the corresponding converged parameters as a dataset and train a separate VAE to obtain the latent vectors. In this example, we further demonstrate that the latent vectors obtained from our framework can be accurately clustered using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)
Permuter et al. 2003
. Specifically, after performing PCA on the latent vectors, we input them into a GMM, which identifies three distinct clusters. In our labeling convention, cluster
0
corresponds to the SPT phase, cluster
1
to the PM phase, and cluster
2
to the Ising phase. For each
h_{1}
-dependent dataset, we can visualize the GMM cluster predictions, as shown in Fig.
3
(c). By compiling the GMM predictions across all datasets, we can directly construct the full phase diagram, as illustrated in Fig.
3
(d).

In addition, we propose a method based on the VAE and GMM framework to identify phase transition points. After obtaining the predicted GMM labels, we segment each label set using a sliding window of fixed size
w
and stride
s
.
Specifically, let the scanned external field values be denoted as
\{h_{2}(j)\}_{j=1}^{N}
, with corresponding GMM predicted labels
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, where
h_{2}(j)
and
y_{j}
are one-to-one matched. For each window center
i
, we define the parameter and label sets within the window as

H_{i}=\{h_{2}(i-w/2),\ldots,h_{2}(i),\ldots,h_{2}(i+w/2)\},

(16)

W_{i}=\{y_{i-w/2},\ldots,y_{i},\ldots,y_{i+w/2}\}.

(17)

For each window
W_{i}
, the variance of the labels is computed as

\mathrm{Var}(W_{i})=\frac{1}{w}\sum_{j=i-w/2}^{i+w/2}(y_{j}-\overline{y}_{i})^{2},

(18)

where
\overline{y}_{i}
is the mean label value within
W_{i}
.
To identify the phase transition points, we plot
\mathrm{Var}(W_{i})
as a function of the window center
h_{2}(i)
and locate the peaks. The peak positions are approximately determined by finding indices
i
satisfying

\mathrm{Var}(W_{i-1})<\mathrm{Var}(W_{i})>\mathrm{Var}(W_{i+1}).

(19)

The
h_{2}(i)
corresponding to these peaks are then taken as the estimated locations of phase transitions. As an example, in Fig.
3
(e), we set the window size to
w=501
, containing
250
data points to the left and right of each center, and use a stride of
s=50
, resulting in
69
windows in total. By computing and plotting the variance, we observe two prominent peaks around each
h_{1}\in{0.3,0.6,0.9,1.2}
, with the peak centers corresponding to the phase transition points.

IV.3
Conditional Diffusion for generate cluster state

In this experiment, we use the above Cluster-Ising model

H(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(X_{i-1}Z_{i}X_{i+1}+\lambda Y_{i}Y_{i+1}\right)

(20)

to demonstrate the ability of the latent-space diffusion model to generate both cluster states and antiferromagnetic states.
For this model, we can define the following string order parameter to characterize the two different phases
Smacchia et al. 2011
:

\mathcal{S}=\langle\sigma^{x}_{1}\sigma^{y}_{2}\left(\prod_{k=3}^{N-2}\sigma^{z}_{k}\right)\sigma^{y}_{N-1}\sigma^{x}_{N}\rangle.

(21)

when
\lambda<1
, it is in the cluster phase, and the ground state has a finite string order (
\mathcal{S}\in(0,1]
).
When
\lambda>1
, the system enters the anti-fragmentation phase, where the ground state becomes an anti-fragmented state, the string order parameter vanishes (
\mathcal{S}=0
).

We consider an 11-qubit system. We use the same circuit structure as shown in Fig.
2
(a), extending it to 11 blocks. To construct the training dataset, we sample the ground state with
\lambda\in[0,0.8]
using a step size of 0.001, and the ground state with
\lambda\in[1.2,2.0]
using a step size of 0.001. The two datasets are then combined to form the complete training set.

For Conditional Diffusion, after training a VAE on this dataset, we perform conditional diffusion in the latent space. Specifically, we use the trained diffusion model to generate 1000 samples conditioned on the cluster label and 1000 samples conditioned on the anti-fragmentation label. These generated latent vectors are then passed through the pre-trained VAE decoder to produce new, previously unseen cluster and anti-fragmentation states. We evaluate the quality of the generated states by computing the string order parameter. The training procedure for the CVAE follows the same approach as in the Ising model case.

For the conditional diffusion model, latent dimensions of 4, 8, and 16 yield the best performance, with latent = 4 generating states over the widest range and concentrating around regions with high string order. For the CVAE, latent dimensions of 8, 16, and 32 perform best, while latent = 4 can produce states with
\mathcal{S}\approx 0.8
, but most generated states concentrate in regions with low string order.

Overall, the conditional diffusion model demonstrates stronger generative capability than the CVAE. However, when the latent dimension is increased to 128, the latent space produced by the VAE fails to capture features related to the phase transition, resulting in the breakdown of the conditional diffusion model. In contrast, the CVAE still retains some generative ability at this latent dimension. This conclusion is further supported by the average string order parameter
\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle

V
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we present a pioneering approach by investigating the parameter distributions of parametrized quantum circuits. Based on this, we propose a novel classical-quantum hybrid framework built upon the VQE. This framework efficiently learns the optimized parameters found at the minimum by VQE and successfully executes a range of quantum phase transition learning tasks. These tasks include quantum phase detection, classification, and the targeted generation of quantum states belonging to distinct phases. Crucially, our method does not require the VQE optimization to converge to the global minimum, significantly enhancing its practical utility on NISQ devices. Furthermore, we developed an efficient technique within this framework for generating phase-specific quantum states. Collectively, our work establishes a new methodology and paradigm for studying quantum phase transitions and many-body physics in the NISQ device. The effectiveness of our approach is demonstrated across diverse phase transitions, ranging from symmetry-broken phase transition to topological phase transition.

While our framework demonstrates significant capabilities, several limitations and opportunities for future improvement merit discussion. Firstly, the variational autoencoder (VAE) architecture underlying our approach imposes a prior that encourages the encoded feature vectors to approximate a standard normal distribution. This constraint may not be optimal for achieving maximal distinctiveness between different quantum phases. Consequently, the clusters representing distinct phases in the latent space often lack sharply defined boundaries, and data points from different phases can exhibit overlap. This overlap can occasionally lead to misclassifications or erroneous clustering for data points belonging to specific phases. To address this, future work should explore alternative network architectures or training objectives specifically designed to enhance phase separability like ViT
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
, U-Net
Ronneberger et al. 2015
, etc. Secondly, our current methodology for quantum state generation is intrinsically tied to specific model Hamiltonian and requires case-by-case training. Generating states for a new model necessitates retraining the framework with data specific to that model and its phase labels. To achieve broader applicability and efficiency, a promising direction is to develop a unified, model-agnostic generation network. Such a network could be trained on data encompassing multiple different models and their corresponding phases. Inspired by advances in multi-modal learning and architectures like Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training(CLIP)
Radford et al. 2021
, future iterations could potentially accept prompts (e.g., specifying the target model and phase) to generate phase-specific quantum states across diverse systems without requiring model-specific retraining. Thirdly, and crucially for practical impact, a highly promising avenue is to extend the applicability of our framework to directly enhance variational quantum algorithms. Specifically, the high-quality, phase-distinct quantum states generated by our framework could serve as optimized initial states or ansätze seeds for the VQE when applied to Hamiltonians within those specific phases. By leveraging these pre-trained, phase-aware initializations, we anticipate significant improvements in VQE performance, such as faster convergence rates, higher fidelity to the true ground state, and potentially escaping local minima that plague random or naive initializations.

Moreover, CNNs without attention fail to shape the latent space into a Gaussian-like distribution, which breaks the generative capability of the model.
