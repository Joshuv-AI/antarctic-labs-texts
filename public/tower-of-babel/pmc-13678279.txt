Local Adaptation With Gene Flow Has a Polygenic Basis in a Threatened Coastal Songbird
Authors: Jonathan D. Clark, Haw Chuan Lim, Brian J. Olsen, Adrienne I. Kovach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpecies that span heterogenous environments often exhibit local adaptation despite gene flow among populations. In such cases, the maintenance of local adaptation is controlled by several factors, including the strength of selection, migration, assortative mating, and genomic architecture. The song sparrow ( Melospiza melodia ) is a widespread songbird with diverse subspecies, including the coastally adapted Atlantic song sparrow (M. m. atlantica). Despite extensive gene flow with a generalist subspecies, Atlantic song sparrow populations have been found to maintain their coastally adaptive phenotypes. We produced a new chromosome‐level reference assembly for the species and whole‐genome data to explore the genomic architecture of divergence with gene flow and the genetic basis of adaptation in this subspecies. Adaptation in the Atlantic song sparrow has a polygenic basis, with loci under divergent selection scattered throughout the genome rather than concentrated in low‐recombination linkage blocks. Signatures of fine‐scale population structure, limited genomic differentiation, and divergent selection on a small set of genes suggest a role for natural selection in maintaining this subspecies boundary. Our findings highlight the complex interplay among selection, gene flow, and recombination in shaping adaptation with gene flow and provide insights into the genomic architecture of ecological divergence within a widespread, morphologically diverse species.

Introduction

Species with broad ranges that span heterogeneous environments often exhibit heritable trait divergence among populations due to differing selection pressures, i.e., local adaptation (Rundle and Nosil 2005; Savolainen et al. 2013). Locally adapted populations may experience maladaptive gene flow where the ranges of adjacent, divergently adapted populations abut (Lenormand 2002). When divergently adapted subpopulations experience extensive gene flow, differentiation typically becomes limited to the genomic regions that underpin local adaptation while the neutral genomic background is homogenized among populations (Feder et al. 2012; Tigano and Friesen 2016). Consequently, high‐resolution genomic data are necessary to detect population structure in such cases (Funk et al. 2012). However, such cases also present an opportunity to identify the genetic basis of local adaptation, as loci under divergent selection should be easily detectable against the low differentiation of the genomic background (Tigano and Friesen 2016).

Systems where local adaptation is maintained despite gene flow can also provide insights into the early processes of ecological speciation, as ecologically driven divergence among interbreeding populations can eventually lead to ecological speciation under certain circumstances. For example, ecological speciation can occur between divergently adapted populations that interbreed when selection is sufficiently strong to create post‐zygotic reproductive isolation (Rundle and Nosil 2005). Ecological speciation can also result when assortative mating becomes coupled with ecologically driven trait divergence, creating pre‐zygotic barriers, as in the case of so‐called “magic” traits (Servedio et al. 2011).

The ability of interbreeding populations to maintain adaptive divergence despite gene flow is controlled by several factors, including the strength of selection, extent of migration, effects of assortative mating, and interactions between physical linkage within the genome and genomic architecture (Tigano and Friesen 2016). In the simplest cases, where a single causal locus of large effect underpins a locally adaptive phenotype, strong selection alone may be sufficient to maintain divergence among interbreeding populations (e.g., von Holdt et al. 2018; Barrett et al. 2019; Miranda et al. 2021). However, when the genomic architecture of local adaptation involves the effects of numerous genes, it is common for divergent loci to be restricted to recombination cold spots in the genome—such as centromeres and inversions—causing sets of co‐adaptive alleles to avoid recombination and be inherited as a linkage block (Burri et al. 2015; Ravinet et al. 2017).

There are several examples of adaptation to different selective regimes maintained by chromosomal inversions in diverse species, including mice (Hager et al. 2022), birds (Taylor and Campagna 2016; Funk et al. 2021), marine fishes (Berg et al. 2017; Wilder et al. 2020; Akopyan et al. 2022), and snails (Koch et al. 2021). In three closely related, hybridizing bumble bee species (genus Bombus), loci resistant to gene flow among species are limited to genomic regions around centromeres (Christmas et al. 2021). These cases illustrate that for populations with extensive gene flow to maintain divergent adaptations typically require either strong selection with a simple genetic basis or that co‐adaptive gene complexes under divergent selection be co‐located in regions of suppressed recombination.

On the other hand, theory predicts that non‐independence of beneficial and deleterious alleles throughout the genome due to linkage prevents selection from acting efficiently (Hill and Robertson 1966). Some experimental evidence has supported this, demonstrating that a higher recombination rate can more effectively incorporate new beneficial mutations while eliminating deleterious ones (Haddrill et al. 2007). Therefore, when the genetic basis of adaptation is polygenic, high recombination could result in the more efficient sorting of beneficial sets of alleles in response to strong selection.

A species that provides an ideal opportunity to study the basis of local adaptation with gene flow is the song sparrow ( Melospiza melodia ), a widespread North American songbird that exhibits phenotypic differentiation related to its regional environment. The song sparrow is a complex of 25 recognized subspecies, each associated with a specific breeding habitat ranging from subarctic islands to hot southwestern deserts (Aldrich 1984; Patten and Pruett 2009). The overlap between subspecies' ranges and climate zones suggests that the phenotypic diversity of this species is largely a result of local adaptation across its range (Aldrich 1984; Patten and Pruett 2009). The ranges of several subspecies abut in broad contact zones, where there is the opportunity for gene flow. Thus, for these parapatric subspecies pairs to remain phenotypically divergent in the absence of intrinsic genetic incompatibilities requires mechanisms of assortative mating, divergent natural selection, or some combination of the two.

Some song sparrow subspecies are widespread generalists, while other subspecies are geographically and ecologically restricted specialists. The Atlantic song sparrow (M. m. atlantica) is a specialist subspecies of the song sparrow that is associated with tidal salt marsh and coastal dune habitats in eastern North America, found breeding from the Outer Banks of North Carolina to Cape Cod, Massachusetts (Figure 1; Todd 1924; Patten and Pruett 2009). Many avian species specialized to tidal salt marshes and coastal dunes are experiencing precipitous population declines due to coastal degradation and sea level rise (Correll et al. 2017; Field et al. 2017). Some data suggest the Atlantic song sparrow has declined by as much as 97% in portions of its range over the last three decades (Watts 2025).

Coastal habitats present many evolutionary challenges to terrestrial vertebrates, such as high salinity, limited access to freshwater, high heat with limited cover, and periodic tidal flooding. The Atlantic song sparrow exhibits adaptive phenotypes typical of other avian species adapted to tidal habitats (known as the “salt marsh syndrome”; Greenberg et al. 2006), including a large bill, plumage with more pronounced dark streaking, and, implicitly, the ability to tolerate or avoid high salt intake (Figure 2). Each of these three divergent phenotypes has hypothesized adaptive significance.

The adaptive basis of a large bill in these habitats serves to reduce evaporative water loss during thermoregulation via two primary functions. First, the higher surface‐area of a larger bill functions to increase non‐evaporative cooling as blood can be shunted to the bill for radiative cooling—that is, the Greenberg‐Tattersall Corollary of Allen's Rule (Greenberg et al. 2012; Danner and Greenberg 2015; Olsen et al. 2019). Second, increased bill volume provides greater space for internal vascular surface area in the nasal turbinates which likely reduces water loss during respiration (Danner et al. 2016). Darker plumage has been suggested to benefit bird species in coastal environments, as increased eumelanin (black pigment) content prevents microbial degradation of feathers by keratinolytic bacteria, which are more abundant and potent in hot, moist habitats (Goldstein et al. 2004; Gunderson et al. 2008; Peele et al. 2009). Some passerine species adapted to saline environments exhibit physiological adaptations to cope with salt absorption and excretion (Goldstein 2006). Though no studies have thoroughly assessed salt intake or excretion ability in the Atlantic song sparrow, the subspecies is capable of excreting hyperosmotic urine (Clark 2024) and known to consume osmoconforming prey (Rumfelt 2024).

In the Atlantic song sparrow, these potentially adaptive phenotypic differences exist despite adequate opportunity for gene flow with morphologically distinct inland populations. Specifically, throughout the majority of the Atlantic song sparrow range, it is parapatric with the eastern song sparrow (M. m. melodia), a widespread generalist subspecies found breeding in a variety of shrubby habitats east of the Rocky Mountains (Patten and Pruett 2009). Notably, the eastern song sparrow breeds in coastal habitats north of the Atlantic song sparrow range, demonstrating its ability to utilize these habitats at higher latitudes where summer temperatures are cooler.

Despite high levels of gene flow detected between these subspecies across their broad contact zone, populations of the Atlantic song sparrow appear to maintain their coastally adaptive phenotypes (Clark et al. 2022). Phenotypic differences between the Atlantic and eastern song sparrows, however, are subtle and, at sites proximal to the coast, clinal (Patten and Pruett 2009; Clark et al. 2022), suggesting that the genetic basis of local adaptation in the Atlantic song sparrow is likely polygenic. Therefore, causal genes underpinning adaptive divergence may be localized to regions of suppressed recombination or, alternatively, these genes may be randomly scattered throughout the genome if selection is sufficiently strong and recombination is high.

Here, we examined the genome of the Atlantic song sparrow across its range in comparison to the eastern song sparrow subspecies to explore the genomic architecture of divergence with gene flow and uncover the genetic basis of coastal adaptation in this subspecies. We produced a new chromosome‐level reference assembly for the song sparrow and used whole‐genome resequencing of individuals across the ranges of the Atlantic and eastern song sparrow subspecies to (1) characterize population structure, (2) discern the genomic landscape of divergence with gene flow in this system, and (3) identify candidate genes for coastal adaptation in the Atlantic song sparrow. We hypothesized that genes under selection were likely to be associated with traits of the salt marsh syndrome, such as osmoregulation (salinity tolerance), skeletal development (bill size), or melanin synthesis (plumage pigmentation).

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

We sampled song sparrows during the breeding season (May–August) across the eastern United States in 2021 and 2022, choosing sites representative of the entire Atlantic song sparrow range and an equal breadth of the eastern song sparrow range in the states of Maine (ME), New York (NY), New Jersey (NJ), Delaware (DE), Maryland (MD), Virginia (VA), and North Carolina (NC). In total, we sampled 11 sites, targeting ≥ 5 individuals per site (Figure 2); Table S1. Sites were assigned to one of three categories. Inland sites in the eastern song sparrow range were categorized as “inland” and were specifically chosen to be > 250 km from the Atlantic song sparrow range to prevent the potential for any admixture (Clark et al. 2022). Coastal sites in the Atlantic song sparrow range were divided into two categories. The southernmost sites on barrier islands (Assateague, Hog, and Pea islands), where there was little‐to‐no chance of direct gene flow with inland birds, were categorized as “barrier islands.” The remaining coastal sites on the mainland—where there was the possibility of admixture with birds from nearby inland habitats—were categorized as “coastal mainland.”

We used mist‐nets and audio playback to capture adult song sparrows, then to confirm that each bird was a member of the targeted breeding population, we identified breeding condition by the presence of a cloacal protuberance (breeding male) or a brood patch (breeding female). A blood sample (50–100 μL) was collected from each bird via the brachial vein and stored in 500 μL Queen's lysis buffer at room temperature. Birds were banded with a uniquely numbered aluminum band issued by the United States Geological Survey before release. Sampling methods were approved by the University of New Hampshire Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approvals 190401 and 220401) and conducted with proper federal, state, and local banding permits. Additional blood samples in Queen's lysis buffer from Rochester, NY were provided by the Braddock Bay Bird Observatory, and some samples were taken from a prior study (from Assateague, MD, Blacksburg, VA, Claytor Lake, VA, and Frostburg, MD; Clark et al. 2022).

In total, blood samples were collected from 60 individuals. DNA was extracted from each blood sample using the Quick‐DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research) following manufacturer specifications with 50 μL of blood‐lysis solution and a one‐hour digestion. DNA was eluted using 200 μL of 10 mM Tris–HCl. The concentration of double‐stranded DNA in each extract was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) with the high‐sensitivity assay kit.

Reference Genome Assembly

We generated a new, high‐quality reference genome for the song sparrow in collaboration with the Vertebrate Genomes Project (VGP; Rhie et al. 2021) from a single additional individual. For the reference individual, we selected a breeding‐condition female song sparrow (band 2831‐61162) of the nominate subspecies that was captured in the salt marsh edge at Wharton Point on Maquoit Bay in Brunswick, ME (Coordinates: 43.869° N, 69.993° W) on July 29th, 2020. We collected 100 μL of blood in 100% ethanol before releasing the bird and stored the sample at −20°C.

DNA was extracted from this sample by the VGP and sequenced at 30.5 X coverage on a Sequel II HiFi (PacBio), producing long‐read data. Additionally, Hi‐C data were generated using the Arima Genomics Hi‐C kit 2 at 103.7 X coverage. The standard VGP assembly pipeline (v. 2.0) was used to generate a genome assembly from these data, which included hifiasm (v. 0.18.8), solve (v. 3.7), and YaHS (v. 1.2a.2). Raw data, sequence evaluations, and curated assemblies can be found on the VGP Genome Ark page for the song sparrow (https://www.genomeark.org/genomeark‐all/Melospiza_melodia.html). The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline was used to annotate the resulting reference assembly. The final reference assembly was submitted to the NCBI Reference Sequence Database. Syteny with the white‐throated sparrow ( Zonotrichia albicollis ) genome (GCF_047830755.1) was assessed using RagTag (v. 2.0.01; Alonge et al. 2022).

Whole‐Genome Re‐Sequencing

Sequencing libraries were generated for each individual using the KAPA HyperPlus Library Prep Kit (Roche) following manufacturer protocol with 250 ng of genomic DNA suspended in a 35 μL volume. To conserve supplies, half volumes of kit reagents were used, diluted to the correct volume with 10 mM Tris–HCl, for each step of the library preparation. The KAPA Unique Dual‐Indexed Adapter Kit (Roche) was used to barcode library molecules with a unique dual‐index to minimize the possibility of index hopping (MacConaill et al. 2018). Following enzymatic fragmentation (digested at 37°C for 20 min) and end repair and A‐tailing (ERAT; at 65°C for 30 min), samples were quantified and equimass pooled in groups of 6 for size selection (n = 10); samples were randomized across pools to prevent the confounding of population structure with potential batch effects. Size selection of 500–850 bp library molecules was performed for each pool using a lane of a BluePippin (Sage Science) with a 1.5% agarose gel cassette.

Low‐cycle PCR was used to amplify post‐size‐selection library pools (3 cycles) with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche) and Illumina TruSeq Primer Mix (Roche). After an initial denaturation at 98°C for 45 s, PCR cycle conditions followed manufacturer guidelines: denaturation at 98°C for 15 s, annealing at 60° for 30 s, extension at 72° for 30 s. After the final PCR cycle, there was a final extension at 72°C for 1 min, and the PCR products were then held at 4°C.

Post‐PCR library pools were purified using SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter) and quantified. Post‐PCR library pools were then assessed using a TapeStation (Agilent) and equimolar pooled into two final libraries (containing 30 samples each) for submission. The two final libraries were submitted to the Genomic Sciences Laboratory at North Carolina State University for sequencing on one lane each of a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with S4 chemistry (150 bp, paired‐end reads) and 5% PhiX spike‐in, targeting approximately 15X coverage.

Variant Discovery and SNP Filtering

Sequencing data were received demultiplexed by unique, dual‐index barcodes, and FastQC (v. 0.11.5) was used to assess the quality of each library. The program AdapterRemoval (v. 2.3.1) was then used to trim and merge reads, remove adapter sequences, and filter for Phred quality (‐‐trimns, ‐‐trimqualities, ‐‐minquality 35, ‐‐minlength 25; Schubert et al. 2016). Reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie 2 (v. 2.4.1; ‐‐phred33, ‐‐very‐sensitive‐local, ‐I 149, ‐X 900), and the resulting BAM files were sorted using Samtools (v. 1.10; Li et al. 2009; Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Next, Picard (v. 2.18.1) was used to validate, mark duplicate reads in, and index the resulting BAM files. The program Qualimap (v. 2.2.1) was used to examine the filtered alignments (García‐Alcalde et al. 2012).

The mpileup and call commands of the program BCFTools (v. 1.10.2) were used to call genotypes from the alignments (‐d 1000, ‐‐min‐MQ 30) and genotypes were output as a VCF file using the multiallelic caller (Li et al. 2009). Next, VCFTools (v. 0.1.16) was used to filter the resulting raw VCF to only retain biallelic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; ‐‐min‐alleles 2, ‐‐max‐alleles 2, ‐‐remove‐indels) with less than 20% missing data (‐‐max‐missing 0.8), an average depth of > 8 X and < 50 X (‐‐min‐meanDP 8, ‐‐max‐meanDP 50), and an average site quality > 30 (‐‐minQ 30; Danecek et al. 2011). A minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5% (‐‐maf 0.05) was then applied. From the resulting VCF, VCFTools was used to assess the average depth of coverage for each individual (‐‐depth) and pairwise relatedness (‐‐relatedness2). Individuals with an average depth < 7 X (n = 5) and an individual from each pair of close relatives (n = 2)—defined here as 3rd‐degree relatives or closer (Phi > 0.06; Manichaikul et al. 2010)—were removed from the dataset using VCFTools (‐‐remove), retaining 53 total individuals.

When removing individuals to generate the final filtered dataset, the above VCFTools filters were reapplied to the dataset and individual genotypes with a depth < 5 X were converted to missing data (‐‐minDP 5). Finally, individuals in this MAF‐filtered dataset were assessed for the proportion of missing SNPs per individual using VCFTools (‐‐missing‐indv), which was < 20% for all individuals. For analyses based on the site frequency spectrum that require no MAF filter and the inclusion of invariant positions, the removed individuals were also excluded from the raw VCF produced by mpileup and call without any additional SNP filtering.

Linkage Pruning and Statistical Phasing

Analyses of population structure and selection scans (see below), required additional SNP filtering of the MAF‐filtered dataset: SNPs were pruned for linkage disequilibrium (LD) for population structure analyses, and SNPs were converted to statistically phased haplotypes for selection analyses. First, to examine the degree of LD in these data, linkage decay (using mean r 2) was calculated with the program PopLDdecay (v. 3.42) at a maximum window size of 300 kbp (‐MaxDist 300; Zhang et al. 2019) using the MAF‐filtered dataset. Then, an LD‐pruned dataset was created with BCFTools (+prune) by excluding one from each pair of SNPs in the MAF‐filtered dataset within a window of 25 kbp (‐w 25000) that had an r 2 > 0.10 (‐l 0.1). To create a statistically phased haplotype dataset, the program SHAPEIT4 (v. 4.2.2) was run on the MAF‐filtered dataset (that included all 53 individuals) with a window size of 500 kbp on each scaffold (Delaneau et al. 2013). Only the 62/353 scaffolds that were > 1 Mbp were included in the phasing, however, 9 of these scaffolds contained no SNPs that passed filters and were, therefore, dropped. Thus, the phased haplotype dataset represented 96.4% of the total genome. VCFs of phased scaffolds were then combined into a single phased VCF with BCFTools (concat). Finally, VCFTools (‐‐keep) was used to subset the phased VCF into individual VCFs for each of the three subpopulations: barrier islands (n = 20), coastal mainland (n = 15), and inland (n = 18).

Population Structure

To explore population structure, a principal components analysis (PCA) was conducted on the LD‐pruned dataset. The PCA was executed in R using the dudi.pca function of the ade4 (v. 1.7–22) package without scaling (Dray and Dufour 2007). Missing genotypes were imputed as the mean genotype across individuals, where genotypes were scored as 0, 1, or 2. Then, to test the ability of PCA to reliably discriminate a priori populations, a discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) was run using the adegenet package (v. 2.1.10; Jombart 2008; Jombart et al. 2010). Three DAPCs were conducted: (a) with subspecies assignment as group (melodia or atlantica; K = 2), (b) with assigned population as group (inland, coastal mainland, or barrier island; K = 3), and (c) with assigned population as group but each barrier island separated as its own population (K = 5). All DAPCs were performed retaining the first 4 PCs (9.9% cumulative variance), as subsequent PCs were generally uninformative of population structure.

Genome‐Wide Divergence

Using pixy (v. 1.2.10.beta2), fixation index (F ST; Weir and Cockerham 1984), nucleotide diversity (π), and absolute sequence divergence (d xy) were calculated across the genome in non‐overlapping, 10‐kbp windows to characterize the genomic landscape of divergence between subspecies from the VCF that included invariant positions and did not have a MAF filter (Korunes and Samuk 2021). Because of the possibility for gene flow between the eastern and Atlantic subspecies at coastal mainland sites, these statistics were calculated for the most divergent groups based on PCA, inland birds (n = 18) and barrier island birds (n = 20).

Selection Scan

When selection favors a particular allele, linkage disequilibrium can result in decreased haplotype diversity in regions of the genome flanking that allele. Therefore, genomic regions that exhibit decreased haplotype diversity around a specific allele in a particular population can indicate local selection acting on that region of the genome (Sabeti et al. 2007). These signatures of selection can be summarized with the cross‐population extended haplotype homozygosity statistic (xpEHH), which is elevated when a particular haplotype is strongly favored in one population while remaining polymorphic in another (Sabeti et al. 2007). The R package rehh (v. 3.2.2) was used to conduct a haplotype‐based selection scan on the phased data (Gautier et al. 2017) based on xpEHH to identify specific loci exhibiting signatures of divergent selection between the Atlantic and eastern song sparrow subspecies. The selection scan was conducted by independently comparing the two coastal groups (barrier islands and coastal mainland) to the inland population.

First, the statistically phased VCFs of each group (inland, coastal mainland, and barrier islands) were read into R for each chromosome using the function data2haplohh without polarization. The data were then filtered to remove alleles with low minor allele frequency (< 5%) within each group using the subset function. Next, the integrated extended haplotype homozygosity (iES) statistic was calculated across each chromosome for each population using the scan_hh function. The integrated haplotype homozygosity statistic (iHS) was then calculated for each population from iES using the function ihh2ihs. Finally, the xpEHH statistic was calculated between each subpopulation pair using the ies2xpehh function (Sabeti et al. 2007). The two‐sided p‐values of xpEHH scores with a false discovery rate (FDR) correction (p.adjust.method = “fdr”) were used to identify markers in regions exhibiting signatures of divergent selection between these subspecies, where a marker with ‐log(p‐value) > 10 was considered significant.

Identifying Genes Associated With Coastal Adaptation

Genes were identified around markers exhibiting signals of selection based on xpEHH and considered as putative candidates of divergent adaptation between the subspecies. Gene symbols in the reference annotation (GCF_035770615.1) that overlapped or occurred within 10 kbp of each outlier marker were retrieved. Genes identified as under selection from both the inland–barrier islands and inland–coastal mainland comparisons were considered to have the strongest support for association with coastal adaptation in this system. The program PANTHER (v. 18.0), the UniProt database, and the literature were then queried for information on the putative functions of these identified candidate genes (Thomas et al. 2022; The UniProt Consortium 2023).

Results

Reference Genome

The final reference assembly (bMelMel2.pri.cur.20230821; GCF_035770615.1) was assembled into a total of 353 scaffolds, including 31 complete autosomal chromosomes (some with unlocated scaffolds) as well as the complete Z and W chromosomes. The three smallest micro‐chromosomes were not identified. The contig N50 was 23.12 Mbp and the scaffold N50 was nearly 3.6 times as large at 82.36 Mbp. In total, the assembly contained 20,371 annotated genes. At 1.55 Gbp, the final assembly was larger and more contiguous than recent song sparrow genome assemblies for other subspecies—M. m. maxima, M. m. morphna, and M. m. gouldii—which range from 0.98–1.40 Gbp (Feng et al. 2020; Louha et al. 2020; Benham, Cicero, et al. 2024; GCA_036924335.1). The wide discrepancy in recent reference assembly size among song sparrow subspecies merits further attention to determine whether it is solely the result of technical artifacts of genome assembly methodologies or a biological phenomenon (Benham, Cicero, et al. 2024).

Genomic Data

The final VCF of re‐sequenced birds contained 20 individuals from southern barrier islands, 15 from the coastal mainland, and 18 from inland sites. After all filtering, average individual coverage was 14.6 X (ranging from 7.0–31.6 X), and average genotype missingness was 2.5% (ranging from 0.03%–19.5%). Before MAF and individual genotype depth filtering, the dataset contained 37,255,533 biallelic SNPs, of which 11,496,474 SNPs were retained in the final MAF‐filtered dataset. The phased haplotype dataset retained the majority of SNPs from the MAF‐filtered dataset, 11,493,090. Linkage disequilibrium was notably low in these data, as linkage decayed to a genome‐wide average of r 2 < 0.10 at a distance of 310 bp, decreasing to r 2 = 0.03 at a distance of 300 kbp (Figure A1). A total of 1,114,777 SNPs were retained in the LD‐pruned dataset.

Population Structure

PCA clearly delineated individuals by population grouping in the first two principal components, with PC1 (3.4% variance explained) segregating subspecies (Figure 3). All birds from inland sites were separated from coastal birds and tightly clustered together, with birds from southern barrier islands separating to the other extreme and coastal mainland sites falling between. PC2 (2.4%) primarily discriminated birds from Pea Island, North Carolina from the other two southern barrier island populations. Birds (n = 4) from the eastern shore of the Chesapeake Bay (from below 38.5° N) and the Atlantic coast of Delaware (from below 38.7° N) clustered slightly more with Assateague Island birds than with other coastal mainland sites.

Subsequent PCs were not indicative of population structure, nor significantly informative based on the scree plot (Figure A2, Figure A3). Inland birds showed no substructure in the full‐data PCA, so a separate PCA was conducted on the inland birds alone to determine whether there was any substructure among inland populations. No geographic patterns emerged, consistent with panmixia within the eastern song sparrow across the sampling area (Figure A4). DAPC was able to reliably separate populations at all three levels (subspecies, population, and islands), and the same four birds from the Chesapeake Bay and Delaware exhibited some overlap with the Assateague cluster (Figure 3, Figure A5).

Genome‐Wide Divergence

Genome‐wide F ST between the subspecies was low (mean F ST = 0.027). Patterns of F ST across the genome showed few, isolated regions of the genome with slightly elevated differentiation against the genomic background (maximum window mean F ST = 0.430; Figure 4). However, most windows with visually elevated F ST had relatively few variant positions, and SNPs in these windows did not individually have exceptionally high F ST. For example, of the 29 windows with a mean F ST > 0.25, there was an average of 17 SNPs per window, versus a genome‐wide average of 363 SNPs per window. Therefore, due to the minimal linkage disequilibrium observed in this system, a window‐based F ST outlier approach may lack the resolution needed to reliably identify regions of the genome associated with phenotypic divergence between these subspecies.

Manhattan plots of genome‐wide statistics of divergence (F ST and d xy) for chromosomal scaffolds in 10‐kbp windows between the barrier islands (M. m. atlantica) and inland populations (M. m. melodia). Yellow and blue bolded windows contained SNPs identified as under divergent selection from the selection scan (see Figure 6).

Absolute sequence divergence between the subspecies was low (mean d xy = 6.00e‐03), tightly correlated with nucleotide diversity for both subspecies (R 2 atlantica = 0.983, R 2 melodia = 0.988), and incongruent with F ST (R 2 = 0.011). The tight correlation between d xy and π, and their incongruence with F ST, is consistent with signals of recent divergence and high gene flow observed in other systems (Figure 5; Henderson and Brelsford 2020). Overall, genome‐wide patterns of nucleotide diversity were similar for both subspecies across the genome (R 2 = 0.951), but average nucleotide diversity was lower in the Atlantic song sparrow (π atlantica = 5.65e‐03, π melodia = 6.02e‐03).

Selection Scan and Candidate Genes

The xpEHH tests for both comparisons yielded several, discrete genome‐wide peaks proximal to genes that exhibited signatures of divergent selection between the subspecies (Figure 6). Several of those peaks (n = 11) were shared between both the inland–barrier islands and inland–coastal mainland comparisons (Table 1). Of the gene‐containing genomic regions exhibiting signatures of divergent selection detected in both comparisons, most were under positive selection in the Atlantic song sparrow population (i.e., negative xpEHH; n = 7). Three of these genomic regions (i.e., 1, 4, and 6 from Table 1) were flanking putative centromeres, and one of these regions (1 from Table 1) contained a linked set of three genes under selection. Four of these loci were in regions of elevated F ST (1, 3, 4, and 6 from Table 1; Figure 4).

Several genes were identified in the regions exhibiting signatures of divergent selection between the Atlantic and the eastern song sparrow shared by both the inland–barrier islands and inland–coastal mainland comparisons (n = 20; Table 1), some of which had plausible associations with osmoregulatory functions. For example, one gene (ANO3) was a member of a chloride transmembrane transporter family (Tian et al. 2012). Other genes had known associations with the kidney, such as S100A16 and TRIP13 (Zimmer et al. 2003; Park 2015; Sun et al. 2020, 2022; Jin et al. 2021). A predicted gene (LOC134425495) that exhibited signatures of selection was orthologous to CYP11A1, a cholesterol side‐chain cleavage enzyme involved in steroidogenesis of hormones such as aldosterone, which thereby plays a role in salt/water balance (Miller and Auchus 2011).

Discussion

Fine‐Scale Population Structure Within a Specialist Subspecies

Prior efforts were able to discern limited population structure between the Atlantic and eastern song sparrows to some extent, but lacked definitive resolution. Specifically, Clark et al. (2022) delineated the Atlantic and eastern song sparrows as distinct subpopulations using a reduced‐representation sequencing approach, yielding thousands of SNPs but did not clearly differentiate populations nor identify substructure within the Atlantic song sparrow. Ultimately, whole‐genome data and range‐wide sampling were necessary to accurately identify the limited regions of the genome that exhibit signatures of divergent selection between these subspecies and characterize population substructure between and within subspecies.

In the Atlantic song sparrow, we found a complex population structure across its 1000 km breeding range along the Atlantic coast. Coastal mainland sites from the Delaware Bay north to New York constituted one subpopulation, which likely experiences some gene flow from inland populations. The Chesapeake Bay and barrier islands off the coast of Delaware and Maryland made up another distinct subpopulation. Barrier islands off the southern coast of Virginia, which are more isolated from inland birds, made up a third distinct subpopulation. The most southerly and isolated Atlantic song sparrow population, from a barrier island off the coast of North Carolina, represented the most distinct subpopulation. This illustrates that there is likely limited inter‐island gene flow among barrier island populations of the Atlantic song sparrow, and potentially unique standing variation within local island populations.

In contrast to the fine‐scale population structure of the Atlantic song sparrow subspecies, we found panmixia in the eastern song sparrow across a 1500 km north–south sampling area, that is, over 10° of latitude. Populations of the eastern song sparrow from inland sites across the sampled area may have been expected to exhibit isolation by distance (IBD), due to the breadth of its breeding range sampled here. However, panmixia within this subspecies is likely a result of its high dispersal ability and unpredictable migratory behavior (Weatherhead and Boak 1986; Davis and Arcese 1999; Kelly et al. 2016). Whole‐genome studies of some other song sparrow subspecies have not reported population structure within subspecies (Carbeck et al. 2023; Mikles et al. 2023). However, the Channel Islands song sparrow—a nonmigratory subspecies that breeds across several islands off the coast of California—has been shown to exhibit population structure among extant island populations which span a temperature gradient based on reduced‐representation genomic data (Gamboa et al. 2022). Generally, it appears that the extent of substructure varies among song sparrow subspecies due to differences in the extent of geographic isolation, life history traits, and local environmental conditions.

Genomic Architecture of Divergence With Gene Flow

Systems where local adaptation is maintained despite gene flow can shed light on the evolutionary processes that maintain divergence and may ultimately lead to ecological speciation (Rundle and Nosil 2005), particularly the interplay between genomic architecture and selection. We found that loci under divergent selection were generally scattered throughout the genome, rather than clustered in large linkage blocks such as chromosomal inversions. In such a scenario, where multiple unlinked loci contribute to adaptation, maintaining divergence in the face of gene flow likely requires sufficiently strong selection in the absence of assortative mating or other forms of reproductive isolation (Tigano and Friesen 2016).

The selection scan demonstrated the majority of divergent loci were the result of positive selection in the Atlantic song sparrow, consistent with the intense selection pressures of coastal habitats largely driving and maintaining divergence between these subspecies. Notably, a high recombination rate—such as that observed here—can lead to more efficient sorting of co‐adaptive sets of alleles in response to strong selection (Hill and Robertson 1966; Haddrill et al. 2007). We also found that some peaks exhibiting signals of divergent selection based on extended haplotype homozygosity were proximal to putative centromeres, suggesting that the recombination landscape may play a minor role in the maintenance of co‐adapted gene complexes between these subspecies. It is not unprecedented for centromeres to house genes associated with divergence in systems with high gene flow, such as in hybridizing bumble bee species (Bombus spp.) where genomic islands of divergence are largely limited to pericentromeric regions throughout the genome (Christmas et al. 2021).

Both temperature and salinity—principal habitat differences between these two subspecies—have been found to be the primary drivers of differentiation among three other coastal song sparrow subspecies in contact that maintain divergence in the San Francisco Bay area (Mikles et al. 2023). Although the locally adaptive phenotypes of the Atlantic song sparrow appear to be somewhat resistant to maladaptive gene flow, demographic modeling suggests that these two subspecies are likely experiencing secondary contact following a period of allopatric divergence, so it is possible this system is not in migration–selection balance and that selection may not be strong enough to prevent the geographically restricted Atlantic song sparrow from being swamped with maladaptive alleles from the eastern song sparrow over time eventually resulting in fusion (Clark et al. 2022).

Notably, F ST peaks between the Atlantic and eastern song sparrow subspecies did not consistently correspond with regions exhibiting signatures of divergent selection between the subspecies based on xpEHH. This misalignment between windowed F ST and the haplotype‐based selection scan may be a result of extremely low LD—which reduces the power of windowed F ST to identify regions under divergent selection—incomplete selective sweeps between the populations, and/or gene flow preventing fixation of alleles under divergent selection (Jensen et al. 2016). Many F ST peaks represented regions of low genetic diversity in the genome, which can inflate F ST, rather than regions of elevated divergence driven by selection.

Genetic Basis of Coastal Adaptation

Elucidating the genetic basis of coastal adaptation in the Atlantic song sparrow contributes to our understanding of genotype–phenotype associations in avian traits and provides insights into parallel adaptation to coastal environments in passerines. We identified several genes exhibiting signatures of divergent selection in the Atlantic song sparrow genome, some of which had plausible associations with traits of the avian salt marsh syndrome. For example, several of these genes have known roles in osmoregulatory processes, consistent with the physiological challenges posed by high‐salinity coastal habitats (Greenberg and Maldonado 2006; Greenberg et al. 2006).

Parallel adaptation often arises through lineage‐specific genetic changes. Consistent with the pattern, despite the numerous studies that have examined the genetic basis of coastal adaptation in other passerine species, many genes identified as targets of selection here have not been associated with traits relevant to the salt marsh syndrome in other species previously. For example, none of the genes associated with divergence in the Atlantic song sparrow were identified as candidates of selection in the three coastal song sparrow subspecies of the San Francisco Bay area (Mikles et al. 2023). Similarly, neither a study that characterized the genetic basis of divergent coastal adaptation between the saltmarsh sparrow (Ammospiza caudacuta) and Nelson's sparrow ( A. nelsoni ; Walsh, Clucas, et al. 2019) nor a study that examined adaptation to hot, saline environments in the mangrove yellow warbler ( Setophaga petechia ; Chavarria‐Pizarro et al. 2019) found genes associated with coastal adaptation that were shared with those identified here in the Atlantic song sparrow. Comparisons of freshwater‐, brackish‐, and saline‐adapted subspecies of the Savannah sparrow ( Passerculus sandwichensis nevadensis , P. s. alaudinus, and P. s. beldingi) in California also found no genes exhibiting signatures of selection in coastal habitats that were shared with those identified here (Benham, Walsh, and Bowie 2024). Notably, two genes identified as putative candidates of selection for the Atlantic song sparrow using reduced‐representation genomic data, SENP5 and COL12A1, were not detected by the selection scan here, and are therefore possibly spurious candidates given the limitation of reduced representation methods to detect genes under selection.

Although some genes identified here are novel candidates of coastal adaptation in a passerine species, some genes under selection in the Atlantic song sparrow have been identified as targets of selections in other coastal–inland passerine subspecies comparisons, emphasizing how parallel evolution to a selective regime is a complex process that can occur through a combination of shared and lineage‐specific genetic mechanisms (Walsh, Benham, et al. 2019). Specifically, two genes, TRIP13 and PCSK2, were divergent between a coastal–inland subspecies pair in a congener, the swamp sparrow ( M. georgiana georgiana and M. g. nigrescens; Deane‐Coe et al. 2018). PCSK2 is a convertase with roles cleaving several hormone precursors, including pro‐vasopressin (Pan et al. 2006). PCSK2 also has been associated with melanin synthesis in avian plumage, as it is the convertase responsible for cleaving ACTH to produce the agonist of MC1R α‐MSH (e.g., Ducrest et al. 2025, Oliver Brown et al. 2025). Another gene detected as a target of selection here, COL15A1, has been suggested as influencing variation in body‐size extremes between other song sparrow subspecies, which notably also vary widely in both bill size/shape and plumage pigmentation (Carbeck et al. 2023). Building our understanding of the genetic basis of trait divergence across avian species may help us conserve adaptive variation and understand how population will respond in the face of changing environments.
