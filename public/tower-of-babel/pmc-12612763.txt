Implementing PST in older adults facing major surgery: a randomised controlled pilot study
Authors: Victoria Tang, Lejla Pepic, Erika Higuchi, Christina Keny, Elizabeth Macias Lopez, Ezenwa C Onyema, Harleen Sandhu, Veronica Yank, Patrick J Raue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepressive symptoms are common in the growing geriatric surgical population and are associated with important patient-centred surgical outcomes, including postoperative delirium, discharge to postacute care facility and decline in functional status. Few interventions have been developed to address depressive symptoms in the perioperative setting.Methods and analysisWe designed a feasibility and acceptability study of a nine-session problem-solving therapy (PST) telehealth perioperative intervention aimed at reducing postoperative functional decline and depressive symptoms among at-risk older adults undergoing major surgical procedures. Acceptability will be evaluated using a patient-centred five-question survey, assessing participant satisfaction and perceived usefulness of the perioperative intervention. A feasibility assessment will rely on objective measures including ease of participant recruitment, frequency and timing of delivery of intervention sessions and retention of participants throughout the duration of the intervention. With respect to the efficacy of the proposed PST intervention, the primary outcome of interest is postoperative functional status, as measured by the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 at the 6-month postoperative time point. The secondary outcome of interest is the degree of depressive symptoms as assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 at both 3 months and 6 months postoperatively. The broader goals of this study include: (1) assessing the feasibility of implementing a PST perioperative intervention for older surgical patients at risk of postoperative functional decline, (2) demonstrating the acceptability of the PST intervention and (3) assessing the preliminary impact of the PST intervention on postoperative functional status and depressive symptoms.Ethics and disseminationThe study received ethical approval from the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Board. Results of this study will be published in peer-reviewed scientific journals with further dissemination at local institutional meetings and professional conferences.Trial registration numberNCT06174701.Keywords: Psychosocial Intervention, GERIATRIC MEDICINE, Depression & mood disorders, Adult surgery, Clinical Trial

Introduction

In the setting of an ageing national population, the number of older adults undergoing major surgical procedures is projected to increase substantially in the coming decades.1 Older adults are a unique and complex surgical population with increased likelihood of multiple comorbid medical conditions, baseline functional limitations and geriatric syndromes including cognitive dysfunction and frailty, all of which place them at greater risk for postoperative complications.2,5 Owing to increased medical complexity, the perioperative period is a high-risk period for older adults, with high rates of postoperative functional and cognitive decline relative to their younger counterparts.6 Several studies have identified specific preoperative factors associated with increased risk of postoperative functional decline among older adults. One such emerging risk factor is psychosocial vulnerability, which encompasses various factors including the quality of social support relationships as well as the overall mental and emotional well-being of the patient.7,9 Figure 1 describes a potential framework for psychosocial vulnerability as a determinant of physiological postoperative outcomes. Exposure to a stressor, for example, a major surgical procedure, can lead to a range of poor physiologic outcomes including postoperative anxiety, depression and functional decline. We hypothesise that psychosocial vulnerability is a key moderating variable in this relationship, impacting the ability to respond adequately to stressors and therefore resulting in poor physiological outcomes. As such, psychosocial vulnerability presents a key modifiable target for perioperative interventions with the goal of improving postoperative outcomes.10

Conceptual framework for psychosocial vulnerability as a modifiable target in the physiologic response to stressors.

Studies across various surgical specialties have demonstrated a high rate of depression among older surgical patients, with an estimated one in four older adults experiencing depressive symptoms in the preoperative period.11 In older adults, greater severity of preoperative depressive symptoms is associated with poorer postoperative functional outcomes, including higher rates of non-home discharge and decline in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), in a dose-dependent manner.4 8 9 11 Despite increased recognition of psychosocial vulnerability as a predictor of poor postoperative functional recovery, traditional surgical ‘prehabilitation’ interventions overwhelmingly focus on optimisation of patients’ medical and physical status via exercise and nutritional interventions, with few perioperative interventions aimed at assessing or addressing preoperative psychosocial risk factors.7 12 13 Studies have shown that the integration of multimodal perioperative interventions, including mental health and social support, has allowed for improved postoperative functional status among older adults following major surgery.14 While perioperative mental health interventions may help reduce postoperative functional decline in older adults, significant gaps remain regarding their delivery and feasibility.14

Our research group has used a variety of mixed-methods approaches across various stakeholder groups to identify potential targets for interventions aimed at addressing psychosocial vulnerabilities perioperatively. In a qualitative study of older adults’ lived experience in the postoperative setting, we extracted unifying themes characterising the postoperative challenges faced by older adults.15 Participants described substantial interactions between postoperative recovery and psychosocial well-being, with approximately one in three older adults reporting depressive symptoms at 3 months postoperatively.15 Moreover, further thematic analysis demonstrated that many older adults felt poorly prepared for the postoperative recovery period, with inadequate education, support and resources provided to help them manage their postoperative symptoms and overall well-being.15 These findings are further supported by another qualitative study, which demonstrated not only that older adults experienced significant psychosocial challenges in the perioperative recovery period, but also that additional personal and psychosocial support, beyond what can reasonably be provided by family members, was needed to help patients and caregivers navigate these psychosocial postoperative challenges.16 The need for perioperative psychosocial interventions and support is further reflected by the opinions of various clinicians involved in the care of elderly patients in the postoperative period, many of whom have identified psychosocial distress as an important determinant of a patient’s ability to navigate postoperative challenges after a major surgery.17 The perspectives gleaned from these qualitative studies highlight the need for interventions to address emotional well-being in the perioperative period, particularly among older adults with preoperative psychosocial risk factors.

Problem-solving therapy (PST) has emerged as an evidence-based intervention aimed at providing individuals with the tools to identify and manage anticipated problems in their lives, operating under the premise that problem-solving skills serve as an important moderator between stressful major life events and overall well-being.10 PST emphasises a stepwise approach including problem identification, selection and implementation of solution strategies with the goal of providing a general problem-solving framework that can be applied to future problems.10 Under the guidance of the interventionist, the patient defines the problem, generates potential solutions, systematically evaluates and selects an implementable solution, and subsequently monitors the implementation and solution outcome.18 PST has shown efficacy among older patient populations, with a systematic review by Kiosses et al demonstrating that PST is more effective at reducing depressive symptoms in older patients than other psychological interventions including supportive and reminiscence therapy and has comparable efficacy to pharmacotherapy with paroxetine.19 PST holds promise for use in older adults whose depressive symptoms are often coexistent with executive and cognitive dysfunction, conditions that confer challenges with planning and initiating goal-oriented behaviours.19 20 Additionally, PST is advantageous in that it can be delivered across diverse practice settings, including institutional settings and in patients’ personal homes via multiple modalities including telephone, internet and in-person, with largely comparable efficacy across these modes of delivery.19 Despite its demonstrated efficacy in reducing depressive symptoms among older adults, no studies to date have assessed the effectiveness of a PST intervention among older adults undergoing major surgery.

Our pilot study proposes a nine-session telehealth PST intervention delivered in the preoperative and immediate postoperative period to older surgical patients with preoperative depressive symptoms or those with significant risk factors, with the goal of assessing the feasibility and efficacy of perioperative PST in promoting postoperative functional recovery and reducing postoperative depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This pilot study is expected to start recruitment in June 2024 and is estimated to end by July 2026. The pilot will involve a prospectively registered (NCT06174701) randomised controlled trial (RCT) of a nine-session PST perioperative intervention compared with usual perioperative care accompanied by an educational brochure on mental health. Older adults with preoperative depressive symptoms or evidence of functional dependence, as determined by the inclusion criteria described in table 1, undergoing a planned major elective surgical procedure will be randomised to one of the two aforementioned interventions. Participants scheduled for surgery more than 4 weeks after recruitment will be excluded to minimise variation in post-baseline endpoint measures. We chose to recruit participants prior to surgery because our previous qualitative research demonstrated that preoperative anxiety and depressive symptoms were particularly distressing for older adults.15,17 These findings highlighted the critical need to address psychological symptoms in the preoperative setting and directly informed our decision to initiate recruitment and begin the intervention before surgery.

The purpose of this pilot study is to determine whether and how to align PST with older adult needs in the perioperative context. Our initial design of the study is based on the research team’s long-standing clinical experience with older surgical patient populations in the preoperative setting, specifically a prehabilitation programme and in the postoperative inpatient setting. We plan to learn and iterate on the optimal dose in this patient population through exit interviews of those participants that received the intervention. The initial dose proposed in this protocol was chosen based on the PST-Primary Care manual and typical use in research and practical settings, which averages about 9 sessions (5–13 sessions).21,25 Telehealth was decided on based on patient feedback in the clinic setting (eg, increase accessibility) and evidence that (1) teletherapy is effective and feasible in older adults with depression and anxiety and (2) PST telehealth delivery is no different than in-person delivery in homebound older adults with depression.

Participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control group of usual care, with 35 individuals in each arm. Randomisation will be performed in REDCap, and the research assistant, who is blinded prior to randomisation and throughout the first baseline assessment, will send the randomisation packet to participants by mail or email as appropriate. Although blinding of participants and most of the research team is not possible after group assignment, only those assessing study outcomes will remain blinded to group allocation to minimise bias in outcome measurement. Personnel involved in outcome assessment, including statisticians, will be blinded to the randomisation group. The intervention will be delivered via a telehealth platform, while the educational brochure provided with usual care will be mailed or emailed to the participants prior to their planned surgical procedure. The study is intended to help assess the feasibility of delivering and evaluating PST in an older surgical population and to produce preliminary data for effect size estimates to be used for sample size determination and planning for a larger multicentre trial.

The sample size was determined based on feasibility and recruitment rates observed in prior studies. Enrolling approximately 90 participants within 1 year is achievable and appropriate for a pilot feasibility study of this scope. This number will allow us to assess recruitment capacity and estimate effect sizes to inform future, larger-scale studies. The pilot study was approved by University of California San Francisco IRB # 22-37881, with reliance on UTHealth Houston where the interventionists are employed.

Participants and recruitment

Study participants will be older adults, ≥ 65 years of age, scheduled to undergo major elective surgical procedures who are at risk for poor postoperative functional outcomes. We defined major surgery as ‘major operation as defined by the American Medical Association (ie, >1% 30 day in-hospital mortality).26 At-risk populations were defined as all patients ≥80 years of age as well as patients between 65 and 79 years of age with either a positive preoperative depression screen as determined by a Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) score ≥5 or who exhibit dependency in activities of daily living (ADL) (Katz Index ≤5) or IADL (Lawton-Brody Scale ≤4 or ≤7 for men and women, respectively). All participants (self-referred or clinician referred) are asked, “Has anyone spoken to you about life expectancy? ‘If yes, they were asked ‘how long is your life expectancy?” See table 1 for a complete description of study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants will also be asked if they are engaged with external therapists. In the full RCT, a sensitivity analysis will be performed to account for engagement with external therapists.

We will recruit 90 older surgical patients from 2 main surgical centres: (1) Perioperative Optimisation of Senior Health clinic at University of Texas Southwestern Medical Centre in Dallas, Texas and (2) MD Anderson in Houston, Texas. The target recruitment number is based on preliminary recruitment work in the clinic. Given our success in recruiting 131 patients for the cohort over 1.5 years, we anticipate that we will be able to recruit 90 older adults who are undergoing major surgery in the preoperative period over the 2-year study time frame.27

The first 20 participants will be enrolled during a run-in period for training and interventionist learning curve stabilisation. The subsequent 70 enrolled participants will be randomised 1:1 via blocked randomisation to either the PST intervention or the standard care plus preoperative mental health educational handout control group. Study recruitment will occur through two possible pathways: (1) Self-referral: Interested participants contact the study team themselves based on recruitment flyers present in waiting rooms and clinic offices or (2) Clinician referral: Clinicians provide the name and contact information for patients who may qualify for the study based on the aforementioned study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Enrolment and consent will be completed over the phone, with the option for mail-in consent forms based on participant preference. Participants will receive a US$10 Amazon gift card after each of four assessments, for a total of US$40. Participants in the intervention arm who also complete a one-time, 15 min recorded user experience interview will receive an additional US$10 Amazon gift card, bringing their total compensation to US$50 in gift cards. Intervention arm

Participants randomised to the intervention group will receive weekly problem-solving and coping skills training both before and after surgery, including approximately 2–3 sessions preoperatively and 6–7 sessions postoperatively, for a total of about nine sessions. PST content and structure is described in table 2. The content and timing of sessions do not change if surgery is postponed. PST interventionists who deliver the therapy will be trained and credentialed by a PST expert trainer. During the intervention part of the study, after the initial 20-patient run-in training period, an expert trainer will monitor intervention fidelity by rating the audiotapes of one session per interventionist each month, using a standardised PST fidelity rating form. Some variation between interventionists is expected; as long as fidelity criteria are met, no further action is required. If fidelity standards are not met, the interventionist will receive feedback and undergo continued monitoring and review until fidelity is achieved. An expert trainer is a clinician who has been certified in PST and has enough experience in PST to draw on when training novices in the intervention.28 Each PST session will be documented via patient PHQ-9 and completed PST session worksheets. The study will involve three interventionists who will be assigned longitudinally to each of the participants such that each participant is working with one interventionist for the entire course of the nine-session intervention. Essential components of the PST intervention will include: (1) defining the nature of the problem, (2) generating a wide range of possible solutions, (3) systematically evaluating potential solutions and selecting the most optimal ones to implement and (4) monitoring and evaluating the solution outcome.

If surgery is delayed by 4 weeks or less, participants will continue the intervention as originally scheduled, with no changes to the timeline or delivery. If the planned surgery is delayed by more than 4 weeks, the participant will be withdrawn from the study. Up to 20 participants of the 35 in the intervention group will be offered the option to participate in a one-to-one 15 min one-time-only semistructured interview within 2 weeks of the last PST session. The purpose is to gather qualitative data on user experience. The research team developed and pilot-tested an interview guide that covered the following broad topics regarding user experience with PST such as (1) the overall experiences with PST and (2) recommendations for improvement.

Control arm

The participants randomised to the control arm will receive ‘enhanced usual care’ in the form of an educational mental health handout that will be mailed or emailed to them prior to surgery. The handout is a three-page document with techniques for reducing stress and fear while preparing for surgery, with information on physical preparation, mental preparation, relaxation techniques and breathing exercises as well as links to various online resources (see the Enhanced Usual Care document in online supplemental materials).

Baseline measures

Baseline and outcome measures will be collected using various validated patient-centred questionnaires. For the training participant group (n=20), baseline measures will be collected at the day of enrolment with an additional interval assessment at 1-month post-baseline. For the main participant group (n=70), the baseline measures will be collected at the day of enrolment, with additional interval assessments at 1, 3 and 6 months post-baseline. Demographic information

Baseline demographic information will be collected on all participants. This includes age, sex, race, ethnicity, highest level of educational attainment and family income.

Medical history and comorbidities

Medical history and comorbidities will be collected using an eight-question survey adapted from the Health and Retirement Study. This survey assesses key comorbid conditions which have been identified in previous literature as key risk factors for functional status decline among community-dwelling elderly patients.29 The survey includes eight yes-or-no responses to the question: Has a doctor ever told you that you have: (1) high blood pressure or hypertension; (2) diabetes or high blood sugar; (3) cancer or a malignant tumour of any kind except skin cancer; (4) chronic lung disease except asthma such as chronic bronchitis or emphysema; (5) heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure or other heart problems; (6) stroke or transient ischaemic attack; (7) emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems and (8) arthritis or rheumatism.

Primary outcomes

Feasibility and acceptability

Assessment of the feasibility and acceptability of the PST perioperative intervention will involve both objective measures of engagement and participation as well as subjective, patient-reported feedback on the intervention delivery. For intervention acceptability, participants will be asked to complete the Acceptability Survey and Questionnaire, a five-question survey to be administered after PST session 9 by a team member who is not the interventionist for the participant. The intervention acceptability questions are described in box 1 and include questions related to participant satisfaction with the intervention, perceived usefulness of the problem solving and coping strategies with respect to perioperative problem solving, as well as satisfaction with the timeline of intervention delivery and were based off of the Acceptability of Intervention Measure.30 Feasibility of the PST intervention will be determined based on data regarding success and ease of participant recruitment, retention of participants throughout the entire nine-session intervention, and the ability to deliver the intervention sessions with the intended frequency. We clarify that the purpose of assessing efficacy is to determine the feasibility of collecting outcome measures, rather than to formally evaluate treatment effects. The results will provide critical information such as rates of enrolment, retention, adherence and the practicality of assessing the chosen outcomes to support and optimise the design of a larger, multicentre randomised clinical trial, including more accurate sample size calculations.

Physical function

Functional status decline will be assessed using the brief WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0), a 12-item survey evaluating the participant’s ability to perform various day-to-day functions.31 32 Degree of independence in ADL and IADL will be assessed using the Katz Index and Lawton Scale, respectively.33,35

Secondary outcomes

Pain

Pain will be assessed using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) which has been validated for use in assessing pain across multiple aetiologies.36,38 Our study includes only the first question from the 11-item BPI short-form: Throughout our lives, most of us have had pain from time to time (such as minor headaches, sprains and toothaches). Have you had pain other than these everyday kinds of pain today?

Cognition

Cognition will be assessed using the Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire which is a 10-item questionnaire validated for use in the assessment of cognitive impairment among elderly adults.39 Adjustments are made on the basis of participant education levels.

Mental health

The secondary outcome of depressive symptoms will be assessed using the PHQ-9 which has been validated for use in older patients.40 41 For the purposes of our study, a positive depressive screen will be defined as a PHQ-9 score of ≥5. The Behavioural Activation for Depression Scale-Short Form (BADS-SF) will be used to measure behavioural responses to depression. This is a nine-item short form survey which evaluates primary measures of activation and avoidance behaviours.42 The BADS questionnaire was chosen because it reliably assesses the key mechanisms targeted by behavioural activation which are increased engagement in rewarding activities and reduced avoidance, both considered central to the behavioural model of depression.43,45 As a validated and sensitive tool, BADS aligns with our intervention’s goals and provides a measure of behavioural change and progress.43 45 Self-efficacy will be assessed using the General Perceived Self-Efficacy Scale 10 item survey which evaluates participant ability to cope and adapt to stressful life events.46 Anxiety symptoms will be evaluated using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7-item survey (GAD-7) validated for use in detecting GAD among older adult populations.47

Social support

Degree of social and emotional support will be evaluated using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Emotional Support and Instrumental Support short-form surveys. These eight-item surveys assess the degree of daily support available to the participant.48

Service use

Service use will be assessed by documenting incidences of rehospitalisation and institutionalisation, including facility type, treatment received, reason for and duration of rehospitalisation or institutionalisation.

Other outcomes

Additional outcomes, including participant death, loss to follow-up and withdrawal from the study will be tracked using a study status form.

Ethics and dissemination

Patient and public involvement

Patients were first involved in this research throughout the prepilot where interventionists were trained and provided with feedback on timing of the intervention at the exit interview. Our previous work with patients in the clinic informed the research question and outcome measures based on the priorities and preferences participants shared with the study team. Informal discussions with participants helped inform the timing of the design to begin prior to surgery.49 50 Additionally, in this pilot study, we continue to collect this information from our patient participants through qualitative interviews. As per the acceptability survey questions presented in table 2, participants will also be asked to assess their overall experience with the intervention including their satisfaction with the intervention strategies, sufficiency of the intervention timeline and willingness to participate in the intervention again in the future, which will be used to aid in future larger-scale applications of the PST intervention.

Data management and safety

Participant data will be captured using standardised case report form (CRF) documents. Completed CRFs, copies of informed consent documents and other key source information (surveys, QoL instruments, WHODAS measurements) will be retained in patient binders that are stored in locked cabinets in a secure area. For the intervention training participant group (n=20), CRF measurements will be collected at two time points: day of enrolment (baseline measure) and 1-month post-baseline. For the main trial participant group (n=70), the same CRF assessments will be collected at four time points: day of enrolment (baseline measure), 1, 3 months and 6 months post-baseline. Data from the assessments will be entered into and stored in a secure online database management portal (REDCap) by the research team.51 52 Audio recordings of the PST sessions will be required during the initial training portion of the study with the initial 20 participants. Audio recordings will be optional, though encouraged, during the main part of the study with the subsequent 70 participants. Sessions will be recorded for quality assurance purposes and will be stored using myTransfer, a password-protected virtual private network (VPN) secure server hosted by the University of California San Francisco, for the PST Trainer or a senior interventionalist to access. The recordings will be destroyed at the end of all relevant record retention requirements.

Data analysis

The data analysis will consist mainly of descriptive statistics related to feasibility and acceptability and will not include data from the initial 20 patients recruited during the interventionist training period. Analysis will include assessing the success of randomisation by comparing the balance of patient characteristics between the intervention and control groups. Recruitment success will be assessed by examining the percentage of patients recruited, screened and entered into the study. The primary outcome of functional status, as assessed by changes in the brief 12-item WHODAS score from baseline, as well as the secondary outcome of depressive symptoms as assessed by changes in the PHQ-9 score from baseline, will be calculated and reported for the intervention and control arms for descriptive reporting purposes rather than between-group analysis. Feasibility of intervention delivery will involve analysis of the percentage of participants who completed the entire nine-session intervention as well as data on frequency of session delivery throughout the study. For both the intervention and control arms, completion rate of all baseline and interval assessment surveys will be assessed to determine the appropriate degree of survey burden. Acceptability data will involve analysis of patient responses to the five-question Acceptability Survey and Questionnaire delivered at the end of the nine-session intervention. We will be able to report qualitative and survey data on feasibility and acceptability of the intervention in this patient population and identify areas of iterative process improvement for the planned future trial. We plan to adjust for differences in timing of post-baseline measures in the future larger RCT.

Participant consents

Participants will be provided with a plain language informed consent form (see the consent form document in the online supplemental materials). Participant consent will be obtained over the phone with consent forms sent to the participant via email. For participants unfamiliar with or unable to navigate the online consent platform, paper consents will be mailed to the participant with return postage to be signed and sent back. All consent forms will be stored securely in REDCap.

Harms

There is minimal risk to participants enrolled in the study. Potential risks include loss of privacy, emotional discomfort associated with potentially sensitive survey questions and participant fatigue incurred during the intervention sessions or due to survey burden during the assessments. To mitigate privacy risks, we will be storing all data on secure platforms including the secure online database management portal REDCap for baseline and interval measures as well as myTransfer for session recordings. Given the potentially sensitive nature of the data collected, including questions pertaining to mental health and substance use, participants will be informed that they are not obligated to answer any questions they do not feel comfortable answering and can terminate the sessions and assessments at any time.

If a participant endorses PHQ-9 item 9 (suicidal ideation) or suicidal ideation at any other point, the study team member on the phone, who has been trained by the Principal Investigator in using the Raue Suicide Risk Assessment, will immediately conduct a further risk assessment.53 Based on the results: if ‘mild risk’ (ie, passive SI) is identified, the team member will advise the participant to consult their primary care provider, provide the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (988), and continue the study; for ‘intermediate risk’ (ie, active SI) the participant will remain on the line while the case is immediately reviewed with the principal investigator, who will determine appropriate action, such as recommending a clinical appointment, establishing a safety plan and, with the participant’s knowledge, contacting their clinician or a family member; and for ‘high risk’, (ie, suicidal intention or specific detailed plan), the participant will be kept on the phone while a collaborator is contacted on another line to call 911.

Dissemination

The feasibility and acceptability results of this pilot study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed scientific journals as well as at local institutional meetings and national conferences. As per NIH policy, submission of the accepted manuscript to the PubMed Central (PMC) repository will be made by the publisher to ensure the work is freely accessible online.

Trial status

The study has been registered in the Clinical Trials Registry, National Clinical Trial number NCT06174701.

Discussion

Depressive symptoms are a key element of psychosocial vulnerability and are known to represent an important comorbid factor influencing surgical outcomes. To date, few studies have attempted to estimate the potential protective effects of addressing depression and psychosocial vulnerability on postsurgical functional outcomes in older adults. While other perioperative mental health interventions are being trialled for older surgical patients with preoperative anxiety and depression, our study is unique in that it focuses on functional recovery as a primary outcome of the intervention, and it focuses on older adults with preoperative depressive symptoms as well as those with specific risk factors.54 Furthermore, while other studies have trialled surgical mental health interventions including personalised coping strategies and behavioural activation, our study is the first of its kind to implement PST as a perioperative intervention in older surgical patients.55 56 PST is a promising intervention for a variety of reasons. Of note, we acknowledge that other EBPI, such as behavioural activation, have similar strengths to PST.54 57 PST is a promising intervention for a variety of reasons. It has been shown to be effective at reducing depressive symptoms in older individuals and is a scalable intervention that can be delivered across multiple settings and modalities as well as performed by a variety of providers when appropriately trained. PST can also be delivered remotely by phone or videoconference, not requiring that patients and providers be in the same location. This increases the likelihood of adherence to the session schedule and therefore the quality of the intervention. Scalability and flexibility make it practical to deliver sessions longitudinally and in multiple settings (home, hospital, nursing facility), so that continuity of care can be provided without disruption during an extended engagement from the preoperative period to 6 months postoperative or longer. A particular benefit of being able to mitigate depression and psychosocial vulnerability using this intervention is that it will allow us to estimate the magnitude of the effects of this risk factor on functional outcome in the control group, and to determine how modifiable this risk factor is by intervention with respect to our long-term functional outcome. We believe that this will represent a major proof of concept and hence an important contribution to the literature, that can only be obtained in an unbiased fashion by the type of experimental design we intend to implement in the larger multicentre trial.

There are several limitations to our proposed study. As a pilot study, the sample size is inherently limited; therefore, formal frequentist statistical hypothesis testing of treatment effect will not be meaningful due to the risk of type 2 error. We do not propose frequentist between-group comparisons for this reason and instead will report mostly descriptive data relevant to feasibility and acceptability. Similarly, a relatively small sample recruited in a single geographic region may lack generalisability due to a limited range of comorbid conditions and small group sizes for the possible range of surgical procedures. The small sample size will also make it infeasible to control for clustering of treatment effects within interventionists and to assess potential effect modification by important covariates such as sex, which have known association with both frequency of depressive symptoms and surgical outcome.

Despite these limitations, PST is an exciting therapeutic option that may be more effective than pharmacotherapy for management of depressive symptoms among older adults in the perioperative period. Each year, more than 4 million older adults undergo major surgery in the USA, and approximately one in four experience significant depressive symptoms prior to their procedure.11 26 By reaching this population, our PST intervention has the potential to benefit up to 1 million older surgical patients annually by addressing preoperative mood symptoms and improving perioperative outcomes. PST may be effective as a perioperative intervention to promote functional recovery and enhance patient adjustment to postsurgical life, helping to support patients’ resilience to any new and persistent circumstances that may arise from surgical interventions.
