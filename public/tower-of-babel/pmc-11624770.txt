Death of the paediatric manikin: a scoping review
Authors: Teleireoluwa Apara, Tom Hogan, Jennifer L H Peterson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhether the manikin should die in simulation training is a controversial area, with some educators stating that manikin death is essential in providing realistic training, while others state that the psychological burden of manikin death could derail learning and impair psychological safety. This scoping review aims to explore the existing literature regarding death of the manikin in paediatric and neonatal simulation education.MethodsThe literature was searched for publications regarding paediatric and/or neonatal manikin death in simulation training. The same search strategy was used across MEDLINE (OVID), Embase (OVID), CINAHL (EBSCO) and PsycInfo (OVID) databases. Articles were screened against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.Results810 articles were identified. 807 were excluded (duplicates/did not meet criteria). Three articles were suitable for inclusion in the final review. Included studies were analysed using an inductive thematic analysis approach.ConclusionsThere is a paucity of research in this important area of simulation training. From the limited research available, the following themes were identified: death of the paediatric manikin can increase scenario realism; participation in paediatric manikin death scenarios was not more stressful than participating in standard simulation; and the debrief has a crucial role in mitigating the potentially negative impacts of paediatric manikin death for learners.Death of the paediatric and neonatal manikin can provide a beneficial educational experience for participants but requires considered and experienced facilitation.

Introduction

Within medical education, simulation-based training allows healthcare professionals (HCP) to recreate realistic scenarios that they are likely to confront in clinical practice using a tactile ‘hands-on’ approach in a low-risk environment.1 The Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health describes simulation training as having an ‘increasingly important role in paediatric training, and that it can help ensure improved patient and family experience, safety and outcomes’.1 Additionally, professional organisations around the world have begun to use simulation for assessment and recertification of essential clinical skills, such as the Newborn Resuscitation Program (NRP)2 and the paediatric advanced life support programmes.3 Champions of simulation education suggest that simulated training leads to improved knowledge, teamwork and increased proficiency in procedural skills compared with traditional methods of training.4 5 Research shows that simulation-based education is a positive educational experience for participants and also confers immediate and sustained improvements in educational outcomes.6

Despite these benefits, implementing simulation in medical education is not without complications. A commonly cited shortcoming of simulation education is the potential cost implications faced by training centres wanting to employ these technologies and develop the specialised staff that are needed to run a simulation programme.7

Additionally, critics have stated that simulation can merely mimic, but never replicate reality.8 Authentic replication of clinical situations and outcomes is a crucial factor in establishing and maintaining realism within the simulation and ensuring the simulation is a useful training modality. An element of simulation education that has attracted attention among simulation educators is whether death of the manikin should occur during a simulation session. Death of the manikin could feasibly occur in a variety of scenarios. Through a mixed-methods approach, Tripathy et al9 iteratively developed their theoretical framework of simulated manikin death, outlining three categories by which death of the manikin within a simulation session may occur. These included ‘expected death’ (where both facilitators and participants are aware prior to the simulation scenario that manikin death should be expected), ‘unexpected death’ (where only participants are unaware that manikin death will occur) and ‘death due to inaction/action’ (where manikin death is determined by the actions of participants)10 and acknowledges that these various categories of manikin death may have different impacts on the participants.

Simulation educators may be concerned that where there is the death of the manikin, there is the possibility for participants to feel a loss or potential responsibility for the death, leading to a compromise in the culture of psychological safety that simulation education is founded on.11 12 Some studies support this concern. The Fraser et al study found higher self-reported rates of anxiety and emotional distress for medical students exposed to a scenario in which the adult manikin died compared with students for whom the manikin survived (p<0.05).13 The negative effect of this simulation on the medical students was sustained over time with fewer students in the manikin death group passing their subsequent examination (Objective Structured Clinical Examination) 3 months later (86% pass rate in the manikin survived group vs 70% pass rate in the manikin died group).13

However, within adult simulation education, there has been research demonstrating that running scenarios in which the manikin dies does not have any negative consequences, and in some circumstances can be advantageous. Weiss et al ran an observational study involving medical students who participated in adult resuscitation simulation sessions in which the adult manikin died. One group of students had a prebrief which included disclosure that the manikin would die during the scenario (expected death) and the other group did not (unexpected death). This study demonstrated that there was no difference between the groups’ perceived self-efficacy scores.14 Similarly, the Philippon et al study in 2016 ran simulation for multidiscipline emergency medicine HCPs.15 Baseline anxiety scores were assessed using a standardised anxiety scoring tool. The study found that for professionals who had experienced the adult manikin death scenario, there was a reduction in anxiety levels when a similar simulation was run 3 months later. These findings suggest that the scenario, the context and the level of experience of the participants all contribute to whether or not a death of the adult manikin simulation is appropriate and beneficial to learners.

Given the highly emotive impact of paediatric and neonatal death, it is important for HCPs to have opportunities to develop their knowledge and skills, and chances to practise being able to process and regulate their own emotions in these contexts, ideally before they encounter them in clinical practice. Simulation may present a useful educational environment to develop such skills. However, with the concerns highlighted above from adult simulation research, there is the potential for involvement in paediatric or neonatal manikin death to negatively impact learners.

For this reason, this review aims to summarise and discuss the current literature around the impact of manikin death in paediatric and neonatal simulation education.

Aim

This scoping review aims to investigate the appropriateness of paediatric or neonatal manikin death in simulation-based training as a learning and teaching method for HCPs.

Methods

The study protocol was developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses process.16

Patient and public involvement

Given that this review focused on HCP education and was a review of previously published research, there was no formal patient or public involvement in the design of this review.

Search strategy

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework was used as a conceptualising framework to formulate the review questions, keywords and search process. The PICO elements were outlined: Population=HCPs (particular interest in paediatric or neonatal professionals); Intervention=simulation scenarios involving death of the paediatric/neonatal manikin; Comparison=simulation scenarios where the paediatric/neonatal manikin survives; Outcome=evaluation (qualitative or quantitative methods) of participant performance and/or experience of the simulation. Boolean operators were used to combine keywords and blocks. Additionally, the authors used the databases’ specific thesauri, truncation and phrase searches to ensure the search was as comprehensive as possible. The search strategy was developed by TA and JP with expert support from the hospital medical librarian. The search strategy (figure 1) and inclusion/exclusion criteria (figure 2) were agreed prior to conducting the search. The MEDLINE (OVID), Embase (OVID), CINAHL (EBSCO) and PsycInfo (OVID) databases were searched using the preagreed search strategy. Publications in English were eligible. Funding was not available for translation of articles published in other languages. The search was performed on 26 April 2023.

Study selection and critical appraisal

All articles identified by the initial search were screened for duplicates. All article titles and abstracts were then screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria by TA, with JP performing an independent crosscheck of a random 10% of the articles. A random number generator was used to identify which articles were selected for the 10% check by JP.17 Cohen’s K score was calculated for the crosschecked articles. A third author (TH) was available in the event of any disagreement. A record of all screened articles and reasons for exclusion was maintained (online supplemental appendix A). All included articles were appraised using the relevant Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist.18

The analysis process

Data from included articles (study author, country of publication, year of publication, study design, primary study objectives, outcome measures, results, conclusions and limitations or areas of further study) were extracted and summarised into a data extraction matrix for analysis of important and/or generalisable themes from across the studies. Included studies were analysed using an inductive, reflexive thematic analysis approach. Initial themes were identified by TA and JP independently and refined following discussion into generalised themes across the studies. The final set of themes was reviewed and agreed with TH.

Results

The initial search identified 810 articles (figure 3). Following removal of duplicates and screening against the inclusion/exclusion criteria, eight articles were identified for inclusion in the full review. Of these eight articles, five were excluded after full-text reading as they did not fulfil the inclusion criteria (no manikin death (four articles); adult manikin death only (one article); see online supplemental appendix A). This resulted in three articles being included in the final review (figure 3).

All excluded articles and reasons for exclusion can be viewed in online supplemental appendix A. For quality assurance, a random 10% of the screened articles were crosschecked by JP. There was substantial agreement between the two screening authors (TA and JP) with a Cohen’s K score of 0.96 indicating 96% agreement. Any disagreement was resolved following discussion and review of the third author (TH). For clarity, as two papers were written by the same primary author (Lizotte, M),19 20 the year of publication (2015 or 2017) will be cited alongside the author name when these articles are discussed.

The three included articles consisted of two analyses of the same randomised controlled trial and one qualitative study using focus group discussion to develop a framework for an educational approach to death of the simulated paediatric manikin (figure 4). The Lizotte 2015 and 2017 studies analysed data from the same set of paediatric trainees (42 qualified doctors) from a single centre in Canada. The third study (McBride et al)21 was conducted at a single centre in the USA with a focus group of 14 participants consisting of an equal number of paediatric nurses and doctors. All three studies focused on death in the acute clinical setting, rather than a simulated palliative or expected end-of-life situation. Additionally, all three studies involved only fully qualified doctors and/or nurses, with no involvement of medical or nursing students in the simulations.

Effect of paediatric manikin death on participant stress and performance

The Lizotte (2017) article is the only study to objectively assess the impact of paediatric manikin death on participant performance and stress levels. The article reports participant performance in two simulated neonatal resuscitation scenarios. It is worth noting that the participants in this study were qualified doctors with experience in their field (paediatric residents and neonatal fellows). Participants performed two simulations where a term neonate was born pulseless. They were randomised to start with either survival (manikin responded to appropriate resuscitation) or death scenario (manikin remained pulseless despite resuscitation). Participants were blinded to their allocated order prior to the simulation session. The primary objectives of the study were to gain subjective and objective measurements of participants’ stress levels, measured by salivary cortisol levels and self-directed questionnaires completed before and after the simulation. The secondary outcome was to obtain a comparison of the impact of manikin death on trainees’ performance measured against the NRP algorithm. The study measured salivary cortisol levels from both groups at different time points: few hours ahead of the simulation (T0), just before the simulation (T1) and immediately after the simulation prior to the debrief (T2). The data show a statistically significant rise in salivary cortisol level between T0 and T1 (p<0.001) and then again between T1 and T2 (p<0.001)—however, this cortisol rise was not scenario dependent (p=0.41). Additionally, objective measures of performance using the NRP algorithm showed no difference between the groups (median performance score 80% for survival scenario and 82% for death scenario regardless of the scenario order (p=0.99)). The study shows that while participation in simulation is a stressor for participants, this is independent of the survival outcome for the manikin within the scenario.

Manikin death can increase realism

HCPs across the three studies reported that manikin death added to the realism of the simulation scenario. Simulation facilitators should aim to ensure that the simulations they run reflect, as accurately as possible, the clinical reality of medicine. Realism within the simulation is connected to increased student engagement within a simulation as well as improvement of transferability of skills.22 It remains important to consider the objective of the simulation and the participants involved, as the degree of realism is not directly proportional to the learning obtained from an individual session.

HCPs within the included studies reported that their previous experiences of acute paediatric emergency simulations which always involve the manikin surviving can be at odds with their lived experiences of clinical medicine, where children may have died from the condition in question; for example, a decompensated sepsis presentation. HCPs stated that for the manikin to always survive in every scenario is unrealistic and prevents the opportunity for participants to develop their experience with paediatric death through simulation education. Additionally, participants across the included studies felt that while, when the paediatric manikin dies, it initially could make them feel like they had ‘failed’ in the scenario, during the debrief, the experience allowed for deeper reflection and self-assessment, which was perceived as beneficial.

Death as failure

A common theme across the studies was that death of the manikin within the emergency simulation scenario was perceived by participants as reflective of an inadequacy in the resuscitation care they had provided. Professionals within the Lizotte (2015) study reported that experiencing a scenario where the manikin did not respond to resuscitation interventions felt ‘unusual’. Unfamiliarity with death being a potential outcome for the scenario contributed to the participants’ perception that the manikin death was due to their inadequate management of the scenario. Reassurance from the facilitator that death of the manikin was a learning objective for the scenario and the opportunity to participate in a facilitated debrief allowed participants to work through their discomfort. All participants agreed that experiencing death of the paediatric manikin scenarios was beneficial for their training. Participants emphasised the importance of a psychologically safe debrief after this type of simulation to allow full discussion about the emotional, practical and clinical elements of the scenario. Conversely, participants agreed that a disclosure that the manikin would, or may, die in the scenario was not required in the prebrief or in the predefined learning objectives.

Discussion

All studies within this review took into consideration the potential for paediatric manikin death to compromise the psychological safety of participants within the simulation. While taking part in simulation was shown to increase participant stress levels, this was independent of whether the manikin died in the scenario and instead reflected the stress of participating in the simulation itself. Additionally, the Lizotte (2017) study demonstrated there was no impact on participant performance within the scenarios regardless of whether or not the manikin died. This would suggest that even if the perception is that scenarios involving death of the manikin would be more stressful, when participants encounter this within the simulation it did not impair their cognitive processing and they continued to be able to perform as a resuscitation team. It is pertinent to note that the only participants in this review who underwent this objective stress level and performance assessment were qualified doctors with experience in their field (paediatric residents and neonatal fellows). The wider literature, beyond the scope of this review, is, however, similarly reassuring that death of the manikin does not irrevocably impair the learning process for participants. A study by Demaria et al,23 which evaluated the impact of manikin death in adult resuscitation simulation scenarios, demonstrated that while there was an acute increase in participant’s anxiety immediately following simulation in which the adult manikin had died, there was an improved performance in a repeat simulation 6 months later. As identified in this review, participants were supportive of the role of manikin death within simulation both for increasing realism of the simulated scenarios and for the learning opportunities this affords.

Facilitators considering implementing paediatric manikin death within their education programmes need to be cognisant of the potential perception of participants that where manikin death occurs it is due to inadequate management or human error within the scenario and therefore reflects poor performance. This may reflect participant unfamiliarity with the outcome of death within simulation scenarios. Experienced facilitators are needed to manage the subsequent debriefs in a way that allows group exploration of the team performance and management decisions within the scenario while maintaining psychological safety. In simulations where the learning objectives require the manikin to die regardless of the management provided by the participants, the facilitator should make this clear to the group at the start of the debrief as this declaration can help alleviate the perception that inadequate performance led to the manikin’s death.

In the case of a simulation in which the participants are managing the manikin suboptimally and in a manner in which the patient would be likely to die if this management were being performed in real life, the appropriateness of continuing the scenario or simply ending it early and discussing the management issues through the debrief remains unclear. Facilitation of a debrief in situations where the manikin death was not predetermined and occurred due to participant action/inaction requires a high level of debriefing skill and experience. There are potential advantages in recognising and discussing clinical management or human factor performance that falls below the level of expectations as this, when facilitated well, can cultivate an environment for professionals to develop their skills and promotes an open, self-reflective approach to provision of care. 24 25 These debriefs should aim to maintain psychological safety through the clear discouragement of blame, while empowering participants to recognise their own position and contribution to the management and/or human factor learning within that experience.

This review highlights the importance of the debrief for participants. Debriefs following death of the paediatric manikin need skilled management and incorporation of the additional psychological and emotional elements inherent to this type of simulation. From the studies within this review there was no evaluation of a specific model preferable for debriefing this type of simulation; however, from the qualitative participant data, it would seem preferable for facilitators to use one which incorporates designated time and space for discussion of the emotional and communication elements of the simulation scenario. The issue of whether paediatric manikin death should be discussed in the prebrief and, if so, how extensive this discussion should be remains unclear. In research on adult manikin death, an article by Truog and Meyer describes the concept of providing a prebriefing session where the possibility of manikin death is extensively discussed with participants. Truog and Meyer postulate that this may lead to minimisation of participant distress.26 However, this approach may undermine the realism of this type of simulation and may influence the subsequent decision-making or performance of the participants within the scenario. From the studies within this review, participants reported that a disclosure that paediatric manikin death may, or would, occur was not integral in maintaining participant trust or the psychological safety of the simulated educational experience.

Strengths and limitations

This review highlights that research is limited in the area of death of a paediatric manikin in simulation. This topic is important as educators need to know how best to deliver this type of simulation learning in a psychologically safe manner to a range of different professionals managing paediatric and neonatal deaths in a variety of contexts. This review includes all studies specific to paediatric manikin death; however, only three relevant studies were found in the literature, with all three focusing on death within acute emergency scenarios. Additionally, the included studies have relatively small sample sizes and the majority of the represented views are from paediatric trainees, with only a small number of paediatric nurses (seven nurses). None of the studies investigated the effect of death of the paediatric manikin in a palliative or planned end-of-life care scenario, such as a compassionate extubation. None of the studies explored death of the paediatric manikin in other professional contexts or disciplines, such as involvement of palliative care professionals or emergency department staff.

Areas for future research

Future research needs to expand in this area and the topic of death of the paediatric manikin should be evaluated through simulation research in multiple contexts, professional groups and simulation modalities (figure 5). Future studies should aim to evaluate the impact of having actors or simulated patients depicting the parent/family member within the scenario to increase the emotional depth and reality of the scenario and provide participants with communication skills education. Future research should also evaluate the impact of augmented or virtual reality simulation within this sphere. This potentially could be useful for less experienced staff for whom participation within a ‘live’ simulation might be overwhelming, but participation in a facilitated virtual simulation where the paediatric manikin dies may provide a psychological safer space to access this education and develop their experience and skills.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates the absence of research into the efficacy and impact of death of the paediatric manikin with HCP curricula. From the limited research available, for qualified paediatric doctors and nurses, there were no reported deleterious effects from participating in simulation scenarios in which the paediatric or neonatal manikin may die. Professionals report that participation in these scenarios can increase realism of the simulation and provides a valuable educational opportunity. Establishing and maintaining psychological safety for participants is key for this type of simulation education. For the experienced professionals involved in the studies in this review, this involved the learning objectives for the scenario being cleared articulated in the debrief and there being time for them to participate in the debrief about the emotional as well as the practical elements of the scenario. Further research is needed to evaluate optimal approaches to this unique type of debrief across different professional groups, levels of participant clinical experience and modality of the simulation itself.
