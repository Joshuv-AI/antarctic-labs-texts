Volumes of moduli spaces of bordered Klein surfaces
Elba Garcia-Failde, Paolo Gregori, Kento Osuga

Abstract
. We generalise Mirzakhani’s recursion to volumes of moduli spaces of bordered Klein surfaces, which include non-orientable surfaces. On these moduli spaces, the top form introduced by Norbury diverges as the lengths of 1-sided geodesics approach zero. However, when integrated over Gendulphe’s regularised moduli space, on which the systole of 1-sided geodesics is bounded below by ϵ ∈ R > 0 \epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0} , it returns a finite value. Using Norbury’s extension of the Mirzakhani–McShane identities to non-orientable surfaces, we derive an explicit formula for the volume of the moduli space of one-bordered Klein bottles, as well as a recursion for arbitrary topologies that fully captures the dependence on Gendulphe’s regularisation parameter ϵ \epsilon . We further relate these results to refined topological recursion, showing that, for a fixed refinement parameter, the volumes of moduli spaces of Klein surfaces with Euler characteristic − 1 -1 are governed by this procedure, and we conjecture the same holds for general topologies.



1  Introduction

The aim of the present article is twofold: one is to extend Mirzakhani’s recursion into the non-orientable setting, and the other is to explore its relation to refined topological recursion. The former is of interest in hyperbolic geometry and topology, whereas the latter plays a role in the context of mirror symmetry. These two perspectives lead us to an intriguing one-parameter deformation that continuously interpolates between orientable and non-orientable surfaces.

1.1   Motivation and background

For
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
2-2g-n<0
, let
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
be the moduli space of oriented hyperbolic surfaces of genus
g
with
n
boundaries of lengths
L_{[n]}=(L_{1},...,L_{n})
. It is well-known that
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
admits a symplectic form
\omega_{{\rm WP}}
, called the Weil–Petrersson form, from which one may define its symplectic volume
V^{+}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
, called the
Weil–Petersson volume
. Mirzakhani proved in
[
23
]
that the Weil–Petersson volumes
V^{+}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
satisfy a recursive equation, which is now called
Mirzakhani’s recursion
. It is a recursion on the negative surface Euler characteristic,
2g-2+n
, and enjoys an interpretation in terms of pants-decompositions. Mirzakhani derived such a recursion by applying the so-called Mirzakhani–McShane identities
[
22
,
23
]
, which classify the fates of ortho-geodesics incident to the first boundary component for every fixed
(g,n)
. It was further shown in
[
24
,
25
]
that
V^{+}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
is related to
\psi
-class intersection numbers on the moduli space of stable curves, Virasoro constraints, and a
\tau
-function of the Korteweg–de Vries hierarchy.

Shortly after, Chekhov, Eynard, and Orantin showed that Mirzakhani’s recursion could be reformulated in terms of a universal procedure called
topological recursion

[
4
,
9
]
.
The set of initial data forms a spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
, which consists of a Riemann surface
\Sigma
together with a choice of unstable correlators
(\omega_{0,1},\omega_{0,2})
. The output is a sequence of stable correlators
(\omega_{g,n})_{2g-2+n>0}
, which are meromorphic sections of
K_{\Sigma}^{\boxtimes n}
. Although it was originally discovered in the context of Hermitian matrix integrals, it was shown in
[
11
]
that Mirzakhani’s recursion is actually an instance of topological recursion on a specific spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\rm Mir}
. It turns out that topological recursion can be applied to far more general enumerative problems, e.g. Gromow–Witten invariants of a toric Calabi-Yau threefold
X

[
1
,
12
]
. In the context of mirror symmetry, topological recursion is thus considered as the B-side mirror of Gromow–Witten theory on
X
, because the corresponding spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{X}
is the Hori–Vafa mirror curve of
X

[
15
]
.

Recently, a one-parameter deformation of topological recursion, called
refined topological recursion
, was proposed in
[
17
,
29
,
28
]
inspired by
\beta
-deformed matrix integrals
[
5
]
. The set of initial data is upgraded to a refined spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{\mathfrak{b}}
, which includes unstable correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
with
(g,n)=(0,1),(0,2)
, and additionally
(\frac{1}{2},1)
carrying a deformation parameter
\mathfrak{b}
. The output is a sequence of refined stable correlators
(\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}_{g,n})_{2g-2+n>0}
, labelled by an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and a half-integer
g\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. It has then been shown by
[
6
,
7
]
that refined correlators
\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}_{g,n}
on a certain refined spectral curve
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{b}}_{\rm Hur}
are related to
b
-Hurwitz numbers
H_{g,n}^{b}
, which count embedded graphs
\Gamma
on possibly non-orientable surfaces, weighted by a
measure of non-orientability

\rho_{\Gamma}(b)
introduced by Chapuy and Dołęga
[
3
]
. The parameters
b
and
\mathfrak{b}
are related by
\mathfrak{b}=-\frac{b}{\sqrt{1+b}}
, and
b
intuitively measures “how non-orientable” the embedded graph
\Gamma
is, in a suitable sense — in particular, when
b=0
, only orientable contributions remain nonzero.

Motivated by these recent developments, intriguing questions arise:

Q1:

Can we consider volumes of moduli spaces of non-orientable surfaces, and can we extend Mirzakhani’s recursion into the non-orientable setting?

Q2:

Can we deform such a non-orientable analogue of Mirzakhani’s recursion by means of the parameter
b
, and interpret this deformation geometrically in terms of a measure of non-orientability?

We will provide a full answer to
Q1
and report partial progress on
Q2
in this article, which we summarise below.

1.2   Main results

In
[
27
]
, Norbury considered a top-form
\nu_{\rm N}
, which we call the Norbury form, on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}^{-}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
of non-orientable hyperbolic surfaces of Euler characteristic
2-2g-n
, with
n
boundaries of lengths
L_{[n]}
, where
g\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is
half
of the non-orientable genus (some technical details are omitted for now). Since the Norbury form
\nu_{\rm N}
is invariant under the action of the mapping class group, one may wish to take it as a non-orientable analogue of the Weil–Petersson volume form. However, it turns out that
\nu_{\rm N}
has singularities on
\mathcal{M}^{-}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
, hence one faces the immediate issue that the naive integral of the Norbury form
\nu_{\rm N}
diverges.

Gendulphe
[
13
]
resolved this issue by introducing a
regularised moduli space

\mathcal{M}^{-,\epsilon}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
, on which the Norbury form remains finite for a sufficiently small regularisation parameter
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Gendulphe’s regularisation is geometrically natural:
\epsilon
is interpreted as the lower bound of the systole of 1-sided curves, regardless of the topology. Therefore, one can now define
V_{g,n}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{[n]})
, the non-orientable analogue of the Weil–Petersson volume, by the integral of the Norbury form
\nu_{\rm N}
over Gendulphe’s regularised moduli space
\mathcal{M}^{-,\epsilon}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
, with a suitable normalisation constant. We call
V_{g,n}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{[n]})
the
Gendulphe–Norbury volume
.

As a trade-off for being able to define finite volumes
V_{g,n}^{-,\epsilon}
, the regularised moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}^{-,\epsilon}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
become more complicated, which makes concrete computations more difficult. This is partly why no significant progress has been made after the introduction of Gendulphe’s regularisation. Our first main result breaks this phase and yields the explicit expressions below.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Gendulphe–Norbury volumes of the moduli spaces of two-bordered real projective planes
(g,n)=(\frac{1}{2},2)
and of one-bordered Klein bottles
(g,n)=(1,1)
are given as:

\displaystyle V_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{[2]})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\cosh\frac{L_{1}+L_{2}}{4}\cosh\frac{L_{1}-L_{2}}{4}}{\sinh^{2}\frac{\epsilon}{2}},

\displaystyle V_{1,1}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{1})

\displaystyle=-{\rm Li}_{2}\left(-\frac{\cosh^{2}\frac{L_{1}}{4}}{\sinh^{2}\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right).

Note that both of them blow up as
\epsilon\to 0
as expected. Let us emphasise that, since
\mathcal{M}^{-,\epsilon}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
are complicated due to Gendulphe’s regularisation, it is remarkable in particular that the volume for one-bordered Klein bottles
V_{1,1}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{1})
admits such a simple expression in terms of the dilogarithm function. One should also notice a clear contrast to the orientable case in which all the Weil–Petersson volumes
V_{g,n}^{+}(L_{[n]})
are polynomials in
L_{[n]}
, while polynomiality is drastically broken in the non-orientable setting.
While the computation for
(g,n)=(\frac{1}{2},2)
has been previously obtained in the physics context (see
[
31
]
), our derivation offers a geometric perspective, and the result for
(g,n)=(1,1)
is new.

In
[
27
]
, Norbury also derived non-orientable analogues of McShane–Mirzakhani identities, which we call Norbury identities. Indeed, we used the Norbury identity for one-bordered Klein bottles to derive the expression for
V_{1,1}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{1})
above. For
2g-2+n>1
, we can also utilise the Norbury identity for the corresponding topology to derive a recursive equation for the Gendulphe–Norbury volumes
V_{g,n}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{[n]})
, in close analogy with Mirzakhani’s approach
[
23
]
. The computations become less transparent, partly due to Gendulphe’s regularisation, but the final recursion formula exhibits a clear structure. It turns out that the recursion formula for what we call the
total volume

V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}(L_{[n]}):=V_{g,n}^{+}(L_{[n]})+V_{g,n}^{-,\epsilon}(L_{[n]})

enjoys an even more elegant form, where we conventionally set
V_{g,n}^{+}(L_{[n]})=0
when
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}
. Note that this way of packaging is also considered as natural in the context of
b
-Hurwitz numbers. Our second main result is the derivation of the recursion formula for the total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}(L_{[n]})
, providing a solution to a problem originating in Norbury’s work
[
27
]
nearly 20 years ago:

Theorem 1.2
.

For
g\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with
2-2g-(n+1)<-1
, the total volumes
V_{g,n+1}^{\epsilon}(L_{0},L_{[n]})
satisfy

\displaystyle L_{0}V_{g,n+1}^{\epsilon}(L_{0},L_{[n]})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;R(L_{0},L_{i},p)V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}(p,L_{[n]\backslash\{i\}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,dq\,D(L_{0},p,q)\bigg(2V_{g-1,n+2}^{\epsilon}(p,q,L_{[n]})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}^{\rm stable}\sum_{J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}=[n]}V_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}^{\epsilon}(p,L_{[J_{1}]})V_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}^{\epsilon}(q,L_{[J_{2}]})\bigg)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;\mathcal{E}(L_{0},p;\epsilon)V_{g-\frac{1}{2},n+1}^{\epsilon}(p,L_{[n]}).

where
R,D,\mathcal{E}
can be computed explicitly from Norbury’s identities, uniformly for all
(g,n)
.

Let us highlight the differences from Mirzakhani’s recursion formula. The first difference is the
2
in the second line in front of
V_{g-1,n+2}^{\epsilon}(p,q,L_{[n]})
. This is because when a handle is created by gluing a pair of pants, it can be either an orientable handle or a non-orientable handle in the non-orientable setting, and both contributions have the same weight
D
in the recursion. The second difference is that in the the third line
g_{1},g_{2}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, since
g\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Finally, the last term is a new contribution, which is interpreted as a gluing of a cross-capped pair of pants. It is also worth noting that the gluing by
\mathcal{E}
is sensitive to Gendulphe’s regularisation parameter
\epsilon
which blows up as
\epsilon\to 0
.

We will now change our perspective to refined topological recursion. Since the formalism itself has already been proposed in
[
17
,
29
]
, a key nontrivial task is to determine the spectral curve
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{b}}_{\rm total}
that reproduces the total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}(L_{[n]})
from the refined correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
. In addition to the data included in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm Mir}
, which in particular sets
\Sigma=\mathbb{C}
, one needs to find
\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}_{g,n}
for
(g,n)=(\frac{1}{2},1)
. Guided by extensive computational experiments, we found the following well-supported candidate:

\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}^{\mathfrak{b}}(z)\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\left(-\frac{2\pi}{\tan 2\pi z}+\frac{e^{2z\epsilon}-e^{-2z\epsilon}}{2z}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{e^{2(z-\frac{k}{2})\epsilon}}{z-\frac{k}{2}}-\frac{e^{-2(z+\frac{k}{2})\epsilon}}{z+\frac{k}{2}}\right)\right)dz,

where
z
is the standard coordinate on
\mathbb{C}
. The summation is convergent when
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, matching the domain of Gendulphe’s regularisation parameter
\epsilon
.

Due to technical difficulties and subtleties that only appear in the refined setting, some properties regarding refined topological recursion on
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{b}}_{\rm total}
remain conjectural at the moment of writing. As supporting evidence that our refined spectral curve is the correct one, nonetheless, we prove that refined stable correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
constructed on
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{b}}_{\rm total}
recover total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}
when
(g,n)=(0,3),(\frac{1}{2},2),(1,1)
. Omitting some details, we propose the following concrete dictionary between the refined correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
and the total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}
:

Theorem 1.3
.

For
(g,n)\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with
2-2g-n=-1
, the refined correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
and the total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}
are related by

\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\cdot V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}(L_{[n]})=(1+b)^{g}\cdot(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{n}}^{-1}).\;\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}_{g,n}\Big|_{b=1},

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
is the termwise inverse Laplace transform and
\mathfrak{b}=-\frac{b}{\sqrt{1+b}}
.

The overall factor
(1+b)^{g}
is inspired by the relation between refined topological recursion and
b
-Hurwitz numbers
[
6
,
7
]
. We note that the proof of this theorem is highly nontrivial, in particular, for the case
(g,n)=(1,1)
. Having passed these nontrivial tests, it is natural to conjecture that the relation holds not only for
2-2g-n=-1
, but for all
(g,n)
.

Conjecture 1.4
.

The relation between the refined correlators
\omega_{g,n}^{\mathfrak{b}}
and the total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon}
holds for all
(g,n)
with
2-2g-n<0
.

We note that when
b=0
, this conjecture reduces to the proven relation between topological recursion correlators
\omega_{g,n}
and Weil–Petersson volumes
V_{g,n}^{+}
.

1.2.1 Future directions

Having summarised our main results, a natural question arises: what happens if the parameter
b
is left unfixed in Theorem
1.3
? To be more precise, let us
define
refined total volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
by

\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\cdot V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}(L_{[n]})\coloneqq(1+b)^{g}\cdot(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{n}}^{-1}).\;\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}_{g,n},

for all
(g,n)
with
2-2g-n<0
. It is easy to see that, under suitable technical assumptions,
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
depends polynomially on
b
. This naturally raises the question: can we assign geometric meanings to
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
, analogous to the measure of non-orientability introduced by Chapuy–Dołęga for
b
-Hurwitz numbers? For instance, does each coefficient of
b
in
V_{1,1}^{\epsilon,b}
admit a geometric interpretation?

V_{1,1}^{\epsilon,b}=\frac{L^{2}}{48}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+b\left(\frac{L^{2}}{48}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\right)-b^{2}\left(\frac{L^{2}}{48}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+{\rm Li}_{2}\left(-\frac{\cosh^{2}\frac{L_{1}}{4}}{\sinh^{2}\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right)\right).

To this end, we give the expected form of the recursion for the refined volumes
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
in Question
5.8
. One can immediately see that it reduces to the recursion for total volumes when
b=1
and to Mirzakhani’s recursion when
b=0
. Thus, one can view that
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
provides a continuous interpolation between orientable and non-orientable surfaces. In a forthcoming work with N. Chidambaram and A. Giacchetto, we will present one such construction, in line with the refinement of lattice point counts
[
26
,
8
]
and of the Witten–Kontsevich theorem
[
33
,
18
]
.

Organisation

This paper is organised as follows. In Section

, we review hyperbolic geometry on non-orientable surfaces and give precise definitions of the Norbury form and Gendulphe’s regularisation. We then explicitly compute

2.1.1 Twisting coordinates and orientation of the first boundary component

When

For a non-orientable surface

Step 1:

For a function

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}\pi_{*}^{\gamma}(f\circ\ell)\colon&\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{[n]})&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}_{+}\\

The first step is to realise that

\displaystyle\pi_{*}^{\gamma}(f\circ\ell)(X)

The third step utilises the fact that the covering space

where

We now turn to computing

L_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma}R(L_{0},L_{i},\ell_{\gamma})+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}D(L_{0},\ell_{\gamma^{\prime}},\ell_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}})+\sum_{\bar{\gamma},\alpha}E(L_{0},\ell_{\bar{\gamma}},\ell_{\alpha}).

We then evaluate each summation contribution separately by considering a pants-decomposition and unfolding to the corresponding cover of the moduli space following the same idea outlined in Section
3.2.1
. In the non-orientable setting, however, a case-by-case analysis is required depending on whether
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
or
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}
, making the argument more intricate than in the orientable case. Moreover, we will highlight some subtleties regarding orientation choices that arise only in the non-orientable setting. To enhance transparency, we will divide the derivation into three lemmas.

One of the important points of Lemma
4.3
, Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.6
below is to show that the correct factorial appears in the denominator after unfolding to the corresponding covering space.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the McShane–Norbury identity is a sum over simple closed multi-geodesics on a surface
K
, which are related to an embedded pair of pants
P_{1}
whose first boundary is the first boundary
\beta_{1}
of
K
: for the first sum over two-sided geodesics
\gamma
that bound
P_{1}
together with
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{j}
; for the second sum over unordered pairs
\{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\}
of two-sided geodesics which bound
P_{1}
together with
\beta_{1}
, and for the last sum over one-sided geodesics
\alpha
and two-sided geodesics
\nu
which bound a Möbius strip minus a disk that contains
\alpha
(which can also be seen as a pair of pants
P_{1}
, bounded by
\beta_{1},\nu
and
\alpha
, on which
\alpha
has been antipodally glued to itself). Therefore, every term is labeled by a multi-geodesic or by the corresponding embedded pair of pants. Moreover, recall that to be able to apply the integration technique of Section
3.2.1
, the sums should also be viewed as sums over distinct mapping class group orbits. The three different lemmas correspond to the three different types of orbits.

In short, the main idea of the proofs will be to enumerate the number of multi-geodesics, or equivalently the corresponding embedded pairs of pants, which give rise to distinct orbits of the mapping class group after unfolding.

Lemma 4.3

Fix

Lemma 4.4
.

When

, with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}^{-,\epsilon}_{g,n+1}(L_{0},L_{[n]})}\frac{\nu_{{\rm N}}}{(3g-3+n+1)!}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}}D(L_{0},L_{i},\ell_{\gamma^{\prime}},\ell_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}pdp\,qdq\,D(L_{0},p,q)\bigg(V_{g-1,n+2}^{+,\epsilon}(p,q,L_{[n]})+2V_{g-1,n+2}^{-,\epsilon}(p,q,L_{[n]})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}\sum_{J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}=[n]}\sum_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\{\pm\}}^{(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})\neq(+,+)}V_{g_{1},|J_{1}|+1}^{\kappa_{1},\epsilon}(p,L_{J_{1}})V_{g_{2},|J_{2}|+1}^{\kappa_{2},\epsilon}(q,L_{J_{2}})\bigg),

Let

\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}

\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}

and

in the derivation. In this case, there are two possible scenarios:
K\backslash P_{1}
is either connected or disconnected. Before investigating factorials and normalisation, however, let us mention special phenomena in the non-orientable setting, one in the connected case and the other in the disconnected case.

When
K\backslash P_{1}
is connected, the original surface

\beta_{1}^{\prime}
of

with

\beta_{1}

\beta_{1}^{\prime}
of

\beta_{2}^{\prime}
of

either regularly or antipodally. Notice that in this non-orientable setting,

can be either orientable or non-orientable. On the one hand, if
K\backslash P_{1}
is orientable the gluing of

with

must produce a non-orientable handle, in order for

\gamma^{\prime}
regularly and along
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
antipodally, which produces a non-orientable handle. These two situations gives rise to two distinct pants-decompositions, which in turn determine two distinct mapping class group orbits; hence they both contribute. This is why we have

is disconnected, it consists of two disjoint surfaces,

and

, of genus

and with

K_{1}
and

can be either orientable or non-orientable; however, we exclude the case where both

and

are orientable, as this would render

and the one along

and of the the first boundary
\beta_{1}^{\prime\prime}
of
K_{2}

\beta_{1}
, and the orientations of
\beta_{1}^{\prime}
and
\beta_{1}^{\prime\prime}
to determine each other), but that would yield the same pants-decomposition, so there is no additional contribution in this case.

We will now examine the case
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
in detail, leaving the case

, where

In the connected scenario, when

In the disconnected scenario, choose a specific pants-decomposition and fix

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(3g-3+n+1)!}(3g-3+n+1)(3g-3+n)\binom{3g-3+n-1}{3g_{1}-3+n_{1}+1}

When

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(3g-3+n+1)!}(3g-3+n+1)(3g-3+n)\binom{3g-4+n}{1}\binom{3g-5+n}{3g^{\prime}_{1}-2+n_{1}+1}

Let us elaborate on the differences between Stanford’s approach and Corollary

5.2.1 Conjecture on refined topological recursion

5.2.3 Comments on poles along the anti-diagonal

Due to the complicated pole structure of
\omega_{g,n}
, there exists an extra layer of subtleties on the action of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{L}
in the refined setting, which originates from poles along the anti-diagonal. Let us demonstrate it with a toy example. Take

\omega(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\left(z_{1}^{4}+3z_{1}^{3}z_{2}+3z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}+3z_{1}z_{2}^{3}+z_{2}^{4}\right)}{2z_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{3}\left(z_{1}+z_{2}\right)^{3}}.

It is a symmetric bidifferential without residues, and in terms of
z_{1}
, it has poles at
z_{1}=0
and
z_{1}=-z_{2}
. Then, one finds:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{L_{2}}.\;\omega(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{4z_{1}^{2}}+\frac{L_{2}^{2}e^{-L_{2}z_{1}}}{4z_{1}^{2}}+\frac{L_{2}e^{-L_{2}z_{1}}}{2z_{1}^{3}}

and

(5.9)

(\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{L_{1}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{L_{2}}).\;\omega(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{L_{2}^{2}L_{1}}{4}+\frac{L_{1}L_{2}(L_{1}-L_{2})\theta(L_{1}-L_{2})}{4}=\frac{L_{1}L_{2}\max(L_{1},L_{2})}{4},

where
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. One can see that the step function appears as a consequence of the pole at
z_{1}+z_{2}=0
.

Since
\omega_{g,n}
in the refined setting have poles along the anti-diagonal, we will encounter phenomena
similar to the above toy example. In practice, however, it is sometimes convenient to impose some inequalities among the lengths
L_{[n]}
from the beginning and avoid the appearance of the step function. In particular, these conditions on
L_{[n]}
may help us to universally choose the contour for inverse Laplace transform, because otherwise contours may differ term by term.

5.2.4 Comments on generic value of
b

Let us mention a crucial aspect of Conjecture
5.7
which explains one of the main reasons why we introduce refined topological recursion in the present paper. Namely, from the perspective of refined topological recursion, an interesting question arises: what if we keep
b
unfixed? Although some properties remain conjectural, refined topological recursion indicates us a natural way of deforming the recursion formula of Corollary
4.9
by the parameter
b
. More concretely, one can pose the following question:

Question 5.8
.

For
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with
2g-2+n>0
, let us recursively define
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
as below. Can we find the geometric meaning of
V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}
, for generic
b
? The initial cases are defined as

\displaystyle V_{0,3}^{\epsilon,b}(L_{[3]})\coloneqq

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle V_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{\epsilon,b}(L_{[2]})\coloneqq

\displaystyle b\left(\log\left(2\cosh\frac{L_{1}}{2}+2\cosh\frac{L_{2}}{2}\right)-2\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\right),

\displaystyle V_{1,1}^{\epsilon,b}(L_{[1]})\coloneqq

\displaystyle(1+b)\left(\frac{L^{2}}{48}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\right)-b^{2}\left(\frac{L^{2}}{48}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+{\rm Li}_{2}\left(-\frac{\cosh^{2}\frac{L_{1}}{4}}{\sinh^{2}\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right)\right),

and for
2-2g-(n+1)<-1
,
V_{g,n+1}^{\epsilon,b}
are recursively defined by

\displaystyle L_{0}V_{g,n+1}^{\epsilon,b}(L_{0},L_{[n]})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;R(L_{1},L_{i},p)V_{g,n}^{\epsilon,b}(p,L_{[n]\backslash\{i\}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,dq\,D(L_{0},p,q)\bigg((1+b)V_{g-1,n+2}^{\epsilon,b}(p,q,L_{[n]})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}\sum_{J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}=[n]}V_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}^{\epsilon,b}(p,L_{[J_{1}]})V_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}^{\epsilon,b}(q,L_{[J_{2}]})\bigg)

(5.10)

\displaystyle+b\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\;\mathcal{E}(L_{0},p;\epsilon)V_{g-\frac{1}{2},n+1}^{\epsilon,b}(p,L_{[n]}).

See Remark
6.6
for a justification of the definition of
V_{1,1}^{\epsilon,b}
. The symmetry of
V_{g,n+1}^{\epsilon,b}
in terms of permutations of
(L_{0},L_{[n]})
is not
a priori
guaranteed from the recursive definition, and this is related to
RTR1
of Conjecture
5.4
. It is worth noting that Question
5.8
remains meaningful without reference to refined topological recursion, and can therefore be addressed purely within hyperbolic geometry, if preferred.

5.2.5 Additional comments on Stanford’s proposal

By naively applying Conjecture
5.7
to
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}
and taking it as an auxiliary definition of
V_{\frac{1}{2},1}^{\epsilon}(L_{1})
, one arrives at

\displaystyle V_{\frac{1}{2},1}^{\epsilon}(L_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{L_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-\frac{k}{2}L_{1}}\right)=\frac{1}{2L_{1}\tanh\frac{L_{1}}{4}}.

This is independent of
\epsilon
and has no artificial cut-off function
\theta(L_{1}-\epsilon)
unlike
L_{1}\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{\frac{1}{2},1}(L_{1})
in (
4.7
), the one proposed in
[
31
]
. Therefore, no
\epsilon
-regularisation information is encoded in
V_{\frac{1}{2},1}^{\epsilon}
. This geometrically makes sense because Gendulphe’s regularisation imposes conditions only on lengths of 1-sided geodesics, not on the boundary length of the non-primitive 2-sided geodesic.

Putting it another way, let us consider the ordinary Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
acting on
L_{1}{V}^{\epsilon}_{\frac{1}{2},1}(L_{1})
above and
L_{1}\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{\frac{1}{2},1}(L_{1})
to compare with
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}
in Definition
5.1
. The former blows up because it has a singularity at
L_{1}=0
. The latter, however, gives a finite result as below:

\displaystyle\varpi_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{L}_{z_{1}}.\;L_{1}\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{\frac{1}{2},1}(L_{1})\right)dz_{1}=\left(\frac{e^{-2\epsilon z_{1}}}{2z_{1}}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-2(z_{1}+\frac{k}{2})\epsilon}}{z_{1}+\frac{k}{2}}\right)dz_{1},

where strictly speaking the equality holds for
{\rm Re}(z_{1})>0
and the sum converges with the assumption that
\epsilon>0
. We note that since the termwise inverse
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
drops contributions from infinity, it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{L_{1}}.\,(\mathcal{L}_{z_{1}}.\,\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{\frac{1}{2},1}(L_{1}))=V_{\frac{1}{2},1}^{\epsilon}(L_{1})
, and again information of the
\epsilon
-regularisation is lost.

Although
\varpi_{\frac{1}{2},1}
satisfies
RSC1
and
RSC2
in Remark
5.2
, it does not meet
RSC3
. Thus, it is not a good candidate for a refined spectral curve. Yet importantly, one can easily see that
\varpi_{\frac{1}{2},1}
and
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}
are related by

\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z_{1})=\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\left(-\frac{dy(z_{1})}{y(z_{1})}-\Delta_{z_{1}}\varpi_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z_{1})\right).

This exhibits the difference between our approach and Stanford’s approach, from the perspective of refined topological recursion. We will show evidence that our
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}
indeed gives the correct
\omega_{1,1}
and
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}
in the next section.

6  Euler characteristic
-1
from refined topological recursion

In order to provide supporting evidence, we prove that Conjecture
5.4
and Conjecture
5.7
hold, nontrivially, for
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}
and
\omega_{1,1}
. Let us emphasise that although refined topological recursion requires tedious residue computations, everything boils down to local expansions, hence, in practice, it is easier (does not mean easy) than the real analytic integrals of Corollary

6.1   Two-bordered real projective planes

Proposition 6.1
.

Conjecture
5.4
holds for
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}
. Furthermore, it can be explicitly calculated as

\displaystyle\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})=

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{b}\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}}-\mathfrak{b}d_{z_{1}}\frac{\Delta_{z_{1}}\omega_{0,2}(z_{1},z_{2})}{4\omega_{0,1}(z_{1})}-\mathfrak{b}d_{z_{2}}\frac{\Delta_{z_{2}}\omega_{0,2}(z_{2},z_{1})}{4\omega_{0,1}(z_{2})}

(6.1)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\left(\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}}+\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}+\frac{k}{2})^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{dz_{2}}{(z_{2}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}}+\frac{dz_{2}}{(z_{2}+\frac{k}{2})^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Note that if (
6.1
) holds, then one can easily verify that Conjecture
5.4
holds. In particular, one can check that there is no pole as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
and
z_{1}\to\frac{k}{2}
for each
k\geq 1
. Thus, we will derive such an explicit expression.

First of all, one has

\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=\frac{k}{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Res_{z=z_{i}}-\Res_{z=0}-\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=-\frac{k}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Res_{z=-z_{i}}\right)\frac{\eta^{z}(z_{1})}{4\omega_{0,1}(z)}\,\mathfrak{b}\,dx(z)\,d_{z}\frac{dx(z_{2})}{(x(z)-x(z_{2}))^{2}}=0.

This is because the integrand is invariant under
\sigma:z\mapsto-z
, which implies that the residues at
z=\frac{k}{2}
and
z=z_{i}
cancel the residues at
z=-\frac{k}{2}
and
z=-z_{i}
, respectively. Furthermore, the integrand is regular at
z=0
, so that there is no contribution from the residue at
z=0
. Then, by integrating by parts and also by utilising (
5.5
), the formula for
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}
can be brought into the following form:

(6.2)

\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})=\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=\frac{k}{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Res_{z=z_{i}}-\Res_{z=0}-\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=-\frac{k}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Res_{z=-z_{i}}\right)\\
\left(\frac{\eta^{z}(z_{1})}{8\omega_{0,1}(z)}\Delta_{z}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z)\Delta_{z}\omega_{0,2}(z,z_{2})-\mathfrak{b}\frac{\Delta_{z}\omega_{0,2}(z,z_{1})\Delta_{z}\omega_{0,2}(z,z_{2})}{8\omega_{0,1}(z)}\right).

We denote the contribution from the first term in the second line of (
6.2
) by
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(0)}
. The integrand of
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(0)}
is invariant under the involution
\sigma:z\mapsto-z
, hence all residues cancel except the one at
z=0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(0)}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mathfrak{b}\left(\epsilon-2\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e^{-k\epsilon}}{k}\right)\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}}=2\mathfrak{b}\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}}.

We next consider the contributions from the second term in the second line of (
6.2
), which is independent of
\epsilon
. Since the integrand is anti-invariant under the involution
\sigma
, the residue at
z=0
vanishes. We denote by
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(1)}
and
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(2)}
the sums of residues at
z=\pm z_{1},\pm z_{2}
and at
z=\pm\frac{k}{2}
for
k\geq 1
, respectively. Explicitly, one finds by tedious computations that

\displaystyle\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(1)}(z_{1},z_{2})=

\displaystyle-\mathfrak{b}d_{z_{1}}\frac{\Delta_{z_{1}}\omega_{0,2}(z_{1},z_{2})}{4\omega_{0,1}(z_{1})}-\mathfrak{b}d_{z_{2}}\frac{\Delta_{z_{2}}\omega_{0,2}(z_{2},z_{1})}{4\omega_{0,1}(z_{2})},

\displaystyle\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{(2)}(z_{1},z_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\left(\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}}+\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}+\frac{k}{2})^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{dz_{2}}{(z_{2}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}}+\frac{dz_{2}}{(z_{2}+\frac{k}{2})^{2}}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Conjecture
5.7
holds for
(g,n)=\big(\frac{1}{2},2\big)
. More concretely,

(6.3)

L_{1}L_{2}\cdot V_{\frac{1}{2},2}^{\epsilon}(L_{[2]})=(1+b)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{1}}^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{2}}^{-1}).\;\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}\Big|_{b=1}.

Proof.

Let us assume
L_{1}\geq L_{2}
, and first consider
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{2}}.\;\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})
. Note that in general we have

\Res_{z=-u}\frac{1}{(z+u)^{m}}e^{zL}=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}L^{m-1}e^{-uL}.

Thus, thanks to Proposition
6.1
, the termwise inverse Laplace transform reduces to taking residues at the pole of
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}
. This can be explicitly computed as below:

(6.4)

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{2}=0}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle 2L_{2}\mathfrak{b}\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\mathfrak{b}L_{2}^{2}}{2}\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}^{2}},

(6.5)

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{2}=-\frac{k}{2}}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}L_{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}e^{-\frac{k}{2}L_{2}}\left(\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}}+\frac{dz_{1}}{(z_{1}+\frac{k}{2})^{2}}\right),

(6.6)

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{2}=-z_{1}}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle-\mathfrak{b}\pi e^{-L_{2}z_{1}}\left(\frac{L_{2}^{2}\,dz_{1}}{z_{1}\sin 2\pi z_{1}}+\frac{L_{2}\,dz_{1}}{z_{1}^{2}\sin 2\pi z_{1}}+\frac{2\pi L_{2}\cos 2\pi z_{1}\,dz_{1}}{z_{1}\sin^{2}2\pi z_{1}}\right).

Note that the pole at
z=\frac{k}{2}
in (
6.5
) cancels the corresponding pole in (
6.6
); hence,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{2}}.\;\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})
has poles only at
z_{1}=0
or
z_{1}=-\frac{k}{2}
.

Since we assumed
L_{1}\geq L_{2}
, one can now choose the contour for
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
such that it encloses the origin and the negative real axis, regardless of the terms. From (
6.4
) and (
6.5
), we have

(6.7)

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{1}=0}\,e^{z_{1}L_{1}}\Res_{z_{2}=0}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})\,e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle 2L_{1}L_{2}\mathfrak{b}\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)-\frac{\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}^{2}}{2},

(6.8)

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{1}=-\frac{k}{2}}\,e^{z_{1}L_{1}}\Res_{z_{2}=-\frac{k}{2}}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})\,e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}e^{-\frac{k}{2}(L_{1}+L_{2})}.

And for (
6.6
), we have contributions from
z_{1}=0
and
z_{1}=-\frac{k}{2}

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{1}=0}\,e^{z_{1}L_{1}}\Res_{z_{2}=-z_{1}}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}L_{1}L_{2}(L_{1}-L_{2}),

\displaystyle\Res_{z_{1}=-\frac{k}{2}}\,e^{z_{1}L_{1}}\Res_{z_{2}=-z_{1}}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{z_{2}L_{2}}=

\displaystyle\,\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}e^{-(L_{1}-L_{2})\frac{k}{2}}.

Combining all, we obtain:

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{1}}^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{L_{2}}^{-1}).\;\omega_{\frac{1}{2},2}=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}\left(2\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)-\frac{L_{1}}{2}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\left(e^{-(L_{1}+L_{2})\frac{k}{2}}+e^{-(L_{1}-L_{2})\frac{k}{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}\left(2\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)-\frac{L_{1}}{2}+\log\left(1+e^{-\frac{L_{1}+L_{2}}{2}}\right)-\log\left(1+e^{-\frac{L_{1}-L_{2}}{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathfrak{b}L_{1}L_{2}\left(\log\left(2\cosh\frac{L_{1}}{2}+2\cosh\frac{L_{2}}{2}\right)-2\log\left(2\sinh\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\right),

where in the second equality we resummed the series in
k
under the assumption that
L_{1}\geq L_{2}
. Thus, we arrive at the theorem by setting
b=1
, which means
\mathfrak{b}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. If we assume
L_{1}\leq L_{2}
, then we still obtain the same result by choosing a contour differently for the inverse Laplace transform of (
6.6
). Equivalently, one can repeat exactly the same steps by exchanging the order of inverse Laplace transforms, i.e.
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{1}}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{2}}
with an appropriate choice of contours. This demonstrates how we should deal with poles along the anti-diagonal.

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=\frac{k}{2}}\eta^{z}(z_{1})d_{z_{1}}\frac{\Delta_{z}\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z)}{4\omega_{0,1}(z)}

We note that the last summation involving

Appendix A
Towards refined topological recursion for the volumes

In the appendix we first derive the combinatorial formulae required to prove refined topological recursion (with the parameter
b=1
) for the volume of the moduli space of Klein bottles. We then present evidence for, and identify potential obstructions to, extending this result to establish refined topological recursion (again with
b=1
) for the volumes of moduli spaces of Klein surfaces in general.

A.1   Combinatorial formulae

We present a collection of combinatorial lemmas which are useful to simplify computations arising in refined topological recursion. In practice, we need to substitute
X=e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}
to use lemmas below in Section
6.2
.

The first lemma is used in Lemma
6.3
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
X>1
,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\geq 1}^{i\neq k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i-k)(i+k)}+(-1)^{k}\sum_{j\geq 1}^{j\neq k}\left(\frac{X^{-2(j+k)}}{k(j+k)}+\frac{X^{-2(j-k)}}{k(j-k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\left(\frac{X^{-4k}-1}{2k}+2\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{kX^{2k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j}\left(X^{2j}+\frac{1}{X^{2j}}\right)\right).

Proof.

First, notice that

(A.1)

\sum_{i\geq 1}^{i\neq k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i-k)}=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}\right).

Also,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}^{i\neq k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+k)}=

\displaystyle-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2k}+\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+k)}=-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2k}+(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}\right),

where the finite sum stops at
i=k
in contrast to the finite sum in (
A.1
). Thus, we have

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\geq 1}^{i\neq k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i-k)(i+k)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\geq 1}^{i\neq k}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i-k)}-\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+k)}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2k^{2}}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Next, one can manipulate the following sum as below:

(A.2)

\sum_{j\geq 1}^{j\neq k}\frac{X^{-2(j-k)}}{(j-k)}=\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{X^{-2j}}{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{X^{2j}}{j}\right)=-\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{X^{2j}}{j},

where the second equality holds because the first sum is a convergent series when
X>1
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}^{j\neq k}\frac{X^{-2(j+k)}}{(j+k)}=-\frac{1}{2k}\frac{1}{X^{4k}}+\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{X^{-2(j+k)}}{(j+k)}=-\frac{1}{2k}\frac{1}{X^{4k}}-\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}\frac{1}{X^{2j}},

where the finite sum stops at
j=k
in contrast to the finite sum in (
A.2
). Combining all of the above, one arrives at the lemma.
∎

The following lemma is necessary to show the forthcoming lemma:

Lemma A.2
.

(A.3)

\frac{1}{k}\left(X^{2k}+\frac{1}{X^{2k}}\right)-\frac{2}{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}.

Proof.

Let us consider
\phi_{k}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{k}\left(X^{2k}+\frac{1}{X^{2k}}\right)
. Then there exists a set of constants
c_{j}
such that

\phi_{k}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{j}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}.

It is clear that
\phi_{k}(X)
satisfies
\left(X\frac{d}{dX}\right)^{2}\phi_{k}(X)=4k^{2}\phi_{k}(X)
, and this differential equation induces the following recursive equation for
c_{j}
starting from
c_{k}
:

\forall\;\;0\leq j\leq k-1,\qquad(2j+1)(2j+2)c_{j+1}=(k-j)(k+j)c_{j}.

This can be solved by induction on
k-j
and one obtains:

c_{j}=\frac{k}{j}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}c_{k}.

We note the above equation holds even when
j=0
which gives
c_{0}=2c_{k}
. Finally, the boundary condition
\phi(\pm 1)=\frac{2}{k}
fixes
c_{k}=\frac{1}{k}
.
∎

Below is used in Theorem
6.5
.

Lemma A.3
.

\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{4}{j}+k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}=-\frac{2}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\left(X^{2k}+\frac{1}{X^{2k}}\right)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j}\left(X^{2j}+\frac{1}{X^{2j}}\right).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k
. When
k=1
, it is straightforward to check that the lemma holds. Let
k\geq 1
and we assume that the lemma holds for all
k^{\prime}\leq k
. Then, by utilising Lemma
A.2
, one finds:

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{k+1}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left(X^{2k+2}+\frac{1}{X^{2k+2}}\right)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}\left(X^{2j}+\frac{1}{X^{2j}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{k+1}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left(X^{2k+2}+\frac{1}{X^{2k+2}}\right)+\frac{1}{k}\left(X^{2k}+\frac{1}{X^{2k}}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\frac{2}{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{4}{j}+k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\frac{1}{j}\binom{k+j}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}+\frac{2}{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\frac{2}{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{4}{j}+k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{4}{j}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2k+2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(j\binom{k+j}{2j-1}+j\binom{k+j-1}{2j-1}+k\binom{k+j}{2j-1}\right)\left(X-\frac{1}{X}\right)^{2j},

where the first equality is due to the induction hypothesis, and we used Lemma
A.2
at the second equality. The last line can be further simplified by explicitly writing binomials in terms of factorials and one arrives at the lemma.
∎

A.2   Towards Conjecture
5.7

The relation between Mirzakhani’s recursion and the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion was originally proven in
[
10
]
by direct computations with several clever manipulations. One can also prove it in terms of Airy structures and Virasoro constraints
[
19
]
. Since the latter is not known in the refined setting at the moment of writing, a realistic approach for Conjecture
5.7
is to extend the original proof by Eynard and Orantin to our problem.

Let us first briefly explain how
[
10
]
originally discovered the relation between the topological recursion correlators
\omega_{g,n}
and the Weil–Petersson volumes
V_{g,n}
in the unrefined/oriented setting.

For
(g,n)
with
2g-2+n>0
, we symbolically denote the Eynard–Orantin recursive formula by

(A.4)

\omega_{g,n}=\text{EO}\Big(\omega_{0,1},\omega_{0,2};\{\omega_{h,m}\}_{0<2h-2+m<2g-2+n}\Big).

Let us next define
U_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
by

U_{g,n}(L_{[n]})\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{-1}\cdot(\mathcal{L}_{L_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{L_{n}}^{-1}).\;\omega_{g,n},

where
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
is the inverse Laplace transform, not the termwise one
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
. Since the
\omega_{g,n}
have poles only at ramification points, it can be easily shown that
U_{g,n}(L_{[n]})
are polynomials in
L_{1},...,L_{n}
, and hence, taking the inverse is straightforward:

\omega_{g,n}=(\mathcal{L}_{z_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{z_{n}}).\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\cdot U_{g,n}(L_{[n]}).

One can check by explicit computations that
U_{0,3}=V_{0,3}
and
U_{1,1}=V_{1,1}
. For
2g-2+n>1
, the symbolic formula (
A.4
) can be written as

(A.5)

U_{g,n}(L_{[n]})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{-1}\cdot(\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{i}}\cdots\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{L_{n}}).\;\text{EO}\Big(\omega_{0,1},\omega_{0,2};\{(\mathcal{L}_{z_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{z_{m}}).\prod_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}\;U_{h,m}\}_{0<2h-2+m<2g-2+n}\Big).

This provides a recursion formula for
U_{g,n}
, induced from the Eynard–Orantin recursion for
\omega_{g,n}
. It was then shown in
[
10
]
that the recursion for
U_{g,n}
precisely coincides with Mirzakhani’s recursion in Theorem
4.1
, which implies that
U_{g,n}=V_{g,n}
for all
(g,n)
. The nontrivial manipulations in
[
10
]
transform the dependence of
\omega_{0,1}
and
\omega_{0,2}
in (
A.5
) to the functions
R
and
D
in Mirzakhani’s recursion.

There are two main obstacles to extend this idea to the refined setting, even if one assumes the properties of Conjecture
5.4
. Let us summarise the two issues one would encounter after naively following this approach.

(1)

The manipulations in
[
10
]
rely on the facts that
V_{g,n}
and
\omega_{g,n}
are related through the Laplace transform and its inverse, and that
\omega_{0,2}
also admits an expression in terms of the ordinary Laplace transform. Thus, in the refined setting, the first task is to write
\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z)
as the Laplace transform of some object. However, one finds that it is written in the following integral form, with the assumption that
\text{Re}(z)>0

\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}(z)=\frac{b}{2}\left(-\int_{2\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tanh\frac{q}{4}}e^{-zq}dq-\int_{-2\epsilon}^{2\epsilon}\frac{1}{2\tanh\frac{q}{4}}e^{-zq}dq\right)dz.

Since this cannot be written as a Laplace transform, in particular, as an integral from
q=0
to
q=\infty

\epsilon>0
, one cannot directly apply some of the clever manipulations of
[
10
]
. Note that ignoring the second integral coincides with Stanford’s approach
[
31

, but at the same time we know that the second integral term is necessary to obtain Theorem

\omega_{\frac{1}{2},1}

\mathcal{E}
in Corollary
4.9

(2)

The second issue is that, in the manipulation presented in
[
10
]
, the recursion formula needs to be transformed into the following form (c.f. the second-to-last equality in

\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})=\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\Res_{z=\frac{k}{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\Res_{z=z_{i}}\right)\frac{\eta^{z}(z_{0})}{2\omega_{0,1}(z)}{\rm Rec}_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]}).
