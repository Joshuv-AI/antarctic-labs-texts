Introduction

Falls prevention is a public health priority to increase the number of years lived without disability and reduce health costs in older people [1].

The “world guidelines for falls prevention and management for older adults: a global initiative” (WGFs) [1] have published an algorithm for falls prediction (low, intermediate or high risk) and proposed measures requiring health resources adapted to the risk level [1]. The WGFs algorithm has been assessed in four existing cohorts which differ, particularly in terms of fallers rates (20.0% [2], 21.2% [3], 32.3% [4], 32.6% [5]). However, these studies agree that under 10% of participants were classified in the intermediate-risk group [2–5], percentage strongly lower than that observed for the high-risk group (19% [3] , 22.3% [2], 28.1% [4] , 31.3% [5] of all 65 + and 83 to 96% of all fallers) [4, 5].

We performed a real-life study to estimate the distribution of fall-risk categories according to the WGFs algorithm in a population of old volunteers.

Methods

We performed a cross-sectional study in 65-85-year-old volunteers. Information on WGFs was diffused in an open-day meeting to inform older persons on falls prevention (meeting announced by different local media). An observational study assessed the falls risk of unselected seniors attending this meeting and wishing a personalized assessment of their falls-risk based on the WGFs algorithm [1].

This observational study using anonymized data and standard recommended tests received an ethical approval by our University (UM 2025–06405).

The outcomes administered by exercise professionals included the WGFs questionnaire and tests: Timed Up and Go (TUG) with a cutoff set at 15 s (WGFs) and 10 s [5], and gait speed > or ≤ 0.8 m/sec. Frailty was assessed using the Fried’s questionnaire.

We anticipated a higher proportion of fallers in our selected sample than in previous studies (45 vs. 30%) [2–5]. A group of 57 fallers was necessary to test this hypothesis (5% one-sided significance, 90% power ; NQuery Software).

The proportion of participants classified into each WGFs fall-risk category (low, intermediate, high) was calculated with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. We tested the impact of applying an alternative TUG cut-off (> 10 s instead of > 15 s) on the distribution of risk categories.

Results

Among the 165 participants tested (mean age: 78.3 ± 6.1, 82% females) (Fig. 1), the number of fallers was significantly higher (51.2% 95% CL: 43.6–58.8%). than in previous studies testing the WFGs [1–5].

The low risk concerned 37.6% (n = 62), the intermediate risk 15.1% (n = 25) and the high risk 47.3% (n = 78) of participants. In non-fallers, 69.6% (n = 55) had a low risk of falls, and, in fallers, 90.1% (n = 78) were at high risk.

Five more low-risk participants were classified as intermediate risk using a TUG cutoff of > 10 s [5].

Discussion

We tested 65-85-year-old volunteers who were concerned by a falls risk. This was reflected by the higher percentage of persons reporting at least one fall in the past year (51.2%) than in previous studies [1–5].

In previous studies testing the WFGs, the intermediate group was low: 0.007% (risk based on TUG only) [2]; 10.1% (TUG and walking speed) [4]; 5.9% (TUG only) [3] and 0.7% (TUG only) [5] of the total sample vs. 15.1% in our study (based on TUG and walking speed according to the WGFs). Herein, reducing the TUG cutoff to over 10 s (as proposed recently to increase the number of 65 + at intermediate risk [5]) did not largely increase this group.

According to the WGFs, the majority of fallers were classified as high-risk (91% in our study vs. 83% [4] and 96% [5] in existing cohorts). Considering the high prevalence of fallers in the general population (around 30% in 65+) [1] and the WGFs recommendations for the high-risk group (individualized tailored intervention), it could be difficult to implement these guidelines in around 25% of all 65+, due to a lack of sufficiently trained general practitioners, geriatricians or falls clinics in most countries.

Our study has limitations, including a relatively low number of participants and the sex imbalance. However, it does have several strengths, including the real-world setting and a high response rate.

Implications for practice and/or policy, and research

Our results suggest that in addition to the screening of people at risk of falling by healthcare professionals as proposed by the WGFs, informing the general public about the WGFs would (i) raise awareness among older people, (ii) direct them towards a local and less costly assessment by exercise professionals, and (iii) help identify people at high risk of falling (47.3% of assessed persons in our sample) or at intermediate risk (15.1% of all 65 + in our sample), (iv) who could then be directed to appropriate health resources proposed by the WGFs.

Based on data from our study and previous ones, most fallers aged 65 + would be classified at high risk of falling by using the WGFs, which makes it difficult to propose the multifactorial assessments and tailored interventions recommended by the WGFs for approximately 20% of 65+. In addition, the rate of incident falls seems not largely different between the moderate and high-risk groups in comparison with the low-risk group in a previous study (IRR = 2.19 and 2.52, respectively) [5]. Overall, these results would suggest, for feasibility and cost-effectiveness, to distinguish among high-risk patients, - a group at high risk of fall-related injuries, since these patients are at higher risk of functional decline and admission to a nursing home. Cognitive impairment, difficulty to perform some activities of the daily life, and inability to rise from the floor could help distinguish those patients more than frailty. A multifactorial assessment and individualized tailored intervention could thus be reserved for patients at high risk of fall-related injuries and recommendations for patients at moderate risk could be applied to the reconfigured moderate to high-risk group. Future studies will need to assess the number of 65 + in each group, the feasibility of applying WGFs falls prevention measures to these reconfigured groups, and the cost-effectiveness of the WGFs vs. this WGFs-modified strategy.
