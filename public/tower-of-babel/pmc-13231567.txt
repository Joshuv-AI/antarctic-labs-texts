Left atrial dissection with oozing rupture after cardiac myxoma resection: a case report of conservative management guided by transesophageal echocardiography
Authors: Yoshiyuki Kobayashi, Yusuke Nagamine, Kenta Okamura, Makoto Sasaki, Tomoyuki Minami, Minori Tateishi, Kaori Mori, Shoichiro Ono, Aya Saito
Published: 2026

Background

Left atrial dissection (LAD) is an uncommon cardiac complication defined by separation of the left atrial wall layers, creating an abnormal blood-filled space [1,2]. It occurs predominantly after cardiac procedures, most commonly following mitral valve surgery. Other reported causes include coronary artery bypass grafting, aortic valve procedures, percutaneous coronary interventions, and catheter ablation, while LAD after cardiac myxoma resection is particularly rare [1]. The clinical presentation of LAD ranges from asymptomatic incidental findings to life-threatening hemodynamic collapse due to mitral inflow obstruction or rupture. Accordingly, management strategies range from conservative observation to emergency surgical repair, depending on hemodynamic status and anatomical features. For LAD, where assessment of the mitral valve region and posterior left atrial wall is critical, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) offers excellent visualization and can be performed at the bedside, making it a key perioperative imaging modality. We report a case of LAD with oozing rupture following cardiac myxoma excision that was managed without additional definitive atrial repair, guided by serial TEE.

In this report, “conservative management” refers to a non-repair strategy for the atrial dissection. We use “intramural hematoma” to describe a thrombosed LAD cavity and “oozing rupture” to denote diffuse seepage-type bleeding without an identifiable perforation.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with chest discomfort. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left atrial mass, and subsequent computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed a suspected left atrial myxoma, along with an incidental thymic tumor (Fig. 1). The patient was referred for surgical resection of both tumors. Because no embolic events were evident, elective surgery was performed via median sternotomy. Following completion of total thymectomy, cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. Myocardial protection was provided intermittently and antegradely through the aortic root cannula. A right atrial incision was chosen based on tumor location. The left atrial tumor had a broad base, originating primarily from the fossa ovalis and extending toward the right inferior pulmonary vein orifice (Fig. 2A, B). Because the involved atrial wall area was limited and full-thickness atrial wall excision with reconstruction was considered excessively invasive, the tumor was excised together with the muscle layer of the atrial wall (Fig. 2C). On direct inspection, the resulting defect was assessed as non–full thickness and managed as an endocardial defect. This was repaired with a 2.5- × 1.5-cm bovine pericardial patch, and the atrial septal defect was closed using a separate 2- × 1-cm patch. During separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, a minor right ventricular perforation occurred during routine temporary epicardial pacing with an alligator clip cable and required repair; separation was subsequently completed without further events. Intraoperative TEE performed before sternal closure revealed a small hematoma beneath the atrial wall patch, which was considered likely to resolve spontaneously (Fig. 3A). The aortic cross-clamp time was 96 min, and the cardiopulmonary bypass time was 137 min.

Two hours after admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), the patient abruptly developed 300 mL of bloody mediastinal drainage accompanied by hypotension. Emergency re-exploration revealed a subepicardial hematoma on the posterior aspect of the heart without an identifiable bleeding source. TEE demonstrated a large hypoechoic cavity within the posterior wall of the left atrium and fluttering of a detached endocardial layer (Fig. 3B). The atrial patches remained intact, and no obvious leakage was identified. A diagnosis of LAD with oozing rupture was made.

Additional definitive surgical repair was avoided because of marked tissue fragility and concern for further tissue damage. Because the procedure had been limited to left atrial manipulation, and highly pressurized left ventricular inflow into the dissection cavity was neither expected nor observed on TEE, a conservative (non-repair) strategy was initially pursued. This included platelet and fresh frozen plasma transfusion, compression, and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support.

Serial TEE demonstrated blood pooling with progressive partial thrombus formation within the dissection cavity (Fig. 3C). Although the hematoma compressed the true left atrial lumen, pulmonary venous and mitral inflow remained preserved. After hemostasis was achieved, the patient returned to the ICU with gauze packing and an open sternum. Deep sedation, controlled ventilation, and strict blood pressure control with IABP support were continued.

On postoperative day (POD) 2, signs of circulatory compromise developed. Contrast-enhanced CT suggested pulmonary venous stenosis caused by gauze packing (Fig. 4), which was subsequently removed. Persistent left atrial wall hematoma precluded chest closure at that time (Fig. 5A). On POD 5, the patient returned to the operating room for attempted chest closure. TEE showed a marked reduction in hematoma size, permitting successful complete closure (Fig. 5B). The IABP was removed on POD 6, and extubation was achieved on POD 8. Brain MRI showed no new cerebral infarction; however, the patient developed flaccid quadriparesis consistent with ICU-acquired weakness. She was transferred to a rehabilitation facility on POD 42 and discharged 2 weeks later with full recovery. Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT at 4 months confirmed complete resolution of the LAD. Histopathological examination demonstrated cardiac myxoma resected with adequate margins.

Discussion

This case highlights LAD as a rare complication of cardiac surgery and demonstrates the practical value of repeated TEE in selecting and monitoring conservative (non-repair) management when additional definitive repair carries prohibitive risk.

LAD is an uncommon complication, with reported incidences of 0.16%–0.84% after mitral valve surgery, [3, 4] and is increasingly recognized with the widespread use of TEE. It most commonly involves the posterior left atrial wall [1,2]. In the setting of mitral valve surgery, LAD typically results from disruption of the atrioventricular groove, allowing pressurized left ventricular blood to create a false cavity between atrial wall layers. By contrast, LAD after cardiac myxoma resection is exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of cases reported[ 5–7]. All reported cases occurred after left atrial myxoma excision, with management ranging from radical surgical repair for oozing-type bleeding to conservative treatment following postoperative detection on routine imaging (Table 1). The mechanism of LAD after myxoma resection likely differs from that after mitral valve surgery because the operative field is not directly exposed to left ventricular pressure. Tissue-related vulnerability may therefore play a relatively greater role, although supporting evidence remains limited. The posterior left atrial wall develops embryologically through incorporation of the common pulmonary vein and may retain venous wall characteristics, making it structurally distinct from other atrial regions [8,9]. Douglas et al. reported that the posterior left atrial wall contains a vessel wall component beneath the endocardium and that this component is covered externally by myocardium [8]. Schwartzman et al. used the term atrial ‘intima’ to describe the tissue interposed between the endocardium and the myocardium; cadaveric studies demonstrate that this ‘intima’ can be readily separated from the underlying myocardium [10], suggesting a potential cleavage plane for dissection during surgical manipulation.

Cardiac myxoma is generally considered safely resectable because it typically arises from the atrial septum with a stalk. When the tumor involves the left atrial wall, however, resection becomes more complex, and partial-layer resection by shaving the endocardium with some underlying muscle may be permissible when full-thickness excision is impractical [11]. Ohira et al. attributed LAD in a similar case to bleeding from intramural vessels beneath a pericardial patch, emphasizing the importance of meticulous atrial wall hemostasis [7]. In our case, because pulsatile color Doppler flow was not observed in the false lumen, leakage from suture needle holes was also considered. Regardless of the initial entry source, persistent patency of the false cavity implies re-entry somewhere along the dissected plane, likely facilitated by tissue fragility. In retrospect, the small posterior left atrial hematoma observed before chest closure may have represented an early sign of atrial wall injury. Prophylactic application of fibrin glue between the patch and atrial wall may be considered in patients undergoing similar shaving procedures.

Management of LAD remains controversial, ranging from conservative observation to surgical intervention. Decisions should be guided by the extent of dissection, hemodynamic stability, and the presence of rupture. In hemodynamically stable patients without annular disruption, conservative (non-repair) management with close observation may be reasonable,12–14 as supported by the interval decrease of the intramural cavity in our case. By contrast, patients with severe hemodynamic compromise generally require surgical evacuation of the hematoma and repair of the entry site [3]. Surgical outcomes, however, are not uniformly favorable, with reported mortality rates of 9.8%–12.7% [1, 2]. In our patient, findings were interpreted as oozing-type bleeding rather than blow-out rupture, and a non-repair strategy was chosen because marked tissue fragility raised concern that surgical repair could exacerbate injury. We speculate that an unidentifiable micro-perforation occurred within the dissected atrial wall, analogous to false-lumen rupture in aortic dissection (Fig. 6).

Conservative management in this case should be understood as a bundled damage-control strategy guided by serial TEE. Key components included correction of coagulopathy, gauze packing with planned delayed sternal closure, [15] and strict hemodynamic control under deep sedation. IABP support was used as an adjunct to reduce peri-annular and atrioventricular groove stress, [3] despite its limited direct effect on left atrial pressure, as TEE demonstrated extension of the dissection toward the annulus.

In selected patients, a non-repair strategy for LAD may be pursued under close echocardiographic surveillance; however, prompt conversion to definitive surgery is warranted when warning signs emerge. Practical triggers include (i) uncontrolled active bleeding not amenable to compression or packing, (ii) hemodynamic instability attributable to LAD-related obstruction or valvular failure, and (iii) concerning TEE findings such as new pulsatile inflow into the false lumen, progressive cavity expansion, or signs of annular disruption. In our case, the cavity did not progress and decreased in size by POD 5, permitting delayed sternal closure.

TEE plays a central role in diagnosing and monitoring LAD by providing detailed visualization of dissection extent, false-lumen flow, and potential pulmonary venous or mitral inflow obstruction [4]. In this patient, repeated TEE examinations were essential for monitoring hematoma evolution and guiding decisions regarding chest closure. Contrast-enhanced CT was used to supplement assessment of the spatial extent of the hematoma. MRI may be useful for differential diagnosis when an atrial mass is incidentally detected,2,5,6 but it is generally impractical in the immediate postoperative setting, particularly when mechanical circulatory support devices are present.

During conservative (non-repair) management, major concerns include ongoing or progressive external rupture, pulmonary venous or mitral inflow obstruction, and systemic thromboembolism. Theoretical thromboembolic risk exists when an intramural cavity communicates with the left atrial chamber [16]. The role of antithrombotic therapy remains uncertain, with reported strategies ranging from early avoidance to continued anticoagulation [13,14]. In the present case, antithrombotic therapy was initially withheld because bleeding control was prioritized; anticoagulation was later administered from POD 12 to POD 34 for postoperative atrial fibrillation. Brain MRI on POD 15 revealed no acute infarction. Careful neurological monitoring remains essential during conservative management.

Conclusion

LAD with oozing rupture after cardiac myxoma resection is an extremely rare but potentially lethal complication. Conservative (non-repair) management may be considered in highly selected patients when definitive surgical intervention carries prohibitive risk. TEE plays a pivotal role in diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment guidance, and clinicians should remain vigilant for complications such as pulmonary venous obstruction, external rupture, and thromboembolic events during conservative management.
