Understanding the barriers for prevention and detection of chronic kidney disease among healthcare professionals in Syria: a qualitative study
Authors: Fatema Ahmed, Qingyuan Ye, Li Li, Waleed Ksebe, Haya Radwan, Chen Wu, Kefang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe risk of acquiring chronic kidney disease (CKD) is rising in Middle East countries, particularly due to the high prevalence of diabetes and hypertension. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) play a vital role in patient education, risk management and early detection of CKD, thereby impacting health outcomes across countries with varying resource levels. This study aimed to identify the obstacles to primary and secondary prevention of CKD among HCPs in Syria.Study designWe conducted a descriptive qualitative study design underpinned by semistructured interviews. Data were analysed using thematic analysis.SettingParticipants were recruited across the Syrian Arab Republic, encompassing diverse geographical areas, hospital types and community centres.ParticipantsA purposive sample of 12 HCPs was interviewed using online semistructured interviews. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and thematically analysed from November 2023 to May 2024.ResultsSeveral factors hindered CKD primary prevention and early detection among HCPs in Syria. These include individual factors such as a lack of knowledge, awareness and training. Interpersonal factors include unclear role delineation and poor communication among HCPs. Community/societal factors include a lack of government financial support, lower clinical priority for CKD, inadequate supplies for CKD primary prevention and diagnosis, a lack of patient education resources, limited access to nephrology services and a fragmented healthcare system.ConclusionsThe primary and secondary prevention of CKD in Syria is still nascent. Comprehensive strategies addressing individual, interpersonal and community/societal barriers are necessary to advance CKD primary prevention and early detection efforts. The study contributes to advancing the primary and secondary prevention of CKD and guiding targeted interventions and collaborative efforts to improve early detection of CKD in Syria. Analysing the local context of Syria and the availability of international resources for CKD primary prevention and early detection provides a decision-making framework that outlines actionable strategies for both domestic capacity-building and international organisational support.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is projected to become the fifth leading cause of death worldwide by 2040.1 Given the associated complications and increased mortality rates,2 3 primary prevention and early detection of CKD are of paramount importance. Early detection and control of risk factors play pivotal roles in this endeavour.4 5 In high-income countries, many general practitioners (GPs) perceive early-stage CKD as a complex and poorly defined clinical entity, which they do not consistently recognise as a disease; for example, GPs in France face challenges in managing early-stage CKD due to diagnostic and prognostic uncertainty, lack of disease recognition, patient management constraints, limited resources and multidisciplinary support.6 Many countries have initiated public awareness campaigns in response to these challenges, developed prevention and intervention strategies and provided comprehensive CKD care.7

In contrast to high-income countries, CKD is more challenging in conflict-affected and resource-limited areas.8 9 A recent study revealed a 70% increase in cases of CKD in the Middle East and North Africa over the last three decades, a rate significantly higher than any region in the world.10 The situation is particularly dire in Syria, where the destruction of healthcare infrastructure exacerbates these challenges. Research on Syrian refugees in Turkey,11 12 Lebanon13 and other neighbouring nations highlights the high prevalence of CKD among these vulnerable groups.14 Moreover, evidence indicates that Syrian refugees face substantial barriers to CKD primary prevention and early detection, such as restricted access to healthcare, insufficient knowledge about CKD risk factors and difficulties navigating healthcare systems in host countries.11 15 However, the specific barriers to CKD primary and secondary prevention within Syria remain largely unexplored.

This research was therefore launched to address this gap by exploring the hurdles to CKD primary prevention and early detection in Syria, thereby contributing to the more significant endeavour to enhance CKD prevention and health outcomes in war-torn and resource-limited contexts. This study aimed to identify the obstacles to preventing CKD among healthcare professionals (HCPs) in Syria.

Methods

Design

This descriptive qualitative study used in-depth online interviews with HCPs, with all participants providing written consent before each interview. This design was chosen to provide a descriptive account of HCPs’ views and practices regarding conservative CKD care and to offer guiding insights to improve the quality of clinical practice.16 The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research—a 32-item checklist for interviews and focus groups17 (online supplemental material 1).

We employed the maximum variation sampling to capture a wide diversity of individual stories and experiences among HCPs and capture the full spectrum of HCPs’ perspectives and experiences in primary prevention and early detection of CKD in Syria.

Patient or public contribution

An advisory group, including three nephrologists, one of whom is a co-author, reviewed, piloted, refined and designed the topic guide.

Study setting and sampling

We recruited HCPs, including registered nephrologists, nephrology nursing practitioners, general physicians and general nurses, from four hospitals and three primary healthcare centres in Latakia, Tartous and Damascus, Syria. Invitations were sent via text messages. We invited 45 participants, 20 of whom accepted to participate in this study. The sample size of 12 was determined based on saturation, assessed by reviewing transcripts after each interview and noting that no new codes or themes emerged. This point was determined to have occurred after the tenth interview; the final two interviews were conducted to confirm this.

Data generation

Semistructured interviews were conducted online by the principal investigator using the interview guide (online supplemental material 2). The interviews were audio-recorded, lasting 30–45 min each. Before the formal interview, a pilot interview was conducted with three HCPs to refine the questions. The interviews were conducted online by the principal investigator (FA). She is a PhD candidate in nursing with formal training in qualitative methods and prior experience conducting sensitive interviews. No prior relationship existed between the interviewer and any of the participants. At the beginning of each interview, the principal investigator briefly introduced the research project and tailored the questions to participants’ knowledge levels and professional roles. For instance, when interviewing nurses, particularly those working in healthcare centres, the language and complexity of the questions were adjusted to ensure clarity and relevance. Interviews were conducted in a private, quiet room within participants’ homes in the evening, after their workday, to ensure confidentiality and minimise potential distractions. No repeat interviews were carried out.

Data analysis

To ensure the accuracy of our findings, we compared the transcripts with the original audio recordings using NVivo V.14 for data management and coding. This verification process was conducted by two independent researchers (FA and WK). We conducted a thematic analysis based on the socioecological model,18 and the findings on barriers to primary prevention and early detection were grouped into three conceptual framework domains: individual, interpersonal and community/societal. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment because participants indicated during the consent process that they did not want to receive them.

Rigour and reflexivity

The criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability and authenticity proposed by Lincoln and Guba were used to ensure the reliability of our study.19 Strategies to enhance transferability involved providing detailed descriptions and saturating interview data. Prospective participants were given the chance to verify study details and the research team’s credentials. The transferability of our findings to other contexts, settings or groups was supported by the fact that the participants selected for this study represented a diverse range of GPs and specialists across various age groups, levels of experience and practice settings. To increase dependability, we discussed data interpretations within the research team and maintained auditable documentation to ensure accuracy and consistency. The research team consisted of both Syrian and non-Syrian members. Throughout the analysis, we engaged in ongoing discussion to challenge our assumptions about healthcare delivery in conflict settings. The codebook was developed collaboratively to minimise individual bias. Confirmability was achieved through the development of a comprehensive analysis codebook and the research team’s reflexivity. To manage political and cultural sensitivities, the interview guide was piloted and refined with local nephrologists. The interviewer emphasised confidentiality and obtained verbal consent before written consent. Interviews were conducted online from a private setting to ensure participant safety and candid responses. Questions were phrased to focus on professional experiences rather than political opinions.

Individual factors

Lack of knowledge and awareness of primary and secondary prevention of CKD

The prevailing theme identified in our research is the significant deficit in awareness regarding the primary prevention and early detection of CKD. This knowledge deficit also extends to the practical application of clinical practice guidelines among HCPs. Many nurses and doctors in health centres struggle to implement these guidelines and determine when specialist consultation is necessary. As one doctor said,

The lack of proper guidance from doctors results in patients, such as diabetics, not undergoing kidney function tests because they are unaware of the need for them. Consequently, only a small percentage of patients undergo early detection for kidney disease. (Doctor1)

Most HCPs cited the pervasive lack of patient knowledge about CKD as a critical issue. It has spurred some to deepen their understanding of CKD practice guidelines. Conversely, others reported that their limited knowledge hindered their ability to prevent CKD effectively, as one nurse pointed out.

The main issue is that non-specialist doctors often do not emphasize the importance of early CKD screening for at-risk patients. (Nurse4)

There is a general lack of knowledge about what CKD entails, even among healthcare providers, which often leads to mismanagement or late referrals. (Nurse5)

Unfamiliarity with guidelines

Effective primary prevention and early detection of CKD present significant challenges, particularly due to unfamiliarity and difficulty adhering to established clinical guidelines. A common issue among HCPs is inconsistent adherence to crucial protocols, such as kidney function assessments, before interventions like blood transfusions. One doctor highlighted, “There are instances where necessary pre-transfusion kidney function assessments are ignored by doctors, leading to late-stage diagnoses due to unrecognized renal damage.” (Doctor4)

This inconsistency extends to the prevention of risk factors that contribute to the progression of CKD. Many physicians struggle to recognise and address subtle changes that may indicate deteriorating kidney function. For instance, “Cardiologists sometimes fail to perform necessary kidney function analyses, leading to delayed diagnosis of CKD in patients with cardiac issues.” (Doctor3)

One nurse explained that a lack of knowledge about timely referrals to nephrologists also exacerbates the issue.

In cases where CKD manifests without obvious symptoms, especially in patients with risk factors such as hypertension or diabetes, inadequate monitoring and oversight by healthcare professionals allows the disease to progress unnoticed until critical complications arise. (Nurse1)

Another significant barrier is the HCPs’ limited familiarity with CKD guidelines and counselling protocols. A revealing comment from a doctor highlighted this issue.

Very few doctors tend to conduct a urine analysis. Analysis of urine is, in fact, a massive mirror of renal function. (Doctor4)

Perceived lack of adequate training

Most nurses highlighted a significant issue related to CKD in the nursing education and training framework. This inadequacy in the educational curriculum has clinical and personal repercussions for both the staff and patients involved.

Our education curricula did not cover CKD extensively, which has resulted in a lack of understanding among nursing staff about crucial procedures like urine and sediment analysis for CKD detection. (Nurse 5)

Personally, I was unaware of the importance of regular kidney function tests until it was critically necessary for my father. (Nurse 2).

Interpersonal factors

Lack of clear role delineation between HCPs

Most HCPs identified a systemic issue that prevents effective patient care, particularly in defining and adhering to the roles and responsibilities of various specialists such as endocrinologists, general physicians and nephrologists. This absence of clear delineation often leads to a lack of coordination, which can result in delays in essential interventions that could significantly alter the progression of the disease.

Endocrinologists often do not facilitate early nephrologist involvement in diabetes management, missing opportunities for early CKD intervention. (Doctor5)

Many physicians do not routinely perform necessary kidney function tests, and non-specialists often manage CKD without consulting nephrologists, leading to suboptimal care. The term “encroachment of specialties” highlights this issue.

Poor communication between HCPs

Nephrologists are concerned about the late detection of CKD, often diagnosed at advanced stages due to insufficient communication among HCPs.

There is a critical need for increased collaboration among specialists—endocrinologists, cardiologists, and nephrologists—to prevent kidney damage. (Doctor4)

Community and societal factors

Lack of government financial support

The healthcare sector faces significant challenges, which are further exacerbated by financial constraints and low living standards. A key factor impacting healthcare during this period is the emigration of doctors, nurses and scientific talent to other countries. This trend is particularly alarming as it results in a critical shortage of skilled HCPs in public health centres, which frequently serve as the primary sources of care for the majority of the population. Without adequate funding, HCPs lack the resources to implement widespread screening programmes, establish coordinated care pathways or incentivise preventive measures. This results in fragmented kidney care, where CKD is often underdiagnosed and managed inconsistently.

The migration of doctors to better-paying opportunities abroad or private practices due to inadequate financial incentives has resulted in a critical staff shortage in our healthcare centers. (Nurse3)

Insufficient supplies for CKD primary prevention and early detection

A critical concern in healthcare facilities is the shortage of laboratory analysis equipment, tools and materials essential for the primary prevention and detection of CKD. The scarcity affects the HCPs, including doctors, nurses and laboratory personnel, and hampers their ability to provide optimal care. Combined with frequent power outages at health centres, this situation further exacerbates the challenges faced by HCPs in providing timely and effective services.

Patients often find that necessary medications are unavailable at health centers, leading them to forego further visits. (Doctor5)

Shortages in raw materials needed for routine urine and creatinine analyses hinder our ability to prevent and monitor CKD effectively. (Nurse6)

Lower priority of CKD as a clinical issue

In Syria, CKD primary prevention and early detection are often overshadowed by other prevalent common health concerns, such as cancer, infectious diseases and immunisation, which receive significant support from the Ministry of Health and various organisations. Raising education and awareness about CKD should be a primary focus, particularly among individuals with a history of diabetes and high blood pressure.

Patients often arrive at hospitals in the final stages of CKD because the condition does not receive the same attention as other diseases, leading to overwhelming pressures on dialysis services and limited options for disease prevention. (Doctor1)

Lack of patient education resources

Syrian HCPs have expressed significant concern about the lack of educational programmes or government initiatives aimed at addressing primary and secondary prevention of CKD. This gap has left many high-risk individuals without important prevention resources. Doctors and nurses require specialised health programmes to address this growing issue. The lack of knowledge often leads patients to seek medical attention late in the progression of CKD, subsequently increasing healthcare costs and patient outcomes.

Currently, there are no established health and awareness programs that effectively target high-risk individuals for early detection and routine monitoring of CKD. (Doctor2)

In my experience, there has been little to no effort to implement CKD health education within our work environment, leaving many patients uninformed about their condition and necessary health practices. (Nurse2)

The role of health educators is frequently overlooked, reducing the effectiveness of potential educational interventions for CKD. (Nurse2)

Limited access to nephrology

The shortage of nephrologists in Syria severely restricts access to nephrology services, making timely consultations difficult and limiting specialised care and preventative health education. HCPs, particularly Doctor 1, have emphasised the persistent shortage, which results in delayed diagnosis, inefficient prevention of kidney disease and heightened strain on the healthcare system.

There is a chronic shortage of medical staff, including nephrologists, which limits our capacity to provide specialized care and to engage in preventive health education. (Doctor1)

A fragmented healthcare system

The ongoing conflict in Syria has profoundly affected the healthcare sector, leading to a lack of coordination and integration among hospitals and health facilities. Physicians and nurses perceive ongoing services and patient follow-up as fragmented, with a notable absence of consistent communication between healthcare institutions and family doctors. This fragmentation hinders the continuity of care and the transfer of vital clinical information, impeding effective treatment planning.

The absence of a reliable communication network among hospitals, health centers, and family doctors leads to disjointed care and missed opportunities for early intervention. (Doctor2)

Syria’s lack of an electronic medical record (EMR) system hampers HCPs’ ability to share patient information effectively. An EMR system, essential to comanaging CKD primary prevention and early detection efforts, ensures that all critical health information is accessible across departments. Doctor 4 emphasises the importance of this gap in preventive care for at-risk patients.

The absence of collaboration between departments like cardiology and internal medicine often results in poor management of patients with renal issues, highlighting a critical need for structured interdepartmental communication protocols. (Doctor4)

The emigration of HCPs due to poor financial conditions, combined with the destruction of health centres in conflict zones, has further strained Syria’s healthcare system. This has forced many patients to seek treatment in safer areas, overwhelming HCPs and exacerbating the fragmentation of the system.

Post-conflict challenges have severely strained healthcare resources, with health centers overwhelmed by an influx of patients from conflict-affected regions, exacerbating the system’s fragmentation. (Nurse3)

While a referral system is in place, its lack of proper activation further complicates patient care continuity and follow-up. (Doctor6).

We found notable differences in the perspectives of physicians and nurses. Physicians act as gatekeepers, determining when to screen, who should order the test and which speciality is responsible for early CKD; nurses act as corridor witnesses, recounting how empty strip boxes, powerless labs or unheard instructions allow patients to drift toward late-stage disease. Both lament knowledge gaps, but doctors internalise them as collective professional lapses, while nurses externalise them to structural shortages and blurred role boundaries.

Discussion

This qualitative study sheds light on the multifaceted challenges faced by HCPs in primary prevention and early detection of CKD in Syria, a region deeply scarred by war. At the individual level, HCPs reported limited knowledge and awareness of primary prevention and early detection of CKD, unfamiliarity with guidelines and lack of adequate training as barriers; at the interpersonal level, they were hindered by the lack of clear role delineation and poor communication; at the community level, they reported challenges, including a lack of financial support from the government, lower clinical priority for CKD, insufficient supplies, lack of patient education resources, limited access to nephrology and the fragmented healthcare system. Comparing my findings with those in a systematic review,20 our results are not isolated or unique to the specific group studied.

Individual factors

At the individual level, our study has identified a lack of health knowledge and awareness of primary prevention and early detection among HCPs. This finding is consistent with those in developed countries such as the USA and Germany, demonstrating a widespread lack of understanding regarding CKD among HCPs.21

Moreover, the unfamiliarity with clinical guidelines on CKD among HCPs is evident in Syria. This mirrors the evidence that most patients with stage 3 CKD did not receive recommended guideline-directed monitoring and management. HCPs also reported a perceived lack of adequate training. Continuous education and training are paramount to eliminating barriers. For example, in the USA, the National Kidney Disease Education Program has shown that improving provider practices, such as nurses’ training and resources, can lead to greater CKD knowledge and awareness.22 Integrating nephrology into nursing education has also enhanced nurses’ knowledge of CKD primary prevention and early detection in Akure, South-West Nigeria.23 The Syrian American Medical Society and the Syrian Expatriate Medical Association have developed training programmes to address healthcare issues in Syria, but CKD-specific training is not covered. To improve CKD knowledge among HCPs, they may develop a culturally adapted programme by integrating valid evidence, such as evaluating general practice nurses’ knowledge of CKD risk factors and screening practices after completing a case-study-based asynchronous e-learning module.24

Interpersonal factors

In this study, the lack of clear role delineation and poor interpersonal communication were identified as barriers to primary prevention and early detection of CKD. This problem is not unique to Syria, but also exists in other countries.25 26 Several countries have implemented strategies such as shared EMRs,27 electronic consultations (eConsult)28 and the Situation, Background, Assessment and Recommendation method29 to improve coordination and communication. These advancements highlight the importance of aligning healthcare communication systems with modern developments. However, the Syrian healthcare system remains severely underdeveloped and continues to rely predominantly on paper-based records. Therefore, feasible and context-specific solutions are urgently needed to address these challenges in Syria.

Community and societal factors

Most barriers to CKD primary prevention and early detection stem from community and societal levels. Our study identified several significant challenges that align with global trends observed in resource-limited settings. These include a lack of government financial support for the healthcare sector, the emigration of skilled HCPs and a critical shortage of essential medical supplies.30 31 Many low-income and middle-income countries face substantial challenges in addressing CKD, largely due to the ‘brain drain’ phenomenon, where skilled HCPs migrate to high-income countries. This migration is driven by economic disparities, poor working conditions and limited career advancement opportunities in their home countries.

Additionally, these nations often struggle with the lack of medical supplies and equipment necessary for the primary prevention and diagnosis of CKD.32 33 Given Syria’s severe economic constraints, achieving the level of CKD primary prevention and early detection seen in other countries may be unattainable without external support. Therefore, the involvement of the international community and expatriate nephrologists is crucial in guiding care delivery and improving patient outcomes.34

CKD often receives lower priority in healthcare systems. In Syria, the primary focus of healthcare concerns has been directed toward diabetes mellitus and cancer, which has directly diminished the attention given to conservative CKD care.35 Most HCPs in our study perceive CKD more as a theoretical concept rather than a practical healthcare issue, a finding consistent with previous research.36 37 This lack of prioritisation further exacerbates the challenges of effectively addressing CKD. To overcome this barrier, there is an urgent need to emphasise the importance of primary prevention and early detection of CKD, alongside other major health concerns.

The lack of patient education resources and limited access to nephrology services are critical barriers at the community and societal levels that hinder effective CKD primary prevention and early detection. Patient education resources, such as informational brochures, workshops, online platforms and community health campaigns, are often insufficient or entirely unavailable, especially in low-resource settings.38 39 The reported lack of patient education resources by Syrian HCPs has a direct and severe impact on patients. It is supported by qualitative studies in Dutch hospitals showing that CKD patients commonly experience uncertainty, fear, and isolation, often because they find medical information unavailable or hard to understand.40 This corroborates the HCPs’ observations, indicating that the problem is not merely a logistical issue but a critical failure in patient-centred care, as demonstrated in a systematic review of qualitative studies.41 The lack of resources prevents patients from maintaining a sense of control. It leaves their specific needs for information about their future unmet, thereby increasing their anxiety and sense of disempowerment.

Limited access to nephrology services further compounds the challenge. In Syria, resource-oriented strategies, such as rebuilding healthcare infrastructure and securing international support, are crucial for addressing these barriers and enhancing kidney health outcomes. Prioritising these areas could enhance the availability and accessibility of patient education resources and nephrology services, ultimately strengthening efforts in primary prevention and early detection of CKD.

The fragmented healthcare system in Syria is characterised by a lack of coordination between community health centres and hospitals, as well as between the private and public sectors.34 42 The absence of patient registration and follow-up systems further complicates CKD primary prevention and early detection efforts, as these systems are critical for tracking patient progress, managing risk factors and ensuring continuity of care. Without such systems, opportunities for controlling risk factors, early diagnosis and treatment are often missed, resulting in inadequate primary prevention and early detection of CKD.43 To address these challenges, establishing inter-institutional linkages should be a key focus for future research. This includes determining effective communication methods, standardising file creation and tracking processes, and fostering collaboration across healthcare sectors. Managers and international organisations should provide support to Syria in these areas to strengthen CKD primary prevention, early detection and treatment, and improve overall healthcare outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents one of the first qualitative investigations to explore HCPs’ perspectives on barriers to primary prevention and early detection of CKD in a war-affected region. Including a diverse group of HCPs from health centres and hospitals gave us valuable insights into the challenges hindering CKD primary prevention and early detection in Syria. It captures the barriers to CKD primary prevention and early detection solely from the perspective of HCPs. While this offers invaluable insights into systemic and clinical challenges, we will directly incorporate the voices of patients or individuals at risk for CKD.34 44

However, interviews were conducted with HCPs from multiple sites, and the findings of this study are more likely to reflect general barriers to CKD primary prevention and early detection rather than to capture the specific nuances or unique perspectives of any single healthcare facility.

Recommendations for further research

To build a complete picture, future research must directly engage the HCPs and patients in Syria. Combining these patient-derived data with the HCP perspectives identified in this study would enable the development of truly comprehensive, co-designed and person-centred interventions for CKD prevention and early detection in Syria. Given the multiple layers of barriers, further studies should assess the most feasible and changeable obstacles that can be addressed to enhance CKD primary and secondary prevention efforts in limited-resource countries. Develop multimodal interventions that incorporate evidence-based components of CKD prevention strategies to strengthen the primary prevention and early detection of CKD.

Implications for policy and practice

This study is important for advancing primary prevention and early detection efforts for CKD in Syria. By analysing the local context in Syria and the availability of international resources for CKD primary and secondary prevention, this analysis provides a decision-making framework outlining actionable strategies for both national capacity-building and international organisational support. Specifically, the study offers a detailed portrayal of the barriers to primary and secondary CKD prevention in Syria, spanning individual, community and societal challenges. It highlights the need for a comprehensive approach that integrates targeted training programmes, improved communication systems, community engagement and effective resource allocation. Without such systematic procedures, early diagnosis and treatment opportunities are often missed, leading to inadequate CKD management. These findings are crucial for guiding targeted interventions and collaborative efforts to enhance CKD prevention in the country.

Conclusions

This study highlights multilayered barriers to primary prevention and early detection of CKD in Syria. Given the rising prevalence of CKD in Syria, which cannot be overlooked, these findings underscore the urgent need for feasible approaches to CKD primary and secondary prevention. Syria has been profoundly affected by prolonged conflict, resulting in a complex socioeconomic and political landscape. The current challenges in Syria demand that the Syrian Ministry of Health recognise CKD as a public health priority. Given the profound resource constraints, we further recommend that these national efforts be supported by international organisations and expatriate medical associations, who can provide technical expertise, training and initial resource allocation.
