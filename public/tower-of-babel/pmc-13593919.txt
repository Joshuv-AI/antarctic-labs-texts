Association Between Self‐Rated Health and eHealth Literacy in Higher Vocational Nursing Students: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Li Huang, Yufang Chen, Xin Du, Aiqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the association between self‐rated health and eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students.DesignCross‐sectional study.MethodsUsing stratified random cluster sampling, 727 nursing students from a vocational college in Henan Province, China, were recruited. Multiple linear regression analysis assessed the association, adjusting for confounders such as sociodemographic characteristics, family socioeconomic status and academic stress.ResultsAfter adjusting for covariates, participants with poor self‐rated health had significantly lower eHealth literacy scores than those with good health. Participants reporting fair self‐rated health also had significantly lower scores; though the difference was substantially smaller.ConclusionSelf‐rated health is significantly associated with eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students. Whether interventions combining health management and digital skills training can improve eHealth literacy in this population remains to be tested in future longitudinal and intervention studies.ImpactDespite the large proportion of higher vocational nursing students in China's nursing workforce, evidence linking their health perceptions to digital competencies remains limited. Introduction

In today's increasingly digitalised healthcare environment, eHealth literacy has become an essential competency for nursing professionals. eHealth literacy refers to individuals' ability in the information age to locate, comprehend and apply health information from the internet to address health‐related issues (Norman and Skinner 2006). For nursing students, developing eHealth literacy is critical to effectively accessing, understanding, evaluating and using credible digital health resources to inform clinical decisions and patient education (Rathnayake and Senevirathna 2019). As future frontline providers, nursing students' eHealth literacy directly influences their capacity to support patients in navigating online health information, thereby affecting the quality of nursing care and health outcomes.

Several studies report that undergraduate nursing students generally exhibit moderate to upper‐intermediate levels of eHealth literacy (Agapito et al. 2024; Kim and Oh 2021; Sinan et al. 2023). While they demonstrate competence in locating and applying digital health information, they often lack the critical appraisal skills necessary to distinguish high‐quality sources from low‐quality ones (Tubaishat and Habiballah 2016). Most existing research focuses on undergraduate students, with limited attention given to higher vocational nursing students in China. Higher vocational nursing students refer to individuals enrolled in China's higher vocational education system pursuing a 3‐year program, typically aged 17 to 24, and mostly recent high school graduates (Wang et al. 2024). This program focuses on clinical nursing skills and prepares them for careers in nursing. As of 2021, diploma‐educated registered nurses constituted 48.7% of China's nursing workforce (National Health Commission 2023). The proportion of higher vocational nursing students has steadily grown over the past decade, positioning vocational nursing education as a crucial component of China's healthcare education system. In China, these diploma‐level nurses predominantly serve in grassroots medical institutions and community health service centres, where they act as frontline providers for chronic disease management, elderly care and home‐based nursing services. Given their significant representation in the nursing workforce and the increasing digitalization of clinical practice, understanding their eHealth literacy capabilities and gaps is essential. Therefore, clarifying the eHealth literacy status of these students and its key correlates would facilitate better alignment between vocational nursing education and the practical demands of digital health care.

The health status of higher vocational nursing students also warrants attention. As future healthcare providers, their own health influences both personal development and the quality of care they will deliver in clinical practice. Self‐rated health (SRH) serves as a key indicator reflecting individuals' health perceptions, behaviours and awareness (McKay et al. 2024; Pisinger et al. 2009), and has been widely recognized as a reliable predictor of objective health outcomes (e.g., mortality, chronic disease incidence) and healthcare utilization across diverse populations (Friedman and Teas 2023; Ma et al. 2024). For higher vocational nursing students, SRH may be especially significant. This group faces unique challenges: shorter, intensive programs compress health management time (Bak et al. 2020), educational constraints foster feelings of inferiority, and low professional identity leads to learned helplessness (Wang et al. 2025). These factors can directly impact their physical and mental health. SRH, as a key measure of health perception, behaviour and awareness, can help provide a more comprehensive assessment and understanding of their health status when facing these challenges.

According to the Health Belief Model (HBM), people's perceptions of health issues directly shape their health behaviours. These include perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits of action, barriers to action, self‐efficacy and cues to action (Alyafei and Easton‐Carr 2024). In the current digital era, an individual's subjective assessment of their own health may affect their willingness to actively acquire and apply digital health information. Previous research has supported this link; in other populations, individuals with better SRH usually demonstrate higher eHealth literacy (Xu et al. 2022). Yet the picture is not so straightforward. Qualitative findings reveal a bidirectional influence: some people in good health see little need to seek online health information, while others believe that maintaining good health is essential for putting online health guidance into practice (Marklund et al. 2021). This indicates that the relationship is probably more complicated than a simple linear association. However, these conclusions are drawn from other populations, and their applicability to higher vocational nursing students remains uncertain. This group faces distinctive challenges, including compressed curricula, demanding clinical practicum requirements, and a still‐developing professional identity. These factors may alter how they prioritize health concerns and thereby reshape the dynamics between SRH and eHealth literacy.

Because higher vocational nursing students account for nearly half of China's nursing workforce, any relationship between their health perceptions and their capacity to engage with digital health information carries direct implications for nursing practice, workforce development and patient education. Relevant information regarding this relationship may aid nurse educators in implementing effective interventions and facilitating the integration of digital health competencies into vocational nursing curricula. To our knowledge, this relationship remains insufficiently understood in higher vocational nursing students. The relationship between SRH and eHealth literacy among this group warrants considerable attention. To extend this line of inquiry to this group in China, the present study utilized data from the Henan College Students' eHealth Literacy Project to examine the relationship between SRH and eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a cross‐sectional, descriptive design and adhered to the principles outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (von Elm et al. 2008). The completed STROBE checklist is provided in File S1.

Participants

This study is part of the Henan College Students' eHealth Literacy Project, which investigates eHealth literacy and its influencing factors among college students in Henan Province, with the goal of developing evidence‐based improvement strategies. A stratified random cluster sampling approach was employed to recruit higher vocational nursing students from a higher vocational institution. Two sampling frames were created based on academic year (first‐year and second‐year). From each frame, six intact classes were selected using simple random sampling from the full list of teaching classes. The questionnaires were distributed via the Wenjuanxing platform from 10 to 25 December 2023.

Data Quality Control

An electronic questionnaire was generated using the Wenjuanxing platform and administered to participants after obtaining their informed consent. The platform settings were configured to disable time limits for answering, require responses to all items (making all questions mandatory and preventing submission of incomplete questionnaires), and restrict submissions to one response per IP address. Additionally, validity verification was implemented by the research team. To ensure data quality, the following criteria were applied for exclusion from analysis:

Questionnaires completed in less than 90 s (as verified by backend platform timestamps) were excluded to mitigate the risk of response haste;

Questionnaires completed in less than 90 s (as verified by backend platform timestamps) were excluded to mitigate the risk of response haste;

Responses exhibiting systematic invalid patterns (e.g., straight‐line responding, such as selecting the same option throughout the entire questionnaire) were excluded.

Sample Size and Power

The minimum sample size was calculated using the G*power 3.1.9.7 program for multiple regression analysis. Based on a significance level of 0.05, an intermediate effect size f 2 of 0.15, power (1 − β) of 0.95 and number of predictors of 12, the resulting sample size was 184. Considering the stratified random cluster sampling design, the sample size was adjusted for the design effect (Deff = 1.5), yielding an adjusted sample size of 276. To ensure adequate statistical power and account for potential non‐response or invalid questionnaires, a total of 805 electronic questionnaires were distributed via the Wenjuanxing platform, yielding 727 valid responses (validity rate: 90.3%).

Measurements

EHealth Literacy

The Chinese version of the eHealth Literacy Scale, originally developed by Norman and Skinner (2006) and culturally adapted by Guo et al. (2013), was used. This version demonstrates excellent reliability and validity, with a Cronbach's α of 0.94 and an average item‐level content validity index (I‐CVI) of 1.00 (SD = 0.02) (Chao 2024). The scale includes 8 items across three dimensions: (1) applied competence in accessing online health information and services, (2) critical evaluation competence and (3) decision‐making competence. Responses are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1 point) to ‘strongly agree’ (5 points), producing total scores ranging from 8 to 40. Higher scores reflect greater eHealth literacy. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.93).

SRH

SRH was measured using a single item from the 12‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐12) (Ware et al. 1996): ‘In general, how would you rate your current health status?’ The original SF‐12 item's response options included: Excellent, Very good, Good, Fair and Poor. For the present study, SRH was collected as a pre‐specified three‐category variable in the questionnaire, with response options of good, fair and poor. Recent studies have shown that SRH is frequently operationalized using categorical groupings, including three‐category classifications, depending on study design and analytical considerations (Peng et al. 2023). In addition, the response format of single‐item SRH varies across studies and surveys (Cullati et al. 2020). Retaining the three pre‐specified categories preserves the ordinal gradient of self‐rated health status. Therefore, no post hoc regrouping of SRH categories was performed.

Covariates

Baseline covariates included age (in years), sex, grade level (first‐year/s‐year), geographic origin (urban/rural), prior secondary‐level vocational training (yes/no), only‐child status (yes/no), academic stress, household economic status, monthly living expenses (in RMB), family structure and parental education levels. Academic stress was assessed via self‐report as ‘minor’, ‘average’, or ‘significant’. Household economic status was categorized as ‘relatively poor’ or ‘average or above’ (the latter combining ‘average’ and ‘relatively good’ due to sparse cell counts in the highest category), based on local conditions (Yang and Qi 2025). Monthly living expenses were grouped into two categories: < 800 RMB and ≥ 800 RMB (the latter combining 800–1500 RMB and > 1500 RMB). Family structure was classified as intact versus non‐intact; no participants reported single‐parent family status. Maternal and paternal education levels were categorized as primary school or below, middle school and high school or above (the latter combining high school/technical secondary school and college or higher due to small cell counts in the highest category) (Gao et al. 2023).

Statistical Analysis

Multivariable linear regression was used to estimate the association between SRH and eHealth literacy. Because the questionnaire required responses to all items, we did not apply any missing data imputation or case‐wise deletion procedures. Two regression models were constructed:

Model 1: Adjusted for sex, age, grade level, geographic origin and secondary vocational education experience.

Model 1: Adjusted for sex, age, grade level, geographic origin and secondary vocational education experience.

Model 2: Extended Model 1 with additional adjustments for only‐child status, academic stress, household economic status, monthly living expenses, family structure and parental education levels.

Model 2: Extended Model 1 with additional adjustments for only‐child status, academic stress, household economic status, monthly living expenses, family structure and parental education levels.

To ensure model stability, we first recoded covariates with empty or sparse categories before running the analysis. Family structure was treated as intact versus non‐intact; household economic status and monthly living expenses were each collapsed into two categories; and parental education was grouped into three levels. We then checked for multicollinearity using VIF and used the Durbin‐Watson statistic to see if residuals were independent.

Given the anticipated low intra‐class correlation, conventional multivariable linear regression was used without clustering adjustment; the design effect (Deff = 1.5) had been incorporated into the sample size calculation. This assumption was verified by calculating the ICC and conducting sensitivity analyses with cluster‐robust standard errors. Homoscedasticity of residuals was assessed using the Breusch–Pagan test, with p > 0.05 taken to indicate homoscedasticity. The distribution of residuals was inspected using normal Q–Q plots. Because the outcome was measured on a bounded Likert‐type scale, mild deviations at the tails of the plot were anticipated; given the large sample size, such mild deviations were not expected to materially affect the regression estimates.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association Between Self‐Rated Health and eHealth Literacy

In linear regression analysis, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables (Model 1, Table 2), participants with poor SRH had lower eHealth literacy scores (B = −6.16, 95% CI: −8.89, −3.43; β = −0.16, p < 0.001), and those with fair SRH also scored lower (B = −1.12, 95% CI: −1.97, −0.27; β = −0.10, p = 0.010) compared to the good SRH group. These associations persisted after adjusting for additional covariates in Model 2. Participants with poor SRH had significantly lower scores (B = −6.09, 95% CI: −8.85, −3.32; β = −0.16, p < 0.001), and the fair SRH group remained significantly lower as well (B = −1.10, 95% CI: −1.95, −0.25; β = −0.09, p = 0.011) (Table 2). The models accounted for 4% (Model 1: adjusted R 2 = 0.04) and 5% (Model 2: adjusted R 2 = 0.05) of the variance in eHealth literacy, respectively. The absolute mean difference between poor and good SRH groups was 6.03 points on the 8–40 eHEALS scale, corresponding to approximately one standard deviation (Cohen's d = 1.0). Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no concerning multicollinearity (all VIF < 5), and the Durbin‐Watson statistic of 1.99 suggested no autocorrelation in residuals.

Sensitivity Analysis

Given that only 2.5% of participants (n = 18) reported ‘poor’ self‐rated health, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the small cell size influenced the stability of our estimates. In this analysis, the ‘poor’ and ‘fair’ categories were combined into a single group, with ‘good’ as the reference category. As shown in Table 3, students with ‘good’ self‐rated health demonstrated higher eHealth literacy scores compared with those in the combined ‘poor/fair’ group in both the partially adjusted model (Model 1: B = −1.34, 95% CI: −2.19, −0.49; β = −0.11, p = 0.002) and the fully adjusted model (Model 2: B = −1.30, 95% CI: −2.15, −0.45; β = −0.11, p = 0.003).

Additionally, to address the stratified cluster sampling design, we calculated the intra‐class correlation coefficient for eHealth literacy at the class level and found it to be low (ICC = 0.009). Sensitivity analyses using cluster‐robust standard errors with class as the clustering variable (n = 12 classes across 2 grade‐level strata) yielded a significant linear trend for SRH (B = 1.62, 95% CI: 0.35, 2.90; p = 0.018). Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between SRH status and eHealth literacy levels among higher vocational nursing students. The findings indicate that students who rated their health as poor or fair exhibited lower levels of eHealth literacy. This association remained significant after adjusting for sociodemographic variables, family background and academic stress, suggesting a significant association between health perception and eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students. The models explained only a modest proportion of variance (adjusted R 2 = 0.04–0.05). The poor‐SRH group showed a sizeable between‐group difference in eHealth literacy scores (approximately 6 points, or about one standard deviation; Cohen's d = 1.0); however, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of participants reporting poor SRH.

The Relationship Between SRH and eHealth Literacy

As future frontline nursing professionals, the health status of higher vocational nursing students carries significant public health implications. Approximately 2.5% of participants rated their health as ‘poor’ and 53.6% as ‘fair’, indicating that SRH status in this population is suboptimal. The poor‐SRH group exhibited a substantially larger eHealth literacy deficit than the fair‐SRH group. Higher vocational nursing students often encounter multiple stressors, including anxiety regarding academic qualifications, heavy academic workloads (e.g., clinical skills assessments), and uncertainty surrounding professional identity (Cao et al. 2025; Jie 2018). These pressures may be associated with the neglect of personal health management, thereby compromising their overall well‐being.

Previous research on the relationship between SRH and eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students remains limited. This study provides new empirical evidence for this group. Linear regression analysis showed that, after two levels of model adjustment, participants with ‘poor’ SRH scored 6.09 points lower in eHealth literacy compared to those with ‘good’ health (95% CI: −8.85, −3.32). Those with ‘fair’ SRH showed a 1.10‐point reduction (95% CI: −1.95, −0.25). These findings suggest that suboptimal SRH was associated with lower eHealth literacy. As a comprehensive indicator encompassing physical, psychological and social adaptation dimensions (Fayers and Sprangers 2002), SRH has been associated with eHealth literacy across various occupational groups. Prior studies indicate that individuals with better SRH tend to demonstrate higher eHealth literacy levels (Neter et al. 2021). Research involving Danish undergraduate and graduate nursing students found significant positive correlations between SRH and multiple components of eHealth literacy, particularly in electronic information acquisition, self‐management and information interaction (Holt et al. 2020). Similarly, a study of adolescents found that those with good health perception reported significantly higher eHealth literacy levels compared to those with poorer health ratings (Yilmazel and Özden 2025). Consistent with prior findings in other populations, individuals with poorer SRH in our sample of higher vocational nursing students exhibited markedly lower eHealth literacy, extending this association to an understudied group.

The association between SRH and eHealth literacy may operate through multiple mechanisms. A qualitative study revealed that when facing abundant or complex health information, Generation Z individuals experience psychological discomfort due to excessive cognitive load, leading them to actively or passively avoid information to alleviate stress (Jia and Li 2024). Higher vocational nursing students frequently face challenges such as limited foundational knowledge, low learning motivation and weak critical thinking skills. Additionally, those with suboptimal SRH often experience heightened health anxiety (Mewton and Andrews 2013). As a result, higher vocational nursing students with poor SRH may struggle with cognitive overload when processing health‐related information. They must manage both the complexity of disease information and emotional reactions related to personal health concerns. Participants reporting ‘poor’ SRH in this study also described greater academic pressures, further exacerbating their cognitive burden. Such cognitive burden may drive students away from health information seeking, in turn weakening their eHealth literacy. Furthermore, individuals with suboptimal SRH may inherently lack sufficient health literacy. This contributes to a passive approach to health management and reduced confidence in engaging with the healthcare system (Storey et al. 2020). These students may encounter cognitive barriers when accessing and applying online health information, which could be related to lower eHealth literacy. Individuals with lower levels of eHealth literacy are more likely to engage in problematic online health information‐seeking behaviours, which can contribute to cyberchondria. The anxiety and fear induced by frequent exposure to—or misinterpretation of—health information may worsen their perception of personal health, thereby further degrading their SRH. A study of university students in Taiwan, China, found that eHealth literacy scores independently predicted engagement in health‐promoting lifestyles (Chao 2024). Students with lower eHealth literacy performed worse in domains such as health responsibility, physical activity and nutritional habits (Chao 2024). Taken together, these pathways suggest a potentially bidirectional association between SRH and eHealth literacy; however, the cross‐sectional design precludes establishing directionality, and these proposed mechanisms warrant testing in longitudinal studies.

Implications for Practice

Although causal inference is precluded by the cross‐sectional design, our findings generate several hypotheses for nursing education that warrant testing in future intervention studies. This study carries practical relevance for nursing education, workforce development and patient outcomes. Poorer self‐rated health was associated with markedly lower eHealth literacy in this cross‐sectional sample. SRH may help educators identify students who could benefit from further assessment or targeted digital‐literacy support. With targeted reinforcement, these future frontline nurses would be better prepared for daily practice. They could help patients use digital health tools safely and assess online health information during routine care. Integrating digital health resource appraisal into health management courses may prove more effective than isolated training. This approach enables students to develop the competency needed to support patient education, remote monitoring and informed self‐management decisions in clinical settings (Kim and Oh 2021). Healthcare and educational institutions may also explore mentorship programs. In these programs, nurses or senior students with strong eHealth literacy support patients and peers in using digital resources safely (Dijkman et al. 2026; Sanders et al. 2021; Terry et al. 2019). These implications remain provisional; given the cross‐sectional design, causal inference is precluded and the effectiveness of these strategies awaits verification through future intervention studies.

Strengths and Limitations

This study examines the association between SRH and eHealth literacy in higher vocational nursing students. A couple of strengths are worth noting. One is its specific focus on this understudied population. Unlike most studies that target general populations or undergraduate nursing students, this research reveals how SRH relates to eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students, adding a fresh perspective to the literature. Another strength lies in the rigorous control of various confounders, enhancing the robustness of the observed association. However, several limitations must be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the regression models explained only a modest proportion of variance (adjusted R 2 = 0.04–0.05), suggesting that SRH represents a partial rather than dominant correlate. Relatedly, only 2.5% of participants (n = 18) reported poor SRH, which limits the precision of estimates for this subgroup; although pooling the ‘poor’ and ‘fair’ categories in a sensitivity analysis yielded consistent results, findings specific to the poor‐SRH subgroup should be interpreted with caution. Second, the sample was drawn from a single regional institution using an online survey platform, which may limit generalizability. Findings were broadly consistent in a cluster‐robust sensitivity analysis; however, the small number of classes (n = 12) limits the precision of cluster‐adjusted inference. Third, both eHealth literacy and SRH were self‐reported, which may have introduced information bias or recall bias. Additionally, the mandatory‐response format may have introduced selection bias if students with limited digital literacy were less able to complete the survey. Finally, despite adjustment for sociodemographic and contextual factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as mental health status, general health literacy, quality of digital access and personality traits cannot be ruled out. Future studies can draw on samples from multiple institutions and examine how the relationship between SRH and eHealth literacy varies across individuals with differing mental health profiles, health literacy levels and digital access conditions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals a significant association between SRH and eHealth literacy among higher vocational nursing students. Poorer self‐rated health was associated with lower eHealth literacy scores. By examining an often‐overlooked group—higher vocational nursing students—this research offers fresh perspectives on the relationship between health perception and eHealth literacy in this specific context. These findings generate the hypothesis that students with suboptimal health could benefit from combined health‐management and digital‐literacy support. The effectiveness of embedding such training in nursing curricula should be examined in future longitudinal and intervention studies. These efforts are expected to foster a more capable and health‐conscious nursing workforce.
