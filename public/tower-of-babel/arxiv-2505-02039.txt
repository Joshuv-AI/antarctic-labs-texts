Spectral flow and Robin domains on metric graphs
Ram Band, Marina Prokhorova, and Gilad Sofer

Abstract
. This paper is devoted to the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian on a finite metric graph. We prove an index theorem relating the nodal deficiency of an eigenfunction f f with (1) the Morse index of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, (2) its positive index and the first Betti number of the graph. We then generalize this result, replacing nodal points of f f with its “Robin points” (these are points with a prescribed value of f ′ / f \nicefrac{{f^{\prime}}}{{f}} , known as the Robin parameter, or delta coupling, or cotangent of Prüfer angle). This provides the Robin count, a generalization of the nodal and Neumann counts of an eigenfunction. We relate the Robin count deficiency with the positive index of the Robin map (a generalization of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map). In addition, we show that two of the relevant indices are independent of the Prüfer angle. Our main tool is the spectral flow of the Laplacian with special families of boundary conditions. As an application of our results, we show that the spectral flow of these families is related to topological properties of the graph, such as its Betti number, the number of interaction vertices, and their positions with respect to the graph cycles.

These index theorems may also be classified according to the space on which the Laplacian acts:
a manifold, a metric graph, or a discrete graph.
Experience shows that results obtained for one of these structures

Nevertheless, there are currently two significant gaps within the theory:
the lack of index theorems for the nodal deficiency via the magnetic Laplacians on manifolds,

\displaystyle-d^{2}f/dx^{2}=\mu f\text{ on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle f\text{ satisfies Neumann-Kirchhoff conditions}\text{ at vertices }v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{B}.

Mixed traces.

For the rest of the paper, we fix an orientation on the graph, so that each edge is directed.
Given a function
f\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
and a point

at an edge

\tau_{0}f(x)=f(x)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\tau_{0}^{\prime}f(x)=f^{\prime}(x)

the value and derivative of

at

where the derivative
f^{\prime}
is taken
according to the orientation of

We generalize these two traces introducing an angle parameter

Namely, we define the pair of

\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}

as the rotation of the “usual” pair of Dirichlet and Neumann traces:

(1.8)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tau_{\alpha}\\
\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}\end{array}\right)\coloneqq T_{\alpha}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tau_{0}\\
\tau_{0}^{\prime}\end{array}\right),\text{ where }T_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{array}\right)

is the matrix of the rotation of the plain by an angle
\alpha
.
In other words,

\tau_{\alpha}f(x)=\cos(\alpha)f(x)-\sin(\alpha)f^{\prime}(x)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f(x)=\sin(\alpha)f(x)+\cos(\alpha)f^{\prime}(x).

For
\alpha=0
this provides the standard Dirichlet and Neumann traces.
For
\alpha=\pi/2
we obtain the negative Neumann trace and the Dirichlet trace:

\left(\tau_{\pi/2},\tau_{\pi/2}^{\prime}\right)=\left(-\tau_{0}^{\prime},\tau_{0}\right).

Generalization of the delta vertex condition.

Our proof of Theorem

loop of vertex boundary conditions, so called
\delta(t)

(it is also called Robin condition sometimes).
This condition at a vertex

is continuous at

The “coupling parameter”

an element of the one-point compactification

of
\mathbb{R}

The value

corresponds to the usual Dirichlet condition

Spectral flow.

Another tool in our proof of theorems above is the spectral flow,
which was first introduced by Atiyah, Patodi, and Singer in
[
6
]
.
Roughly speaking, the spectral flow counts (with signs) the number of eigenvalues passing through
zero from the start of the path to its end, see Fig.
1.3
.
The spectral flow has been used for proving index theorems
in a variety of different settings over past years,
and was first introduced for quantum graphs in
[
44
]
.

The Schrödinger operator on a finite graph has compact resolvent, so every shift of it is Fredholm.
For such operators, one can consider the spectral flow through an arbitrary spectral level
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
counting the number of eigenvalues passing through
\mu
; we will denote it
\spf_{\mu}
.
The spectral flow of a loop of operators does not depend on the choice of
\mu
:
it counts the number of positions the spectrum is shifted by when the parameter runs the loop.
In this case, we usually omit the subscript
\mu
and write
\spf
instead of
\spf_{\mu}
.
But if the ends of a path of operators do not coincide, then the choice of
\mu
matters.

In Section
3
we recall the definition of the spectral flow and some of its basic properties.
For a detailed exposition see
[
26
,
45
]

or a recent book
[
30
]
.
See also the paper
[
47
]
of the second author,
about the spectral flow in finite quantum graphs and some applications,
including properties of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
families and of their generalizations.

Self-adjointness.

In order to simplify computations, we denote the pair (column) of traces
(\tau_{\alpha},\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime})
by
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
,
so that

(2.1)

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}=T_{\alpha}\hat{\tau}_{0}\;\text{ and }\;\hat{\tau}_{0}=T_{\alpha}^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}=T_{-\alpha}\hat{\tau}_{\alpha},

where
T_{\alpha}
is the rotation matrix (
1.8
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Each operator
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
is self-adjoint.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
satisfy fixed self-adjoint boundary conditions
(say, Neumann-Kirchhoff conditions) at vertices not belonging to
\mathcal{B}
.
By the Green’s identity

\left\langle Hf,g\right\rangle_{\Gamma}-\left\langle f,Hg\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\left\langle f,g^{\prime}\right\rangle^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}-\left\langle f^{\prime},g\right\rangle^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}\right)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left\langle J\hat{\tau}_{0}f,\hat{\tau}_{0}g\right\rangle^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}},

where
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=T_{\pi/2}

and
|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}
denote the difference of the corresponding values at
v_{+}
and
v_{-}
,
so that
\tau_{\alpha}f|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}=\tau_{\alpha}f(v_{+})-\tau_{\alpha}f(v_{-})
and so on.
Substituting (
2.1
) in this formula
and taking into account that
T_{-\alpha}
is unitary and commutes with
J=T_{\pi/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle Hf,g\right\rangle_{\Gamma}-\left\langle f,Hg\right\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left\langle JT_{-\alpha}\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}f,T_{-\alpha}\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}g\right\rangle|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left\langle J\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}f,\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}g\right\rangle|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\left\langle\tau_{\alpha}f,\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}g\right\rangle^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}-\left\langle\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f,\tau_{\alpha}g\right\rangle^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}\right).

If
f
and
g
satisfy (
1.12
) at points of
\mathcal{B}
,
that is,
\tau_{\alpha}
-traces of
f
and
g
are continuous at these points,
then the last expression can be simplified:

(2.2)

\left\langle Hf,g\right\rangle_{\Gamma}-\left\langle f,Hg\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\left\langle\tau_{\alpha}f(v),\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}g|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f|^{v_{+}}_{v_{-}},\tau_{\alpha}g(v)\right\rangle\right).

If
f
and
g
additionally satisfy (
1.13
) at points of
\mathcal{B}
,
that is, belong to the domain of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
, then the last equality takes the form

\left\langle Hf,g\right\rangle_{\Gamma}-\left\langle f,Hg\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\left\langle\tau_{\alpha}f(v),t\cdot\tau_{\alpha}g(v)\right\rangle-\left\langle t\cdot\tau_{\alpha}f(v),\tau_{\alpha}g(v)\right\rangle\right)=0.

Hence the operator
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
is symmetric.
Since the boundary conditions have the right dimension,
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
is self-adjoint.
∎

Lower bounds.

It is well known that, for every individual boundary condition, a Hamiltonian on
\Gamma
is bounded from below.
We need an analogue of this property for our families of boundary conditions.

Proposition 2.2
.

For every angle
\alpha
and
\varepsilon>0
, the family
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{\overline{R}}
is uniformly bounded from below
on the complement of the interval
(0,\varepsilon)
if
\alpha\neq 0\;\mathrm{mod}\;\pi

and on the complement of the interval
(-\infty,-\varepsilon^{-1})
if
\alpha=0\;\mathrm{mod}\;\pi
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}
be the (closed symmetric) quadratic form associated with the self-adjoint operator
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
.
The lower bound of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
coincides with the lower bound of
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}
.
For an arbitrary boundary condition the associated quadratic form is given by

[
19
, thm. 1.4.11]
;
its domain is contained in the first Sobolev space

H^{1}(\Gamma)=\oplus_{e\in\mathcal{E}}H^{1}(e)
.
Since each
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
is bounded from below,
we can exclude any finite number of points, in particular
t=0,\infty
, from consideration.

Consider first the case
\alpha=0
.
For
t\neq\infty

(2.3)

\dom\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{t}=H^{1}(\Gamma)\cap C(\Gamma),\quad\quad\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{t}[f,f]=\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{\Gamma}+t\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left|f(v)\right|^{2},

so for
t\geq-\varepsilon^{-1}
the lower bound of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(t)
is greater or equal
than the lower bound of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(-\varepsilon^{-1})
.

Let now
\alpha\neq 0
.
For
t\neq 0
the Dirichlet part of vertex boundary conditions at
v\in\mathcal{B}
is trivial
and thus
\dom\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}=H^{1}(\Gamma)\cap C\left(\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{B}\right)
is independent of
t\neq 0
.
Writing the formula for
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}
from
[
49
, app. A]
in a more convenient form,
for
t\neq 0,\infty
we get

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}[f,f]=\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{\Gamma}+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\cot\alpha\left(\left|f(v_{+})\right|^{2}-\left|f(v_{-})\right|^{2}\right)-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{t\sin^{2}\alpha}\left|f(v_{+})-f(v_{-})\right|^{2},

so for
t\in(-\infty,0)\cup[\varepsilon,\infty)
the lower bound of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)

is greater or equal than the lower bound of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(\varepsilon)
.
This completes the proof of the proposition.

The spectral set

Proposition 2.5
.

The spectral set (

and possibly also some horizontal spectral curves

described by Proposition (

See

Note that (

Proof.

We follow the methods of the proof of
[

.
By Proposition
2.4
, a spectral curve

can be supposed to be analytic locally outside of a discrete set of exceptional values of

Excluding a discrete set of values does not influence our conclusions,
and we additionally exclude

By

one can choose eigenfunctions

In terms of the associated quadratic form,

(2.6)

\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}\left[f(t),g\right]=\left\langle Hf(t),g\right\rangle=\lambda(t)\left\langle f(t),g\right\rangle\text{ for every }g\in\dom\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}.

Let
\partial_{t}
denote the derivative with respect to

.
The domain of
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}
does not depend on

contains the derivative

Differentiating both sides of (

, we obtain

(2.7)

\left(\partial_{t}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}\right)\left[f,f\right]+\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}\left[\partial_{t}f,f\right]=\left(\partial_{t}\lambda\right)\left\langle f,f\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\partial_{t}f,f\right\rangle.

On the other hand, substituting

in (

\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}\left[f,\partial_{t}f\right]=\lambda\left\langle f,\partial_{t}f\right\rangle.

Taking (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) together, we obtain

(2.9)

\partial_{t}\lambda\cdot\left\|f\right\|^{2}=\left(\partial_{t}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}^{t}\right)\left[f,f\right].

Choosing a normalized eigenfunction
f=f(t)
(that is,
\left\|f\right\|=1
)
and substituting (
2.3
,
2.4
) in (

), we see that

\partial_{t}\lambda=\begin{cases}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left|f(v)\right|^{2}&\text{if }\alpha=0\\
\left(t\sin\alpha\right)^{-2}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\left|f(v_{+})-f(v_{-})\right|^{2}&\text{if }\alpha\neq 0\end{cases}

Both expressions in this formula are non-negative, so the spectral curve
\lambda(t)
is non-decreasing.
If
\partial_{t}\lambda>0
everywhere except some discrete set of points,
then
\lambda(t)
is a strictly increasing function.

Suppose now that
\partial_{t}\lambda=0
at some non-singular point
t_{0}\neq 0,\infty
.
For
\alpha=0
this implies
f(t_{0})\in\dom H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
.
For
\alpha\neq 0
this implies continuity of

at points
v\in\mathcal{B}
;
together with continuity of the
\tau_{\alpha}
-trace (
1.12
) this implies
continuity of the

and
\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f
at
v
, so from (
1.13
) we get
t_{0}\cdot\tau_{\alpha}f(v)=0

and thus
f(t_{0})\in\dom H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
as well.
Since

also belongs to the domain of
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t_{0})
,
by Proposition
2.3

\lambda(t)
is a horizontal spectral curve.
The levels of such horizontal spectral curves belong to the spectrum of

,
so the set consisting of these levels is discrete and bounded from below.
∎

3.
Spectral flow

Continuity properties.

In this paper we often deal with
families
of operators. For
this, we need an appropriate notion of continuity. For bounded operators
we always use the norm topology.

With a closed linear subspace
L
of a fixed (finite- or infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space
V

we associate the orthogonal projection
P_{L}\colon H\to H
with the range
L
.
The distance between two subspaces
L
and
L^{\prime}
of
V
is defined as the norm
\left\|P_{L}-P_{L^{\prime}}\right\|
.
This provides the so called
gap metric
and
gap topology
on the space of closed subspaces of
V
.
In other words, a family
L(t)\subset V
is said to be continuous
if the family
P(t)
of orthogonal projections onto
L(t)
is continuous.

With a closed operator in a Hilbert space we associate its graph.
The gap topology on subspaces then provides the so called
graph topology
on closed operators.
In other words, a family
S(t)
of closed operators is said to be
graph
continuous
if the family
P(t)
of orthogonal projections onto the
graphs of
S(t)
is continuous in the norm topology. The graph topology
is the most convenient in the context of differential operators with
boundary conditions.

If operators
S(t)
are self-adjoint, as is the case in this paper,
then graph continuity is equivalent to continuity in the uniform resolvent
sense, that is, norm continuity of the family
t\mapsto(S(t)+\lambda)^{-1}

for some (and then every) constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

Spectral flow.

The spectral flow is well defined for graph continuous families of self-adjoint Fredholm operators.
Let us briefly recall its definition and properties.
To simplify exposition, we consider only the case of operators with compact resolvents
(we deal only with such operators in this paper).

Let
S(t)
,
t\in[a,b]
be a graph continuous one-parameter family
of self-adjoint operators with compact resolvents
(so that each operator has a discrete real spectrum) and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.
One can always choose a partition
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{N}=b

and positive numbers
\varepsilon_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,N
such that

\mu+\varepsilon_{j}\notin\mathrm{Spec}\left(S(t)\right)\;\;\text{for}\;\;t\in\left[t_{j-1},t_{j}\right],j=1,\ldots,N.

Definition
.

The spectral flow of
S(t)
through
\mu
is defined by the formula

(3.1)

\spf_{\mu}(S(t))_{t\in[a,b]}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\rank\one_{[\mu,\,\mu+\varepsilon_{j}]}\left(S(t_{j})\right)-\rank\one_{[\mu,\,\mu+\varepsilon_{j}]}\left(S(t_{j-1})\right)\right),

where
t_{j}
and
\varepsilon_{j}
are as above,

\one_{X}(S)
denotes the spectral projection of the operator
S
corresponding to a subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}
,
and
\rank\one_{X}
is the rank of the projection (that is, the dimension of its range).
This sum does not depend on the choice of a partition and numbers
\varepsilon_{j}
.

For
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
the family
S(t)-\mu
is graph continuous if and only if
S(t)
is graph continuous, and

\spf_{\mu}(S(t))=\spf_{0}(S(t)-\mu).

Remark
.

The definition above is one of two standard versions of the spectral flow.
The alternative version is obtained by replacing closed intervals
\left[\mu,\mu+\varepsilon_{j}\right]

in (
3.1
) by semi-closed intervals
(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon_{j}]
.
These two versions of the spectral flow differ by the number

\dim\ker\left(S(b)-\mu\right)-\dim\ker\left(S(a)-\mu\right).

If these dimensions, that is, the multiplicities of
\mu
in
S(a)
and
S(b)
, coincide
(which is often the case in this paper),
then the two versions of the definition provide the same value of the spectral flow.
In particular, the spectral flow of a loop of operators is the same for both versions of the definition.

The spectral flow
\spf_{\mu}
for graph continuous families has the following well known properties
(in fact, it is defined by these properties and the normalization condition, see
[
45
]
):

(1)

If
\mu
lies in the resolvent set of each
S(t)
, then
\spf_{\mu}\left(S(t)\right)=0
;

(2)

The spectral flow is additive with respect to concatenation of paths;

(3)

The spectral flow is homotopy invariant in the class of loops, or paths with fixed ends.

Quantum graphs.

Let
v
be a vertex of
\Gamma
and
f\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
.
The boundary values
f_{e}(v)
,
e\in\mathcal{E}_{v}
form a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{E}_{v}}
,
and similarly for the derivatives
\nabla f_{e}(v)
.
A vertex boundary condition at

for a Schrödinger operator

is a linear
condition on these boundary values
f_{e}(v)
and
\nabla f_{e}(v)
,
that is, it is given by a subspace
L^{v}
of
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{E}_{v}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{E}_{v}}

A (local) boundary condition for

is given by a tuple of vertex boundary conditions
\left(L^{v}\right)_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
,
or equivalently by the subspace

L=\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{V}}{L^{v}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2d},\quad d=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\deg(v)=2\left|\mathcal{E}\right|.

We will denote the corresponding unbounded operator on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
by
H_{L}
.
The subspaces

should satisfy additional requirements which guarantee the self-adjointness
of the corresponding operator

, e.g. the dimension condition
\dim L^{v}=\left|\mathcal{E}_{v}\right|=\deg(v)
.

Remark
.

More generally, every subspace

of
\mathbb{C}^{2d}
determines a global boundary condition for
H
;
we will use such boundary conditions in the proof of Theorem
A
in the appendix.
Similarly to the local case,
L
should satisfy additional requirements
in order to guarantee self-adjointness of
H_{L}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The operator
H_{L}
depends graph continuously on the boundary condition
L\subset\mathbb{C}^{2d}
.

Proof.

Let
H_{\mathrm{max}}
and
H_{\mathrm{min}}=H_{\mathrm{max}}^{*}
be the maximal and minimal operators
corresponding to the differential expression
H
,
with
\dom(H_{\mathrm{max}})=H^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\dom(H_{\mathrm{min}})
being the set of functions

vanishing on the ends of all edges together with their first derivatives.
Let
G_{\mathrm{max}}
and
G_{\mathrm{min}}
be the graphs of

and
H_{\mathrm{min}}
correspondingly;
they are closed subspaces of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Gamma)\oplus L^{2}(\Gamma)
.
The quotient
\mathbb{C}^{2d}=\dom(H_{\mathrm{max}})/\dom(H_{\mathrm{min}})
can be identified, as a vector space,
with the quotient
G_{\mathrm{max}}/G_{\mathrm{min}}
.

The graph
G_{L}
of

is a closed subspace of
G_{\mathrm{max}}
;
it is obtained as the inverse image of

under the trace map

Since the trace map is surjective,

depends continuously of

as a closed subspace of

and thus also as a closed subspace of

.
∎

Let
L(t)
,

be a continuous family of self-adjoint boundary conditions for

.
By the proposition above, the family

is graph continuous, so its spectral flow through an arbitrary

is well defined.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that the family
H_{L(t)}
is uniformly bounded from below. Then

(3.2)

\spf_{\mu}\left(H_{L(t)}\right)_{t\in[a,b]}=\Mor\left(H_{L(a)}-\mu\right)-\Mor\left(H_{L(b)}-\mu\right)

for every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let us write
H_{t}
instead of
H_{L(t)}
for brevity.
Let

be less then both
\mu
and the common lower bound of
H_{t}
,

.
Choose
t_{j}
and
\varepsilon_{j}
,

For

Proposition 4.1
.

The Robin map
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{B}}(\mu)\colon\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{B}}

if and only if

Proof.

For
\xi=0

Let
\xi,\eta
be arbitrary vectors in

Proposition 4.2

Let
\mu\notin\mathrm{Spec}\left(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(\infty)\right)
and

(4.1)

\dim\ker\left(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)-\mu\right)=\dim\ker\left(\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{B}}(\mu)+t\right).

Proof.

Suppose that
\mu
is an eigenvalue of

\left(\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{B}}(\mu)\xi\right)(v)=\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f(v_{-})-\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}f(v_{+})=-t\cdot\tau_{\alpha}f(v)=-t\xi(v),

Conversely, assume that

Betti number.

The first Betti number (the number of independent cycles) of a connected
graph
\Gamma
is given by the formula

\beta(\Gamma)=\left|\mathcal{E}\right|-\left|\mathcal{V}\right|+1.

The last summand
1
in this formula stands for the “one connected component” of
\Gamma
;
for a non-connected graph, this summand should be replaced by the number of connected components.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a subset of
\mathcal{V}
including only vertices of degree two
and
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}=\overline{\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{B}}
be the graph obtained by cutting
\Gamma
at points of
\mathcal{B}
.
Cutting
\Gamma
at one vertex of degree two increases the number of vertices
by one and leaves the number of edges unchanged,
so
\left|\mathcal{V}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\right|=\left|\mathcal{V}\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
and
\left|\mathcal{E}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\right|=\left|\mathcal{E}\right|
. Therefore,

(5.1)

\beta(\Gamma)-\beta(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})=\left|\mathcal{B}\right|-\left|\pi_{0}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\right|+1,

where
\left|\pi_{0}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\right|
is the number of connected components of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}
.

If
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)
is the set of
\alpha
-Robin points of

,
then the connected components of the cutted graph

are
\alpha
-Robin domains of

, so

(5.2)

\beta(\Gamma)-\beta(\Gamma_{\alpha}^{f})=\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|-\nu_{\alpha}(f)+1.

Deformation at Robin points of an eigenfunction.

Let
(\lambda,f)

for

In this section we compare these two indices with the Morse indices of

6.
Differences of Dirichlet, Robin, and Neumann counts

Paths.

Let again
(\lambda,f)
be an eigenpair of the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H
.
There are two natural ways to connect the operator
H^{f}_{0}(\infty)=H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)

with the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H=H^{f}_{0}(0)
.
Both are given by the family
H^{f}_{0}(t)
, but in the first case
t
is running the interval
\left[-\infty,0\right]
,
while in the second case
t
is running the interval
\left[0,\infty\right]
in the
opposite direction
,
from
\infty
to
0
.
We write these paths symbolically as
H_{0}^{f}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H
and
H_{0}^{f}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H
.

Similarly, there are two natural ways to connect
H=H_{\alpha}^{f}(0)
with
H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)=H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)
:
both are given by the family
H_{\alpha}^{f}(t)
,
with
t
running either the interval
\left[0,\infty\right]
in the positive direction,
or the interval
\left[-\infty,0\right]
in the negative direction (from
0
to
-\infty
).
We write these paths as
H\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)
and
H\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)
.

Concatenating a path going from
H^{f}_{0}(\infty)
to
H
with a path going from
H
to
H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)
,
we obtain a path going from
H^{f}_{0}(\infty)
to
H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)
via
H
.
Every half of this combined path can be chosen from two alternatives,
so there are four natural choices for the combined path.
We write them as
H^{f}_{0}(\pm\infty)\dashrightarrow H\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\pm\infty)
(with the corresponding choice of signs),
or shorter as
H^{f}_{0}(\pm\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\pm\infty)
,
and depict them by arrows as shown on pictures below.

Spectral flow.

Suppose now that
(\lambda,f)

satisfies Assumption
1.2
and
\varepsilon>0
is small enough.
Taking into account the additivity of the spectral flow under concatenation of paths,
we see that Theorem
1.7
can be written as follows.

(6.1)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\right)=0,

(6.2)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda-\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)\right)=0.

Using these equalities, we can now easily compute the spectral flow
through
\lambda\pm\varepsilon
of the other three paths.
Indeed, in the combined path

H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H\dashrightarrow H^{f}_{0}(\infty)

the middle part

is contractible,
so (
6.3
) is homotopic to the path
H^{f}_{0}(t)
,

,
and the first part of Theorem
1.6
provides

\spf_{\mu}\left(H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\right)-\spf_{\mu}\left(H^{f}_{0}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\right)=\spf_{\mu}\left(H_{0}^{f}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[-\infty,\infty\right]}=\left|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f)\right|

for every

Similarly,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)\right)=-\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)\right)=\left|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f)\right|-\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|=\nu_{0}(f)-\nu_{\alpha}(f),

In a similar manner, (

together with the first part of Theorem

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda-\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(-\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\right)=\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda-\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(-\infty)\right)=-\left|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f)\right|,

(6.9)

\displaystyle\spf_{\lambda-\varepsilon}\left(H^{f}_{0}(\infty)\dashrightarrow H_{\alpha}^{f}(\infty)\right)=\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|-\left|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f)\right|=\nu_{\alpha}(f)-\nu_{0}(f).

7.
Spectral flow and Betti number

In this section we present two results, which relate the Betti number of the graph
with the spectral flow of appropriate operator families.

Deformation to Neumann conditions.

Let
\alpha=\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
and
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a set of degree two vertices.
The boundary condition
\delta_{\pi/2}^{v}(\infty)
means the vanishing of
f^{\prime}
at each side of
v
.
Therefore,
H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)=H(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})
is the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
on the graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}=\overline{\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{B}}
obtained by cutting
\Gamma
at points of
\mathcal{B}
.

The one-parameter family
H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(t)
,
t\in\left[0,\infty\right]

connects the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H=H(\Gamma)
with the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})
.
The following result relates the spectral flow of this family
(or equivalently the Morse index of the two-sided Neumann-to-Dirichlet operator)
with the number of cycles removed from
\Gamma
in the process of cutting.
See also Fig.
7.1
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
H
be the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian on
\Gamma
. Then

\Mor\left(\Lambda_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\varepsilon)\right)=\spf_{\varepsilon}\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[0,\infty\right]}=\beta(\Gamma)-\beta(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\;\text{ for }\varepsilon>0\text{ small enough}.

Proof.

The first equality is given by the second part of Theorem
1.6
,
so we only need to prove the second equality.
By Proposition
3.4
,

\spf_{\varepsilon}\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[-\infty,0\right]}=\Mor\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)-\varepsilon\right)-\Mor\left(H-\varepsilon\right).

The Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H
on the connected graph
\Gamma
is non-negative,
with one-dimensional kernel consisting of constant functions, so

\Mor\left(H-\varepsilon\right)=\dim\ker(H)=1.

The Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)=H(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})

on the graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}
is also non-negative;
its kernel consists of functions which are constant on connected components of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}
.
Hence

\Mor\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)-\varepsilon\right)=\dim\ker\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)\right)=\left|\pi_{0}\left(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right|,

the number of connected components of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}
.
Taking all this together, we obtain

\spf_{\varepsilon}\left(H_{\pi/2}^{\mathcal{B}}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[-\infty,0\right]}=\left|\pi_{0}\left(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right|-1.

Combining this formula with the first part of Theorem
1.6

and using the additivity of the spectral flow under concatenation, we obtain

\spf_{\varepsilon}\left(H_{\pi/2}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[0,\infty\right]}=\spf_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)_{t\in\left[-\infty,\infty\right]}-\spf_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)_{t\in\left[-\infty,0\right]}=\left|\mathcal{B}\right|-\left|\pi_{0}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}})\right|+1.

It remains to apply equality (
5.1
).
∎

.

Our next result expresses the Betti number of

Theorem 7.2
.

Let

be an eigenpair of the Neumann-Kirchhoff Laplacian
H
.
Suppose that
(\lambda,f)
satisfies Assumption
1.2
and the eigenvalue

\alpha
and

\spf_{\lambda}\left(H_{\alpha}^{f}(t)\right)_{t\in\left[-T,T\right]}=\beta(\Gamma).

, the family

from Proposition

) for

Hence, in addition to the constant spectral curve at the level

there are exactly
\nu_{\alpha}(f)-1

\lambda
at

.
The rest

\left|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(f)\right|-\left(\nu_{\alpha}(f)-1\right)=\beta(\Gamma)

of strictly increasing spectral curves intersect the level
\lambda
at values of parameter

,
that is,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since the number of intersections is finite,
all of them happen in the interval
t\in\left[-T,T\right]
for

large enough.

This appendix is devoted to the proof of the “spectral flow = winding number” formula
for loops of boundary conditions on a quantum graph.

Generalization of these results, as well as a more detailed exposition,

Unitary form of boundary conditions.

For a Hilbert space

\mathcal{U}(V)
the set of unitary operators
V\to V

and equip it with the norm topology.

A self-adjoint boundary condition for
H
at a vertex

(A.1)

i(U^{v}-1)\vec{\rule{0.0pt}{6.02773pt}\smash{f}}(v)+(U^{v}+1)\nabla\vec{\rule{0.0pt}{6.02773pt}\smash{f}}(v)=0,\;\;\text{where}\;\;U^{v}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{E}_{v}}),

is the vector of boundary values
f_{e}(v)
,
and
\nabla\vec{\rule{0.0pt}{6.02773pt}\smash{f}}(v)\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{E}_{v}}

\nabla f_{e}(v)
,
e\in\mathcal{E}_{v}

See

A (local) boundary condition for

or equivalently by the unitary operator

More generally, a

Winding number.

Let
U_{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
t\in S^{1}
be a loop of unitary operators.
Its
winding number
is defined as the degree of the composition

S^{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{U}\hskip 1.63885pt}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\det}\hskip 1.63885pt}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C})\cong S^{1}

and is denoted
\wind(U_{t})
.
Here
\det\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C})
is the determinant map
and
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C})=\left\{e^{i\alpha}\,:\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
is oriented in the direction of increasing of
\alpha
.
The winding number is a homotopy invariant of a loop and provides an isomorphism

(A.2)

\wind\colon\pi_{1}(\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d}))\to\mathbb{Z},

so two loops
S^{1}\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
with the same winding number are homotopic.

The following theorem is the main result of the appendix.
It could be deduced from
[
38
, Theorem 4.1]

or
[
48
, Theorem 3]
. However, it is instructive to give a proof for quantum graphs in their own language.

Theorem A
.

Let
H
be the Laplacian on a finite metric graph
\Gamma
, not necessarily connected.
Let
U_{t}
,
t\in S^{1}
be a loop of unitary operators in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and
H_{t}
be the corresponding loop of self-adjoint realizations of
H
.
Then the spectral flow of
H_{t}
is equal to the winding number of
U_{t}
:

(A.3)

\spf(H_{t})=\wind(U_{t}).

For local boundary conditions
U_{t}=\oplus_{v}U^{v}_{t}
, this can be written as

(A.4)

\spf(H_{t})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\wind(U^{v}_{t}).

Proof.

Since the determinant is multiplicative with respect to the direct sum of operators,

\wind(U_{t})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\wind(U^{v}_{t})
, so (
A.4
) follows from (
A.3
).

1. Let us first check the equality (
A.3
) for the graph consisting of one edge
e=[0,\ell]

and taken with the following loop of boundary conditions:

(A.5)

f^{\prime}(0)=t\cdot f(0),\quad f^{\prime}(\ell)=0,

where
t
runs the extended real line
\mathbb{\overline{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}\cong S^{1}

and
t=\infty
corresponds to the condition
f(0)=0
.
In other words, we take the
\delta(t)
condition
\nabla f=tf
at one vertex
and the fixed condition
\nabla f=0
at the other vertex.
In the unitary picture, the boundary condition at the first vertex is given by the unitary

U_{t}=(i-t)/(i+t)
.
The corresponding loop
\mathbb{\overline{R}}\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C})
makes one rotation in the positive direction,
so the right hand side of (
A.3
) is equal to
1
.
The spectrum of each unbounded operator in this family can be explicitly computed
and it can be easily seen that
zero eigenvalue exists only for the value
t=0
and has multiplicity
1
.
The corresponding spectral curve is a monotonously increasing function of
t
,
so it intersects zero spectral level at
t=0
in the positive direction.
Therefore, the spectral flow of this loop is equal to
1=\wind(U_{t})

and hence (
A.3
) is satisfied.

2. Let now
\Gamma
be an arbitrary graph with an arbitrary loop of boundary conditions.
If two loops in
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
have the same winding number
m
, then they are homotopic,
so by Proposition
3.1
the corresponding loops of Hamiltonians are also homotopic
and their spectral flows coincide.
Hence we only need to check (
A.3
) for
one
loop in
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

with the winding number
m
, for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For
m=0
take a constant loop, then both the spectral flow and winding number vanish.
For
m<0
, reverse the direction of the loop;
then the signs of both the spectral flow and the winding number are reversed.
Therefore, we can suppose that
m>0
.

Choose an edge
e\in\Gamma
, and let
\Gamma_{e}
be the subgraph of
\Gamma

obtained by removing the edge
e
.
Fix an arbitrary boundary condition at
\Gamma_{e}
and
the concatenation of
m
loops of boundary conditions (
A.5
) at
e
.
Let
U_{t}
and
H_{t}
be the corresponding loops of unitary operators in
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

and unbounded operators on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
.

Since the boundary conditions on
e
and
\Gamma_{e}
are independent one from another,
the loop
H_{t}
is in fact a loop of operators on the disjoint union
e\sqcup\Gamma_{e}
,
or equivalently the orthogonal sum of two loops of operators on
L^{2}(e)
and
L^{2}(\Gamma_{e})
.
The second loop is constant, so its spectral flow vanishes.
The first loop, on
L^{2}(e)
, is the concatenation of
m
copies of the loop described in (1).
Both the spectral flow and winding number are additive under direct sums and under concatenation of loops,
so

\spf(H_{t})=0+m\cdot 1=m=\wind(U_{t}),

and thus (
A.3
) holds for this loop of boundary conditions.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Another proof of the first part of Theorem
1.6

Let
U^{v}_{t}
be the unitary operator which determines the vertex boundary condition for
H^{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(t)
at
v
.
For
v\notin\mathcal{B}
the loop
U^{v}_{t}
is constant, so its winding number vanishes.
For
v\in\mathcal{B}
the loop
U^{v}_{t}
corresponds to the
\delta_{\alpha}(t)
loop of boundary conditions at
v
.
We claim that its winding number is equal to
1
;
then (
A.4
) provides

\spf(H_{t})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}1=|\mathcal{B}|.

In order to prove the claim, note first that
\delta_{\alpha}(t)
condition depends continuously on
(\alpha,t)
,
so the homotopy type of the loop
\mathbb{\overline{R}}\ni t\mapsto U^{v}_{t}
does not depend on
\alpha
.
Hence it is enough to compute the winding number of
U^{v}_{t}
in the simplest case, for
\alpha=0
.
In this case, we have the
\delta(t)
boundary condition, which can be written as follows:

(A.6)

A_{t}\begin{pmatrix}f_{+}\\
f_{-}\end{pmatrix}+B_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f_{+}\\
\nabla f_{-}\end{pmatrix}=0,\;\;\text{where}\;\;A_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&-t\end{pmatrix},\;B_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

(here we denoted
f_{\pm}=f(v_{\pm})
for brevity;
recall that
\nabla f_{+}=f^{\prime}_{+}
and
\nabla f_{-}=-f^{\prime}_{-}
).
The unitary operator
U^{v}_{t}
is then defined by the formula
U^{v}_{t}=-(A_{t}-iB_{t})^{-1}(A_{t}+iB_{t})
,
see
[
19
, proof of thm. 1.4.4]
.
Substituting our values of
A_{t}
and
B_{t}
, we obtain

U^{v}_{t}=-\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
i&-t-i\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
i&-t+i\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{t+2i}\begin{pmatrix}-t&2i\\
2i&-t\end{pmatrix},

so
\det U^{v}_{t}=(t-2i)/(t+2i)
.
When
t
runs
\mathbb{\overline{R}}
in the positive direction,
the complex number
(t-2i)/(t+2i)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C})
makes one revolution in the counter-clockwise direction.
Therefore,
\wind(U^{v}_{t})=1
.
This proves our claim and thus the first part of Theorem
1.6
.
∎
