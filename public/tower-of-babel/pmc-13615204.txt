The Patient Experience of Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein Antibody-Associated Disease Post-Diagnosis: Results from an International Survey
Authors: Jonathan D. Santoro, Gabrielle deFiebre, Julia Lefelar, Panayotes Demakakos, Danielle Hartigh, Magali Periquet, Khaleda Ahmadyar, Zara Morrison, Jacqueline Palace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionData on the post-diagnosis experience of individuals with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) and their caregivers are limited. We explored post-diagnosis treatment experience and satisfaction with care in individuals with MOGAD and associated caregiver burden.MethodsAn online 57-question survey was distributed internationally to adults (aged ≥ 16 years) or caregivers assisting an adult or answering on behalf of a child (aged < 16 years) from October to December 2024.ResultsOverall, 261 responses were collected (adults, 219; children, 42) across 27 countries. Of those prescribed a preventative treatment, 43% of adults (n = 71/167) and 42% of children (n = 13/31) stopped treatment; common reasons were side effects and lack of efficacy. Most adults (73%) and children (64%) reported difficulties accessing/trying to access MOGAD treatment and care. Substantial dissatisfaction was reported for chronic symptom management for adults (35%) and psychological/emotional support for children (31%). Compared with individuals with MOGAD (N = 261), caregivers (of adults, n = 61; of children, n = 42) were significantly more likely to report mental health impacts (26% vs 92%, respectively; p < 0.001).ConclusionPersistent unmet care and support needs exist for individuals with MOGAD. Treatments were often stopped/changed, highlighting the need for more efficacious, tolerable, and approved therapies. Caregivers experience significant mental health impacts; improved support is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40120-026-01010-1.

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) is a rare, inflammatory demyelinating central nervous system disease, for which the first diagnostic criteria were published in 2023 [1]. The annual incidence and prevalence of MOGAD are estimated to be 3.4–4.8 people per million and 1.3–2.5 per 100,000, respectively [2]. Increased disease awareness and use of MOG antibody assays will likely increase the number of people identified as having MOGAD [2, 3].

MOGAD is distinct from diseases such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) and multiple sclerosis (MS), although symptomatic overlap can make diagnosis of MOGAD challenging in both adult and pediatric populations [4]. Clinical features of MOGAD often include optic neuritis (ON), transverse myelitis, and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) or brain lesions [1], although these can differ between adult and pediatric populations [5–8]. Diagnostic trajectory, relapse pattern, treatment decision-making, dependency on caregivers, and support needs also differ between adults and children with MOGAD [6, 8].

To date, limited studies have investigated MOGAD from the patient perspective and disease burden has not been well researched. Previously, authors from this group distributed a survey to 204 patients and their caregivers, and reported that diagnosis of MOGAD is often delayed, affecting patients’ physical health and quality of life (QoL) [9]. Additional survey/interview studies have highlighted the underappreciated burden of MOGAD and reported impacts across health-related QoL (HRQoL) domains [10–12]. Research into the caregiver burden of MOGAD is also limited.

This study was designed to address these knowledge gaps by exploring post-diagnosis treatment experience and satisfaction with care in individuals with MOGAD, and the associated caregiver burden.

Methods

Survey Development

The survey was co-created by neurologists, The MOG Project, the Siegel Rare Neuroimmune Association (SRNA), and UCB. The MOG Project and the SRNA are patient advocacy organizations that provide education and support for individuals with MOGAD and related rare neuroimmune disorders, as well as their caregivers and families.

The survey was not designed as a formally psychometrically validated instrument. Rather, content validity was supported by iterative development involving neurologists with MOGAD expertise, patient advocacy organizations, industry partners, literature review insights, behavioral science models, validated scales where applicable, and patient advocacy group workshops. Survey items were reviewed for clarity, relevance, acceptability, and coverage of key domains including diagnostic experience, treatment access, treatment satisfaction, chronic symptoms, support service utilization, and caregiver impact.

The final survey included 57 questions (multiple-choice, multiple-response, and free-text) and took approximately 30 min to complete; no questions were compulsory (full survey in the Supplement). The survey was distributed from October to December 2024 via the social media platforms of The MOG Project and the SRNA; to increase international reach, the survey was made available in English, French, and Spanish. Responses were collected using QuestionPro.

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Children’s Hospital Los Angeles and the University of Southern California (reference number CHLA-21-01922). The study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants gave informed consent, including consent for their anonymized, aggregated data to be shared as research findings.

Participants

Survey participants were individuals with MOGAD aged ≥ 16 years old (‘adults’), or caregivers either assisting an adult (aged ≥ 16 years; included in the ‘adults’ group) to complete the survey or answering on behalf of a pediatric patient (aged < 16 years; ‘children’). Throughout this paper, ‘participants’ refers to people with MOGAD (adults or children), even if the information was provided by a caregiver answering on their behalf. Some questions asked caregivers to provide information about their own experience; the term ‘caregivers’ is used when referring to these responses. Clinician confirmation of MOGAD diagnosis was not collected as the survey was anonymous and distributed internationally through patient advocacy organizations rather than through treating clinicians or a clinical registry.

Statistical Analysis

Anonymized data were cleaned and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 and SAS. Adult and pediatric populations were analyzed separately. Conventional content and thematic analyses were used to analyze free-text responses. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to (1) compare the post-diagnosis experience of patients diagnosed before and after the 2023 MOGAD diagnostic criteria [1] were published, and (2) to investigate the mental health burden on patients and their caregivers. Pre- and post-2023 diagnostic criteria analyses were primarily descriptive and were interpreted cautiously due to optional item-level responses and small subgroup sizes, particularly among children.

Results

Participants

In total, 261 responses were received from 219 adults (84%) and 42 caregivers (41 parents, 1 non-parent) on behalf of children (16%) across 27 countries. Most participants were female (adults: n = 172/219, 79%; children: n = 26/42, 62%), and approximately half were from the USA (adults: n = 124/219, 57%; children: n = 21/42, 50%) (Table 1). Most had received a confirmed MOGAD diagnosis since 2020, and approximately 50% since 2023 (when the diagnostic criteria were published [1]); mean time from first symptoms to diagnosis was 4.1 years for adults and 0.5 years for children (Table 2). Among adults, the most common comorbidity was mental health disorder (n = 63/219, 29%); in children, ‘no other health issues’ was most commonly selected (n = 20/42, 48%), followed by ‘other’ (n = 9/42, 21%) (Table 1). Most participants had relapsing MOGAD (referred to as “relapsing/multiphasic MOGAD” in the survey question); many had experienced ≥ 2 relapses since diagnosis (adults: n = 68/113, 60%; children: n = 13/20, 65%), with 23% of adults (n = 28/124) and 18% of children (n = 4/22) having experienced ≥ 5 (Fig. 1). ON was the most common attack phenotype for both adults (n = 173/219, 79%) and children (n = 31/42, 74%). ADEM or brain/brainstem attack was more prominent in children (n = 24/42, 57%) than in adults (n = 37/219, 17%) (Table 2). Symptom Burden

Vision loss/eye pain was the most impactful MOGAD symptom experienced by adults (n = 87/171, 51%) and children (n = 18/30, 60%) (Fig. 2). During attacks, adults and children were more likely to experience all surveyed symptoms to a severe degree compared with between attacks (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). The largest numerical increases in symptom severity were seen in adults for changes in vision, eye pain, and headaches, and in children for changes in vision, headaches, and fatigue. Although less severe than during attacks, participants also experienced symptoms between attacks, with fatigue, weakness/physical tiredness, and changes in vision being the top three symptoms in adults and children (based on a combined percentage of moderate and severe symptoms) (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d).Fig. 2Most impactful symptoms. Most adults had been hospitalized due to MOGAD (Supplementary Fig. 2a), with 9% (n = 20/219) hospitalized ≥ 5 times since diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Nearly all children (n = 41/42, 98%) had been hospitalized due to MOGAD (Supplementary Fig. 2c), with approximately a fifth hospitalized ≥ 5 times (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Treatment Access and Decision-Making

Most participants reported difficulties accessing/trying to access MOGAD and care; the most common difficulty was delayed diagnosis/misdiagnosis (adults: n = 86/219, 39%; children: n = 14/42, 33%; Fig. 3). There was a numerically lower frequency of reported access difficulties among participants diagnosed after publication of the 2023 diagnostic criteria [1], although participants still reported difficulties accessing care (adults with no difficulties: 24.5% pre-publication vs 30.1% post-publication; children with no difficulties: 29.4% pre-publication vs 40.0% post-publication); however, these exploratory subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. Participants wanted to be included in treatment decision-making, with 92% of adults (n = 201/219) always wanting to be involved. In practice, 70% of adults (n = 154/219) felt always involved, with 15% (n = 32/219) often involved. Only around half of adults (n = 111/219, 51%) felt their wishes were completely considered when treatment decisions were made.

Preventative Treatment Experience

Of 219 adults, 52 (24%) had never been prescribed a preventative medication (n = 26/52, 50% were newly diagnosed; n = 26/52, 50% were on ‘watch and wait’). Similarly for children, 26% (n = 11/42) had never been prescribed preventative treatment (n = 4/11, 36% were newly diagnosed; n = 7/11, 64% were on ‘watch and wait’) (Table 3). Of the remaining participants who had been prescribed preventative treatment (adults: n = 167/219, 76%; children: n = 31/42, 74%), 57% of adults (n = 96/167) and 58% of children (n = 18/31) were taking the medication originally prescribed, whereas 43% of adults (n = 71/167) and 42% of children (n = 13/31) had been prescribed treatment but had since stopped taking it (data not collected on whether participants switched treatments or stopped entirely; long-term treatment may not have been required) (Table 3, Fig. 1). Treatment experience

Approximately a quarter of adults (n = 42/166, 25%) and a third of children (n = 10/31, 32%) who were taking preventative treatment had a longest stable period without relapse of less than 1 year, whereas approximately a quarter of both adults (n = 40/166, 24%) and children (n = 8/31, 26%) had not yet experienced a relapse while receiving preventative treatment (Table 3).

Participants were asked to select which preventative treatments they were currently taking (it was possible to select multiple); 191 treatments were selected by the 159 adults who responded. The most common current treatment was intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg; n = 54/191, 28%), followed by B cell-depleting therapy (n = 39/191, 20%). For children, 28 participants responded and selected 36 current treatments; the most common were IVIg (n = 21/36, 58%) and prednisone (n = 5/36, 14%) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Most adults (63%) were very satisfied/satisfied with their current preventative treatment, whereas 38% were neutral/unsatisfied/very unsatisfied. Current treatment was reported as very good/good for most children (68%) (Table 3).

Among adults and children, it was most common that the patient’s neuroimmunologist/neurologist expert in MS or MOGAD made the decision to switch to their current treatment (adults: n = 43/71, 61%; children: n = 9/12, 75%). Only 7% of adults (n = 5/71) reported that it was their own decision. The most common reason for participants’ most recent switch was relapse (adults: n = 26/70, 37%; children: n = 6/11, 55%).

The most common previous preventative treatment was oral prednisone for adults (68 participants reporting 120 treatments; n = 36/120, 30%) and children (13 participants reporting 22 treatments; n = 6/22, 27%) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Reasons given for previous treatments being ranked as poor/very poor included “it caused side effects (had a bad reaction)” and “it was ineffective (relapsed)”; reasons given for previous treatments being ranked as good/very good included “it was effective at managing symptoms”, “it had relatively few/no side effects for me”, and “it was easy to take” (Fig. 4). In general, severe symptoms were reported in a low proportion of adults (1–29%) and children (0–11%) after starting preventative treatment; however, some continued to experience severe fatigue and weakness/physical tiredness. Treatment Goals

Among adults (n = 165), the top three treatment goals in order of importance were to reduce the frequency of relapse, reduce the severity of relapse, and prevent long-term disability. Among children (n = 31), these were to reduce the severity of relapse, reduce the frequency of relapse, and prevent long-term disability. Participants were also asked to specify what their single most important treatment goal was; among adults and children, this was to prevent relapse (n = 59/124, 48% and n = 14/23, 61%, respectively). Free-text responses reinforced the importance of avoiding relapse and staying stable, preventing long-term disability, and improving QoL/returning to ‘normal’.

Satisfaction with Care and Support Services

In total, 70% of adults (n = 152/217) and 83% of children (n = 34/41) were reported to be satisfied/very satisfied with their treatment management; however, 35% of adults (n = 71/202) were dissatisfied/very dissatisfied with their chronic symptom management (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Satisfaction with treatment management and chronic symptom management was similar regardless of diagnosis pre- or post-publication of the 2023 diagnostic criteria [1] (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Most participants were not satisfied with the psychological/emotional, practical, or financial support services received for MOGAD (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Open communication with family, friends, and caregivers was the emotional support most often utilized by adults (n = 139/217, 64%) and children (n = 32/40, 80%); most other services were used infrequently (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Among adults and children, there was low awareness of available financial support services, and a substantial proportion were not using these services as they deemed them unnecessary. Support for prescription medication costs was the financial support service most often utilized and found helpful by adults (n = 82/214, 38%) and children (n = 13/41, 32%) (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). There was limited utilization of practical support services (which included medication delivery and personal care assistance); most participants were either unaware of the services/not offered them, or felt that they were unnecessary (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). Online forums were the social support service most often used by adults (n = 133/217, 61%) and children and their caregivers (n = 26/41, 63%); many participants felt that other social support services were unnecessary or were unaware of them (Supplementary Fig. 8g, h). Free-text responses regarding the type of support that participants felt had been most helpful to their recovery highlighted the range of services being utilized, although adult participants placed particular emphasis on the usefulness of support groups, patient advocacy groups, online information, and friends and family.

Caregiver Burden

Approximately half (n = 112/219, 51%) of adults reported not having a caregiver; 37% (n = 82/219) were cared for by their spouse/partner, and 8% (n = 18/219) by another family member. Children were almost always cared for by a parent (n = 41/42, 98%), with one having a professional caregiver (n = 1/42, 2%).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest patient survey to investigate MOGAD burden, providing important insights from patients and caregivers. Most participants had experienced relapses and been hospitalized, encountered difficulties when trying to access care and treatment, and underutilized support services. Patient demographic and disease profiles, therapeutic interventions, and the disability status of individuals surveyed matched those previously reported in the literature for both adults and children [13, 14].

The survey responses characterize the unmet needs related to symptom management. Despite preventative treatment, participants reported that symptoms persist between attacks (particularly fatigue and weakness/physical tiredness). Around one-third of adults were dissatisfied with their chronic symptom management. Moreover, despite preventative treatment, participants continued to relapse and be hospitalized. Approximately a quarter of adults and a third of children reported their longest stable period on preventative treatment without relapse as being < 1 year. Previous treatments were often ranked as poor/very poor due to side effects and lack of efficacy, resulting in relapse; despite this, most participants who were taking a preventative treatment were currently satisfied. This finding likely reflects the distinction between prior and current treatment experience. Participants may have discontinued earlier therapies because of inefficacy, relapse, tolerability or side effects, yet reported greater satisfaction after switching to a regimen perceived as more effective, better tolerated, or more aligned with their treatment goals. Satisfaction may also reflect trust in the treating clinician and the limited availability of approved MOGAD-specific therapies, rather than complete disease control. Reducing the frequency and severity of relapses were important treatment goals for participants, highlighting the need for more efficacious, tolerable, and approved therapies to address these unmet needs, improve outcomes, and reduce disease burden.

The diagnosis and treatment of rare diseases are often associated with issues such as low disease awareness and insufficient specialists [15]. Patients with rare diseases often face diagnostic delays driven by a multitude of factors, including patients discounting their symptoms, clinicians underestimating the scale/impact of symptoms, and the degree of travel necessary [16, 17]. In this study, many participants were newly diagnosed, which skewed results for the mean time from first symptoms to confirmed diagnosis (adults, 4.1 years; children, 0.5 years). A previous study conducted by authors from this group found that 60.6% of patients received a MOGAD diagnosis within 6 months of their first MOGAD-related health problems, whereas 14.3% waited ≥ 7 years between first symptoms and diagnosis [9]. The challenges patients faced throughout the MOGAD diagnostic pathway (e.g., seeing a median of four doctors before diagnosis) were associated with increased frustration and physical impairment, and reduced HRQoL [9]. Another survey study of 74 neurologists and their patients with MOGAD found that median physician-reported time from symptom onset to preliminary and definitive diagnosis was 19.0 and 64.0 days, respectively [12]. Patients experienced suboptimal disease management, which affected their QoL and ability to work [12]. Further, the shorter mean time to diagnosis among children may reflect differences in clinical presentation and healthcare pathways. Pediatric MOGAD more commonly presents with ADEM or brain/brainstem attacks [8, 18], which may prompt hospitalization, neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid evaluation, and earlier neuroimmunologic testing. In this cohort, nearly all children had been hospitalized due to MOGAD. In addition, many of the children included in our study were diagnosed more recently, during a period of increasing clinician awareness and broader availability of MOG antibody testing. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, however, as diagnostic delay was self-reported and may be influenced by recall bias and cohort composition.

Study participants faced challenges when accessing/trying to access care, often due to delayed diagnosis/misdiagnosis or limited access to MOGAD specialists. This remained true among those diagnosed after the 2023 diagnostic guidelines were published [1]. These findings reinforce the need for improved MOGAD awareness among clinicians and a refined diagnostic pathway. Participants were frequently unaware of available support services; efforts should be made to improve awareness of, and access to, these services.

Caregivers experienced broad impacts and significant mental health burden, with similar reports in studies of caregivers of people with NMOSD. In one study, caregivers provided a mean of 40.3 h of care per week, which negatively affected travel/leisure activities, financial savings, and career advancement; additionally, 58% reported difficulty fulfilling work responsibilities [19]. In another NMOSD study, caregivers experienced substantial psychological burden, which was linked to a lack of post-diagnosis support and mistrust of medical professionals [20]. Rare diseases are generally associated with substantial caregiver burden, particularly when treatment options are limited and standards of care are not yet well established; interventions to improve QoL and productivity for caregivers are therefore required [21], as supported by our results. These findings support incorporation of caregiver assessment into routine MOGAD care. Practical models may include structured screening of caregiver needs at clinic visits, routine assessment of caregiver mental health, early referral to social work when burden is identified, education regarding relapse recognition and treatment navigation, and facilitated connection to peer support, financial assistance, and practical support services. Such approaches may be particularly important for caregivers of children, who reported broad impacts on emotions, family life, work, and social life.

Of interest, many participants were unsure whether they had relapsing or monophasic MOGAD (despite the survey providing definitions), suggesting a lack of disease awareness. We report that 92% of adults always wanted to be included in treatment decisions, suggesting this was a cohort of patients who wanted to be engaged. Improved patient–clinician communication may improve disease awareness and bolster trust in healthcare teams to manage MOGAD effectively.

Together, the unmet needs identified in this study suggest that MOGAD care should extend beyond relapse prevention. Clinical care pathways could incorporate routine screening for fatigue, weakness, pain, vision-related disability, mood symptoms, and functional impairment between attacks. Depending on patient needs, referrals to neuro-ophthalmology, rehabilitation therapies, pain management, psychology or psychiatry, and social work may help address persistent symptoms and psychosocial burden. In addition, clinicians and patient organizations may play complementary roles in improving awareness of available psychological, financial, practical, and peer support services.

This study had some limitations. Around half of the participants were from the USA (as many participants were recruited via the USA-based patient organizations The MOG Project and SRNA), and the sample was predominantly made up of white individuals, although 27 countries were represented in the sample. Selection bias is likely as participants were recruited through patient advocacy organizations and social media platforms. Individuals engaged with these organizations may be more likely to have recurrent or severe disease, higher health literacy, greater healthcare access, or stronger motivation to participate in research. In addition, although the survey was offered in three languages, individuals who did not read English, French, or Spanish were less likely to participate, limiting generalizability to other linguistic and geographic populations. Distribution of the survey in further languages may have improved participation. In addition, most participants were recently diagnosed, and results to some questions (e.g., “What is the longest period you have been stable on a preventative treatment for MOGAD prior to a relapse?”) may be underestimated due to short disease duration. The survey’s length and lack of financial compensation may have dissuaded participants, and longitudinal recall of prior experience (sometimes years) may have introduced recall bias. Results were also calculated based on the number of questions answered. The analysis does not include a summary of missing data, neither does it specifically capture how many unanswered questions were apportioned to non-compulsory survey questions. Finally, MOGAD diagnosis, disease characteristics, and treatment were self-reported without clinician confirmation, making diagnostic misclassification possible. This limitation is partially mitigated by recruitment through MOGAD-specific patient organizations, but the absence of antibody assay confirmation, medical record review, or clinician verification should be considered when interpreting our findings. It should also be noted that we did not collect a systematic breakdown of prior diagnoses, including prior MS diagnosis. Our study was not designed to compare the effectiveness of acute relapse treatments and, as such, we did not systematically collect detailed acute relapse treatment data, such as corticosteroid dose, intravenous versus oral steroid use, plasma exchange, IVIg given acutely, timing of therapy, relapse severity, or objective treatment response; this would be a valuable area for future studies to explore.

Conclusion

Multiple unmet care and support needs persist for patients with MOGAD, and patients continue to experience challenges and delays when accessing treatment. Participants are often unaware of support services available to them; therefore, improved access to and awareness of these services are needed. Treatments are often stopped or changed, highlighting the need for more efficacious, tolerable, and approved therapies. Caregivers experience significant mental health impacts, and thus, improved support is needed. Reducing the frequency and severity of relapses and preventing future relapses are important treatment goals, which would decrease disease burden for people with MOGAD and their caregivers.
