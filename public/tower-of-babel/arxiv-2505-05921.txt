Functional limit theorems and phase transition for step reinforced random walks with regularly varying memory
Aritra Majumdar, Krishanu Maulik

Abstract
. We study a class of generalized step reinforced random walks with regularly varying memory. At every step, the walker chooses a step from the past with probability proportional to a given regularly varying sequence, called the memory sequence. Then it either repeats the chosen step with probability p p or uses an innovation with probability 1 − p 1-p . Under finite variance assumption of the innovation steps, we identify a phase transition for the suitably normalized walk, from functional weak convergence to a Gaussian process to almost sure and L 2 L^{2} convergence to a process, which is a random multiple of a power law function. The phase transition is based on the boundedness of the quadratic variation process of a martingale driving the growth of the walk. We show that the traditional exponential timescale and time-dependent spatial normalization in the critical regime are not universal for all memory sequences. A linear timescale is universal in the critical regime, but there is no universal spatial normalization. The spatial normalization can be smaller, larger or of the same order as the traditional spatial normalization n ​ log ⁡ n \sqrt{n\log n} , observed in the literature. We also provide functional law of large numbers for the linearly normalized process, viewed at a linearly scaled time. The convergence is almost sure and in L 1 L^{1} under the assumption of a finite first moment of the innovation steps. A stronger finite variance assumption gives us L 2 L^{2} convergence. We use the functional law of large numbers and fluctuation results, obtained for the step reinforced walk to prove similar results for the corresponding barycenter process.

1.
Introduction

Usual random walks consider an i.i.d. sequence of
innovation
random variables
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
as the step sizes. We assume the innovations to have finite mean, which will be taken as
0
without loss of generality. However, such walks do not account for memory effect and anomalous growth.
Step Reinforced Random Walks (SRRW)
was introduced to consider these issues. The dynamics of an SRRW is described as follows. The first step is the innovation step:
X_{1}=\xi_{1}
. At each subsequent epoch
n+1
, with
n\geq 1
, we select a past epoch
\beta_{n+1}
, randomly among
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The variables
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
are called the
recollection epochs
. With probability
p
, the walker recalls and repeats the step taken at the epoch
\beta_{n+1}
; else it takes a fresh innovation step
\xi_{n+1}
. We denote the indicator for the successful recall as
\alpha_{n+1}
and call
p
as the
memory parameter
. Then, the step at the
(n+1)
-th epoch is given by

{}X_{n+1}=\alpha_{n+1}X_{\beta_{n+1}}+(1-\alpha_{n+1})\xi_{n+1}.

(1.1)

The corresponding walk is given by
S_{0}=0
and
S_{n}=S_{n-1}+X_{n}
, for
n\geq 1
. The random variables
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p
. We assume
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
,
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
and
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
to be independent of each other.

Traditionally, the recollection epoch
\beta_{n+1}
is chosen uniformly over the past epochs
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Bertenghi and Laulin
[
3
]
introduced a generalization, where the law of the chosen epoch
\beta_{n}
is proportional to a regularly varying sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
of index
\gamma>-1
. The sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
will be called the
memory sequence
. The probability mass function of the recollection epoch
\beta_{n+1}
is given by
\mathbb{P}(\beta_{n}=k)={\mu_{k}}/{\nu_{n}}
, for
1\leq k\leq n
,
where the sequence
\{\nu_{n}\}
is given by

\nu_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}
. We call the corresponding random walk, whose increments are given by (
1.1
), a
Step Reinforced Random Walk Regularly Varying Memory (SRRW-RVM)
.

For
p=0
, i.e. in the absence of any recollection, we recover the usual mean zero random walk with independent and identically distributed increments. For the other extreme value of the memory parameter
p=1
, the walk grows linearly with the random slope
X_{1}=\xi_{1}
, namely
S_{n}=n\xi_{1}
. Hence, we shall rule out these two trivialities and consider only the values of the memory parameter
p\in(0,1)
. The asymptotic behavior of the walk is easier to analyze when the innovations are symmetric Rademacher random variables. In that case, the SRRW-RVM will be referred to as the
SRRW-R-RVM
. When the memory sequence is constant, the recollection epochs are uniformly distributed over the past epochs recovering the traditional SRRW. Further, by a suitable reparametrization of the memory parameter, one recovers the
Elephant Random Walk (ERW)
introduced by Schütz and Trimper
[
19
]
, from the SRRW-R-RVM. In
[
14
]
, the author considered a particular choice of memory sequence given by

{}\mu_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1+{\gamma}/{i}\right)

(1.2)

, symmetric Rademacher innovations and reparametrized memory parameter corresponding to the ERW.

Bertenghi and Laulin
[
3
]
established a law of large numbers and functional central limit theorem for the walk when
\widehat{p}<p<p_{c}
and
\gamma\geq 0
. In this case, the walk is diffusive. The present article extends this analysis to the remaining parameter regimes and, most importantly, develops a general theory for the critical case
p=p_{c}
. At criticality, the slowly varying part of the memory sequence determines whether the normalized walk has a Gaussian weak limit or an almost-sure random limit and determines the spatial normalization.

For the memory sequence given by (
1.2
)
[
14
]
established a phase transition at the critical value
p_{c}=(\gamma+1/2)/(\gamma+1)
of the memory parameter, in the framework of ERW. For the subcritical regime
0\leq p<p_{c}
, the walk is diffusive with a Gaussian process limit. The covariance kernel of the limiting process has a removable singularity at
\widehat{p}=\gamma/(\gamma+1)
, which was not treated separately in
[
14
]
. For this special power-law memory, the critical regime was also studied in
[
14
]
, including almost-sure upper-order behaviour and a functional limit under an accelerated timescale. Our critical results are different in scope: we treat arbitrary regularly varying memory sequences and show that its slowly varying component can determine both the critical normalization and the mode of convergence.

1.1.
Main contributions of the present work

The present article develops a law of large numbers and fluctuation theory for the SRRW-RVM introduced in
[
3
]
, covering all values of memory parameter
p\in[0,1]
and all regularly varying memory sequences
\{\mu_{n}\}
with index
\gamma>-1
.

Under the additional assumption of finite second moment of the innovation sequence, we obtain the critical value of the memory parameter
p_{c}
. The critical regime provides a bridge between Gaussian fluctuation behavior in the subcritical phase and almost sure and
L^{2}
random limiting behavior in the supercritical phase. However, in the critical regime, depending on the memory sequence, either behavior may occur. We define a martingale sequence

M_{n}=a_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\mu_{k},\quad n\geq 1,

(1.3)

where the sequence
\{a_{n}\}
is regularly varying of index
-p(\gamma+1)
(see Theorem 4 of
[
8
]
), given by
a_{1}=1
and

a_{n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1+p\frac{\mu_{i+1}}{\nu_{i}}\right)^{-1},\quad\text{for $n\geq 2$}.

(1.4)

The martingale sequence
\{M_{n}\}
controls the asymptotic growth of the SRRW-RVM. The normalization
\{v_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}\}
for the asymptotic variance of this martingale sequence is regularly varying of index
2\gamma+1-2p(\gamma+1)
and determines the phase transition between two modes of convergence, including the limiting behavior in the critical regime. Note that, by Karamata’s theorem,
\{v_{n}\}
has positive index of regular variation and is unbounded for the subcritical regime
p<p_{c}
, while it has negative index of regular variation and is bounded for the supercritical regime
p>p_{c}
. For unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
, the normalized SRRW-RVM converges weakly to a Gaussian process, while when
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded, the normalized SRRW-RVM converges almost surely and in
L^{2}
to a process which is a nondegenerate random scalar multiple of a deterministic power law function. In the critical regime, the limiting process is a scalar random multiple of the square root function irrespective of the sequence
\{v_{n}\}
being bounded or not. Whenever
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded, the scalar random variable is Gaussian, while it need not be so when
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded. The sequence
\{v_{n}\}
is slowly varying in the critical regime and is a function of the slowly varying part of the memory sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
. The phase transition is controlled not merely by the memory parameter
p
, but by the asymptotic variance scale of the natural martingale. At the critical value
p=p_{c}
, the slowly varying asymptotic variance scale, which is a function of the slowly varying component of the memory sequence, determines both the normalization and even the mode of convergence.

The results show that the critical regime is sensitive to the slowly varying component of the memory sequence. Thus, although the critical value
p_{c}
and the square-root form of the limiting process are universal within the regularly varying class, the critical spatial normalization and the mode of convergence are not. This provides a finer classification of critical step-reinforced random walks than is available for the previously studied power-law or uniform memories.

The exponential timescale traditionally used in the critical regime does not provide a universal nondegenerate scaling limit. However, we show that the linear timescale is universal. At linear timescale in the critical regime, the spatial normalization is time independent. Yet, there is no universal spatial normalization in the critical regime, but depends on the slowly varying asymptotic variance scale
\{v_{n}\}
.

All the limit results are in terms of the convergence of the normalized process in the space of r.c.l.l. functions. We provide complete and rigorous proof of the tightness-at-zero argument to establish convergence in
D([0,\infty))
. This requires an estimate for equicontinuity in probability at
0
. The details have been provided in Section
5
. For proving almost sure and
L^{2}
convergence of the process, convergence of supremum over compact sets has also been shown in this article. This analysis is also missing in the ERW/SRRW literature. Using the functional convergence, further limits of continuous functionals can be obtained easily.

Following
[
1
,
12
]
, we obtain the almost sure and
L^{1}
laws of large numbers only under first moment assumptions for the SRRW-RVM as well, but we do so additionally in the process sense. This process law of large numbers also yields corresponding convergence results for continuous functionals.

We also apply the functional versions of the laws of large numbers and the fluctuation results of the SRRW-RVM to it’s associated barycenter process, using a transfer theorem developed in
[
15
]
. The process convergence results for the fluctuations of the barycenter process in
L^{2}
require careful analysis, especially near zero, which has been provided in details.

(Continuity of Gaussian limit process)
.

The limiting processes satisfy the following properties:

(a)

For fixed
\gamma
, both the processes
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
and
\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}
are continuous in
p\in[0,p_{c})
under the topology of weak convergence, as processes in
C([0,\infty))
.

Furthermore, the process
\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}
is also continuous from below at
p=p_{c}
, while the collection of processes
\{\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}:p\in(p_{c}-\epsilon,p_{c})\}
, is not tight, for any
\epsilon>0
.

(b)

For any fixed
p<1
, the SRRW-RVM is in subcritical regime for all large enough
\gamma
. As
\gamma\to\infty
, the limiting Gaussian process
\mathcal{G}^{(\bullet\gamma)}
converges weakly to
\mathcal{B}/\sqrt{1-p}
in
C([0,\infty))
, where
\mathcal{B}
is a standard Brownian motion.

Remark 2.11
.

The Gaussian limit process in the subcritical regime under the
\sqrt{n}
-normalization is not tight as
p\uparrow p_{c}
. However, once we use the common normalization
\sigma_{n}
for all the regimes, the Gaussian limit process in the subcritical regime converges to the Gaussian limit process in the critical regime weakly as a process, when
p\uparrow p_{c}
, provided
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded at
p=p_{c}
.

The limit process under the linear timescale, when
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded, is a nondegenerate random multiple of a deterministic power law function. The index of the power law function in the critical regime is
1/2
, irrespective of
\{v_{n}\}
being bounded or unbounded. The index
1/2
of the power law function in the critical regime is the limit of the index of the power law function in the supercritical case, but the random variable is distributed as Gaussian, whenever
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded, unlike the almost sure limit. In case of almost sure limit and symmetric Rademacher innovations, the limit is non-Gaussian and platykurtic.
The above continuity property of the limiting process
\mathcal{L}
and similarity of the structure of the limiting process in the critical regime between the bounded and unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
show that the critical regime provides a bridge between the subcritical weak Gaussian process limit and the supercritical almost sure and
L^{2}
limit.

We also record interesting behavior as the index of regular variation
\gamma
of the memory sequence increases to
\infty
. In such a limiting scenario, the random variable
\beta_{n}
puts nearly entire weight on
n-1
. Thus, the SRRW-RVM selects the same last step a geometric number of times before getting replaced by a fresh innovation to be repeated again an independent geometric number of times. Thus, we get fewer number (based on the memory parameter
p
) of distinct i.i.d. steps, which is an independent geometric multiple of an innovation. This suggests the
\sqrt{n}
-diffusive normalization and a multiple (dependent on
p
) of the standard Brownian motion as the limiting process.

Remark 2.12
.

In
[
14
]
, a generalization of the ERW, which they refer to as the Amnesic Elephant Random Walk (AERW) was considered. The SRRW-RVM with symmetric Rademacher innovations, memory parameter
p
and the memory sequence
\mu_{n}={\Gamma(n+\gamma)}/(\Gamma(\gamma+1)\Gamma(n))
is equivalent to the AERW with the corresponding memory parameter
p^{\prime}=(p+1)/{2}
. Then, with
p^{\prime}\in[1/2,1]
, Theorems 2.1, 2.3 and 2.7 of
[
14
]
follow directly from Theorems
2.1
,
2.4
and
2.2
above respectively. Our proofs allow arbitrary regularly varying memory sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
of index
\gamma>-1
and also fill the gap in the proof corresponding to
p=\gamma/(\gamma+1)
in the setup of the present article, which corresponds to
p^{\prime}=(\gamma+1/2)/(\gamma+1)
in the setup of
[
14
]
. The case
p^{\prime}<1/2
corresponds to a negative reinforcement in our model (where the incremental steps are given by
X_{n+1}=-\alpha_{n+1}X_{\beta_{n+1}}+(1-\alpha_{n+1})\xi_{n+1}
in (
1.1
)). Although our results are presented in the context of a positively reinforced step-reinforced random walk, the same techniques can also be used to obtain analogous limit theorems (for example, Theorems 2.1, 2.3 and 2.7 of
[
14
]
) for the negatively reinforced walk. In fact, such a walk is diffusive for all
p\in[0,1]
and
\gamma>-1
.

We end this section with results on the barycenter process. We define the barycenter sequence
\{\overline{S}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
of SRRW-RVM
\{S_{n}:n\geq 0\}
as

\overline{S}_{0}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i},\quad\text{for $n\geq 1$}.

We then have the following functional law of large numbers and phase diagram for the barycenter process.

Theorem 2.13

(Barycenter process)
.

Let
\{\overline{S}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the barycenter process associated with SRRW-RVM
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
with a zero-mean innovation sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
.

If the memory parameter
p<1
, then we have

\left(\frac{1}{n}\overline{S}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}:t\geq 0\right)\to\left(0:t\geq 0\right)

almost surely in
(D([0,\infty)),\mathcal{D})
and in
L^{1}
in
(D([0,\infty)),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and hence in
(D([0,\infty)),d_{J})
. For
p=1
, the limit process is
(t\xi_{1}/2:t\geq 0)
. If the innovation sequence has finite variance, the above convergence results are also in
L^{2}
in
(D([0,\infty)),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and hence in
(D([0,\infty)),d_{J})
.

If the innovations are zero-mean and finite-variance, we also have

\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}{\overline{S}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}:t\geq 0\right)\begin{cases}\to_{{\rm w}}\left(\mathcal{K}(t):t\geq 0\right),&\text{if $0\leq p<p_{c}$ {\rm(}unbounded $\{v_{n}\}${\rm)}},\\
\to_{{\rm w}}\left(\frac{2}{3}(2\gamma+1)Z\sqrt{t}:t\geq 0\right),&\text{if $p=p_{c}$ and unbounded $\{v_{n}\}$},\\
\to_{{\rm a.s.}}\left(\frac{2}{3}(2\gamma+1)\frac{M_{\infty}}{v_{\infty}}\sqrt{t}:t\geq 0\right),&\text{if $p=p_{c}$ and bounded $\{v_{n}\}$},\\
\to_{{\rm a.s.}}\left(\frac{\gamma+b}{b(1+b)}\frac{M_{\infty}}{v_{\infty}}t^{b}:t\geq 0\right),&\text{if $p_{c}<p\leq 1$ {\rm(}bounded $\{v_{n}\}${\rm)}},\end{cases}

in
(D([0,\infty)),\mathcal{D})
, where
b=p(\gamma+1)-\gamma
. When
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded, the convergence results also hold in
L^{2}
in
(D([0,\infty)),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and hence also in
(D([0,\infty)),d_{J})
. The subcritical limit process
\mathcal{K}
is a continuous, real valued, centered Gaussian process starting at the origin. The covariance kernel of the process
\mathcal{K}
, for
0\leq s\leq t
, is given by

\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{K}(s)\mathcal{K}(t))\\
=\begin{cases}s(1-2b)\left[\frac{(\gamma+2)(\gamma-1)}{6(1+b)(2-b)}\cdot\frac{s}{t}+\frac{1}{b(1-b)}\left\{\frac{(\gamma+b)(\gamma+1-b)}{(1+b)(2-b)(1-2b)}\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{-b}-\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{2}\right\}\right],&\text{for }p\neq\widehat{p}\\
s\left[\frac{(\gamma+2)(\gamma-1)}{12}\cdot\frac{s}{t}+\frac{3\gamma(\gamma+1)+2}{4}-\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{2}\log\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)\right],&\text{for }p=\widehat{p}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

The limit random variable
M_{\infty}
in case of bounded
\{v_{n}\}
is as given in Theorem
2.2
.

Remark 2.14
.

As noted earlier, the walk considered in
[
14
]
is a special case of the SRRW-RVM considered in this article. The barycenter process corresponding to the walk in
[
14
]
was studied in
[
9
]
, where a continuous mapping was used to transfer the process weak convergence of the underlying walk to the barycenter process. However, the analysis in
[
9
]
had several limitations. The analysis did not consider the error term due to the transform. The covariance kernel of the Gaussian process limit in the subcritical regime was not correct. In
[
9
]
, exponential timescale was used in the critical regime, but it was not explained how the continuous mapping theorem was applied. In the supercritical regime, only process weak convergence was obtained. Even then, the application of the continuous mapping theorem was flawed, as the convergence of the underlying process was only in finite dimensional distributions. We settle these issues about the barycenter process and provide a complete picture. The phase diagram for the barycenter process is the same as that of SRRW-RVM and the spatial normalizations are same as well. The limit processes in the critical and the supercritical regimes are constant multiples of the corresponding limit processes for SRRW-RVM.

3.
Fluctuations when Asymptotic Variance Scale is Bounded

In this section, we consider the fluctuations of the SRRW-RVM
\{S_{n}\}
with bounded asymptotic variance scale
\{v_{n}\}
, as given in Theorem
2.2
. We first introduce the required notation and establish several preliminary results.
The first result shows that the steps of SRRW-RVM
\{S_{n}\}
are dependent due to recollection, but are identically distributed.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
n\geq 1
, the marginal distribution of the
n^{th}
increment
X_{n}
of an SRRW-RVM
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, is same as the common distribution of the innovation sequence.

Proof.

The independence of
\alpha_{n}
,
\beta_{n}
and
\xi_{n}
in (
1.1
) gives, for
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\leq x|\mathcal{F}_{n})=(1-p)\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}\leq x)+{p}{\nu_{n}^{-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\mathbb{1}_{[X_{k}\leq x]}
.
Unconditioning and noting that
X_{1}=\xi_{1}
, we have
X_{n}{=}_{\rm d}\xi_{1}
, using induction.
∎

In order to study the martingales central to the discussion in this article, we introduce the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
associated to the process
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is the trivial
\sigma
-field,
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\sigma(\xi_{1})
and, for
n\geq 2
,

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma\left(\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n},\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=2}^{n},\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=2}^{n}\right)
.

From (
1.1
), we immediately get, for
n\geq 1
,

{}\mathbb{E}(X_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})=\frac{p}{\nu_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\mu_{k}=\frac{p}{\nu_{n}}Y_{n},

(3.1)

where
Y_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}X_{k}
. This gives
\mathbb{E}(Y_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})=\left(1+{p\mu_{n+1}}/{\nu_{n}}\right)Y_{n}
. Then, it follows immediately from (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) that

\mathbb{E}(M_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})=M_{n},

(3.2)

making
\{M_{n}\}
a martingale. The corresponding martingale differences are given by

\Delta M_{n}=a_{n}\mu_{n}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}(X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})\right),\quad\text{for $n\geq 2$}\qquad\text{and}\quad\Delta M_{1}=\mu_{1}X_{1}.

(3.3)

Further, considering the conditional expectation of
S_{n}
, given by

\mathbb{E}(S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})=S_{n}+pM_{n}/(a_{n}\nu_{n})
,
we have the next martingale of interest, which is given by,

L_{1}=X_{1},\quad\text{and for $n\geq 2$,}\quad L_{n}=S_{n}-p\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}M_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(X_{k}-\mathbb{E}(X_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})).

(3.4)

Note that
\{M_{n}\}
is a martingale transform of
\{L_{n}\}
with

\Delta M_{n}=a_{n}\mu_{n}\Delta L_{n},\quad\text{for $n\geq 1$.}

(3.5)

The following proposition summarizes the growth of the variance of
M_{n}
, which in turn determines the phase transition based on the boundedness of the sequence
\{v_{n}\}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be an SRRW-RVM with the zero mean innovation sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}^{2}=1
. When
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded,
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an
L^{2}
-bounded martingale, while for unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(M_{n}^{2})\sim\begin{cases}\frac{a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}}{2(1-p)(\gamma+1)-1}n,&\text{if }0\leq p<p_{c},\\
v_{n}^{2},&\text{if }p=p_{c},\end{cases}.

(3.6)

Proof.

Using the expression for the conditional expectation and the martingale difference
\Delta M_{n}
from (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), along with Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

{}\mathbb{E}(M_{n}^{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}-p^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{a_{k+1}\mu_{k+1}}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}(M_{k}^{2})\leq v_{n}^{2}.

(3.7)

The result follows immediately when
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded.

Next, we consider unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
. For the second term of (
3.7
), observe that,

p^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{a_{k+1}\mu_{k+1}}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}M_{k}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{k+1}}{\nu_{k}}\right)^{2}v_{k}^{2}=\oh\left(v_{n}^{2}\right)

using Kronecker’s lemma, since the sequence
\{{\mu_{n+1}^{2}}/{\nu_{n}^{2}}\}\sim\{(\gamma+1)^{2}/n^{2}\}
is summable and
v_{n}^{2}\uparrow\infty
. This gives

\mathbb{E}\left(M_{n}^{2}\right)\sim v_{n}^{2}
and (
3.6
) for
0\leq p<p_{c}
follows from Karamata’s theorem.
∎

We are now ready to proceed with the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

(a)

The result follows immediately from Proposition
3.2
.

(b)

We now show that
M_{\infty}
is platykurtic, when
\xi_{1}
is symmetric Rademacher random variable. For Rademacher innovations, we have
\left|X_{n}-\mathbb{E}(X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})\right|\leq 2
almost surely. Writing
M_{n}=M_{n-1}+\Delta M_{n}
and expanding
(M_{n-1}+\Delta M_{n})^{4}
, we have using (
3.5
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}M_{n}^{4}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}M_{n-1}^{4}+a_{n}^{4}\mu_{n}^{4}\mathbb{E}(\Delta L_{n})^{4}+6a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}(M_{n-1}^{2}(\Delta L_{n})^{2})+4a_{n}^{3}\mu_{n}^{3}\mathbb{E}(M_{n-1}(\Delta L_{n})^{3})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}M_{n-1}^{4}+16a_{n}^{4}\mu_{n}^{4}+24a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}M_{m}^{2}+32a_{n}^{3}\mu_{n}^{3}\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}|M_{m}|

which then gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}M_{n}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 16\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{4}\mu_{k}^{4}+24\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}M_{m}^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}+32\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}|M_{m}|\sum_{k=2}^{n}a_{k}^{3}\mu_{k}^{3}

For bounded
\{v_{n}\}
, all the sums on the right side converge. As
M_{n}
is
L^{2}
bounded, the martingale
\{M_{n}\}
then becomes
L^{4}
-bounded and converges to
M_{\infty}
in
L^{4}
.

Note that
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
being a mean zero martingale sequence,
\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}=0
. We study the kurtosis of
M_{\infty}
, given by
\kappa:={\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{4}}/{(\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{2})^{2}}
. For
M_{\infty}
to be platykurtic, we show

3(\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{2})^{2}-\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{4}>0.

(3.8)

Towards proving (
3.8
), for
x>0
and
n\geq 1
, define

a_{n}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1+{x\mu_{i+1}}/{\nu_{i}}\right)^{-1}
.
Note that
a_{n}(p)\equiv a_{n}
and, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}(kp)\sim a_{n}^{k}
as
n\to\infty
.

Due to Lemma
3.1
,
X_{n}
also has symmetric Rademacher distribution, giving
X_{n}^{2}=1
and
\mathbb{E}X_{n}=0
. Then, the sequence
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
defined through (
3.1
) has zero mean. Hence, for
n\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{3}=\left(1+{3p\mu_{n}}/{\nu_{n-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{3}
,
giving
\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{3}=0
. Similarly, the second and fourth moments of
Y_{n}
satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{2p\mu_{n}}{\nu_{n-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}+\mu_{n}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{4}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{4p\mu_{n}}{\nu_{n-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{4}+6\mu_{n}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2p\mu_{n}}{3\nu_{n-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}+\mu_{n}^{4}.

Therefore, from the above quantities, we have

b_{n}=\left(1+\frac{4p\mu_{n}}{\nu_{n-1}}\right)b_{n-1}+\frac{12p^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}}{\nu_{n-1}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{8p\mu_{n}^{3}}{\nu_{n-1}}\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}+2\mu_{n}^{4},

where
b_{n}=3(\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{2})^{2}-\mathbb{E}Y_{n}^{4}
. Solving the recursion, we obtain

a_{n}(4p)b_{n}=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(4p)\mu_{k}^{4}+8p\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{a_{k}(4p)\mu_{k}^{3}}{\nu_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}Y_{k-1}^{2}+12p^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{a_{k}(4p)\mu_{k}^{2}}{\nu_{k-1}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}Y_{k-1}^{2}\right)^{2}.

As
a_{n}(4p)\sim a_{n}^{4}
, taking limits, we get

3(\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{2})^{2}-\mathbb{E}M_{\infty}^{4}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}(4p)\mu_{n}^{4}\\
+8p\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}(4p)\mu_{n}^{3}}{\nu_{n-1}}\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}+12p^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}(4p)\mu_{n}^{2}}{\nu_{n-1}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}\right)^{2},

which gives (
3.8
).

(c)

We first prove that the result holds marginally, using (
3.4
). By Karamata’s theorem,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/(a_{n}\nu_{n})=\infty
holds for
p>\widehat{p}
, in particular for bounded
\{v_{n}\}
.
Define

\eta_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}\sim\frac{\gamma+1}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\cdot\frac{1}{a_{n}\mu_{n}},

(3.9)

a regularly varying sequence of index
p(\gamma+1)-\gamma
, for
p>\widehat{p}
. Next consider the martingale transform
\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-1}\Delta L_{k}\}
of
\{L_{n}\}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-1}\Delta L_{k}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-2}\mathbb{E}(\Delta L_{k})^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-2}\mathbb{E}(X_{k})^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-2}<\infty
,
using (
3.9
) and boundedness of
\{v_{n}\}
. Thus, the martingale
\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{-1}\Delta L_{k}\}
is
L^{2}
-bounded and converges almost surely and in
L^{2}
. Since
\eta_{n}\uparrow\infty
, by Kronecker’s lemma and (
3.9
), we have

a_{n}\mu_{n}L_{n}\to 0\quad\text{almost surely and in $L^{2}$.}

(3.10)

Furthermore, an application of Toeplitz lemma to the martingale
\{M_{n}\}
yields

pa_{n}\mu_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}M_{k}\to\frac{p(\gamma+1)}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}M_{\infty}\quad\text{almost surely and in $L^{2}$.}

(3.11)

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we get the required marginal convergence for
t=1
from (
3.4
). A normalization argument gives the marginal convergence for
t>0
.

Next we establish the almost sure and in
L^{2}
process convergence of SRRW-RVM. By Proposition 2.3 of
[
13
]
, it is enough to show for any
T>0
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{p(\gamma+1)}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}t^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}M_{\infty}\right|\to 0\quad\text{almost surely and in $L^{2}$.}

(3.12)

For almost sure convergence of (
3.12
), fix
T>0
. For any
0<t_{0}<T
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{p(\gamma+1)}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}t^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}M_{\infty}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\frac{a_{n}\mu_{n}}{a_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}-t^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\right|\sup_{l\geq 1}\left|a_{l}\mu_{l}S_{l}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+t_{0}^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\left(\sup_{l\geq 1}\left|a_{l}\mu_{l}S_{l}\right|+\frac{p(\gamma+1)}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\left|M_{\infty}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+T^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\sup_{l\geq\lfloor nt_{0}\rfloor}\left|a_{l}\mu_{l}S_{l}-\frac{p(\gamma+1)}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}M_{\infty}\right|.

Since
\{{a_{n}\mu_{n}}\}
is regularly varying of index
{\gamma-p(\gamma+1)}
, which is negative for
p\geq p_{c}
, using Proposition 0.5 of
[
16
]
and the almost sure marginal convergence at
t=1
, we obtain the negligibility of the first and third terms on the RHS of the above inequality, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, letting
t_{0}\downarrow 0
gives the negligibility of the second term, proving (
3.12
) almost surely.

For
L^{2}
process convergence, we use the decomposition (
3.4
) to obtain

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{p(\gamma+1)t^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}M_{\infty}\right|\\
\leq a_{n}\mu_{n}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|L_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right|+a_{n}\mu_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\left|M_{k}-M_{\infty}\right|\\
+|M_{\infty}|\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}-\frac{\gamma+1}{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}t^{p(\gamma+1)-\gamma}\right|.

Observe
\mathbb{E}L_{n}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\Delta L_{k})^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}X_{k}^{2}=n
. Then, using Doob’s
L^{2}
-inequality and Karamata’s theorem, the first term on the right side above is
L^{2}
-negligible:

a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}L_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}\right)\leq 4a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}L_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}^{2}\leq 4{nT}a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}=\Oh\left(\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\right)\to 0.

The second term on the right side above is
L^{2}
-negligible by Toeplitz lemma and part (
(a)
) of this theorem, while the third term is
L^{2}
-negligible using Karamata’s theorem and Proposition 0.5 of
[
16
]
.

∎

4.
Laws of Large Numbers when Innovations have Finite Variance

In this section we prove the laws of large numbers. However, for simplicity and ease of understanding, we provide the proofs only for innovation sequences with finite variance, which we assume to be
1
without loss of generality. The complete proofs, using truncation arguments, are deferred till Section
9
.

We first provide the marginal laws of large numbers for the martingale sequences
\{M_{n}\}
and
\{L_{n}\}
, in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be an SRRW-RVM with zero mean innovation sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. Then we have, for all
p\in[0,1)
,

{M_{n}}/({a_{n}\nu_{n}})\to 0
and
{L_{n}}/{n}\to 0
almost surely and in
L^{1}
. Further, for finite variance innovations, the convergence is in
L^{2}
.

We first prove the result under the assumption that the innovations have finite variance.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
for unit variance innovations.

Assume, without loss of generality, the variance of
\xi_{n}
to be
1
.
Lemma
3.1
gives
\mathbb{E}(\Delta L_{n})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}X_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}^{2}=1
for Rademacher innovations. Then, using (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(\Delta L_{n})^{2}/{n^{2}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}X_{n}^{2}/{n^{2}}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(\Delta M_{n})^{2}/(a_{n}^{2}\nu_{n}^{2})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}^{2}/\nu_{n}^{2}<\infty.

So,

\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta M_{k}/(a_{k}\nu_{k})\right\}
and
\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta L_{k}/k\right\}

are
L^{2}
-bounded martingales, and converge almost surely and in
L^{2}
.
Further note that
(a_{n+1}\nu_{n+1})/(a_{n}\nu_{n})=\nu_{n+1}/(\nu_{n}+p\mu_{n+1})\geq 1
making
\{a_{n}\nu_{n}\}
an increasing sequence, which, being regularly varying of index
(1-p)(\gamma+1)
, increases to
\infty
. Thus, Kronecker’s lemma gives the results.
∎

Next, we obtain the functional law of large numbers for unit variance innovation.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
for unit variance innovations.

Since we consider innovations with unit variance, we prove almost sure and in
L^{2}
convergence. For
p\in[0,1)
, using (
3.4
), the result follows immediately for
t=1
from Lemma
4.1
, and extension to any
t>0
follows by spatial normalization. For process convergence, again, due to Proposition 2.3 of
[
13
]
, it is enough to show for any
T>0
,

S^{*}_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}/n\to 0

in appropriate mode of convergence, where

S^{*}_{K}:=\sup_{0\leq k\leq K}|S_{k}|
.

We first consider almost sure convergence. As
S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}=0
for
0\leq t<1/n
, we have for any
T>0
and
t_{0}>0
,

\frac{1}{n}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}|\leq\sup_{\frac{1}{n}\leq t\leq t_{0}}\left(t\frac{\left|S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right|}{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right)+\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq T}\left(t\frac{\left|S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right|}{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right)\leq t_{0}\sup_{l\geq 1}\frac{|S_{l}|}{l}+T\sup_{l\geq\lfloor nt_{0}\rfloor}\frac{|S_{l}|}{l}.

Using the marginal almost sure convergence for
t=1
and
t_{0}>0
being arbitrarily small, the right side above is almost surely negligible.

Next, we prove
L^{2}
convergence for symmetric Rademacher innovations. Using (
3.4
) and careful applications of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the first step and Doob’s
L^{2}
inequality in the next, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(S^{*}_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{1\leq l\leq\lfloor nT\rfloor}L_{l}^{2}\right)}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor-1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{M_{k}}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}L_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor-1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{M_{k}}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\right)^{2}}.

Then
S^{*}_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}/n\to 0
in
L^{2}
follows using Lemma
4.1
.

The case
p=1
follows easily as
S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}=\lfloor nt\rfloor\xi_{1}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
uses the unit variance assumption on the innovation sequence only through Lemma
4.1
.

5.
Process Weak Convergence for SRRW-R-RVM

In this section, we study the fluctuations of the SRRW-RVM process in the subcritical regime
p\in[0,p_{c})
as well as in the critical regime
p=p_{c}
with unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
. We first introduce two additional martingales that will be useful in the analysis. For simplicity, we shall prove some of the results in this section only for SRRW-R-RVM, with the corresponding proofs for general innovations being deferred to Section
9
.

5.1.
Two martingales for the normalized SRRW-RVM process

In this subsection, we introduce two martingales that will be important in studying the SRRW-RVM process for unbounded
\{v_{n}\}
. We distinguish four subcases: namely
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
,
p=\widehat{p}
,
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
and finally
p=p_{c}
. In the first three subcases,
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded, while, for
p=p_{c}
, we consider only those
\{\mu_{n}\}
such that
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded. As noted in Corollary
2.9
, we can use the normalization
\sigma_{n}
for all four subcases. However, we use the equivalent diffusive
\sqrt{n}
-normalization in the first three subcases as it simplifies the calculations. In the fourth subcase, the normalization is not explicit, but for interesting illustrative examples in Section
8
, we simplify
\sigma_{n}
and provide more explicit rates.

As noted earlier, the decomposition (
3.4
) plays an important role in the analysis, but it needs to be further simplified before it is applied. Using (
3.3
) – (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=L_{n}+p\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}M_{k}=L_{n}+p\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Delta M_{l}=L_{n}+p\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\sum_{k=l}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{k}\nu_{k}}\Delta M_{l}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}L_{n}+p\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{l})\Delta M_{l},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}=\infty$,}\\
L_{n}+p\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\overline{\eta}_{l}-\overline{\eta}_{n})\Delta M_{l},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}<\infty$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-pa_{l}\mu_{l}\eta_{l})\Delta L_{l}+p\eta_{n}M_{n},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}=\infty$,}\\
\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1+pa_{l}\mu_{l}\overline{\eta}_{l})\Delta L_{l}-p\overline{\eta}_{n}M_{n},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}<\infty$,}\end{cases}

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}N_{n}+p\eta_{n}M_{n},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}=\infty$,}\\
\overline{N}_{n}-p\overline{\eta}_{n}M_{n},&\text{when $\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}<\infty$,}\end{cases}

(5.2)

where, we define,

\displaystyle\eta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{l}\nu_{l}},

\displaystyle N_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-pa_{l}\mu_{l}\eta_{l})\Delta L_{l}

\displaystyle\text{when }\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}

\displaystyle=\infty,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\overline{\eta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a_{l}\nu_{l}},

\displaystyle\overline{N}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1+pa_{l}\mu_{l}\overline{\eta}_{l})\Delta L_{l}

\displaystyle\text{when }\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}

\displaystyle<\infty,

where the sequences
\{\eta_{n}\}
and
\{\overline{\eta}_{n}\}
are regularly varying of index
p(\gamma+1)-\gamma
. (Note that
\eta_{n}
has already been defined in (
3.9
).) Thus, for the case
\sum_{n}1/({a_{n}\nu_{n}})=\infty
, we consider the joint weak convergence of
(N_{n},\eta_{n}M_{n})/{\sqrt{n}}
, while for the case
\sum_{n}1/({a_{n}\nu_{n}})<\infty
, we consider the joint weak convergence of
(\overline{N}_{n},\overline{\eta}_{n}M_{n})/{\sqrt{n}}
. Then an appropriate linear transformation would give the weak convergence of
S_{n}/\sqrt{n}
. For the case
p=\widehat{p}
, the proof is similar, but more subtle – see Remark
5.1
. For
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
, we have
\sum_{n}1/({a_{n}\nu_{n}})=\infty
and was considered by
[
3
]
. For
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
, we have
\sum_{n}1/({a_{n}\nu_{n}})<\infty
, but the process convergence requires more careful analysis of tightness near
0
.

Remark 5.1
.

For the case
p=\widehat{p}
, in (
5.1
), the term
-p\sum_{l=1}^{n}a_{l}\mu_{l}\eta_{l}\Delta L_{l}
(respectively,
p\sum_{l=1}^{n}a_{l}\mu_{l}\overline{\eta}_{l}\Delta L_{l}
) dominates due to Karamata’s theorem, but cancels with the term
p\eta_{n}M_{n}=p\eta_{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}a_{l}\mu_{l}\Delta L_{l}
(respectively,
-p\overline{\eta}_{n}M_{n}=-p\overline{\eta}_{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}a_{l}\mu_{l}\Delta L_{l}
), leaving out terms which are diffusive in growth. This motivates a more careful analysis using triangular normalizing matrices. See Remark
5.2
.

5.2.
Subcritical regime for SRRW-R-RVM

In the subcritical regime, both terms in (
5.2
) contribute. We obtain a functional martingale central limit theorem for an appropriate triangular array of bivariate martingale difference sequence, in each of the cases
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
,
p=\widehat{p}
and
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
, using the Corollary to Theorem 2 of
[
20
]
. While the martingale difference arrays are defined differently across the cases, their quadratic variation processes are computed and the Lindeberg conditions checked, in a unified way.

For
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
, we use the diagonal normalizing matrix

\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}=n^{-1/2}\diag(1,{\overline{\eta}_{n}})
for
n\geq 1
,
and the corresponding triangular array of bivariate martingale difference sequence is, for
n\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
,

\Delta\overline{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\prime}_{n,k}:=\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}(\Delta\overline{N}_{k},\Delta M_{k})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left((1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}\overline{\eta}_{k}),a_{k}\mu_{k}\overline{\eta}_{n}\right)^{\prime}\Delta L_{k}.

Correspondingly, for
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
, we use the diagonal normalizing matrix

\boldsymbol{V}_{n}=n^{-1/2}\diag(1,{\eta_{n}})
for
n\geq 1
, as in
[
3
]
,
and the corresponding triangular array of bivariate martingale difference sequence is, for
n\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
,

\Delta\boldsymbol{T}^{\prime}_{n,k}:=\boldsymbol{V}_{n}(\Delta N_{k},\Delta M_{k})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left((1-pa_{k}\mu_{k}\eta_{k}),a_{k}\mu_{k}\eta_{n}\right)^{\prime}\Delta L_{k}.

For
p=\widehat{p}
, we need an upper triangular normalizing matrix, as suggested in Remark
5.1
, given by,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\begin{pmatrix}1&{p\eta_{n}}\\
0&\frac{1}{a_{n}\mu_{n}}\end{pmatrix},

when
\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}=\infty
,

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\begin{pmatrix}1&{-p\overline{\eta}_{n}}\\
0&\frac{1}{a_{n}\mu_{n}}\end{pmatrix},

when
\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}<\infty
,

for
n\geq 1
, and the corresponding triangular array of bivariate martingale difference sequence is, for
n\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
,

\Delta\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\prime}_{n,k}:=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}(\Delta N_{k},\Delta M_{k})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left(1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})\right),\frac{a_{k}\mu_{k}}{a_{n}\mu_{n}}\right)^{\prime}\Delta L_{k},&\!\!\!\text{if }\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}=\infty,\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}(\Delta\overline{N}_{k},\Delta M_{k})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left(1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}(\overline{\eta}_{k}-\overline{\eta}_{n})\right),\frac{a_{k}\mu_{k}}{a_{n}\mu_{n}}\right)^{\prime}\Delta L_{k},&\!\!\!\text{if }\sum_{n}\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}<\infty.\end{cases}

Remark 5.2
.

While it seems that
\Delta\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\prime}_{n,k}
have two distinct forms depending on the summability of
(a_{n}\nu_{n})^{-1}
, actually they lead to one single expression, as both
(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})
when
\sum_{n}1/(a_{n}\nu_{n})=\infty
and
(\overline{\eta}_{n}-\overline{\eta}_{k})
when
\sum_{n}1/(a_{n}\nu_{n})<\infty
, lead to same quantity
\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}1/(a_{l}\nu_{l})
. We use this common quantity.

Remark 5.3
.

While the second diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{V}_{n}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}
seem to differ from that of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}
, using Karamata’s theorem, the second diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{V}_{n}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}
have the same order as that of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{V}}_{n}
. The alternate form helps in calculation.

Next, we collect results on the limiting quadratic variation, a Lindeberg condition and the subsequent martingale central limit theorem, corresponding to each of the bivariate martingale processes defined above. We state the results for SRRW-RVM, but prove them only for SRRW-R-RVM in this section, leaving the general proof for Section
9
. We indicate explicitly the proofs that do not use this additional assumption. We start with the limiting quadratic variation.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be an SRRW-RVM, with the zero mean innovation sequence satisfying
\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}^{2}=1
. Then we have:

For
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
, the quadratic variation process of
\{\overline{\boldsymbol{T}}_{n,k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, for each
t\geq 0

\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{T}}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\to_{L^{1}}\boldsymbol{\overline{W}}(t):=\frac{1}{(p(\gamma+1)-\gamma)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{2}t&\frac{\gamma t^{(1-p)(\gamma+1)}}{1-p}\\

with
\overline{\boldsymbol{W}}(t)

t>0

\overline{\boldsymbol{W}}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}
.

For
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})

\{\boldsymbol{T}_{n,k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, for each
t\geq 0

\langle\boldsymbol{T}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\to_{L^{1}}\boldsymbol{W}(t):=\frac{1}{(p(\gamma+1)-\gamma)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{2}t&-\frac{\gamma t^{(1-p)(\gamma+1)}}{1-p}\\

with
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
positive definite for
t>0

\boldsymbol{W}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}
.

For

\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\to_{L^{1}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}(t):=t\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{2}+(\gamma+1-\gamma\log t)^{2}&\gamma+1-\gamma\log t\\

For the Lindeberg conditions, the following bounds on the norms of each of the martingale difference arrays are useful.

We now provide the proofs of the above results. Some of the results have been proved here only for SRRW-R-RVM, for the ease of understanding the main ideas. The proofs for general innovations have been deferred to Section

Proof of Lemma
5.4
for SRRW-R-RVM.

For the case
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
, as
X_{k}^{2}=1
for SRRW-R-RVM, the quadratic variation process is

\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{T}}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\mathbb{E}(X_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})-p^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}-p^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}

(5.4)

where

\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}(1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}\overline{\eta}_{k})^{2}&a_{k}\mu_{k}(1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}\overline{\eta}_{k})\overline{\eta}_{n}\\
a_{k}\mu_{k}(1+pa_{k}\mu_{k}\overline{\eta}_{k})\overline{\eta}_{n}&a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\overline{\eta}_{n}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Using Karamata’s theorem, for each
t\geq 0
,

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\to\overline{\boldsymbol{W}}(t)
and, for each fixed

\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}
converges to the zero matrix.
As
Y_{n}^{2}/\nu_{n}^{2}\to 0
in
L^{1}
from Lemma

, the last term of (
5.4
) converges to the zero matrix by Toeplitz lemma.

Similarly, for the case
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
, the quadratic variation process is given by

\langle\boldsymbol{T}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{n,k}\mathbb{E}(X_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})-p^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{n,k}-p^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}

where

\boldsymbol{Q}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}(1-pa_{k}\mu_{k}\eta_{k})^{2}&a_{k}\mu_{k}(1-pa_{k}\mu_{k}\eta_{k})\eta_{n}\\
a_{k}\mu_{k}(1-pa_{k}\mu_{k}\eta_{k})\eta_{n}&a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\eta_{n}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The rest of the proof for the case
p\in(\widehat{p},p_{c})
, is analogous to that of the case
p\in[0,\widehat{p})
and is omitted.

Again, for the case
p=\widehat{p}
, the quadratic variation process is given by

\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}\rangle_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\mathbb{E}(X_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})-\widehat{p}^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}-\widehat{p}^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\left(\frac{Y_{k-1}}{\nu_{k-1}}\right)^{2}

where

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}(1+\widehat{p}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k}))^{2}&\left(a_{k}\mu_{k}(1+\widehat{p}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k}))\right)/(a_{n}\mu_{n})\\
\left(a_{k}\mu_{k}(1+\widehat{p}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k}))\right)/(a_{n}\mu_{n})&\left(a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\right)/\left(a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

We consider the limits of each element of
\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}
separately. We note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{k})+\frac{\eta_{n}-\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}v_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{k})+\frac{\eta_{n}-\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}\mu_{k},

and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{k})^{2}+2\frac{\eta_{n}-\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{k})\\
+\frac{(\eta_{n}-\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor})^{2}}{n}v_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}.

We then use the limits from Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
to conclude
\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{n,k}\to\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}(t)
. Further, for each fixed

converges to the zero matrix. Finally, the limiting quadratic variation process for the case
p=\widehat{p}

p\in[0,\widehat{p})
.
∎

6.
Continuity of the limiting process

In this section, we study the continuity of the limiting process with respect to the memory parameter and the index of regular variation. The proof does not use the Rademacher structure of the innovation sequence.

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

(a)

It is straightforward to check that the covariance kernel of the process
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
given in (
2.1
) is continuous in
p\in[0,p_{c})
, including at
p=\widehat{p}
. The covariance kernel of the process
\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}(s)L^{(p\bullet)}(t)\right)=(2(1-p)(\gamma+1)-1)\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s)G^{(p\bullet)}(t)\right).

(6.1)

Multiplication by
(2(1-p)(\gamma+1)-1)
removes the discontinuity of the covariance kernel of the process
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
at
p=p_{c}
and the covariance kernel of the process
\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}
becomes continuous in
p\in[0,p_{c}]
. Thus, we have finite dimensional convergence of
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
for
p\in[0,p_{c})
and of
\mathcal{L}^{p\bullet}
for
p\in[0,p_{c}]
.

To establish the continuity of
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
for
p\in[0,p_{c})
, it is enough to show the tightness of
\{\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}:p\leq p^{\prime}\}
for any
p^{\prime}<p_{c}
. We use Kolmogorov’s continuity criterion (see Theorem 1.8 of
[
17
]
) to establish the tightness. Since the processes are mean zero Gaussian processes starting at
0
, we need to check the moment condition only.

Now, for all
p\in[0,p_{c})
and for
0\leq s\leq t
, we have,

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s)\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(t)\right)\geq\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s)^{2}\right)
.
Then, further using Remark
2.5
, we get

\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(t)-\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s))^{4}=3(\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(t)-\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s))^{2})^{2}

\leq 3(\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(t))^{2}-E(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s))^{2})^{2}=3(t-s)^{2}\varsigma^{4}
.
As
\varsigma^{2}\equiv\varsigma^{2}(p)
is increasing in
p\in[0,p_{c})
and
\varsigma^{2}(p^{\prime})<\infty
for every
p^{\prime}<p_{c}
,
\{\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(t)-\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(s))^{4}:p\leq p^{\prime}\}
is bounded.

We also observe that
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(1))^{2}=\zeta^{2}(p)\to\infty
as
p\uparrow p_{c}
. Thus,
\{\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}(1)\}
and, hence the collection
\{\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}\}
, is not tight in any interval to the left of
p_{c}
.

To show the continuity of
\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}
for
p\in[0,p_{c}]
, we again establish the tightness of
\{\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}:p\in[0,p_{c}]\}
. Arguing similarly as for the process
\mathcal{G}^{(p\bullet)}
and using (
6.1
). we get
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}(t)-\mathcal{L}^{(p\bullet)}(s))^{4}\leq 3(t-s)^{2}(2\gamma+1-p)/(1-p)\leq 3(t-s)^{2}(2\gamma+1-p_{c})/(1-p_{c})<\infty
for
p\leq p_{c}
and verify the relevant Kolmogorov’s continuity criterion.

(b)

As
\gamma\uparrow\infty
, we have
p_{c}\to 1
. Thus, for any
p<1
, we have
p<p_{c}
for any large enough
\gamma
. So the walk will eventually be in the subcritical regime. From (
2.1
), it is immediate that
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}^{(\bullet\gamma)}(s)\mathcal{G}^{(\bullet\gamma)}(t))\to s/(1-p)
for
0\leq s\leq t
as
\gamma\to\infty
.

∎

7.
Barycenter Process

We apply the functional laws of large numbers and fluctuations for SRRW-RVM to a continuous map, developed in
[
15
]
, to obtain the asymptotic behavior of the barycenter process. The transform at the center of the analysis in
[
15
]
is given in Section 7.3 of the paper. The transform
\mathfrak{g}:D_{0}([0,\infty))\to C_{0}([0,\infty))
is given, for any
f\in D_{0}([0,\infty))
, as

\mathfrak{g}(f)(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(u)du,&\text{when $t>0$},\\
0,&\text{when $t=0$}.\end{cases}

(7.1)

We summarize the properties of the transform in the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

The transform
\mathfrak{g}:D_{0}([0,\infty))\to C_{0}([0,\infty))
given in (
7.1
) is linear and continuous.
Furthermore, the transform
\mathfrak{g}
is a contraction under the the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We refer to Lemma 7.10 of
[
15
]
for a proof of the facts that, for any
f\in D_{0}([0,\infty))
, the function
\mathfrak{g}(f)\in C_{0}([0,\infty))
and the map
\mathfrak{g}:D_{0}([0,\infty))\to C_{0}([0,\infty))
is continuous.

To prove that
\mathfrak{g}
is a contraction under
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, take any
T>0
. Then, for any
0\leq t\leq T
, we have,
|\mathfrak{g}(f)(t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|f(s)|ds/t\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|f(s)|
. Thus, we have

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\mathfrak{g}(f)(t)|\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|f(t)|,

(7.2)

which gives the contraction.
∎

The following lemma studies the error in approximating the barycenter process with the transform of the original sequence.

Lemma 7.2
.

We consider a sequence of random variables
\{U_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
with
U_{0}=0
and the corresponding barycenter sequence given by
\overline{U}_{0}=0
and, for
n\geq 1
,
\overline{U}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}/n
. Then, for any
T>0
, we have

\sup_{[0,T]}|\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}|\leq\sup_{[0,T]}|U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}|.

(7.3)

The error process
\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor})
satisfies, for any
0<\epsilon<T
,

\sup_{[\epsilon,T]}\left|\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor})\right|\leq\frac{2}{n\epsilon}\sup_{[0,T]}|U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}|.

(7.4)

Furthermore, if the sequence of random variables
\{U_{n}\}
grows sublinearly, that is
U_{n}/n\to 0
almost surely, then, for any sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}
going to
\infty
, the error process satisfies

\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\left\|\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor})\right\|_{\infty}\to 0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(7.5)

Proof.

For any
T>0
and
0\leq t\leq T
, we have
|\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}|U_{i}|/\lfloor nt\rfloor\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}|
, which gives (
7.3
).

From the proof of Lemma 7.4, p. 42 of
[
15
]
, we obtain, for
t>0
, that

\displaystyle\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}\left(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\right)(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{nt-\lfloor nt\rfloor}{nt}\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{nt-\lfloor nt\rfloor-1}{nt}{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor},

and hence, using (
7.3
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}\left(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\right)(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{nt}\left(\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}+U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right)\leq\frac{2}{nt}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}U_{\lfloor ns\rfloor},

which gives (
7.4
).

Finally, the proof of Lemma 7.4 of
[
15
]
actually shows that, for
0\leq t\leq T
, we have

\sup_{n}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\overline{U}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}(U_{\lfloor nt\rfloor})|<\infty

almost surely and (
7.5
) follows.
∎

This transform allows the asymptotic almost sure or weak behavior of a sequence to be transferred to its barycenter process.

Theorem 7.3

(Transfer theorem for fluctuations of barycenter process)
.

We consider a sequence of random variables
\{U_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
with
U_{0}=0
and the corresponding barycenter sequence given by
\overline{U}_{0}=0
and, for
n\geq 1
,
\overline{U}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}/n
. Further, assume that the sequence of random variables
\{U_{n}\}
grows sublinearly, that is
U_{n}/n\to 0
almost surely.

If the sequence
\{U_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfies an invariance principle with respect to an appropriate normalization
\Xi_{n}
going to
\infty
:

\frac{1}{\Xi_{n}}U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\to U_{\infty}\quad\text{almost surely (weakly respectively) in $(D_{0}([0,\infty)),\mathcal{D})$.}

(7.6)

Then the barycenter sequence
\{\overline{U}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
also satisfies an invariance principle with respect to the same normalization
\Xi_{n}
:

\frac{1}{\Xi_{n}}\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\to\overline{U}_{\infty}:=\mathfrak{g}(U_{\infty})\quad\text{almost surely (weakly respectively) in $(D_{0}([0,\infty)),\mathcal{D})$.}

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is a continuous transform, using the continuous mapping theorem for the almost sure or weak convergence accordingly, from (
7.6
), we have:

\frac{1}{\Xi_{n}}\mathfrak{g}\left(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\right)\to\overline{U}_{\infty}=\mathfrak{g}\left(U_{\infty}\right)\quad\text{almost surely (weakly respectively) in $(D_{0}([0,\infty)),\mathcal{D})$.}

(7.7)

Since,
\Xi_{n}\to\infty
, using (
7.5
), we have

\frac{1}{\Xi_{n}}\left\|\overline{U}_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}-\mathfrak{g}\left(U_{\lfloor n\cdot\rfloor}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\to 0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(7.8)

Combining (
7.7
) and (
7.8
), the result follows.
∎

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq\epsilon}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}\overline{S}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{\gamma+b}{b(1+b)}M_{\infty}t^{b}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq\epsilon}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\right|+\frac{\gamma+b}{b(1+b)}M_{\infty}\epsilon^{b}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{\gamma+b}{b}M_{\infty}t^{b}\right|+\frac{(\gamma+b)(2+b)}{b(1+b)}M_{\infty}\epsilon^{b}.

(7.11)

Again, the first term on the right side of (
7.11
) is negligible in
L^{2}
by Theorem
2.2
(
(c)
) and the second term on the right side of (
7.10
) is negligible in
L^{2}
as
\epsilon\to 0
, since
M_{\infty}
has finite second moment. Combining (
7.10
) and (
7.11
), we have, for any

,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|a_{n}\mu_{n}\overline{S}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\frac{\gamma+b}{b(1+b)}M_{\infty}t^{b}\right|\to_{\rm L^{2}}0,

which proves (
7.9
).

Finally, we consider the limiting covariance kernel in the subcritical case. Applying Corollary
2.9
to Theorem
7.3
, we get
\mathcal{K}{=}_{\rm d}{\sqrt{1-2b}}\,\mathfrak{g}(\mathcal{G})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is the limiting process mentioned in Theorem
2.4
and Corollary
2.9
.
Note that, for all
0\leq s\leq t
, we have

{}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v)du\,dv\right)=2\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{v}\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v))du\,dv+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v))du\,dv.

(7.12)

Plugging in (
2.1
) for
0\leq u\leq v\leq s
in the first term on the right side of (
7.12
), we have,

\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{v}\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v))du\,dv=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{b(1-b)}\left[\frac{(\gamma+b)(\gamma+1-b)}{3(2-b)(1-2b)}-\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{6}\right]s^{3},&\text{for $b\neq 0$},\\
\frac{5\gamma(\gamma+1)+2}{12}s^{3},&\text{for $b=0$}.\end{cases}

(7.13)

Again, plugging in (
2.1
) for
0\leq u\leq s\leq v\leq t
in the second term on the right side of (
7.12
), we have,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v))du\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{b(1-b)}\left[\frac{(\gamma+b)(\gamma+1-b)}{(1-2b)}\frac{s^{2-b}}{2-b}\frac{t^{1+b}-s^{1+b}}{1+b}-\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{2}s^{2}(t-s)\right],&\text{for $b\neq 0$},\\
\frac{3\gamma(\gamma+1)+2}{4}s^{2}(t-s)-\frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{2}s^{2}t\log\left(\frac{s}{t}\right),&\text{for $b=0$}.\end{cases}

(7.14)

Plugging (
7.13
) and (
7.14
) back in (

(1-2b)\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{G}(u)\mathcal{G}(v)du\,dv\right)/(st)
for
0\leq s\leq t
is given by (

).
∎

Example 8.1

We start with the memory sequence given by (

\nu_{n}=n\mu_{n+1}/(\gamma+1)
for all

, we have
a_{n}\sim\Gamma(\gamma+3/2)n^{-(\gamma+1/2)}
and
\sigma_{n}\sim\sqrt{n\log n}
, which is the spatial normalization used for
S_{n}
under the critical regime in
[

,

]
.

In general, for the explicit rates of
\sigma_{n}
, we compute the rate of
a_{n}
first, which is determined by
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\mu_{k+1}}/{\nu_{k}}
.

Lemma 8.2
.

For
p=p_{c}
,

\left\{\log a_{n}+(\gamma+1/2)(\gamma+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\mu_{k+1}}/{\nu_{k}}\right\}

is convergent.

Proof.

From (
1.4
), we have for
p=p_{c}
,

-\log a_{n}=\frac{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}}{\gamma+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\mu_{k+1}}{\nu_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Oh\left(\frac{\mu_{k+1}^{2}}{\nu_{k}^{2}}\right).

The result then follows, as
\mu_{k+1}/\nu_{k}\sim(\gamma+1)/k
from Karamata’s theorem.
∎

We first assume the memory sequence to be embedded into a regularly varying function.

Assumption 8.3
.

There exists a function
\mu:[1,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, which is eventually monotone and regularly varying of index
\gamma
, such that
\mu_{n}=\mu(n)
.

We also consider the integrated version of the function
\mu
, given by

\nu(x)=\int_{1}^{x}\mu(s)ds
,
to compare with the sequence
\{\nu_{n}\}
. By Karamata’s theorem,
\nu
is regularly varying of index
(\gamma+1)>0
and thus diverges to
\infty
. The next lemma compares the sequences
\{\mu_{n+1}/\nu_{n}\}
, appearing in Lemma
8.2
, and
\{\mu(n+1)/\nu(n+1)\}
.

Lemma 8.4
.

For
\mu
in Assumption
8.3
and its integrated version
\nu
, the sequence

\left\{\left|\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\nu_{n}}-\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\nu(n+1)}\right|\right\}

is summable.

Proof.

Using
\mu_{n+1}=\mu(n+1)
and Karamata’s theorem, first note that

\left|\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\nu_{n}}-\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\nu(n+1)}\right|=\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\nu_{n}\nu(n+1)}\left|\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}\right|=\Oh\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\mu_{n}}\right)\left|\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}\right|

(8.1)

For eventually monotone increasing
\mu
, choose
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu
is monotone increasing on
[N_{0},\infty)
. Then for all
n\geq N_{0}
,

\displaystyle\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{k}^{k+1}(\mu(x)-\mu_{k})dx\geq\nu(N_{0})-\nu_{N_{0}-1},\quad\text{and}

(8.2)

\displaystyle\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{k}^{k+1}(\mu(x)-\mu_{k})dx

\displaystyle\leq\nu(N_{0})-\nu_{N_{0}-1}+\sum_{k=N_{0}}^{n}(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k})\leq\mu_{n+1}+\nu(N_{0}).

(8.3)

Using
\nu_{n}\to\infty
and
\nu(n+1)\to\infty
, we have
|\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}|=\Oh(\mu_{n})
and the result follows from (
8.1
). For eventually monotone decreasing
\mu
, choose
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu
is monotone decreasing on
[N_{0},\infty)
. Then for all
n\geq N_{0}
, the inequalities (
8.2
) and (
8.3
) are reversed and
|\nu(n+1)-\nu_{n}|
is bounded. The result then follows from (
8.1
) as
\gamma>-1
.
∎

Lemma
8.4
allows us to rewrite Lemma
8.2
as follows.

Corollary 8.5
.

For
p=p_{c}
,
\left\{\log a_{n}+(\gamma+\frac{1}{2})(\gamma+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\mu(k+1)}/{\nu(k+1)}\right\}
is convergent.

The explicit rate of the sequence
\{a_{n}\}
and the normalization
\sigma_{n}
depends on
\ell(x)
, the slowly varying part of
\mu
, where we write
\mu(x)=x^{\gamma}\ell(x)
. We consider two broad subclasses of the function
\ell
and analyze
\sigma_{n}
in the next two subsections.

8.1.
Regularly varying function of
\log n

Some common choices of slowly varying functions are
\log x
,
(\log x)^{-1}
,
\log\log x
,
\exp((\log\log x)^{\alpha})
with
0<\alpha<1
etc., all of which are monotone, differentiable and regularly varying functions of
\log x
with the derivative being regularly varying again. We consider such functions as the slowly varying factor of
\mu
.

Assumption 8.6
.

Suppose
\mu(x)=x^{\gamma}\ell(x)
satisfies:

(i)

For some
B\geq 1
, the slowly varying function
\ell(x)=f(\log x),x\geq e^{B}
, where, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
f:[B,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is regularly varying of index
\alpha
having the form

{}f(x)=x^{\alpha}\exp\left(\int_{\log B}^{\log x}\zeta(s)ds\right),

(8.4)

where the function
\zeta
is integrable on
[\log B,x)
for all
x>0
and
\zeta(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.

(ii)

We further assume the function
\zeta
to be one of the following three types:

(a)

The function
\zeta
is the identically zero function.

(b)

The function
\zeta
is nonincreasing (and hence positive), regularly varying of index
-\rho
with
\rho\in(0,1]
, such that
\int_{\log B}^{\infty}\zeta(x)dx=\infty
, and is also eventually differentiable with monotone derivative.

(c)

The function
\zeta=-\widetilde{\zeta}
, where
\widetilde{\zeta}
satisfies the conditions in (b) above.

Remark 8.7
.

The form (
8.4
) of
f
is motivated by the Karamata’s representation of a regularly varying function. We ignore possible oscillations of the slowly varying factor. Three choices of
\zeta
in Assumption
8.6
(
ii
) correspond to the cases where the slowly varying factor of
f
is constant, diverges to
\infty
or converges to
0
.
Further, observe that for
\rho>1
, the integral in (
8.4
) converges, and can be absorbed in the constant. For simplicity, we assume the convergent factor in Karamata representation of
f
to be a constant. Further, since we consider ratios of the form
\mu(n)/\nu(n)
in Corollary
8.5
, we take the constant to be
1
. We also rule out
\rho<0
to ensure
\zeta(x)\to 0
, as required in Karamata’s representation.

Since
\zeta^{\prime}
is assumed to be eventually monotone in Assumptions
8.6
(
iib
) and
8.6
(
iic
), by the monotone density theorem (see Theorem 1.7.2 of
[
7
]
), we get
x|\zeta^{\prime}(x)|\sim\rho\zeta(x)
, as
x\to\infty
. Furthermore, we disallow
\rho=0
to guarantee
|\zeta^{\prime}|
to be regularly varying of index
-(\rho+1)
.

As
\zeta
is differentiable,
f
is also twice differentiable. We gather the formulas for
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
f
be the regularly varying function defined in (
8.4
). Then we have, for all large enough
x
,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle={(\alpha+\zeta(\log x))}\frac{f(x)}{x}\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\alpha+\zeta(\log x))(\alpha-1+\zeta(\log x))+\zeta^{\prime}(\log x)\right\}\frac{f(x)}{x^{2}}.

Further, the functions
|f^{\prime}|
and
|f^{\prime\prime}|
are regularly varying of indices
\alpha-1
and
\alpha-2
respectively, except when
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
and
\zeta
is identically zero. If
\alpha=0
and
\zeta
is the zero function, then
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
both are identically zero functions. If
\alpha=1
and
\zeta
is the zero function, then
f^{\prime\prime}
is the identically zero function.

We are now ready to obtain explicit rates for the normalization
\sigma_{n}
under Assumption
8.6
.

Theorem 8.9
.

For
\mu(x)=x^{\gamma}\ell(x)
satisfying Assumption
8.6
and for
p=p_{c}
, we have

a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\sim{C_{\mu}}{\ell_{n}^{{1}/{(\gamma+1)}}}/n
,
where
C_{\mu}
is a positive constant, possibly depending on the memory sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
. The sequence
\{v_{n}\}
is unbounded
(
respectively, bounded
)
when
\alpha+\gamma+1
is positive
(
respectively, negative
)
. Further, when
\alpha+\gamma+1>0
, we have

v_{n}^{2}\sim\frac{\gamma+1}{\alpha+\gamma+1}a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}n\log n\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{n}^{2}\sim\frac{\gamma+1}{\alpha+\gamma+1}n\log n.

Proof.

Applying integration by parts twice in succession, the expression

\nu(x)-\frac{x^{\gamma+1}f(\log x)}{\gamma+1}+\frac{x^{\gamma+1}f^{\prime}(\log x)}{(\gamma+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\gamma+1)^{2}}\int_{1}^{x}y^{\gamma}f^{\prime\prime}(\log y)dy

is a constant.
If
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
and
\zeta\equiv 0
, then
f^{\prime\prime}\equiv 0
and the integral above disappears; else, from Lemma
8.8
,
f^{\prime\prime}
is regularly varying and hence
y\mapsto f^{\prime\prime}(\log y)
is slowly varying. Karamata’s theorem gives

\int_{1}^{x}y^{\gamma}f^{\prime\prime}(\log y)dy\sim x^{\gamma+1}f^{\prime\prime}(\log x)/(\gamma+1)=\Oh\left(x^{\gamma+1}f(\log x)(\log x)^{-2}\right)
.
Combining and using Lemma
8.8
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(n)}{\nu(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma+1}{n}\left[1-\frac{\alpha+\zeta(\log\log n)}{(\gamma+1)\log n}+\Oh\left((\log n)^{-2}\right)\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma+1}{n}+\frac{\alpha+\zeta(\log\log n)}{n\log n}+\Oh\left(\frac{1}{n(\log n)^{2}}\right).

Thus, from Corollary
8.5
, we obtain

\log a_{n}+\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{k}^{-1}+\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha+\zeta(\log\log k)}{(\gamma+1)k\log k}

is convergent. The remaining sums can be replaced by the corresponding integral and a convergent error sequence using the integral test – see Theorem 8.23 of
[
2
]
. Combining,

-\log a_{n}=\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log n+\frac{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}}{\gamma+1}\left(\alpha\log\log n+\int_{\log B}^{\log\log n}\zeta(x)dx\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log C_{\mu},

which then gives

a_{n}\sim\sqrt{C_{\mu}}n^{-(\gamma+1/2)}\ell_{n}^{-(\gamma+1/2)/(\gamma+1)}
,
where
C_{\mu}
is a positive constant depending on the function
\mu
. Plugging in
\{\mu_{n}\}
, we get the rate for
a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}
.

To study boundedness of
\{v_{n}\}
, we check the summability of
\{a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\}
. We again use the integral test, for which we need to check the function
x\mapsto x^{-1}f(\log x)^{1/(\gamma+1)}
, or equivalently
x\mapsto{x^{-(\gamma+1)}}f(\log x)
to be eventually decreasing (since
\gamma+1
is positive). The derivative of the latter function is eventually negative iff (using Lemma
8.8
)

{f^{\prime}(x)}/{f(x)}=(\alpha+\zeta(\log x))/{x}<\gamma+1

eventually. This is true as the left side goes to
0
as
x\to\infty
, by Assumption
8.6
.

By the integral test, we have
\{a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\}
to be summable iff
\int_{1}^{x}s^{-1}f(\log s)^{1/(\gamma+1)}ds
converges iff
\int_{0}^{\log x}f(s)^{1/(\gamma+1)}ds
converges iff
\int_{0}^{x}f(s)^{1/(\gamma+1)}ds
converges. The condition for the boundedness of
\{v_{n}\}
then follows from the fact that the function
f
is a regularly varying function of index
\alpha
. Remaining results follow from Karamata’s theorem.
∎

8.2.
Slower than

We next consider a large class of slowly varying functions, so that the resulting SRRW-RVM grows at a rate slower than
\sqrt{n\log n}
in the critical regime. As in Section

, we consider a regularly varying function
\mu
of index

, so that
\mu_{n}=\mu(n)
and
\mu(x)=x^{\gamma}\ell(x)
. We make the following assumptions on the slowly varying

.

Assumption 8.26
.

We assume that the function
\mu(x)=x^{\gamma}\ell(x)
satisfies:

(i)

For some
B>1
, the slowly varying function
\ell(x)
has the form, for
x>B
,

\ell(x)=\exp\left(\int^{\log x}_{\log B}\delta(s)ds\right)
.

(ii)

The function
\delta
is monotone decreasing to
0
as
x\to\infty
and integrable on
(\log B,x)
for all
x>0
.

(iii)

We assume that
\int^{\infty}_{\log B}\delta(x)=\infty
and that
\delta
is regularly varying of index
-\rho
, for some
\rho\in(0,1]
. Also
\delta
is assumed to be differentiable at least
m
times where
m\rho>1
, with
\delta^{(k)}
being the derivative of the
k
-th order, for
1\leq k\leq m
, and that
|\delta^{(k)}|
is regularly varying of index
-(\rho+k)
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. (Further, denote the function
\delta
as
\delta^{(0)}
.)

Remark 8.27
.

As in Remark
8.7
, the slowly varying function
\ell
is motivated as a special case of Karamata’s representation. In particular, we assume
\rho>0
to ensure the existence of the integer
m
, which is crucial for estimating the order of
\{a_{n}\}
in Theorem
8.29
.

The slowly varying function is still given by
\ell(x)=f(\log x)
, where

f(x)=\exp\left(\int^{x}_{\log B}\delta(s)ds\right).

(8.5)

Compare (
8.5
) with (
8.4
).
Examples of such functions
\delta
include
{x^{-\rho}}
,
x^{-1}(\log x)^{-\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1
.

We show that the functions
\mu
satisfying Assumption
8.26
lead to another different class of normalizations for the corresponding SRRW-RVM in the critical regime. We estimate
\{a_{n}^{2}\mu_{n}^{2}\}
,
\{v_{n}^{2}\}
and
\{\sigma_{n}^{2}\}
under Assumption
8.26
. Since,
\delta
is differentiable
m
times, so is the function
f
, with the
k
-th derivative denoted by
f^{(k)}
, for
1\leq k\leq m
.

Lemma 8.28
.

Suppose the function
\mu
satisfies Assumption
8.26
. For
1\leq k\leq m
,

g_{k}(x):={f^{(k)}(x)}/{f(x)}-(\delta(x))^{k}

is a polynomial in
\delta^{(l)}(x)
,
0\leq l<k
, and no term is solely a power of
\delta(x)
. In particular, we also have
g_{k}(x)=\Oh(|\delta^{\prime}(x)|)
, for all
1\leq k\leq m
.

Proof.

We use induction. For
k=1
, (
8.5
) gives
f^{\prime}(x)=\delta(x)f(x)
, leading to
g_{1}\equiv 0
.

We have
f^{(k)}(x)=f(x)\left(g_{k}(x)+\delta(x)^{k}\right)
. Differentiating and using the expression for
f^{\prime}(x)
, we get

f^{(k+1)}(x)=f(x)\left(g_{k}^{\prime}(x)+k\delta(x)^{k-1}\delta^{\prime}(x)+g_{k}(x)\delta(x)+\delta(x)^{k+1}\right)
,
giving

g_{k+1}(x)=g_{k}^{\prime}(x)+k\delta(x)^{k-1}\delta^{\prime}(x)+g_{k}(x)\delta(x)
.

Assume the result holds for some
k\geq 1
. By the induction hypothesis, the last two terms for
g_{k+1}
are polynomials with the required properties. Since
\delta(x)\to 0
, they are also
\Oh(|\delta^{\prime}(x)|)
. The first term of
g_{k+1}
has the same properties due to the properties of
g_{k}
. Comparing the indices of regular variation,
g_{k}^{\prime}(x)=\Oh(|\delta^{\prime}(x)|)
proving the induction step.
∎

Theorem 8.29
.

Let the function
\mu
satisfy Assumption

\{\mu_{n}\}
. Further, if
\int_{1}^{\infty}f(s)^{1/(\gamma+1)}ds=\infty

v_{n}^{2}\sim C_{\mu}\int_{1}^{\log n}f(s)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}ds\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}^{2}\sim{n}{\ell_{n}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\int_{1}^{\log n}f(s)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}ds.

Proof.

Successive applications of integration by parts gives us,

\nu(x)=\frac{x^{\gamma+1}}{\gamma+1}\left[f(\log x)+\sum_{k=2}^{m}(-1)^{k-1}\frac{f^{(k-1)}(\log x)}{(\gamma+1)^{k-1}}\right]+\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(\gamma+1)^{m}}\int_{0}^{x}s^{\gamma}f^{(m)}(\log s)ds+\Oh(1).

(8.6)

Using

and
\delta(\log x)^{m}
slowly varying, and Lemma

\int_{0}^{x}s^{\gamma}(|\delta^{\prime}(\log s)|+\delta(\log s)^{m})f(\log s)ds\sim x^{\gamma+1}(|\delta^{\prime}(\log x)|+\delta(\log x)^{m})\ell(x)/(\gamma+1)

Then, from (

\displaystyle\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\gamma+1}\ell(x)}{\gamma+1}\left[1+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{\delta(\log x)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{k}+\Oh\left(\delta^{\prime}(\log x)\right)+\Oh\left(\delta(\log x)^{m}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{x\mu(x)}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\delta(\log x)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{-1}\left[1+\Oh\left(\delta^{\prime}(\log x)\right)+\Oh\left(\delta(\log x)^{m}\right)\right].

Using

is regularly varying of index

and thus

Then Corollary
8.5

9.1.
Law of large numbers for SRRW-RVM

The results in Section
4
are proved under the finite variance assumption on the innovation sequence. To treat general zero mean innovations, we consider the centered and truncated step sizes given by

\widetilde{X}_{n}:=X_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|\leq n]}-\mathbb{E}(X_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|\leq n]}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})
. Then the corresponding truncated versions of the martingales
M_{n}
and
L_{n}
are given by

\widetilde{M}_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}\widetilde{X}_{k}
and

\widetilde{L}_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}_{k}
.

We first extend the proof of Lemma
4.1
. It suffices to prove almost sure and
L^{1}
convergence under the assumption of zero mean innovation sequence.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Note the sequence
\{a_{n}\nu_{n}\}
is regularly varying of index
(1-p)(\gamma+1)
, is increasing and diverges to infinity. A careful restatement of Theorem 2.19 of
[
11
]
gives

\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}{\widetilde{M}_{n}}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}{\widetilde{L}_{n}}\to 0\quad\text{almost surely and in $L^{2}$.}

(9.1)

Due to the Borel-Cantelli lemma, only finitely many terms in the tail errors contribute:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\mu_{n}|X_{n}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|>n]}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|X_{n}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|>n]}<\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.

(9.2)

Next, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|X_{n}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|>n]}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|\xi_{1}|>n]})+\frac{p}{\nu_{n-1}}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\mu_{l}|X_{l}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{l}|>n]}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|\xi_{1}|>n]})+\frac{p}{\nu_{n-1}}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\mu_{l}|X_{l}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{l}|>l]}.

The first term goes to
0
as
\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1}|)<\infty
. By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, only finitely many terms contribute in the second sum, which is therefore negligible almost surely. Then, using Toeplitz lemma and Cesaro averaging, we have

\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}(|X_{k}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(|X_{k}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\to 0

(9.3)

almost surely.

Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}(|X_{n}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{n}|>n]})=\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|\xi_{1}|>n]})\to 0
, Toeplitz lemma and Cesaro averaging give

\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}(|X_{k}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]})\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(|X_{k}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]})\to 0.

(9.4)

Finally, consider the decompositions

\displaystyle\frac{L_{n}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{L}_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})

\displaystyle\frac{M_{n}}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{M}_{n}}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}+\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}X_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}-\frac{1}{a_{n}\nu_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}(X_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}).

Applying (
9.1
) - (
9.4
), we get the required almost sure and
L^{1}
convergence for
L_{n}/n
and
M_{n}/(a_{n}\nu_{n})
.
∎

Due to Remark
4.2
, we need to consider Theorem
2.1
for process
L^{1}
convergence only.

Proof of Theorem

(Process

For process

]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\widetilde{L}_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}^{2}}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left(|X_{k}|\mathbbm{1}_{[|X_{k}|>k]}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor-1}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|M_{k}|)}{a_{k}\nu_{k}},

Appendix A

In this appendix, we provide two auxiliary results, related to the regularly varying sequences
\{a_{n}\}
,
\{\mu_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}
. The first lemma considers the growth rate of certain sums.

Lemma A.1
.

For
p=\widehat{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\eta_{n}-{\eta}_{k})

\displaystyle\sim(\gamma+1)n,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{n}-{\eta}_{k})

\displaystyle\sim(\gamma+1)^{2}a_{n}\nu_{n},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{n}-{\eta}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2(\gamma+1)^{2}n.

Proof.

In the case
p=\widehat{p}
, we rewrite the factor
(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})
as
\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}1/{(a_{j}\nu_{j})}
and interchange the order of the summation, followed by applying Karamata’s theorem twice in succession. The first two assertions then follow easily. We provide the details of the third assertion for illustration. In this case, we need extra care to handle the square of
(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})
. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(\eta_{n}-\eta_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{j}^{2}\nu_{j}^{2}}+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\frac{a_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}}{a_{i}\nu_{i}a_{j}\nu_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{a_{j}^{2}\nu_{j}^{2}}v_{j}^{2}+2\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}\nu_{i}a_{j}\nu_{j}}.

(A.1)

We get the required result as the first term of (
A.1
) satisfies

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}v_{j}^{2}/(a_{j}^{2}\nu_{j}^{2})\sim(\gamma+1)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{-1}\sim(\gamma+1)^{2}\log n=\oh(n)

using Karamata’s theorem, while the second term of (
A.1
) satisfies

2\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}\nu_{i}a_{j}\nu_{j}}\sim 2(\gamma+1)\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{a_{i}\mu_{i}}{a_{j}\nu_{j}}\sim 2(\gamma+1)^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}1\sim 2(\gamma+1)^{2}n,

using Karamata’s theorem twice in succession.
∎

The next result uses the uniform convergence of regularly varying functions on compact intervals.

Lemma A.2
.

For
p=\widehat{p}={\gamma}/({\gamma+1})
, and for all
0<s<t<\infty
, we have

\left(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\right)a_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\to(\gamma+1)\log({t}/{s}).

Proof.

For
p=\widehat{p}
,
\{a_{n}\mu_{n}\}
is slowly varying. Then, by Proposition 0.5 of
[
16
]
, for any
0<s<t<\infty
, we have

\sup_{\lfloor ns\rfloor\leq k\leq\lfloor nt\rfloor}\left|\frac{a_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}}{a_{k}\mu_{k}}-1\right|=\sup_{u\in[s,t]}\left|\frac{a_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}}{a_{\lfloor nu\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor nu\rfloor}}-1\right|\to 0.

Using this uniform convergence, we get

(\eta_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-\eta_{\lfloor ns\rfloor})a_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}=\sum_{k=\lfloor ns\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor-1}(a_{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mu_{\lfloor ns\rfloor})/(a_{k}\nu_{k})\sim(\gamma+1)\sum_{k=\lfloor ns\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor-1}1/k\sim(\gamma+1)\log({t}/{s}).
