The Correlation between Inflammatory Markers and Carotid Atherosclerotic Plaque Characteristics
Authors: Lin Wang, Tong Chen, Chun Yuan, Thomas Hatsukami, Xihai Zhao, Mi Shen, Peiyi Gao, Shengjun Sun, Xingquan Zhao, Binbin Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to explore the relationship between serum inflammatory biomarkers and the carotid atherosclerotic plaque characteristics, given prior evidence suggesting a key role of inflammation in the development of atherosclerosis.MethodsIn this prospective study, patients with carotid atherosclerotic plaque were recruited. Serum high-sensitivity CRP (Hs-CRP), homocysteine (Hcy) concentrations, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) were obtained for all enrolled patients. Carotid atherosclerosis characteristics (such as intraplaque hemorrhage [IPH] and lipid-rich necrotic core [LRNC]) were determined by three-dimensional high-resolution vessel wall imaging. The associations between Hs-CRP, Hcy, NLR, and plaque characteristics were assessed.ResultsIn total, 128 patients (84.4% men; mean age, 58.0 ± 8.7 years) were included. Multivariate logistic regression indicated that increased Hs-CRP levels were associated with the presence of LRNC (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.07–1.40, p = 0.003) and IPH (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.10–1.45, p = 0.001). Multivariate linear regression confirmed a significant correlation between Hs-CRP level (β = 3.24, 95% CI: 0.66–5.81, p = 0.014) and the IPH volume. For plaque burden, higher Hs-CRP levels were associated with larger max normalized wall index (NWI) (β = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.00–0.02, p = 0.005) and larger Max wall thickness (β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.14, p = 0.006). NLR and Hcy levels did not show significant associations with the carotid plaque characteristics.ConclusionsElevated Hs-CRP levels were found to be closely associated with plaque burden and vulnerable plaque characteristics. The relationship between the elevated Hs-CRP and plaque vulnerability highlights its potential role in risk stratification and early intervention strategies. Further validation in larger, multicenter population studies is required to confirm these associations.

Introduction

Ischemic strokes resulting from carotid atherosclerosis represent 15–20% of all cases [1]. Individuals with carotid plaque faced a greater risk of all-cause death than those without the condition. Furthermore, even with guideline-directed therapy, stroke survivors remain vulnerable to recurrent strokes and coronary events [2–4]. Crucially, the composition and vulnerability of carotid plaques, rather than merely their presence, are key determinants of ischemic events. Based on previous experience, high-risk plaques (HRPs) are defined by features such as a large lipid-rich necrotic core (LRNC), intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), and ulceration, which predispose them to rupture, and subsequent thrombus formation and distal embolization [5–7].

Apart from traditional risk factors, inflammation is considered a key factor in the development of atherosclerosis and thrombosis [8]. Consequently, there is significant interest in identifying serum inflammatory biomarkers associated with carotid disease. Reviews suggest that markers including lipids, interleukins, homocysteine (Hcy), and adipokines may reflect plaque-related risk [8]. Notably, circulating leukocyte counts and their ratios, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), are also recognized as accessible indicators of systemic inflammation [9]. The association between raised high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP), a classical inflammatory marker, and increased carotid intima-media thickness has been confirmed by meta-analyses [10].

Advances in vascular imaging now allow for the noninvasive assessment of these HRP features. Three-dimensional high-resolution vessel wall imaging (3D HR-VWI) has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing plaque morphology and composition [11]. Multi-contrast VWI techniques provide detailed visualization of components like LRNC and IPH, showing high consistency with histopathological findings, enabling the quantification of atherosclerotic burden and specific compositional features [11]. However, the specific relationship between the key circulating inflammatory markers (such as NLR, Hs-CRP, and Hcy) and the characteristics of carotid plaque composition observed on VWI remains incompletely defined.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between levels of NLR, Hs-CRP, and Hcy and the compositional characteristics of carotid atherosclerotic plaques, as assessed by 3D VWI. We hypothesized that elevated levels of NLR, Hs-CRP, and Hcy are associated with the presence and extent of high-risk compositional features in carotid plaques.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

Post hoc analysis was performed on a prospective study, with the study design described elsewhere [12]. We included adult patients presenting with carotid atherosclerotic plaque, defined by 3D HR-VWI. The following exclusion criteria were used: (1) unavailable or incomplete Hs-CRP, Hcy, NLR, or HR-VWI data; (2) suspected cardiogenic embolism or cerebral infarction secondary to cerebral hemorrhage, trauma, tumor, abnormal clotting mechanism, aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation, or unknown etiology; (3) receipt of thrombolysis, emergency interventional therapy, or stenting in symptomatic arteries; (4) non-cerebrovascular disease with focal or global brain symptoms, or unclear diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease; (5) severe inflammatory diseases such as acute and chronic infections, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and/or cirrhosis; (6) recent trauma, surgery, severe heart failure, liver or kidney insufficiency, autoimmune disease, hematologic disease, and/or peripheral vascular disease; (7) contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

All patients underwent bilateral carotid 3D HR-VWI scans within 7 ± 3 days of admission. Arteries located ipsilateral to ischemic symptoms were designated as symptomatic arteries following a validated method [13].

Clinical Assessment

Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected from the clinical record. Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus were defined according to previous literature. Coronary heart disease is a condition due to atherosclerosis that generally leads to angina pectoris or myocardial infarction. Family history of stroke refers to stroke occurrence in one or more of the patient’s first-degree relatives. Additionally, smoking history (past or current), aspirin use, and statin use were also recorded.

Image Analysis Protocol

All carotid MRI examinations were conducted on a 3T scanner (Trio-Tim, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with an 8-channel head-carotid surface coil (TSImaging Healthcare, Beijing, China). The imaging protocol consisted of three-dimensional time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography, pre-/post-contrast T1-weighted SPACE sequences, and three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequences. Detailed acquisition parameters for these sequences are summarized in the online supplementary Table S1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551280).

The standardized evaluation of all VWI datasets was conducted by two neuroradiologists specializing in VWI interpretation, with blinding to clinical data. Interobserver differences were resolved by consensus, with low-quality images excluded from subsequent analyses. Qualified images underwent quantitative processing with specialized software (TSImaging Healthcare, Beijing, China).

The carotid artery structures – including lumen boundaries, outer wall contours, and plaque components – were manually segmented slice by slice in the axial plane. Key parameters measured included lumen area, wall area, maximum wall thickness (Max WT), and the maximum cross-sectional areas of IPH, LRNC, and calcification. Derived parameters included total vessel area (lumen area + wall area) and normalized wall index (NWI = [wall area/total vessel area]×100%). Luminal stenosis severity was quantified using TOF-MRA reconstructions with maximum intensity projection postprocessing, adhering to NASCET criteria [14]. Two independent reviewers classified plaque components using multi-contrast MRI protocols [15]: IPH – hyperintense signal >150% muscle signal intensity on TOF-MRA, T1-SPACE, and MPRAGE sequences; calcification – hypointense signals across all imaging sequences; LRNC – isointensity on TOF-MRA and T1-SPACE, and hypointensity on post-T1-SPACE.

Laboratory Analysis

On admission to the emergency department or hospital ward, all laboratory tests were performed using blood collected from the anterior cubital vein after a 12-h fast. Serum levels of routine blood parameters, lipids, Hs-CRP, and Hcy were measured by standard test methods. The NLR represents the ratio between the absolute neutrophil count and the absolute lymphocyte count.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were analyzed statistically using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]). For categorical variables, data are shown as numbers with percentages (%). Linear and logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the associations between inflammation markers and plaque characteristics. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated with adjustments in all regression analyses for age, gender, BMI, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and prior use of aspirin and statins. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 128 patients with carotid atherosclerotic plaques were included. Baseline characteristics revealed a mean age (58.0 ± 8.7) years, with 108 (84.4%) male participants. Hs-CRP had a median level of 1.5 (IQR 0.66–3.35) mg/L, NLR had a median level of 2.36 (IQR 1.71–3.21), and Hcy had a median level of 12.0 μmol/L (IQR 9.90–16.48). Discussion

This research aimed to investigate the relationship between systemic inflammatory markers and carotid atherosclerotic plaque characteristics. After accounting for a range of conventional cardiovascular risk factors, high Hs-CRP levels remained significantly associated with carotid atherosclerotic plaque characteristics such as the presence of IPH and LRNC in this study. However, we did not find correlations between NLR and Hcy levels and carotid plaque characteristics.

In this cohort of patients with carotid atherosclerotic plaques, elevated Hs-CRP levels were significantly correlated with the occurrence of both LRNC and IPH. These cross-sectional findings indicate an association between vulnerable plaque characteristics and elevated systemic inflammatory activity, as reflected by hs-CRP. In previous studies, components such as IPH and LRNC were often accompanied by more severe plaque inflammation [16, 17]. Consistent with previous research by Garcia et al. [18], they observed that increased hs-CRP levels may be related to the presence of macrophages and T lymphocytes in plaque, which is associated with the phenomena of instability that can lead to development of an ischemic event. A recent PARISK study identified novel associations between Lp(a) concentrations and carotid atherosclerotic plaque characteristics [19]. The study found that elevated plasma Lp(a) levels were associated with higher prevalence of IPH and LRNC. Although Hs-CRP was the serum inflammation marker, similar conclusions were obtained, it was further verified that inflammation played a role in the process of atherosclerosis. These parallel findings lend further support to the hypothesis that low-grade chronic inflammation, as indicated by levels of the inflammatory marker CRP, prospectively defines risk of atherosclerotic complications, thus adding to prognostic information provided by traditional risk factors [20, 21].

At the same time, our multivariate analyses revealed that Hs-CRP maintained significant positive associations with IPH volume after comprehensive adjustment for confounders. Our study also demonstrated the clinical value of Hs-CRP in plaque burden. Consistent with our results, Newby et al. [22] also indicated that Hs-CRP functions as an inflammatory biomarker that promotes the development and advancement of atherosclerosis, leading to increased plaque burden. The higher the level of Hs-CRP, the larger the NWI and Max WT, while both NWI and Max WT are important indicators to evaluate the plaque burden. Lu et al. [17] found that Hs-CRP participates in the inflammation within plaques and shows a significant positive correlation with carotid plaque NWI in a study of seventy-one carotid plaques. As one of the important components of the plaque, the presence of IPH may lead to a sudden increase in plaque burden.

However, our study did not find associations between inflammatory biomarkers and the occurrence of calcification. Despite extensive research, the role of calcification in carotid plaques remains complex and controversial. There remains considerable heterogeneity in the results of previous studies. Some studies have indicated that calcification is a marker of stable plaques [23], while others have demonstrated that calcification location and morphology are imaging markers of unstable plaques [24–26]. In addition to this, different calcification morphologies may be associated with different levels of inflammation [27]. It should be noted that the present study did not differentiate between subtypes of calcification. This lack of differentiation may have obscured potential associations between specific calcification patterns, inflammatory activity, and plaque vulnerability. Future studies that incorporate a detailed classification of calcification morphology are warranted to clarify these relationships. Therefore, the relationship between inflammatory markers and plaque calcification is complex and unknown, and further studies are needed to explore it.

Besides, our results indicated that Hcy levels and NLR were not associated with the presence or volume of plaque components and overall plaque burden. Despite this, NLR remains an important marker and mediator of systemic inflammation [28]. As a cost-effective and readily accessible marker, NLR is simple to assess. Ruan et al. [29] reported a correlation between NLR levels and carotid plaque instability among 528 patients who experienced cerebral infarction. NLR may represent a promising inflammatory marker for predicting the likelihood of unstable carotid plaques. Another investigation also uncovered correlations between NLR and carotid artery stenosis [30]. In addition to this, previous studies also highlighted the importance of elevated serum Hcy in driving the advancement of atherosclerotic disease [31, 32]. In a cohort of hypertensive Chinese patients, Hcy was shown to play a significant role in the development and progression of carotid atherosclerosis [33]. The discrepancy between our findings and some previous studies reporting an association between NLR and plaque instability might be attributed to differences in the study population, particularly the phase of the ischemic event. The inflammation, including neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, is dynamic after a stroke. Studies focusing on the hyperacute or acute phase may observe a stronger correlation with plaque features compared to a cohort like ours, which, while symptomatic, may represent a broader range of post-event timelines.

The occurrence of atherosclerosis is a complex process with many factors involved. More studies with larger sample sizes to further explore the relationship between Hcy and NLR and plaque characteristics are warranted.

In this study, our findings highlight the association of Hs-CRP levels with carotid plaque vulnerability. Hs-CRP, functioning as an acute-phase protein and marker of inflammation, can increase dramatically up to 10,000 times its normal plasma concentration response to infectious or inflammatory stimuli and tissue damage [34]. Existing literature supports the role of Hs-CRP as a reliable inflammatory biomarker in ischemic stroke, highlighting its relevance in cerebrovascular pathology. Clarke et al. [35] suggested that CRP facilitates the formation and progression of atherosclerosis. Our findings align with and extend this understanding by providing direct evidence linking Hs-CRP levels to specific HRP components (IPH, LRNC) and burden metrics (NWI, MWT).

This study still has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may have resulted in insufficient power to demonstrate significant associations between inflammatory markers (Hs-CRP, Hcy, NLR) and certain plaque characteristics. This is particularly relevant for Hcy, a marker influenced by numerous genetic, nutritional (e.g., vitamin B levels), and lifestyle factors, which we could not fully account for. The lack of association observed for Hcy may be attributed to these unmeasured confounders or limited statistical power. Moreover, the predominance of male participants (84.4%) in our cohort limits the generalizability of our findings to female populations. The association between Hs-CRP and plaque characteristics observed here warrants further validation in studies with balanced sex distribution or those conducting sex-stratified analyses. Although a larger sample size might have yielded more robust findings, the current results still offer valuable insights into the associations between inflammation and plaque composition. Second, serum inflammatory markers were measured only once, upon hospital admission. This single measurement may not fully capture the chronic inflammatory state or potential fluctuations over time that could be relevant to plaque vulnerability and long-term risk. Measuring biomarkers at the acute stage of ischemic events may more precisely characterize the inflammatory milieu associated with symptomatic plaques. Additionally, despite our efforts to exclude patients with major inflammatory conditions, trauma, or surgery, unmeasured or residual confounding factors (e.g., subclinical infections, undiagnosed autoimmune conditions, or other sources of inflammation not captured in medical records) could potentially influence both systemic inflammatory marker levels and plaque characteristics. Finally, as a single-center study conducted at a tertiary referral hospital in China, our cohort may not fully represent the broader population of patients with carotid atherosclerosis in terms of ethnic diversity, geographic distribution, and healthcare settings. This may affect the generalizability of our findings. Multicenter studies involving diverse populations are needed to confirm and extend our observations. The consistency of our Hs-CRP findings with established biological pathways linking inflammation to plaque vulnerability lends biological plausibility to the observed associations, mitigating concerns about confounding to some extent.

Conclusions

In this study, Hs-CRP levels were significantly correlated with high-risk carotid plaque characteristics, and the extent of plaque burden. These findings suggest that Hs-CRP may represent a sensitive biomarker for identifying high-risk atherosclerotic plaques. However, while elevated Hs-CRP levels indicate systemic inflammation related to atherosclerosis, they may also reflect the presence of unstable plaques with hemorrhage and necrotic cores. Thus, high Hs-CRP levels might be more a consequence of plaque instability than a direct causal factor in plaque development. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm our findings and assess the association between multiple inflammatory markers and carotid atherosclerosis, as well as their role in the pathogenesis and progression of vulnerable plaques.
