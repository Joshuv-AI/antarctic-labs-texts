Paradox-free classical non-causality and unambiguous non-locality without entanglement are equivalent Preprint: APS/123-QED
Hippolyte Dourdent, Kyrylo Simonov, Andreas Leitherer, Emanuel-Cristian Boghiu, Ravi Kunjwal, Saronath Halder, Remigiusz Augusiak, Antonio Acín

Abstract
Closed timelike curves (CTCs) challenge our conception of causality by allowing information to loop back into its own past. Any consistent description of such scenarios must avoid time-travel paradoxes while respecting the no-new-physics principle, which requires that the set of operations available within any local spacetime region remain unchanged, irrespective of whether CTCs exist elsewhere. Within an information-theoretic framework, this leads to process functions: deterministic classical communication structures that remain logically consistent under arbitrary local operations, yet can exhibit correlations incompatible with any definite causal order—a phenomenon known as non-causality. In this work, we provide the first complete recursive characterization of process functions and of (non-)causal process functions. We use it to establish a correspondence between process functions and unambiguous complete product bases, i.e., product bases in which every local state belongs to a unique local basis. This equivalence implies that non-causality of process functions is exactly mirrored by quantum nonlocality without entanglement (QNLWE)—the impossibility of perfectly distinguishing separable states using local operations and causal classical communication—for such bases. Our results generalize previous special cases to arbitrary local dimensions and any number of parties, enable systematic constructions of non-causal process functions and unambiguous QNLWE bases, and reveal an unexpected connection between certain non-signaling inequalities and causal inequalities.

At their core lies a tension between events that have already occurred and the freedom of agents to influence them, a tension that has fueled a long-standing debate across physics and philosophy. Stephen Hawking famously argued that time-travel should be impossible, proposing the so-called chronology protection conjecture to “make the universe safe for historians”

More nuanced accounts were offered by philosophers such as David Lewis, who, within his modal realist framework, argued that consistency can be restored through the Leibnizian notion of “compossible facts”, i.e. possible facts that can coexist with one another: what an agent can do relative to one set of facts may be impossible relative to another, more comprehensive, set that includes further facts
53

40
;
57

Analyses such as the billiard-ball-through-a-wormhole scenario
38
demonstrated that apparently inconsistent setups can nonetheless admit self-consistent solutions. This led to the formulation of the “no new physics” principle
40
, which asserts that the physical laws (in particular, the set of possible physical operations) within any local spacetime region remain unchanged, regardless of whether CTCs exist elsewhere
20
. Building on this idea, several consistent models have been proposed to investigate the interaction of quantum systems with operational, information-theoretic analogs of spacetime CTCs. Two prominent examples are the Deutsch-CTC (D-CTC) model
32
, motivated by the Everett interpretation, and the Post-selected-CTC (P-CTC) model
61
;
21
;
67
, inspired by quantum teleportation and based on post-selecting a Bell effect to implement a quantum channel to the past. While these frameworks successfully avoid causal paradoxes, this is achieved at the cost of abandoning either the “no new physics” principle or the assumption of freedom of choice, that is, the ability of agents to perform arbitrary operations in locally well-defined regions. Nevertheless, an alternative approach rooted in the study of quantum causality offers a framework free from these pathologies.

The notion of indefinite causal orders originates from the causaloid formalism

;
47

This framework demonstrates that indefinite causal orders can emerge in scenarios involving spatially separated parties that control laboratories in which quantum theory holds locally - they are allowed to perform free local quantum operations - while no assumptions on a fixed global spacetime structure are made. In this information-theoretic context, local events are defined by classical outcomes of quantum operations in the laboratories.
Causal relations between two events are then defined as one-way non-signaling constraints that encapsulate the order of operations. (For analysis and discussions on the relation between information-theoretic and space–time causal structures, cf. Ref

In particular,

2
through condition
(ii)
, which yields a complete recursive characterization of process functions. To better understand why this additional requirement is necessary—and how it eliminates these inconsistencies—it is helpful to examine the causal structure underlying a process.

We represent the causal structure of a process as a directed graph, where nodes correspond to parties
\{A_{k}\}_{k}
and directed edges encode functional dependencies between outputs and inputs. For the tripartite GYNI process, this graph forms a causal loop
A_{1}\to A_{3}\to A_{2}\to A_{1}

\bm{w}_{3-GYNI}^{a_{1}}

a_{1}
removes the dependence of
x_{1}

A_{2}
to
A_{1}
. The resulting reduced graph
A_{1}\to A_{3}\to A_{2}
therefore appears one-way signaling. This masking effect occurs whenever a single arrow is removed from a loop, and the same reasoning applies in the
n-
partite case.

Consequently, the characterization of Ref.
(
68
, Theorem 2)
can be made sufficient only by additionally requiring that reduced functions obtained from interventions that preserve signaling structure—namely, non-erasing local operations, with the identity channel
\mathrm{Id}_{k}:x_{k}\rightarrow a_{k}:=x_{k}
as the simplest example (when

)—are valid process functions.

Finally, in Appendix
H
, we show that the tripartite characterization of Ref.
(
13
, Theorem 3)
, while not explicitly stating condition
(ii)
, implicitly assumes it by interpreting reduced parties as classically controlling (or “switching”) the causal order between the remaining parties. This interpretation coincides with the set of admissible—i.e., logically consistent—tripartite causal structures identified in Ref.
69

III.2
), it constrains it to intertwined classical switches and excludes trivial non-causal structures such as global loops. The resulting characterization captures precisely the non-causal tripartite processes, in which each party can influence the causal order between the other two while remaining non-self-signaling.

III.2
Causal and non-causal process functions

From unambiguous product bases to process functions

First, we demonstrate that process functions can be constructed from unambiguous product bases. Let us consider an

VI
Discussion

In this work, we have established an equivalence between
process functions
, namely the class of one-round deterministic classical communication that generate valid probability distributions under arbitrary local operations, and
unambiguous complete product bases
, namely product bases in which every local state belongs to a unique local basis. While the latter notion was originally introduced in quantum information theory, where local indistinguishability of product states has found applications in tasks such as data hiding
33
;
39
, the former arose in a very different context: as a paradox-free, information-theoretic model of CTCs
16
;
68
, and more generally as the class of deterministic classical processes with indefinite causal order that remain logically consistent. In particular, process functions are precisely those deterministic classical communication structures that relaxed the idea of well-defined causal structures while still being in accordance with the
no-new-physics
and
freedom-of-choice
principles.

By establishing a direct correspondence between these two frameworks, our results open new avenues in both directions, enabling systematic constructions and analyses that were previously unavailable, as well as a new interpretation of these phenomena.

On the one hand, our second construction (Theorem
8
) establishes a direct bridge between unambiguous QNLWE bases and process functions.
In particular, since all bipartite process functions are necessarily causal, our result implies that
no bipartite unambiguous QNLWE basis can exist
, generalizing the well-known fact that there is no two-qubit product basis exhibiting QNLWE
72
to arbitrary dimensions (as unambiguity holds trivially for qubits).
More generally, this construction provides a straightforward method for certifying whether a given quasi-process function is valid, simply by testing whether it can be encoded in an unambiguous product basis via our formalism.

On the other hand, our first construction (Theorem
6
) offers new insight into process functions through the lens of unambiguous product bases, allowing the systematic generation of new examples of non-causal process functions.
Remarkably, this construction is formally identical to that introduced by Augusiak, Acín
et al.
on Bell inequalities without violations
12
;
11
, where the unambiguity condition is introduced as “property (P),”.
For all non-causal process function maximally violating the causal inequality Eq. (
19
), one can also associate a Bell inequality without violation—specifically, a nonsignaling inequality of the form
\sum_{\bm{i}}P(\bm{i}|\bm{o}=\bm{w}(\bm{i}))\leq 1
.
Such inequalities can be viewed as instances of
local orthogonality
inequalities
41
, derived from the multipartite principle that events involving distinct outcomes of the same local measurement must be exclusive, implying that the sum of probabilities of mutually exclusive events cannot exceed unity.
Consequently, nontrivial local orthogonality inequalities arising from UPBs
12
;
11
will always correspond to causal inequalities that can be maximally violated only by invalid quasi-process functions.
For example, the tripartite GYNI UPB mentioned in section
III.1
is associated with a global causal loop quasi-process.
From our result, every causal inequality constructed from a UPB cannot be maximally violated by process functions.

Beyond this conceptual equivalence, we have provided the first complete recursive characterizations of process functions (Theorem
2
) and of (non-)causal process functions (Theorem
4
). These characterizations yield practical and easily verifiable criteria for membership, thereby enabling a more systematic study of logically consistent classical processes with indefinite causal order, in both information-theoretic and space-time frameworks. Let us emphasize, however, that the physical interpretation of these seemingly exotic cyclic information-theoretic causal structures may depend on how they are embedded into spacetime
71
.

We also note that our analysis is restricted to process functions acting on finite-dimensional systems.
It is worth noting that process functions can also be defined for continuous variables
13
.
Furthermore, while we focused here on deterministic cases, there also exist statistical and dynamical classical processes compatible with logical consistency that are non-causal
51
.
Such processes are expected to admit a correspondence with separable measurements—an extension we leave for future work.

Our results provide a new interpretative framework for for process functions and their possible non-causality, formulated in terms of event labeling.
The unambiguity condition ensures that any pair of global events is pairwise exclusive, and that each local state
\mathinner{|{\psi^{j}_{k}}\rangle}\in\mathcal{S}^{(k)}
is uniquely and unambiguously labeled by a pair of classical variables
(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})
, corresponding to a local output and input, respectively.
In contrast, the completeness of
\mathcal{S}
guarantees that every global state
\mathinner{|{\psi^{j}}\rangle}\in\mathcal{S}
, and its associated global event
(\bm{a}^{j}|\bm{x}^{j})
, can be uniquely identified by the collection of classical outputs
\bm{a}^{j}
alone.
In other words, while local states require fine-grained labeling—both
a_{k}^{j}
and
x_{k}^{j}
are necessary for their identification—global states admit a coarse-grained labeling based solely on their output strings
\bm{a}^{j}
.
Phrased differently, the identification of local events is setting-dependent, whereas the identification of the pairwise exclusive global events they compose is in general setting-independent.
Our results show that this specific event-labeling structure precisely captures the condition of logical consistency for process functions.
Furthermore, non-causality and QNLWE arise exactly when no party (or, in the non-genuine case, no output-reduced subset of parties) generates a trivial local event set of the form
(a_{k}|x_{k}=x_{0})_{a_{k}}
; that is, when no party’s local events can be identified in a coarse-grained, setting-independent manner from outputs alone.
In such cases, the fine-grained labeling remains essential for all parties, reflecting the intrinsically non-causal structure of the underlying process.

Finally, Steffinlongo and Dourdent recently showed that the SHIFT basis—equivalently, the Lugano process—can also be implemented using local operations with quantum control of classical communication (LOSupCC)
65
.
Moreover, various Boolean process functions without a global past can be transformed into such a resource.
This observation opens a pathway toward defining a hierarchy of deterministic classical communication classes, together with a corresponding hierarchy of unambiguous product bases, in direct analogy with the framework introduced in Ref.
75
.
Our results provide a natural foundation for such a program, by offering a principled generalization to arbitrary local dimensions.
This, in turn, could shed new light on the operational advantages of indefinite causal order in information-processing tasks beyond the standard LOCC paradigm.

Proof of Lemma
3

Any non-causal process function

Fix such a deterministic strategy and such a causal order. Then there exists a party

In order to bound the success probability

\displaystyle P_{\rm succ}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{k}d_{k}}\Bigl(\sum_{\bm{i}:i_{l}\neq i^{\prime}}P(\bm{o}=\bm{w}(\bm{i})|\bm{i})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\bm{i}:i_{l}=i^{\prime}}P(\bm{o}=\bm{w}(\bm{i})|\bm{i})\Bigr).

(53)

For the first term, each probability is at most
1
, and there are

such inputs because the local external input of party

\sum_{\bm{i}:i_{l}\neq i^{\prime}}P(\bm{o}=\bm{w}(\bm{i})|\bm{i})\leq(d_{l}-1)\prod_{k\neq l}d_{k}.

For the second term, we restrict to inputs with

\displaystyle P_{\rm succ}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\prod_{k}d_{k}}\left((d_{l}-1)\prod_{k\neq l}d_{k}+\prod_{k\neq l}d_{k}-1\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{k}d_{k}}\left(d_{l}\prod_{k\neq l}d_{k}-1\right)

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{k}d_{k}}\left(\prod_{k}d_{k}-1\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\prod_{k}d_{k}}.

(59)

Since this bound holds for every extremal causal strategy (and therefore for every causal correlation by convexity), the optimal value achievable by causal correlations is

\beta^{C}=1-\frac{1}{\prod_{k}|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}.

(60)

|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{S}^{(k)}_{x_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{(k)}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}
, so that

|\phi\rangle=|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle=|(a^{\prime}_{k}|x^{\prime}_{k})\rangle,

(79)

for some
a_{k},a^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
. Within a fixed setting
x_{k}
, (
30
) ensures that distinct vectors are orthogonal. Hence, for any
|(b_{k}|x_{k})\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
with
b_{k}\neq a_{k}
,

\langle(b_{k}|x_{k})|\phi\rangle=0,

(80)

because
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
. But since the same vector
|\phi\rangle
lies in
\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}
. By symmetry, we obtain the inverse inclusion
\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
as well, so that

\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}=\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}.

(81)

Therefore, the assumption that
x_{k}
and
x^{\prime}_{k}
are distinct setting labels is wrong, and we conclude that the intersection of
\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
must be empty.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be unambiguous. Then
\mathcal{S}
satisfies pairwise exclusivity: for any distinct
|\psi^{j}\rangle,|\psi^{j^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
x_{k}^{j}=x_{k}^{j^{\prime}}
and
a_{k}^{j}\neq a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let
|\psi^{j}\rangle,|\psi^{j^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathcal{S}
with
j\neq j^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is an orthonormal basis, we obtain:

\langle\psi^{j}|\psi^{j^{\prime}}\rangle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\langle{(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})}|(a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}|x_{k}^{j^{\prime}})\rangle=0.

(82)

Therefore, there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that

\langle(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})|(a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}|x_{k}^{j^{\prime}})\rangle=0.

(83)

Due to (
30
), local vector are orthogonal only if their settings match:

x_{k}^{j}=x_{k}^{j^{\prime}}.

(84)

On the other hand, (
30
) guarantees that, for any
|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle,|(a^{\prime}_{k}|x_{k})\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
for some
x_{k}
and
a_{k}\neq a_{k}^{\prime}
, we have

\langle(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})|(a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}|x_{k}^{j})\rangle=0,

(85)

which, together with completeness of

means that
\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
is an orthonormal basis. Therefore, (

) implies that
a_{k}^{j}\neq a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}

∎

We proceed by showing that unambiguity of
\mathcal{S}
provides existence of a unique map between joint outcomes and joint settings in the following Lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be unambiguous. Then there exists a unique function
\bm{w}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
such that

\mathbf{W}(\mathcal{S})=\{(\bm{a}|\bm{x}):\bm{x}=\bm{w}(\bm{a})\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X},

(86)

where
\mathcal{X}=\bigtimes_{k}\mathcal{X}_{k}
and
\mathcal{A}=\bigtimes_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Proof.

For each party
k
, consider the labelled local set
\mathcal{S}^{(k)}
where the labels
(x_{k},a_{k})
are assumed fixed in advance. Define the local evaluation map that associates an event to local state

\mathbf{E}_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{(k)}\to\mathcal{A}_{k}\times\mathcal{X}_{k},\qquad\mathbf{E}_{k}(|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle)=(a_{k}|x_{k}),

(87)

which is injective by construction of the labels. Set
\mathbf{E}:=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{k}
.

Next, define the local-factor map

\mathbf{L}:\ \mathcal{S}\longrightarrow\bigtimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{S}^{(k)},\qquad\mathbf{L}(|\psi^{j}\rangle)=\{|(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n},

(88)

where
(a_{k}^{j}|x_{k}^{j})
is the unique label of
|\psi_{j}^{(k)}\rangle
. By Lemma
10
, every local vector of every basis element appears with a unique setting–outcome label, and therefore no two distinct global basis vectors share the same local vector. Hence
\mathbf{L}
is injective.

Define now the global event map

\mathbf{W}:=\mathbf{E}\circ\mathbf{L}:\ \mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X},\qquad\mathbf{W}(|\psi^{j}\rangle)=(\bm{a}^{j}|\bm{x}^{j}),

(89)

which is injective, since both
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{L}
are injective. Consider an arbitrary joint outcome
\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}
and the corresponding fiber

\mathcal{F}(\bm{a}):=\{\bm{x}\!:(\bm{a}|\bm{x})\in\mathbf{W}(\mathcal{S})\}.

(90)

Suppose,
ad absurdum
, that there exist two distinct vectors
|\psi^{j^{\prime}}\rangle\neq|\psi^{j}\rangle
in
\mathcal{S}
, both associated with the same joint outcome
\bm{a}
but with different joint settings
\bm{x^{\prime}}\neq\bm{x}
. Then, by Lemma
11
, there exists a party
k
at which the local vectors
|\psi^{j}_{k}\rangle
and
|\psi_{k}^{j^{\prime}}\rangle
share the same setting
x_{k}=x_{k}^{\prime}
but different outcomes
a_{k}\neq a_{k}^{\prime}
, which contradicts that the two events have identical joint outcome
\bm{a}
. Therefore, each
\mathcal{F}(\bm{a})
contains at most one element, that is,
|\mathcal{F}(\bm{a})|\leq 1
.

Because
\mathcal{S}
is a complete orthonormal product basis, we have
|\mathcal{S}|=\prod_{k}d_{k}=|\mathcal{A}|
, and since
\mathbf{W}
is injective,

|\mathbf{W}(\mathcal{S})|=\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}|\mathcal{F}(\bm{a})|=|\mathcal{A}|.

(91)

With every fiber having size at most one and the sum equal to
|\mathcal{A}|
, it follows that

|\mathcal{F}(\bm{a})|=1\qquad\forall\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}.

(92)

Thus, for every joint outcome
\bm{a}
, there exists a unique joint setting
\bm{x}
such that
(\bm{a}|\bm{x})\in\mathbf{W}(\mathcal{S})
. We therefore define the map

\bm{w}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X},\qquad\bm{w}(\bm{a})=\bm{x}.

(93)

Uniqueness of
\bm{w}
follows immediately from the uniqueness of each fiber.

Appendix E
Unambiguity and weak unambiguity

Although we adopt (
30
) as the working definition of unambiguity, it can be relaxed by requiring only the structural properties established in Lemmas
10
and
11
. This leads to a weaker notion introduced below.

Definition 13

(Weak unambiguity)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a complete
n-
partite orthonormal product basis. It is called weakly unambiguous if: (i) for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and any two distinct settings
x_{k}\neq x^{\prime}_{k}
,

\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}\cap\mathcal{S}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}^{(k)}=\emptyset,

(109)

and, (ii) for any distinct basis vectors
|\psi^{j}\rangle,|\psi^{j^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that

x_{k}^{j}=x_{k}^{j^{\prime}},\qquad a_{k}^{j}\neq a_{k}^{j^{\prime}}.

(110)

By construction, every unambiguous basis in the sense of (
30
) is weakly unambiguous, and every weakly unambiguous basis
\mathcal{S}
determines a process function
\bm{w}
. However, the converse does not hold: not every weakly unambiguous basis is unambiguous. Consider the bipartite basis:

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(0|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(0|0)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},

(111)

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(0|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(1|0)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},

(112)

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(0|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(2|0)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{2}\rangle},

(113)

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(1|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(0|1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\mathinner{|{0+1+2}\rangle},

(114)

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(1|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(1|1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\mathinner{|{0-1}\rangle},

(115)

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{(1|0)}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(2|1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}(\mathinner{|{0+1}\rangle}-2\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}),

(116)

where
\mathinner{|{0+1+2}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}+\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}+\mathinner{|{2}\rangle})
, and
\mathinner{|{0\pm 1}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}\pm\mathinner{|{1}\rangle})
. The corresponding local settings are

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\},

(117)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\},

(118)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathinner{|{0+1+2}\rangle},\mathinner{|{0-1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{0+1}\rangle}-2\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}.

(119)

The local settings within each party are non-overlapping and satisfy the disjointness condition of Lemma
10
, and pairwise exclusivity (Lemma
11
) can be verified directly by inspection of the indices
x_{k}
and
a_{k}
. Nevertheless, it is not unambiguous in the sense of (
30
), because some vectors belonging to different local settings are accidentally orthogonal, e.g.,
|2\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{0}^{(2)}
and
|0-1\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(2)}
.

Despite this distinction, it suffices to work with unambiguous bases. The following Lemma shows that any weakly unambiguous basis can be made unambiguous by suitable local rotations, without altering the corresponding process function.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be weakly unambiguous but not unambiguous, and let
\bm{w}
denote its associated process function. is the associated process function. Then there exists an unambiguous basis
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with process function
\bm{w}^{\prime}
such that

\bm{w}^{\prime}=\bm{w}.

(120)

Proof.

If
\mathcal{S}
is weakly unambiguous but not unambiguous, then there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and distinct settings
x_{k}\neq x_{k}^{\prime}
for which

\langle(a_{k}|x_{k})|(a^{\prime}_{k}|x^{\prime}_{k})\rangle=0

(121)

for some
a_{k},a_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
. Since each
\mathcal{S}_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
is an orthonormal basis on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}
, one may redefine it via a local unitary
V_{x_{k}}^{(k)}\in\mathbf{U}(d_{k})
such that

|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle\mapsto V_{x_{k}}^{(k)}|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle.

(122)

chosen so that overlaps between vectors belonging to different settings become nonzero. Such unitaries exist generically since the set of
V_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
leaving any given overlap between local vectors from different settings zero has measure zero in
\mathbf{U}(d_{k})
. With finitely many constraints of the form
\langle(a_{k}^{\prime}|x_{k}^{\prime})|V_{x_{k}}^{(k)}|(a_{k}|x_{k})\rangle\neq 0
for all
a_{k},a_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
and distinct
x_{k}\neq x_{k}^{\prime}
, almost every choice of
V_{x_{k}}^{(k)}
satisfies all constraints simultaneously. These local transformations preserve orthonormality within each setting, the product structure of the global basis
\mathcal{S}
, and all event labels
(a_{k}|x_{k})
. Consequently, the set
\mathbf{W}(\mathcal{S})
and its induced process function remain unchanged, yielding an unambiguous basis
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with process function
\bm{w}^{\prime}=\bm{w}
.
∎

Appendix F
Different ambiguous product bases and their connection to process functions

In this section, we examine various cases of ambiguous product bases and their relation to process functions.

F.1
Ambiguous QNLWE product basis generating a non-causal quasi-process function

The canonical example of QNLWE is known as the domino basis
23
, the two-qutrit QNLWE basis

\displaystyle\{\mathinner{|{0+}\rangle},\mathinner{|{0-}\rangle},\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle},\mathinner{|{+^{\prime}0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{11}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-2}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-^{\prime}0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2+^{\prime}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2-^{\prime}}\rangle}\}

(123)

The associated local operations are projections in
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{k}=0}
,
\{\mathinner{|{+}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{k}=1}
or
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{+^{\prime}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-^{\prime}}\rangle}\}_{x_{k}=2}
for both Alice and Bob, with
\mathinner{|{\pm}\rangle}=(\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}\pm\mathinner{|{1}\rangle})\sqrt{2}
and
\mathinner{|{\pm^{\prime}}\rangle}=(\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\pm\mathinner{|{2}\rangle})\sqrt{2}
. This example does not satisfy unambiguity (
30
). Therefore, the local label
(a_{k}|x_{k})
of both
\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}
is ambiguous.

This labeling ambiguity can lead to a violation of the pairwise exclusivity of events (
31
), despite each state being orthogonal. Consider for instance the two states
\mathinner{|{11}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle}
. They are orthogonal, since
\mathinner{\langle{1|2}\rangle}=0
in the computational basis. The associated events, written in the form
(a_{1}a_{2}|x_{1}x_{2})
, should be
(11|00)
and
(02|10)
respectively, which are orthogonal—i.e., they share at least one common local input, with distinct outputs assigned to it. However, the event associated with
\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle}
could actually also be
(02|11)
, which is not orthogonal to
(11|00)
. This ambiguity arises because the state
\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}
can be obtained in two different measurement contexts.

In fact, one can verify that there is no globally consistent assignment of events for all the states in the domino basis that would satisfy Eq.(
31
): for example,
\mathinner{|{11}\rangle}
enforces the label
(02|10)
for
\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle}
, while
\mathinner{|{0+}\rangle}
enforces the label
(02|11)
, leading to a contradiction.

Nevertheless, it is possible to construct a non-valid quasi-process function from this basis: the two-way-signaling process
(x_{1}:=a_{2}\oplus_{3}2,x_{2}:=a_{1}\oplus_{(2)}1)
, where
\oplus_{3}
is the addition modulo 3, and
\oplus_{(2)}
the operation that flips 0 and 1 into one another while leaving 2 unchanged. To verify this, one can retrieve the domino basis from this non-valid process using the local unitaries
U^{x_{k}=0}=\mathbbm{1}
,
U^{x_{k}=1}=H\oplus\mathbbm{1}
(Hadamard on the
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\}
subspace) and
U^{x_{k}=2}=\mathbbm{1}\oplus H
(Hadamard on the
\{\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}
subspace) for both parties, which, when applied to the computational basis, correspond to projections in the corresponding local bases. Note that under unitaries satisfying the unambiguity constraint Eq.(
47
), the process no longer generates a valid basis.

F.2
Ambiguous product basis generating a valid process function

Consider the following product basis in
2\otimes 3
.

\displaystyle\{\mathinner{|{00}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{01}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{02}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{1+}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{1-}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{12}\rangle}\},

(124)

where the qubit belongs to Alice and the qutrit belongs to Bob. Alice performs a local projection in
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\}_{x_{1}=0}
, while Bob has two choices:
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{2}=0}
and
\{\mathinner{|{+}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{2}=1}
. The product basis is ambiguous, as
\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}
appears in both bases
x_{2}=0
and
x_{2}=1
. Thus unambiguity (Eq.(
30
)) is not satisfied. The associated events
(a_{1}a_{2}|x_{1}x_{2})
are:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathinner{|{00}\rangle}\rightarrow(00|00),&\mathinner{|{1+}\rangle}\rightarrow(10|01),\\[4.30554pt]
\mathinner{|{01}\rangle}\rightarrow(01|00),&\mathinner{|{1-}\rangle}\rightarrow(11|01),\\[4.30554pt]
\mathinner{|{02}\rangle}\rightarrow(02|00)/(02|01),&\mathinner{|{12}\rangle}\rightarrow(12|01)/(12|00).\\
\end{array}

(125)

Unlike the domino example, here pairwise exclusivity of events (
31
) is always satisfied, and a specific labeling
(\mathinner{|{02}\rangle}\rightarrow(02|00))
,
(\mathinner{|{12}\rangle}\rightarrow(12|01))
give rise to a valid process function,
(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=a_{1})
, which correspond to a one-way signaling from Alice to Bob. However, the other possible labels do not give a valid function
x_{2}=w_{2}(a_{1})
.

F.3
Ambiguous local product basis generating a non-causal quasi-process function

Consider another product basis in
2\otimes 3
, given as the following:

\{\mathinner{|{00}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{01}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{1+}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{1-}\rangle},\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle},~\mathinner{|{-2}\rangle}\}

(126)

where on both sides there are more than one local bases. On Alice’s side,
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}\}_{x_{1}=0}
and
\{\mathinner{|{+}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-}\rangle}\}_{x_{1}=1}
. On Bob’s side,
\{\mathinner{|{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|{1}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{2}=0}

\{\mathinner{|{+}\rangle},\mathinner{|{-}\rangle},\mathinner{|{2}\rangle}\}_{x_{2}=1}

Let us recall that two-way signaling is a form of non-causality forbidden in single round scenarios, as it can give rise to logical antinomies. Thus this basis cannot be discriminated by single-round LOCC, as it involves two-way signaling. However, unlike the domino basis, the basis Eq.(

from
\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}
(without distinguishing
\mathinner{|{0}\rangle}
from
\mathinner{|{1}\rangle}

\mathinner{|{+2}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{-2}\rangle}

a_{2}\neq 2

a_{1}=0

\mathinner{|{00}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{01}\rangle}

a_{1}=1

\mathinner{|{1+}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{1-}\rangle}
.

F.4
Conclusions

As unambiguity is always satisfied with qubits, all qubit product bases are associated with valid process functions. In the qudit case, unambiguity is not systematic. When it is not satisfied, various examples arise: some ambiguous product bases which exhibit QNLWE are never associated with valid process functions, such as the domino basis (cf.
F.1
). Some ambiguous product bases that do not exhibit QNLWE can admit a label with locally orthogonal events associated with a valid process function (cf.
F.2
), while others cannot (cf.
F.3
). In the latter case, the basis is not locally measurable by single-round LOCC nor LOPF, but can nevertheless be measured by multi-round LOCC: in other words, bases of this form could be deemed “single-round non-local”.
We leave a more general and systematic study of these instances for future work.

b
. Hence,
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
is a valid bipartite process function for all
f_{A}
. By analogous reasoning,
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
and
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
are valid for all
f_{B}
and
f_{C}
, which together imply that the tripartite function
\bm{w}
is valid.

Note however that if
A_{C}\setminus(A_{C}\cap A_{B})=\emptyset
(which can occur if
A_{C}=A_{B}
or
A_{C}=\emptyset)
the argument breaks down, as we cannot conclude that
a\notin A_{B}\Rightarrow a\in A_{C}\setminus(A_{C}\cap A_{B})
, which was required to prove that a non-constant first component implies the second component is constant. The global loop
\bm{w}(a,b,c)=(c,a,b)
is such an example. Its output reduced functions are given by
\bm{w}^{a}(b,c)=(a,b)
,
\bm{w}^{b}(a,c)=(c,b)
,
\bm{w}^{c}(a,b)=(c,a)
, which are valid bipartite functions. In the case of
\bm{w}^{a}
, for example, there is no value of
a
for which the first second is constant, therefore
A_{C}=\emptyset
. However, the global loop does not satisfy the fixed point condition.

Note that the non-self-signaling argument from Case A) does not depend on the whether the sets are empty or not, thus we do not need to reprove it for every case.

Case B)
Now let us assume that
A_{C}\setminus(A_{C}\cap A_{B})=\emptyset
, which can occur if and only if
A_{B}=A_{C}
or
A_{C}=\emptyset
.

Case B.1)
If
A_{B}=A_{C}=A
, this corresponds to a scenario where
\bm{w}_{y}(a,c)\equiv\bm{w}_{y}(a)
and
\bm{w}_{z}(a,b)\equiv\bm{w}_{z}(a)
. The reduced functions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{A}}(b,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{y}(f_{A}(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c))),\bm{w}_{z}(f_{A}(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c))))

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{B}}(a,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(f_{B}(\bm{w}_{y}(a)),c),\bm{w}_{z}(a))

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{C}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,f_{C}(\bm{w}_{z}(a))),\bm{w}_{y}(a))

while the output reduced functions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{a}(b,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{y}(a),\bm{w}_{z}(a)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{b}(a,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c),\bm{w}_{z}(a)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{c}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c),\bm{w}_{y}(a)).

Note that
\bm{w}^{a}
is valid. Validity of
\bm{w}^{b}
implies that either
\bm{w}_{z}(a)=z_{0}
, or that
\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)=\bm{w}_{x}(b)
, or both. Validity of
\bm{w}^{c}(a,b)
implies that either
\bm{w}_{y}(a)=y_{0}
or
\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)=\bm{w}_{x}(c)
, or both. Let us go through both cases implied by the validity of
\bm{w}^{b}
:

•

\bm{w}_{z}(a)=z_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
are valid as they will have at least one constant component. The last one is given by
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}(a,b)=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,f_{C}(z_{0})),\bm{w}_{y}(a))
. If
\bm{w}^{c}
is valid, then either
\bm{w}_{y}(a)=y_{0}
or
\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)=\bm{w}_{x}(c)
:

–

\bm{w}_{y}(a)=y_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
is valid as the second component is constant.

–

\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)=\bm{w}_{x}(c)
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
is valid as the first component is constant.

•

\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)\equiv\bm{w}_{x}(b)
.

The validity of
\bm{w}^{c}(a,b)=(\bm{w}_{x}(b),\bm{w}_{y}(a))
implies that either
\bm{w}_{x}
or
\bm{w}_{y}
is constant:

–

\bm{w}_{x}(b)=x_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
have the first component constant, therefore they are valid. Lastly,
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}(b,c)=(\bm{w}_{y}(f_{A}(x_{0})),\bm{w}_{z}(f_{A}(x_{0})))
is also valid as both components are constant.

–

\bm{w}_{y}(b)=y_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
are valid as they have at least one constant component;
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}(a,c)=(\bm{w}_{x}(f_{B}(y_{0})),\bm{w}_{z}(a))
is also valid as the first component is constant.

Therefore, for
A_{B}=A_{C}=A
, validity of
\bm{w}^{a}
,
\bm{w}^{b}
,
\bm{w}^{c}
for all
a,b,c
implies the validity of
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
for all
f_{A},f_{B},f_{C}
.

Case B.2)
If
A_{C}=\emptyset
, then
A=A_{B}
. Therefore,
\bm{w}_{y}(a,c)=\bm{w}_{y}(a)
. The reduced functions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{A}}(b,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{y}(f_{A}(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c))),\bm{w}_{z}(f_{A}(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)),b)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{B}}(a,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(f_{B}(\bm{w}_{y}(a)),c),\bm{w}_{z}(a,f_{B}(\bm{w}_{y}(a)))),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{C}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,f_{C}(\bm{w}_{z}(a,b))),\bm{w}_{y}(a)),

and the output reduced functions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{a}(b,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{y}(a),\bm{w}_{z}(a,b)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{b}(a,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c),\bm{w}_{z}(a,b)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{c}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(b,c),\bm{w}_{y}(a)).

Now let us consider two possibilities, either
\bm{w}_{y}(a)
is constant,
\bm{w}_{y}(a)=y_{0}
, or it is a function of
a
:

•

\bm{w}_{y}(a)=y_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
are valid as they have at least one constant component. The validity of
\bm{w}^{b}
implies that for any value
f_{B}(y_{0})\in B=B_{A}\cup B_{C}
, the reduced function
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}(a,c)=(\bm{w}_{x}(f_{B}(y_{0}),c),\bm{w}_{z}(a,f_{B}(y_{0})))
is one-way-non-signaling, thus valid.

•

\bm{w}_{y}(a)
is a function of
a
. Then the validity of
\bm{w}^{c}
implies
\bm{w}_{x}(b,c)=\bm{w}_{x}(c)
. Now, either
\bm{w}_{x}(c)
is constant,
\bm{w}_{x}(c)=x_{0}
, or it is a function of
c
:

–

\bm{w}_{x}(c)=x_{0}
. Then
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
are valid as their first components are constant. Lastly,
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}=(\bm{w}_{y}(f_{A}(x_{0})),\bm{w}_{z}(f_{A}(x_{0}),b))
is valid as the first component is constant.

–

\bm{w}_{x}(c)
is a function of
c
. Then the validity of
\bm{w}^{b}
implies that
\bm{w}_{z}(a,b)\equiv\bm{w}_{z}(b)
. All-together, we have arrived at a loop:
\bm{w}(a,b,c)=(\bm{w}_{x}(c),\bm{w}_{y}(a),\bm{w}_{z}(b))
, which we know cannot be ruled out as invalid just through the validity of outout-reduced functions.

Therefore, except in the case of the loop, validity of
\bm{w}^{a}
,
\bm{w}^{b}
,
\bm{w}^{c}
implies the validity of
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{B}}
,
\bm{w}^{f_{C}}
.

In the case of the loop, the output reduced functions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{A}}(b,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{y}\circ f_{A}\circ\bm{w}_{x}(c),\bm{w}_{z}(b)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{B}}(a,c)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}(c),\bm{w}_{z}\circ f_{B}\circ\bm{w}_{y}(a)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{f_{C}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w}_{x}\circ f_{C}\circ\bm{w}_{z}(b))),\bm{w}_{y}(a)),

If
\bm{w}^{f_{A}}
is valid for all
f_{A}
, then either
\bm{w}_{x}(c)
or
\bm{w}_{y}(a)
or
\bm{w}_{z}(b)
is constant. By inspection, if one of them is constant and the other two are functions, then all reduced functions are valid.
