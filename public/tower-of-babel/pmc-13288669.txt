Active deformation in the basal organ of Corti in gerbil
Authors: Kaitlyn H. Wong, C. Elliott Strimbu, Elizabeth S. Olson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Optical coherence tomography has allowed in vivo recording of sound-induced vibrations of different regions within the organ of Corti complex (OCC), including the basilar membrane (BM), outer hair cell/Deiters cell (OHC/DC) region, and reticular lamina (RL). In the hook region of the gerbil cochlea, where measurements can be made with a predominantly transverse optical axis, the three regions have different and characteristic motion responses: The OHC/DC region has greater motions than the other two regions at frequencies below the best frequency (sub-BF); the RL region typically has the greatest BF peak and smallest sub-BF motion. The phase of the OHC/DC-region motion increasingly lags BM motion phase as frequency increases; the RL-region motion phase leads BM, but with a relatively small value. All three regions are compressively nonlinear in the BF peak, but only the OHC/DC region shows sub-BF compressive nonlinearity. Here, we describe the strains that arise when the motion transitions between these regions. Large strains (deformations) were observed in the OHC body close to the RL. A region of large strain can be as short as a single 2.7-μm measurement pixel, or it can extend over several pixels, with extensive strains appearing more often at 70 than at 50 dB sound pressure level. Beyond this RL region of large strain, over a distance that can exceed 20 μm, the OHC/DC region displayed nearly spatially unvarying motion; this region vibrated as a relatively rigid body. And beyond this, at sub-BF frequencies, another region of high strain was present, indicating stretching/compressing or bowing/tilting of the DC microtubular stalk.

Significance

The sensory tissue of the cochlea responds actively to a sound stimulus: cell-based forces amplify and enhance the vibration of the sensory tissue. Measurements employing optical coherence tomography have identified major vibration patterns along a sensory-tissue-spanning line that includes the active outer hair cells (OHCs). The motion is nonuniform, and strains exist as vibration morphs from one region to the next. The largest strains were close to the apical, stereociliary side of the OHC, while the basal-OHC/Deiters cell region moved almost as a rigid body. The base of the Deiters cell, tethering OHCs to the basilar membrane, underwent substantial strain. Strain patterns were sound level dependent, echoing the nonlinearity of cochlear responses. These findings guide and constrain models of cochlear amplification.

Introduction

In the cochlea, a sound is represented in the pattern of vibration along the long, narrow strip of sensory tissue, the organ of Corti complex (OCC, which refers to cells of the organ of Corti and the acellular basilar membrane [BM] and tectorial membrane [TM] that bind it). To first order, the sound is sorted by frequency, with high frequencies represented in the cochlear base and low frequencies in the cochlear apex. In gerbil, the sensory tissue is ∼11 mm long, with width and thickness about 200 μm × 100 μm, but these vary longitudinally to produce the cochlea’s place-frequency map.1 Figure 1 shows a labeled schematic and a labeled brightness scan (B-scan) of a transverse/radial cross section of the OCC. The BM is a major structural element of the OCC, composed of radial collagen fibers and a ground substance.2 The TM is a gel-like, collagenous structure containing many specialized proteins.3,4 The inner and outer pillar cells (PCs) are microtubule-based structural cells that connect the BM to the apex of the OC, the reticular lamina (RL).5,6 OHCs are supported by Deiters cells (DCs), which cup the OHCs at their base; this region, containing the nuclei of both cells, is referred to as the OHC/DC region. The DCs contain microtubular structures that extend from the DC feet at the BM to form the DC cup and then extend further as the phalangeal processes, which run from the DC cup to the RL with a transverse/longitudinal slant.7 The RL comprises the apical surfaces of the PCs, the actin-rich termination of DC phalangeal processes, and the cuticular plates of the OHCs and inner hair cells.5 The cuticular plates are composed of a dense matrix of F-actin, one to a few micrometers in thickness. HC stereocilia are anchored into the cuticular plate by rootlets that penetrate deeply into the plate.8,9,10 In this paper, we describe the dynamic strains (strains produced due to the vibratory response to sound) that exist along a line that includes the OHCs due to the motion response to sound stimulation.Figure 1Anatomy of the organ of Corti complex as visualized with OCT. In the classic description of the cochlear response to sound stimulation, transverse motion of the OCC creates shearing radial motion between the RL and TM, causing HC stereocilia to pivot back and forth, modulating the open probability of the mechanoelectrical transduction (MET) channels at the stereocilia tips, and thus the flow of cations through the HCs. This modulates the transmembrane voltage, and in OHCs, it activates a piezoelectric protein, prestin, that causes the OHCs to contract and expand and/or exert contracting/expanding forces.11,12,13,14 This OHC action, termed electromotility, is thought to be the fundamental process enhancing the motion of the OCC in a place- and frequency-dependent manner, which is known as cochlear amplification. Prestin is distributed throughout the OHC basolateral membrane, but not at the cuticular plate.10,15 The basal pole contains prestin at a reduced density compared with the lateral wall, and charge movement associated with electromotility was measured as greatest at the center of the OHC’s longitudinal extent, with a gradual tapering off toward the apical and basal ends.16 In measurements where the OHC was in a microchamber with one end depolarized and the other hyperpolarized by a voltage applied between the chamber and the bath, one side contracted and the other lengthened.17 Stimulating an OHC with a patch electrode produced a longitudinal strain of up to 1% for 20 mV of quasi-DC stimulation,18 while AC stimulation with a microchamber produced motions of ∼1 nm/mV for OHCs of length ∼50 μm, corresponding to strains of ∼2 × 10−5/mV.17,19 A primary goal of the present paper was to observe the quantitative and qualitative character of in vivo sound-induced strains in the OCC, particularly the OHC region.

In past decades, in vivo measurements of sound-evoked motion in the cochlear base were taken solely at the BM because optical techniques could not penetrate within the sensory tissue. BM motion was tuned at the local best frequency (BF) and was compressively nonlinear with input sound pressure level (SPL). Nonlinearity was limited to frequencies near the BF, serving to greatly enhance the BF peak at low-moderate SPL. This observation of tuned amplification was interesting with respect to OHC electromotility, because electromotility as measured in isolated OHCs was broadband.19 With the advent of optical coherence tomography (OCT) (and a related method developed by T. Ren20), sound-evoked motions of intra-OCC structures became measurable. One of the first important new findings was that in the OHC region, amplification was broadband, extending sub-BF.20,21 These boosted, nonlinear sub-BF responses were in keeping with the broadband action of electromotility and could be seen as in vivo evidence of electromotility.

OCT builds an image by measuring the reflectivity in a line along the optical axis. To form a two-dimensional (2D) B-scan image, the optical axis is swept perpendicular to the axial scan’s (A-scan) optical axis, and to form a familiar transverse-radial B-scan image (Figure 1B), the optical axis should be primarily transverse and the scanning radial. Measurements in the hook region of the gerbil cochlea (∼45 kHz BF) made with a primarily transverse view produced a familiar B-scan image, in which the RL region and faint TM could be identified.22,23,24 OCT can also measure vibrations along the direction of the optical axis. These measurements revealed three basic forms of regional motion: (1) BM displacement peaked and was compressively nonlinear at frequencies around the local BF. (2) The OHC/DC region peaked at the local BF at low/moderate SPL, was low pass at moderate/high SPL, and showed wideband compressive nonlinearity. An increasing phase lag with frequency relative to (re:) BM motion accumulated ∼0.75 cycle at frequencies close to the BF. (3) RL motion peaked and was compressively nonlinear at frequencies around the local BF. The peak displacement was larger at the RL than at the BM or OHC/DC region. Sub-BF, RL motion could be smaller than the motion at the BM and display expansive nonlinearity at the highest SPL measured (80 dB). At the RL, the phase typically led the BM by less than 0.25 cycle throughout the frequency range of measurement.

In this contribution, we explore the transitions between the BM, OHC/DC, and RL regions by displaying tuning curves and 1D strain maps from many points within an A-scan that spans the regions. The optical axis was primarily transverse with a smaller negative (basal-pointing) longitudinal component. To give a brief preview of findings, close to and within the RL region and extending into the OHC body, strains could be large due to the transition between RL-like and OHC-like motion that occurred over just one or two imaging pixels (2.7 μm/pixel). Close to the RL, at frequencies around the BF peak, the strain was in phase with RL displacement. About 15 μm from the RL surface of the OHC region, over an A-scan expanse that could exceed 30 μm, a region of nearly uniform, sub-BF amplified motion was observed. Considering previous studies,25,26 this was likely the OHC/DC region, swinging approximately perpendicular to the longitudinally slanting phalangeal processes.

Materials and methods

Animal preparation

Adult Mongolian gerbils (Meriones unguiculatus) of both sexes were used. The experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Columbia University. The gerbils were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injections of ketamine (40 mg/kg) and sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). The gerbils were given the analgesic buprenorphine (0.2 mg/kg) at the start of the surgery and then between 6 and 8 h after the surgery. Lidocaine (2%) was administered subcutaneously at the start of the surgery as a local anesthetic. To maintain an even plane of anesthesia, supplemental doses of pentobarbital were administered throughout the experiment. A tracheotomy was performed to facilitate breathing, and oxygen was sometimes flowed over the tracheotomy. Temperature was maintained with a temperature-controlled warming blanket supplemented with warming lamps and disposable hand warmers. The head was attached to a two-dimensional (2D) goniometer using dental cement to allow for small adjustments to the head position. The pinna, most of the cartilaginous ear canal (EC), and tissue covering the tympanic bulla were removed. A drop of 38% phosphoric acid gel (Pulpdent, Waterford MA) was placed on the bulla for ∼5 min to weaken the bone, and the bulla was gently opened using forceps to view the round window (RW). The cochlear condition was assessed and monitored using 2f1-f2 distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) in response to tones f1 and f2 with a ratio of f2/f1 = 1.2. The tones were presented at 50 and 70 dB SPL. The DPOAE responses were robust and indicative of healthy cochleae (Figure S1 in supplemental information). Once the experiment concluded, the gerbils were euthanized with pentobarbital.

Acoustic stimulation and recording

Acoustic stimuli were delivered closed field to the EC using a Radio Shack dynamic speaker. A Sokolich ultrasonic microphone was placed ∼1 mm away from the tympanic membrane and coupled to the speaker tube to measure DPOAEs and EC pressures. Multitone stimuli were generated by a Tucker Davis Technologies System 3, which operated at a 130-kHz sampling rate (130,208.33 Hz). The multitone stimuli used were zwuis tone complexes, comprising 35 approximately equally spaced frequencies chosen so that no harmonics or distortion products overlap with a primary frequency up to the third order.27,28 The zwuis stimuli were delivered for 1 s. Measurements were made at SPLs ranging from 40 to 80 dB SPL (for each individual tone in the complex) in 10-dB steps.

For OCT, a Thorlabs Telesto 320 spectral domain OCT system with a center wavelength of 1,300 nm and a 5X LSM03 objective lens was used to perform imaging and vibrometry. According to the manufacturer, the system has axial and lateral resolutions in water of ∼4 and 10 μm, respectively. The axial resolution is set by the light source bandwidth and the lateral resolution by the objective lens. The imaging acquisition process starts with a 1D reflectivity map (the A-scan noted above) along the depth of the preparation, the optical z axis. Adjacent pixels in an A-scan are ∼2.7 μm apart. The OCT system uses scanning mirrors to sweep the optical beam along the x or y axis, producing a 2D reflectivity image, the B-scan. If the OCT system sweeps the optical beam in the third orthogonal direction, it yields a 3D volumetric image.

At the beginning of each experiment, the ThorImage program was used to orient the preparation and identify the regions of interest within the OCC. All measurements were made through the RW to obtain a view of the OCC that was primarily transverse in the hook region, where the BF is ∼45 kHz. A series of A-scans recorded at 130 kHz at the same location with concurrent acoustic stimulation produces a motion scan (M-scan), which simultaneously captures sub-pixel displacements of structures along the A-scan. An objective of the current study was to find spatial displacement variations, and a finely spaced data set was desired. Along an A-scan, all of the pixels within the OCC were selected for vibration analysis. The vibrometry data of each pixel were deemed significant if the amplitude at the stimulus frequency was above the noise floor by at least 2.5 times the standard deviation of the noise. The noise floor was determined from a set of adjacent points in the time-frequency Fourier transform. In G986 from an earlier period of experiments, a subset of A-scan pixels was chosen to analyze for displacement. This resulted in a relatively sparse, but still useful, data set for this study. B-scans were acquired before and after each vibrometry measurement and evaluated to ensure that the position of the preparation had not shifted significantly during the recording. Custom C++ software developed using the Thorlabs Spectral Radar software development kit was used for data collection and initial processing of vibrometry data. Further analysis and post-processing were performed in MATLAB. A 3D volumetric image and the planar approximation algorithm29 were used to determine the anatomical longitudinal, radial, and transverse (l,r,t) components of the optical axis for each experiment; the results are included in the figure captions. (The (l,r,t) components form a unit vector; they add in quadrature to 1 [or close to 1 due to rounding]. For example, l,r,t values of −0.6, 0.1, 0.8 would indicate that the measured motion was the sum of −0.6 times the longitudinal component of motion, 0.1 times the radial component of motion, and 0.8 times the transverse component of motion.)

Sumdiff and sumorig are found as follows: sumdiff was found as the magnitude of the frequency-by-frequency complex difference between the two curves, which were then summed across frequency. Sumorig was found as the magnitude values of each of the two original curves summed across frequency, and these two values were averaged. If sim was greater than 0.7, by this metric, the two curves were greater than 70% similar and were considered to be within the same region (OHC/DC, RL, or BM) and were color-coded similarly—green, orange, or blue, respectively. If sim was less than 0.7, the two adjacent curves were considered to be in different groups. The fraction (sumdiff/sumorig) could be greater than 1 and sim would be negative; in those cases, the adjacent curves were clearly in different regional groups. Using sim <0.7 to separate regions was relaxed for a few curves; these are identified with an asterisk in Table S1, which is located in the supplemental information. Transitional curves, with sim slightly less than 0.7, are plotted with dashed lines. To our knowledge the “sim” metric is not a standard metric. It is based on the calculation for finding the area between two data sets (the sumdiff term). Dividing by the sumorig term makes the output dimensionless. The sim metric was useful for color-coding the responses into different regions for illustration. (The selection of the 0.7 cutoff was subjective; it did a reasonable job of grouping the 70-dB SPL frequency responses as one would sort them by eye. The metric-based color-coding was only used in the frequency response curves of displacement; it played no role in the heat maps of displacement and strain.)

We report from four gerbil cochleae, with a fifth in supplemental information. These preparations are used because responses were out of the noise at many pixels along an A-scan. The RL, BM, and OHC/DC-region responses observed here are consistent with those in previous studies from the gerbil hook region.22,23,24 What is new here is the observation and analysis of transitions between regions. Animal numbers are for laboratory record-keeping purposes.

Results

Introductory observations

Figure 2 shows displacements from the BM, RL region, and OHC/DC region from G1047. The OHC/DC-region and RL-region responses are plotted along with the BM responses to facilitate comparisons. The OHC/DC region (Figures 2C, 2E, and 2G) initially is in phase with the BM at the lowest measured frequencies and then lags the BM with a phase delay that increases with increasing frequency (Figure 2G). At 80 dB SPL, the phase of the OHC/DC region often shifts up to become in phase with the BM at higher frequencies, and a notch appears in the response magnitude, seen in Figures 2C and 2E at ∼37 kHz. At ∼46 kHz, the phase shift and notch are even seen at 70 dB SPL. This behavior has been attributed to cancellation between BM and internal OHC motion and subsequent dominance of BM motion.22 The OHC/DC-region gain displays sub-BF nonlinearity and boosting (increased responses relative to the BM responses). Sub-BF, the OHC/DC region has a larger magnitude response than the BM at all SPLs (Figure 2E). In the BF peak, at 50 dB SPL, the OHC/DC region has a larger amplitude than the BM, but at higher SPLs, the OHC/DC region has a low-pass character without a notable peak. The RL region (Figures 2D, 2F, 2H, and 2F) has the highest BF responses, higher than those at the BM at all SPLs. Sub-BF, the RL responses are below BM responses. The RL phase leads the BM phase slightly throughout the frequency range. In the remaining figures, we present displacement and strain results from four preparations, G1047, G1025, G1041, and G986 (and G1049 in supplemental information). Table S1 contains strain values at BF and BF/2. We begin with detailed sets of results from preparations G1047 and G1025.

The methods section described the l,r,t values that provide the longitudinal, radial, and transverse components of the optical axis. For G1047, the l,r,t values were ∼-0.62, −0.17, and 0.77, and those were typical for the basal region observed in this study. The radial component is relatively small, and transverse value is dominant, but there is a large enough longitudinal value so that, along one A-scan, different transverse/radial cross sections will be probed at the BM, OHC/DC, and RL regions. Based on these l,t values, the depth-distance spanning the OCC (∼90 μm, Figure 3I), and trigonometry, the longitudinal distance between the BM surface and RL surface measured in a single A-scan measurement would be ∼54 μm. The longitudinal distance between the BM surface and the OHC/DC region in a single A-scan would be about half that, 27 μm. The deeper, RL and OHC/DC, points were more basal than the BM points, so the measured traveling wave phase at these points would be biased to a phase lead relative to BM. Therefore, to find the phase at the OHC/DC and RL region in the same transverse section as the BM, a lag (a negative phase) must be added to the measured phase. A quantitative analysis of this problem was done in Frost et al.,29 and we can use those results to quantify the predicted correction here. In that paper, l,t values were ∼0.8, 0.6, signifying that the measurements were dominantly longitudinal, and in that case, the deeper points were more apical than the BM points. The depth distance from the BM to the OHC/DC region was ∼58 μm, and using trigonometry, the longitudinal distance between the BM surface and OHC/DC would be ∼46 μm. The phase needed to correct for the longitudinal shift was found to be ∼0° at low frequencies, increasing to about +75° at the BF; the corrected phase curve tilted up from the original phase curve (see Figure 6D of Frost et al.29). In the present data sets, the corrected phase curves of RL and OHC/DC regions will tilt down, starting at 0 at low frequencies. Following from the Frost results and the longitudinal distances noted above (in the current set, 54 μm at the RL, 27 μm at the OHC/DC, and in Frost 46 μm at the OHC/DC) for the data of Figure 2 at BF, the phase correction would have a value of about −80° at the RL and about −40° that at the OHC/DC. Considering Figures 2D and 2H, this correction would bring the RL phase values more in line with BM phase values at frequencies close to BF. This correction would make the OHC/DC phase lag the BM slightly more than it does in the uncorrected phases. Summary of results

The key observations of the phases, displacements, and strains were consistent among the preparations, with small differences reflected in slightly different colors in the heat maps. At BF/2 the greatest displacement was in the OHC/DC region, which comprised a relatively large region that moved similarly, with low strain. The RL region displayed the greatest displacement amplitude at BF and the smallest at BF/2. Many of the strain values in the BM were low or in the noise, indicating that the points within the BM moved together. There were generally two regions of high strain. At 50 and 70 dB and BF, there was a region of high strain in the transition between the OHC mid-body to the RL. A second region of high strain occurred at the transition between the relatively small sub-BF displacement of the BM and the large sub-BF displacement of the OHC/DC region. The OHC/DC region exhibited a substantial frequency-dependent phase delay relative to RL and BM, and this phase difference augmented the strains due to amplitude differences alone.

Discussion

Summary of observations related to OCC anatomy

The mechanics of the cochlea produce place- and frequency-dependent amplification of a sound-induced nanometer-scale fluid-mechanical wave, traveling from the base of the cochlea toward its apex. Amplification is due to the electromotile OHCs that are embedded within the OCC, but how OHC-based electromotility and other cellular processes collectively operate to achieve amplification remains a fascinating, open question. OCC motion, until recently observable only at the BM in the high-frequency cochlear base, has now been observed deeply within the OCC, revealing lively intra-OCC motion, as described above. These intra-OCC motions, and the resulting intra-OCC strains, provide clues to the workings of cochlear amplification and the cellular mechanics that govern functional amplification. In this section, we relate the observations above to the OCC anatomy and to previous findings from the literature.

The RL is the deepest region of the OC, adjacent to the stereocilia, the site of MET. Unlike the OHC/DC region, the RL displayed little/no sub-BF boosting, and the phase-frequency slope was relatively similar to that of the BM.23 The similarity of BM and RL motion is likely related to their connection via the structural, microtubule-rich supporting cells, PCs and DCs. The BM is a primary structural component of the OCC, but in point-stiffness measurements, the cells of the OC increased the stiffness measured at the BM by a factor of about two generally, and of about 10 at the outer PC.31,32,33 In measurements and analysis in excised gerbil cochleae, the DCs were found to have a stiffness ∼2.5 times that of the OHCs.34 The RL is a tiled structure formed by the apices of HCs, PCs, and the phalangeal processes of DCs. The apices of all these cells are rich in the structural protein actin. In HCs, the cuticular plate anchors the stereocilia and has been characterized as a dense actin gel.35 Actin gels can have significant stiffness, depending on the actin density and cross-linking molecules,8,36 and the RL is considered to be stiff,34 although in recent observations, it was not completely rigid.24

OHCs are the pumping heart of cochlear amplification. Voltage-induced stresses in the OHC lateral membrane produce electromotility: contraction and elongation along the OHC length in response to depolarization and hyperpolarization. In excised OHCs, the electromotile amplitude for an AC stimulus up to 50 kHz was ∼1 nm/mV for an OHC of length ∼50 μm, corresponding to a strain of ∼2 × 10−5 per mV.17,19 The largest strains measured in the current study were generally ∼1 × 10−4 at 50 dB SPL, at the BF. To compare the excised OHC strains to in vivo strains, we estimate OHC transmembrane voltage at 50-dB SPL using results from the literature: Starting with extracellular voltage measured close to the BM in gerbil, OHC transmembrane voltage was estimated as ∼2 mV at ∼30- to 40-dB SPL at the 20-kHz BF.37 In in vivo intracellular measurements in guinea pig, OHC AC receptor potentials were ∼1 mV at 30 dB at the cell’s 1-kHz BF38 and ∼5 mV at 50 dB at the cell’s 16-kHz BF.39 Based on those quantities, at 50-dB SPL, we expect OHC transmembrane voltage at the BF to be ∼5 mV. Then, our measured strains per mV at 50 dB were ∼2 × 10−5/mV, which agrees with the excised OHC strain values. However, at 50 dB, the relatively high strains in vivo were present over short distances compared with the ∼20-μm length of an OHC.30 In vivo OHC-based force is mechanically loaded by surrounding cells and structures, and the character and size of electromotility would reflect those loads. These results reinforce the benefit of high spatial resolution of imaging systems to measure the strains present over small distances within the OCC.

In vivo, voltage-induced OHC strains and stresses work within the structural framework of the OCC, overpowering forces dissipating the traveling wave to permit it to travel further and attain a greatly increased BF peak. In the observations here, the region of largest strain was close to the RL (Figure 16). At 70 dB, it could occupy a length that would include much of the OHC body; this strain is likely due to active electromotile contraction and elongation of the OHC. Beyond that (shallower in the B-scan), the OHC/DC region moved nearly as a rigid body. Between the OHC/DC and BM regions, especially sub-BF, there was a second region of large strain. Based on the distance, this occurred well beyond the OHC body, corresponding to strain within the base of the DC. A microtubule stalk runs through the DC from the RL to the BM.7 The microtubular structure within DCs and PCs likely is critical to productively directing electromotile forces; in this vein, a mouse mutant lacking the microtubule binding protein GAS2 had reduced DC stiffness and diminished cochlear function.40

Two primary observations of this study are the substantial electromotile strain extending into the OHC body from RL, and the low-strain, nearly rigid-body OHC/DC-region motion, which is likely driven by electromotility. Either or both of these motions could be critical for the operation of the cochlear amplifier. The OHC/DC-region motion has been observed and discussed in previous studies of the gerbil base.22,25,26,41 Below, we will consider these two observations in turn.

Transition from RL to OHC/DC

The motions in the RL and OHC/DC regions differ in both amplitude and phase. The transition between these regions was relatively abrupt at 50 dB compared with 70 dB, signifying that strain was more localized at the lower SPL. For example, in G1047, pixels 78 and 77 at/close to the RL moved similarly at 70 dB (an inclusive distance of 5.4 μm, 4.2 μm transverse), and adjacent pixel 76 was an “intra-RL” point (Figures 5D–5F). At 50 dB, pixels 78 to 75 moved similarly, an inclusive distance of 10.8 μm, 8.3 μm transverse (Figures 5G–5I); the next measurable pixel, 73, was in the OHC/DC region. “RL-region” motion also extended through more pixels at 50 than at 70 dB in G1047 (Figure 4), G1025 (Figure 8), and G1041 (Figure 11). (In G986, due to relatively sparse sampling, the extent of the RL-region motion could not be evaluated. In G1049 in the supplemental information, the observation was not supported because at both 70- and 50-dB SPL, a transition from RL-like to intra-RL occurred between the same two pixels [Figures S4 and S5].) The observed SPL dependence of the pattern of electromotile strain is likely related to the saturation of MET, which is thought to be the fundamental basis for cochlear nonlinearity.14 Thus, level-dependent motion patterns, well known within the OCC, are also present within the OHC body. The RL region of the OHC includes the cuticular plate, which anchors the stereocilia rootlets, and it is an important site of the calcium pumps that maintain endolymphatic calcium10,42,43; possibly, the nonlinear SPL dependence of current flow through the OHC affects mechanics that then affect the spatial pattern of electromotility. Alternatively, the shifting of the balance of active to passive forces as SPL increases would alter the external constraints on OHCs and could alter electromotile patterns.

At 70 dB, the transition between RL and OHC/DC motion could proceed relatively smoothly, with intra-region points having characteristics intermediate between RL and OHC/DC regions. To distill this section, OHC electromotile strains were large in the transition between the RL and OHC/DC regions. In the BF peak, the region of strain generally extended through a wider distance at 70- than at 50-dB SPL. Thus, the OHC/DC and RL region motions were less coupled at 50 than at 70 dB, and since lower SPL motions show relatively more BF-peak amplification, this uncoupling might be advantageous to functional cochlear amplification.

OHC/DC region

In measurements made through the RW at the 25-kHz BF location, Cooper et al.25 observed an abrupt increase in motion from the BM to the OHC/DC region (their Figure 6). They surmised that the OHC/DC motion was elliptical, with a long axis running approximately perpendicular to the long axis of the DC phalangeal processes. Frost et al.26 decomposed the motion with measurements from two angles (at 70 and 80 dB SPL) and found the motion to be a high-aspect-ratio ellipse (nearly lineal motion) with nearly equal longitudinal and transverse components (drawn as lineal motion in the green arrows in Figure 16). From the l,r,t values noted in the figure captions, when viewing through the RW to the 45-kHz place, our optical axis was primarily transverse but with a substantial negative longitudinal component (Figure 16). With this angle of observation, a motion perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the phalangeal processes will be approximately in line with the optical axis, and thus, we are well positioned to detect that motion. As noted above, the OHC/DC-region motion we observed extended to positions within the DCs, beyond the OHCs. The extensive and uniform OHC/DC motion we observed likely includes the nuclear region of OHCs and DCs, swinging perpendicular to the phalangeal processes. Another noteworthy aspect of the OHC/DC motion is the phase, which, from low frequency to the BF, smoothly accumulated about 0.75 cycle phase lag relative to the BM.45 This is most clearly presented in Figures 2G, 4B, 8B, and 11B, where OHC/DC and BM phases are plotted together. The steeper phase-versus-frequency slope could be interpreted as traveling wave motion that is slower in the OHC/DC region than on the BM and the RL. The concept of coupled traveling waves has a long history in models of cochlear operation.46,47,48 However, the physical mechanism for the phase delay is not divulged by these data, and other mechanisms could be responsible for the accumulating phase of the OHC/DC region. Notably, because the RL leads the BM motion by close to 0.25 cycle in the BF peak, the OHC/DC and RL regions are nearly in phase there, and close to the BF, the OHC/DC phase-frequency slope is no longer steeper than the BM phase. Regardless of what the physical mechanism is, the phase difference between OHC/DC, BM, and RL motion could be critical to cochlear amplification, and phase differences have been employed in essentially every model of cochlear amplification. It is worth reinforcing that the phase relations depend on viewing angle; for example, in Frost et al. and Van Der Heijden,29,45 measurements were made with an apically pointing longitudinal/transverse viewing angle, and then the OHC/DC region led BM motion. In measurements in the second turn of the gerbil cochlea, the OHC/DC region moved primarily longitudinally, and pronounced viewing-angle dependence could be observed within a single experiment (repeated in several preparations).49

It is possible that the OHC/DC motion is a by-product of cochlear amplification, not central to its operation50; it is distant from the RL (where MET occurs) and distant from the BM (supporting the cochlear traveling wave). The anatomically promoted, diagonally swinging motion sketched in Figure 16 might represent uncoupling of the electromotile motion of the OHC base from the RL, allowing strains at the RL side of the OHC to provide the essential active force of the cochlear amplifier. Recent cochlear models51 and concepts52 employ active forcing at the RL and do not employ the OHC/DC motion for amplification, although they may predict it. For example, in Samaras and Meaud,51 while the model does predict sub-BF OHC motion boosting, force generation by the OHCs is transmitted to the OCC through the RL and PC joint rather than via the OHC base and DCs. These findings were made under the conditions that the stiffness of the OHCs and DCs is comparable, and the stiffness of the RL and PC joint is relatively stiff.51

On the other hand, in several conceptual models of cochlear operation, the longitudinal/transverse OHC/DC motion has been proposed to be central to cochlear amplification.41,53,54,55 In these models, the OHC/DC motion is the source of force generation, and motion within the fluid tunnels has functional importance. Guinan (2025) introduced a two-wave system in which OHC electromotility generates longitudinal fluid flow, which changes the OCC cross-sectional area in a wavelength-dependent manner, producing narrow-band traveling wave amplification.54,55 A diagram in Figure 3 from Guinan et al.55 illustrates the mechanism. He et al., (2022) propose that the OHC/DC force produces differences in BM and RL motion (both magnitude and phase), causing pressure gradients and longitudinal fluid flow in the cochlear partition, which power a global hydrodynamical mechanism for cochlear amplification.53 This was illustrated in Figure 5 from He et al.53 While these conceptual models are detailed, physics-based models are needed to fortify these emerging theories.

Conclusion

Active deformations within the OCC are nonuniform. The pattern of active electromotile strain within the OHC body varied with stimulus level, with strains at 50 dB more spatially contained than they were at 70 dB. The RL and OHC/DC body move with different amplitudes and phases, and positions between them took intermediate motion values, including amplitude dips and phase shifts. With the limited optical resolution of OCT, it can be difficult to identify thin anatomical features such as the RL, and the spatial variations in motion we observe underscore the benefit of future improvements in resolution. Measurements that can be analyzed into more than one direction are also of high priority since they would allow for computation of strain along the anatomical directions, using methods such as those described in Frost et al.26

The nuclear region where OHCs and DCs are coupled (OHC/DC region) moved as a nearly rigid body; based on previous studies, the motion is likely longitudinally/transversely slanting. This motion has been invoked in conceptual models of cochlear mechanics, and its inertial effect might be useful to consider in the development of future models. To accommodate the motion of the OHC/DC region, which can be much larger than and out of phase with BM motion, the DC base must stretch and compress, and a second region of large strain was observed there. Thus, the microtubular stalk of the DC experiences strain, and this supportive structure is likely needed to constrain and control OCC motion.
