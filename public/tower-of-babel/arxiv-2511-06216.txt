Adaptive Multi-view Graph Contrastive Learning via Fractional-order Neural Diffusion Networks
Yanan Zhao, Feng Ji, Jingyang Dai, Jiaze Ma, Keyue Jiang, Kai Zhao, Wee Peng Tay

Abstract
Graph contrastive learning (GCL) learns node and graph representations by contrasting multiple views of the same graph. Existing methods typically rely on fixed, handcrafted views-usually a local and a global perspective—which limits their ability to capture multi-scale structural patterns. We present an augmentation-free, multi-view GCL framework grounded in fractional-order continuous dynamics. By varying the fractional derivative order α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in(0,1] , our encoders produce a continuous spectrum of views: small α \alpha yields localized features, while large α \alpha induces broader, global aggregation. We treat α \alpha as a learnable parameter so the model can adapt diffusion scales to the data and automatically discover informative views. This principled approach generates diverse, complementary representations without manual augmentations. Extensive experiments on standard benchmarks demonstrate that our method produces more robust and expressive embeddings and outperforms state-of-the-art GCL baselines.



I
Introduction

Contrastive learning is a self-supervised paradigm that trains representations by pulling semantically similar pairs together and pushing dissimilar pairs apart in the embedding space. It uncovers useful structure without labels and has produced strong results in computer vision, natural language processing, and recommendation systems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Applied to graph data, this paradigm is known as
Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL)
, where positive pairs are typically derived from different views (e.g., node-, subgraph-, or graph-level perturbations) of the same instance and negatives from other, unrelated instances.

A central challenge in GCL is how to produce distinct, semantically meaningful views. Prior approaches follow two broad strategies. Augmentation-based methods synthesize multiple views by perturbing the graph (e.g., edge deletion, feature masking), while augmentation-free methods obtain different views by applying distinct encoders to the same input. Despite these differences, most methods still rely on a small set of fixed, handcrafted views—commonly a “local” and a “global” perspective—which constrains their ability to represent multi-scale structure. For example, BGRL
[
4
]
creates two views via structural and feature perturbations, and PolyGCL
[
5
]
constructs low-pass and high-pass views through spectral filtering. Such designs increase view diversity but remain rigid and often require manual tuning. This motivates two questions:

Q1

How can we adaptively generate a diverse set of views that capture multi-scale semantics in GCL, moving beyond fixed, handcrafted local and global perspectives?

Q2

Can this be achieved without relying on heuristic augmentations?

To answer these questions, we propose a novel
multi-view augmentation-free
framework grounded in fractional-order continuous dynamics. Our key insight is to leverage
fractional-order differential equations (FDEs)

[
6
]
to model graph diffusion and generate a continuum of semantically diverse views, each parameterized by a distinct fractional derivative order. Prior works, which rely on ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, simulate graph diffusion as continuous-time heat flow and serve as smooth analogues of message passing in models like GCN. Unlike such ODE-based approaches, FDEs model graph diffusion by introducing the fractional derivative operator
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{\alpha}}{\,\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1]

[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. When
\alpha=1
, the standard ODE is recovered; however, for
\alpha<1
, the operator incorporates non-local information through memory effects, allowing the diffusion to reflect long-range temporal dependencies and attenuated propagation.

By leveraging FDEs,
multi-view
GCL emerges naturally: the fractional order
\alpha\in(0,1]
provides a continuous control of diffusion scale. Varying
\alpha
yields a continuum of views — small
\alpha
emphasizes local components, while large
\alpha
produces broader global aggregation. This gives a principled, augmentation
-
free mechanism to generate diverse and complementary views without handcrafted filters (e.g., filter
-
based methods such as PolyGCL) or task
-
specific perturbations (e.g., BGRL). Moreover,
\alpha
is a learnable continuous parameter, so the model can automatically adapt diffusion scales to dataset heterogeneity. To our knowledge, this is the first work to exploit the multi
-
scale behaviour of fractional
-
order dynamics for contrastive learning. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a novel multi-view, augmentation-free GCL framework based on neural diffusion encoders governed by
fractional-order dynamics
. This approach is conceptually simple, computationally efficient, and highly flexible, with each feature view controlled by a continuous parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
, naturally spanning a spectrum of diffusion scales.

•

Our main theoretical result shows that embeddings produced by FDE encoders at different fractional orders are provably distinct, and that the separation between these views grows as the gap between the orders
\alpha
increases. To our knowledge, this is the
first
formal analysis of this multi-scale distinguishability in the context of contrastive learning, despite prior empirical use of FDEs in GNNs.

•

We tackle two core challenges in GCL: (i) dimension collapse — mitigated by including encoders with small fractional orders that yield less energy
-
concentrated, higher
-
rank embeddings; and (ii) view collapse — prevented via a regularized contrastive objective that penalizes alignment of dominant directions across views, promoting diverse, complementary representations without requiring negative samples.

•

The fractional orders
\alpha
are treated as learnable parameters, allowing the model to discover informative diffusion scales from data instead of relying on manual tuning or grid search. To control complexity, we propose a view
-
learning strategy that prunes redundant scales while retaining diverse, complementary views.

•

We present a rigorous stability analysis, deriving explicit perturbation bounds that quantify how input, parameter, and topological disturbances affect encoder outputs. Empirically, our approach consistently outperforms state
-
of
-
the
-
art robust GCL methods under both black
-
box and white
-
box attack scenarios.

•

We evaluate on a broad set of homophilic and heterophilic graph benchmarks. Empirical results demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms state
-
of
-
the
-
art GCL baselines, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed multi
-
scale view generation mechanism.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

Problem formulation

Consider an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}
is a finite set of
N
nodes and
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is the set of edges. The raw features of the nodes are represented by the matrix
\mathbf{X}
, with the
i
-th
row
corresponding to the feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
of node
v_{i}
. The weighted symmetric adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})
has size
N\times N
, where
a_{ij}
denotes the edge weight between nodes
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
. We denote the complete graph information by
\mathcal{X}=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X})
.

In unsupervised feature learning, an encoder
f_{\theta}
maps each raw feature
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to a latent representation
\mathbf{y}_{i}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{X},v_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{F}
, forming the embedding matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F}
for downstream tasks such as node classification. Multi-view GCL introduces self-supervision by encouraging consistency among node embeddings
\mathbf{z}_{i}
produced by multiple encoders
f_{\theta_{k}}
,
k\geq 2
.

III-B

Graph neural diffusion models: from GCN to FDE

Traditional
graph neural network (GNN)
models, such as GCN and GAT
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
, rely on (discrete) graph message passing for feature aggregation. In the
k
-th iteration, the message passing step is given by
\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{Y}^{(k-1)}
, where
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
is the normalized adjacency matrix of
\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{Y}^{(k-1)}
is the output from the
(k-1)
-th iteration. In contrast,
[
7
]
introduces a continuous formulation based on heat diffusion, where the feature evolution
\mathbf{Y}(t)
is governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\mathbf{Y}(t)=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))

(1)

with the initial condition
\mathbf{Y}(0)
set to
\mathbf{X}
or its transformation. Here,
t
serves a role analogous to the layer index in GCNs, and
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))
defines the spatial diffusion. Let
\overline{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{I}-\overline{\mathbf{A}}
denote the
normalized Laplacian
. The GRAND model
[
7
]
is given by
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))=-\overline{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{Y}(t)
.

This formulation is further extended in
[
15
]
by incorporating
FDEs
. Specifically, for each
order parameter

\alpha\in(0,1)
, the corresponding fractional-order differential operator
D^{\alpha}_{t}
is defined as

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(t-\tau)-f(t)}{\tau^{1+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function.

The operator
D^{\alpha}_{t}
extends the concept of
\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}
(from
eq.

1
) such that as
\alpha\to 1
,
D^{\alpha}_{t}
converges to
\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}
. Thus, it is natural to define
D^{1}_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}
, allowing
\alpha
to be selected from the interval
(0,1]
. By generalizing
eq.

1
, we define the dynamics of the features
\mathbf{Y}(t)
under an FDE as:

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{Y}(t)=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t)).

(2)

The precise definition of
D^{\alpha}_{t}
may vary slightly depending on the context (see
appendix

A
), but all formulations share a common characteristic: for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
D^{\alpha}_{t}
is defined via an integral. This implies that
\mathbf{Y}(t)
depends on the entire history of
\mathbf{Y}(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}<t
. Consequently, unlike the solution to
eq.

1
, the dynamics of
\mathbf{Y}(t)
exhibit a “memory effect.”

Our
main insight
for GCL lies in leveraging the fractional order parameter
\alpha
to systematically generate a diverse set of distinct encoders, enabling augmentation-free GCL. The multi-view framework is naturally facilitated by the continuous and parameterizable nature of
\alpha
, which allows for seamless integration and optimization through gradient descent. Our theoretical analysis focuses on the F-GRAND-l model
[
15
]
given by

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{Y}(t)=-\overline{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{Y}(t).

(3)

The analysis shall be performed within the framework of graph signal processing (GSP), which is reviewed next.

III-C

Graph Signal Processing

GSP
[
39
]
provides a useful framework to analyze the impact of different
\alpha
values on the generated views. We present a concise overview here. Since the normalized Laplacian
\overline{\mathbf{L}}
is symmetric, it admits an orthogonal decomposition
\overline{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\mathop{\intercal}}
. Here, the diagonal entries
\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{N}
of
\bm{\Lambda}
are the eigenvalues of
\overline{\mathbf{L}}
, also known as the
graph frequencies
, and the
i
-th column
\mathbf{u}_{i}
of
\mathbf{U}
is the eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
. Each
\mathbf{u}_{i}
represents a signal on the graph
\mathcal{G}
. For small
\lambda_{i}
,
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is smooth, meaning the signal values vary gradually across the edges. Conversely, for large
\lambda_{i}
,
\mathbf{u}_{i}
tends to be spiky, emphasizing local variations.

Any signal
\mathbf{x}
(e.g., a column of the feature matrix
\mathbf{X}
) can be expressed as a
spectral decomposition
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i},\quad\text{where }c_{i}=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{i}\rangle.

In GSP, the coefficients
c_{i}
are referred to as
Fourier coefficients
, which quantify the response of
\mathbf{x}
to the basis vector
\mathbf{u}_{i}
. A signal
\mathbf{x}
is said to have
large smooth components
if
|c_{i}|
is relatively large for small indices
i
, and it is considered
energy concentrated
if
|c_{i}|
is small for most indices
i
.

IV-A

Contrasting encoders

IV-A
1
Multi-view generation

We adopt a non
-
Markovian random
-
walk interpretation
[
15
]
to illustrate how varying the fractional order
\alpha
produces multi
-
scale views. Under the FDE model
eq.

3
, let
{}_{i}\mathbf{P}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the probability distribution of a fractional-order random walker over the node set
\mathcal{V}
when initialized at
v_{i}
; its
j
-
th entry is the probability of finding the walker at node
v_{j}
at time
t
. The node embeddings are recovered as
\mathbf{Y}(t)=\sum_{i}{}_{i}\mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{Y}(0)_{i}
, where
\mathbf{Y}(0)_{i}
is the initial feature (row) vector of
v_{i}
.
For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the random walk on the graph
\mathcal{G}
evolves in infinitesimal time intervals
\Delta\tau>0
according to the following dynamics:

1.

The walker remains at the current node for a random duration sampled from a heavy-tailed distribution
\psi_{\alpha}(n)=d_{\alpha}n^{-(1+\alpha)}
, where
d_{\alpha}>0
ensures normalization. Smaller values of
\alpha
result in heavier tails, leading to longer expected waiting times and a stronger memory effect.

2.

After waiting, the walker either transitions from node
i
to a neighboring node
j
with probability
(\Delta\tau)^{\alpha}d_{\alpha}|\Gamma(-\alpha)|\frac{W_{ij}}{d_{i}}
for
i\neq j
, or remains at node
i
with probability
1-(\Delta\tau)^{\alpha}d_{\alpha}|\Gamma(-\alpha)|
.

This formulation provides an intuitive understanding of how the fractional parameter
\alpha
governs the scale of information propagation over the graph:

•

Local view
: As
\alpha\to 0
, the walker exhibits long waiting times and infrequent transitions, resulting in highly localized behavior and aggregating information primarily from nearby nodes.

•

Global view
: As
\alpha\to 1
, the walker transitions more frequently and explores a wider portion of the graph, enabling global aggregation and capturing long-range dependencies.

To further elucidate how multi-scale views naturally arise from fractional-order derivatives, we provide a mathematical analysis of the distinct representations generated by FDE encoders with varying fractional orders
\alpha
. For clarity and conciseness, we present an informal summary of the results here, while a
detailed and rigorous
explanation is deferred to
appendix

B
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first
formal mathematical analysis of this phenomenon in the context of contrastive learning.

Assuming the model

As discussed in
section

I
, the effectiveness of augmentation-free GCL relies on the ability of different encoders to generate feature representations with distinct views. We argue that
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{g}}(t)
emphasizes
global
structural patterns as
\alpha_{g}\to 1
, while
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)
captures finer
local
details as
\alpha_{l}\to 0
. Specifically, by
1
1
, the smooth components (associated with small graph frequencies
\lambda_{i}
) dominate in
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{g}}(t)
. Each such component is spanned by a vector
\mathbf{u}_{i}

\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)
, non-smooth components
\mathbf{u}_{i}
(corresponding to large graph frequencies
\lambda_{i}

\mathbf{u}_{i}

\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)

\alpha_{l}\longleftrightarrow

By selecting distinct
\alpha

), enriching graph contrastive learning. Numerical experiments demonstrate that embeddings

Dimension collapse occurs when features are confined to a low-dimensional subspace within the full embedding space
[
40
]
, which is a critical issue to address in contrastive learning. We argue that
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)
, generated with a small
\alpha_{l}
, effectively mitigates this problem. As established in
1
2
,
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)

\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{g}}(t)

\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)
can be expressed as
\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}c_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}

To support this claim, we perform principal component analysis (PCA) on
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{l}}(t)
and
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{g}}(t)
for the Cora and Wisconsin datasets. The results, shown in
fig.

IV-B

The adaptive FD-MVGCL model

We now present the full details of the proposed adaptive FD-MVGCL model. Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
with adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
and initial node features
\mathbf{X}
, we denote the input as
\mathcal{X}=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X})
.
Our objective is to learn multiple FDE encoders
f_{\theta_{k}}
for
k\geq 2
in an unsupervised manner, with each encoder capturing structural patterns at a specific scale.
In the adaptive FD-MVGCL model, all encoders
f_{\theta_{k}}
share the same architecture (see
fig.

4
). For clarity, we describe the structure of a single encoder below:

•

Feature projection:
Apply a linear transformation with learnable weights
\mathbf{W}_{k}
to the input features, i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{X}
. This step typically increases the feature dimension to enhance the model’s expressiveness and ability to capture complex patterns.

•

Fractional diffusion:
Initialize the diffusion process with
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{k}}(0)=\mathbf{Z}_{k}
, where
\alpha_{k}
is the fractional order. Compute
\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{k}}(t)
using the FDE-based encoder, as described in
eq.

2
.

•

Activation and output:
Stop the diffusion process at time
T
and apply a non-linear activation function
\sigma
(e.g., ReLU) to obtain the final output, denoted by
\mathbf{Y}_{k}=\sigma(\mathbf{Y}_{\alpha_{k}}(T))
.

The model parameters
\theta_{k}
include the learnable weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{k}
, the fractional order
\alpha_{k}
, and the diffusion time
T
. Unlike prior FDE-based models such as FROND
[
15
]
and DRAGON
[
14
]
, which rely on manually tuning
\alpha
via grid search, we treat the
\alpha
values as
learnable parameters
. This allows the model to adaptively generate informative views tailored to the input data.

We utilize
K
encoders
f_{\theta_{k}}
,
k=1,2,\dots,K
, each generating an output
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
. To optimize the model parameters, we employ a
contrastive loss

\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k^{\prime}})
, where
k^{\prime}=k+1\bmod K
, i.e., this loss is applied to each pair of consecutive outputs
(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k+1})
, with wrap-around from
\mathbf{Y}_{K}
to
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
.
For simplicity,
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is defined as the mean cosine similarity, referred to as
cosmean
(see
eq.

24
). This consecutive-pair scheme balances representational alignment and computational efficiency by enforcing consistency only between successive views. Additionally, the loss applied to
(\mathbf{Y}_{K},\mathbf{Y}_{1})
ensures global consistency across all encoders. This approach reduces the computational complexity from comparing all possible pairs (
\mathcal{O}(K^{2})
) to only consecutive pairs (
\mathcal{O}(K)
), maintaining scalability without compromising representation quality.

IV-B
1
Model/View Collapse

As discussed in
section

IV-A2
, directly minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{0}
can lead to view collapse, where two encoders produce nearly identical representations. To address this, we leverage the observation in
fig.

2
that both
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{k^{\prime}}
exhibit dominant components. Let
\mathbf{c}_{k}
and
\mathbf{c}_{k^{\prime}}
represent the unit directional vectors of these components. To prevent collapse, we introduce a penalty term that discourages alignment between these vectors by incorporating their inner product
\langle\mathbf{c}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle
. The resulting
regularized cosmean
loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k^{\prime}})=\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k^{\prime}})+\eta\left\lvert\langle\mathbf{c}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\right\rvert,

where
\eta
is a regularization weight. This regularization term encourages diversity between the two representations, ensuring they remain distinct without relying on negative samples, unlike traditional contrastive objectives
[
28
,
5
]
. The impact of this regularization is further analyzed in
section

V-C5
. Finally, the overall contrastive loss across all encoders is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k^{\prime}}\right).

(4)

Encoder reduction strategy

To manage training costs as
K
increases, we propose an encoder reduction strategy (
algorithm

1
). Initially,
K
encoders are created with fractional orders
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
uniformly sampled from the range
(0.01,1]
. Both the weights
\mathbf{W}_{k}
and the fractional orders
\alpha_{k}
are optimized jointly using gradient descent on the loss
\mathcal{L}
defined in
eq.

4
. After each training phase, if any
\alpha_{k}
values are too similar in log-scale, only one is randomly retained, while the corresponding encoders are reinitialized. The number of encoders
K
is then reduced accordingly. This process is repeated until all
\alpha_{k}
values are sufficiently distinct. The final number of encoders is denoted by
\tilde{K}
.

Finally, for downstream tasks, we compute a weighted average of the multiple views as
\mathbf{Y}=\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}\beta_{k}\mathbf{Y}_{k}
, where
\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}\beta_{k}=1
. The weights
\beta_{k}
are tuned on a validation set for each downstream task.

Stability

Graph data in real-world applications often contain noisy node features or missing edges
[
41
]
. To assess the robustness of FD-MVGCL, we perform a unified stability analysis that accounts for perturbations in input features, model parameters, and graph structure.
2
establishes the stability of the FDE encoder, while the overall stability of the model is formalized in
3
. Detailed proofs are provided in
section

B-B
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}(t)
denote the solution to the original FDE encoder in
eq.

2
, and let
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
be the solution to the perturbed system:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{t}^{\alpha}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)&=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(0)&=\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}.\end{aligned}

(5)

Define the total perturbation magnitude as

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{0}-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}\rVert+\sup_{\mathbf{Y}\in A}\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},{\mathbf{Y}})-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{Y}})\rVert,

(6)

where
A
denotes a compact set where the solutions of both systems are well defined. Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
. Then, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, there exist a sufficiently large positive constant
T
such that both the functions
\mathbf{Y}(t)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
are defined on
[0,T]
, and the following perturbation bound holds:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert=O\left(\varepsilon T^{\alpha-1}\right),

(7)

for
t\in[0,T]
.

2
extends both Theorem 6.20 and Theorem 6.21 from
[
42
]
by providing a unified bound that accounts for both initial state and functional perturbations. When the perturbation is solely due to the initial state, i.e.,
\varepsilon:=\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{0}-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}\rVert
, the theorem simplifies to Theorem 6.20. Similarly, when the perturbation arises only from the governing function, i.e.,
\varepsilon:=\sup_{\mathbf{Y}\in A}\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},{\mathbf{Y}})-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{Y}})\rVert
, it reduces to Theorem 6.21. Thus,
2
unifies these scenarios, offering a comprehensive stability result for simultaneous perturbations.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\dots<\alpha_{\tilde{K}}\leq 1
and let

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}}\beta_{k}\mathbf{Y}_{k}(t),

(8)

where each
\mathbf{Y}_{k}(t)
is the solution to the feature dynamics in
eq.

2
, denote the solution at evolution time
t
of the FD-MVGCL model with
K
encoders.
Let
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
be the perturbed solution of FD-MVGCL where each encoder is a perturbed system in
eq.

5
.
For sufficiently small combined perturbation
\varepsilon
in
eq.

6
, and a sufficiently large time horizon

such that both the functions
\mathbf{Y}(t)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert=O\left(\varepsilon T^{\alpha_{\tilde{K}}-1}\right)

Overall performance

Node classification results on heterophilic and homophilic datasets are reported in

On heterophilic datasets, it substantially outperforms baseline methods, securing the best or second-best results on most benchmarks. On homophilic datasets, FD-MVGCL remains competitive with leading approaches such as GraphACL and PolyGCL. We also report the average ranking, computed as the arithmetic mean of its valid per-dataset ranks (excluding OOM and missing results), where lower values indicate better overall performance. FD-MVGCL attains the lowest average rank on heterophilic datasets and shares the lowest rank with GraphACL on homophilic datasets, demonstrating its strong generalization and consistency across diverse graph types.

The stronger performance gains on heterophilic graphs reflect the inherent difficulty of unsupervised learning in such settings. Unlike homophilic graphs, where simple clustering methods often suffice, heterophilic graphs require more sophisticated multi-scale representations to capture complex structural patterns. These results demonstrate that FD-MVGCL effectively learns expressive embeddings across diverse graph structures by adaptively balancing local and global diffusion scales. 1
Attack methods

We consider four
black-box
topology attacks in the evasion setting: Random, PRBCD
[
52
]
, Nettack
[
53
]
, and Metattack
[
54
]
. We also evaluate two
white-box
attacks (i.e., PGD
[
55
]
and PRBCD) that jointly perturb the graph structure and node features. All models are trained on clean graphs, with adversarial perturbations applied only during inference. The details of these attack methods are as follows:

Random attack

Randomly adds noisy edges by selecting node pairs across the graph. The number of added edges is controlled by a perturbation ratio relative to the original edge count.

PRBCD

Projected Randomized Block Coordinate Descent attack is a structure-based attack that perturbs the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
by iteratively adding or removing edges to maximize the classification loss of a surrogate GNN (e.g., GCN). It uses a projected randomized block coordinate descent strategy under a fixed budget of edge modifications, enabling efficient and scalable adversarial perturbations. In the white-box setting, PRBCD further extends to jointly perturb node features by leveraging full access to model parameters and gradients.

Netttack

A targeted structure-based attack that perturbs the graph to mislead node classification. It estimates homophily and heterophily probabilities, removing edges to same-class nodes to reduce classification confidence and adding edges to different-class nodes to induce misclassification. Guided by a surrogate GNN, it greedily selects the most influential edges to modify within a given budget.

Metattack

A global structure-based attack that perturbs the adjacency matrix by computing meta-gradients of a surrogate GNN. It modifies the graph structure to maximize the overall classification loss, effectively degrading performance across the entire graph.

PGD

Projected Gradient Descent is a white-box attack that simultaneously perturbs the graph structure and node features to maximize the classification loss of the target model. It performs iterative gradient-based updates within a predefined perturbation budget, projecting the modifications back to the feasible space after each step. By leveraging full access to model parameters and gradients, PGD enables strong and precise attacks.

V-E

Complexity analysis

The training time complexity of FD-MVGCL consists of solving the FDE encoders and computing the contrastive loss. For a graph with
N
nodes and
|\mathcal{E}|
edges, each FDE is solved over
E\coloneqq T/h
time steps, where
h
is the discretization step size and
T
is the total integration time. At each step, the function
\mathcal{F}\left(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}_{j}\right)
is evaluated and cached. Assuming
\mathcal{F}
follows the GRAND model
[
7
]
, each evaluation costs
C=|\mathcal{E}|d
, where
d
is the feature dimension. Solving one FDE costs
\mathcal{O}(EC+E\log E)
, with
\log E
term from fast convolution. For
\tilde{K}
FDEs, the total cost is
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{K}(EC+E\log E))
. The loss computation adds an additional cost
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{K}N)
, resulting in a total training complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{K}(EC+E\log E+N))
.

Training/inference time and GPU memory (during training) comparisons with baselines (e.g., GraphACL, PolyGCL and EPAGCL) are shown in
table

IX
. Although FD-MVGCL requires more GPU memory on large graphs (e.g., Roman and Ogbn-arxiv) due to maintaining intermediate states for multiple FDE solvers, it consistently avoids out-of-memory (OOM) issues and achieves lower overall training and inference costs compared with strong baselines. Moreover, apart from the orders
\alpha
, each diffusion module of FD-MVGCL introduces
no other
learnable parameters, thereby contributing to its relatively low computational cost.

VI
Conclusion

We have introduced FD-MVGCL, an adaptive multi-view, augmentation-free graph contrastive learning framework that leverages fractional-order graph neural diffusion to generate diverse, multi-scale representations. By treating the fractional order
\alpha
as a learnable parameter, our approach dynamically adapts diffusion scales to the data, eliminating the need for handcrafted augmentations, negative sampling, or manual tuning. Extensive experiments demonstrate that FD-MVGCL achieves state-of-the-art performance across both homophilic and heterophilic benchmarks, while maintaining superior robustness under adversarial perturbations. This work establishes fractional-order dynamics as a principled and effective paradigm for multi-view contrastive learning on graphs. While FD-MVGCL achieves strong performance, it assumes a static graph structure. Future work will focus on extending our framework to dynamic graphs, enabling fractional diffusion to model evolving temporal and topological patterns.

Appendix A
Fractional-order derivatives and GNNs

The works
[
15
,
14
]
extend the ODE-based approaches by incorporating graph neural
FDE
, generalizing the order of the derivative to positive real number
\alpha
. The motivation is the solution to an FDE that encodes historical information and thus retains the “memory” of the evolution.

A-A

Fractional-order derivatives

Recall that the first-order derivative of a scalar function
f(t)
is defined as the rate of change of
f
at a time
t
:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}f(t)}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}

It is local in the sense that
f^{\prime}(t)
is determined by the value of
f
in a small neighborhood of
t
.

Fractional-order derivatives
D^{\alpha}_{t}
generalize integer derivatives and the domain of
\alpha
is extended to any positive real number. By the relation
D^{\alpha+1}_{t}=D^{\alpha}_{t}D^{1}_{t}
, it suffices to define
D^{\alpha}_{t}
for the order parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For the encoder design in this paper, we only consider
\alpha\in(0,1]
. The domain is large enough for us to choose encoders that generate different views. Moreover, solving a higher-order FDE requires additional information such as the initial derivative, which is usually not available.

Although there are different approaches to defining
D^{\alpha}_{t}
, they all share the common characterization that the derivative is defined using an integral. Hence, the operator is “global” as historical values of the function are used. We show two approaches below.

The
left fractional derivative

[
62
]
of
f(t)
is defined by the following integral

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(t-\tau)-f(t)}{\tau^{1+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

On the other hand, the
Caputo fractional derivative

[
63
]
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f^{\prime}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

One can find other approaches and more discussions in
[
64
]
.

A-B

Incorporating FDE

To build a diffusion model based on FDE, it usually suffices to replace the integer order derivative with a fractional-order derivative in the governing differential equation. For example, the ODE in the GraphCON model
[
11
]
is equivalent to a system of equations:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Z}(t)}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\sigma\left(\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{Y}(t),t)\right)-\gamma\mathbf{Y}(t)-\nu\mathbf{Z}(t)\\
&\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}(t)}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{Z}(t).\end{aligned}

Replacing
\,\mathrm{d}/\,\mathrm{d}t
with
D^{\alpha}_{t}
, one obtains its FDE version as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &D_{t}^{\alpha}\mathbf{Z}(t)=\sigma\left(\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{Y}(t),t)\right)-\gamma\mathbf{Y}(t)-\nu\mathbf{Z}(t)\\
&D_{t}^{\alpha}\mathbf{Y}(t)=\mathbf{Z}(t).\end{aligned}

A numerical solver is provided in
[
15
]
.

In this section, we provide a rigorous discussion of the formal version of

\displaystyle e_{\alpha}(\lambda,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\lambda^{n}t^{\alpha n}}{\Gamma(\alpha n+1)},\quad\lambda>0,\ t\geq 0,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function.

We make the following
assumptions
regarding the encoder. The function
\mathcal{F}
in
eq.

2
is the simple
-\overline{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{X}
as described after
eq.

1
. For
D^{\alpha}_{t}
, we consider the left fractional derivative. For the model, we apply skip-connection after a diffusion of length
\tau
, and there are
m
-iterations so that the total time is
T=m\tau
. We shall consider
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and this a mild assumption, as we have seen that
D^{1}_{t}=\,\mathrm{d}/\,\mathrm{d}t
is the limit of

when
\alpha\to 1
. The following formal version of
1
analyzes the (asymptotic) properties of the Fourier coefficients of the output features.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a connected graph and

are the order parameters. For subscript
s
stands for either
l
or

n_{s}\geq 1
be the integer such that
n_{s}\alpha_{s}<1\leq(n_{s}+1)\alpha_{s}
. Consider a graph signal
\mathbf{x}
with Fourier coefficients
\{c_{i},1\leq i\leq N\}

\mathbf{z}_{\alpha_{s}}(T)
be the output features of the encoders with order parameters
\alpha_{s}
. Its Fourier coefficients are
\{c_{\alpha_{s},i}(T),1\leq i\leq N\}

1.

We have the expression:

\displaystyle c_{\alpha_{s},i}(T)=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{s}}b_{\alpha_{s},i,j}\tau^{-j\alpha_{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right)c_{i},

and
b_{\alpha_{s},i,j}>0

2.

For fixed
l
and
j
, the coefficient
b_{\alpha_{s},i,j}
is decreasing w.r.t.
i
.

3.

For fixed
i
and

, we have
b_{\alpha_{l},i,j}>b_{\alpha_{g},i,j}

Proof.

Let
\overline{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\mathop{\intercal}}
be the orthogonal eigendecomposition of
\overline{\mathbf{L}}
. As
\overline{\mathbf{L}}
is positive semi-definite, its (ordered) eigenvalues satisfy

. Notice we have
\lambda_{2}>0
as
\mathcal{G}
is connected. To solve the FDE

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}(t)=-\mathbf{U}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\mathop{\intercal}}\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}(t),

and hence

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{U}^{\mathop{\intercal}}\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}(t)=-\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\mathop{\intercal}}\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}(t).

Therefore, the spectral decomposition of the dynamics
\mathbf{z}_{\alpha}(t)
follows the equation
D^{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}(t)=-\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}(t)

For consistency, we set

the constant function independent of
\alpha
. Therefore, by

c_{\alpha,i}(t)=e_{\alpha}(\lambda,t)c_{\alpha,i}(0),1\leq i\leq N
, where
c_{\alpha,i}(0)=c_{i}
depends only on the input feature. Recall we perform diffusion for a time
\tau
followed by a skip-connection for
m

\begin{split}&c_{\alpha,i}(T)=\Big(1+\ldots+e_{\alpha}(\lambda,\tau)\big(1+e_{\alpha}(\lambda,\tau)\big)\Big)c_{\alpha,i}(0)\\

(10)

The case

to be any positive number. We assume

By

\displaystyle e_{\alpha_{s}}(\lambda_{i},\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{j}\Gamma(1-j\alpha_{s})}\tau^{-j\alpha_{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right).

(11)

The Gamma function

is positive and strictly decreasing on the interval
(0,1]

&\qquad\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{j}\Gamma(1-j\alpha_{s})}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{i+1}^{j}\Gamma(1-j\alpha_{s})}.\end{aligned}

As compared with

By taking the difference between the original and perturbed dynamics, we obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle D_{t}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)).

Integrating both sides with the fractional integral operator
I_{t}^{\alpha}
and then taking norms gives

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}(0)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(0)+I_{t}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)))\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{Y}(0)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(0)\rVert

\displaystyle\qquad+I_{t}^{\alpha}\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert.

(13)

Using the triangle inequality, we bound the integrand of the second term on the right-hand side of
eq.

13
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert\\
&\qquad\qquad\leq\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert.\end{aligned}

(14)

Given that
\mathcal{F}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
, it follows that:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(t))-\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq L\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert.\end{aligned}

(15)

By definition of the combined perturbation
\varepsilon
, the second term on the right-hand side of
eq.

14
is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t))\rVert\\
&\qquad\leq\sup_{\mathbf{Y}\in A}\lVert\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W},{\mathbf{Y}})-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{Y}})\rVert\leq\varepsilon.\end{aligned}

(16)

Combining
eq.

13
,
eq.

15
and
eq.

16
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert\\
&\qquad\leq LI_{t}^{\alpha}\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert+(1+\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)})\varepsilon\\
&\qquad\leq LI_{t}^{\alpha}\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert+(1+\frac{T^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)})\varepsilon,\end{aligned}

(17)

where we have used the assumption that the initial perturbation satisfies
\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{0}-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}\rVert\leq\varepsilon
.
Applying the fractional Grönwall inequality
[
63
, Lemma 6.19.]
to
eq.

17

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert\leq\left(1+\frac{T^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\right)\varepsilon e_{\alpha}(-L,t^{\alpha}).

Since the Mittag-Leffler function

O\left(\frac{1}{1+LT^{\alpha}}\right)
, it follows from
eq.

18

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert\mathbf{Y}(t)-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\rVert&\leq\left(1+\frac{T^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\right)\varepsilon O\left(\frac{1}{1+LT^{\alpha}}\right)\\
&=O\left(\varepsilon T^{\alpha-1}\right)\end{aligned}

(19)

for
t\in[0,T]
.
This completes the proof of
2
.

Contrastive loss functions

In scenarios where explicit negative samples are absent, non-contrastive methods instead optimize agreement between positive pairs. Representative approaches include knowledge-distillation frameworks such as BGRL
[
4
]
, which relies on cosine similarity, and redundancy-reduction techniques such as Barlow Twins
[
40
]
and VICReg
[
48
]
. To illustrate how different loss functions are formulated in this context, we consider two feature representations (or “views”), denoted by
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
. Each consists of
N
samples, where
\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}
represent the feature vectors of the
i
-th sample in the two views. Based on this setup, the Euclidean loss, Cosine similarity loss, Barlow Twins loss, VICReg loss and CCA loss are defined as follows.

Euclidean Loss

measures the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm between two feature representations
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, encouraging them to be as close as possible. It is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Euclidean}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}-\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Cosmean Loss

measures the cosine similarity between two feature representations
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, encouraging their alignment. It is mathematically expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cosmean}}=1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\langle\mathbf{Y}_{l,i},\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rangle}{\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}\rVert_{2}\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rVert_{2}}

(24)

where
\langle\mathbf{Y}_{l,i},\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rangle
is the inner product of these two vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}
, and
\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}\rVert_{2}
and
\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rVert_{2}
are their respective
\ell_{2}
-norms. The cosmean loss is minimized when the feature vectors are perfectly aligned in the same direction, achieving maximum cosine similarity.

Barlow Twins Loss

[
40
]
is designed to encourage similarity between two feature representations
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, while reducing redundancy across dimensions within each representation. It is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Barlow Twins}}=\sum_{i}\left(\left(1-\mathbf{C}_{ii}\right)^{2}\right)+\lambda\sum_{i}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbf{C}_{ii}^{2}

where
\mathbf{C}=\frac{\mathbf{Y}_{l}^{{}^{\mathop{\intercal}}}\mathbf{Y}_{g}}{N}
is the cross-correlation matrix of the normalized feature representations
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, and
\lambda
is a trade-off parameter. The first term minimizes the difference between the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{C}
and 1, ensuring the features from
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
are highly correlated. The second term minimizes the off-diagonal elements, promoting decorrelation between features and reducing redundancy. By balancing these two objectives, Barlow Twins Loss enables learning robust and diverse feature representations.

VICReg Loss

(Variance-Invariance-Covariance Regularization Loss)
[
48
]
is designed to align two feature representations,
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, while ensuring variance preservation and minimizing redundancy. It consists of three components: variance regularization, invariance loss, and covariance regularization. The loss is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{VICReg}}=\eta_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}+\eta_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}+\eta_{3}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}},

where
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
and
\eta_{3}
are hyper-parameters controlling the relative contributions of each term. The invariance component
\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}
is computed as the mean-squared Euclidean distance of the corresponding samples from
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l,i}-\mathbf{Y}_{g,i}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The variance component
\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}
ensures that each feature dimension in
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
has sufficient variance to potentially prevent collapse. It is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}=&\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\max(0,\varepsilon-\sqrt{\text{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_{l}[:,j])})\\
&\qquad\qquad+\max(0,\varepsilon-\sqrt{\text{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_{g}[:,j])})\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{Y}_{l}[:,j]
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}[:,j]
are the
j
-th feature columns of
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, respectively.
\text{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_{l}[:,j])=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{Y}_{l}[i,j]-\mu_{l,j})^{2}
is the variance of the
j
-th feature in
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
(with
\mu_{l,j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{Y}_{l}[i,j]
), and
\varepsilon
is a small positive constant to enforce nonzero variance. And the covariance regularization term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}
reduces redundancy by decorrelating different feature dimensions within each representation. It is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\text{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}_{l})_{j,k}^{2}+\text{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}_{g})_{j,k}^{2}\right)

where
\text{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}_{l})_{j,k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{l,i,j}-\mu_{l,j}\right)\left(\mathbf{Y}_{l,i,k}-\mu_{l,k}\right)
represents the off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix for
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and likewise for
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
. The hyperparameters
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
, and
\eta_{3}
control the relative contributions of variance regularization, invariance loss, and covariance regularization, respectively. By balancing these three terms, VICReg ensures robust feature alignment while maintaining diversity and decorrelation, making it well-suited for unsupervised learning tasks.

CCA Loss

(Canonical Correlation Analysis Loss)
[
34
]
is self-supervised objective that balances invariance and decorrelation between two augmented views of the same data. Given two normalized feature matrices
\mathbf{Y}_{l}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{g}
, the CCA-style learning loss is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CCA}}=\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l}-\mathbf{Y}_{g}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lambda\left(\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{l}^{{}^{\mathop{\intercal}}}\mathbf{Y}_{l}-\mathbf{I}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{g}^{{}^{\mathop{\intercal}}}\mathbf{Y}_{g}-\mathbf{I}\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)

where
\lambda
is a non-negative trade-off parameter. The first (invariance) term encourages the two augmented views to produce similar representations, thereby maximizing cross-view correlation. The second (decorrelation) term regularizes each view to be approximately uncorrelated, ensuring that different feature dimensions encode diverse and complementary semantic information.
