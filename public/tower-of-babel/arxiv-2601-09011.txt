Fisher’s fundamental theorem and regression in causal analysis

Fisher’s fundamental theorem and regression in causal analysis

Fisher’s fundamental theorem describes the change caused by natural selection as the change in gene frequencies multiplied by the partial regression coefficients for the average effects of genes on fitness. Fisher’s result has generated extensive controversy in biology. I show that the theorem is a simple example of a general partition for change in regression predictions across altered contexts. By that rule, the total change in a mean response is the sum of two terms. The first ascribes change to the difference in predictor variables, holding constant the regression coefficients. The second ascribes change to altered context, captured by shifts in the regression coefficients. This general result follows immediately from the product rule for finite differences applied to a regression equation. Economics widely applies this same partition, the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition, as a fundamental tool that can in proper situations be used for causal analysis. Recognizing the underlying mathematical generality clarifies Fisher’s theorem, provides a useful tool for causal analysis, and reveals connections across disciplines.

Introduction

Regression is widely used to associate predictor variables with outcomes. For example, fitness may be expressed as a function of multiple phenotypic characters or multiple attributes of a genotype. Each partial regression coefficient describes the association between the value of a predictor variable and the observed values.

In some cases, a causal interpretation is placed on the association described by the partial regression coefficients. Everyone understands that such associations do not necessarily provide accurate causal information. However, as commonly noted: “Correlation is not causation. But it is a good place to start.” Often there is no practical alternative. So the relations between regression and causal understanding are important.

Observing how outcomes change as context changes provides some causal insight. One common method is to study how the predicted mean value of a regression equation changes between two situations.

For example, Fisher studied the regression equations for the change in mean fitness between ancestor and descendant populations
4
,
5
,
6
. He regressed the fitness of individuals on their genetic composition. He then split the total change in mean fitness into two terms. The first term ascribed genetic changes to natural selection and held constant the original regression coefficients.

Fisher derived the change associated with that first term to be what he called the genetic variance in fitness. He concluded that the change in fitness caused directly by natural selection is exactly that genetic variance in fitness. He described the result as the fundamental theorem of natural selection.

Fisher also noted that the second term of the total change in mean fitness accounted for the change in context, or the change in the environment. It was later shown that the second term is exactly the change in the regression coefficients when evaluated in the context of the changed populations
17
,
9
.

Much of the controversy surrounding Fisher’s theorem arises from confusion about the generality of the regression decomposition and differing interpretations of what the partition means causally
17
,
3
,
8
. This article provides a simple derivation and explanation of the general underlying regression result.

In particular, the change in the mean value of an outcome variable in a regression is equal to the change in the average predictor values multiplied by the regression coefficients when held constant at their values from the initial context, plus the change in the regression coefficients evaluated in the context of the changed mean values of the predictors. I show how that result follows immediately from the general expression from the product rule of finite differences.

Several prior articles have clearly explained that Fisher’s theorem is a partitioning of total change into parts
17
,
3
,
8
,
13
. But both Price and Ewens argued that such a partition is strange and unhelpful, and many others have agreed.

Interestingly, there is a long and valued literature in economics that uses the partitioning of total change in a regression, the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition
1
,
14
,
7
. Much of econometrics uses regression to describe or predict outcomes based on multiple predictor variables. Then, for a change in context, the obvious challenge is to understand the causes of change in the outcome. Based on regression, we get the same two components described above: the change in predictors holding constant the regression coefficients and the change in the regression coefficients evaluated in the changed context.

Overall, the evolutionary biology literature has often expressed confusion, skepticism, or scorn about Fisher’s theorem. By contrast, economics has found the same analysis to be a fundamental tool to understand broad change in outcomes when in a changing context.

This article has the limited goal of showing the simple underlying mathematics. The use of regression and the analysis of change are so common across disciplines that it pays to understand the theory. How one interprets cause always has a significant subjective component. But one should base that subjectivity on a clear understanding of the objective aspects.

Product rule for finite differences

Finite differences have the form
\Delta y=y^{\prime}-y
. The prime denotes the value of the variable in a changed context. Let
z=bx
be the product of
b
and
x
. Then we can write the finite difference for
z
as

\Delta z=\Delta\left\lparen bx\right\rparen=b^{\prime}x^{\prime}-bx=\left\lparen b+\Delta b\right\rparen\left\lparen x+\Delta x\right\rparen-bx,

which simplifies to the product rule for finite differences

\Delta z=b\Delta x+x^{\prime}\Delta b.

On the right side, the first product term is the change in
x
holding
b
constant, and the second product term is the change in
b
multiplied by
x^{\prime}
, the value of
x
in the changed context. This is a particular type of chain rule expansion.

We often need the difference of sums. Let

z=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x_{i}=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}},

in which bold variables are vectors, and the dot denotes the dot product of two vectors, which as shown is the sum of the element-wise products of the vectors. Then the product rule for finite differences extends to

\Delta z=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

(1)

If the differences are small, we write the difference operator as a differential,
\Delta\rightarrow\textrm{d}
. Then, recalling that
x^{\prime}=x+\Delta x
and replacing differences by differentials, we obtain

\textrm{d}z=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bm{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{b}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

For small differences, the final term is the second order product of two small values, which is negligible relative to the first order smallness of the prior terms. Thus, in the limit we drop the final term and get the product rule for differentials

\textrm{d}z=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bm{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

However, many realistic analyses apply to finite differences with measurements at two separated times. So, the less known product rule for finite differences in eqn
1
is often a useful expression, as shown in the next section.

Difference in a regression model

Write the value of our focal variable as a regression equation

z_{j}=b_{0}x_{0j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}x_{ij}+\epsilon_{j}

Here, we have a population of
z
values indexed by
j
. Each instance of
z_{j}
depends on the predictors,
x_{ij}
, with each predictor weighted by its associated partial regression coefficient,
b_{i}
. The term
\epsilon_{j}
is the residual or error.

In the intercept term,
b_{0}x_{0j}
, going forward we set
x_{0j}=1
for all
j
, so that
b_{0}
is the intercept. This allows us to write the regression more compactly in a way that matches the notation of other key steps in this article as

z_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x_{ij}+\epsilon_{j}=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}+\epsilon_{j}.

(2)

Each instance
j
depends on the vector of predictors,
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}
, with each predictor weighted by its associated partial regression coefficient in
\bm{\mathrm{b}}
. Subsequent sums for
i
also run over
i=0,1,\dots,n
.

In the underlying population of
z
values, suppose each value
z_{j}
occurs with frequency
q_{j}
. Then the mean value of
z
is

\displaystyle\bar{z}=\sum_{j}q_{j}z_{j}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{z}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}b_{i}\sum_{j}q_{j}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}b_{i}\bar{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\bar{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}.

Here, by standard least squares for regression, the average residual is zero,
\sum_{j}q_{j}\epsilon_{j}=0
.

For the regression of
z
, we want the difference between the current mean value and the mean value in a changed context,
\Delta\bar{z}=\bar{z}^{\prime}-\bar{z}
, which we obtain by applying the general expression for finite differences in eqn
1
, yielding

\Delta\bar{z}=\Delta\left\lparen\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\bar{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\right\rparen=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta\bar{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bar{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}^{\prime}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

(3)

It is of course possible to interpret particular predictors as interactions between causal factors. But the point here concerns the general structure of the problem rather than the specific causal model.

Fisher’s fundamental theorem

Fisher’s theorem is simply the application of the general expression for the difference in the mean value of a regression variable, given in eqn
3
. Let the variable of interest be fitness, denoted
w\equiv z
, and the difference in mean fitness be
\Delta\bar{w}\equiv\Delta\bar{z}
.

For simplicity, assume a haploid genetic model, in which the multiple loci in an individual are denoted by
x_{ij}
for the
i=1,\dots,n
loci in the
j
th individual, with
x_{0j}=1
as the indicator variable for the intercept. Suppose that at each locus there is an allele that takes on values of either
0
or
1
. More complex genetics can be handled within similar regression expressions, but the added notation does not provide additional insight
9
.

With those assumptions, the average value of a predictor in the regression is the frequency of the allele with value
1
, or in standard genetic language,
p_{i}=\bar{x}_{i}
is the gene frequency at the
i
th locus. For the intercept,
p_{0}=1
.

Now the change in mean fitness follows immediately from eqn
3
as

\Delta\bar{w}=\Delta\left\lparen\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}\right\rparen=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{p}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

(4)

This expression describes the total change exactly. Fisher emphasized that the first term focuses on the change in gene frequencies caused by natural selection,
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{p}}
, while holding constant the average effect of each gene on fitness, given by the partial regression coefficients,
\bm{\mathrm{b}}
.

Main result

If, without loss of generality, we assume that in the initial population
\bar{w}=1
, and, following Fisher, define the marginal fitness of allele
i
as
w_{i}=p_{i}^{\prime}/p_{i}
, then

\Delta p_{i}=p_{i}\left\lparen w_{i}-1\right\rparen=p_{i}\alpha_{i},

for which Fisher defined
\alpha_{i}=w_{i}-1
as the average excess in fitness. Then the first term of the finite difference of the regression in eqn
4
is

\Delta\bar{w}_{\mathrm{ns}}=\bm{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{p}}=\sum_{i}b_{i}\Delta p_{i}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\alpha_{i}b_{i},

(5)

which is often called the additive genetic variance in fitness, as shown in the following subsection. Fisher called this the fundamental theorem of natural selection. The result is that the partial change in mean fitness caused by the change in gene frequencies while holding constant the average effects is the additive genetic variance in fitness.

In Fisher’s causal perspective, changes in gene frequencies,
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{p}}
, are caused directly by natural selection, and changes in the partial regression coefficients,
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{b}}
, are caused by change in context.

Details on genetic variance

This subsection shows why the expression on the right side of eqn
5
is a genetic variance. These details can be skipped on first reading.

From eqn
2
, we can write the regression expression for fitness as

w_{j}=\sum_{i}b_{i}x_{ij}+\epsilon_{j}=g_{j}+\epsilon_{j},

in which
g_{j}=\sum_{i}b_{i}x_{ij}
is the genetic value of an individual, and here we are indexing fitness by
j
rather than
i
as above. Thus, compactly,
w=g+\epsilon
, the sum of the modeled genetic and residual effects. Next, recall that
q^{\prime}_{j}=q_{j}w_{j}
for our assumed mean fitness of
\bar{w}=1
, and
p_{i}=\sum_{j}q_{j}x_{ij}
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta p_{i}=\sum_{j}q^{\prime}_{j}x_{ij}-\sum_{j}q_{j}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}q_{j}\left\lparen w_{j}-1\right\rparen x_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(x_{i},w).

Then, in eqn
5
, we can equivalently write

\sum_{i}b_{i}\Delta p_{i}=\mathrm{Cov}\left\lparen\sum b_{i}x_{i},w\right\rparen=\mathrm{Cov}(g,w)=\mathrm{Var}(g),

because
g
and
\epsilon
are uncorrelated by least squares regression. Thus, the expression in eqn
5
is the genetic variance for the particular model of genetic effects.

The Price equation

The Price equation is another simple product rule expression for finite differences
16
,
10
. This equation is widely used in ecology, evolutionary biology, and other disciplines. Define the population mean of a variable as
\bar{z}=\sum_{j}q_{j}z_{j}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{z}}
, in which
q_{j}
is the frequency of the value
z_{j}
. Then, by our standard expression for finite differences

\Delta\bar{z}=\Delta\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{z}}\mskip 5.0mu+\mskip 5.0mu\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{z}}.

(6)

The Price equation is often expressed in terms of covariance and expectation functions, which can be obtained by noting that we can write frequency changes in terms of fitness as in the prior section as
\Delta q_{j}=q_{j}\left\lparen w_{j}-1\right\rparen
, again assuming that
\bar{w}=1
in the initial population. Also note that from the prior section we have for the relation between frequencies and fitness
q_{j}^{\prime}=w_{j}q_{j}
. Then we can expand eqn
6
as

\displaystyle\Delta\bar{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}q_{j}\left\lparen w_{j}-1\right\rparen z_{j}+\sum_{j}q_{j}w_{j}\Delta z_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(w,z)+\mathrm{E}(w\Delta z).

Discussion

The product-rule partition and its application to regression are purely algebraic and hold without any causal assumptions. Causality enters only when one interprets the regression coefficients as effects, such as Fisher’s average effects, and when one chooses a what-if counterfactual comparison that specifies what is held fixed across contexts
11
,
15
.

Fisher’s theorem corresponds to the counterfactual in which the regression coefficients linking individual genes to fitness are held constant while gene frequencies change. The remainder term collects changes in that mapping due to genetic interactions, genetic background, and environment.

This perspective explains both the usefulness of Fisher’s partition for a particular causal question and why disagreements persist when readers prefer a different counterfactual analysis.

Similar analysis shows up in demography and economics, and also in thermodynamics, where changes are routinely partitioned by asking what would happen if one quantity changed while others were held fixed
12
,
2
,
7
.
