Federated sparse-learning framework for detecting low-rate stealth attacks in resource-constrained IoT devices
Authors: R. Anandhi, G. Umarani Srikanth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-rate stealth attacks present a major challenge in Internet of Things (IoT) environments because their slow, irregular, and noise-like traffic patterns evade traditional rate-based intrusion detection systems. To address this problem, this paper proposes FSL-IDS, a hierarchical federated intrusion detection framework designed for resource-constrained IoT deployments. The framework integrates sparse representation learning, hierarchical temporal modeling, and federated optimization across distributed IoT, fog, and cloud layers. At the device level, a sparsity-constrained encoder captures subtle deviations in local traffic behavior, while fog nodes employ a Hierarchical Ensemble for Correlated Events (H-EFCE) to identify cross-device temporal attack patterns. At the cloud layer, a Federated Gradient Aggregation Module (FGAM) performs robust global model aggregation, and a Lightweight Quantized Model Optimization Layer (LQMOL) enables efficient deployment on constrained edge devices. The proposed system was evaluated on the IoTID20 dataset containing heterogeneous smart-home IoT traffic, including Mirai, DoS, scan, authentication attacks, and stealth anomalies. To address the scarcity of low-rate stealth samples, a Synthetic Stealth Attack Injection Module (SSAIM) was used to augment training data while preserving realistic traffic characteristics. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed architecture significantly improves stealth attack detection compared with multiple baseline methods, including federated learning algorithms and classical anomaly detection models. The FGAM-based cloud model achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.992 and F1-score of 0.965, outperforming FedAvg, FedProx, GRU-Autoencoder, Temporal Convolutional models, and Isolation Forest baselines under identical dataset and split conditions. Ablation experiments confirm that each architectural component contributes to performance gains, with the removal of the sparse encoder, temporal ensemble, or SSAIM module reducing detection accuracy and recall for low-rate stealth anomalies. Sensitivity analysis further shows stable detection performance across varying proportions of synthetic stealth traffic, while evaluation on limited real stealth samples confirms consistent detection capability. Additionally, model compression experiments demonstrate that 6-bit quantization provides an effective balance between efficiency and security, reducing edge-device energy consumption by 43% while maintaining near-optimal detection performance; further compression below this threshold leads to noticeable degradation in stealth-attack recall.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT) has brought millions of low-cost, low-power devices into homes, industries, and public infrastructures, creating a digital ecosystem where physical objects communicate autonomously and continuously. While this interconnected landscape enables automation and real-time decision-making, it also exposes a vast attack surface in which adversaries target devices that possess minimal computational power and often lack sophisticated security mechanisms1–4. Traditionally, IoT intrusion detection systems have focused on identifying high-volume or burst-based attacks such as classical DoS, DDoS, and botnet-driven floods, because these threats create abrupt and easily measurable changes in network activity5–7. However, in recent years attackers have increasingly adopted a more subtle technique known as low-rate stealth attacks, in which harmful traffic is delivered deliberately slowly, irregularly, and in patterns designed to imitate typical idle communication behavior. Instead of overwhelming a device or network link, the attacker manipulates timing, inter-arrival rates, and micro-level statistical properties to gradually consume resources, interfere with legitimate processes, or probe system weaknesses without triggering rate thresholds or anomaly signatures used by conventional defenses8–11. Detecting these attacks on resource-constrained IoT hardware is exceptionally difficult for two reasons: first, their traffic patterns closely resemble legitimate intermittent communication common to battery-powered sensors and actuators; second, embedded devices lack the processing capability to execute complex time-series models or deep neural networks that might otherwise capture subtle temporal deviations. As a result, low-rate stealth attacks frequently pass through lightweight IDS solutions undetected and can persist for long periods, enabling adversaries to stage reconnaissance, maintain quietly sustained disruptions, or escalate into more damaging multi-stage intrusions12–17.

Compounding the challenge is the heterogeneity of IoT environments, where devices vary widely in hardware architectures, operating schedules, communication protocols, and traffic rhythms. A threshold or anomaly score calibrated for one device may be entirely inappropriate for another, and building separate, fully trained models for each individual device is computationally and economically impractical. Cloud-based detection is also unsuitable for many deployments because continuous offloading of traffic flows introduces latency, consumes bandwidth, and raises privacy concerns, especially in health-care, smart-home, and industrial settings where sensitive information may be inferred from metadata alone18–21. These limitations have motivated researchers to explore distributed learning methods, yet most existing approaches rely on bulk exchange of model parameters or assume stable and abundant computational resources that small IoT chips simply do not have. Moreover, many current federated learning (FL)-based intrusion detection systems were designed with large datasets and powerful edge gateways in mind and do not address the subtle statistical irregularities characteristic of stealthy low-rate attacks. Such attacks often appear as minor drifts or tiny inconsistencies in temporal correlations—changes so small that simple histogram-based features, rolling averages, or classical machine-learning models overlook them entirely22–24.

In this context, there is a pressing need for a new class of intrusion detection mechanisms that can recognize the fine-grained behavioral deviations associated with low-rate stealth attacks while still remaining feasible to execute on small embedded hardware25–27. This work responds to that gap by introducing a federated sparse-learning intrusion detection framework tailored specifically for resource-constrained IoT devices. The central idea is that instead of relying on computationally expensive models or raw traffic exchanges, each device learns a compressed internal representation of its communication behavior through a sparsity-driven encoder.

Beyond domain-specific IoT intrusion detection studies, several works provide foundational perspectives on machine learning techniques and their evolution. Singh et al.28 present an analytical overview of classical and modern machine learning algorithms, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and applicability across diverse domains, including AI- and IoT-driven systems. Similarly, Singh and Khanna29 discuss current trends in artificial intelligence, emphasizing the growing need for efficient, adaptive, and scalable learning models as AI systems migrate toward resource-constrained and edge-based environments. Recent advances in optimized learning architectures are illustrated by Pabitha et al.30, who demonstrate that carefully designed and optimized neural models can achieve high performance while controlling computational overhead. Although these studies are not focused on intrusion detection, they provide important conceptual motivation for adopting lightweight, efficient, and well-structured machine learning models in embedded IoT security frameworks.

Sparse representations are particularly valuable for stealth attack detection because they naturally highlight rare events or deviations in timing, micro-burst patterns, or protocol-level sequences that ordinary traffic tends to suppress. When encoded sparsely, even slight anomalies can produce noticeable distortions in the latent space, making them easier to classify with a lightweight model. The proposed system incorporates a small meta-adaptation layer that adjusts the encoder and classifier to each device’s unique communication style with only a small number of local observations, allowing the framework to maintain accuracy despite the high variability of IoT operating patterns. Instead of exchanging raw data or large parameter sets, devices share only compact updates derived from their sparse models through a communication-efficient federated learning process. This design preserves privacy, reduces bandwidth requirements, and enables the collective knowledge of the network to grow organically as each device encounters new variations of stealth behavior.

By aligning sparse encoding, adaptive personalization, and federated training, the framework addresses several long-standing shortcomings in IoT intrusion detection. First, it enhances sensitivity to the ultra-low-intensity deviations characteristic of stealthy low-rate attacks, which rarely generate obvious spikes in packet count or connection duration. Second, it allows the model to generalize across heterogeneous devices by capturing shared structural properties of benign and malicious low-rate traffic without enforcing a one-size-fits-all threshold. Third, it substantially reduces computational and memory overhead, making it suitable for devices with very limited RAM, storage, or processing cycles. Finally, because it relies on distributed incremental learning, the system can adapt over time to evolving attack strategies without retraining from scratch or imposing heavy communication burdens. Together, these capabilities aim to provide a practical, scalable, and resilient defense mechanism for modern IoT environments where stealthy, slow-burning attacks increasingly erode the boundaries between benign behavior and malicious intent. As IoT deployments continue to expand into critical domains such as smart manufacturing, energy grids, logistics, and medical technologies, the ability to detect such subtle threats early and efficiently becomes essential for maintaining reliable operation, safeguarding data integrity, and preventing the long-term footholds that stealth attacks are designed to establish.

Novel contributions

Although federated learning-based intrusion detection, edge–fog–cloud architectures, and model compression techniques have been individually explored in prior studies, this work introduces a novel, attack-driven integration specifically designed for low-rate stealth attacks (LRSA) under severe IoT resource constraints. Unlike existing hierarchical FL-IDS frameworks that primarily target high-rate attacks or assume capable edge gateways, the proposed FSL-IDS is explicitly engineered to surface ultra-low-intensity temporal deviations that closely resemble benign IoT behavior.

The novelty of this work lies not in proposing another hierarchical IDS, but in (i) enforcing sparsity at the representation level to amplify rare stealth deviations, (ii) introducing fog-level temporal ensemble correlation to capture distributed low-rate attack sequences, and (iii) combining variance-aware federated aggregation with synthetic LRSA augmentation and low-bit quantization to ensure robustness and deployability on memory- and energy-constrained IoT devices.

To the best of our knowledge, no existing IDS framework jointly integrates sparse latent learning, temporal multi-device correlation, LRSA-specific synthetic augmentation, and quantization-aware federated deployment within a unified IoT–Fog–Cloud pipeline. This design directly addresses a gap left by prior hierarchical FL-IDS approaches, which typically overlook stealthy low-rate behaviors and practical embedded deployment constraints.

Literature review

The collected literature addresses a wide spectrum of approaches for improving security in IoT Recent advances in federated learning and lightweight security for IoT reveal a clear pattern: distributed model training and compact representations are promising directions, but practical constraints—communication cost, device heterogeneity, and subtle adversarial behavior—remain insufficiently addressed. Mahmoudi et al.13 tackle one of these central operational issues by proposing FedCau, a proactive stopping policy designed to reduce both communication and computation in federated learning. Their work demonstrates how smarter stopping rules can cut unnecessary rounds without sacrificing global performance, an idea that is directly relevant for IoT settings where each communication round incurs battery and bandwidth costs. Yet FedCau and similar strategies often assume reasonably capable edge nodes; applying such policies to severely resource-limited IoT endpoints requires further compression of model updates and more aggressive sparsity-aware schemes to keep per-node overhead minimal.

On the malware detection front, several contemporary studies emphasize ensemble learning and feature engineering as effective defenses for networked devices. Nobakht et al.15 present DEMD-IoT, a deep ensemble combining convolutional networks and traffic-level representations, which yields strong detection rates in IoT malware benchmarks. Lee et al.9 complement this direction by showing the value of opcode-category features for static analysis, illustrating that tailored, domain-aware features can significantly boost classification performance. Murali et al. (2023) explore a biologically inspired angle—treating evolving malware variants as antigens to which antivirus systems must adapt—pointing to the practical challenge of concept drift in malware signatures. Together, these works suggest two useful lessons for IoT IDS design: (1) ensembles and rich feature transforms can mask weaknesses of single models, and (2) defense systems must accommodate the continual evolution of attack patterns rather than relying solely on static signatures.

Data and dataset concerns are also front and center. Kaur et al.6 survey the evolution of IoT security datasets and underscore many persistent problems—dataset heterogeneity, label noise, and limited representation of stealthy, low-intensity attacks. Lei et al.10 empirically examine label sources for IoT malware classification and expose how inconsistencies in labeling pipelines can lead to biased evaluation and overoptimistic reported performance. These findings are consequential for any model that claims robust cross-device generalization: models trained on biased or narrow datasets will likely fail to spot subtle low-rate behaviors in the wild. The dataset surveys point to the need for careful dataset curation and, where possible, synthetic injection of low-rate stealth traces to stress-test detection pipelines.

Architectural and deployment considerations form another strand of the literature. Kumar and Agrawal8 provide a survey of edge–fog–cloud paradigms for multi-dimensional IIoT data, showing that judicious placement of computation across hierarchical layers can alleviate latency and bandwidth pressures. Kar et al.5 similarly review offloading approaches and highlight the tradeoffs between centralized training and in-device processing. These system-level studies support the practical rationale for federated and edge-centric IDS designs: shifting lightweight encoding and initial inference to the device reduces sensitive data movement and can maintain responsiveness—provided the local computations themselves are sufficiently compact.

From a model-compression and quantization perspective, several recent works give useful technical tools. Yang et al.24 and Hao and Wu3 investigate low-bit quantization and compression of transformer-style models, revealing that while learned features may be low-rank, weights often are not, which complicates naive compression. Techniques such as oscillation-free quantization and careful low-rank approximations can, however, produce compact models suitable for constrained hardware. These principles apply beyond vision transformers: for lightweight IDS encoders and classifiers, conscious quantization and low-rank design choices can materially reduce memory and compute without large accuracy losses.

Security of the federated process itself is another important concern. Although not purely about detection, access control mechanisms and hierarchical trust models (e.g., Riad et al.19) show how multi-authority schemes can enforce selective sharing and reduce the risk of malicious participants. When applied to federated learning, such access control ideas can help mitigate poisoning risks and unauthorized aggregation, issues especially sensitive in heterogeneous IoT deployments. Beyond domain-specific IoT intrusion detection studies, several works provide foundational perspectives on machine learning techniques and their evolution. Singh et al.28 present an analytical overview of classical and modern machine learning algorithms, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and applicability across diverse domains, including AI- and IoT-driven systems. Similarly, Singh and Khanna29 discuss current trends in artificial intelligence, emphasizing the growing need for efficient, adaptive, and scalable learning models as AI systems migrate toward resource-constrained and edge-based environments. Recent advances in optimized learning architectures are illustrated by Pabitha et al.30, who demonstrate that carefully designed and optimized neural models can achieve high performance while controlling computational overhead. Although these studies are not focused on intrusion detection, they provide important conceptual motivation for adopting lightweight, efficient, and well-structured machine learning models in embedded IoT security frameworks.

Taken together, the literature paints a coherent but incomplete picture. Modern detectors (DEMD-IoT, opcode-based classifiers) achieve strong static detection on curated datasets but struggle when attacks masquerade as benign, low-rate behavior or when dataset labels are noisy. Federated learning frameworks and communication-aware protocols (FedCau) offer a route to distributed intelligence, yet they need to be combined with compression-friendly encoders and quantization strategies to be practical on extremely constrained devices. Moreover, the federated setting introduces fresh attack surfaces (poisoning and backdoors) and policy challenges, which call for integration of secure aggregation and access control.

This gap motivates a hybrid approach that combines sparsity-aware encoding, federated aggregation with communication-efficient update policies, and local meta-adaptation to personalize detectors to each device’s baseline traffic. Such a strategy leverages the strengths identified in the literature—ensemble robustness, feature specialization, and communication-savvy FL—while explicitly targeting the Achilles’ heel that remains largely unaddressed: detection of low-rate stealth attacks in highly constrained, heterogeneous IoT environments. In short, the state of the art supplies the components; what remains is an integration that balances sensitivity to micro-level anomalies with the severe resource limits of real IoT hardware, a challenge your proposed federated sparse-learning IDS aims to meet. Proposed work

The proposed research introduces a Federated Sparse-Learning Intrusion Detection Framework (FSL IDS) designed specifically to detect Low-Rate Stealth Attacks (LRSA) in heterogeneous and resource-constrained IoT ecosystems. Low-rate stealth attacks are particularly challenging because they mimic legitimate intermittent communication, remain under conventional detection thresholds, and exploit the limited computational capabilities of embedded IoT devices. The FSL IDS framework addresses these challenges by integrating sparse feature encoding, lightweight local anomaly detection, meta-adaptation, and communication-efficient federated learning in a three-tier IoT–Fog–Cloud architecture.

Dataset details

The proposed Federated Sparse-Learning Intrusion Detection System (FSL-IDS) is developed and evaluated using the IoTID20 dataset, which captures realistic network traffic generated in a smart-home IoT environment. The dataset contains flow-level network records collected from heterogeneous IoT devices, including smart plugs, IP cameras, motion sensors, and smart thermostats, communicating over common IoT protocols. Each flow is represented using over 80 statistical features covering packet counts, byte distributions, inter-arrival timings, directional asymmetry, session-level behaviors, and burst patterns. The dataset includes a total of 596,000 flows, comprising predominantly benign traffic, several thousand high-rate attacks (e.g., DoS, scanning, authentication abuse, replay attacks), and fewer than 300 naturally occurring low-rate stealth attacks (LRSA), reflecting the extreme class imbalance typical of real IoT deployments.

Stealth attack data augmentation strategy

Low-rate stealth attacks are intrinsically under-represented in real IoT traffic due to their slow execution rate, prolonged duration, and noise-like deviation from benign behavior. This scarcity poses a significant challenge for stable representation learning, particularly in hierarchical federated settings where each client observes only a small local data subset. To address this limitation without compromising evaluation integrity, the proposed framework incorporates a Synthetic Stealth Attack Injection Module (SSAIM) that is applied exclusively during the training phase.

To address the scarcity of LRSA samples without inflating evaluation performance, the framework incorporates a Synthetic Stealth Attack Injection Module (SSAIM), applied strictly during training. SSAIM generates synthetic LRSA samples through constraint-guided temporal perturbation of real benign flows, preserving protocol semantics, packet-size distributions, flow-level statistics, and inter-packet arrival patterns. Temporal consistency is maintained to mimic prolonged low-rate attack execution. Perturbations are bounded within empirically observed deviations to avoid artificial or trivially separable patterns. Using SSAIM, the number of LRSA samples was increased to 50,000, representing 20% of the training set. No synthetic samples were included in validation or test sets, and all reported evaluation metrics are computed exclusively on real stealth attack flows. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that performance gains plateau beyond ~ 20% synthetic fraction, ensuring that the augmentation enhances robustness without overfitting.

To prevent performance inflation or information leakage, synthetic samples are never included in validation or test sets, and train–test partitioning is performed prior to any augmentation. All detection results reported in the evaluation are computed solely on real stealth attack samples. Within the federated hierarchy, SSAIM is confined to local training updates, while global model aggregation through FGAM mitigates overfitting to synthetic distributions by validating gradient consistency across fog nodes. This design ensures that synthetic augmentation improves robustness to class imbalance while preserving generalization to real-world stealth attack behaviors.

System architecture

The FSL IDS framework is structured as a three-tier IoT–Fog–Cloud architecture, where each tier is responsible for specific tasks in LRSA detection. Figure 1 shows the model diagram.

IoT device layer

The IoT device layer performs lightweight, real-time intrusion detection under strict resource constraints. Each device implements a sparsity-driven encoding module (SAE-M), a local anomaly detector, and a meta-adaptation layer for continuous calibration. These components highlight subtle LRSA deviations while minimizing memory and computational overhead. Average inference latency per flow is 1.4 ms, and the module consumes under 5 MB memory and < 0.5 W energy, demonstrating deployability on typical embedded IoT hardware.

Fog layer

The fog layer functions as an intermediate aggregation and processing tier, enhancing detection capabilities by analyzing latent representations from multiple IoT devices. Fog nodes perform both temporal ensemble classification and coordination of federated updates, providing a balance between localized device computation and cloud-level global model optimization.

Cloud layer

The cloud layer serves as the central orchestration hub for global model training, secure aggregation, synthetic augmentation, and memory-efficient model deployment. Results and discussion

The performance of the proposed Federated Stealth Learning Intrusion Detection System (FSL-IDS) was evaluated using the IoTID20 dataset, a publicly available intrusion detection dataset containing network traffic collected from a smart home IoT environment. The dataset comprises over 80 statistical network-flow features extracted from traffic generated by IoT devices such as smart plugs, IP cameras, motion sensors, and smart thermostats. Attack categories include Mirai, DoS, scan attacks, fake authentication attempts, replay attacks, and low-rate stealth anomalies (LRSA). To address the lack of low-rate stealth samples, the dataset was augmented using the Synthetic Stealth Attack Injection Module (SSAIM) at the cloud layer.

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.10, TensorFlow 2.15, Scikit-learn, and PyTorch 2.2, with federated learning simulations executed using Flower (FLWR) 1.9. Experiments were executed on a system equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, 128 GB RAM, and Ubuntu 22.04 LTS environment. Edge-level simulations were deployed using Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (4 GB) and ESP32 nodes to evaluate model compression effects.

Performance was assessed using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, AUC-ROC, and Detection Latency. Findings

The evaluation results consistently demonstrate that the proposed FSL-IDS framework significantly outperforms traditional and contemporary intrusion detection systems, especially in identifying low-rate stealth anomalies (LRSA). The introduction of sparse latent encoding, temporal ensemble reasoning, and federated multi-layer aggregation enables accurate detection of stealthy threats that are typically camouflaged within benign traffic.

At the IoT layer, the integration of the sparse autoencoder and one-class classifier provides a compact, resource-efficient mechanism for early anomaly detection. Despite operating on constrained hardware, the system achieves 95% F1-score, indicating that the model generalizes well across heterogeneous devices.

At the fog layer, temporal aggregation significantly enhances multi-device situational awareness. The fog-level H-EFCE improves LRSA detection by over 10%, aligning with its design objective of interpreting subtle, distributed attack signals. This intermediate layer plays a critical role in stabilizing federated updates and filtering noise before cloud aggregation.

At the cloud layer, FGAM—integrating robust validation, secure aggregation, and SSAIM augmentation—delivers near-perfect performance with 0.992 AUC-ROC. The robustness mechanism ensures that poisoned, inconsistent, or malformed updates do not affect the global model. SSAIM is particularly impactful: augmenting the dataset with synthetic LRSA traces boosts recall by 36%, resolving the data imbalance issue inherent in IoTID20.

Model compression via LQMOL confirms the feasibility of deploying the global model back to resource-constrained IoT devices. The 6-bit quantized model retains 98.9% of the original accuracy while reducing computational and memory requirements, proving the practicality of continuous model updates across the federated hierarchy.

Conclusion

This study introduced FSL-IDS, a hierarchical federated intrusion detection framework explicitly designed to detect low-rate stealth attacks (LRSA) in resource-constrained IoT ecosystems. By integrating sparse latent encoding, hierarchical temporal ensemble modeling, and multi-tier federated adaptation, the framework addresses the inherent difficulty of identifying slow, irregular, and distributed attack patterns that typically evade conventional anomaly detection systems. The proposed architecture distributes intelligence across IoT devices, fog nodes, and the cloud, enabling efficient representation learning, cross-device correlation modeling, and global knowledge refinement while preserving data privacy. Experimental evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed system. The cloud-level Federated Gradient Aggregation Module (FGAM) achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.992 and F1-score of 0.965, surpassing both fog-layer (0.971) and device-layer (0.945) detectors, confirming the advantage of hierarchical learning. Comparative analysis against multiple baseline methods—including FedAvg, FedProx, GRU Autoencoder, Temporal Convolutional models, and Isolation Forest—under identical dataset and split conditions shows that FSL-IDS consistently provides superior detection accuracy and robustness for stealth attack scenarios. The fog-level H-EFCE mechanism captured temporal dependencies across multi-device streams, improving recall by 8–12% for prolonged stealth attack sequences. Ablation study results further confirm that each architectural component contributes to the overall performance, where removing the sparse encoder, temporal ensemble module, or SSAIM augmentation leads to measurable degradation in stealth attack detection accuracy and recall. The LQMOL model compression strategy achieved 43% energy savings with only a 1.1% accuracy reduction, enabling real-time deployment on memory- and power-limited embedded IoT hardware. Quantization sensitivity experiments additionally indicate that 6-bit precision provides an effective balance between computational efficiency and detection reliability, while lower precision levels introduce noticeable degradation in stealth attack recall. Sensitivity analysis with varying synthetic stealth traffic proportions demonstrates stable model performance, while evaluation on available real stealth samples confirms the framework’s ability to generalize to real-world low-rate attack patterns. Moreover, the end-to-end latency remained between 1.4 and 7 ms across system layers, validating the suitability of FSL-IDS for real-world continuous monitoring. Future work may include integrating adaptive trust scoring for federated nodes, extending the model to handle adversarial concept drift, and enabling on-device continual learning for long-term autonomous operation.
