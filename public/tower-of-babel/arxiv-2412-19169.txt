Accelerating Stochastic Gravitational Wave Backgrounds Parameter Estimation in Pulsar Timing Arrays with Flow Matching
Bo Liang, Chang Liu, Tianyu Zhao, Minghui Du, Manjia Liang, Ruijun Shi, Hong Guo, Yuxiang Xu, Li-e Qiang, Peng Xu, Wei-Liang Qian, Ziren Luo

Abstract
Pulsar timing arrays are essential tools for detecting the stochastic gravitational wave background, but their analysis faces significant computational challenges. Traditional methods like Markov-chain Monte Carlo struggle with high-dimensional parameter spaces where noise parameters often dominate, while existing deep learning approaches fail to model the Hellings-Downs correlation or are validated only on synthetic datasets. We propose a flow-matching-based continuous normalizing flow for efficient and accurate pulsar timing array parameter estimation. By focusing on the 10 most contributive pulsars from the NANOGrav 15-year dataset, our method achieves posteriors consistent with Markov-chain Monte Carlo, with a Jensen-Shannon divergence below 10 − 2 10^{-2} nat, while reducing sampling time from 50 hours to 4 minutes. Powered by a versatile embedding network and a reweighting loss function, our approach prioritizes the stochastic gravitational wave background parameters and scales effectively for future datasets. It enables precise reconstruction of stochastic gravitational wave background and opens new avenues for exploring vast observational data and uncovering potential new physics, offering a transformative tool for advancing gravitational wave astronomy.

The stochastic gravitational wave background signal is modeled as a common process affecting all pulsars, characterized by an amplitude
A_{\mathrm{GW}}
and a spectral index
\gamma_{\mathrm{GW}}
. The power spectral density of the stochastic gravitational wave background-induced timing residuals follows a power-law spectrum given by

P_{\mathrm{SGWB}}(f)=\frac{A_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{GW}}}f^{-3}_{\mathrm{ref}},

(1)

where
f
is the frequency
and
f_{\mathrm{ref}}=1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

is the reference frequency. To capture the spatial correlations between pulsars due to the stochastic gravitational wave background, we incorporated the Hellings-Downs-correlation matrix
\chi_{IJ}
, defined by

\begin{split}\chi_{IJ}=\frac{3}{2}\Bigg[\left(\frac{1-\cos\theta_{IJ}}{2}\right)\ln&\left(\frac{1-\cos\theta_{IJ}}{2}\right)\\
&-\frac{1}{6}\left(1-\cos\theta_{IJ}\right)+\frac{1}{3}\Bigg],\end{split}

(2)

where
\theta_{IJ}
is the angular separation between pulsars
I
and
J
. We simulated
\chi_{IJ}
by calculating the angular separations between the ten selected pulsars and evaluating the HD function for each pair. Each pulsar also has its own intrinsic red noise, with amplitude
A_{r}^{(i)}
and spectral index
\gamma_{r}^{(i)}
, following a similar power-law spectrum:

P_{\mathrm{red}}^{(i)}(f)=\frac{A_{r}^{(i)\,2}}{12\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{-\gamma_{r}^{(i)}}f^{-3}_{\mathrm{ref}}.

(3)

In order to better fit the real situation, we have individually processed the white noise level
\sigma
for each pulsar, and set
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
where
\sigma_{i}
is a specific value obtained for each selected pulsar after processing the NG15 data with the ENTERPRISE software, and the average level of these values is roughly between 100-500 ns.

This approach allowed us to accurately simulate both the common stochastic gravitational wave background signal—including its HD correlations—and the individual noise characteristics of each pulsar.

Before training, we pre-processed the simulated residuals to optimize the performance of our machine learning model. This involved rescaling the residuals to ensure that they were within a suitable range for neural network training. The ten pulsars contribute a total of 4,944 projected residuals, providing a rich dataset for analysis. We partitioned the 1.5 million generated time series into two sets: 98% were used for training the continuous normalizing flows, and the remaining 2% were reserved for model validation. This dataset enabled us to effectively train and validate our model.

Model Architecture.—

In this Letter, we employ continuous normalizing flow to fit the posterior distribution of the NANOGrav15 dataset
Agazie et al. 2023
, modeling it with a power-law spectrum and incorporating Hellings-Downs correlations. To optimize the continuous normalizing flows, we utilize flow matching
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Dax et al. 2023
, a novel technique in artificial intelligence that accelerates the training of continuous normalizing flows. This method, known as flow-matching parameter estimation, enhances efficiency and precision in modeling complex distributions.

A continuous normalizing flow defines a transformation of a probability density through a continuous-time dynamical system. Given a base distribution
p_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
and a target distribution
p_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})
, the transformation is defined by the ordinary differential equation
Chen et al. 2019
:

\frac{d}{dt}\psi_{t,x}(\bm{\theta})=v_{t,x}(\psi_{t,x}(\bm{\theta})).

(4)

Where,
\psi_{t,x}(\bm{\theta})
describes the transition from the base distribution
p_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
to the target distribution
p_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})
.

To train the CNF, we employ the flow-matching loss function
Tong et al. 2024
;
Lipman et al. 2023
, which minimizes the difference between the vector field
v_{t,x}(\psi_{t,x}(\bm{\theta}))
and the optimal vector field
u_{t}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}_{1})
that transports
p_{0}
to
p_{1}
. The flow-matching loss is given by:

\mathcal{L_{\mathrm{FM}}}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t)}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}\sim p(\bm{\theta})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x|\bm{\theta}_{1})}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{t}\sim p_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{1})}\parallel r\parallel^{2}.\ \

(5)

Where
p(\bm{\theta})
is the logit normal sampling strategy
Esser et al. 2024
, with
m=0,s=1
.
Where
r
is the residual vector field:

r=v_{t,x}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-u_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{1})

(6)

Different from flow matching, in this work we are inspired by stable diffusion 3
Esser et al. 2024
. We define the
p_{t}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}_{1})
as:

p_{t}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}_{1})=\mathcal{N}(t\bm{\theta}_{1},1-t).

(7)

Thus the optimal vector field
u_{t}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}_{1})
is defined as
Liu et al. 2022
;
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
:

u_{t}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}_{1})=\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{0}.

(8)

In practice,
{}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{1})
can be approximated by sampling from the conditional distribution
p(\bm{\theta}_{1}|\bm{\theta}_{t})
.

In pulsar timing array research, a key challenge lies in effectively addressing the high-dimensional noise parameters, which can span tens of dimensions, compared to the two-dimensional SGWB parameters associated with the Hellings-Downs correlation. The dominance of noise parameter dimensions often limits the focus on stochastic gravitational wave background signals, leading to high computational costs and convergence difficulties in Markov-chain Monte Carlo analysis. To tackle these challenges, we introduce a hyperparameter
\lambda
into the loss function, defined as:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{SGWB}}_{\mathrm{FM}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{Noise}}_{\mathrm{FM}}\,\text{.}

(9)

This approach enables flexible reweighting of the model’s attention, allowing it to prioritize the fitting of the stochastic gravitational wave background. By balancing the contributions of stochastic gravitational wave background and noise parameters, our method improves both the efficiency and accuracy of parameter estimation.

Our model architecture comprises two main components, as illustrated in
Figure

1
. The
first component
is an embedding network designed to process the 4,944 residuals from the ten pulsars and perform feature extraction. Since our flow network ultimately fits a two-dimensional vector field, the embedding network is essential for reducing the high-dimensional input data to a manageable size. We employ a long short-term memory network as the embedding network
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
, which compresses the features of each pulsar into 100 dimensions. These features are further refined and compressed into 50 dimensions using a residual network
He et al. 2015
. The resulting compressed features, denoted as
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{50}
, serve as conditional inputs to the flow network.

The
second component
is the flow network itself, consisting of 56 residual blocks with gradually decreasing sizes. The vector field
\mathbf{v}_{t,x}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
is parameterized by the neural network
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and conditioned on the embedded features
\mathbf{c}
and the time parameter
t
. Specifically, the flow network takes as input the triplet
(\bm{\theta}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})
and outputs the vector field:

\mathbf{v}_{t,x}(\bm{\theta}_{t})=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}).

(10)

To implement our normalized flow and embedding networks, we chose the
PyTorch

Paszke et al. 2019
framework combined with the
numpy

Harris et al. 2020
library, as well as the
zuko

Rozet et al. 2022
and
lampe

Rozet et al. 2021
software packages.
The model was trained for 100 epochs utilizing a batch size of 512, employing the Adam optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2017
for efficient convergence.
The initial learning rate was set to 0.0001 and was gradually reduced to zero using a cosine annealing schedule. Training was conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU and took approximately 20 hours. Remarkably, sampling from the trained continuous normalizing flow to produce posterior distributions involves solving the ODE, which takes approximately 240 seconds for 8,192 samples using a Runge-Kutta solver
Kidger et al. 2021
;
Chen 2018
. In contrast, generating an equivalent number of posterior draws using the traditional Markov-chain Monte Carlo pipeline takes approximately 50 hours on an Apple M3 Max chip. This significant reduction in computation time demonstrates the efficiency and practicality of our approach for large-scale PTA data analysis.

By effectively handling real data and capturing the full Hellings-Downs correlations—overcoming the limitations of previous studies that used neural parameter estimation methods—our flow-matching-based continuous normalizing flow approach underscores the potential of advanced machine learning techniques to revolutionize pulsar timing array data analysis. Our method offers a viable and alternative to traditional Markov-chain Monte Carlo methods for current and future datasets, efficiently addressing the complexities and computational demands that have challenged past methodologies.

As the pulsar timing array dataset grows increasingly large, the spectral constraints on stochastic gravitational wave background will become more precise due to the expansion of the dataset, which will help reveal its origins. At the same time, future PTA observations will deepen our understanding of this signal and its astrophysical and cosmological interpretations. However, with the growth of the dataset, traditional Markov-chain Monte Carlo-based techniques face the challenge of exponential growth in computational time. Machine learning techniques offer a promising alternative, with the potential to enhance parameter estimation for pulsar timing array data and complement existing Markov-chain Monte Carlo methods. These advancements will enable more efficient analysis of larger and more complex pulsar timing array datasets, paving the way for precise reconstructions of gravitational wave backgrounds and uncovering potential new physics.

Discussion.—

In this study, we introduced continuous normalizing flows as a highly expressive technique for parameter estimation in pulsar timing array data analysis. While continuous normalizing flows have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in handling pulsar timing array data, their performance is currently limited by the variable sequence lengths inherent in pulsar timing residuals. This limitation arises because traditional embedding networks struggle to effectively process sequences of varying lengths, potentially hindering the model’s ability to capture all relevant temporal features. To overcome this challenge, future work could explore the adoption of more advanced sequence-to-sequence models, such as Transformer
Vaswani et al. 2017
or Mamba
Gu and Dao 2023
, which are designed to handle variable-length sequences more efficiently. These models, with their ability to capture long-range dependencies and process sequences of arbitrary length, may further enhance the performance of deep learning techniques in pulsar timing array data analysis, pushing the boundaries of gravitational wave astronomy.
