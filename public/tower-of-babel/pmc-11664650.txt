Ion Migration at Metal Halide Perovskite Grain Boundaries Elucidated with a Machine Learning Force Field
Authors: Mikhail
R Samatov, Dongyu Liu, Long Zhao, Elena A Kazakova, Dmitrii A Abrameshin, Abinash Das, Andrey S Vasenko, Oleg V Prezhdo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal halide perovskites are promising optoelectronic materials with excellent defect tolerance in carrier recombination, believed to arise largely from their unique soft lattices. However, weak lattice interactions also promote ion migration, leading to serious stability issues. Grain boundaries (GBs) have been experimentally identified as the primary migration channels, but the relevant mechanism remains elusive. Using molecular dynamics with a machine learning force field, we directly model ion migration at a common CsPbBr3 GB. We demonstrate that the as-built GB model, containing 6400 atoms, experiences structural reconstruction over several nanoseconds, and only Br atoms diffuse after that. A fraction of Br atoms near the GB either migrate toward the GB center or along the GB through different migration channels. Increasing the temperature not only accelerates the ion migration via the Arrhenius activation but also allows more Br atoms to migrate. The activation energies are much lower at the GB than in the bulk due to large-scale structural distortions and favorable non-stoichiometric local environments available at GBs. Making the local GB composition more stoichiometric by doping or annealing can suppress the ion migration. The reported results provide valuable atomistic insights into the GB properties and ion migration in metal halide perovskites.

Metal halide perovskites (MHPs) are highly attractive for developing next-generation electronic and optoelectronic devices, because they exhibit simultaneously several key properties, including strong light absorption, tunable bandgap, high carrier mobility, and long carrier lifetime.1−4 This distinctive feature highlights their potential in many advanced technologies such as solar cells,5−7 photodetectors,8,9 light-emitting diodes,10 and field-effect transistors.11 The advantages are largely believed to arise from the unique electronic structure and soft lattice of MHPs.12 At the same time, weak lattice interactions tend to yield high defect densities in experiments.13−15 Although MHPs exhibit excellent defect tolerance in carrier recombination,16,17 many defects lead to severe stability issues by assisting ion migration.18−22 Ion depletion and accumulation in MHPs can generate more defects, disrupt local stoichiometric structures, and eventually result in degradation.19,21 Migrating ions not only create various defects, such as vacancies and interstitials,23−25 but also promote phase segregation in mixed MHPs.26−28 Moreover, non-stoichiometric local structures can facilitate transformation of MHPs to non-perovskite phases and structural decomposition.29−32 Experimentally, MHPs are usually prepared from solution as polycrystalline films, in which grain boundaries (GBs) are identified as the primary ion migration channels.33−36 A number of GB passivation strategies have been employed for developing high-performance MHPs.37−40 Still, the relevant ion migration mechanism remains elusive and many contradictory observations are reported.41 Indeed, due to the relatively weak lattice interactions, various structural distortions and defective configurations are expected to appear at MHP GBs. Such complexity and diversity make it challenging to comprehensively characterize the ion migration behavior in experiments.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is a powerful approach to investigate ion migration by modeling structural evolution of MHPs at the atomistic level. Defect assisted ion migration can be investigated in detail. Typically, such simulations employ interatomic forces from ab initio quantum mechanical calculations. On the basis of the ab initio MD (AI-MD) method, the diffusion of halide vacancies in the bulk of MHPs has been determined to occur on picosecond time scales and involve significant interactions with the A-site cations.42−45 However, ab initio calculations strongly limit the simulation time scales and system sizes.46−48 They are too costly computationally for properly sampling the complex structural variations and dynamics of MHP GBs. Machine learning (ML) force fields (FFs) provide an ideal solution to this problem, because they can be trained with small models using ab initio data and applied to large systems with comparable accuracy.49−53 Our previous ML-based MD (ML-MD) studies have demonstrated that MHP GBs undergo relaxation over hundreds of picoseconds and exhibit large-scale structural fluctuations in equilibrium.54 Further, such fluctuations can be efficiently suppressed by compressive strain.55 These observations are consistent with the soft nature of MHP lattices and provide important insights into understanding the GB behavior. Modeling these processes is far beyond the capacities of AI-MD, indicating the indispensable role of ML-MD in studying ion migration at MHP GBs. CsPbBr3 is an excellent choice for studying the GB properties in MHPs. It is widely studied for many commercial applications and exhibits the highly desirable thermostability.56,57 This advantage largely eliminates thermally induced structural deformations near GBs, simplifying the investigation of ion migration.

In this work, we establish the mechanism of ion migration at a MHP GB by modeling the structural evolution of the CsPbBr3 ∑5 (120) GB using the ML-MD approach. A large model containing 6400 atoms is simulated for 10 ns to allow reliable observation of ion diffusion. Both the system size and the simulation time are magnitudes larger than those of AI-MD simulations. We show that the two grains undergo sliding at the GB within hundreds of picoseconds, after which the GB relaxes over a few nanoseconds. Following the equilibration, only Br atoms exhibit diffusion during the simulation. All migratable Br atoms are located near the GBs, indicating clearly that GBs strongly assist the ion diffusion. We develop an approach to distinguish uniquely migratable from non-migratable atoms according to atom mobility. The distribution of the migratable Br atoms reflects the diffusion channels. The Br atoms can migrate both across and along the GB through different channels, resulting in three-dimensional diffusion. By comparing the simulation results at different temperatures, we demonstrate that the diffusion coefficients follow the Arrhenius equation, and that more Br atoms become migratable at higher temperatures. While the overall system remains stoichiometric, the high-mobility of Br ions at the GB region tends to coordinate with scarcity of Cs atoms and abundance of Pb atoms. These locally non-stoichiometric GB configurations play an important role in composing the migration channels. We show that the activation energies for the ion migration are much lower at the GB than in the bulk due to the favorable non-stoichiometric local environments and large-scale structural distortions available at the GB. Confirming GBs as the primary ion migration channels in MHPs, even without any other defects, the simulations provide novel and valuable insights into the ion migration mechanism.

The ML FF was developed using the DeePMD-kit package58 in our previous studies54,55 and implemented in the LAMMPS code59 for MD simulations. The training sets were generated from AI-MD simulations. The accuracy of the trained ML FF model was verified by comparing the ML-predicted potential energies and the ab initio results. The computational details and the ML FF validation can be found in refs.54,55 We built the GB model using the Atomsk code60 by combining two CsPbBr3 slab models in opposite directions. A large GB model containing 6400 atoms was employed, where two periodic GBs were separated by 53.07 Å to avoid their interactions. The MD simulations were carried out at 300, 350, and 400 K in canonical ensembles. We ran the simulation at each temperature for 10 ns with a time step of 1 fs and saved the structures every 1 ps. The structures were visualized with the VESTA program.61 The structural analysis was conducted using the OVITO package62 and our homemade scripts.

Figure 1a displays the local structure of the as-built CsPbBr3 ∑5 (120) GB model, consisting of two grains with an angle to each other. The whole model is shown in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information. This defective GB configuration contains unsaturated Pb and Br atoms, which may lead to structural deformations. We heated the GB model to 300 K and modeled its structural evolution over 10 ns in the MD simulation. The mean squared displacements (MSDs) of all atoms along different directions are plotted in Figure 1b. The MSD along the x axis exhibits a steep increase at the beginning of the simulation. This change is due to a fast sliding between two grains within about 100 ps because of the unstable GB structure.54,55 The MSDs along the y and z axes are rarely affected, indicating this sliding is relatively rigid. The system takes a few nanoseconds to relax, as indicated by the constantly increasing total MSD, and reaches equilibrium after that. Figure 1c shows the MSDs of different elements. We calculated these results relative to the structure at 500 ps to eliminate the initial jumps. All atoms experience the structural relaxation until around 3 ns as indicated by their increasing MSDs, which is consistent with the total MSD. The MSDs of Cs and Pb atoms become stable after this period, indicating the system reaches equilibrium. Nevertheless, the MSD of Br atoms remains increasing. This MSD change corresponds to the equilibrium diffusion of Br atoms with the help of GBs, because the bulk diffusion in MHPs is difficult without defects. MHP GBs have been experimentally identified as the primary ion migration channels, but the relevant atomistic simulations are rarely reported. Our results demonstrate that a relatively slow structural relaxation is essential for observing the equilibrium diffusion in MHPs, which can hardly be included in short-term MD simulations. A scheme of the evolution of the GB model is illustrated in Figure 1d. It first experiences a fast sliding between two grains on a time scale of picoseconds and then relaxes in several nanoseconds. After this relatively long-term relaxation, the system is in equilibrium and the Br atoms diffuse at the GB regions. The MSD results over 10 ns can be found in Figure S2 of the Supporting Information.

The mobility of different Br atoms is further investigated. Figure 2a plots the z coordinates of all Br atoms in the final structure of the MD simulation and their maximum displacements during the equilibrium diffusion; i.e., the displacements induced by grain sliding and structural relaxation in the first 3 ns are not accounted. The maximum displacements of most Br atoms are around 3 Å, close to the Pb–Br bond length, arising from vibrations of the CsPbBr3 lattice. Some Br atoms near the GBs exhibit much larger displacements, indicating that they have deviated from the initial positions. The migratable Br atoms distribute in a wide region of around 25 Å at each GB. These results suggest that large models are needed in GB simulations to ensure that the two GBs are properly separated by bulk-like regions, avoiding artificial interactions due to the periodic boundary conditions. It is important to emphasize that not all Br atoms near the GBs are migratable. We distinguish the migratable atoms based on their maximum displacements. Specifically, we set a cutoff for the data points in Figure 2a, e.g., the red dashed line, and take the atoms distributed above this cutoff. Figure 2b shows the MSD results of the “migratable” Br atoms determined by different cutoffs. The slopes of the MSD curves after 3 ns represent the mobility of the selected Br atoms, i.e., the diffusion coefficients. Small cutoffs like 2.0 and 2.5 Å lead to relatively flat MSD curves, indicating most of the selected Br atoms are non-migratable. A medium cutoff of 3 Å significantly changes the shape of the MSD curve, and further increasing the cutoff value continuously enlarging the slope. This trend indicates an untrivial dependence of the diffusion MSD slope upon the cutoff value as illustrated in Figure 2c. The slope shows a transition from nonlinear to linear, implying the majority of the Br atoms above the cutoff gradually change from non-migratable to migratable atoms. In addition, the nonlinear part of the MSD slope curve can be properly fitted with a Gaussian function, and the peak center represents the cutoff for distinguishing the migratable Br atoms. This cutoff is plotted in Figure 2a as the example, which is close to the upper boundary of the distribution of bulk-like Br atoms. This method provides a reasonable approach to identify the migratable Br atoms at the GB regions. Moreover, the dependence of the number of migratable Br atoms upon the cutoff is plotted in Figure 2d. The atom number rapidly decreases when many atoms possess similar maximum displacements. The cutoff line intersects with the curve at the relatively flat region, implying most non-migratable Br atoms have been eliminated. The small amount of migratable Br atoms also highlights the necessity of using large models in MHP GB studies.

Panels a and b of Figure 3 display the distribution of the migratable Br atoms during the equilibrium diffusion. The positions of these Br atoms at each time step are plotted as partially transparent solid red circles, and the final positions of all atoms are plotted as scattered dots. Some special patterns composed of red regions appear around the GB, which reflects the Br migration channels. The front view plot indicates the Br atoms mainly migrate along the lattice instead of vertically along the GB. This behavior is similar to the previously reported defect-assisted ion migration in MHP bulk.19 The top view plot demonstrates the Br atoms either migrate toward the GB center or along the GB because most channels are either horizontal or vertical. The combination of bulk diffusion and GB diffusion enables the Br atoms to migrate along different directions of the GB as shown in the side view plot (Figure 3b). Especially, the defective GB structures not only serve as migration channels but also attract neighboring Br atoms to the GB center, resulting in three-dimensional migration. Figure 3c shows the MSD evolutions of the migratable Br atoms along different directions. The results are calculated relative to the structure at 3 ns and exhibit similar slopes with time, indicating the bulk and GB channels possess comparable capacities in transporting Br atoms. Moreover, the local environment of different Br atoms is characterized by the radial distribution function (RDF) as drawn in Figure 3d. The close RDF curves imply that the migratable Br atoms largely maintain the bulk-like configuration, although many of them have very different structures in the as-built model (Figure 1a). Nevertheless, the RDF peaks and valleys are slightly smeared compared to those of the non-migratable Br atoms, which suggests a transition from crystalline to amorphous structures. The sliding and relaxation procedures dramatically modify the GB configuration and create special channels for ion migration.

We performed ML-MD simulations of the GB model at 350 and 400 K to study the temperature dependence of Br diffusion. 400 K provides a representation of operating conditions of various MHP applications, such as solar cells. Figure 4a displays the relationships between the number of migratable Br atoms and the displacement cutoff at different temperatures. The same Gaussian fitting approach as Figure 2c was employed to obtain the cutoff values, and the relevant results as indicated by the filled circles. Higher temperatures not only enhance the lattice vibrations, i.e., enlarge the maximum displacements of non-migratable Br atoms, but also activate more Br atoms becoming migratable. The increasing number of migratable Br atoms may lead to significant structural variations of MHP GBs and even change the ion migration mechanism. Figure 4b shows the temperature-dependent diffusion coefficients of migratable Br atoms along different directions. The data points are derived from the MSD slopes and fitted with the Arrhenius equation D = D0 exp(−Ea/kBT), where D is the diffusion coefficient, D0 is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature. The results of the y and z axes exhibit similar trends because both of them originate from the bulk diffusion. This GB diffusion is slower than the bulk diffusion at 300 K but becomes much faster at higher temperatures. The relevant Ea and D0 results are given in Figure 4c. The D0 along the x axis is remarkably larger than the others, indicating the ion migration along the GB follows a different mechanism. The large Ea of the diffusion along the x axis leads to strong temperature dependence. It should be noted that the Ea of bulk diffusion along y and z axes are significantly smaller than those reported in bulk MHP studies,63 which can be attributed to the special GB structures. Moreover, the Cs–Br and Pb–Br coordination numbers of different Br atoms are plotted in Figure 4d. The coordination numbers were calculated from the RDF curves. The distance cutoffs of the Cs–Br and Pb–Br pairs were set to 6 and 4 Å, corresponding to their first coordination spheres. Each Br atom in pristine CsPbBr3 coordinates with two Pb atoms and is surrounded by four Cs atoms. The non-migratable Br atoms maintain this configuration regardless of the temperature increase, while the migratable Br atoms tend to coordinate with more Pb atoms and fewer Cs atoms. Indeed, the diffusion of Br atoms is intermediated by successively interacting with different Pb atoms. The soft and defective GB structures are expected to stabilize the intermediates through structural distortions, leading to relatively low Ea for Br migration. Furthermore, considering higher temperatures simultaneously produce more migratable Br atoms and enhance their mobility, the ion migration at MHP GBs can be significantly enhanced with the presence of external heat, resulting in harmful thermal stability issues. More temperature-dependent results can be found in Figures S3–S6 of the Supporting Information.

Ion migration in MHPs has been identified as the origin of many detrimental issues, such as instability under working conditions and current–voltage hysteresis, but the corresponding mechanism, including migration species and migration channels, is not fully understood. Our simulation results demonstrate that Br atoms are the only migratable species at the CsPbBr3 (120) GB. Migration of organic cations, such as MA and FA, has been experimentally observed in hybrid organic–inorganic MHPs.33 The low mobility of the corresponding Cs cation in the current system can be attributed to its rigid structure lacking internal degrees of freedom, compared to organic molecules. The computed RDFs indicate that the GBs largely maintain the perovskite structure, and as a result, the Cs atoms remain confined within the perovskite lattice. Indeed, doping MHPs with trace amounts of Cs cations is reported to suppress ion migration and thus enhance the structural stability.64−66 On the other hand, the migration of Br atoms is associated with the non-stoichiometric GB structures involving more Pb atoms and fewer Cs atoms, implying that the lack of Cs atoms facilitates Br migration. The Pb atoms remain in their equilibrium positions but mediate Br migration. Therefore, we anticipate that other MHP GBs possessing similar ion distributions are also responsible for halide ion migration. Moreover, making the local GB composition more stoichiometric can be regarded as a theoretical principle for suppressing the ion migration at MHP GBs.

In conclusion, we have established the atomistic mechanism of ion migration at the CsPbBr3 ∑5 (120) GB by directly modeling its structural evolution over 10 ns using at 6400 atom model. The as-built GB is in a metastable state and undergoes significant structural reconstruction at a finite temperature. Two grains slide in opposite directions within hundreds of picoseconds, and take a few nanoseconds to relax. After that, Cs and Pb atoms vibrate around their equilibrium positions, while a fraction of Br atoms continue to diffuse. The migratable Br atoms are located near the GB but distribute over a relatively wide region. The migratable Br atoms are spatially mixed with the non-migratable atoms. We have distinguished the two types of Br atoms according to their maximum displacements during the equilibrium diffusion. For this purpose, we have developed an approach based on the variation of the MSD slope for Br atoms selected with different cutoffs. The spatial distribution of the migratable Br atoms during the diffusion illustrates the migration channels. The bulk channels transport Br atoms to the GB center, and the GB channels allow Br atoms to migrate along the GB, enabling three-dimensional diffusion. Increasing the temperature not only enhances the mobility of migratable Br atoms, but also activates more Br atoms in the GB region, making them migratable. This untrivial dependence amplifies the influence of high temperatures on the ion migration. The diffusion behavior follows the Arrhenius equation, and the fitted activation energies are much lower than those reported in bulk MHP simulations. The difference can be attributed to the large-scale structural distortions of GBs as well as the special local environment of migratable Br atoms containing more Pb atoms and fewer Cs atoms. Making the local GB composition more stoichiometric, e.g., by annealing or doping, can be regarded as a theoretical principle for suppressing the ion migration. The established ion migration mechanism generates valuable insights into the role of GBs in MHPs and suggests theoretical guidelines for reducing the detrimental effects of GBs on MHP performance.
