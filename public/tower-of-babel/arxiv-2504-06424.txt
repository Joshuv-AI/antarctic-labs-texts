The Density Finite Sums Theorem
By Bryna Kra and Joel Moreira and Florian K. Richter

Abstract
For any set A A of natural numbers with positive upper Banach density and any k ⩾ 1 k\geqslant 1 , we show the existence of an infinite set B ⊂ ℕ B\subset\mathbb{N} and a shift t ⩾ 0 t\geqslant 0 such that A − t A-t contains all sums of m m distinct elements from B B for all m ∈ { 1 , … , k } m\in\{1,\ldots,k\} . This can be viewed as a density analog of Hindman’s finite sums theorem. Our proof reveals the natural relationships among infinite sumsets, the dynamics underpinning arithmetic progressions, and homogeneous spaces of nilpotent Lie groups.

1.  Introduction

1.1.  Infinite sumsets

In 1927, van der Waerden
[
32
]
proved a classic result in Ramsey theory: no matter how one partitions the natural numbers

\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
into finitely many pieces, some piece contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.
Settling a long standing conjecture of Erdős and Turán, Szemerédi
[
30
]
proved in 1975 a density version of van der Waerden’s theorem.
Namely, he showed that any set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
whose

upper Banach density
, meaning

\mathsf{d}^{*}(A)\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup\left\{\dfrac{|A\cap\{M+1,\dots,M+N\}|}{N}:M\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

is positive contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

Around the same time, in a major breakthrough, Hindman
[
13
]
settled a conjecture of Graham and Rothschild showing that no matter how one partitions the natural numbers into finitely many pieces, some piece always contains an IP-set (also called a finite sums set)

\displaystyle\big\{b(i_{1})+\dots+b(i_{m}):m\in\mathbb{N},i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\in\mathbb{N}\big\}

(1.1)

for some strictly increasing sequence
b\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
.
Erdős
[
5
]
then tried to find the underlying behavior connecting these two results, writing “I have tried to formulate a conjecture which would be in the same relation to Hindman’s theorem as Szemerédi’s theorem is to van der Waerden’s.”
We believe that our main theorem comes as close as is possible to giving a density version.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{N}
has positive upper Banach density then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a strictly increasing sequence
b\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and an integer
t\geq 0
such that

\big\{b(i_{1})+\dots+b(i_{m}):1\leq m\leq k,\penalty\ i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\in\mathbb{N}\big\}

(1.2)

is a subset of
A-t
.

The shift is in general necessary: for example, the odd numbers do not contain a configuration of the form (
1.2
) when
k\geq 2
.
Furthermore, an example of Straus (published by others, see for example
[
14
]
) exhibits a set with density arbitrarily close to 1 that contains no shift of an IP-set, explaining the need to curtail
m
.

The
k=2
case of
Theorem

1.1
was proved in our previous work
[
24
]
.
The methods used there do not generalize to the setting of
Theorem

1.1
, which was conjectured in
[
24
, Conjecture 1.5]
and
[
25
, Conjecture 2.4]
.
Apart from the initial translation of
Theorem

1.1
into a dynamical statement (
Theorem

1.2
), our proof of
Theorem

1.1
is new even in the case
k=2
and gives, in particular, a simpler proof of the main result in
[
24
]
.

Theorem

1.1
follows a long history of results on infinite sumset configurations in large subsets of the integers, initiated by various conjectures of Erdős from the late 1970s and early 1980s (see
[
8
, p. 85]
,
[
5
, p. 305]
,
[
6
, pp. 57–58]
, and
[
7
, p. 105]
).
Hindman
[
14
,
15
]
gave various examples and provided reformulations and refinements of some of these conjectures.
Early results include the existence
[
29
]
of sumsets
B+C
with
B\subset\mathbb{N}
infinite and
C\subset\mathbb{N}
finite but arbitrarily large, and the existence
[
1
]
of restricted sumset configurations
\{b+c:b\in B,\penalty\ c\in C,\penalty\ b<c\}
where
B
and
C
are infinite.
In
[
4
]
, it was proved that any set of density strictly greater than
1/2
contains
B+C
with both
B
and
C
infinite.
In
[
28
]
, the weakest of Erdős’s conjectures was resolved.
A more streamlined proof
[
17
]
simplified many of the technical arguments in
[
28
]
.
The generalization to
k
-fold sumsets
B_{1}+\cdots+B_{k}
with
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
infinite was proved by the authors in
[
23
]
.
Recent related results include work on analogs of Erdős’s conjectures in more general groups
[
3
]
, work on unrestricted sumsets
[
22
,
21
]
, and work on sumsets in the primes
[
31
]
.
We refer to our survey
[
25
]
for further references and variations.

Our proof of
Theorem

1.1
relies on ergodic theory: the first step is to reduce the combinatorial problem to a dynamical statement, following Furstenberg’s framework for recasting combinatorial questions in terms of properties of orbits in a measure preserving system.
Our main dynamical theorem, from which we derive
Theorem

1.1
in
Section

2
, states the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
T
be a homeomorphism of a compact metric space
X
and let
\mu
be a
T
-invariant Borel probability measure on
X
.
For every point
a\in X
with

\mu(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}})=1,

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for every open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
, there exist an integer
t\geq 0
, points
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
, and a strictly increasing sequence

c\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{c(n)}a=x_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{c(n)}x_{1}=x_{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\ldots,\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{c(n)}x_{k-1}=x_{k},

(1.3)

all hold, and
x_{i}\in T^{-t}E
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

The proof that
Theorem

1.2
implies
Theorem

1.1
is contained in
Section

2
and uses by now well-understood techniques. The proof of
Theorem

1.2
then occupies the rest of the paper.

1.2.  Outline of the proof of
Theorem

1.2

We describe the strategy used to prove
Theorem

1.2
, referring as needed to terminology and notation introduced later in the paper.

We start by introducing a new class of measures on
X^{k+1}
defined by a recurrence property which we call
progressive
(
Definition

3.1
). We show that if a measure on
X^{k+1}
is progressive, then any open set
U\subset X^{k+1}
with positive measure contains a point
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
satisfying (
1.3
) – we call such points
Erdős progressions
(
Definition

2.1
). Thus every progressive measure is supported on the closure of the set of Erdős progressions.

Our goal, then, is to associate to each system
(X,\mu,T)
a measure
\sigma
on
X^{k+1}
that is progressive.
In the extreme case when the system is a rotation on a compact abelian group, there is a concise algebraic description of the set of Erdős progressions, which allows for a simple and explicit construction of a progressive measure (see
Example

4.1
).
More generally, if
X
is a nilmanifold and
(X,\mu,T)
is a nilsystem (see
Definition

4.3
), one can also give an explicit description of a progressive measure as the Haar measure on a suitable sub-nilmanifold.
In the opposite case of weakly mixing systems, we can easily show that the product measure is progressive due to the randomness inherent in such a system (see
Example

4.2
).

Motivated by the insights of these two contrasting examples, we construct in
Section

4
for any system
(X,T)
a measure
\sigma
on
X^{k+1}
(see
Definition

4.5
)
consisting of a structured component arising from the maximal pronilfactor of
(X,T)
and a random component that is relatively independent over the maximal pronilfactor.
The proof of our main result then reduces to two tasks:

(i)

Showing that for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\sigma(\{a\}\times T^{-t}E\times\cdots\times T^{-t}E)>0
;

(ii)

Proving that
\sigma
is a progressive measure.

Since (i) and (ii) together imply that
\{a\}\times T^{-t}E\times\cdots\times T^{-t}E
contains an Erdős progression, namely, a point
(a,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
satisfying (
1.3
), the conclusion of
Theorem

1.2
follows.

The proof of (i) is carried out in
Section

5
and uses Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem, that the maximal pronilfactor is the characteristic factor for multiple ergodic averages, that products of pronilsystems are pronilsystems, and that orbit closures in pronilsystems are uniquely ergodic.
Our proof of (ii), which occupies
Section

6
, includes a novel extension (
Theorem

6.3
) of Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem, several advanced properties of uniformity norms (established in
Section

6.3
), and again basic facts about pronilsystems such as being closed under products and that every orbit closure in a pronilsystem is uniquely ergodic.

2.  Translation to measure preserving systems

In this section, we show that
Theorem

1.1
follows from
Theorem

1.2
.

By a
topological system
we mean a pair
(X,T)
where
X
is a compact metric space and
T\colon X\to X
is a homeomorphism.
Given a compact metric space
Y
, let
\mathcal{M}(Y)
denote the set of all Borel probability measures on
Y
.
By a
measure preserving system
we mean a tuple
(X,\mu,T)
where
(X,T)
is a topological system and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is invariant under
T
.
All spaces are implicitly endowed with their Borel
\sigma
-algebras and so we do not include the
\sigma
-algebras in our notation.
A measure preserving system is said to be
ergodic
if any
T
-invariant Borel subset of
X
has either measure
0
or measure
1
.

Building on Furstenberg’s dynamical approach to combinatorial problems
[
12
]
, in our earlier work
[
24
]
we established a connection between sumsets in
\mathbb{N}
of the form (
1.2
) for
k=2

and “Erdős progressions” in topological systems.
We extend that definition to handle sumsets of the form (
1.2
).

Definition 2.1

(cf.
[
24
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

Given a topological system
(X,T)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a point

(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k})\in X^{k+1}

is a
(k+1)
-term Erdős progression
if there exists a strictly increasing sequence
c:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that

T^{c(n)}x_{0}\to x_{1},\hskip 20.00003ptT^{c(n)}x_{1}\to x_{2},\hskip 20.00003pt\dots\hskip 20.00003ptT^{c(n)}x_{k-1}\to x_{k}

(2.1)

as
n\to\infty
.

The next lemma demonstrates the connection between
(k+1)
-term Erdős progressions and sumsets.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a topological system
(X,T)
, and open sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\subset X
.
If there exists an Erdős progression
(x_{0},\dots,x_{k})\in X^{k+1}
with
x_{j}\in U_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
, then there exists a strictly increasing sequence
b\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that

\big\{b(i_{1})+\dots+b(i_{m}):i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\subset\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}x_{0}\in U_{m}\}

for
m=1,\dots,k
.

Proof.

The special case
k=2
was established in
[
24
, Theorem 2.2]
, and the proof of the general case is essentially the same, with only slightly more complicated notation.

Let
c\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a strictly increasing sequence such that (
2.1
) holds as
n\to\infty
.
Set
U_{j,0}=U_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
.

Each
U_{j,0}
is a neighborhood of
x_{j}
, and so there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
b(1):=c(n_{1})
satisfies
T^{b(1)}x_{j-1}\in U_{j,0}
for
j=1,\dots,k
.
Set
U_{j,1}:=U_{j,0}\cap T^{-b(1)}U_{j+1,0}
for each
j=1,\dots,k-1
and set
U_{k,1}:=U_{k}
.

Note that
U_{j,1}
is an open set that contains
x_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
and so there exists
n_{2}>n_{1}
such that
b(2):=c(n_{2})
satisfies
T^{b(2)}x_{j-1}\in U_{j,1}
for
j=1,\dots,k
.
We then set
U_{j,2}:=U_{j,1}\cap T^{-b(2)}U_{j+1,1}
for each
j=1,\dots,k-1
and set
U_{k,2}:=U_{k}
.

We continue inductively, noting that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
j=1,\dots,k
the open set
U_{j,i}
contains
x_{j}
, so that we can
find some
n_{i}>n_{i-1}

such that
b(i):=c(n_{i})
satisfies
T^{b(i)}x_{j-1}\in U_{j,i-1}
for
j=1,\dots,k
.
We then let
U_{j,i}:=U_{j,i-1}\cap T^{-b(i)}U_{j+1,i-1}
for each
j=1,\dots,k-1
and set
U_{k,i}:=U_{k}
.

Let
m\leqslant k
and fix
i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}
in
\mathbb{N}
.
We need to verify that
T^{b(i_{1})+\dots+b(i_{m})}x_{0}\in U_{m}
.
We have

T^{b(i_{m})}x_{0}\in U_{1,i_{m}-1}\subset U_{1,i_{m-1}}

because
i_{m}-1\geq i_{m-1}
.
Noting that, from the construction,
U_{1,i_{m-1}}\subset T^{-b(i_{m-1})}U_{2,i_{m-1}-1}
, and since
i_{m-1}-1\geq i_{m-2}
, we have

T^{b(i_{m-1})+b(i_{m})}x_{0}\in T^{b(i_{m-1})}U_{1,i_{m-1}}\subset U_{2,i_{m-1}-1}\subset U_{2,i_{m-2}}.

Proceeding in this manner, we deduce that

T^{b(i_{m-h})+\dots+b(i_{m-1})+b(i_{m})}x_{0}\in U_{h+1,i_{m-h-1}}

for
h=0,\dots,m-1
.
As
U_{m,0}=U_{m}
, the desired result is the case
h=m-1
.
∎

To connect
Lemma

2.2
to
Theorem

1.1
,
we use a version of Furstenberg’s correspondence principle.

Theorem 2.3

(Correspondence principle, cf.
[
11
,
12
]
)
.

Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with positive upper Banach density, there exists a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, a point
a\in X
with
\mu(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}})=1
,
and an open set
E\subset X
such that
A=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}a\in E\}
and
\mu(E)>0
.

Throughout we work with invertible measure preserving transformations, but for our combinatorial conclusions, we need the measure to be supported on the forward orbit closure of
a
.
This explains the slight differences in the formulation of the correspondence principle from those used to prove density regularity of finite configurations, such as arithmetic progressions.

Using
Theorem

2.3
and
Lemma

2.2
, we can quickly derive
Theorem

1.1
from
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof that
Theorem

1.2
implies
Theorem

1.1
.

Suppose
A\subset\mathbb{N}
has positive upper Banach density.
Invoking
Theorem

2.3
, we find a system
(X,\mu,T)
, a point
a\in X
, and an open set

E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
and such that
A=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}a\in E\}
and
\mu(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}})=1
.
Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem

1.2
gives an integer
t\geq 0
, points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X
with
T^{t}x_{i}\in E
for each
i=1,\dots,k
, and a strictly increasing sequence
c\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that (
1.3
) holds, which is equivalent to the assertion that
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
forms an Erdős progression.
Setting
U_{j}=T^{-t}E
for all
1\leq j\leq k
,
the desired conclusion then follows from
Lemma

2.2
.
∎

We are left with establishing
Theorem

1.2
.
We end this section with a standard argument reducing
Theorem

1.2
to the statement in
Theorem

2.4
.
We start by recalling some definitions.
A
Følner sequence
in
\mathbb{N}
is a sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|(\Phi_{N}+1)\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}=1.

Note that, throughout, although we work with homeomorphisms we only use Følner sequences in
\mathbb{N}
. We restrict to such Følner sequences because we must be able to conclude that the sequence
(b(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that we construct is a sequence in
\mathbb{N}
and not just in
\mathbb{Z}
.
This necessitates our assumption in Theorems
1.2
and
2.3
that the forward orbit closure of the point
a
have full measure.
Recall that whenever
A\subset\mathbb{N}
has positive upper Banach density, there is a Følner sequence

\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}

exists and is positive.

Given a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, a point
a\in X
is
generic
for
\mu
along a Følner sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, written
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, if

\mu=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{T^{n}a},

where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure at the point
x\in X
and the limit is taken in the weak* topology.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, every Følner sequence
\Phi
, every
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and every open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
, there exists an integer
t\geq 0
and an Erdős progression of the form
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
x_{i}\in T^{-t}E
for
i=1,\dots,k
.

Proof that
Theorem

2.4
implies
Theorem

1.2
.

Suppose
X,T,a,\mu,E
are as in
Theorem

1.2
.
Using ergodic decomposition of the measure
\mu
, we can find a
T
-invariant ergodic measure
\tilde{\mu}
such that
\tilde{\mu}(E)\geqslant\mu(E)
and
\tilde{\mu}(\overline{\{T^{n}a:n\in\mathbb{N}\}})=1
.
It follows from
[
12
, Proposition 3.9]
that there exists a Følner sequence
\Phi
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\tilde{\mu},\Phi)
.

By
Theorem

2.4
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist an Erdős progression
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and an integer
t\geq 0
such that
x_{i}\in T^{-t}E
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Therefore, there exists a strictly increasing sequence
c\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that (
1.3
) holds.
∎

The proof of
Theorem

2.4
occupies the rest of the paper.

3.  A variation on recurrence in
X^{k+1}

Starting with this section, we depart significantly from earlier approaches
[
28
,
17
,
23
,
24
]
to similar problems.

Fix a topological system
(X,T)
.
If
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is an Erdős progression and
U_{j}
is a neighborhood of
x_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq k
, then there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a\in T^{-n}U_{1}
and
x_{j}\in U_{j}\cap T^{-n}U_{j+1}
for
1\leq j\leq k-1
. In
X^{k+1}
, we can write this as

(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k})\in(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}\times U_{k})\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-n}(U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\times X)

where

T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}=\underbrace{T\times T\times\ldots\times T}_{k+1\penalty\ \text{times}}

denotes the product transformation.
This leads us to consider when the intersection

(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}\times U_{k})\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-n}(U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\times X)

is nonempty, motivating our next definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological system and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that a probability measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
is
progressive
if for all open sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\subset X
with

\tau(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})>0

there exist infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\tau\big((X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k-1}\times U_{k})\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-n}(U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\times X)\big)>0.

(3.1)

The notion of a measure being
progressive
depends on the value of
k
but we omit this from our terminology.

Though we view this as a form of recurrence, it differs from standard notions in several ways.
For example, the measure
\tau
is not assumed to be
T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}
invariant, and (
3.1
) is not of the form
\mu(B\cap S^{-n}B
).
While usually there is no distinction between requiring a single return to the set and infinitely many returns, in our setting of distinct sets
U_{i}
, one can construct choices of the sets showing that the two notions are different.

The next proposition shows that a progressive probability measure produces Erdős progressions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(X,T)
be a topological system. Let
a\in X
, let
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\subset X
be open sets, and let
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
be a progressive measure satisfying
\tau(\{a\}\times X^{k})=1
.
If
\tau(X\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})>0
, then there is an Erdős progression
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
x_{j}\in U_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
.

Proof.

Let
V=U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k}
.
Using the assumption that
\tau
is progressive, we can find
c(1)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\tau\big((X\times V)\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-c(1)}(V\times X)\big)>0.

Since
V
is open, it follows that the intersection above contains some point
(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
in the support of
\tau
.
From the assumption that the closed set
\{a\}\times X^{k}
has full measure, it follows that
v_{0}=a
.
Letting
V_{1}\subset X^{k}
be an open ball centered at
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
with a sufficiently small radius, we have all the following properties.

•

\tau(\{a\}\times V_{1})>0

•

\operatorname{diam}(V_{1})\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diam}(V)

•

\{a\}\times\overline{V_{1}}\subset(\{a\}\times V)\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-c(1)}(V\times X)

We proceed inductively, setting
V_{0}=V
and constructing, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an open set
V_{n}\subset X^{k}
and a recurrence time
c(n)\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

\displaystyle\overline{V_{n}}\subset V_{n-1}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pt\operatorname{diam}(V_{n})\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diam}(V_{n-1})
;

(ii)

\tau(\{a\}\times V_{n})>0
;

(iii)

\{a\}\times\overline{V_{n}}\subset T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-c(n)}(V_{n-1}\times X)
;

(iv)

c(n+1)>c(n)
.

The case
n=1
is already established.
The inductive step is the same: using
(ii)
and the hypotheses on
\tau
we can find
c(n+1)>c(n)
for which

(\{a\}\times V_{n})\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-c(n+1)}(V_{n}\times X)

has positive
\tau
measure.
Since
V_{n}
is open, it follows that there exists a point in this intersection that belongs to the support of
\tau
.
By intersecting a small ball around that point with
\{a\}\times X^{k}
, we can find an open set
V_{n+1}\subset X^{k}
satisfying properties
(i)
–
(iii)
.

We now produce the desired Erdős progression.
Using property
(i)
, the intersection

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\{a\}\times\overline{V_{n}}

is a singleton. Calling this point
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, then property
(iii)
gives that

T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{c(n)}(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})\in V_{n-1}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It thus follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{c(n)}(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}),

as desired.
∎

We remark that the Erdős progression found in the proof of
Proposition

3.2
is in the support of
\tau
. Therefore it follows that the support of
\tau
contains a dense set of Erdős progressions.
In our earlier work
[
24
]
we constructed a measure which was not only progressive, but had the property that almost every
x\in X^{3}
is an Erdős progression.
Due to the reliance on
Proposition

3.2
in the current work, we do not know if the measure we produce has this stronger property.
It would be interesting to know whether the conclusion of
Proposition

3.2
can be strengthened to match earlier work.

Using
Proposition

3.2
, we can now derive
Theorem

2.4
from the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, every Følner sequence
\Phi
, every
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and every open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
, there exists an integer
t\geq 0
and a progressive measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
such that

\sigma(X\times T^{-t}E\times\cdots\times T^{-t}E)>0

(3.2)

and
\sigma(\{a\}\times X^{k})=1
.

Proof that
Theorem

3.3
implies
Theorem

2.4
.

We apply
Proposition

3.2
with
U_{j}=T^{-t}E
for all
1\leq j\leq k
and
\tau=\sigma
.
The hypotheses of
Theorem

3.3
allow us to conclude from
Proposition

3.2
that there is an Erdős progression
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
x_{j}\in U_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
.
∎

4.  Constructing a progressive measure

4.1.  Two motivating examples

To motivate our approach to constructing a progressive measure, we consider two special cases that represent complementary behaviors: irrational rotations and weakly mixing systems.

Example 4.1
.

Let
X=\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be the unit circle and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be irrational. The map
T\colon X\to X
given by
Tx=x+\alpha\mod 1
is ergodic with respect to the Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}
.
In this case,
(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is an Erdős progression if and only it is an arithmetic progression in
\mathbb{T}
, meaning that

(a,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(a,a+\beta,\dots,a+k\beta)

for some
\beta\in\mathbb{T}
.
In particular, the set of all Erdős progressions starting at
a
is a subtorus of
\mathbb{T}^{k+1}
.
Writing
\sigma
for the Haar measure on this subtorus, we can describe
\sigma
dynamically via the formula

\sigma=\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{a}\times\delta_{T^{n}a}\times\dots\times\delta_{T^{kn}a}

with the limit taken in the weak* topology.
Open sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\subset\mathbb{T}
satisfy

\sigma(\mathbb{T}\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})>0

precisely when there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{T}
with
a+i\beta\in U_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
In this case, choosing an integer
n
such that
n\alpha\approx\beta
, we have that
a+i\beta\in U_{i}\cap T^{-n}U_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\dots,k-1
.
This in turn implies that

\sigma\Big(\big(\mathbb{T}\times U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\big)\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-n}\big(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k}\times\mathbb{T}\big)\Big)>0

showing that
\sigma
is progressive.

In view of this example, a natural candidate for
\sigma
in the general setting is

\sigma=\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{a}\times\delta_{T^{n}a}\times\cdots\times\delta_{T^{kn}a}

(4.1)

where
\Phi
is a Følner sequence along which the limit exists.
where one would pass to a subsequence of
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
if necessary to guarantee the existence of the limit.
Unfortunately, even in the complementary case of weakly mixing systems, we are unable to prove that the measure defined by (
4.1
) is progressive.
It would be interesting to know whether it always is.
This makes it necessary for us to take a different approach for weakly mixing systems, illustrated by the following example.

Example 4.2
.

If
(X,\mu,T)
is weakly mixing, then we can take
\sigma
to be the product measure
\sigma=\delta_{a}\times\mu\times\cdots\times\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
.
To verify that
\sigma
is progressive, fix
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\subset X
with
\mu(U_{i})>0
for each
i
.
Weak mixing implies each of the sets
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mu(U_{i}\cap T^{-n}U_{i+1})>0\}
has full density with respect to every Følner sequence.
Setting
U_{0}=U_{k+1}:=X
for convenience, we conclude that

\begin{split}\big\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sigma\bigl((U_{0}\times\cdots\times U_{k})\cap T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{-n}(U_{1}\times\cdots&\times U_{k+1})\bigr)>0\big\}\\
&\supset\bigcap_{i=0}^{k}\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mu(U_{i}\cap T^{-n}U_{i+1})>0\}\end{split}

has full density with respect to every Følner sequence.
This shows that
\sigma
is progressive and hence satisfies the conclusion of

Theorem

3.3
.
In fact, one can prove that for
\mu\times\cdots\times\mu
-almost every
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in X^{k}
the point
(a,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is an Erdős progression;
when
\mu
is weakly mixing with respect to
T
, it follows that
\mu\times\cdots\times\mu
is ergodic with respect to
T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}
and hence almost every point is both generic for and in the support of this measure.

To define
\sigma
in general, we construct a measure that combines structured behavior, like that of
Example

4.1
, with mixing behavior, like that of
Example

4.2
. To make this precise, we use the notion of pronilfactors.

4.2.  Pronilfactors

Let
(X,\mu,T)
and
(Y,\nu,S)
be measure preserving systems. We say that
(Y,\nu,S)
is a
factor
of
(X,\mu,T)
if there exists an almost surely defined and measurable map
\pi\colon X\to Y
called the
factor map
such that
\pi\circ T=S\circ\pi
holds
\mu
-almost everywhere and
\pi\mu=\nu
.
Of particular importance for us are the factors which are nilsystems.

Definition 4.3

(Nilsystems, pronilsystems)
.

Let
G
be an
s
-step nilpotent Lie group and let
\Gamma\subset G
be a discrete and cocompact subgroup.
The compact manifold
X=G/\Gamma
is an
s
-step nilmanifold
.

The group
G
acts on
X
by left translation, and there is a unique Borel probability measure
\mu
on
X
that is invariant under this action, the
Haar measure
on
X
.
Letting
T\colon X\to X
denote left translation by a fixed element of
G
, the resulting measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
is called an
s
-step nilsystem
(as usual endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra).

An inverse limit of
s
-step nilsystems is called an
s
-step pronilsystem.

We note that we can view the inverse limit in the category of measure preserving systems or in the category of topological dynamical systems, as both describe the same system.

A topological system
(X,T)
is
uniquely ergodic
if there is a unique
T
-invariant measure. A pronilsystem is uniquely ergodic if and only if every point is generic for
\mu
along every Følner sequence.
It is a classical result that for all
s
-step pronilsystems
(X,\mu,T)
the properties (i) minimal; (ii) transitive; (iii) ergodic; (iv) uniquely ergodic; are equivalent.
(See
[
20
, Theorem 11, Chapter 11]
for a summary and discussion.)
Moreover, every orbit closure in a pronilsystem supports a unique invariant measure (see
[
27
,
26
]
).

For each
s\in\mathbb{N}
, every ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
has a maximal factor that is isomorphic to an
s
-step pronilsystem (see
[
20
, Chapter 16]
); this system is called the
s
-step pronilfactor
of
(X,\mu,T)
and is denoted
(Z_{s},m_{s},T)
.
By a standard abuse of notation we use
T
to denote the transformation not only in the system but also in the pronilfactor.

4.3.  Constructing the measure
\sigma

We are now ready to construct the probability measure
\sigma
on
X^{k+1}
that we later show to be progressive.
This is carried out in two steps: first we define a progressive measure on
Z^{k+1}_{k-1}
where
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
is the
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of the given system
(X,\mu,T)
, and then we lift this to a measure on
X^{k+1}
.

For the first step, when
(X,\mu,T)
is a pronilsystem, the measure
\sigma
described in (
4.1
) is progressive; this follows from
Theorem

4.9
below but a direct proof is more involved than the special case in
Example

4.1
of a rotation.

For the second step, we need to overcome the obstacle that the definition of
\sigma
in (
4.1
) depends on the single point
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, but the map
\pi_{s}\colon X\to Z_{s}
to the
s
-step pronilfactor is, a priori, only a measurable map and only defined almost everywhere.
To remedy this issue, we restrict attention to systems where the map
\pi
is continuous.

Definition 4.4
.

We say that
(X,\mu,T)

has topological pronilfactors
if for every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a continuous factor map
\pi_{s}\colon X\to Z_{s}
, where
(Z_{s},m_{s},T)
denotes the
s
-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
.

Fortunately, it is possible to reduce
Theorem

3.3
to the case when
(X,\mu,T)
has topological pronilfactors.
This maneuver has been used in related situations, such as
[
19
]
, and was first adapted to this setting by Host
[
17
]
.
See
[
23
, Section 5]
for a more complete discussion. The reduction of
Theorem

3.3
to the case of topological pronilfactors is proven in the next section

If
(Y,\nu,S)
is a factor of
(X,\mu,T)
with factor map
\pi\colon X\to Y
, then the
conditional expectation
of a function of
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
onto
Y
, denoted
\mathbb{E}(f\mid Y)
, is the unique function in
L^{2}(\nu)
such that

\int_{\pi^{-1}(B)}f\,\mathsf{d}\mu=\int_{B}\mathbb{E}(f\mid Y)\,\mathsf{d}\nu

for all Borel measurable sets
B\subset Y
.

Using this, we can define the measure
\sigma
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic system with topological pronilfactors and fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
denote the
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
and let
\pi_{k-1}\colon X\to Z_{k-1}
denote the corresponding continuous factor map.
Given a Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
define the measure
\xi\in\mathcal{M}(Z_{k-1}^{k})
by setting

\xi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{T^{n}(\pi_{k-1}a)}\otimes\delta_{T^{2n}(\pi_{k-1}a)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\delta_{T^{kn}(\pi_{k-1}a)}.

(4.2)

We then define the measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
via the formula

\int_{X^{k+1}}f_{0}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\sigma=f_{0}(a)\int_{Z_{k-1}^{k}}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\,\mathsf{d}\xi

(4.3)

for all continuous functions
f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
.

The limit in (
4.2
) exists and is in fact independent of
\Phi
, since
(Z_{k-1}^{k},T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k})
is a topological pronilsystem and hence every point has a uniquely ergodic orbit closure.
It follows from this that

\xi
is the unique measure on
\mathcal{M}(Z_{k-1}^{k})
such that
(\pi_{k-1}(a),\dots,\pi_{k-1}(a))
is a generic point (along
\Phi
) with respect to the transformation
T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}

We stress that the measures
\xi
and
\sigma
depend on the choice of the point
a
, as well as the system
(X,\mu,T)
and the positive integer
k
.
For ease of reading, we omit these dependencies from the notation.
Note also that while
\xi
is defined on a
k
-fold product space indexed by
\{1,\dots,k\}
, the measure
\sigma
is defined on a
(k+1)
-fold product space indexed by
\{0,1,\dots,k\}
and distinguishes the zeroth coordinate.

For a measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k})
and each
i=1,\dots,k
, we let
\tau_{i}
denote the
i
-th marginal
of
\tau
, meaning the projection of
\tau
onto the
i
-th coordinate.
Given two measures
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
defined on the same space, we say that
\tau\leqslant\tau^{\prime}
if
\tau(A)\leqslant\tau^{\prime}(A)
for all measurable sets
A
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Fix an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
with topological pronilfactors, an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, and a point
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
For every
1\leq i\leq k
, the
i
-th marginal
\sigma_{i}
of the measure
\sigma
in
Definition

4.5
satisfies
\sigma_{i}\leq i\mu
.

Proof.

Note that
\xi_{i}
, the
i
-th marginal of the measure
\xi
from (
4.2
), is
T^{i}
-invariant. Since the system
(Z_{k-1},T)
is uniquely ergodic, we have that
\xi_{i}\leqslant im_{Z_{k-1}}
and hence

\int_{Z_{k-1}}g\,\mathsf{d}\xi_{i}\leqslant i\int_{Z_{k-1}}g\,\mathsf{d}m_{Z_{k-1}}

(4.4)

whenever
g\colon Z_{k-1}\to[0,1]
is Borel measurable.
Therefore, for any Borel set
A\subset X
, we have

\sigma_{i}(A)=\int_{Z_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\mid Z_{k-1})\,\mathsf{d}\xi_{i}\leqslant i\int_{Z_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\mid Z_{k-1})\,\mathsf{d}m_{Z_{k-1}}=i\mu(A).\hskip 10.00002pt\qed

We also record the following which follows directly from the definition.

Lemma 4.7
.

The measure
\sigma
defined in
Definition

4.5
is invariant under
{\rm{Id}}\times T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}
.

4.4.  Outlining the proof of
Theorem

3.3

To prove
Theorem

3.3
, we need to verify that
\sigma
has the required properties, which are captured by the following two theorems.

Theorem 4.8
.

Fix an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
with topological pronilfactors.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Følner sequence, let
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and let
\sigma
be the measure described in
Definition

4.5
.
Then for every open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
, there is an integer
t\geq 0
such that
\sigma(X\times T^{-t}E\times\cdots\times T^{-t}E)>0
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Fix an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
with topological pronilfactors.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Følner sequence, and let
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
Then the measure
\sigma
described in
Definition

4.5
is progressive.

These ingredients, together with the ability to pass to an extension with topological pronilfactors, combine to give a quick proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Proof that Theorems
4.8
and
4.9
together imply
Theorem

3.3
.

Fix an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
, a Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, a point
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and an open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
.
By
[
23
, Proposition 5.7 and Lemma 5.8]
, there exist an ergodic system
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\mu},\tilde{T})
with topological pronilfactors and a continuous factor map
\pi\colon\tilde{X}\to X
such that

(X,\mu,T)
is a factor of
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\mu},\tilde{T})
.
Moreover, there is a Følner sequence
(\tilde{\Phi}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\tilde{a}\in\mathsf{gen}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\Phi})
such that
\pi(\tilde{a})=a
.
Let
\tilde{\sigma}
denote the measure on
(\tilde{X})^{k+1}
defined by (
4.3
) and let
\tilde{E}:=\pi^{-1}(E)
.
Applying Theorems
4.8
and
4.9
to
\tilde{\sigma}
, it follows that
\tilde{\sigma}

is a progressive measure, that
\tilde{\sigma}(\{\tilde{a}\}\times X^{k})=1
, and that there exists an integer
t\geq 0
satisfying
\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{X}\times\tilde{T}^{-t}\tilde{E}\times\cdots\times\tilde{T}^{-t}\tilde{E})>0
.

Letting
\sigma
to be the push-forward of
\tilde{\sigma}
under the map
\pi\times\pi\times\dots\times\pi
, it follows that
\sigma
satisfies the conclusion of
Theorem

3.3
.
∎

5.  Proof of
Theorem

4.8

We are left with proving Theorems
4.8
and
4.9
.
The proof of the former is given in this section, whereas the proof of the latter is in
Section

6
.
In both proofs we relate certain dynamical averages of
\sigma
to the Furstenberg joining of
(X,\mu,T)
.

Definition 5.1

(The Furstenberg joining)
.

Given a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\lambda
be the Borel probability measure on
X^{k+1}
uniquely determined by

\displaystyle\int_{X^{k+1}}f_{0}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\lambda=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\int_{X}f_{0}\cdot T^{n}f_{1}\cdots T^{kn}f_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\mu

(5.1)

for all
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
.
The measure
\lambda
determined this way is called the
Furstenberg joining
of
(X,\mu,T)
.

This joining is well defined, as it is shown in
[
18
]
that the limit exists and is independent of the choice of Følner sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.

As we show,
Theorem

4.8
follows quite quickly from the following theorem and Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix an ergodic measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
with topological pronilfactors,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
Let
\sigma
be the measure described in
Definition

4.5
.
For any
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in C(X)
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\int_{X^{k+1}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes g_{k})\,\mathsf{d}\sigma=\int_{X^{k+1}}\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes g_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\lambda

(5.2)

where
\lambda
is the Furstenberg joining.

Proof that
Theorem

5.2
implies
Theorem

4.8
.

Since
\mu
is a Borel measure on the compact metric space
X
, it is regular. Therefore, given an open set
E\subset X
with
\mu(E)>0
, one can use Urysohn’s lemma to find
g\in C(X)
with
\int g\,\mathsf{d}\mu>0
and
0\leq g\leq\boldsymbol{1}_{E}
.
Thus

\int_{X^{k+1}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{E}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{E})\,\mathsf{d}\sigma\geq\int_{X^{k+1}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g\otimes\cdots\otimes g)\,\mathsf{d}\sigma

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Combining this observation with
Theorem

5.2
gives that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\int_{X^{k+1}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{E}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{E})\,\mathsf{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g\otimes\cdots\otimes g\,\mathsf{d}\lambda.

By the definition of
\lambda
, this last expression is equal to

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\int_{X}T^{n}g\cdots T^{kn}g\,\mathsf{d}\mu

and this is positive by Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem
[
11
, Theorem 11.13]
.
We have shown that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\sigma(X\times T^{-t}E\times\cdots\times T^{-t}E)>0,

which is a stronger statement than what is required in the conclusion of
Theorem

4.8
.
∎

It remains to prove
Theorem

5.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

Fix
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in C(X)
.
Our goal is to establish (
5.2
).
It follows from
[
18
, Theorem 12.1]
that

\int g_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes g_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\lambda=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{L}|}\sum_{\ell\in\Phi_{L}}\int_{Z_{k-1}}T^{\ell}\mathbb{E}(g_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})\cdots T^{k\ell}\mathbb{E}(g_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\,\mathsf{d}m_{k-1}

where
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
is the
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
.
Combined with (
4.3
) it follows that, in order to establish (
5.2
), it suffices to prove

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\int_{Z_{k-1}^{k}}T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\xi=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{L}|}\sum_{\ell\in\Phi_{L}}\int_{Z_{k-1}}T^{\ell}h_{1}\cdots T^{k\ell}h_{k}\,\mathsf{d}m_{k-1}

whenever
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}:Z_{k-1}\to\mathbb{C}
are bounded and measurable.
By a standard approximation argument and
Lemma

4.6
it suffices to establish this when the functions
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}
are continuous, which we henceforth assume.
Writing
\tilde{a}=\pi_{k-1}(a)
we have the following from the definition of
\xi
:

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&\int_{Z_{k-1}^{k}}T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\,\mathsf{d}\xi\\
&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{L}|}\sum_{\ell\in\Phi_{L}}h_{1}(T^{n+\ell}\tilde{a})\cdots h_{k}(T^{n+k\ell}\tilde{a}).\end{split}

(5.3)

On the other hand, ergodicity of
(X,\mu,T)
ensures ergodicity, and hence unique ergodicity, of the pronilfactor
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
, and so it follows that

\begin{split}\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}&\int_{Z_{k-1}}T^{\ell}h_{1}\cdots T^{k\ell}h_{k}\,\mathsf{d}m_{k-1}\\
&=\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{L}|}\sum_{\ell\in\Phi_{L}}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}(T^{\ell+n}\tilde{a})\cdots h_{k}(T^{k\ell+n}\tilde{a}).\end{split}

(5.4)

Consider now the commuting nilrotations

\displaystyle S=T\times T\times\cdots\times T

\displaystyle R=T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}

on
(Z_{k-1})^{k}
.
Together they induce an action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on
Z_{k-1}^{k}
.
Set

Y:=\overline{\{S^{n}R^{\ell}(\tilde{a},\dots,\tilde{a}):n,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\}}\subset Z_{k-1}^{k}

to be the orbit closure of
(\tilde{a},\dots,\tilde{a})\in\mathbb{Z}_{k-1}^{k}
under

and

. Since
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
is a pronilsystem, the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-system
(Y,S,R)
is also a pronilsystem and hence is uniquely ergodic
[
20
, Theorem 17, Chapter 11]
.
Therefore, there is a unique invariant mean for these averages, which implies (see for example
[
2
, Lemma 1.1]
) that the expressions in (
5.3
) and (

) coincide, finishing the proof.
∎

6.2.  An extension of Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem

We derive
Theorem

6.1
from the following more general result, which is an
extension of Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem that may be of independent interest.

Given a vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, we write
T_{v}=T^{v_{1}}\times\cdots\times T^{v_{k}}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological system, let
u,v\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and let
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Følner-sequence in
\mathbb{N}
.
If
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k})
is
T_{v}
-invariant and
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subset X
are such that
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{k}\subset X^{k}
satisfies
\nu(A)>0
, then

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\nu(A\cap T_{u}^{-n}A)>0.

(6.1)

When
u=v
, the conclusion of
Theorem

6.3
follows quickly from the mean ergodic theorem.

The next example shows that in general one cannot drop the assumption that
A
is a product set in
Theorem

6.3
.

Example 6.4
.

Let
T\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
be an irrational rotation
T\colon x\mapsto x+\alpha
and let
\nu
denote the Haar measure on the subtorus
H:=\{(x,2x):x\in\mathbb{T}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
With
v=(1,2)
and
u=(2,1)
we have that
\nu
is
T_{v}
-invariant, but
T_{u}^{-n}H=H+(0,3n\alpha)
is disjoint from
H
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular (
6.1
) does not hold with
A=H
.

Consider the case that
(X,\mu,T)
is a measure preserving system,
u=(1,\dots,k)
,
v=(1,\dots,1)
, and
\nu
is the diagonal measure on
X^{k}
, meaning that
\nu
is the push-forward of
\mu
under the diagonal embedding
x\mapsto(x,x,\ldots,x)
of
X
into
X^{k}
.
Taking
A=B\times\cdots\times B
in the theorem specializes (
6.1
) to

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\mu(B\cap T^{-n}B\cap\cdots\cap T^{-kn}B)>0

which is Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem. We do not, however, give a new proof of Furstenberg’s theorem, as it is an ingredient in our proof of
Theorem

6.3
.

When applying
Theorem

6.3
to prove
Theorem

6.1
, we take
u=(1,\dots,1)
,
v=(1,\dots,k)
; this interchanges the roles of
u
and
v
in Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence.

Proof of
Theorem

6.3
.

Define

c=\operatorname{LCM}(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
to be the least common multiple of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
and let
w_{i}=\frac{c\,u_{i}}{v_{i}}
, so that

T_{v}^{-w_{i}}(X^{i-1}\times A_{i}\times X^{k-i})=T_{u}^{-c}(X^{i-1}\times A_{i}\times X^{k-i}).

It follows that

\displaystyle T_{u}^{-cn}A

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}T_{u}^{-cn}(X^{i-1}\times A_{i}\times X^{k-i})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}T_{v}^{-w_{i}n}(X^{i-1}\times A_{i}\times X^{k-i})

\displaystyle\supset\bigcap_{i=i}^{k}T_{v}^{-w_{i}n}A.

It is clear that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\nu(A\cap T^{-n}_{u}A)\geqslant\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\Phi_{N}\\
c|n\end{subarray}}\nu(A\cap T^{-n}_{u}A)

and so taking
\Psi_{N}=\Phi_{N}/c=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:cn\in\Phi_{N}\}
,
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\nu(A\cap T^{-n}_{u}A)

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}\nu(A\cap T^{-cn}_{u}A)

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}\nu\left(A\cap\bigcap_{i=i}^{k}T_{v}^{-w_{i}n}A\right)

as in (
6.10
).
Since
\nu
is
T_{v}
-invariant, the last expression is positive by applying Furstenberg’s multiple recurrence theorem to the system
(X^{k},\nu,T_{v})
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.1
.

Since
G\colon X^{k}\to[0,1]
is continuous and has positive integral, there exist open sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
and
c>0
such that
c\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{U_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{U_{k}}\leqslant G
and
\nu(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{k})>0
.
Applying

6.3
with

6.3.  Uniformity norms

For the proof of
Theorem

6.2
we make use of the structure theory of measure preserving transformations via uniformity seminorms
[
18
,
19
]
.
Roughly speaking, these seminorms capture the idea that functions orthogonal to the pronilfactor do not contribute to multiple ergodic averages.
We use this in the proof of
Theorem

6.2
to, roughly speaking, replace the function
G
by a function that is measurable with respect to
Z_{k-1}^{k}
without changing the
L^{2}(\sigma)
norm. This reduces the proof of
Theorem

6.2
to analyzing the behavior in pronilsystems, which have additional structure.

Definition 6.5

(Uniformity Norms)
.

Given a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and
s\geqslant 0
, the
s
-step uniformity seminorm
\|f\|_{U^{s}(X,\mu,T)}
of a function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is defined inductively as

\displaystyle\|f\|_{U^{0}(X,\mu,T)}=\int_{X}f\,\mathsf{d}\mu

(6.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{U^{s+1}(X,\mu,T)}^{2^{s+1}}=\lim_{H\to\infty}\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\|T^{h}f\cdot\overline{f}\|_{U^{s}(X,\mu,T)}^{2^{s}}.

(6.3)

The fact that the limit always exists and that
\|\cdot\|_{U^{s}(X,\mu,T)}
defines a seminorm on
L^{\infty}(\mu)
for
s\geqslant 1
is proven in
[
18
]
.

The mean ergodic theorem gives

\|f\|^{2}_{U^{1}(X,\mu,T)}=\|\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{I})\|_{2}^{2}

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra of invariant sets and so

\|f\|_{U^{1}(X,\mu,T)}=\left|\int_{X}f\,\mathsf{d}\mu\right|,

(6.4)

when the system
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic, in agreement with the standard definition.
The main result in
[
18
]
states that given an ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
, for each
s\geqslant 1
, all functions
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
satisfy

\|f\|_{U^{s+1}(X,\mu,T)}=0\iff\mathbb{E}(f\mid Z_{s})=0,

(6.5)

where
(Z_{s},m_{s},T)
denotes the
s
-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be ergodic, let
k\geqslant 2
, let
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Følner sequence, and let
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{k+1})
be invariant with respect to the transformation
{\rm{Id}}\times T\times T^{2}\times\dots\times T^{k}
.
Assume that the marginals
\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}
of
\tau
satisfy
\tau_{i}\leqslant i\mu
for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
.
Then for any
f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and any bounded sequence
b\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}b(n)\cdot(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes T^{n}f_{1}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}f_{k-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\tau)}

(6.6)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle C_{k}\cdot\|b\|_{\infty}\cdot\min\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}:1\leq i\leq k-1\}

where
C_{k}
is a constant depending only on
k
.

For the proof of
Theorem

6.6
, we make use of various properties of uniformity seminorms, which we collect in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a measure preserving system.

(i)

For all
k\geqslant 1
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, we have

\|f\otimes\overline{f}\|_{U^{k}(X\times X,\mu\times\mu,T\times T)}\leqslant\|f\|_{U^{k+1}(X,\mu,T)}^{2}.

(ii)

Let
c\geqslant 1
be an integer.
For all
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and all
k\geqslant 1
we have that

\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}\leqslant\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T^{c})},

and for all
k\geqslant 2
we have that

\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T^{c})}\leqslant c^{\frac{k}{2^{k}}}\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}.

(6.7)

(iii)

For any
k\geqslant 1
and integers
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\geq 1
, there is a constant
C
, independent of the system, with the following property: for all
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
with
\|f_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\leqslant 1
and all Følner sequences
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T^{c_{1}n}f_{1}\cdots T^{c_{k}n}f_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq C\min\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}.

(iv)

Let
k\geqslant 2
,
c\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\rho\in\mathcal{M}(X)
be invariant with respect to
T^{c}
and satisfy

\rho\leqslant C\mu

(6.8)

for some constant
C>0
.
Then for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, we have

\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\rho,T^{c})}\leqslant C^{1/2^{k}}\cdot c^{\frac{k}{2^{k}}}\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}.

(6.9)

Proof.

Part
(i)
is proved in the appendix of
[
10
, Equation (A.5)]
.
Part
(ii)
is a special case of
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Part
(iii)
follows by combining
[
16
, Proposition 1]

and
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Finally, for part
(iv)
, using (
6.8
) and that
\rho
is

-invariant, it follows quickly that

\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\rho,T^{c})}^{2^{k}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T^{c})}^{2^{k}}

using (
6.4
) for the case

and (

) to prove the cases

by induction.
Thus (

) follows from (

).
∎

We include a short lemma to clarify how to decompose averages over Følner sequences along residue classes.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
a(n)
be a bounded sequence taking values in a Banach space, let
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{N}
, let
c\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\Psi=(\Psi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be the Følner sequence defined by
\Psi_{N}:=\Phi_{N}/c
.
Then

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}a(n)\right\|\leqslant\frac{1}{c}\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}a(cn+j)\right\|.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
is a Følner sequence, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\Big|(\Phi_{N}-j)\cap c\mathbb{N}\Big|\to\dfrac{1}{c}

as
N\to\infty
.
Hence we have that

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\left|\Phi_{N}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{c-1}c\Psi_{N}+j\right)\right|\to 1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{|\Psi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}\to\frac{1}{c}

(6.10)

as
N\to\infty
.
It follows that as
N\to\infty
, we have that

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\left\|\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}a(n)\right\|-\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}a(cn+j)\right\|\to 0.

The conclusion is obtained by using the triangle inequality.
∎

On the way to proving
Theorem

6.6
, we first establish the following related result, corresponding roughly to the case
b(n)=1
in

6.6
.

.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a measure preserving system,

, and let

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T^{n}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}f_{k})\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leqslant D_{k}\min\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}:1\leq i\leq k\}

(6.11)

where

is a constant depending only on

Proof.

Let
c=\operatorname{LCM}(2,\dots,k)
be the least common multiple of the first

for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Let

be the constant in part (iii) of

Set

\Psi_{N}=\Phi_{N}/c=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:cn\in\Phi_{N}\}

which defines a Følner sequence.
Then, in view of

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T^{n}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}f_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{c}\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}\left(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}T^{cn+j}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{cn+j}f_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\right)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}T^{cn+j}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{cn+j}f_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq D_{k}\min\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}

(6.12)

for each fixed integer

.
For each

g_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(T^{j}f_{i})(x_{i}).

S=T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{k}
and note that

S^{c_{i}n}g_{i}=\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\otimes T^{cn+j}f_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{1}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus we can rewrite (
6.12
) as

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}S^{c_{1}n}g_{1}\cdots S^{c_{k}n}g_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq D_{k}\min\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}.

By part
(iii)
of
Lemma

6.7
applied to the system
(X^{k},\nu,S)
and our choice of
D_{k}
, we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Psi_{N}}S^{c_{1}n}g_{1}\cdots S^{c_{k}n}g_{k}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq D_{k}\min\{\|g_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X^{k},\nu,S)}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}.

For
i=1,\dots,k
, we have

\|g_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X^{k},\nu,S)}=\|T^{j}f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\nu_{i},T^{i})}

and so the conclusion follows by applying part
(iv)
of
Lemma

6.7
.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
(Y,\tau)
be a probability space,
\Phi\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set,
b\colon\Phi\to\mathbb{C}
, and
(v_{n})_{n\in\Phi}
be a collection of vectors in
L^{2}(\tau)
.
Then

\left\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|}\sum_{n\in\Phi}b(n)v_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\tau)}^{2}\leqslant\|b\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\left\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|}\sum_{n\in\Phi}v_{n}\otimes\overline{v_{n}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\tau\times\tau)}.

Proof.

We expand the square and obtain

\left\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|}\sum_{n\in\Phi}b(n)v_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\tau)}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\Phi}b(n)\overline{b(m)}\int_{Y}v_{n}\overline{v_{m}}\,\mathsf{d}\tau\leqslant\|b\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\Phi}\left|\int_{Y}v_{n}\overline{v_{m}}\,\mathsf{d}\tau\right|.

(6.13)

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we estimate

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\Phi}\left|\int_{Y}v_{n}\overline{v_{m}}\,\mathsf{d}\tau\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\Phi}\left|\int_{Y}v_{n}\overline{v_{m}}\,\mathsf{d}\tau\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\Phi}\int_{Y\times Y}\big(v_{n}\otimes\overline{v_{n}}\big)\cdot\big(\overline{v_{m}}\otimes v_{m}\big)\,\mathsf{d}(\tau\times\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{Y\times Y}\left|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|}\sum_{n\in\Phi}v_{n}\otimes\overline{v_{n}}\right|^{2}\,\mathsf{d}(\tau\times\tau)=\left\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi|}\sum_{n\in\Phi}v_{n}\otimes\overline{v_{n}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\tau\times\tau)}^{2}.

Combining this with (
6.13
), we obtain the conclusion.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.6
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}((X\times X)^{k-1})
be the pushforward of
\tau\times\tau
under the map

\varphi\colon(x_{0},\dots,x_{k},y_{0},\dots,y_{k})\mapsto(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\dots,x_{k-1},y_{k-1}).

Note that
\nu
is invariant under
(T\times T)\times(T^{2}\times T^{2})\times\dots\times(T^{k-1}\times T^{k-1})
and that each of the
k-1
marginals
\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)
of
\nu
are
\nu_{i}=\tau_{i}\times\tau_{i}
and hence satisfy
\nu_{i}\leqslant i^{2}(\mu\times\mu)
for
i=1,\dots,k-1
.
Applying
Theorem

6.9
to the system
(X\times X,\mu\times\mu,T\times T)
with
k-1
instead of
k
and
f_{i}\otimes\overline{f_{i}}
instead of
f_{i}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T\times T)^{n}(f_{1}\otimes\overline{f_{1}})

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\otimes\dots\otimes(T\times T)^{n}(f_{k-1}\otimes\overline{f_{k-1}})\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle D_{k-1}\cdot\min\Big\{\big\|f_{i}\otimes\overline{f_{i}}\big\|_{U^{k-1}(X\times X,\mu\times\mu,T\times T)}:1\leq i\leq k-1\Big\}.

Part
(i)
of
Lemma

6.7
implies that

\|f_{i}\otimes\overline{f_{i}}\|_{U^{k-1}(X\times X,\mu\times\mu,T\times T)}\leqslant\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu\,T)}^{2}

and so we deduce that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Bigl\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T\times T)^{n}(f_{1}\otimes\overline{f_{1}})

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\otimes\dots\otimes(T\times T)^{n}(f_{k-1}\otimes\overline{f_{k-1}})\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}

(6.14)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle D_{k-1}\cdot\min\big\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu\,T)}^{2}:1\leq i\leq k-1\big\}.

Setting

v_{n}=\boldsymbol{1}\otimes T^{n}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}f_{k-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\in L^{2}(\tau),

we note that

v_{n}\otimes\overline{v_{n}}=\left((T\times T)^{n}(f_{1}\otimes\overline{f_{1}})\otimes\dots\otimes(T\times T)^{n}(f_{k-1}\otimes\overline{f_{k-1}})\right)\circ\varphi.

Since
\nu=\varphi_{*}(\tau\times\tau)
, combining
Lemma

6.10
(applied to each
\Phi=\Phi_{N}
) with (
6.14
), it follows that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\dfrac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}b(n)\cdot v_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\tau)}^{2}\leqslant{}\|b\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot D_{k-1}\cdot\min\big\{\|f_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu\,T)}^{2}:1\leq i\leq k-1\big\}.

Taking square roots on both sides, we obtain the desired conclusion with
C_{k}=\sqrt{D_{k-1}}
.
∎

6.4.  Proof of
Theorem

6.2

For the proof of
Theorem

6.2
we need one more lemma.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic system, let
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
for some Følner sequence
\Phi
, and denote by
(Z_{k-1},m_{k-1},T)
the
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of
(X,\mu,T)
.
Assume
(Y,S)
is a
(k-1)
-step pronilsystem.
Then for every
g\in C(X)
,
y\in Y
, and
F\in C(Y)
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)F(S^{n}y)\right|\leqslant\big\|\mathbb{E}(g\mid Z_{k-1})\big\|_{L^{1}(m_{k-1})}\cdot\|F\|_{\infty}.

(6.15)

We stress that (
6.15
) requires the function
g
to be continuous, as we could otherwise modify it on the orbit of
a
without changing the right-hand side.

Proof.

Pick an increasing sequence
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)F(S^{n}y)\right|=\lim_{j\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{j}}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N_{j}}}g(T^{n}a)F(S^{n}y)\right|.

By refining
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
if necessary, we can assume that
(a,y)
is generic along
(\Phi_{N_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for a measure
\rho
on
X\times Y
with respect to
T\times S
.

Since
(Y,S)
is a pronilsystem, there is an invariant measure
\nu
on
Y
for which
y\in\mathsf{gen}(\nu,\Phi)
.
Replacing
Y
with the support of
\nu
we may assume that
(Y,\nu,S)
is ergodic.
Since
a\in\mathsf{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
we have that the first marginal of
\rho
is
\mu
and the second marginal is
\nu
.
Letting
\tilde{g}=\mathbb{E}\bigl(g\mid Z_{k-1})\circ\pi_{k-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{j}}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N_{j}}}g(T^{n}a)F(S^{n}y)\right|

\displaystyle\penalty\ =\,\left|\int_{X\times Y}g\otimes F\,\mathsf{d}\rho\right|\leqslant\left|\int_{X\times Y}(g-\tilde{g})\otimes F\,\mathsf{d}\rho\right|+\left|\int_{X\times Y}\tilde{g}\otimes F\,\mathsf{d}\rho\right|.

Note that

\bigl\|(g-\tilde{g})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\bigr\|_{U^{k}(X\times Y,\rho,T\times S)}=\bigl\|g-\tilde{g}\bigr\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=0.

Thus
(g-\tilde{g})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}
is orthogonal to the maximal
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of
(X\times Y,\zeta,T\times S)
for almost every ergodic component
\zeta
of
\rho
by
[
20
, Proposition 18, Chapter 8]
.
On the other hand, since
(Y,\nu,S)
is an ergodic
(k-1)
-step nilsystem it is a factor of
(X\times Y,\zeta,T\times S)
.
Thus
(Y,\nu,S)
is a factor of the maximal
(k-1)
-step pronilfactor of
(X\times Y,\zeta,T\times S)
.
Therefore, as
\boldsymbol{1}\times F
is measurable with respect to the latter factor,
the functions
(g-\tilde{g})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}
and
\boldsymbol{1}\otimes F
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\zeta)
for almost every ergodic component
\zeta
of
\rho
, and hence

\int_{X\times Y}(g-\tilde{g})\otimes F\,\mathsf{d}\rho=0.

We are left with

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)F(S^{n}y)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\int_{X\times Y}\tilde{g}\otimes F\,\mathsf{d}\rho\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|F\|_{\infty}\cdot\int_{X}\left|\tilde{g}\right|\,\mathsf{d}\mu=\|F\|_{\infty}\cdot\int_{Z_{k-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}(g\mid Z_{k-1})\right|\,\mathsf{d}m_{k-1},

and the claim follows.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.2
.

By a standard approximation argument, it suffices to show that for all
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in C(X)
with
\|f_{i}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(f_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\sigma)}=0.

(6.16)

To ease the notation,
we write
g_{i}=\mathbb{E}(f_{i}\mid Z_{k-1})\circ\pi_{k-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Note that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(f_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})=f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})

holds
\sigma
-almost everywhere.
Using (
6.5
), we have that
\|f_{i}-g_{i}\|_{U^{k}(X,\mu,T)}=0
for each
i
.
In view of
Lemma

4.6
,
\sigma
satisfies the assumptions of
Theorem

6.6
, which now implies that

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes g_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\sigma)}=0.\end{split}

On the other hand,
Theorem

6.9
implies

\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes f_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes f_{k})\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes g_{k})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\sigma)}=0.

This shows that (
6.16
) is equivalent to

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes g_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}T_{\text{\!\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{1}\otimes g_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes g_{k})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\sigma)}=0.\end{split}

(6.17)

Using the definition of
\sigma
, we can rewrite (
6.17
) as

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{2}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\big)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}=0,\end{split}

(6.18)

where
\xi
is as in (
4.2
).
Fix
1>\varepsilon>0
and choose
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in C(Z_{k-1})
such that

\|\mathbb{E}(f_{i}\mid Z_{k-1})-h_{i}\|_{L^{2}(m_{k-1})}<\varepsilon.

(6.19)

As the functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
are uniformly bounded by
1
, we can assume that the functions
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}
are as well.

To prove (
6.18
), we establish the following three statements:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\big)\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\big(T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\big)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}\leqslant k^{2}\varepsilon,\end{split}

(6.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&f_{1}(T^{n}a)\cdot\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{2}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a))\cdot\big(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}\leqslant k^{2}\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon},\end{split}

(6.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a))\cdot&(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\\
&\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}=0.\end{split}

(6.22)

Since
\varepsilon
can be taken arbitrarily small, (
6.18
) follows by combining (
6.20
), (
6.21
), and (
6.22
) and using the triangle inequality.

To prove (
6.20
) we first combine (
6.19
) with the fact that
m_{k-1}
is
T
-invariant and (
4.4
) to deduce that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{i}\mid Z_{k-1})-T^{n}h_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\xi_{i})}<\varepsilon\sqrt{i},

where
\xi_{i}
is the
i
-th marginal of
\xi
.
This, in turn, implies that

\big\|\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\big)-\big(T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}\leqslant k^{2}\varepsilon

by estimating coordinate-wise, and hence (
6.20
) follows by averaging.

To prove (
6.21
) we first note that, by the same argument, we have

\Big\|\big(T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{2}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{k}\mid Z_{k-1})\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)-\big(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\Big\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}\leqslant k^{2}\varepsilon.

(6.23)

Defining
g(x)=f_{1}(x)-h_{1}(\pi_{k-1}(x))
, we then have that

f_{1}(T^{n}a)-h_{1}(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a))=g(T^{n}a).

Since
\pi_{k-1}
is continuous, the function
g
is continuous.
Multiplying (
6.23
) by
f_{1}(T^{n}a)
and using the triangle inequality, we reduce (
6.21
) to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}&g(T^{n}a)\cdot\big(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(6.24)

To establish (
6.24
) we use
Lemma

6.11
.
Defining
Y=Z_{k-1}^{k}
,
S=T\times\cdots\times T
, and
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=h_{2}(x_{1})\cdots h_{k}(x_{k-1})
, we then have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}

\displaystyle g(T^{n}a)\cdot\big(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{N\to\infty}\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)\cdot\big(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\big)\biggr\|_{L^{1}(\xi)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{Y}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)\cdot F(S^{n}y)\bigg|d\xi(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{y\in Y}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{n}a)\cdot F(S^{n}y)\bigg|.

Since
\pi_{k-1}
is continuous, the function
g
is continuous and therefore we can apply
Lemma

6.11
to conclude that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}\big(f_{1}(T^{n}a)-h_{1}(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a))\big)\cdot F(S^{n}y)\right|\\
\leqslant\big\|\mathbb{E}(g\mid Z_{k-1})\big\|_{L^{1}(m_{k-1})}\cdot\|F\|_{\infty}.\leqslant\big\|\mathbb{E}(f_{1}\mid Z_{k-1})-h_{1}\big\|_{L^{2}(m_{k-1})}\cdot\|F\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon,

and the estimate in (
6.24
) follows.

To finish the proof, we are left with verifying (
6.22
). For this we use the definition (
4.2
) of
\xi
and the fact that orbit closures in pronilsystems are uniquely ergodic pronilsystems.
We have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}

\displaystyle\biggl\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a)\bigr)\cdot(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}h_{k})\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\xi)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{Z_{k-1}^{k}}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(\pi_{k-1}(T^{n}a)\bigr)\cdot(T^{n}h_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}h_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt-\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}(T^{n}h_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes T^{n}h_{k})\bigg|^{2}\,\mathsf{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{M}|}\sum_{m\in\Phi_{M}}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(T^{n}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)h_{2}\bigl(T^{n+m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\cdots h_{k}\bigl(T^{n+(k-1)m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt-\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(T^{n+m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\cdots h_{k}\bigl(T^{n+km}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\bigg|^{2}.

Since
Z_{k-1}
is a pronilsystem, arguing as at the end of the proof of
Theorem

5.2
we can interchange the order of the limits, and this last expression becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Phi_{M}|}\sum_{m\in\Phi_{M}}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(T^{n}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)h_{2}\bigl(T^{n+m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\dots h_{k}\bigl(T^{n+(k-1)m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Phi_{N}}h_{1}\bigl(T^{n+m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)h_{1}\bigl(T^{n+2m}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\cdots h_{k}\bigl(T^{n+km}(\pi_{k-1}a)\bigr)\bigg|^{2}.

Making the change of variables
n\mapsto n-m
in the second term shows that the two limits are identical, concluding the proof.
∎
