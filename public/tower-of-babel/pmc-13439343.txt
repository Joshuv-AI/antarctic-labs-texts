Investigating the effects of reaction time on scaling dynamics in produced water and its implications for enhanced re-injection practices
Authors: Seyed Alireza Mostafavi, Siavash Riahi, Marzieh Mavaddat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Produced water, major byproduct of oil production, presents significant environmental challenges due to its volume and toxicity. Effective management through re-injection is the most environmentally and economically viable approach, yet inorganic scale precipitation from incompatible water mixing poses a critical challenge. Compatibility investigations are essential to understand scale types and quantities before substantial investments, with reaction kinetics playing a pivotal role. This study examines the impact of reaction time on scale precipitation in produced water management, relevant to desalination processes such as pre-treatment and brine disposal. Water samples from two oil-producing fields were mixed, with field facilities allowing either immediate reinjection or 14-day retention. Precipitated scales were filtered from one container after 1 day and from another after 14 days under operational conditions. Results showed that scales filtered after 1 day exceeded 500 mg/L, while those after 14 days were below 150 mg/L, indicating over 350 mg/L of scales re-dissolved into the liquid phase. SEM–EDX analysis revealed that some scales increased in precipitation after 14 days, while others re-dissolved. These findings highlight the critical role of chemical reaction kinetics in scale precipitation, offering insights for optimizing pre-treatment and re-injection systems, enhancing scale management and environmental sustainability in produced water treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53810-y.

Introduction

Water is one of the three commonly produced fluids in any hydrocarbon reservoir1. Environmental issues make it the most problematic unwanted byproduct of oil and gas reservoirs2,3. Salt, heavy metals, dispersed oil and grease, and naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM) usually dissolved in the produced water cause environmental problems2,4. Besides, environmental concerns about produced water as a toxic fluid directly relate to its volume. According to the records, the global water–oil ratio in 2018 was reported to be around 3 and is expected to increase up to 12 barrels of water per barrel of produced oil by 20255. The large quantity of the produced water and the environmental issues clarifies the necessity of effluent management.

Surface discharge, treatment for reuse, and disposal or re-injection are the main methods of produced water management. Re-injection of effluent into abandoned wells is the best economic and environmental method for onshore fields6–9. Based on available data, one out of 5 water injection projects do not achieve sustainable waste management due to permeability reduction10. The most important challenge associated with produced water re-injection for reservoir engineers is permeability reduction due to inorganic scale precipitation11,12. In addition to the challenges posed by inorganic sales deposition in the reservoir, scaling can rapidly impair pumps, pipelines, and injection equipment.

An inorganic scale starts to precipitate when the related component in the liquid phase becomes supersaturated. Supersaturation is the saturation condition at which minerals of a specific type cannot dissolve in the liquid phase anymore and start to precipitate13. A variety of dissolved minerals in formation and injection waters may participate in inorganic scaling phenomena; accordingly, each scale has its properties and also corresponding precipitation factor. It is found in the literature that the most important factors affecting supersaturation and geochemical reactions are temperature, total dissolved solids (TDS), pressure, and CO2 partial pressure1,14,15. Temperature can either increase or decrease inorganic scale precipitation depending on the minerals. For instance, with regard to calcium carbonate, precipitation increases with an increase in temperature while barium sulfate precipitation decreases with the same change in temperature. Pressure is another parameter that can affect scale precipitation. For example, while iron sulfide scale precipitation is directly proportional to the pressure, strontium sulfate scale is inversely related to pressure. Total dissolved solids and the presence of some other minerals can affect scale precipitation, too. High reactivity of formation waters along with reactive ions (e.g., Ca2+ and SO42−) increases the scaling challenge16. As an instance, the solubility of calcium carbonate decreases as the Mg2+, Ba2+, Sr2+, Mn2+, and SO42− ion contents in water samples increase17–19. Moreover, CO2 partial pressure has an undeniable effect on carbonate scale precipitation. An increase in CO2 partial pressure reduces CaCO3 scale precipitation20. Despite the main parameters which may influence inorganic scales are pretty well known and different water compatibilities have been studied all around the world, results are not utilizable for other fields. The specific sedimentary environment and thermodynamic conditions of each reservoir affect formation water characteristics and have a large impact on scale precipitation. Therefore, compatibility investigations are essential before any field scale operational investment in water injection projects21,22.

Based on the previous studies, scale generation generally happens as a result of mixing incompatible waters or changing the thermodynamic conditions14. Supersaturation is indeed necessary and sufficient for different types of inorganic scale generation but precipitate of inorganic scales in crystal forms is a time consuming. Supersaturation, aggregation, nucleation, and crystal growth are inevitable steps of the crystallization process. When an anion and a cation reach their supersaturation condition and collide, they form micro-aggregation. These micro-aggregations serve as nucleation centers, with nucleation occurring over time. Crystal growth progresses through the attraction of inorganic sales to the nuclei. Furthermore, in addition to the parameters influencing supersaturation, reaction time plays a crucial role in affecting the precipitation of inorganic scales1,23. Reaction time plays a key role in determining appropriate injection and pre-injection strategies. Understanding the impact of reaction time can support the selection of the most effective approach for produced water management.

Despite the fact that both injection water samples in a water mixing process are separately in equilibrium, their equilibrium is disturbed due to the mixing waters with different compositions. Hence, a new thermodynamic equilibrium should be established with the new composition. Accordingly, compatibility investigations are designed to model the precipitation of inorganic scales through injection operations. Inorganic scale precipitation can be predicted using geochemical software or experimental methods. ‌Bottle test is the most common method to check the compatibility between fluids in the laboratory. There are three crucial factors affecting the reliability of the compatibility tests. The reliability of the experiments highly depends on the appropriate setting of experimental terms and conditions. Determination of the time needed for precipitates to form in compatibility tests depends on operation approaches. Considering the compatibility between the formation and injection water is meant to be investigated, as the formation and injection water are in contact for a long time in the reservoir, water samples should be set to be in contact with each other for a long time. When compatibility between different injection waters is desired, the reaction time between the fluids is limited by the capacity of the surface waste storage. In this case, the actual operational reaction time between the fluids must be taken into account in compatibility studies, even if chemical reactions may not be complete.

Numerous studies consider the compatibility of waters through laboratory experiments. However, some of them do not even mention the exposure time or mixing period specified in their experiments24–26, whereas some others have considered a wide range of reaction time in their compatibility checks. As an example, Gandomkar and Rahimpour27 investigated the effects of low-salinity water floods on recovery factors in secondary and tertiary enhanced oil recovery in limestone reservoirs. In their study, compatibility investigations were done before the core flooding experiments. All the experiments were performed at the temperature of 60 °C and the blend duration for mixing formation water was 24 h. Although the author mentioned that the reaction among some minerals needs more time, the impact of the reaction time was not clarified. In another study by Ghasemian et al.28, the possibility of inorganic scale precipitation was part of their questions as the injection water contained Mg2+, Ca2+, and SO42− ions. Therefore, compatibility analyses were conducted for injection and formation brine at 90 °C and 24 h was their sample reaction time. In two mentioned studies exposure time, 24 h, is not seems to be relevant to operational conditions. In another study by Abbasi et al.29, compatibility experiments were performed to determine the type and amount of scale generated when injection and formation waters were mixed. Experiments were done at 80 °C and contact time was 72 h. Bijani et al. optimized the salinity and composition of injected low-salinity water into sandstone reservoirs, considering minimum scale deposition30. They synthesized formation and injection water in the laboratory using pure solid salts and deionized water. The experiments were conducted at reservoir temperature and ambient pressure, and the reaction time was set to three days. In the studies carried out by Abbasi et al. and Bijani et al. the exposure time of 72 h was not adequate. Shirazi et al. in another study31, investigated symbiotic and individual behavior of potential determining ions. They carried out compatibility bottle tests for three synthetic generated smart waters and formation water at 60 °C and reaction time of 2 weeks. Various compatibility tests were also conducted by Maghsoudian et al.32 for injection waters. In their study, the injection waters and formation brine were mixed at 80 °C and the samples were kept in an oven for 1 month. Two studies conducted by Shirazi et al. and Maghsoudian et al. have considered a more appropriate reaction time for compatibility tests in comparison with other studies. In one of the recent studies Valadbeygian et al. employed bottle tests to assess the static and dynamic evaluation of formation damage due to barium sulfate scale during water injection in carbonate reservoirs33. They synthetically generated the formation and injection water in the laboratory and mixed them in various ratios. The 5-h reaction time was taken into consideration in their study which is not sufficient.

Despite the mentioned efforts which have led to finding the main parameters that influence inorganic scales, there still exist several limitations in previous studies. First, due to the expenses of standard water sampling and fluid conservation, some of the researchers generated synthetic water samples in the laboratory instead of using real water samples. Besides reaction times used in previous studies (from hours to months) to the best of the author’s knowledge, are not operationally relevant. The present work addresses this by quantifying time-dependent differences in scale precipitation using real produced waters, to reduce experimental errors related to synthetic samples, and linking the results to practical mitigation strategies. The objective of this study is to contribute to the prevention of the inorganic scales and to facilitate the discovery of optimal operational procedures for sustainable waste management and treatment solutions for minimizing scaling-related issues in surface facilities and equipment integrity.

Materials and methods

Two nearby fields named “A” and “B” are producing oil with high water oil ratios. Large amounts of produced water have intensified the environmental concerns and increased the necessity of waste water management. The production history of both fields shows an average water production rate of 4000 bbl/day and 1500 bbl/day for fields “A” and “B”, respectively. There is also another filed “C” in the vicinity of the other two fields with an abandoned well which makes produced water re-injection the best practical, economical, and environmental management solution for this case. A set of experimental investigations are deigned to examine the compatibility of the produced waters before any injection operation. Accordingly, produced water samples were collected from each field to better understand the active reactions that might occur with real water samples. This approach enhances the reliability of our study compared to using synthesized water samples in the laboratory. To ensure accurate results, the composition of the samples was verified at the time of sampling and again prior to experimentation. Samples exhibiting significant compositional changes were discarded to maintain data reliability. Strict standards for sample collection and storage were followed, including low-temperature maintenance and the use of appropriate preservatives to prevent scale precipitation during transport. To prevent unwanted mineral scale precipitation (primarily carbonates, sulfates, and potentially silicates) during transport from the field to the laboratory, after measuring the pH, the produced-water samples were acidified using concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl) or to generate an acidic fluid with pH lower than 2. Low-pH preservation minimizes changes in water chemistry relevant to scaling ions (Ca2+, Ba2+, Sr2+, HCO3−, SO42−, etc.). Prior to compatibility testing the preserved samples, pH was restored to the field determined value34. To determine the composition and other chemical properties of produced water samples, the inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS) technique was used to identify and measure the major elements that participated in them. Additionally, other properties like pH, electrical conductivity, total suspended solids, and alkalinity were evaluated for each sample. The bottle-test method as a standard method for compatibility screening, was used in this study35. To ensure that our work aligns adequately with the actual operational conditions in the produced water reinjection process, we adjusted the exposure time for the combined fluids based on the operational realities and limitations we encountered. In that region, one of the water storage pits could store water for up to 14 days, before injecting into field “C”, while the other one had to be emptied just 1 day after storage. Therefore, it was crucial for us to thoroughly examine both scenarios to accurately assess the amount and types of precipitated scales. Consequently, compatibility experiments were conducted with exposure times of 1 day and 14 days to better observe the results in both cases. In order to generate a homogeneous mixture and thereby prevent the settlement of scales, the mixing process was continued throughout the entire exposure time using a Wheel test apparatus designed in accordance with NACE 1D182 with low stirring speed of 3 RPM to approximate the continuous production of water in the field and its inflow into the storage pit. Research has demonstrated that continuous agitation accelerates scaling phenomena by increasing particle collisions, nucleation, and crystal growth25,36.

In all cases, the pressure and temperature conditions were the same as the field site surface conditions. According to the volume of produced water from the two fields, a 3:1 ratio mixture of the produced water from fields “A” and “B” is planned to be injected into field “C”. In the first step, the collected produced water samples from each field were filtered with 0.45-micron filter paper to purify the liquid from suspended solids and materials. Then the samples from both fields were mixed with the desired mixing ratio and kept in two different bottles. Mixed water samples were kept at field site surface condition (i.e. 14.7 psi pressure and 24 °C temperature). Compatibility experiments were performed for one of these bottles after one day while the other bottle was examined after 14 days. To investigate precipitated scale types and amounts, generated solids should be separated from the liquid phase. The scale separation from the liquid phase was performed with a Buchner funnel and Millipore filtration setup with 0.45-micron filter paper. The filtrated precipitated scales were dried in an oven at 105 °C and weighed with a 4-digit analytical balance. Dried samples were then kept in a desiccator to cool down.

Further analysis is required to determine scale types and their morphology. This could be achieved with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) instruments. SEM provides high resolution, up to 700,000 times magnified images from the solid sample, while EDX provides a quick nondestructive determination of the elemental composition of the sample. It is important to note that the optimal scale for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, given the structure of porous media, ranges between 5 and 10 nm. In this study, images were acquired at magnifications from 100 times to 5000 times. Given the complexity of real water samples used in this compatibility study, initial SEM analysis was initially concentrated on bulk scales. Subsequent analysis examined specific microstructural features and morphologies at magnification levels. Besides, results from EDX experiments conducted at various points on the collected scale surface, were utilized based on the stoichiometric relationships established between the elements, to estimate the composition of the salt precipitated in the precipitated scale. This estimation is made considering both visual observations of the scale’s morphology and the ionic composition of the water samples.

As presented in Table 4, the total precipitated scales of 550 mg/l on the first day decreased to 134 mg/l after 14 days. Therefore, the scale behavior must be taken into account, specifically regarding its generation and/or re-dissolution over time. A deeper look into the weight of the precipitated scales in Table 4 shows that almost all of the minerals precipitated from the water mixture with a 14-day reaction time are less in amount compared to the scales precipitated from the mixture with a 1-day reaction time. To be particular, chlorine, sulfur, and oxygen are the most re-dissolved elements based on their weight changes. Since most of the time-dependent scales are precipitated in the form of salts, efforts were made to identify the probable salts that precipitated from the reduced ions.

By comparing the molarity of each ion in Table 4, it can be concluded that most of the iron sulfide, calcium sulfate, and sodium chloride scales precipitated on the first day were re-dissolved in the liquid after 14 days. Sample color alteration from dark on the first day to light after 14 days confirms the re-dissolution of iron sulfide. Moreover, the comparison shows that as time passed, iron sulfide was converted to iron oxide in presence of oxygen. On the other side, silicon is the only element with a higher amount in EDX analysis after 14 days of interaction duration. To be specific, silica concentration in solid phases has increased up to more than 3.5 times with respect to the value measured with a 1-day reaction time. This indicates that generation of silica content scales is time consuming. In other words, although thermodynamic supersaturation provides the driving force for precipitation, the actual formation of solid silica phases is slow process, particularly the polymerization of monomeric silicic acid into oligomers, colloids, and eventually deposited scale38. It should be noted that, the frequent detection of high Na and Cl signals may partly arise from NaCl (halite) precipitation during sample drying and preparation for SEM analysis, rather than solely from true operational scaling in the field or during the mixing experiments. This evaporation artifact is common in SEM studies of high-salinity brines and can obscure minor phases; therefore, the presence of NaCl should be interpreted cautiously and not necessarily attributed to downhole or surface scaling processes. Nevertheless, the main aim of this work was to quantify the differences between two operationally meaningful time points (1 and 14 days) rather than to track scale precipitation in each time. This approach also helped mitigate the practical limitations associated with extensive SEM analysis.

Conclusions

Produced water re-injection is an environmentally sustainable approach for managing oilfield wastewater. However, the formation of inorganic scales due to fluid–fluid interactions during water mixing can cause injectivity impairment, thereby reducing operational efficiency or even leading to system shutdowns. This intensify environmental challenges by increasing waste and energy consumption. Compatibility testing identifies the types and quantities of precipitated scales, while varying reaction times in these tests provides insights into scale dynamics, informing strategies to mitigate scaling and improve environmental performance.

This study investigated the impact of reaction time on scale precipitation by examin the compatibility of produced water under field-relevant conditions, with retention periods of 1 or 14 days before re-injection. Water samples from two oilfields were mixed, and precipitated scales were filtered after 1 day and 14 days. The results indicated that scales filtered after 1 day reached 550 mg/L, primarily consisting of iron sulfide, calcium sulfate, and sodium chloride. In contrast, the concentrations after 14 days dropped below 150 mg/L, indicating over 350 mg/L of scales re-dissolved. This significant reduction highlights how extended storage influences fluid–fluid interactions, promoting scale re-dissolution and reducing scaling risks.

Elemental analysis through bulk-scale EDX revealed that chlorine, sulfur, and oxygen were the most re-dissolved elements, while silicon increased after 14 days. Molarity calculations and EDX comparisons confirmed that iron sulfide, calcium sulfate, and sodium chloride scales, which were dominant on day 1, largely re-dissolved by day 14. Conversely, SEM–EDX analysis showed increased precipitation of iron oxide and silicon dioxide after 14 days, driven by atmospheric oxygen reacting with iron sulfide to form iron oxide. These findings underscore the role of chemical kinetics in controlling scale formation and dissolution in produced water systems.

The study underscores the significance of reaction time in fluid–fluid interactions and scale dynamics. Sufficient reaction time during compatibility testing is essential to account for addressing scale precipitation/re-dissolution, in addition to operational constraints. The results offer practical insights for designing re-injection systems with pre-treatment strategies to reduce scaling, thereby enhancing injectivity and minimizing environmental impact. If the objective is to maximize solids separation at the surface, filtration should be performed after mixing (i.e., on the first day), when solid formation is high. In contrast, if the goal is to minimize sedimentation during injection and reduce potential damage to injection equipment caused by suspended solids, injection should be performed after 14 days of wastewater storage. Furthermore, re-dissolved minerals such as calcium and sulfur present opportunities for resource recovery, supporting a circular economy in water management. These findings contribute to the sustainable re-injection of produced water, optimizing operational efficiency and enhancing environmental outcomes in oilfield applications.

Finally, its recommended that that future investigations of scales in hypersaline brines should routinely combine SEM–EDX with at least one phase-specific technique—most preferably X-ray diffraction (XRD) when sufficient sample mass is available—to distinguish genuine scaling phases from preparation artifacts and thereby improve interpretive reliability.
