Prognostic significance of lymph node ratio in adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction: a study of patients after radical gastrectomy
Authors: Xiangzhi Qin, Zhenguo Gao, Ling Tian, Zhenhong Wang, Guobiao Chen, Honghua Song, Yan Xing, Zhenbing Lv, Jiani Hu, Yunhong Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) is increasing worldwide. Lymph node metastasis is a crucial prognostic factor in AEG patients. This study investigated the utility of lymph node ratio (LNR, the ratio of metastatic nodes to the total number of examined lymph nodes) in predicting the prognosis of AEG patients.Patients and MethodsThe study conducted a retrospective analysis of 257 patients with AEG who underwent radical gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy. The patients were categorized into low-LNR (L-LNR) and high-LNR (H-LNR) groups, and their corresponding clinicopathological characteristics and survival rates were compared. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of LNR.ResultsThe cutoff value for dividing the L-LNR group, and the H-LNR group was 0.20. In the L-LNR group, the 1, 3, and 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates and overall survival (OS) rates were higher than H-LNR group. Patients in the L-LNR group had better prognoses than those in the H-LNR group (P<0.001).Univariate analysis identified that vascular invasion, lymphatic invasion and LNR were risk factors for DFS and OS. Additionally, multivariate analysis demonstrated that LNR was independent risk factor for DFS and OS. In the subgroup analysis stratified by TNM stage, patients in the L-LNR group demonstrated better DFS and OS compared to those in the H-LNR group. The ROC curve identified that the LNR has good sensitivity and specificity for prognostic prediction in AEG patients, especially in stage III.ConclusionsPatients with LNR greater than 0.2 experienced significantly shorter OS and DFS in patients with AEG, especially in TNM stage III.Keywords: Lymph node ratio, Adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction, Prognosis, Survival analysis

Introduction

Gastric cancer is the fifth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading causes of cancer-related deaths [1–3]. While the overall incidence of distal gastric cancer has declined in Western countries over the past two decades, the incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction cancer (AEG) has been increasing by 4–10% annually [4]. At present, it accounts for nearly half of all newly diagnosed gastric cancer cases. Among these cases, Siewert type I is most prevalent in developed countries, whereas adenocarcinoma type II and III are more common in developing nations [5]. High-risk factors for AEG include obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and gastroesophageal reflux disease [6]. Due to its unique anatomical location and aggressive nature, AEG is gaining increased attention as an independent disease entity [7, 8]. According to the definition in the 8th edition of the TNM staging criteria published by the AJCC, AEG generally refers to adenocarcinoma that occurs within approximately 5 cm above and below the anatomical esophagogastric junction [9].

Currently, the primary treatment for AEG involves radical surgical resection, the aim of which is to completely remove the primary lesion and all regional lymph nodes where metastasis is possible, while ensuring surgical safety [10]. According to the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system for gastric cancer, Siewert type I and Siewert type II AEG are grouped and treated according to esophageal cancer stage. However, the extent of lymph node dissection remains debatable [11]. The pattern of lymph node metastasis varies among Siewert types of AEG. Feith et al. found that the mediastinal lymph node metastasis rates of Siewert type I, type II, and type III AEG were 65%, 12%, and 6%, respectively [12]. Similarly, the rate of intraperitoneal lymph node metastasis in Siewert type I AEG was lower than that in Siewert type II AEG, with Siewert type III AEG having the highest rate of intraperitoneal lymph node metastasis [13]. Due to the difference in the location of the tumor in AEG patients, the number and extent of lymph nodes dissected are different, and the number of positive lymph nodes do not provide a comprehensive and accurate prognosis for all patients with Siewert type AEG.

Studies have demonstrated that TNM staging alone does not offer a comprehensive picture of patient outcomes [14, 15]. Therefore, researchers have sought additional prognostic indicators beyond TNM classification [6]. Lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the ratio of metastatic lymph nodes to the total number of lymph nodes examined, is considered one of the most promising prognostic factors, independent of cancer stage. LNR has shown promise as a prognostic indicator in various cancers, including rectal, breast, thyroid, and colon [16, 17]. However, there is limited research examining the prognostic role of LNR in AEG patients [18, 19]. For instance, the study by Zhang et al. [19] included only patients with Siewert type II AEG, and the research results may not be applicable to all types of AEG patients. Additionally, some studies included patients with fewer than 15 dissected lymph nodes, which may affect the accuracy of LNR calculation [20–22]. In the study conducted by Sisic et al. [22], the majority of patients had Siewert type I or II AEG.

This study analyzed the clinicopathological data of 257 patients to evaluate the prognostic value of the LNR for patients with AEG after surgery, and explored whether LNR could serve as a prognostic factor for patients in pStage II/III. The results of this study are expected to help formulate the optimal management strategies and select more effective postoperative chemotherapy regimens.

Patients and methods

Patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 257 patients who underwent curative surgery for AEG. The Siewert types were precisely defined based on pathological mapping and macroscopic measurements of the distance between the tumor epicenter and the esophagogastric junction. The surgical management of Siewert tumors is tailored to tumor location and extent. For Siewert type I tumors, a right transthoracic (RT) approach is utilized. In contrast, Siewert type III tumors are addressed via the abdominal transhiatal (TH) approach through the diaphragmatic hiatus. Siewert type II tumors are stratified by esophageal involvement: lesions with < 3 cm extension were resected via the TH approach through the esophageal hiatus, whereas those with > 3 cm involvement underwent the RT approach. Both surgical approaches necessitate systematic lymphadenectomy in the following anatomic territories: perigastric regions (stations 1, 2, 3a), supra-pancreatic zones (stations 7, 8a, 9, 11p, 11d), para-aortic region (station 16a2lat), diaphragmatic hiatus (stations 19, 20), and lower mediastinal compartments (stations 110, 111, 112). However, the RT approach was required complete mediastinal lymphadenectomy, extending to the upper mediastinal nodal basins (stations 105, 106recL, 106recR) and middle mediastinal stations (107, 108, 109 L, 109R). For patients with clinically confirmed lymph node metastasis in the upper or middle mediastinum, the RT approach was selected, with therapeutic dissection of the upper mediastinal nodes and middle mediastinal nodes [23]. Lymph node station classification was defined according to the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma (14th edition) and the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer (11th edition) [24, 25].

Postoperative pathological staging was performed according to the eighth edition of the AJCC gastric cancer TNM classification system. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed primary adenocarcinoma of esophagogastric junction cancer (2), curative resection for AEG (3), postoperative pathology indicating dissection of 16 or more lymph nodes, and (4) no history of gastrectomy or other malignancies. Exclusion criteria included (1) preoperative chemoradiation (2), distant metastases, and (3) incomplete medical records or follow-up information. The patient selection process is detailed in Fig. 1, which outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study was conducted in accordance with STROCSS 2019 guidelines [26].

Definition of LNR

LNR was calculated as the ratio of metastatic lymph nodes to the total number of dissected lymph nodes. The optimal cutoff values for LNR were determined using the minimum p-value method of the X-tile software (version 3.6.1; Yale University, New Haven, CT) [27]. The X-Tile program can help divide patients into subgroups by determining the optimal cutoff points of a continuous or ordinal categorical variable based on the maximum x2 statistic value on the log-rank test. The patients were classified into those with low LNR (the L-LNR group: LNR 0-<20% corresponding to < 0.20) and high LNR (the H-LNR group: LNR 20–100% corresponding to ≥ 0.20).

Adjuvant chemotherapy

Patients diagnosed with pStage II, III, or IV disease generally received adjuvant chemotherapy post-operatively, involving four 2-week adjuvant cycles of intravenous docetaxel (50 mg/m2 ), folinic acid (200 mg/m2 ), fluorouracil (2600 mg/m2 ) (24 h infusion) and oxaliplatin (85 mg/m2 ) on day 1 [28, 29]. For patients with pStage II, III, and IV disease, the completion rates of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy were 72.5% (45/62), 74.6% (106/142), and 86.6% (26/30), respectively.

Follow-Up

All patients were monitored for at least five years after the date of surgery. Patients who did not experience recurrence following their procedure had their data recorded for five years at which point their data was censored. Postoperative surveillance mainly included cervical/thoracic/abdominal CT scans or ultrasounds of the neck and abdomen, along with other related laboratory tests, including CEA, CA19-9, CA72-4, complete blood counts, and biochemistry. These tests were conducted at least once every six months during the first two years after surgery, and once every year between years three and five [30].

Evaluations and statistical analyses

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval between surgery and the detection of recurrence or progression. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval between the date of surgery and death. DFS and OS curves were created using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to create univariable and multivariable survival analyses to identify prognostic factors. Time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were then created based on cumulative sensitivity and specificity to objectively assess prognostic predictive ability [31]. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22.0; IBM Corp. New York, USA) and the R 4.0.3 Software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 299 patients underwent radical resection for adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction cancer with lymph node dissection between January 2014 and December 2018; 257 of them were eligible for this study. Patients were classified into two groups: the L-LNR group with LNR less than 0.20 and the H-LNR group with LNR equal to or greater than 0.20. Among 257 patients, 167 were male (64.9%) and 90 were female (35.1%). Tumor distribution by Siewert classification was as follows: type I (n = 86, 33.5%), type II (n = 80, 31.1%), and type III (n = 91, 35.4%). Histopathological analysis revealed moderately/highly differentiated adenocarcinoma in 152 cases (59.1%) and poorly differentiated/undifferentiated carcinoma in 105 cases (40.9%). The median lymph node yield was 28 nodes (range: 19–41) in the H-LNR group and 30 nodes (range: 21–46) in the L-LNR group. Pathological T-stage distribution included pT1 (n = 32, 12.5%), pT2 (n = 96, 37.3%), pT3 (n = 79, 30.7%), and pT4 (n = 50, 19.5%). Lymphovascular invasion was present in 169 cases (65.8%) and absent in 88 cases (34.2%), while vascular invasion was identified in 197 cases (76.7%) and absent in 60 cases (23.3%). According to pTNM staging, cases were stratified as stage I (n = 22, 8.5%), stage II (n = 63, 24.5%), stage III (n = 142, 55.3%), and stage IV (n = 30, 11.7%). Survival analysis

The median time of follow-up was 42.7 months (range: 10.3–83.5 months). The 1, 3, and 5-year DFS were 92.1%, 74.9%, and 50.0% in the L-LNR group and 84.1%, 31.4%, and 9.3% in the H-LNR group. (P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Univariable analyses demonstrated that vascular invasion, lymphatic invasion, and LNR were significant independent predictors of lower DFS. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that vascular invasion (HR = 1.715, 95%CI: 1.298–2.341, P < 0.05), lymphatic invasion (HR = 1.025, 95%CI: 0.572–3.457, P < 0.05), and LNR (HR = 2.490, 95%CI: 1.842–6.425, P < 0.05) were significant independent predictors of lower DFS (Table 2). The 1, 3, and 5-year OS rates were 97.3%, 76.7%, and 54.6% in the L-LNR group and 96.0%, 32.7%, and 15.9% in the H-LNR group (P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). Univariable analysis demonstrated that older age, vascular invasion, lymphatic invasion, and LNR were significant predictors of lower OS. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that older age (HR = 1.283, 95%CI: 1.152–1.972, P < 0.05), vascular invasion (HR = 0.907, 95%CI: 0.743–1.579, P < 0.05), and LNR (HR = 3.467, 95%CI: 1.192–8.104, P < 0.05) were significant independent predictors of lower OS (Table 3).

ROC curves of survival indicators for pathological N factor and LNR

Time-dependent ROC curves, based on cumulative sensitivity and dynamic specificity, were generated for DFS and OS. The changes in the area under the ROC curve (AUC) are shown in Fig. 3A–F. In the overall cohort, the AUCs for DFS were similar between the pathological N factor and LNR, while the N factor demonstrated better AUCs for OS. In the pStage II subgroup, the prognostic performance was comparable between the two metrics. However, in patients with pStage III disease, LNR demonstrated visibly larger AUCs for both DFS and OS (Fig. 3E, F). While the overall prognostic accuracy of LNR, as measured by the AUC, was not statistically superior to the established pN factor, it demonstrated a notable trend towards better performance in the high-risk stage III subgroup. More importantly, the theoretical and practical advantage of LNR lies in its capacity to harmonize risk assessment across varying lymphadenectomy practices, thereby mitigating the stage migration effect inherent in the pN system. A comparison using DeLong’s test confirmed that these observed differences in the overall and pStage II cohorts were not statistically significant (P > 0.05)(Figs. 4 and 5).

Discussion

The primary findings from this study are as follows: (1) The optimal cutoff value for LNR was defined as 0.20. This value can provide a basis for further research on the most meaningful cutoff value (2). The 5-year DFS and OS rates were significantly higher in the L-LNR group than in the H-LNR group (3). The subgroup analysis shows that lymph node metastasis that exceeded 20% was a good predictive value in AEG pStage III patients undergoing radical resection.

The TNM staging system predicts survival based on the severity of local involvement of the primary tumor, the severity of lymph node metastasis, and the presence of distant metastasis, which has important value in evaluating the prognosis of AEG patients. However, it has been suggested that the current TNM staging system is insufficient to comprehensively assess the prognosis of AEG patients [32, 33]. A study from Germany concluded that because the biology of AEG is different from esophageal and gastric cancer, neither the AJCC 7th edition TNM staging system for esophageal and gastric cancer is suitable for AEG [34]. This limitation underscores the need for complementary prognostic tools, such as LNR, which synthesizes metastatic node count and surgical thoroughness into a unified metric. Our study reinforces the role of LNR in AEG, particularly for stage III patients, where it outperformed the pathological N factor in predicting DFS and OS. By harmonizing prognostication across heterogeneous dissection practices, LNR mitigates stage migration biases inherent to N staging. For example, patients share an identical LNR of 0.20 despite differing N classifications (pN1 vs. pN2), emphasizing its capacity to unify risk stratification. These findings align with growing evidence that LNR refines prognostic precision in AEG [35, 36].

Accurate N staging is extremely important to determine the patient’s postoperative adjuvant management regimen. The NCCN guideline recommends that for patients with AEG, there should be an indication that at least 15 LNs should be removed to achieve adequate nodal staging [37]. Previous studies on the correlation between the total number of excised LNs and survival have shown that smaller LN harvest is associated with poorer survival [38]. In our study, all patients received adequate lymph node dissection (median 31, IQR (interquartile range) 22–45) to ensure the quality of the procedure. The key advantage of utilizing LNR is that it summarizes the total number of lymph nodes cleared and the number of metastatic lymph nodes. A study involving 735 patients from the SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results) database highlighted that LNR and LODDS (log odds of positive lymph nodes) staging schemes could be considered as new options for prognostic prediction of AEG [39]. Consistent with their results, our study showed that LNR was an independent prognostic factor for DFS and OS. Therefore, the LNR should become a complementary predictor to TNM staging for the prognosis of patients with AEG.

The optimal cut-off for LNR in AEG remains undetermined. A previous study using a published cut-off value stratified LNR into four categories: 0, < 0.30, 0.31–0.60, and > 0.60. The results demonstrated that LNR is a significant predictor of survival. However, it’s important to note that 32.2% of patients in that study had 14 or fewer lymph nodes harvested [21]. Another study categorized LNR into 0, < 0.125, 0.125–0.425, and > 0.425, suggesting that LNR is an independent prognostic factor for survival. However, no subgroup analyses were conducted to validate the prognostic significance of LNR across different pathological stage [35].

The prognostic significance of the LNR arises from its dual capacity to reflect tumor biology and surgical quality. Elevated LNR (> 0.20) may signify aggressive subclinical dissemination, warranting intensified adjuvant therapy. Although current guidelines lack LNR specific protocols, our stage III subgroup analysis suggests LNR could refine therapeutic decision-making. For instance, stage III patients with H-LNR may benefit from triplet regimens (e.g., FLOT) over doublet therapies, aligning with gastric cancer paradigms where platinum-based regimens improved 3-year DFS in stage III patients with LNR > 0.25 [40, 41]. Conversely, stage III patients with L-LNR could avoid over-treatment, minimizing toxicity without compromising survival. However, existing evidence supporting LNR guided therapy primarily derives from gastric cancer cohorts, such as Hwang et al. [40] demonstrated that platinum-based chemotherapy enhances outcomes in stage III patients with LNR > 0.1 and lymphovascular invasion, while additional studies corroborate the utility of LNR in adjuvant therapy selection [41]. While these findings offer valuable insights, our focus on adenocarcinoma of AEG underscores the need for future studies to validate the role of LNR in AEG specific chemotherapy customization.

Expanding the clinical application of LNR and exploring its integration with the TNM staging system is a crucial step toward enhancing the accuracy of prognostic prediction. Preliminary studies in other cancers have already investigated the integration of LNR with the TNM staging system. A modified TRM staging system, which replaces the N classification in the TNM system with the R classification, has also been developed and compared with both the standard TNM staging system and the combined TNM + R system to evaluate the potential clinical significance of LNR [42, 43]. Future research will further integrate LNR with the TNM staging system to address the limitations of traditional TNM staging in prognostic prediction and improve the accuracy of survival estimates in patients with AEG.

There are some limitations in our study. Firstly, regarding statistical power, our sample size may be underpowered for the subgroup analysis of pStage II patients (n = 63), potentially explaining the comparable performance between LNR and the pN factor in this cohort. However, our analysis was adequately powered for the predominant pStage III subgroup (n = 142), where the most robust findings for LNR were observed. Secondly, this study is a single-center retrospective study. The patients inclusion may be affected by factors such as region and patient source, resulting in insufficient sample representativeness. Thirdly, in this study, patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy were excluded to enhance homogeneity. Therefore, the prognostic relationship with LNR in patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy still needs to be further explored.

Beyond the quantitative lymph node ratio, the anatomical pattern of lymphatic spread may provide an additional layer of prognostic refinement. Future studies should aim to integrate the Siewert classification with detailed lymph node mapping data. For instance, investigating whether ‘atypical’ metastatic patterns (e.g., mediastinal node involvement in Siewert type III tumors, or extensive abdominal node involvement in Siewert type I tumors) signify a particularly aggressive tumor biology and are independent predictors of poor survival. Such an approach could move beyond LNR as a standalone metric and towards a combined, anatomically-informed prognostic model that better captures the biological heterogeneity of AEG and guides more personalized adjuvant strategies.

Another consideration in our study is the classification of LNR groups. While many studies separate node-negative (LNR = 0) patients from those with a low positive LNR, our analysis focused on establishing a single prognostic cutoff within a spectrum of LNR values to identify a high-risk population. This approach, while providing a clear and practical clinical threshold, does not isolate the distinct and favorable prognosis associated with the LNR = 0 group. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to explore a more granular, multi-category LNR stratification that can fully capture the prognostic continuum from node-negative disease to a high burden of nodal involvement.

Conclusions

In summary, our study indicates that LNR greater than 0.2 can serve as a valuable prognostic indicator for individuals with AEG, particularly those in pStage III. LNR offers the potential to identify individuals at elevated risk of recurrence. Future research should focus on multi-center or international cohort data to validate these findings.
