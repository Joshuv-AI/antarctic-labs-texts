Second primary cancers in lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinaemia—cumulative burden without generalized excess cancer risk
Authors: Lars Munksgaard, Lars Moeller Pedersen, Lise Mette Rahbek Gjerdrum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySecond primary cancers (SPCs) are a survivorship concern in lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (LPL/WM), but estimates may be influenced by competing mortality and surveillance. We assessed cumulative incidence, relative risk and predictors of SPCs. We studied 521 patients diagnosed with LPL/WM in Region Zealand, Denmark, during 2000–2023. Cancers diagnosed >6 months before or after diagnosis were classified as prior malignancies and SPCs, respectively. Cumulative incidence was estimated with death as a competing event. Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) and absolute excess risks (AERs) used regional rates. SPC predictors were evaluated using Fine–Gray and Cox models. Median age was 72 years and median follow‐up was 8.1 years. Prior malignancy was present in 64 patients (12.3%); 68 (13.1%) developed an SPC. Cumulative incidence was 9.3% at 5 years and 14.6% at 10 years; 166 patients (31.9%) died without a preceding SPC. With 1‐year latency, overall cancer risk was not increased (SIR 1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.84–1.40; AER 21.3 per 10 000 person‐years). Haematological cancers showed borderline excess risk, driven by myelodysplastic syndrome. No covariates were associated with SPC risk. SPCs are clinically relevant in LPL/WM, although excess risk appears selective rather than broadly increased. Long‐term surveillance should consider myeloid malignancies.Keywords: competing risk, LPL/WM, myelodysplastic syndrome, SIR, SPC

INTRODUCTION

Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (WM) is an indolent lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) characterized by bone marrow infiltration and the production of monoclonal immunoglobulin M 1 , 2 although a small fraction may produce immunoglobulin A or immunoglobulin G or non‐secretory (LPL/WM). Over the past decades, advances in diagnosis and the introduction of bendamustine‐based regimens and Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors (BTKis) have improved survival, shifting clinical focus towards long‐term disease trajectories, including late complications and survivorship‐related outcomes. 3

Second primary cancers (SPCs) represent an important source of long‐term morbidity and mortality in patients with LPL/WM. 4 An increased risk of SPCs has been consistently reported across lymphoid malignancies, including LPL/WM and related monoclonal gammopathies compared with the general population. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Elevated risks have been observed for both solid tumours and haematological malignancies, including aggressive lymphomas and therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms. 5 However, prior reports are heterogeneous with respect to design, follow‐up duration and representation of contemporary treatment eras. Importantly, the substantial competing risk of death in LPL/WM may lead to biased estimates of cumulative SPC incidence if not appropriately accounted for. 5 , 8

Recent population‐based analyses underscore the continued clinical relevance of SPCs in LPL/WM. A Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program of 18 population‐based central cancer registries in the U.S. (SEER‐18) population‐based study including more than 4000 patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2018 reported a significantly increased overall risk of secondary malignancies (standardized incidence ratio [SIR] ~1.5) corresponding to a substantial absolute excess risk. 9 In addition, registry data have suggested site‐specific patterns, including an increased risk of cutaneous malignancies. 10 , 11 However, important knowledge gaps persist, including the impact of treatment exposure and the occurrence of malignancies preceding LPL/WM diagnosis.

The development of SPCs in LPL/WM is likely multifactorial. Chronic immune dysregulation and impaired humoral immunity may increase susceptibility to malignancies. 12 Furthermore, exposure to cytotoxic therapies, particularly alkylating agents and purine analogues, has been associated with therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms, typically with a latency of several years. 13 Conversely, intensified medical surveillance at the time of LPL/WM diagnosis may lead to earlier detection of pre‐existing or asymptomatic cancer. Accordingly, analytical approaches that account for latency, surveillance bias and competing risks are essential for accurate risk estimation.

In this population‐based cohort study of patients diagnosed with LPL/WM between 2000 and 2023, we aimed to (1) quantify the incidence and cumulative risk of SPCs using competing risk methodology; (2) compare SPC risk with that of the general population; (3) evaluate clinical and treatment‐related predictors of SPC; and (4) characterize and formally compare the distribution of malignancies diagnosed before and after LPL/WM.

METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, population‐based cohort study including all consecutive patients diagnosed with LPL/WM in Region Zealand between 1 January 2000, and 31 December 2023. Patients were identified through regional haematology records, pathology records and electronic medical records at Zealand University Hospital. In Region Zealand, diagnostic work‐up, treatment and follow‐up of LPL/WM are centralized at a single haematology centre. As patients with haematological malignancies in Denmark are generally managed in hospital‐based specialist care, the electronic hospital record system is expected to capture virtually all referred patients with LPL/WM in the region. A formal registry‐based completeness estimate was not available, and we cannot exclude that a very small number of patients may not have been referred to hospital care for unknown reasons. LPL/WM diagnoses were established according to contemporaneous consensus criteria by the Second International Workshop for Waldenström Macroglobulinaemia (IWWM‐2) and the European Consortium for WM 1 , 2 and verified through systematic medical record review. Pathology samples were reviewed by a haematopathologist at Zealand University Hospital. The index date was defined as the date of LPL/WM diagnosis. Patients were followed from index date until the occurrence of first SPC, death or end of follow‐up (31 December 2024), whichever came first.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records, including demographics, laboratory variables at WM diagnosis, bone marrow involvement, disease status (symptomatic vs. asymptomatic), comorbidity burden assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and detailed WM treatment exposure. Treatment exposures were captured longitudinally, including regimens and start/stop dates, and categorized into predefined classes: alkylating agents, purine analogues, bendamustine, anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibodies (CD‐20 found on the surface of B cells), targeted therapies including BTKis and others. Treatment exposure was recorded if the patient completed at least one full cycle of the respective regimen, irrespective of whether the initially planned number of cycles was subsequently completed.

Identification and classification of malignancies

All malignancies diagnosed before and after LPL/WM diagnosis were identified through detailed medical record review and required histopathological confirmation. Cancer diagnoses were characterized by anatomical site, histology and date of diagnosis. Prior cancer was defined as malignancies diagnosed at least 6 months before the diagnosis of LPL/WM, whereas SPCs were defined as cancers diagnosed at least 6 months after the LPL/WM diagnosis to reduce potential bias. Progression or relapses of LPL/WM, histological transformation of LPL/WM, recurrence of a previously diagnosed malignancy and non‐melanoma skin cancers were not considered SPCs. Malignancies diagnosed prior to LPL/WM were not applied as outcome events but incorporated in: (i) descriptive analyses of prevalence, site distribution and time interval from prior cancer to LPL/WM diagnosis; (ii) comparative analyses of cancer site distributions before and after LPL/WM; and (iii) adjustment as a binary covariate (prior malignancy: yes/no) in multivariable models.

Statistical analysis

The primary end‐point was first SPC occurring after LPL/WM. To mitigate potential surveillance bias around the time of WM diagnosis, sensitivity analyses excluded SPCs diagnosed within 12 months. Sensitivity analyses excluding patients with pre‐LPL/WM malignancy were also prespecified.

SIRs were calculated as the ratio observed to expected SPC cases. Expected counts were derived by applying age‐, sex‐ and calendar period‐specific cancer incidence rates in the Danish background population in the NORDCAN database, Region Zealand, an interactive digital database providing up‐to‐date cancer statistics for Nordic populations, to the corresponding person‐time at risk in the WM cohort. 14 Person‐time was accrued from LPL/WM diagnosis until SPC, death, censoring or end of follow‐up, depending on the analysis. Absolute excess risk (AER) was calculated per 10 000 person‐years.

The primary analysis used a 1‐year latency period to reduce reverse causation and detection bias related to increased diagnostic intensity at LPL/WM diagnosis. Sensitivity analyses evaluated latency periods of 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 years. SIR analyses considered only the first occurrence of each cancer type and excluded patients with a prior malignancy at the same anatomical site. Exploratory analyses stratified haematological SPC risk according to treatment exposure. To contextualize non‐significant site‐specific analyses, post hoc Poisson power analyses estimated power and the minimum detectable SIR using the expected number of events for each end‐point.

Time‐to‐event analyses were calculated from the date of WM diagnosis and only the first SPC was considered. The cumulative incidence of SPC was estimated using the Aalen–Johansen estimator with death without prior SPC treated as a competing event. Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models and complementary cause‐specific Cox models were used. To reduce overfitting, the primary multivariable analysis used a prespecified core model including age, sex, calendar period, a modified CCI excluding age and malignancy components, MYD88 (Myeloid differentiation primary response 88 gene) mutation status categorized as mutated (reference), wild type or unknown and prior malignancy diagnosed at least 6 months before LPL/WM. Each treatment exposure was then evaluated in a separate model adjusted for the same core covariates; patients were classified as unexposed until treatment initiation and exposed thereafter. The full candidate model was retained as a sensitivity analysis only. The proportional hazards assumption for the cause‐specific Cox models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, including both covariate‐specific tests and a global test.

p‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 521 patients diagnosed with LPL/WM between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2023 were included (Figure 1).

The median age at LPL/WM diagnosis was 72 years (interquartile range [IQR], 66–77), and 60% were male. The cohort contributed 3118 person‐years of follow‐up, with a median follow‐up time of 8.1 years (IQR, 4.5–10.6). During follow‐up, 166 patients (31.9%) died without developing an SPC, highlighting death as a major competing event. MYD88 L265P mutation status was available in 310 patients (59.5%), of whom 96% were positive. CXCR4 (C‐X‐C Motif Chemokine Receptor 4 gene) testing was not routinely performed.

Cancer characteristics

At the time of LPL/WM diagnosis, 64 patients (12.3%) had a history of prior malignancy, whereas 68 patients (13.1%) developed an SPC during follow‐up. Malignancies occurred both before and after LPL/WM diagnosis in 7 patients (1.3%), and 10 patients (1.9%) experienced more than one SPC during follow‐up. The median interval from prior cancer to LPL/WM diagnosis was 7.0 years (IQR 4.3–11.6), while the median time from LPL/WM diagnosis to SPC was 4.1 years (IQR 1.9–6.4).

The distribution of cancer subtypes before and after LPL/WM diagnosis was broadly similar, with solid tumours comprising the majority in both settings (Table 2). Female genital and prostate cancers were more frequent prior to LPL/WM diagnosis, whereas head and neck, lung and urothelial cancers appeared more common after LPL/WM diagnosis. However, these differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they did not consistently reach statistical significance and are limited by small sample sizes.

Compared with the background population in Region Zealand, the overall cancer risk, using a 1‐year latency restriction, was not significantly increased relative to the general population. A total of 62 cancers were observed compared with 56.7 expected, corresponding to an SIR of 1.09 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.84–1.40) and an AER of 21.3 per 10 000 person‐years (Table 4). Power was reasonable for all cancers and solid cancers, but limited or low for several site‐specific end‐points (see Table S1 for Power Analyses). Clinical and treatment‐related predictors of second primary cancers

In the reduced core model, no covariate was significantly associated with the cumulative incidence of SPC in Fine–Gray analyses (Table 5). In cause‐specific Cox models, age was associated with the instantaneous hazard of SPC (hazard ratio 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.01–1.07; p = 0.019). No treatment exposure was significantly associated with SPC risk when added individually to the core model.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by inspection of Schoenfeld residual plots and formal tests based on Schoenfeld residuals (data not shown). While the global test was not statistically significant, rituximab and bendamustine exposure showed evidence of non‐proportionality. Given the limited sample size and low number of events, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, we performed a series of predefined sensitivity analyses addressing potential sources of bias, including latency and surveillance effects around the time of WM diagnosis. These analyses demonstrated that the estimated 10‐year cumulative incidence of SPC was robust across multiple analytical approaches (Table S2). Exclusion of SPCs diagnosed within 6 months of WM diagnosis resulted in a lower 10‐year incidence (14.6%), suggesting a modest contribution from early events potentially related to surveillance bias. Further exclusion of patients with prior malignancy yielded similar estimates (14.3%). Landmark analyses in 12 months showed consistent results, with 10‐year cumulative incidences of 18.2%. Overall, these findings support the stability of the primary estimates.

Haematological malignancies

The elevated risk of AML/MDS remained consistent across different latency periods (0.5, 1 and 2 years), while overall estimates for haematological malignancies showed modest attenuation over time (Table S3). Among 11 patients who developed haematological SPCs, 10 had received prior chemotherapy, including 7 treated with bendamustine and 7 treated with alkylating agents or purine analogues (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this population‐based cohort of patients with WM, we demonstrate that SPCs constitute a clinically meaningful long‐term outcome, with a 10‐year cumulative incidence approaching 15% when accounting for the competing risk of death.

However, despite the substantial cumulative burden of SPCs, the overall cancer risk was not significantly increased compared with the background population (SIR 1.09). The data, therefore, do not demonstrate a generalized excess cancer risk, although power analyses indicate that modest site‐specific increases cannot be excluded.

The distinction between cumulative incidence and excess population‐based cancer risk is central to the interpretation of our findings. Although SPCs accumulated steadily over time, particularly in this elderly cohort with prolonged follow‐up, the overall cancer incidence remained comparable to that expected in the background population. These findings emphasize that a high cumulative incidence does not necessarily imply generalized excess cancer susceptibility, but may instead reflect ageing, prolonged observation time and substantial competing mortality. Importantly, nearly one‐third of patients died without developing SPC, highlighting the importance of competing‐risk methodology in WM, where conventional Kaplan–Meier approaches may overestimate absolute event probabilities.

Previous registry‐based studies, including SEER analyses, have reported an increased overall risk of secondary malignancies in WM. 9 , 15 , 16 However, these studies differ in several important methodological aspects, including variation in latency definitions, handling of competing mortality and inclusion of prior malignancies. Our findings suggest that, when competing mortality and latency‐related surveillance bias are rigorously addressed, the excess cancer risk in WM appears more nuanced than previously reported; however, the present study was not powered to exclude modest excess risks for several individual cancer sites.

The most consistent signal in our study was observed for haematological malignancies, particularly the combined AML/MDS end‐point, which showed a marked relative increase compared with the background population. This pattern persisted across latency analyses, strengthening the argument against detection bias and supporting a biologically meaningful association. These findings are consistent with prior reports linking WM and its treatment to therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms 5 , 17 and align with historical concerns regarding leukaemogenic effects of cytotoxic therapies, particularly alkylating agents and purine analogues. 13 Although causal inference cannot be established in this observational setting, the concentration of haematological SPCs among treated patients and the persistence of risk beyond early follow‐up are compatible with a treatment‐related contribution to long‐term myeloid cancer risk.

In contrast, the risk of solid tumours was generally comparable to that of the background population. Melanoma demonstrated a numerically increased SIR, but the analysis was underpowered and the confidence interval was wide. Thus, the absence of statistically significant solid tumour associations should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of the absence of site‐specific risk. This interpretation is broadly consistent with prior WM studies reporting inconsistent associations across individual solid tumour subtypes. 11 , 15 Clinically, these findings support continued adherence to standard age‐ and sex‐appropriate cancer screening in patients with LPL/WM, rather than intensified generalized cancer surveillance. Particular attention should nevertheless be paid to persistent or unexplained cytopenias after cytotoxic therapy, given the observed signal for myeloid malignancies.

To address overfitting, the revised regression strategy used a prespecified clinical core model and evaluated treatment exposures individually rather than simultaneously. No covariate was associated with SPC cumulative incidence in Fine–Gray models, whereas age was associated with the cause‐specific hazard of SPC after removal of age from the CCI. This distinction is clinically plausible, as age may increase the instantaneous risk of cancer while also being closely related to competing mortality. The global proportional hazards tests were not statistically significant, and treatment‐specific findings should be interpreted cautiously because of limited event counts.

The observed association between WM therapy and myeloid SPCs is biologically plausible and consistent with prior literature implicating alkylating agents, purine analogues and bendamustine in therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms. 13 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Potential mechanisms include cumulative genotoxic exposure, genomic instability, prolonged immunosuppression and impaired immune surveillance. In addition, disease‐related immune dysregulation may further contribute to long‐term cancer susceptibility in WM and related disorders. 12

The shift towards continuous targeted therapies may change the future SPC profile in WM. Reduced use of purine analogues and alkylating agents could plausibly lower the risk of therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms, whereas prolonged targeted therapy may have distinct long‐term effects through immune modulation, infection susceptibility, dermatologic toxicity and intensified follow‐up. In chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL), second malignancy risk remains increased, particularly among treated patients, but emerging real‐world data suggest lower short‐term SPM incidence after first‐line covalent BTKis than after chemoimmunotherapy. Because BTKis and venetoclax were rare in our cohort, our data cannot determine whether similar patterns will emerge in WM; this will require longer follow‐up of contemporary cohorts. 22

The strengths of this study include its population‐based design, detailed clinical data and comprehensive analytical framework integrating competing‐risk methods, complementary regression modelling and comparison with the background population. The consistency of the findings across multiple sensitivity analyses, including latency adjustments, exclusion of early events and restriction to patients without prior malignancy, supports the robustness of the primary conclusions and reduces the likelihood that the results are primarily explained by surveillance bias or reverse causation.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The observational design precludes causal inference, and confounding by indication remains a concern, particularly in analyses of treatment exposure. Although power analyses were added, many site‐specific analyses remained underpowered to detect modest associations. Furthermore, treatment exposure was modelled at an aggregated level, without detailed information on cumulative dose, treatment sequencing and interactions between therapeutic modalities. Residual confounding related to lifestyle factors and healthcare utilization could not be fully addressed. Finally, comparisons with registry‐based studies should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in data sources, case ascertainment and analytical methodology.

In conclusion, SPCs represent an important long‐term survivorship outcome in LPL/WM, but this study did not demonstrate a generalized excess cancer risk after accounting for competing mortality and latency‐related surveillance bias. The most consistent excess risk was observed for myeloid malignancies, particularly AML/MDS. Patients with LPL/WM should remain engaged in standard age‐ and sex‐appropriate cancer screening, while clinicians should maintain awareness of possible therapy‐related myeloid malignancies during long‐term follow‐up.
