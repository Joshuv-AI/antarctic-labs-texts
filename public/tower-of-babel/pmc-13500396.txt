Two-stage reconstruction using basic fibroblast growth factor-impregnated artificial dermis and acellular fish skin graft in a dog with distal extremity soft tissue sarcoma
Authors: Sho GOTO, Mitsuhiko TERAUCHI, Ryota IWASAKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 13-year-old Chihuahua presented with a distal forelimb soft tissue sarcoma and underwent surgical excision. To close the resulting large skin defect, a two-stage approach was performed using a basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)–impregnated artificial dermis composed of collagen gelatin sponge (CGS), followed by application of an acellular fish skin graft (FSG). After 10 days of bFGF-impregnated CGS application, uniform granulation tissue had formed, and an FSG was subsequently applied. Complete epithelialization was achieved 25 days after FSG placement without additional grafting. At 320 days postoperatively, no postoperative complications or local recurrence were observed. However, given the potential risk of promoting residual tumor cell proliferation, local bFGF use in malignant tumor-associated wounds should be restricted to carefully selected cases.

Soft tissue sarcomas (STSs) in dogs are primarily treated by surgical excision, and complete resection with recommended margins of 2–3 cm laterally and one fascial layer deep to the tumor is associated with good local tumor control [4, 8, 9, 11, 25]. However, the optimal lateral margin for STSs arising in the distal extremities remains controversial. Previous studies have reported that STSs at these locations tend to be less infiltrative and more well-circumscribed, as they are frequently of low-grade histologic subtypes, such as perivascular wall tumors [1, 29]. In low-grade STSs at these locations, marginal resection has been associated with low local recurrence rates, suggesting that marginal excision may represent an acceptable treatment option [29]. Nevertheless, even when minimal lateral margins are applied, primary closure of large skin defects in the distal extremities is often impossible after tumor resection because of the paucity of elastic skin. Therefore, reconstructive surgery, such as relaxing incisions, local or distant skin flaps, tissue expansion and free skin grafts, are commonly required in the distal extremities [3, 11, 16, 26, 28, 33, 35, 37]. However, these reconstructive procedures in the extremities are associated with complications such as wound dehiscence and necrosis of skin flaps and grafts because of the greater distance from potential donor areas, constant motion, vascular compromise, and infection. Although limb amputation is considered a radical surgical treatment for STSs of the extremities, it is often declined by owners due to cosmetic concerns and may be an inappropriate treatment option in dogs with pre-existing comorbidities, such as osteoarthritis or neurologic disease.

In recent years, the use of acellular fish skin grafts (FSGs) as xenografts has been noted as an alternative approach for wound healing in both human and veterinary medicine (dogs and cats), including the treatment of traumatic wounds, burns, diabetic foot ulcers, and skin defects following tumor excision [2, 6, 14, 15, 18, 20, 21, 30, 38]. FSGs are derived from the skin of wild Atlantic cod and are rich in omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are associated with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. Furthermore, even after decellularization, FSGs retain a porous three-dimensional extracellular matrix (ECM) structure consisting of type I collagen, which provides a biologically active scaffold that facilitates adhesion and migration of epithelial cells and fibroblasts, thereby promoting wound healing [10, 13]. A previous study evaluating repeated application of FSGs for large skin defects following excision of tumors in the distal extremities of dogs, including mast cell tumors and STSs, reported that complete epithelialization was achieved in all cases within 7–15 weeks without severe adverse events [21]. Although this innovative treatment approach has demonstrated excellent clinical outcomes, the reported median of five applications (range, 4–9) of FSGs suggests that it may result not only in high overall treatment costs but also in an increased number of sedation or anesthesia procedures.

In human medicine, it has been reported that large, full-thickness skin defects can be successfully treated with two-stage skin grafting [32]. In that study, commercially available recombinant human basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; Fibrast Spray; Kaken Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) was used to impregnate an artificial dermis composed of collagen gelatin sponge (CGS; Pelnac G plus; Gunze Co., Ltd., Ayabe, Japan). The positively charged bFGF ionically binds to the negatively charged gelatin component, resulting in sustained release of bFGF at effective local concentrations for approximately 10 days [34]. While a waiting time is required for well-vascularized tissue to regenerate in the recipient bed before skin grafting, the use of bFGF-impregnated CGS has been shown to effectively shorten this period. We hypothesized that, in dogs with large skin defects, a similar two-stage approach using bFGF-impregnated CGS to induce granulation tissue formation followed by the application of FSGs could reduce the number of FSG applications required. The present report describes the clinical outcome of a dog with a large skin defect following STS excision that was managed using a two-stage treatment approach combining a bFGF-impregnated CGS and FSG.

A 13-year-old castrated male Chihuahua, weighing 5.0 kg (body condition score, 8/9), was presented to Morita Animal Hospital with a 4-month history of a progressively enlarging subcutaneous mass located at the distal aspect of the right forelimb, adjacent to the carpal joint. No clinical signs, including lameness, were observed. On physical examination, the subcutaneous mass measured 2.7 cm in its longest diameter, was mildly mobile, and was well-circumscribed. No enlargement of the peripheral lymph nodes was detected. The dog had been receiving medical management for myxomatous mitral valve disease. Fine-needle aspirates revealed a population of spindle-shaped cells, present singly or in loosely cohesive small clusters. The cells had oval nuclei with mild to moderate anisokaryosis and a single small or indistinct nucleolus. On the basis of these clinical findings, STS was suspected. Histopathological examination of tissue core biopsy samples was recommended to the owner prior to initiating treatment; however, the owner declined further examination and treatment. One month later, the dog was re-presented because the mass had increased in size and the dog had begun to lick the lesion. On day 9, an increase in wound exudate was noted, and cytological examination revealed neutrophils containing phagocytosed bacteria, suggestive of infection. Questioning of the owner revealed that the prescribed amoxicillin–clavulanate tablets had not been appropriately stored at home. After instructing the owner on correct storage, antimicrobial therapy with amoxicillin–clavulanate was continued from day 10, as susceptibility testing supported its use. On day 10, granulation tissue formation was observed under the CGS silicone film (Fig. 2B). Therefore, the film was removed under general anesthesia, and an FSG (Kerecis VET; Kerecis, Reykjavik, Iceland) was sutured to the wound using nonabsorbable sutures on day 10 (Fig. 2C). After application of the FSG, the wound was covered with a three-layer dressing consisting of a non-adherent contact layer (Adaptic; Solventum, Tokyo, Japan), a second antimicrobial Hydrofiber layer (Aquacel Ag Advantage; ConvaTec, Tokyo, Japan), and a third outer absorbent layer (Melolin; Smith & Nephew, Tokyo, Japan), and was immobilized with a modified Robert-Jones bandage along with a plastic splint as described above. Subsequently, the dressing and bandage were changed every 1–2 days to evaluate the wound condition and to lavage it with sterile saline (0.9% NaCl). One week after FSG application (day 17), the FSG had been incorporated into the wound bed, and epithelialization was observed from the wound edges without evidence of infection (Fig. 2D). This epithelialization then progressed rapidly, leading to complete wound closure by day 35 (Fig. 2E–G). Although intermittent lameness was observed at that time, it had resolved by day 75. Histopathological examination revealed a grade 1–2 STS. The tumor was well demarcated, and no vascular invasion was identified. Although no overt tumor exposure at the surgical margins was observed, neoplastic cells were present close to the margins. After discussion with the owner, no adjuvant therapy was performed. At the time of writing this report (day 320), most of the surgical site was covered with hair, although a small alopecic scar remained (Fig. 2H and 2I). No complications, such as epidermal disruption or decreased range of motion of the carpus, were observed, and no evidence of local tumor recurrence was identified.

This is the first report describing a two-stage approach combining initial granulation tissue formation using bFGF-impregnated CGS followed by application of FSG. In the present case, several alternative reconstructive approaches were considered preoperatively, including free skin grafting [3, 11, 26, 28, 33, 35], multiple punctate relaxing incisions [11], and second intention healing alone [25].

Free skin grafting was not selected in this case because adequate postoperative immobilization was considered difficult due to the patient’s temperament. In addition, the skin defect was expected to exceed 50% of the limb circumference. Because second-intention healing alone is associated with an increased risk of complications in large distal extremity wounds, its use has been recommended primarily for wounds involving less than 30% of the limb circumference [11]. Reported complications include incomplete healing due to tension counteracting myofibroblast contraction [12], restriction of joint range of motion secondary to wound contraction over a joint, and the development of fragile epithelium prone to trauma [25]. For these reasons, second-intention healing alone and multiple punctate relaxing incisions, which similarly rely on extensive second-intention healing over a large periarticular area, were not selected in this case.

Basic fibroblast growth factor stimulates angiogenesis and promotes granulation tissue formation during wound healing [22, 23, 27]. In the present case, uniform granulation tissue was achieved within 10 days after application of the bFGF-impregnated CGS. This favorable wound bed allowed close adherence of the FSG across the entire recipient surface. An experimental study using a rabbit ear full-thickness skin defect model reported increased hemoglobin content at 7 days in wounds treated with a bFGF-impregnated CGS compared with a collagen sponge [34]. In addition, in a rat model of full-thickness skin defects, bFGF-impregnated CGS was shown at postoperative day 14 to increase vascular density within the granulation tissue compared with CGS alone and to promote the formation of thicker granulation tissue without excessive wound contraction [19]. In contrast, in a porcine full-thickness wound model comparing bovine dermal collagen matrix, collagen glycosaminoglycan matrix, and FSG, vascular density at 7 days after application was lowest in the FSG-treated wounds [5]. On the basis of these findings, bFGF-impregnated CGS was used with the intention of establishing a well-vascularized recipient bed before FSG placement in the present case. This two-stage approach was expected to maximize the scaffold function of the preserved porous three-dimensional ECM of the FSG and promote epithelialization. In this case, epithelialization progressed rapidly, and complete wound closure was achieved by postoperative day 35 after only a single FSG application. This healing period was comparable to previous reports for free skin grafting [3, 32] and was shorter than the 7 to 15 weeks reported in studies that used multiple FSG applications alone [21].

Notably, complete epithelialization was observed without marked contraction or epidermal fragility, as reported in canine case series using FSG alone [21]. This finding suggests that the present approach may provide functionally acceptable wound healing. Previous literature in human medicine indicates that the application of acellular dermal matrix over joints or exposed tendons minimizes contraction and preserves normal range of motion [24]. Additionally, a comparative study of human burn injuries treated with split-thickness skin grafts and FSGs demonstrated that the FSG group achieved epithelialization significantly earlier than the split-thickness skin grafts group, and that regenerated skin hydration was significantly higher in the FSG group [38]. Furthermore, regenerated skin elasticity and water-content capacity in the FSG group recovered to levels comparable to those of normal skin. The two-stage treatment approach used in our case may achieve more rapid epithelialization with improved scar quality compared with second-intention healing alone in dogs. While this approach requires at least two procedures under general anesthesia or sedation, it is feasible without specialized techniques or equipment, as the required wound management is comparable to that of second-intention healing alone.

The major limitation of this case is the uncertain oncologic safety of local bFGF administration to a wound after malignant tumor excision. Although bFGF promotes fibroblast proliferation and angiogenesis, if residual tumor cells are present in the wound, bFGF can also stimulate their proliferation and thereby increase the risk of local recurrence and metastasis [7, 17, 31, 36]. In human medicine, recombinant human bFGF preparations are contraindicated at sites affected by malignant tumors or with a history of malignancy. This concern is particularly important in the present case. In this small-breed dog, application of wide (2–3 cm) lateral margins would likely have resulted in entire circumferential skin loss of the distal extremity, with potential compromise of distal perfusion and increased risk of edema or tissue necrosis. Considering these risks, the relatively long clinical history and slow growth of the mass, gross appearance, and the reported predominance of low-grade STSs in the distal extremities [1, 29], resection with minimal lateral margins and a deep fascial plane was selected in this case, as described in a previous study [29]. However, because no preoperative tissue biopsy had been performed, the histologic grade and infiltrative nature of the tumor had not been assessed at the time bFGF was administered. In addition, postoperative histopathological examination demonstrated tumor cells close to the surgical margin, indicating that microscopic residual disease could not be excluded. Therefore, local bFGF administration may have increased the risk of local recurrence. In this case, bFGF was selected to promote the formation of a well-vascularized recipient bed and potentially reduce the number of FSG applications and overall treatment duration. However, a previous report has described granulation tissue formation within 9–14 days in canine distal extremity tumor cases following surgical excision using artificial dermis alone [33], which was comparable to the period observed in our case. Because the granulation tissue formed under each approach was not histologically evaluated and compared, it remains uncertain whether the favorable outcome observed in this case was attributable to bFGF-enhanced angiogenesis or to the staged optimization of the recipient bed prior to FSG placement. Taken together, this case alone does not provide clear evidence that bFGF contributed a clinically meaningful additional benefit beyond that achievable with artificial dermis alone. Until further evidence clarifies its safety and benefit, local bFGF administration should generally be restricted to skin defects in which preoperative tissue biopsy has excluded an underlying malignant tumor. If it is nevertheless considered for use in malignant tumor-associated wounds, it should be limited to highly selected cases in which histopathologic evaluation confirms a low-grade tumor and sufficiently wide surgical margins are expected to be achievable, and owners should be informed in advance that early local recurrence cannot be excluded. Informing the owner of this risk is particularly important in exceptional cases such as the present one, in which a low-grade STS is located in the distal extremity and wide surgical margins are difficult to obtain because of the anatomic location, as limb amputation may become the principal salvage treatment if recurrence occurs. Given the inherent limitations of a single case, variables such as wound size, dressing materials, and patient-related factors may have influenced the outcome. Further clinical investigations, including larger population studies and comparative studies, are warranted to evaluate whether this treatment approach contributes to improved functional and cosmetic outcomes and to clarify the healing time compared with other skin reconstructive procedures.

In conclusion, this two-stage approach using bFGF-impregnated CGS followed by FSG achieved relatively early epithelialization with a single FSG application and resulted in functionally and cosmetically acceptable skin regeneration. However, the oncologic safety of local bFGF use in canine distal extremity soft tissue sarcoma could not be established from this case alone. Given the potential risk of promoting proliferation of residual tumor cells, the use of local bFGF in malignant tumor-associated wounds should be restricted to carefully selected cases.
