Urinary incontinence (UI) affects the quality of life of a large proportion of the adult female population.1 Symptoms are underreported to physicians owing to embarrassment, social stigma, acceptance of symptoms as a normal part of aging, and lack of physician inquiry.2 A previous narrative review reported that 61.5% to 84% of females with symptoms do not seek medical UI care.2 The direct and indirect costs associated with UI in Canada approximate $2.6 billion annually, with older adults who live at home often spending $1400 to $2100 a year on incontinence supplies. 3,4 Most patients can be treated without surgery or other advanced modalities. We discuss and summarize the evaluation and treatment of UI experienced by females based on a review of current evidence (Box 1).

Evidence used in this review

We searched PubMed and MEDLINE from inception until September 2024 using the terms “urinary incontinence” with keywords “women or female,” “symptoms,” “diagnosis,” and “treatment.” We cross-referenced selected articles for a list of other relevant articles, including a combination of systematic reviews, practice guidelines, randomized controlled trials, and cohort studies. This search was restricted to English-language publications.

Who is at risk for UI and what causes it?

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is the involuntary loss of urine with increases in intra-abdominal pressure, which occurs with exercising or coughing, for example. Urge urinary incontinence (UUI) is involuntary urine loss associated with a sudden strong urge to urinate, usually accompanied by frequency and nocturia (i.e., 2 or more voids).1 Mixed UI has elements of both types. Less common types of UI in females (e.g., incontinence associated with chronic retention of urine) are not discussed here.1

A 2020 and a 2008 survey of adults in Canada reported that approximately 29% to 34.5% of adult Canadian females experience incontinence.5,6 The 2023 International Consultation of Incontinence reported a 10% to 39% prevalence of isolated SUI in adult females.1 The prevalence of UUI is 1% to 7%, and doubles from ages 50 to 80 years.1,7 A population-based Canadian survey of 518 adult females aged 18 to 90 years found a prevalence of UUI of 11%.6

Incontinence occurs when the normal relationship between the bladder, urethra, pelvic floor, and nervous system is disrupted. Stress urinary incontinence is caused by injury to pelvic floor muscular and fascial tissues that support the urethra and bladder neck (causing hypermobility of the urethra) and injury to the urethral sphincter.1,8,9

Urge urinary incontinence may be idiopathic or neurogenic (e.g., due to stroke, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson disease, spinal cord injury).1,10 Urge urinary incontinence is traditionally attributed to uninhibited detrusor contractions during bladder filling, although the association between symptoms and urodynamic findings is weak. The cause is often idiopathic and multifactorial, with mechanisms including urothelial changes, neural control abnormalities, and pelvic floor dysfunction.1 Genitourinary syndrome of menopause — i.e., the atrophy of the genital and lower urinary tracts resulting from decreased estrogen levels — can also contribute to UI (particularly to UUI).11

History

A guide to taking a history, including questions regarding the overall impact on the patient’s quality of life (e.g., activity, sexual function) and whether they desire treatment, is provided in Table 1. Severity of UI can be indicated by the frequency of incontinent episodes and the number of absorptive pads used per day. Patients with red flags for cauda equina syndrome (e.g., fecal incontinence, urinary retention, saddle anesthesia, and severe lower back pain or sciatica with leg weakness) should be referred immediately to the emergency department (Table 2).19 Patients with red flags for cancer should be urgently referred to the appropriate specialist; red flags include persistent hematuria of 3 or more red blood cells per high power field, pelvic mass, or substantial pain associated with UI.17,20,21

Obstetric history is important. According to a cohort study that included 91 002 women, SUI is 2 to 3 times more common after vaginal birth than with cesarean section due to direct pelvic floor muscle and connective tissue injury and possibly related to nerve injury that affects function of the pelvic floor muscles.8 Risks are increased with macrosomia (> 4000 g), prolonged second stage of labour, forceps delivery, and advanced maternal age.8 In a meta-analysis of 6054 patients, constipation was significantly associated with risk of UI (odds ratio [OR] 2.46, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.79 to 3.38).22

Other risk factors for SUI include age, obesity, and conditions that repeatedly increase intra-abdominal pressure (i.e., repetitive valsalva), parity, hysterectomy and other pelvic surgeries, pelvic organ prolapse, diabetes, family history, smoking, high fluid intake, bladder irritants (e.g., caffeine, alcohol), connective tissue disorders, and race.1 A 2021 clinical review reported that 37% to 54% of females with pelvic organ prolapse had concurrent SUI symptoms.23 The risk of SUI is at least twofold higher in White females than in Black females, but Black females are 25% more likely to report UUI than White females.1,24,25 Culprit medications include diuretics (e.g., furosemide), sedatives, muscle relaxants, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (owing to coughing) and α-blockers (e.g., tamsulosin). People who smoke and formerly smoked have higher risk of SUI than people who do not smoke (OR 2.48 and 2.20, respectively).26

Urge urinary incontinence is linked to advanced age and neurologic conditions,1,13 with the prevalence of UUI increasing from 4% to 9% from age 50 to 80 years.27

Physical examination

Physical examination is best done when the patient has a “comfortably full” bladder.

A cough stress test is indicative of SUI.10,17 Patients cough while supine or standing; leakage with cough has a positive predictive value of 78% to 97%, although a negative test does not exclude SUI.17,24

Examination for pelvic organ prolapse may be performed in the lithotomy position with the patient performing a valsalva while the clinician looks for a bulge at the vaginal opening or beyond. A split speculum exam (using 1 blade of the speculum) can also be helpful to distinguish whether the anterior wall, posterior wall, or vault of the vagina is descending with strain. Anterior vaginal prolapse can be associated with urinary retention.

Signs of genitourinary syndrome of menopause include the loss of labial and vaginal architecture, prominent urethral orifice or prolapse, pale or friable mucosa, and limited elasticity or vaginal moisture.

Diagnostic tests

Postvoid residual should be measured by noninvasive pelvic ultrasound (following natural void rather than catheterization to exclude substantial urinary retention).1 In general, a PVR in the bladder less than 50 mL is considered adequate bladder emptying, and greater than 200 mL is considered inadequate emptying. 1 Urinary tract infection and microscopic hematuria should be ruled out with urinalysis and, when indicated, a urine culture. 1,7,15 If infection is present, the patient should be reassessed after treatment. Advanced diagnostic tests (e.g., cystoscopy, urodynamic testing) or urinary tract imaging are not routinely required.12,13

How is UI treated?

Treatment options include conservative management, pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), pessaries, and medications. Surgical treatment or neuromodulation may be options if these therapies are ineffective.

Conservative management is the first-line treatment for SUI, UUI, and mixed UI.1 This includes behavioural therapies, noninvasive therapies, and optimization of comorbidities ()Appendix 1 and Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.241715/tab-related-content).

Behavioural management involves strategies such as bladder training and PFMT.28 Constipation may exacerbate symptoms and should be addressed.17,22 Weight reduction in those with a body mass index higher than 30 can reduce SUI; in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 338 females with overweight, 52% in the weight-loss intervention group (mean weight loss 8%) achieved greater than or equal to a 70% reduction in SUI episodes at 6 months, versus 33% of control patients (p = 0.009).29 For UUI, 41% of participants in the weight-loss group achieved a 70% or more reduction of UUI episodes at 6 months versus 29% of control patients (p = 0.04).29 Fluid reduction, avoidance of caffeine, and smoking cessation are generally supported by guidelines, although recent systematic reviews suggest they may be ineffective. 7,10,13,15,17,30,31 Restricting fluids 2 to 4 hours before bedtime or after 6 pm can reduce nighttime incontinence.10

Pelvic floor muscle and bladder training

Females with UI usually have decreased pelvic muscle tone.32 Pelvic floor muscle training is the voluntary and repetitive isolated contraction and relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles to increase pelvic floor resting pressure, prevent pelvic floor muscle descent during increases in intrabdominal pressure, strengthen urethral closure, and decrease central nervous system stimulation of the detrusor muscle.28 To increase muscle tone, patients must be correctly contracting the muscles (movement that “lifts and squeezes” around the urethra, vagina, and anus) and be adherent to regular exercise of increasing intensity.33

A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that among females with SUI treated with PFMT, compared with no treatment, 74% reported an SUI cure or improvement versus 11% who did not receive treatment (risk ratio [RR] 6.33, 95% CI 3.88 to 10.33).28 For females with UUI, cure or improvement was reported in 67% versus 29% (RR 2.39, 95% CI 1.64 to 3.47).28

Pelvic floor muscle training guided by a trained health care provider leads to greater improvements in quality of life, muscle strength, and cure rates of UI than unsupervised PFMT, which highlights the importance of correctly performing exercises.34 An RCT comparing supervised versus home-based PFMT in patients with SUI found cure rates of 61.8% and 28.6%, respectively.35 Use of mobile apps has been shown to increase PFMT adherence.36 Stopping midstream urination is not recommended as a regular practice and clinicians should not advise it.17

A 2025 comparative interview-based observational study found that barriers to PFMT include cost, limited availability of trained professionals, medical comorbidities, lack of patient awareness of or confidence in treatment efficacy, anxiety regarding assessment, and discomfort.37

Bladder training for UUI can be initiated under the guidance of a physiotherapist or physician. It aims to reduce a patient’s voiding frequency by 15 to 30 minutes each week through urgency suppression techniques (e.g., repetitive pelvic floor contraction and mental distraction techniques). Timed voiding on a specific schedule may be helpful in patients with cognitive or physical limitations.38,39

Pessaries

Intravaginal continence pessaries are a nonsurgical treatment option for SUI. They provide support to the bladder neck and stabilize the urethra; they can also treat pelvic organ prolapse. In a systematic review of 8 trials with 787 females, 53% of patients using continence pessaries reported substantial symptomatic improvement of SUI at 3 months and 32% at 12 months, likely due to discontinuation.40 Potential adverse effects include vaginal discomfort or ulceration (9% to 24% of patients), vaginal discharge (13% to 28%), odour (13%), and vaginal bleeding (4% to 17%).41 For postmenopausal patients with significant vaginal atrophy, local estrogen may be used before fitting a pessary, to reduce the incidence of discomfort, ulceration, bleeding, or discharge.41–43

Traditional pessaries come in various sizes and shapes (an example is shown in Figure 1). They require fitting by a trained health care provider (i.e., physician, nurse, or pelvic floor physiotherapist). Patients are taught to clean and reinsert their pessaries at least every 3 months. Those who are unable to do so should have an alternate caregiver or health care provider assist them, or the pessary should be removed. Some pessaries need to be removed before intercourse.

Newer intravaginal silicone devices allow patients to self-fit and self-manage continence pessaries as needed for activities such as exercise, and are commercially available without a prescription.44 In 1 RCT assessing an intravaginal device, 66.7% of females achieved at least a 50% reduction in pad weight compared with 22.2% using a placebo vaginal ring (p = 0.01).44 Bladder control tampons45 are nonabsorbent disposable devices that offer structural support, but using them can be expensive as they are disposable. Menstrual tampons should not be used to treat SUI as they absorb moisture in the vagina, which can be detrimental to mucosal integrity.46

Beta-3 agonist medications (e.g., mirabegron)

Activation of β-3 receptors mediates detrusor muscle relaxation at low bladder volume, which allows for urine collection in the bladder. Beta-3 receptor agonists therefore promote detrusor relaxation and inhibit bladder contraction.1

A 2020 systematic review found that mirabegron results in 1.04 fewer incontinence episodes per day, 2.2 fewer urgency episodes, and 2 fewer voids than placebo.48 The efficacy of mirabegron is comparable to anticholinergics, with a number needed to treat to achieve 1 cure of UI ranging from 8 to 12.7 According to current guidance from the American Urological Association, clinicians should consider prescribing mirabegron before antimuscarinics in older adults and those at high risk for anticholinergic and cognitive adverse effects.13

Mirabegron should not be used in patients with uncontrolled hypertension (i.e., systolic blood pressure > 180 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure > 110 mm Hg).49 An analysis of data from 10 studies with 11 261 patients found no increased risk of treatment-emergent hypertension compared with antimuscarinic agents (3.9% compared with 3.2%, respectively).47 There is no established suggested frequency for monitoring of blood pressure.50 Caution is advised in patients with an elevated PVR,13 although urinary retention is rare (< 1%). Limited understanding exists of long-term effects beyond 36 months.48

Adverse effects include headache (4.1%), nasopharyngitis (3.9%), constipation (2.8%), and tachycardia (0.9%).17,47,48 Adherence to mirabegron reduces over time, likely owing to adverse effects, symptom resolution, and lack of efficacy.48

Antimuscarinic medications

Antimuscarinic medications (a subclass of anticholinergics) inhibit detrusor muscle contraction by blocking acetylcholine receptors in smooth muscle and therefore enhance capacity to retain urine.1 Antimuscarinic medications should be used with caution for the treatment of UUI, given the association with cognitive impairment and increased risk of incident dementia with exposure longer than 3 months.51–54 A meta-analysis including 621 548 participants found that antimuscarinic use for 3 to 9 months was associated with increased dementia risk (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.29), and long-term (> 12 mo) had an even higher risk (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.85).55 However, causation has not been established, as incontinence can be an early symptom of dementia.51

Other adverse effects include dry mouth, constipation, and blurred vision, leading to high discontinuation rates; 1 systematic review of 30 observational studies found continued use at 1 year of 12% to 25%.56 Antimuscarinics are contraindicated in patients with untreated narrow angle glaucoma, gastroparesis and severe constipation, or cognitive impairment.51

Local estrogen therapies

In females who are hypoestrogenic with genitourinary syndrome of menopause, local vaginal estrogen leads to improvements in symptoms of urgency and frequency, reduces recurrent urinary tract infections, and may also improve UI.12,57–59 Symptoms should improve by 3 months of use but will return if the medication is discontinued. Any estrogen is contraindicated in females with undiagnosed vaginal bleeding (Table 4). Systemic estrogen is not indicated for genitourinary syndrome of menopause owing to a lack of efficacy compared with local estrogen.60 A 2025 systematic review showed that vaginal estrogen is not associated with an increased risk of breast cancer recurrence, and that estradiol formulations containing 4 μg and 10 μg are associated with negligible serum estradiol levels (< 20 pg/mL).61

Duloxetine

Duloxetine, a selective serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, is approved in Europe but not in Canada or the United States for the treatment of SUI.1,17,64 It is thought to enhance the contractile effect of noradrenaline on urethral smooth muscle.1 In a meta-analysis of 4 placebo-controlled RCTs (n = 1913), duloxetine reduced incontinence episodes compared with placebo (mean difference −13.56%, 95% CI −21.59% to −5.53%) and modestly improved quality of life (standardized mean difference 0.24, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.33, I2 = 0%).65 The number needed to treat was 8 (95% CI 6 to 13), and numbers needed to harm were 7 (95% CI 6 to 8) for adverse events (i.e., nausea) and psychiatric effects (i.e., agitation, anxiety, or insomnia; 95% CI 6 to 9).65

Which patients should be referred to a specialist?

Referral to a urogynecologist or urologist should be made for patients who do not respond to first-line options as outlined above, or who express a desire to pursue second-line treatments. Patients with red flags (Table 2) should be referred as appropriate. Other indications for referral include pelvic organ prolapse beyond the introitus or complex incontinence (e.g., previous pelvic radiation), and a PVR of greater than 200 mL. Patients with microscopic hematuria should be referred to a urologist.14,17,24

Surgical and injectable treatments

The most common surgery performed for SUI is the midurethral sling (or tension-free vaginal tape procedure [TVT]).66 This involves placing a small strip of permanent mesh under the urethra for support66 and is usually performed in outpatient settings. A 2017 systematic review of 34 studies of women with SUI estimated cure rates of as high as 92% at 6 months and 81% at 5 years.67 The nonmesh Burch colposuspension has similar efficacy at 5 years and can be performed with a minimally invasive laparoscopic approach.68 The Burch procedure carries a higher risk of future surgery for pelvic organ prolapse (3.3% v. 1.1% for TVT69), whereas TVT carries risks of mesh exposure or extrusion through vaginal wall or other tissues (1.4% to 2.1%) and bladder perforation (3.6% to 10.1%).24

Urethral bulking involves injecting a substance (e.g., polyacrylamide hydrogel) in the submucosa of the urethra to improve urethral closure and help SUI, which is less invasive but has a lower cure rate than surgery.70

For UUI, intravesical onabotulinumtoxinA may improve symptoms of UI.71,72 A systematic review found that in 59 of 99 patients randomized to onabotulinumtoxinA, there were 3.88 (95% CI −6.15 to −1.62) fewer incontinence episodes per day than with placebo.72 These injections must be repeated under cystoscopy every 3 to 12 months and can cause urinary retention.73

Neuromodulation for UUI

Guideline-recommended third-line treatments for UUI include percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation and sacral neuromodulation. 10 Both of these minimally invasive procedures are thought to modulate the afferent input to the spinal cord, which results in inhibition of detrusor overactivity in patients with UUI.1

What are the novel emerging treatments for UI?

To date, there is not enough evidence to support the use of newer and emerging minimally invasive, energy-based therapies; e.g., CO2 or Erbium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet laser, radiofrequency therapy or high-intensity focused electromagnetic stimulation chair to treat UI.74–77 At present, these are not recommended by any major gynecologic or urologic professional society, including the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada.14,78

Conclusion

Despite effective treatment options, UI remains highly prevalent among adult females and has a substantial negative impact on quality of life. Effective first-line treatments for all types of UI are lifestyle modifications and pelvic floor physiotherapy. Further management options include pharmacotherapy, surgery, and neuromodulation in select patients, depending on the cause and type of UI. Important clinical questions, particularly the efficacy of several emerging treatments, require further research (Box 2).
