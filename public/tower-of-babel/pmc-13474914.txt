Clustering longitudinal multivariate trajectories using an ensemble of principal component trees
Authors: Bastian Pfeifer, Simon Grabner, Andrea Berghold, Markus Loecher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeClustering longitudinal data is challenging, particularly when measurements are high-dimensional, irregularly sampled, or noisy. We aim to provide a flexible and interpretable framework for identifying meaningful temporal subgroups and their key features.MethodsWe introduce TAPIO, an ensemble-based clustering approach with longitudinal extensions, including longTAPIOtrajectories, longTAPIOsample, and longTAPIOMLD. These variants integrate dimension reduction and cluster-specific feature importance, allowing robust clustering of univariate and multivariate trajectories, as well as regularly and irregularly sampled longitudinal data.ResultsSimulation studies demonstrate that TAPIO accurately recovers cluster structure, identifies relevant features, and performs competitively with existing methods. longTAPIOtrajectories excels on regularly sampled data, while longTAPIOMLD outperforms alternatives for irregular measurements. Applications on a clinical cohort reveal patient subgroups with distinct survival patterns driven by key cardiac measures, and analyses of high-dimensional longitudinal proteomics data uncover molecularly distinct clusters with interpretable protein-level importance profiles.ConclusionTAPIO offers a scalable, interpretable framework for longitudinal clustering that accommodates complex multivariate trajectories and high-dimensional data. Its ability to identify both meaningful clusters and their defining features has potential to advance patient stratification, biomarker discovery, and longitudinal data analysis in biomedical research.

Introduction

The increasing dimensionality and complexity of biomedical data pose significant challenges for identifying interpretable and clinically meaningful patterns. Clustering is an essential technique for high-dimensional data analysis because it can reveal novel patterns in complex, noisy datasets [1]. In biomedical applications, clustering biomarkers or clinical variables across patients helps to identify shared molecular or phenotypic profiles, enabling the detection of clinically meaningful subgroups and uncovering sources of patient-level heterogeneity [2–4]. In longitudinal multivariate settings, where multiple biomarkers or clinical variables are measured repeatedly over time, advanced clustering techniques are particularly important. The capability to cluster temporal trajectories, enables the identification of dynamic patterns of disease progression and treatment response over time. These trajectory-based clusters support precision medicine by delineating biologically and clinically meaningful subgroups, facilitating disease-subtype discovery and improving risk stratification [5–7]. However, the number of available methods remain small, and existing methods frequently lack sufficient interpretability to support reliable clinical or biological insight [8] and/or are not well-suited for high-dimensional data analysis.

Here, we present our novel methodology called TAPIO (Tree Affinities with Principal Components) and its extension longTAPIO for clustering multivariate longitudinal trajectories. TAPIO is an ensemble clustering framework developed for high-dimensional data that repeatedly samples small subsets of features, embeds them via Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and aggregates multi-level hierarchical partitions into a stable affinity matrix. This feature-sampling strategy enhances robustness while avoiding the curse of dimensionality, and the loadings of the principal components naturally provide cluster-specific feature importance scores, giving the method strong interpretability. The longTAPIO method extends this idea to longitudinal data by extracting one-dimensional representations of irregular or regularly sampled trajectories and constructing subject-wise distances before ensemble clustering, enabling interpretable patient stratification directly from temporal biomarker profiles. It should be noted that the core TAPIO algorithm has already been shown to perform well on high-dimensional omics data for disease subtype discovery (see [9]). In the presented paper, we focus primarily on extending TAPIO to longitudinal settings and improving the overall interpretability of the resulting cluster solutions.

Related work

A comprehensive review of available methods for longitudinal feature clustering is presented in [5]. The authors review and evaluate model-based [10–12] as well as algorithmic-based [13–15] clustering techniques. Our longTAPIO algorithm falls within the class of algorithmic-based approaches, which offer several advantages, particularly for high-dimensional longitudinal data analyses. They typically do not require assumptions about the functional form of trajectories or random-effect distributions, making them highly flexible for complex patterns. They are also computationally efficient and scale well with a large number of features, repeated measurements, and individuals. When the number of clusters is known a priori, algorithmic methods such as k-means can provide strong clustering performance while maintaining high computational efficiency.

In [14], for instance, the KmL3D framework was introduced, employing a specialized version of k-means to cluster joint multivariate trajectories. The distance metric between individuals is defined as the Minkowski distance between their respective trajectory matrices. In [13], an algorithmic-based hierarchical agglomerative clustering method for multivariate longitudinal data, called clusterMLD, was introduced. Its dissimilarity metric is inspired by the Chow test statistic and employs B-splines to quantify the merging cost between two groups of trajectories. Notably, unlike KmL3D [14], clusterMLD can efficiently handle sparse and irregular repeated measurements.

However, both approaches lack interpretability, as they do not identify the features that characterize each cluster. This limitation is particularly critical in domains such as medicine and constitutes one of the core contributions of our manuscript.

Proposed methods

The proposed TAPIO method consists of three novel developments.

First, instead of performing clustering based on the entire dataset, including the full set of features, we suggest that a certain amount of features are sampled without replacement and to compute the first principal component based on this data subset. Hierarchical clustering is then executed on this embedded single feature to construct a dendrogram, which ultimately is split at multiple levels to derive multiple cluster partitions. These partitions are utilized to generate an affinity matrix. This process is iterated multiple times, resulting in an ensemble of affinity matrices, which are aggregated into a single matrix on which hierarchical clustering is performed.

Second, based on the above described scheme, we evaluate cluster-specific feature relevance by computing the contribution of each feature to the principal components. For each ensemble member, the feature contribution to the principal component is scaled by the pair-wise affinity of the elements of the considered cluster.

Third, we propose longTAPIO which extends TAPIO to longitudinal multivariate data by extracting one-dimensional representations of irregular or regularly sampled trajectories.

More precisely, the contribution of a single feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f \in \{1,\ldots , p\}$$\end{document} in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m^{\text {th}}$$\end{document} ensemble member is defined based on its squared correlation with the first principal component. The feature contribution is computed as9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w^{(m)}_{f} = \frac{\left( \textrm{corr}^{(m)}(f,\text {PCA}_{1})\right) ^2}{\sum _{g \in S_m} \left( \textrm{corr}^{(m)}(g,\text {PCA}_{1})\right) ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_m$$\end{document} denotes the subset of features sampled in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m^{\text {th}}$$\end{document} ensemble member, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^{(m)}_{f} = 0$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f \notin S_m$$\end{document}. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{corr}^{(m)}(f,\text {PCA}_{1})$$\end{document} denotes the correlation between feature f and the first principal component in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m^{\text {th}}$$\end{document} ensemble member. This correlation is computed from the PCA loadings as10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{corr}^{(m)}(f,\text {PCA}_{1}) = \ell ^{(m)}_{f,1} \, \sqrt{\lambda ^{(m)}_{1}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell ^{(m)}_{f,1}$$\end{document} denotes the loading of feature f on the first principal component and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda ^{(m)}_{1}$$\end{document} denotes the corresponding eigenvalue of the covariance matrix, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{\lambda ^{(m)}_{1}}$$\end{document} is the standard deviation of the first principal component.

For a specific cluster C inferred from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{agg}$$\end{document} (see Eq. 3), the cluster-specific feature importance vector11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {I}_{C} = (I_{C,1}, \ldots , I_{C,p})^\top \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is computed by aggregating feature contributions across all ensemble members and all pairwise sample affinities within the cluster. Specifically, the importance of a single feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f \in \{1,\ldots , p\}$$\end{document} for cluster C is defined as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I_{C,f} = \frac{1}{|C|^{2}} \sum \limits _{m=1}^{M} w^{(m)}_{f} \sum \limits _{\begin{array}{c} i,j\in C \end{array}} A^{(m)}_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Consequently, features that contribute strongly to principal components and consistently associate highly affine sample pairs receive larger importance values. The resulting values in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {I}_{C}$$\end{document} are normalized by dividing by the maximum importance value observed across all clusters and features:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{I}_{C,f} = \frac{I_{C,f}}{\max _{C,f} I_{C,f}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the maximum is taken over all clusters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1, \ldots , C_K$$\end{document} and features f = 1, … , p. This normalization ensures that the resulting feature importance values are comparable across clusters. Importantly, the described mechanism also applies to the longitudinal extensions of TAPIO without modification.

Evaluation strategy

Our evaluation strategy comprised four components.

First, we assessed the performance of longTAPIO on simulated univariate trajectories (see next Synthetic longitudinal univariate data section) and compared it to the kml method [15]. It should be noted, however, that TAPIO was primarily developed for high-dimensional multivariate data, leveraging its built-in dimension reduction techniques. In the univariate case, no dimension reduction was required.

In the second analysis, we compared longTAPIO and its variants to the KmL3D method [14], explicitly clustering multivariate trajectories (see Synthetic longitudinal multivariate data section). This evaluation was performed on both regularly and irregularly sampled repeated measures. However, in the case of irregularly repeated measures, KmL3D [15] was not applicable, and we instead compared against the clusterMLD method [13]. In both evaluation scenarios, we used the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [20] to compare the clustering performance of longTAPIO with that of the competing methods.

The ARI Index measures the agreement between two partitions while correcting for agreement expected by chance. Let U denote the true partition (ground-truth) and V the computed partition of n samples. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{rs}$$\end{document} denote the number of samples assigned to cluster r in U and cluster s in V, with marginal cluster sizes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_r = \sum _s n_{rs}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_s = \sum _r n_{rs}$$\end{document}. For readability, we define the following quantities: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A = \sum _{r,s} \left( {\begin{array}{c}n_{rs}\\ 2\end{array}}\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = \sum _r \left( {\begin{array}{c}a_r\\ 2\end{array}}\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C = \sum _s \left( {\begin{array}{c}b_s\\ 2\end{array}}\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D = \left( {\begin{array}{c}n\\ 2\end{array}}\right)$$\end{document}. The ARI Index is then defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{ARI} = \frac{ A - \frac{BC}{D} }{ \frac{1}{2}(B + C) - \frac{BC}{D} }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The ARI takes values in the interval [-1,1], where 1 indicates perfect agreement between the computed clustering solution and the ground-truth partitions. Values close to 0 indicate random agreement, and negative values indicate agreement less than expected by chance.

Third, we evaluated TAPIO’s and longTAPIO’s ability to compute reliable feature importance attributes (see Cluster-specific feature importance section). Finally, we applied our algorithm to real-world data to demonstrate its practical utility (see Applications section).

Unless otherwise stated, we set the number of randomly selected features to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = \lfloor \sqrt{p} \rfloor$$\end{document}, following a commonly used heuristic in ensemble learning and random forest models. The number of dendrogram cut levels was set to d = K, where K denotes the true number of clusters in the simulated data. In case no ground-truth was available the number of clusters was determined by the silhouette coefficient [21]. The number of ensemble iterations was fixed to M = 500 to ensure stability of the aggregated affinity matrix while keeping computational costs manageable.

Synthetic longitudinal univariate data

Following the simulation set-up in [14], we define three clusters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_3$$\end{document}, each containing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_i = 50$$\end{document} individuals. The mean trajectories of the clusters over time t are\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mu _1(t) = 0, \quad \mu _2(t) = -t, \quad \mu _3(t) = t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The function generateArtificialLongData() from the kml package includes a personal variation term that introduces between-individual variability within clusters. For an individual j in cluster i, a cluster-specific random deviation is drawn:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \eta _{ij} \sim \mathcal {N}(0, \tau ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where τ controls the magnitude of this individual-level variability. This deviation shifts the entire trajectory of individual j upward or downward while preserving the shape of the cluster trajectory. The resulting underlying trajectory for individual j becomes\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\mu }_{ij}(t) = \mu _i(t) + \eta _{ij}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The residual (within-individual) variation is modeled as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon _{ij}(t) \sim \mathcal {N}(0, \sigma ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Thus, the observed measurement is the sum of the cluster mean, the personal deviation, and the residual noise:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y_{ij}(t) = \mu _i(t) + \eta _{ij} + \varepsilon _{ij}(t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Explicitly, this yields\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} &Y_{1j}(t) = 0 + \eta _{1j} + \varepsilon _{1j}(t), \quad \\&Y_{2j}(t) = -t + \eta _{2j} + \varepsilon _{2j}(t), \quad \\&Y_{3j}(t) = t + \eta _{3j} + \varepsilon _{3j}(t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Thus, the dataset consists of three clusters of 50 individuals each, with cluster-specific linear trajectories, individual-level random deviations, and Gaussian residual noise.

Applications

Clinical cohort and longitudinal measurements

We evaluated our method on the heart.valve dataset, a benchmark commonly used in the R ecosystem to demonstrate longitudinal and survival joint-modeling methods. It is included in several packages dedicated to mixed-effects and joint modeling frameworks, such as joineR [22] and joineRML[6], where it serves as an example for fitting multivariate longitudinal models, joint models, and survival analyses. The dataset originates from a prospective observational cohort of patients undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement at the Royal Brompton Hospital, London, between 1991 and 2001 [23]. The study collected repeated postoperative measurements of cardiac structure and function alongside key baseline clinical information to investigate recovery trajectories after valve surgery.

For each patient, the dataset includes a unique identifier (NUM) and multiple follow-up examinations recorded at irregular time intervals (TIME), together with the maximum postoperative follow-up duration in years (FUYRS) and vital status at the end of follow-up (status; 1 = death, 0 = censored). Longitudinal outcomes considered in our analysis were left-ventricular mass index (LVMI) and ejection fraction (EF), representing structural and functional cardiac remodeling, respectively. Additional baseline covariates included body surface area (BSA) and preoperative serum creatinine (CREAT), the latter serving as a marker of renal function. The dataset is unbalanced, as the number and timing of follow-up measurements vary across individuals, making it well-suited for multivariate longitudinal clustering techniques that accommodate irregular measurement schedules and heterogeneous follow-up profiles.

Longitudinal high-dimensional omics data

To showcase the capability of longTAPIOtrajectories to handle high-dimensional data we retrieved data analyzed in [24]. The authors provide data of repeated measurements from the same human participants across multiple molecular layers, including bulk plasma proteomics, complete blood counts (CBC), single-cell transcriptomics (scRNA-seq), and single-cell chromatin accessibility (scATAC-seq). Longitudinal sampling in these data allows tracking within-individual temporal dynamics of proteins, gene expression, and chromatin states, capturing how molecular systems change over time due to biological processes, environmental exposures, or disease progression. By measuring multiple molecular modalities repeatedly, the study enables identification of coordinated temporal patterns, stable versus variable features, and dynamic subgroups that would remain hidden in cross-sectional analyses. Here, we put our focus on the clustering of the longitudinal bulk proteomics data to detect cluster-specific key driver genes. The data set consists of 1,156 proteins which were regularly measured from six donors over ten time-points.

Clustering of the data described in Clinical cohort and longitudinal measurements section was conducted using the longTAPIOMLD for irregular repeated measurements. We identified two distinct clusters of patients utilizing the silhouette coefficient [21] on the aggregated affinity matrix (see Eq. 3). Summary and outlook

In this work we introduced TAPIO and its longitudinal extensions, demonstrating their ability to cluster complex multivariate trajectories while providing interpretable, cluster-specific feature importance. Simulation studies showed that the method reliably identifies relevant features and performs competitively across univariate and multivariate settings. In particular, longTAPIOtrajectories performed on par with existing trajectory-based clustering methods, while longTAPIOMLD showed clear advantages for irregularly sampled data. Surprisingly, longTAPIOsample was quite competitive, despite not accounting for any temporal ordering of the repeated measurements. Applications to a clinical cohort and high-dimensional proteomics data further illustrated the practical utility of our approach. In the clinical dataset, longTAPIOMLD identified patient subgroups with distinct survival patterns driven mainly by ejection fraction. In the proteomics analysis, longTAPIOtrajectories uncovered clusters with distinct molecular signatures and provided interpretable protein-level importance.

Overall, TAPIO offers a flexible and transparent framework for longitudinal clustering. Its enhanced interpretability makes it a promising tool for biomedical research, with potential to support improved patient stratification and biomarker discovery.

Promising future directions of research include refinements of both the dimensionality reduction step and the selection of clustering hyperparameters within the proposed framework. For instance, projecting onto the first principal component is appealing due to its enhanced interpretability; however, in very noisy datasets, this component may capture variance that is not necessarily informative for clustering. As a potential extension of the proposed framework, we therefore investigate a stochastic variant of the dimensionality reduction step. Instead of deterministically selecting the first principal component, we consider sampling a principal component according to its explained variance. This probabilistic selection mechanism introduces additional variability into the ensemble while maintaining a preference for components explaining larger proportions of variance, potentially improving robustness and clustering stability. First results (see Fig. 8) indicate that this approach can improve performance while being less sensitive to hyperparameter choices, such as the number of selected features and the number of dendrogram cuts. Although preliminary results indicate improved performance, the interpretability of this stochastic principal component selection remains an open question, as sampling a component introduces an additional source of randomness into the ensemble.

Furthermore, future work could explore alternatives to the silhouette coefficient not only for determining the optimal number of clusters, but also for guiding the selection of additional hyperparameters such as the number of sampled features k or the dendrogram cut depth d. While such criteria are in principle applicable, their direct use in the proposed ensemble setting is non-trivial due to the stochastic nature of the algorithm. Consensus-based combinations of multiple evaluation metrics or perturbation-based approaches for identifying noise-robust partitions may therefore be promising directions.
