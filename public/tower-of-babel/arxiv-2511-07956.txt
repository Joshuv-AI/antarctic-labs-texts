Detecting Parity-Violating Gravitational Wave Backgrounds with Pulsar Polarization Arrays Preprint: KUNS-3079
Qiuyue Liang, Kimihiro Nomura, Hidetoshi Omiya

Abstract
Pulsar timing arrays probe isotropic stochastic gravitational wave (GW) backgrounds in the nanohertz band but are insensitive to its parity-violating component. Motivated by recent progress in pulsar polarization arrays, we study the response of pulsar polarimetry to GWs and evaluate its potential to detect circular polarization in isotropic stochastic GW backgrounds, which characterizes parity violation. Based on geometric optics, we derive the rotation of the polarization of electromagnetic waves induced by propagation through a GW background. We show that the cross-correlation between pulsar timing and polarimetry signals isolates the circular polarization component from the GW intensity, sharing the same Hellings-Downs angular pattern. With future facilities such as the SKA, timing-polarimetry correlations could reach sensitivities to the circular polarization of GWs comparable to those of the current astrometric methods.

II
Redshift and polarization rotation of electromagnetic waves by plane GWs

II.1
Setup of the problem

In this section, we derive the redshift and the polarization rotation of electromagnetic waves induced by plane GWs.
We first present the setup of the problem.
We model the GW as a perturbation,
h_{ij}
, in Minkowski spacetime,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-dt^{2}+[\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}(t,\bm{x})]dx^{i}dx^{j}~,

(1)

and adopt the transverse-traceless (TT) gauge,
h^{i}_{~i}=\partial_{i}h^{i}_{~j}=0
.
Throughout this work, we restrict our analysis to linear order in
h_{ij}
.
1
1

1

We will focus only on linear perturbations throughout the paper; therefore, the summation convention between upper and lower spatial indices will not be specified.

We consider the regime where the wavelength of the electromagnetic waves is much shorter than that of the GW, as is relevant for pulsar observations.
In this limit, geometric optics applies to the electromagnetic waves: the photon wave vector
k^{\mu}
obeys a null geodesic equation, and the polarization vector
\epsilon^{\mu}
is perpendicular to the wave vector and is parallel transported along the light ray (see Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle k^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}k^{\nu}=0~,\qquad g_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}=0~,

(2)

\displaystyle k^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\epsilon^{\nu}=0~,\qquad g_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}\epsilon^{\nu}=0~,

(3)

where
\nabla_{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative associated with the metric (
1
).
The polarization vector
\epsilon^{\mu}
is complex in general and is defined with the normalization condition,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\epsilon^{\mu}\epsilon^{\nu*}=1\,,

(4)

where the asterisk (*) denotes the complex conjugate.
Note that there is a gauge freedom
\epsilon^{\mu}\to\epsilon^{\mu}+\alpha k^{\mu}
with constant
\alpha
.

Let us express the solutions for
k^{\mu}
and
\epsilon^{\mu}
as

\displaystyle k^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bar{k}^{\mu}+\delta k^{\mu}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bar{\epsilon}^{\mu}+\delta\epsilon^{\mu}\,,

(6)

where
\bar{k}^{\mu}
and
\bar{\epsilon}^{\mu}
denote the solutions in Minkowski spacetime, and
\delta k^{\mu}
and
\delta\epsilon^{\mu}
represent the perturbations induced by the GW.
In this section, we will find
\delta k^{\mu}
and
\delta\epsilon^{\mu}
to linear order in
h_{ij}
.

We study a setup in which a pulsar emits electromagnetic waves that are observed on Earth.
For simplicity, we treat both the observer and the pulsar as moving along timelike geodesics.
In the TT gauge, the worldlines of fixed spatial coordinates satisfy the geodesic equation.
Therefore, even in the presence of the GW, we can assume that the observer (Earth) is located at the spatial origin,
\bm{x}_{E}=\bm{0}
, and the pulsar remains at a fixed spatial position,
\bm{x}_{P}=L\,\hat{\bm{n}}
, where
L
is the distance between the Earth and the pulsar, and
\hat{\bm{n}}
is the unit vector pointing from the Earth to the pulsar, satisfying
\delta_{ij}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}=1
.
In this setup, the four-velocities of the observer (Earth)
u_{E}^{\mu}
and the pulsar
u_{P}^{\mu}
are both given by
u_{E}^{\mu}=u_{P}^{\mu}=(1,\bm{0})
.

In the unperturbed Minkowski spacetime, the photon propagates in the direction
-\hat{\bm{n}}
, with a constant null wave vector,

\displaystyle\bar{k}^{\mu}=\omega_{P}(1,-\hat{\bm{n}})~,

(7)

where
\omega_{P}
denotes the angular frequency of the emitted light.
The unperturbed trajectory of the photon,
\bar{x}^{\mu}
, is obtained by integrating
d\bar{x}^{\mu}/d\lambda=\bar{k}^{\mu}
with respect to the affine parameter
\lambda
, yielding

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(t_{E}+\omega_{P}(\lambda-\lambda_{E}),-\omega_{P}(\lambda-\lambda_{E})\hat{\bm{n}})\,.

(8)

Here, the light ray departs from the pulsar at
\lambda=\lambda_{P}=0
and reaches the Earth at
\lambda=\lambda_{E}\equiv L/\omega_{P}
.
2
2

2

Our convention of the affine parameter differs from that of Book and Flanagan
16
, in which
\lambda=0
is set at Earth and
\lambda=-L/\omega_{P}
at the pulsar.

We take the photons to be observed on Earth at
t=t_{E}
, so that the emission time at the pulsar is
t_{P}=t_{E}-L
.

II.2
Perturbed light ray and redshift

Let us first solve the geodesic equation (
2
) to linear order in
h_{ij}
.
The linearized geodesic equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta k^{\mu}}{d\lambda}=-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{~\nu\rho}\,\bar{k}^{\nu}\bar{k}^{\rho},

(9)

where
{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{~\nu\rho}
denotes the Christoffel symbol.
For the metric (
1
) including the GW, the components of Eq. (
9
) take the following form,

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta k^{0}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}\,\partial_{0}{h}_{ij}\,\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta k^{i}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=\omega_{P}^{2}\,\partial_{0}{h}_{ij}\,\hat{n}^{j}-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}(\partial_{k}h_{ij}+\partial_{j}h_{ik}-\partial_{i}h_{jk})\,\hat{n}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\,.

(11)

Integrating these equations yields

\displaystyle\delta k^{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}\,I_{ij}(\lambda)\,\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}+C^{0}~,

(12)

\displaystyle\delta k^{i}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\omega_{P}\,\Delta h_{ij}(\lambda)\,\hat{n}^{j}+\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}J_{jki}(\lambda)\,\hat{n}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}+C^{i}~,

(13)

with integration constants
C^{0}
and
C^{i}
, and

\displaystyle\Delta h_{ij}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\equiv h_{ij}(\lambda)-h_{ij}(0)~,

(14)

\displaystyle I_{ij}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\partial_{0}h_{ij}(\lambda^{\prime})~,

(15)

\displaystyle J_{jki}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\partial_{i}h_{jk}(\lambda^{\prime})~.

(16)

Here,
h_{ij}(\lambda)\equiv h_{ij}(\bar{x}^{\mu}(\lambda))
.
The integrals can be evaluated along the unperturbed trajectory
\bar{x}^{\mu}(\lambda)
, since we consider only to linear order in
h_{ij}
.
Moreover, in Eq. (
13
), we used the relation
d/d\lambda=\omega_{P}(\partial_{0}-\hat{n}^{i}\partial_{i})
, which holds along the unperturbed trajectory.
The integration constants
C^{0}
and
C^{i}
will be determined later.

Integrating
dx^{\mu}/d\lambda=k^{\mu}
with
k^{\mu}=\bar{k}^{\mu}+\delta k^{\mu}
, we obtain the perturbed trajectory,
x^{\mu}(\lambda)
, to linear order in
h_{ij}
as

\displaystyle x^{\mu}(\lambda)=\bar{x}^{\mu}(\lambda)+\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\delta k^{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime})+X^{\mu}~,

(17)

where
X^{\mu}
are integration constants.

The eight integration constants
C^{0},C^{i}
and
X^{\mu}
are determined by the following four conditions
16
: 1) the light ray intersects the worldline of the pulsar (
\bm{x}_{P}=L\,\hat{\bm{n}}
) at
\lambda_{P}=0
; 2) the emitted angular frequency in the pulsar’s local frame is
\omega_{P}
;
3) the light ray reaches the Earth at
x_{E}^{\mu}=(t_{E},\bm{0})
; and 4) the wave vector
k^{\mu}
is null. These conditions lead to
X^{i}=0
,
C^{0}=0
, and

\displaystyle C^{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{P}}{\lambda_{E}}\,\hat{n}^{j}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\Delta h_{ij}(\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\omega_{P}}{2\lambda_{E}}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,[h_{jk}(\lambda^{\prime})+\omega_{P}I_{jk}(\lambda^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2\lambda_{E}}\hat{n}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,J_{jki}(\lambda^{\prime})~,

(18)

\displaystyle X^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,h_{ij}(\lambda^{\prime})~.

(19)

Note that we define the origin of the affine parameter,
\lambda=0
, to correspond to the emission at the pulsar even in the presence of the GW. In this convention, the affine parameter value at the Earth is shifted from
\lambda_{E}=L/\omega_{P}
to
\tilde{\lambda}_{E}
due to the GW, which is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{E}=\lambda_{E}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,[h_{ij}(\lambda^{\prime})+\omega_{P}I_{ij}(\lambda^{\prime})]~.

(20)

Evaluating Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) at
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}_{E}
, we obtain the wave vectors, Eq.(
5
), at the pulsar (
P
) and at the Earth (
E
), to linear order in
h_{ij}
, as

\displaystyle k^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle=\bar{k}^{\mu}+(0,C^{i})~,

(21)

\displaystyle k^{\mu}(E)

\displaystyle=\bar{k}^{\mu}+\bigg(-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}I_{ij}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}~,

(22)

\displaystyle\omega_{P}\,\Delta h_{ij}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{j}+\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}J_{jki}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}+C^{i}\bigg)\ ,

(23)

respectively.

The angular frequency of the photons observed on Earth is given by
\omega_{E}=-g_{\mu\nu}(E)\,u_{E}^{\mu}k^{\nu}(E)
, where
g_{\mu\nu}(E)
denotes the metric evaluated at the observation point, and
u_{E}^{\mu}=(1,\bm{0})
.
To linear order in
h_{ij}
, it reads

\displaystyle\omega_{E}=\omega_{P}-\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{2}I_{ij}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}~.

(24)

The fractional frequency shift, i.e., the redshift
z
, is then

\displaystyle z\equiv\frac{\omega_{P}-\omega_{E}}{\omega_{P}}=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda\,\partial_{0}h_{ij}(\lambda)\,,

(25)

which reproduces the well-known result

.
Changing the integration variable from

to

, we obtain

\displaystyle z(t_{E})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\int_{t_{E}-L}^{t_{E}}dt\,\partial_{0}h_{ij}(t,\bm{x})\Big|_{\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}(t)}\,,

(26)

where
t_{E}
is the observation time.
In the integrand, the unperturbed trajectory
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\equiv(t_{E}-t)\hat{\bm{n}}
is substituted after evaluating the partial derivative with respect to
t
.
This redshift is the observable in PTAs as deviations in the pulse periods.

II.3
Polarization rotation

We now turn to the evolution of the polarization vector
\epsilon^{\mu}
along the light ray.
In the unperturbed Minkowski spacetime, the transport equation is simply given by
d\bar{\epsilon}^{\mu}/d\lambda=0
, whose solution is
\bar{\epsilon}^{\mu}=\text{const}
.
Exploiting the gauge freedom, we can further choose

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}^{0}=0\,,\qquad\bar{\epsilon}^{i}=\text{const}~.

(27)

Moreover, by assuming linearly polarized light (we will show later that this is justified), we regard
\bar{\epsilon}^{i}
as real.
The vector
\bar{\epsilon}^{i}
satisfies the normalization condition in flat space,

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}=1~.

(28)

Let us study the perturbation
\delta\epsilon^{\mu}
induced by the GW. To linear order in
h_{ij}
, the parallel transport equation (
3
) reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta\epsilon^{\mu}}{d\lambda}=-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{~\nu\rho}\,\bar{k}^{\nu}\bar{\epsilon}^{\rho}~.

(29)

Using the metric (
1
), the components of this equation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta\epsilon^{0}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\,\partial_{0}h_{ij}\,\hat{n}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\delta\epsilon^{i}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\,\partial_{0}h_{ij}\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}(\partial_{k}h_{ij}+\partial_{j}h_{ik}-\partial_{i}h_{jk})\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}\,.

(31)

Integrating these equations along the light ray gives

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon^{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}I_{ij}(\lambda)\,\hat{n}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+D^{0}~,

(32)

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon^{i}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta h_{ij}(\lambda)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}J_{j[ik]}(\lambda)\,\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}+D^{i}~,

(33)

where
D^{0}
and
D^{i}
are integration constants determined later, and the brackets denote anti-symmetrization on the last two indices,
J_{j[ik]}=J_{jik}-J_{jki}
.

To define the polarization angle at the pulsar and at the Earth, it is necessary to introduce a reference frame at each location.
First, the reference frame at the pulsar can be constructed as follows.
At the pulsar, we introduce a space-like reference vector
p_{P}^{\mu}
.
We require that
p_{P}^{\mu}
is normalized as
g_{\mu\nu}p_{P}^{\mu}p_{P}^{\nu}=1
, orthogonal to the pulsar’s four-velocity,
g_{\mu\nu}u_{P}^{\mu}p_{P}^{\nu}=0
, and parallel transported along the worldline of the pulsar,
u_{P}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}p_{P}^{\nu}=0
.
Let us write
p_{P}^{\mu}=\bar{p}^{\mu}+\delta p_{P}^{\mu}
, where
\bar{p}^{\mu}
denotes the reference vector in Minkowski spacetime and
\delta p_{P}^{\mu}
is the perturbation due to the GW.
Obviously, the unperturbed vector
\bar{p}^{\mu}
is constant.
We can choose
\bar{p}^{\mu}
to be orthogonal to both the pulsar’s four-velocity and the light ray in Minkowski spacetime.
Therefore,
\bar{p}^{\mu}
is expressed as
\bar{p}^{\mu}=(0,\bar{\bm{p}})
with

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\bar{p}^{i}\bar{p}^{j}=1~,\qquad\delta_{ij}\hat{n}^{i}\bar{p}^{j}=0~.

(34)

Solving the parallel transport equation
u_{P}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}p_{P}^{\nu}=0
with
u_{P}^{\mu}=(1,\bm{0})
, we obtain, to linear order in
h_{ij}
, the reference vector at the pulsar as

\displaystyle p_{P}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,~\bar{p}^{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}h_{ij}(P)\,\bar{p}^{j}\bigg)~,

(35)

where
h_{ij}(P)
denotes the metric perturbation at the pulsar’s position, and integration constants have been omitted since they are irrelevant to the polarization rotation.

Using the reference vector
p_{P}^{\mu}
, we can introduce a tetrad associated with the pulsar by the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization as follows
35
.
We identify the four-velocity as the timelike basis vector;
e_{(t)}^{\mu}(P)=u_{P}^{\mu}
.
Using the photon’s wave vector at the pulsar,
k^{\mu}(P)
, a space-like basis vector orthogonal to
e_{(t)}^{\mu}(P)
is given by

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{(k)}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{\mu}(P)}{\omega_{P}}-u_{P}^{\mu}~.

(36)

Notice that
\omega_{P}=-u_{P}^{\mu}k_{\mu}(P)
.
The other two orthonormal basis vectors can be obtained using
p_{P}^{\mu}
as

\displaystyle e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{P}^{\mu}-(p_{P}^{\nu}k_{\nu}(P)/\omega_{P})\,e_{(k)}^{\mu}(P)}{[1-(p_{P}^{\lambda}k_{\lambda}(P)/\omega_{P})^{2}]^{1/2}}~,

(37)

\displaystyle e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{\mu}}_{\nu\rho\sigma}u_{P}^{\nu}\,e_{(k)}^{\rho}(P)\,e_{(\parallel)}^{\sigma}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\varepsilon^{\mu}}_{\nu\rho\sigma}u_{P}^{\nu}k^{\rho}(P)\,p_{P}^{\sigma}}{[\omega_{P}^{2}-(p_{P}^{\lambda}k_{\lambda}(P))^{2}]^{1/2}}~.

(38)

Here,
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
is the antisymmetric tensor normalized as
\varepsilon_{0123}=+\sqrt{|\det[g_{\mu\nu}]|}
.
These basis vectors satisfy
g_{\mu\nu}(P)\,e_{(\alpha)}^{\mu}(P)\,e_{(\beta)}^{\nu}(P)=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
with
(\alpha,\beta)=(t,k,\parallel,\perp)
, where
\eta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Minkowski metric.
The basis vectors are expanded to linear order in
h_{ij}
as

\displaystyle e_{(k)}^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,-\hat{n}^{i}+\frac{\delta k^{i}(P)}{\omega_{P}}\bigg)~,

(39)

\displaystyle e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,\bar{p}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(P)\,\bar{p}^{j}

\displaystyle\qquad+\hat{n}^{i}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(P)\,\bar{p}_{j}\,}{\omega_{P}}-\frac{1}{2}h_{jk}(P)\,\bar{p}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\bigg]\bigg)~,

(40)

\displaystyle e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(P)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\hat{n}^{j}\bar{p}^{k}-h_{ij}(P)\,\bar{\varepsilon}_{jkl}\,\hat{n}^{k}\bar{p}^{l}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(P)\,\bar{p}^{k}}{\omega_{P}}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{n}^{j}h_{kl}(P)\,\bar{p}^{l}\bigg]\bigg)~,

(41)

where
\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}
is the three-dimensional antisymmetric tensor in flat space normalized as
\bar{\varepsilon}_{123}=+1
.
Note that
e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}
reduces to
\bar{p}^{\mu}=(0,\bar{\bm{p}})
in the absence of the GW.

Similarly, at the Earth, we introduce a spatial reference vector
p_{E}^{\mu}
that satisfies
g_{\mu\nu}p_{E}^{\mu}p_{E}^{\nu}=1
,
g_{\mu\nu}u_{E}^{\mu}p_{E}^{\nu}=0
, and
u_{E}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}p_{E}^{\nu}=0
, where

is the four-velocity of the observer (Earth).
We write
p_{E}^{\mu}=\bar{p}^{\mu}+\delta p_{E}^{\mu}
, where
\bar{p}^{\mu}
is identical to that in Eq. (

\delta p_{E}^{\mu}
is derived from the parallel transport equation.
To linear order in
h_{ij}
, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{E}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,\bar{p}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(E)\,\bar{p}^{j}\bigg)~,

(42)

where
h_{ij}(E)
denotes the metric perturbation at the Earth’s position, and, again, we have omitted the integration constants that are irrelevant to the polarization rotation.
We can construct a tetrad associated with the Earth as
e_{(t)}^{\mu}(E)=u_{E}^{\mu}
, and

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{(k)}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{\mu}(E)}{\omega_{E}}-u_{E}^{\mu}~,

(43)

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{(\parallel)}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{E}^{\mu}-(p_{E}^{\nu}k_{\nu}(E)/\omega_{E})\,e^{\mu}_{(k)}(E)}{[1-(p_{E}^{\lambda}k_{\lambda}(E)/\omega_{E})^{2}]^{1/2}}~,

(44)

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{(\perp)}(E)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{\mu}}_{\nu\rho\sigma}u_{E}^{\nu}\,e^{\rho}_{(k)}(E)\,e^{\sigma}_{(\parallel)}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\varepsilon^{\mu}}_{\nu\rho\sigma}u_{E}^{\nu}\,k^{\rho}(E)\,p_{E}^{\sigma}}{[\omega_{E}^{2}-(p_{E}^{\lambda}k_{\lambda}(E))^{2}]^{1/2}}~,

(45)

where
k^{\mu}(E)
is the photon’s wave vector at the Earth (
23
), and
\omega_{E}=-u_{E}^{\mu}k_{\mu}(E)
is the observed angular frequency (
24
).
Expanding to linear order in
h_{ij}
yields

\displaystyle e_{(k)}^{\mu}(E)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,-\frac{\omega_{P}}{\omega_{E}}\hat{n}^{i}+\frac{\delta k^{i}(E)}{\omega_{P}}\bigg)~,

(46)

\displaystyle e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(E)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,\bar{p}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(E)\,\bar{p}^{j}

\displaystyle\qquad+\hat{n}^{i}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(E)\,\bar{p}_{j}}{\omega_{P}}-\frac{1}{2}h_{jk}(E)\,\bar{p}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\bigg]\bigg)~,

(47)

\displaystyle e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(E)

\displaystyle=\bigg(0,\frac{\omega_{P}}{\omega_{E}}\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\hat{n}^{j}\bar{p}^{k}-h_{ij}(E)\,\bar{\varepsilon}_{jkl}\,\hat{n}^{k}\bar{p}^{l}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(E)\,\bar{p}^{k}}{\omega_{P}}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{n}^{j}h_{kl}(E)\,\bar{p}^{l}\bigg]\bigg)~.

(48)

By construction, the basis vectors
e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(P)
and
e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(P)
are orthogonal to the photon’s wave vector at the pulsar.
Accordingly, the polarization vector at the pulsar,
\epsilon^{\mu}(P)
, can be expressed as a linear combination of these two basis vectors.
The same property also holds at the Earth.

In what follows, we will assume that the pulsar emits linearly polarized light whose polarization is parallel to the basis vector
e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(P)
:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu}(P)=e^{\mu}_{(\parallel)}(P)~.

(49)

In general, the initial polarization may have a nonzero angle with respect to
e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(P)
, and circular polarization may also be present. However, our results will show that the initial polarization angle is irrelevant to the polarization rotation, and circular polarization is not affected by the GWB.
For the unperturbed component, Eq. (
49
) indicates that

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}^{i}=\bar{p}^{i}~.

(50)

Meanwhile, by substituting Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) evaluated at the pulsar (
\lambda=0
) and Eq. (
40
) into Eq. (
49
), the integration constants
D^{0}
and
D^{i}
are determined as

\displaystyle D^{0}

\displaystyle=0~,

(51)

\displaystyle D^{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(P)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\hat{n}^{i}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(P)\,\bar{\epsilon}_{j}\,}{\omega_{P}}-\frac{1}{2}h_{jk}(P)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\bigg]~.

(52)

Using these results, the polarization vector at the Earth can be obtained as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{0}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}I_{ij}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}~,

(53)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{i}(E)

\displaystyle=\bar{\epsilon}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(E)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}J_{j[ik]}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{n}^{i}\bigg[\frac{\delta k^{j}(P)\,\bar{\epsilon}_{j}\,}{\omega_{P}}-\frac{1}{2}h_{jk}(P)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\bigg]~.

(54)

One can directly verify that this

is indeed orthogonal to

given in Eq. (

Moreover, we see that when the unperturbed polarization vector
\bar{\epsilon}^{i}
is real, the perturbed polarization vector also remains real. Consequently, no circular polarization of the photons is generated.

We now relate the above discussion to the rotation of the linear polarization angle in pulsar array systems. In geometric-optics limit, the photon field
A^{\mu}
is expressed as
A^{\mu}=a\,\epsilon^{\mu}\,e^{i\theta}
with the scalar amplitude
a
and the phase
\theta
(see Appendix

The wave vector is then defined as the gradient of the phase,
k^{\mu}\equiv\nabla^{\mu}\theta
.
At leading order in geometric-optics,
|\nabla^{\mu}a|\ll|\nabla^{\mu}\theta|
, the field strength tensor
F^{\mu\nu}\equiv\nabla^{\mu}A^{\nu}-\nabla^{\nu}A^{\mu}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle F^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=i\,a\,(k^{\mu}\epsilon^{\nu}-k^{\nu}\epsilon^{\mu})\,e^{i\theta}\,.

(55)

Then, the electric field vector in the observer’s inertial frame,
E^{\mu}
, is defined using the observer’s four-velocity

as

\displaystyle E^{\mu}\equiv u_{E}^{\nu}\,{F^{\mu}}_{\nu}(E)~,

(56)

where
F^{\mu\nu}(E)
is the field strength tensor evaluated at the observation point (Earth).
From

and Eq. (

E^{0}=0
and, to linear order in

,

\displaystyle E^{j}=i\,\omega_{P}\,a\,e^{i\theta}\bigg(\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\frac{\delta k^{0}(E)}{\omega_{P}}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\delta\epsilon^{j}(E)+\hat{n}^{j}\,\delta\epsilon^{0}(E)\bigg)~.

(57)

The electric field vector measured by the observer lies in the two-dimensional plane spanned by
e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(E)
and
e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(E)
.
Thus, we can write

\displaystyle E^{\mu}=E_{(\parallel)}\,e_{(\parallel)}^{\mu}(E)+E_{(\perp)}\,e_{(\perp)}^{\mu}(E)~,

(58)

where

\displaystyle E_{(\parallel)}

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}(E)\,E^{\mu}\,e_{(\parallel)}^{\nu}(E)~,

(59)

\displaystyle E_{(\perp)}

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}(E)\,E^{\mu}\,e_{(\perp)}^{\nu}(E)~.

(60)

Using Eqs. (
47
), (
48
) and (
57
), and noting that
\delta_{ij}\hat{n}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}=0

\displaystyle E_{(\parallel)}

\displaystyle=i\,\omega_{P}\,a\,e^{i\theta}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}(E)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\bar{\epsilon}^{j}+\frac{\delta k^{0}(E)}{\omega_{P}}+\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\,\delta\epsilon_{i}(E)\bigg)

\displaystyle=i\,\omega_{P}\,a\,e^{i\theta}\bigg(1+\frac{\delta k^{0}(E)}{\omega_{P}}\bigg)~,

(61)

\displaystyle E_{(\perp)}

\displaystyle=i\,\omega_{P}\,a\,e^{i\theta}\bigg(\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\,\delta\epsilon^{i}(E)\,\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}h_{kl}(E)\,\bar{\epsilon}^{l}\bigg)

\displaystyle=i\,\omega_{P}\,a\,e^{i\theta}\bigg(\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}J_{j[ik]}(\lambda_{E})\,\hat{m}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}\bigg)~.

(62)

In the second line of each equation, we have used Eq. (
54
). We have also introduced a unit vector in three-dimensional Euclidean space,
\hat{m}^{i}
, which is orthogonal to both
\hat{n}^{i}
and
\bar{\epsilon}^{i}
:

\displaystyle\hat{m}^{i}\equiv\bar{\varepsilon}_{ijk}\hat{n}^{j}\bar{\epsilon}^{k}\,.

(63)

In terms of
E_{(\parallel)}
and
E_{(\perp)}
, the photon’s polarization angle
\chi
measured from the
e_{(\parallel)}^{i}(E)
-axis is given by (see, e.g., Section 2.4 of Ref.
72
)

\displaystyle\tan 2\chi=\frac{2\,\text{Re}\big[E_{(\parallel)}E_{(\perp)}^{*}\big]}{|E_{(\parallel)}|^{2}-|E_{(\perp)}|^{2}}\,,

(64)

where
\text{Re}[\cdots]
stands for the real part.
3
3

3

The definition of the polarization angle
\chi
in Eq. (

) is general and applies also to elliptically polarized light.
For linearly polarized light, Eq. (

) reduces to
\tan\chi=|E_{(\perp)}|/|E_{(\parallel)}|
.

Substituting Eqs. (

) and (
62
), and assuming that

is small, we obtain the rotation of polarization angle induced by the GW as

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\hat{m}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda\,\partial_{[i}h_{j]k}(\lambda)~.

(65)

As in the case of the redshift (

\displaystyle\chi(t_{E})=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\hat{m}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\int_{t_{E}-L}^{t_{E}}dt\,\partial_{[i}h_{j]k}(t,\bm{x})\Big|_{\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}(t)}~.

(66)

Since

depends on time,

is observed as a polarization angle varying with the observation time
t_{E}
.

Notice that the second term in Eq. (

term in Eq. (

It should also be noted that, although the unperturbed polarization vector

appears in Eq. (

Indeed, by introducing any orthonormal basis vectors
(\bar{e}_{(1)}^{i},\bar{e}_{(2)}^{i})

as linear combinations of

and

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{\omega_{P}}{2}\bar{\varepsilon}^{ab}\bar{e}_{(a)}^{i}\bar{e}_{(b)}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{E}}d\lambda\,\partial_{i}h_{jk}(\lambda)~,

(67)

where

Thus, the initial polarization angle is irrelevant, which allows us to choose
\bar{p}^{i}
to be aligned with the unperturbed polarization direction.

II.4
Response functions

We now show how the observables—the redshift and the rotation of polarization angle—respond to GWs.
We express the GWs as a superposition of plane waves,

\displaystyle h_{ij}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=+,\times}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\tilde{h}_{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times e^{P}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,e^{-2\pi if(t-\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{x})}~,

(68)

where
f
is the frequency,
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
is the unit vector along the propagation direction of each plane wave, and
P(=+,\times)
labels the GW polarizations.
For each propagation direction
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
, the polarization tensors
e^{P}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})
are constructed as

\displaystyle e^{+}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{i}\hat{u}_{j}-\hat{v}_{i}\hat{v}_{j}~,

\displaystyle e^{\times}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{i}\hat{v}_{j}+\hat{v}_{i}\hat{u}_{j}~,

(69)

where
\hat{\bm{u}}
and
\hat{\bm{v}}
, together with
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
, form an orthonormal triad.
They are parametrized as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)~,

(70)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\phi,-\cos\phi,0)~,

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta)~.

(72)

Plugging Eq. (
68
) into Eqs. (
26
) and (
66
), the redshift
z
and the rotation of polarization angle
\chi
can be written as

\displaystyle z(t_{E})

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=+,\times}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,F^{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{h}_{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,e^{-2\pi ift_{E}}~,

(73)

\displaystyle\chi(t_{E})

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=+,\times}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,R^{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{h}_{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,e^{-2\pi ift_{E}}~,

(74)

where

\displaystyle F^{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}e^{P}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})}{1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}}\big[1-e^{2\pi ifL(1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}})}\big]~,

(75)

\displaystyle R^{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\big[\hat{\Omega}^{i}e^{P}_{jk}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})-\hat{\Omega}^{j}e^{P}_{ik}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\big]\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\hat{m}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}}{1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\big[1-e^{2\pi ifL(1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}})}\big]~,

(76)

are the response functions for the redshift and the polarization rotation, respectively.
Note that the response functions satisfy the complementary relations,

\displaystyle R^{+}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle=-F^{\times}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})~,\quad R^{\times}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})=F^{+}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})~.

(77)

For example, using the definitions of the polarization tensors (
69
) and
\hat{\bm{m}}=\hat{\bm{n}}\times\bar{\bm{\epsilon}}
, the first relation can be derived as

\displaystyle(\hat{\Omega}^{i}e^{+}_{jk}-\hat{\Omega}^{j}e^{+}_{ik})\bar{\epsilon}^{i}\hat{m}^{j}\hat{n}^{k}

\displaystyle=(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bar{\bm{\epsilon}})[(\hat{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})-(\hat{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})]

\displaystyle\quad-(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{m}})[(\bar{\bm{\epsilon}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})-(\bar{\bm{\epsilon}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})]

\displaystyle=-(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})[(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{m}})(\bar{\bm{\epsilon}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})-(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bar{\bm{\epsilon}})(\hat{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})]

\displaystyle\quad+(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})[(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{m}})(\bar{\bm{\epsilon}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})-(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bar{\bm{\epsilon}})(\hat{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})]

\displaystyle=-(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})[(\hat{\bm{m}}\times\bar{\bm{\epsilon}})\cdot(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\times\hat{\bm{u}})]

\displaystyle\quad+(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})[(\hat{\bm{m}}\times\bar{\bm{\epsilon}})\cdot(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\times\hat{\bm{v}})]

\displaystyle=2(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\hat{n}^{i}\hat{n}^{j}e^{\times}_{ij}~,

(78)

where we have used the identity
(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{C})(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{D})-(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{C})(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{D})=(\bm{A}\times\bm{B})\cdot(\bm{C}\times\bm{D})
in the third equality.
The relations between
F^{P}
and
R^{P}
provide the geometric origin of the sensitivity to the parity-violating component, exploited in the cross-correlation analysis presented in Sec.
III
.

III
Correlation analysis with Pulsar timing and polarimetry

III.1
Cross-correlation created by SGWB

In this section, we study how the measurements of the pulsar timing redshift and the rotation of polarization angle can be used to detect the SGWB. We model the SGWB as a stationary, isotropic, and Gaussian random field. Throughout this section, we adopt the polarization basis of GWs given in Eq. (
69
). The statistical properties of the background are encoded in the two-point function of the Fourier amplitudes
76
,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\braket{\tilde{h}_{+}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{+}^{*}(f^{\prime},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})}&\braket{\tilde{h}_{+}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{\times}^{*}(f^{\prime},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})}\\
\braket{\tilde{h}_{\times}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{+}^{*}(f^{\prime},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})}&\braket{\tilde{h}_{\times}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{\times}^{*}(f^{\prime},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\delta^{2}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}-\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})\begin{pmatrix}I(f)&-i\,V(f)\\
i\,V(f)&I(f)\end{pmatrix},

(79)

where the angle brackets denote ensemble averages.
The
2\times 2
Hermitian matrix on the right-hand side defines the Stokes parameters of the SGWB. In particular,
I(f)
represents the intensity, while
V(f)
quantifies the circular polarization and thus encodes the parity violation of the SGWB.
We omit the other Stokes parameters,
Q
and
U
, which characterize the linear polarizations of the SGWB, since they do not contribute to the isotropic component
76
.
The intensity
I(f)
is related to the GW energy density per unit logarithmic frequency, normalized by the critical density of the universe,
\Omega^{I}_{\rm GW}(f)
, through

\displaystyle\Omega^{I}_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{32\pi^{3}}{3H_{0}^{2}}f^{3}I(f)~,

(80)

where
H_{0}
is the Hubble constant.
For the circular polarization parameter
V(f)
, we introduce the corresponding dimensionless parameter
\Omega^{V}_{\rm GW}(f)
as

\displaystyle\Omega^{V}_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{32\pi^{3}}{3H_{0}^{2}}f^{3}V(f)~,

(81)

in analogy with
\Omega^{I}_{\rm GW}(f)
, which describes the difference between left- and right-handed modes.

An effective method for detecting a stochastic background is correlation analysis
34
;
8
.
In pulsar observations, we cross-correlate observables from distinct pulsars.
The relevant observables in our case are the timing redshift
z
and the rotation of polarization angle
\chi
.
We begin by considering the cross-correlation of the timing redshifts of pulsars
A
and
B
.
From Eq. (
73
), the Fourier component of the redshift of pulsar
A
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{A}(f)=\sum_{P=+,\times}\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,F^{P}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(82)

where
F^{P}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})
is the response function of pulsar
A
’s redshift,

\displaystyle F^{P}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\hat{n}_{A}^{i}\hat{n}_{A}^{j}e^{P}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})}{1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}_{A}}\big[1-e^{2\pi ifL_{A}(1+\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}_{A})}\big]~,

(83)

with
L_{A}
denoting the distance to pulsar
A
, and
\hat{\bm{n}}_{A}
the unit vector pointing from the Earth to pulsar
A
.
From Eq. (
79
), the cross-correlation of the redshifts of pulsar
A
and
B
is

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{z}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{z}_{B}^{*}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta(f-f^{\prime})\big[\Gamma^{I}_{AB}(f)\,I(f)+\Gamma^{V}_{AB}(f)\,V(f)\big]~.

(84)

Here, we introduced the overlap reduction functions (ORFs),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{I}_{AB}(f)

\displaystyle\equiv\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\big[F^{+}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,F^{+*}_{B}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\qquad+F^{\times}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,F^{\times*}_{B}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\big]~,

(85)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{V}_{AB}(f)

\displaystyle\equiv-i\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\big[F^{+}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,F^{\times*}_{B}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\qquad-F^{\times}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,F^{+*}_{B}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\big]~.

(86)

In pulsar systems, where
f\sim 10^{-9}\,\text{Hz}
and
L\sim 10^{2}
–
10^{3}\,\text{pc}
, the factor
2\pi fL
is typically large.
In this long-distance regime, the pulsar term, represented by the exponential factor in Eq. (
83
), oscillates rapidly and therefore averages out in the two-point correlation. Therefore, both the parity-even ORF
\Gamma^{I}_{AB}
and the parity-odd ORF
\Gamma^{V}_{AB}
become independent of the frequency
f
, with
\Gamma^{I}_{AB}
reducing to the Hellings–Downs curve
38
and
\Gamma^{V}_{AB}
vanishing
46
.

\displaystyle\Gamma^{I}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{3}\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi_{AB})~,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{V}_{AB}

\displaystyle=0~.

(87)

Here
\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi_{AB})
is a function of the separation angle
\xi_{AB}
between pulsars
A
and
B
, given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1-\cos\xi}{2}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1-\cos\xi}{2}\right)\ln\left(\frac{1-\cos\xi}{2}\right)~.

(89)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{z}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{z}_{B}^{*}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\frac{8\pi}{3}\Gamma_{\text{HD}}(\xi_{AB})\,I(f)~.

(90)

The vanishing of
\Gamma^{V}_{AB}
reflects the parity symmetry of the configuration: the Earth and the two pulsars always lie in the same plane, making the system invariant under a parity transformation.
Since the circular polarization
V
is parity-odd, its response necessarily vanishes.

From Eq. (
74
), the Fourier component of the rotation of polarization angle of pulsar
A
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{A}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=+,\times}\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,R^{P}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,\tilde{h}_{P}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})~.

(91)

Here,
R^{P}_{A}(f,\hat{\bm{\Omega}})
is the response function of pulsar
A
’s polarization rotation, related to

through

,

as in Eq. (

Using these relations, the other cross-correlations can be obtained as

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{\chi}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{\chi}_{B}^{*}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\frac{8\pi}{3}\,\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi_{AB})\,I(f)~,

(92)

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{z}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{\chi}_{B}^{*}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=-i\,\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\frac{8\pi}{3}\,\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi_{AB})\,V(f)~.

(93)

Notably, the

. This parity-odd estimator enables a clean separation of the circular polarization

from the intensity

.
Moreover, the angular correlation pattern consistently reduces to the Hellings–Downs curve. As discussed in Ref.
48
, the Hellings–Downs curve arises from Lorentz symmetry, which reflects the conformal properties of the two-point correlation of scalar quantities on the celestial sphere.

III.2
Sensitivity forecast

So far, we have only considered contributions from the SGWB.
In practice, each observable consists of a signal induced by the GWs and an additional stochastic noise contribution.
For each pulsar
A
, we work in the Fourier domain and define the measured quantities
\tilde{d}_{A}^{(z)}(f)
for the timing redshift and
\tilde{d}_{A}^{(\chi)}(f)
for polarimetry as

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{(z)}_{A}(f)

\displaystyle=\tilde{z}_{A}(f)+\tilde{n}^{(z)}_{A}(f)~,

(94)

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{(\chi)}_{A}(f)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\chi}_{A}(f)+\tilde{n}^{(\chi)}_{A}(f)~,

(95)

where
\tilde{n}^{(z)}_{A}(f)
and
\tilde{n}^{(\chi)}_{A}(f)
represent the noise contributions in the timing redshift and polarimetry measurements, respectively.
The signal components
\tilde{z}_{A}(f)
and
\tilde{\chi}_{A}(f)
obey the ensemble relations derived in the previous subsection, while the noise terms
\tilde{n}_{A}^{(z)}(f)
and
\tilde{n}_{A}^{(\chi)}(f)
account for instrumental and propagation effects that are not modeled explicitly.
We assume that the noise is stationary, Gaussian, and uncorrelated between different pulsars. Under this assumption, it can be characterized by one-sided power spectral densities (PSDs) for each measurement channel,

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{n}^{(z)}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{n}^{(z)*}_{B}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\delta_{AB}\,S^{(z)}_{A}(f)~,

(96)

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{n}^{(\chi)}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{n}^{(\chi)*}_{B}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,\delta_{AB}\,S^{(\chi)}_{A}(f)~.

(97)

We further assume that cross-channel couplings are negligible, so that the timing and polarimetry noises are uncorrelated:

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{n}^{(z)}_{A}(f)\,\tilde{n}^{(\chi)*}_{B}(f^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(98)

For simplicity, we consider the case of white noise for both the pulsar timing residuals (i.e., the time integral of the redshift) and the pulsar polarimetry measurements.
We assume that all pulsars are observed with the same cadence
\delta t
, and that the timing residuals and polarimetry measurements have root-mean-square (rms) uncertainties of
\sigma_{t}
and
\sigma_{p}
, respectively.
The corresponding one-sided PSDs are given by

\displaystyle S_{A}^{(z)}(f)

\displaystyle=2(2\pi f\sigma_{t})^{2}\,\delta t~,

\displaystyle S_{A}^{(\chi)}(f)

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{p}^{2}\,\delta t~.

(99)

Intrinsic spin noise or contributions from the interstellar medium can be incorporated into
S_{A}^{(z)}(f)
or
S_{A}^{(\chi)}(f)
with their measured or modeled frequency dependence; however, we neglect them here.

Looking ahead, timing measurements are expected to reach
\delta t\sim 2\,\text{weeks}
and
\sigma_{t}\sim 30\,{\rm ns}
in the SKA era
42
;
59
.
For polarimetry, recent observations already achieve
\sigma_{p}\sim 0.5\,\text{deg}
per epoch
20
.
For forecasts, we may utilize an optimistic uncertainty
\sigma_{p}\sim 0.1\,\text{deg}
. With these values, the noise level of pulsar polarimetry is approximately
10^{23}
times larger than that of pulsar timing at
f=1/{\rm yr}
.

To target the circular polarization of the SGWB,
V
, we construct a cross-correlation statistic between the timing and polarimetry measurements of distinct pulsars.
Each frequency bin is weighted by the inverse noise PSDs and by the overlap reduction function, which encodes the angular dependence of the pulsar pairs.
Following the procedure for computing the optimal cross-correlation statistic in PTAs
11
;
79
, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for the Stokes parameter
V
can be estimated as

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{H_{0}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{N_{p}^{2}T_{\rm obs}}{24}\int^{1/2\delta t}_{1/T_{\rm obs}}\frac{df}{f^{6}}\frac{\left(\Omega^{V}_{\rm GW}(f)\right)^{2}}{S^{(z)}(f)S^{(\chi)}(f)}~,

(100)

where
T_{\text{obs}}
represents the observation time span, and
N_{p}
is the number of pulsars.
This expression assumes that the noise power spectra are identical for all pulsars and that the number of pulsars is large,
N_{p}\gg 1
.
In this regime, the summation over pulsar pairs
\sum_{A<B}\Gamma_{\rm HD}(\xi_{AB})^{2}
can be approximated by an integral, which leads to the factor
1/24
in Eq. (
100
).

If the specturm is flat,
\Omega^{V}_{\rm GW}(f)
is independent of
f
, the SNR roughly reads

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}

\displaystyle\sim 5\left(\frac{\Omega^{V}_{\rm GW}}{0.012}\right)\left(\frac{T_{\rm obs}}{20\,{\rm yr}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{N_{p}}{200}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{30\,{\rm ns}}\right)^{-1}

(101)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\frac{\sigma_{p}}{0.1\,{\rm deg}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\delta t}{2\,{\rm week}}\right)^{-1},

(102)

where we have used
H_{0}=70\,\text{km/s/Mpc}
.
Thus, the PTA-PPA cross-correlation would place a limit
\Omega_{\rm GW}^{V}\lesssim 0.012
on the Stokes parameter
V
of the SGWB, which might be achievable in the SKA era.

IV
Conclusion

We have investigated the rotation of electromagnetic wave polarization during propagation through GWs and explored the joint use of pulsar polarimetry and timing measurements to detect circular polarization of the SGWB, which would indicate parity violation.
Starting from geometric optics in a GW background, we derived the perturbed photon geodesic and the transport equation for the polarization vector, obtaining expressions for the GW-induced rotation of polarization angle as well as for the timing redshift.
The rotation of polarization angle is a pseudo-scalar under parity transformation, and its antenna pattern is related to that of the timing response through the exchange of the GW’s
+
and
\times
polarizations.

Assuming a stationary, isotropic, and Gaussian SGWB, we computed the cross-correlations constructed from the pulsar timing redshift
z
and the rotation of polarization angle
\chi
.
We found that the
z
-
z
and
\chi
-
\chi
correlations depend solely on the SGWB intensity
I
, whereas the mixed
z
-
\chi
correlation isolates the circular polarization component
V
. These correlations share the same Hellings–Downs angular pattern.
Consequently, combining pulsar timing and polarimetry measurements allows separate inference of the SGWB intensity
I
and circular polarization
V
, without requiring any assumptions about their relative amplitudes.

The detection significance of the
z
-
\chi
correlation is determined by the timing and polarimetry noise power spectral densities and the effective bandwidth in Eq. (
100
), particularly the lower cutoff frequency.
Adopting optimistic parameters (
N_{p}\sim 200,T_{\rm obs}\sim 20~{\rm yr},\delta t\sim 2~{\rm week},\sigma_{t}\sim 30~{\rm ns}
and
\sigma_{p}\sim 0.1~{\rm deg}
) yields a constraint of
\Omega_{\rm GW}^{V}\lesssim 0.012
.
Although this sensitivity appears lower than that of current PTA measurements for
\Omega_{\text{GW}}^{I}
, our method provides a valuable null test with a sensitivity comparable to the current constraint from astrometry survey
43
.
These estimates should be regarded as order-of-magnitude. Although the forecasts lack the precision required for a clear detection, our study lays the groundwork for investigating the parity-violation signal of the SGWB in the PPA system, and the methodology can be extended to other stellar objects with well-characterized polarization and timing models.

Appendix A
Geometric optics in curved spacetime

In this Appendix, we briefly review geometric optics in curved spacetime and derive the equations of light propagation, Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
).
For a detailed discussion, see, e.g., Sec. 22.5 of Ref.
58
.

We start from the Maxwell equation in vacuum,
\nabla_{\nu}F^{\nu\mu}=0
.
Here,
\nabla_{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative associated with the spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, and
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\nabla_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\nabla_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the electromagnetic field strength tensor with
A_{\mu}
being the gauge field.
For the gauge field, we impose the Lorenz gauge condition,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0~.

(103)

In this gauge, the Maxwell equation
\nabla_{\nu}F^{\nu\mu}=0
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\nabla^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}A^{\mu}-{R^{\mu}}_{\nu}A^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0~,

(104)

where
R_{\mu\nu}
denotes the Ricci tensor.

Geometric optics is valid when the wavelength of light,
\lambda
, is much shorter than a characteristic length scale
L
, which represents the curvature radius of the background spacetime or a typical scale over which the amplitude, polarization, and wavelength of the light vary.
It is convenient to formally express the gauge field as

\displaystyle A^{\mu}(x)=[a^{\mu}(x)+s\,b^{\mu}(x)+\cdots]\,e^{i\theta(x)/s}~.

(105)

Here,
\theta(x)
is a real function representing the phase of the electromagnetic wave, while
a^{\mu}(x)
,
b^{\mu}(x)
, and so on are, in general, complex vector functions.
We have also introduced an auxiliary parameter
s
, which is ultimately set to unity, to indicate that terms proportional to
s^{n}
scale as
(\lambda/L)^{n}
in the short-wavelength limit.

The wave vector of the electromagnetic wave,
k^{\mu}
, is defined as the gradient of the phase:
k^{\mu}\equiv\nabla^{\mu}\theta
.
It is also conventional to decompose the vector function
a^{\mu}
as
a^{\mu}=a\,\epsilon^{\mu}
, where
a\equiv\sqrt{a^{\mu}a^{*}_{\mu}}
denotes the scalar amplitude and
\epsilon^{\mu}
is the polarization vector.
(The asterisk indicates complex conjugation.)
By definition, the polarization vector is normalized as
\epsilon^{\mu}\epsilon^{*}_{\mu}=1
.

Substituting the expansion (
105
) into the Lorenz gauge condition (
103
), we obtain, at leading order,
k_{\mu}a^{\mu}=0
, or, equivalently,

\displaystyle k_{\mu}\epsilon^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0~,

(106)

which is the second equation in Eq. (
3
).
On the other hand, substituting Eq. (
105
) into the Maxwell equation (
104
) yields the null condition
k_{\mu}k^{\mu}=0
at leading order.
Taking the derivative of this equation and noting that
\nabla_{\mu}k_{\nu}=\nabla_{\nu}k_{\mu}
follows from
k_{\mu}=\nabla_{\mu}\theta
, we find

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}k^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0~,

(107)

which is the geodesic equation (
2
).

At the next order in
s
, from Eq. (
104
) we obtain

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}a^{\mu}=-\frac{1}{2}a^{\mu}(\nabla_{\nu}k^{\nu})~.

(108)

From the definition
a^{\mu}=a\,\epsilon^{\mu}
with
a=\sqrt{a^{\mu}a^{*}_{\mu}}
, the above equation can be recast into the evolution equations for the scalar amplitude and the polarization vector as

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}a

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}a\,(\nabla_{\nu}k^{\nu})~,

(109)

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\epsilon^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0~.

(110)

Equation (
110
) is the first of Eq. (
3
), indicating that the polarization vector is parallel transported along the light ray.
