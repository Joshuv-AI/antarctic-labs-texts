Robust estimation of a Markov chain transition matrix from multiple sample paths
Lasse Leskelä, Maximilien Dreveton

Abstract
Markov chains are fundamental models for stochastic dynamics, with applications in a wide range of areas such as population dynamics, queueing systems, reinforcement learning, and Monte Carlo methods. Estimating the transition matrix and stationary distribution from observed sample paths is a core statistical challenge, particularly when multiple independent trajectories are available. While classical theory typically assumes identical chains with known stationary distributions, real-world data often arise from heterogeneous chains whose transition kernels and stationary measures might differ from a common target. We analyse empirical estimators for such parallel Markov processes and establish sharp concentration inequalities that generalise Bernstein-type bounds from standard time averages to ensemble-time averages. Our results provide nonasymptotic error bounds and consistency guarantees in high-dimensional regimes, accommodating sparse or weakly mixing chains, model mismatch, nonstationary initialisations, and partially corrupted data. These findings offer rigorous foundations for statistical inference in heterogeneous Markov chain settings common in modern computational applications.

Introduction

Markov chains are fundamental models for stochastic dynamics, with applications ranging from population and queueing models
[

,
25
]
to

[
3
,
9
,
24
]

[

,

.
A key task in such settings is the estimation of the transition probability matrix from observed data
[
12
,
27
,
31
]
.
This problem has a long history: classical works such as
[
2
,
7
]

studied the limiting properties of estimators under long time horizon. More recent research has focused
on nonasymptotic bounds that provide finite-sample guarantees,
particularly for sparse models and high-dimensional settings
[

,
15
,
35
,
36
,
34
,
11

.
However, this literature almost exclusively considers data from a single chain, typically assumed to be stationary and well-specified.

In contrast, many practical settings involve heterogeneous sources with multiple chains, where the transition dynamics and stationary behaviour may vary across trajectories and differ from a shared target model with transition matrix
P
.
This arises in applications like cohort studies, epidemiology, and distributed systems. We study the problem of estimating
P
and its stationary distribution
\pi
from
M
such paths of length
T
.
A natural approach aggregates observed transitions and state visits into empirical estimators
\hat{P}
and
\hat{\pi}
. While well understood for homogeneous, stationary chains, their performance under heterogeneity remains largely unexplored.

We develop a general theoretical framework for this setting, quantifying how estimation errors depend on heterogeneity in transition matrices, initial distributions, and mixing properties. We derive nonasymptotic error bounds and consistency guarantees that hold even in high-dimensional regimes, where the number of states, trajectories, and trajectory lengths may all grow. Our results are particularly suited to sparse or slowly mixing chains and allow for partial data corruption.
This work extends several foundational results from the single-trajectory literature—such as concentration inequalities for empirical averages
[
26
,
28
,
17
]
and transition matrix estimation
[
35
,
36
,
18
]
—to the setting of
ensemble-time averages
over multiple heterogeneous trajectories. In particular, we generalise a Paulin–Bernstein concentration inequality
[
28
]
, and the analysis of the empirical transition matrix in
[
35
]
, from the single-trajectory setting to the multi-trajectory regime.

The work presented in this article was originally motivated by the study of community detection in temporal networks,
in which interactions between intra- and inter-community node pairs evolve over time according to binary Markov chains with transition matrices
P
and
Q
, respectively
[
4
,
5
]
. A widely used clustering procedure follows a three-stage approach:

(i)

an initial (usually noisy) assignment of community labels,

(ii)

estimation of interaction parameters via empirical averages conditioned on the initial partition,

(iii)

refinement of the partition using likelihood-based updates
[
1
,
14
]
.

In this setting, errors from stage (i) propagate into stage (ii), contaminating the empirical estimation process. As a result, the empirical estimator for
P
incorporates interaction sequences not only from correctly identified intra-community node pairs but also from misclassified inter-community pairs, which in fact follow the dynamics of the matrix
Q
. This highlights the need for a robust understanding of empirical estimators under model mismatch and heterogeneous trajectory sources—a regime that is the focus of this work.

2
Model description

Fix integers
M,T\geq 1
, and let
\Omega
be a nonempty finite set. The objective is to estimate the transition matrix
P
of a Markov chain on
\Omega
from
M
independent sample paths. These sample paths are arranged in a data matrix
(X_{m,t})

with rows indexed by
m=1,\dots,M
and columns by
t=0,\dots,T
.

2.1
Parameter estimators

A natural estimator for
P
is the
empirical transition matrix

\hat{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\times{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}
, defined by

\hat{P}_{i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{N_{i,j}}{N_{i}}&\text{if }N_{i}>0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where the state and transition counts are given by

N_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)},\qquad N_{i,j}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)},

(2.2)

with indicator variables
X_{m,t}^{(i)}=1(X_{m,t}=i)
.
Furthermore, if
P
is irreducible and aperiodic, its stationary distribution
\pi
may be estimated by the
empirical distribution

\hat{\pi}_{i}\ =\ \frac{N_{i}}{MT}.

(2.3)

In the setting where all sample paths are realisations of a Markov chain with transition matrix
P
,
it is well known that
\hat{P}
coincides with the maximum likelihood estimate
[
2
]
.

2.2
Approximate Markov setting

To investigate the robustness of these estimators under model misspecification, we consider a more general setting in which the observed sample paths are independent but not identically distributed. Specifically, each row of the data matrix
(X_{m,t})
is a realisation of an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain with:

•

transition matrix
P_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\times{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}
,

•

initial distribution
\mu_{m}
,

•

stationary distribution
\pi_{m}
,

•

pseudo-spectral gap
\gamma_{m}
.

We expect the estimators (
2.1
) and (
2.3
)
to perform well when
P_{m}\approx P
and
\pi_{m}\approx\pi
for most
m
,
and when the total sample size
MT
is large.

2.3
Large-scale regime

To analyse asymptotic consistency properties of the estimators, we introduce a scale parameter
\nu\to\infty
,
and allow the model dimensions and parameters to vary with
\nu
. Specifically, we write

(\Omega,M,T)=(\Omega^{(\nu)},M^{(\nu)},T^{(\nu)}),\qquad(\pi,P)=(\pi^{(\nu)},P^{(\nu)}),\qquad(\mu_{m},P_{m},\pi_{m})=(\mu_{m}^{(\nu)},P_{m}^{(\nu)},\pi_{m}^{(\nu)}),

and let
\mathbb{P}^{(\nu)}
denote the distribution of the data matrix under this setting. For notational simplicity, we omit the dependence on
\nu
where it is clear from context.
This flexible framework allows for analysis of:

•

large ensembles (
M\to\infty
) of long chains (
T\to\infty
),

•

large state spaces with
{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\to\infty
,

•

sparse models where
\pi(i)\to 0
or
P(i,j)\to 0
for most
i,j
.

3.1
Nonasymptotic error bounds

Recall the heterogeneous Markov model in Section
2.2
,
in which each row
m
of the data matrix is a Markov chain on
\Omega
with
transition matrix
P_{m}
, initial distribution
\mu_{m}
,
stationary distribution
\pi_{m}
,
and
pseudo-spectral gap
\gamma_{m}
.
To quantify how close the model parameters
are from the target parameters
P
and
\pi
,
we denote

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\|P_{m}-P\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\qquad\Delta_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{m}\|P_{m}-P\|_{\infty},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\|\pi_{m}-\pi\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\qquad\delta_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{m}\|\pi_{m}-\pi\|_{\infty}.

We denote
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}=\min_{i}\bar{\pi}_{i}

and

\bar{\pi}_{\rm max}=\max_{i}\bar{\pi}_{i}
,
where

\bar{\pi}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\pi_{m}(i)

is the long-term average frequency of state
i
,
and the minimum pseudo-spectral gap by
\gamma_{\rm min}=\min_{m}\gamma_{m}
.
The deviation from stationarity is quantified by

\eta\ =\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}D_{2}(\mu_{m}\|\pi_{m}),

(3.2)

where
D_{2}
refers to the Rényi divergence of order two (see Appendix
A.1
for details).

The following theorem presents a generic error bound for estimating the transition matrix
P

using the empirical transition matrix
\hat{P}
defined in (
2.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
0<\epsilon\leq 1
,

\|\hat{P}-P\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon+\min\left\{\frac{2\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}},\,\Delta_{\infty}\right\}

(3.3)

holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-w_{1}}-2e^{-w_{2}}
, where

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}M\gamma_{\rm min}T}{144+64/(\gamma_{\rm min}T)}-\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}-\frac{1}{2}M\eta,

(3.4)

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\epsilon^{2}\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}MT}{19{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}-2\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}.

(3.5)

Furthermore, the following theorem presents an error bound for estimating the distribution
\pi

using the empirical distribution
\hat{\pi}
defined in (
2.3
).

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
0<\epsilon\leq 1
,

\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon+\delta_{1}

(3.6)

holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-w_{3}}
, where

w_{3}\ =\ \frac{\gamma_{\rm min}MT\epsilon^{2}}{16(1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T))\bar{\pi}_{\rm max}+40\epsilon}-\frac{1}{2}M\eta-\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}.

(3.7)

Example 3.3
.

Assume that each row of the data matrix corresponds to a stationary Markov chain with transition matrix
and stationary distribution

P_{m}\ =\ \begin{cases}P,&m\in\mathcal{M}_{0},\\
Q,&m\in\mathcal{M}_{1},\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{m}\ =\ \begin{cases}\pi,&m\in\mathcal{M}_{0},\\
\rho,&m\in\mathcal{M}_{1}.\end{cases}

Here,
P
denotes the true (correct) transition matrix,
while
Q
represents a perturbed (incorrect) transition matrix.
Denote the number of perturbed chains by
M_{1}=|\mathcal{M}_{1}|
.

Under this model, the parameters

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{1}}{M}\|P-Q\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\qquad\Delta_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|P-Q\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{1}}{M}\|\pi-\rho\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\qquad\delta_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\pi-\rho\|_{\infty},

quantify the discrepancy between the true and perturbed chains.
Due to stationarity (
\eta=0
), the error bounds simplify.
We obtain the following probabilistic guarantees:
the estimated transition matrix
\hat{P}
satisfies (Theorem
3.1
)

\|\hat{P}-P\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon+\min\left\{\frac{2\frac{M_{1}}{M}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}},\,1\right\}\|P-Q\|_{\infty}

with probability at least

1-4|\Omega|^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}M}{144}\min\left\{T^{\prime},\ \frac{\epsilon^{2}T}{|\Omega|}\right\}\right),

with normalised time parameter
T^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}T}{1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T)}
,
and the estimated stationary distribution
\hat{\pi}
satisfies
(Theorem
3.2
)

\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon+\delta_{1}

with probability at least

1-2|\Omega|\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}MT^{\prime}}{56}\right).

3.2
Estimation from clean data

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
3.1
,
we obtain the following result for data matrices with unperturbed rows.

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider a data matrix
(X_{m,t})
in which the rows are
mutually independent, irreducible and aperiodic, stationary Markov chains with
transition matrix
P
,
stationary distribution
\pi
,
and pseudo-spectral gap
\gamma
.
Then
for any
0<\epsilon\leq 1
,

\|\hat{P}-P\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon,

(3.8)

holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-w_{1}}-2e^{-w_{2}}
, where

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\pi_{\rm min}M\gamma T}{144+64/(\gamma T)}-\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}-\frac{1}{2}M\eta,

(3.9)

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\epsilon^{2}\pi_{\rm min}MT}{19{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}-2\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert},

(3.10)

and
\pi_{\rm min}=\min_{i}\pi(i)
.

3.3
Consistency

As corollaries of the error estimates in Section
3.1
,
we obtain sufficient conditions for the consistency of
\hat{P}
and
\hat{\pi}

in asymptotic large-scale regimes described in Section
2.3
.
The consistency results are conveniently described in terms of a normalised time parameter

T^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{\gamma_{\rm min}T}{1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T)},

where
\gamma_{\rm min}=\min_{m}\gamma_{m}
denotes the minimum pseudo-spectral gap of the transition matrices
P_{m}
.
The quantity
T^{\prime}\leq T
corresponds to an effective time that is discounted
by the worst-case mixing behaviour of the model, and is closely
related to the mixing times of Markov chains
[
28
, Proposition 3.4]
.
The quantity
MT^{\prime}
can then be interpreted as the number of well-mixed samples in the data matrix.
We also note that

T^{\prime}\ \asymp\ \begin{cases}(\gamma_{\rm min}T)^{2},&\quad\gamma_{\rm min}T\ll 1,\\
\gamma_{\rm min}T,&\quad\gamma_{\rm min}T\gtrsim 1,\end{cases}

so that in particular,

T^{\prime}\asymp T
in well-mixing regimes with
\gamma_{\rm min}\asymp 1
.

The following result establishes sufficient conditions under which the empirical transition matrix
\hat{P}

defined by (
2.1
)
is a consistent estimator of
P
.

Theorem 3.5
.

If

MT^{\prime}\gg\frac{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}}
,

T^{\prime}\gg\frac{\eta}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}}
,
and

\min\{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}},\,\Delta_{\infty}\}\ll 1
,
then

\|\hat{P}-P\|_{\infty}\ll 1
with high probability.

Analogously, the following result provides conditions under which the empirical distribution
\hat{\pi}

defined by (
2.3
) is a consistent estimator of
\pi
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If

MT^{\prime}\gg\log{\lvert\Omega\rvert}
,

T^{\prime}\gg\eta
,
and

\delta_{1}\ll 1
,
then

\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\ll 1

with high probability.

Let us compare the assumptions of
the consistency theorems
in a stationary setting (
\eta=0
)
with bounded state space (
{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\asymp 1
).
In this case
Theorem
3.5
requires that

\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}MT^{\prime}\gg 1
,
and

\min\{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}},\,\Delta_{\infty}\}\ll 1
.
For estimating the transition matrix,
a lower bound on the minimum long-term average state frequencies
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}
is needed: If
\pi_{m}(i)

is very small for most
m
, then we do not observe many jumps out of state
i
,
and estimating the row
P_{i,:}
becomes
difficult.
In contrast,
Theorem
3.6
only requires that

MT^{\prime}\gg 1

and

\delta_{1}\ll 1
.
Here we do not need a lower bound on
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}
:
If some state
i
is rare in that
\bar{\pi}_{i}
is very small, then we estimate
\pi_{i}

to be close to zero by not empirically observing visits to
i
.

Remark 3.7
.

We note that

\min\{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}},\,\Delta_{\infty}\}\asymp\Delta_{1}

in balanced regimes with
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}\asymp 1
.
On the other hand, it may happen that

\Delta_{\infty}\ll 1\lesssim\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}}
in regimes with
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}\ll 1

(see Example
3.8
).

Example 3.8
.

Fix an integer
n\geq 3
.
Let
\Omega=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Let
P(i,j)=\frac{1}{n}
and
P_{m}(i,j)=\frac{1(j\neq i)}{n-1}
.
We see that
P
(resp.
P_{m}
) is
the transition matrix of a symmetric random walk on the complete graph on
\Omega
, where the walk
can (resp. cannot) jump to its current state.
The transition matrices
P_{m}
and
P
are irreducible and aperiodic,
and all have the uniform distribution on
\Omega
as their unique stationary distribution
\pi_{m}=\pi
.
A simple computation shows that
\|P_{m}-P\|_{\infty}=\frac{2}{n}
.
It follows that
\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}=\frac{1}{n}
together with

\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{\infty}=\frac{2}{n}
.
It follows that
\Delta_{\infty}\ll 1\asymp\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\bar{\pi}_{\rm min}}
for
large state spaces with
{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\gg 1
.

Robust estimation under fully corrupted rows

Let us consider a setting in which
M_{0}
rows of the data matrix
(X_{m,t})
are Markov chains
as before, and the remaining
M_{1}=M-M_{0}
rows are arbitrary, considered as fully corrupted data.
When we do not know which of the rows are corrupted, a natural estimate of

is still
the empirical transition matrix
\hat{P}
of the full data matrix given by (

).
When
M_{1}/M
is small, we expect that
\hat{P}
still provides a reasonably accurate estimate of

.
The following result quantifies this.

As before, we assume that among the
M_{0}
rows,
each row
m
is an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain with initial distribution
\mu_{m}
,
transition matrix
P_{m}
,
stationary distribution
\pi_{m}
, and pseudo-spectral gap

.
We define
\delta_{1},\delta_{\infty},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{\infty}
as in (

),
and

as in (

),
with the sums and the maxima taken over the

and with the normalising constant

replaced by
M_{0}
.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
\delta_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}\pi_{\rm min}
, then
for any
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{4}\pi_{\rm min}
,

\|\hat{P}-P\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \epsilon+\min\left\{\frac{3\Delta_{1}}{\pi_{\rm min}},\,\Delta_{\infty}\right\}+\frac{4M_{1}/M}{\pi_{\rm min}}

(3.11)

holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-w_{1}}-2e^{-w_{2}}-2e^{-w_{3}}
, where

w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
are given by (
3.4

), and (

The starting point is a Paulin–Bernstein concentration inequality (Theorem

4.1
Concentration of ensemble-time averages

We will generalise a Paulin–Bernstein concentration inequality
[
28
, Theorem 3.4, Proposition 3.10]

for the standard time average

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(X_{t-1})
of a single Markov chain
to the ensemble-time average

\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(X_{m,t-1})

of multiple independent Markov chains.

Remark 4.1
.

A simple approach for deriving a concentration bound is to represent the ensemble-time average
as an ordinary time average of the
\Omega^{M}
-valued Markov chain
(X_{:,t})

according to

\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(X_{m,t-1})\ =\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}F(X_{:,t-1}),

where
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{m}(x_{m})
, and then apply

[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
.
Such a direct approach does not lead into a good concentration inequality
for large ensembles with
M\gg 1
.
The reason is that, even though the variance
\operatorname{Var}_{\Pi}(F)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})

for
\Pi=\bigotimes_{m}\pi_{m}
being the stationary distribution of
(X_{:,t})

scales well with
M
, the same is not in general true for the term
\Delta_{F}=\max_{x\in\Omega^{M}}{\lvert F(x)-\Pi(F)\rvert}
.
In particular, for indicator functions
f_{m}=1_{\{i\}}
, we see that
\Delta_{F}
is of order
M

even though
\max_{x}{\lvert f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m})\rvert}\leq 1
.

Instead of the aforementioned simplistic approach, we will refine the analysis and
modify the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]

to properly account for the smoothing effect due to averaging over multiple independent Markov chains.
As a result, we obtain the following sharp concentration inequality.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{M}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

and for any
s>0
,
the ensemble-time average

S=\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_{m}(X_{m,t-1})-\pi_{m}(f_{m}))
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(S\geq s)\ \leq\ \exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}MTs^{2}}{16(1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T))V+40\Delta s}+\frac{1}{2}M\eta\bigg),

(4.1)

where
\Delta=\max_{m}\max_{x}{\lvert f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m})\rvert}
and

V=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\sum_{x}(f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m}))^{2}\pi_{m}(x)
.

Proof.

Let us first prove (
4.1
) in the stationary case in which
the rows
X_{m,:}
are stationary Markov chains. In this case we denote
the law of the random matrix
(X_{m,t})
by
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
.
Denote

\Delta_{m}=\max_{x}{\lvert f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m})\rvert}

and

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})=\sum_{x}(f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m}))^{2}\pi_{m}(x)
,
and define

g_{m}(x)\ =\ \begin{cases}\frac{f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m})}{\Delta_{m}},&\quad\Delta_{m}\neq 0,\\
0,&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
S=\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m}\Delta_{m}S_{m}
, where

S_{m}\ =\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{m}(X_{m,t}).

Fix an integer
m
such that
\Delta_{m}>0
.
We note that
\pi_{m}(g_{m})=0
and
\|g_{m}\|_{\infty}=1
.
The last inequality in the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
then implies that

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{\theta S_{m}}\leq e^{\psi_{m}(\theta)}\qquad\text{for all $0<\theta<\gamma_{m}/10$},

where

\psi_{m}(\theta)\ =\ 2\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(g_{m})\frac{T+1/\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}\frac{\theta^{2}}{1-\frac{10\theta}{\gamma_{m}}}\ =\ 2\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})\frac{T+1/\gamma_{m}}{\Delta_{m}^{2}\gamma_{m}}\frac{\theta^{2}}{1-\frac{10\theta}{\gamma_{m}}}.

Then

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{\theta\Delta_{m}S_{m}}\leq e^{\psi_{m}(\Delta_{m}\theta)}\qquad\text{for all $0<\theta<\frac{\gamma_{m}}{10\Delta_{m}}$}.

Denote
\epsilon=\min_{m:\Delta_{m}>0}\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\Delta_{m}}
.
Then for any
0<\theta<\epsilon/10
, we find that

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{\theta MTS}\ =\ \prod_{m:\Delta_{m}>0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{\theta\Delta_{m}S_{m}}\ \leq\ e^{\sum_{m:\Delta_{m}>0}\psi_{m}(\Delta_{m}\theta)}.

Here

\displaystyle\sum_{m:\Delta_{m}>0}\psi_{m}(\Delta_{m}\theta)

\displaystyle\ =\ 2\sum_{m:\Delta_{m}>0}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})\frac{T+1/\gamma_{m}}{\Delta_{m}^{2}\gamma_{m}}\frac{\Delta_{m}^{2}\theta^{2}}{1-\frac{10\Delta_{m}\theta}{\gamma_{m}}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2\underbrace{\sum_{m}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})\frac{T+1/\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}}_{\alpha}\frac{\theta^{2}}{1-\frac{10\theta}{\epsilon}}.

Hence by Markov’s inequality, for any
0<\theta<\epsilon/10
and all
s\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(S\geq s/T)\ =\ \mathbb{P}_{\pi}(MTS\geq Ms)\ \leq\ e^{-\theta Ms}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}e^{\theta MTS}\ \leq\ \exp\bigg(M(\psi(\theta)-\theta s)\bigg),

(4.2)

where

\psi(\theta)\ =\ 2\frac{\alpha}{M}\frac{\theta^{2}}{1-\frac{10\theta}{\epsilon}}

To minimise the right side in (
4.2
), we note that

\psi(\theta)-\theta s\ =\ \frac{\alpha\epsilon^{2}}{50M}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{1-\eta}-s_{1}\eta\right),

where
\eta=\frac{10\theta}{\epsilon}
and

s_{1}=5\frac{Ms}{\alpha\epsilon}
.
The function
\psi_{1}(\eta)=\frac{\eta^{2}}{1-\eta}-s_{1}\eta

has derivatives

\psi_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)=\frac{2\eta-\eta^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}-s_{1}

and

\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=\frac{2}{(1-\eta)^{3}}
.
Hence
\psi_{1}
is strictly convex on
(0,1)

and attains its minimum at
\eta=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s_{1}}}
.
As a consequence,
\psi(\theta)-\theta s
is minimised at

\theta=\frac{\epsilon\eta}{10}
.
A simple computation shows that the minimum value equals

\displaystyle\psi(\theta)-\theta s

\displaystyle\ =\ -\frac{\alpha\epsilon^{2}}{50M}\left(\sqrt{1+s_{1}}-1\right)^{2}.

To simplify the above expression, we apply the inequality
1
1

1

This can be checked by squaring things so that we see a difference between 4th order polynomials.
This gives a shaper bound that the simple Taylor bound

(1+x)^{1/2}-1=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{2}(1+s)^{-1/2}ds\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{x}{(1+x)^{1/2}}
.

\sqrt{1+x}-1\geq\frac{x/2}{\sqrt{1+x/2}}

which implies that
((1+x)^{1/2}-1)^{2}\geq\frac{x^{2}/4}{{1+x/2}}
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\psi(\theta)-\theta s\ \leq\ -\frac{\alpha\epsilon^{2}}{50M}\frac{s_{1}^{2}/4}{{1+s_{1}/2}}\ =\ -\frac{\epsilon s^{2}}{8\frac{\alpha\epsilon}{M}+20s}.

By plugging this into (
4.2
),
we see that

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(S\geq s/T)\ \leq\ \exp\bigg(-\frac{M\epsilon s^{2}}{8\frac{\alpha\epsilon}{M}+20s}\bigg).

(4.3)

Then observe that
\epsilon\geq\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}}{\Delta}
and

\alpha\ =\ \sum_{m}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})\frac{T+1/\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}\ \leq\ \frac{T+1/\gamma_{\rm min}}{\gamma_{\rm min}}MV,

with
V=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{m}}(f_{m})
,
so that

\frac{M\epsilon s^{2}}{8\frac{\alpha\epsilon}{M}+20s}\ \geq\ \frac{Ms^{2}}{8(\frac{T+1/\gamma_{\rm min}}{\gamma_{\rm min}}V)+20s\frac{\Delta}{\gamma_{\rm min}}}\ =\ \frac{\gamma_{\rm min}Ms^{2}}{8(T+1/\gamma_{\rm min})V+20s\Delta}.

By plugging the above inequality into (
4.3
) and substituting
sT
in place of
s
,
we find that

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(S\geq s)\ \leq\ \exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}MTs^{2}}{8(1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T))V+20\Delta s}\bigg).

(4.4)

Finally, let us prove the claim in the general nonstationary context.
By Lemma
A.2
, we see that

\mathbb{P}(S\geq s)\ \leq\ e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}D_{2}(\mu_{m}\|\pi_{m})}\ \mathbb{P}_{\pi}(S\geq s)^{1/2}\ =\ e^{\frac{1}{2}M\eta}\ \mathbb{P}_{\pi}(S\geq s)^{1/2}

By combining this with (
4.4
), the claim follows.
∎

4.2
Concentration of empirical state frequencies

Proposition 4.3
.

For any state
i

and any
s>0
,
the empirical frequency
N_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}

of state
i
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\bigg({\Big\lvert\frac{N_{i}}{MT}-\bar{\pi}_{i}\Big\rvert}\geq s\bigg)\ \leq\ 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}MTs^{2}}{16(1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T))\bar{\pi}_{i}+40s}+\frac{1}{2}M\eta\bigg).

Proof.

Fix
f_{m}(x)=1_{\{i\}}(x)
and define

S=\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_{m}(X_{m,t-1})-\pi_{m}(f_{m}))
.
Then

S=\frac{N_{i}}{MT}-\bar{\pi}_{i}
.
Note that

\Delta\ =\ \max_{m}\max_{x}{\lvert f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m})\rvert}\ \leq\ 1

and

V\ =\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\sum_{x}(f_{m}(x)-\pi_{m}(f_{m}))^{2}\pi_{m}(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\pi_{m}(i)(1-\pi_{m}(i))\ \leq\ \bar{\pi}_{i}.

By applying
Theorem
4.2
, we find that

\mathbb{P}(S\geq s)\ \leq\ \exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma_{\rm min}MTs^{2}}{16(1+1/(\gamma_{\rm min}T))\bar{\pi}_{i}+40s}+\frac{1}{2}M\eta\bigg).

By repeating the same argument for
f_{m}(x)=-1_{\{i\}}(x)
, we see that the above bound is valid
also for
\mathbb{P}(S\leq-s)
.
Hence the claim follows.
∎

4.3
Concentration of transition frequencies

In this section we will derive a concentration inequality for the empirical transition probabilities
out from a particular state
i
, given that the empirical frequency of visiting
i
is concentrated
to an interval. This analysis extends a martingale concentration argument in
[
35
]

from a single Markov chain to a heterogeneous ensemble of Markov chains.

Denote the state and transition frequencies of the chain
m
by

N_{m,i}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{m,i,j}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)}

(4.5)

and let

\tilde{P}_{i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}\sum_{m}\frac{N_{m,i}}{N_{i}}P_{m,i,j}&\quad\text{if $N_{i}>0$},\\
\frac{1}{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}&\quad\text{else},\end{cases}

(4.6)

where
P_{m,i,j}=P_{m}(i,j)
is the
(i,j)
-entry of the transition matrix
P_{m}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(X_{m,t})
be a random array in
\Omega^{M\times(T+1)}

in which the rows are independent, and
each row is a Markov chain with transition matrix
P_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\times{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}
.
Then the empirical transition matrix
\hat{P}
computed by (
2.1
)
is concentrated around
\tilde{P}
defined by (
4.6
)
according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lVert\hat{P}_{i,:}-\tilde{P}_{i,:}\rVert_{1}\geq\epsilon,\,s_{1}\leq N_{i}\leq s_{2})

\displaystyle\ \leq\ (1+{\lvert\Omega\rvert})\exp\bigg(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}s_{1}}{6\sqrt{2}{\lvert\Omega\rvert}s_{2}/s_{1}+2\sqrt{2}{\lvert\Omega\rvert}^{1/2}\epsilon}\bigg)

for all
i\in\Omega
,
\epsilon>0
, and
0<s_{1}\leq s_{2}
.

Again, a simple approach would be to apply a martingale concentration argument to a vector-valued Markov chain

(X_{:,t})
, but this would lead to a suboptimal bound due to similar issues as in Remark
4.1
.
To properly incorporate the concentration properties due to the two dimensions of averaging, both
over the ensemble members
m
and the time slots
t
.
Instead, we will here study an alternative martingale corresponding to the time series obtained by
vectorising the data matrix
(X_{m,t})
by stacking its columns on top of each other.

The starting point for proving Proposition
4.4
is to note that

N_{i,j}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{i}\tilde{P}_{i,j}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}P_{m,i,j}.

Then we may write

N_{i,j}-N_{i}\tilde{P}_{i,j}\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=0}^{T}U_{m,t,i,j}

(4.7)

in terms of

U_{m,t,i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&\qquad t=0,\\
X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}(X_{m,t}^{(j)}-P_{m,i,j}),&\qquad t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(4.8)

The zeros are added to obtain a more convenient indexing, with

U_{:,:,i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(T+1)}
having the dimensions
as the data array
X
.
The challenge is to represent these indicators as martingale differences.
To resolve this, we change the indexing of time as follows.
Let

\bar{U}_{:,i,j}=\operatorname{vec}(U_{:,:,i,j})

be a vector
obtained by stacking the columns of the matrix
U_{:,:,i,j}
on top of each other.
Then
\bar{U}_{:,i,j}
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
with
K=M(T+1)

which shall be viewed as a random process
k\mapsto\bar{U}_{k,i,j}

with time parameter
k
.
We define an associated cumulative process by

\bar{V}_{k,i,j}\ =\ \sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\bar{U}_{k^{\prime},i,j},\qquad k=0,\dots,K,

(4.9)

with the empty sum considered as zero. Equality (
4.7
) can
then be written as

\bar{V}_{K,i,:}\ =\ N_{i,:}-N_{i}\tilde{P}_{i,:}.

(4.10)

The random process
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,:}
is adapted to the filtration

(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots)

of sigma-algebras

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(\bar{X}_{1},\dots\bar{X}_{k})
in which
\bar{X}_{k}
indicates
the
k
-th element of the vectorised data matrix
\bar{X}=\operatorname{vec}(X)
.
The next result confirms that the process is a martingale with bounded increments.

Lemma 4.5
.

The random process
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,:}
defined by (
4.9
) is a martingale with respect to

(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots)
,
with increments bounded by

\lVert\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(4.11)

Proof.

Define

Y_{m,t,i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&\qquad t=0,\\
X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)},&\qquad t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(4.12)

Define
\bar{Y}_{:,i,j}=\operatorname{vec}(Y_{:,:,i,j})
.
The entries of these matrices and their vectorisations are related by

Y_{m,t,i,j}=\bar{Y}_{m+Mt,i,j},

where
(m,t)\mapsto m+tM
is a bijection from
\{1,\dots,M\}\times\{0,\dots,T\}
into
\{1,\dots,K\}
.
We will verify that for any
k=m+tM
,

\mathbb{E}(\bar{Y}_{k,i,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&\qquad t=0,\\
X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}P_{m,i,j},&\qquad t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(4.13)

The case with
t=0
is immediate.
Assume next that
k=m+tM
for some
t\geq 1
.
Then, because the rows of
X
are independent, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\bar{Y}_{k,i,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1})

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\Big[X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)}\,\big|\ X_{\ell,s}:\ell+sM<m+tM\Big]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\Big[X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)}\,\big|\ X_{m,s}:m+sM<m+tM\Big]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\Big[X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}X_{m,t}^{(j)}\,\big|\ X_{m,1},\dots,X_{m,t-1}\Big].

Because
X_{m:}
is a Markov chain with transition matrix
P_{m}
,
the last expression on the right equals
X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}P_{m,i,j}
,
confirming (
4.13
).
By comparing (
4.13
) with (
4.8
) and (
4.12
),
we find that

\bar{U}_{k,i,j}\ =\ \bar{Y}_{k,i,j}-\mathbb{E}(\bar{Y}_{k,i,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}),

so that
\mathbb{E}(\bar{U}_{k,i,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1})=0
for all
k=1,\dots,K
.
It follows that
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,j}
, and hence also
the vector-valued process
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,:}
, is a martingale.

To verify (
4.11
),
let us fix an integer
k=m+tM
.
The inequality is trivial when
t=0
because then
\bar{U}_{k,i,:}=0
.
Assume next that
t\geq 1
.
Then (
4.8
) implies that

\displaystyle\lVert\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\sum_{j\in\Omega}\big(X_{m,t}^{(j)}-P_{m,i,j}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\sum_{j\in\Omega}\big(X_{m,t}^{(j)}+P_{m,i,j}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\Big(1+\|P_{m,i,:}\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Because
\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
,
the claim follows.
∎

We will derive a concentration inequality
for
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,:}
by
applying a Freedman–Tropp matrix martingale concentration inequality.
To do this, we need bounds for associated predictable quadratic variation terms

\displaystyle W^{\rm col}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\Big[\bar{U}_{k,i,:}(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big],

(4.14)

\displaystyle W^{\rm row}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\Big[(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big].

(4.15)

The following result provides simple formulas for these terms.

Lemma 4.6
.

The predictable quadratic variation terms (
4.14
)–(
4.15
)
can be written as

\displaystyle W^{\rm col}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}(1-\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}),

(4.16)

\displaystyle W^{\rm row}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}\big(\operatorname{diag}(P_{m,i,:})-(P_{m,i,:})^{\top}P_{m,i,:}\big).

(4.17)

Proof.

Fix an integer
k=m+tM
with
t\geq 1
.
By recalling the definition (
4.8
), we see that

\bar{U}_{k,i,j}\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}(X_{m,t}^{(j)}-P_{m,i,j})

By noting that

\sum_{j\in\Omega}\big(X_{m,t}^{(j)}-P_{m,i,j}\big)^{2}\ =\ 1-2P_{m,i,X_{m,t}}+\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2},

we see that

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{k,i,:}(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\ =\ \sum_{j\in\Omega}\bar{U}_{k,i,j}^{2}\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(1-2P_{m,i,X_{m,t}}+\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\big).

Because
\mathbb{E}[P_{m,i,X_{m,t}}\,|\,X_{m,t-1}]=\sum_{j\in\Omega}P_{m,i,j}^{2}=\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}

on the event
X_{m,t-1}=i
,
the rowwise independence of
X
and the Markov property of
X_{m:}
imply that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\bar{U}_{k,i,:}(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(1-\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\big).

(4.18)

Similarly, the entries of the matrix

(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\lvert\Omega\rvert}\times{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}
satisfy

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{k,i,j}\bar{U}_{k,i,j^{\prime}}\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\big(\delta_{jj^{\prime}}X_{m,t}^{(j)}-X_{m,t}^{(j)}P_{m,i,j^{\prime}}-X_{m,t}^{b^{\prime}}P_{m,i,j}+P_{m,i,j}P_{m,i,j^{\prime}}\big).

The rowwise independence of
X
and the Markov property of
X_{m:}
imply that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\bar{U}_{k,i,j}\bar{U}_{k,i,j^{\prime}}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(\delta_{jj^{\prime}}P_{m,i,j}-P_{m,i,j}P_{m,i,j^{\prime}}\big),

or in matrix form,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\,(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\ =\ X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(\operatorname{diag}(P_{m,i,:})-(P_{m,i,:})^{\top}P_{m,i,:}\big).

(4.19)

Recall that
\bar{U}_{k,i,j}=0
for all
k=m+tM
such that
t=0
.
Therefore, by plugging (
4.18
) into (
4.14
), we see that

\displaystyle W^{\rm col}_{K,i}\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\Big[\bar{U}_{k,i,:}(\bar{U}_{k,i,:})^{\top}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(1-\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\big),

so that (
4.16
) follows.
Similarly, by plugging (
4.19
) into (
4.15
), we see that

W^{\rm row}_{K,i}\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{m,t-1}^{(i)}\big(\operatorname{diag}(P_{m,i,:})-(P_{m,i,:})^{\top}P_{m,i,:}\big),

which confirms (
4.17
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

We fix a state
i
and consider
k\mapsto\bar{V}_{k,i,:}

defined by (
4.9
) as a matrix-valued random process
on state space
\mathbb{R}^{1\times{\lvert\Omega\rvert}}
.
Lemma
4.5
implies that
this process is a martingale, with increments bounded by

\lVert\bar{U}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}
almost surely.
Lemma
4.6
implies that the associated
quadratic variation terms (
4.14
)–(
4.15
) are given by

\displaystyle W^{\rm col}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}(1-\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}),

\displaystyle W^{\rm row}_{K,i}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}\big(\operatorname{diag}(P_{m,i,:})-(P_{m,i,:})^{\top}P_{m,i,:}\big).

The spectral norm of
W^{\rm col}_{K,i}
,
considered as a 1-by-1 matrix,
is bounded by

\lVert W^{\rm col}_{K,i}\rVert_{2}\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}(1-\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2})\ \leq\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}\ =\ N_{i}.

By applying Lemma
A.3

and noting that
1-2P_{m,i,j}+\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1+\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and

\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
, we see that

\displaystyle\lVert W^{\rm row}_{K,i}\rVert_{2}\ \leq\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m,i}\left(\sum_{j\in\Omega}P_{m,i,j}^{2}\big(1-2P_{m,i,j}+\lVert P_{m,i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}\big)\right)^{1/2}\ \leq\ \sqrt{2}N_{i}.

We conclude that

\max\{\lVert W^{\rm row}_{K,i}\rVert_{2},\lVert W^{\rm col}_{K,i}\rVert_{2}\}\ \leq\ \sqrt{2}N_{i}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

We may now apply a matrix Freedman–Tropp martingale concentration
inequality
[
32
, Corollary 1.3]
to conclude that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\lVert\bar{V}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon,\ N_{i}\leq s_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}\big(\lVert\bar{V}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon,\ \max\{\lVert W^{\rm row}_{K,i}\rVert_{2},\lVert W^{\rm col}_{K,i}\rVert_{2}\}\leq\sqrt{2}s_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ (1+{\lvert\Omega\rvert})\exp\Big(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}/2}{\sqrt{2}s_{2}+\sqrt{2}\epsilon/3}\Big).

By recalling (
4.10
),
we note that
\hat{P}_{i,:}-\tilde{P}_{i,:}=\frac{1}{N_{i}}(N_{i,:}-N_{i}\tilde{P}_{i,:})=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\bar{V}_{k,i,:}

on the event that
N_{i}>0
.
It follows that
for any
0<s_{1}\leq s_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lVert\hat{P}_{i,:}-\tilde{P}_{i,:}\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon,\,s_{1}\leq N_{i}\leq s_{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\big(\lVert\bar{V}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon N_{i},\,s_{1}\leq N_{i}\leq s_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}\big(\lVert\bar{V}_{k,i,:}\rVert_{2}\geq\epsilon s_{1},\,N_{i}\leq s_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ (1+{\lvert\Omega\rvert})\exp\Big(-\frac{(\epsilon s_{1})^{2}/2}{\sqrt{2}s_{2}+\sqrt{2}\epsilon s_{1}/3}\Big)

\displaystyle\ =\ (1+{\lvert\Omega\rvert})\exp\Big(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}s_{1}}{6\sqrt{2}s_{2}/s_{1}+2\sqrt{2}\epsilon}\Big).

Finally, the bound
\lVert x\rVert_{1}\leq{\lvert\Omega\rvert}^{1/2}\lVert x\rVert_{2}
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lVert\hat{P}_{i,:}-\tilde{P}_{i,:}\rVert_{1}\geq\epsilon,\,s_{1}\leq N_{i}\leq s_{2})\ \leq\ (1+{\lvert\Omega\rvert})\exp\bigg(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}s_{1}}{6\sqrt{2}{\lvert\Omega\rvert}s_{2}/s_{1}+2\sqrt{2}{\lvert\Omega\rvert}^{1/2}\epsilon}\bigg).

∎

6
Conclusions

This article established consistency results and explicit nonasymptotic error bounds for estimating the transition matrix of a Markov chain from multiple parallel sample paths. We showed that a simple estimator—the empirical transition matrix—is consistent under mild assumptions on the chains’ mixing properties. This aligns with known results for estimation from a single sample path
[
18
,
35
,
36
]
.

A key advance of this work is the ability to rigorously quantify the trade-off between the number of observed paths (
M
) and their length (
T
), thereby generalizing classical settings. In addition, we developed a robust estimation framework that accounts for perturbations in the dynamics and allows for the presence of fully corrupted sample paths.

Several important directions remain open for future research. These include extending the analysis to continuous-time Markov processes, e.g. by following the ideas in
[
17
]
, and to positive recurrent chains on countably infinite state spaces. Robust parameter estimation in such settings would be valuable for applications such as performance analysis of queueing systems and forecasting in epidemiological models. Furthermore, these developments could provide a foundation for tackling more complex scenarios involving partially observed Markov chains, commonly encountered for example in distributed queueing systems
[
19
,
21
]
and stochastic epidemic models
[
8
,
13
]
.

A.1
Information theory

The
Rényi divergence
of order
\alpha>1
is defined by the formula

D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=(\alpha-1)^{-1}\int(\frac{dP}{dQ})^{\alpha}dQ
when
P
is absolutely continuous
against
Q
, and
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\infty
otherwise.
Rényi divergences and
\chi^{2}
-divergences are related by the formula

D_{2}(P\|Q)=\log(1+\chi^{2}(P\|Q))
,
see
[
29
,
33
]
for details.

Lemma A.1
.

For any probability measures
P,Q
, any
\alpha>1
, and any measurable set
A
,

P(A)\ \leq\ e^{(1-1/\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)}\,Q(A)^{1-1/\alpha}.

Proof.

We assume that
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
,
because otherwise
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\infty
and the claim is trivial.
Hölder’s inequality applied to the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{dP}{dQ}
implies that

\displaystyle P(A)\ =\ \int\frac{dP}{dQ}1_{A}\,dQ\ \leq\ \left(\int\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\alpha}dQ\right)^{1/\alpha}\left(\int 1_{A}^{\beta}dQ\right)^{1/\beta},

where
\frac{1}{\beta}=1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
.
Because

\int(\frac{dP}{dQ})^{\alpha}dQ=e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)}

and
\int 1_{A}^{\beta}dQ=Q(A)
,
the claim follows.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
) be the law of a random matrix
(X_{m,t})
with independent rows, in which
each row
m
is a Markov chain with transition matrix

(resp.
\nu_{m}

Then for any

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\mathcal{A})\ \leq\ e^{(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})\sum_{m}D_{\alpha}(\mu_{m}\|\nu_{m})}\ \mathbb{P}_{\nu}(\mathcal{A})^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Proof.

We may write

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}=\bigotimes_{m}f_{m}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}=\bigotimes_{m}g_{m}
, where
f_{m},g_{m}
denote path distributions
of the Markov chains, with probability mass functions

\displaystyle f_{m}(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})

\displaystyle\ =\ \mu_{m}(x_{0})P_{m}(x_{0},x_{1})\cdots P_{m}(x_{T-1},x_{T}),

\displaystyle g_{m}(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})

\displaystyle\ =\ \nu_{m}(x_{0})P_{m}(x_{0},x_{1})\cdots P_{m}(x_{T-1},x_{T}).

Because Rényi divergences tensorise

[
20
,
33

we find that

D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\|\mathbb{P}_{\nu})=\sum_{m}D_{\alpha}(f_{m}\|g_{m}).

Because

\frac{f_{m}(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})}{g_{m}(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})}=\frac{\mu_{m}(x_{0})}{\nu_{m}(x_{0})}
,
we also find that

D_{\alpha}(f_{m}\|g_{m})=D_{\alpha}(\mu_{m}\|\nu_{m}).

Hence

D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\|\mathbb{P}_{\pi})=\sum_{m}D_{\alpha}(\mu_{m}\|\nu_{m}),

and the claim follows by Lemma

A.2
Linear algebra

Lemma A.3
.

For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the spectral norm of
U=\operatorname{diag}(u)-uu^{\top}

is bounded by

\lVert U\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}(1-2u_{i}+\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2})
.

Proof.

Denote by
e_{i}
the
i
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Note that

\displaystyle\lVert Ux\rVert_{2}^{2}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u_{i}x_{i}-u_{i}(u^{\top}x)\big)^{2}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\big((e_{i}-u)^{\top}x\big)^{2}.

Then by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\lVert Ux\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lVert e_{i}-u\rVert_{2}^{2}\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Because the latter inequality holds for all
x
,
we conclude that

\lVert U\rVert_{2}^{2}\ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lVert e_{i}-u\rVert_{2}^{2}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}(1-2u_{i}+\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}).

∎
