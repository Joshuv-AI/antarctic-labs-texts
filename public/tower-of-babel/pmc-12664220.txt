A real-world retrospective study to assess the effectiveness and safety profile of siponimod in Chinese patients with relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis
Authors: Yao Zhang, Hongbo Liu, Zheng Li, Ming Zhou, Min Cai, Lyra Liu, Anne Lin, Yan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on its favourable benefit–risk profile in clinical trials, siponimod was approved in China in 2020 for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis (RMS). We evaluated its real-world (RW) effectiveness and safety profile in patients with RMS in China.MethodsThis was a retrospective chart review of adults (≥ 18 years) with RMS receiving their first siponimod prescription (index date) between 01 August 2020 and 01 August 2022 at two hospitals in China. Inclusion criteria were ≥ 3 months of siponimod treatment and ≥ 1 clinical visit after the index date. Annualised relapse rate (ARR), proportion of relapse-free patients, proportion of patients free of MRI activity, and incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were evaluated.ResultsOf 113 included patients (mean age, 37.2 years; 64.6% women), 70 (61.9%) received ≥ 1 disease-modifying treatment (other than siponimod) before index. Most patients (89.4%) were diagnosed with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis, and 85.0% remained on siponimod for > 12 months. Overall, 16 patients (14.2%) experienced ≥ 1 relapse (14 patients had one relapse and two had two relapses), and 85 patients (75.2%) remained relapse-free. Of 46 patients with ≥ 1 MRI assessment, 36 (78.3%) were free of MRI activity. The mean group-based ARR was 0.1 per patient-year. Overall, 71 patients (62.8%) experienced ≥ 1 TEAE, and 56 (49.6%) had TEAEs related to siponimod. The most common TEAEs by preferred term were increased alanine transaminase (23.0%) and increased aspartate transaminase (13.3%). No new safety signals were identified.ConclusionsThis RW study confirmed favourable benefit–risk profile of siponimod in Chinese patients with RMS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04343-w.Keywords: Siponimod, Relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis, Annualised relapse rate, Real-world, Chinese patients

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory neurological condition that leads to severe non-traumatic disability [1]. Based on its clinical course, MS is classified into relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS), secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS), and primary progressive MS [1]. The term clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) denotes the first clinical onset of a potential MS case [2].

In China, MS is classified as a rare disease, with a national incidence of 0.1 per 100,000 person-years in children and 0.3 per 100,000 person-years in adults, as well as an age- and sex-adjusted incidence of 0.2 per 100,000 person-years [3].

Although there is no definitive cure for MS, several disease-modifying treatments (DMTs) are available to reduce the risk of relapse recurrence and disability progression [4, 5]. Sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor (S1PR) modulators approved for MS treatment include fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, and ponesimod [6]. Although further research is needed to fully understand their therapeutic potential, S1PR modulators seem to offer several advantages among other MS treatments, including oral administration, good tolerability, a favourable safety profile under appropriate monitoring, and the potential to slow neurodegeneration [6]. They all have similar mechanisms of action and indications, although second-generation S1PR modulators similar to siponimod have been reported to be more effective in more advanced forms of MS, like SPMS, in addition to having a more favourable safety profile and shorter washout periods [6, 7]. In 2019, siponimod received its first global approval from the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of relapsing forms of MS (RMS) in adults, including CIS, RRMS, and active SPMS [8, 9]. The phase II, adaptive, dose-ranging BOLD study showed a reduction in lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as well as in the annualised relapse rate (ARR) over a treatment period of 6 months in adult patients with RRMS on siponimod [8]. In the largest phase III study, EXPAND, siponimod was found to significantly reduce the risk of disability progression, relapse rate, and MRI activity compared with placebo, in patients with SPMS [10]. Based on these positive clinical outcomes, siponimod was approved in China in May 2020 for the treatment of RMS in adults, which includes CIS, RRMS, and active SPMS [11].

This study aims to further investigate the real-world (RW) effectiveness and safety profile of siponimod among adult patients with RMS in China.

Methods

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study was to describe the effectiveness of siponimod in terms of ARR in Chinese patients with RMS from the RW population. The secondary objectives were to evaluate the proportion of patients with no relapse at 12 months after siponimod initiation; proportion of patients free of MRI activity at 12 months after siponimod initiation and to describe the safety profile of siponimod in patients with RMS in a Chinese RW population.

Study design and settings

This was a multicentre, non-interventional, retrospective chart review of adult patients with RMS initiating siponimod treatment. Data were collected retrospectively through medical records review and abstraction conducted at a single time point per patient by the investigator’s site staff between 01 August 2020 and 01 August 2022 (identification period) (Fig. 1). The study was conducted at two tertiary care hospitals in China.

Study schematic. aIdentification period: time in which patients meet the selection criteria. bIndex date: date of first recorded prescription of siponimod. cObservation period: from the index date to first of data abstraction date, patient withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, or death

The index date was defined as the date of first record of siponimod prescription in the medical records within the identification period of a patient diagnosed with RMS. The observation period lasted from the index date to the date of medical records abstraction, patient withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, or death, whichever occurred first. Effectiveness data were collected from the index date to the end of the observation period. Safety data were collected during siponimod treatment and up to 30 days after the last dose of siponimod.

Data source

Patients’ medical records (e.g. consultation notes, discharge summaries, laboratory test results, recorded prescription data, imaging data, and any other documentation of communication with other healthcare providers) were collected and entered by site staff into web-based electronic case report forms (eCRFs).

Patient population

Patients were included in the analysis during the identification period if they were diagnosed with RMS (including CIS, RRMS, and active SPMS, as defined by clinical staff based on patients’ records in medical charts), were aged ≥ 18 years at the index date, were on siponimod treatment for ≥ 3 months after the index date, attended at least one documented clinical visit after the index date, and provided written informed consent to participate. Patients with RMS treated with siponimod (including participation in clinical trials) before the index date were excluded.

The full analysis set (FAS) comprised all patients from the enrolled population who had a non-missing index date. Analyses of effectiveness endpoints (i.e. ARR, proportion of patients who remained relapse-free at 12 months after the index date, and proportion of patients who remained MRI activity-free at 12 months after the index date) are based on the FAS. In this study, the FAS was the same as the enrolled population.

The safety analysis set (SAF) comprised patients from the enrolled population who received at least one dose of siponimod during the observation period. Safety endpoints were analysed based on the SAF.

Study outcomes

Demographic and baseline characteristics evaluated before or at the index date included age, sex, medical history and comorbidities (in the year prior to the index date), MS history (including clinical relapse in the year prior to the index date), brain MRI results prior to the index date, and MS treatment history (from diagnosis to the index date). MRI information was provided by clinical staff through the CRF based on records in patients’ medical charts. MRI information collected included contrast media, whether there was a new T1 Gd-enhancing lesion, number of new T1 Gd-enhancing lesions, any new or enlarging T2 lesions, number of new or enlarging T2 lesions, and whether the physician considered this record as free of new MRI activity. Siponimod dose interruptions after the index date (defined as any days with no drug taken during treatment exposure) were also evaluated. Clinical disability was assessed on the basis of the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score, as documented in the patients’ medical charts.

The study outcomes evaluated after the index date included clinical relapse information to calculate ARR, brain MRI outcomes, laboratory assessments, and safety outcomes. These are defined in Supplementary Table 1.

The safety outcomes of interest are also described in Supplementary Table 1. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were defined as any adverse events (AEs) that were not present before siponimod initiation, or an already present AE that worsened either in intensity or frequency following treatment initiation. All AEs recorded in this study were TEAEs. TEAEs were coded based on the preferred terms and system organ classes in MedDRA [12].

Statistical analysis

There was no hypothesis testing for effectiveness endpoints. All effectiveness endpoints (i.e. ARR and proportions of patients who were relapse- and MRI activity-free at 12 months after the index date) were summarised descriptively with or without statistical modelling.

For the primary endpoint analysis, ARR was calculated using the total number of relapses after the index date divided by the total person-days of follow-up after the index date, multiplied by 365.25. The total follow-up duration in years was calculated as the difference between the study end date and the index date + 1, divided by 365.25. Both group- and patient-based ARR were presented. Group-based ARR was calculated as the total number of relapses divided by the total follow-up duration (days), multiplied by 365.25 (formula below).

Patient-based ARR was calculated as the total number of relapses per patient divided by the total follow-up duration per patient (days), multiplied by 365.25. Standard deviation (SD) was calculated for patient-based ARR.

Adjusted ARR estimates with 95% CIs were presented, calculated using a negative binomial regression model with log-link function. The dependent variable in the negative binomial regression was the total number of relapses for each patient. To account for variations in the total follow-up duration among patients in the study, the natural log of the total follow-up duration in the study (years) was used as offset. Model covariates included age (in years), relapse number in the year prior to the index date, and MS type at the index date (RRMS or non-RRMS).

For the secondary endpoint analysis, the proportion of patients treated with siponimod who remained relapse-free at 12 months after the index date and the proportion of patients treated with siponimod who were free of MRI activity at 12 months after the index date were summarised by counts and percentage. Patients who switched to other DMTs or discontinued the siponimod treatment early within the first 12 months were excluded from the group without documented relapse.

The proportions of TEAEs were calculated as the number of patients with safety events divided by the total number of patients included in the SAF. Additionally, the exposure-adjusted incidence rate (EAIR) and confidence interval (CI) were summarised for TEAEs. The EAIR was defined as the number of patients with a particular AE divided by the total time of exposure to siponimod among patients at risk of initial occurrence of the event.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of patients being on other DMTs on ARR. To this end, ARR was recalculated considering the overall number of relapses occurring while on siponimod treatment from the index date to the end of the study (or start of subsequent DMTs for patients who switched to other DMTs during the study). The following endpoints were reported: total number of relapses; total follow-up duration in years (calculated as the total follow-up duration in days/365.25); ARR (patient- and group-based); and adjusted ARR with 95% CI.

Ethic approval

This non-interventional study involved a retrospective review of patients’ medical charts. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and EC approval was received from the Ethical Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital on 8 December 2022. Regulatory submissions were performed according to national and local requirements for non-interventional studies.

Data were collected through a review of medical records conducted by the investigator’s site staff. Patients were asked by the site staff to sign an Informed Consent Form prior to participation in the study. For those who are deceased or cannot be contacted, waivers of informed consent were granted by The Ethical Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital and The Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University.

During data entry in the eCRF, patients were anonymized by assigning each participant a unique study number. Personal identifiable information was not collected, and all data protection and privacy regulations were followed during the capture, forwarding, processing, and storage of patient data.

No experiments involving humans or human tissue samples were conducted during the study.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 113 enrolled patients, 73 (64.6%) were female, and the mean (SD) age was 37.2 (11.2) years (Table 1). Around half the patients had at least one comorbidity (n = 53; 46.9%). Overall, 70 patients (61.9%) received at least one DMT (except siponimod) before the index date; the most common prior treatments were teriflunomide (n = 46, 65.7%) and steroids (n = 39, 34.5%). A total of 49 patients (43.3%) had an Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) [13] assessment prior to the index date, with a mean (SD) EDSS score of 2.6 (1.9) and a median of 2.0 (Supplementary Table 2). Most patients (n = 101, 89.4%) were diagnosed with RRMS, 11 patients (9.7%) with active SPMS, and 1 patient (0.9%) with CIS (Supplementary Table 2). In total, 96 patients (85.0%) were on siponimod treatment for ≥ 12 months after the index date for a median (Q1, Q3) duration of 18.2 (14.8, 23.3) months (Table 1). Only six patients (5.3%) had one or more dose interruptions, with most of these patients (n = 5, 83.3%) having only one dose interruption. Three patients interrupted treatment due to AEs, two due to unknown reasons, and one due to conception planning (Table 1).

Treatment discontinuation and dose change

A total of 31 patients (27.4%) discontinued siponimod treatment, for various reasons: AEs (n = 9, 8.0%), lack of adherence/non-compliance (n = 6, 5.3%), lack of efficacy (n = 5, 4.4%), pregnancy (n = 1, 0.9%), economic reasons (n = 1, 0.9%), and unknown reasons (n = 9, 8.0%).

Of the 113 patients, seven patients (6.2%) had at least one dose change (defined as dose reduction, escalation, or interruption that is not due to treatment initiation titration or maintenance dosage). Of these, five patients (71.4%) changed dose once. The most commonly reported reason for dose changes was AEs (n = 5, 71.4%; two patients with increased alanine transaminase [ALT], one patient with decreased neutrophil count, one patient with hepatotoxicity, and one patient with increased hepatic enzyme).

Effectiveness of Siponimod

The mean (SD) patient-based ARR and group-based ARR were 0.12 (0.38) per patient-year and 0.10 per patient-year, respectively (Table 2). After adjusting for factors including age, relapse number in the year prior to index, and MS subtype at index, the adjusted ARR was 0.08 (95% CI: 0.04, 0.13).

In the sensitivity analysis with extended follow-up until the end of study, a total of 18 patients (15.9%) had a relapse, with a mean (SD) total number of relapses of 1.1 (0.3). Most of these patients (n = 16, 88.9%) had one relapse. The mean (SD) total follow-up duration was 1.8 (0.5) years. The mean (SD) patient-based ARR was 0.1 (0.2), the group-based ARR was 0.1, and the adjusted ARR (95% CI) was 0.08 (0.04, 0.13) (Supplementary Table 3).

Safety profile

Overall, 71 patients (62.8%) experienced at least one TEAE, with an EAIR of 0.6 (95% CI: 0.5, 0.8), and 56 patients (49.6%) had TEAEs related to siponimod with an EAIR of 0.4 (95% CI: 0.3, 0.6) (Table 5). Most of the TEAEs were either mild (n = 50, 44.2%) or moderate (n = 16, 14.2%), and only six TEAEs (5.3%) were severe. Severity information was missing for 28 patients (24.8%).

The most common TEAEs (≥ 10%) by system organ class (SOC) were investigations (42.5%), hepatobiliary disorders (12.4%), and infections and infestations (10.6%). The most common TEAEs by preferred term (PT) were increased ALT (23.0%), increased aspartate transaminase (13.3%), and decreased lymphocyte count (12.4%).

TEAEs leading to siponimod treatment discontinuation were reported in nine patients (8.0%) (Table 5), of which the most common were investigations (7.1%) based on SOC, and increased ALT (2.7%) based on PT.

TEAEs of special interest were observed in 61 patients (54.0%) (95% CI: 44.4, 63.3) (Table 5). The most commonly reported (≥ 10%) TEAEs of special interest by SOC were investigations (n = 46, 40.7%), hepatobiliary disorders (n = 14, 12.4%), and infections and infestations (n = 12, 10.6%). No opportunistic infections, bradyarrhythmia, hypertension, bronchoconstriction, macular oedema, seizures or epilepsy, malignancies or skin malignancies, cases of Hy’s law, serious TEAEs, or deaths were reported. No new or unexpected safety signals were identified.

Discussion

This study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of siponimod in China in RW settings. Among the 113 patients included in the study, most had RRMS at initial diagnosis (92.9%) and at index (89.4%), which is in line with the general understanding that approximately 85.0–90.0% of patients with MS are initially diagnosed with RRMS [1, 14].

Siponimod treatment has shown significant effectiveness in reducing ARR in SPMS patients, with a notable decrease from 60 to 12% over one year in a previous real-world study [15]. The adjusted ARR of 0.08 per patient per year reported in this study reinforces these findings. As described in Table 6, our ARR was similar to that observed in patients with SPMS in the EXPAND study (0.07) [16], but the BOLD study reported a higher rate of 0.2–0.6 for patients with RRMS [8]. We reported relapses in 14.2% of patients, which was higher than that in a RW study by Regner-Nelke et al. conducted in Germany (7.4%).17 In addition, as compared with our study, the EXPAND extension part (extended to up to 5 years of follow-up) reported a lower ARR of 0.04 (in patients with SPMS) [18]. The proportion of patients (78.3%) free from MRI activity at 12 months found in the present study was lower than that observed in the EXPAND study (89.0% over a median duration of 21 months) [18] and slightly higher than that reported by Regner-Nelke et al. (70.4% over 12 months) [17]. These differences must be interpreted with caution, as the studies differed in purpose, setting (e.g. prospective vs. retrospective), patient selection (the EXPAND study only included patients with SPMS), MRI monitoring frequency, outcome definition (e.g. relapse- and MRI activity-free), and follow-up duration.

The most common TEAEs reported in this study were investigations (43%) according to the MedDRA classification by POC and ALT (23%) based on MedDRA PT. These results align with Stavrogianni et al.’s study, which reported that 78% of patients experienced ≤ grade 3 lymphopenia, and 24% of patients experienced elevated levels of ALT [15]. Our study observed a lower rate of patients with at least one TEAE (62.8%) than that reported in the EXPAND study for patients with SPMS (89.0%), although the proportions of TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation were comparable (8.0% in both studies) [18]. The proportion of patients with at least one TEAE observed in the present study was also lower than that reported in the BOLD study, which focused on patients with RRMS (69.0% for patients receiving 1.25 mg of siponimod and 98.0% for those receiving 2 mg) [8]. These differences may reflect the different ways in which AEs are captured in clinical trials and RW settings.

The elevated liver enzyme levels observed in the present study were mostly mild and did not lead to dose modification. Six patients reported severe hepatic TEAEs (increased ALT: n = 4; increased AST: n = 1; increased hepatic enzyme: n = 1; hepatotoxicity: n = 1). Of these, three patients discontinued the treatment; these patients recovered from the events by the end of study. In these six patients, no events of relevant clinical symptoms or increased bilirubin were reported. It is important to note that the TEAE profile observed in the present study was in line with the adverse drug reactions listed in the siponimod product label [19].

This study has several strengths. The two hospitals represented the top hospitals at the country and provincial levels, with a representative pool of patients with RMS. Additionally, site selection was planned to ensure the inclusion of sufficient sample size from hospitals that had a large pool of patients with RMS, along with appropriate resources of facilities, staff, and investigators experienced in treating patients with MS. This study has some limitations, particularly methodological ones, commonly associated with retrospective chart reviews, such as selection bias and limited availability and validity of data. We minimised the risks associated with these limitations by adopting broad eligibility criteria, an appropriate data collection time frame, clear definitions for the variables of interest, and detailed eCRF completion guidelines. However, some variables may have been incomplete or not available, since they were not specifically collected for the study. Although this study collected qualitative information on lab test measurements (i.e. whether the lab values are elevated, and researchers were asked to assess the severity and record it), the specific numerical values were not available, so metrics such as the fold increase in abnormal elevation are not available. Additionally, this study was not designed to evaluate the 'no evidence of disease activity' state. Incorporating such information in future research could provide useful therapeutic guidance. Finally, relatively rare AEs, such as opportunistic infections and cancer, were not observed in this study, and future research should confirm their frequency in these patients.

Conclusion

This study provides RW evidence confirming phase II and III clinical trial data on the favourable benefit–risk profile of siponimod for patients with RMS in China. The effectiveness and safety profiles of siponimod in our study were comparable with those reported in previous pivotal studies.
