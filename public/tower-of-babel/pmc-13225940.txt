Black Pleural Effusion Related to Lung Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Koichi Yano, Kyoko Yokosuka, Takuto Ishida
Published: 2026

An 81-year-old man with Alzheimer’s dementia was admitted for fever and sputum discharge four days prior. The patient had undergone gastric cancer surgery 15 years previously, and had no other significant medical history.

Chest computed tomography indicated massive pleural effusion and collapse of the right lung (Fig. 1A). Thoracostomy tube placement revealed black fluid (Fig. 1B). Blood tests demonstrated hemoglobin levels of 9.4 g/dL and red blood cell (RBC) 333×104/µL. Tests on pleural fluid showed cell count of 2,011/µL (neutrophils 1,385/µL, lymphocytes 0/µL, monocytes 138/µL, eosinophils 488/µL), total protein 10.7 g/dL, red blood cell (RBC) 113/µL, lactase dehydrogenase (LDH) 6,726 U/L, glucose 0 mg/dL, total bilirubin level 10.6 mg/dL, direct bilirubin level 1.3 mg/dL and pH was not examined. Cytological examination of the pleural fluid showed no evidence of malignancy, and cultures were negative. Further investigation of malignancy could not be conducted because of the patient’s poor performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] performance status [PS] 4). He was treated for aspiration pneumonia with ampicillin/sulbactam but died two weeks after admission.

(A) Massive pleural effusion (white arrow) and collapse of the lung (red arrow) on computed tomography at admission. (B) Black pleural fluid in chest drain bag.

Postmortem examination revealed a mass invading the pleura in the right upper and lower lobes, which was established as lung adenocarcinoma based on immunohistochemical staining. Thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) were positive, and cytokeratin 5/6 (CK 5/6) was negative (Fig. 2).

Histopathology slides. (A) Tumor cells with enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei showing a papillary growth pattern (hematoxylin and eosin stain, magnification ×20). (B) Positive for TTF-1 (Immunohistochemistry, magnification ×20). (C) Positive for CEA (Immunohistochemistry, magnification ×20). (D) Negative for CK 5/6 (Immunohistochemistry, magnification ×20).

Black pleural effusion is a rare entity. The most common causes include pancreaticopleural fistula, malignant melanoma, and fungal infection (e.g., Aspergillus niger or Rhizopus oryzae).1 Lung carcinoma has also been reported to cause black discoloration of the pleural effusion.2,3,4,5

Black discoloration of pleural effusion could have been due to hemorrhage and hemolysis (e.g., lung carcinoma and pancreaticopleural fistula), melanin deposition (e.g., malignant melanoma), black-pigment spores (e.g., Aspergillus niger), or necrotic debris (e.g., Rhizopus oryzae).1

Routine pleural fluid tests, such as cell count, total protein, LDH, glucose, bacterial culture, and cytology, are not sufficient to differentiate between these causes. Fungal culture, amylase, and bilirubin levels are useful for narrowing down the underlying disease.

In our case, the indirect bilirubin content of the pleural effusion was high and postmortem examination demonstrated adhesions of the right pulmonary apex and pleura. Elevated indirect bilirubin levels in the pleural fluid suggest that the black pleural effusion resulted from hemorrhage and hemolysis.6 In a previously reported case of lung adenocarcinoma, slow bleeding allowed for the asymptomatic development of massive black pleural effusion.3 Similarly, prolonged microbleeding from the site of pleural adhesions may have contributed to the development of the black pleural effusion in the present case. In acute hemorrhage, RBC counts in pleural fluid are typically high; however, the low levels observed in our patient are more consistent with chronic hemorrhage and hemolysis. Taken together, pulmonary adenocarcinoma was considered to be the most likely cause of the black pleural effusion.

In previous cases, diagnosis of lung cancer was made using biopsy.2,3,4,5 In our case, owing to the patient’s poor condition, a biopsy could not be performed. Therefore, we were forced to rely on cytology for diagnosis. The uniqueness of this case is derived from the postmortem examination that led to a diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma despite negative cytological findings.

Although black pleural effusion is rare, clinicians should be aware that it can result from chronic bleeding and hemolysis associated with lung cancer, even when the cytological findings are negative.
