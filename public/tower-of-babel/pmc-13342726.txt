Anesthetic challenges in tracheal resection: A case series on fast track extubation with cross-field ventilation for patients undergoing tracheoplasty
Authors: Shefali Gautam, Shashank Kumar, Anurag Rai, Shailendra K. Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTracheal resection and anastomosis present significant anesthetic challenges owing to the shared airway and surgical field between the anesthesiologist and the surgeon. Ensuring adequate ventilation while maintaining optimal surgical access requires meticulous planning and coordination. This case series describes four patients who underwent tracheal resection and anastomosis, where distal tracheal intubation with cross-field ventilation was employed to maintain oxygenation during airway transection and reconstruction. Fast-track extubation in the operating room was successfully achieved in all cases to minimize airway trauma and promote early recovery. Perioperative anesthetic strategies were individualized according to patient anatomy, lesion location, and associated comorbidities. The use of cross-field ventilation provided effective control over airway dynamics and minimized the risk of hypercapnia during tracheoplasty. This experience highlights the importance of individualized anesthetic planning, precise airway management, close interdisciplinary collaboration, and timely extubation to ensure optimal outcomes in patients undergoing tracheal surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Tracheal stenosis can result from various etiologies, including post-intubation injury, trauma, malignancy, or inflammatory conditions.[123] In severe cases, the definitive treatment is surgical resection with end-to-end anastomosis. Shared airway anatomy, requirement for uninterrupted ventilation, and the risk of intraoperative complications make the anesthetic management more difficult and demanding. This case series presents a standardized anesthetic approach utilizing distal tracheal intubation with cross-field ventilation for optimal surgical exposure and patient safety.

CASE PRESENTATIONS

Case 1: A 31-year-old man referred to the department of thoracic surgery with complaints of breathing difficulty for 1 month. The patient had a history of mechanical ventilation and tracheostomy for 30 days due to head injury from a road traffic accident. The patient was asymptomatic at the time of discharge, but after a few days, he complained of difficulty in breathing and respiratory distress after mild exertion and so reported to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, where he was diagnosed with tracheal stenosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) neck and thorax revealed stenosis in the lower part of trachea at the level of T1 [Figure 1].

Case 2: A 20-year-old male reported to the department of thoracic surgery with his attendant presenting as a case of tracheal stenosis after head injury due to a road traffic accident. He underwent decompressive craniotomy and was put on mechanical ventilation for one and half months with tracheostomy. He was discharged with a tracheostomy tube in situ after 2 months of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) stay. He was given multiple trials of decannulation of tracheostomy tube, but it failed. His CECT neck and thorax revealed a mild to moderate stenotic segment of approximately 2.5 cm in length in the middle part of the trachea; the lower limit of thickening was approx. 2.6 cm above the carina [Figure 2].

Case 3: A 16-year-old boy was referred to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology as a case of post-intubation tracheal stenosis. He had a history of viral pneumonia and was intubated. He was discharged from the ward but later developed inspiratory stridor. He subsequently reported to the Otorhinolaryngology Out-Patients Department (OPD). He was investigated further and transferred to the department of thoracic surgery after high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) neck and thorax revealed focal narrowing of the cervical segment of trachea 25 mm below the vocal cord involving a segment of approximately 14.5 mm and diameter 10 × 3.8 mm [Figure 3].

Case 4: A 46-year-old male presented to the emergency department with respiratory distress. According to the patient’s attendant, he suddenly developed difficulty in breathing following a viral infection. He was immediately intubated with 6.0 mm internal diameter (ID) endotracheal tube and subsequently underwent tracheostomy with continued oxygen support. On further investigation, he was diagnosed as a case of growth in the subglottic larynx with >50% narrowing with the tracheostomy tube in situ. Evidence of soft tissue lesion measuring 23 × 12 × 12 mm in size is seen in the subglottic region of the larynx causing severe luminal narrowing >70–80% (CECT neck and thorax finding) [Figure 4].

ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

Preanesthetic evaluation was conducted for all patients, including assessment of investigations such as CECT scan of the neck and thorax detailing the tracheal diameter of the stenosed segment. In the operating room, standard ASA monitors including noninvasive blood pressure, SpO2, and electrocardiogram, and baseline vital parameters were recorded. One 18 G peripheral intravenous (IV) line was secured, and fluid administration was initiated. After premedication and nebulization, a dexmedetomidine infusion was started, and once adequate sedation was achieved, as was confirmed by comfortable passage of a fiberoptic bronchoscope, endotracheal intubation was performed using a pediatric fiberoptic bronchoscope. A neuromuscular monitor was applied after sedation. We initially placed a 5/5.5 mm internal diameter (ID)-cuffed endotracheal tube (ETT) passing through the stenotic segment depending upon the stenosis. Once the proximal ETT was in place, Injection Vecuronium 0.1 mg/kg iv was administered. Anesthesia was maintained on oxygen, medical air, and propofol infusion at 10–15 mg/kg/hour. Intermittently Inj. Fentanyl 0.5 mcg/kg IV and Inj. Vecuronium 0.01 mg/kg were administered. Also, one more 18 G IV cannula and arterial cannula were placed after securing the airway. We performed bilateral ultrasound-guided superficial cervical plexus block with 10 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine each side for analgesia. After surgical incision below the stenotic segment, the trachea was intubated distal to the stenotic segment by a sterile armored ETT 7/7.5 mm ID before proceeding with the tracheal resection. For performing cross-field ventilation, we used a fresh sterile anesthesia circuit over the surgical field and connected to the anesthesia workstation [Figure 5]. Once the ventilation is confirmed, we remove the small size oral ETT. This technique ensured uninterrupted oxygenation and ventilation while providing optimal surgical access. The stenotic segment of the trachea was removed with tracheal mobilization from both distal and proximal with end-to-end anastomosis of both distal and proximal parts of the trachea at the membranous or posterior side [Figure 6]. Now, a normal size ETT of 7/7.5 mm ID was placed orally just proximal to the site of tracheal anastomosis and the distal armored ETT was removed. Before completing the anterior suturing, the ETT was carefully repositioned past the anastomotic site under fiberoptic guidance to maintain continuous ventilation while preventing airway compromise. Hemodynamic stability was ensured throughout the procedure with invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring. Ventilation was maintained using a pressure-regulated volume control mode with lung-protective settings, including a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, a frequency of 14–18/min, a positive end expiratory pressure of 5–6 cmH2O, a targeted plateau pressure of 30 cmH2O, and end tidal CO2 values of 35–45 mmHg. Apart from superficial cervical plexus block, multimodal analgesia was ensured using intravenous paracetamol (15 mg/kg), diclofenac sodium (1 mg/kg), and fentanyl (2 mcg/kg).

Following the completion of the procedure, check bronchoscopy was conducted to clear secretions from the tracheobronchial tree and to perform a final assessment of the integrity of the anastomosis. For reversal, Inj. Sugammadex 2 mg/kg was administered when the TOF value was 0.9 and above. We extubated the patient under deep sedation in the operating room itself over an airway exchange catheter.

DISCUSSION

Managing the airway during tracheal resection demands a dynamic and coordinated approach as it involves balancing effective ventilation with uninterrupted surgical exposure. This case series explores our experience with cross-field ventilation and fast track extubation in the operating room, emphasizing their role in managing patients undergoing tracheoplasty.

A thorough preoperative assessment is crucial for determining the severity of tracheal stenosis, optimizing pulmonary function, and planning airway management. Imaging modalities such as high-resolution CT and bronchoscopy provide vital information about the location, length, and degree of stenosis.[4] Virtual bronchoscopy, reconstructed from CT data, provides noninvasive 3D visualization of the airway, aiding in evaluation of tracheal pathologies, anastomotic integrity, and restenosis, especially when conventional bronchoscopy is high-risk. Pulmonary function tests may offer additional insights but are often effort-dependent and less reliable. Patient optimization includes cessation of smoking, aggressive pulmonary hygiene, and the use of preoperative corticosteroids to minimize airway edema. An individualized approach to airway control is essential as each patient presents unique anatomic and physiological challenges.

Various anesthetic techniques have been described for tracheal surgery, including standard endotracheal intubation with intermittent ventilation, jet ventilation, high-frequency positive pressure ventilation (HFPPV), and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) for complex cases.[5] However, distal tracheal intubation with cross-field ventilation is the most widely used due to its effectiveness in maintaining ventilation while preserving surgical access. After induction of anesthesia, initial airway control is achieved using a standard ETT, ideally placed proximal to the stenotic segment. Once the surgical field is exposed, the ETT is withdrawn, and a sterile, reinforced ETT is inserted beyond the site of resection. Ventilation is maintained using a separate anesthesia circuit connected to the sterile ETT, ensuring uninterrupted oxygenation and ventilation while enabling clear visualization of the surgical field.[6]

Cross-field ventilation maintains adequate ventilation, preserves surgical access, facilitates precise anastomosis, and minimizes surgical contamination.[7] In a previous case study conducted by Gautam et al., a patient of tracheal mass was managed with distal tracheal intubation with cross-field ventilation and after resection of the mass, end-to-end anastomosis was performed. While effective, this technique demands close coordination between the surgical and anesthesia teams to ensure seamless transitions between ventilation modes.[8] Surgeons must be adept at repositioning the distal ETT during anastomosis to prevent undue traction on the fresh suture line. Compared to alternative techniques, jet ventilation offers an unobstructed surgical field but is associated with potential barotrauma, air trapping, and inadequate CO2 clearance.[9] HFPPV provides a relatively still surgical field but carries a risk of barotrauma and requires meticulous control of tidal volumes. ECMO is reserved for patients with severe airway obstruction but has limitations due to risks of coagulopathy, vascular injury, and hemodilution.[10]

Extubation was performed postoperatively in the operating room itself unless there is a clear indication for continued mechanical ventilation. Removing the ETT soon after surgery helps protect the newly formed tracheal anastomosis from the potential adverse effects of positive pressure ventilation and mechanical trauma caused by the tube cuff. Successful extubation is typically achieved in the operating room following an anastomotic leak test, where positive pressure (20–30 cm H2O) is applied to assess airway integrity.[11] The fast track extubation approach is further supported by the findings of Mulakaluri et al., who reported successful on-table extubation in all patients undergoing supracarinal tracheal reconstruction using cross-field ventilation and the apnea–ventilation–apnea technique. Their study highlighted early extubation as a safe, cost-effective strategy that reduced Intensive Care Unit (ICU) stays and postoperative complications, with a 0% in-hospital mortality rate and minimal adverse events.[12] Their experience validates the strategy of on-table extubation, provided that airway integrity is confirmed via anastomotic leak testing and the patient is hemodynamically stable with preserved respiratory reflexes. Patients should be carefully monitored in a high-dependency unit (HDU) or ICU to detect early complications such as airway edema, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, anastomotic dehiscence, or pneumonia.[13] Postoperative care strategies include multimodal analgesia, humidified oxygen therapy, avoidance of coughing and straining, and head flexion positioning to reduce tension on anastomosis.[11]

Distal tracheal intubation with cross-field ventilation remains the gold standard for airway management in tracheoplasty, offering a balance between effective ventilation and optimal surgical exposure. While alternative techniques may be beneficial in selected cases, cross-field ventilation provides superior control over airway dynamics, reduced risk of hypercapnia, and enhanced patient safety.[14] This case series underscores the importance of individualized anesthetic planning, meticulous execution of airway strategies, close interdisciplinary collaboration, and fast track extubation in the operating room for successful outcomes in tracheal resection and anastomosis.

CONCLUSIONS

Cross-field ventilation offers superior control over airway dynamics and minimizes the risk of hypercapnia during tracheoplasty. This series emphasizes the value of individualized anesthetic planning, precise execution of airway strategies, interdisciplinary coordination, and early extubation to optimize outcomes in tracheal surgery.
