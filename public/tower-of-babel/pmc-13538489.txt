Atomically dispersed Ti on MnOx-Fe2O3 tailors O 2p orbitals for CO oxidation and H2O/SO2 resistance
Authors: Yongqi Zhao, Pu Wang, Junjie Jiang, Ziwei Zhao, Aorui Xiu, Xiaolong Liu, Tingyu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transition-metal oxides offer a low-cost alternative to precious metals for environmental catalysis, yet CO oxidation under humid and sulfur-laden conditions remains challenging due to rapid poisoning. Herein, we report an atomic dispersion of Ti‑decorated MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst that achieves high activity and remarkable H2O/SO2 resistance. Atomically dispersed Ti provides electron-accepting 3d states from O 2p orbitals, which redistributes and stabilizes selected O 2p states while modifying their hybridization with neighboring Mn/Fe 3d orbitals. The localized electronic restructuring strengthens CO adsorption and facilitates lattice oxygen activation via a Mars-van Krevelen (MvK)-like mechanism, lowering the apparent activation energy to 64.59 kJ/mol. Simultaneously, Ti-O-Fe interfaces weaken SO2 adsorption (from −1.96 eV to −0.19 eV) and suppress H2O competition, retaining >55% CO conversion under 10 vol% H2O and 10 ppm SO2, far surpassing the parent oxide (<10%). The orbital tuning strategy enables scalable poison resistant catalysts for industrial emission control, reducing reliance on precious‑metal systems.

Introduction

The catalytic oxidation of carbon monoxide (CO) to carbon dioxide (CO2) is a cornerstone reaction in environmental remediation, with critical implications for mitigating toxic gas emissions from automobile exhausts, industrial flue gases, and indoor air1–3. Despite decades of research, achieving efficient CO oxidation under real-world conditions, where water vapor (H2O) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) are omnipresent, remains a formidable challenge4–7. While precious metal catalysts (e.g., Pt, Pd) exhibit exceptional activity, their scarcity, high cost, and vulnerability to poisoning have spurred the search for earth-abundant, cost-effective alternatives8–11. Transition metal-based oxides, particularly Fe-Mn composites, have emerged as promising candidates due to their tunable redox properties, oxygen vacancy formation capability, and synergistic interactions between Fe and Mn species12–14. The superior CO oxidation performance of Fe-Mn-based materials arises from their high surface area, confined active sites, and structural stability, which collectively enhance reactivity compared to bulk or disordered counterparts15,16. However, a persistent bottleneck in their practical deployment is the rapid deactivation induced by competitive adsorption of H2O and irreversible binding of SO2, which block active sites and alter surface chemistry17,18. Addressing the limitation requires a fundamental redesign of the catalyst’s surface functionality to decouple activity from poison resistance.

The coexistence of H2O and SO2 in realistic reaction environments (e.g., automotive exhaust with ~5–10% H2O, industrial flue gas with <35 mg/m3 SO2) poses a dual threat to catalyst stability. H2O molecules competitively occupy oxygen vacancies or metal sites, hindering CO adsorption and oxygen activation, while SO2 tends to form stable sulfate/sulfite species on the catalyst surface, leading to permanent deactivation. Traditional Fe-Mn oxides rely on bulk-phase interactions (e.g., Fe3+/Fe2+ redox couples, Mn4+/Mn3+) to generate oxygen species for CO oxidation, but their surface sites are inherently prone to poisoning by these small molecules15,19,20. Recent advances highlight that surface engineering, rather than bulk composition modification, is key to enhancing poison resistance. For instance, defect engineering (e.g., oxygen vacancies) or dopant incorporation (e.g., Ce, Cu) can modulate surface electronic states, but these approaches often sacrifice intrinsic activity or provide only marginal improvements in H2O/SO2 tolerance21–24. Thus, there is an urgent need for a precise, atomic-level strategy to functionalize Fe-Mn surfaces with species that can selectively repel H2O/SO2 while maintaining CO adsorption and oxygen activation capabilities25.

Catalysts featuring atomically dispersed metal species offer a highly promising approach by confining metal atoms in isolated coordination environments, enabling precise control over reactivity and selectivity26,27. Among these, atomically dispersed titanium (Ti) has garnered attention for its high stability (strong Ti–O bonds), moderate electronegativity, and unique ability to regulate oxygen species mobility28,29. Unlike transition metals (e.g., Mn, Fe) that may directly participate in CO adsorption, Ti preferentially interacts with lattice oxygen or surface hydroxyl groups, creating an electronic environment that favors O2 dissociation without excessive CO adsorption competition30. Crucially, atomically dispersed Ti can act as “steric barriers” or “electronic regulators” to suppress the adsorption of H2O and SO2: their localized electric fields may weaken the binding of polar H2O molecules, while their oxophilicity can outcompete SO2 for surface oxygen sites, preventing sulfate formation31,32. Although Ti SACs have been explored in methane conversion and NO reduction33–35, their role in CO oxidation, particularly in synergizing with Fe-Mn supports to combat H2O/SO2 poisoning, remains underexplored.

Here, we report the design of MnOx-Fe2O3 spherical catalysts decorated with 1 wt% atomically dispersed Ti (1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3), where atomic-scale Ti anchoring reconstructs the surface microenvironment to achieve exceptional H2O/SO2 resistance. By leveraging the synergistic interaction between atomically dispersed Ti and the Fe-Mn matrix, we demonstrate that Ti modifies the local coordination geometry and electronic structure of adjacent Fe/Mn sites, enhancing the stability of active oxygen species and forming a dynamic “protection layer” that repels H2O/SO2. Experimental results reveal that 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 maintains >55% CO conversion under humid (10 vol% H2O) and sulfurous (10 ppm SO2) conditions, far surpassing pristine MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst at 250 °C. Combined operando spectroscopy (in-situ Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS)), X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and density functional theory (DFT) calculations elucidate the mechanism: atomically dispersed Ti preferentially bind H2O/SO2 via weaker interactions than CO, ensuring CO molecules retain priority access to active sites, while the reinforced Fe-O-Mn network stabilizes oxygen vacancies for continuous reaction. This work provides a blueprint for designing high-performance, poison-resistant catalysts through targeted surface engineering, with implications for scalable environmental catalysis applications.

Results

Preparation of atomic-level Ti dispersion on MnOx-Fe2O3

Figure 1a systematically elucidates the synthetic strategy and structural characteristics of the 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst, which was rationally designed to achieve atomic-level Ti dispersion while preserving the intrinsic properties of the MnOx-Fe2O3 support. The synthesis commenced with a controlled hydrothermal process (detailed in the synthesis of materials), wherein Fe and Mn precursors co-assembled into uniform spherical nanoparticles through hydrolysis-condensation reactions, forming the MnOx-Fe2O3 composite. The initial step was optimized to ensure Mn dopants were incorporated into the Fe2O3 lattice rather than existing as discrete phases, a critical design parameter verified by subsequent characterization. The subsequent atomically dispersed Ti modification employed titanium tetrabutoxide as the precursor, leveraging its moderate reactivity to facilitate Ti4+ coordination onto oxygen vacancies of MnOx-Fe2O3 without forming TiO2 clusters. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses (Fig. 1b–d) provide direct visualization of the material’s architecture: the MnOx-Fe2O3 spheres maintain their well-defined morphology (50–100 nm) after Ti incorporation, with high-resolution imaging revealing ordered lattice arrangements characteristic of Fe2O3. Quantitative lattice fringe analysis (Fig. 1e) identifies distinct spacings of d = 0.27 nm (corresponding to Fe2O3 (012)) and d = 0.36 nm ((104) planes), while the absence of Mn-specific lattice fringes confirms Mn atoms are atomically dispersed within the Fe2O3 matrix rather than forming Mn-rich domains36,37. Complementary Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy and Energy-Dispersive X-ray mapping (Supplementary Figs. 1–3) reveals a multi-elemental perspective, showing that xTi/MnOx-Fe2O3 (x = 0.5, 1, 3) retains its spherical morphology with uniform distribution of Fe, Mn, and O across the surface. For 0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3, Ti is atomically dispersed (no clusters/aggregates) and homogenously distributed. In contrast, 3Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 exhibits a relatively high overall Ti dispersion but shows localized aggregation in some regions (Supplementary Fig. 3), accompanied by edge enrichment in EDX mapping, which is a feature absent in 0.5Ti/1Ti samples. Most critically, aberration-corrected STEM (AC-STEM) imaging (Fig. 1f–k with 500 pm scale bar and Supplementary Fig. 4 with 5 nm scale bar) provides definitive evidence of atomic-scale Ti dispersion in 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) images reveal isolated bright spots (corresponding to Ti atoms) uniformly embedded in the oxide matrix, with no observable clusters or aggregates. These observations demonstrate that 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 is an atomically dispersed catalyst with a robust structure: Ti atoms are predominantly atomically dispersed, without inducing morphological, crystalline, or textural alterations to the MnOx-Fe2O3 support. Structural stability investigations through X‑ray diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. 5) reveal that the characteristic peaks of hematite (α-Fe2O3) remain unchanged following Ti introduction, confirming the preservation of the parent crystal structure without phase segregation or amorphization36. To elucidate the coordination environment of Ti at the atomic level, we performed XANES and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurements at the Ti K-edge. Figure 2a–c presents the k2-weighted Fourier-transform EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) curves in R-space for the 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 sample. In its FT-EXAFS spectrum, the dominant peak at ~2.58 Å is unambiguously assigned to the Ti-O-Fe scattering path, which exhibits striking consistency with the corresponding theoretical fitting derived from opj-files38. Notably, compared to the reference TiO2 spectrum (where the Ti-O-Ti path gives a peak at 2.62 Å), the peak shows a distinct shift, indicative of a shorter Ti–O bond length in Ti-O-Fe relative to TiO228,39. Crucially, we rigorously evaluated the necessity of Ti-O-Ti backscattering paths by performing comparative fittings under identical constraints. The model incorporating Ti-O-Ti yielded a significantly higher R-factor (0.0221) compared to the Ti-O-Fe model (0.0109), quantitatively confirming that the experimental data are far better described by atomically dispersed Ti anchored to Fe sites rather than Ti–Ti clustering (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table2). Consequently, no characteristic peak corresponding to the Ti–Ti bond (~2.52 Å) was observed, further corroborating the atomic dispersion of Ti on the MnOx-Fe2O3 surface. Given the high resolution of wavelet transform (WT) analysis in both k- and R-spaces, Fig. 2d–f compares the WT analysis of the 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 sample, TiO2, and Ti foil. As shown in Fig. 2d, e, both 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 and TiO2 exhibit a dominant peak at ~4.0 Å−1 in k-space and ~2.5 Å in R-space, attributable to the Ti-O scattering path. The markedly higher intensity of the peak for 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 is ascribed to the partial positive charge carried by Fe in the Ti-O-Fe moiety, which alters the charge distribution of the Ti–O bonds relative to those in Ti-O-Ti of TiO2. By contrast, Ti foil displays a sole principal peak at ~4.0 Å−1 in k-space and ~2.5–4.0 Å in R-space, arising solely from Ti–Ti bonding. The absence of the feature in 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 strongly suggests the absence of detectable Ti clusters. Fig. 2g presents the Ti K-edge XANES spectra of 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 together with references. The edge energy of Ti in 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 is significantly higher than that of Ti foil but lower than that of TiO2, indicating that the Ti atoms are in a valence state intermediate between metallic Ti and Ti4+ in TiO2. Quantitative fitting results in k- and R-spaces are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table2. Fitting reveals that the first-shell Ti-O coordination number in 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 approaches four, while the second-shell Ti-O-Fe coordination number is also close to four. The electronic structures and oxidation states of surface elements in MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 were further probed by X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 2h, analysis of the O1s region reveals that the lattice oxygen (Oα) peak shifts from 530.2 to 528.8 eV, and the chemisorbed/defect oxygen (Oβ) peak shifts from 531.4 to 530.3 eV upon Ti doping28,40–42. In contrast, the Ti 2p features (Fig. 2i) appear at binding energies of 463.9 eV (2p1/2) and 458.1 eV (2p3/2), consistent with the coexistence of Ti4+ and Ti3+. Accompanying this, the relative content of Oβ increases markedly (Supplementary Table3), whereas the oxidation-state ratios of Fe and Mn remain essentially unchanged, such as Fe3+/Fe2+ and Mn4+/Mn3+ show no significant variation (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10, Supplementary Table3). We attribute it to the preferential formation of Ti-O-Fe interfaces, in which localized electronic confinement modulates the Fe–O bonds, converting lattice oxygen atoms into highly active Oβ species. Because the transition metals (Feδ⁺/Mnδ⁺) do not directly participate in electron transfer, their valence states are preserved. It is noteworthy that Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 11) exhibits only the characteristic peaks of α‑Fe2O3. However, as the Ti atomic loading increases, a noticeable shift of the spectral features is observed, from 1432 to 1289 cm−1. The downshift is primarily attributed to the symmetric stretching and bending vibrations of O-Ti-O units, which modulate the local electron density around O atoms within the Fe2O3 lattice37. These observations provide compelling evidence that Ti is incorporated into the Fe2O3 matrix at the atomic level and preferentially engages in strong interactions with oxygen atoms in the Fe-O framework, likely forming Ti-O-Fe bridge structures. Such Ti incorporation reorganizes the local O-mediated coupling network and modifies the adsorption response of neighboring Mn/Fe sites. Collectively, the Raman data corroborate the atomic dispersion of Ti and highlight its role in tailoring the local electronic environment of the oxide support through direct metal-oxygen bonding38,43.

Enhanced catalytic performance of atomic-level Ti dispersion

The catalytic activities of xTi/MnOx-Fe2O3 (x = 0, 0.5, 1, 3) toward CO oxidation were evaluated under simulated clean and harsh (10 ppm SO2 and 10 vol.% H2O were added) conditions. Figure 3a shows the CO conversion as a function of temperature over the series of catalysts without H2O and SO2. Compared with the Fig. 3a, incorporation of 0.5 and 1 wt% Ti decreased the T50 (temperature for 50% conversion) by 20 and 15 °C, respectively. In contrast, under an atmosphere containing 10 ppm SO2 and 10 vol% H2O (Fig. 3d), the 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst exhibited the highest activity. Notably, it even surpassed the 0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst, which attributed to the fact that the mere 0.5 wt% Ti is insufficient to provide comprehensive protection for the active sites. The unmodified MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst achieved only 40.1% conversion at 350 °C. Upon Ti incorporation, the T50 values shifted markedly to 279.6 °C (0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3), 248.5 °C (1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3), and 294.6 °C (3Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3), respectively. The 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst exhibited a reduction in catalytic temperature exceeding 100 °C relative to the parent material, demonstrating that atomic dispersion of Ti substantially enhances low‑temperature activity and improves tolerance to SO2 and H2O. Notably, an activity inversion occurs at approximately 300 °C. Below the temperature, 0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 outperforms 3Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 due to its superior atomic dispersion, which optimizes the Ti-O-Fe electronic interfaces for lattice oxygen activation. Conversely, above 300 °C, 3Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 exhibits higher activity than 0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3. The inversion is attributed to SO2 deposition on 0.5 wt% Ti arising from insufficient Ti loading, whereas the high density of Ti-O-Fe interfaces in 3Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 effectively mitigates sulfur poisoning. In-situ DRIFTS (Supplementary Fig. 12) shows 0.5Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 yields a marginal decrease in sulfate bands (1199, 1162 cm−1), while 1Ti and 3Ti suppress them. The persistent 1356 cm−1 band (physisorbed SO2) across all samples rules out simple site-blocking. Limited suppression at 0.5 wt% arises from insufficient Ti-O-Fe interfacial density to block SO2 oxidation. Conversely, aggregation at 3 wt% slightly reduces physisorption while maintaining electronic suppression of sulfation. The kinetic analyses of all catalysts were conducted according to the Arrhenius equation under two conditions, namely absence of H2O and SO2 and presence of 10 ppm SO2 and 10 vol% H2O, with the results presented in Fig. 3d, e. Notably, the 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst exhibited the lowest apparent activation energies (Ea) across all environments (56.04 and 64.59 kJ mol−1, corresponding to the two conditions), both substantially lower than those of the pristine MnOx-Fe2O3 catalyst (81.06 and 84.14 kJ mol−1). We rationalize the significant reduction in Ea by electronic interactions between Ti4+ and Mn3+/Fe3+, which facilitate CO adsorption and activation at the metal-support interface. Furthermore, Ti-O-Fe interfaces may facilitate oxygen exchange by modifying the local O-coordinated electronic environment, thereby synergistically accelerating oxygen migration across the catalyst surface. Further evaluate the practical potential of the catalyst, the performance of 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 were compared with well-established noble-metal and transition-metal oxide catalysts reported in the literature. Remarkably, 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 exhibits exceptional catalytic performance under both pristine and SO2/H2O-containing atmospheres (Fig. 3c and e and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). It outperforms previously reported transition metal oxides and rivals the activity of various noble metal catalysts under clean conditions. While the benchmark 1 wt% Pt/CeO2 shows slightly higher SO2 tolerance, 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 surpasses the 0.5 wt% Pt/CeO2 benchmark and maintains noble-metal-comparable performance in both activity and sulfur resistance at lower temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 13)44. To further elucidate the enhanced resistance to SO2 and H2O imparted by atomic-level Ti modification, we conducted stability tests for MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 at 250 °C, with results shown in Fig. 3g. Under initial conditions with 10 vol.% H2O, both catalysts maintained stable performance. Notably, 1Ti/ MnOx-Fe2O3 achieved 85.1% CO conversion, significantly outperforming MnOx-Fe2O3 (75.0%). Upon introducing 10 ppm SO2, MnOx-Fe2O3 underwent rapid and continuous deactivation, with conversion dropping to 10.0% within 6 h. In stark contrast, 1Ti/ MnOx-Fe2O3 exhibited a much slower decay rate, retaining a 55.3% CO conversion after SO2 exposure, which confirm that highly dispersed Ti incorporation transforms the deactivation behavior from rapid failure to sustained partial activity. The superior durability originates from two synergistic effects of the Ti-O-Fe interface: (i) the Ti-O-Fe interface weakens the accumulation of hydroxyl and sulfate species, as indicated by in situ DRIFTS, thereby mitigating competitive site occupation under humid and sulfur-containing atmospheres; and (ii) the redox flexibility of Ti4+/Ti3+ enables partial reversal of oxidative restructuring of the catalyst framework, thereby protecting Mn-Fe active centers from sulfate deposition21,31.

Improvement effect of atomic-level dispersed Ti

The in situ DRIFTSs of CO adsorption coupled and DFT calculations for MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 to uncover the intrinsic origin of the enhanced catalytic activity induced by atomic‑level dispersed Ti. The introduction of Ti atoms, particularly Ti4+ with an empty 3d configuration (t2g0eg0, no unpaired electrons), imparts a distinctive empty-orbital character45,46, which is expected to influence the adsorption and activation of reactant molecules on the catalyst surface (Fig. 4a–c). As shown in Fig. 4d, e, under a CO-only atmosphere, both MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 exhibit gaseous CO adsorption bands in the range of 2050–2200 cm−1. Notably, the adsorption bands at 2176 and 2113 cm−1 are significantly more intense on 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 than on MnOx-Fe2O3 prior to purging (Supplementary Fig. 14). Even after subsequent N2 purging for 30 min, although the CO signal decreases substantially, the residual intensity on 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 remains markedly higher than that on the parent oxide (Supplementary Fig. 15). This persistent surface coverage under inert flow reflects that the atomically dispersed Ti substantially strengthens the interaction between CO and the catalyst surface. Additionally, the pronounced accumulation of formate species on 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3, contrasted with their absence on the parent oxide, highlights the activation of lattice oxygen by atomic Ti. Interestingly, both samples display vibrational features in the 1300–1600 cm−1 region (Supplementary Table 6). The band at ~1566 cm−1 is assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibration (νas) of surface formate species. To further elucidate the mechanism of CO on the catalyst surface, additional in-situ DRIFTSs experiments were conducted under O2 and reactive CO + O2 atmospheres (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Under O2-only conditions, a weak but discernible feature emerges in the 1450–1500 cm−1 region, assigned to weakly adsorbed oxygen species (O2*) at coordinatively unsaturated Ti-O-Fe sites47. This O2-derived signal is markedly weaker than the prominent CO-induced bands (2100–2200 cm−1 for CO adsorption and 1300–1400 cm−1 for formate), confirming the existence of the L-H pathway via surface-adsorbed oxygen species, albeit with a limited contribution under O2-only conditions. Under reactive CO + O2 conditions, the monodentate carbonate peak in the 1300–1400 cm−1 region remains relatively stable, suggesting a saturated, non-passivating carbonate background. Concomitantly, a distinct new peak appears at 1622 cm−1, attributed to carbonate (CO32−) species indicative of a shift toward more strongly coupled, bridging/bidentate configurations at the Ti-O-Fe interface48,49. Collectively, these spectroscopic insights and the DFT calculations lead us to propose that CO oxidation over 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 follows a dual-pathway synergistic mechanism. In this regime, the MvK-like lattice-oxygen participation is predominant, enabled by the Ti-O-Fe interface facilitating oxygen mobility and formate intermediacy. Concurrently, the L-H-type adsorbed-oxygen pathway plays a supporting role, together sustaining the high catalytic performance through a cooperative redox cycle50,51.

To rationalize the enhancement, we compared the structural models before and after introducing Ti atoms into MnOx-Fe2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 18). As illustrated in Fig. 4f, DFT‑calculated adsorption energies follow the order: 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 (−1.74 eV) > MnOx-Fe2O3 (−0.47 eV). Further electron localization function (ELF) analysis of the optimized structures (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Fig. 4g and j) indicates that atomically dispersed Ti doping induces a redistribution of electron density within the oxide framework. In combination with the structural refinement, Ti incorporation shortens neighboring Mn–O bonds from 2.12 to 2.06 Å and Fe–O bonds from 1.99 to 1.89 Å (Supplementary Fig. 20), suggesting a modified local bonding environment around the active sites. The combined ELF and projected density of states (PDOS) results as evidence that Ti doping reorganizes the local O-mediated coupling network and thereby alters the adsorption response of the surface toward different molecules. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21a and d, O 2p PDOS provides direct support for such local restructuring associated with Ti doping. Specifically, the spin-up O 2p peak shifts from −2.59 to −2.71 eV, and the spin-down peak from −1.90 to −2.02 eV, indicating that Ti modifies the O-coordinated electronic environment adjacent to the active site. As revealed by the total PDOS for pristine MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 22), no pronounced difference has been presented, indicating that the global Mn/Fe-centered electronic framework has only been modestly affected upon Ti incorporation. Moreover, Mn‑PDOS and Fe‑PDOS before and after doping show negligible variation (Supplementary Fig. 21b, c, e, f), confirming that Ti does not strongly perturb the intrinsic electronic ground states of Mn or Fe centers. Instead, Ti primarily modulates the local electronic environment through its interaction with oxygen ligands and the nearby Mn-O-Fe ensemble.

The promotional effect of Ti is elucidated by the complementary orbital coupling channels between CO and the transition-metal active sites (Fig. 4h and k). First, the filled CO 5σ orbital donates electron density to the metal dz2 orbital along the Metal-C axis, forming a σ-donation interaction. The orbital overlap stabilizes the end-on CO adsorption configuration and establishes the primary Metal-C bonding framework. In addition, CO adsorption is primarily stabilized by σ donation from the filled CO 5σ orbital to empty or partially empty Mn-centered states, together with enhanced polarization through Ti-modulated Mn/Fe-O network. The metal-to-CO back-donation transfers electron density from the metal d state into the CO antibonding orbital, weakening the internal C–O bond while strengthening the Metal-CO interaction. From the perspective of electron-transfer pathways (Fig. 4i and l), pristine MnOx-Fe2O3 features moderate d-p hybridization between Mn/Fe 3d orbitals and O 2p orbitals (ΔA1 = 1.18 eV, ΔA2 = 2.64 eV, ΔA3 = 4.18 eV), forming a relatively stable Mn-O-Fe covalent network. In contrast, the vacant Ti 3d orbitals in 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 act as electron acceptors and interact with O 2p orbitals (ΔA1 = 1.10 eV, ΔA2 = 2.37 eV, ΔA3 = 4.26 eV), thereby introducing a localized perturbation into the interfacial coupling network. These findings reveal that Ti incorporation shortens adjacent Mn–O and Fe–O bonds, enhancing Ti-O-Fe electronic modulation and altering O 2p states, in agreement with a local reorganization of the oxygen-mediated bonding environment.

Further probe the impact of SO2 and H2O on the catalyst surface, we acquired in situ DRIFTSs spectra for both MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 under atmospheres containing 10 ppm SO2 or 10 vol.% H2O. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, upon introducing SO2, new bands emerged on both catalysts. Broad peaks in the ranges of 1117–1180 and 1128–1208 cm−1 are assigned to surface-adsorbed sulfate species, while features at 1351 and 1356 cm−1 originate from physiosorbed gaseous SO2. Similarly, after H2O introduction (Supplementary Fig. 23), additional bands appeared corresponding to carbonate species and hydroxyl group vibrations (3100–3600 cm−1)17,52,53. Notably, following Ti incorporation, the intensities of sulfate and hydroxyl bands on 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 were markedly attenuated, indicating effective suppression of SO2/H2O-induced occupation or degradation of active components. XPS findings are fully consistent with these DRIFTS observations. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 24–26 and Supplementary Table 7, both oxygen speciation and transition-metal oxidation states evolved after reaction; however, the magnitude of change for 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 was far smaller than for MnOx-Fe2O3. Complementary DFT calculations shed light on the altered SO2 adsorption behavior upon Ti doping. Figure 5c–f presents the SO2 adsorption configurations and energetics for the two systems. On the MnOx-Fe2O3 surface, SO₂ can adsorb effectively on the surface Mn site with a large adsorption energy of −1.96 eV (Fig. 5c). Such strong adsorption indicates that SO2 can occupy and block the Mn-related active sites, providing a clear thermodynamic origin for sulfur poisoning. The Bader charge of SO2 is −0.43 e (Fig. 5d), suggesting electron accumulation on the adsorbed SO2 species during the poisoning process. Remarkably, upon Ti doping (Fig. 5e), the SO2 adsorption energy decreases dramatically from −1.96 eV on MnOx-Fe2O3 to only −0.19 eV on 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3. Corresponding, the SO2 Bader charge changes from −0.43 e on the undoped surface to −0.21 e after Ti doping, indicates that Ti doping redistributes the adsorption interaction away from a strong Mn-SO2 poisoning mode and converts it into a much weaker, lattice-O-involved adsorption state. In other words, Ti does not suppress SO2 adsorption by directly repelling SO2 through a Ti-SO2 interaction; rather, it weakens poisoning indirectly by tuning the surface oxygen electronic structure and altering the adsorption configuration. To further clarify how the local electronic modulation affects gas adsorption, we additionally examined the optimized adsorption geometries, PDOS, and magnetic moments of Mn active site for SO2 adsorption on MnOx-Fe2O3 (S1) and 1Ti/ MnOx-Fe2O3 (S2) models (Supplementary Fig. 27). The influence of Ti on SO2 adsorption is much pronounced. On S1, SO2 adopts a bidentate bridging configuration, in which its two oxygen atoms coordinate with neighboring Mn and Fe sites, with O-Mn and O-Fe distances of 2.28 and 2.40 Å, respectively. In S2, however, SO2 no longer adsorbs in the same simple bridging manner, but becomes locally reconstructed: besides the coordination of SO2 oxygen atoms to surface Mn and Fe sites, an additional short S···O (lattice oxygen) bond with a length of 1.65 Å has been formed, suggesting a lattice-oxygen-involved interaction, such S–O bond does not energetically be favorable as evidenced by small adsorption energy. In particular, Ti doping induces an adjacent lattice oxygen to move slightly outward toward the vacuum side. The uplifted lattice oxygen can approach the S atom of SO2 and participate in the interfacial interaction, leading to a possible three-coordinate SO2 adsorption configuration involving the adsorbed SO2, nearby metal sites, and the activated surface lattice oxygen.

Furthermore, the SO2-PDOS provides additional confirmation. For SO2 adsorbed on MnOx-Fe2O3 without Ti (Fig. 5g), the SO2-derived states exhibit several pronounced features in the valence region from approximately −8 to −3 eV, together with additional states near the Fermi level and around 4–5 eV above the Fermi level. These states overlap with the Mn 3d orbitals, indicating electronic coupling between SO2 and the Mn active site. After Ti doping (Fig. 5h), the SO2-derived states become more delocalized and less concentrated around the Mn 3d states near the active site, indicating that the strong Mn-centered SO2 bonding is disrupted. Although weak interactions with lattice oxygen are present, the overall adsorption is significantly weakened, as reflected by the much less negative adsorption energy. In addition, SO2-related states appear more clearly near the Fermi level and in the low-lying unoccupied region, suggesting that Ti doping promotes electronic communication between the adsorbate and the oxide surface. The magnetic moment of the Mn site slightly increases from 4.60 to 4.6 Μb, indicates that Ti doping does not simply enhance electron transfer to SO2; instead, it modifies the adsorption configuration and redistributes the interfacial charge through a more complex surface-adsorbate interaction. Specifically, SO2 adsorption on pristine MnOx-Fe2O3 is described as a bent SO2 bridge in which the two O atoms simultaneously interact with neighboring Mn and Fe sites (Fig. 5i). The SO2 molecule deviates from its original linear geometry; the two O atoms approach Mn/Fe, while the S center bends outward. The corresponding orbital schematic now illustrates the interaction between the O-centered lone-pair/electron-density region of SO2 and the d orbitals of Mn and Fe, together with the coupled M-O sigma donation and O-M back-donation components. With Ti doping (Fig. 5j), Ti does not directly coordinate SO2, but it modifies the neighboring lattice oxygen by shifting its O 2p states toward the Fermi level and inducing a slight outward displacement. The activated lattice oxygen can interact with the S atom of SO2 and promote a three-coordinate adsorption geometry. It promotes CO adsorption/activation, while transforming SO2 adsorption from a strong Mn-centered poisoning state into a weak lattice-O-involved adsorption state. The selective regulation explains the improved SO2 tolerance of Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 and provides a clear electronic-structure basis for the superior durability of the catalyst under sulfur-containing CO oxidation conditions.

To understand why Ti doping changes the SO2 adsorption mode, the PDOS of the lattice oxygen involved in SO2 binding was compared for MnOx-Fe2O3 and 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3 in the absence of SO2 (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). For the pristine Mn-doped surface, the lattice O 2p states are mainly located below the Fermi level, with strong contributions in the valence region from approximately −5 to −1 eV. Only weak O 2p states are observed above or very close to the Fermi level, suggesting that the lattice oxygen is relatively electronically saturated and less available for additional interaction with adsorbates. After Ti doping, the O 2p PDOS becomes more intense and is redistributed toward the upper valence region near the Fermi level, which indicates that Ti doping increases the electronic availability of the neighboring lattice oxygen. Structurally, the oxygen also moves slightly outward from the surface, making it geometrically accessible to the S center of SO2. Therefore, Ti doping activates the surface lattice oxygen in both electronic and geometric senses. Importantly, the lattice oxygen activation does not enhance SO2 poisoning. Instead, it changes the adsorption pathway. The great change of adsorption energy (−1.96 to −0.19 eV) means that the Ti-activated lattice oxygen competes with and disrupts the strong Mn-centered SO2 adsorption mode, producing a weakly bound SO2 state that can be more easily removed from the catalyst surface. Because SO2 contains electron-deficient sulfur and oxygen lone-pair orbitals, the activated lattice O 2p states can participate in S–O bond formation or strong S···O interaction with the adsorbed SO2 molecule.

Deeply, the key difference is that, after Ti doping, SO2 adsorption is no longer dominated solely by the Mn-SO2 interaction. The optimized structure suggests that a lattice oxygen atom becomes involved in SO2 binding, forming an additional S-O interaction between SO2 and the oxide lattice. The interaction changes the adsorption mode from a mainly metal-site adsorption configuration to a cooperative adsorption configuration involving both the Mn site and a reactive lattice oxygen. The 3d0 electronic configuration of Ti4+ creates a strong electron-acceptor center, which localizes part of the otherwise delocalized charge near the Ti-O-Fe interface42,54. Consequently, less charge is available for transfer to SO2, diminishing its electron affinity and ultimately reversing the direction of net charge flow. The electronic confinement effect underlies the weakened SO2 adsorption and contributes to the enhanced poisoning resistance of the Ti-modified catalyst55,56. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ti doping orchestrates a subtle yet effective reconfiguration of the O-coordinated electronic landscape without disrupting its intrinsic Mn/Fe electronic states. Such localized electronic regulation strengthens the interaction with CO while suppressing the formation of strongly stabilized sulfur-containing surface species, thereby simultaneously boosting CO oxidation activity and enhancing resistance to H2O and SO2 poisoning. The mechanistic insight provides a rational basis for designing multicomponent oxide catalysts with superior activity and durability through targeted orbital engineering.

Discussion

In this study, we have demonstrated that the atomic dispersion of Ti on MnOx-Fe2O3 serves as an effective orbital‑tuning strategy to tackle the long‑standing trade‑off between activity and poison resistance in CO oxidation. Unlike conventional approaches relying on bulk doping or oxygen‑vacancy engineering, our work reveals that atomically dispersed Ti species function primarily as electronic modulators rather than direct redox centers. The unique property facilitates the redistribution of electron density from the O 2p orbitals to the Ti center, thereby optimizing the local electronic environment for selective molecular activation. A key conceptual advancement lies in the decoupling of adsorption pathways for reactants and poisons. Typically, in transition metal oxides, strong adsorption of SO2 on Mn sites leads to irreversible sulfate formation. Here, the Ti-O-Fe interface reconstructs the potential energy surface for SO2 adsorption. By shifting the interaction from a strong chemical bond with Mn (Eads = −1.96 eV) to a weak, lattice-oxygen-mediated interaction (Eads = −0.19 eV), we effectively convert a poisoning event into a transient physical interaction. The mechanism differs fundamentally from steric hindrance models; instead, it is an electronic effect driven by the upward shift of O 2p states, which reduces the electron density available for SO2 back-donation. The insight provides a new dimension for designing sulfur-resistant catalysts beyond merely blocking active sites.

Methods

Synthesis of materials

Materials

All chemical reagents used in this study were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). These included aqueous manganese nitrate solution (Mn(NO3)2, 50%), iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 99%), tetrabutyl titanate (C16H36O4Ti, 98%), hydroxypropyl cellulose (C3H7O*), aqueous ammonia (NH3·H2O, 28%), and anhydrous ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.9%). All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification unless otherwise stated.

Preparation of MnOx-Fe2O3

Fe2O3 was prepared by a hydrothermal method followed by calcination. First, 17.50 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O was dissolved in 200 mL of deionized water under stirring for 30 min. Then, 20 g of NH3·H2O was added dropwise to the solution with continuous stirring for another 30 min to ensure homogeneous mixing. The resulting suspension was transferred into a 250 mL Teflon‑lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 12 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed repeatedly with deionized water until neutral pH was reached. The solid was dried at 100 °C for 12 h, followed by calcination in a muffle furnace at 500°C for 4 h to afford Fe2O3. For the Mn‑doped sample, 1.90 g of the as‑prepared Fe2O3 was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water under stirring. A pre‑prepared aqueous solution containing 0.41 g of Mn(NO3)2 was then added dropwise to the suspension. The mixture was stirred at 50 °C in a water bath for 1 h to ensure uniform incorporation of Mn species. Water was removed using a rotary evaporator, and the resulting solid was dried at 100 °C for 12 h. Finally, the powder was calcined at 500 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace. The product was designated as MnOx-Fe2O3.

Preparation of 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3

To prepare the Ti‑modified catalyst, 1.98 g of MnOx-Fe2O3 was dispersed in 150 mL of anhydrous ethanol under vigorous stirring for 30 min to ensure uniform wetting. Then, 0.1 g of hydroxypropyl cellulose was added and stirring continued for another 30 min to achieve homogeneous dispersion. Separately, 0.085 mL of tetrabutyl titanate (C16H36O4Ti) was dissolved in 30 mL of anhydrous ethanol, and 0.5 mL of deionized water was added to initiate hydrolysis. The titanium precursor solution was then introduced dropwise into the MnOx-Fe2O3 suspension, and the mixture was stirred vigorously at 70 °C in a water bath for 12 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed three times each with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol. The solid was dried at 80 °C for 12 h, followed by calcination in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 4 h. The final product was denoted as 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3. Using the same procedure, samples with varying nominal Ti contents were synthesized, and catalysts after exposure to poisoning atmospheres were labeled as 1Ti/MnOx-Fe2O3‑S.

Characterization

TEM images were acquired on a Tecnai G2 F30 S‑TWIN microscope (FEI, USA) operated at 300 kV, with a spatial resolution of 0.19 nm for scanning TEM (STEM). Energy‑dispersive X‑ray spectroscopy was performed with an energy resolution of 130 eV. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images were taken on a JEOL JEM ARM 200CF working at 200 kV, and equipped with a CMOS camera (GATAN 1095). AC-STEM characterization was performed on a FEI Titan Themis Z G2 300 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating at 300 kV. Phase composition was determined by X‑ray diffraction (XRD) on an Empyrean diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands) using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) generated at 45 kV and 200 mA. Data were collected in continuous mode from 5° to 90° with a scan rate of 10° min−1. At SSRF’s BL20U beamline (China), X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements were performed in transmission mode with ionization chambers monitoring the sample, Ti, and TiO2 foils. Raman spectra were recorded on an inVia spectrometer (Renishaw, UK) using a laser power set to 1% of the maximum output to minimize sample heating. Specific surface area and porosity were measured by N2 physisorption at 77 K on a Nova 2000E instrument (Quantachrome, USA). Prior to analysis, samples were degassed at 200 °C for 6 h under vacuum. Surface areas were calculated using the BET model. X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out on an AXIS SUPRA+ spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV). High‑resolution spectra of Fe 2p, Mn 2p, Ti 2p, C 1s, and O 1s were recorded to determine elemental composition and oxidation states. DRIFTS measurements were performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (USA) fitted with a mercury-cadmium-telluride detector. In-situ DRIFTS experiments were conducted to probe the reaction mechanisms under different atmospheres. General Pretreatment: For all experiments, 50 mg of the catalyst was pretreated in N₂ at 400 °C for 1 h, then cooled to 300 °C. (i) CO adsorption DRIFTS: Background spectra were collected in N₂. Subsequently, the gas was switched to a mixture of 1% CO balanced with N₂. Spectra were recorded until saturation. (ii) CO + O2 + SO2 DRIFTS: Background spectra were first collected. Then, a reaction gas mixture (1% CO + 16% O2 balanced with N2) was introduced for 60 min. Finally, 10 ppm SO2 was introduced into the system for an additional 60 min while maintaining the CO/O2 flow. (iii) Background spectra were first collected: Then, a reaction gas mixture (1% CO + 16% O2 balanced with N2) was introduced for 60 min. Finally, 10 vol% H2O vapor was introduced into the system for an additional 60 min while maintaining the CO/O2 flow. All spectra were collected in the range of 800–4000 cm−1.

CO oxidation performance evaluation

CO oxidation activity was assessed in a fixed‑bed reactor system consisting of three integrated modules: a gas‑mixing unit, a reaction zone, and an online detection system. The gas‑mixing unit supplied certified gas mixtures of 10% CO/N2, 1% SO2/N2, O2, 10 vol% H2O and N2. For each test, 200 mg of catalyst (40–60 mesh) was loaded into the reactor. The total gas flow rate was set to 200 mL/min, corresponding to a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 60,000 h−1 g(Cat.)−1. Reactions were conducted over a temperature range of 100–350 °C. In the reaction zone, the premixed gases passed through a quartz tube housed in a three‑stage temperature‑controlled furnace, where they contacted the catalyst bed and underwent CO oxidation. The online detection system continuously monitored effluent gas composition using a BRUKER-INVENIOs FT-IR spectromete, enabling real‑time analysis of reactant conversion and product formation. During stability tests, 10 vol% H2O and 10 ppm SO2 were co‑fed with the reaction mixture to simulate realistic flue‑gas conditions. DFT calculations

Geometry optimizations and electronic structure analysis were carried out with the ViennaAbInitio Simulation Package57. Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package. The projector-augmented wave method was used to describe the core–valence interactions, and the exchange–correlation energy was treated using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation58. Dispersion interactions were corrected using the DFT-D3 method. The kinetic energy cutoff was set to 450 eV. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a Γ-centered 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh. During the optimization, the convergence criteria for electronic self-consistency and ionic relaxation were 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively. Given that the confined nature of the Fe-3d, Mn-3d, and Ti-3d orbitals and the strong self-interfactions error in normal PBE functional, DFT + U methods is implemented in the calculation of the electronic properties with U value for Mn-3d, Fe-3d, and Ti-3d being 3.5, 3.5, and 3.0 eV, respectively.

The Fe2O3(104) surface was selected according to the experimentally observed lattice fringes. A slab model containing four atomic layers and a vacuum region of 15 Å was constructed, with the bottom two layers fixed during structural relaxation. MnOx-Fe2O3 was modeled by substituting three Fe atoms with Mn atoms, including one surface Mn atom and two subsurface/bulk Mn atoms. The Ti-modified model was constructed by replacing a neighboring Fe atom with Ti to form a Ti-O-Fe interfacial motif adjacent to the Mn-O-Fe ensemble.
