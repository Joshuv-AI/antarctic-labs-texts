Genetic Insights in Hindbrain Abnormalities Through Network Analysis Expose Key Biological Pathways in Hindbrain Development
Authors: Suus A M van Noort, Deborah A Sival, Dineke S Verbeek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe number of known developmental disorders affecting the hindbrain is rapidly increasing due to advances in neuroimaging and genetic technologies. Nevertheless, it remains largely unknown why the development of the hindbrain is affected in many genetic disorders. We aim to unveil new insights into the biological pathways essential for hindbrain development by investigation of the pathogenetics of hindbrain abnormalities. In this work, an updated gene list of abnormalities of the hindbrain was generated, and genes were subsequently grouped according to most prevalent association in (1) predominantly cerebellar, (2) cerebellar and brainstem and (3) brainstem malformations. Brain-specific gene co-expression networks were generated to identify functional relationships and novel genes that were not yet linked to hindbrain malformations. The results showed that shared biological pathways underlie distinct hindbrain processes, even when cells originate from different primordia. Key players in hindbrain development include genes encoding transcription factors and extracellular signaling molecules. Notably, brainstem abnormalities are biologically distinct, with a smaller role for ciliogenesis. Through co-expression analysis, we identified candidate genes for hindbrain malformations including TRRAP and NCAM1. The identification of essential biological pathways in this study uncovers additional important challenges in genetic hindbrain malformations, such as how defects in apparently ubiquitous processes result in brain-specific phenotypes, and how timing and repair mechanisms influence the pathogenesis of affected pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12311-025-01948-2.Keywords: Hindbrain, Cerebellum, Genetic, Development

Introduction

The development of the human posterior cranial fossa structures is one of the most complex processes within the embryonic and fetal brain [1–3]. With the advances in neuroimaging and genetic technologies, the number of known developmental disorders affecting the hindbrain is increasing. However, why the development of the hindbrain is affected in many genetic disorders remains insufficiently understood. We aim to unveil new insights into the biological pathways essential for hindbrain development by investigation of the pathogenetics of hindbrain abnormalities. The main structure developing from the hindbrain is the cerebellum, attached to the brainstem by the cerebellar peduncles. The cerebellum plays an essential role in coordinating movements and participates in higher cognitive processes [1–3]. Developmental abnormalities can therefore greatly affect these functions.

In humans, the embryologic development of the posterior fossa structures starts at around 3 weeks of gestation and continues until about 20 months post-a-term age [4, 5]. The hindbrain, also called the rhombencephalon, gives rise to the cerebellum, pons and medulla. Respectively, the cerebellum derives from the primitive most rostral segment (rhombomere 1), the pons from the rostral half (metencephalon) and medulla from the lower half (myelencephalon) (Fig. 1a). The first step in the separation of midbrain and hindbrain territories, is the formation of the isthmic organizer. The isthmic organizer releases secreted factors, that pattern the differentiation of the nervous tissue and induce cell fate [6] (see Fig. 1b). Several genes play a role in the formation and function of the isthmic organizer, most importantly OTX2 and GBX2, which have reciprocal repressing activity, HOX and FGF8. FGF signaling creates a genetic feedback loop for regulation of cell fate and patterning through FGF8, WNT1 homeobox genes, EN1 and EN2 transcription factors [7, 8].

Patterning and segmentation are particularly evident in the hindbrain. Organization of hindbrain neuronal subtypes and nuclei is achieved by early subdivision into the eight rhombomeres (Fig. 1b). Rhombomeres are thought to acquire their individual identities under the influence of HOX genes. The cerebellum arises from the anterior rhombomeres (r1), whereas the posterior rhombomeres (r2 to r8) will give rise to the migratory precursors of a variety of ventral nuclei (e.g., olivary and pontine nuclei). Under the influence of ATOH1 and PTF1A [9, 10] the rostral rhombomere 1 gives rise to the cerebellar germinal matrix of the rhombic lips (Fig. 1c) and ventricular zone respectively. Initially, this forms the external granular layer of the cerebellum, which then promotes Purkinje cell (PC) migration by secreting Reelin. Under influence of Tubulin and O-glycosylation genes the PC migration process continues [11, 12]. The external granular layer cells subsequently migrate deeper to form the internal granular layer [13] (Fig. 1d). Sonic hedgehog (SHH) factors, secreted by Purkinje cells, are crucial for further cerebellar patterning and foliation, and will contribute to brainstem development [14, 15]. Hippo and Notch signaling pathways play a central role in cranial nerve development from neural crest cells, and cellular migration [15–17]. For a comprehensive overview of mid- and hindbrain patterning see e.g., Leto et al. [18], and Lowenstein et al. [19] (cerebellar development), and Mendez-Maldonado et al. [15] (cranial nerve development).

The understanding of developmental disorders affecting the hindbrain has increased over the last decades, largely due to advances in neuroimaging and genetics. Subsequently, posterior cranial fossa defects are often classified based on either the understanding of the embryologic origin [5, 20] or the observed anatomical abnormalities [9, 21]. The clinical spectrum of gene mutations causing hindbrain malformations is heterogeneous and ranges from severe phenotypes with early fetal mortality to mild phenotypes with cerebellar signs (e.g., ataxia, oculomotor problems).

In this paper, we use known hindbrain disorder genes and brain-specific gene co-expression analysis to identify relationships between genes and their biological functions. Hereby, we aim to identify key biological processes in hindbrain development, and expose new candidate genes involved in abnormal hindbrain development.

Overview of Genetic Hindbrain Disorders

With the current advances in RNA and DNA sequencing technologies, still only ± 35% of the cases with a posterior fossa malformation have an identified genetic cause [22]. Despite efforts to provide an overview of cerebellar malformation genetics and associated clinical phenotypes (e.g., Aldinger and Doherty [23]), new underlying genetic defects are increasingly identified. Below, we provide a brief genetic overview of hindbrain disorders classified according to predominant anatomical abnormality (Table 1), that is updated with recently identified genes.

Predominantly Cerebellar Malformations

Cerebellar malformations, which involve incomplete and abnormal development, result from various genetic mutations. Cerebellar hypoplasia has been linked to neuronal migration problems during development [11, 24–26] such as due to homozygous RELN and VLDLR mutations and hemizygous OPHN1 mutations [27, 28]. Cerebellar dysgenesis occurs in stem cell division orientation errors, for instance in conditions like CHARGE syndrome [29], Porretti-Boltshauser syndrome [30] and GPSM2 mutations [31]. Cerebellar hyperplasia is observed in transcription (up)regulation mutations (e.g., Sotos syndrome) [32] and somatic mutations affecting the PI3K–AKT3–mTOR proliferation pathway [33, 34]. Macrocerebellum has recently reported in FGFR3 mutation, which plays a role in regulation of cell proliferation as well [35].

The characteristic cerebellar Dandy Walker malformation is associated with FOXC1 deletions or duplications, which play a role in the development of the external granular layer [36], and mutations in extracellular matrix genes LAMC1 and NID1 [37]. In addition, an autosomal dominant heterozygous deletion of ZIC1 and ZIC4,[38, 39] and recently biallelic CAPN15 mutations [40],were found to contribute to Dandy Walker malformation. Few new disease genes have been identified in recent years, even though the known Dandy Walker genes account for less than 5% of cases [41]. Similarly, rhombencephalosynapsis lacked a known genetic origin for long [42], but is now associated with transcription regulation MN1 gene-mutations [43, 44], and exocytosis associated homozygous truncating EXOC2 and RAB11B mutations [45–47]. Last, Chiari malformations, traditionally considered either acquired or a posterior cranial fossa constriction problem, are increasingly acknowledged to have a genetic origin [48]. These include candidate genes that affect the skull base or connective tissue development (not considered in this work) [49]. Overcrowding within the cranial space due to affected brain morphology might also explain part of these malformations in the future.

Cerebellar and Brainstem Malformations

Pontocerebellar hypoplasia (PCH) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder associated with autosomal recessive mutations in genes involved in exocytosis and vesicle transport [50–55], neuronal migration, mitochondrial function [56], tRNA splicing endonucleases and RNA processing. For a complete review of the pathways and phenotypes above see van Dijk, 2018 [52]. Several new PCH disease genes have been found since, with a wide variety of biological functions. These include known pathways such as endosomal activity (biallelic mutations in VPS51) [57, 58] and spliceosome encoding genes (PPIL1 and CDC40 mutations) [59], but also CoA biosynthesis (loss of function mutations in the COASY gene) [60], dephosphorylation (MINPP1 mutations) [61, 62], and transcription factors (homozygous mutations in the PRDM13) [63]. Several disorders show similar imaging patterns to PCH [64], such as deletions of the CASK gene with a role in synaptic transmembrane protein anchoring and ion channel traffic [65], CHMP1A mutations involved in lysosomal sorting [66] or cerebello-facio-dental syndrome with mutations in BRF1 affecting tRNA pathways [67]. Pontocerebellar hypoplasia is also observed in glycosylation disorders, due to gene mutations in PMM2, B3GALNT2, DPM2 and SLC35A2 [68].

Joubert Syndrome (JS) is characterized by a pathognomonic molar tooth sign and vermis hypoplasia. The disorder is genetically heterozygous with mutations in 40 genes causing ciliary dysfunction (reviewed by Gana et al. [69]). JS gene mutations are mostly autosomal recessive, except for OFD1 which is X-linked, and the recently reported autosomal dominant SUFU mutation [70]. Mutations in five genes, CPLANE1, CC2D2A, AHI1, CEP290, and TMEM67 each account for 6–9% of cases in large cohorts [71], but for several genes only one or few cases are described [72]. There are other ciliopathies such as Meckel-Gruber syndrome, with early lethality [72, 73]. Novel candidate ciliary genes are still being identified such as CEP295, TMEM17 and TTC23 [74].

Finally, there is a group of cobblestone malformations, cortical dysplasia with dysplastic cerebellum with cysts and brainstem hypoplasia, due to neuronal migration disorders caused by mutations in post-translational protein modification genes such as POMT1, POMT2, LARGE, FKRP or COL4A1 [75].

Predominantly Brainstem Malformations

There is a small group with predominantly brainstem malformations, associated to genes that influence cranial nerve development and axon guidance, such as CHN1, SALL4, KIF21A, PHOX2A, TUBB3 and TUBB2B [76–78]. Cranial nerve palsies and hearing loss result from HOXA1 loss of function mutations, affecting rhombomere development.

Notably, malformations of the hindbrain often lack a genetic cause, and may be linked to non-genetic mechanisms, such as prematurity or prenatal cerebellar hemorrhage [22, 79]. It is unclear how many genetic causes remain unidentified.

Brain Specific co-expression Analysis in Hindbrain Disorder Genes

Generation of a Comprehensive Gene List

We curated a list of genes linked with posterior fossa malformations focussing on hindbrain malformations, based on information from previous overview publications [16, 22], genes derived from a search in the Human Phenotype Ontology database [80] (search August 2022) associated with the abnormal posterior cranial fossa morphology terms (HP: 0000932, HP: 0001317 and HP: 0002363) and additional literature search in PubMed (“Rhombencephalon/abnormalities”[Mesh] AND development [Text Word]) through abstract screening. The final list was manually curated with information provided in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Men (OMIM) database [81] and Clinvar [82]. Genes were screened on prenatal or early postnatal life disease onset. Gene mutations causing primarily cranial vault malformations of the posterior fossa were excluded. Genes associated exclusively to posterior fossa malignancy were excluded. Genes carrying variants of unknown significance (VUS) were excluded from further analysis.

We obtained a list of 217 genes associated with malformations of the posterior fossa focussing on the hindbrain (Supplementary Table 1). For most genes, an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern was observed (150 genes), but also autosomal dominant (50 genes), X-linked (13 genes), and both autosomal dominant and recessive inheritance patterns (4 genes) were observed. Additionally, somatic mosaicism (2 genes) was reported. The genes were subsequently uniquely grouped according to most reported prevalent malformation as reviewed, namely (1) predominantly cerebellar malformations, 110 genes (110C); (2) cerebellar and brainstem malformations, 93 genes (93CB) and (3) predominantly brainstem malformations, 14 genes (14B).

Brain-specific co-expression Network Analysis

Co-expression analysis is used to recognize the functional relationship between genes and to identify novel genes involved in the pathogenetic mechanism under investigation [83]. We used MetaBrain [84], a gene co-expression network analysis tool integrating more than eight-thousand RNA-seq samples from multiple brain regions to generate brain specific gene co-expression networks. Metabrain combines RNA-seq and genotype data to interfere brain cell types in which expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL; a locus that explains a fraction of the genetic variance of a gene expression phenotype) operate and systematically conducts Mendelian randomization, co-localization and co-regulation analyses to find shared genetic effects between eQTLs and brain-related genome-wide association studies traits. The analyses prioritize probable causal genes. For all three unique gene subgroups (110C, 93CB, and 14B) we created gene co-expression networks (Supplementary Table 2).

Evaluation of the content of the gene co-expression networks showed 10 original (“seed”) genes were present in multiple networks after enrichment (Fig. 2a). Five genes from the 110C gene list were co-expressed with the 93CB genes (COG1, WASHC5, IFT172, NPHP4, SMARCD1). Additionally, three genes from the 93CB list (TUBA1A, TUBB and CEP290) and two genes from the 110C list (USP9X and EXOC2) were co-expressed with the 14B genes. The presence of these 10 seed genes in the networks did not coincide with an overlap in their protein function.

In addition, we identified 40 new co-expressed genes that were present in more than one network, that were not original “seed” genes, and might therefore be candidate genes for hindbrain disease. From the co-expressed new genes, 33 genes were present in the 110C and the 93CB networks, 5 genes were co-expressed in both 110C and 14B networks, and 2 genes were co-expressed in both 93CB and 14B gene networks (Fig. 2b). The corresponding 40 proteins could be categorized in three functional groups including (1) transport and protein-protein interactions, (2) DNA and RNA processes, and (3) dynein and actin cytoskeleton control. For seven of these genes a human disease was registered in the OMIM database for which in at least one patient a posterior fossa abnormality was described. An example hereof is DYNC1H1 associated with cortical dysplasia with other brain malformations-13 (OMIM: 614563), a neurodevelopmental disorder with impaired intellectual development. For some of these co-expressed genes transgenic mouse models existed in which hindbrain abnormalities were reported including NCAM1 and TRRAP [85, 86]. Thus, these genes might be candidates for genetically unexplained hindbrain abnormalities.

Biological Pathway and Expression Analysis

We performed a biological pathway analysis to explore whether shared biological mechanisms might underlie the distinct hindbrain pathology. Using Metascape, an integrated functional enrichment analysis tool [87] we performed pathway enrichment analysis in the gene-co expression networks, via (1) pathway enrichment analysis in the specific gene network subsets and (2) a comparative multi-query analysis for all genes. Metascape utilizes the hypergeometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg p-value correction algorithm in order to identify all gene ontology terms that contain a statistically greater number of common genes than expected by chance [87]. Biological pathways were considered significantly enriched when α = < 0.01.

Gene enrichment and pathway analysis in the 110C brain specific gene co-expression network identified early transcription processes such as chromatin organization (p = 3.040 * 10− 17), RNA biosynthetic process (p = 4.865 * 10− 14) and mRNA metabolic process (p = 1.176 * 10− 13) as the most significant pathways (see Supplementary Table 3 for complete overview of enriched biological pathways). In the 93CB brain specific gene co-expression network, the top three enriched pathways were mostly ciliopathy associated, namely ciliopathies (p = 1.632 * 10− 56), non-motile cilium assembly (p = 1.220* 10− 19), and ncRNA metabolic process (p = 3.213 * 10− 16). Last, the top three significantly enriched pathways in the 14B brain specific gene co-expression network were associated to microtubule and cytoskeletal dynamics, specifically kinesins (p = 3.332 * 10− 18), signaling by Rho GTPases, Miro GTPases and RHOBTB3 (p = 6.750 * 10− 18) and cytoskeleton-dependent intracellular transport (p = 2.230 * 10− 16).

Finally, we assessed which significantly enriched pathways were shared in the three groups. We observed ciliopathies (p = 1.632 * 10− 56), microtubule cytoskeleton organization (p = 2.350 * 10− 30) and chordate embryonic development (p = 1.193 * 10− 28) as the most significant shared pathways underlying the three different groups of hindbrain disorders (see Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4).

An additional cell specific enrichment analysis (CSEA) [90–92] across brain regions and development was performed (see Supplementary Data) to investigate the gene expression profiles in different developmental stages and identify specific patterns of overexpression. In the 110C gene network, enrichment analysis across brain regions and development identified significant expression in all investigated brain regions in early fetal and early mid-fetal periods. Expression in the cerebellum was significant in late mid fetal (p = 2.46 * 10− 4) and late fetal (p = 0.008) periods as well. In the 14B gene network, enrichment analysis across brain regions and development identified significant expression in early fetal (cerebellum, p = 2.62 * 10 − 4; and thalamus, p = 0.030), early mid-fetal (amygdala, p = 0.016; and cortex, p = 0.014) and late mid fetal periods (cerebellum, p = 0.040; and cortex, p = 0.007). These results confirm early gene expression in the studied groups, most prominently in the cerebellum. There were no significantly enriched brain regions in the different developmental stages in the 93CB gene network. This suggests these genes are ubiquitously expressed over brain regions during development. (see Supplementary Data).

Discussion

In the present work, we have provided a brief overview of the complexity of the genetics of hindbrain abnormalities and associated structural malformations. In order to better understand the underlying biology of hindbrain development, we used the genetics of hindbrain abnormalities to map key shared molecular pathways in their pathogenesis. Using this approach, we identified different implicated processes based on the underlying main brain abnormality namely, early transcription processes in predominantly cerebellar defects, ciliary dysfunction in cerebellum-brainstem defects and neuronal migration problems in brainstem and cranial nerve defects. The brainstem abnormalities are biologically moderately different to the cerebellar and cerebellum-brainstem. The shared pathways between the three subgroups were mostly involved in transport (vesicle or microtubule), neuronal migration and neuronal development, functionally diverse and quite fundamental biological processes. Last, by generating brain-specific gene-co-expression networks, we identified putative candidate genes for genetically unexplained hindbrain abnormalities.

In early cerebellar development, during or after the formation of the isthmic organizer, our data exposed the importance of chromatin topology while analyzing the genes linked to predominantly cerebellar abnormalities. Based on these results we may conclude that predominantly cerebellar abnormalities can be a consequence of genome organization problems. Chromatin modification has been shown to be important for granule cell progenitors to respond to SHH signaling during cerebellar development [18, 93] and changes in chromatin topology and conformation have previously been described in neural development [94]. Additionally, midbrain-hindbrain boundary specification has been linked to a genetic network involving not only FGF signaling but also CHD7, coding for chromatin modifier chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 7 [95, 96]. Notably, our study suggests that cilia play a minor role in predominantly brainstem malformations, in contrast to malformations of both cerebellum and brainstem. Primary cilia are microtubule-based organelles that act as antennae, that receive and transduce chemo- and mechanosensory signals. Cilia function as structural checkpoints for cell cycle progression and as a signaling hub that regulates diverse pathways such as WNT and SHH signaling. These ciliary signaling routes have been unveiled by our work and by others [18, 96–98], to have essential roles during early cerebellar development and medial cerebellar fusion [99]. The hindbrain abnormalities seen in ciliopathies can be attributed to failure of expansion of granular progenitor cells due to the loss of their ability to receive the SHH signal from Purkinje cells during external granular layer expansion [100, 101]. The pathways involved in predominantly brainstem abnormalities included mostly kinesin and cytoskeletal dynamics. This is probably due to the importance of neuronal migration during brainstem and cranial nerve development. Adequate patterning of neuronal specification, migration, and topographic connectivity during development is essential to establish an interconnected network in the mature hindbrain [102, 103]. In addition, neuronal progenitors of cranial nerves display programmed migratory behaviors sending axons along defined trajectories along very long distances. It must be noted however, that kinesins also play a key role in anterograde ciliary trafficking, especially kinesin II motors [97]. This observation establishes a link to ciliary problems in the brainstem abnormalities subgroup as well.

Comparing the three investigated anatomical subgroups, we show the underlying biological pathways between the groups have large overlap as well. Our work exposes that shared mechanisms may underlie different hindbrain processes, even if the cells originate from different primordia. For example, this is illustrated by cerebellar and cranial nerve development which are both influenced by SHH signaling as discussed above. It is well-known that many cerebellar malformations in humans are also associated with abnormalities in other brain regions and organ systems. This can be explained due to many hindbrain development genes being essential for central nervous system and organ development in general, and expressed in the brain and other tissues. This is illustrated by the gene expression data, which in part shows early-development and brain specific expression, but also reflects some genes are ubiquitously expressed over multiple brain regions and time. How pathology is influenced by the timing of gene expression during development is not explored in this work, but undoubtedly relevant. The observed overlap in pathways from the enrichment analysis suggests there are few essential pathways underlying the development of abnormalities of the hindbrain being influenced by multiple biological processes.

There is an extensive and growing number of genes that when mutated can affect hindbrain development. Here, we identified 40 candidate genes which may play a role in hindbrain development. We hypothesize that mutations in these genes may clarify some of the human hindbrain abnormalities without a molecular diagnosis in the future. The top candidate genes include NCAM1 and TRRAP, since knockout mice for these genes have been reported to exhibit posterior fossa abnormalities [85, 86]. Ncam1 knock-out mice exhibit a 10% reduction in brain weight, with small hippocampus [85, 104], olfactory bulb and cerebellar dysmorphology. NCAM1 encodes a membrane-bound neural cell adhesion glycoprotein important for migration of precursor cells and pathfinding of axons, and shown to be developmentally expressed in the cerebellum [105]. It has been hypothesized that NCAM1 plays a role in Purkinje cell layer formation in early cerebellar development [106]. A one base-pair duplication in NCAM1 has been reported as pathogenic in humans and linked to intellectual disability [107]. Second, Trrap is an essential gene during embryogenesis and in proliferating cells, which encodes a transformation/transcription domain-associated protein, playing a role in microtubule dynamics and histone acetylation. Null mutations in Trrap cause lethality in mice due to cell cycle progression problems [108]. However, viable Trrap conditional mutants exhibit microcephaly due to premature differentiation of apical neuronal progenitors into intermediate progenitors and neurons [86, 109]. In these mutant mice, no abnormalities in major neurogenesis pathways, such as Pax6, Wnt or SHH, were observed. Notably, specific deletion of Trrap in Purkinje cells can cause cerebellar degeneration [110]. Recently, a TRRAP missense mutation was associated with a phenotype of intellectual disability [111]. Interestingly, hindbrain defects were identified in this cohort with cerebellar hypoplasia in 6 individuals, and brainstem atrophy in 2 individuals. The region in which the TRRAP variants were identified was highly constrained, which might indicate mutations outside this region to be associated with prenatal lethality, in line with loss of function lethality in mice. We are aware that other candidate genes for hindbrain abnormalities in humans might be present in the gene co-expression networks.

The current work illustrates the remaining challenges in identifying why many different genes can disrupt the hindbrain developmental pathways. First, how different pathways affect the developmental processes in different hindbrain cell types remains to be further elucidated. Data on gene expression specifically in hindbrain cellular subtypes [112] will play an important role in unraveling when and how the different genetic insults exert their action. Furthermore, many of the genetic defects discussed here produce disruption in processes that are not unique for brain cells. How genes acting in apparently ubiquitous processes result in brain-specific phenotypes remains largely unknown. Last, the role of repair mechanisms, such as glia-to-neuron transitions [113], is still insufficiently explored, while it probably affects the pathogenesis of pathways. Studies combining single cell RNA sequencing with lineage-tracing, might uncover relevant repair mechanisms in the future.

We recognize several limitations to this work. First, the rapid identification of new genetic defects affecting hindbrain development in humans poses a challenge when generating current gene lists, that might be outdated at time of publishing. In addition, the magnitude of genetic data may have led to the inadvertent omission of rare variants with potential clinical significance. Second, co-expression gene analysis allows solely to hypothesize gene functional relations, whereas genes involved in the same pathway may not necessarily co-express in time or in cell type. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn on temporal or anatomical relations. Nevertheless, analyzing co-variations of gene expression through a bioinformatic approach can improve understanding of these complicated biological processes. Last, the analysis does not account for gene mutation type in pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In short, we briefly reviewed recent findings in hindbrain genetic abnormalities. Using the hindbrain abnormality disease genes, we identified key biological processes involved in hindbrain development and propose candidate genes for genetically unexplained hindbrain abnormalities. Given that our work identified shared pathways in the three groups of hindbrain abnormalities, diagnostic genetic testing in unexplained cases with a hindbrain abnormality likely requires a holistic approach focussing on all genes reported to cause hindbrain disorders in humans and mice. The identification of novel disease genes in combination with data on the earliest patterning events with brain architecture and cell fate will further increase the understanding of the pathogenesis of hindbrain disorders in the future.
