Abstract
We present a sample of 127 gas-bearing dwarf galaxies around 56 late-type host galaxies within 30 Mpc using 21-cm H i data from the WALLABY, MHONGOOSE, and ALFALFA surveys. We characterize the environment of each dwarf galaxy based on its host galaxy halo and derive optical properties using the DESI Legacy Surveys for 110. The gaseous satellites span log ⁡ ( M H ​ i / M ⊙ ) = 5.7 − 9.7 \log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot})=5.7-9.7 and log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) = 5.6 − 10.0 \log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=5.6-10.0 , with a median velocity line-width of W 50 = 37 W_{50}=37 km ​ s − 1 \rm km~s^{-1} , comparable to the Local Group gaseous dwarf galaxies. We assess the H i mass sensitivity of the data by injecting model dwarf galaxies and find M H ​ i , lim = 10 6.7 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}=10^{6.7}M_{\odot} for WALLABY and M H ​ i , lim = 10 5.4 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}=10^{5.4}M_{\odot} for MHONGOOSE at 10 Mpc. With this sensitivity, our sample shows lower average gas-to-stellar mass ratios ( M H ​ i / M ⋆ M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\star} ) than literature dwarf galaxy samples. The abundance of gaseous satellites per host is low and increases with host mass: 0 − 2 0-2 for isolated dwarf galaxy hosts and 0 − 5 0-5 for Milky Way-mass spiral hosts. These numbers are consistent with the Milky Way, M31, and star-forming satellite abundances from recent deep optical surveys. The inferred quenched fractions and gas-depleted satellites indicate that environmental quenching is effective in Milky Way-mass hosts, likely driven by gas stripping processes.



I
Introduction

The lowest-mass “dwarf” (
M_{\star}\leq 10^{9}~M_{\odot}
) galaxies are the most abundant type of galaxies in the Universe
(
Binggeli et al. 1990
)
and the building blocks for larger systems
(
Blumenthal et al. 1984
;
White & Frenk 1991
;
Navarro et al. 1995
)
. Dwarf galaxies in low-density environments often contain more gas – the fuel for star formation – than stars
(
Schombert et al. 2001
;
Geha et al. 2006
;
Scholte et al. 2024
)
; and collectively, the baryonic matter makes up only a small percentage of the total mass compared to the dark matter
(
Garrison-Kimmel et al. 2017
;
Read et al. 2017
;
Behroozi et al. 2019
;
Munshi et al. 2021
;
Lelli 2022
)
. This suggests that dwarf galaxies are inefficient in forming stars, which emphasizes the need to include baryonic processes, such as stellar feedback and photoionization, in dwarf galaxy evolution models
(
Somerville & Davé 2015
;
Wright et al. 2024
)
. Dwarf galaxies are particularly sensitive to baryonic processes because of their low masses and shallow gravitational potentials (see reviews by
Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin 2017
;
Sales et al. 2022
).

External processes, such as galaxy environment, also play a crucial role in shaping a dwarf galaxy’s baryonic content. This is clearly demonstrated by dwarf galaxies in our own Local Group (LG;
McConnachie 2012
). Over 90% of the satellite dwarf galaxies of the Milky Way (MW) or M31 are devoid of gas
(
Grcevich & Putman 2009
;
Spekkens et al. 2014
;
Putman et al. 2021
)
and quenched of star formation
(
Wetzel et al. 2015
)
. Within the MW/M31 dark matter halos, only
2-4
(usually the most massive) dwarf satellites still contain gas, while dozens more are gas-poor
(
Putman et al. 2021
)
. This is in direct contrast with the Local Group “field” dwarf galaxies unassociated with either host
(
McConnachie 2012
)
and the isolated dwarf galaxies outside the Local Group
(
Geha et al. 2012
;
Karachentsev et al. 2018a
)
, which tend to be gas-rich and star-forming. The MW/M31 environment removes gas in the dwarf satellites and ultimately quenches their star formation.

Several physical mechanisms can lead to gas stripping and the eventual quenching of satellite galaxies (see review by
Cortese et al. 2021
), and their relative strengths have been examined in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of MW-like systems (e.g.,
Fillingham et al. 2015
;
Simpson et al. 2018
;
Akins et al. 2021
;
Simons et al. 2020
;
Samuel et al. 2022
;
Engler et al. 2023
;
Christensen et al. 2024
;
Rodríguez-Cardoso et al. 2025
). Across all simulations, the leading mechanism for dwarf satellite gas loss is ram pressure stripping (RPS;
Gunn & Gott 1972
), the direct removal of satellite gas by the host gaseous halo, quenching the dwarf satellites within
1-2
Gyr timescales. RPS is a hydrodynamical process acting on the gas component, and not directly on the stars (or dark matter), which are effectively collisionless
(
Boselli & Gavazzi 2006
)
. Gas loss via RPS is often accompanied by tidal forces from the host galaxy, which, under close and/or repeated encounters, can remove gas, stars, and dark matter outside the tidal radii of the satellites
(
Mateo et al. 2008
;
Peñarrubia et al. 2008
;
Riley et al. 2025
)
and disturb the internal structure of the satellites
(
Mayer et al. 2001
;
Mayer et al. 2006
;
Serra et al. 2023
)
. Independent of the host environment, cosmic reionization
(
Gnedin 2000
;
Somerville 2002
)
can directly quench the smallest “ultra-faint” (
M_{\star}\leq 10^{5}~M_{\odot}
) dwarf galaxies
(
Brown et al. 2014
;
Weisz et al. 2014
;
Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin 2017
)
.

Theoretical models are often tailored to MW-like systems because of the strong observational constraints available. This motivates the question of how representative the MW satellite population is, which is investigated by a large and growing body of literature on satellites around external galaxies. Such observations include targeted deep searches of individual host galaxies (e.g.,
Spencer et al. 2014
;
Smercina et al. 2018
;
Smercina et al. 2020
;
Müller et al. 2019
;
Müller et al. 2024
;
Bennet et al. 2019
;
Garling et al. 2021
;
Mutlu-Pakdil et al. 2022
;
Mutlu-Pakdil et al. 2024
;
Davis et al. 2024
), as well as large samples that enable statistical comparisons across host galaxies, e.g., the Satellites Around Galactic Analogs (SAGA) survey
(
Mao et al. 2024
;
Geha et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
and the Exploration of Local VolumE Satellites (ELVES) survey
(
Carlsten et al. 2022
)
. These studies have found that the MW and its satellite properties are typical (i.e., within reasonable host-to-host scatter) among the external analogs
(
Carlsten et al. 2022
;
Mao et al. 2024
)
. Host-to-host scatter in satellite populations is non-negligible, which can originate from the mass
(
Carlsten et al. 2022
)
, merger history
(
Smercina et al. 2022
)
, environment
(
Mutlu-Pakdil et al. 2024
)
, and morphological type of the host galaxy
(
Trentham & Tully 2009
;
Crnojević et al. 2019
)
. On a distinctively smaller mass scale, new surveys have also started to characterize satellites of Magellanic-mass dwarf hosts
(
Hunter et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
, which constrains the dependence of satellite quenching on host masses across a broader range
(
Jahn et al. 2022
;
Nadler et al. 2023
)
.

It remains unclear how satellite baryons, particularly the gaseous ones, are shaped by environment beyond the Local Group. Gas is directly subject to RPS; it is an ideal tracer for galaxy environment
(
Haynes et al. 1984
)
that is unconstrained in optical satellite studies. Previous work has found a low number of gas-rich dwarf satellites per spiral host
(
Karunakaran et al. 2022
;
Zhu & Putman 2023
)
or Local Group analog
(
Pisano et al. 2011
)
, indicating an effective satellite stripping picture similar to that in the MW/M31
(
Putman et al. 2021
)
. However, the data were insufficient to simultaneously constrain the baryonic (H
i
and stellar) masses and properties of these satellites. In this work, we present a new sample of gaseous satellite galaxies around late-type hosts using data from two recent H
i
surveys, WALLABY (the Widefield ASKAP L-band Legacy All-sky Blind surveY;
Koribalski et al. 2020
;
Westmeier et al. 2022
;
Murugeshan et al. 2024
) and MHONGOOSE (MeerKAT HI Observations of Nearby Galactic Objects - Observing Southern Emitters;
de Blok et al. 2024
). We derive the baryonic properties of the dwarf galaxies, assess the impact of environment on dwarf galaxy baryons, and compare the satellite population with those of the MW, M31, and external analogs.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
describes the H
i
surveys used in this work. Section
III
details our methodology, including host galaxy selection and characterization (Section
III.1
), satellite search via H
i
source finding (Section
III.2
), H
i
sensitivity assessment (Section
III.3
), optical photometry fitting and stellar mass calibration (Section
III.4
), and satellite environment characterization (Section
III.5
). Section
IV
presents our main results, covering the satellite H
i
properties (Section
IV.1
), and gas-stellar mass relations (Section
IV.2
), trends of satellite depletion with host mass (Section
IV.3
), and the abundance of gaseous satellites per host (Section
IV.4
). Then, Section
V
compares our H
i
sample with deep optical studies. Section
VI
discusses our findings in the context of environmental gas loss theory. Finally, Section
VII
summarizes our main findings. We adopt
H_{0}=70~\rm km/s/Mpc
throughout.

II
Data

This paper uses two recent 21-cm H
i
surveys, WALLABY and MHONGOOSE, as the data sources for nearby H
i
-bearing dwarf satellite galaxies. WALLABY
(
Koribalski et al. 2020
)
is an ASKAP wide-field survey. So far, Phase 1
(
Westmeier et al. 2022
)
and Phase 2
(
Murugeshan et al. 2024
)
of the WALLABY pilot surveys have been completed, each covering
\sim
180 deg
2
regions of the sky (in a few
\sim 30-120
deg
2
footprints), and collectively detected over 2000 H
i
sources up to
z\approx 0.09
. The data we use have a spatial resolution of 30″ and a channel spacing of 3.91
\rm km~s^{-1}
. The typical rms noise per channel for the WALLABY pilot surveys is
\sigma\approx 1.75\rm~mJy
per beam
(
Westmeier et al. 2022
;
Murugeshan et al. 2024
)
. The ongoing full survey observations will operate at a similar sensitivity to Pilot Phase 2, but will eventually cover 14000 deg
2
of the southern sky. In this work, we use data from the two publicly available pilot surveys. We describe our selection method for nearby spiral host galaxies in Section
III.1
below.

MHONGOOSE is a deep MeerKAT survey of thirty star-forming galaxies in the southern sky within distances of 30 Mpc
(
de Blok et al. 2024
)
. Each target galaxy has a uniform angular coverage of 1.5
\deg
in diameter. With the primary scientific goal of studying low column density accretion, the target galaxies in MHONGOOSE were selected to have a range of masses, not to exhibit strong tidal features, and not to reside within the densest environments. This selection makes the MHONGOOSE targets ideal host galaxies for satellite studies: their environments are not biased by large-scale structures such as galaxy clusters or massive groups, and the deep sensitivity allows the detection of low-mass gaseous satellites. In this work, we utilize the full-depth MHONGOOSE data cubes with a 55-hour integration time on each host galaxy field, which contain new low-mass companion galaxies relative to the 5.5-hour single-track data listed in Table 7 of
de Blok et al. 2024
. We describe our H
i
source finding method to uncover these low-mass sources in Section
III.2
. MHONGOOSE data cubes are available in a range of spatial resolutions; the cubes we use in this work (
r15_t00
1
1

1

At this resolution, the beam size is most comparable to WALLABY, and the high column sensitivity is ideal for the detection of faint dwarf galaxies; see Table 4 of
de Blok et al. 2024
.
) have a spatial resolution of 30″ and a channel spacing of 1.38
\rm km~s^{-1}
. The typical rms noise per channel at this resolution is
\sigma\approx 0.154\rm~mJy
per beam
(
de Blok et al. 2024
)
.

Because of the scope and strategies of the individual surveys, WALLABY and MHONGOOSE differ by approximately an order of magnitude in H
i
sensitivity (see, e.g., Fig. 2 in
de Blok et al. 2024
). This leads to varying abilities in recovering the lowest-mass dwarf galaxy population around each host galaxy. In Section
III.3
below, we describe our method to systematically assess the dwarf satellite mass completeness for all host galaxies in our sample across the different surveys.

When presenting results, we often include the sample from our previous work of gaseous dwarf satellites from the ALFALFA survey (
Zhu & Putman 2023
; 46 H
i
-bearing dwarf galaxies around 15 spiral hosts). The ALFALFA survey is a wide-field survey of
\sim
31500 extragalactic H
i
sources, mapping
\sim
7000 deg
2
of the northern sky accessible to the Arecibo telescope
(
Giovanelli et al. 2005
;
Haynes et al. 2011
;
Haynes et al. 2018
)
. The average rms noise per channel from the final survey catalog is
\sigma\approx 2.41\rm~mJy
per beam
(
Haynes et al. 2018
)
. Being a single-dish survey, the spatial and velocity resolutions of ALFALFA (4′ and 10
\rm km~s^{-1}
) are inferior compared with those of WALLABY and MHONGOOSE. We also extensively compare with the H
i
-bearing dwarf galaxies within 2 Mpc in the Local Group
(
Putman et al. 2021
)
, and with gaseous dwarf galaxy samples in various environments in the literature (Section
IV.2
).

III
Methodology

III.1
The Host Galaxy Sample

This section describes our host galaxy sample, first on how we selected the host galaxies from the WALLABY and MHONGOOSE surveys (Section
II
), and then on the derivation of their physical properties. The host sample contains 56 H
i
-bearing galaxies within 30 Mpc: 41 from WALLABY and MHONGOOSE, and 15 from ALFALFA in our previous work
(
Zhu & Putman 2023
)
. The host galaxies span several orders of magnitude in mass, from isolated dwarf hosts to M31-mass spiral galaxies, as summarized in Figure
1
and Table
1
.

From the WALLABY pilot survey catalogs
(
Westmeier et al. 2022
;
Murugeshan et al. 2024
)
, we select nearby galaxies that are Magellanic mass or higher. We apply the following cuts: (i)
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\geq 10^{8}M_{\odot}
and
W_{50}\geq 120

\rm km~s^{-1}

(see
Zhu & Putman 2023
), and (ii) a velocity-flow distance of
D_{\rm flow}=V_{\rm los}/H_{0}\leq 30
Mpc. The distance cut is to achieve a reasonable sample size by covering a large enough volume, while maintaining an H
i
sensitivity down to dwarf galaxy masses. After the initial selection, we manually inspect each galaxy and exclude those whose halo region, approximated by a radius of 300 kpc around the galaxy
2
2

2

The 300 kpc radius is an upper limit and always greater than
R_{200}
of the WALLABY hosts; see Table
1
.
, is not fully covered by the WALLABY footprints. This step excludes about 20 galaxies close to the field edge with partial halo coverages that are subject to significantly higher noise as a result (see noise variation maps in Appendix A of
Westmeier et al. 2022
). We also excluded one galaxy in an extremely close binary system, PGC 46579 (equivalently MCG -03-34-054), which is
\sim
1.1 arcmin away from its companion, MCG -03-34-053. This results in 15 host galaxies from WALLABY, as summarized in the first section of Table
1
. Some host galaxies are in Local Group-like pairs, while others contain massive satellites analogous to the MW-LMC system.

The MHONGOOSE survey maps the H
i
emission in fields of 1.5
\deg
in diameter around 30 target galaxies (see Section
II
and
de Blok et al. 2024
). We select host galaxies out of these 30. First, we note that the target galaxies span a large range in mass, from low-mass dwarfs (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}<10.5
) to MW-mass spirals (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}\approx 12
). As we later demonstrate in Section
III.3
, the H
i
sensitivity around the MHONGOOSE dwarf target galaxies is deep enough for the detection of the lowest-mass gaseous dwarf galaxies (
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},\rm lim}/M_{\odot}\approx 5.4
). We thus include the dwarf targets in our host sample, as long as they are relatively isolated, to study the gaseous satellites of low-mass hosts. One dwarf target, UGCA 015 (equivalently J0049-20), is itself a satellite of a nearby spiral galaxy, NGC 247
(
de Blok et al. 2024
)
, and is excluded from our sample.

Under the uniform 1.5
\deg
field coverage, the projected halo areas of the MHONGOOSE target galaxies are not always fully covered. We further excluded three galaxies with low halo coverage (
R_{\rm cover}/R_{200}<0.7
; see Figure
1
) from our analyses. In some cases, the MHONGOOSE cube captures another galaxy of comparable or higher (e.g., NGC 4808 in the field of UGCA 307) stellar mass relative to the target galaxy. These represent pair-like environments, where the non-target galaxy typically has low halo coverage; we exclude all such non-target galaxies in our host sample. In total, 26 MHONGOOSE target galaxies are included as hosts, as listed in the second part of Table
1
. Most (20 out of 26) have full projected virial radius coverage (
R_{\rm cover}/R_{200}\geq 1
); we indicate hosts with partial halo coverage in the results (Section
IV
).

With this H
i
-selected host galaxy sample, we cross-match with optical surveys to obtain their stellar masses (
M_{*}
) and distances (
D
; typically redshift independent). Our WALLABY hosts are well-studied nearby galaxies, all of which are covered in the
z=0
Multiwavelength Galaxy Synthesis project (z0MGS;
Leroy et al. 2019
). We thus obtain their distances
3
3

3

Distances from z0MGS are compiled from the Extragalactic Distance Database (EDD;
Tully et al. 2009
, incorporating high-precision TRGB distances where available), CosmicFlows
(
Tully et al. 2016
)
, and the Lyon Extragalactic Database (LEDA;
Paturel et al. 2003
).
and stellar masses from
Leroy et al. 2019
, unless targeted high-precision distance measurements exist in the literature (NGC 4666 from
Shappee et al. 2016
). For the MHONGOOSE hosts, we follow the published distances and stellar masses in
de Blok et al. 2024
(see their Table 1), where distances are also from z0MGS
(
Leroy et al. 2019
)
wherever available, and the stellar masses are derived from WISE.

Satellite galaxies reside in the dark matter halos of the host galaxies. We derive the host halo masses using the median stellar mass-halo mass (SMHM) relation from the empirical modeling work, UniverseMachine
(
Behroozi et al. 2019
)
. Given the literature stellar masses of the host galaxies (Table
1
), we solve for their halo masses
M_{\rm halo,peak}
based on the median SMHM relation for star-forming central galaxies at redshift
z=0
from
Behroozi et al. 2019
. We assume here that
M_{\rm halo,peak}\approx M_{200}
. That is, the halo mass derived from
Behroozi et al. 2019
, which follows the definition of
Bryan & Norman 1998
, is approximately consistent with
M_{200}

(
White 2001
)
at
z\approx 0
. Given
M_{200}
, we calculate the virial radius (
R_{200}
) based on the standard definition,
M_{200}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\cdot 200\rho_{\rm crit}R_{200}^{3}
. Finally, we define the host scaling velocity as the point mass escape velocity evaluated at
r=R_{200}
,
V_{\rm esc}(R_{200})=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_{200}}{R_{200}}}
.

\pm 300

\pm 500

III.3
Sensitivity and Completeness Assessment

Rigorous assessment of H
i
sensitivity is required to distinguish sensitivity-limited non-detections from true gas loss. Our host galaxy sample (§
III.1
) collates sources from different surveys and at a range of distances. For wide-field surveys like the WALLABY pilot surveys
(
Westmeier et al. 2022
;
Murugeshan et al. 2024
)
and ALFALFA
(
Haynes et al. 2011
;
Haynes et al. 2018
)
, field-to-field variations also contribute to inhomogeneities in the local noise levels. As a result, the H
i
sensitivity and completeness of the detected gaseous dwarf population can vary substantially within our host sample.

We have developed a flexible injection pipeline to assess the H
i
mass sensitivity (
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}
) of a given data cube. The pipeline is detailed in Appendix
B
. In this section, we present the sensitivity results of the host galaxies in our sample (Table
1
) as derived from this pipeline, assess their completeness to the Local Group gas-rich dwarf galaxy population, and summarize the scaling relations of the different H
i
surveys (Figure
2
).

Using the injection pipeline, we added the H
i
fluxes of modeled low-mass dwarf galaxies (Appendix
B.1
) into the WALLABY and MHONGOOSE host galaxy data cubes (§
III.2
). We derived the H
i
mass thresholds at 25%, 50%, 90% recovery rates (Appendix
B.2
), which represent the minimum detectable H
i
masses in these fields. We adopt the 50% detectability thresholds from the injection tests as the sensitivity limits for the host galaxies, as denoted in Table
1
. The scatter points in Figure
2
display the mass thresholds versus the host galaxy distances. The average sensitivities of the WALLABY (wide-field; in blue) and MHONGOOSE (deep targeted; in orange) host galaxies are separated by
\sim
1.6 dex: the 15 WALLABY hosts have a median sensitivity threshold of
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}/M_{\odot}\approx 7.1
, and the 26 MHONGOOSE hosts have a median of
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}/M_{\odot}\approx 5.5
.

Sensitivity can be converted to completeness relative to a reference population. Here, we chose the Local Group (LG) gaseous dwarf population from
Putman et al. 2021
as the reference population because of the data depth (thanks to proximity). There are 26 LG dwarf galaxies within 2 Mpc with H
i
detections, where the median H

\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot}\approx 6.9
. We use the cumulative
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
distribution of these 26 to convert from an H

mass to the percentile of LG gaseous dwarf galaxies above that mass, which is shown in the right-hand y-axis of Figure

\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}/M_{\odot}\approx 7.1
) converts to
\sim
38% complete relative to the LG population; the MHONGOOSE hosts’ median sensitivity (
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}/M_{\odot}\approx 5.5
) converts to
\sim

We use the empirical sensitivity limits from the injection tests (scatter points in Figure
2
) to characterize the depths of the surveys. Given that the minimum detectable H
i
mass (
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}
) scales with distance (
D_{\rm Mpc}
) and the integrated flux limit (
S_{V,\rm lim}
),

M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}(M_{\odot})=2.356\times 10^{5}\cdot D_{\rm Mpc}^{2}\cdot\big(\frac{S_{V,\rm lim}}{\rm Jy\cdot km~s^{-1}}\big)

(1)

we can characterize an integrated signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to represent the survey sensitivity in units of the typical noise levels (
\sigma
in Jy/beam/channel; see Section
II
) of the surveys. The SNR is given by the summation of fluxes divided by the rms noise in each channel
i
(assuming the source is spatially unresolved, which is mostly true for the low-mass dwarf galaxies),

\displaystyle\rm SNR

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm chan}}{S_{i}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm chan}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\approx\frac{(S_{V,\rm lim}/V_{\rm res})}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{\rm chan}}}\approx\frac{S_{V,\rm lim}}{\sigma\sqrt{2W_{50}V_{\rm res}}}

(2)

where
V_{\rm res}
is the velocity resolution (in
\rm km~s^{-1}
),
N_{\rm chan}
is the number of velocity channels, and
W_{50}
(in
\rm km~s^{-1}
) is the velocity FWHM of the source. In equation
2
, we have made two simplifications, (i) the noise per channel
\sigma_{i}
averages out to
\sigma
such that
\sum\sigma_{i}^{2}\approx N_{\rm chan}\sigma^{2}
, and (ii) the total number of velocity channels of a source can be approximated as
N_{\rm chan}\approx 2W_{50}/V_{\rm res}
, which is a reasonable approximation for dwarf galaxies harboring Gaussian-like emission profiles.

Combining equations
1
and
2
, we obtain the final form of a survey’s H
i
sensitivity,

M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}(M_{\odot})=2.356\times 10^{5}\cdot D_{\rm Mpc}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{2W_{50}V_{\rm res}}\cdot\rm SNR\cdot\sigma

(3)

We fit for the effective SNR of each survey (equation
3
) taking the following inputs:
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}
and
D_{\rm Mpc}
from the host injection test,
W_{50,\rm inject}=25
km/s from our model assumption (§
B.1
), and
V_{\rm res}
and
\sigma
from the WALLABY and MHONGOOSE surveys (see Section
II
). We obtained effective SNRs of 9.02 for WALLABY and 8.66 for MHONGOOSE via least-squares fitting.

), which we will use to evaluate the observational completeness in our results (Section

). At 10 Mpc, for example, the WALLABY pilot surveys (with

) are only
0.15

).

III.4
Optical photometry and stellar mass calibration

This section describes the stellar mass calibration for the dwarf galaxies. For candidate H
i
detections with visible optical counterparts (§
III.2
), we measure optical photometry in the
g
- and
r
-bands from Legacy DR10
(
Dey et al. 2019
;
Lang et al. 2016
)
. We calculate dereddened optical magnitudes using the total Sérsic flux in each band and the
Schlegel et al. 1998
dust reddening map with updated Galactic Extinction coefficients for the DECam filters from the Legacy documentation following
Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011
. Sérsic fluxes are measured using an updated version of the Bayesian Sérsic profile fitting tool
pysersic

(
Pasha & Miller 2023
;
Asali et al. 2025
)
. We use the
pysersic
autoprior function to generate a prior on the total flux and
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
for each source, and we set uniform priors between
0<e<0.9
for ellipticity,
0<\phi<2\pi
for position angle, and
0.3<n<8.0
for Sérsic index for all sources.

While most galaxy brightness profiles can be reasonably approximated as Sérsic profiles, we explicitly check how our measured photometry is affected by this choice of model by comparing to aperture photometry. Aperture and Sérsic-derived fluxes can differ because the Sérsic profile integrates light to large radii and can capture extended low–surface-brightness emission whereas our aperture, defined at 3
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
from our best fit Sérsic model, can capture bright star-forming clumps or substructure not well described by the smooth Sérsic fit. When we translate these fluxes into stellar mass estimates, we find typical residuals between the two methods of within 0.3 dex in stellar mass. Stellar masses are calculated using the optical color calibration from
de los Reyes et al. 2025
, assuming a Chabrier IMF. We ensure all literature relations compared against in this work use a Chabrier IMF, since the offset due to different IMF assumptions is expected to dominate the systematic uncertainty between mass calibrations
(
Bell & de Jong 2001
;
Bell et al. 2003
)
. Beyond IMF differences, stellar mass calibrations can still vary at the
\sim
0.1–0.2 dex level depending on methodology (e.g., SED fitting vs. color calibrations;
Pacifici et al. 2023
;
de los Reyes et al. 2025
), but these differences are minor and do not affect our main results.

We manually inspected all cases where the Sérsic- and aperture-derived stellar masses differed by more than 0.3 dex. In four such cases, we report the optical magnitudes and stellar masses from aperture photometry, as these galaxies exhibit strong non-Sérsic features or significant residuals relative to the best-fit profile. These include bright systems with bars, prominent bulges, or intense star-forming clumps.

III.5
Characterizing satellite environment

We characterized the environment of gaseous dwarf galaxies identified in Section
III.2
, considering only systems within
\pm 300
km/s of their host galaxies’ systemic velocity. Galaxies with larger line-of-sight velocity differences (
|\Delta V_{\rm los}|>300
km/s) are extremely unlikely to be bound satellites of MW-mass or lower hosts
(
McConnachie 2012
;
Mao et al. 2024
)
and are excluded from the environment analysis.

Satellites are defined as galaxies gravitationally bound to the host galaxy, requiring the satellite-host relative velocity (
\Delta\vec{V}
) at the satellite location (
r
) to be lower than the local escape velocity,
|\Delta\vec{V}(r)|<V_{\rm esc}(r)
. Given the host halo mass
M_{200}
, the escape velocity can be approximated under a point-mass assumption as
V_{\rm esc}(r)=\sqrt{2GM_{200}/r}
. In our sample, host masses vary considerably, so we scale the gravitationally bound condition by host halo properties,

\frac{|\Delta\vec{V}(r)|}{V_{\rm esc}(R_{200})}<\frac{V_{\rm esc}(r)}{V_{\rm esc}(R_{200})}=\left(\frac{r}{R_{200}}\right)^{-1/2}

(4)

In equation
4
, the halo mass term on the right-hand side cancels out, leaving only a distance term scaled by the host’s virial radius (
r/R_{200}
). In observations, however, only projections of the three-dimensional (3D) separations are available: the on-sky distance between a dwarf galaxy and its host (
d_{\rm proj}
) is a 2D projection of the spatial separation
r
, and the line-of-sight velocity difference (
\Delta V_{\rm los}
) is a 1D projection of the velocity difference
|\Delta\vec{V}|
. We can express equation
4
in the observable plane
(d_{\rm proj},\Delta V_{\rm los})
as

\frac{|\Delta V_{\rm los}|}{V_{\rm esc}(R_{200})}<\frac{1}{R_{V}\sqrt{R_{d}}}\left(\frac{d_{\rm proj}}{R_{200}}\right)^{-1/2}

(5)

where
R_{d}=r/d_{\rm proj}\geq 1
and
R_{V}=|\Delta\vec{V}|/\Delta V_{\rm los}\geq 1
. We adopt the lower limits for the geometric factors,
R_{d}=R_{V}=1
, and classify galaxies that meet this condition (equation
5
) as satellite candidates; the remainder are considered unbound. This inclusive definition of satellites prioritizes sample completeness at the cost of including potential interlopers (unassociated galaxies that appear close in projection; see §
VI
). The resulting classifications and projected phase-space distributions are presented below.

IV
Results

We have built a sample of 127 gaseous dwarf galaxies located near 56 nearby host galaxies – 41 from WALLABY and MHONGOOSE (as described in §
III.1
) and 15 from ALFALFA
(

The dwarf galaxy masses we present in Table

IV.3
Satellite gas depletion versus host mass

In the previous section, we presented the atomic gas fraction (
f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
; Figure
6
) of our dwarf galaxy sample, which does not show a clear trend with the satellite-host separation scaled by host mass (right panel of Figure
5

The left panel of Figure
7
shows the H
i
mass distribution of the satellite candidates, excluding unbound galaxies (§
III.5
). Gray dashed contours indicate fractions of the host galaxy’s baryon budget,
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}=10^{-N}f_{b}M_{\rm 200,host}
(
N=1,2,3
…), where
f_{b}=\Omega_{b}/\Omega_{m}\approx 15.8\%
is the cosmic baryon fraction
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. Neutral gas in satellite galaxies only has a small contribution to the host baryon budget: the most gas-rich satellites amount to an average of
\sim 10^{-3}
(at most
\sim 10^{-2}
) of the host’s
f_{b}M_{200}
, which converts to an average of
3.5\%
of the host disk’s H
i
mass (Table
1
). Dwarf hosts of
M_{200}\leq 10^{11}M_{\odot}
rarely contain gaseous satellites (downward arrows), which will be described in §
IV.4
. For Magellanic-mass hosts and above (
M_{200}>10^{11}M_{\odot}
), satellites occupy a wide range of
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
: up to
\sim 10^{-2}f_{b}M_{200}
, and down to approximately the host H
i
sensitivity limits (gray triangles) as low as
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\approx 10^{6}M_{\odot}
.

Satellite H
i
masses generally increase with their stellar masses (point colors; also see Figure
5
). But some satellites of MW-mass hosts (
M_{200}\geq 10^{12}M_{\odot}
) are unusually gas poor for their stellar masses (
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\approx 10^{6.5}-10^{7.5}M_{\odot}
at
M_{\star}\approx 10^{8}-10^{9}M_{\odot}
; dark brown points). This suggests enhanced gas depletion, which we further quantify below.

Metrics of gas depletion (e.g.,
Cortese et al. 2021

\Delta\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
) as the difference between the measured
\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
of a dwarf galaxy and the expected average fraction (
<\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}>

\Delta\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}=\log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\star})-<\log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\star})>

(6)

where the expected fraction,
<\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}>

6

\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}-\log M_{\star}
dependence using linear regression, obtaining
<\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}>=-0.34\log M_{\star}+2.14
and a scatter of
\sigma\approx 0.67
dex. We chose our deep subsample (mostly MHONGOOSE galaxies) as the baseline for gas fractions because it is least biased by H
i
incompleteness. If using a field sample from the literature (Figure
6
) instead,
<\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}>
will adopt a similar slope but a higher intercept due to missing the most gas-poor dwarfs. Importantly,
\Delta\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
represents the
relative
degree of gas depletion within our satellite sample; it may be biased low relative to a field sample at similar sensitivities.

V
Comparison with optical studies: the abundance of dwarf satellites

In this section, we compare our H
i
sample with deep optical surveys of dwarf satellites around nearby host galaxies: the ELVES survey of spiral hosts
(
Carlsten et al. 2022
)
, the SAGA survey of Milky Way-analog hosts
(
Mao et al. 2024
)
, and the ELVES-Dwarfs survey of isolated Magellanic-mass dwarf hosts
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. We first define how we select galaxies from each survey to perform a fair comparison (§
V.1
), then compare the abundance of gas-rich satellites from our H
i
sample with the star-forming satellites from the optical surveys, and estimate the total (gas-rich/star-forming and quenched) satellite populations (§
V.2
).

V.1
Gaseous versus star-forming: defining the comparison samples

We select star-forming dwarf satellites from the optical surveys to compare with our H
i
sample. Star formation requires the presence of a cold gas reservoir. At the lowest star formation rates (SFR) traceable by H
\alpha
(
\rm SFR\lesssim 10^{-4}M_{\odot}/yr
, produced by a single O star), the expected H
i
mass based on the star-forming main sequence is
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\approx 10^{7}M_{\odot}

(
McGaugh et al. 2017
)
. Typical star-forming dwarf galaxies should therefore be detectable in H
i
given the sensitivities of our surveys (Figure
2
). Conversely, H
i
-bearing galaxies at
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\geq 10^{7.5}M_{\odot}
are almost ubiquitously detected in H
\alpha

(
Meurer et al. 2006
;
Van Sistine et al. 2016
)
.

However, the equivalence can break down at low masses. Dwarf galaxies exhibit time variability or “burstiness” in star formation histories
(
McQuinn et al. 2010a
;
McQuinn et al. 2010b
;
Weisz et al. 2011a
;
Weisz et al. 2011b
;
Iyer et al. 2020
)
. Gas-rich systems may appear quenched when observed during quiescent episodes of their star-forming cycles
(
Mintz et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, different indicators for star formation, like in SAGA (H
\alpha
and NUV emission) versus ELVES (morphology and color), can yield a
20-40\%
difference in the ultimate quenched fractions
(
Karunakaran et al. 2023
;
Geha et al. 2024
)
.

To summarize, for massive dwarf galaxies (e.g.,
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\geq 10^{7.5}M_{\odot}
), the gas-rich and star-forming classifications are largely interchangeable. For lower-mass galaxies (
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}<10^{7.5}M_{\odot}
), star formation classifications can be complicated by the selected tracer, tracer sensitivities, and intrinsic burstiness. As a consistency check, we cross-matched our ALFALFA sample with the ELVES survey in our previous work
(
Zhu & Putman 2023
)
, and found all eight H
i
-rich dwarf galaxies in ALFALFA were indeed classified as late-type (star-forming) in ELVES. We therefore assume that the sample-wise misclassification rate is low, and that the gas-rich dwarfs in our H
i
sample are comparable with the star-forming population in the optical surveys.

(

)
.

(

(

. Below the sensitivity limits of current extragalactic optical surveys (

(

) are likely lower limits; accounting for the undetected low-mass population may increase it to MW/M31 levels (
>90\%
;
Wetzel et al. 2015
).

VI
Comparison with gas stripping theory

The satellite abundance presented in the previous section suggests that environmental gas loss is effective in MW-mass hosts. Around these MW-mass hosts, we also identified several massive satellite candidates (
M_{\star}\approx 10^{8}-10^{9}~M_{\odot}
) that are highly gas-depleted (see Section
IV.3
). At these stellar masses, stellar feedback alone is unlikely to drive significant gas loss (e.g.,
Jones et al. 2025
) and active gas-removal mechanisms need to be invoked.

In this section, we examine our satellite sample in the context of ram pressure stripping (RPS) theory, the leading quenching mechanism for dwarf satellites in MW-like hosts (see the Introduction). The strength of ram pressure is set by
P_{\rm ram}=\rho_{\rm host}V_{\rm sat}^{2}

(
Gunn & Gott 1972
)
, where the stripping medium (
\rho_{\rm host}
) is the circumgalactic medium (CGM) of the star-forming host galaxy
(
Tumlinson et al. 2017
;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023
)
. While RPS likely also occurs in lower-mass hosts, the CGM densities of dwarf halos
(
Christensen et al. 2016
;
Hafen et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Piacitelli et al. 2025
)
are lower and far less constrained. We therefore focus on satellite candidates of MW-mass host galaxies (
\log M_{200,\rm host}/M_{\odot}>12
; identified in §
IV.3
), where RPS is expected to be more effective and the CGM properties better characterized.

We construct a simple model to estimate ram pressure across the satellite position-velocity phase space (
d_{\rm proj}-\Delta V_{\rm los}
; §
III.5
). For the stripping medium (
\rho_{\rm host}
), we adopt the density profile of a MW-like CGM from our previous work
(
Zhu et al. 2024a
)
, following the parameterization of
Miller & Bregman 2015
and scaled to match the LMC constraint of
Salem et al. 2015
. This results in electron densities of
n_{e}(r)=1.1\times 10^{-4}\rm cm^{-3}
at
r=50
kpc and
n_{e}(r)=1.3\times 10^{-5}\rm cm^{-3}
at
r=200
kpc, comparable with empirical modeling results of MW-mass halos
(
Faerman et al. 2020
)
.

We then apply a simple de-projection to estimate the satellite velocities (
V_{\rm sat}
) in the phase space. Assuming randomized viewing angles and spherical symmetry (§
III.5
), the average projected distances and line-of-sight velocities relate to their 3D counterparts via
<d_{\rm proj}>=\sqrt{2/3}\cdot r
, and
<\Delta V_{\rm los}>=\sqrt{1/3}\cdot V_{\rm sat,3D}
. Under this
averaged
de-projection, ram pressure can be expressed by,

\begin{split}P_{\rm ram}(r)&=\rho_{\rm host}(r)\cdot V_{\rm sat}(r)^{2}\\
&\approx\rho_{\rm host}(r=\sqrt{3/2}\cdot d_{\rm proj})\cdot(\sqrt{3}\cdot\Delta V_{\rm los})^{2}\end{split}

(7)

We compute
P_{\rm ram}
across the projected
(d_{\rm proj},\Delta V_{\rm los})
phase space, shown as the grayscale shadings in Figure
13
. The modeled
P_{\rm ram}
values are instantaneous estimates at given points in the phase space, without assuming past orbital histories. As expected, ram pressure increases with closer distance (i.e., higher host CGM densities) and higher satellite velocities, ranging from
P_{\rm ram}>10^{-12}~\rm dyne/cm^{2}
(darkest shading) to
P_{\rm ram}<10^{-15}~\rm dyne/cm^{2}
(lightest shading near zero velocity).

In Figure
13
, satellite candidates of MW-mass hosts are shown in the projected phase space, color-coded by gas depletion (see Figure
7
). The color map is scaled to
\pm 2\sigma
where
\sigma\approx 0.67
dex is the scatter in our gas fraction fit (§
IV.3
). The light-blue shaded regions denote escape velocity curves for the range of MW-mass hosts in our sample, used to identify the satellite candidates (§
III.5
). Unbound field galaxies (not shown here) fall beyond these escape velocity curves.

Satellite-host spatial (
d_{\rm proj}
) and velocity (
\Delta V_{\rm los}
) separations (similar to Figure
3
) for the satellite candidates of Milky Way-mass hosts (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}>12
; see Figure
7
). Colors of the data points represent the degree of satellite gas depletion (equation
6
; see §
IV.3
). Light-blue shaded regions mark the escape velocity curves (equation
4
; see §
III.5
) for the full range of host halo masses shown here (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}\in[12,12.44]
). Gray-scale shadings show contours of estimated ram pressure strengths (annotating
\log P_{\rm ram}
values in
\rm dyne/cm^{2}
) based on a Milky Way-like CGM model and simple de-projections (equation

; see §
VI
). Two low-mass “outlier” satellites (also see the right panel of Figure

) are marked in open stars. Names of the Milky Way’s and M31’s gaseous satellites are annotated.

The phase space with large distances and velocity separations (i.e., closer to the
V_{\rm esc}
contours) is more contaminated by interlopers. N-body simulations of satellites in galaxy groups and clusters have identified a contour,
V_{\rm los}/\sigma_{\rm 3D}=-\frac{4}{3}(d_{\rm proj}/R_{200})+2

(
Oman & Hudson 2016
)
, where
\sigma_{\rm 3D}
is the 3D velocity dispersion of satellites in a (cluster) halo, above which the interloper fraction quickly rises from
\sim 50\%
to
100\%
(also see
Rhee et al. 2017
;
Smith et al. 2019
). We expect a similar distribution in the phase-space interloper fraction in MW-like halos because of the orbital self-similarity in N-body simulations of varying halo masses
(
Wetzel 2011
)
.

The two low-mass (
M_{\star}\leq 10^{6.5}M_{\odot}
) satellite candidates in our sample, first identified in Figure
7
, are here marked with open stars in Figure
13
. They lie very close to the
V_{\rm esc}
boundary, and are very likely foreground/background interlopers rather than true satellites. Gas stripping is likely negligible for field galaxies. At
M_{\star}\approx 10^{6}M_{\odot}
, they also exceed the typical mass threshold for quenching by reionization (
M_{\star}\leq 10^{5}M_{\odot}
;
Brown et al. 2014
;
Weisz et al. 2014
) and are expected to remain gas-bearing.

Two other familiar cases of low-mass dwarf galaxies in massive halos are Leo T of the Milky Way and LGS 3 of M31, which fall in the phase space of large separation and low velocity. This combination produces a very low ram pressure (
P_{\rm ram}\lesssim 10^{-15}\rm~dyne/cm^{2}
; Figure
13
). If
P_{\rm ram}
in the orbital histories of Leo T and LGS 3 remained low and comparable to the
z=0
value as estimated here, stripping would indeed be highly incomplete.
Emerick et al. 2016
showed that for a simulated Leo T-mass galaxy, RPS is incomplete
12
12

12

Such low-mass satellites are often entirely quenched in cosmological simulations
(
Rodríguez-Cardoso et al. 2025
)
, which can be biased by excessive stripping due to lack of numerical resolution
(
Hopkins et al. 2018
)
. Here, we refer to idealized high-resolution simulations for the RPS effectiveness.
at
P_{\rm ram}\leq 6.7\times 10^{-14}\rm~dyne/cm^{2}
and becomes complete at
P_{\rm ram}\approx 2.7\times 10^{-13}\rm~dyne/cm^{2}
, which can naturally be achieved in a first infall orbit into a MW-like halo
(
Rocha et al. 2012
;
Zhu et al. 2024b
)
. The fact that Leo T and LGS 3 remain gas-rich at
z\approx 0
, while all other satellites of the MW/M31 at similar stellar masses are quenched, may result from an unusually low ram pressure throughout their orbits.

We consider the degree of satellite gas depletion across the phase space. The most depleted satellite candidates in our sample (dark red points; also see Figure
7
), including M31’s NGC 185, do not appear close to perigalacticon, where ram pressure peaks. One or two of the depleted galaxies with high negative velocities may be interlopers. The remaining ones, including NGC 185, are found at intermediate projected distances to the hosts (
d_{\rm proj}\approx 200
kpc; 3D distances are higher), consistent with “backsplash” galaxies that have interacted with the host halos and are moving outward
(
Borrow et al. 2023
)
. Previous work has found that a significant fraction of quenched satellites are backsplash galaxies at large distances
(
Simpson et al. 2018
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2024
;
Benavides et al. 2025
)
.

Few galaxies are detected in the first-infall phase space near the orbital pericenter (similar to the LMC, small
d_{\rm proj}
and large
\Delta V
). At perigalacticon, satellites have the highest velocities and are least likely to be detected. The data point next to LMC is NGC 4668, an interacting Magellanic analog of NGC 4666 (Figure
10
; also see
Walter et al. 2004
). Both NGC 4668 and the LMC show no sign of gas depletion from the
\Delta\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
values. At these high masses (
M_{\star}>10^{9}M_{\odot}
), gravitational restoring forces are much stronger, and RPS can only remove a small fraction of the satellite’s cold gas even at the orbital pericenter
(
Salem et al. 2015
)
.

Overall, we find no clear correlation between gas depletion and the satellite-host separations at
z\approx 0
(Figure
13
). This could indicate that some key orbital constraints are lost in the projected phase space. Future work can improve constraints on satellite stripping in several ways.

(i) Accurate distance measurements for dwarf galaxies, such as from TRGB
(
McQuinn et al. 2017
;
Cohen et al. 2018
;
Karachentsev et al. 2018b
)
or SBF
(
Carlsten et al. 2019
;
Greco et al. 2021
)
methods, will help distinguish true satellites from interloping field galaxies.

(ii) Improved statistics on the 3D velocity distributions of satellite halos from high-resolution N-body simulations
(
Wetzel 2011
;
Rocha et al. 2012
;
Nadler et al. 2023
;
Santos-Santos et al. 2025
)
are needed to constrain satellite dynamical histories, as proper motions are only observable in the Local Group
(
Fritz et al. 2018
;
Battaglia et al. 2022
)
.

(iii) Deep H
i
observations and better statistics for low-mass field galaxies (e.g.,
McQuinn et al. 2021
;
Jones et al. 2025
) will refine the baseline gas fractions and improve depletion estimates for satellites (equation
6
).

(iv) High-resolution RPS simulations that resolve multiphase gas in dwarf satellites
(
Salem et al. 2015
)
, model star formation and feedback
(
Emerick et al. 2016
;
Grønnow et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2024b
)
, and account for density substructures in the host CGM (
Simons et al. 2020
; Zhu et al.,
in prep
), will calibrate RPS theory at the lowest-mass end, and provide more accurate predictions for dwarf satellites.

VII
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we studied gas-bearing dwarf galaxies around late-type host galaxies (Milky Way-mass and lower; Table
1
), using H
i
data from WALLABY and MHONGOOSE. We combine these data with the ALFALFA data in
Zhu & Putman 2023
to form a sample of 56 host galaxies within 30 Mpc. We find 127 gaseous satellites and use deep optical imaging from Legacy DR10
(
Dey et al. 2019
)
for 110 of them to derive baryonic properties (
\log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot})=5.7-9.7,\log(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})=5.6-10.0
). We identify the dwarf galaxies to be either satellite galaxies within the host projected
R_{200}
, possible satellites beyond
R_{200}
, or unbound.

The proximity of the host galaxies and new survey data enable the detection of H
i
masses comparable to Local Group (LG) dwarf galaxies (Table
2
). We developed an injection test (Appendix
B
) in conjunction with the H
i
source finder SoFiA-2
(
Westmeier et al. 2021
)
to assess the H
i
sensitivity limits for each host (§
III.3
). The typical H
i
mass sensitivity at 10 Mpc is
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}=10^{6.7}M_{\odot}
for WALLABY and
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},lim}=10^{5.4}M_{\odot}
for MHONGOOSE (Figure
2
).

Our main results are summarized as follows:

•

The median
\pm 34\%
H
i
mass range of our dwarf galaxies is
\log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot})=7.56_{-0.52}^{+0.81}
for WALLABY and ALFALFA and
\log(M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot})=7.03_{-0.78}^{+1.19}
for MHONGOOSE, with a median line-width of
W_{50}=37_{-15}^{+40}~\rm km~s^{-1}
(Figure
4
).

•

The satellite stellar and H
i
mass distributions span a similar range, correlation, and scatter as the Local Group population (Figure
5
). Our sample shows lower average gas fractions (
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\star}
) than field samples outside the Local Group (Figure
6
). This result also indicates that H
i
sensitivity of the data is key to consider when comparing gas fractions across samples.

•

Satellites are more gas-depleted around more massive, Milky Way-like host galaxies (Figure
7
). In particular, we identify a small group of highly depleted massive satellites (
M_{\star}\geq 10^{7.6}M_{\odot}
) around the Milky Way-mass hosts.

•

The abundance of H
i
-bearing satellites is low overall and increases with host mass: dwarf hosts (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}\leq 11
) have
0-2
within their projected halos, while Milky Way-mass spirals (
\log M_{200}/M_{\odot}\geq 12
) have
0-5
, averaging at
N_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}~sat}\approx 3
(Figure
9
). Hosts with massive companions tend to host more gaseous satellites (Figure
10
).

•

For Milky Way-mass spirals, the number of H
i
-bearing satellites agrees with the Milky Way and M31 (§
IV.4
) as well as the star-forming satellite abundance from deep optical surveys (Figure
12
). The inferred satellite quenched fraction for classical dwarf masses (
M_{\star}\geq 10^{5.75-6.75}~M_{\odot}
) is
f_{q}=40-70\%
(§
V
).

•

We estimated ram pressure strengths in Milky Way-mass host halos across satellite phase space (Figure
13
), which helps identify potential cases of first infall, backsplash, and low-mass interloper dwarf galaxies (§
VI
).

The low abundance of H
i
-bearing satellites is a key result of this work, and it shows that effective environmental quenching around spiral galaxies is not unique to the Milky Way and M31. Future deep H
i
observations of field galaxies, such as with MeerKAT or more local WALLABY observations, will help calibrate gas fractions in low-density environments and refine gas-depletion measurements (
\Delta\log f_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
; §
IV.3
). Improved line-width corrections and kinematic modeling of a resolved subsample will enable studies of Tully-Fisher scaling relations
(
Tully & Fisher 1977
;
McGaugh et al. 2000
;
Lelli et al. 2016b
)
. Finally, high-resolution simulations can further quantify the roles of different gas loss mechanisms in low-mass satellite galaxies (§
VI
).

Appendix B
Injection Test for Sensitivity Assessment

In this section, we describe our H
i
flux injection pipeline for the sensitivity of a data cube. The pipeline includes two main steps, (i)
Modeling
(§
B.1
): we model the three-dimensional H
i
emission of gaseous dwarf galaxies across a range of masses; (ii)
Injection and source recovery
(§
B.2
): we inject, that is, add the fluxes of the modeled dwarfs into the host H
i
cubes, and rerun source finding using SoFiA-2 on the injected cubes (§
III.2
). The recovery rate, defined as the ratio of detected sources to total injected sources as a function of
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
, is then used to quantify the sensitivity limit of the host field, as presented in Section
III.3
.

B.1
Modeling H
i
in dwarf galaxies: from radial profiles to three-dimensional cubes

We model a dwarf galaxy’s H
i
distribution following the observed column density profiles of nearby dwarf galaxies. From the literature, we select low-mass gaseous dwarfs that have spatially resolved radial profiles, including targeted deep imaging of Leo T
(
Adams & Oosterloo 2018
)
, as well as low-mass dwarfs with
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}\leq 10^{7}M_{\odot}
from the LITTLE THINGS survey
(
Hunter et al. 2012
;
Hunter et al. 2021
)
. Based on these observed galaxies, we calibrate a simple model that outputs the H
i
column density profile,
\Sigma_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}(R)
, given an input
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
.

We adopt the simple Sérsic-profile parametrization for the H
i
column density profile of a galaxy, following
Hunter et al. 2021
,

\log\Sigma_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}(R)=\log\Sigma_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}^{0}-0.434(R/R_{0,\rm H\,\textsc{i}})^{1/n_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}}

(B1)

where
\Sigma_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}^{0}
is the central density,
R_{0,\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
is the scale radius, and
n_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
the Sérsic index. We derive an average radial profile that scales only with the galaxy’s
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
, and model the density profiles for a uniform grid of
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
to bracket the transition between recovery rates 0 to 100%. Based on partial correlation tests, the Sérsic index is least correlated with
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
, and the scale radius is most correlated with
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
. We therefore adopt the simplifications that the Sérsic index is a constant (
n_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}=0.743
), and the scale radius is a linear function of
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
(
R_{0,\rm H\,\textsc{i}}=0.278\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}-1.292
), where the values fit from the observed profiles from literature. We solve for the central density
\Sigma_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}^{0}
given a target
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
following equation
B1
. Panel (1) of Figure
14
summarizes the column density profiles of the observed galaxies and our modeled galaxies.

We use the
SimulatedGalaxyCube
method in the
pyBBarolo
software
(
Di Teodoro & Fraternali 2015
)
to model three-dimensional (3D) cubes of low-mass dwarf galaxies from the column density profiles (Figure
14
, panel 1).
pyBBarolo
takes the input of gas column densities as a function of angular radius to create 3D cubes. For a model galaxy at a given mass, the column density profile is independent of distance, but the angular size does scale with the distance
D
to the observer,
R\propto 1/D
. Low-mass sources at larger distances inevitably become unresolved (i.e., on-sky sizes comparable to or smaller than the beam size). We need to correct for the beam dilution effect, where the observed intensity becomes lower than the underlying physical intensity for unresolved sources. To do so, we calculate the theoretical integrated flux of a galaxy (see, e.g.,
Meyer et al. 2017
for a review),
S_{V,\rm theory}=M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/(2.356\times 10^{5}\cdot D^{2})
, and multiply the intensities in all pixels of the 3D model cube by
S_{V,\rm theory}/S_{V,\rm model}
. This step ensures that both the analytical radial profile (equation
B1
) and the correct total fluxes are preserved at any distance
D
, regardless of whether the source is resolved.

The column density profile is the most important input to
pyBBarolo
, as it determines the model galaxy’s H
i
mass. A set of additional input parameters can be specified. We use the WALLABY and MHONGOOSE survey-specific information (pixel scale, velocity channel size, and beam size) to set the grid sizes for these 3D model cubes. We choose to keep most other variables constant across masses for simplicity, setting them to reasonable values for low-mass dwarfs: the maximum rotation velocity
V_{\rm max}=17.67
km/s, the dispersion velocity
V_{\rm disp}=5
km/s, and the inclination angle
i=45\deg
. Collectively, the inclination angle and maximum rotation velocity set the velocity width via
W_{50}=2\sin(i)\cdot V_{\rm max}=25
km/s, which is approximately the average
W_{50}
for Local Group gaseous dwarfs
(
Putman et al. 2021
)
.

B.2
Injection and source recovery

To determine how sensitive a host galaxy cube is to a typical dwarf galaxy of a certain
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},test}
, we inject (i.e., add the fluxes of) many copies of the
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},test}
model cube (§
B.1
) to a uniform 3D grid within the host cube. The model dwarf is scaled to the distance of the host (Table
1
). The grid spacing is selected such that injected models are separable by the source finder, while the total injected number (
N_{\rm inject,tot}
) is sufficient for a robust recovery rate calculation. Given the sizes of our WALLABY and MHONGOOSE host cubes (§
III.2
),
N_{\rm inject,tot}
is typically a few hundred. Then, we rerun source finding using SoFiA-2 as described in §
III.2
and report the recovered number of sources,
N_{\rm recover}
, at this mass. Because of the large number of injected sources,
N_{\rm inject,tot}
is typically much greater than the original number of real detections or false positives (§
III.2
), most of the SoFiA-2 detections in this step are indeed injected model dwarfs. The recovery rate is thus given by
N_{\rm recover}/N_{\rm inject,tot}
. We repeat this step iteratively for a grid of
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},test}
until the entire mass range is scanned, where the recovery rate varies from
0\%
to
100\%
.

The empirical
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
thresholds for the host galaxies are a key result of this injection test, and are summarized in Section
III.3
. Here in Figure
14
, we show the example recovery rates as a function of injected
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i},test}
, panel (2a) for a WALLABY host galaxy (NGC 6221), and panel (2b) for a MHONGOOSE host galaxy (ESO300-G014). Each galaxy is selected to be at the median distance of its survey’s subsample (Table
1
), so the result is representative. We calculate the
M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}
thresholds at
25,50,90\%
recovery rates by linearly interpolating the recovery rate results, as annotated by the circles in Figure
14
. The lowest mass model in our injection mass grid is set to be
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}=5.4
based on the smallest observed gas-rich dwarf galaxies (LGS 3, Leo T-like mass; see, e.g.,
Hunter et al. 2012
;
Adams & Oosterloo 2018
). For some MHONGOOSE galaxies within 10 Mpc, the recovery rate is
>50\%
at the lowest model mass, indicating that
\log M_{\rm H\,\textsc{i}}/M_{\odot}=5.4
is an upper limit (Table
1
) and the true sensitivities are deeper.
