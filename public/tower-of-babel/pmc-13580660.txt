Clinical and Multimodal Imaging Features of Familial Exudative Vitreoretinopathy: The Genetic and Phenotypic Spectrum
Authors: Tülin Öğreden, Ilkay Kilic Muftuoglu, Gürkan Erdoğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the genetic, clinical, and multimodal imaging findings in a cohort of patients with familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR).MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included patients diagnosed with FEVR at a tertiary referral center. Clinical findings, fluorescein angiography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), OCT angiography (OCTA), treatment records, and whole-exome sequencing results were reviewed.ResultsA total of 28 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 22 (78.6%) were probands and 6 (21.4%) were family members.Fifteen patients (53.6%) were male and 13 (46.4%) female. Mean age was 11.75±9.63 (2–47) years, with a mean follow-up of 21.57±14.24 (2–50) months. Visual acuity ranged from light perception to 20/20, with a median BCVA of 20/32. Among 56 eyes, mild disease (stage 1–2) was observed in 67.9%, compared with 32.1% for advanced disease (stage 3–5). Genetic variants were identified in 17 patients (60.7%), whereas no disease-associated variant was detected in 11 (39.3%). The most commonly affected genes were FZD4 (21.4%), LRP5 (17.9%), KIF11 (14.3%), TSPAN12 (3.6%), and NDP (3.6%). Clinical manifestations ranged from asymptomatic peripheral vascular anomalies to advanced retinal detachment and severe vision loss. One LRP5-mutant patient exhibited PFV, stage 5 disease, and glaucoma. The four KIF11-mutant cases demonstrated marked phenotypic variability. A FEVR phenotype was also identified in a case with an EPHA2 mutation. OCT, OCT-A, and fluorescein angiography were available in 29 (51.8%), 10 (17.9%), and 27 eyes (48.2%), respectively. Combined imaging revealed posterior pole, vitreoretinal interface, and retinal microvascular abnormalities, including foveal and vitreomacular alterations, that were not fully reflected by stage classification alone. Laser photocoagulation was performed in 26.8% of eyes, combination therapy in 14.2%, and anti-VEGF monotherapy in 1.8%.ConclusionFEVR demonstrated marked genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity, while multimodal imaging provided complementary information beyond conventional stage classification.

Introduction

Familial Exudative Vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) is an inherited developmental vitreoretinopathy, and its nomenclature reflects several key clinical and pathophysiological characteristics of the disease. The term “familial” indicates its strong genetic basis, “exudative” refers to the consequences of abnormal retinal vascular development, and “vitreoretinopathy” emphasizes that the disease extends beyond a simple vasculopathy to involve the vitreoretinal interface and retinal tissue. Since its first description in 1969, FEVR has been increasingly recognized as a clinically and genetically heterogeneous disorder.1

Over time, the conceptual framework of FEVR has evolved substantially, driven by two pivotal advances. The first was the development of advanced imaging modalities, which enabled a more comprehensive characterization of the disease and subsequently influenced the refinement of staging systems. Early classification systems, such as that proposed by Pendergast and Trese, were based primarily on ophthalmoscopic findings and focused on structural disease manifestations.2,3 The system revised by Kashani et al offers a more dynamic, pathophysiology-focused perspective, including fluorescein angiography findings such as leakage and exudation4 More recently, OCT- and OCTA-derived biomarkers have further expanded disease characterization at the microanatomical and microvascular levels and may contribute to future refinements in FEVR classification. These systems are better viewed as classifications than grading scales, given the non-linear and highly variable clinical course of FEVR.

The second pivotal advance occurred in the field of genetics. Initially, FEVR was defined as an autosomal dominant disorder associated with mutations on chromosome 11q, and by 2014, five associated genes on different chromosomes and different inheritance pathways had been identified.3,5,6 With some genes recently reported and requiring further validation, this number has almost tripled in the last decade. Advances in genetics have shown that mutations in multiple genes, particularly those involved in Wnt signaling, converge on common pathogenic mechanisms in FEVR. As a result, FEVR is now increasingly viewed as a pathway-driven disorder rather than a purely clinical entity.

Accordingly, the present study aims to characterize the genetic and clinical spectrum and multimodal imaging findings of FEVR, and to explore genotype-associated phenotypic differences.

Materials and Methods

This single-center, observational retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital, including patients followed between July 2022 and March 2026. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital (Approval No:35 Date: 11.02.2026). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before enrollment.

The diagnosis of FEVR was based on established clinical and angiographic criteria. Patients were included if they demonstrated peripheral retinal avascularity in at least one eye, were born at term or, if preterm, had a disease course inconsistent with retinopathy of prematurity, and exhibited vitreoretinal traction, subretinal exudation, or retinal neovascularization at any age. Genetic confirmation was not required for FEVR, as a causative variant cannot be identified in all clinically affected patients.

The five-stage classification proposed in 2014 was used for disease staging.4 For genotype–phenotype analysis, disease severity was dichotomized as mild (stage 1–2) or severe (stage ≥3), and genotype-specific associations were evaluated using a one-vs-rest approach.

Data were obtained from routine clinical evaluations and medical records, including age, sex, family history, and ophthalmic findings such as visual acuity, FEVR stage, laterality, treatment status, and complications (tractional retinal detachment, macular dragging, and the need for vitreoretinal surgery). Imaging data, including fundus imaging (CX-1, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), fluorescein angiography(CF-1, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Canon OCT-A1, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), and OCT-angiography (OCTA) (BMizar, TowardPi, China) images were also analyzed. Genetic data obtained by whole-exome sequencing (WES), based on targeted exonic capture and next-generation sequencing, were retrospectively reviewed.

Inclusion criteria were patients of any age with a clinical diagnosis of FEVR. Cases were classified as probands (index cases) or affected family members.

Exclusion criteria included uncertain diagnosis, the presence of retinal disorders that may mimic FEVR (such as retinopathy of prematurity, Coats disease, or Norrie disease), and incomplete clinical data.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 17.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using both graphical and statistical methods. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as number (n) and percentage (%).

Genotype-specific associations with disease severity were evaluated using a one-vs-rest approach. Disease stage was dichotomized as mild (stage 1–2) or severe (stage ≥3). Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons because of the limited number of patients within individual genetic subgroups. Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given the rarity of FEVR, subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and intended to identify potential genotype–phenotype trends rather than establish definitive associations.

Results

A total of 56 eyes from 28 patients were included in the study. Twenty-two patients (78.6%) were probands, and 6 (21.4%) were family members.

Fifteen patients (53.6%) were male and 13 (46.4%) were female. The mean age was 11.75 ± 9.63 years (range, 2–47), and the mean follow-up duration was 21.57 ± 14.24 months (range, 2–50). Visual acuity ranged from light perception to 20/20, with a median BCVA of 20/32.

Disease-associated genetic variants were identified in 17 of 28 patients (60.7%), whereas no causative variant could be detected in the remaining 11 patients (39.3%). Among genetically solved cases, FZD4 was the most frequently affected gene, accounting for 21.4% of the cohort. This was followed by LRP5 mutations in 17.9% and KIF11 mutations in 14.3% of patients. Variants in TSPAN12 and NDP were each identified in 3.6% of cases. Asymmetry was observed in a substantial proportion of cases, being present in 22 patients (78.6%). The right eye showed a more advanced stage in 13 patients (46.4%), the left eye in 9 (32.1%), while both eyes were at a similar stage in 6 (21.4%).

Stage 1 was observed in 24 eyes (42.9%), followed by stage 2A in 10 (17.9%), stage 2B in 4 (7.1%), stage 3A in 9 (16.1%), stage 3B in 4 (7.1%), stage 4A in 1 (1.8%), stage 4B in 1 (1.8%), and stage 5 in 3 (5.4%). Cataract was present in 7 of 56 eyes (12.5%).

Clinically apparent strabismus was present in 13 patients (46.4%), including manifest exotropia in 8 (28.6%), esotropia in 1 (3.6%), and exotropia with a vertical deviation in 4 (14.3%). When microtropia was also included, the prevalence of ocular misalignment increased to 67.9% (19/28).

Observation was the most common management approach, applied in 32 eyes (57.1%), followed by laser photocoagulation in 15 eyes (26.8%). Combined laser photocoagulation and surgery was performed in 4 eyes (7.1%), anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy alone in 1 eye (1.8%), and combined anti-VEGF and laser treatment in 4 eyes (7.1%). Although vitreoretinal surgery was required in selected eyes, no patient underwent strabismus surgery during the study period. Including genetically unsolved cases, severe disease (stage ≥3) was observed in 7 of 10 eyes (70.0%) with LRP5 mutations and in all eyes (100%) with NDP mutations. Severe involvement was identified in 1 of 2 eyes (50.0%) with TSPAN12 mutations, 2 of 8 eyes (25.0%) with KIF11 mutations, and 2 of 12 eyes (16.7%) with FZD4 mutations. Among genetically unsolved cases, severe disease was present in 4 of 22 eyes (18.2%).

Exploratory one-vs-rest analysis suggested an association between LRP5-associated disease and severe retinal involvement (OR: 7.42, p=0.008), although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small subgroup sizes. No significant association was identified for the remaining genotypes, likely due to the limited sample size and sparse subgroup distribution.

Peripheral avascular retina was the most common fundus finding, observed in 31 eyes (55.4%), followed by optic disc or vascular dragging in 14 eyes (25.0%) and macular dragging/ectopia in 12 eyes (21.4%). Advanced structural complications, including radial retinal folds, subretinal exudation, and tractional retinal detachment, were also identified in a subset of eyes. Fluorescein angiography predominantly demonstrated peripheral nonperfusion in 24 eyes (88.9%), while fluorescein leakage, anomalous branching, bulbous vascular endings, and neovascularization were also frequently observed, highlighting persistent vascular activity and abnormal retinal vascular remodeling.

OCT and OCT-A analyses further demonstrated significant microstructural and microvascular alterations, including foveal hypoplasia in 11 eyes (37.9%), vitreoretinal adhesions in 9 eyes (31.0%), and outer retinal atrophy in 5 eyes (17.2%). Among eyes evaluated with OCT-A, reduced vessel density and abnormal foveal avascular zone morphology were prominent findings, observed in 70.0% and 60.0% of cases, respectively. Case 20 was a 3-month-old male infant. The patient was born at term and had no known family history. Ocular examination revealed megalocornea, shallow anterior chambers, and retrolental white fibrovascular tissue. Orbital MRI demonstrated a linear stalk extending from the optic nerve head to the posterior lens surface, accompanied by posteriorly layered fluid accumulation. Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous LRP5 mutation. Given the severe phenotype, Osteoporosis-Pseudoglioma Syndrome was investigated, and bone mineral density was found to be moderately reduced. Combined lensectomy and vitrectomy were performed for glaucoma management; however, separation of the retinal tissue from the posterior lens surface and dissection of the fibrovascular tissue were not feasible intraoperatively. Discussion

FEVR is a hereditary vitreoretinopathy with genetic heterogeneity, variable expressivity, and incomplete penetrance.6 Numerous studies, ranging from case series to large cohort analyses, have consistently highlighted this prominent feature of FEVR.1,3,5–13

Approximately one-fifth of the patients in our cohort were affected family members identified through family screening. Because FEVR may remain asymptomatic or present with only subtle retinal findings, examination of at-risk relatives is widely recommended and may facilitate the detection of otherwise unrecognized disease.3–5,14

In FEVR, there is generally no marked sex predilection, although males may be more frequently affected in X-linked NDP-associated disease. Consistent with this, our cohort showed a slight male predominance, which was most pronounced among patients with NDP variants. This finding is likely related to the X-linked inheritance pattern of NDP mutations, whereas FZD4, LRP5, and TSPAN12 variants are typically inherited in autosomal dominant or recessive patterns.3

Visual outcomes in Familial Exudative Vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) are highly variable and largely depend on disease stage and the presence of retinal detachment. Previous studies have consistently shown that most patients with early-stage disease maintain functional vision over long-term follow-up, whereas advanced stages, particularly those complicated by tractional retinal detachment, are associated with significantly poorer visual outcomes. Longitudinal cohort studies have reported that more than 75% of patients retain a visual acuity of 20/40 or better, while severe visual loss occurs predominantly in eyes with advanced vitreoretinal pathology. Despite these challenges, vitreoretinal surgery may provide meaningful visual improvement in selected advanced cases.2,15,16 Similarly, visual outcomes in our series were heterogeneous, with BCVA ranging from light perception to 20/20 and a median value of 20/32. The broad spectrum of visual acuity in our cohort likely reflects the complex nature of FEVR. Disease stage, retinal structural changes, and genetic background may all contribute to visual outcomes.

Staging remains the most widely used method for assessing disease severity in FEVR and was therefore used as the primary severity metric in our study. However, FEVR does not always follow a progressive course, and staging functions, to some extent, as a classification system. Current classification systems are primarily based on peripheral avascularity, neovascularization, and retinal detachment, making them clinically practical and treatment-oriented. However, several clinically relevant features—including macular dragging, retinal folds, foveal hypoplasia, vitreoretinal interface abnormalities, cataract, and anterior segment changes—are not adequately represented. Given the complexity of the disease, we also documented all clinically relevant ocular findings separately.

Previous studies have also demonstrated considerable variability in stage distribution. Chokchaitanasin et al reported stage 1 disease as the most common presentation, accounting for 38.0% of eyes, whereas stage 5 disease was observed in 16.3% of cases.16 Similarly, Lähteenoja et al found that stage 1 was the most frequent stage, representing 40% of examined eyes.12 In our cohort, stage 1 disease was the most common presentation, accounting for 42.9% of eyes, while advanced disease (stages 4–5) was relatively uncommon, comprising only 9% of cases. Cataract was observed in 12.5% of eyes.

Advances in imaging modalities have expanded the concept of FEVR beyond a purely peripheral vascular disorder by revealing microstructural, vitreoretinal, and previously unrecognized abnormalities. Studies by Brijesh et al and Hasegawa et al systematically described a range of OCT and OCTA abnormalities in FEVR, highlighting the value of these techniques in detecting disease features that may not be apparent on conventional examination.17,18 Redden et al reported the presence of hyperreflective preretinal granules in FEVR.19 In our cohort, fluorescein angiography demonstrated not only peripheral nonperfusion but also persistent vascular activity and remodeling, including leakage, neovascularization, anomalous branching, bulbous vascular endings, and vascular shunting. In a subset of eyes, delayed choroidal perfusion was also observed, suggesting that vascular alterations in FEVR may extend beyond the retinal circulation alone.

OCT and OCTA further revealed substantial involvement of the posterior pole. Foveal hypoplasia was the most common OCT finding, followed by vitreoretinal adhesions, while OCTA frequently demonstrated abnormalities or loss of the foveal avascular zone, reduced vessel density, and disorganization of the superficial retinal vascular network. Additional posterior pole abnormalities included retinal and vascular dragging, retinal folds, and narrowing of the retinal vascular arcades. En face OCTA also enabled detection of subtle round-to-oval irregular retinochoroidal degenerative changes within vascularized retinal regions that were not readily appreciable on conventional fundus examination. These observations suggest that FEVR extends beyond the peripheral retina and is associated with microvascular and vitreoretinal abnormalities.

The identification of new disease-associated genes has established Wnt signaling as a central pathway in FEVR pathogenesis. This pathway links multiple gene products involved in retinal vascular development. This signaling cascade comprises both canonical (β-catenin–dependent) and non-canonical (β-catenin–independent) pathways that regulate vascular growth, cellular differentiation, and tissue morphogenesis.20–22

The best-characterized disease-associated genes include NDP, FZD4, LRP5, and TSPAN12, which constitute the core components of the canonical Norrin/Wnt–β-catenin signaling pathway.21,22 Additional genes, such as CTNNB1 and WNT2B, are also linked to Wnt signaling and further support its central role in retinal vascular development.21 Other genes, including KIF11, ZNF408, RCBTB1, JAG1, ATOH7, and DOCK6, have been implicated through alternative mechanisms, highlighting the broader genetic architecture of FEVR.14,23,24

In our cohort, the most frequently identified genes were FZD4, LRP5, KIF11, TSPAN12, and NDP. Given the limited size of individual genetic subgroups, genotype-based analyses were considered exploratory and intended to identify potential phenotype patterns rather than establish definitive genotype–phenotype associations. Although no clear genotype–severity association could be established in our cohort, certain trends were observed. NDP- and LRP5-associated cases tended to exhibit more severe disease, whereas FZD4 variants were more frequently associated with milder phenotypes.

Although certain genotype-specific trends were observed, the KIF11-associated family demonstrated that the same pathogenic variant may result in markedly different clinical manifestations, even among closely related individuals. Furthermore, severe disease was also encountered in patients without an identifiable pathogenic variant. These findings highlight the complexity of genotype–phenotype relationships in FEVR and suggest that genotype alone may not reliably predict disease severity.

The patient carrying an EPHA2 variant presented with a clinical phenotype consistent with severe FEVR despite the absence of pathogenic variants in currently established FEVR-associated genes. Although a causal relationship cannot be inferred from a single case and the detected variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, the finding is noteworthy. EPHA2 variants have previously been associated with congenital and developmental lens abnormalities, and experimental studies have suggested potential interactions between EPHA2 signaling and the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway involved in retinal vascular development25 Whether this finding is incidental or reflects a possible role for EPHA2-related pathways in FEVR-like phenotypes remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Our LRP5-associated case illustrated the overlap between FEVR and persistent fetal vasculature (PFV). Previous reports have shown that severe FEVR may present with retrolental fibrovascular tissue, microphthalmia, and other PFV-like features, creating diagnostic challenges between these entities.26 The presence of a stalk extending from the optic nerve head to the posterior lens surface together with retrolental fibrovascular proliferation was consistent with this overlapping phenotype. In addition, angle-closure glaucoma and anterior segment abnormalities suggested developmental involvement beyond the retinal vasculature. Similar ocular and systemic manifestations have been reported in patients with LRP5 mutations.27 In particular, Osteoporosis-Pseudoglioma Syndrome should be considered in severe cases.28 In our patient, bone mineral density was found to be moderately reduced. Abnormalities in Wnt signaling have been implicated in numerous human diseases. These include; developmental disorders such as tooth agenesis; endocrine and metabolic conditions including hyperparathyroid tumors and type 2 diabetes; and several cancer predisposition syndromes, including familial adenomatous polyposis and Wilms tumor.22 This highlights the importance of systemic evaluation, particularly in pediatric patients.

Treatment in FEVR is highly individualized and depends on disease stage, vascular activity, and the presence of tractional complications. While early-stage disease may remain stable under observation or peripheral laser ablation, advanced stages frequently require combined medical and surgical approaches to preserve retinal anatomy and visual function.

More than half of the eyes in our cohort were managed conservatively, reflecting the predominance of early-stage disease in the study population. Laser photocoagulation represented the most frequently applied intervention and was primarily used in eyes with peripheral avascularity and active vascular changes. The FZD4-mutant case highlighted an unusual exudative activation pattern following laser treatment, underscoring the dynamic nature of FEVR. Combination treatments, including laser with surgery or anti-VEGF therapy, were generally required in more advanced stages characterized by tractional or exudative complications. These findings support the stage-dependent and highly individualized management approach currently adopted in FEVR.

Reported rates of strabismus in FEVR vary considerably across studies.3,16 Strabismus was a frequent accompanying finding in our cohort. Exotropia constituted the predominant pattern, either as isolated manifest exotropia or in combination with a vertical deviation, whereas esotropia was uncommon. The high prevalence of strabismus likely reflects the asymmetric and macula-involving nature of FEVR, particularly in eyes with macular dragging, retinal folds, anisometropia, or severe unilateral visual impairment. Microtropia was also relatively common, suggesting that subtle sensory misalignment may accompany even clinically milder disease. Retinal detachment remains one of the leading causes of visual loss in FEVR and frequently necessitates surgical intervention to preserve anatomical integrity and visual function. However, surgical outcomes and complication rates are variable.

This study is limited by its retrospective, cross-sectional design, relatively small cohort, referral-center bias, incomplete imaging data, small genotype-specific subgroups, and the possibility of unidentified disease-associated genes. Despite these limitations, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of the genetic and clinical spectrum of FEVR.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the value of integrating multimodal imaging with genetic analysis in the evaluation of FEVR. The wide range of clinical findings observed, including marked intrafamilial differences and atypical presentations, highlights the complexity of the disease. Despite substantial advances in molecular genetics, important questions remain regarding the mechanisms underlying interocular asymmetry and the incomplete correlation between genotype and clinical severity. Further studies involving larger cohorts and comprehensive genetic analyses are needed to better understand these relationships.
