Biomarkers of brain injury in foals with neonatal maladjustment syndrome
Authors: Javier Perez Quesada, Kinnidy Coley, David Wong, Nimet Browne, Myriah Albrecht, Katarzyna Dembek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlasma concentrations of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glial-fibrillary-acidic protein (GFAP), and astrocytic protein S100B, might be linked to the pathophysiology of Neonatal Maladjustment Syndrome (NMS).Hypothesis/Objectives To measure serum concentration of BDNF and S100B, plasma concentration of GFAP, and neurosteroids (progesterone and 17α-hydroxyprogesterone) in foals with NMS, foals presented for other diseases, including premature, and healthy foals, and determine their association with outcome.AnimalsFourteen healthy foals, 19 NMS foals, 39 foals hospitalized for other diseases < 7 days of age. MethodsIn this prospective, longitudinal study, concentrations of biomarkers were determined in all foals on admission, at day 1 and 2 of hospitalization. Plasma or serum concentration of biomarkers was measured with ELISA and single-molecule-array technology.ResultsForty-eight foals survived, 10 did not survive. Serum BDNF concentration was lower after 24 h of hospitalization in NMS foals (baseline: 3.0, 1.9-4.1 g/mL; day 1: 2.0, 1.2-2.8 pg/mL) (P = .02). Serum S100B was higher in septic foals with NMS (marginal means, 95% CI, 435.3, 290-580 ng/mL) compared to septic foals without NMS (238.9, 164.6-313.2 ng/mL) on admission (P = .01). Pregnane concentrations were higher in NMS foals compared to healthy and sick foals over the first 48 h of hospitalization (P < .05).Conclusions and clinical importanceHigher BDNF, S100B, and pregnane concentrations were consistent with NMS, and might allow for neurological assessment.

Introduction

Neonatal Maladjustment Syndrome (NMS) remains a relevant clinical challenge due to the poorly understood mechanisms underlying its characteristic neurological abnormalities, which include impaired suckling, ataxia, and seizures. 1 The incidence of NMS is 1%-2% and can result in important morbidity.2 Effective strategies for improving outcomes heavily rely on early detection and immediate intervention, underscoring the need for a better understanding of the mechanisms of neurological dysfunction and pathogenesis of this disease. Recent research highlights the potential role of neurosteroids, particularly pregnanes, in the pathophysiology of NMS. Neurosteroids are critical for brain function and development, influencing neural processes through their regulatory effects. Plasma pregnane concentrations are higher in foals with NMS, suggesting their contribution to the observed neurological manifestations.3–5 These neurosteroids modulate neurotransmitter receptors, including gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptors (inhibitory neurotransmission) and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (excitatory neurotransmission).6 Through their actions, they influence behavior, neuronal excitability, and plasticity, influencing neurological development and function. However, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these alterations remain unknown.

Current research has also focused on identifying other biomarkers of neurological damage in neonatal foals with suspected NMS. Among these, phosphorylated neurofilament heavy chain and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 have been investigated.7 The utility of these neuroproteins in diagnosing or monitoring the progression of NMS remains limited, and their value as prognostic markers is minimal, highlighting the need for more reliable and clinically informative biomarkers.7,8

In human neonates and other species, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) has emerged as an important biomarker in neurological disorders due to its involvement in neuronal survival, growth, and plasticity. Serum BDNF concentrations reflect neuronal injury and repair processes, and reduced levels have been associated with various neurological disorders in other species.9–12 Similarly, the astrocytic proteins glial-fibrillary-acidic protein (GFAP) and S100B are promising as biomarkers of neurological damage in human neonates.13–16 GFAP serves as a marker of astrocyte activation in response to brain injury and inflammation.13,14 S100B is studied for its role in assessing brain injury in human neonates.15,16 Serum S100B concentrations are elevated in calves with neurological disorders, particularly in those diagnosed with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy.17,18

The primary goal of this study is to gain insight into the pathophysiology and mechanisms of NMS by analyzing blood concentrations of neurosteroids (progesterone and 17α-hydroxyprogesterone) and markers of cellular damage (GFAP, S100B, and BDNF). By investigating these biomarkers in both healthy foals and foals hospitalized for other neonatal conditions, the study aims to determine how these biomarkers contribute to the severity and clinical presentation of NMS, as well as other diseases, including prematurity and sepsis. We propose that the neurological dysfunction observed in NMS foals might be influenced by potentially altered concentrations of neurosteroids and neuroproteins.

Materials and methods

Animals

This prospective clinical study was carried out during 2 foaling seasons (January 2023-July 2024) at 4 institutions in the United States. Neonatal foals (n = 72) ≤ 7 days old of any breed and sex were eligible for the study, and they were included after owner consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria were steroid administration to the mare during parturition or the foal before hospital admission, due to potential alterations in circulating neurosteroids, and euthanasia for nonmedical reasons. Hospitalized foals were classified into 2 major groups: NMS and foals presented for evaluation of other diseases. The healthy control group consisted of full-term foals (≥330 days of gestation) that were clinically healthy based on physical examination, a normal complete blood count (CBC), a biochemistry profile, and IgG concentrations (>800 mg/dL).19 The NMS foals were classified based on clinical signs consistent with NMS and using an established neurobehavioral scoring system (NMS score)3 to better categorize signs. The total score ranges from 0 to 20 and evaluates key variables including mentation and reaction to stimuli, ability to stand and bond with the mare, ability to suckle, paroxysms, and ear position.4 To ensure specificity in identifying NMS foals, only those with a score of 8 or higher were considered as having NMS in this study. Foals were also classified into the other disease group, non-NMS based on clinical diagnosis.

Additionally, hospitalized foals were classified as septic based on the sepsis scoring system.19 Septic foals had a sepsis score of ≥12, a positive blood culture or both. Finally, hospitalized foals were also categorized based on gestation length into premature foals (<330 days of gestation) and full-term foals. Premature foals were assessed for clinical signs of prematurity including tendon laxity, floppy ears, short silky hair coat, domed forehead, and incomplete ossification of carpal and tarsal bones.20 Foals discharged from the hospital were considered survivors. Foals that died or were subjected to euthanasia due to a grave medical prognosis were defined as non-survivors.

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to the principles for the humane treatment of animals in veterinary clinical investigations as stated by the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine and National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Clinical data

History obtained from medical records included medications (mare and foal), duration of pregnancy, maternal illness, premature lactation, assisted parturition, dystocia, passing and appearance of fetal membranes, and parity. Foal clinical information included signalment, physical examination, CBC, serum biochemistry, and blood IgG concentration. For consistency, the sepsis and NMS scores were calculated by the same author for each foal, based on clinical history, physical examination, and laboratory findings.

Study design

Blood samples from hospitalized foals for laboratory tests and hormone and biomarker measurements were collected on admission (median age 24 h, IQR 12-48 h) before any medication was given. Subsequent blood samples were collected on days 1 and 2 of hospitalization. Blood from healthy foals was collected during their routine examination at a median age of 17 h (IQR 12-24 h) (Time 0 h), and 24 and 48 h later. Blood samples were placed in plain tubes containing no anticoagulant and chilled aprotinin-EDTA tubes. Aprotinin (500 kU/mL of blood) was added to preserve protein integrity. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum and plasma were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Blood samples for CBC and serum biochemistry were analyzed with Advia 120 Hematology Analyzer, Siemens Healthineers, Malver, Pennsylvania and Cobas C 501, Roche Diagnostics Corporation, Indianapolis, Indiana, respectively. IgG concentrations were measured with immunoturbidimetric assay, Rapid DVM Test II, Value Diagnostics, MAI Animal Health, Melksham, Wiltshire, UK.

Hormone concentrations

Serum S100B, serum BDNF, and plasma GFAP concentrations were determined using human-specific immunoassays according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Human S100B ELISA assay (Millipore, catalog # EZHS100B) was used to determine the concentration of S100B. The assay had a working range of 2.7-2000 pg/mL, a recovery of 81% (SD 5.1%), and linearity at dilutions up to 1:16 for equine samples. The mean inter- and intra-assay coefficient of variation was 5.7% (SD 3.1%) and 4% (SD 3.5%), in foal samples, respectively.

Plasma GFAP concentrations were measured with an ELISA kit (R&D Systems, catalog number DY008), with a working range of 0.313-20 ng/mL. The assay was linear at dilutions up to 1:16 with a recovery of 84.3% (SD 3.2%). For equine samples, the mean inter- and intra-assay coefficient of variation was 8.7% (SD 5.7%) and 7.1% (SD 7%), respectively.

For GFAP and S100B assays, absorbance was read at 450 and 630 nm on Versa Max plate reader (Molecular Devices). SoftMax Pro 5.0 Software (Molecular Devices) was used to subtract the 630 nm readings from the 450 nm readings and values from blank wells were subtracted from values of samples and standards. To generate a curve from the standards, the mean absorbance of duplicate wells was plotted in a logarithmic scale, and a curve was generated by a 4-parameter algorithm, as described in the kit manuals.

These ELISA assays were validated in our laboratory for equine samples using dilutional parallelism and demonstrating linearity (Figure S1), and inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation < 15%.

Serum BDNF concentrations were measured with a single molecule array (Simoa)® BDNF Discovery kit. The ultra-sensitive Simoa digital biomarker detection assay from Quanterix is used to quantify the concentration of biomarkers in plasma, serum, and CSF (Quanterix, Billerica, MA). The Simoa platform uses 2.7 μm antibody labeled beads that bind to individual molecules of analyte and are loaded onto a disc containing 200 000 microwells that each hold only one bead. The immunocomplex “sandwich” is formed with biotinylated detection antibodies that allow for digital and analog detection of analyte at as low as fg/mL concentrations. The Simoa method has not been reported in the horse. In dogs, the use of this technology allowed for the quantification of neurofilament light chain in serum or plasma and CSF at picogram concentrations and showed promise as a diagnostic, prognostic, and treatment response biomarker.21

The kit had a working range of 0.0026-60 000 pg/mL, a limit of detection of 0.0042 pg/mL, and a mean recovery of 110%. The assay was linear at dilutions up to 1:16 and had mean inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation of 12.12% (SD-4.3%) and 7.9% (SD-5.2%) for equine samples, respectively. Plasma progesterone and 17α-hydroxyprogesterone concentrations were measured with radioimmunoassay (RIA) (MP Biomedicals, catalog # 0727010-CF and 0727110-CF), previously validated for horses with a sensitivity of 0.02 and 0.03 ng/mL and a range of 0.15-80 ng/mL and 0.1-25 ng/mL, respectively.22

Data analysis

Sample size and power analysis

A power analysis was performed to estimate the required sample size using G*Power 3.1. Progesterone concentration was selected as the primary variable for this analysis because of its documented availability in neonatal foals; in contrast, concentrations of GFAP, BDNF, and S100B have not been well reported in equine neonates.3,5 Based on previous data comparing admission progesterone concentration in healthy foals (mean 2.3 ng/mL; SD = 2.7) and septic foals (mean 10.5 ng/mL; SD = 8.7), the calculated effect size was 1.27. For a 2-tailed test with an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 80%, a minimum sample size of n = 11 per group was required for between-group comparisons.

Statistical methodology

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data were not normally distributed, results are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) or marginal means with confidence intervals for the mixed models. Additionally, the assumption of normality was verified by subjecting the model residuals to a Shapiro–Wilk test. A mixed-effects model was used to evaluate the impact of time, sepsis score and foal group on hormone and biomarker concentrations, where individual animals served as the random intercept in the mixed model. To control the family-wise error rate and adjust P-values for multiple comparisons, the Tukey’s test was used for all pairwise comparisons within the model. The model included an interaction term between the foal group and time to evaluate whether the change in biomarker over time differed between groups.

The Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc analysis was used to compare 3 or more groups (laboratory and clinical findings in 3 groups of foals on admission), while the Mann–Whitney rank sum test was employed for comparisons between 2 groups (concentration of biomarkers in survivors and non-survivors on admission). All analyses were conducted using SPSS and GraphPad Prism, with significance set at P < .05.

Results

Study sample

A total of 72 foals (≤7 days old) were included in the study: 81% (58/72) were hospitalized, and 19% (14/72) were healthy. Of hospitalized foals, 33% (19/58) were classified as NMS, 67% (39/58) as other disease. Additionally, 57% (33/58) of hospitalized foals were classified as septic. Sixty-three percent (12/19) of NMS foals were also categorized as septic, and 21 foals were septic without evidence of NMS. Nineteen percent (11/58) of hospitalized foals were premature, and 81% (47/58) were full-term foals. Forty-five percent (5/11) of premature foals had clinical signs consistent with NMS. The median ages of the NMS group, healthy foals, and foals with other diseases were not significantly different from one another (P > .05) (Table 1).

Plasma GFAP concentration in healthy, NMS, and foals presented for other diseases

There was a significant foal group × time interaction for plasma GFAP concentration (P = .02). Plasma concentrations of GFAP were consistently higher across all 3-time points (0, days 1 and 2) in healthy foals (3.3, 1.48-4.92; 2.94, 1.45-4.43; 2.73, 1.47-3.99, ng/mL) compared to foals with NMS (1.2, 0.3-2.43; 0.86, 0.17-1.55; 0.96, 0.14-1.8, ng/mL) and other diseases (1.02, 0.6-1.43; 1.14, 0.66-1.63; 0.87, 0.52-1.21 ng/mL), respectively (P = .02, P = .04, P = .04) (Figure 1A). Additionally, no significant differences in plasma GFAP concentrations were observed between survivors (0.31, 0.31-1.18 ng/mL) and non-survivors (0.38, 0.31-0.68 ng/mL) on admission in hospitalized foals (Figure 1B), P = .12. The sepsis score was included in the model as a covariate. Results indicated that the baseline sepsis score did not have a statistically significant effect on GFAP concentration, F(1, 55.7) = 0.03, P = .86.

Plasma 17α-hydroxyprogesterone and progesterone concentration in septic foals with and without NMS

Progesterone concentration was higher in septic foals with NMS (14.6, 5.8-23.3; 22.5, 5.2-39.9 ng/mL) compared to septic foals without NMS (4.35, 0.14-8.5; 2.44, 0.44-5.32 ng/mL) on days 0 and 1, P = .04, P = .03, respectively (Figure 7D). On day 1, 17α-hydroxyprogesterone was higher in septic foals with NMS (3.65, 0.7-6.6 ng/mL) compared to septic foals without NMS (1.2, 0.05-2.5 ng/mL), P = .02 (Figure 7E).

Discussion

This study reports circulating concentrations of astroglial (GFAP, S100B) and neuronal (BDNF) biomarkers, alongside pregnanes, in healthy and critically ill neonatal foals, including those with suspected NMS, prematurity, and other neonatal diseases. Overall, the data demonstrate that biomarker concentrations differ between healthy and critically ill foals. Admission concentrations of BDNF and S100B were significantly higher in septic foals with concurrent NMS compared with septic foals without NMS, suggesting a potential association with neurological dysfunction in the presence of systemic illness. Similarly, pregnane concentrations were associated with NMS, including in foals with concurrent sepsis, and correlated with clinical severity.

Neonatal maladjustment syndrome is an important disorder in newborn foals. Identifying reliable biomarkers might enhance understanding of pathophysiology, diagnostic accuracy, treatment, and outcomes. Evidence from human neonates shows that prematurity and sepsis influence concentration of neurological biomarkers such as BDNF and GFAP. In sepsis-associated encephalopathy, serum GFAP increases, with specificity and sensitivity of 77.7% and 75.9%, respectively.23 BDNF concentrations decrease with sepsis.24 Lower GFAP and BDNF are reported in premature infants, indicating that systemic illness and developmental stage significantly affect these biomarkers.25

Astrocyte development is critical for brain maturation, neurovascular coupling, synaptic regulation, and neural repair.26,27 Astrocytes can protect neural tissue by maintaining ion homeostasis and metabolic support, but dysfunction might worsen injury through impaired buffering, metabolic disruption, and pro-inflammatory mediator release.28,29

GFAP, an astrocytic intermediate filament protein, is a potential biomarker for neonatal neurologic dysfunction.13,16 GFAP concentrations remained high in healthy foals throughout the study, whereas critically ill foals, including those with NMS, exhibited lower concentrations. No significant differences in GFAP concentrations were observed between septic foals with NMS and septic foals without NMS. These findings contrast with observations in human neonates, where higher GFAP is associated with astrocytic injury in conditions such as hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy and traumatic brain injury.30,31 As in foals, neonatal calves with perinatal asphyxia show no significant differences in GFAP between affected and healthy calves.18 In adult dogs, GFAP is a specific predictor of spinal cord injury outcomes.32 No definitive explanation can be established; however, our results might reflect developmental or functional differences in astrocytes in equine neonates compared with other species.

S100B, a calcium-binding protein primarily expressed by astrocytes and Schwann cells,15,26,27 differed significantly in the intake sample, with septic foals with NMS exhibiting higher S100B concentrations compared with septic foals without NMS. S100B is higher in human neonates with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy33 and in neonatal calves with perinatal asphyxia, where it reflects glial activation and neural injury.17 Furthermore, in human neonates, serum S100B concentrations measured on the first day of life are the best predictor of adverse neonatal outcomes, including death.16 These findings suggest that S100B might be useful as an early biomarker for neurological injury and prognosis in foals. The higher concentrations on admission observed in septic foals with NMS might reflect an early, transient astroglial response linked to neurological dysfunction and systemic critical illness.

BDNF concentrations decreased from those at admission in foals with NMS, suggesting evolving neuronal dysfunction or altered neurotrophic support during illness. In humans, BDNF is a key regulator of neuronal survival, plasticity, and repair, and its concentrations are influenced by neuroinflammation and injury. In human studies, BDNF plays a central role in maintaining neuronal survival and plasticity, making it a sensitive indicator of neural health.9–12 In the present study, circulating BDNF concentrations in foals (range 1.06-6.46 pg/mL) were markedly lower than those reported in human neonates (5.53-11.53 ng/mL),34 suggesting that BDNF might not play as crucial physiological role in equine neural development as it does in humans. Despite this, intake BDNF concentrations were significantly higher in septic foals with NMS compared with septic foals without NMS, suggesting an early association between neuronal signaling and neurological dysfunction in critical illness. These findings suggest species-specific differences in the neurotrophic functions of BDNF, while also highlighting its potential role as an indicator of neuronal dysfunction in pathological conditions such as NMS and sepsis. To accurately measure this low concentration of BDNF, the highly sensitive Single Molecule Array (SIMOA) technique developed by Quanterix was utilized. SIMOA® employs microfluidic technology to detect single molecules, enabling quantification of biomarkers at femtogram-per-milliliter concentrations. This method has also been successfully utilized in other species, such as dogs, to measure biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain, demonstrating its broad applicability.21

Our study found that foals diagnosed with NMS had higher concentrations of progesterone and 17α-hydroxyprogesterone compared to healthy foals and those with other illnesses across all 3 time points. Additionally, premature foals also exhibited elevated concentrations of both pregnanes. These findings align with previous research, which has similarly indicated that altered steroid hormone concentrations can reflect neurodevelopmental disturbances. The dysregulation of 17α-hydroxyprogesterone and progesterone might therefore contribute to the neurological dysfunction observed in affected foals.3–5 In utero, high levels of pregnanes maintain the unconscious state of the foal, and undergo a rapid decline at birth to facilitate the transition to consciousness. In foals with NMS, this drop does not occur, potentially contributing to their lower responsiveness to environmental stimuli and development of signs of neurological disease.35

Collecting cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from foals with suspected NMS could provide an overview of the CNS cellular environment, which influences overall neurological function.36 The previously discussed biomarkers are also expressed in various other systems and organs. Schwann cells, which provide support to peripheral nerve fibers, as well as mature glial cells in the gastrointestinal tract and hepatic stellate cells in the liver, also produce these biomarkers.37 Consequently, plasma and serum biomarker concentrations might not exclusively reflect CNS lesions, necessitating careful interpretation in the context of neurological disease. Human studies have extensively compared the concentrations of GFAP, BDNF, and S100B between serum and CSF, highlighting important differences in their diagnostic utility and pathophysiological interpretation. In conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and cerebral amyloid angiopathy, serum GFAP exhibits greater sensitivity to early or subclinical stages of disease, while CSF GFAP might reflect more advanced or CNS–localized injury.38–40 Similarly, in human patients with multiple sclerosis, circulating BDNF levels have been associated with neurological recovery, suggesting that peripheral BDNF might provide insight into central neurotrophic processes.41 In humans, both serum and CSF concentrations of S100B increase after CNS injury, though serum concentrations rise rapidly within hours of spinal cord injury and tend to normalize after 24 h, indicating its potential value as an early and transient biomarker of acute neural damage.42

The main limitations of this study stem from the nature of NMS as a clinical diagnosis, which can lead to variability, as NMS might present with clinical signs that overlap with those of other neurological or systemic conditions in foals, such as sepsis, prematurity, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy or electrolyte abnormalities. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging can provide valuable diagnostic information for foals with neurological dysfunction. However, these neurodiagnostic methods are not routinely performed in neonatal foals.43 Plasma biomarker concentrations in foals with NMS could resemble those observed in foals suffering from different diseases, complicating the differentiation between these conditions. Ideally, those biomarkers should be compared between foals with NMS and those with other causes of neurological disease, eg, meningitis or electrolyte abnormalities. However, these diseases are uncommon and were not diagnosed in our foal sample. Another limitation stems from the ancillary tools used to describe the study sample and refine the diagnosis of NMS, including the sepsis score, neurobehavioral score (NMS score), N/L ratio, and RPR. While these measures aim to describe disease severity and assess the likelihood of several neonatal diseases, such as NMS, sepsis, or prematurity, each has inherent weaknesses. The sepsis score lacks sensitivity and specificity, particularly in the early neonatal period.19,44 NMS scoring systems are not standardized and often rely on incomplete or inconsistently documented clinical data. The N/L ratio is influenced by age, stress, and corticosteroid use, while RPR might be affected by concurrent inflammation or thrombocytopenia.45,46 These limitations might lead to diagnostic misclassification, introducing heterogeneity into study samples and confounding interpretation of biomarker data. Another limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size of foals diagnosed with NMS, which might limits the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the limited number of non-survivors further constrains our ability to assess potential differences in biomarker profiles and disease progression between surviving and non-surviving foals.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that BDNF and S100B show promise as potential biomarkers for studying neurological function in a larger cohort of critically ill foals. In contrast, GFAP does not appear to have a significant association with NMS but could serve as a marker of general critical illness.
