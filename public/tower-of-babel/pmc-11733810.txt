Congenital Heart Disease Surgery in Low-Resource Settings
Authors: Bistra Zheleva, Liesl Zühlke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In high-income countries, survival from congenital heart disease (CHD) has reached over 90%, and morbidity is improving with the advances in specialized medicine and chronic disease management. However, these advances have not been reflected in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In this issue of JACC: Advances, a new major review of surgical outcomes from LMICs demonstrates that surgical successes are possible there too.

Wittenberg et al1 present promising findings from a retrospective analysis of the International Quality Improvement Collaborative (IQIC) for congenital heart disease data for atrial septal defect (ASD) surgery from 2010 to 2020. The study demonstrates that surgical programs in LMICs experienced an over five-fold reduction in in-hospital mortality and over two-fold reduction of major infection after ASD surgery over a 10-year period.

The IQIC is a multinational LMIC learning collaborative with a CHD surgery outcomes registry. The IQIC has been collecting data since 2008 and now includes over 80 sites in more than 30 countries. It provides benchmarking data to all participating sites, with the overall goal of guiding quality improvement efforts and reducing mortality for congenital heart disease.2

CHD is a global problem, as demonstrated by several recent publications. The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2017 estimated that CHD caused 261,247 deaths globally in 2017, with 180,624 deaths among infants.3 The study also found that most of the CHD mortality was in LMICs.

Recent data have also demonstrated an increased contribution of birth defects to under-5 and infant mortality globally, especially in countries with improved overall childhood mortality.4,5 CHD is one of the 2 leading causes of birth defects globally that account for over half of all neonatal deaths due to birth defects.5 As these data are starting to emerge, LMICs are beginning to develop an interest in increasing their pediatric and congenital heart services, especially surgical.

As one of the most common and less complex congenital cardiac defects, ASD repair is typically one of the first operations that many new centers undertake as they start a cardiac surgical program. Outcomes for this condition have improved over time in high-income countries, with an expectation of 100% survival and minimal morbidity. It is important to have evidence like the one presented by the IQIC demonstrating that success is possible and high-quality care is possible in LMICs as well.6

An important aspect of the experience described in this paper is the focus on quality improvement. The CHD improvements we have observed in high-income countries are due to medical advances in screening, diagnosis, and treatment. However, success in this highly specialized cardiac field would not have been possible without the relentless focus on quality, such as infection prevention, multidisciplinary approach and team communication, and constant review of outcomes. The Breakthrough Collaborative Series7 is a model for collaborative learning, aiming to achieve “breakthrough” improvements in clinical care by applying quality improvement science principles. The IQIC used the format of this model when it was founded in 2010 to invite congenital heart programs from LMICs to share data and learn from each other and from other experts, as well as from their joint efforts with the goal to improve outcomes for CHD surgery.

Wittenberg et al1 describe that participation in the IQIC was associated with improvement in surgical outcomes for CHD, including mortality, major infection, ventilation, and length of intensive care unit stay. This is due to improvement activities such as regular virtual learning sessions, specialized courses, and in-person collaborative learning meetings for sharing with other sites, as well as the regular reporting on benchmarked data for each participating hospital.

Quality improvement collaborative principles are not well known outside of high-income countries, and there are only few examples of successful programs from LMICs.8,9 To our knowledge, the IQIC has the only surgical database for centers from LMICs of this magnitude and reach. It has the potential to inform many hospital practices and CHD health policies in low-resource settings. This study offers an opportunity to educate broader audiences on the benefits of quality improvement collaborative participation for highly specialized fields such as CHD surgery in LMICs. Providing CHD surgery in LMICs is often a lonely endeavor. Many of the programs in the IQIC are the single referral center for CHD patients for large regions and/or countries. The IQIC not only teaches the principles of quality improvement but also builds a community of likeminded individuals and centers with similar experience where they can discuss problems, learn from, and inspire each other. This community is reflected in the authorship1, representing diverse sets of expertise from multiple continents. Too often, issues pertaining to LMICs are reported solely by those in the Global North, further widening the inequity gap.10

Despite the success described by the authors, it is important to acknowledge that the work is far from complete. The IQIC demonstrated reductions in ASD mortality and infections over the 2 periods; however, intensive care unit length of stay did not change. Mortality, albeit very low, is still higher than is expected for this condition. Both points indicate that there may still be health systems issues outside of the hospital walls, such as delayed presentation due to inadequate screening and detection. In addition, the nature of ASDs is such that it is often not discovered until adulthood, and the IQIC excludes patients older than 18 years, thus preventing participating hospitals from addressing long-term outcomes.

The GBD study estimated that 11,998,283 people live with CHD globally, an 18.7% increase from 1990 to 2017, causing 589,479 years of living with disability.3 These numbers are important because atrial septal defects, the second most common CHD, while not an immediate contributor to infant and overall childhood mortality, can be a contributor to long-term morbidity and mortality, especially if left unrepaired. There are very few population-level longitudinal studies of CHD, especially from LMICs. Data from Denmark show higher long-term mortality in ASD patients compared with a sex- and birth year-matched general population cohort,11 so we can expect that this problem will be even higher with LMICs where many children with CHD are never diagnosed. ASD, therefore, is a prime example of the lifelong nature of CHD, perhaps precisely because it is considered a so-called simple lesion. It behooves us all to not think about a job well done after a successful surgery but to work toward developing lifelong care and chronic disease management programs for all CHD patients, regardless of where they live.
