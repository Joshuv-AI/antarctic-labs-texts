Trust in federal statistical agencies and vaccine uptake among U.S. adults
Authors: Nathaniel J Maxey, Jemar R Bather, Taliyah S Griffin, Plamena P Powla, Felix M Pabon-Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:In the United States (US), COVID-19 vaccination saved 3.2 million lives from 2020 to 2023, yet vaccine uptake remains a public health challenge. Seasonal influenza vaccination also remains an important public health priority because influenza contributes substantially to preventable morbidity and mortality each year. Examining the administration of COVID-19 and influenza vaccines provides an opportunity to understand the factors influencing vaccine uptake.Methods:Repeated cross-sectional data were obtained from the Household Pulse Survey. COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake were based on self-reported vaccination status since August 2024. Independent variables of interest were (1) trust in federal statistics, (2) confidence in the US Census Bureau, and (3) confidence in US statistical agencies. Weighted logistic regression quantified the association between trust measures and non-vaccine uptake, adjusting for sociodemographic factors.Results:Compared to those who trusted federal statistics, individuals who did not trust federal statistics had significantly higher odds of COVID-19 non-vaccine uptake (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.61, 95% CI: 2.05–3.32) and influenza non-vaccine uptake (aOR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.58–2.21). Similarly, individuals with very little confidence in the U.S. Census Bureau (COVID-19: aOR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.16–4.02; influenza: aOR: 2.67, 95% CI: 1.74–4.10) or U.S. statistical agencies (COVID-19: aOR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.52–3.43; influenza: aOR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.35–2.57) exhibited a greater likelihood of non-vaccine uptake.Conclusion:Distrust in federal statistical agencies was associated with lower COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake, highlighting the importance of institutional trust in vaccination behavior. Unlike prior studies focused on trust in healthcare systems or government broadly, this study specifically identifies trust in federal statistical agencies as a factor associated with vaccine decision-making. Future longitudinal and mixed-methods research should examine how institutional trust develops over time and whether communication and transparency-focused interventions can improve vaccine uptake and public confidence in health information.Keywords: Vaccine uptake, COVID-19, Vaccination, Influenza vaccination, Weighted logistic regression, Survey

Introduction

In the United States (US), the COVID-19 vaccination saved an estimated 3.2 million lives between December 2020 and November 2022 [1]. Despite this success, vaccine uptake remains an ongoing public health challenge, with an estimated 240,797 COVID-19 deaths between December 2020 and June 2021 potentially preventable through vaccination [2–4]. By December 2021, one year after COVID-19 vaccines became available, only 63% of the US population had completed the primary series of an approved vaccine [5]. While COVID-19 vaccination rates showed some progress, similar challenges in vaccine uptake persist with other preventable diseases. Among adults 18 years and older, influenza vaccination coverage during the 2023–24 season was approximately 45%, compared with approximately 47% during the 2022–23 influenza season [6]. With this vaccination disparity, the success of federal, state, and local efforts to curb hesitancy largely depends on public trust [7].

Such trust is vital for encouraging the public to adopt protective measures that limit disease transmission and mitigate negative health impacts [7]. However, sociodemographic factors such as age, race, and educational attainment significantly affect the trust required for vaccine uptake [8–12]. Racial/ethnic differences are evident, with African American and Hispanic populations demonstrating higher levels of vaccine hesitancy compared to their White counterparts [11,12]. This hesitancy often reflects distrust in government, shaped by historical and ongoing discrimination [8–14].

One potential explanation for these findings is that federal statistical agencies play a central role in generating and communicating population health information used to guide public health recommendations [7,11]. Distrust in these institutions may therefore reduce confidence in the validity of vaccine-related data, perceived disease risk, or the credibility of public health messaging more broadly [7,11]. This may be associated with how individuals interpret vaccination recommendations and engage with preventive health interventions [13,14].

Given widespread distrust in federal statistics, further research is needed on its link to vaccine uptake [13–15]. Using data from the Household Pulse Survey, we investigated the relationships of COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake with trust in federal statistics, confidence in the US Census Bureau, and confidence in US statistical agencies, controlling for sociodemographic factors. We hypothesized lower trust in federal statistical agencies would be significantly associated with increased odds of non-vaccine uptake.

Methods

Study design and setting

We used cross-sectional data from three waves of the Household Pulse Survey, a national survey conducted by the US Census Bureau collected on October 15–29, 2024; December 17, 2024–January 3, 2025; and February 21–March 7, 2025 [16]. This 20-min survey included various topics, including mental health, social isolation, and socio-demographic factors. Households were randomly selected from the Master Address File, and one individual from each household was invited to participate. As with many large national online surveys, participation in the Household Pulse Survey was voluntary, which may introduce the potential for nonresponse bias despite the application of survey weighting procedures. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [17].

Analytic sample

Between October 15 and October 29, 2024, 9355 individuals participated in the Household Pulse Survey. Of these, 1.5% (n = 144) were excluded due to missing information on COVID-19 and influenza vaccination status, 1.2% (n = 111) due to unavailable trust or confidence measures, and 0.8% (n = 78) due to missing covariate data (marital status: n = 37, number of children: n = 34, and employment status: n = 7). These exclusions resulted in a final unweighted sample of 9022 participants.

Between December 17, 2024, and January 3, 2025, 9404 individuals participated in the Household Pulse Survey. Of these, 1.9% (n = 178) were excluded due to missing information on COVID-19 and influenza vaccination status, 1.2% (n = 115) due to unavailable trust or confidence measures, and 0.9% (n = 77) due to missing covariate data (marital status: n = 38, number of children: n = 24, and employment status: n = 15). These exclusions resulted in a final unweighted sample of 9034 participants.

Between February 21, 2025, and March 7, 2025, 6740 individuals participated in the Household Pulse Survey. Of these, 1.8% (n = 119) were excluded due to missing information on COVID-19 and influenza vaccination status, 1.3% (n = 90) due to unavailable trust or confidence measures, and 1.0% (n = 68) due to missing covariate data (marital status: n = 26, number of children: n = 36, and employment status: n = 6). These exclusions resulted in a final unweighted sample of 6463 participants.

COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake

We assessed COVID-19 and influenza vaccination uptake using one item: Have you received the following vaccines this fall (i.e., since August 2024)? This item was asked separately for COVID-19 and influenza vaccination. We derived each outcome as a binary variable, denoting ‘1’ for not receiving the vaccine and ‘0’ for receiving the vaccine.

Trust in federal statistical agencies

The Household Pulse Survey included questions about trust in federal statistics, confidence in the US Census Bureau, and confidence in US statistical agencies. The Household Pulse Survey did not provide respondents with a formal definition of ‘U.S. statistical agencies.’ For interpretive purposes, this study contextualized the term using publicly available Household Pulse Survey documentation describing federal statistical agency partners involved in survey development and data collection, including agencies such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Center for Health Statistics [14]. For trust in federal statistics, participants were asked, “The population count, the crime rate, and the unemployment rate are examples of statistics produced by the federal government. Personally, how much trust do you have in federal statistics in the United States? Would you say that you tend to trust federal statistics, or you tend not to trust them?” Response options included “Tend to trust federal statistics” or “Tend not to trust federal statistics.” We treated this as a binary variable in the analysis. For confidence in the US Census Bureau and US statistical agencies, participants were asked, “Please indicate how much confidence you, yourself, have in the US Census Bureau” and “Please indicate how much confidence you, yourself, have in the US statistical agencies.” Response options included “A great deal,” “Quite a lot,” “Some,” and “Very little.” We dichotomized each confidence variable as very little versus other categories. Dichotomization was used to distinguish respondents expressing particularly low institutional confidence from all other respondents and to improve interpretability across survey waves.

Covariates

We controlled for the following sociodemographic factors: age (measured continuously in years), Sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Other/Multiracial), Hispanic (yes, no), educational attainment (bachelor’s degree or higher, some college education/associate’s degree, high school or less), employed (yes, no), marital status (married, not married but in a domestic partnership, divorced/separated/widowed, never married), number of children (measured continuously), and health insurance coverage (private, Medicare, Medicaid, other, unknown).

Statistical analysis

We used the survey and gtsummary R packages to produce design-weighted descriptives [18,19]. The Household Pulse Survey provides design-based person-level survey weights developed by the U.S. Census Bureau to account for unequal probabilities of selection, nonresponse, and population calibration, and analyses conducted using the survey package incorporated these weights into both point and variance estimation to generate design-adjusted standard errors and confidence intervals [16,18]. Bivariate analyses were based on Wilcoxon rank-sum and chi-squared tests. Multivariable analyses included survey-weighted logistic regression modeling each outcome (COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake) as a function of each primary condition variable. We fit unadjusted models and models adjusted for age, gender identity, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, employment status, marital status, number of children, and health insurance coverage. In wave-specific analyses, we stacked the October 2024, December 2024, and February 2025 waves (October as reference), built a pooled survey design, estimated weighted vaccine uptake prevalence by wave, and modeled uptake on wave; analyses were conducted in R Version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Associations between trust in federal statistical agencies and vaccine uptake

In October 2024, weighted logistic regression models demonstrated persistent relationships between trust in federal statistical agencies and vaccine uptake, even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors. Table 2 shows both unadjusted and adjusted associations. Individuals who tend not to trust federal statistics had significantly higher odds of not receiving the COVID-19 vaccine compared to those who trust federal statistics (unadjusted OR [OR]: 2.59, 95% CI: 2.05–3.28, p < 0.001; adjusted OR [aOR]: 2.61, 95% CI: 2.05–3.32, p < 0.001). Similarly, those with very little confidence in the US Census Bureau had higher odds of non-vaccine uptake (OR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.41–4.56, p = 0.002; aOR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.16–4.02, p = 0.015), as did individuals with very little confidence in US statistical agencies (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.69–3.68, p < 0.001; aOR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.52–3.43, p < 0.001) relative to those with at least some confidence in these institutions (Table 3).

Discussion

Principal’s findings and broader implication

Consistent with the hypothesis, lower trust in federal statistical agencies was significantly associated with not taking the COVID-19 or influenza vaccine, and this relationship persisted after adjusting for sociodemographic factors. Additionally, individuals with very little confidence in the US Census Bureau or US statistical agencies had a higher likelihood of not receiving the COVID-19 vaccine compared to individuals with at least some confidence in these institutions. Similarly, individuals who tend not to trust federal statistics had higher odds of not receiving the influenza vaccine than those who tend to trust federal statistics. Furthermore, people with very little confidence in the US Census Bureau or US statistical agencies also had increased odds of not receiving the influenza vaccination. While weighted prevalence estimates indicated declines in influenza and COVID-19 vaccine uptake across waves, regression results showed trust in federal statistical agencies continued to be significantly associated with vaccination behavior, even after accounting for sociodemographic factors.

Influenza vaccination prevalence fluctuated between 2014 and 2021, and during our survey waves we observed further declines in both influenza and COVID-19 uptake [20]. These patterns underscore the importance of understanding the factors shaping vaccine uptake rather than viewing coverage as static. In our analysis, trust emerged as a significant factor associated with vaccination uptake, with lower confidence in federal statistical agencies associated with reduced likelihood of vaccination. This aligns with prior work showing institutional trust plays a vital yet nuanced role in vaccine behavior, where the nature of trust may be critically associated with vaccine uptake [21–24]. The role of institutional trust, however, is complex and context dependent. For example, low confidence in governmental institutions has been shown to negatively affect vaccine trust [21–24]. Our findings similarly indicate lower trust in federal agencies is associated with reduced uptake.

One potential explanation for these findings is that federal statistical agencies play a central role in generating and communicating population health information used to guide public health recommendations [7,11]. Distrust in these institutions may therefore reduce confidence in the validity of vaccine-related data, perceived disease risk, or the credibility of public health messaging more broadly [21–24]. This may be associated with how individuals interpret vaccination recommendations and engage with preventive health interventions [21–24].

While many studies document an association between greater trust in healthcare providers and higher vaccine acceptance, recent research suggests skepticism toward official narratives can, in some contexts, motivate preventive health behaviors [23,24]. The timing of our data collection, particularly around significant political events, may also have introduced dynamics that may have been associated with trust and vaccine uptake differently than in other settings. Further research is needed to disentangle how trust, risk perception, and sociopolitical context interact to shape vaccination behavior [25–32].

Strengths, limitations, and future research directions

A strength of the Household Pulse Survey is it is nationally representative and conducted by the US Census Bureau. This recent data provides a unique perspective over other studies that relied on data from 2020 to 2021 [20]. However, the limitations of self-reported data in vaccination studies are well documented in the literature [33–35]. Self-reported data may introduce information bias because participants may report false information on their vaccination status.

Additionally, because the Household Pulse Survey was administered by the U.S. Census Bureau, responses regarding trust in federal statistics and statistical agencies may have been influenced by survey source effects or social desirability bias. Potential participants were informed during recruitment that the survey was conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau, and individuals with lower levels of institutional trust may therefore have been less likely to participate, introducing possible selection or nonresponse bias into trust-related estimates. These potential biases may affect the accuracy and representativeness of the findings and should be considered when interpreting the results. Additionally, voluntary participation in the survey may have contributed to nonresponse bias despite the application of survey weighting procedures. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, as the data provide only a snapshot in time and make it difficult to assess how institutional trust and vaccination behavior evolve over time [35]. Because vaccine uptake was relatively common in some subgroups, odds ratios may somewhat overestimate the magnitude of association relative to prevalence ratios. Residual confounding is also possible because some factors associated with both institutional trust and vaccine uptake, such as political orientation, household income, and urban/rural residence, were not included in the adjusted analyses.

Future research could extend these findings by linking electronic health record data on vaccine uptake and tracking changes in institutional trust and vaccination behaviors over time. New survey waves may also help monitor evolving trends in COVID-19 and influenza vaccination. Longitudinal studies are especially important for assessing causal relationships and understanding how trust in federal statistical agencies may be related to vaccine-related decision-making. Future work should additionally incorporate qualitative and mixed-methods approaches to better understand the drivers of distrust, including historical experiences, perceptions of institutional credibility, communication strategies, and community perspectives. Incorporating these perspectives may support the development of more effective public health outreach, trust-building interventions, and vaccination promotion efforts while reducing information bias through the inclusion of objective health behavior measures and a broader range of vaccination determinants.

Conclusions

The significant associations between trust in statistical agencies, federal statistics, and the U.S. Census Bureau and vaccination suggest that institutional trust is an important factor in public health initiatives. Individuals with lower trust in agencies such as the U.S. Census Bureau had higher odds of not receiving COVID-19 and influenza vaccines. Weighted analyses also indicated declining COVID-19 and influenza vaccine uptake across survey waves relative to October 2024. Taken together, these findings highlight the association between institutional trust and vaccination behaviors.
