Perceptions of self-efficacy, everyday stress, and symptoms of anxiety and depression among traumatized women in comprehensive treatment
Authors: Sofia Svensén, Ingeborg Bolstad, Liv Skomakerstuen Ødbehr, Gerry Larsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Experience of trauma may cause serious consequences for mental health and self-image. Self-efficacy and perceptions of everyday stress have shown to impact mental health in a great manner. The aim of this study was to explore the longitudinal effects of a 12-week comprehensive treatment programme, including physical exercise, nutrition, social training, and psychoeducation, in addition to traditional psychopharmacology and psychotherapy, on self-efficacy, hassles and uplifts, and symptoms of anxiety and depression in traumatized women in a clinical context.Method: Forty-eight female inpatients with a mean age of 43.7 years (SD = 10.7) receiving psychiatric treatment in a Norwegian psychiatric clinic participated in the longitudinal study. Patients responded to the questionnaires General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), the Stress Profile (hassles and uplifts), and the Hopkins Symptoms Checklist (HSCL-10) at three points in time: at baseline, at the end of treatment, and three months after treatment. As the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests for dependent samples were used to analyse the data. However, as the residuals were considered normally distributed, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted.Results: All instruments, except for uplifts, showed significant changes during treatment, e.g. self-efficacy increased from 25.0 to 26.0, p = .036. A hierarchical regression conducted at baseline, with symptoms of psychological distress as the dependent variable, and age, self-efficacy, hassles, and uplifts as predictors, showed that self-efficacy was a significant predictor. At three months after treatment, the same hierarchical regression model revealed that all included predictors except age were significant, and the total variance explained by the model was 61.6 percent.Conclusion: Three months after completed treatment the results indicated associations between an increase in self-efficacy, a reduced frequency of daily hassles and a higher frequency of daily uplifts and a reduction in psychological distress.KEYWORDS: Women, trauma, self-efficacy, hassles, uplifts, anxiety, depression, comprehensive treatment

Introduction

Experiences of trauma may cause serious consequences for mental health, and the view of oneself. The event may awaken feelings of impuissance and helplessness, not being able to handle the situation (Flint & Eytan, 2024), and negative changes in thoughts and mood may arise (Bäärnhielm et al., 2024). This might make the individual doubt his or her ability to cope with upcoming situations, in other words, the perceived self-efficacy. A meta-analysis consisting of 27 articles studied the impact of self-efficacy on health-related outcomes after a collective trauma (Luszczynska et al., 2009). The results showed that both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies were united in the positive effects of self-efficacy on mental health, and the weighted effects were medium to large.

Schönfeld et al. (2016) argue that self-efficacy is a valuable aspect of treatment, since it can be influenced. To illustrate this, a randomized control trial (RCT) showed that self-efficacy can increase through psychotherapy, leading to improved mental health. Patients low in self-efficacy pre-intervention showed a greater decrease in symptoms of psychological distress post-intervention (Clarke et al., 2014).

Self-efficacy might also affect the experience of everyday hassles and uplifts and individuals low in self-efficacy have a higher tendency to experience daily situations as hassles (Schönfeld et al., 2016). Everyday stress is a phenomenon that everyone has to deal with and individuals perceive everyday stressful events in various ways. Lazarus (1984) called these experiences ‘hassles and uplifts’ and argued that how these daily situations are experienced has a profound impact on mental health. This has been shown in several studies, see Larsson et al. (2016) for a review. According to Hochwälder and Saied (2018), there are also gender differences, where women tend to experience hassles more intensely than men.

Attempts to reduce perceptions of the frequency and intensity of daily hassles have typically used Lazarus’ theoretical framework (Lazarus, 1991; Lazarus, 1999) as a foundation (see, e.g. Larsson et al., 2016). A recent example of an RCT was conducted by Furman et al. (2024), who examined the effects of cognitive behavioural therapy and pharmacological treatment on hassles and uplifts. There were no differences in perceptions of hassles and uplifts measured pre-intervention. Post-intervention, the results showed a decrease in hassles for both treatment methods, and a significant increase in uplifts in the pharmacologically treated group.

Treatment programmes for anxiety and depression have increasingly been complemented by physical activity (Andersson et al., 2022; Merom et al., 2008; Singh et al., 2023), healthy nutrition (Lai et al., 2014; Li et al., 2017) and social support (Harandi et al., 2017). These are aspects of the intervention that individuals can do themselves after some instruction to improve their mental health. However, there is sparse research on physical activity and healthy nutrition in traumatized women (Nair & Kampman, 2022; Sheppard-Perkins et al., 2022), while the use of social support for this group has been more researched (Hirai et al., 2020; Lehavot et al., 2018; Tirone et al., 2021). To our knowledge there have been no intervention studies among traumatized women involving all three aspects (physical activity, nutrition and social support), as completements to traditional psychopharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatment.

To summarize, research shows that people’s perceived self-efficacy can have a positive effect on their experiences of daily stress and mental health. Successful attempts to enhance self-efficacy and coping with everyday stress have been reported. An unresolved issue, however, is whether those findings also apply to severely traumatized individuals receiving a comprehensive treatment. Recent studies in Norway of adult women suffering from the most severe forms of sexual assault under Norwegian legislation, have shown that spontaneous recovery of mental health problems occurred for most, but not all, women, and only often several years after the traumatic event or series of events. A key factor in the improvement appeared to be an increase in perceived daily uplifts and a reduction in perceived daily hassles (Stensvehagen et al., 2022). In contrast to the participants in the Stensvehagen et al. (2022) study, where all participants experienced sexual traumas, the participants of the present study had various types of trauma experiences. A key question underlying the present study is to investigate whether the recovery process can be enhanced for severely traumatized individuals through a comprehensive treatment programme.

The aim of the present study was to explore the longitudinal effects of a 12-week comprehensive treatment programme, including physical exercise, nutrition, social training, and psychoeducation, in addition to traditional psychopharmacology and psychotherapy, on perceptions of self-efficacy, daily hassles and uplifts, and symptoms of anxiety and depression in severely traumatized women in a clinical context. The following hypothesis was formulated: the treatment programme will covary to an increase in patients perceived self-efficacy, a lower frequency of perceived daily hassles, a higher frequency of perceived daily uplifts, and a reduction in perceived symptoms of anxiety and depression. It was assumed that the posited changes in self-efficacy, and daily uplifts and hassles, would reduce these symptoms.

Material and methods

Participants were female inpatients in a psychiatric clinic in Norway. They were referred to a 12-week treatment programme provided by the clinic by specialist mental health services. We had no access to the psychiatric diagnoses, but according to the head of the clinic, approximately 95 percent of the patients had multiple trauma experiences and often a long history of mental illness. To be admitted, patients had to be over 18 years, without suicidal tendencies, not psychotic, with no substance abuse or self-harm behaviour, and showing no aggression towards others.

The clinic provided the usual treatment for traumatic care, consisting of individual and group psychotherapy, and pharmacological treatment. The individual therapy, given once a week, consisted of intensive short-term dynamic psychotherapy (Ecker et al., 2024) and eye movement desensitization reprocessing therapy (de Jongh et al., 2024). Individual psychodynamic, cognitive, and affect-focused therapy, given twice a week, were also performed. The therapy was provided by a psychologist and, in some cases, by specialist nurses. Group therapy, given once a week, was performed by two specialist nurses using psychodynamic (Messer & McWilliams, 2007), psychoeducational (Donker et al., 2009), and eclectic (Vasile, 2021) therapies.

Physical activity, cooking and eating healthy food, and aspects of social support were included in the treatment. Patients were encouraged to participate in at least three sessions per week of physical exercise, such as horse backriding, walking, resistance training and yoga. During the stay, presentations on psychoeducational topics were held (Donker et al., 2009) once a week, covering, e.g. the causes of anxiety symptoms, trauma, and emotions. In addition, patients were included in preparing and cooking the meals at the clinic to learn more about healthy nutrition. Communal eating and spending time in the social areas provided patients with social training. Some patients also had special social training projects in their treatment plan. Relatives were seen as a valuable resource for patients and the staff encouraged good relationships with them.

A person-centered approach was used. The conviction that the person herself held the key to better mental health and quality of life permeated the stay at the clinic, focusing on patients’ resources rather than their weaknesses.

Ethics

The project was reviewed and approved by the Norwegian Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK) (#20009). The participants were informed about the aim of the project, that participation was voluntary, and that they could withdraw their written consent at any time without giving a reason. They were also told that withdrawal would not affect their treatment.

Participants and procedure

This was a longitudinal intervention study. Respondents were recruited from January 2019 to January 2023. Patients were invited to participate in the study when they arrived at the psychiatric clinic. Respondents were fully informed about the aim of the study, and 137 women gave their consent to participate. No patients declining participation were reported. Each patient received a tablet upon arrival and could use a personal code to gain access to the questionnaires. Data were collected three times: at baseline (T0) (all instruments included) and during week 11 (end of treatment) (T1) (all instruments, but self-efficacy), and three months after leaving the clinic (T2) (all instruments included).

The number of respondents that participated at all three data collection points and answered all instruments was 48. In the following section, the demographic characteristics of the latter group are presented. The mean age was 43.7 years (SD = 10.7). Half of the respondents (50.0%) were living with someone, a majority (56.3%) had primary/secondary school as their highest educational level and almost one-third (29.3%) were employed.

Measures

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) is a self-report questionnaire measuring people’s beliefs about their capacity to cope with daily hassles and obstacles, as well as experiences of stressful life events, in a general adult population (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). The scale is well-established and has been translated into many languages. It is unidimensional and has achieved high reliability scores.

The questionnaire consists of ten statements, e.g. ‘I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough’ and ‘It is easy for me to stick to my aims and accomplish my goals’. The respondents are asked to indicate how well the statements describe them with four options, ranging from 1 = ‘not at all true’ to 4 = ‘exactly true’ (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was .90 at T0, and .89 at T2 (the GSES was not used at T2). The responses were added to give a total score ranging from 10 to 40.

The Stress Profile is a self-report psychosocial instrument (Setterlind & Larsson, 1995) including measures of stress in everyday life, so-called hassles and uplifts. The questionnaire originally contained 224 questions, but shorter versions have been developed through the years. Larsson et al. (2021) conducted a study where the questionnaire consisted of a total of 22 items, 13 measuring hassles and 9 measuring uplifts, finding that it yielded high reliability scores. In the present study, a selection of items from the Stress Profile was used in combination with newly developed items particularly relevant to the current respondent group, such as ‘the joy of being well’ and ‘concern for practical matters (e.g. finances, home, garden, car)’ with a total of 11 questions, four items concerning uplifts and seven measuring hassles. Cronbach’s alpha in the study for hassles was .70 at T0, .67 at T1, and .70 at T2, and for uplifts .72 at T0, .83 at T1, and .76 at T2. The respondents were asked to consider whether the statements had applied during the past month. There were five response options, ranging from 1 = ‘never’, to 5 = ‘very often’. The individual responses were converted to means ranging from 1 to 5, one for hassles and one for uplifts.

The Hopkins Symptoms Checklist (HSCL-10) is a self-report questionnaire measuring psychological distress widely used in clinical research and has been translated into many languages. Originally the scale consisted of 58 items measuring five underlying dimensions of symptoms: somatization, obsessive-compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, anxiety and depression. The HSCL-10 is a well-established shorter version of the scale that measures the presence of anxiety and depression and consists of ten items. The respondents are asked to react to statements about symptoms of anxiety and depression during the past week, e.g. ‘being scared for no reason’ and ‘worthless feeling’. All statements have four response options, ranging from 1 = ‘not at all’ to 4 = ‘extremely’ (Derogatis et al., 1974). The scores for each person were converted to a mean that could range from 1 to 4. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was .83 at T0, .90 at T1, and .88 at T2.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 29.0 was used to perform the analysis and p-values < .05 were considered statistically significant. The Shapiro-Wilks test and plots were used to assess the normality of the distribution. These indicated non-normality, and hence non-parametric tests were used.

However, as the residuals were considered normally distributed, which is a necessary assumption of a multiple regression analysis, and had a linear relationship with dependent variable (Suleiman et al., 2015), this enabled the use of a hierarchical multiple regression analysis. The instruments were included in the steps of the regression analysis in accordance with their assumed temporal stability. Age, considered to be most stable, was included in the first step, thereafter self-efficacy, and finally, hassles and uplifts, which were considered the most unstable of the included variables. The symptom score (HSCL-10) was used as dependent variable.

Due to the dropout rate, an intent to treat analysis were conducted, and it resulted in similar results.

Other statistical tests used were Spearman’s rho, a chi-square test, Friedman’s test, and Wilcoxon’s test.

Results

Dropout analysis

No statistically significant differences in demographic variables were found between those who participated at all three data collection points and those who did not. Further analysis revealed no significant differences between the group’s baseline scores on the symptoms scale (HSCL-10) (Mann–Whitney, dropouts median = 2.8, n = 51, non-dropouts median = 2.8, n = 59, z = −.042, p = .97).

The hierarchical regression revealed that age had no impact on symptoms of psychological distress at baseline or at three months after treatment. At baseline, self-efficacy was the only significant predictor in all models. The adjusted r2 in the baseline model reached .243, meaning that the model could explain 24 percent of the variance on the symptoms scale.

In week 24, three months following treatment, self-efficacy still had a significant effect on the dependent variable symptoms, now in addition to hassles and uplifts. The adjusted r2 reached .616, which implied that the model could explain almost 62 percent of the variance in the symptoms scale. However, hassles had the highest beta value (.510, compared to −.291 for uplifts and −.247 for self-efficacy), indicating the strongest associations with symptoms of psychological distress.

Comparison of changes within and between the scales over time

Differences between the data collection points were calculated and divided into three groups based on percentiles (33rd, 66th, and 100th percentile), e.g. the difference between data collection in week 24 compared to baseline (group 1, n = 12: highest improvement, group 2, n = 20: medium improvement: group 3, n = 16: lowest improvement). No comparison was made for data collection in week 11 (end of treatment) as self-efficacy was not measured at that point.

Comparisons between the groups were analysed in relation to demographic variables, as well as self-efficacy, uplifts and hassles. The results showed that group 1 (with the highest improvement between baseline and week 24 in symptoms of psychological distress) had higher levels of perceived uplifts than group 3 (the lowest improvement in symptoms of psychological distress between baseline and week 24), Mann–Whitney, group 1 (Median = 3.0, n = 12), group 3 (Median = 2.5, n = 18).

Comparisons with normative values

Finally, the present results will be compared to normative values of the questionnaires used. Beginning with self-efficacy, the mean scores (24.43 at T0 and 25.45 at T2) are lower than the mean value of 28.3 in a Norwegian study measuring self-efficacy in the general population (Bonsaksen et al., 2018). Turning to daily hassles, the mean scores (3.72 at T0 and 3.54 at T2) roughly correspond to the means reported by Stensvehagen et al. (2022) in a sample of women suffering from severe sexual assault. An identical similarity was found regarding daily uplifts. However, the present result differs considerably from results in two healthy groups. In a mixed-gender group of military veterans (n = 2512) (Larsson et al., 2024), the mean hassle score was 2.28 and the mean uplift score was 3.77. The veterans results resemble findings obtained in a group of female frontline supervisors and mid-level leaders (n = 861) (Setterlind & Larsson, 1995). Finally, for the HSCL-10, the means obtained in the present study (2.80 at T0 and 2.74 at T2) differ notably from the mean of 1.68 of women with depression in primary health care (Rodríguez-Barragán et al., 2023).

Discussion

The aim of the study was to explore the longitudinal effects of a 12-week comprehensive treatment programme on self-efficacy, hassles and uplifts, and symptoms of anxiety and depression in traumatized women in a clinical context. The treatment included physical exercise, nutrition, social training, and psychoeducation, in addition to traditional psychopharmacology and psychotherapy and lasted for twelve weeks. It was hypothesized that the treatment programme would covary with an increase in patients perceived self-efficacy, a higher frequency of perceived daily uplifts, a lower frequency of perceived daily hassles, and a reduction in perceived symptoms of anxiety and depression. Comparisons of the outcomes between the onset of treatment and three months after the end of treatment in the areas of self-efficacy, hassles, uplifts, and symptoms of psychological distress showed significant changes in the predicted directions on all instruments, except for uplifts where no change was found. This means that this part of the hypothesis was mostly confirmed. The hypothesis also stated that the presumed changes in self-efficacy, and daily uplifts and hassles, would be associated with a reduction of symptoms. The results show that this part of the hypothesis was confirmed. The results in this group of severely traumatized women are also in line with previous research showing that self-efficacy and perceptions of hassles and uplifts can be affected by psychotherapy, with an impact on the mental health of individuals with depression and anxiety (Clarke et al., 2014; Furman et al., 2024).

Self-efficacy was significantly associated with anxiety and depression symptoms in the regression analyses performed at baseline and three months after the end of the treatment, whereas hassles and uplifts only made significant contributions to the explained variance in the symptoms scores three months after treatment. These results indicate that the impact of hassles and uplifts on symptoms of mental illness increased through treatment. Wang (2022) argues that people with mental health problems tend to view themselves as helpless and regard hassles as a stable element in life that is impossible to influence. However, through an increase in self-efficacy and a belief in one’s ability to cope with different aspects of life, one’s view on daily hassles may change and a more positive approach towards everyday stressors might occur. One reason for this transformation might be the person-centered care received at the clinic, where the focus is on the patient’s resources rather than the shortcomings. This might help patients to realize their capacity to handle situations that arise, leading a decrease in feelings of stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984).

Significant correlations were revealed between self-efficacy, hassles and uplifts. This concurs with the finding of Andersson et al. (2014) that low self-efficacy increases the perception of hassles and decreases uplifts. Stensvehagen et al. (2022) showed that perceptions of hassles and uplifts had an influence on mental health improvements in severely traumatized women.

The present study indicates that the comprehensive treatment provided at the clinic is a way to enhance the recovery process of this group. However, because of the comprehensive approach of the treatment, it is difficult to ascertain the relative importance of the different treatment components, and the treatment experience may be heterogenous, because of choices related to treatment. It is also possible that there might be synergic interactions between the components.

The analysis of the demographic variables showed that most respondents had lower levels of education and were often unemployed. Previous research has shown similar findings on the association between mental illness and lower education, unemployment, and lower socio-economic conditions (Macintyre et al., 2018). The lack of information on the contextual situation of patients when they leave the clinic makes it difficult to make definitive statements about further long-term effects of the treatment. Returning home to one’s old habits might decrease the likelihood of maintaining lifestyle changes. Nevertheless, self-efficacy is crucial to lifestyle changes, and perceptions of daily hassles and uplifts have previously been shown to affect long-term mental health (Annesi & Powell, 2024; Lu, 1991; Maddux, 2013).

The presented comparisons with reported normative values are important in our view. Although these normative values are collected in different kinds of groups, they show that although the group in the present study changed favourably over time as predicted, they still reported scores that were markedly less favourable than the above comparators. Thus, although the noted improvement is a positive step and could signal the beginning of a more favourable mental health status, there is still a long way to go. Our interpretation is that a history of psychological trauma often leads to a long recovery path where continuous formal and informal support are needed (cf. Stensvehagen et al., 2022).

Methodological considerations

The current study has some limitations that may impact on the generalizability of the findings. One limitation is that the results rely on self-report questionnaires, which may result in over – or underestimation of the phenomena measured (Lira et al., 2022) or socially desirable responses (Reisinger, 2022). However, as the patients were aware that we were genuinely interested in their experiences of the treatment and their mental health, we believe that most of the respondents answered the questionnaires without notable bias.

There is also quite a large number of dropouts; many respondents did not complete the questionnaires on all occasions. The initial number of respondents was 137, while only 48 responded to all instruments at all three data collection points. This is a common problem in longitudinal studies (Caruana et al., 2015). Intent-to-treat analysis might be a way to handle dropouts. This analysis implies that all participants answering the first data collection are included in further analyses even though they, in some cases, do not complete the treatment (McCoy, 2017). However, this method demands two, or more, randomized groups, something that was not the case in current study. Although the assumptions were not reached, we conducted an analysis in accordance with the method, and it resulted in similar results.

However, a dropout analysis showed no differences at baseline in symptoms of psychological distress, and the longitudinal design is a strength, exploring changes over time.

Another potential weakness is the absence of a control group. To explore the effects of an intervention, an RCT with randomized groups is considered the gold standard (Zabor et al., 2020). This was unfortunately not possible to implement as the study was conducted in a clinic offering comprehensive treatment to all patients. An alternative would have been to include a control group receiving traditional treatment in another clinic, but this would have involved contextual differences, which would have made it difficult to compare the groups. It should also be noted that the comprehensive treatment programme included several parts, and we have no data related to the relative importance of each part. Another issue is the obtained significant changes in the study. It cannot be ruled out that these changes are due to other explanations, such as for example regression to the mean (see e.g. Barnett et al. (2005)).

The longitudinal design is a strength, and the instruments included are well established. Cronbach alphas´ in this study are considered to be high, which indicates good reliability.
