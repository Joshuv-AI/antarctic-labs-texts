The impact of social and environmental factors on triggering multiple sclerosis onset, before and during the COVID-19 pandemic: a retrospective study from Iran
Authors: Naghmeh Abbasi Kasbi, Fereshteh Ghadiri, Abdorreza Naser Moghadasi, Faezeh Khodaie, Kosar Kohandel, Nasim Rezaeimanesh, Maryam Karaminia, Mohammad Ali Sahraian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, inflammatory, and demyelinating disease of the central nervous system. The first presentation’s possible triggers are still controversial among scientists. The objective of this study is to investigate and compare the potential social, environmental, and physical factors that may have contributed to the onset of MS before and during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.MethodsA questionnaire was designed in the MS research center of Sina Hospital and also distributed as an online Google Form on social media among Iranian MS patients. Demographic information, MS disease-related data, and possible patients reported MS triggers were recorded. They were containing stressful life events, COVID-19 and other infections, COVID-19 and other vaccines, pregnancy or labor, head trauma, surgery, and weight loss. Patients were divided into two groups regarding the time of MS diagnosis (before and during the COVID-19 pandemic).ResultsOf 920 participants, 670 (72.8%) were female, and the mean age ± SD was 35.63 ± 8.1. The majority of patients (69.2%) had non-progressive forms of MS, and only 7.6% needed assistance for ambulation. 69% of participants were diagnosed with MS before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. There was a statistically significant difference between the most common first MS symptom before and after the beginning of the pandemic (visual type (n: 317 (49.9%)) before and sensory type (n: 170 (59.6%)) after the COVID-19 pandemic). A stressful life event was the most common patient-reported trigger of MS first presentation in both groups. (56.1% before and 54% after the COVID-19 pandemic). Comparing two groups, economic problems (AOR: 1.81; 95% ACI: 1.23–2.65) and job losses (AOR: 2.89; 95% ACI: 1.37–6.08) were significantly more common triggers for the initial presentation of MS after the pandemic, while the stress of occupational or educational exams (AOR: 0.52; 95% ACI: 0.34–0.79) was more prevalent before the pandemic.ConclusionPatients believe that stressful life events are closely linked to triggering their first MS symptoms. Since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, economic problems and job losses have increased; however, occupational or educational exams stress decreased. Caring for social stress by societies may affect MS development or delay MS onset.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-024-03956-x.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Incidence, Trigger, COVID-19

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory and demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS), which may lead to disability in young individuals [1]. The etiology of MS has still remained unclear; however, the incidence or development of MS is related to both environmental and genetic factors, including age, gender, geographic region, positive family history, race, and exposure to viruses [2]. Meanwhile, other factors may trigger the first MS episode and its exacerbation, such as stressful events, viral infections like Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), vaccines, head trauma and concussion, anesthesia, extreme physical activity, and labor [3–6].

Stress may have a crucial influence on the onset of MS and its clinical course [7]. Certain individual life events, such as divorce and periods of homelessness, are considered potential risk factors for MS [8]. Some patients conveyed that their family problems affected the disease’s onset or recurrence [9].

The previous investigations indicate that infection is another factor that can affect MS onset, as diverse viruses may trigger inflammatory demyelinating diseases like MS. For instance, it has been conveyed that Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection at a specific age plays a major role in the development of the disease [10].

An increased MS onset with the incidence of influenza virus in the same year highlights the possible role of infections in triggering MS first presentation, as well as how direct toxicity mediated by human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs) or abnormal immune activation by their components can subsequently induce MS [11, 12]. The development of relapsing-remitting MS following the long-term reactivation of the Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV) emphasizes the role of viruses in disease onset [13].

Meanwhile, during the recent Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, it is mentioned that the infection caused by COVID-19 can trigger demyelination and autoimmune diseases such as MS [14]. Even though the MS flare-up in milder COVID-19 cases may only last temporarily, there is still a significant chance that the illness will worsen or return [15].

In actuality, nothing was known about the immunological status of people with MS, how they would respond to Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, and which risk factors, in contrast to the general population, might result in a catastrophic COVID-19 outcome or death [16].

A demyelinating disease of CNS (probable MS) shortly after the onset of COVID-19 infection and the fact that none of the plaques were active on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could suggest that the lesions were old, and SARS-CoV-2 infection acts as a predisposing factor for clinical symptoms [17].

Moreover, cases of CNS demyelination, mostly transverse myelitis, and MS-like MRIs (first diagnosis or relapse) following a variety of approved COVID-19 vaccines have been reported so far [18]. The role of the COVID-19 pandemic in triggering MS incidence has received increased attention in recent years.

To the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of data on the identification and prevalence of MS first presentation triggers during the COVID-19 pandemic. In the present study, we intend to investigate the prevalence of various presenting symptoms that led to the diagnosis of MS and the factors that trigger these symptoms based on MS patients’ reports. In addition, we compare these factors based on the MS diagnosis date (before and during the COVID-19 pandemic).

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study examined patient-reported social, environmental, and physical triggers of MS onset before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. between February 2022 and May 2022.

Participants

The population of our study was all Iranian MS patients who had met the revised 2017 McDonald Criteria [19], by MS experts. They participated in the social media network for Iranian MS patients, associated with our MS Research Center at Tehran Medical University, where only individuals with MS are involved.

Data collection

A multiple-choice questionnaire was designed and approved by two MS experts in the MS Research Center of Tehran University of Medical Sciences. It was piloted on five MS patients. The final questionnaire was distributed on social media that mentioned, and MS patients were asked to complete the online electronic Google form of the questionnaire. All responses were based on self-reported.

Variables

Demographic indexes counting: gender, age, educational level, employment status, marital status, and also MS disease-related data including MS type, first MS symptoms, current disease-modifying treatment (DMT), disability level, and MS diagnosis time (before and during the COVID-19 pandemic). In the following, other information like body mass index (BMI), COVID-19 infection and vaccination history, using a substance and/or alcoholic drinks, smoking, using vitamin D before MS diagnosis, and possible patient-reported MS triggers before the first presentation were analyzed. Patient-reported MS triggers led to the MS diagnosis by a physician, encompassing surgery, head trauma, pregnancy or labor, COVID-19 or other infections, COVID-19 or other vaccinations, weight loss, and 15 stressful life events. The details of 15 stressful life events were divorce, marriage, immigration, stress of the occupational or educational exams, job losses, economic problems, stress of suffering from other diseases, family disputes, close ‘family members’ serious disease, death of relatives with COVID-19 infection or other reasons, working pressure, military service, severe anxiety, and depression. Participants with incomplete questionnaires (without MS diagnosis time and more than 30% unanswered questions or duplicates (by both names and ages) were excluded.

Fingolimod, natalizumab, rituximab, ocrelizumab, cladribine, and mitoxantrone were considered high-efficiency drugs.

The disability level was estimated by a question about the need for ambulation (unassisted ambulation or assisted ambulation).

Predisposing factors, which are patients’ beliefs about what makes them susceptible to disease, may have developed long before the onset of symptoms. These factors were assessed by asking patients to complete a questionnaire.

These factors were patient-reported and subjective, with no certain diagnosis by experts in hand.

The first MS symptom was defined in the questionnaire as the first self-reported symptom that led a visit a physician and MS diagnosis.

Some patients’ habits before MS diagnosis, such as using alcoholic drinks, cigarettes, substances, and vitamin D, were recorded.Since then, various investigations have identified several significant risk factors for the incidence or progression of MS, including vitamin D deficiency, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and substance abuse. Additionally, certain studies have categorized these risk factors as habitual. Consequently, we have decided to evaluate these aforementioned factors as habitual risk factors for MS [20–25].

Patient-reported triggers are defined as the factors that are believed (by the patient) to provoke the first presentation of MS, which led to a visit a physician and MS diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and proportion for quantitative and qualitative variables, respectively. The logistic regression method was used to specify the possible association between the first symptoms that led to MS diagnosis and MS diagnosis time (before and during the COVID-19 pandemic). The binary logistic regression method was also used to determine the possible association between patient-reported triggers and MS diagnosis time (before and during the COVID-19 pandemic), adjusting for possible confounders using SPSS, version 26. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

We informed all the participants about the aim of the questionnaire at the time of registration in their native language (Farsi). Participation was optional and anonymous. The patients were insured that all information obtained during the survey would be confidential. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1399.728). Furthermore, all the steps taken in this study adhere to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

In this current study, a stressful life event was reported to be the most probable trigger for MS onset among the patients before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, our study revealed that pregnancy and weight loss were ranked as the second and third most prevalent triggers for the onset of multiple sclerosis prior to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, COVID-19 infection and vaccination were reported as the second and third most common triggers subsequent to the COVID-19 pandemic. This also accords with the previous findings, which showed that stress is one of the most common stimulators for developing MS, followed by infections and pregnancy or the postpartum period, respectively [26].

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, occupational or educational exams were the most common sources of stress for a significant number of individuals with MS (20%). However, among individuals diagnosed with MS during the COVID-19 pandemic, economic problems emerged as the predominant stressor (23.9%). The notable rise in job losses and economic challenges reported as triggers for MS onset during the COVID-19 pandemic should be viewed as social consequences, which can potentially lead to physical implications. It has previously been mentioned that the COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in a reduced workforce and widespread unemployment [27]. According to the findings of the present study, a previous investigation has identified significant differences between individuals with MS and the general population in terms of experiencing negative emotions and symptoms such as depression, obsession, anxiety, phobia, difficulties in relationships, and somatization disorder. These findings indicate the substantial influence of psychosocial factors on the onset of MS [28].

The patients in our study reported their possible predisposing factors for MS with a high range of persistent stress, followed by depression, family disruption, irritability, lack of vitamin D, death of close ones, genetics, familial history of MS, and diet, respectively. Most of the patients (39.2%) in our study had not used vitamin D supplements before the MS diagnosis. In some Iranian patients’ perspective on traditional medicine, diet is considered a potentially influential factor in relation to the development of MS. Notably, there is a mention of an increased risk of MS among individuals who frequently consume foods characterized as having a cold nature [29].

Among the patients diagnosed with MS during the COVID-19 pandemic, a total of 8.8% reported COVID-19 infection as a contributing factor to the onset of their MS. Furthermore, the majority of these patients (56%) experienced a time interval of less than a month between the occurrence of COVID-19 infection and their initial presentation of MS symptoms. These findings align with numerous case reports highlighting the association between COVID-19 infection and the triggering of MS occurrences [30–33].

Although no evidence has indicated a correlation between vaccinations against certain viruses and the risk of developing MS until 2021 [34], similar to our investigation, it has been reported that among cases of CNS demyelination following the COVID-19 vaccination, MS diagnosis or relapse is among the most frequently reported presentations [18]. Our study showed that the number of patients attributing MS onset to COVID-19 vaccination is relatively low and significantly less compared to those associating it with COVID-19 infection. It is important to consider and emphasize this information when addressing concerns about potential side effects of COVID-19 vaccines among patients. Interestingly, our study also indicates an increase in the number of Iranian MS patients who have received COVID-19 vaccinations, as compared to the previous study [35].

A majority of the patients (96.4%) reported no substance use prior to the MS diagnosis. However, it is important to note that this finding may be influenced by the subjective nature of the survey, introducing potential bias.

Among patients diagnosed with MS prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, visual symptoms were the most frequently reported initial presentations (49.9%), consistent with previous studies [36]. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, sensory symptoms became the most prevalent initial presentation (59.6%). The significant association between more sensory and pain types of MS first presentation and the MS diagnosis during the COVID-19 pandemic could be defensible with COVID-19 sensory symptoms [37], which led to more following-up, referring to the neurologists and disease diagnosis.

One of the limitations of our study is its reliance on self-reported questionnaires, which can introduce bias. Despite providing clear definitions for all questions, some respondents may not provide accurate answers due to factors such as social desirability or recall bias.

Another potential limitation to consider is the possibility that patients diagnosed with MS prior to the COVID-19 pandemic may show decreased interest in participating in a web-based survey compared to those newly diagnosed. This could be influenced by the longer duration of their disease, potentially resulting in a lower participation rate in research studies. It is noteworthy that nearly one-third of the participants in this study were diagnosed during the COVID-19 pandemic (since 2020). This finding could be explained by the fact that patients who have been diagnosed recently are more concerned and willing to participate in different research projects on their diseases. The study suggests that a combination of factors, including environmental and lifestyle elements, may play a role in MS development and progression. By understanding these potential triggers, healthcare professionals can offer more comprehensive care and support to individuals with MS.

One of the strengths of this study lies in its ability to capture additional probable triggers or predisposing factors as reported by the patients themselves. So, all participants had the opportunity to express their own beliefs clearly. Recognizing these aspects could be valuable and the subject of future follow-up studies investigating MS triggers.

This study aimed to evaluate the potential factors that contribute to the onset of MS in the context of the recent COVID-19 pandemic. While there is typically a strong emphasis on disease-specific aspects, the influence of social and economic factors resulting from pandemics on the development of other diseases often goes unnoticed. It is essential to recognize and consider these additional stressors, as they can significantly impact the triggering or exacerbation of various diseases in the future. Governments should enhance their preparedness for unforeseen pandemics to reduce the economic consequences. By implementing practical measures, countries can effectively minimize the impact of future pandemics on their economies.

Conclusion

The study identified stressful life events as a most frequent patients reported MS trigger both before and after the pandemic. The second and third triggers was recognized pregnancy and weight loss before pandemic and COVID-19 infection, and vaccination after pandemic. Job loss and economic problems were more prevalent after COVID-19 pandemic; however, Occupational or educational exams stress had significantly lower prevalence before COVID-19 pandemic. Healthcare providers should advise patients about stress management, and public health initiatives should promote it. Recognizing these factors could help prevent the development and exacerbation of MS.
