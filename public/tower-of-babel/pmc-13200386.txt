Marine-derived oral nanovesicles from Sargassum fusiforme ameliorate steatohepatitis by activating the HO-1 pathway to inhibit NF-κB signaling and restore small intestinal homeostasis
Authors: Guoen Li, Zhuoyan He, Jinhui Zhu, Shuqi Si, Lin Liu, Yulong Sun, Ziming Jiao, Ganglin Wang, Shuaimin Lu, Tingming Fu, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarine algae, notably the edible brown seaweed Sargassum fusiforme (Hijiki), harbor a vast repertoire of bioactive molecules with potent anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory properties. However, their clinical translation is severely hampered by poor systemic bioavailability and susceptibility to gastrointestinal degradation. To circumvent these bottlenecks, we engineered Sargassum fusiforme-derived nanovesicles (SF-NVs) as a robust, intrinsic oral delivery platform. These nanovesicles effectively encapsulate and protect bioactive cargos, significantly augmenting their stability and therapeutic efficacy within the hostile gut environment.ResultsIn this study, bioactive nanovesicles were isolated from the edible brown alga Sargassum fusiforme using two complementary methodologies: sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation for high-purity characterization and hollow fiber membrane concentration for scalable, clinically translatable production. The resulting Sargassum fusiforme-derived nanovesicles (SF-NVs) exhibited a spherical morphology (mean diameter:120.8 ± 5.0 nm diameter) and a stable zeta potential (-45.2 ± 1.3 mV). Lipidomic profiling revealed a signature dominated by Hex1Cer (31%), notably distinct from terrestrial plant vesicles by its RNA-free composition. Mechanistically, in vitro assays demonstrated that SF-NVs were internalized by macrophages, where they triggered the upregulation of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). This activation served as a critical checkpoint, subsequently blocking the NF-κB signaling cascade by suppressing IKKα/IκBα phosphorylation and p65 nuclear translocation, thereby attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Elemol was identified as a key bioactive constituent contributing to this anti-inflammatory activity. In HFD-induced MASH models, oral administration of hollow fiber membrane concentrate (HFMC) successfully ameliorated steatosis, liver injury, and systemic inflammation. Crucially, the therapeutic efficacy was driven by the restoration of small intestinal homeostasis; HFMC suppressed HFD-induced chronic intestinal inflammation and enhanced mucosal barrier integrity. By mitigating the inflammatory surge at the enteric level and modulating luminal lipid levels, SF-NVs reprogrammed hepatic lipid metabolism—suppressing lipogenesis while promoting β-oxidation and lipid export. These findings suggest that SF-NVs act through the HO-1-NF-κB axis to rectify gut-liver axis dysregulation, offering a potent marine-derived strategy for MASH treatment.ConclusionThis study provides the first evidence that SF-NVs can effectively treat MASH by orchestrating the HO-1-mediated anti-inflammatory response and restoring small intestinal homeostasis. Introduction

Chronic metabolic inflammation underpins the pathogenesis of diet-induced disorders—including obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD)—through persistent activation of innate immune pathways in metabolic tissues [1–3]. Central to this process is the NF-κB signaling cascade, a master regulator that amplifies pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), driving insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis [4]. While pharmacological NF-κB inhibitors show preclinical efficacy, their clinical utility is hampered by systemic toxicity, low oral bioavailability, and off-target effects [5–7]. This impasse necessitates the development of orally deliverable, food-derived anti-inflammatory agents that combine bioactivity with inherent biocompatibility.

Marine algae, particularly Sargassum fusiforme (hijiki), represent an untapped reservoir of bioactive compounds with validated metabolic benefits. For centuries consumed in East Asia as both food and medicine, this brown seaweed exemplifies the "medicinal food" paradigm, rich in anti-inflammatory polysaccharides (e.g., fucoidan), terpenoids (e.g., fucoxanthin), and polyphenols [8–11]. Critically, its historical use confers an exceptional safety profile, positioning it as an ideal candidate for chronic interventions. However, the therapeutic potential of Sargassum fusiforme extracts is limited by poor bioavailability of macromolecules and digestive degradation of labile compounds, restricting systemic delivery of active constituents.

Recent advances in plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNVs) offer a transformative solution. These naturally encapsulated lipid carriers protect bioactive cargoes from gastrointestinal degradation while enhancing their transepithelial transport and cellular internalization [12–15]. Unlike synthetic nanoparticles requiring complex surface engineering, PELNVs inherently conform to food-grade standards, serving as green nanoplatforms for oral nutraceuticals. Building on this, we hypothesized that Sargassum fusiforme-derived nanovesicles (SF-NVs) could synergistically combine the seaweed’s intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties with superior delivery capabilities of natural vesicles.

Here, we pioneer the isolation and functional characterization of SF-NVs (patent: ZL202310302272.6), establishing them as a first-in-class marine-derived nanoplatform for metabolic intervention. We demonstrate that SF-NVs trigger the activation of the HO-1 pathway, which serves as a master switch to block NF-κB-mediated inflammation. Beyond systemic anti-inflammatory effects, oral SF-NVs specifically rectify small intestinal metabolic homeostasis by suppressing HFD-induced enteric inflammation and reinforcing barrier integrity, thereby alleviating hepatic steatosis and injury through the gut-liver axis. Characterized by exceptional gastrointestinal stability, targeted intestinal delivery, and a robust safety profile, SF-NVs fulfill the rigorous requirements for both functional foods and next-generation nanotherapeutics. By bridging marine bioresource utilization with advanced drug delivery, this work establishes a sustainable and transformative paradigm for treating diet-induced metabolic disorders.

Materials and methods

Isolation of nanovesicles from Sargassum fusiforme

To isolate nanovesicles from Sargassum fusiforme, we employed two distinct methods: ultracentrifugation and hollow fiber membrane concentration. Briefly, fresh Sargassum fusiforme was weighed and homogenized with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a 1:5 (g/v) ratio using a high-speed grinder. The homogenate was centrifuged sequentially at 1,000 g for 10 min, 3,000 g for 20 min, and 10,000 g for 40 min, with the supernatant collected after each step while discarding the pellet. The final supernatant was subjected to either ultracentrifugation or hollow fiber membrane concentration.

For ultracentrifugation-based isolation, the supernatant was ultracentrifuged using an Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA) at a force of 150,000 g for 90 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS, layered onto a sucrose gradient (60%, 45%, 30%, and 8%), and ultracentrifuged again at 150,000 g for 1.5 h at 4 °C. Nanoparticles from the 8%–30% sucrose interface were extracted, purified by additional ultracentrifugation (150,000 g, 1.5 h, 4 °C) to remove sucrose residuals, and finally resuspended in PBS for storage at − 80 °C.

For hollow fiber membrane concentration, the final supernatant was passed through a 200-nm hollow fiber column (D-020-M020-05-P, Duoning Biotech, Shanghai, China) to enrich nanovesicles. The resulting concentrate and filtrate were designated as Sargassum fusiforme hollow fiber membrane concentrate (HFMC) and hollow fiber filtrate (HF-Fil), respectively. The HFMC can be further purified via density gradient ultracentrifugation to obtain purified nanoparticles (HF-SF-NVs) for structural and functional comparison with SF-NVs.

Comprehensive physical characterization of SF-NVs, HFMC, HF-Fil and HF-SF-NVs

To analyze the morphology of nanovesicles, 10 μL of suspension was deposited onto a copper grid, stained with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid hydrate for 1 min at room temperature, and air-dried overnight. Morphological characterization was then performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-F200, Japan). For quantitative analysis, the concentration and size distribution of nanovesicles were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) on a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Additionally, hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured via dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Lipidomic analysis

Lipidomic analysis of SF-NVs was performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China) using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Briefly, lipids were extracted from the sample with methanol, followed by solvent evaporation under a nitrogen stream. The dried lipid extract was reconstituted in 90% (v/v) isopropanol/acetonitrile and subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis for comprehensive lipid profiling. Lipid identification and data analysis were conducted utilizing the LipidSearch software.

Biomacromolecular profiling analysis

Protein analysis was initiated by extracting total proteins from SF-NVs using ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The extracted proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE (12% gel), and target protein bands were excised for in-gel tryptic digestion. The resulting peptides were subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on Easy-nLC1200/Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for protein identification. MS raw data were analyzed against the UniProt database using PEAKS Studio to characterize the proteomic profile.

RNA analysis was performed by isolating total RNA from SF-NVs using TRIzol reagent, followed by DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RNA concentration was quantified using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the Qubit™ RNA HS Assay Kit, and Agilent 5300 Fragment Analyzer. RNA band was evaluated by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the respective manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells and Kupffer cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Both cell types were routinely maintained in their respective media: RAW 264.7 cells in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, Grand Island, USA), and Kupffer cells in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Genimi, Woodland, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Pen-Strep). Cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For quantitative PCR analysis and Western blotting, cells were treated under various conditions. Specifically, they were incubated with either nanovesicles (1.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL) or elemol (different concentrations) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to LPS (1 μg/mL) for an additional 6 h.

Relative cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. After treatment, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1–4 h. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 600–650 nm. Relative viability (%) was calculated as: (ODtreated-ODblank)/ODcontrol-ODblank) × 100.

Endocytosis of SF-NVs

Internalization of SF-NVs by macrophages was assessed using PKH26-labeled SF-NVs. Kupffer cells and RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were incubated with PKH26-labeled SF-NVs (final concentration: 1.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL) for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. After incubation, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-internalized SF-NVs. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and then counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/mL) for 10 min. Laser confocal microscopy (excitation: 551 nm, emission: 567 nm; scale bar: 20 μm) was employed to visualize and quantify PKH26 fluorescence, with Z-stack imaging to confirm intracellular localization.

Intracellular ROS assay

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H₂DCFDA). Cells were pre-treated with or without SF-NVs (1.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL) for 12 h, washed twice with PBS, and then exposed to fresh DMEM containing H₂DCFDA (5 μg/mL) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After LPS stimulation (concentration specified per experimental design), cells were further incubated for 1 h (quantification) or 4 h (visualization). DCF fluorescence intensity was quantified using a microplate reader (excitation: 490 nm, emission: 525 nm). For subcellular localization, laser confocal microscopy (excitation: 488 nm, emission: 525 nm; scale bar: 20 μm) was performed, with DAPI counterstaining to demarcate nuclei. All imaging and quantification steps were conducted under light-protected conditions to prevent dye photobleaching.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells and tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), with rigorous RNase-free conditions maintained throughout the procedure to prevent degradation. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol for template input normalization. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Green I dye (Invitrogen) and gene-specific primers. The housekeeping gene GAPDH was co-amplified as an internal reference to normalize expression levels of target genes. Relative quantification of gene expression was calculated via the 2^(− ΔΔCt) method, comparing threshold cycle (Ct) values between experimental and control groups. All primer sequences used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). Sequencing and subsequent bioinformatic analysis were performed by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai). Briefly, after quality control and mRNA enrichment, cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced. The raw reads were then aligned, assembled, and normalized. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted across groups, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05 and |log₂FC|≥ 1. The functional enrichment analysis included Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses, which are displayed as bubble charts. Some of the DEGs involved in the inflammatory response and antioxidant are presented as heatmaps.

siRNA transfection

HO-1 siRNA (siHO-1) and negative control siRNA (siNC) were designed and synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 cells were transfected with siHO-1 or siNC using lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were treated with either SF-NVs or elemol for a further 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were stimulated with LPS for 6 h.

Cellular proteomics and bioinformatics analysis

To investigate SF-NV-induced proteomic alterations in RAW264.7 macrophages, cells were treated with or without SF-NVs (1.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL) for 24 h, followed by LPS stimulation for 6 h to model inflammatory responses. Proteins were extracted under denaturing conditions, reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (55 °C, 40 min), alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide (dark, 20 min) to prevent disulfide bond reformation, and precipitated with ice-cold acetone. After trypsin digestion (1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio, 37 °C overnight), peptides were desalted and analyzed via LC–MS/MS on a Thermo EASY-nLC 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (MS1: 120,000 resolution; MS2: HCD fragmentation, 15,000 resolution). Raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 against the Mus musculus UniProt database (release 2025.01), with differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) defined by |fold change|> 1.5 and p < 0.05 (Student’s t-test).

Bioinformatic validation included: (1) Volcano plots visualizing log₂ (fold change) versus − log₁₀ (p-value) to identify upregulated and downregulated DEPs; (2) Heatmaps revealing treatment-specific expression patterns; (3) GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment to annotate biological functions.

Quasi-targeted metabolomics analysis

The quasi-targeted metabolomics analysis of SF-NVs was conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) to characterize their metabolic composition. Briefly, 1 mL of SF-NVs was lyophilized and reconstituted in pre-chilled 80% methanol, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation at 15,000 g (4 °C, 15 min), a portion of the supernatant was diluted to 53% methanol using LC–MS grade water. The diluted samples were recentrifuged under identical conditions (15,000 g, 4 °C, 15 min), and the final supernatant was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolite profiling was conducted using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer based on the NovoDB (Novogene Database) established by Novogene. Raw data files generated by HPLC–MS/MS were processed via SCIEX OS (Version 1.4) for peak integration and correction, where the peak area of each metabolite reflected its relative abundance within SF-NVs.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cultured cells, liver, and small intestine tissues with ice-cold RIPA buffer. Following extraction, the proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting MyD88 (1:2000, Huabio, Hangzhou, China), phosphorylated IKKα (p-IKKα; 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), IKKα (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα;1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), IκBα (1:1000, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Fatty Acid Synthase, (FAS, 1:1000, Cell Signaling), PGC1 alpha/beta (CY6630, 1:1000, Abways, Shanghai, China,), MTTP (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, USA), CPT1A(1:1000, Huabio), ZO-1(1:5000, Proteintech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), Occludin (1:5000, Proteintech) β-actin (1:1000, Proteintech) and β-tubulin (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, and protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent substrates.

Immunofluorescence

NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation was assessed via immunofluorescence. RAW264.7 cells were treated with or without SF-NVs (1.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL) for 24 h, stimulated with LPS for 6 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight with an anti-NF-κB p65 primary antibody, followed by a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, 10 min), and samples were mounted with anti-fade medium. Subcellular localization of p65 was analyzed using laser confocal microscopy to quantify nuclear translocation intensity.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.2) to evaluate the binding affinity and interaction modes between the candidate drug and its target protein, following an established protocol [16]. Briefly, the three-dimensional structure of HO-1 was predicted with AlphaFold (v2.3.2). The model was then prepared using PyMOL, which involved removing water molecules and non-essential ligands, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning Kollman charges. After energy minimization with AutoDock Tools, the protein structure was saved in PDBQT format. The ligand structure of elemol (Compound CID: 92138) was obtained from the PubChem database. Docking simulations were conducted with a grid box centered on the protein’s binding site. The conformation with the highest predicted binding affinity (lowest binding energy) was selected as the optimal pose for subsequent analysis, and protein–ligand interactions were visualized using PyMOL.

Assessment of nutritional composition of HFMC

HFMC underwent comprehensive nutrient profiling through standardized analytical methods: Protein content was quantified via the Kjeldahl method, involving acid digestion, distillation, and titration of nitrogen to calculate crude protein (N × 6.25); total fat was determined by Soxhlet extraction using petroleum ether, followed by gravimetric measurement after solvent evaporation; carbohydrates were calculated by difference: 100% – (moisture + ash + protein + fat), with moisture measured by oven drying (105 °C) and ash by incineration (550 °C); total polysaccharides were quantified using the phenol–sulfuric acid method, where hydrolyzed samples reacted with phenol for spectrophotometric detection at 490 nm; alginate and fucoxanthin were quantified via liquid chromatography.

Biodistribution of nanovesicles in vivo

DiR red fluorescent dye (1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide; Invitrogen) was used to label nanovesicles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mice were administered DiR-labeled nanovesicles either via intraperitoneal injection or orally. At designated time points post-administration, the mice were euthanized, and tissue samples (including blood, liver, stomach, spleen, intestine, heart, lung, and kidney) were harvested to track nanovesicle distribution. The DiR fluorescent signals in all collected samples were quantified using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) to compare organ-specific accumulation kinetics between the two delivery routes.

Animal study

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. For the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute inflammation model, mice were randomized into four groups: control+PBS (PBS injection, n = 5), control + SF-NVs (SF-NVs only, 9 × 101⁰ particles, n = 5), LPS (15 mg/kg in 200 μL PBS, i.p., n = 5), and LPS + SF-NVs (SF-NVs pre-treatment 30 min prior to LPS, n = 5). After 6 h of LPS challenge, blood and tissues (spleen) were harvested for analysis. Serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) were quantified via ELISA, and monocyte cytokine mRNA expression (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in spleen were analyzed by RT-qPCR.

In the high-fat diet (HFD)-induced MASH model, mice were divided into: normal chow diet (NCD, 10% kcal fat, n = 8), NCD + HFMC (oral HFMC, 2.55 × 1011 particles, n = 8), HFD (60% kcal fat, 16 weeks, n = 8), and HFD + HFMC (oral HFMC every other day beginning at week 7, n = 8). Post-study, mice were euthanized under anesthesia, with blood and tissues collected for endpoint analyses. Metabolic assessments included fasting blood glucose and glucose tolerance tests (GTT; 2 g/kg glucose i.p., measured at 0/30/60/90/120 min), with serum cytokines (IL-6), lipid markers (TG, TC), and hepatotoxicity indicators (ALT, AST) analyzed using commercial kits; parallel TC/TG measurements and cytokine mRNA profiling were performed in liver and small intestine tissues.

All procedures complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Global 3R Initiative, approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (xmsq2024-0203).

Histopathological analysis

For histopathological evaluation, tissue samples were processed for H&E staining, Oil Red O staining, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) as described [17, 18]. Specifically, for H&E staining, tissue samples were fixed overnight in 4% formalin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut, dewaxed, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. For Oil Red O staining, frozen sections were fixed in 10% formalin, treated with propylene glycol, and stained with Oil Red O solution at 60 °C for 6 min. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin and examined under a light microscope. For IHC analysis of IL-6, TNF-α and CD68, paraffin-embedded tissue samples were dewaxed and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed, followed by blocking. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL-6, CD68 and TNF-α, respectively. After washing, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Antigen visualization was achieved using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. The DAB reaction was terminated by rinsing with tap water as soon as a light yellow color developed. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin for microscopic evaluation.

Intestinal proteomics and bioinformatics analysis

Proteomic analysis of small intestinal tissues was performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Briefly, tissue proteins were extracted with 8 M urea buffer containing protease inhibitors. After centrifugation, protein concentration was determined, and 100 µg aliquots were subjected to reduction, alkylation, and overnight digestion with trypsin. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 StageTips. For mass spectrometry, peptides were separated on a reversed-phase column with an 80-min acetonitrile gradient. A spectral library was first generated from fractionated samples analyzed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode on a Q Exactive HF-X instrument. Subsequently, all individual samples were analyzed in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. The DIA data were searched against the UniProt Mus musculus database (release 2025–01-26) using Spectronaut software (v15.7) with a 1% false discovery rate threshold. Functional annotation of identified and differentially expressed proteins was conducted through GO and KEGG pathway analyses.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test (two-tailed, assuming equal variance) was applied to assess significant differences between experimental samples and their matched controls across pairwise comparisons. For multi-group analyses involving three or more independent cohorts, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc adjustment was employed to control Type I error inflation while evaluating inter-group differences. Statistical significance was uniformly defined as P < 0.05, with all tests conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2.

Results and discussion

Ultracentrifugation-purified SF-NVs: hexosylceramide-rich lipidomic profiling with RNA-free cargo

Nanovesicles derived from Sargassum fusiforme (SF-NVs) were isolated using a sequential procedure involving grinding, differential centrifugation, ultracentrifugation, and subsequent sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation with stepwise concentrations of 60%, 45%, 30%, and 8%. This gradient separation yielded three distinct bands, among which the top band was collected and further purified by an additional ultracentrifugation step (Fig. 1A). Characterization revealed that SF-NVs exhibited a spherical morphology under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) yielded a mean diameter of 120.8 ± 5.0 nm and a concentration of 1.5 × 1012 particles/mL (obtained from 50 g fresh Sargassum fusiforme), while surface charge analysis indicated a zeta potential of − 45.2 ± 1.3 mV (Fig. 1C, 1D); The inter-batch coefficients of variation (CV) for particle size and zeta potential were 4.1% and 2.93%, respectively (Fig. 1C, 1D), demonstrating excellent batch-to-batch consistency and the high reproducibility of the SF-NVs preparation process. In preliminary experiments, vesicles from the middle and bottom bands were also collected and purified via ultracentrifugation. NTA characterization revealed particle sizes of 172.6 nm and 191.2 nm, with concentrations of 1.1 × 1012 and 7.5 × 1011 particles/mL, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A). However, as the top-band vesicles (SF-NVs) exhibited the smallest size, the highest yield, and superior anti-inflammatory activity (Supplementary Fig S1B) compared to the other fractions, they were selected for all subsequent experiments.

Isolation and characterization of SF-NVs. (A) Schematic of SF-NVs isolation via sequential grinding, differential centrifugation, ultracentrifugation (UC), and sucrose gradient purification. (B) Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image confirming the spherical morphology of SF-NVs (scale bar, 200 nm). (C) Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) quantified the concentration (1.5 × 10¹² particles/mL) and hydrodynamic diameter (mean: 120.8 ± 5.0 nm) of SF-NVs across three different batches, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 4.1%. (D) Zeta potential analysis demonstrated high colloidal stability (-45.2 ± 1.3 mV) attributed to electrostatic repulsion, with a CV of 2.93% across the three batches. (E) Lipidomic profiling via LC-MS/MS identifying 38 lipid classes, dominated by Hex1Cer (31%). (F) Coomassie brilliant blue staining revealing predominant low-MW (~10 kDa) proteins. (G) Agarose gel electrophoresis confirming the absence of detectable RNA.

Lipidomic profiling of SF-NVs via LC − MS/MS identified 38 lipid classes, dominated by Hex1Cer (Hexosylceramide, 31%), phosphatidylinositol (PI, 17%), diglycerides (DG, 12%), monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG, 10%) and sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG, 9%) (Fig. 1E). The exceptionally high Hex1Cer proportion distinguishes SF-NVs from most plant-derived nanovesicles, aligning closely only with the lipid profiles reported for ginseng and aloe-derived nanovesicles [19]. Protein analysis using Coomassie brilliant blue staining revealed a predominant low-molecular-weight band (~ 10 kDa) (Fig. 1F). Subsequent mass spectrometry (MS) identification demonstrated that these proteins are primarily chloroplast-derived components, including ATP synthase beta subunit (Fragment), cytochrome b6-f complex subunit 4, elongation factor Tu, chloroplastic (Fragment) and ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase large (RuBisCO) chain (Fragment) (Supplementary Table S2), suggesting that SF-NVs likely originate from chloroplast membrane compartments. Consistent with the results from agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1G), the QubitTM RNA HS Assay and Agilent 5300 Fragment Analyzer further confirmed that RNA was undetectable in SF-NVs (Supplementary Fig. S1C, S1D)—a characteristic that starkly contrasts with most plant-derived nanovesicles (e.g., ginger, yam, or ginseng exosomes), which typically harbor endogenous functional miRNAs and non-coding RNAs capable of regulating recipient cell gene expression [20–23]. This inherent lack of RNA not only eliminates risks of off-target genetic interference but also positions SF-NVs as superior therapeutic delivery vectors, particularly for RNA-sensitive applications where precise cargo control is critical. Collectively, SF-NVs represent marine-derived natural vesicles with unique features—notably high Hex1Cer content and absence of endogenous RNA—distinguishing them from terrestrial plant-derived nanovesicles and underpinning their therapeutic potential.

SF-NVs attenuate LPS-induced inflammation in macrophages by suppressing oxidative stress and MyD88-dependent NF-κB activation

Based on the well-documented anti-inflammatory properties of the source plant Sargassum fusiforme, we sought to validate whether nanovesicles derived from this alga retain similar bioactivities. To this end, we first evaluated their cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 cells using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 2A, cells treated with varying concentrations of SF-NVs for 24 h exhibited no significant reduction in viability, indicating that SF-NVs are biocompatible and suitable for subsequent experiments. Next, we investigated the cellular internalization dynamics of SF-NVs using PKH26-labeled vesicles incubated with both Kupffer cells and RAW264.7 cells for 24 h. PKH26, a red fluorescent lipophilic membrane dye, is commonly used for labeling extracellular vesicles [24]. Laser confocal microscopy revealed efficient uptake of SF-NVs (red fluorescence) by both macrophage types, with clear perinuclear localization around DAPI-stained nuclei (blue fluorescence) (Fig. 2B), demonstrating their ability to deliver cargo intracellularly. To evaluate anti-inflammatory effects, we quantified M1-polarization markers in LPS-stimulated macrophages via qPCR. LPS robustly upregulated the expression of key pro-inflammatory genes—IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α—in both Kupffer cells (Fig. 2C) and RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 2D). Notably, SF-NVs treatment markedly downregulated the expression of these cytokines in LPS-activated macrophages (Fig. 2C, 2D), indicating potent suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. Collectively, these results demonstrate that SF-NVs not only exhibit excellent macrophage compatibility and efficient cellular internalization but also functionally recapitulate the anti-inflammatory properties of their plant source by inhibiting critical cytokine production, positioning them as promising therapeutic agents for inflammation-driven pathologies. Given the well-documented antioxidant properties of Sargassum fusiforme [25], we postulated that its derived nanovesicles would confer antioxidant effects in macrophages. To assess the direct free radical scavenging capacity of SF-NVs, we first employed the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, a widely used method for evaluating the antioxidant potential of compounds [26]. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in absorption values in the presence of SF-NVs, confirming their intrinsic antioxidant activity (Fig. 2E). Next, we employed H2DCFDA, a cell-permeable fluorescent probe used to detect reactive oxygen species (ROS) [27], and observed that SF-NVs significantly reduced LPS-elevated ROS levels in macrophages (Fig. 2F). This reduction was corroborated by laser confocal microscopy, showing markedly diminished fluorescence intensity in LPS-stimulated macrophages treated with SF-NVs versus LPS alone (Fig. 2G). These properties are crucial in mitigating oxidative stress, which is often a key contributor to the pathogenesis of inflammation [28]. Given the central role of NF-κB in inflammation [29, 30], we sought to determine whether SF-NVs exert their anti-inflammatory effects by modulating this pathway. Following LPS stimulation, there was a marked increase in the expression levels of MyD88 and the phosphorylation of IKKα and IκBα in RAW264.7 cells. In contrast, SF-NVs significantly attenuated the expression of MyD88 and the activation of IKKα and IκBα compared to the LPS treatment group (Fig. 2H). Notably, SF-NVs also significantly reduced the mRNA levels of MyD88 (Supplementary Fig. S2A), suggesting that transcriptional downregulation is the primary driver of this effect. While we systematically investigated post-translational pathways including proteasomal degradation as well as P-body and m6A mediated mRNA stability, no significant involvement was observed (data not shown). These results indicate that SF-NVs attenuate TLR4 signaling specifically by suppressing MyD88 at the transcriptional level, though the precise upstream mediators warrant further study. The primary function of IKKα is to phosphorylate IκB proteins, thereby promoting their ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This event facilitates the release of NF-κB dimers (such as p65/p50), allowing their translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, where they initiate the transcription of target genes involved in immune and inflammatory responses [6, 31]. Next, we investigated whether SF-NVs diminished the nuclear localization of p65, which was enhanced following LPS stimulation. As illustrated in Fig. 2I, LPS treatment led to a substantial increase in the nuclear localization of p65; however, the addition of SF-NVs significantly attenuated this effect. To gain a systems-level understanding of the anti-inflammatory activity of SF-NVs, we performed proteomic profiling. A total of 648 differentially expressed proteins were identified between the LPS and LPS + SF-NVs groups, among which 366 were downregulated (including key inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, TLR2, and CXCL10) and 282 were upregulated (Fig. 2J, 2 K). Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis revealed that these differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in immune and inflammatory related responses, including innate immune response, inflammatory response, and response to oxidative stress (Fig. 2L). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated marked enrichment in inflammation-related signaling cascades, particularly the TNF, NF-κB and Toll-like receptor pathways (Fig. 2M). In summary, SF-NVs exert anti-inflammatory effects through synergistic suppression of oxidative stress and inhibition of NF-κB signaling activation.

Heme oxygenase-1 mediates the anti-inflammatory actions of SF-NVs

To delineate the transcriptional dynamics underlying the observed proteomic changes, we conducted comprehensive RNA sequencing on macrophages stimulated with LPS in the presence or absence of SF-NVs. Transcriptomic profiling identified 3,774 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 1,682 significantly downregulated and 2,092 upregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly involved in the regulation of immune system processes, defense responses, and inflammatory signaling (Fig. 3B). Notably, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted significant enrichment in the TNF, NF-κB, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 3C), providing transcriptional corroboration of our proteomic findings. Among the top-ranked DEGs, we identified a distinct cluster of inflammatory regulators (e.g., Il1a, Il1b, Il6) and antioxidant-related genes, most notably Hmox1, Gpx4, and Prdx6 (Fig. 3D). Based on its pivotal role in redox homeostasis and immune regulation, heme oxygenase-1 (Hmox1/HO-1), encoded by the Hmox1 gene, was selected for further functional validation. qPCR analysis confirmed that while LPS stimulation induced a baseline increase in HO-1 mRNA, co-treatment with SF-NVs markedly synergized this effect, leading to a substantial further upregulation (Fig. 3E). This observation aligns with the established role of HO-1 as a feedback inhibitor that limits excessive cytokine production to maintain tissue homeostasis [32, 33]. To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms, we examined the expression of Nrf2, a master transcription factor that governs antioxidant genes, including HO-1. qPCR analysis revealed that SF-NVs treatment significantly upregulated Nrf2 mRNA levels compared to LPS stimulation alone (Supplementary Fig. S2B), suggesting that SF-NVs enhance the antioxidant response, at least in part, through transcriptional activation of the Nrf2 pathway. Beyond Nrf2, emerging evidence points to potential crosstalk between Nrf2 and PPAR signaling, which may coordinately regulate antioxidative and anti-inflammatory responses. Whether PPAR signaling directly mediates the protective effects of SF-NVs remains an area for future investigation. To definitively establish the functional necessity of HO-1 in this therapeutic process, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown in macrophages. Following 54 h of transfection with siHO-1, HO-1 expression was effectively silenced, as confirmed in Fig. 3F. Crucially, in HO-1-deficient cells, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of SF-NVs was significantly compromised. The SF-NVs-mediated suppression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) was partially but significantly reversed upon HO-1 depletion compared to negative control (siNC) groups (Fig. 3G).These findings establish HO-1 as a pivotal molecular effector of SF-NVs-driven immunomodulation, providing a robust mechanistic foundation for its role in rectifying metabolic inflammation.

Elemol contributes to the anti-inflammatory effects of SF-NVs by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway

To elucidate the specific constituents responsible for the modulation of macrophage inflammatory responses by SF-NVs, we conducted comprehensive compound profiling via quasi-targeted metabolomics. Identification of specific bioactive cargo is essential to confirm that their efficacy is rooted in a concrete material basis rather than just the physical presence of a nanostructure. Our analysis revealed that carbohydrates and their derivatives constituted the predominant class (69%) of small molecules within SF-NVs, followed by lipids (10%), amino acids (5%), plant hormones (4%), organic oxygen compounds (3%), and terpenoids (2%) (Fig. 4A; positive and negative total ion chromatograms in Supplementary Fig. S3A, S3B). Based on relative abundance and chemical diversity, we selected five representative metabolites—melezitose, syringetin 3-O-galactoside, diosgenin, elemol (a sesquiterpene alcohol), and artemisic acid—for anti-inflammatory validation (Fig. 4B; extracted ion chromatograms in Supplementary Fig. S3C–G). Notably, only elemol significantly suppressed the LPS-induced expression of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in macrophages, whereas the other candidates showed no significant effects (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S3H). The discovery of elemol as a primary active small molecule within SF-NVs provides direct evidence that these vesicles carry specific chemical agents that drive their pharmacological activity.

We subsequently assessed whether elemol mediates its anti-inflammatory effects through the NF-κB pathway, which was previously implicated in SF-NVs’ action. As shown in Fig. 4D, LPS treatment robustly induced the expression of MyD88 and the phosphorylation of IKKα and IκBα; however, both elemol and SF-NVs markedly attenuated these effects. This observation is consistent with the data in Fig. 2H , demonstrating that the chemical constituent elemol partially recapitulates the ability of SF-NVs to impair NF-κB activation by stabilizing IκBα and inhibiting IKK kinase.

To systematically investigate the regulatory mechanisms of elemol, we performed an in-depth re-analysis of the RNA sequencing data. Volcano plot analysis revealed that elemol treatment resulted in 1,548 differentially expressed genes (948 upregulated and 600 downregulated) compared to the LPS-treated group (Fig. 4E). The downregulated genes were notably enriched in inflammatory mediators such as Il1a, Il1b, Cxcl16, and Csf2, whereas the upregulated genes included key antioxidants such as Hmox1, Cat, and Gpx4 (Fig. 4E). GO and KEGG pathway analyses further indicated that these DEGs were strongly associated with pivotal inflammatory and immune-related signaling pathways, notably the TNF, NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 4F, 4G).

Based on the finding that SF-NVs suppress inflammation partially through the upregulation of HO-1, and given that elemol is a key component of SF-NVs capable of inducing this enzyme, we examined whether elemol alleviates inflammation via a similar mechanism. Docking analysis revealed a stable binding conformation of elemol within the active site of HO-1, with a calculated binding affinity of -5.4 kcal/mol, stabilized by hydrogen bonding with SER-174 and ASP-173 (Fig. 4H). qPCR further confirmed that elemol significantly augmented HO-1 expression relative to LPS treatment alone (Fig. 4I). To assess the functional role of HO-1 in the anti-inflammatory effects of elemol, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown. qPCR analysis confirmed successful HO-1 downregulation following siRNA transfection (Fig. 4J). Subsequently, HO-1 knockdown partially reversed the suppressive effect of elemol on pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4K), confirming the functional necessity of this pathway. Collectively, our results demonstrate that elemol represents one of the active constituents in SF-NVs, contributing to their therapeutic effect primarily through the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway and the activation of the HO-1-mediated antioxidant response.

While this study identifies elemol as a bioactive constituent within SF-NVs, we acknowledge certain limitations regarding its functional attribution. Our quasi-targeted metabolomics provided a comprehensive profile of the small-molecule cargo, yet the absolute quantification of elemol within the vesicles remains to be established. In our validation experiments, a concentration of 25 μg/mL elemol was utilized to confirm its anti-inflammatory potential; however, we recognize that this may exceed the endogenous levels delivered by SF-NVs at equivalent doses. Notably, elemol maintained significant inhibitory activity at lower concentrations (5 μg/mL; Supplementary Fig. S3I), suggesting it contributes to, rather than solely determines, the pharmacological potency of the vesicles.

The efficacy of SF-NVs likely arises from a synergistic interplay among multiple constituents, where elemol serves as a representative bioactive component. Furthermore, vesicle-mediated delivery may enhance the intracellular bioavailability of hydrophobic compounds like elemol, potentially amplifying their functional impact compared to free-form administration. Nevertheless, we emphasize that elemol is one of several contributors to the bioactivity of SF-NVs. Definitively partitioning the relative contribution of elemol versus other cargo will require absolute quantification and rigorous dose-correlation studies, which remain a priority for our future investigations.

Spleen-liver accumulation of intraperitoneal SF-NVs attenuates LPS-induced systemic inflammation

To evaluate the tissue biodistribution profile of intraperitoneally (i.p.) administered SF-NVs, we performed near-infrared imaging using DiR-labeled SF-NVs in mice. Post-injection (24–48 h), SF-NVs were detected in systemic circulation (blood), primary reticuloendothelial organs (liver and spleen), and pulmonary tissue (lungs), with sustained fluorescence signals persisting for 48 h (Fig. 5A). This distribution pattern—characterized by significant splenic and hepatic accumulation—aligns with typical nanoparticle clearance pathways via the mononuclear phagocyte system, while pulmonary presence suggests potential vascular permeation or macrophage-mediated trafficking. Critically, detectable SF-NVs in blood at 48 h confirm their systemic bioavailability and extended circulation half-life following i.p. delivery, an advantageous feature for sustained anti-inflammatory therapy.

To evaluate the prophylactic efficacy of SF-NVs, we employed an LPS-induced acute inflammation model (Fig. 5B), where SF-NVs were administered 24 h prior to the LPS challenge. SF-NVs significantly suppressed LPS-elevated serum levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines—IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 5C), which drive septic shock and tissue damage via NF-κB/MAPK cascades. Consistent with systemic effects, SF-NVs downregulated mRNA expression of these cytokines in splenic mononuclear cells (Fig. 5D), indicating direct modulation of immune cell responses. This dual attenuation of peripheral and splenic inflammation underscores SF-NVs’ capacity to disrupt cytokine amplification loops across compartments, likely through NF-κB pathway inhibition (as previously identified in macrophages) and ROS scavenging. Collectively, SF-NVs confer protection against acute inflammation by synergistically targeting cytokine production at transcriptional and systemic levels, positioning them as promising nanotherapeutics for inflammatory disorders.

Scalable-oriented hollow fiber membrane concentration for oral delivery: SF-NVs with preserved physicochemical integrity and anti-inflammatory bioactivity

To overcome the scalability limitations of ultracentrifugation for clinical translation, hollow fiber membrane concentration was adopted to efficiently isolate orally deliverable nanovesicles from Sargassum fusiforme. Following tissue homogenization and differential centrifugation, the resulting supernatant was processed through a 200-nm hollow fiber column (Fig. 6A) to enrich for nanovesicles. The obtained retentate and permeate were designated as hollow fiber membrane concentrate (HFMC) and hollow fiber filtrate (HF-Fil), respectively. Subsequent comprehensive characterization revealed distinct properties of the two fractions. TEM analysis confirmed the successful enrichment of spherical nanovesicles in the HFMC (Fig. 6B). NTA revealed that the HFMC exhibited a high particle concentration (1.7 × 1012 particles/mL) and an average hydrodynamic diameter of 170.3 nm (Fig. 6C), while zeta potential measurements indicated strong colloidal stability (–39.10 mV) (Fig. 6D), a desirable feature for gastrointestinal transit. In contrast, the HF-Fil (permeate) exhibited a substantially lower particle concentration ( 9.3 × 109 particles/mL), a smaller average diameter (132.6 nm), and a zeta potential of –23.05 mV (Fig. S4A, S4B). These data indicate that hollow fiber membrane filtration effectively enriches nanovesicles from the crude Sargassum fusiforme extract, yielding a retentate (HFMC) characterized by high particle density, a favorable size distribution, and excellent colloidal stability.

Given that HFMC possessed the key physical attributes required for oral delivery, we next validated the identity and integrity of these enriched vesicles by subjecting the HFMC product to sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation. This process yielded three distinct bands, identical to the profile observed during standard SF-NVs isolation. The uppermost band was collected and further refined via ultracentrifugation to obtain hollow fiber-concentrated and ultracentrifugation-purified vesicles, designated as HF-SF-NVs. TEM imaging confirmed that HF-SF-NVs maintained a characteristic spherical morphology (Fig. 6E), with an average size of 120.0 nm (Fig. 6F) and a zeta potential of –47.07 mV (Fig. 6G)—parameters closely matching those of conventionally isolated SF-NVs (Fig. 1B–D).

To directly attribute the observed bioactivity to the vesicle fraction, we compared the anti-inflammatory efficacy of HFMC, HF-SF-NVs, and HF-Fil, benchmarking them against standard SF-NVs. As shown in Fig. 6H, HFMC significantly suppressed the LPS-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in macrophages. Notably, the anti-inflammatory potency of HF-SF-NVs was significantly higher than that of the crude HFMC and comparable to that of conventionally purified SF-NVs. In striking contrast, the HF-Fil fraction, which is depleted of nanovesicles, exhibited negligible activity across all cytokine readouts. Collectively, these results confirm that the anti-inflammatory activity of HFMC is primarily attributable to the nanovesicles themselves rather than soluble components.

Nutrient profiling demonstrated that the HFMC is predominantly aqueous (97.5% water), with ash (1.5%), carbohydrates (0.87%), and crude protein (0.13%) as major solutes, alongside bioactive marine components including dietary fiber (0.73%), total polysaccharides (0.41%), alginate (0.24%), and fucoxanthin (Table 1). Collectively, hollow fiber membrane concentration generates an orally administrable Sargassum fusiforme concentrate rich in nanovesicles with preserved anti-inflammatory potency, positioning it as a viable candidate for oral nanotherapeutics targeting gut-inflammatory pathologies.

To evaluate the gastrointestinal biodistribution and therapeutic potential of orally administered HFMC, we employed DiR labeling to track the vesicles purified from HFMC. At 4 h post-administration, fluorescence signals were predominantly localized in the stomach, small intestine, and large intestine, with most tissues clearing by 6 h except for the large intestine (Fig. 7A). This targeted intestinal accumulation highlights the potential of HFMC as an oral nanotherapeutic capable of modulating the gut-liver axis—a key pathway in the progression of metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD).

Using a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced MASH model (Fig. 7B), we found that HFMC treatment significantly attenuated HFD-driven weight gain (Fig. 7C) and improved systemic metabolic dysfunction. This was evidenced by a notable reduction in fasting blood glucose levels (Fig. 7D) and a decreased area under the curve (AUC) in the glucose tolerance test (GTT) (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, HFMC suppressed systemic inflammation, significantly lowering serum IL-6 levels (Fig. 7F).

Treatment with HFMC also restored systemic and hepatic lipid homeostasis, as shown by decreased serum total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) (Fig. 7G), alongside a significant reduction in hepatic TC and TG content (Fig. 7H). Mechanistically, these metabolic improvements were driven by a coordinated reprogramming of hepatic fat metabolism: HFMC downregulated FASN-mediated lipogenesis while simultaneously promoting PGC-1α/CPT1-driven fatty acid oxidation and MTTP-dependent lipid export (Fig. 7I).

This metabolic shift conferred robust hepatoprotection, significantly mitigating hepatic injury as indicated by lowered serum ALT and AST levels (Fig. 7J). Histopathological analysis further confirmed these findings, revealing alleviated steatosis, ballooning degeneration, and immune cell infiltration. Oil Red O staining likewise showed a marked reduction in lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 7K). Additionally, HFMC treatment effectively attenuated hepatic inflammation, evidenced by the decreased expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in immunohistochemical analyses (Fig. 7K).

These results establish HFMC as a multi-targeted oral therapeutic that alleviates HFD-induced metabolic dysregulation by interrupting pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades via the suppression of Kupffer cell activation (as previously shown in Fig. 2C); and directly reprogramming hepatic lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipogenesis (FASN) while enhancing fatty acid oxidation (PGC-1α/CPT1) and lipid export (MTTP).

Oral HFMC suppress microenvironmental inflammation and restore small intestinal homeostasis

Numerous studies have demonstrated that modulating intestinal homeostasis can effectively reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and mitigate systemic inflammation [34, 35]. Given that oral HFMC exhibit precise intestinal targeting (Fig. 7A), we hypothesized that they ameliorate fatty liver disease by acting directly on the gut microenvironment. Our investigation confirmed that HFMC alleviated HFD-induced intestinal inflammation, as evidenced by the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in the small intestine (Fig. 8A). Concurrently, the treatment significantly reduced luminal cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Fig. 8B).

Importantly, HFMC restored HFD-impaired small intestinal homeostasis by upregulating the expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 8C). This molecular restoration was consistent with morphological improvements observed via H&E staining, which showed a recovered mucosal architecture with an increased number of intact crypts and a notable replenishment of mucin-producing goblet cells in the HFMC group compared to the HFD group (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, CD68 immunohistochemical staining revealed a marked reduction in macrophage infiltration within the intestinal lamina propria, further supporting the attenuation of microenvironmental inflammation (Fig. 8D).

To further delineate the mechanisms underlying these protective effects, we re-mined the quantitative proteomic data from the small intestinal tissues. Proteomic profiling identified 1,759 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the HFD and NCD groups (901 upregulated; 858 downregulated). Comparison between the HFMC and HFD groups revealed 1,654 DEPs, comprising 731 upregulated and 923 downregulated proteins (Fig. 8E). A comparative intersection analysis delineated a core set of 738 common protein candidates (Fig. 8F).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these 738 intersecting proteins were prominently associated with the gut–liver axis, particularly cholesterol metabolism and ABC transporters (Fig. 8G). The proteomic data specifically confirmed the elevated expression of key ABC transporters involved in cellular lipid efflux and detoxification, including Abca1, Abcb1a, Abcb1b, Abcb6, Abcb11, Abcc2, Abcc6, Abcc10, and Abcg5. Furthermore, the data confirmed the restoration of the intestinal barrier at a systems level, showing upregulated expression of tight junction proteins (Cldn (Claudin)-3, Cldn-7, and Cldn-8) and the basement membrane component Col4a2 (Fig. 8H).

Despite the robust evidence supporting intestinal barrier restoration and lipid-transport modulation, the ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) profile of SF-NVs, particularly the role of gut microbiota-mediated metabolism, remains to be fully elucidated. Given that microbial metabolites can significantly influence (either positively or negatively) the bioavailability and functional outcomes of natural bioactive components, the protective effects of HFMC may involve a complex interplay between the vesicles and the gut microecology. Future studies incorporating metagenomic and metabolomic approaches are warranted to clarify how microbial biotransformation affects the stability and activity of SF-NVs. Investigating these ‘vesicle-microbiota’ interactions will be a key priority in our next stage of research to optimize the nutraceutical applications and overcome clinical limitations of orally delivered nanovesicles.

Collectively, these findings indicate that HFMC suppress microenvironmental inflammation and restore small intestinal homeostasis by reinforcing the mucosal barrier and modulating lipid-transport pathways, thereby alleviating fatty liver disease via the gut–liver axis.

Comprehensive biosafety assessment of orally administered HFMC

To comprehensively evaluate the in vivo biosafety profile of orally administered HFMC—a critical prerequisite for therapeutic translation—mice received HFMC via oral gavage every 48 h over a 31-day exposure period, simulating subchronic toxicity assessment guidelines for nanomaterials. The animal experimental procedure is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S5A. Histopathological analysis of major organs (heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, stomach, and intestines) revealed no structural abnormalities, inflammatory infiltrates, or degenerative changes in HFMC-treated mice compared to vehicle controls (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The integrity of intestinal epithelium—a primary barrier against nanoparticle translocation—remained uncompromised, consistent with in vitro models demonstrating that biologically derived nanovesicles lack cytotoxicity or barrier disruption in gastrointestinal tissues. Collectively, these findings confirm that prolonged oral administration of HFMC induces no overt histopathological damage across vital organs, supporting their biocompatibility for oral therapeutic applications targeting inflammatory and metabolic disorders.

Conclusion

This study successfully isolated bioactive nanovesicles from the edible brown alga Sargassum fusiforme using differential isolation methodologies, establishing their dual anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions through integrated in vitro and in vivo analyses. In vitro, SF-NVs were internalized by macrophages and exerted potent anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting ROS production and blocking the NF-κB signaling pathway (via suppression of IKKα/IκBα phosphorylation and p65 nuclear translocation). Crucially, we demonstrated that the efficacy of SF-NVs is rooted in a concrete chemical material basis rather than a purely structural phenomenon. Through metabolomic screening, elemol was identified as a key active constituent that involves anti-inflammatory regulation by inhibiting NF-κB signaling and concurrently upregulating Hmox1 (HO-1) expression.

In vivo, SF-NVs conferred significant protection against LPS-induced acute systemic inflammation, while the HFMC demonstrated enhanced efficacy in mitigating HFD-associated metabolic inflammation and liver injury. By re-mining quantitative proteomic data, we further elucidated that HFMC restore small intestinal homeostasis by suppressing microenvironmental inflammation, modulating lipid-transport pathways (particularly ABC transporters), and reinforcing the intestinal barrier. These findings collectively establish SF-NVs as potent modulators of the NF-κB signaling pathway, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for metabolic disorders rooted in the gut–liver axis. By leveraging nanotechnology to harness the bioactive potential of traditional seaweed medicine, this work provides a foundational framework for transforming marine resources into orally deliverable nanotherapeutics, advancing both sustainable resource utilization and next-generation drug development for inflammation-related pathologies.
