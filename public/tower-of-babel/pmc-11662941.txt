Bacterial Rps3 counters oxidative and UV stress by recognizing and processing AP-sites on mRNA via a novel mechanism
Authors: Mohammad Afsar, Ankita Shukla, Faiz Ali, Rahul Kumar Maurya, Suman Bharti, Nelam Kumar, Mohammad Sadik, Surabhi Chandra, Huma Rahil, Sanjay Kumar, Imran Ansari, Farheen Jahan, Saman Habib, Tanweer Hussain, Manju Yasoda Krishnan, Ravishankar Ramachandran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLesions and stable secondary structures in mRNA severely impact the translation efficiency, causing ribosome stalling and collisions. Prokaryotic ribosomal proteins Rps3, Rps4 and Rps5, located in the mRNA entry tunnel, form the mRNA helicase center and unwind stable mRNA secondary structures during translation. However, the mechanism underlying the detection of lesions on translating mRNA is unclear. We used Cryo-EM, biochemical assays, and knockdown experiments to investigate the apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) endoribonuclease activity of bacterial ribosomes on AP-site containing mRNA. Our biochemical assays show that Rps3, specifically the 130RR131 motif, is important for recognizing and performing the AP-endoribonuclease activity. Furthermore, structural analysis revealed cleaved mRNA product in the 30S ribosome entry tunnel. Additionally, knockdown studies in Mycobacterium tuberculosis confirmed the protective role of Rps3 against oxidative and UV stress. Overall, our results show that prokaryotic Rps3 recognizes and processes AP-sites on mRNA via a novel mechanism that is distinct from eukaryotes.

Introduction

Translation of mRNA on ribosomes is a dynamic process involving several conformational switches that facilitate ribosome translocation and ensures an adequate supply of codons to the ribosomal decoding center (1). The abundance of RNA molecules in cells makes them highly susceptible to damage from exogenous and endogenous agents. The most frequent types of mRNA damage are methylation, 8-hydroxyguanosine and apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP)-sites (2,3). It has been shown that the presence of AP sites in mRNA can completely halt translation, while base modifications significantly slow it down (4). However, the mechanism of RNA repair or regulation is far less studied compared to DNA repair (3). In Escherichia coli, AlkB, whose human homolog is hABH3, repairs methylation damage in RNA (5,6). Interestingly, human Xrn1 performs 5′-3′ exoribonuclease activity on decapped or cleaved mRNA molecules and can perform 5′-3′ exoribonuclease activity post-translationally or co-translationally (7,8). In bacteria, the endoribonuclease RelE cleaves mRNA at the A-site of the 30S ribosome, while SmrB recognizes collided ribosomes and cleaves mRNA near the mRNA entry tunnel (9). Furthermore, in bacteria, ribosomes undergo turnover by the 3′-5′ exonuclease RNaseR during prolonged starvation (10,11). In eukaryotes, Cue2 cleaves mRNA between trailing and stalled ribosomes (12). Additionally, in eukaryotes, the stalled ribosomes in the coding frame are recognized by the Pelota–HBS1L complex, which recruits Rli1/ABCE1 to split the subunits. After splitting, the subunits are targeted by E3 ubiquitin enzymes (Hel2/ZNF598, Slh1/ASCC3), which degrade the nascent peptide and recycle the ribosome for the next round of translation (13). In bacteria, the RqcU protein recognizes collided ribosomes and cleaves the mRNA. RqcH and RqcP work together and add an alanine tail to the nascent polypeptide, which is further released from the 50S ribosome and is cleaved by the ClpXP protease machinery (14). Recent reports suggest that human uS3 possesses an extra-ribosomal AP-endonuclease/lyase activity on AP-site containing DNA (15–17). However, the detection of AP sites on mRNA during or after translation is poorly studied either in prokaryotes or eukaryotes.

Protein synthesis metabolism, and consequently protein synthesis, can be significantly impacted by lesions on mRNA (18–24). These lesions may be induced by oxidative stress, UV-irradiation, alkylating stress or innate errors in replicating enzymes (25–28). Amongst these, AP-sites are the most common with a higher percentage of occurrence in RNA than DNA (29), and therefore detection and removal of AP-sites is of crucial importance. As previously demonstrated, uS3 in eukaryotes might play a significant role during the clearance of no-go decay (NGD) mRNAs by modulating the endoribonuclease reaction (30). uS3 has two domains viz an N-terminal KH homology domain and a C-terminal S3 domain. The KH domain of the UV131 + endonuclease from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii exhibits AP-endonuclease activity on double-stranded DNA (31). In human uS3, the residues from 144–189 of the S3 domain are specifically involved in removing cyclobutane dimers from damaged DNA (32). Recently, a Cryo–EM structure of the 70S ribosome in a transcription-translation coupled state with RNA polymerase (RNAP) and NusG, revealed the mRNA’s path near the KH and S3 domain residues (33,34). Although the KH domain is located away from the mRNA entry tunnel, the studies suggested that a 55–64 peptide region plays a role in mRNA surveillance, as suggested by crosslinking between processed AP sites and positively charged residues (35,36). However, the interaction between the KH domain and mRNA is highly unstable, potentially leading to missed recognition of AP sites by the KH domain. In contrast, the S3 domain, positioned within the mRNA entry tunnel, forms a complex network of interactions with incoming mRNA. It was suggested that the S3 protein might recognize abasic sites through a conserved ‘RRA’ motif, triggering AP-endoribonuclease activity at these sites (30).

In the present study, we determined high resolution Cryo-EM structures of the E. coli ribosome complexed to a couple of AP site containing mRNA, carried out activity assays with purified ribosomes and Rps3, along with knockdown experiments, to investigate the regulatory mechanism that prevents the entry of AP-site-containing mRNA into the active center of the bacterial ribosome. We found that the bacterial ribosome efficiently cleaves mRNA at AP sites. Correspondingly, the biochemical assays with purified mycobacterial Rps3 and its mutants revealed that a conserved 130RR131 motif is important for recognizing and cleaving AP sites, showing that Rps3 has extra-ribosomal AP-endoribonuclease activity too. Our Cryo-EM structures reveal cleaved mRNA products in the entry tunnel, which is lined by Rps3, Rps4, and Rps5, confirming Rps3’s AP-endoribonuclease function while associated with the ribosome. After cleavage, the 30S ribosome adopts a ‘rotated head’ conformation, which may allow for the recruitment of downstream processing factors. Knockdown studies of the rps3 gene in Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra support the biochemical assays and demonstrated that rps3-knockdown leads to severe growth impairment under oxidative and UV stresses. Overall, our results reveal a novel mechanism of AP-site recognition in mRNA by Rps3, distinct from the endonuclease activity of eukaryotic uS3 on AP-DNA.

Materials and methods

Isolation of the E. coli ribosome

Ribosomes from E. coli MRE600 were isolated as described earlier (15). Briefly, E. coli MRE600 was grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium and harvested in buffer A (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 10 mM Magnesium acetate, 100 mM Ammonium chloride and 3 mM DTT). The resuspended pellet was lysed by sonication at 10% amplitude (2 s ON and 2 s OFF) for 10 min and the lysed cells were subsequently centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 45 min at 4°C to obtain clear supernatant. The supernatant was then loaded onto a 40% sucrose cushion in buffer B (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 10 mM Magnesium acetate, 500 mM Ammonium chloride, 250 mM KCl and 3mM DTT) and ultra-centrifuged (Beckman coulter; SW28.1 rotor) at 23 000 rpm for 22 h at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in buffer C (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 10 mM Magnesium acetate, 500 mM Ammonium chloride, 200 mM KCl and 2 mM DTT). The solution was again centrifuged at 17 000 rpm for 4 h at 4°C and the clear supernatant was again ultracentrifuged at 35 000 rpm (Beckman coulter; 70 Ti rotor) for 3 h at 4°C to remove loosely bound protein factors or associated endonucleases. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet (70S ribosome) was dissolved in storage buffer D (20mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 10 mM Magnesium acetate, 50 mM Ammonium chloride, 200 mM KCl and 3mM DTT). For the dissociation of the 70S ribosomes, the pellet was resuspended in dissociation buffer (20mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 1 mM Magnesium acetate, 100mM Ammonium chloride, 50mM KCl and 3mM DTT) and kept on continuous shaking for 1 h at room temperature. The resuspended and dissociated ribosomes were then loaded onto a 10–40% sucrose density gradient and centrifuged at 36 000 rpm for 4 h at 4°C. The gradient was fractionated and the corresponding 30S, 50S and 70S ribosomal peaks were collected. The individual subunits were then buffer exchanged with the storage buffer (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 10 mM Magnesium acetate, 10mM Ammonium chloride, 50 mM KCl and 3 mM DTT) through overnight dialysis. The concentrations of individual subunits and the whole ribosome were calculated by measuring the absorbance at 254 nm. The quality of the final purified fractions is shown in Supplementary Figure S1A. The purified ribosomes were then divided into small aliquots, flash frozen into liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Purification of initiator tRNAMet

The initiator tRNAMet (i-tRNAMet) was overexpressed in E. coli MG1655 Δfmt Kan / pBR322 metY strain in 2.4 L culture in the presence of 12.5 μg/ml tetracycline (37). The pellet was resuspended in 1× TE buffer (10mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 1 mM Na2-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)) followed by addition of equal volume of water-saturated phenol. The pellet was dissolved by vortexing three times for 30 s and 1 min incubation on ice for each time. The lysed cells were then centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and equal volume of water-saturated phenol was added. This solution was further centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C after vortexing for 1 min. The supernatant was collected and added 2.5 volumes of ethanol with 1:10 volume of 3M sodium acetate pH 5.2. Subsequently, the solution was incubated overnight at −20°C. Next day, the solution was centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and pellet was washed with cold 70% ethanol. Furthermore, the pellets were allowed to dry completely and resuspended in 1× TE buffer. The tRNA solution was loaded on agarose gel and bands corresponding to the i-tRNAMet was excised and added into elution buffer 50mM Tris-Cl, pH 8, 5mM EDTA, 0.1M LiCl, 0.05% water-saturated phenol and followed by overnight incubation at room temperature on slow rocking. The solution was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min and supernatant was collected, filtered and incubated with DEAE-Sepharose beads which were pre-equilibrated in 0.1M Tris-Cl pH 8, 0.1M LiCl. Subsequently, the solution was incubated overnight on constant rocking at room temperature. Next day, the flow through was collected in 500μl fractions and samples were loaded on 1.5% agarose gel in 1X TBE buffer. The fractions containing the i-tRNAMet were precipitated in 2.5 times vol. of ethanol + 1:10 vol. of 3M sodium acetate pH 5.2 at −20°C. The pellets were then washed with cold 70% ethanol, followed by resuspension in nuclease free water and stored in −80°C for further use.

Cloning, expression and purification of the Rps3 and their mutants

The genomic DNA of M. tuberculosis H37Rv was used to amplify the Rps3 gene by using appropriate primers (Supplementary Table S1) and with BamHI/HindIII restriction sites following standard polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocols. The amplified products were digested with restriction enzymes and ligated into a predigested pGEX-KG vector. The ligated products were transformed into E. coli DH5α cells and clones were selected using the Ampicillin selection marker. Positive colonies were grown overnight in liquid LB broth medium, and the plasmids were isolated using Macherey-NagelTM Nucleospin plasmid QuickpureTM Kit. Positive clones were then transformed into E. coli BL21 DE3 cells for protein expression. The cells were grown at 37°C up to 0.4 optical density (O.D.) and the protein expression was induced by adding 0.3mM Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The induced cultures were then grown for overnight at 16°C. The overnight grown bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm (Eppendorf 5810R, Rotor A-4–62). The collected cells were lysed in buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH = 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 2mM DTT, 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) with sonication at 18% amplitude (10 s ON, 10 s OFF). The lysed cells were then subsequently centrifuged at 10 000 rpm in centrifuge. The supernatant was collected and loaded onto pre-equilibrated Glutathione-Agarose beads (PierceTM Glutathione agarose, ThermoFischer Scientific). The glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged protein was eluted in buffer containing of 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 2 mM DTT and 20 mM reduced glutathione. The eluted fractions were subjected to thrombin cleavage at 4°C overnight to remove the GST-tag. The cleaved product was then purified by size exclusion chromatography by using the Superdex S200 increase 10/300 GL columns (Cytiva) in buffer (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 2% Glycerol, 2 mM DTT). The purified fractions were frozen in the presence of 30% glycerol. The single-point mutants were purified by using the above-mentioned strategy. For deletion mutants we applied the same strategy, except that for the size exclusion chromatography step, we used Superdex 75 10/300 GL columns (Cytiva) for purification. The quality of the purified fractions of Rps3 and their mutants is shown in Supplementary Figure S1B and C.

mRNA endoribonuclease assays involving the ribosome, Rps3 and their mutants

The purified fractions of the ribosome, Rps3 and their mutants were used to perform the endoribonuclease activity on chemically synthesized AP-site containing mRNA molecules. For endoribonuclease activity, the reaction mixture (20 μl) contains the 100 nM AP-site containing mRNA substrate and 0–100 nM of the 30S ribosomes:i-tRNAMet (1:2 molar ratio), Rps3 and/or its mutants were used. The reaction was also supplemented with the reaction buffer containing 50mM HEPES-NaOH pH 8.0, 10mM MgCl2, 5mM KCl and 2% glycerol. The reactions were assembled on ice and then incubated at 37°C for 20 min, followed by quenching by adding 5 μl of stop solution containing 98% formamide, 10mM EDTA, 0.1% Bromophenol blue. The samples were then heated at 100°C for 5 min and chilled on ice. The reaction products were then loaded on 15% polyacrylamide gels comprising 8 M Urea. Gels were scanned using Image quant LAS 4000 and the respective intensities were measured and quantified using Image QuantTL 8.1 software.

Single particle Cryo-electron microscopy of damaged mRNA bound to 30S ribosomes

Sample preparation

The purified fractions of the 30S ribosome were used to reconstitute the complexes with AP-site containing mRNA-structured-AP and mRNA-linear-AP mRNA molecules. First, the ribosomes were incubated at 42°C for 10 min after which the concentration of MgCl2 was reduced to 2 mM by diluting the preparation. Subsequently, the ribosomal samples were incubated at 37°C for 10 min to enhance the receptivity to the mRNA. Around 0.6 μM mRNA was then added to the reaction containing 10 mM MgCl2 and 0.4 μM ribosomes. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 10 min prior to grid preparation.

Cryo-EM data collection, processing and model fitting

The Cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios 300KeV machine at the National Electron Cryo-Microscopy Facility, Bengaluru (InSTEM). Around 3.5 μl of the samples were blotted onto holey carbon coated copper grids Quantifoil R2/2 and 300 mesh with 6.5 s blot time, 10 s wait time, 100% humidity at 4°C and then flash frozen into liquid ethane. The data were collected at −1.5 to −3.5 nm defocus value with 59 K magnification. We collected movies using the Falcon3 Electron Detector. Motion correction was performed using MotionCor2.0 to align the individual frames with whole image correction and patch motion correction (5 × 5) (38). After the motion correction we checked all micrographs for the quality, and best micrographs were selected for contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation. The CTF for each image was determined by using GCTF (39). First, we manually picked nearly 10 000 particles for each data set and classified them using 2D classification. The good classes were selected and used as templates for auto-picking in Relion3.0 (40). The auto-picked particles were then subjected to 2D classification. After two rounds of 2D classification, the particles were finally selected for 3D classification. The good classes were selected, and we performed 3D-auto refinement without mask. The refined particles were then subjected for CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing. The subsequent particles were then 3D-auto refined using the mask. Postprocessing was done to the resultant maps with proper masking, B-factor sharpening and resolution estimation to avoid over fitting. Subsequently, we used the Multibody refinement on head and body part of the 30S ribosome from the consensus 3D-auto refinement. The half maps from both bodies were subjected to density modification in Phenix 1.14–326 (41). The density modified maps were then used to prepare the composite maps. The coordinates were refined in consensus and composite maps using rigid body refinements (42). The structures were further modeled using COOT 0.9.8.7 (43) and refined using rigid body refinement program implemented in Phenix (44). The Cryo-EM data collection, processing and refinement statistics is shown in Supplementary Table S2. The complete data processing tree is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Growth kinetics and gene expression of mycobacterial strains

To make the knockdown construct, the complete gene fragment Rps3 (Rv0707) of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb.) H37Rv was cloned into pMV361 vector between the EcoRI and HindIII restriction sites, in the reverse orientation with respect to the hsp60 promoter. For bacterial studies, we used the avirulent strain Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (M.tb.H37Ra). The M.tb.H37Ra was grown in Middlebrook (MB) 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA), 0.2% glycerol (v/v) and 0.05% tween 80 at 37°C till the OD600 reached to 0.4–0.8. The recombinant construct and empty vector (EV) were electroporated into M.tb.H37Ra strain and plated on MB7H10 media with kanamycin (30μg/ml) antibiotic. The Mycobacterial strains, M.tb.H37Ra-EV and the knock-down (KD) M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD were grown to mid-log phase in MB7H9 medium at 37°C with shaking. RNA was extracted from both the strains using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and reverse transcribed with the GeneSureTM H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. qPCR was performed in triplicate with SigA as the control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. For growth kinetics study, cultures were diluted to OD600 of 0.01 by using MB7H9 medium, grown at 37°C and optical density was measured at various time points over 16 days.

Macrophage infection and intracellular survival assay

Human monocyte cell line THP-1 was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) and differentiated into macrophages using 50 ng/ml phorbol 12- myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h. Cells were incubated for additional 24 h resting (without antibiotics) to allow complete differentiation. The differentiated cells were then infected with M.tb.H37Ra-EV or M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in RPMI media. The infected cells were lysed at different time points with 0.1% Triton X-100. Lysates were appropriately diluted in PBS containing 0.05% tween80 (PBST) and spread on MB7H10 medium containing kanamycin to estimate CFU.

DNA damage-sensitivity assays

M.tb.H37Ra-EV and M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD strains were grown in MB7H9 broth to a cell density corresponding to an OD600 of around 0.6. The cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1 and incubated for 8 and 24 h with 0.01% MMS (Methyl methane sulfonate) and two different concentrations (2.5 or 5 mM) of H2O2 (hydrogen peroxide) for 8, 24 and 48 h at 37°C under shaking conditions (45). After incubation, cells were serially diluted and spotted (10 μl) on MB7H10 agar plates containing 30 μg/ml kanamycin. Similarly, the serial dilutions of both the strains (untreated) were spotted on 7H10 agar plates and irradiated with 25 J/m2 UV light. The plates were incubated at 37°C and survival was determined by visible growth from 10-fold dilutions.

Results

Endoribonuclease activity on mRNA is actively performed by ribosomes at the apurinic/apyrimidinic site (AP-site)

Several ribosome quality control (RQC) factors rescue the ribosome when it stalls during translation at damaged sites or stable secondary structures in mRNA (46–49). One of these pathways is known as NGD and stimulates downstream processing by cleaving the mRNA and dissociation of ribosomal subunits (14,50,51). However, the identity of a probable mRNA AP-endoribonuclease near the entry tunnel on the ribosome is still unclear. Consequently, we conducted AP-endoribonuclease activity assays utilizing purified 70S ribosomes and their respective subunits (30S and 50S) on mRNA containing an AP-site (AP-mRNA). We additionally employed undamaged mRNA with the identical sequence to eliminate the possibility of any exoribonuclease or endoribonuclease associated with ribosomes during purification. Abasic sites are naturally introduced into DNA/RNA through the loss of a nitrogenous base or by the action of uracil DNA glycosylases (in the case of DNA), which hydrolyze the N-glycosidic bond between the nitrogenous base and sugar moiety. These abasic sites are unstable and difficult to purify. For in vitro experiments, we used rSpacer tetrahydrofuran ring as an abasic site chemical mimetic to generate the abasic site in RNA, which has also been previously used as an AP-site model (36,52,53). We designed AP-mRNA molecules with AP-sites and placed them in various locations, such as the A site, the P site, near the S3 domain and near the KH domain. In addition, we designed a structured pseudoknot with mRNA in the 5′-untranslated region (5′-UTR) and an AP-site at the P site (Figure 1).

Preparation of apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) site containing mRNA molecules. The positions and the sequence of AP site containing AP-mRNA molecules on the 30S ribosome with respect to initiator-tRNAMet and Rps3 protein in the mRNA channel (PDB ID: 6ZTJ).

Our initial in vitro experiments show that purified 70S ribosome alone can efficiently cleave AP-mRNA at the abasic site (Figure 2A, lane 2–5) while it is not able to process the undamaged mRNA (Figure 2A, lane 7–10). Previous biochemical studies have suggested that the Dom34–Hbs1 complex in yeast (pelota in mammals) cleaves mRNA at the A site of the ribosome (54–56). However, high resolution Cryo-EM structures (PDB ID: 5M1J) showed that the α-helix1 from N terminal of Dom34 which was supposed to perform endoribonuclease activity is located ∼40 Å from the mRNA (57) (Supplementary Figure S1D). The latter results point to the presence of a mRNA AP-endoribonuclease on the ribosome itself and negate the likelihood of an external mRNA AP-endoribonuclease being recruited into the narrow mRNA entry tunnel.

Next, we split the 70S ribosome into its constituent 30S and 50S subunits and performed the mRNA AP-endoribonuclease activity of each subunit. We found that the 30S ribosomal subunit cleaves damaged mRNA at the AP-site (Figure 2B lanes 1–5), but was not able to perform AP-endoribonuclease activity on undamaged mRNA (Figure 2B lanes 6–10), while the 50S ribosome is unable to perform endoribonuclease activity on both kinds of mRNA molecules (Figure 2C). These results indicate the presence of the endoribonuclease in the 30S ribosomal subunit (Figure 2D).

30S ribosome is able to perform endoribonuclease activity on AP-mRNA in the presence of initiator-tRNAMet

Following the initial screening for endoribonuclease activity of the 70S ribosome, and its 30S and 50S subunits, we reconstituted the 30S ribosome with initiator-tRNAMet (i-tRNAMet) in the presence of several AP-mRNA molecules. When the AP-site was placed at A site on 30S ribosome in case of mRNA-AP23, the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex exhibited the AP-endoribonuclease activity on the damage-containing mRNA molecules (Figure 3A lanes 1–5) while it was unable to cleave the undamaged mRNA molecules (Figure 3A lanes 6–10). Subsequently, we positioned the AP-site in the P-site; the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex shows AP-endoribonuclease activity with greater efficiency as compared to the AP-site at A site (Figure 3B lanes 1–5). We subsequently placed the AP-site near the S3 domain in mRNA-AP26 and found that, 30S:i-tRNAMet complex efficiently performed the AP-endoribonuclease activity (Figure 3C lanes 1–5). Further we tested the endonuclease activity of 30S:i-tRNAMet complex after placing the AP-site near the KH domain. We found that the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex can also exhibit endoribonuclease activity (Figure 3D lane 1–5), though to a lesser extent as compared to other AP-mRNA molecules. With addition to these linear mRNA molecules, we also used a pseudoknot-containing structured AP-mRNA molecules with an AP-site near the A site on the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex and we found that the complex could perform AP-endoribonuclease activity (Figure 3E lanes 1–5). Lanes 6–10 in Figure 3A,B,C,D and E represents the presence of respective undamaged mRNA molecules. Overall, these results show that the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex can recognize and process the AP-site in linear and structured mRNA molecules. The quantification of cleaved product demonstrates that, 30S:i-tRNAMet complex recognizes the AP-site more efficiently when it is near the S3 domain (Figure 3F).

Rps3 protein performs endoribonuclease activity on AP-site containing mRNA molecules

Domain organization analysis of Rps3 reveals the presence of an N-terminal KH domain (1–104 amino acid residues) and a C-terminal S3 domain (111–206 amino acid residues) linked by a loop region (Supplementary Figure S1E). GST tagged Rps3 from yeast demonstrates AP-endonuclease activity on abasic site containing double-stranded-DNA (AP-ds-DNA) via the β-elimination reaction (15,32,58,59). In order to understand the role of Rps3 in the recognition of AP-sites in mRNA, we cloned and purified GST-tagged Rps3 from M. tb.H37Rv. Before performing endoribonuclease activity assays, the GST-tag was cleaved off. We found that the purified Rps3 protein exhibits AP-endoribonuclease activity on the AP-mRNA substrate (Figure 4A, lanes 1–5) and is not able to perform endoribonuclease activity on undamaged mRNA molecules (Figure 4A, lanes 6–10). Next, to identify metal dependency of the enzyme, we performed AP-endoribonuclease activity assays with increasing concentration of MgCl2. Rps3 shows optimum endoribonuclease activity in the presence of 10 mM MgCl2 (Supplementary Figure S3A). To further validate the metal dependency of the reaction, we conducted AP-endoribonuclease activity assays in the presence of 10mM MgCl2 and increasing concentration of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a metal chelator. As expected, the endoribonuclease activity of Rps3 decreases in the presence of EDTA (Supplementary Figure S3B). The latter in vitro experiments suggest that the AP-endoribonuclease activity of Rps3 is MgCl2-dependent. Next, we identified the residues responsible for the observed endoribonuclease activity. First, we deleted the KH domain as it is located outside of the mRNA entry tunnel and is unlikely to be involved in AP-endoribonuclease activity on translating AP-mRNA. We purified the S3 domain alone to test its AP-endoribonuclease activity and discovered that it can perform AP-endoribonuclease activity with nearly the same efficiency as the full-length protein (Figure 4B). While the KH domain shows only residual endoribonuclease activity on AP-mRNA (Figure 4C). We then hypothesized that the highly conserved ‘RR’ motif might play an important role for recognizing the damaged site on mRNA (Supplementary Figure S3C). This hypothesis is supported by reported genetic complementation studies in yeast that identified the active involvement of the ‘RR’ motif in endoribonucleolytic cleavage in NGD complexes (30). We then did alanine scanning mutational analysis of the RR motif and performed AP-endoribonuclease activity assays. The AP-endoribonuclease activity of the Rps3 130RR131/130AA131 mutant was found to be reduced by almost 4-fold (Figure 4D). Similar to the above assays involving the 30S:i-tRNAMet complex, we used mRNA molecules with different locations of the AP-site in the assays involving the mutants too. The endoribonuclease assays with mRNA-linear-AP substrate and Rps3 show the presence of cleaved products (Figure 4E lanes 1–5), however we did not observe cleaved products in mRNA-linear-WT which is devoid of AP-site (Figure 4E lanes 6–10). We then used mRNA-AP23, mRNA-AP26 and mRNA-AP33 for endoribonuclease activity assays and found that Rps3 can perform the endoribonuclease activity on all the tested substrates (Figure 4F,G,H lane 1–5). On the other hand, we did not observe any cleavage in assays involving the undamaged mRNA substrates (Figure 4F,G,H lane 6–10). In summary, our assay results indicate that Rps3 can perform AP-endoribonuclease activity on various types of AP-mRNA substrates. Additionally, quantification of the cleaved products demonstrated that Rps3 can perform AP-endoribonuclease activity on various types of AP-mRNA, with the highest efficiency observed on structured-AP-mRNA (Figure 4I).

The high resolution structures reveal the presence of post-cleavage mRNA molecule in the mRNA entry tunnel of 30S ribosome

To gain structural insights into mRNA AP-endoribonuclease activity of the ribosome, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the 30S ribosome with two AP-site-containing mRNA molecules, respectively. These structures include one with a pseudoknot-structured mRNA (mRNA-structured-AP) and another involving a linear AP-mRNA (mRNA-linear-AP) and are resolved to 3.34 and 3.88 Å, respectively. Structural analysis of the linear AP-mRNA-30S ribosome complex revealed the presence of a cleaved product (5′-UGUUCA-3′) within the mRNA entry tunnel, with a resolution ranging from 3.4 to 4.1 Å. Interestingly, the cleaved mRNA product was located near the positively charged regions of Rps3, Rps4 and Rps5 (Figure 5A and B). Residues R130, R131 and R163 of Rps3 are within hydrogen-bonding distance of the nitrogenous base of the cleaved mRNA product. Additionally, R20 from Rps5 and R47 from Rps4 are within hydrogen-bonding range of the mRNA’s phosphodiester backbone (Figure 5C). Furthermore, I60, V18 and F32 from Rps5 help stabilize the phosphodiester backbone of the cleaved mRNA molecule (Figure 5C). The linear AP-mRNA-bound 30S ribosome also displays the presence of immature H28 and a partially broken H44 within the mRNA exit tunnel (Supplementary Figure S4A), as previously observed in bacterial RimP- and RsmA-bound 30S ribosome structures (60). Similarly, in the case of the AP-site-containing structured mRNA, we observed the cleaved product (5′-UAAGU-3′) near Rps3, Rps4 and Rps5 (Figure 5D and E) with a resolution range of 3.2–3.9 Å, showing a conserved interaction pattern with the ribosomal proteins (Figure 5F). Analysis of the exit tunnel revealed the presence of immature H28 and a broken H44 within the mRNA exit tunnel (Supplementary Figure S4B). The density for the broken H44 was not well-resolved enough to accurately model the residues. This destabilization of H44 may be due to the absence of Rps1 in the mRNA exit tunnel. The density of partial H44 near the ribosome body is weak in the presence of AP-site-containing linear mRNA and is entirely absent with structured AP-mRNA (Supplementary Figure S4C and D). In addition to these observations, we detected SD-aSD base pairing at the A site on the platform after applying a 6 Å low-pass filter to the reconstructions. However, due to the low resolution around the SD-aSD sequence, we were unable to model the residues with more detail (Supplementary Figure S4E and F). These findings further support that the AP-endoribonuclease activity of Rps3 occurs within the mRNA entry tunnel of the 30S ribosome.

The path of the mRNA molecule inside the entry tunnel is determined by the state of translating ribosomes

Following these experiments, we analysed the canonical route of mRNA into the entry tunnel of several mRNA-bound ribosomal complexes. In the crystal structure of the 70S ribosome bound to an extended mRNA molecule within the entry tunnel (PDB ID: 6BY1) (Figure 6A) (61), the mRNA molecules are positioned near the positively charged residues of Rps3, namely R131, R130 and K135. There is an extended mRNA density beyond this positively charged patch also which highlights an intricate network of interactions between the S3 domain of Rps3 and the mRNA. Furthermore, high-resolution cryo-EM reconstructions of RNAP and the 70S ribosome in the collided state demonstrated the mRNA route into the entry tunnel near the S3 domain of the Rps3 protein (PDB ID: 6ZTM) (Figure 6B) (34). Interestingly, the cryo-EM structure of the RNAP and 70S ribosome complex in the transcription-translation coupled state, in the presence of NusA and NusG, revealed an altered mRNA path. Here, the upstream mRNA from the RNAP exit tunnel interacts with the KH and S3 domains respectively (PDB ID: 6XDQ) (Figure 6C) (62). Based on the above reports, we further examined the present cryo-EM structures of the 30S ribosome bound to structured and linear mRNA molecules to gain additional insights into the mRNA route. The cryo-EM maps clearly show mRNA near the S3 domain of Rps3 within the entry tunnel (Figure 6D and E). Overall, our observations indicate that the mRNA entry route is adjacent to the S3 domain of Rps3, except in the transcription-translation coupled state, where the route also involves the KH domain alongside the S3 domain (Figure 6F).

(C) The density map of the transcription–translation-coupled complex shows density for the mRNA molecule extending from the S3 domain to the KH domain. The head domain of 30S ribosome adopts a hyper-rotated conformation with AP-site containing mRNA molecules

The presence of mRNA helicase activity in ribosomes has been previously demonstrated in vitro using a reconstituted protein translation assay with mRNA containing secondary structures (63,64). Additionally, mutations of positively charged residues located at the mRNA entry tunnel significantly affect ribosome helicase activity and translational efficiency (63). It has been suggested that the translocation of ribosomes on mRNA provides the energy needed to unwind structured helices in mRNA (63); however, the detailed mechanism remains unclear. As mentioned earlier, the Rps3, Rps4 and Rps5 proteins are part of the mRNA entry tunnel (Supplementary Figure S4G).

Our first aim was to explore structural differences between AP-mRNA-bound 30S ribosomal subunits and the translation pre-initiation complex, which includes the 30S, IF1, IF3 and mRNA molecules (PDB ID: 5LMN) (65). We then analyzed the initiation complex containing the 30S subunit bound with IF1, IF2 and initiator tRNA (PDB ID: 6O7K) (66), the circularized tmRNA-SmpB-bound 30S ribosome (PDB ID: 6Q95) (67) and the ribosome in its inactive conformation (PDB ID: 6W7M) (68).

In the AP-mRNA-bound 30S ribosome, the distance between H18 and H34 was ∼16.1 Å, while in the pre-initiation complex, it was reduced to ∼8.4 Å. The head domain of the AP-mRNA-bound ribosome was rotated by 6.2° relative to H28, located in the latch region between the head and body domains of the 30S ribosome (Figure 7A). In the initiation complex, the distance between H18 and H34 was ∼8.0 Å, similar to that in the pre-initiation complex, but the head domain was rotated by only ∼3° compared to the AP-mRNA-bound 30S ribosome (Figure 7B).

Beak region in head is also rotated by 6.2° from the H28. (B) H18 and H34 are present 8.0Å apart in initiation 30S ribosome complex (30S, IF-1, IF-2, mRNA, PDB ID: 6O7K), and beak region shows the rotation of 3° in comparison with AP-mRNA bound 30S ribosome. (C) Stalled ribosomes were rescued by tmRNA–SmpB complex. The superimposition of the tmRNA-SmpB bound 30S ribosome (PDB ID: 6Q95) with AP-mRNA bound 30S ribosome shows the rotation of head domain by 9.4° with 10 Å distance between the H18 and H34. (D) Furthermore, we determined the structural adaptation between the inactive conformation of 30S ribosome (PDB ID: 6W7M) and AP-mRNA bound 30S ribosome. This analysis shows that structured AP-mRNA 30S ribosome shows the rotation of head domain by 4.2° and distance between H18 and H34 is 11Å.

When we superimposed the AP-mRNA-bound 30S ribosome with the tmRNA–SmpB complex, we found that the distance between H18 and H34 was ∼10 Å, significantly shorter than in the AP-mRNA-bound ribosome, with the head domain rotated by 9.4° (Figure 7C). In the inactive conformation of the 30S ribosome, the distance between H18 and H34 was ∼11 Å, also shorter than in the AP-mRNA-bound ribosome, with the head domain rotated by 4.2° (Figure 7D).

Overall, our analysis shows that in the presence of structured and linear AP-mRNA, the 30S ribosome adopts a partially inactive conformation, which may subsequently be recognized by the ribosome rescue system.

Rps3 protein plays a major role in rescuing the bacterium from oxidative and UV irradiation

To characterize the role of Rps3 protein in vivo, we created a knockdown strain in Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD) by expressing the antisense transcripts of rps3 to suppress the expression of chromosomal rps3 gene. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that the knockdown mutant expressed 77.7% Rps3 transcripts compared to the empty vector carrying strain (M.tb.H37Ra-EV) during exponential growth in vitro (Figure 8A). Furthermore, the normal growth analysis of rps3 knockdown strain and wild type M.tb. showed almost similar growth pattern during over 20 days of monitoring (Figure 8B). Subsequently, we analyzed the intracellular survival of M. tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD and wild type strain in macrophages THP-1 to monitor the intracellular survival of bacterium. PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages were infected with M.tb.H37-RaEV and M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD strains at an MOI of 10:1. The intracellular CFU values of M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD strain were significantly lower compared to the M.tb.H37Ra-EV strain at each time points (Figure 8C) which may be due to the intracellular stress conditions. To elucidate the role of Rps3 in rescuing of M.tb.H37Ra growth under oxidative stress (H2O2), alkylating agent (Methyl methane sulfonate), and UV irradiation conditions, we performed cell viability assays in above mentioned stress conditions. In these experiments, stress was induced by exposing the both (M.tb.H37-RaEV and M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD) strains to H2O2, Methyl methane sulfonate (MMS) and UV-irradiation. After incubation, the cells were washed, and their viability was assessed by spotting ten-fold serial dilutions of the cultures on supplemented Middlebrook 7H10 agar plates containing kanamycin (30 μg/mL). In the presence of H2O2, M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD strain exhibited a pronounced growth defects showing a 10-fold (8 h exposure), 100-fold (24 and 48 h exposure) and a 10-fold decrease in plating efficiency after UV irradiation compared to the M.tb.H37Ra-EV strain (Figure 8D and E). In contrast MMS did not significantly affect the growth (Figure 8F). The lack of MMS effect is not entirely clear, probably because MMS primarily introduces alkylating damages in the double stranded DNA whereas H2O2 and UV can cause damage to both DNA and RNA pools in the cells. These results indicated that M.tb.H37Ra-Rps3KD strain exhibits severe growth defects under oxidative and UV stress conditions and further underscores the roles of Rps3.

Discussion

AP-sites on DNA are efficiently repaired by the DNA Base-Excision-Repair system (69–71). On the other hand, the RNA repair system is relatively less evolved and this is exacerbated by the formation of more stable AP-sites in RNA. This imposes a significant burden on cells, and has led to the development of an efficient RNA surveillance system to identify and degrade aberrant RNA molecules (72–74). Ribosomes are generally understood to be key players that act by detecting damage in translating mRNA molecules (75–80). Translation of oxidized mRNA molecules leads to the generation of short peptides as well as inactive protein molecules (81).

The eukaryotic ribosomal protein uS3, including in humans, has been shown to exhibit AP-endonuclease activity on DNA molecules. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports of the prokaryotic Rps3’s extra-ribosomal or ribosomal AP-endoribonuclease activities have been reported. In vitro experiments using purified bacterial ribosome in the absence of other translation initiation/elongation factors revealed the presence of AP-endoribonuclease activity on AP-site containing mRNA molecules. The latter observation pointed towards the presence of an AP-endoribonuclease on the ribosome.

In our assays, we found that purified Rps3 exhibits higher activity on mRNA-structured-AP, possibly due to the structural platform provided by mRNA folding, which facilitates P-site recognition. On the other hand, the 30S ribosome shows higher activity against mRNA-AP26 probably due to the easier access to the AP-site. Surprisingly, we also observed AP-endoribonuclease activity on mRNA-linear-AP, where the AP-site is located at the P-site and is expected to form base pairing with i-tRNAMet. The AP-endoribonuclease activity on mRNA-linear-AP may take place after the displacement of i-tRNAMet.

Genetic complementation assays in yeast and associated biochemical assays have demonstrated the role of the uS3 protein in detecting damaged sites in aberrant mRNA (30). Recent findings revealed that the human uS3 peptide region (residues 53–64) crosslinks with AP-site-containing mRNA and may cleave the phosphodiester backbone. However, further experimental evidence is needed to confirm the presence of endoribonuclease activity within the 53–64 peptide region of the mRNA entry tunnel. A recent report involving the Cryo-EM structure of the E. coli RNAP bound to the 70S ribosome in the presence of NusG shows mRNA passing through the KH-domain region residues (70–79) of Rps3. This corresponds to the peptide region of human Rps3 and suggests a path taken by co-translating mRNA molecules (33,34,62,82).

In this study, we used purified Rps3 fractions to investigate its functions as an endoribonuclease on abasic mRNA and found that Rps3 actively participates in phosphodiester bond hydrolysis at the AP-site. We mutated the 130RR131 motif to 130AA131 and performed endoribonuclease activity assays with the Rps3 mutants. These mutants exhibited significantly lower activity, confirming an important role for the 130RR131 motif in endoribonucleolytic cleavage. Additionally, we observed some residual activity in the S3 domain-deleted version of Rps3, suggesting that the peptide region (residues 70–79) from the KH domain also contributes to endoribonuclease activity, although to a minimal extent.

The electrostatic potential analysis of the S3 protein from human to E. coli reveals the presence of a positively charged region in the loop of the KH domain (residues 55–64 in humans, 71–82 in M. tuberculosis and 72–83 in E. coli), which may serve as a niche for binding the phosphate backbone of mRNA molecules. In contrast, the solvent-exposed surface area of the S3 domain shows a higher concentration of positive charges, presumably providing a more favorable binding surface for nucleic acids (Supplementary Figure S5). The lower positive charge seen on the KH domain may contribute to its weaker affinity for mRNA, whereas the S3 domain can bind more strongly to mRNA molecules and effectively scan for AP-sites. Similarly, yeast genetic complementation assays have shown that the Rps3 mutant with the 116RR117 motif accumulates degraded products of NGD mRNA compared to the wildtype Rps3. This accumulation could result from two factors: first, the involvement of the peptide regions 53–64 (corresponding to 70–79 in prokaryotes) and the S3 domain, or second, the recruitment of an external endoribonuclease at the A-site of the ribosome. Finally, our in vitro experiments indicate that the 130RR131 residues are crucial for efficiently cleaving AP-site-containing mRNA molecules.

We next determined the Cryo-EM structures of the 30S ribosome in the presence of AP-site containing mRNA, viz. 5′-UTR pseudoknot containing structured and linear mRNA molecules, respectively. Interestingly, we observed density for the mRNA moiety from the AP-site at the mRNA entry tunnel. The presence of the cleaved product in the mRNA entry tunnel supports the presence of an endoribonuclease within the mRNA entry tunnel. However, we did not observe any density for the 5′-UTR region in the exit tunnel, which may be attributed to the back-flipped conformation of the anti-Shine-Dalgarno (aSD) sequence, a conformation also seen in the inactive state of the 30S ribosome. Furthermore, the H44 helix appears to be flexible in the presence of both linear and structured mRNA molecules, protruding outside the mRNA exit channel (60,68,83). The low-pass filtered density maps of both reconstructions show the presence of the SD and aSD pairing in the A site of the platform. These structural changes in the 30S ribosome lead to the adoption of a partial inactive conformation. The hyper-rotated head domain creates additional space at the A site of the 30S ribosome, which may facilitate the recruitment of downstream processing factors such as tmRNA or RNase R (11,67). tmRNA has been shown to recognize nonstop-containing mRNA molecules and may also help remove the cleaved mRNA product from the entry tunnel. In this context, as mentioned earlier, SmrB in prokaryotes is recruited to the site of stalled/collided Ribosomes and is a suggested endonuclease that cleaves damaged mRNA upstream of the site (84). On the other hand, in eukaryotes, Xrn1-mediated exonucleolytic decay is the primary way by which the NGD goes forward (85) while Cue2 is the suspected backup pathway endonuclease that cleaves mRNAs between trailing and stalled ribosomes (12).

Under oxidative and UV stress conditions, both RNA and DNA pools within cells become damaged. While the DNA repair system effectively repairs damaged DNA, lesions in mRNA are recognized by the mRNA surveillance system. To investigate the role of Rps3 in bacterial cell survival under stress conditions, we created a knockdown strain of Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra. The Rps3 knockdown strain exhibited increased sensitivity to oxidative and UV stress, although it showed no effect under methyl methane sulfonate-induced stress conditions. Notably, the Rps3 knockdown strain grew normally in enriched medium. These observations further highlight the role of Rps3 in recognizing and cleaving abasic sites in mRNA molecules. Furthermore, the knockdown experiments support that Rps3 plays a distinct and definite role in helping the bacterium counter oxidative and UV stress conditions.

Taken together, we conclude that the RR motif and to a lesser extent the KH domain of Rps3 play an important role in detecting and cleaving AP-site containing mRNA molecules. In the presence of AP-site containing mRNA molecules, the 30S ribosome adopts a hyper rotated conformation and creates a prominent space at the A site to recruit the downstream RQC processing factors (Figure 9).
