Adherence and Persistence Among Individuals Living With Type 2 Diabetes and Using a Free Combination of Basal Insulin and an Injectable Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1 Receptor Agonist Versus a Fixed‐Ratio Combination Therapy: A Japanese Database Study
Authors: Takeshi Miyatsuka, James Brash, Eleanor Davies, Elisabeth de Laguiche, Dorte Vistisen, Can Yin, Atif Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate adherence to and persistence with free combination therapy (daily basal insulin plus an injectable glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist [GLP‐1 RA]) versus fixed‐ratio combination (FRC) therapy (basal insulin plus a GLP‐1 RA) among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) in Japan.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective, observational cohort study used DeSC‐IQVIA Integrated claims data from Japan to identify adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with confirmed T2D and ≥ 1 prescription for a GLP‐1 RA (with predefined basal insulin persistence window) or an FRC during October 2019 to September 2023. Study index was date of first GLP‐1 RA or FRC prescription; follow‐up was 365 days. The primary objective was to evaluate adherence to and persistence with free combination therapy versus FRC therapy.ResultsAge (mean [standard deviation]) at index was statistically significantly higher in the free combination group (67.0 [13.85] years, n = 2063) than in the FRC group (65.7 [13.85] years, n = 2386) (p = 0.002). Odds of optimal adherence at end of follow‐up were statistically significantly higher with FRC therapy than with free combination therapy (adjusted odds ratio [95% confidence interval]: 1.96 [1.73–2.23]; p < 0.001). Risk of discontinuation by end of follow‐up was statistically significantly lower with FRC therapy than with free combination therapy (adjusted hazard ratio [95% confidence interval]: 0.66 [0.61–0.71]; p < 0.001). Adherence and persistence findings were similar in elderly individuals.ConclusionsIn this real‐world, adult population with T2D in Japan, FRC therapy was associated with improved adherence and persistence versus free combination therapy.Keywords: basal insulin, database research, fixed‐ratio combination, GLP‐1 analogue, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Diabetes, mainly comprising type 2 diabetes (T2D), remains a global challenge, with 589 million adults affected in 2025 and diabetes prevalence projected to reach 853 million by 2050 [1]. In Japan, this challenge is particularly significant, with ~10.8 million adults (12.0% of the adult population) estimated to have had diabetes in 2024; 31.3% of these individuals are estimated to have undiagnosed disease [1]. The prevalence of diabetes is highest among older adults [1]; this is especially pertinent for Japan's rapidly ageing population, with 29.3% of the population being ≥ 65 years old in 2024 [2].

Japan presents unique challenges in diabetes management. Despite a relatively low proportion of adults having a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 (4.6%) [3], the high T2D prevalence may be due to increased metabolic vulnerability; in Japanese populations, central obesity significantly increases diabetes risk at lower BMI thresholds compared with in Western populations [4, 5].

Despite the availability of various glucose‐lowering medications, glycaemic control among individuals with T2D in Japan receiving treatment often remains inadequate [6]. This is influenced by suboptimal adherence to treatment, which varies by agent type; in real‐world Japanese claims studies, a high proportion of individuals with T2D receiving oral glucose‐lowering agents achieved adherence levels of ≥ 80% (proportion of days covered) [7]. Additionally, a database study noted that 42% of individuals interrupted their basal insulin treatment within a year of initiation, while 22% discontinued treatment [8]; hypoglycaemia concerns, weight gain or treatment regimen complexity may contribute to these rates of interruption and discontinuation [8, 9]. Although fixed‐ratio combinations (FRCs) of basal insulin and a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1 RA) may offer advantages through simplified dosing, real‐world efficacy data may be limited owing to the relatively recent introduction of these FRCs to the Japanese market (IDegLira, 2019; iGlarLixi, 2020) [10, 11].

Notably, there is limited real‐world evidence (RWE) in Japan comparing adherence and persistence in individuals managing multiple concurrent injectable medications (free combination therapy) versus those receiving single‐injection FRC therapy. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant given Japan's ageing population and the documented challenges that elderly individuals with T2D face with comorbidities and complex medication regimens [12].

This retrospective cohort study investigated adherence and persistence among individuals with T2D in Japan who were using free combination therapy (daily basal insulin plus an injectable GLP‐1 RA) versus those using once‐daily FRC therapy (basal insulin plus a GLP‐1 RA), with particular focus on the elderly population (aged ≥ 75 years).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Overview

This was a retrospective, observational cohort study that utilised DeSC‐IQVIA Integrated claims data from Japan spanning 1 October 2019 to 30 September 2023. Treatments were captured as prescription records; reported data from annual health check‐ups for those aged ≥ 40 years would typically include variables such as glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and BMI. Study index was defined as the date the first prescription for a GLP‐1 RA or a once‐daily FRC was received, with prescriptions issued within the study period being considered for inclusion. The baseline study period comprised ≥ 365 days preceding index; follow‐up comprised the 365 days immediately following index (Figure S1).

This study employed a new‐user active comparator cohort design to minimise common biases of observational research. The new‐user design was implemented by restricting the analysis to individuals initiating a GLP‐1 RA or an FRC without prior exposure to these medications, bolus insulin or pre‐mixed insulin in the baseline study period. The active comparator design compared two active treatment intensification strategies: free combination therapy (GLP‐1 RA added to existing basal insulin) and FRC therapy (switch from basal insulin to an FRC). This minimised confounding by indication because all individuals had similar disease severity warranting treatment intensification beyond basal insulin alone.

Data Source

The DeSC‐IQVIA Integrated Claims Data from Japan (IQVIA Solutions Japan G.K.) comprises anonymised patient‐level claims data from Japan [13]. As one of the largest scale claims databases in Japan, it covers > 8.3 million individuals with ≥ 1 clinical event recorded in the past 12 months and includes data across three panels: the Health Insurance Association, National Health Insurance and the Latter‐Stage Elderly Healthcare System (LSEHS). Including LSEHS data enriches the database for individuals aged ≥ 75 years. The database has cumulated data for over 30 million individuals from January 2012 to September 2023.

Medications are captured as prescription records that include product, quantity, strength, indication and date of consultation. Adjudicated prescription claims include medical prescriptions, Diagnosis Procedure Combination, dental claims and specific health check‐ups. The database also contains yearly health check‐up data for individuals aged ≥ 40 years, typically including HbA1c, fasting blood glucose and low‐ and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, diastolic and systolic blood pressure, BMI and self‐reported current smoking status; however, these clinical parameters were only available for ~20% of the study population. All data are anonymised at the individual level.

Participant Selection

Individuals aged ≥ 18 years at index with a confirmed T2D diagnosis before index and ≥ 1 prescription for a GLP‐1 RA or an FRC during the study period were eligible. Furthermore, eligible prescriptions had to be dispensed from outpatient, pharmacy or hospital discharge settings. Individuals in the free combination group were also required to have a basal insulin persistence window starting with the basal insulin prescription nearest to index and extending up to or beyond the end of the index GLP‐1 RA prescription, with prescription gaps of ≤ 30 days allowed. Treatments for inclusion were: for basal insulin—insulin degludec, insulin detemir, insulin glargine U100, insulin isophane and intermediate‐acting insulin lispro; for GLP‐1 RAs—dulaglutide, exenatide, exenatide extended‐release, liraglutide, lixisenatide and semaglutide; and for FRC therapy—IDegLira and iGlarLixi.

Additional inclusion criteria ensured continuous database enrolment for ≥ 365 days before and after index. For individuals receiving free combination therapy, the cohort assignment date was determined by the first GLP‐1 RA prescription that met the basal insulin persistence requirement.

Individuals with confirmed type 1 diabetes or secondary or gestational diabetes in the index month, or who were pregnant in the 180 days before index or during follow‐up were excluded. Individuals with any prescriptions for a GLP‐1 RA, free combination therapy, bolus insulin or pre‐mixed insulin in the baseline period were also excluded, as were those with missing or invalid demographic data (e.g., age, sex).

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate adherence and persistence among individuals with T2D who received free combination therapy (daily basal insulin plus an injectable GLP‐1 RA) versus those who received FRC therapy. The secondary objective was to investigate adherence and persistence in eligible individuals aged ≥ 75 years (elderly subgroup).

Study Definitions

Adherence was assessed using the medication possession ratio (MPR), defined as the percentage of days covered by pharmacy‐dispensed medication over a prespecified period (i.e., the extent to which an individual had their medicine available to take over time). In this study, MPR was calculated as follows: MPR = (sum of days' supply during follow‐up/365 days) × 100%. Given that the actual prescription duration is not directly recorded in Japanese claims data, the days' supply between consecutive prescriptions was imputed using the gap method. This method precluded the overlapping of prescription episodes. The median gap (days) between prescriptions was calculated for the entire cohort and stratified by treatment class and dose categories for insulin (≤ 300, 301–600 and > 600 U) and by product name and dose for GLP‐1 RAs. The days' supply for each pair of consecutive claims was calculated as the minimum of either the actual gap (days) to the next claim or the median gap (days) for the corresponding treatment category [8]. For the free combination group, the MPR was calculated separately for each component, with the lowest of the two values at participant level used to represent overall adherence. Adherence was categorised as poor, low, moderate or optimal (MPR: < 40%, 40%–59%, 60%–79% or ≥ 80%, respectively).

Persistence was defined as treatment duration (days) from initiation to discontinuation. Discontinuation was defined as a gap of > 30 days between the end of a prescribed supply and the start of the next prescription [8]; this threshold was selected based on previous adherence studies [14, 15] and clinical practice patterns in Japan. Participants were classified as continuous or discontinuous (≤ 30 days or > 30 days between prescriptions, respectively). For participants in the discontinuous category, persistence was calculated up to the end date of the last prescription before the first gap of > 30 days; subsequent prescriptions were not considered. For the free combination group, persistence was calculated separately for each component; a combined persistence metric was also calculated, using the shortest duration for the two components at participant level to represent overall persistence.

For both adherence and persistence, measurements began at treatment initiation (index) and continued for 365 days (follow‐up).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were generated for all baseline characteristics and outcome measures. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation [SD]) and median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons of baseline characteristics between treatment groups, pairwise chi‐square tests with post hoc Bonferroni adjustment (categorical variables) and analysis of variance with post hoc Tukey's honestly significant difference test (continuous variables) were used, consistent with the statistical analysis plan for the parent multi‐cohort study. For the two‐group comparison presented here, these methods were mathematically equivalent to standard chi‐square and independent t‐tests, respectively.

The odds of optimal adherence (MPR: ≥ 80%) were evaluated using logistic regression models adjusting for sex, age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), hypertension status and polypharmacy status. Confounding variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). ORs > 1.0 indicated higher odds of achieving optimal adherence in the FRC group than in the free combination group. Significant p‐values for comparing MPR and persistence between treatment groups were determined post hoc using a t‐test.

Time to discontinuation was analysed using survival analysis methods. Kaplan–Meier estimators were used to generate survival curves and to calculate median persistence times. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess association between treatment type and discontinuation risk, adjusting for the same covariates as the adherence models. Results were presented as adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the global Schoenfeld residuals test for each Cox model. HRs < 1.0 indicated lower discontinuation risk in the FRC group than in the free combination group. The statistical methods outlined for adherence and persistence were also used in age‐stratified analyses to assess differences between elderly and non‐elderly participants (aged ≥ 75 years and < 75 years, respectively) across treatment groups.

Individuals with missing or invalid data for key variables, such as age and sex, were excluded. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and structured query language within the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model framework, which is maintained by the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) collaborative. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05; for baseline characteristic comparisons, this definition applied after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

DeSC‐IQVIA Integrated Claims Data from Japan identified 47 480 individuals who received their first GLP‐1 RA prescription and 13 904 individuals who received their first FRC prescription during the study period. Of these individuals, 2063 and 2386 met all eligibility criteria in the free combination and FRC groups, respectively (Figure 1).

Adherence

A lower proportion of participants in the free combination group than in the FRC group achieved optimal adherence at end of follow‐up (28.7% [n = 592] vs. 43.4% [n = 1036]; Table 2). MPR (mean [SD]) was also significantly lower in the free combination group (60.2% [27.87%]) than in the FRC group (68.5% [26.75%]) (p < 0.001). In the free combination group, the proportion of participants with optimal adherence was lower with daily basal insulin (35.0% [n = 721]) than with injectable GLP‐1 RA (65.7% [n = 1356]) (Table 3).

Persistence

Throughout follow‐up, a greater proportion of participants receiving free combination therapy versus FRC therapy discontinued treatment (Table 2). In the first 180 days after index, 48.9% of participants receiving free combination therapy and 35.3% of those receiving FRC therapy had discontinued treatment. By end of follow‐up, a greater proportion of participants in the free combination group than in the FRC group had discontinued treatment (73.5% vs. 58.9%; Table 2). In the free combination group, 65.8% of participants had discontinued the basal insulin component by end of follow‐up, while 44.5% had discontinued the injectable GLP‐1 RA component (Table 3). Persistence (mean [SD]) was lower in the free combination group (212.2 [138.09] days) than in the FRC group (256.2 [138.52] days). In the free combination group, the proportion of participants discontinuing treatment during follow‐up was higher for daily basal insulin (65.8% [n = 1358]) than for injectable GLP‐1 RAs (44.5% [n = 918]) (Table 3).

Compared with those in the free combination group, participants in the FRC group had a statistically significantly lower risk of discontinuation by end of follow‐up (HR [95% CI]: 0.66 [0.61–0.71]; p < 0.001) (Figure 2B,C). Findings were similar in elderly individuals (HR [95% CI]: 0.57 [0.51–0.65]; p < 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was not violated for the primary comparison (global Schoenfeld test p = 0.608) or for the elderly subgroup analysis (p = 0.374).

Discussion

This large‐scale real‐world study demonstrated that FRC therapy (basal insulin plus a GLP‐1 RA) provided significant clinical advantages in terms of improved adherence and persistence over free combination therapy (daily basal insulin plus an injectable GLP‐1 RA) in Japanese individuals with T2D, with particularly marked benefits in elderly individuals. Over the 365‐day follow‐up, FRC therapy was associated with nearly doubled odds of optimal adherence and a 34% reduction in discontinuation risk compared with free combination therapy. The elderly subgroup demonstrated even stronger associations, with 2.5‐fold higher odds of optimal adherence and a 43% reduction in discontinuation risk. To our knowledge, this study is the first to analyse adherence to and persistence with free combination therapy versus FRC therapy using Japanese data. Notably, a US‐based study reported similar findings when comparing iGlarLixi with free combination therapy, with a significantly greater proportion of individuals being adherent to treatment (41.3% vs. 18.7%) and persistent with it (44.8% vs. 36.3%) versus free combination therapy [16], adding further evidence that simpler treatment regimens are associated with improved adherence [17].

Importantly, the combined adherence metric used for free combination therapy showed that only 28.7% of participants had optimal adherence and were successfully maintaining both medications concurrently, revealing the real‐world challenge of dual‐therapy management. In the free combination group, component‐specific analysis revealed marked differences: optimal adherence was achieved by 65.7% of participants for the GLP‐1 RA component but by only 35.0% for the basal insulin component, representing a 47% relative reduction. This differential adherence to the components explains why combined adherence, which used the lowest of the two values, was only 28.7% despite reasonable adherence to the GLP‐1 RA component.

The substantial treatment burden in our study population highlights a critical clinical challenge. In the current study, > 90% of participants in both treatment groups were already receiving ≥ 5 concurrent medicines at baseline, substantially exceeding the 64% of older adults with T2D reportedly receiving 4–6 medications in a multinational meta‐analysis [18]. This suggests that their treatment intensification for T2D occurred in an already complex medication landscape. Previous research has shown that polypharmacy negatively affects glycaemic control and increases hypoglycaemia risk [18, 19]. Despite the high baseline polypharmacy in this study, 43.4% of participants receiving FRC therapy achieved optimal adherence, compared with only 28.7% of those receiving combination therapy, underscoring the clinical value of treatment simplification.

Reducing diabetes treatment complexity has been specifically recommended for older adults [20]. Our results contribute to previous evidence showing improved adherence to and persistence with FRC therapy [21] by demonstrating a 147‐day difference in median persistence (FRC therapy: 333 days; free combination therapy: 186 days), representing ~5 additional months of treatment continuity.

The implications of these findings extend beyond individual patient management. Older adults with T2D frequently experience functional limitations or cognitive impairment, increasing their reliance on caregivers, particularly when initiating insulin treatment [22]. The treatment burden placed on caregivers is particularly relevant in Japan, where caregiving responsibilities are often managed within families [23]. A recent Japanese study found that a greater proportion of family members and care workers than of nurses considered it burdensome to assist with or prepare injections; among the highest burdens for family members were helping when injecting insulin and handling hypoglycaemia [9]. The finding that caregivers (along with physicians and individuals with diabetes) would prioritise less frequent injections and simpler treatments supports the clinical value of FRC therapy in reducing caregiver burden in this context [9].

Adherence and persistence also affect costs associated with diabetes management, with healthcare costs for individuals with diabetes already being higher than the average medical payment per person in Japan [24]. Findings from a previous Japanese database study demonstrated that individuals with T2D who were non‐adherent to diabetes therapy in the year following diagnosis had significantly higher cumulative healthcare expenditure and occurrence of microvascular complications than those who were adherent [25]. Although total healthcare costs were similar for individuals who continued, interrupted or discontinued basal insulin in another Japanese database study, those who continued treatment had lower hospitalisation rates and inpatient costs than individuals in the other two groups [8]. Considering these findings, our observed 147‐day difference in median persistence between FRC therapy and free combination therapy suggests the potential for meaningful cost reductions through improved treatment continuity. The potential for simplifying treatment regimens combined with Japan's universal healthcare [26, 27] creates favourable conditions for improved real‐world adoption of FRC therapy in Japan. Future studies should assess the economic impact of the adherence and persistence differences observed in the current study.

Importantly, while ample evidence exists on adherence to individual regimen components, no standardised method yet captures adherence to more complex regimens, underscoring the need for more robust measures [28]. A key strength of our study included the use of combined metrics for free combination therapy, taking the lower MPR value and shorter persistence duration between components as an indicator of overall adherence and overall persistence, respectively. This conservative approach may provide a more realistic assessment of real‐world treatment effectiveness than studies that rely on component‐specific analyses alone [16].

Other methodological strengths that enhance the validity and generalisability of our findings included the use of the OMOP Common Data Model and the OHDSI framework that ensured standardised and reproducible analyses, while the new‐user active comparator cohort design minimised common biases inherent to observational studies (e.g., time‐on‐treatment bias, prevalent user bias). The broad inclusion criteria and large sample size (n = 4449) also support the generalisability of these findings to routine clinical practice in Japan.

There are also some limitations to note. Claims data are collected for administrative purposes rather than for research, possibly introducing information bias through variations in coding practices, potential misclassification or underreporting of clinical events. A structural asymmetry between comparators may have introduced differential misclassification of non‐persistence that mechanically favoured the FRC group. Specifically, in the free combination group, discontinuation of either component was classified as non‐persistence and the combined adherence metric used the lower MPR of the two components. However, basal insulin discontinuation in this group may partly reflect intentional de‐intensification following improved glycaemic control with the GLP‐1 RA, rather than non‐adherence per se, whereas component‐level discontinuation was not possible in the FRC group. Nevertheless, the substantial rate of GLP‐1 RA discontinuation (44.5%) alongside basal insulin discontinuation (65.8%) suggests that general treatment attrition, rather than selective insulin de‐intensification alone, was a material contributor. Of note, data on insulin dose were limited to broad categories, precluding formal distinction between de‐intensification and non‐adherence at the individual level; future studies with access to granular dose titration data could more precisely characterise the nature of treatment discontinuation in free combination regimens.

An additional potential limitation was the imputation of days' supply using median prescription gaps that assumed regular refill patterns that may not reflect real‐world variability, particularly during titration periods, potentially affecting persistence and adherence estimates. The requirement for continuous enrolment for follow‐up meant that the study findings applied to individuals who were still alive 1 year after index, potentially implying that the study population was healthier than the equivalent real‐world population. This potential selection bias was mitigated by the extensive coverage of the claims database: > 8.3 million individuals covered from three insurance panels spanning employer‐based, self‐employed and elderly populations, within Japan's universal healthcare system where the uninsured population is negligible. Nevertheless, the database captures a subset of national payers, and the requirement for continuous enrolment may have excluded individuals with interrupted coverage or early mortality—potentially selecting for a more stable population. The new‐user active comparator design also helped to mitigate between‐group selection bias by ensuring comparable indication for treatment intensification across comparators. Other limitations included the incomplete capture of mortality events in the database, which may have led to an overestimation of discontinuation rates, and the low proportion of individuals with HbA1c values recorded in the database, which precluded in‐depth analyses of the effects of adherence and persistence on glycaemic control. Lastly, while factors such as education level, household income, occupation and diabetes duration are established determinants of treatment adherence, these variables were not consistently captured for all participants and are therefore not accounted for in the analysis. Despite these limitations, the large sample size, comprehensive population coverage and robust analytical approach provide valuable RWE for clinical decision‐making in the management of T2D in Japan.

These findings provide comprehensive RWE supporting the potential of simpler treatment regimens, such as FRC therapy, to improve adherence to and persistence with diabetes therapy, thereby providing clinical benefits to adults with T2D in Japan, particularly among elderly individuals. Such evidence may contribute to improvements in the quality of diabetes care, both in Japan and in other countries facing the serious challenge of a super‐aged society.
