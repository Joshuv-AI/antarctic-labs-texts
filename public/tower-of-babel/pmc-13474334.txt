Gallstone ileus with spontaneous passage of the obstructing gallstone and resolution of small bowel obstruction: a case report
Authors: Tony Cheuk Tung Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallstone ileus is a rare cause of mechanical small bowel obstruction that typically occurs in elderly patients with longstanding gallstone disease. It results from migration of a gallstone into the gastrointestinal tract through a biliary-enteric fistula, where it may become impacted within the bowel lumen. We present the case of an elderly female with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and heart failure who presented with abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting. Imaging established the diagnosis of gallstone ileus, and prior imaging demonstrated underlying cholelithiasis. Follow-up imaging several days later showed spontaneous passage of the obstructing gallstone with resolution of the bowel obstruction. This case highlights gallstone ileus as an important but uncommon cause of small bowel obstruction in elderly patients and demonstrates that spontaneous resolution may occur in selected cases.

Introduction

Gallstone ileus is a rare complication of gallstone disease and accounts for approximately 1%-4% of all cases of mechanical bowel obstruction, although this proportion increases significantly in elderly patients [1]. The condition results from migration of a gallstone into the gastrointestinal tract through a biliary-enteric fistula, most commonly a cholecystoduodenal fistula.

Following entry into the gastrointestinal tract, most gallstones pass without complication. However, large stones may become impacted within the bowel lumen, most frequently within the terminal ileum, due to its relatively narrow diameter and reduced peristaltic activity [2].

Computed tomography (CT) has become the imaging modality of choice in suspected gallstone ileus as it allows direct visualization of the ectopic gallstone, bowel obstruction and pneumobilia, as well as identification of the biliary-enteric fistula [3].

Case report

An 80-year-old female presented with a 24-hour history of abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting. Her past medical history included insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and heart failure.

On examination, the abdomen was distended with mild diffuse tenderness. Laboratory investigations revealed an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 106 mg/L and lactate of 2.14 mmol/L, while hemoglobin and white cell count were within normal limits.

Initial abdominal radiography revealed multiple dilated small bowel loops with visible valvulae conniventes, consistent with small bowel obstruction (Fig. 1). Review of prior CT imaging performed in 2025 confirmed cholelithiasis within the gallbladder, supporting the presumed origin of the ectopic calculus. Following the initial CT diagnosis, the patient was reviewed by the general surgical team. Given her advanced age, multiple comorbidities, hemodynamic stability and the lack of imaging features suggesting bowel ischemia or perforation, an initial conservative approach was adopted. Management consisted of bowel rest, intravenous fluid resuscitation, nasogastric decompression as required, analgesia and close clinical observation with serial abdominal examinations. During admission, the patient’s abdominal symptoms gradually improved with resolution of vomiting and abdominal distension. Follow-up CT performed 6 days later confirmed resolution of the small bowel obstruction with restoration of normal bowel caliber, and the previously identified gallstone within the terminal ileum was no longer present (Fig. 5), consistent with spontaneous passage of the obstructing stone. Discussion

Gallstone ileus is an uncommon but clinically important cause of small bowel obstruction, particularly in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities [1,8]. The pathogenesis typically involves chronic inflammation of the gallbladder wall associated with longstanding gallstone disease. Repeated inflammatory episodes may result in adhesion formation between the gallbladder and adjacent bowel, eventually leading to erosion and formation of a biliary-enteric fistula [4].

The most common type of fistula is cholecystoduodenal, accounting for approximately 70%-80% of cases [5]. Once a gallstone enters the gastrointestinal tract, it may migrate distally through the bowel. Stones measuring greater than 2-2.5 cm are more likely to become impacted and cause obstruction [3].

The terminal ileum is the most frequent site of obstruction due to its relatively narrow lumen and reduced peristaltic activity. Less commonly, obstruction may occur in the jejunum, colon or stomach.

CT imaging has significantly improved the diagnosis of gallstone ileus. The classical radiological findings include Rigler’s triad, which consists of small bowel obstruction, pneumobilia and an ectopic gallstone within the gastrointestinal tract [2]. CT also allows identification of the fistulous communication and assessment for complications such as bowel ischemia or perforation.

Management traditionally involves surgical enterolithotomy, which removes the obstructing gallstone. Additional procedures such as cholecystectomy and fistula repair may be performed either concurrently or in a staged manner depending on patient fitness and surgical risk [6]. Although surgery remains the standard treatment, conservative management may be considered in selected patients who are clinically stable and have no evidence of bowel ischemia, perforation or peritonitis. Spontaneous passage of the obstructing gallstone has been reported, particularly when the stone has migrated distally within the gastrointestinal tract [7,8]. In elderly patients with significant comorbidities, the risks associated with operative intervention may outweigh the potential benefits, making close clinical and radiological observation a reasonable alternative in carefully selected cases.

In the present case, the patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout admission and there were no clinical or radiological features of bowel ischemia, perforation or peritonitis. Given her advanced age and significant comorbidities, a conservative approach was adopted following multidisciplinary discussion with the surgical team. Interval CT performed 6 days later confirmed spontaneous passage of the ectopic gallstone with complete resolution of the small bowel obstruction, thereby avoiding the need for operative intervention.

Conclusion

Gallstone ileus remains an important but uncommon cause of small bowel obstruction, particularly in elderly patients. CT imaging plays a critical role in establishing the diagnosis and identifying associated biliary-enteric fistulas.

This case demonstrates the typical imaging features of gallstone ileus and highlights the potential for spontaneous passage of the obstructing gallstone with subsequent resolution of bowel obstruction. Recognition of these imaging findings is essential in guiding appropriate management.
