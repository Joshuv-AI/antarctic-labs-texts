Computational Equivalence of Spiked Covariance and Spiked Wigner Models via Gram-Schmidt Perturbation
Guy Bresler, Alina Harbuzova

Abstract
In this work, we show the first average-case reduction transforming the sparse Spiked Covariance Model into the sparse Spiked Wigner Model and as a consequence obtain the first computational equivalence result between two well-studied high-dimensional statistics models. Our approach leverages a new perturbation equivariance property for Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, enabling removal of dependence in the noise while preserving the signal.



Accepted for presentation at the Conference on Learning Theory (COLT) 2025.

Introduction

A fundamental challenge in modern statistics is understanding the trade-off between statistical resource, in the form of data quantity or quality, and computational resource.
For a majority of structured statistical problems of modern interest, the best computationally efficient algorithms require significantly more data than what is theoretically sufficient in the absence of the computational constraints. This phenomenon, also known as “computational-statistical gap”, appears across a wide range of problems, including sparse linear regression
[
ZWJ14
]
, sparse principle component analysis (PCA)
[
BR13b
]
, tensor PCA
[
RM14
]
, submatrix detection
[
MW15
]
, community recovery
[
HWX15
]
, and many others
[
BB20
]
. A thriving line of research aims to determine the fundamental limits of computationally efficient algorithms for the extensive array of modern statistical problems.

Traditional computational complexity theory
focuses on problems whose input data are worst-case, i.e., adversarially chosen. In contrast, statistics and machine learning problems usually involve data generated by a benign process, often modeled by specific distributions. As a result, traditional worst-case complexity techniques, which primarily rely on reductions that relate one problem to another, do not directly apply to the modern
average-case
or
distributional
problems.

To address this gap, a recent line of work focused on developing the

,

). These average-case reductions relate two problems of interest by precisely transforming one data distribution into another. This fundamental relationship between problems goes beyond merely transferring computational feasibility and demonstrates that their associated statistical and computational phenomena has the same root.

In parallel, researchers have sought to analyze the limits of
restricted classes of algorithms
, including sum-of-squares
[

,

[

. The limitations of this approach to hardness analysis are clear – each time a new class of algorithms gains prominence, the entire collection of statistics problems of interest must be analyzed from scratch, one at a time. In this sense, by elucidating connections between problems themselves in an algorithm-independent way, average-case reductions are “future-proofed”.

A central triumph of the worst-case complexity theory is the
classification of problems into equivalence classes
, which constitutes a major conceptual simplification:
one may study any
single
problem from the class in place of them all. In contrast, existing average-case reductions do not establish equivalence classes like NP-complete problems for the worst-case setting. Instead, they create partial order of hardness: webs of reductions starting at planted clique or lattice problems relate diverse statistical problems but lack the
two-way

,

[

, but only for planted submatrix problems that differ in entry distributions rather than structure. The paper

[

]
makes a significant conceptual advance by showing that random graphs with correlations in the form of added triangles constitute an equivalence class. While this result achieves the first equivalence between problems with different dependence structure, the setting remains artificial.

The motivation for this paper is the eventual goal of classification of statistical problems into equivalence classes with the implication that problems in the same class are in a precise sense fundamentally the same, rather than coincidentally similar. In this work we take a step towards such classification by proving several computational equivalence results between two of the most well-studied high-dimensional statistics problems: the Spiked Covariance Model and Spiked Wigner Model.

For both models, it is often assumed that the signal vector

Sparse PCA has a vast literature and was the first well-studied high-dimensional statistics problem shown to have a computational-statistical gap

1.4
Average-Case Reductions

Any average-case problem is associated with a distribution over instances. An
average-case reduction
is an (efficient) algorithm (or Markov kernel) that maps instances of one average-case problem to another, with the requirement that the
distribution
of the outputs is close in total variation to the target distribution. The existence of such reduction guarantees that any algorithm solving the target problem can be used to solve the source problem.

We formally define average-case reductions between problems like
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
and
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
for detection in Sec.
A
and for recovery
4
4

4

As explained in Sec.
B
we slightly restrict the allowed class of recovery algorithms to simplify the analysis.
in Sec.
B
.
A key consequence of these reductions (Lem.
A.2
and
B.2
) is that computational complexities are transferred: if an
O(d^{C})
-time average-case reduction
from one point to another (e.g. from
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
to
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
) is proved, then the existence of an
O(d^{M})
-time algorithm in the small neighborhood of the latter point (
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
) yields an
O(d^{M}+d^{C})
-time algorithm for the first point (
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
). All our reductions run in
O(d^{C}=d^{2+\gamma})
-time.

We pay special attention to what we call “average-case reductions at the computational threshold”, which are formalized in Def.
6
, Sec.

5

As described in Sections

2
Overview of Ideas

The
\mathsf{SpCov}
and
\mathsf{SpWig}
data distributions are different in a fundamental way. While both can be viewed as “noisy observations” of the rescaled signal
uu^{\top}
, the entries of the
\mathsf{SpWig}
matrix are jointly independent, whereas the entries of the empirical covariance matrix for
\mathsf{SpCov}
samples exhibit the complex dependence structure of the Wishart distribution. This distinction makes devising an average-case reduction from
\mathsf{SpCov}
to
\mathsf{SpWig}
challenging,
as few techniques are known for removing dependencies while preserving the underlying signal.

To address this challenge, we introduce several novel techniques for dependence removal and structure manipulation. This section overviews the main ideas.

We first use a CLT result for Wishart matrices to show that the two models’ distributions are close in total variation if
n\gg d^{3}
,
establishing a bijective equivalence between
\mathsf{SpCov}
and
\mathsf{SpWig}
in this regime.
9
9

9

Since the models are close in TV, no reduction is needed.

For the more challenging
n\ll d^{3}
regime, where the models are genuinely different (their total variation distance is nearly 1), we develop new tools to remove the dependencies while preserving the signal. To start, we overcome the
n\gg d^{3}
barrier by leveraging a CLT for
subsets
of Wishart matrices, and build an average-case reduction with canonical parameter correspondence (
5
) from
\mathsf{SpCov}
to
\mathsf{SpWig}
for all
n\gg d^{2}
.

Next, we introduce a more powerful dependence-removal method based on Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, which succeeds at the computational threshold in the range
n\gg d
for sparse signal
k\leq\sqrt{d}
. The key challenge, described below, is showing a certain
perturbation equivariance
property of Gram-Schmidt.

2.4
Two Novel Procedures: “Flipping” and Bernoulli Denoising

“Flipping”.

Our matrix
Y
from (
11
) and the
\mathsf{SpWig}
distribution differ due to the cross-term.
Observe that the cross-term
ug^{\top}\mathtt{GS}(X^{(2)})
is sparse only in the rows
but not the columns
. To obtain the
\mathsf{SpWig}
signal structure, which is sparse in both rows and columns, we propose the following procedure we call “flipping”.

Let
\dot{Z}^{(1)}
and
\ddot{Z}^{(1)}
be the outputs of the cloning procedure applied to
Z^{(1)}
.
We create two copies of the projection of interest,
Y^{(1)}=(\dot{Z}^{(1)})^{\top}\mathtt{GS}(Z^{(2)})
and
Y^{(2)}=(\ddot{Z}^{(1)})^{\top}\mathtt{GS}(Z^{(2)})
.
Notice that the cross-term
ug^{\top}\mathtt{GS}(X^{(2)})
of
Y^{(1)}
is sparse in the rows and the cross-term
\mathtt{GS}(X^{(2)})^{\top}gu^{\top}
in
\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}Y^{(2)}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}^{\top}
, i.e., the flipped copy, is sparse in the columns. We consider

Y=\mathsf{sign}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}Y^{(1)}\odot(Y^{(2)})^{\top}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\,,

where
\odot
denotes the entrywise multiplication.
Observe that conditioned on
u
and
g
,
Y
has jointly independent entries. Because
\mathsf{sign}(\mathcal{N}(a,1)\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,1))\sim\textsf{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
for any
a
, the signal in
Y
is sparse in both directions. In particular, only entries
Y_{ij}
, where both
i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(u)
, contain the signal.

This “flipping” procedure is a subroutine of our orthogonalization reduction in Sec.
9
, and is analyzed as part of Theorem
9.2
.

Bernoulli Denoising.

Following the flipping procedure, we use our novel Bernoulli Denoising step described in Section
6.1
, which allows us to remove unwanted perturbations in the means of the entries of
Y
that contain the signal.

Notice that our flipping procedure involves discretization of
Y
to
\pm 1
values. It turns out that we can show that entries
Y_{ij}
for
i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(u)
are of the form
\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)
, where
\mathsf{Rad}(t)
denotes a random variable taking
\pm 1
values such that
\E\mathsf{Rad}(t)=t
. Here
\Delta\ll a
represents an undesired noise whose size we do not know and
a
is the desired signal level. We propose a denoising procedure designed to reduce
\Delta
relative to
a
when given multiple samples from
\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)
. The main idea is to construct a polynomial of the noisy samples and fresh
\mathsf{Rad}(a)
samples, such that the resulting variable is distributed as
\mathsf{Rad}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}C(a^{M}-(-\Delta)^{M})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}
for some constant
C
and power
M
depending on the number of available noisy samples:

\text{poly}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\underbrace{\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta),\dots,\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)}_{N\text{ noisy inputs}},\underbrace{\mathsf{Rad}(a),\dots,\mathsf{Rad}(a)}_{\text{fresh r.v.s}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\sim\mathsf{Rad}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}C(a^{M}-(-\Delta)^{M})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\,.

Then, in a setting when
a\approx 1
and
\Delta\ll 1
, this procedure decreases the undesired noise relative to the constant mean:

\Big|\frac{\Delta}{a}\Big|^{M}\ll\frac{\Delta}{a}\,.

Equally important, it keeps the signal
a\approx a^{M}\approx 1
almost the same. The algorithm is outlined as Algorithm
4
with its guarantees stated in Lemma
6.1
.

The output of these procedures – flipping and Bernoulli Denoising – is a matrix of
\pm 1
entries that resembles the spiked Wigner matrix in that it has independent entries and correct sparse signal strength and structure. Our final step – Gaussianization of matrix entries, by now a standard procedure in the average-case reductions literature (see Section
5.3
) – lifts the entries of
Y
from discrete
\pm 1
values to Gaussian-distributed entries while retaining the signal in the entry means, concluding our reduction in Section
9
.

5.1
Properties of Total Variation

The analysis of our reductions will make use of the following well-known facts and inequalities concerning total variation distance (see, e.g.,
[
PW25
]
). Recall that the total variation distance between distributions
P
and
Q
on a measure space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
is given by
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}}|P(A)-Q(A)|
.

Fact 5.1

(TV Properties)
.

The distance
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
, satisfies the following properties:

1.

(Triangle Inequality) For any distributions
P,Q,S
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, it holds that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,S)+d_{\mathrm{TV}}(S,Q)\,.

2.

(Conditioning on an Event) For any distribution
P
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
and event
A\in\mathcal{B}
, it holds that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}P(\,\cdot\,|A),P\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=1-P(A)\,.

3.

(Conditioning on a Random Variable, Convexity) For any two pairs of random variables
(X,Y)
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
each taking values in a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, it holds that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(X),\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(Y^{\prime})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(X|Y=y),\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime}=y)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\,,

where we define
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(X|Y=y),\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime}=y)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=1
for all
y\not\in\textnormal{supp}(Y^{\prime})
.

4.

(DPI) For any two random variables
X,X^{\prime}
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
and any Markov Kernel
A:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, it holds that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(A(X)),\mathcal{L}(A(X^{\prime}))\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{L}(X),\mathcal{L}(X^{\prime})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\,.

The following simple consequence of the data processing inequality will be useful.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
A
is independent of
X
and
X^{\prime}
, then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X+A,X^{\prime}+A)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X,X^{\prime})\,.

and

\mathsf{KL}(P\|Q)=\E_{X\sim P}\log\frac{dP}{dQ}(X)
.

Lemma 5.3

(KL-Divergence Between Gaussians)
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be positive definite matrices. Then

\mathsf{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1})\|\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\log\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}-n+\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}\Sigma_{1})+(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{-1}(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})\Big]\,.

In particular, by Pinsker’s inequality,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},I_{n}),\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},I_{n}))\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},I_{n})\|\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},I_{n}))}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\|_{2}\,.

) and the bound (

) above, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4

(TV Between Gaussians with Shifted Means)
.

Let

be random variables with

independent of

and

Finally, we will need the following standard lemma bounding the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between isotropic Gaussians with a random shift. Recall that the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two distributions
P
and
Q
on a measure space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
is defined as
\chi^{2}(P\|Q)=\E_{X\sim Q}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\frac{dP}{dQ}(X)-1\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}^{2}
.

Lemma 5.5

(
\chi^{2}
-Divergence Between Mixtures of Isotropic Gaussians)
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a random variable independent of
X
. Then

1+\chi^{2}(X+A\|X)=\E\exp(\langle A,A^{\prime}\rangle)\,,

where
A^{\prime}
is an i.i.d. copy of
A
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple application of the so-called “Ingster trick”.
Note that

X+A\sim\E_{A}\mathbf{P}_{A}\,,

where
\mathbf{P}_{A}
is the law
\mathcal{N}(A,I_{d})
.
We start by writing the likelihood ratio

\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)=\frac{\exp(-(X-A)^{\top}(X-A)/2)}{\exp(-X^{\top}X/2)}=\exp(X^{\top}A-A^{\top}A/2)\,.

This now implies, from the definition of
\chi^{2}
-divergence and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(X+A\|X)+1

\displaystyle=\E_{X\sim\mathbf{P}_{0}}\Big(\E_{A}\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)\Big)^{2}=\E_{A,A^{\prime}}\E_{X\sim\mathbf{P}_{0}}\Big(\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A^{\prime}}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)\Big)\,.

To evaluate the inner expectation w.r.t.
X
, one can complete the square, obtaining

\E_{X\sim\mathbf{P}_{0}}\Big(\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{A^{\prime}}}{d\mathbf{P}_{0}}(X)\Big)=\exp(\langle A,A^{\prime}\rangle)\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.1
Bernoulli Denoising

Recall that
\mathsf{Rad}(a)
denotes a random variable taking
\pm 1
values, such that
\E\mathsf{Rad}(a)=a
. In our reduction, we work with Rademacher random variables of the form
\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)
, where
\Delta\ll a
represents an undesired noise whose size we do not know. We propose a denoising procedure designed to reduce
\Delta
relative to
a
when given multiple samples from
\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)
. The main idea is to construct a polynomial of the noisy samples and fresh
\mathsf{Rad}(a)
samples, such that the resulting variable is distributed as
\mathsf{Rad}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}C(a^{M}-(-\Delta)^{M})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}
for some constant
C
and power
M
depending on the number of available noisy samples:

\text{poly}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\underbrace{\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta),\dots,\mathsf{Rad}(a+\Delta)}_{N\text{ noisy inputs}},\underbrace{\mathsf{Rad}(a),\dots,\mathsf{Rad}(a)}_{\text{fresh r.v.s}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\sim\mathsf{Rad}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}C(a^{M}-(-\Delta)^{M})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}\,.

Then, in a setting when
a\approx 1
and
\Delta\ll 1
, this procedure decreases the undesired noise relative to the constant mean:

\Big|\frac{\Delta}{a}\Big|^{M}\ll\frac{\Delta}{a}\,.

Equally important, it keeps the signal almost the same. The algorithm is outlined in Algorithm
4
with its guarantees stated in Lemma

.

\mathtt{Denoise}(X_{1},\dots,X_{N},a)

.

Appendix A
Definitions of Average-Case Reductions for Detection

In this section we formally define the statistical detection problems and associated average-case reductions. Analogous definitions for recovery problems and reductions are in Sec.
B
.

A.1
Detection Problems

We identify two main ingredients of a planted problem: a
collection of planted distributions
and a
problem task
. In this section we focus on the detection task.

Collections of Planted Distributions.

We will define a planted problem on a collection of planted distributions
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
over
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}P_{u}^{\mu,N}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\,.

Here
N
is the main sequence index (in our case this will be dimension),
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
is a fixed problem parameter independent of
N
,
u\in\mathcal{U}
is the planted signal from an allowed collection
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{N}
, and
P_{u}^{\mu,N}
is a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
corresponding to parameter
\mu
and the planted signal
u
.
For both Spiked Wigner and Spiked Covariance Model we take
d
as the sequence index and consider
\mathcal{U}_{d}\in\{\mathcal{B}_{d}(k),\overline{\mathcal{B}_{d}}(k)\}
. For
\mathsf{SpCov}
we consider
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,d}_{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
, where
\mu=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and for
\mathsf{SpWig}
we consider
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,d}_{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
, where
\nu=(\alpha,\beta)
.

Both models describe
families
of such collections, as parameters
\mu,\nu
vary. Each fixed point of
\mu
or
\nu
corresponds to a specific planted problem, whose complexity might differ from the one of a different planted problem with parameters
\mu^{\prime}
or
\nu^{\prime}
. Moreover, every value of the dimensionality parameter
d
implies a separate collection of distributions.

For simplicity, we often conflate the notation for a problem defined on a collection of distributions and the collection of distributions itself, i.e.
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
, when it is clear whether we consider detection or recovery from the context.

Detection Task.

Fix
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{N}
and consider a collection of planted distributions
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
. Let
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
be a function mapping a parameter
\mu
to a corresponding “null hypothesis” parameter
\mu^{0}=f(\mu)
. Denote the collections of distributions induced by
\mu
and
\mu^{0}=f(\mu)
as follows:

\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}:=\mathcal{P}^{\mu^{0},N}_{\mathcal{U}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}:=\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}\,.

The detection problem for
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
is a task of distinguishing between
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}
and is defined as follows.

Definition 3

(Detection Problem)
.

Given a sample
X
from an unknown distribution
P_{u^{\star}}\in\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}\bigcup\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}
, the detection problem is the task of distinguishing between events
H_{0}=\{P_{u^{\star}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}\}
and
H_{1}=\{P_{u^{\star}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}\}
. In particular, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}\to\{0,1\}
solves the detection problem with error
\delta_{N}
if the Type
I+II
error does not exceed
\delta_{N}
, i.e.,

\sup_{P_{u^{\star}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim P_{u^{\star}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{A}(X)=1\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}+\sup_{P_{u^{\star}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim P_{u^{\star}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{A}(X)=0\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\leq\delta_{N}\,.

For both Spiked Wigner and Covariance Models, a natural detection problem is to distinguish between the
null hypothesis
, where the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) parameter
\lambda
(corresp.
\theta
) is zero, and the
planted hypothesis
, where
\lambda
(corresp.
\theta
) take some nonzero value. Formally, for parameters
\mu=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
for
\mathsf{SpCov}
and
\nu=(\alpha,\beta)
for
\mathsf{SpWig}
, we consider functions
\mu_{0}=f(\mu)=(\alpha,-\infty,\gamma)
,
\nu_{0}=g(\nu)=(\alpha,-\infty)
. In correspondence to the definition above, the detection task for
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
is to distinguish between
H_{0}:\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu_{0})
and
H_{1}:\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
and for
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
between
H_{0}:\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu_{0})
and
H_{1}:\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
.

Moreover, we say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
solves the
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
(or
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
) detection problem with Type
I+II
error
\delta
if in the definition above we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\delta_{N}\leq\delta\,.

A.2
Average-Case Reductions in Total Variation

We now formally define an average-case reduction between two detection problems. We start with a definition for mapping
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
for fixed dimensionality parameter
N
.

Definition 4

(Average-Case Reduction for Detection from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
)
.

Fix parameters
\mu,\nu
and two collections of planted signals
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{N},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
be two collections of planted distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}_{N}}
respectively. Consider the two induced detection problems with corresponding hypothesis
\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}},\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}
and
\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime},H_{0}},\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime},H_{1}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}
.

We say that a
O(N^{C})
-time (possibly randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}_{N}}
for
C=O(1)
is an
average-case reduction with TV-error
\epsilon_{N}
from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
if for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
, there exists some
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\text{if }X\sim P^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{0}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}},\text{ then }

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X),{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{0}})\leq\epsilon_{N}\,,

\displaystyle\text{if }X\sim P^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{1}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}},\text{ then }

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X),{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{1}})\leq\epsilon_{N}\,.

The following lemma states the complexity implications of such a reduction.

Lemma A.1

(Implication of Reduction for Detection from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
be two detection problems defined as above. Assume there exists an
O(N^{C})
-time average-case reduction for detection from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
with parameter
\epsilon_{N}
from the definition above. If there is an
O(N^{M})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
achieving Type
I+II
error at most
\delta_{N}
, there exists a
O(N^{M}+N^{C})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
achieving Type
I+II
error at most
2\epsilon_{N}+\delta_{N}
.

Proof.

Given a sample
X
from
P_{u}\in\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{0}}\bigcup\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U},H_{1}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}
and the
O(N^{M})
-time detection algorithm
\mathcal{B}:\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}_{N}}\to\{0,1\}
, consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
defined as

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\,.

Clearly, the runtime of
\mathcal{A}
is a sum of the runtimes of the two subroutines
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}
:
O(N^{M}+N^{C})
.
From the definition of total variation distance,

\displaystyle\Big|\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X^{\prime}\sim{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{0}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}(X^{\prime})=1\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}-\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{0}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=1\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{0}},\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{N},\text{ for }X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{0}}

\displaystyle\Big|\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X^{\prime}\sim{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{1}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}(X^{\prime})=0\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}-\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{1}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=0\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}{Q}^{\nu,N}_{u^{\prime},H_{1}},\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{N},\text{ for }X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{1}}\,.

This yields the following bound on the Type
I+II
error for algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{0}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=1\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}+\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}_{X\sim{P}^{\mu,N}_{u,H_{1}}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathcal{B}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\mathcal{A}^{\text{red}}(X)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=0\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\leq\delta_{N}+2\epsilon_{N}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Notably, while Def.
4
of an average-case reduction is specific to a particular value of the dimensionality parameter
N
, we study the complexity of problems
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu),\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
, where both
\mathsf{SpCov}(\mu)
and
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
describe a
sequence
of collections of distributions with
d=1,2,\dots
. To address this, we define an average-case reduction between problems
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}_{N=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}_{N=1}^{\infty}
, where
\mu,\nu
are independent of the dimensionality parameter
N
.

Definition 5

(Average-Case Reduction for Detection, Sequence)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}_{N=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\{$}}}\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\}$}}}_{N=1}^{\infty}
for fixed parameters
\mu,\nu
independent of
N
.
We say that an
O(N^{C})
-time algorithm
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}\to\{0,1\}\}
is an
average-case reduction for detection from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
if
for any sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}=\mu
, there exist a sequences
\{\nu_{i},N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and an index
i_{0}
, such that

1.

\lim_{i\to\infty}\nu_{i}=\nu
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}N_{i}=\infty
;

2.

for any
i>i_{0}
,
\mathcal{A}_{N_{i}}
is an average-case reduction for detection from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N_{i}}_{\mathcal{U}_{N_{i}}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu_{i},N_{i}}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N_{i}}}
with the total variation parameter
\epsilon_{N_{i}}
from Def.
4
;

3.

\lim_{i\to\infty}\epsilon_{N_{i}}=0
.

Note that fully analogous definition for recovery is presented in Def.
9
.

A.3
Implications of Average-Case Reductions for Phase Diagrams

We now apply Lemma
A.1
to the Def.
5
of an average-case reduction to obtain an implication of average-case reductions to
phase diagrams. We state the following lemma for the case of reductions between
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}
and
\mathsf{SpWig}
to simplify the notation
18
18

18

One can easily generalize the statement to the case of a reduction between any planted detection problems
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
with well-defined “Hard” regions in their phase diagrams.
.

Lemma A.2

(Implication of Reduction for Detection for Phase Diagrams)
.

Consider the detection problems associated with
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu),\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
for
\mu\in\Omega^{\mathsf{Cov}}_{\gamma},\nu\in\Omega^{\mathsf{Wig}}
and assume there exists an
O(d^{C})
-time average-case reduction for detection from
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)
to
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
(Def.
5
).
Fix
\epsilon>0
and assume there is a
O(d^{M})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu^{\prime})
for all
\nu^{\prime}\in B_{\epsilon}(\nu)
achieving asymptotic Type
I+II
error
\delta
. Then there exists a
O(d^{M}+d^{C})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)
achieving asymptotic Type
I+II
error at most
\delta
.

An analogous implication holds for average-case reduction for detection from
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
to
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)
.

Proof.

Consider any sequence
\{\mu_{i}=(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}=\mu
and
\beta_{i}\leq\beta
(i.e. the SNR in
\mu_{i}
is at most the SNR in
\mu
). From Def.
5
, there exist sequences
\{\nu_{i},d_{i},\epsilon_{d_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and an index
i_{0}
, such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\nu_{i}=\nu
,
\lim_{i\to\infty}\epsilon_{d_{i}}=0
,
\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{i}=\infty
and for any

, there exists an average-case reduction for detection from
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)
in dimension
d_{i}
to
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu)
in dimension
d_{i}
with total variation parameter
\epsilon_{d_{i}}
from Def.
4
.

Consider index
i^{\prime}
, such that for all
i>i^{\prime}
we have
\nu_{i}\in B_{\epsilon}(\nu)
.
From Lemma
A.1
, we conclude that since there exists an
O(d^{M})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathsf{SpWig}(\nu_{i})
in dimension
d_{i}
achieving an error
\delta_{d_{i}}
, there exists an
O(d^{M}+d^{C})
-time algorithm for detection for
\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu_{i})

d_{i}

\delta_{d_{i}}+2\epsilon_{d_{i}}

\mu_{i}

harder

\mu

\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)
in dimension
d_{i}

\delta_{d_{i}}+2\epsilon_{d_{i}}

\mathsf{SpCov}_{\gamma}(\mu)

\mathsf{SpCov}_{gamma}(\mu_{i})
.

Finally, note that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\delta_{d_{i}}+2\epsilon_{d_{i}}\leq\delta+0=\delta\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

\mu_{i}\in\Omega^{\mathsf{Cov}}_{\gamma}

for all

Appendix B
Definitions of Average-Case Reductions for Recovery

In this section we formally define the statistical recovery problems and associated average-case reductions. We extends our definitions for average-case reductions for detection from Sec.
A
.

B.1
Recovery Problems

Similarly to detection problems, the recovery problems are defined on a collection of planted distributions
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
over
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
. The difference lies in the task - signal recovery instead of the signal detection.

Recovery Task.

Fix parameter
\mu
and a collection of allowed signals
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{N}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
be a collection of planted distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
and let
\ell_{N}:\mathcal{U}_{N}\times\widehat{\mathcal{U}_{N}}\to[0,+\infty]
be a loss function.

Definition 7

(Recovery Problem)
.

Given a sample
X\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}
from an unknown distribution
P_{u^{\star}}^{\mu,N}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
, a
recovery problem on
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
with a loss function
\ell_{N}
is a task of producing an estimate
\widehat{u^{\star}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{U}_{N}}
, such that the expected loss
\ell_{N}
is minimized. In particular, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{N}}\to\widehat{\mathcal{U}_{N}}
solves the recovery problem with expected loss at most
\epsilon_{N}
if

\sup_{u^{\star}\in\mathcal{U}_{N}}\E_{X\sim P_{u^{\star}}^{\mu,N}}\ell_{N}(u^{\star},\widehat{u^{\star}}=\mathcal{A}(X))\leq\delta_{N}\,,

for some
\delta_{N}\geq 0
.

While most of our results for
\mathsf{SpWig}
and
\mathsf{SpCov}
are applicable to an arbitrary choice of the loss function
\ell_{d}
and the threshold
l^{\star}_{d}
, our reduction at the computational threshold in Section
9
slightly alters the signal
u
, preserving the support and the strength. Thus, we slightly restrict the recovery problems to the following setting.

We assume
\widehat{\mathcal{U}_{d}}=\{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:\|u\|_{0}\leq k\}
- that is, the recovery algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{d}}\to\widehat{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
produces a
k
-sparse unit
\widehat{u^{\star}}
. Moreover, we let

\ell_{d}(u^{\star},\widehat{u^{\star}})=1-\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\langle u^{\star},\widehat{u^{\star}}\rangle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}^{2}\,,

and let
l^{\star}_{d}=1-k^{-1/2}d^{2\delta}
for some small
\delta>0
, i.e., we say that
\mathcal{A}
solves the recovery problem if

\sup_{u^{\star}\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\E_{X\sim P_{u^{\star}}^{\mu}}\ell_{d}(u^{\star},\widehat{u^{\star}}=\mathcal{A}(X))\leq\epsilon_{d}\leq 1-k^{-1/2}d^{2\delta}\,.

This choice of
\ell_{d}
aligns with the most common approach to planted rank-1 signal estimation - solving
\text{arg}\max_{u:\|u\|_{2}=1,\|u\|_{1}=k}{u^{T}\Sigma u}
, where
\Sigma
comes from either
\mathsf{SpWig}
or the empirical covariance matrix of
\mathsf{SpCov}
samples.

[
CLM16
]
show that the best achievable loss
\ell_{d}^{\min}
by (any) algorithm for
\mathsf{SpCov}
is

\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\ell_{d}^{\min}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}^{2}\leq\inf_{\widehat{u^{\star}}}\E\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\ell_{d}(u^{\star},\widehat{u^{\star}})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}^{2}=O(1)\frac{(\theta+1)k}{n\theta^{2}}\,.

For
\theta=\sqrt{k^{1+\eta}/n}=\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\cdot k^{\eta/2}
with
\eta\in(0,1)
, we have
\ell_{d}^{\star}=O(k^{-\eta/2})=o(1)
, and therefore, our restriction for an algorithm to achieve expected loss at most
l_{d}^{\star}=1-k^{-1/2}d^{2\delta}=1-o(1)
is mild.

Moreover, we say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
solves the
\mathsf{SpWig}
(or
\mathsf{SpCov}
) recover problem with loss
\delta
if in the definition above we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\delta_{N}\leq\delta\,.

B.2
Average-Case Reductions in Total Variation

We now formally define an average-case reduction between two recovery problems. We start with a definition for mapping
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
for fixed dimensionality parameter
N
.

Definition 8

(Average-Case Reduction for Recovery from
\mathcal{P}^{\mu,N}_{\mathcal{U}}
to
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}
)
.

Fix parameters
\mu,\nu
and two collections of planted signals
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{N},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{N}

\mathcal{Q}^{\nu,N}_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}

O(N^{C})
-time average case reduction for recovery with parameters
\epsilon_{N},f,C^{\prime}

Proof.

Given a sample
