Emotions, emotion regulation, and eating behavior in women with a healthy weight and women with obesity: A daily life study
Authors: Mégane Ackermans, Vera Heininga, Peter de Jong, Nienke Jonker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a worldwide public health issue, and it is crucial to identify individual risk factors for excessive food intake, as these may help inform obesity prevention/treatment efforts. One potential reason for which some individuals, but not others, overeat and become obese is food consumption in response to emotions, particularly in the absence of effective emotion regulation (ER) strategies. The current study used Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) to assess the effect of emotions and ER on eating in daily life in women with obesity (n = 46) and women with a healthy weight (n = 54). After completing a baseline questionnaire, participants responded to an EMA survey regarding momentary emotions, ER, and eating behavior five times per day for seven days. Women with obesity used less adaptive ER than women with a healthy weight in daily life. Positive emotions were prospectively associated with more unhealthy food choices in daily life. Negative emotions and ER were not prospectively associated with any eating behavior in daily life. Our findings do not support the notion that improving ER in response to negative emotions could be an effective intervention to reduce food consumption. Yet, in light of our findings regarding positive emotions, future studies could test whether improving ER in response to positive emotions could help individuals manage unhealthy food choices.Keywords: daily life, eating, emotion regulation, emotions, experience sampling, food intake, obesity

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a worldwide public health issue, and global obesity rates have tripled in recent decades (WHO, 2025). Obesity is defined as “abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a risk to health” and is characterized by a body mass index (BMI) > 30 (as opposed to a healthy weight, characterized by a BMI < 25; WHO, 2025). Although the modern obesogenic environment is characterized by high availability of energy‐dense food, powerful food cues, and a sedentary lifestyle (Adams & Sterrett‐Hong, 2021; Zheng et al., 2009), many individuals do manage to avoid becoming obese. As such, individual differences must be responsible for differing eating behaviors, leading some individuals to become obese and others to maintain a healthy weight. It is crucial to identify (malleable) individual risk factors for excessive food intake, as these may help inform obesity prevention and treatment efforts (Appelhans et al., 2011). One potential reason for which some individuals engage in excessive consumption (i.e., consuming more calories than are physiologically required) and become obese is that they eat in response to emotions (e.g., Debeuf et al., 2018; Evers et al., 2018). The current study therefore focuses on food consumption in response to emotions, particularly in the absence of effective emotion regulation (ER) strategies. The moment‐to‐moment emotional processes through which eating decisions emerge in daily life and how these dynamic, within‐person processes differ between individuals with and without obesity constitutes an important research gap. Addressing this research gap could help us gain an understanding of psychological mechanisms underlying obesity, which could help inform obesity interventions.

Positive emotions and eating

Positive emotions can affect eating. In the current study, we refer to emotions as “complex and multifactorial immediate responses, which translate into physical and psychological changes in the individual [which can] in turn, significantly influence thought and behavior” (Fuente González et al., 2022, p.2). We refer to positive emotions as responses informing an individual of a favorable, satisfactory stimulus or situation (Fuente González et al., 2022). Theories suggest that, on the one hand, food is often associated with celebratory occasions, such as weddings and birthdays, creating an associative learning mechanism in which positive emotions become associated with food (Devonport et al., 2019). Positive emotions have also been identified as a justification to rationalize food indulgence according to the self‐licensing theory (De Witt Huberts et al., 2012). Furthermore, the hedonic properties of food may be heightened when experiencing positive emotions, such that individuals derive more pleasure from eating when in a good mood (Evers et al., 2013). On the other hand, positive emotions may increase personal resources making it easier to resist palatable food (Fredrickson, 2001) or may generate a desire to maintain a good mood by not giving in to indulgence (Andrade, 2005; Fedorikhin & Patrick, 2010). Despite mixed theories, the general trend in the literature points toward greater food consumption following positive emotions, as evidenced by meta‐analyses of laboratory studies (Cardi et al., 2015; Evers et al., 2018). However, little is known about positive emotions and eating in daily life (Engel et al., 2016).

Indeed, most studies on positive emotions and eating are based on retrospective questionnaire data (e.g., Crockett et al., 2015; Kass et al., 2019; Moss et al., 2021; Ozier et al., 2008). These only describe trait‐level patterns rather than state‐level behaviors and do not account for the recall bias often occurring in self‐report studies. Other studies were experimentally conducted in a laboratory setting (e.g., Ahlich et al., 2026; Appelhans et al., 2011; Evers et al., 2010). This may cause distorted eating because of heightened self‐awareness and cannot account for social aspects of eating or reactivity to emotions in daily life (Reichenberger et al., 2018). Though some studies have used Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) to test the relationship between positive emotions and eating (e.g., Boh et al., 2016; Reichenberger et al., 2018), few studies have compared the relationship between positive emotions and eating in individuals with overweight or obesity versus individuals with a healthy weight (Evers et al., 2018). Evidence from retrospective studies suggest individuals with overweight report less or equivalent eating following positive emotions than individuals with a healthy weight (van Strien et al., 2016). One study that did use EMA to assess (positive) emotions preceding eating events in daily life found that individuals with a healthy weight and individuals with overweight or obesity were more cheerful before eating than at non‐eating moments, with no difference between the groups (Boh et al., 2016). However, this study only assessed what individuals ate and did not include other measures of eating behavior such as overeating or hedonic versus physiological motivations for eating. Another EMA study assessed the role of (continuous) BMI in the relationship between emotions and eating and found that a lower BMI was related to more eating out of hunger in response to positive emotions than a higher BMI, but the study did not specifically assess differences between weight groups (Reichenberger et al., 2018). As such, research from both retrospective and EMA studies suggest that a higher weight is related to less or equivalent eating in response to positive emotions than a lower weight, although daily life studies specifically assessing a differential relationship between positive emotions and eating in individuals with obesity versus individuals with a healthy weight are currently lacking. These are needed to improve our understanding of the role of positive emotions in obesity.

Negative emotions and eating

Negative emotions may also play an important role in eating. We refer to negative emotions as responses informing an individual of an unfavorable stimulus or situation (Fuente González et al., 2022). Theories suggest that experiencing negative emotions can lead individuals to eat more to provide comfort or distraction (Crockett et al., 2015). Such eating in response to negative emotions is then negatively reinforced by the alleviation of negative emotions and positively reinforced by the rewarding properties of food (Parker et al., 2006). Despite theories suggesting increased intake following negative emotions, empirical studies have shown mixed results, as evidenced by a meta‐analysis of laboratory studies that found that, overall, negative emotions were not related to increased or decreased consumption (Evers et al., 2018). Weight status, however, may be relevant in this relationship; a recent review found that individuals with obesity reported eating more in response to negative emotions than healthy‐weight individuals (Braden et al., 2025). Here, also, retrospective and laboratory studies contain several limitations, and assessing the relationship between negative emotions and eating in daily life is important. Although several studies have used EMA to test this relationship, many studies focused specifically on binge eating or loss of control eating as an outcome (e.g., Bicaker et al., 2022; Heron et al., 2014), whereas others only assessed stress as a predictor rather than a range of negative emotions (Reichenberger et al., 2021). Moreover, some studies did include individuals with obesity but did not assess differences according to weight status (e.g., Ahlich et al., 2023; Franja et al., 2021). One study did use EMA to assess the role of (continuous) BMI in the relationship between negative emotions and eating and found that a higher BMI was related to more eating out of hunger in response to negative emotions than a lower BMI, but the study did not specifically assess differences between weight groups (Reichenberger et al., 2018). As such, findings from both retrospective and EMA studies suggest that a higher weight is related to more eating in response to negative emotions than a lower weight, although no EMA study has compared the relationship between negative emotions and eating in individuals with obesity versus individuals with a healthy weight. Such studies are needed to improve our understanding of the role of negative emotions in obesity.

In addition to a research gap regarding differing relationships between emotions and eating in daily life according to weight status, previous EMA studies did not account for how individuals regulate negative emotions. This is crucial given that rather than negative emotions per se being responsible for overeating, the regulation of negative emotions may play an important role (Evers et al., 2010).

Emotion regulation, eating, and weight

Individuals differ in the way they tend to regulate their emotions. ER refers to “an individual's ability to identify, understand, and accept emotional experiences; control impulsive behaviors when distressed; and flexibly modulate emotional responses as situationally appropriate” (Gouveia et al., 2019, p. 274). ER strategies can impact one of the three core emotion components: cognition, behavior, and physiological arousal. As such, emotions can be regulated with (1) cognitive strategies (e.g., distraction, reappraisal, rumination), (2) behavioral strategies (e.g., suppression, engagement), and (3) physiological strategies (control of autonomic arousal; De France & Hollenstein, 2017). Some of these ER strategies (e.g., rumination, suppression) are risk factors for psychological maladjustment and have been characterized as maladaptive. The use of such strategies is related to maladaptive behaviors, including overeating, binge eating, and eating in response to emotions without being hungry (Evers et al., 2010; Selby et al., 2008). In contrast, adaptive strategies (e.g., engagement, relaxation, distraction, reappraisal; Dixon‐Gordon et al., 2014) are related to behavior that is in line with long‐term goals (De France & Hollenstein, 2017), which may include weight loss/maintenance.

Several theoretical models explain why maladaptive ER or a lack of adaptive ER could be associated with overeating. These models view eating as a form of ER that is used when an individual does not have access to other ER strategies. For instance, the psychosomatic theory and the affect regulation theory suggest individuals overeat in an attempt to decrease negative emotions (Hawkins & Clement, 1984; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1957), and the theory of emotional eating views eating as a coping strategy to regulate emotional distress (Bruch, 1974). If an individual lacks effective ER strategies and turns to eating to regulate emotions, this may result in overweight or obesity in the long term. Some studies have indeed shown that individuals with obesity use less adaptive and/or more maladaptive ER than healthy‐weight individuals. Specifically, one study found that adults with obesity reported using more emotion suppression than those with a healthy weight (Andrei et al., 2018), and others found that children, adolescents, and adults with obesity reported more difficulties in ER than those with a healthy weight (Obara‐Gołębiowska, 2025; Percinel et al., 2018). Another study found that adolescents with obesity reported using more maladaptive strategies (specifically, rumination and self‐blame) and less adaptive strategies (specifically, reappraisal, acceptance, and positive refocusing) than adolescents with a healthy weight (Tan et al., 2018). Taken together, these results suggest individuals with obesity likely employ less adaptive and more maladaptive ER than individuals with a healthy weight. However, no study has assessed ER differences in daily life between individuals with obesity and with a healthy weight. Given the limitations of retrospective studies, assessing these differences in daily life constitutes an important step to close this research gap.

In addition to improving our understanding of ER differences according to weight status in daily life, further research is needed regarding the relationship between ER and eating in daily life. The relationship between ER and eating has thus far mostly been examined via laboratory and retrospective studies. For instance, an experimental study using an ER manipulation in a laboratory setting found that those given reappraisal instructions ate less than those given suppression or no instructions (with no difference between the latter two groups; Taut et al., 2012), whereas another found that those given suppression instructions ate more than those given reappraisal or no instructions, (with no difference between the latter two groups; Evers et al., 2010). As such, laboratory studies showed mixed results, with adaptive ER causing less or unchanged eating and maladaptive ER causing more or unchanged eating. Studies using retrospective questionnaires are more convergent, with two such studies showing that individuals who reported greater ER difficulties also reported more habitual overeating (Obara‐Gołębiowska, 2025) and more eating in response to emotions (Guerrini‐Usubini et al., 2023). However, given the aforementioned limitations of retrospective and laboratory studies, assessing the relationship between ER and eating in daily life is paramount. Addressing these limitations, some studies have used EMA to assess this relationship in daily life. Several studies have found that poorer ER was related to more loss of control eating in binge eaters (e.g., Parker et al., 2021; Romano, Peterson, Anderson, et al., 2025), although these studies focused specifically on loss of control eating (in samples of binge eaters) rather than other measures of eating behavior. One study that included overeating as an outcome measure found that poorer ER mediated the association between negative affect and overeating in women, but only included women with ED pathology (Romano, Peterson, Forester, et al., 2025). Moreover, one EMA study found that distraction was related to less food consumption, whereas suppression was not related to food consumption, although the study focused specifically on the application of these strategies in response to a desire for food, rather than in response to emotions (Lopez et al., 2021). Finally, one study found that maladaptive ER was not associated with snacking, eating out of hunger, or eating out of a desire to eat in daily life, but the study only assessed ER retrospectively and did not include a momentary ER measure (Debeuf et al., 2018). As such, the (prospective) association between momentary ER and a range of eating outcomes in daily life remains to be tested.

The current study

The purpose of this study was therefore to assess the relationship between emotions and eating and between ER and eating in women with obesity and women with a healthy weight in an ecologically valid fashion. We only focused on women in our study because women have been found, on average, to report eating in response to emotions more often than men (Guerrero‐Hreins et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2020; Zaiser et al., 2025). Moreover, women more often attribute psychological reasons (including eating in response to emotions) as a cause of their own overweight or obesity (Henning et al., 2024) and more often seek weight loss treatment than men (Crane et al., 2017). EMA allowed us to employ longitudinal sampling with high frequency, thereby providing insight into emotions and ER on a moment‐to‐moment basis in a natural setting (as opposed to a laboratory setting), and how these may contribute to excessive food consumption.

More specifically, the current study was designed to test the following three hypotheses: (1) positive and negative emotions are prospectively associated with increased eating in the 3 h thereafter, and these associations are relatively stronger in individuals with obesity; (2) individuals with obesity use less adaptive and more maladaptive ER strategies than individuals with a healthy weight; (3) less adaptive ER and more maladaptive ER in response to negative emotions is prospectively associated with increased eating in the 3 h thereafter in both individuals with a healthy weight and with obesity.

METHOD

Participants

The participants were Dutch women recruited via FlyCatcher, a Dutch internet research organization with its own panel representative of the Dutch population (FlyCatcher Panel). Individuals can create an account to become a member of FlyCatcher Panel to participate in a variety of online studies. Panel members receive an email with an invitation to participate in a study if it pertains to their demographic. In the current study, the participants consisted of two groups based on a screening survey: a healthy‐weight group (self‐reported BMI 18.15–25) and an obese group (self‐reported BMI > 30). For the healthy‐weight group, women with a current eating disorder diagnosis and/or treatment were excluded because this could complicate interpretation of findings. For the obese group, women with current weight‐loss or eating disorder treatment were excluded as treatment may alter the relationship between emotions and eating (Power et al., 2025).

According to our power analysis (see Supporting Information), we required 50 participants per group to achieve 80% power. In total, 340 participants met the inclusion criteria according to the screening survey and were invited to participate in the study. Of these participants, 247 (73%) completed the baseline survey (125 obese, 122 healthy weight). A total of 219 participants began the EMA portion of the study (110 obese, 109 healthy weight). To minimize the risk of biased responses and enhance the likelihood of accurately capturing daily‐life relationships, we only included respondents who completed at least 70% of the EMA questionnaires, resulting in the targeted sample size of 100 (46 obese, 54 healthy weight). We did not conduct complete case analysis because this would mean discarding all participants with missing values, leading to greatly reduced power. There was no difference between the two weight groups on attaining 70% compliance (χ 2[1, N = 219] = 1.02, p = .31). There was also no difference in age, t(188) = 1.20, p = .21, baseline adaptive ER, t(213) = 0.35, p = .72, or baseline maladaptive ER, t(205) = 1.36, p = .18 between those who did and did not attain 70% compliance.

The average BMI was 34.49 in the obese group (SD = 3.92, range = 30.04–49.02) and 22.37 (SD = 1.72, range = 18.51–24.98) in the healthy‐weight group. The participants of the obese group were on average slightly older (M = 54, SD = 12.74) than those in the healthy‐weight group (M = 50, SD = 17.63), t(245) = 2.15, p = .03. Information on education levels can be found in the Supporting Information.

Measures

Screening Survey

Weight status

The participants filled out their height and weight. Their BMI was then calculated, and only participants with a healthy weight or with obesity were invited for the study.

Eating disorder history

The participants were asked: “Have you ever been diagnosed with an eating disorder?” If they indicated “yes,” they were asked “With which eating disorder have you been diagnosed?” and “Is this eating disorder diagnosis currently applicable?” The participants were subsequently asked: “Have you ever been in treatment for an eating disorder (e.g., with a psychologist or psychiatrist?)” and “Have you ever been in treatment to lose weight (e.g., with a dietitian, coach or psychologist?)”.

Baseline survey

Eating pathology

To assess eating disorder symptoms for descriptive purposes, we used the Dutch version of the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE‐Q; Fairburn & Beglin, 2008). The EDE‐Q assesses eating disorder thoughts and behaviors in the past 28 days. It contains 28 items, six of which are open questions and 22 of which are rated on a 7‐point scale. We use the total score of the 22 items as a measure of overall eating pathology (in line with Aardoom and colleagues' [2012] study which found total EDE‐Q score to be a valid assessment of eating disorder pathology). Internal consistency of total EDE‐Q score was good in the obese group (Cronbach's alpha 0.83) and in the healthy‐weight group (Cronbach's alpha 0.87).

Emotion regulation

We assessed ER with the Regulation of Emotion Systems Survey (RESS; De France & Hollenstein, 2017), a 48‐item questionnaire assessing the use of six ER strategies to downregulate the experience of negative emotions 1 : distraction, rumination, reappraisal, suppression, engagement, and relaxation. The RESS was translated from English to Dutch by the first author (a native speaker of both languages), and the translation was verified by the other authors. Responses are rated on a 5‐point scale from 1 (never) to 5 (always). A score was calculated for each of the six ER strategies by averaging all items pertaining to that strategy. A total adaptive ER score was calculated over adaptive ER strategies (reappraisal, engagement, relaxation, distraction; Gross & John, 2003; Sheppes & Meiran, 2007; Zijlstra et al., 2012), and a total maladaptive ER score over maladaptive ER strategies (rumination and suppression; Gross, 2002; Nolen‐Hoeksema & Harrell, 2002; Zijlstra et al., 2012).

EMA questionnaire

Negative and positive emotions

In line with previous EMA studies (Boh et al., 2016; Macht et al., 2004), emotions were assessed with seven items that reflect different aspects of emotional states (Janke & Debus, 1978). In response to the question “How do you feel?” participants rated the extent to which they felt “angry/annoyed,” “anxious/scared,” “sad/gloomy,” “tense/stressed,” “bored,” “calm/relaxed,” and “cheerful/happy” on a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). These emotions were chosen because previous studies found them to be relevant in the context of eating (Macht, 1999; Macht & Simons, 2000). The number of items was kept small to improve compliance and to minimally interfere with daily activities (Macht et al., 2004). The emotions were translated by the first author, and the translation was verified by the other authors. The negative emotions variable was calculated as the within‐person average of the five negative emotions. Internal consistency was acceptable between subjects (Cronbach's alpha 0.70) and good within subjects (Cronbach's alpha 0.88). The positive emotions variable was calculated as the within‐person average of the two positive emotions. Internal consistency was good between subjects (Cronbach's alpha 0.81) and excellent within subjects (Cronbach's alpha 0.92). 2

Momentary emotion regulation

We assessed momentary ER with the 12‐item Regulation of Emotion Systems Survey–Ecological Momentary Assessment (RESS–EMA; Medland et al., 2020). Specifically, it assesses the use of distraction, rumination, reappraisal, suppression, engagement, and relaxation in daily life. After indicating how they felt (negative and positive emotions), the participants were asked to select “the strongest negative emotion experienced in the last hour”, or select “I have not felt any negative emotion since the last measurement”. If they selected the former option, they were then asked to respond to 12 statements beginning with “In response to my emotion …” followed by an action such as “I continually thought about what was bothering me” or “I expressed my feelings.” Each statement was answered on a VAS ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). If the participant indicated not having felt any negative emotion, the ER questions were omitted. The RESS–EMA was translated by the first author, and the translation was verified by the other authors. The variable adaptive momentary ER strategies reflected the within‐person average of adaptive ER strategies (i.e., reappraisal, engagement, relaxation, distraction), and the variable maladaptive momentary ER strategies reflected the within‐person average of the adaptive ER strategies (i.e., rumination and suppression).

Food choice and eating behavior

We assessed four eating‐related variables: (1) food choice, (2) hunger‐driven eating, (3) taste‐driven eating, and (4) overeating. To assess food choice, the participants were presented with 18 food images. They were asked to indicate which image most resembled the first thing they ate since the last measurement. The images were obtained from the authors of a previous EMA study assessing eating‐related cognitions and emotions and were categorized as healthy or unhealthy in line with that study (Boh et al., 2016). This measure of food choice allowed us to assess general patterns of healthy/unhealthy food choices and provided a clear operationalization of healthy and unhealthy options (thereby minimizing noise in the measure). The unhealthy options consisted of hamburger, muffin, cookies, candy bar, chips, cake, ice cream, pizza, and dish with a side of fries, whereas the healthier options consisted of sandwich, yoghurt, cornflakes, salad, fruit, soup, pasta dish, rice dish, and dish with a side of potatoes. The outcome variable “food choice” was coded as 0 = “nothing” (reference category), 1 = “healthy,” and 2 = “unhealthy.” For hunger‐driven and taste‐driven eating, the participants rated on a VAS the extent to which they ate because of hunger (0 = not hunger‐driven – 100 = solely hunger‐driven) and because of taste (0 = not taste‐driven – 100 = solely taste‐driven; Reichenberger et al., 2018). Finally, the participants rated the extent to which they overate (i.e., ate past physiological satiation; 0 = not at all – 100 = very much). If the participants indicated that they had another eating moment, all eating‐related questions were repeated for each additional eating moment, up to four. However, only the measures from the first eating moment at each assessment were used. The full list of EMA questions is shown in Table S1.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Psychology of the University of Groningen (PSY‐2021‐S‐0268). All FlyCatcher Panel members were sent an email from FlyCatcher with a link to a brief screening questionnaire. The participants that met the selection criteria received an email explaining the study and asking for informed consent. After giving consent, the participants received a link with the baseline survey (lasting approximately 20 min), which they had to complete within 1 week. The survey contained the EDE‐Q; RESS; and several questions on sleep, physical activity, and dieting success. Following this survey, the participants completed the EMA questionnaires containing the emotion questions, the RESS–EMA and the eating measures (lasting approximately 2 min) five times per day for seven consecutive days. They received five emails from FlyCatcher per day with a link to the questionnaire at 9.15 a.m., 12.15 p.m., 3.15 p.m., 6.15 p.m., and 9.15 p.m. We chose a quarter past the hour because we believed it would increase compliance in the case that participants had commitments that were scheduled on the hour (e.g., a meeting from 11 a.m. to 12 p.m.). To limit recall bias, the link was deactivated after 60 min, and the participants could no longer respond. The participants received € 1.80 for the initial questionnaire, € 0.10 for each daily questionnaire, and an additional € 10 for completion of the entire study, adding up to a maximum of € 15.30 paid in gift cards. The participants of this study did not give written consent for their data to be shared publicly, and the data are not fully anonymized (FlyCatcher maintains the link between ID numbers and participant information), so supporting data are not publicly available for this study. Analysis plan

Group characteristics, emotions, and eating

The groups were first described in terms of BMI, and t‐tests were conducted for age and EDE‐Q score. To test differences in positive and negative emotions in daily life between the two groups, we conducted two t‐tests with weight status as the predictor variable and positive and negative emotions as outcome variables, respectively. To test differences in eating in daily life, we conducted a Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) with hunger‐driven eating, taste‐driven eating, and overeating as outcome variables. A significant MANOVA was followed up with t‐tests on each eating measure. For food choice, we conducted a Chi‐Squared test with food choice (nothing/healthy/unhealthy) as the outcome variable. A significant Chi‐Square test was followed up with multiple comparison Chi‐Squared tests.

To test whether positive and negative emotions were prospectively associated with increased eating in daily life, and whether this prospective association was stronger in individuals with obesity, we performed a total of eight separate multilevel analyses. To test the prospective relationship between positive emotions and the four eating variables (i.e., food choice, hunger‐driven eating, taste‐driven eating, and overeating), we regressed each eating variable at a given time point (t) on person‐mean centered positive emotion scores at the previous time point (t − 1). To assess whether the association differed between the two groups, we added a cross‐level interaction between the level of positive emotions (level 1) and weight status (level 2). We repeated the four multilevel analyses for negative emotions. To correct for multiple testing and prevent an inflated alpha level, we applied a Bonferroni–Holm correction.

First, to test whether individuals with obesity used less adaptive and more maladaptive ER strategies at baseline than individuals with a healthy weight, we performed a MANOVA with weight status as the independent variable and the mean adaptive and mean maladaptive RESS scores as the dependent variables. Second, to assess the differences in ER strategies in daily life between the two groups, we conducted a MANOVA with mean adaptive and mean maladaptive RESS–EMA scores as outcome variables. Significant MANOVAs were followed up with independent samples t‐tests on adaptive and maladaptive ER. The MANOVA assumptions of multivariate normality, homoscedasticity, and linearity of dependent variables were checked and reported upon if violated.

To test whether less adaptive ER and more maladaptive ER in response to negative emotions were prospectively associated with increased eating, we performed separate multilevel analyses for adaptive and maladaptive strategies for each of the four eating variables (for a total of eight multilevel models). To test the prospective association between adaptive ER and each of these four variables, we regressed each eating variable at a given time point (t) on person‐mean centered adaptive ER (used in the past 3 h) measured at time point (t). The same process was repeated for maladaptive ER. Here we also applied a Bonferroni–Holm correction. Post hoc, we also conducted exploratory analyses by repeating the analyses above with each individual ER strategy.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics

Given that it was not possible to skip questions in the baseline survey, the baseline survey had no missing data. In the EMA portion, of the 3500 measurements (35 timepoints × 100 participants), 3107 (89%) emotion measurements; 597 (17%) ER measurements; 3 3106 (89%) food choices; and 2419 (69%) hunger‐driven, taste‐driven, and overeating measurements were completed. There was no difference in the number of missing data points on any of these variables between the two groups.

Regarding eating pathology, the participants of the obese group had higher EDE‐Q scores (M = 3.64, SD = 2.04) than those in the healthy‐weight group (M = 2.47, SD = 1.92), t(245) = 4.65, p < .001. Group differences regarding emotions and eating scores in daily life are shown in Table 1. On average, women with obesity scored higher on positive emotions and lower on negative emotions in daily life than women with a healthy weight. They also differed on continuous eating measures, F(3,2415) = 17.53, p < .001, η 2 = 0.021. Specifically, women with obesity engaged in less hunger‐driven eating and more taste‐driven eating than women with a healthy weight. The two groups did not differ in overeating. Yet, the groups differed in food choice, χ2(2) = 14.56, p < .001. Specifically, women with obesity less frequently ate healthy foods than women with a healthy weight. All effects were small.

Hypothesis 1: Positive and negative emotions are prospectively associated with increased eating in daily life, and this relationship is stronger in women with obesity. DISCUSSION

Main findings

The current study examined the relationship among emotions, ER, and eating in women with a healthy weight and women with obesity in daily life. Women with obesity used less adaptive ER strategies compared to women with a healthy weight in daily life. In addition, higher positive emotions were prospectively associated with more unhealthy food choices at the next eating moment, but not with overeating, hunger‐driven eating, or taste‐driven eating. Finally, neither negative emotions nor (mal)adaptive ER strategies were prospectively associated with eating at the next eating moment.

Emotions and eating in daily life

Women with obesity experienced more positive and less negative emotions in daily life than women with a healthy weight. They also engaged in more taste‐driven eating and less hunger‐driven eating and made less healthy food choices. In line with Hypothesis 1, we also found that positive emotions were prospectively associated with more unhealthy food choices compared to not eating in the hours thereafter (but no group difference). However, we did not find a prospective association between positive emotions and hunger‐driven eating, taste‐driven eating, or overeating. Our results corroborate a finding from an EMA study that found that individuals experienced more positive emotions before consuming high‐caloric foods than before non‐eating moments (Boh et al., 2016). Moreover, our findings align with those of a previous EMA study, which also found no relationship between positive emotions and hunger‐driven eating, but contrast with the positive relationship between positive emotions and taste‐driven eating in the same study (Reichenberger et al., 2018). However, another EMA study also found no prospective association between positive emotions and overeating in women (Sultson et al., 2022).

Placing our findings in the context of several theories, our results align with the self‐licensing theory, that is, that positive emotions could be used as a justification to rationalize indulgence (De Witt Huberts et al., 2012), at least to rationalize unhealthy food choices. However, this justification for indulgence does not seem to have extended to overeating or taste‐driven eating in our study. In line with this, the proposed increased pleasure derived from the hedonic properties of food (Evers et al., 2013) appears to have been achieved with unhealthy food choices, but without increased overeating or taste‐driven eating in our study. In turn, the lack of overeating or taste‐driven eating in response to positive emotions in our study aligns with the broaden‐and‐build theory of positive emotions, which suggests that positive emotions increase psychological resources allowing for greater self‐regulation (Fredrickson, 2001). Finally, the finding that positive emotions were not prospectively associated with hunger‐driven eating (contrary to Hypothesis 1) may be because hunger‐driven eating is a homeostatic process that is not easily affected by external factors such as emotions.

Overall, given that the relationship with positive emotions was only found for one out of four eating outcome variables in our study, we must consider that this may have been an outcome‐specific effect. The four measures of eating behavior in our study measure different aspects of eating behavior that may be differentially related to emotions. Previous EMA studies have used a variety of eating measures with a variety of results (e.g., Boh et al., 2016; Reichenberger et al., 2018; Sultson et al., 2022), highlighting the complexity of accurately measuring true eating behavior in daily life and how it relates to emotions and to weight. It is possible that only food choice is affected by emotions, as was found in our study, but it is also possible that the other measures did not accurately capture true eating behavior. Future EMA studies are needed to establish which measures best capture eating in daily life, and how these are related to emotions and to obesity. Ultimately, finding robust associations between positive emotions and eating could help determine whether improving the regulation of positive emotions could be a promising treatment target for weight loss. Finally, given that our study only included women, studies including men are needed to determine whether the relationship between positive emotions and unhealthy food choice extends to men.

Contrary to Hypothesis 1, we did not find a prospective association between negative emotions and eating. The finding regarding hunger‐driven eating is in line with the aforementioned EMA study, which also found no association between negative emotions and hunger‐driven eating (Reichenberger et al., 2018), possibly because of the homeostatic nature of hunger‐driven eating. Overall, our null findings corroborate a meta‐analysis of laboratory studies, which found no difference in eating between negative emotion conditions and control conditions (Evers et al., 2018). Although some theoretical models propose that eating is used to regulate negative emotions, our findings suggest this may not be the case for everyone. One testable explanation could be that only subgroups, for example, restrained eaters, eat in response to negative emotions. Indeed, Macht's model (2008) of emotion‐induced eating suggests that restrained eaters eat more in response to negative emotions, whereas non‐restrained eaters do not, a notion that aligns with findings from a meta‐analysis (Evers et al., 2018). In addition to the role of restraint, Macht proposes that emotional arousal also affects eating. That is, high‐arousal negative emotions are proposed to be associated with decreased eating, whereas low‐arousal negative emotions are proposed to be associated with increased eating, especially in restrained eaters. From this perspective, our null findings may reflect heterogeneity in emotion‐induced eating processes. As such, accounting for the role of restraint and emotional arousal in future EMA studies is important to assess whether eating in response to certain negative emotions occurs in certain subgroups of individuals in daily life.

Also contrary to expectations, we did not find an interaction between weight status and emotions on eating. In line with this, the aforementioned meta‐analysis of laboratory studies did not find a differential relationship between emotions and eating depending on weight status (Evers et al., 2018). Moreover, Reichenberger et al. (2018) also did not find BMI to be a moderator of the relationship between negative emotions and taste‐driven eating. However, a higher BMI (compared to a lower BMI) was related to a stronger relationship between negative emotions and hunger‐driven eating (Reichenberger et al., 2018). Another study found that individuals with obesity reported more “emotional overeating” than participants with a healthy weight (Obara‐Gołębiowska, 2025). One testable explanation could be that not weight status, but rather emotional eating status plays an important role in eating in response to emotions. That is, Macht's model (2008) proposes that those scoring high on emotional eating eat more in response to negative emotions than those scoring low on emotional eating. Given that either weight group could score high on emotional eating, we speculate that this could help explain why we found no difference between the groups. However, this is only a speculation, as we did not assess emotional eating in our study. Future EMA studies could include emotional eating assessments, although current measures of emotional eating lack predictive validity (Bongers & Jansen, 2016) and developing alternative ways to assess emotional eating is crucial in order to improve our understanding of how this trait‐level characteristic may influence the relationship between emotions and eating (Evers et al., 2018).

In addition to the potential role of emotional eating, there may be other reasons for why we did not find a difference between the groups regarding emotions and eating. First, it is possible that the quantities of unhealthy food consumed differed between the groups and that this was not captured by our “overeating” measure. Especially because the two groups did not differ on overeating, we speculate that the obese group may not have recognized excessive consumption as “overeating.” Indeed, individuals with obesity typically have impeded satiety signals (Della Guardia & Shin, 2024) and have been found to underreport food consumption (Wehling & Lusher, 2017). As such, the subjective experience of overeating used in our study may not have been an appropriate measure of actual consumption. Future EMA studies could use more objective measures of food intake, such as participant‐captured food images, which have been found to be feasible according to a pilot study (Harrington et al., 2021).

Overall, our study offers preliminary evidence that experiencing positive emotions is prospectively associated with unhealthy food choices in individuals both with and without obesity. It is therefore unclear whether this phenomenon could be a contributing factor to the development and maintenance of obesity. Despite our tentative evidence for the role of positive emotions in food choice, more EMA studies are needed to test the robustness of the relationship between positive emotions and eating. Finally, EMA research taking into account restraint and emotional eating can help determine in which individuals and under which circumstances positive and negative emotions are associated with eating.

Obesity and ER strategies

In the baseline survey, women with obesity and women with a healthy weight did not differ in ER. In daily life, however, women with obesity used less adaptive strategies than women with a healthy weight (in line with Hypothesis 2). The lower use of these adaptive strategies in women with obesity compared to women with a healthy weight in our study suggests that this may play a role in weight gain. Interestingly, we did not find a difference between the two groups regarding the use of strategies considered to be maladaptive (i.e., rumination and suppression; Selby et al., 2008). This indicates that although a lack of adaptive strategies may be related to obesity, the use of maladaptive strategies may not. Our retrospective findings corroborate some other retrospective studies which found no difference in adaptive ER depending on weight status (Andrei et al., 2018; Zijlstra et al., 2012). These studies do, however, contrast with our daily life findings of less adaptive ER in those with obesity. Moreover, the earlier studies did find greater suppression (a maladaptive strategy) in those with obesity, which contrasts with both our retrospective and our daily life findings.

Retrospective ER questionnaires tap into abstract beliefs about how one typically regulates emotions, whereas our daily questionnaires capture momentary fluctuations in ER (Medland et al., 2020). Such momentary assessments of ER may offer insights into behavioral changes across a 3‐h timespan more accurately than the more general trait‐level characteristics. Despite contrasts with previous studies, the greater ecological validity of our study makes it plausible that a lack of adaptive ER plays a role in the difficulty of maintaining a healthy weight. Our study also suggests that maladaptive ER strategies do not affect weight, although more research using EMA is necessary to rule out their potential role and to provide further support for the role of adaptive strategies in healthy weight maintenance. Moreover, future EMA studies could also include men to test whether men with obesity also use less adaptive ER than men with a healthy weight.

ER and eating in daily life

Although we expected maladaptive ER or a lack of adaptive ER to be (partially) responsible for increased eating (Hypothesis 3), we did not find the level of momentary ER to be prospectively associated with any eating variable. Despite several theories suggesting eating is used to downregulate negative emotions, our findings do not corroborate these theories. Placing our findings in the context of these theories, several boundary conditions may be relevant to consider. First, negative emotions remained relatively low in our study, and it is possible that only at a higher level of negative emotions is eating used as a strategy to regulate these emotions. Second, we speculate that these models may not apply for individuals who use non‐food strategies to regulate emotions (e.g., smoking, alcohol, exercise). Third, given that restraint may play an important role in the relationship between emotions and eating (Macht, 2008), it is possible that the role of restraint extends to ER models such that specifically restrained eaters use eating to regulate emotions.

Our findings are in line with another EMA study in adolescents, which found that maladaptive ER was not associated with snacking, eating out of hunger, or eating out of a desire to eat in daily life (Debeuf et al., 2018). Most other studies were retrospective studies and assessed individual ER strategies rather than a combined measure of adaptive and maladaptive strategies. To the extent we can relate our findings to previous research, our findings contrast with two laboratory studies that found that suppression (a maladaptive strategy) was associated with greater consumption than reappraisal (an adaptive strategy; Evers et al., 2010; Taut et al., 2012) and another that found that distraction (an adaptive strategy) was associated with decreased eating in participants with obesity but not with a healthy weight (Appelhans et al., 2011). Some important differences between laboratory studies and EMA studies may offer insight into these contrasting findings. First, a single, “imposed” ER strategy in a laboratory setting may not reflect the dynamic, multidimensional process of ER in real‐life settings. Although some strategies may affect eating at the single, relatively restricted moment of measurement in the laboratory, this only offers a “snapshot representation of a process that is essentially in constant movement” (Evers et al., 2018, p. 205). Second, laboratory studies assessed individual strategies (i.e., suppression, reappraisal, distraction), whereas the broader repertoire of ER strategies assessed in our study may not be perfectly captured with global measures of adaptive and maladaptive ER. Indeed, some research suggests that specific strategies are not inherently adaptive or maladaptive, but rather that their effectiveness depends on the context. Successful ER may rely on employing a diverse repertoire of approaches rather than a single type of strategy (De France & Hollenstein, 2017). Taken together, these differences in context and ER measures can offer insight into the discrepancy between the effect of specific ER strategies on eating in laboratory studies and the null association between global measures of adaptive and maladaptive ER and eating in our study.

Our findings are also in contrast with retrospective studies which found that greater ER difficulties were related to more habitual overeating (Obara‐Gołębiowska, 2025) and more eating in response to emotions (Guerrini‐Usubini et al., 2023). Here, also, methodological differences are of interest. Similar to how self‐reported “emotional eating” has been found not to translate to actual increased food intake following negative emotions (e.g., Evers et al., 2018), it is possible that the retrospectively reported relationship between ER difficulties and increased food intake does not actually translate to increased food intake following poor ER in daily life. EMA measures capture moment‐to‐moment fluctuations of ER and eating that can more accurately capture these dynamic relationships than retrospective questionnaires. Therefore, it is possible that ER is not associated with eating despite contrasts with some retrospective studies. Nonetheless, there are also retrospective studies that do align with our findings. That is, some retrospective studies found no relationship between suppression and food consumption in women with morbid obesity (Zijlstra et al., 2012), no relationship between habitual reappraisal and food consumption (Evers et al., 2010), and no relationship between rumination and food consumption in participants with obesity or with a healthy weight (Appelhans et al., 2011). Overall, we cannot conclude based on the current findings whether improving ER could be an effective intervention to reduce consumption and help individuals with obesity obtain a healthy weight. Future EMA studies could address the limitations discussed below to determine the role of ER in eating and weight gain.

Limitations and strengths

Our study had several strengths. First, the EMA methodology allowed for more ecologically valid results than retrospective questionnaire or laboratory studies. Second, we assessed not only negative but also positive emotions, which are under‐researched candidate triggers of eating (Evers et al., 2013). Third, rather than a general measure of eating, we included both a hedonic (taste‐driven eating) and homeostatic (hunger‐driven eating) determinant of eating, which is important given that they are separate determinants of food consumption. Fourth, this EMA study had a relatively large sample size and high response rate for emotions and eating, allowing for sufficient power to detect relevant relationships between emotions and eating.

Some limitations of the study should also be mentioned. First, we had several models that were overfitted and should be interpreted with caution. The large amount of missing data on ER variables was likely (partially) responsible for this. It is possible that a relationship between ER and eating exists, but given that only 17% of all ER measures were completed, we may not have had enough power to detect it. It is possible that participants indicated that they had not experienced a negative emotion since the last measurement to omit the subsequent ER questions. Second, the low level of negative emotions in the sample made it difficult to reliably test the relationship between negative emotions and eating. Third, BMI was based on self‐reported height and weight, and individuals may have over‐reported or under‐reported their weight. Self‐report was chosen for practical reasons; the participants came from various places throughout the Netherlands and keeping the study fully online allowed us to recruit a large, nationally representative sample. Substantiating the validity of our approach, a recent meta‐analysis found very high correlations between self‐reported and measured weight, with women under‐reporting their weight by an average of only 0.55 kg (Fayyaz et al., 2024). Although women with obesity may particularly under‐report their weight (Visscher et al., 2006), this issue is minimal in our study given that weight was used to place participants in groups, with five BMI points between the two groups. Fourth, although we chose to assess food choice with 18 images because it is convenient and user‐friendly for participants, it did not allow us to precisely assess food content, and there was likely variability in each meal/snack that we could not account for (i.e., depending on the content, a “pasta dish” could be relatively healthy/unhealthy). However, for most dishes, the distinction between healthy (e.g., fruit, salad) and unhealthy (e.g., cake, candy bar) was clear, and the measure could therefore offer important insights into broad eating patterns. Fifth, we chose to only include women as we were more likely to find an effect in women than in men given their higher rates of eating in response to emotions (e.g., Zaiser et al., 2025). As such, our findings cannot be generalized to men.

Future research

Future research should further assess the relationship among emotions, ER, and eating in daily life in individuals with and without obesity. First, more EMA studies, including several eating outcome measures, are needed to test the robustness of the relationship between positive emotions and unhealthy food choice. Second, studies could include dietary restraint to test its potential moderating role in the relationship among emotions, ER, and eating in daily life. Third, studies could assess (subjective) ER strategy effectiveness and/or assess emotions pre‐ and post‐ER implementation to determine the effectiveness of specific strategies in a specific context. Fourth, to minimize missing data, future EMA studies could apply the ER questions to the negative emotion with the highest score on the previous question: “How do you feel?” (i.e., automatically carry the most negative emotion forward to the ER questions). That way, only when all negative emotions are zero would the ER questions be omitted.

CONCLUSION

Overall, our study found that women with obesity used less adaptive ER strategies than women with a healthy weight in daily life. We also found that women with obesity more often engaged in unhealthy eating behaviors than women with a healthy weight. Moreover, positive emotions were prospectively associated with more unhealthy food choices in the hours thereafter, although they were not associated with overeating, taste‐driven eating, or hunger‐driven eating. Finally, negative emotions and adaptive and maladaptive ER strategies were not prospectively associated with eating.
