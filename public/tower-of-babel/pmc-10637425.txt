Smoking and the risk of atopic dermatitis: A two-sample mendelian randomization study
Authors: Zhenni Wang, Minghai Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Atopic dermatitis (AD) is considered to be one of the most common chronic diseases. It has been shown that smoking is associated with atopic dermatitis, but previous studies were mainly observational, which may be biased. The present study conducted a 2-sample mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate the causal relationship. The present study obtained data on “ever smoked” and “atopic dermatitis” from published large-scale genome-wide association studies. The data were obtained from the UK Biobank and BioBank Japan. Three methods were used to perform a 2-sample MR analysis and also performed sensitivity analysis. The odds ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) between smoking and AD calculated by MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and random-effects inverse variance weighting method were 1.096 (95% CI.756–1.587) and 1.159 (95% CI 1.040–1.292), respectively, 1.137 (95% CI .975–1.325). The inverse variance weighting method showed statistical significance between the 2 and a causal relationship between smoking and AD. In conclusion, the results of our MR analysis suggest that smoking is likely to affect the incidence of AD.

1. Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent eczema-like lesions and intense itching.[1] The global prevalence of atopic dermatitis has been gradually increasing over the past 3 decades, and it is estimated that nearly 230 million people suffer from AD,[2] which is considered to be one of the most common chronic diseases. The results of a multinational survey conducted in 12 countries in the Asia-Pacific region found that AD has a significant impact on quality of life, and that people with AD have a significantly lower quality of life in terms of mental and physical well-being.[3] Identifying modifiable risk factors can inform the prevention of disease pairs and reduce disease prevalence.

Tobacco has many deleterious effects on the immune system, such as humoral and cellular immunity.[4] The direct effects of tobacco on the skin are unknown,[5] but it may impair skin barrier function through the effects of reactive oxygen species on keratin-forming cells.[6] Many studies have examined the relationship between tobacco smoke and various atopic diseases, including asthma, allergic rhinitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and AD. A prospective population-based study found enhanced IgE-mediated allergic responses to dust mite, a common allergen in AD, in persistent smokers compared to never-smokers.[7]

To further investigate the causality, strength of association between smoking and AD, we used mendelian randomization (MR). MR refers to the use of genetic variants in nonexperimental data to infer causality of exposure on outcome. MR is similar to traditional randomized clinical trial, but eliminates the effects of confounders and reverse causality by using random assignment of alleles.[8]

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

In the present study, smoking was used as an exposure factor, single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) significantly associated with smoking as an instrumental variable (IV), and AD as an outcome variable. Causal association analysis was performed using the 2-sample MR analysis method, heterogeneity was tested using the Cochran Q test, and finally, sensitivity analysis was performed on the obtained results to verify their reliability. Three core hypotheses were used in this study: The IV is strongly associated with smoking; The IV is independent of each other and the confounders influencing the smoking- AD association; and The IV can only be used to influence the outcome past the association with smoking.[9] (Fig. 1). 2.2. Data source

Genetic data on smoking and AD were obtained from the website (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). Genetic data on atopic dermatitis were obtained from the Biobank Japan, which contained 212,036 men and women of East Asian descent with a SNP count of 8885,777, and genetic data on smoking were obtained from the UK Biobank, which contained 6532 African men and women of African descent with a SNP count of 15,508,734. (Table 1). This MR study was conducted using publicly available studies or shared datasets and therefore did not require additional ethical statements or consent.

2.3. Data processing

Eleven SNPs (P < 5.0 × 10-6) that were strongly associated with smoking were selected for pooling. Normally P < 5.0 × 10-8 has genome-wide significance, but no SNPs were screened at this threshold, so the threshold was appropriately lowered. The Two Samole MR package of R software was utilized to exclude the interference of chain imbalance, and the parameters were set with r2 = .001 and kb = 10000,[10] and finally 8 eligible SNPs were obtained.

2.4. Statistical processing

2.4.1. Weak instrumental variables test.

The assessment of the presence of weak IV bias in the selected IVs was performed by calculating the F value. The total F was calculated as F=N−K−1K×R21−R2, where N is the sample size of smokers included, K is the number of SNPs in the database, and R2 is the proportion of variation in the smokers’ database that is explained by SNPs. The formula for R2 is R2=2×EAF×(1−EAF)×β2SD2, and the formula for a single SNP is F=β2SE2, where EAF is the effect allele frequency, standard error is the standard error, SD is the standard deviation, and β is the allele effect value.[11]

2.4.2. Estimation of causal effect of smoking and atopic dermatitis.

In the present study, Mendelian randomization analyses were performed using the Two Sample MR version 0.5.6 software package in R version 4.2.2. The associations of individual SNPs were first performed and the Wald ratios of individual SNPs were calculated, then the inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method was applied to combine the Wald ratios and assess the association between smoking and atopic dermatitis. In addition, MR-Egger regression[12] and weighted median[13] were used to validate the Mendelian randomization results.

2.4.3. Reliability assessment.

2.4.3.1. Instrumental variables multiple validity test

In Mendelian randomization studies, IVs are not directly associated with outcomes, and genetic variation is pleiotropic, making it difficult to test the exclusivity assumption. Currently, the intercept term of MR-Egger regression is often used to test for the presence of pleiotropy.[12] When the intercept of linear regression of MR-Egger is close to 0, it indicates that there is no multinomiality in IVs, and the exclusivity assumption can be considered to be valid.[14]

2.4.3.2. Sensitivity analysis

In the present study, the Leave-1-out method was used to analyze the sensitivity of the results. The leave-1-out method mainly involves removing each SNP 1 by 1, and then observing whether there is any statistical difference between the results obtained before and after its removal. If P > .05 is obtained after excluding a SNP, it means that the SNP does not have a nonspecific effect on the effect estimates.[15]

3.2. Results of 2-sample mendelian randomization

The results of the presented study showed that the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) between smoking and AD calculated by MR-Egger regression, weighted median and random-effects inverse variance weighting were 1.096 (95% CI .756–1.587), 1.159 (95% CI 1.040–1.292), 1.137 (95% CI .975–1.325 Table 3, Fig. 3).The results of Cochran Q test showed that P was > .05, which indicated that there was no heterogeneity among IVs, so the results of IVW method of fixed effect model were mainly used. According to the results of the IVW method, the risk of atopic dermatitis prevalence increases in smokers and is statistically different [OR, 1.159 (95% CI 1.040–1.292), P = .008 < 0.05].

3.3. Results of reliability assessment

3.3.1. Results of instrumental variable multiple validity test.

The intercept Egger-intercept of the MR-Egger regression was .016, which is close to 0, and the standard error was.053, P = .766, indicating that causal effects are less likely to be influenced by genetic pleiotropy.

3.3.2. Results of sensitivity analysis.

Sensitivity analysis of the results of the IVW method using the leave-1-out method showed that after excluding each SNP in turn, the results of the IVW analysis of the remaining eight SNPs were similar to those of the analysis that included all SNPs, and no SNPs were found to have a large impact on the estimated value of the causal association (Fig. 4).The results of the funnel plot showed that the scatter of the causal associations was basically symmetric, and the results did not have a bias (Fig. 5).

4. Discussion

Defining the causative factors and risk factors of AD is important for its prevention and treatment. In this study, the association between smoking and AD was investigated using MR analysis. The results according to the IVW method showed [OR, 1.159 (95% CI 1.040–1.292), P = .008 < .05], which suggests that smokers have an increased risk of developing atopic dermatitis. A meta-analysis of 17,969 active smokers showed an OR of 1.87 for active smoking and AD, and a meta-analysis of 33,200 passive smokers showed an OR of 1.18 for passive smoking and AD.[16] In conclusion, smoking leads to an increased prevalence of AD.

The exact mechanism by which smoking increases the risk of AD is unclear, and it has been suggested that tobacco smoke has a stimulatory effect on patients genetically predisposed to AD, or that smoking stimulates the development of AD through an allergic reaction, or both. Cigarette smoke induces the release of TNF-α, TNF- α receptors, interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-8 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor.[17]It has been found that after exposure to tobacco smoke, a significant increase in the synthesis and release of TNF-α induced IgE production by B cells; the overproduction of TNF-α weakened host defenses against contamination and led to the development of inflammatory skin diseases.[18] Th1/Th2 imbalance is the primary pathogenesis of AD. Patients with AD exhibit a hyperactive Th2-type inflammatory response. TNF-α alone or in combination with Th2 cytokines modulates the lipid properties of the barrier. TNF-α alone or in combination with Th2 cytokines caused a coordinated reduction in the relative abundance of saturated free fatty acid ≥ C20:0. Free fatty acid ≥ C20:0 was found to be reduced in diseased skin compared to healthy skin.[19] TNF-α alone or in combination with Th2 cytokines reduces ELOVL1 protein expression, which may affect epidermal permeability.[20] Therefore, Th2 cytokines and TNF-α induce both epidermal lipid changes and stratum corneum, which are characteristic of the skin in AD. In addition, TNF-α alone reduces the midpoint temperature of the transition in comparison to control, which may contributes to a higher trans epidermal water loss.[19] This can exacerbate the symptoms of atopic dermatitis. It has been shown that serum IgE is significantly higher in smokers than in nonsmokers.[21] Exposure to tobacco smoke also activates a range of transcription factors, such as the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor,[22] which induces a range of inflammatory responses that in turn affect skin homeostasis and exacerbate AD.[23] Therefore, avoiding exposure to tobacco smoke can prevent and link AD to a certain extent.

Of course, there are some limitations of this study. First, the sample of AD included was an East Asian population, while the sample of smoking was an African-American population, so there may be some influence on ancestry and ethnicity, and there is a need to further unify the ethnicity of the study population and expand the sample of other ethnicities for the study. Second, the data selected for this study came from the genome-wide association study summary database, which lacked individual data and could not exclude the effects of age, gender, and other lifestyle habits. In addition, the mechanism by which smoking increases the risk of AD has not yet been clarified, and further validation of the conjecture of this study is needed at a later stage.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study used a 2-sample MR study and found a causal relationship between smoking and AD – smoking may increases the incidence of AD. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to the effect of tobacco on AD when preventing and treating it. Further studies are needed to examine the biological mechanisms underlying this association.
