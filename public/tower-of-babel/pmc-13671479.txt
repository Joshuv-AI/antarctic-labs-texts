Combined Management of Meniscus Root Tear and High Tibial Osteotomy: A Surgical Strategy Using 3‐Dimensional Printing
Authors: Arash Sherafatvaziri, Morad Karimpour, Ghazaleh Moradkhani, Yasaman Ghahremani, Fardis Vosoughi, Alireza Mirzamohamadi, Ramin Shayan‐moghadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombining high tibial osteotomy with meniscal posterior root repair is technically challenging because of the limited metaphyseal bone stock, the proximity of the transtibial tunnel to the osteotomy site, and the risk of implant conflict or tibial fracture. To address these challenges, we describe a surgical technique that integrates computed tomography‐based 3‐dimensional (3D) preoperative planning with patient‐specific 3D‐printed surgical guides. 3D simulation of the osteotomy plane, fixation plate position, screw trajectories, and transtibial tunnel enables preoperative assessment of the spatial relation among these structures, allowing optimization of tunnel orientation, implant placement, and preservation of adequate bone stock before surgery. Patient‐specific 3D‐printed guides accurately reproduce the virtual plan intraoperatively, facilitating precise execution of both the osteotomy and transtibial tunnel creation.

Meniscus posterior root tears commonly coexist with compartmental osteoarthritis and coronal‐plane malalignment, resulting in loss of meniscal hoop function, increased tibiofemoral contact pressure, and accelerated joint degeneration. 1 High tibial osteotomy (HTO) redistributes load away from the affected compartment and is an established joint‐preserving treatment for symptomatic unicompartmental overload associated with coronal malalignment. 2 However, biomechanical evidence indicates that HTO alone may not adequately restore meniscal extrusion or normalize joint contact mechanics in the presence of a posterior root tear. 3 , 4 Recent clinical studies suggest that concomitant meniscal root repair or reconstruction during HTO may improve meniscal healing and functional outcomes compared with osteotomy alone, although the magnitude of the additional clinical benefit remains debated. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Performing both procedures within the limited proximal tibial metaphysis creates additional technical challenges, including convergence between the transtibial tunnel and osteotomy plane, interference with plate screws, loss of adequate bone stock, compromised fixation, and an increased risk of iatrogenic fracture. 9 , 10 , 11 Patient‐specific instrumentation based on computed tomography (CT)‐derived 3‐dimensional (3D) planning may improve the transfer of the virtual correction plan to the operative field and may help coordinate the osteotomy, fixation construct, and transtibial tunnel trajectory. 12 , 13 We describe a CT‐based 3D planning workflow and a patient‐specific guide for combined meniscal posterior root repair and medial opening‐wedge HTO.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Evaluation, Imaging, and Indications

This technique is indicated for symptomatic posterior meniscal root tears with clinically relevant coronal malalignment and early compartment overload. Preoperative assessment includes long‐leg standing radiographs for mechanical axis analysis, magnetic resonance imaging to confirm the posterior meniscal root tear and evaluate the cartilage, and a thin‐slice CT scan of the entire lower extremity from the hip joint to the ankle with a 1‐mm slice thickness for 3D surgical planning. The CT data are acquired in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format to enable accurate reconstruction of the lower‐limb geometry, assessment of the mechanical and anatomical axes, and patient‐specific preoperative planning.

Preoperative Planning and Guide Production

Thin‐slice CT data of the entire lower extremity, acquired from the hip to the ankle at a slice thickness of 1 mm, are imported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format into Mimics Innovation Suite (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Patient‐specific segmentation is performed to generate a 3D model of the femur, tibia, and fibula. The reconstructed lower‐extremity model is then used to assess all relevant sagital and coronal alignment parameters, including the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle, posterior tibial slope, and anatomic posterior proximal tibial angle, and to determine the planned correction angle (Figures 1 and 2).

(aFTA, anatomical femorotibial angle; CT, computed tomography; mFTA, mechanical femorotibial angle.)

(C) aPPTA measured between the anatomical axis of the tibia and the sagittal tibial plateau orientation. These patient‐specific measurements are incorporated into preoperative planning to determine the correction angle and optimize guide design. The posterior meniscal root footprint is identified on the 3D proximal tibial model. A virtual cylindrical tunnel is created from the anatomic root attachment to the anteromedial tibial cortex. The tunnel trajectory is adjusted to provide direct access to the root footprint while maintaining sufficient bone stock and avoiding the planned osteotomy plane, fixation plate, and screw trajectories (Figure 3).

Virtual planning of the transtibial tunnel. Three‐dimensional planning showing the planned transtibial tunnel extending from the anteromedial tibial cortex to the posterior meniscal root footprint. The tunnel trajectory is optimized to preserve adequate metaphyseal bone stock while avoiding the planned osteotomy plane, fixation plate, and screw trajectories.

A virtual medial opening‐wedge HTO is subsequently simulated. The osteotomy orientation, lateral hinge position, correction angle, plate position, and screw trajectories are planned concurrently with the meniscal root tunnel (Figure 4). Simultaneous visualization of these structures allows the tunnel entry point and orientation to be modified before surgery, thereby reducing the risk of tunnel‐osteotomy convergence and interference between the transtibial tunnel and fixation screws.

Virtual simulation of the osteotomy and fixation construct. Three‐dimensional simulation of the medial opening‐wedge high tibial osteotomy showing the planned osteotomy plane, lateral hinge, fixation plate position, and screw trajectories. Simultaneous planning of the osteotomy and transtibial tunnel minimizes tunnel‐implant convergence.

A patient‐specific surgical guide is designed to conform to the exposed anteromedial surface of the proximal tibia. The same design concept can be applied to patient‐specific guides for either medial or lateral meniscal root repair performed concomitantly with medial opening‐wedge HTO (Figure 5). The undersurface of the guide incorporates patient‐specific osseous contours that permit only 1 intended position on the tibial cortex. The guide includes fixation pin holes, a dedicated access slot for the creation of the transtibial meniscal root tunnel, and a cutting reference that reproduces the planned osteotomy orientation. Thus, a single guide transfers the planned relations among the meniscal root tunnel, osteotomy plane, lateral hinge, and fixation construct to the operative field.

Patient‐specific surgical guide design. Three‐dimensional design of the patient‐specific surgical guide conforming to the anteromedial surface of the proximal tibia. The guide incorporates fixation pin holes, a tunnel‐guiding slot, and an osteotomy reference to accurately reproduce the preoperative plan. The same design concept can be applied to either lateral (A) or medial (B) meniscal posterior root repair.

The 3D bone model and patient‐specific guide are exported as standard tessellation language files. Both components are manufactured using a 3D printer and biocompatible material. A printed tibial model is used before surgery to verify the cortical fit of the guide, the accessibility of the tunnel slot, and the intended relation between the tunnel and osteotomy references. The guide is then sterilized according to the validated institutional protocol (Figure 6).

Three‐dimensional printed model and patient‐specific guide. Representative 3‐dimensional printed tibial model and patient‐specific surgical guide before sterilization. The printed model is used to verify cortical fit, tunnel orientation, and the planned relation between the transtibial tunnel and osteotomy.

Patient Positioning and Setup

The patient is supine on a radiolucent table under general anesthesia with a proximal pneumatic thigh tourniquet. The fluoroscopic C‐arm is positioned on the contralateral side of the operative limb and adjusted to allow unobstructed anteroposterior and lateral imaging throughout the procedure without interfering with the arthroscopic or osteotomy workflow.

Arthroscopic Meniscal Posterior Root Repair

Standard anterolateral and anteromedial arthroscopic portals are established, followed by a systematic diagnostic arthroscopy to evaluate all compartments of the knee (Figure 7). The posterior meniscal root tear is confirmed, and the articular cartilage is carefully inspected to assess the extent of chondral degeneration. The fibrous tissue at the root attachment site is debrided using a motorized shaver and curette until a stable bleeding bony bed is obtained to facilitate biological healing (Figures 8 and 9).

Diagnostic arthroscopy. Portal placement for knee arthroscopy. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals were established to facilitate visualization and instrumentation during the procedure.

Preparation of the posterior meniscal root footprint. Arthroscopic view showing the debridement of the posterior meniscal root attachment using a curette and motorized shaver until a stable bleeding bony surface is obtained.

Prepared meniscal root footprint. Arthroscopic appearance after complete preparation of the posterior meniscal root footprint. Adequate removal of fibrous tissue exposes cancellous bone to optimize biological healing after repair.

Two high‐strength nonabsorbable sutures (No. 2 FiberWire, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are sequentially passed through the posterior meniscal root using a Knee Scorpion Suture Passer (Arthrex). A simple or luggage‐tag configuration is selected according to the tissue quality to achieve secure fixation while minimizing the risk of suture cut‐through (Figure 10).

Suture passage through the posterior meniscal root. Arthroscopic view showing the passage of high‐strength nonabsorbable sutures through the posterior meniscal root using a Knee Scorpion Suture Passer (Arthrex, Naples, FL). A simple or luggage‐tag configuration is selected according to the tissue quality.

Medial Proximal Tibial Exposure

A longitudinal incision is made over the anteromedial proximal tibia. The sartorial fascia is identified and incised. The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are isolated and retracted to expose the posteromedial border of the proximal tibial metaphysis (Figures 11 and 12). The superficial medial collateral ligament is carefully elevated from the tibia as required to obtain adequate exposure while preserving its integrity.

Exposure of the proximal medial tibia. Intraoperative photograph showing the identification of the sartorial fascia and exposure of the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons during the medial approach to the proximal tibia. Right knee, anteromedial view.

Medial tibial exposure. Intraoperative photograph showing the retraction of the hamstring tendons and elevation of the superficial medial collateral ligament to expose the posteromedial proximal tibia while preserving soft‐tissue integrity. Right knee, anteromedial view.

The posteromedial soft tissues are protected with a radiolucent retractor (Figure 13). The anteromedial cortical surface corresponding to the planned guide footprint is fully exposed, and all residual periosteal and soft‐tissue interposition is removed. Complete exposure of the predefined cortical contact area is essential because the residual soft tissue may prevent accurate seating of the patient‐specific guide.

Preparation of the guide footprint. Intraoperative photograph showing the protection of the posteromedial soft tissues with a radiolucent retractor and complete exposure of the anteromedial cortical surface before guide placement.

Patient‐Specific Guide Placement and Fixation

The sterilized patient‐specific guide is positioned on the exposed anteromedial tibial cortex. Its position is adjusted until the entire undersurface is in direct contact with the corresponding bony contours. Correct seating is confirmed visually and manually by the absence of rocking or translation. The guide is temporarily stabilized using fixation pins inserted through the integrated pin holes (Figure 14).

Patient‐specific guide placement. Intraoperative photograph showing the accurate seating of the patient‐specific guide on the exposed anteromedial proximal tibia and temporary fixation with Kirschner wires through the integrated pin holes. Right knee, anteromedial view.

Guide‐Assisted Transtibial Tunnel Creation

The dedicated tunnel access slot incorporated into the patient‐specific guide is used to reproduce the preoperatively planned transtibial trajectory. A guidewire is advanced through the guide toward the anatomic posterior root footprint (Figure 15). The intra‐articular emergence of the guidewire is monitored arthroscopically, whereas its osseous trajectory is confirmed fluoroscopically in the anteroposterior and lateral projections (Figures 16 and 17).

Guide‐assisted transtibial tunnel creation. Intraoperative photograph showing the insertion of the guidewire through the dedicated tunnel‐guiding slot of the patient‐specific guide to reproduce the planned transtibial tunnel trajectory.

Arthroscopic confirmation of guidewire position. Arthroscopic view confirming the accurate emergence of the guidewire at the prepared posterior meniscal root footprint before tunnel drilling.

Fluoroscopic confirmation of tunnel trajectory. Intraoperative anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic images confirming the planned guidewire orientation before creation of the transtibial tunnel.

Once the guidewire position is satisfactory, a cannulated drill is advanced over the wire to create the transtibial tunnel. Arthroscopic inspection confirms that the tunnel aperture corresponds to the prepared meniscal root footprint. The posterior meniscal root attachment is prepared to a stable bleeding bony surface using a curette or a motorized shaver. The previously placed repair sutures are retrieved through the transtibial tunnel, and the meniscal root is anatomically reduced to its native footprint. Final fixation is achieved using a 7 × 25‐mm PEEK interference screw (BIOSURE PK Interference Screw, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA), which shortens the free suture length and enhances the initial fixation strength. 14

Patient‐Specific Guide‐Assisted HTO

After completion of the meniscal root repair, the patient‐specific guide is used on the tibia to identify the preplanned osteotomy plane. The osteotomy line is marked directly through the guide slots, ensuring accurate reproduction of the virtual surgical plan (Figure 18).

Guide‐assisted osteotomy marking. Intraoperative photograph showing the transfer of the planned osteotomy orientation from the patient‐specific guide to the proximal tibia before bone cutting.

A biplanar medial opening‐wedge HTO is performed using an oscillating saw under fluoroscopic guidance while preserving the lateral cortical hinge. Gradual opening is continued until the preoperatively planned correction is achieved (Figure 19).

Medial opening‐wedge high tibial osteotomy. Intraoperative photograph showing the execution of the biplanar medial opening‐wedge high tibial osteotomy using an oscillating saw while preserving the lateral cortical hinge.

Fluoroscopy is used to evaluate the osteotomy orientation, lateral hinge integrity, and correction magnitude. The osteotomy is gradually opened using calibrated osteotomes and wedge spreaders until the planned correction angle is achieved. Mechanical alignment is confirmed fluoroscopically before definitive fixation (Figure 20).

Fluoroscopic assessment of correction. Intraoperative fluoroscopic images showing the gradual opening of the osteotomy and confirmation of the planned correction angle while maintaining lateral hinge integrity.

Osteotomy Fixation

The osteotomy is stabilized using a TomoFix Medial High Tibia Plate (DePuy Synthes, Solothurn, Switzerland). Locking and cortical screws are inserted according to the preoperatively planned trajectories to avoid interference with the transtibial tunnel and interference screw (Figure 21). The osteotomy gap is filled with β‐tricalcium phosphate wedges (ChronOS, DePuy Synthes) to maintain correction and support bone healing.

Locking and cortical screws are inserted according to the preoperative plan to avoid interference with the transtibial tunnel and interference screw.

Final Assessment

The repair is inspected arthroscopically to confirm anatomic reduction, appropriate tension, and stability of the posterior root. Final fluoroscopic images are obtained in the anteroposterior and lateral projections to confirm the planned correction, preservation of the lateral hinge, appropriate plate and screw position, and absence of convergence between the fixation hardware and transtibial tunnel. The final relation among the osteotomy plate, interference screw, cortical fixation, and transtibial tunnel is documented before wound closure (Figure 22). Postoperative Rehabilitation

The knee is immobilized in a hinged knee brace immediately after surgery. Passive and active‐assisted range‐of‐motion exercises are initiated on the first postoperative day, with knee flexion gradually increased as tolerated. Patients remain nonweight‐bearing for the first 6 weeks to protect both the meniscal root repair and the osteotomy site. Partial weight‐bearing is then initiated based on radiographic evidence of early osteotomy healing and is progressively advanced to full weight‐bearing over the following 2 to 4 weeks. Closed‐chain strengthening exercises are introduced after restoration of full weight‐bearing, whereas high‐impact activities and sports are deferred until radiographic union of the osteotomy and satisfactory functional recovery, generally between 6 and 9 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Combining meniscal root repair with HTO addresses 2 principal contributors to medial compartment overload: meniscal dysfunction and malalignment. From a biomechanical perspective, medial meniscus root tears significantly disrupt the meniscus's ability to convert axial loads into hoop stresses, essentially functioning like a total meniscectomy. 7 , 15 Restoring the anatomic root attachment via transtibial repair re‐establishes load distribution, whereas HTO shifts the mechanical axis to offload the repaired compartment. Therefore, it optimizes the biomechanical environment for healing and potentially improves long‐term outcomes. The existing literature supports the benefit of concurrent repair. Ke et al. showed that although overall clinical outcomes might be similar between HTO alone and HTO with meniscal repair, meniscal healing was significantly better in the repair group. 16 Similarly, Kim et al. reported that the remodified Mason‐Allen suture technique combined with HTO improved clinical outcomes and showed complete healing in 64.7% of patients at second‐look arthroscopy. 17 Our approach aims to improve these results through enhanced precision in both components of the procedure.

Interestingly, a systematic review by Kyun‐Ho et al. found that concurrent meniscal root repair with HTO improved Lysholm scores and meniscal healing rates but showed no significant differences in the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities scores or radiological outcomes compared with HTO alone. 6 This highlights the nuanced impact of the combined procedures and underscores the importance of technical precision in achieving optimal outcomes. Also, a study conducted by Wang et al. suggested that patient‐specific factors should guide the decision‐making process regarding combined or isolated procedures, further supporting our personalized approach through 3D planning. 18

However, combining meniscal root repair with HTO requires precise spatial coordination within the limited proximal tibial metaphyseal bone to avoid compromising bone integrity or fixation stability. Conventional techniques relying on intraoperative judgment alone risk tunnel‐implant conflict, compromised fixation, inadequate bone stock, or iatrogenic fracture. CT‐based 3D planning reduces intraoperative uncertainty, by virtual simulation of both the transtibial meniscal tunnel and HTO, to predict and avoid possible conflicts among tunnel trajectory, osteotomy plane, and plate screws and allows modifications prior to surgery. This preoperative optimization significantly reduces the risk of intraoperative complications, such as fracture during screw placement or insufficient tibial bone wedge for secure fixation. Moreover, defining a safe minimum distance between tunnel entry and osteotomy plane (e.g., 7.8 mm in our planning) reduces fracture risk and preserves bone for secure fixation.

Patient‐specific 3D‐printed guides translate the virtual plan into reproducible intraoperative references for both osteotomy orientation and tunnel entry, reducing reliance on approximation and surgeon experience alone and potentially standardizing outcomes across various surgical skill levels. This standardization may reduce intraoperative decision‐making, shorten operative time, and make complex spatial relations, which is difficult to appreciate on 2‐dimensional imaging. A precise preoperative plan also allows for better preparation of equipment and anticipation of potential challenges, further streamlining the procedure. 8 , 11

From a cost‐effectiveness perspective, 3D printing technology has become increasingly affordable and accessible in recent years. Despite the initial investment in hardware and software and the learning curve for surgical teams, it offers economical viabilities through reduced complications, the need for revision surgeries, and improved precision and clinical outcomes. Ultimately, this approach holds significant potential for delaying total knee arthroplasty and improving the quality of life, particularly for younger or active patients, by addressing key factors in osteoarthritis progression.

Despite these advantages, this technique has several limitations. It requires a preoperative CT scan of the entire lower extremity, specialized 3D planning software, and access to additive manufacturing facilities, which may not be readily available in all institutions. The additional planning and guide production introduce extra cost and preoperative preparation time. Furthermore, accurate guide positioning depends on adequate exposure of the anteromedial proximal tibial cortex and meticulous removal of the intervening soft tissue. Improper guide seating may compromise the accuracy of tunnel placement and osteotomy execution. Finally, although this technique improves surgical precision, clinical superiority over conventional techniques remains to be shown in larger comparative studies with long‐term follow‐up.

Potential intraoperative risks include inaccurate guide positioning, inadequate preservation of the lateral cortical hinge during osteotomy, tunnel malposition, and implant conflict if the preoperative plan is not accurately reproduced. Careful verification of guide seating and fluoroscopic confirmation throughout the procedure are therefore essential to ensure the accurate execution of the planned correction and tunnel trajectory. In summary, CT‐based 3D planning combined with patient‐specific surgical guides provides a reproducible strategy for simultaneous meniscal posterior root repair and medial opening‐wedge HTO. By accurately defining the relation among the osteotomy, fixation hardware, and transtibial tunnel before surgery, this technique may reduce technical complexity and improve surgical reproducibility. Further clinical studies are warranted to evaluate the long‐term outcomes and cost‐effectiveness.
