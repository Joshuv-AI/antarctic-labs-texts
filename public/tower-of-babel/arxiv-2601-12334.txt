Worst-case Nonlinear Regression with Error Bounds
Alberto Bemporad

Abstract
This paper proposes an active-learning approach to worst-case nonlinear regression with deterministic error guarantees. Given a known nonlinear function defined over a compact set, we compute a surrogate model, such as a feedforward neural network, by minimizing the maximum absolute approximation error. To address the nonsmooth nature of the resulting minimax problem, we introduce a smooth approximation of the L ∞ L_{\infty} -type loss that enables efficient gradient-based training. We iteratively enrich the training set by actively learning points of largest approximation error through global optimization. The resulting models admit certified worst-case error bounds, either constant or input-dependent, over the entire input domain. The approach is demonstrated through approximations of nonlinear functions and nonconvex sets, as well as through the derivation of uncertain models of more complex nonlinear dynamics within a given model class, and the approximation of explicit model predictive control laws.



1
Introduction

Function approximation methods are fundamental in control systems engineering, both to obtain control-oriented models of dynamical systems and to simplify complex nonlinear control laws for online implementation. It can be used either to learn models or control laws directly from data, or to simplify a known model or control function. In both cases, the typical approach is to collect a sufficiently large set of samples and solve a nonlinear regression problem, for example by training a feedforward neural network (NN) by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE), possibly under regularization on the model parameters.

In control applications, however, it is often crucial to quantify a bound on the approximation error between the true function, when known, and the learned model.
Such bounds are needed, for example, in robust control design to characterize modeling errors treated as additive disturbances, and when approximating control laws to guarantee closed-loop stability and constraint satisfaction despite approximation errors. This issue is particularly relevant for approximating model predictive control (MPC) laws to reduce online computational effort, typically using NNs
[
22
,
12
,
16
]
. However, most existing methods do not guarantee bounds on the worst-case approximation error (WCE) between the exact MPC law and its approximation. The recent contribution
[
1
]
addresses this problem by exploiting Lipschitz continuity, assuming the availability of a sufficiently large training dataset.

In system identification for robust control, set-membership methods
have been used to obtain models with guaranteed bounded error predictions, including nonlinear models
[
21
]
, often under assumptions on the system dynamics. Similar ideas have been applied to approximate nonlinear MPC laws in
[
11
]
under Lipschitz continuity assumptions. Gaussian processes
[
24
]
offer an alternative by providing models with quantified uncertainty, but they rely on restrictive model classes and yield only probabilistic guarantees.

Robustness in nonlinear regression has been studied in the statistics literature mainly to mitigate the effect of outliers in training data
[
14
]
. Here, instead, the goal is to minimize and quantify the WCE over
all
possible inputs in a given set. This problem is related to uncertainty quantification and conformal prediction
[
2
]
, which typically provide probabilistic confidence intervals rather than worst-case guarantees
[
5
]
. Achieving the latter requires different techniques; for example, in
[
13
]
semidefinite programming is used to certify the robustness of NN predictions against input uncertainties.

The standard workflow to obtain nonlinear models with bounded prediction errors is to first identify a model and then estimate its approximation error. However, a small
average
error, such as a low MSE, does not necessarily imply a small
worst-case
error. This motivates methods that directly minimize the WCE during training and reliably quantify it afterward.

Minimax regression
, also known as
Chebyshev regression
or
L_{\infty}
estimation

[
23
, Ch. 7]
, addresses this need by minimizing the maximum deviation between the true function and its approximation over a dataset or a domain. Despite its potential for robust system identification and control, it has received limited attention in the machine learning and control communities, with most existing results restricted to linear models. This is partly due to the nonsmooth optimization problems it entails and to the strong dependence of the solution quality on the choice of the training samples.

1.1
Contribution

In this paper, we consider fitting a parametric model, such as a NN, to a known nonlinear function using an
\ell_{\infty}
loss over a finite set of samples, with the goal of minimizing the WCE over a compact input set. To enable the use of efficient quasi-Newton methods such as L-BFGS
[
18
]
together with automatic differentiation, we replace the nonsmooth maximum operator with a smooth (but arbitrarily close) approximation and solve the resulting problem. We reduce the dependence of the WCE on training samples by proposing an active-learning strategy that iteratively adds points of maximum approximation error, identified via global optimization.

A key difference with respect to active-learning methods for nonlinear regression and classification, which rely on empirical acquisition functions to select potentially informative data
[
27
,
7
]
, is that here the true function can be evaluated at arbitrary inputs during the acquisition process to explicitly identify a point of maximum error. The approach is therefore closely related to corner-case detection via numerical optimization
[
19
]
.

In addition, we propose techniques to quantify the WCE over the entire input set after training, in the form of either constant or input-dependent upper and lower bounds on the approximation error. Finally, we show how the proposed approach can be used to learn sets (e.g., minimally conservative convex inner approximations of given nonconvex sets), uncertain (generally nonlinear) models of nonlinear dynamical systems (e.g., derived from physical principles), and approximate explicit MPC laws with guaranteed bounds on the control error introduced by the approximation.

2
Minimax Nonlinear Regression

Given a nonlinear function
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
over a compact set
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a class of functions
{\mathcal{F}}
,
our goal is to find a simpler function
\hat{f}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\hat{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, that minimizes the WCE between
f(x)
and
\hat{f}(x)
over
{\mathcal{X}}
, and to quantify such an error.

Let
\theta
be the vector of parameters identifying the function
\hat{f}
within
{\mathcal{F}}
,

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
. For example,
\theta
can collect the weights and bias terms of a NN, the coefficients of a polynomial or other basis functions, etc. We want to solve the following problem:

\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta}\left(r(\theta)+\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta)|\right)

(1)

where
r(\theta)
is a regularization term, such as an
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{1}
regularizer, or a penalty introduced
to promote certain properties that
\hat{f}
should have.

Solving Problem (
1
) poses two difficulties: (
i
) it is a bilevel optimization problem, due to the inner maximization problem over
{\mathcal{X}}
; and (
ii
) the inner objective function is non-smooth due to the maximum operator,
which makes applying very efficient quasi-Newton methods, such as L-BFGS
[
18
]
,
more difficult.

To address the first issue, we consider an initial training dataset
(x_{1},y_{1})
,
\ldots
,
(x_{N_{0}},y_{N_{0}})
of
samples, where
x_{k}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
y_{k}=f(x_{k})
, and replace
{\mathcal{X}}
with
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{0}}
, so that
solving the inner maximization problem simply amounts to finding the maximum of a finite number of terms. Accordingly, Problem (
1
) is replaced by

\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta}\left(r(\theta)+\max_{k=1,\ldots,N_{0}}|f(x_{k})-\hat{f}(x_{k};\theta)|\right).

(2)

A relevant problem addressed in this paper is how to actively learn a minimal number of samples
x_{k}
so that the solution of (
2
) provides a solution to (
1
).

We address the second issue by using the following smooth approximation of the max operator:

\theta^{\star}\!\in\!\arg\min_{\theta}\!\big(r(\theta)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\!\log\!\big(\!\sum_{k=1}^{N_{0}}\big(e^{\gamma(y_{k}-\hat{f}(x_{k};\theta))}+e^{\gamma(\hat{f}(x_{k};\theta)-y_{k})}\big)\big)\big).

(3)

Proposition 1

The minimization problem (
3
) has the same set of minimizers as (
2
)
in the limit
\gamma\to+\infty
.

Proof
. See Appendix
A.1
.
\Box

After obtaining the model
\hat{f}(\cdot;\theta^{\star})
by solving (
3
), we can compute the WCE
\bar{e}^{\star}

over the entire set
{\mathcal{X}}
by solving the following problem

\bar{e}^{\star}=\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})|

(4)

by using derivative-free global optimization methods
[
25
,
15
]
.

The main idea for enriching the dataset used in problem (
2
) is to add the obtained minimizer
x^{\star}
and the corresponding value
y^{\star}=f(x^{\star})
to the training dataset and repeat the procedure until the maximum error
\bar{e}^{\star}
is below a given threshold
\epsilon_{\rm err}
, or a preallocated computational budget is exhausted. In the latter case, as the WCE may not decrease at each iteration,
the model
\hat{f}(x;\theta)
that has provided the smallest WCE during the iterations is selected as the final model.
The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

As in most active-learning schemes, we start from an initial number
N_{0}
of samples
x_{k}
purely based on their location in the input space, such as randomly from the uniform distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}
, or by using Latin hypercube sampling (LHS)
[
20
]
, or by gridding
{\mathcal{X}}
uniformly.
Note that as
N_{0}
increases, fewer active-learning steps are expected to be required to achieve a given WCE
\bar{e}^{\star}\leq\epsilon_{\rm err}
, although the training problem (
3
) becomes more complex at each step.

Algorithm 1

Worst-case nonlinear regression with active learning

Input
: Function
f
to approximate, model class
{\mathcal{F}}
,
number
N_{0}
of initial samples to collect, maximum number
M
of active-learning steps,
desired error threshold
\epsilon_{\rm err}
,
smoothness parameter
\gamma
, regularization function
r
, input set
{\mathcal{X}}
.

1.

Generate initial dataset
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{N_{0}},y_{N_{0}})
,
y_{k}=f(x_{k})
;

2.

Set

N\leftarrow N_{0}
;

3.

Repeat
:

0..1.

Solve problem (
3
) and get optimal model parameters
\theta_{N}
;

0..2.

Set
x_{N+1}

\leftarrow
global optimizer of problem (
4
);

0..3.

Set
y_{N+1}\leftarrow f(x_{N+1})
,
e_{N}\leftarrow|y_{N+1}-\hat{f}(x_{N+1};\theta_{N})|
;

0..4.

N\leftarrow N+1
;

4.

Until

e_{N-1}\leq\epsilon_{\rm err}
or
N=N_{0}+M
;

5.

Set

i^{\star}\leftarrow\arg\min_{k=N_{0},\ldots,N-1}e_{k}
,

\bar{e}^{\star}\leftarrow e_{i^{\star}}
, and
\theta^{\star}\leftarrow\theta_{i^{\star}}
;

6.

End
.

Output

.

Note that the objective function in (
2
) can be generalized to
the following MSE-regularized objective:

r(\theta)+\max_{k}\lvert f(x_{k})-\hat{f}(x_{k};\theta)\rvert+\frac{\nu}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(f(x_{k})-\hat{f}(x_{k};\theta)\bigr)^{2}

2.1
Imposing a functional form on a set

Assume that we want to impose that
\hat{f}(x;\theta)\equiv w(x)
for all
x
in a given set
{\mathcal{G}}=\{x:\ g(x)\leq 0,\ h(x)=0\}
, where
w:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}}
, and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}}
are given functions. In Section
5.2
we will see an example with
w
affine and
{\mathcal{G}}
polyhedral.
Consider the indicator function
\delta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
, i.e.,
\delta(x)=1
if
x\in{\mathcal{G}}
and
\delta(x)=0
otherwise,
and either its piecewise affine (PWA) approximation

\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)=\max\left(1-\beta\max(\mathop{\rm col}\nolimits(g(x),\pm h(x),0)),0\right)

(6a)

or its approximation

\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)=e^{{-\beta\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}[g_{i}(x)]_{+}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{h}}|h_{j}(x)|\Big)}}

(6b)

where
\beta>0
. Clearly,
\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)=1
,
\forall x\in{\mathcal{G}}
.
Moreover, for
x\not\in{\mathcal{G}}
,
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow+\infty}\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)=0
.
Then, we define the model

\hat{f}(x;\theta)=\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)w(x)+(1-\hat{\delta}(x;\beta))\hat{v}(x;\theta)

(7)

where
\hat{v}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that this changes the model class
\hat{f}
belongs to. The parameter
\beta
can be either fixed or trainable; in the latter case, we assume
\beta
is a component of
\theta
.

2.2
Saturation

Assume that we want to bound the output of the model in the hyper-box

y_{\rm min}\leq y\leq y_{\rm max}
. For example, we may know that the codomain of
f
is contained in such a hyper-box. To this end, we consider the saturation function

\mathop{\rm sat}\nolimits(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})=\min(\max(y,y_{\rm min}),y_{\rm max})

(8a)

and its smooth approximation (cf.
[
8
, Section 3.B]
):

\sigma_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})=y_{\rm max}+\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(\frac{1+e^{-\eta(y-y_{\rm min})}}{1+e^{-\eta(y-y_{\rm max})}}\right)

(8b)

(all arithmetic operations are component-wise) where
\eta
is either a fixed or a further trainable parameter,
\eta>0
.

Proposition 2

Let
y_{\rm min}<y_{\rm max}
component-wise and
\eta>0
. Then, the function
\sigma

) is (component-wise) strictly monotonically increasing with respect to

for

for

, and is such that

\sigma_{\eta}\left(\frac{y_{\rm min}+y_{\rm max}}{2};y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max}\right)=\frac{y_{\rm min}+y_{\rm max}}{2}
,
\forall\eta>0
. Moreover, it approaches the saturation function
\mathop{\rm sat}\nolimits(y;y_{\rm min}
,
y_{\rm max})
asymptotically as

.

Proof
. See Appendix
A.2
.
\Box

Finally, we define the model

\hat{f}(x;\theta)=\hat{s}(\hat{v}(x;\theta);y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})

(9)

where
\hat{v}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\hat{s}
is either
\mathop{\rm sat}\nolimits
in (
8a
) or
\sigma_{\eta}
in (
8b
)
(we assume that
\eta
is a component of
\theta
in case it is trainable).
Note that this also changes the model class
\hat{f}
belongs to.
Saturation can be combined also with the approach detailed in
Section

by setting

\hat{f}(x;\theta)=\hat{s}((1-\hat{\delta}(x;\beta))\hat{v}(x;\theta)+\hat{\delta}(x;\beta)w(x);y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})
.

3
Input-dependent error bounds

Algorithm
1
provides the global, symmetric, and uniform bound
\bar{e}^{\star}
for
f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})
over
{\mathcal{X}}
, where
\theta^{\star}
collects the optimal model coefficients determined by Algorithm
1
(possibly including
\beta^{\star}
from (
7
) and
\eta^{\star}
from (
9
)). Here, we want to obtain bounds in the more general and therefore less conservative form

-e^{\star}_{\rm min}(x)\leq f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})\leq e^{\star}_{\rm max}(x),\ \forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

3.1
Constant asymmetric error bounds

Asymmetric uniform bounds, i.e., lower and upper bounds that are constant over
{\mathcal{X}}
, can be computed as

\displaystyle e^{\star}_{\rm min}(x)\equiv\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left([\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-f(x)]_{+}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle e^{\star}_{\rm max}(x)\equiv\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left([f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})]_{+}\right)

by global optimization similarly to (
4
).
Clearly, these are tighter than the symmetric bound
\bar{e}^{\star}
returned by
Algorithm
1
, since
-\bar{e}^{\star}\leq-\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}\leq f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})\leq\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}\leq\bar{e}^{\star}
.

3.2
Symmetric input-dependent error bounds

Consider a set
{\mathcal{E}}
of positive functions
\varepsilon:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\psi}}\to\mathbb{R}
parameterized
by a vector
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\psi}}
,
\varepsilon(x;\psi)>0
,
\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
\forall\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\psi}}
.
We consider the problem of learning a function
\varepsilon
that upper bounds
|f(x)-\hat{f}(x,\theta^{\star})|
as tightly as possible.
To this end, we want to solve the following training problem

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}=\arg\min_{\psi}

\displaystyle\rho_{\psi}(\psi)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi))

(11)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi)\geq|e_{k}|,\ k=1,\ldots,N

where
e_{k}=y_{k}-\hat{f}(x_{k},\theta^{\star})
is the prediction error at sample
k
,

\mu:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a monotonically increasing function (e.g.,
\mu(\varepsilon)=\epsilon
,

\mu(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}
) that attempts squeezing the interval
[-\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi),\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi)]

as much as possible, under the regularization
\rho_{\psi}
on the parameters of function
\varepsilon
,
and
N\leq N_{0}+M
is the number of available samples collected by Algorithm
1
.
We call
\varepsilon
an
envelope
function, due to the constraint in (
11
).

To relax the problem into an unconstrained nonlinear program, we rewrite the constraints in (
11
) into their equivalent form

\max\left(0,\max_{k=1,\ldots,N}\left(|e_{k}|-\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi)\right)\right)=0

(12)

and, by introducing an approximation of the maximum violation constraint in (
12
)
similar to the one used in (
3
),
we relax (
12
) into the following unconstrained nonlinear programming problem

\displaystyle\min_{\psi}\rho_{\psi}(\psi)\!+\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi))\!+\!\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\!\Big(1\!+\!\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\gamma(|e_{k}|-\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi))}\!\Big).

(13)

Note that, by Proposition
1
, the last term in (
13
) enforces constraint (
12
) in the limit
\gamma\to+\infty
, being a smooth version of
\max(0,\max_{k=1,\ldots,N}(|e_{k}|-\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi)))
.

Any positive model
\varepsilon(x;\psi)
can be used to learn the envelope functions. In this paper,
we focus on NN models with two hidden layers

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x;\psi)=

\displaystyle W_{3}a^{+}(W_{2}\cdot a(W_{1}\cdot x+b_{1})+b_{2})+b_{3}

(14)

\displaystyle W_{3}\geq 0,\ b_{3}>0

where
a^{+}
is a nonnegative activation function, such as the ReLU or sigmoid function,

a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is any activation function,

W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n}
,
W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
,
W_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{2}}
are weight matrices, and

b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,
b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
,
b_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
are bias terms. The transformation
a^{+}
and the component-wise constraints on
W_{3},b_{3}
ensure that
\varepsilon(x;\psi)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
\psi=\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
. In particular, by setting
a^{+}
and
a
as ReLU functions we get PWA envelopes
\varepsilon
.

To get a global upper-bound function on
{\mathcal{X}}
we compute the following scaling factor
\kappa
via global optimization:

\kappa^{\star}=\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\frac{|f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})|}{\varepsilon(x;\psi^{\star})}\right)

(15)

and define the bounding functions

\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}(x)=\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}(x)=\min(\bar{e}^{\star},\kappa^{\star}\varepsilon(x;\psi^{\star})).

(16)

Note that (
15
) is closely related to split conformal prediction
[
2
]
, where the scaling factor
\kappa^{\star}
is determined on a
calibration dataset rather than optimizing over the entire set
{\mathcal{X}}
, both for constant (homoscedastic) and input-dependent (heteroscedastic) bounds. In the latter case, our approach can also leverage additional calibration data when fitting
\varepsilon(x;\psi)
, so to limit conservativeness when making the envelope function valid over the entire
{\mathcal{X}}
in (
16
).

3.3
Asymmetric input-dependent error bounds

To further reduce the conservativeness of the bounds, the approach described above can be extended to fit
asymmetric

input-dependent upper and lower error bounds. Let us consider two different envelope functions
\varepsilon(x;\psi_{u})
and
\varepsilon(x;\psi_{\ell})
, with

\psi_{u}
,
\psi_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\psi}}
, to learn simultaneously by minimizing

\displaystyle(\psi_{u}^{\star},\psi_{\ell}^{\star})=\arg\min

\displaystyle\rho_{\psi}(\psi_{u})+\rho_{\psi}(\psi_{\ell})

(17)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{u})+\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{\ell}))

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\max\Big(0,\max_{k=1,\ldots,N}(e_{k}-\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{u})),

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,\ldots,N}(-e_{k}+\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{\ell}))\Big)=0.

Similarly to the envelope learning problem in (
11
), we can relax the problem into the unconstrained nonlinear program

\displaystyle\min_{\psi_{u},\psi_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{u})+\varepsilon(x_{k};\psi_{\ell}))+\rho_{\psi}(\psi_{u})+\rho_{\psi}(\psi_{\ell})

(18)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\!\left(\!1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(e^{\gamma(e_{k}-\varepsilon^{u}(x_{k};\psi_{u}))}+e^{\gamma(-e_{k}+\varepsilon^{\ell}(x_{k};\psi_{\ell}))}\big)\!\right)

where the last term penalizes
\gamma
times a smooth approximation of the maximum violation of the constraint in (
17
). Analogously to (
4
), after finding the optimal parameters
\psi_{u}^{\star}
and
\psi_{\ell}^{\star}
, we determine two scaling factors
\kappa_{u}^{\star}
and
\kappa_{\ell}^{\star}
, respectively, by solving the global optimization problems

\displaystyle\kappa_{u}^{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\frac{[f(x)-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})]_{+}}{\varepsilon(x;\psi_{u}^{\star})}\right)

(19a)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\ell}^{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\frac{[\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-f(x)]_{+}}{\varepsilon(x;\psi_{\ell}^{\star})}\right)

(19b)

to get the upper and lower bound functions on
{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}(x)

\displaystyle=\min(\bar{e}^{\star},\kappa_{\ell}^{\star}\varepsilon^{\ell}(x;\psi^{\star}_{\ell}))

(20)

\displaystyle\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}(x)

\displaystyle=\min(\bar{e}^{\star},\kappa_{u}^{\star}\varepsilon^{u}(x;\psi^{\star}_{u})).

Note that, by replacing
\mu(\varepsilon^{u}(x;\psi_{u})+\varepsilon^{\ell}(x;\psi_{\ell}))=\mu(\varepsilon^{u}(x;\psi_{u}))+\mu(\varepsilon^{\ell}(x;\psi_{\ell}))
, the envelope functions
\varepsilon^{u}
and
\varepsilon^{\ell}
can be learned independently. Note also that any function
\hat{f}_{s}
of
x
whose values lie in

[\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}(x),\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})+\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}(x)]
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
is a valid predictor of
f(x)
; in particular, the uncertainty interval can be symmetrized by simply redefining the centered predictor

\hat{f}^{s}(x)=\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})+\frac{\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm max}(x)-\bar{e}^{\star}_{\rm min}(x)}{2}

(21a)

4
Constraint satisfaction

Assume that we are interested in replacing a given constraint
f(x)\leq 0
with an equivalent simpler constraint
\bar{f}(x)\leq 0
, such that

\bar{f}(x)\leq 0\ \implies\ f(x)\leq 0,\ \forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}

(22)

in the least conservative way, i.e., minimizing the size of the set
\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\bar{f}(x)>0,\ f(x)\leq 0\}
of feasible points for
f
that are declared infeasible by
\bar{f}
. The actual values taken by
f(x)
are not relevant
for (
22
), as long as the sign of
f(x)
is correctly predicted by
\bar{f}(x)
, i.e.,

\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits(\bar{f}(x))=-1\ \implies\ \mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits(f(x))=-1,\ \forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
Therefore, we apply the worst-case regression approach developed in Section
2

with
f(x)
replaced by
s(f(x))
and
\hat{f}(x;\theta)
by
s(\hat{f}(x;\theta))
, where
s:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth approximation of the sign function. A possible choice is
s(x)=\tanh(\eta x)
, where
\eta>0
is a given parameter (e.g.,
\eta=10
). Note that
s
is only used during training and then removed after determining the optimal parameter vector
\theta^{\star}
.

After stopping the active-learning process and having fixed the optimal parameter vector
\theta^{\star}
, we
need to make sure to construct
\bar{f}(x)
such that it cannot be negative when
f(x)>0
, so to guarantee that
condition (
22
) is satisfied. To this end, we
compute the smallest value of
\hat{f}(x,\theta^{\star})
when the condition
f(x)>0
(i.e.,
\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits f(x)=1
), by solving the following global optimization problem:

\Delta f\triangleq\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits(f(x)))\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})\right)

(23)

and define
\bar{f}(x)
as in the next proposition.

Proposition 3

Let
\Delta f
the solution of (
23
) and define

\bar{f}(x)\triangleq\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-\Delta f+\epsilon_{f}

(24)

for an arbitrarily small number
\epsilon_{f}>0
. Then, condition (
22
) is satisfied.

Proof
. Assume, by contradiction, that there exists a vector
x
such that
\bar{f}(x)\leq 0
and
f(x)>0
. Then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\bar{f}(x)=\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-\Delta f+\epsilon_{f}

\displaystyle\geq\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})-\hat{f}(x;\theta^{\star})+\epsilon_{f}=\epsilon_{f}

which contradicts the assumption
\epsilon_{f}>0
. Hence, (
22
) must hold.

\Box

Note that, by selecting
\hat{f}
as the composition of a strictly-increasing function
\hat{f}_{m}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

with another function
\hat{f}_{c}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, parameterized by
\theta_{m}
and
\theta_{c}
, respectively,
\hat{f}(x;\theta)=\hat{f}_{m}(\hat{f}_{c}(x;\theta_{c});\theta_{m})
, the resulting constraint (
24
)
is equivalent to the constraint

\hat{f}_{c}(x;\theta_{c}^{\star})\leq\hat{f}_{m}^{-1}\left(\Delta f-\epsilon_{f};\theta_{m}^{\star}\right).

(25)

In particular, if
\hat{f}_{c}
is convex, such as an input-convex NNs
[
26
]
,
the constraint in (
25
) is convex. In the special case
\hat{f}_{c}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n_{f}}(A_{i}x-b_{i})
, we get the convex polyhedral set
\{x:\ Ax\leq b-\Delta+\epsilon_{f}\}
.

Modeling and Controller Approximations

5.1
Discrete-time uncertain models

We are given the continuous-time nonlinear model of a dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(\xi(t),u(t))

(26)

\displaystyle\tau(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\xi(t),u(t))

where
\xi(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
is the state vector and
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
the control input vector,
derived, for example, from first-principles. Our goal is to learn a discrete-time uncertain model of the form

\displaystyle\xi(t+T_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\xi(t),u(t);\theta)+w(t)

(27)

\displaystyle\tau(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{G}(\xi(t),u(t);\theta)+v(t)

where
T_{s}>0
is the sampling time,
\hat{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
and
\hat{G}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}}
are models parameterized by
\theta
, and we treat the modeling errors
w(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
and
v(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}}
as unknown disturbance vectors affecting the state update and output equations, respectively,
w(t)\in{\mathcal{W}}
and
v(t)\in{\mathcal{V}}
, where
{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}}
are unknown hyper-boxes.
Our goal is to learn
\hat{F},\hat{G}
along with the disturbance sets
{\mathcal{W}},{\mathcal{V}}
. For example, if
\hat{F},\hat{G}
are linear models, we would get a conservative uncertain discrete-time linear state-space model of (
26
) with unknown additive disturbances whose bounds are known. Similarly, if
\hat{F},\hat{G}
are NNs with ReLU activation functions, we would get an uncertain PWA discrete-time model of (
26
).

We can solve the above problem by applying the worst-case regression approach of Section
2
component-wise to the functions
F
and
G
, with
x=\mathop{\rm col}\nolimits(\xi(t)
,
u(t))
and

y=f(x)=\xi_{j}(t+T_{s})
for each state
j=1,\ldots,n_{\xi}
, and
y=f(x)=\tau_{j}(t)
for each output
j=1,\ldots,n_{\tau}
. Note that evaluating
\xi(t+T_{s})
requires integrating the continuous-time model (
26
) from
t
to
t+T_{s}
with initial condition
\xi(t)
and constant input
u(t)
. The uncertainty bounds for each component of
F
and
G
can then be obtained by solving (
10
) and combined to form the hyper-boxes
{\mathcal{W}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
.

5.2
Approximate mpQP and explicit linear MPC

Multiparametric quadratic programming (mpQP) problems
[
9
]

\displaystyle z^{\star}(x)=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{z}\frac{1}{2}z^{\prime}Qz+(Fx+f)^{\prime}z

(28)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }Az\leq Bx+b,\quad x\in{\mathcal{X}}

arise in explicit model predictive control (MPC) of linear time-invariant systems
[
6
]
.
The solution
z^{\star}(x)
is a PWA function of the parameter vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
over a polyhedral partition of the parameter set
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
in
N_{r}
regions, and can be computed offline
[
9
]
. As the number of regions
N_{r}
grows considerably with the number of constraints, we want to learn a surrogate model
\hat{f}
of
selected components
f(x)\triangleq[I\ 0\ \ldots\ 0]z^{\star}(x)
of the solution
z^{\star}
over
{\mathcal{X}}
.

A typical reference-tracking formulation used in applications (cf.
[
10
]
)
is

\displaystyle\min_{z}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}

\displaystyle(\tau_{k+1}-r)^{\prime}Q_{\tau}(\tau_{k+1}-r)+\Delta u_{k}^{\prime}Q_{\Delta u}\Delta u_{k}+\rho_{2}\zeta^{2}+\rho_{1}\zeta

(29)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.~}

\displaystyle\xi_{k+1}=\bar{A}\xi_{k}+\bar{B}u_{k},\ k=0,\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=\bar{C}\xi_{k},\ k=0,\ldots,N

\displaystyle u_{k}=u_{k-1}+\Delta u_{k},\ k=0,\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle u_{\rm min}\leq u_{k}\leq u_{\rm max},\ k=0,\ldots,N_{u}-1

\displaystyle\Delta u_{\rm min}\leq\Delta u_{k}\leq\Delta u_{\rm max},\ k=0,\ldots,N_{u}-1

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm min}-V_{\rm min}\zeta\leq\tau_{k}\leq\tau_{\rm max}+V_{\rm max}\zeta,\ k=1,\ldots,N_{c}

\displaystyle\Delta u_{k}=0,\ k=N_{u},\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle\zeta\geq 0,\ k=0,\ldots,N-1

where
k
is the prediction step,

\Delta u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
are the input increments,
(\bar{A},\bar{B},\bar{C})
is a state-space realization of the model of the controlled process

\xi(t+1)=\bar{A}\xi(t)+\bar{B}u(t)
,
\tau(t)=\bar{C}\xi(t)
,
matrix
Q_{\tau}
is the weight on tracking errors,

Q_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}^{\prime}\succeq 0
,
Q_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}\times n_{\tau}}
and

Q_{\Delta u}
the weight of control increments,
Q_{\Delta u}=Q_{\Delta u}^{\prime}\succ 0
,

Q_{\Delta u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
;

N
the prediction horizon,
N_{u}
the number of free control moves,
N_{c}
the number of output constraints in prediction;
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
is a nonnegative slack variable used to soften output constraints via the given ECR (equal concern relaxation) nonnegative vectors
V_{\rm min},V_{\rm max}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}}
,
and is penalized with weights
\rho_{2},\rho_{1}\geq 0
.

Problem (
29
) can be transformed into the mpQP (
28
) by defining

x=\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\xi_{0}
,
r
,
u_{-1})
, where
\xi_{0}=\xi(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
the current state vector,

r=r(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\tau}}
the current output reference value, and
u_{-1}=u(t-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
the last control input applied to the process,
z=\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(u_{0},\ldots,u_{N_{u}-1},\zeta)
the sequence of future control moves optimized over the prediction horizon and slack, and
y=f(x)
the optimal value of
u_{0}
, i.e., the control input
u(t)
that gets applied to the process.

Let us assume
n_{u}=1
for simplicity of notation; in the case
n_{u}>1
we treat the approximation problem component-wise, i.e., we learn a surrogate model
\hat{f}_{i}
for each component
i=1,\ldots,n_{u}
of the optimal solution
f(x)
, using the approaches developed in Section
2
.

Assuming
u_{\rm min}\leq u_{\rm max}
,
\Delta u_{\rm min}\leq\Delta u_{\rm max}
, Problem (
28
) is feasible for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and such that the matrix
Q
is positive definite, so that the solution
z^{\star}(x)
exists and is unique.
When no constraints are active at the optimal solution
z^{\star}(x)
, we have that
f(x)
coincides
with the unconstrained solution

w(x)=-[1\ 0\ \ldots\ 0]Q^{-1}(Fx+f)

(30)

for all and only the values of
x
satisfying

H_{0}x\leq K_{0},\quad H_{0}\triangleq-AQ^{-1}F-B,\ K_{0}\triangleq b+AQ^{-1}f.

(31)

As we wish the surrogate model
\hat{f}
to coincide with the unconstrained solution (
30
)
when the active set is empty, we consider the approximations of the indicator function defined in (
6b
)
with
g(x)=\bar{H}_{0}x-\bar{K}_{0}
and
n_{h}=0
, where
\{x:\ \bar{H}_{0}x\leq\bar{K}_{0}\}
is a minimal
hyperplane representation of
\{x:\ H_{0}x\leq K_{0}\}
.
Moreover, to enforce the constraints on the first control input
u_{0}
,
we also consider the saturation functions (
8b
)
with
y_{\rm min}=\max(u_{{\rm min}},u_{-1}+\Delta u_{\rm min})
,
y_{\rm max}=\min(u_{{\rm max}},u_{-1}+\Delta u_{\rm max})
.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a general active-learning framework for training surrogate models with guaranteed, and possibly minimal, worst-case error bounds. The method applies to broad model classes and control-design tasks, including uncertain system identification and explicit MPC approximation, and its effectiveness was demonstrated numerically.

The approach heavily relies on the ability of the global optimizer to identify worst-case errors, both during active sampling and when computing input-dependent bounds. As the input dimension grows, this step becomes the main computational bottleneck, especially when the input dimension
n_{x}
increases. Future work will focus on reducing this cost through parallelization, for instance by partitioning the domain
{\mathcal{X}}
(or the range of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
) and performing parallel maximization of the approximation error, followed by joint retraining using the newly acquired samples.

Consider the scalar case
y\in\mathbb{R}
for simplicity
and a generic
\eta>0
. Clearly, by setting
\bar{y}\triangleq\frac{(y_{\rm max}-y_{\rm min})}{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{\eta}\left(\frac{y_{\rm min}+y_{\rm max}}{2};y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{\rm max}+\frac{1}{\eta}\log\Big(\frac{1+e^{-\eta\bar{y}}}{1+e^{\eta\bar{y}}}\Big)=y_{\rm max}+\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(e^{-\eta\bar{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{\rm max}-\bar{y}=\frac{(y_{\rm max}+y_{\rm min})}{2}.

The derivative of

with respect to
y

\sigma^{\prime}_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})=\frac{e^{\eta(y-y_{\rm min})}(1-e^{-\eta(y_{\rm max}-y_{\rm min})})}{(1+e^{\eta(y-y_{\rm min})})(1+e^{\eta(y-y_{\rm max})})}

whose denominator is positive. Since
y_{\rm max}>y_{\rm min}
and
\eta>0
, its numerator is also positive
and, hence,
\sigma^{\prime}_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})>0
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
, which implies that
\sigma_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})
is monotonically strictly increasing
with respect to
y
.

\sigma_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})
in (
8b

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to-\infty}\!\sigma_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})

\displaystyle=y_{\rm max}+\frac{\log(e^{\eta(y_{\rm min}-y_{\rm max})})}{\eta}

\displaystyle=y_{\rm min}

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to+\infty}\!\sigma_{\eta}(y;y_{\rm min},y_{\rm max})

\displaystyle=y_{\rm max}+\frac{\log(1)}{\eta}

\displaystyle=y_{\rm max}.
