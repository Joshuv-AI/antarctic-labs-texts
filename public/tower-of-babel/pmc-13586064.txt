Quality Assessment and Management in Organ Transplantation: A Comparative Analysis of the United States, Germany, and Chinese Mainland
Authors: Björn Nashan, Miao Pu, Jie Zhao, Haibo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOrgan transplantation is among the most complex domains of modern medicine, requiring the integration of advanced surgical techniques, individualized immunosuppressive therapy, structured professional training, and comprehensive quality assessment systems. As transplantation programs expand globally, ensuring consistency in clinical outcomes and patient safety has become increasingly dependent on the governance structures that regulate these domains. While Western systems emphasize decentralized, profession‐led models of training and quality assurance, Chinese Mainland has developed a centrally coordinated framework integrating regulatory oversight, standardized education, and data‐driven monitoring. This manuscript provides a comprehensive analysis of the interplay between immunosuppression, transplant surgeon training, and quality assessment systems, comparing Chinese Mainland with the United States and Germany. The findings highlight converging trends toward data‐driven quality management and competency‐based training, while underscoring persistent differences in regulatory philosophy. Chinese Mainland's integrated model offers advantages in standardization and scalability, whereas Western systems provide flexibility and innovation. These complementary approaches may inform future global harmonization of transplantation practice.

Background

Organ transplantation has evolved over the past six decades into a standard therapeutic option for end‐stage organ failure. This progress has been driven by advances in surgical technique, donor management, organ preservation, and, critically, immunosuppressive therapy. Despite these achievements, transplantation remains a high‐risk intervention requiring precise coordination across multiple domains, including pharmacology, clinical decision‐making, and institutional governance.

Modern transplantation systems must address three interdependent pillars: (1) standardized perioperative care, (2) structured training and certification of transplant professionals, and (3) reliable quality assessment and outcome monitoring. Each of these components is shaped by national healthcare structures and regulatory environments, resulting in considerable heterogeneity in global practice.

Transplant centers are increasingly adopting enhanced recovery after surgery protocols to standardize perioperative care [1]; however, these protocols often lack clear guidance on structured immunosuppression and therapeutic drug monitoring [2, 3]. In parallel, formal systems for training and certification of transplant professionals—as well as quality assessment and outcome monitoring—have become widely established, particularly in the United States, Germany, and Chinese Mainland [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

In recent years, Chinese Mainland has undergone substantial reform in organ donation and transplantation, transitioning to a voluntary citizen‐based donation system and establishing a comprehensive legal and administrative framework [13, 14, 15]. Parallel to these developments, a national system for training and certification of transplant surgeons has been implemented, integrating education, institutional accreditation, and regulatory oversight [6]. At the same time, quality assessment mechanisms have expanded, incorporating national data platforms and structured clinical pathways [7, 8].

In contrast, transplantation systems in the United States and Germany are characterized by decentralized governance, with professional societies and institutional accreditation bodies playing a central role in defining training standards and quality metrics. Germany represents a hybrid model, combining legally embedded certification with professional autonomy.

This paper aims to analyze these differing approaches by examining the different quality assessment frameworks. By placing the Chinese model within an international comparative context, we seek to provide insight into the evolving governance of transplantation and its implications for clinical practice.

Methods

This paper is a narrative review and comparative policy analysis of transplantation quality management systems in the United States, Germany, and Chinese Mainland. Sources were identified through targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar and supplemented by official policy documents, annual reports, and regulatory publications from national organizations, including the National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China, China Organ Transplant Response System, Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR), Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, Institut für Qualitätssicherung und Transparenz im Gesundheitswesen (Institute for Quality Assurance and Transparency in Healthcare, IQTIG), Gemeinsamer Bundesausschuss (Joint Federal Committee), and Deutsche Stiftung Organtransplantation (German Foundation for Organ Transplantation). Sources were selected based on relevance to governance, quality indicators, benchmarking, outcome assessment, and quality improvement in transplantation. Data were synthesized qualitatively using a comparative framework focusing on system structure, governance, data sources, participation requirements, strengths, and limitations. The analysis integrates published evidence with the authors’ professional experience in transplantation quality assurance and healthcare governance.

Quality Assessment and Outcome Monitoring

The United States: Registry‐Based Systems

In the United States, quality assessment is driven by national registries such as the SRTR [16] and quality improvement programs such as National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) and Transplant‐Specific Quality Improvement Program (TransQIP) [17, 18]. These systems provide risk‐adjusted outcome data, enabling benchmarking at the center and surgeon level.

Such programs support regulatory compliance and continuous quality improvement, allowing identification of best practices and areas for intervention. However, participation is partly driven by regulatory and financial incentives, reflecting a decentralized governance structure.

A central limitation of historical transplant outcome assessment is the lack of systematic, large‐scale data on postoperative complications. Although registries such as SRTR provide comprehensive survival data, complication rates—including surgical site infections (SSI), urinary tract infections (UTI), and reoperations—have largely been derived from single‐center experiences with heterogeneous definitions and timeframes, limiting comparability and benchmarking. Reported SSI rates after liver transplantation, for example, range from 12% to 30%, reflecting methodological inconsistencies rather than true biological variation.

The NSQIP and TransQIP platforms directly address these limitations by implementing prospective, standardized data collection with uniform definitions, enabling meaningful inter‐center comparisons. During the beta phase, the program expanded to 29 transplant centers and > 4300 donor–recipient pairs, including 1444 liver transplant cases, thereby providing the first comprehensive multicenter dataset capable of evaluating complication‐specific outcomes. Importantly, this dataset demonstrated substantial inter‐center variability in key outcomes even after risk adjustment, including SSI (0%–29%), UTI (0%–10%), and unplanned reoperation/intervention (0%–57%). These findings confirm that outcome variability extends well beyond survival metrics and highlight the need for structured quality improvement initiatives.

Further detailed analyses from the NSQIP Transplant dataset reinforce the clinical relevance of postoperative complications. In a multicenter study including 1684 deceased donor and 109 living donor liver transplant recipients, surgical complications (Clavien‐Dindo ≥ II within 90 days) occurred in 38% of deceased donor and 47% of living donor recipients [19]. The most frequent complications (deceased‐donor liver transplantation [DDLT] vs. living‐donor liver transplantation [LDLT]) were: (1) Biliary complications: 19% versus 31%; (2) Hemorrhage requiring reoperation: 14% versus 9%; (3) Vascular complications: 6% versus 9%.

Management strategies varied considerably (e.g., biliary leaks treated by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography in 35.3%, percutaneous drainage in 38.0%, and reoperation in 26.3%, unknown 0.4%), underscoring practice heterogeneity across centers. Critically, early postoperative complications were well‐defined associated with inferior outcomes: biliary complications (adjusted hazard ratio 5.14), hemorrhage (adjusted hazard ratio 2.54), and vascular complications were all linked to reduced 1‐year survival. These data emphasize that complications are not merely intermediate events but major determinants of long‐term transplant success.

From a methodological perspective, the NSQIP infrastructure provides the foundation for these analyses. The program captures > 150 standardized variables per case, including preoperative risk factors, intraoperative details, and 30‐day postoperative outcomes, collected by trained reviewers with rigorous quality control mechanisms. Data reliability is high, with inter‐rater disagreement rates around 2%, reflecting comprehensive auditing and standardized training processes. This level of data fidelity enables valid risk adjustment and benchmarking, which are essential for identifying outliers and guiding quality improvement.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that TransQIP/NSQIP Transplant represents a paradigm shift in transplantation quality assessment. By moving beyond survival metrics to include granular, risk‐adjusted complication data, the program enables: (1) Identification of clinically meaningful variation across centers; (2) Standardization of outcome definitions and reporting; (3) Evaluation of complication‐specific risk factors and management strategies; (4) Integration of quality improvement into routine transplant practice.

Importantly, the data consistently show that postoperative complications are common, variable, and well‐defined associated with survival, reinforcing the need for structured, data‐driven quality frameworks. In this context, TransQIP provides not only a measurement tool but also a platform for continuous quality improvement and benchmarking, with the potential to align clinical practice, improve outcomes, and inform policy in transplantation (Table 1).

Limitations of TransQIP

The TransQIP/NSQIP Transplant system represents a major advance in transplantation quality assessment through granular, prospective data collection; however, it is not without important limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings.

A primary limitation relates to its scope and temporal focus. Although TransQIP provides detailed information on perioperative and early postoperative complications, its core dataset is largely restricted to short‐term outcomes (typically 30–90 days). As a result, it does not fully capture long‐term graft survival, chronic complications, or late mortality, which remain central determinants of transplant success. This creates a structural separation between TransQIP and long‐term registries such as SRTR, limiting integrated longitudinal analyses.

Another key limitation is the voluntary and incentive‐driven nature of participation. Unlike fully mandated national systems, enrollment in NSQIP/TransQIP is influenced by institutional priorities, resources, and regulatory or financial incentives. This introduces the potential for selection bias, as participating centers may not be fully representative of all transplant programs, potentially skewing benchmarking results.

Despite its high granularity, the system also faces challenges related to risk adjustment and residual confounding. While a large number of variables (> 150 per case) are collected, not all relevant clinical factors—particularly nuanced aspects of surgical decision‐making, center expertise, and perioperative management—can be fully captured. Consequently, inter‐center variability persists even after adjustment, and differences in outcomes cannot always be definitively attributed to quality of care.

The resource‐intensive nature of data collection represents another limitation. Data acquisition requires trained reviewers, standardized abstraction, and continuous auditing. Although this ensures high data quality, it imposes a significant logistical and financial burden on participating centers, which may limit scalability and broader adoption.

In addition, while TransQIP excels in capturing complication data, it remains primarily focused on clinically measurable events such as infections, reoperations, and procedural complications. Important dimensions such as patient‐reported outcomes, functional recovery, and quality of life are not systematically included, leading to a partial representation of overall transplant success.

The system also reveals substantial practice heterogeneity across centers, particularly in the management of complications. While this variability is one of its strengths in identifying differences in care, it simultaneously reflects a limitation: the absence of standardized treatment pathways makes it difficult to distinguish optimal practices from institutional preference, and causal inference remains limited due to the observational design.

Furthermore, although data quality is generally high, inter‐center differences in documentation practices and interpretation of definitions may still occur, particularly in complex clinical scenarios. Even with standardized definitions, subtle variability in reporting can influence outcome comparisons.

Finally, TransQIP operates largely as a measurement and benchmarking tool rather than a regulatory framework. While it effectively identifies variation and supports quality improvement initiatives, it lacks formal mechanisms for enforcement or system‐wide standardization. Implementation of improvement strategies therefore depends heavily on local engagement and institutional commitment, which may vary substantially.

In summary, while TransQIP provides unprecedented insight into complication‐specific outcomes and represents a major step forward in transplantation quality assessment, its limitations include restricted long‐term outcome capture, voluntary participation and potential selection bias, residual confounding despite detailed data collection, high resource requirements, limited inclusion of patient‐centered outcomes, and the absence of centralized regulatory enforcement. These factors should be considered when integrating TransQIP findings into broader clinical and policy frameworks.

Germany: Structured National Quality Control

Germany employs a highly regulated system through IQTIG [20] and the German Foundation for Organ Transplantation [21]. Quality indicators are systematically collected, and deviations from benchmarks trigger structured responses, including peer review, audits, and regulatory interventions. This model emphasizes accountability and transparency, with detailed reporting requirements covering clinical outcomes, complications, and institutional performance.

The QS‐Verfahren Transplantationsmedizin—Bundesqualitätsbericht 2024 (Quality System Procedures in Transplantation Medicine—Federal Quality Report 2024) provides a comprehensive national evaluation of quality assurance in transplantation medicine in Germany under the DeQS‐Richtlinie (Data‐Supported Cross‐Institutional Quality Assurance Guideline) [20]. The system integrates organ‐specific modules—including heart, lung, liver, kidney transplantation, living donation, and mechanical circulatory support—into a unified, legally mandated framework. Its primary objective is to ensure transparency, comparability, and continuous improvement of clinical outcomes through standardized data collection, risk‐adjusted benchmarking, and structured evaluation.

Participation in the system is mandatory for all transplant centers. Data are collected longitudinally and encompass preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative variables, including donor and recipient characteristics, procedural details, complications, graft function, and survival outcomes. Quality is assessed using predefined indicators (QIs) combined with statistical reference ranges. Deviations trigger a structured dialogue process requiring institutional clarification, corrective measures, and, where necessary, escalation to peer review or on‐site audits.

In liver transplantation, quality assessment includes parameters such as early allograft function, primary graft dysfunction, ischemia–reperfusion injury, biliary complications, and patient survival. Risk adjustment incorporates recipient severity (e.g., Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease score), donor characteristics, and ischemia times. Overall survival remains within internationally accepted ranges, although moderate inter‐center variability persists. Postoperative complications—particularly ischemic‐type biliary lesions and vascular events—remain key determinants of long‐term outcomes.

Kidney transplantation demonstrates consistently well‐defined outcomes. Key indicators include delayed graft function, acute rejection, graft survival, and patient survival. Donor characteristics, including expanded criteria donors, cold ischemia time, and immunological risk, are central to risk adjustment. Living donation is assessed separately, with a well‐defined focus on donor safety, perioperative complications, and long‐term follow‐up. Severe donor complications are rare, reflecting stringent regulatory oversight. However, variability between centers and ongoing challenges in organ availability and allocation efficiency remain evident.

Heart transplantation is evaluated through perioperative mortality, primary graft dysfunction, rejection, and long‐term survival. Mechanical circulatory support systems, particularly ventricular assist devices, are incorporated as a complementary module, reflecting their increasing clinical relevance. Outcomes remain stable despite high procedural complexity, although optimization of patient selection and postoperative management remains an area for improvement.

Lung transplantation is characterized by higher clinical complexity and greater variability in outcomes. Indicators include early mortality, primary graft dysfunction, infection, and chronic lung allograft dysfunction. Lower case volumes in some centers limit statistical comprehensiveness, contributing to wider variability. Nonetheless, risk‐adjusted benchmarking indicates that most centers perform within expected ranges. Lower survival compared with other organs reflects both disease severity and procedural challenges.

A central strength of the German system is the structured linkage between quality indicators and regulatory oversight. The “structured dialogue” mechanism allows contextual interpretation of statistical deviations while maintaining accountability. This approach supports continuous quality improvement rather than punitive regulation.

Data completeness and linkage remain critical challenges. While overall documentation rates are high, limitations persist in long‐term follow‐up data and inter‐module integration. Consequently, data completeness itself has emerged as an independent quality indicator. Ongoing efforts focus on digitalization and improved data integration to enhance real‐time monitoring.

From a systems perspective, the German model represents a highly structured, legally embedded approach to quality assurance, contrasting with more registry‐based or voluntary systems internationally. While ensuring comprehensive coverage and standardization, this approach may introduce administrative complexity. Future developments will require refinement of risk adjustment models, particularly in the context of increasingly heterogeneous and higher‐risk transplant populations, as well as evolving donor utilization strategies.

Overall, the report demonstrates that transplantation medicine in Germany operates within a comprehensive, data‐driven quality framework with consistent with outcomes across organ systems. However, persistent inter‐center variability, limitations in follow‐up data, and structural challenges in low‐volume procedures highlight the need for continued system optimization (Table 2).

Limitations of the IQTIG

The German IQTIG quality assurance system for transplantation represents a highly structured and comprehensive framework; however, several inherent limitations must be considered when interpreting its findings and overall effectiveness.

A central limitation relates to data completeness and longitudinal follow‐up. Although documentation rates are generally high, gaps persist in long‐term follow‐up data and in the linkage between procedural and outcome datasets. This may introduce bias, particularly in survival analyses, and limits the comprehensiveness of outcome‐based benchmarking across centers. As a consequence, data completeness itself has increasingly become a relevant quality indicator within the system.

Another important constraint is the limited granularity of clinical data. Compared with international registry‐based systems, the German approach relies on a more aggregated dataset with fewer detailed clinical variables. While this supports standardization and regulatory comparability, it restricts deeper analyses of perioperative processes, complication pathways, and mechanistic drivers of outcomes. This may reduce the system's utility for detailed clinical research and targeted quality improvement at the procedural level.

The risk adjustment models, although integral to benchmarking, also have limitations. They may not fully capture the increasing heterogeneity of transplant populations, particularly with the growing use of marginal donors and the inclusion of higher‐risk recipients. Residual confounding therefore persists, and differences between centers may not be entirely attributable to quality of care but rather to variations in case mix.

Statistical limitations are particularly evident in low‐volume and high‐complexity procedures, such as lung or combined heart–lung transplantation. Small case numbers reduce statistical power and increase variability, making it difficult to distinguish true performance differences from random fluctuation. This affects the reliability and interpretability of center‐level comparisons.

The system's reliance on predefined quality indicators introduces further constraints. While these indicators enable standardization, they inevitably simplify complex clinical realities. Important dimensions such as functional outcomes, patient‐reported outcomes, and quality of life are insufficiently captured, leading to a predominantly outcome‐focused rather than patient‐centered evaluation of quality.

In addition, the system entails a substantial administrative burden. Mandatory documentation, reporting requirements, and participation in structured dialogue processes require significant institutional resources. This may divert time and personnel from clinical activities and potentially affect efficiency at the center level.

The regulatory and centralized nature of the system may also limit flexibility. The integration of new technologies, evolving clinical practices, or innovative treatment strategies can be slower compared with less regulated, more adaptive systems. This rigidity may be particularly relevant in a rapidly advancing field such as transplantation medicine.

Furthermore, although benchmarking is supported by structured dialogue processes, contextual factors are not always fully captured. Differences in regional healthcare structures, referral patterns, resource availability, and institutional specialization may influence outcomes but are only partially accounted for in the evaluation framework.

Finally, the system remains only partially harmonized with international quality frameworks, limiting direct comparability with other transplantation registries and benchmarking systems. Differences in definitions, indicators, and reporting structures complicate cross‐national analyses and the integration of global evidence.

In summary, while the German Quality System provides a comprehensive and transparent framework for national quality assurance, its limitations lie primarily in data completeness, restricted clinical granularity, imperfect risk adjustment, statistical constraints in low‐volume procedures, administrative complexity, and limited adaptability. Addressing these challenges will be essential to further enhance its effectiveness and relevance in an evolving transplantation landscape.

Chinese Mainland: Integrated National Framework

Chinese Mainland has developed a national approach to quality assessment, integrating data reporting, regulatory oversight, and clinical protocols.

The 2024 report [14, 22] describes quality in Chinese Mainland's organ donation and transplantation system as a combination of structural capacity, process control, and outcome monitoring, supported by centralized reporting and national registries. Across the donation system, quality is reflected by increasing organizational maturity and donor activity [14, 23]. The report documents 6744 deceased donations in 2024, a 4.5% increase over 2023, with a national donation rate of 4.79 per million population (PMP). There was marked regional variation: Beijing 10.98 PMP, Hainan 10.64 PMP, and Guangxi 10.44 PMP were among the highest‐performing regions. Donation activity was also concentrated geographically, with the largest absolute numbers in Shandong (818 donations), Guangdong (736), Guangxi (525), Henan (497), and Hubei (407). Quality at the organizational level is further illustrated by the presence of 30 provincial administrative networks and 2701 trained coordinators, indicating an established but still regionally uneven infrastructure. Meanwhile, improving organ utilization efficiency, strengthening the professional development of organ procurement organizations, and building a specialized workforce have become key priorities for advancing the quality of organ donation and procurement.

In liver transplantation, quality is presented through center distribution, perioperative indicators, complications, and survival. In 2024, Chinese Mainland performed 7188 liver transplants, including 6242 deceased‐donor liver transplants (DDLT; 86.8%) and 946 living‐donor liver transplants (LDLT; 13.2%), of which 3 were domino transplants. There were 6055 adult (84.2%) and 1133 pediatric (15.8%) liver transplants. Activity was concentrated in 113 qualified institutions; the leading provinces were Shanghai (1053 cases), Guangdong (703), Zhejiang (689), and Shandong (655). Recipient demographics showed a mean age of 44.0 years, body mass index (BMI) 22.5 kg/m2, and 73.7% male recipients. Quality and safety metrics show lower perioperative burden in LDLT than in DDLT: the report states lower cold ischemia time, anhepatic phase, intraoperative blood loss, and red blood cell transfusion volume for LDLT, with only the operative time being slightly longer. In 2024, the anhepatic phase averaged 48.9 min in DDLT versus 43.4 min in LDLT, and intraoperative blood loss averaged 1227.9 mL in DDLT versus 412.1 mL in LDLT. Total serum bilirubin declined substantially after transplantation in both groups, from 186.1 to 19.5 μmol/L in DDLT and from 180.9 to 19.1 μmol/L in LDLT by 6 months. The 30‐day complication rate was 27.7% in DDLT and 18.8% in LDLT; the main complications were pleural effusion (16.3%), postoperative infection (11.0%), and ascites/peritoneal abscess (8.3%) after DDLT, and infection (11.5%), pleural effusion (6.0%), and ascites/peritoneal abscess (4.6%) after LDLT. The 30‐day mortality was 5.5% for DDLT and 2.6% for LDLT. Long‐term recipient survival was superior in LDLT, with 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year survival of 92.6%, 89.9%, and 87.9%, compared with 82.9%, 71.5%, and 63.5% after DDLT Graft survival followed the same pattern (LDLT: 91.9%, 88.9%, 86.8%; DDLT: 82.2%, 70.5%, 62.6% at 1, 3, and 5 years).

In kidney transplantation, quality is characterized by high procedural volume concentrated in leading provinces and centers, and standardized monitoring. In 2024, Chinese Mainland reported 14,968 kidney transplants, including 11,575 deceased‐donor and 3393 living‐donor procedures, representing increases of 2.2% and 4.8%, respectively, compared with 2023. There were 149 qualified institutions nationwide. The highest provincial volumes were in Guangdong (1634 cases), Shandong (1575), Henan (1312), Hubei (972), and Zhejiang (904). The report highlights the concentration of activity: 10 provinces with ≥ 600 transplants accounted for 66.8% of all kidney transplants, and 14 institutions performing ≥ 250 cases accounted for 37.6% of total activity. In 2024, 761 pediatric kidney transplants were performed, accounting for 5.0% of all kidney transplants and representing a 13.8% increase over 2023. Combined kidney‐related multiorgan transplantation reached 151 cases, including combined pancreas–kidney, liver–kidney, and heart–kidney transplantation. Recipient characteristics included a mean age of 40.2 years, BMI 22.3 kg/m2, and 69.9% male recipients. The report emphasizes that cold and warm ischemia times were reasonably controlled and that serum creatinine decreased significantly after transplantation and remained stable, indicating consistent with graft function. It also notes that quality concerns remain in the subgroup undergoing kidney‐related multiorgan procedures, where infection rates after pancreas–kidney transplantation and graft loss due to recipient death were relatively high.

In heart transplantation, quality is evaluated with more granular process indicators. The report table indicates 1064 heart transplant recipients in the analyzed cohort, including 916 adults and 148 pediatric recipients. Recipient demographics show a median age of 50.0 years, median BMI 21.9 kg/m2, and 73.1% male recipients. The predominant indication was non‐ischemic cardiomyopathy (72.9%), followed by coronary heart disease (11.0%), valvular heart disease (3.8%), and congenital heart disease (1.7%); among pediatric recipients, 77.0% had non‐ischemic cardiomyopathy and 7.8% congenital heart disease. Several quality process measures improved over time. The rate of preoperative cardiopulmonary exercise testing increased from 44.4% in 2022 to 57.2% in 2023 and 61.3% in 2024. Median donor heart ischemia time declined from 3.6 h in 2022 to 3.3 h in 2023 and 3.1 h in 2024; the proportion with ischemia time < 6 h rose to 88.4% in 2024. Postoperative mechanical ventilation decreased to a median of 20.0 h in 2024, with 75.4% of patients extubated within 48 h. The in‐hospital survival rate in 2024 was 95.0% overall, 94.6% in adults, and 97.3% in pediatric recipients. Reported postoperative complications included infection (13.4%), reintubation (6.5%), reoperation (5.8%), tracheotomy (5.5%), and cardiac arrest (2.7%). Among in‐hospital deaths, leading causes included multiple organ failure (30.4%), infection (26.1%), and allograft heart failure (8.7%). These parameters indicate a quality system with explicit attention to donor ischemia, perioperative support, and short‐term safety outcomes.

In lung transplantation, the report provides detailed quality indicators related to case volume, procedure type, perioperative care, and survival. By the end of 2024, Chinese Mainland had 60 qualified lung transplant institutions across 24 provinces, and 925 lung transplants were performed in 2024, following 959 in 2023 and 798 in 2022. The leading center in 2024 was Wuxi People's Hospital with 174 cases (44 single‐lung and 130 bilateral), followed by The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (170 cases: 45 single, 125 bilateral) and The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (111 cases: 42 single, 68 bilateral, 1 combined heart–lung). In 2024, 45 institutions performed lung transplantation. Pediatric activity remained limited but growing: 15 pediatric lung transplants were performed in 2024, while the cumulative number since 2015 reached 84. Among adults in 2024, 79.0% were male, mean age was 57.1 ± 12.1 years, and 50.6% were older than 60 years. Pediatric recipients had a mean age of 11.4 ± 3.8 years and 73.3% were male. Adult indications were mainly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (35.4%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (20.6%), secondary pulmonary fibrosis (10.9%), and pneumoconiosis (10.5%); in children, bronchiolitis obliterans accounted for 9 of 15 cases, followed by pulmonary hypertension and other rare disorders. In 2024, 71.6% of adult procedures were bilateral lung transplants, 28.2% were single‐lung, and 14.9% were emergency procedures; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was used in 81.1% of cases. Median cold ischemia time was 6.0 h for single‐lung and 8.3 h for bilateral transplantation; in pediatric recipients it was 7.0 h. Adult median intraoperative transfusion volume was 1000 mL (interquartile range 50.00–1763.75 mL), compared with 300 mL in pediatric recipients. The median intensive care unit stay among pediatric recipients was 260.0 h, and median hospital stay 30.0 days. The 30‐day survival for adult recipients in 2024 was 85.8%; major causes of death included infection (29.8%), multiple organ failure (18.2%), sudden cardiac death (12.4%), hemorrhagic shock (4.1%), and graft dysfunction (4.1%). For all lung transplant recipients in Chinese Mainland from 2019 to 2024, survival rates were 84.1% at 30 days, 74.3% at 3 months, 67.9% at 6 months, 60.6% at 1 year, 46.3% at 3 years, and 38.8% at 5 years. By procedure type, 1‐year survival was 60.2% after bilateral and 61.5% after single‐lung transplantation, while 3‐year survival was 48.8% and 43.2%, respectively. Outcome heterogeneity by disease was also reported; for example, obliterative bronchiolitis showed particularly consistent with results (91.0%, 30‐day; 81.5, 3‐month; 79.9%, 6‐month; 77.7%, 1‐year; 68.5%, 3‐year survival).

Overall, the report portrays a donation and transplantation system in which quality is increasingly measured through quantitative center distribution, perioperative process variables, complication profiles, and survival curves. The strength of the current approach lies in the breadth of national reporting and in the explicit use of measurable clinical indicators. At the same time, the data consistently show regional concentration of expertise, and in some domains—particularly lung and heart transplantation—quality remains closely linked to high‐volume specialized centers (Table 3).

Limitations

The 2024 national report indicates that, despite substantial progress, the Chinese transplantation system continues to exhibit regional variability in activity and infrastructure, with donation rates and transplant volumes concentrated in specific provinces and high‐volume centers. This suggests that access to transplantation and associated expertise remains uneven geographically. In addition, while the system provides comprehensive national data on volumes, processes, and survival, the reporting of outcomes is predominantly based on aggregated indicators, with less detailed standardization of complication profiles and their management. Furthermore, quality metrics are largely focused on perioperative parameters and short‐ to mid‐term outcomes, whereas the integration of detailed long‐term functional outcomes and patient‐centered measures remains limited. These factors indicate that, although the system is well established at a structural and national level, further refinement in data granularity, regional balance, and outcome characterization may enhance its evaluative capacity.

Comparative Perspective

The comparative analysis of transplantation quality assessment systems across the United States, Germany, and Chinese Mainland reveals three distinct yet complementary paradigms, each reflecting underlying differences in healthcare governance, data infrastructure, and conceptual approaches to quality.

Chinese Mainland has developed a system‐centered model, in which quality is defined at the level of the national transplantation ecosystem. The framework integrates organ donation, allocation, clinical practice, and outcome monitoring within a centralized governance structure led by national authorities. Quality assessment encompasses structural capacity (e.g., number of centers, coordinators), process indicators (e.g., ischemia times, perioperative parameters), and outcomes (e.g., survival and complication rates). The strength of this model lies in its comprehensive national coverage and ability to coordinate large‐scale system development, as reflected by increasing donor activity, high procedural volumes, and expanding infrastructure. However, this approach is comparatively less granular at the individual patient and complication level, and quality remains influenced by regional disparities and concentration of expertise in high‐volume centers.

In contrast, the United States represents a data‐centered model, driven by registry‐based systems such as SRTR and quality improvement platforms like NSQIP/TransQIP. Here, quality is conceptualized primarily through granular, risk‐adjusted clinical data, with a well‐defined focus on postoperative complications and short‐term outcomes. The introduction of TransQIP has addressed a major historical limitation by enabling standardized, prospective collection of complication data across centers, revealing substantial variability in outcomes such as infections and reoperations. This model excels in detailed clinical insight and identification of modifiable risk factors, supporting targeted quality improvement initiatives at the center level. However, it is inherently fragmented, with separation between short‐term complication data and long‐term survival registries, and participation is partly voluntary, introducing potential selection bias. Moreover, the resource‐intensive nature of data collection may limit scalability.

The German approach represents a regulation‐centered model, characterized by legally mandated participation, standardized quality indicators, and centralized oversight through institutions such as IQTIG. Quality is primarily assessed through predefined outcome metrics, including mortality and survival, combined with structured benchmarking and formal review processes. This system ensures a high degree of transparency, comparability, and accountability, supported by mechanisms such as structured dialogue and audit procedures. However, its reliance on aggregated indicators limits clinical granularity, and challenges remain in data completeness, particularly for long‐term follow‐up, as well as in the flexibility to rapidly incorporate new clinical practices or technologies.

Across all three systems, important differences emerge in the definition and operationalization of quality. Chinese Mainland emphasizes system performance and scalability, the United States prioritizes clinical detail and complication‐driven insight, and Germany focuses on standardization and regulatory compliance. These differences are also reflected in data structures: Chinese Mainland integrates longitudinal national datasets, the United States employs highly granular but partially fragmented registries, and Germany uses standardized but comparatively less detailed datasets.

Despite these differences, common challenges are evident. All systems face limitations in risk adjustment for increasingly complex patient populations, incomplete integration of patient‐reported outcomes, and the need for improved long‐term data linkage. Furthermore, variability between centers persists across all models, highlighting that quality is influenced not only by system design but also by institutional experience, case mix, and resource availability.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the three systems are not competing but rather complementary in their strengths. An optimal future framework for transplantation quality assessment would likely integrate Chinese Mainland's national coordination and scalability, the United States’ granular complication‐level analytics, and the German model's regulatory standardization and accountability. Such a hybrid approach could enable more comprehensive, precise, and actionable quality assessment, ultimately supporting improved outcomes in transplantation medicine (Table 4).

A comparison of the limitations across the Chinese, the United States (TransQIP/NSQIP), and German (IQTIG) systems highlights that each reflects a distinct structural trade‐off between scale, granularity, and regulatory control, rather than a hierarchy of quality.

The Chinese system is primarily limited by restricted clinical granularity and evolving risk adjustment. While it captures comprehensive national data across donation, allocation, and transplantation, complication reporting is less standardized and less detailed than in TransQIP. This constrains fine‐grained benchmarking of surgical complications and their management. In addition, although survival and process metrics are well documented, the linkage between perioperative variables and long‐term outcomes is less explicitly developed. A further limitation is regional heterogeneity, with outcomes closely associated with high‐volume centers despite centralized governance. Finally, although national coverage is a strength, external direct comparability with international datasets remain limited due to the differences in data granularity and variable definitions.

In contrast, the United States TransQIP/NSQIP system addresses the issue of granularity but introduces different limitations. Its major strength—prospective, standardized, complication‐level data—is accompanied by fragmentation of outcome assessment. Core datasets focus on short‐term outcomes (typically 30–90 days), while long‐term survival is captured separately in registries such as SRTR. This separation limits integrated longitudinal analyses. In addition, participation is not universally mandatory, which may introduce selection bias and limit full national representativeness. Despite advanced risk adjustment, residual confounding persists, as institutional expertise and practice patterns cannot be fully captured. Furthermore, the system is resource‐intensive, requiring dedicated personnel for data abstraction.

The German IQTIG system represents a regulation‐centered national model, characterized by mandatory participation, standardized quality indicators, and formalized benchmarking. Its limitations lie in restricted clinical depth and data granularity, as the system relies on aggregated indicators rather than detailed perioperative and complication‐level data. This limits mechanistic insight into outcome differences between centers. A key constraint is also incomplete follow‐up data and linkage, which can affect the comprehensiveness of long‐term outcome analyses. In addition, low case volumes in certain procedures, particularly lung transplantation, reduce statistical power and complicate benchmarking. The regulatory structure, while ensuring accountability, is associated with administrative burden and reduced flexibility, potentially slowing the adoption of new clinical practices.

When directly compared, these limitations reflect complementary system characteristics: (1) The Chinese Mainland system provides national integration and scale but is limited in granular complication analysis and regional uniformity. (2) The United States system provides high‐resolution clinical and complication data, but is limited by fragmentation, short‐term focus, and participation variability. (3) The German system provides standardized, mandatory benchmarking, but is limited in clinical detail, data completeness, and flexibility.

Across all three systems, shared challenges remain, including incomplete incorporation of patient‐reported outcomes, limitations in fully capturing complex risk profiles, and persistent inter‐center variability despite risk adjustment.

Overall, the observed limitations are best understood as structural consequences of different design priorities: data granularity (the United States), regulatory standardization (Germany), and central coordination (Chinese Mainland). A more comprehensive future framework would require integration of these complementary strengths while addressing their respective constraints.

Discussion

This comparative analysis highlights that contemporary transplantation systems are converging toward data‐driven quality assessment, standardized training pathways, and structured perioperative management, yet they remain shaped by fundamentally different governance models. As outlined in the manuscript, Chinese Mainland has developed a centrally coordinated framework integrating regulation, clinical practice, and quality monitoring, while the United States relies on registry‐based, decentralized quality improvement systems, and Germany employs a legally embedded national quality assurance structure (Table 5).

A considerable strength of all three systems is board‐certified accreditation for transplant surgeons/physicians. Board‐certified training and certification systems for transplant surgeons represent a critical pillar of modern transplantation, directly linking professional competency with quality management and accountability. In the United States, certification follows a structured fellowship model governed by professional bodies such as American Society of Transplant Surgeons and Transplant Accreditation and Certification Council [4], requiring prior specialty board‐certification, defined operative volumes, and competency‐based assessment. Germany employs a legally embedded certification system under the Bundesärztekammer (German Medical Association) [5], integrating transplantation into formal specialty training with mandatory fellowships, standardized curricula, and documented procedural requirements. In Chinese Mainland, a nationally coordinated system which is established by the National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China and implemented by the China Organ Transplantation Development Foundation, aligns surgeon training, institutional accreditation, and regulatory oversight, with competency‐based education [6], standardized case requirements, and centralized certification processes. Across all three systems, certification frameworks ensure that transplant surgeons meet defined clinical, ethical, and procedural standards. This formalization of training not only supports consistent clinical performance but also enables traceability, benchmarking, and regulatory oversight, thereby forming a foundational component of comprehensive quality assurance systems in transplantation medicine.

Across all systems, there is a clear shift from sole reliance on survival outcomes toward multidimensional quality metrics, including complication profiles, process indicators, and risk‐adjusted benchmarking. The introduction of platforms such as TransQIP in the United States demonstrates the increasing importance of granular complication data, revealing substantial inter‐center variability and emphasizing the impact of perioperative events on long‐term outcomes. Similarly, the German IQTIG system illustrates how standardized indicators combined with regulatory oversight can ensure transparency and accountability at a national level.

The Chinese system reflects a different but complementary approach, characterized by comprehensive national reporting and integration of structural, process, and outcome indicators. The 2024 national report demonstrates increasing procedural volume, improving perioperative metrics, and generally consistent with survival outcomes across organ systems. At the same time, the data reveal concentration of expertise in high‐volume centers and regional variability, suggesting that system maturity is still evolving.

A key observation across systems is the persistent challenge of risk adjustment and data completeness, particularly as transplant populations become more heterogeneous and include higher‐risk recipients. In addition, long‐term outcomes, patient‐reported measures, and functional recovery remain insufficiently captured across all frameworks, indicating an area for future development.

Finally, differences in governance reflect distinct strategic priorities: centralized systems may facilitate rapid standardization and scalability, whereas decentralized models support flexibility, innovation, and clinician‐driven adaptation. These approaches should be viewed as complementary rather than competing paradigms.

Conclusions

Organ transplantation is increasingly regulated and data‐driven, linking quality assessment, training, and clinical care. The United States, Germany, and Chinese Mainland use distinct models—centralized coordination, registry‐based improvement, and legally embedded quality control. All are shifting toward comprehensive outcome evaluation. Key challenges remain in data integration and variability. Future progress depends on better data, risk models, and global standardization.
