A pedestrian perspective street view dataset for prosthetic vision simulation
Authors: Ziyang Fan, Ying Zhao, Lin Gan, Yuxi Wang, Yuerong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional street view datasets are collected from a vehicle-mounted perspective predominantly, which cannot truly reflect pedestrians' perception in complex urban environments. A Pedestrian Perspective Street View Dataset (PPSVD) for the study of simulated prosthetic vision was proposed and constructed in this paper to maximize the reproduction of street scenes from a pedestrian's perspective and meet the complex environmental simulation needs for simulated prosthetic vision research. There were 1200 high-quality instance-level annotations’ images collected from real street scenes to form PPSVD which covered 16 typical object categories of common street elements such as pedestrians, vehicles, buildings or traffic lights etc. The experimental results showed for the segmentation accuracy of target areas, the model was trained on the PPSVD significantly outperformed than those trained on general datasets. Especially, in complex scenes, small targets and occluded situations, the performance of the model was improved even more pronounced. Furthermore, the PPSVD demonstrated significant performance improvements across models of diverse sizes and structures, validating its effectiveness and applicability in various algorithms. Therefore, the proposed PPSVD not only creates a more realistic environment for simulated prosthetic vision experiments but also serves as a crucial data resource for related research.Keywords: Simulated prosthetic vision, Image dataset, Pedestrian perspective, Instance segmentation for street scene, YOLO Model

Background

The visual system, being the most critical sensory modality for humans, facilitates the acquisition of approximately 80% of information in daily activities through visual perception. According to the latest data released in 2024, the total population of individuals with visual impairments (including low vision and blindness) in China has reached 17.31 million, accounting for approximately 17% of the nation's total disabled population. At the 78th World Health Assembly convened by the World Health Organization in 2025, it was highlighted that at least 2.2 billion people worldwide experience near or distance vision impairment. Among these, the number of individuals affected by age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [1] reached 195.6 million in 2020. In May 2023, China introduced the ``Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Age-Related Macular Degeneration in China (2023),'' which indicated that the prevalence of AMD in China increases with age, rising from 2.44% among individuals aged 45–49 to 18.98% among those aged 85–89. Epidemiological data from the ``Guidelines for Retinitis Pigmentosa in China (2019 Edition)'' suggested that the prevalence of retinitis pigmentosa (RP) [2] was approximately 1 in 3784. Given that RP is predominantly a hereditary disorder, it is projected that the number of RP patients in China will reach approximately 3.7 to 3.8 million by 2025.

Travel is one of the essential basic activities in people's daily lives. For patients with visual impairment, the implantation of visual prostheses can restore their environmental perception to enhance travel safety significantly. However, the real travel environment contains a large number of complex and diverse visual elements, including static factors such as buildings, street lamps, traffic lights and vegetation, as well as dynamic targets such as pedestrians and vehicles. Their spatial distribution, scale changes and motion characteristics are all highly complex [3]. In order to explore the demand for information provision methods of prosthesis devices and the influencing factors of dynamic target perception ability when visually impaired patients travel with visual prostheses, it is necessary to reproduce the actual scene for the research of simulated prosthesis vision as realistically as possible.

At present, several datasets of natural scenes, such as MS COCO [4], PASCAL VOC [5], Cityscapes [6], BDD100K [7] and KITTI [8], have been widely used in the research and evaluation of object detection and instance segmentation algorithms. Qiuxi Qiang et al [9] verified the UDS-YOLO model on the Cityscapes dataset. Zhifan W et al [10] conducted extensive experiments on the KITTI dataset, and the results clearly demonstrated the superiority of the DISNet network in segmenting instances of different depths. However, most of these street scene datasets are constructed for autonomous driving applications and mainly collect data from a vehicle perspective. Their shooting perspectives, field of view ranges and imaging characteristics are different significantly from those of the human eye without the real visual perception features of pedestrians. At the same time, vehicle-view street scene datasets are usually collected in the environment of motor vehicle lanes, which is significantly different from the pedestrian sidewalks where people actually move. Moreover, the vehicle's driving speed is much higher than that of pedestrians, resulting in essential differences in the movement patterns and temporal characteristics of targets in the scene compared to pedestrian travel scenarios. Therefore, dure to the existing vehicle-view street scene datasets are difficult to meet the actual needs of research on mobility for patients with visual impairments, it is necessary to construct the pedestrian-view street scene datasets.

In the field of simulated prosthetic vision, with the rapid development of virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) technologies, researchers have gradually begun to conduct spatial perception experiments for prosthetic vision wearers by leveraging virtual environments. Virtual street scenes are usually constructed using game engines such as Unity3D, by simulating urban roads, architectural structures and traffic elements et.al., to form a highly controllable experimental environment [11]. In such studies, the real street scene are not been directly perceived or processed by the subjects or algorithms but conduct analysis of prosthetic visual spatial perception based on the virtual environment to avoid uncontrollable factors in the real environment. However, although virtual street scenes have good controllability, they still have a gap in scene authenticity and complexity compared to the real world. Especially, in the case of pedestrian perspectives with the densely distributed small-scale targets, diverse shapes and frequently dynamic interactions, it poses higher requirements for the authenticity of the data set and the precision of annotations.

To solve above problems, a pedestrian perspective street view dataset (PPSVD) for simulation of artificial visual perception was proposed and constructed in this paper. This dataset was focused on the pedestrian perspective, collected real street view images and provides high-quality instance-level annotations, covering various typical street scene objects and complex travel scenarios. The PPSVD dataset aims to provide a more realistic and challenging experimental platform for target detection and instance segmentation under artificial visual simulation conditions and could offer reliable data support for research related to the safe travel of prosthetic wearers.DATA DESCRIPTION

Dataset structure

The PPSVD dataset comprises two core components: raw images and instance annotation files, both stored in the same directory for convenient access and cross-reference. The folder contains 1200 street scene images in JPG format with a uniform resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels. Files follow the naming convention 00000x.jpg, where x indicates the image sequence number. The same folder also contains 1200 JSON files corresponding one-to-one with the image files, stored in native LabelMe format. Each JSON file records the category labels and polygon contour coordinates of all instances in the corresponding image. The dataset does not include a predefined train/validation/test split, allowing users to partition the data according to their specific research needs.

Dataset analysis

The proposed pedestrian-level street view dataset differs significantly from traditional datasets, which are predominantly captured from a vehicle-centric perspective using cameras mounted on cars. However, the images in the PPSVD dataset were captured by pedestrians in real-world walking scenarios, aiming to closely approximate the pedestrian perspective. Therefore, the PPSVD dataset presented some unique features.

(1)Both the perspective height and field of view align with human vision. As illustrated in the dataset example in Fig. 1, all images are captured from a first-person perspective. This viewpoint provides a realistic perception of the street environment, encompassing the motorway, sidewalks, green belts, and surrounding buildings. The field of view is sufficiently broad to capture the environment where pedestrians are walking.

Dataset comparison

Table 1 presented a comparison of the PPSVD dataset against five commonly used semantic or instance segmentation datasets. The Cityscapes dataset comprises 5000 finely annotated images for core evaluation and 20,000 coarsely annotated images for weakly supervised learning, all at a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels. The BDD100K [7] dataset consists of 50,000 images and 100,000 40-second videos. Its 100,000 keyframe images are utilized for detection and segmentation at 1280 × 720 pixels. CamVid [12], an early semantic segmentation dataset, features a resolution of 960 × 720 pixels. The KITTI [8] dataset, originating from driving scenarios, provides only detection annotations (no segmentation) with a resolution of 1242 × 375 pixels. Similarly, Caltech Pedestrain [13] is derived from urban driving videos in the United States and annotated only pedestrian instances at 640 × 480 pixels. In contrast, the PPSVD dataset is captured from the perspective of real pedestrian walking for instance segmentation task, offering a significantly higher resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels.

Regarding semantic categories, all datasets except the Caltech Pedestrian include classes such as vehicles, pedestrians, road surfaces and vegetation. While the PPSVD dataset shares common categories with existing benchmarks, it places greater emphasis on elements that directly impact pedestrian path planning and decision-making.

In terms of dataset size, although the PPSVD dataset contains the fewest images, its high resolution (3840 × 2160), allows for the preservation of fine details and small targets, making it suitable for diverse hardware deployment requirements.

Regarding application, as shown in Table 1, the first three datasets are primarily utilized in autonomous driving. The CamVid dataset, owing to its early release, serves as a benchmark for fundamental semantic segmentation researche, whereas Caltech Pedestrian focuses on pedestrian detection. Notably, the PPSVD is the only dataset among the six designed for the spatial perception of visual prosthesis wearers, facilitating better perception of real-world street environments.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Dataset collection and preprocessing

The images in the PPSVD dataset were all sourced from real street scenes. Fig. 2 showed the collection and preprocessing of the PPSVD dataset.

Data acquisition and preprocessing of the PPSVD dataset.

Firstly, the data collection stage aims to simulate the perspective of natural pedestrians to obtain real-world street scene video sequences. The collection process followed a standardized protocol to ensure the consistency and reproducibility of the data. The data collection system consisted of a vivo X100S smartphone and a DJI Osmo Mobile SE handheld stabilizing gimbal. The smartphone was connected to the gimbal and simulated the height of the pedestrian's torso. The built-in stabilization function of the smartphone was enabled, and the gimbal was used to counteract the body movements of the collector during walking to ensure the stability of the video stream. The main camera of the smartphone with Sony IMX920 sensor model which has 50 million pixels was used for collection. The DJI Osmo Mobile SE adopts three-axis stabilization technology. During shooting, the equivalent focal length of the main camera was set to 23 mm (displayed as 1x optical zoom on the phone interface), and the phone lens should be perpendicular to the ground. All lens parameters (including focusing, exposure, and white balance) were set to manual locking to avoid automatic adjustments during the shooting process to maintain the consistency of the entire scene. The phone was recorded in landscape mode to match a wider field of view. The equipment was debugged to ensure that the distance from the phone to the ground was 1.65 m, simulating the average eye level of an adult. When passing through intersections or T-junctions, the collector walked at a speed of 1.2 m to 1.5 m per second used the turn control button of the gimbal to smoothly and uniformly moved the phone lens left or right to simulate the safe observation behavior of a visually normal person in such scenarios. It should be noted that throughout the data collection process, the stabilization function of the gimbal should remain operational to ensure that the generated video was free from interference from high-frequency jitter. At the same time, the shooting angle and shooting height of the phone should remain relatively unchanged relative to the ground.

After the data collection, a series of subsequent processes were carried out on the original video data to generate a labeled dataset that could be used for model training and evaluation. Using a custom script, 4 K (3840 × 2160) @30fps videos were extracted at a fixed frequency of one frame per second from the collected footage. The continuous video stream was transformed into a discrete sequence of images. A total of 713 initial images were captured. To ensure data quality, the initial images were accessed for clarity. Images exhibiting overexposure, being out-of-focus, or blurry scenes were excluded. Subsequently, images were screened to exclude scenes with intense shaking, excessive tilt, or misalignment caused by gimbal rotation. Additionally, duplicate images resultin from equipment debugging and other anomalies were removed. After saving the initial 600 images, mirroring techniques were applied, resulting in a final dataset of 1200 images.

Dataset annotation

For this annotation, LabelMe [14]. was selected as the annotation tool. LabelMe is an interactive image annotation tool developed by the Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. It enables annotators to outline contours using polygons and supports various annotation types, making it particularly suitable for generating labels for semantic and instance segmentation tasks. On each image in the PPSVD dataset, the contour of each instance was sketched using LabelMe's Create Polygons tool and marked their instance names, as shown in Fig. 3. After the annotation using LabelMe, the annotation information of each image were independently stored in a JSON format file with the same name as the image.

PPSVD dataset annotation.

The label names of the PPSVD dataset referred to those of the Cityscapes [6] dataset. Cityscapes is an authoritative dataset focused on urban street scene understanding, featuring high-quality, fine-grained annotations. Its labels encompass 30 visual semantic subcategories, which are grouped into 8 super- categories. Of these, 19 semantic categories are commonly used. These are orgnaized into 8 ``object'' categories (supporting instance segmentation) and 11 background categories (restricted to semantic segmentation). Data processing environment

All experiments were conducted on a workstation running Windows 11, with the deep learning model implemented in the PyTorch framework(CUDA 12.9). The hardware configuration consisted of an Intel i7–14700KF processor (8 cores, 16 threads, with a maximum speed of 4.6 GHz), 64GB DDR5 memory, and a GeForce RTX 5090 graphics card with 32GB video memory for training and inference.

Limitations

The following limitations exist when using this dataset. First, the dataset currently contains only 1200 images, which is a relatively small sample size. This may affect the model's generalization ability in different urban environments or complex scenarios. Second, the weather conditions during data collection were rather monotonous, mainly focusing on sunny and cloudy weather. Samples under extreme weather conditions such as rainy, snowy, and foggy days are lacking, which limits the dataset's applicability in all-weather scenarios. Third, some categories have insufficient sample sizes, such as buses, bicycles, and pedestrians. This class imbalance may lead to inadequate recognition and segmentation accuracy for these low-frequency targets. Finally, the imaging device was a consumer-grade smartphone (vivo X100S), which suffers from degraded image quality under low-light, fast-moving, or high dynamic range conditions, including increased noise, motion blur, and loss of detail. Although some effects were mitigated through gimbal stabilization and data screening, the inherent hardware limitations of the device may still constrain model training performance under extreme conditions.
