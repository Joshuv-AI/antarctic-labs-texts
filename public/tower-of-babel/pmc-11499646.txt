Sacubitril/valsartan improves diastolic left ventricular stiffness with increased titin phosphorylation via cGMP-PKG activation in diabetic mice
Authors: Nozomi Furukawa, Hiroki Matsui, Hiroaki Sunaga, Kohzo Nagata, Masaaki Hirayama, Hideru Obinata, Tomoyuki Yokoyama, Kinji Ohno, Masahiko Kurabayashi, Norimichi Koitabashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTitin, a giant sarcomeric protein, regulates diastolic left ventricular (LV) passive stiffness as a molecular spring and could be a therapeutic target for diastolic dysfunction. Sacubitril/valsartan (Sac/Val), an angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor, has been shown to benefit patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. The effect of Sac/Val is thought to be due to the enhancement of the cGMP/PKG pathway via natriuretic peptide. In this study, the effects of Sac/Val on LV diastolic dysfunction are demonstrated in a mouse diabetic cardiomyopathy model focusing on titin phosphorylation. Sac/Val-treated diabetic mice showed a greater increase in myocardial levels of cGMP-PKG than Val-treated and control mice. Conductance catheter analysis showed a significant reduction in LV stiffness in diabetic mice, but not in non-diabetic mice. Notably, diastolic LV stiffness was significantly reduced in Sac/Val-treated diabetic hearts compared with Val-treated or vehicle-treated diabetic mice. The phosphorylation level of titin (N2B), which determines passive stiffness and modulates active contraction, was higher in Sac/Val-treated hearts compared with Val-treated hearts in diabetic mice. Given that alteration of titin phosphorylation through PKG contributes to myocardial stiffness, the beneficial effects of Sac/Val in heart failure might be partly attributed to the induction of titin phosphorylation.Keywords: Titin, HFpEF, Cardiomyopathy, Diabetes, Diastolic dysfunction, Myocardial stiffnessSubject terms: Molecular medicine, Cardiology, Cardiovascular biology, Endocrine system and metabolic diseases

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome in which dyspnea, fatigue, edema, and decreased exercise tolerance occur as a result of cardiac dysfunction and failure of the compensatory mechanism of cardiac pump function. Approximately one-half of patients with HF have preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (HFpEF), and the prevalence of this syndrome is increasing at an alarming pace in developed countries1. In HFpEF, diastolic filling is thought to be impaired as a result of worsening active and/or passive relaxation 2,3. Diastolic LV stiffness is estimated in vivo as the chamber stiffness constant obtained by exponential fitting from the end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship (EDPVR)4,5. In previous reports, diastolic LV stiffness was reported to be related to the onset of heart failure symptoms on exertion in patients with HFpEF and has been considered one of the targets for the treatment of HFpEF6.

Diabetes mellitus has been shown to be associated with the progressive left ventricular remodeling and dysfunction characteristic of HFpEF4–6. In patients with HFpEF, diabetes mellitus is a common comorbidity associated with decreased survival and an increased risk of HF hospitalization or cardiovascular death7–9. Since patients with diabetes mellitus sometimes show cardiac dysfunction even in the absence of coronary artery disease, hypertension, or valvular heart disease, this suggests that the diabetic condition itself might lead to pathological myocardial changes10. A recent study showed that diabetes-induced passive stiffening of cardiomyocytes results from post-translational modification (PTM) of titin, a giant sarcomeric protein, which acts as a molecular spring and determines passive myofilament distensibility11,12. In addition, cGMP-dependent protein kinase (protein kinase G: PKG)-mediated titin phosphorylation is impaired in diabetic hearts and correlates with passive tension12.

The search for effective treatment for HFpEF has long been unsuccessful, but recently, in addition to the demonstrated efficacy of a sodium-glucose cotransporter (SGLT) 2 inhibitor13, treatment with sacubitril/valsartan (Sac/Val), a combination angiotensin receptor and neprilysin (NEP) inhibitor, has shown beneficial effects compared with Val alone in patients with HFpEF14. NEP is a metalloprotease responsible for the degradation of vasoactive peptides, including natriuretic peptides (NPs). NPs, including atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP), have many beneficial effects in failing hearts, including natriuresis, vasodilation, anti-hypertrophic and anti-fibrotic effects, and inhibition of the renin-angiotensin system15. NPs activate their receptor, membrane-bound guanylyl cyclase, and increase intracellular cGMP and exert biological effects through PKG activation16. The positive effects of Sac/Val on HF might be attributed, at least in part, to increased PKG activation through inhibition of NP degradation17.

Interestingly, it has been suggested that Sac/Val might inhibit the progression of asymptomatic cardiac diastolic dysfunction and vascular stiffness in preclinical models of obesity-associated diabetes mellitus18. We hypothesized that cardiac diastolic dysfunction induced by diabetes mellitus is improved by Sac/Val treatment due to titin modification via cGMP-PKG signaling.

Results

Increased LV myocardial stiffness in diabetic mice is decreased by Sac/Val treatment

For a type 1 diabetes model, 10–12-week-old male mice were treated with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of streptozotocin (STZ) (50 mg/kg) for 5 days19. The animals’ body weight and blood glucose levels were assessed 2 weeks and 1–5 months after STZ injection (Fig. 1a). Two weeks after STZ injection, their plasma glucose levels were already above 300 mg/dL, and 1 month after STZ injection, the plasma glucose levels continued to be 400–600 mg/dL, indicating hyperglycemia (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). Body weight was less in diabetic mice than in control mice (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online).

As a preliminary experiment with a small number of animals, whether STZ-induced diabetic mice could be used as a model of diastolic dysfunction was examined using a conductance catheter to analyze cardiac function 4 months after STZ injection (Fig. 1a). The results presented in Supplementary Table S1 showed that cardiac output was significantly lower and LV end-systolic volume was greater in the diabetic model. The systolic function parameters did not differ significantly, and the diastolic function parameters showed an increase in the stiffness constant calculated from left ventricular EDPVR, suggesting that this model could be used as a model of increased diastolic stiffness of the left ventricle.

Four months after STZ injection, the diabetic mice were divided into three groups, receiving oral treatment by gavage with Sac/Val, Val, or the solvent vehicle (Veh) as a control, and continued once daily for 1 month. After these treatments, echocardiographic measurements of cardiac function showed less fractional shortening, an index of contractility, in the diabetes group than in controls (Fig. 1b,c). LV mass (LV Mass) was slightly lower in the diabetes group than in controls (Fig. 1c). There were no changes in LV end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) (Fig. 1b,c). Pressure–volume (PV) loop analysis using conductance catheter measurements (Fig. 2 and Table 1) showed that LV end-systolic pressure (LVESP) was not changed by diabetes, Sac/Val treatment, or Val treatment. End-systolic elastance (Ees) tended to decrease with Val and Sac/Val administration, but remained in the normal range (> 7 mmHg/μL)20, and other LV systolic indices such as preload recruited stroke work (PRSW) and the peak rate of pressure rise (dP/dtmax) were not affected by diabetes and were neither increased nor decreased by Sac/Val or Val administration (Table 1). Tau, a diastolic ventricular relaxation parameter, showed a trend to be higher in the diabetes group than in controls, and an improving trend with Val or Sac/Val treatment, although the difference was not significant. Interestingly, the stiffness constant was markedly higher in the diabetes group than in controls, and it was reduced by Val treatment. It was further reduced by Sac/Val treatment (Table 1). In the diabetic group, despite impaired left ventricular diastolic function, left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) did not increase significantly in the vehicle group, but it did increase significantly in the Val group, and it was significantly lower in the Sac/Val group than in the Val group. It is possible that the improvement in diastolic function by Sac/Val may have affected LVEDP (Table1). Echocardiography showed reduced contractility in the STZ diabetic model, but detailed cardiac function analysis using PV loop data suggested that this mouse model is an animal model with a stronger reduction in diastolic function than in contractility. It was found that Sac/Val administration specifically improved diastolic function.

Cardiac fibrosis and calcium-handling proteins were not ameliorated by Sac/Val treatment

Since PV loop analysis showed that diastolic function was affected by the treatment, myocardial fibrosis and expression of calcium-handling proteins were investigated. Masson’s trichrome staining for fibrosis quantification showed a modest but not significant increase of myocardial fibrosis in diabetic animals, and fibrosis was not significantly altered by Sac/Val or Val treatment (Fig. 3a,b). Western blot analysis showed that diabetes induced an upregulation of the pro-fibrotic cytokine, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) 21. This increase was not affected by either Sac/Val or Val treatment (Fig. 3c, see Supplementary Fig. S4 and S6 online). Expression of the calcium handling protein, sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) 2a, was decreased by diabetes, but it remained unchanged following treatment with Sac/Val or Val (Fig. 3d, see Supplementary Fig. S5, S6 and S10 online). Furthermore, neither treatment affected the expression levels of the sodium-calcium exchanger (Ncx) or the phosphorylation of phospholamban (PLN) (Fig. 3d, see Supplementary Fig. S5, S6, S7 and S10 online). PLN is a protein that suppresses the activity of SERCA2a, and the SERCA2a/PLN expression ratio defines the calcium handling of cardiomyocytes and is decreased in heart failure 22,23. Therefore, the SERCA2a/PLN ratio, which was significantly reduced by diabetes, but not altered by treatment with Sac/Val or Val, was also examined.

These results suggest that reduced diastolic performance in diabetic hearts is likely due to decreased SERCA2a protein expression. The diastolic function improvement attributed to Sac/Val could not be explained solely by changes in SERCA2a or fibrosis 24.

Increased phosphorylation level of titin by Sac/Val treatment in diabetic mice

Since Sac/Val treatment reduced ventricular stiffness independent of myocardial fibrosis, we hypothesized that titin, a molecular spring in the sarcomere, is involved in the molecular mechanism24–27. Titin is the largest sarcomeric protein that is highly expressed in the heart, with a molecular weight of over 3000 kDa. Recent studies showed alteration in the phosphorylation level of titin in HFpEF patients5,28,29. Titin protein expression was evaluated in this study. For this, the heart lysates were subjected to gel staining with Pro-Q Diamond staining for assessment of phosphorylation of titin, and CBB and Sypro-Ruby staining for assessment of total titin, to obtain a band that appeared to be titin (Fig. 4a, see Supplementary Fig. S2 and Fig. S11, S12 and S13 online). After digestion of these bands, LC-mass spectrometry was performed for proteomic analysis, and it was finally concluded that these bands were indeed titin (see Supplementary Fig. S3 online). Next, the phosphorylation sites of titin were analyzed using Pro-Q Diamond staining. Phosphorylation of the PEVK domain, N2B and N2BA regions, has been shown to be critical in stiffness26. The phosphorylated titin detectable by Pro-Q Diamond staining is thought to mainly reflect the PKG phosphorylation site in the N2B region and the PEVK region, which contains the PKG, protein kinase C (PKC), and calcium-calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CaMK) phosphorylation sites27,30. As shown in Fig. 4a, diabetic group N2B phosphorylation was decreased, and N2B phosphorylation was increased by Sac/Val administration (Fig. 4a). This increase in phosphorylation was more pronounced in the Sac/Val group than in the Val group of diabetic mice (Fig. 4a). N2B sites phosphorylated by PKG have been reported to be associated with reduced stiffness through increased phosphorylation27,30, which may be related to improved diastolic function with Sac/Val treatment.

Titin isoform-switching was not changed by Sac/Val treatment in diabetic mice hearts

Alternative splicing gives rise to the titin isoforms, the larger and more compliant N2BA isoform and the smaller N2B isoform31. Titin isoform analysis using Sypro-Ruby staining with 2% acrylamide with 0.5% agarose gel showed that changes in titin isoforms were not affected by the presence or absence of diabetes or drug administration (see Supplementary Fig. S2 online).

Increased cGMP-PKG activation to a greater extent by Sac/Val treatment than val treatment

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying the prominent increase in titin phosphorylation, cGMP-PKG signaling and titin phosphorylation were evaluated. As shown, Sac/Val treatment led to higher phosphorylation than Val treatment. The difference between Sac/Val and Val is that Sac has the effect of activating cGMP-PKG signaling32,33. Vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (VASP) is phosphorylated by cGMP-PKG at Ser239. VASP phosphorylation at Ser239 was increased in Sac/Val-treated hearts, especially in diabetic mice (Fig. 4b, see Supplementary Fig. S8 online). The expression of soluble guanylyl cyclase, sGC, which is a nitric oxide (NO) receptor and activates intracellular cGMP-PKG signaling, was also examined, and the protein level was not affected by Sac/Val treatment with or without diabetes (Fig. 4b, see Supplementary Fig. S9 and S10 online). In addition, the expression of particulate guanylyl cyclase, pGC, which is a natriuretic peptide (NP) receptor and another cGMP/PKG activating pathway, was not affected by these interventions (Fig. 4b, see Supplementary Fig. S9 and S10 online). PKG activity assay using myocardial lysates showed that Sac/Val treatment led to significantly higher PKG activity than vehicle treatment in diabetic mice (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, contrary to PKG activity, PKC activity was comparable between diabetic and control groups both with and without Val or Sac/Val treatment (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

In this study, cardiac diastolic dysfunction induced by diabetes was improved by Sac/Val treatment independent of plasma glucose levels. Functional analysis with a conductance catheter showed increased diastolic LV stiffness in diabetic animals. Sac/Val attenuated the increase in LV stiffness due to diabetes to a greater extent than did Val. Calcium-handling proteins define the relaxation of cardiomyocytes34, and titin is the protein that defines passive tension25,35. Accumulation of collagen fibers, cardiac fibrosis, also defines passive cardiac tissue stiffness35,36. Previous research showed that a decrease in SERCA2a in diabetic mouse models correlated with impaired cardiac function37. In the present study, SERCA2a expression and the SERCA2a/PLN ratio were decreased in the diabetic model, indicating that calcium handling is involved in the impaired diastolic function22,23,34. In contrast, CTGF expression was increased in diabetes, but histological myocardial fibrosis was not significantly increased. Importantly, the improvement in diastolic function with Sac/Val did not appear to be related to these factors.

The present focus was on titin’s role25,27, a protein known for stiffness modulation through site-specific phosphorylation27. Phosphorylation of the titin isoforms N2B and N2BA is largely responsible for the change in stiffness27. Phosphorylation of N2B by PKG and PKA improves stiffness, whereas, conversely, increased phosphorylation of the PEVK region by PKC signaling worsens stiffness27. The phosphorylated titin detectable by Pro-Q Diamond staining is thought to mainly reflect the phosphorylation site in the N2B region and the PEVK region, which contains the PKG, PKC, and CaMK phosphorylation sites30. In a recent report, a comprehensive analysis of phosphoproteins in the heart with HFpEF showed that sites other than N2B and PEVK regions can also be phosphorylated in hearts with diastolic dysfunction38. In our pathological model of diabetic cardiomyopathy, Pro-Q Diamond analysis showed that N2B phosphorylation was particularly increased by Sac/Val administration in diabetic mice, consistent with the results of conductance catheterization. The PKG activity assay using myocardial lysates showed that Sac/Val treatment led to higher PKG activity than vehicle treatment in diabetic mice. Analysis of PKG activity in heart samples from each group showed that Sac/Val treatment led to significantly higher PKG activity and its expression than vehicle treatment in diabetic mice (Fig. 4c). This might be due to the inhibitory effect of Sac/Val on NEP, which suppresses the degradation of natriuretic peptides (NPs), such as ANP and BNP, and activates the downstream cGMP-PKG signaling. Activation of PKG has been reported to improve cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and stiffness, and it is likely that administration of Sac/Val inhibited NP degradation and activated PKG, leading to improvement of diastolic dysfunction26,27,29,39. On the other hand, myocardial PKC activity was not affected by Val and Sac/Val treatment in animals both with and without diabetes. Previous studies have shown that increased activation of PKC and phosphorylation of titin by PKC in both type 1 and type 2 diabetic models were associated with impaired myocardial diastolic function and increased passive stiffness in diabetic models12,38,40–42. However, in the present experimental mouse model, since PKC activation was not affected by Val and Sac/Val administration to diabetic mice, the changes in titin phosphorylation observed on Pro-Q Diamond staining were not thought to be due to PKC, but to PKG. The phosphorylation of titin via PKG activation may independently improve the increase in stiffness, suggesting that increased PKC activity may lead to increased myocardial stiffness via titin, as previously reported, but would not be responsible for the Sac/Val-induced improvement12,26.

It has been reported that changes in the ratio of the two isoforms of titin, N2BA:N2B, are involved in cardiac diseases31,43. This ratio is reportedly increased in diastolic failure, such as HFpEF44,45. In the present study, the ratio of N2BA:N2B was not changed in diabetic animals and with Sac/Val treatment. The present results suggest that phosphorylation of specific sites of titin via cGMP-PKG activation, rather than isoform switching of titin, might be involved in the stiffness modulation by Sac/Val administration.

In general, STZ-induced diabetic cardiomyopathy models have been reported to develop cardiac fibrosis46, although models that do not exhibit fibrosis have also been reported47. In the present study, the profibrotic cytokine CTGF increased modestly, but it had no significant effect on histologically assessed cardiac fibrosis; the increase in CTGF was also not suppressed by the drugs. Both cGMP/PKG activation mechanisms, the NO-sGC and NP-pGC pathways, have been reported to have an inhibitory effect on cardiac fibrosis48,49. However, in the present study, Val and Sac/Val did not significantly change fibrosis, and the pharmacological effect of Sac/Val on fibrosis could not be explained.

Limitations

The major limitation of the present study is that it was not possible to clarify which part of titin is phosphorylated as the pharmacological effect of Sac/Val. Unfortunately, mass spectrometry analysis of the phosphorylation sites of titin could not identify which amino acids in titin are phosphorylated by Val or Sac/Val administration (data not shown). Western blotting using antibodies against phosphorylated titin was also technically challenging. Pro-Q Diamond staining has been reported to reflect phosphorylation of the N2Bus and PEVK regions30. This means that it reflects phosphorylation by PKC, as well as PKG, but based on the results of PKG and PKC activation assays of myocardial samples, the present results appear to reflect changes mainly due to PKG.

It is unclear what is upstream of PKG that causes titin phosphorylation in the present mouse model, but it is probably natriuretic peptide, a substrate that is activated as a result of NEP inhibition by sacubitril. Whether the concentration of natriuretic peptide in mouse blood is altered by Sac/Val administration should have been examined, but, unfortunately, an ELISA system that could correctly measure it in mice is not available.

Another limitation of the present study is the exclusive focus on phosphorylation. Other PTMs, such as oxidation and S-glutathionylation, can also impact titin. These PTMs are known to contribute to changes in myocardial stiffness27. Moreover, the PKG activity assay using heart tissue extracts has been shown to be affected by the redox state of the protein and dimer formation48. Concerning the action mechanism of Sac/Val in treating HFpEF, it is plausible that this drug affects left ventricular diastolic properties through mechanisms other than the cGMP/PKG pathway. Therefore, future studies should include unbiased methods such as RNA sequencing or proteome analysis.

It is necessary to comment on the discrepancy between the PV loop data of the diabetic model in the preliminary experiment and the diabetic model in the drug administration experiment. Although the cardiac output (CO) was decreased in the diabetic model in the preliminary experiment (Supplementary Table S1), CO was unchanged in the drug administration experiment (Table 1). CO is strongly affected by preload and heart rate, which are not accurate PV loop parameters20,50. The possibility that there were differences in preload status and anesthetic intensity in the preliminary experiment and the medication experiment cannot be ruled out. FS in echocardiography is similarly problematic as a hemodynamic parameter. Diabetes significantly reduced FS in the heart of mice. However, the PV loop showed no reduction in many indices of contractility. We believe that this is due to a problem with the FS, which is often affected by preload51, and a problem with the difference in analysis conditions between the PV loop analysis and the echocardiography.

The interpretation of diastolic function in PV loop analysis data should also be mentioned. Tau, a marker of left ventricular relaxation, was slightly prolonged in diabetic mouse hearts and showed a trend toward improvement with Sac/Val treatment, but this was not significant. Since SERCA2a is associated with left ventricular relaxation34, Tau could also be altered in diabetic mice with reduced SERCA2a expression, but the PV loop data were not significant. LVEDP tended to be elevated in diabetic mice, and there was a significant increase when Val was administered, which was suppressed by Sac/Val. This suppression of LVEDP by Sac/Val likely reflects a pharmacological effect, but the reason for the large margin of error and difficulty in obtaining a significant result may be that the catheter data were obtained by open chest examination20. Therefore, the stiffness constant can be interpreted as a more sensitive marker of diastolic function than LVEDP.

In the clinical setting, diabetic cardiomyopathy is commonly referred to as HFpEF in the context of obesity-related metabolic abnormalities in type 2 diabetes mellitus12,42,52. The model used in the present study does not resemble this condition. However, it was shown to be a useful model of diastolic ventricular stiffness, an important hemodynamic change in the pathogenesis of HFpEF, and was used in the present drug intervention experiments. It has also been reported that the STZ-induced diabetes model can be a model of impaired LV diastolic function53. Hopf et al. reported that the changes in titin phosphorylation observed in human diabetic myocardium were similar to those in a mouse model of STZ-induced diabetic cardiomyopathy, but not in a rat model of type 2 diabetes mellitus12. This indicates the validity of using the STZ-induced diabetes model in the titin study. In addition, we have previously reported that proteins that play important roles in cardiac remodeling in STZ-induced diabetic cardiomyopathy models also play important roles in HFpEF patients54,55, and we believe that STZ-induced models can also be a research tool that can lead to a better understanding of the pathogenesis of HFpEF.

Conclusion

In summary, Sac/Val treatment reduced LV diastolic left ventricular stiffness with increased titin phosphorylation in diabetic cardiomyopathy. Given that alteration of titin phosphorylation through PKG contributes largely to myocardial stiffness, the present results show new strategies for HFpEF treatment through the beneficial effects of Sac/Val for diastolic dysfunction, especially in titin dysfunction.

Methods

Animal model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the Committee of Experimental Animal Research of Gunma University (14–063). All animal models and experimental procedures conformed to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Euthanasia techniques for experimental animals were performed in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020), using deep sedation with isoflurane or cervical dislocation or a combination. Nine-week-old male C57BL/6Ncr mice were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). For a type 1 diabetes model, 10–12-week-old male mice were treated with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of STZ (50 mg/kg) for 5 days19. The animals’ body weight and blood glucose levels were assessed 2 weeks and 1–5 months after STZ injection (Fig. 1a).

Reagents

The Sac/Val and Val used in this study were provided by Novartis Pharma AG (Basel, Switzerland). For in vivo studies, a dose of 60 mg/kg/day of Sac/Val or 30 mg/kg/day of Val was used. The bioavailability of Sac/Val in mice is high, and the drug doses used have been reported as the optimal doses in mice56. Using a feeding needle, the experimental mice were given Sac/Val or Val orally once daily for 1 month, beginning 4 months after STZ injection (Fig. 1a).

Echocardiography

After study drug treatment for one month, in vivo cardiac morphology was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography (EUB-7500; Hitachi Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) in conscious mice. M-mode LV end-systolic and end-diastolic dimensions were averaged from 3–5 beats. LV fractional shortening percent and mass were calculated as previously described57. The persons performing the studies and analyses were blinded to the experimental group.

In Vivo hemodynamics

Immediately after echocardiography, in vivo LV function was assessed using a PV conductance catheter, as described previously20,57. Under anesthesia, intubation, and ventilation, the LV apex of the mice was exposed between the seventh and eighth ribs, and a 1.2-Fr PV conductance catheter (Transonic Systems Inc., Ithaca, NY, USA) was advanced through the apex to lie along the longitudinal axis. Data were assessed at steady state and during preload reduction. Data were digitized and analyzed with the ADVantage PV system (ADV500; Transonic Systems Inc.). Load-independent left ventricular stiffness, the stiffness constant, was determined from an exponential curve approximation of the change in the EDPVR by occluding the inferior vena cava and varying the PV loop by varying preload. Other load-dependent diastolic and systolic indices were also evaluated. For details of the parameters, please refer to previous papers20,57.

Tissue preparation

The animals were euthanized after the in vivo hemodynamic measurements using the conductance catheter. Following induction of deep anesthesia with isoflurane, the mice were sacrificed with 5% potassium chloride perfusion to cause diastolic arrest of the heart, which was then immediately excised after rinsing out the residual blood. The intact heart was rapidly frozen and stored at -80 °C until used for western blotting. Promptly dissected mouse hearts were cut into frozen sections and tissue sections and fixed in 10% formalin for histological examination. Individual serial Sects. (2-μm-thick) were prepared for histological analysis. Fibrosis was estimated as described previously57. Tissue was paraffin-embedded, cut into 10-μm-thick slices, and stained with Masson trichrome to measure fibrosis. A total of 4–5 non-overlapping areas were microscopically photographed at 20 × magnification using short-axis sections of the mid left ventricle, and the blue-stained fibrotic areas were analyzed using image analysis software (WinROOF 2021, Mitani Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Tissue fibrosis is shown as the ratio (%) of stained area to total tissue area. The analysis was performed with the experimental group masked.

Titin detection

Tissue samples were homogenized in 8 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 3% SDS w/v, 75 mM DTT, 0.03% bromophenol blue, and 0.05 M TrisCl, pH 6.858 or PTS buffer 12 mM sodium deoxycholate (SDC), 12 mM sodium N-laurylsarcosinate (SLS), and 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 9.0))59. Homogenized samples were obtained by only gentle pipetting. The supernatant was used for Pro-Q Diamond and Sypro-Ruby staining, with 2% or 2.5% acrylamide with 0.5% agarose gel60. To examine titin isoform changes, low-concentration SDS-agarose gel was used61. Electrophoresis was performed, gels were stained with Sypro-Ruby, and photographs of the gels were taken to determine the ratio of titin isoforms.

Western blot analysis

Tissue samples and cells were homogenized on ice in RIPA buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate and 0.1% SDS) containing complete mini and phos STOP solution (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The mixture was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE. Protein concentrations were measured with the Pierce 660-nm Protein Assay Reagent (22,660, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Western blot analysis was performed according to standard procedures with blocking by 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA and using the following primary antibodies: goat polyclonal connective tissue growth factor (CTGF, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-14939), rabbit monoclonal sGC (1:1000, Abcam 189176), pGC (1:1000, Abcam 139188) SERCA2a (1:1000, Abcam, 3625), Ncx (1:1000, Swant R3F1), Phospholamban Phospho-Serine-16 Affinity (1:1000, Badrilla A010-12AP), and mouse monoclonal Phospholamban (1:1000, Badrilla A010-14), and rabbit monoclonal phosphorylated vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (1:1000, VASP, Ser-239) (CST #3114), VASP (1:1000, CST #3112S), and GAPDH (1:1000, CST #2118) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Antigens were detected using Immobilon Western HRP Substrate (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) after incubation with horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), depending on the species from which the primary antibody was derived. The density of bands was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), downloaded from the NIH website. The concentration of the target bands in the acquired images was quantified, and the phosphoprotein bands were quantified as ratios with the total protein band as a loading control.

ELISA

Activity of cGMP-PKG in heart tissue was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit for mice (cGK kit: Cyclex, MBL, Woburn, MN, USA), and PKC activity (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) was measured in the heart tissue according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Multiple-group comparisons were performed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). p < 0.05 was considered significant.
