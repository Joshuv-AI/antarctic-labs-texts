Outbreak of FAdV-4 infection in blue-breasted quail (Excalfactoria chinensis) reveals novel cross-species transmission of virus in domestic poultry and ornamental pet birds
Authors: Man Teng, Yong-Xiao Chai, Qin Luo, Zhi-Feng Peng, Lu-Ping Zheng, Qian-Yue Jin, Jun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis caused by fowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4) is a serious avian disease associated with huge losses to the poultry industry worldwide. Herein, we document the first outbreak of FAdV-4 infection in blue-breasted quail (Excalfactoria chinensis). The causative agent, designated as HNSQ2023, was isolated and characterized as an FAdV-4 epidemic strain using PCR amplification, DNA sequencing, and IFA staining. Comparative genomic analysis of the viral genomes of 79 virulent or non-pathogenic FAdV-4 strains showed that the gene order and shared open reading frames of HNSQ2023 were similar to those of previously reported virulent FAdV-4 strains. It also contains 100% conserved amino acid mutations in major structural proteins such as hexon and fiber-2. This is similar to the virulent FAdV-4 strains, except for an irregular mutation at aa46 (A to T) in fiber-1. Notably, the newly identified HNSQ2023 isolated from Excalfactoria chinensis and the other five epidemic FAdV-4 strains previously isolated from peacocks, chickens, ducks, and pigeons during 2018-2023 formed a close evolutionary sub-branch, distinct from the main branch, mostly composed of Chinese FAdV-4 strains originating from chickens. Furthermore, animal experiments demonstrated that HNSQ2023 retained high pathogenicity to SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis, with 100% morbidity and over 90% mortality in both species. Our data shed new light on the high risk of cross-species transmission of FAdV-4 in domestic poultry and ornamental pet birds, stressing the importance of stricter biosecurity measures and necessary vaccinations for future control of this disease.

Introduction

The fowl adenovirus (FAdV) belongs to the Aviadenovirus genus of the family Adenoviridae (Benkő et al., 2022). It is one of the most important and harmful pathogens to the poultry industry worldwide. Until now, five species of FAdVs, separately designated FAdV-A to FAdV-E, have been characterized and can be subdivided into 12 serotypes based on their genomic differences and serum cross-neutralization tests. Avian diseases associated with FAdV infections include inclusion body hepatitis (IBH), hepatitis-hydropericardium syndrome (HHS), and gizzard erosion (GE) (McFerran and Smyth, 2000; Schachner et al., 2018). Both FAdV-induced IBH and HHS are serious infectious diseases widely prevalent in the poultry industry and cause significant economic losses. Over the past two decades, outbreaks of IBH and HHS have risen rapidly around the world. Importantly, FAdVs not only infect birds via both vertical and horizontal transmission patterns (Asthana et al., 2013; Chandra et al., 2000) but also contaminate various species through cross-transmission. Thus, it poses a significant potential threat to the healthy development of the global poultry industry.

FAdV serotype 4 (FAdV-4), a species belonging to FAdV genotype C (FAdV-C), is the main pathogenic agent in chicken hosts (McFerran and Smyth, 2000). Since the first outbreak in Angara Goth, Pakistan, FAdV-4 has spread throughout the world, including the Middle East (Hess et al., 1999; Ishag et al., 2022), Latin South America (Mazaheri et al., 1998; Ojkić et al., 2008; Toro et al., 1999; Vera-Hernández et al., 2016), Europe (Kaján et al., 2013; Niczyporuk, 2016), India (Dahiya et al., 2002), Japan (Mase et al., 2009), Korea (Choi et al., 2012), and China (Ye et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2015). FAdV-4 principally affects 3- to 6-week-old broilers with typical clinical symptoms including depression and diarrhea, with a 20%∼80% mortality rate. It rarely occurs in breeding and laying flocks, which express low mortality. Characteristically, the heart and liver are the primary organs affected by the virus: accumulation of clear fluid in the pericardium, characteristic yellow and enlarged livers, and multifocal coagulative necrosis are often present in affected birds. Prior to 2014, the prevalence of FAdV-4 was rarely reported in China, but since 2015 it has been frequently reported in central China and spread rapidly throughout the whole country (Cui et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Niu et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2017a). Except for the chicken hosts, FAdV-4 has also been isolated from cherry valley ducks (Chen et al., 2017), mandarin ducks (Shen et al., 2019), geese (Wei et al., 2019), peacocks (Wang et al., 2021), and black-necked cranes (Xue et al., 2023), indicating a high possibility of cross-species transmission.

As the largest base for poultry farming in the world, China raised more than 16.5 billion domestic birds annually during the past 5 years, including 1.25 billion laying hens and 11.0 billion broiler chickens. In addition, China is one of the world's largest markets for raising and trading ornamental pet birds (Liang et al., 2024). Blue-breasted quail is the smallest species in the order of Galliformes that originates from India, Southeast Asia, and Southeast China. Their small size, varied plumage colors, and cute appearance have made Excalfactoria chinensis a popular pet bird (Nakamura et al., 2019). This trend has resulted in the rapid expansion of blue‑breasted quail breeding in Asian countries, particularly in China, primarily through household and large‑scale intensive farming. The trade routes for ornamental pet birds include circulation in pet markets, live poultry markets, and e‑commerce platforms, which usually involve mixed rearing of ornamental birds and poultry, facilitating interspecies contact and pathogen transmission (Liang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023).

Recently, a serious disease caused hundreds of daily deaths in a large-scale breeding farm of Excalfactoria chinensis in central China. The farm had an annual production capacity of over 100,000 birds. To investigate the causative agent of the disease, we performed a series of operations including clinical differential diagnosis, virus isolation, comparative genomics, and animal regression experiments. Unexpectedly, we identified a clinical case induced by FAdv-4 infection, excluding other potential infections such as Marek’s disease virus (MDV), chicken infectious anemia virus (CIAV), avian leukosis virus (ALV), reticuloendotheliosis virus (REV), Avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) and/or Newcastle disease virus (NDV). For the first time, we demonstrate Excalfactoria chinensis as a susceptible host to FAdV-4 and shed new light on this virus’ high risk of cross-species transmission between domestic poultry and pet birds. This implies an urgency for stricter biosecurity measures and necessary vaccinations to face future challenges.

Materials and methods

Ethics Statement

The 3-week-old SPF White Leghorn chickens and 5-week-old unvaccinated Excalfactoria chinensis were kept in positive-pressure isolators. The birds were fed normally according to the nutritional requirements of different ages. All experimental protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of the Institute for Animal Health (IAH), Henan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (HAAS) (permit no. LLSC41023000053), following the protocols of the Laboratory Animal Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Animal Welfare permitted by the State Administration for the Market Regulation and Standardization Administration of China (permit no. GB/T 35892-2018).

Cells, viruses, and antibodies

The Leghorn male hepatoma (LMH) cells (ATCC, CRL-2117) (gift from Prof. Jun Zhao, Henan Agricultural University, China) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1) medium (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) plus penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 mg/mL) (Solarbio, China). The cells were grown at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The MDV strain HNSQ01 (Liu et al., 2023), ALV strain HNXZ2 (Zheng et al., 2022a), MDV/REV co-infection strain HNGS206 (Yu et al., 2013), CIAV strain HN23, NDV strain La Sota, IBV strain M41, IBDV strain B87, and FAdV-4 strain ZZ (preserved in IAH, HAAS, China) served as positive controls. The monoclonal antibody (McAb) 5A5 specific to FAdV-C fiber-2 protein was produced in IAH (HAAS, China), and the Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) was purchased from Abcam (UK).

Differential diagnosis of virus infection

Historical and clinical features of the disease were recorded in a poultry farm. Prior to laboratory diagnosis, the case had already undergone drug treatments. The diseased birds were humanely euthanized with CO2 for routine necropsy examinations and sample collection. Infections of potential viruses were first detected using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR). Briefly, 1 g of liver samples was added to 200 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and crushed with a tissue homogenizer at 4 °C for 10 min. After 3 rounds of freeze-thawing, total cellular DNA and RNA were separately extracted from the tissue homogenate using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 (TaKaRa, Japan). The first strand of cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). The total cellular DNA and synthesized cDNA were used as templates for subsequent PCR amplifications with the conserved primer pairs No.1∼No.8 specific to MDV, CIAV, ALV, REV, IBV, IBDV, NDV, or FAdV (Table 1). Virus isolation

The liver samples collected from FAdV-positive birds were homogenized in PBS buffer at a ratio of 1:10 and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant from the tissue homogenate was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and 200 μL of the filtrate was inoculated onto LMH monolayers in 6-well plates (approximately 10⁶ cells per well) and then incubated for 2 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After washing with PBS three times, the cells were maintained with fresh DMEM/F12 (1:1) medium containing 2% FBS at 37°C. Subsequently, the supernatants and cells were harvested at 72 hours post-infection (hpi). After three blind passages, the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) of the FAdV in LMH was determined.

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

The isolated FAdV viruses were further validated using IFA staining. Briefly, the LMH cells were seeded in 96-well plates (104 cells per well) and cultured at 37°C for 12 h. The monolayer cells were inoculated with FAdV isolates at an MOI of 0.01 and incubated for 48 h. Then, the medium was removed and fixed with cold methanol/acetone (v/v = 1:1) at 4 °C for 15 min. After being blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 60 min, the cells were incubated with the FAdV-C fiber-2 protein-specific McAb 5A5 for 30 min. After three washes with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), the cells were similarly incubated with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488, 1:1000, Abcam, UK). Finally, the cells were washed and imaged using fluorescence microscopy (ZEISS, Germany).

PCR amplification and phylogenetic analysis of hexon genes

For further characterization, viral DNA was extracted from the new FAdV-C isolate-infected LMH cells using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 (TaKaRa, Japan), and the full-length sequences of hexon genes were amplified via PCR using conserved primer pair No.9 (Table 1). This is specific to both FAdV-C serotypes, FAdV-4 and FAdV-10. The PCR products were purified, cloned, sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for DNA sequencing, and finally analyzed using online BLASTN software and the MegAlign in the DNAstar software package v5.01 (DNAstar, Madison, WI, USA). Based on the reference FAdV isolates (Supplementary Table 1), a phylogenetic analysis of hexon genes from the newly obtained fowl isolates was performed using the bio software MEGA 11.0 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand).

Genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The viral DNA extracted from the new FAdV-4 isolate was subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS), and viral genome assembly was conducted at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The similarity of the whole-genome sequences of the new isolates and reference FAdV-4 strains was analyzed using the online tool MAFFT version 7 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). Based on whole genomes of 78 reference FAdV-4 isolates, as listed in Supplementary Table 2 and previously isolated from eight countries, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 11.0. Furthermore, multiple alignments of amino acid sequences with major structural genes, including hexon, fiber-1 and fiber-2, were performed utilizing the DNAstar software package v5.01 (DNAstar, Madison, WI, USA).

Animal experiments

To evaluate the pathogenicity of the new FAdV-4 isolate to both unvaccinated Excalfactoria chinensis and specific pathogen-free (SPF) chickens, two batches of animal experiments were performed using the new FAdV-4 isolate cultivated in LMH cells. Treatment of all animals was conducted in compliance with Arrive Guidelines for Reporting Animal Research (Percie du Sert et al., 2020). For experiment 1, twenty 3-week-old SPF White Leghorn chickens (Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology, China) were randomly divided into two groups (10 birds each). The first group was challenged with a new FAdV-4 isolate (105 TCID50/bird) through the intramuscular (IM) route, while the second group was equally inoculated with a clean supernatant of LMH cell cultures as mock controls. For experiment 2, twenty 5-week-old unvaccinated Excalfactoria chinensis (Henan Kunming Breeding Co., Ltd, China) were used and similarly divided into two groups. One group was challenged with a new FAdV-4 isolate (103 TCID50/bird), and the other group was equally inoculated with an LMH cell culture supernatant as mock controls. Each group of birds was separately housed and fed normally in isolators. Clinical signs and survival status were observed daily and recorded after virus challenge. The visceral organs of dead birds, such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and proventriculus, were examined and sampled for further experiments.

PCR and qPCR

Parts of tissue samples collected from dead birds were used for DNA extraction, and conventional PCR amplification was first performed to exclude the background adenovirus infection in SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis, using the primer pair No.10 listed in Table 1 and targeting the conserved L1 loop region of FAdV hexon genes as previously reported (Meulemans et al., 2001). The viral loads in different organs and tissues in virus-challenged birds were determined by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), using the FastStart Universal SYBR®Green Master (Roche, Switzerland) and the FAdV-4 hexon-specific primer pairs No.11∼ No.12 (Table 1).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis

The other portions of tissue samples collected from dead birds, such as the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys, were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and sent to Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis. Briefly, after fixation, tissue samples were processed, embedded in paraffin wax, and cut into 5 μm sections. Part of the sections were dewaxed in xylene, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), and observed by light microscopy. Other sections were further processed according to standard immunohistochemical (IHC) protocols. The primary antibody was a mouse monoclonal antibody specific for the FAdV-C fiber-2 protein (clone 5A5, 1:200 dilution in 0.01 M PBS). After incubation, sections were treated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody, followed by visualization with DAB. Sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin and examined via light microscopy.

Statistical analysis

For qPCR analysis, all experiments were independently repeated in triplicate, and the data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, USA). For animal experiments, survival data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism Version 6.0, and survival levels between the two groups were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Differences were statistically significant at p < 0.05 or p < 0.01.

Results

Clinical diagnosis of an FAdV infection outbreak in Excalfactoria chinensis

Clinically, the rapid onset of disease showed a high death rate and occurred mainly in both young and adult Excalfactoria chinensis, from the ages of 20 to 50 days old. Compared with healthy birds, sick birds exhibited various clinical symptoms such as lethargy, diarrhea, and general weakness (Fig. 1A-B). Evident gross lesions were mostly observed in the liver, which was enlarged and pale yellow, with scattered pinpoint-sized, grayish-yellow necrotic foci on its surface (Fig. 1C). Such a clinical case of suspected avian neoplastic disease and/or hepatitis has never been reported in Excalfactoria chinensis. Thus, a differential diagnosis was first performed to identify the potential causative pathogens using both PCR and RT-PCR assays, and conserved virus species-specific primers (Table 1). Isolation and identification of FAdV-4 virus from Excalfactoria chinensis

The liver samples collected from four diseased birds, designated HNSQ2023-1 to HNSQ2023-4, which were detected as positive for FAdV infection, were treated, and virus isolation was performed and inoculated onto LMH monolayers. After three blind passages, typical CPEs were produced in all cell cultures, displaying large and round infected cells, and cell aggregation appeared in the form of grape clusters. Furthermore, IFA staining demonstrated that in virus-infected cells, all four FAdV isolates were specifically stained by the FAdV-C fiber-2 protein-specific McAb 5A5 (Fig. 1E). Using the conserved FAdV-C-specific primers (Table 1), the full-length hexon genes were amplified and cloned for DNA sequencing. Surprisingly, all four hexon genes cloned from isolates HNSQ2023-1 to HNSQ2023-4 were completely identical in sequence, as deposited in the GenBank database (Acc. Nos. PX092839, PX092840, PX092841 and PX092842). Evolutionally, as demonstrated in Fig. 2A, all sequences are clustered into the same branch of FAdV-4 in subgroup FAdV-C in the phylogenetic tree generated based on hexon genes originating from all five FAdV genotypes. Taken together, an epidemic FAdV-4 virus, designated as HNSQ2023, was successfully isolated and confirmed to circulate in flocks of Excalfactoria chinensis.Fig. 2Phylogenetic analysis of epidemic FAdVs based on the hexon genes (A) or the whole viral genomes (B). Genomic sequence and phylogenetic analysis of new FAdV-4 strain

The whole genome of HNSQ2023 was sequenced, assembled, and deposited in the GenBank database (Acc. No. PQ058675). The full-length viral genome was 43,713 bp, with a 54.87% GC content. As shown in Fig. 3, the gene order and shared open reading frames (ORFs) of HNSQ2023 were similar to those reported previously for FAdV-4 strains, but without a 33 bp deletion/insertion in ORF29, as found in ON1 and JSJ13 genomes (Pan et al., 2017b; Zhao et al., 2015). The 1966 bp nucleotide deletion (1966Del) containing ORF19 and ORF27 encoded in ON1, KR5 and MX-SHP95 was observed to be omitted from the viral genomes of HNSQ2023 and the other epidemic FAdV-4 strains originated from seven different fowl species (Fig. 3). Compared to the two non-pathogenic strains ON1 and KR5, the similarity of the nucleotide sequence HNSQ2023 was 98.48% and 98.80%, respectively, while for pathogenic FAdV-4 strains, the nucleotide sequence similarity varied from 98.51% to 99.98% (data not shown). Genomic phylogenetic analysis was performed based on the whole viral genomes of HNSQ2023 and 78 reference FAdV-4 strains (Supplementary Table 2). As demonstrated in Fig. 2B, most of the Chinese FAdV-4 isolates and three American strains isolated during 2015-2023 were clustered into two major branches. These were far removed from those of the non-pathogenic FAdV-4 strains ON1 and KR5 from Canada and Japan as well as viruses previously reported in Mexico, Japan, Korea, and India. Molecular characteristics of the major structural proteins of the new FAdV-4 strain

The hexon, fiber-1, and fiber-2 proteins of HNSQ2023 and representative FAdV-4 strains derived from chickens, ducks, geese, pigeons, peacocks, and black-necked cranes were compared and analyzed. As shown in Table 2, the homology of FAdV-4 hexon and fiber-2 proteins derived from Excalfactoria chinensis and six heterologous species was nearly completely identical in amino acid sequences. However, compared to the earliest reported virulent MX-SHP95 strain and non-pathogenic ON1 and KR5, the identities of HNSQ2023 were 98.5%, 98.5%, and 98.6% for hexon and 94.5%, 93.3%, and 94.0% for fiber-2, respectively. Notably, compared to the two non-pathogenic viruses, all epidemic FAdV-4 strains identified from distinct host species, including HNSQ2023 and MX-SHP95, have same amino acid substitutions at position 188 (I to R) in hexon proteins and positions 219 (G to D), 232 (E to Q), 300 (I to T), 305 (S to A), 307 (P to A), 319 (V to I), 329 (V to L), 378 (A to T), 380 (A to T), 435 (T to S) and 453(S to A) in fiber-2 proteins. In addition, the amino acid sequences of fiber-2 proteins from HNSQ2023 and the other epidemic FAdV-4 strains are all 479 aa in length, which is five amino acids more than MX-SHP95 and ON1 strains. The size of fiber-1 proteins in all epidemic FAdV-4 strains is 431 aa, which is one amino acid less than MX-SHP95 and ON1 and two amino acids less than KR5. Pathogenicity of the newly isolated FAdV-4 virus to SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis

The pathogenicity of HNSQ2023 was further evaluated in both SPF chickens and unvaccinated blue-breasted quail. It was verified that all birds in mock control groups were healthy and no deaths occurred during the entire experimental period. However, clinical symptoms and rapid death were observed in virus-challenged birds of both species. As demonstrated in Fig. 4A-B, death occurred rapidly at 2∼3 days post-infection (dpi) in both of the virus-challenged SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis, with the exclusion of other adenovirus background infections (Supplementary Fig. 2). The cumulative morbidities in both species were 100%, with 100% and 90% mortalities in SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis, respectively. The dead birds displayed clear virally induced hepatitis, manifested by pale yellow, friable, swollen livers with scattered grayish-yellow necrotic foci around the edge. In addition, virus-challenged birds showed enlarged and mottled kidneys, with vasodilation and congestion in the renal cortex; vasodilation and congestion on the serosal surface of the small intestine; and an enlarged, dark-red proventriculus with vasodilation and congestion on its serosal surface (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similarly in both species, as demonstrated in Fig. 4C-D, the copy numbers of FAdV-4 hexon genes were highest in livers of dead birds among all the tested organs. Discussion

Since its first outbreak in Pakistan in 1987, FAdV-4 has spread globally and led to significant economic losses in the poultry industry worldwide. As the major causative agent related to hepatitis, FAdV-4 is highly pathogenic in poultry and can transmit horizontally from flock to flock through a fecal–oral route (Balamurugan and Kataria, 2004). Once poultry farms are infected, the virus causes serious morbidity and mortality. In addition to chickens, hepatitis occurs in young goslings and is caused by a goose adenovirus (Ivanics et al., 2010). In another report, an HHS-like disease was identified in pigeons; the supernatant of homogenized liver samples collected from diseased birds was found to cause the inoculated broilers to develop HHS (Naeem and Akram, 1995). Since the first recorded outbreak in broiler chickens (Liu et al., 2016), FAdV-4-caused HHS has occurred frequently in many regions of China in a variety of species, including ducks (Chen et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2019), geese (Wei et al., 2019), peacocks (Wang et al., 2021), and black-necked cranes (Xue et al., 2023). The results suggest that FAdV-4 has high cross-species transmission among domestic poultry and wild birds. This makes monitoring the virus’ epidemiology of great significance for public health and efficient control of the disease.

Most recently, a serious disease occurred at a large-scale poultry farm for blue-breasted quail breeding in central China. It caused hundreds of daily deaths in young and adult chicken flocks. The diseased birds exhibited various clinical signs, including lethargy and diarrhea. Subsequent autopsy revealed a swollen and necrotic liver. Based on the suspected clinical signs and autopsy lesions, we first carried out a diagnosis to differentiate potential causative pathogens, such as MDV, CIAV, ALV, REV, NDV, IBV, IBDV, and FAdV. Combined with the clinical symptoms and experimental data from virus isolation, IFA staining, gene cloning, and DNA sequencing, we identified the disease as FAdV-4-induced hepatitis in Excalfactoria chinensis. Further animal experiments in SPF chickens and unvaccinated Excalfactoria chinensis confirmed that the newly isolated FAdV-4 strain HNSQ2023 is highly pathogenic to both host species and can similarly induce typical clinical signs of hepatitis in virus-challenged birds. In SPF chickens, deaths first occurred at 2 dpi and lasted for two days until all animals died. This is similar to the survival curve previously reported in broiler chickens (Liu et al., 2016). However, for Excalfactoria chinensis, one bird survived until the end of the experiments. In two species, the peak of deaths and pathological characteristics of dead birds were roughly identical, and the viral loads in livers were highest among all the detected organs in both SPF chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis. In addition, the spleens of infected Excalfactoria chinensis exhibited a high viral load, which is consistent with the pathological observation of severe necrotic vacuolar degeneration in this organ. Interestingly, the viral loads in the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and proventriculus from virus-infected Excalfactoria chinensis were found to be significantly higher overall than those in the corresponding visceral organs of virus-infected SPF chickens. Taken together, our data suggest that the blue-breasted quail-derived FAdV-4 virus is highly pathogenic to both chickens and Excalfactoria chinensis; however, the latter may be more tolerant to the virus.

Comparative genomic analysis of circulating strains is necessary to understand the current evolutionary relationship of FAdV-4 viruses. Compared with non-pathogenic FAdV-4 viruses, the viral genes ORF19 and ORF27 are not included in the viral genome of HNSQ2023, as evidenced in virulent Chinese epidemic strains. They have been identified as novel genotypes of FAdV-4 for the unique 1966Del (Pan et al., 2017a; Wang and Zhao, 2019; Ye et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2015). However, insertion of the relative ORF19 and ORF27 gene sequences from non-pathogenic KR5 and ON1 viruses into the epidemic FAdV-4 strain HLJFAd15 has not altered the highly pathogenic characteristics of the rescued chimeric viruses, indicating that 1966Del is unrelated to the increased virulence of this virus (Pan et al., 2018). Interestingly, in the newly isolated HNSQ2023 and epidemic FAdV-4 strains, a 33 bp nucleotide deletion was observed in the ORF29 gene compared to the first Chinese virulent FAdV-4 strain JSJ13 (Ye et al., 2016). Whether such a short deletion in ORF29 is related to the increased virulence and/or cross-species transmission of FAdV-4 needs further study.

Previous studies have confirmed that the main structural proteins of hexon and fiber are related to the virulence of FAdV-4 (Liu et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2021). In this work, we found that the hexon protein of HNSQ2023 was the same as that of previously identified pathogenic FAdV-4 strains from distinct host species such as chickens, ducks, geese, pigeons, peacocks, and black-necked cranes (Chen et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2023). However, different from the representative non-pathogenic ON1 and KR5 strains, a total of 12∼13 amino acid mutations occurred in hexon proteins of virulent FAdV-4 strains, of which the point mutation at aa188 (I to R) may be closely related to increased pathogenicity (Wang and Zhao, 2019). The fiber-1 protein of HNSQ2023 was also similar to the pathogenic FAdV-4 strains, with 20 amino acid mutations compared to the non-pathogenic strains. Interestingly, for the first time, we discovered a mutation between A and T at position aa46 in fiber-1 proteins derived from viruses in distinct domestic hosts and wild birds. Whether such an irregular mutation is related to cross-species transmission of FAdV-4 requires further examination. However, previous research reported that fiber-1 is not associated with virulence (Liu et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2021). Notably, similarly to the highly pathogenic FAdV-4 strains, HNSQ2023 possesses most amino acid mutations in the fiber-2 protein, which is characterized as an important factor determining the pathogenicity and virulence of FAdV-4 (Liu et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2021). Previously, the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) protein was identified as a receptor for FAdV‑4 infection in studies with chickens (Pan et al., 2020); however, no data have been reported on other Galliformes hosts. Whether quails and chickens share common or similar cellular receptors for FAdV-4 infection remains unclear and deserves further study.

Evolutionarily, based on hexon genes and viral genomes, the phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that the newly isolated HNSQ2023 emerged in Excalfactoria chinensis. It belongs to serotype 4 and aligns closely with the current epidemic FAdV-4 strains in China. Surprisingly, the most recently identified FAdV-4 viruses, including HNSQ2023, which circulates in Excalfactoria chinensis and other host species such as peacocks, chickens, ducks, and pigeons, have formed a closely related evolutionary sub-branch in the viral genomic phylogenetic tree. This is convincing evidence with which to elucidate the cross-species transmission of FAdV-4 among domestic chickens, wild birds, and ornamental pet birds, implying that more attention and stricter biosecurity measures are needed in the future. Factually, after the causative pathogen was confirmed, a trivalent inactivated vaccine against Newcastle disease, avian influenza (subtype H9), and fowl adenoviruses disease (group I serotype 4) was produced with the vaccine strains La Sota and YBF13 plus YBAV-4, which were applied in this poultry farm and successfully prevented the recurrence of HHS over the past two years. This demonstrates the necessity and effectiveness of vaccination, which may also block the cross-species transmission of this virus. In the future, it will be necessary to conduct a wide range of studies to monitor the prevalence of the virus in more species. This is necessary to evaluate its cross-species transmission and potential threat to public health and to efficiently control the disease.
