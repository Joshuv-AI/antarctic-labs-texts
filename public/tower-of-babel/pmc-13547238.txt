A 30 m forest dominant height dataset for China in 2020
Authors: Yuling Chen, Guangcai Xu, Haitao Yang, Qinghua Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForest dominant height is a fundamental structural attribute that reflects site conditions and forest growth potential. Here we present a nationwide 30 m resolution forest dominant height dataset for China (FDH-30C). The dataset is calibrated using 1,117 km² of high-density unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) light detection and ranging (LiDAR) data distributed across all eight major vegetation divisions across China as reference data, representing diverse stand ages, structures, and species compositions. To produce spatially continuous estimates, the model used in this dataset integrates 30 geospatial predictors derived from multi-source remote sensing products, including climatic, edaphic, topographic, vegetation, and Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)-based variables. A two-stage hybrid modeling framework combines the UAV LiDAR reference data with these predictors to generate spatially coherent estimates while preserving local accuracy and reducing ecozone boundary effects. The resulting map provides a consistent national baseline for applications such as site-index mapping, growth-and-yield parameterization, biomass and carbon estimation, vertical structure analysis, and the evaluation of spaceborne LiDAR missions.

Background & Summary

Accurate quantification of forest structure is essential for understanding resource availability and guiding management decisions1. Among structural parameters, tree height and diameter at breast height (DBH) are the two most widely used indicators of stand productivity and biomass2,3. Yet, compared with DBH, the accurate acquisition of tree height remains technically challenging due to canopy occlusion, terrain complexity, and limitations of ground-based observations4,5. Within stand-level or plot-level assessments, forest height can be summarized as either mean height or dominant height (hd)6. Mean height provides a general indication of stand development, whereas hd reflects the growth conditions of the most vigorous trees and serves as a robust proxy for site productivity7,8. Because dominant trees are less affected by understory competition and management interventions, their height responds primarily to site factors, making forest dominant height a key metric for evaluating forest growth potential, site quality, and long-term yield capacity8–12. Despite its central role in forest mensuration and modeling, hd has rarely been characterized with spatial consistency across large regions. Existing definitions—whether based on the tallest trees or those with the largest diameters—further complicate its estimation and comparison among studies13. However, identifying dominant trees by diameter is less practical for large-scale applications, as accurate DBH measurements are limited to field inventories14. Consequently, reliable, spatially explicit information on hd remains scarce, restricting its broader integration into forest resource assessments and ecosystem modeling.

Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) has transformed forest structural assessment by directly capturing three-dimensional canopy and stem information through active laser scanning15–20. Recent advances in close-range platforms—particularly unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) laser scanning—have greatly improved spatial resolution, data accessibility, and cost efficiency, enabling reliable measurement of individual trees across diverse forest types20–22. LiDAR-based tree height estimation can generally follow two approaches: an area-based method17,23, which generates a canopy height model to calculate tree or canopy height by aggregating LiDAR-derived metrics that characterize the vertical distribution of returns within predefined spatial units, such as plots or grid cells, and a tree-based method19,22,24, which measures height at the individual tree level. While area-based metrics are computationally convenient, they often mix vegetation and non-vegetation elements, deviating from the forestry definition of tree height23. In contrast, tree-based estimation aligns more closely with ecological reality by identifying individual trees, quantifying their heights, and aggregating these measurements to represent stand structure22. Such an approach, supported by the growing availability of UAV LiDAR data, allows for accurate and consistent assessment of forest vertical structure over extensive areas. With the accumulation of large-scale UAV LiDAR observations across China, this study establishes a solid foundation for mapping hd in a spatially continuous manner. However, the vast geographic extent and environmental heterogeneity of China’s forests introduce a critical challenge—how to systematically integrate these massive, site-specific measurements to generate accurate, nationally consistent hd products for ecological assessment and forest management.

Producing a wall-to-wall hd product for China requires addressing two fundamental challenges. First, forest structure varies substantially across ecozones, raising the question of how to balance regional accuracy with national consistency. While zone-specific models generally improve local performance, they can introduce discontinuities at ecological boundaries25. Rather than seeking to identify a universally superior modeling approach, this study adopts both strategies as complementary pathways for dataset construction, investigating unified frameworks where ecozone variability is treated as a random effect—such as in mixed-effects formulations—to evaluate whether a single model can retain coherence and adaptability26. The second constraint arises from the spatial coverage of close-range LiDAR sampling, which, despite their accuracy at the plot level, remain insufficient to support direct large-scale surface construction. Extending these measurements to unsampled regions is achieved through the integration of multi-source remote sensing predictors within machine learning frameworks, enabling the construction of a spatially continuous hd product17,27–29. These extensive, tree-based measurements form the basis for evaluating strategies to generate continuous, nationally consistent hd product.

To address the absence of spatially consistent stand-height information, this study utilizes more than 1,000 km² of UAV LiDAR data collected across eight major vegetation divisions in China. Forest dominant height is defined as the mean height of the three tallest trees within each 30 m grid, ensuring alignment with operational mensuration practices. Based on these tree-level measurements, we produced national-scale hd maps using a unified machine learning–based mixed-effects modeling framework with ecozone-level random effects, which explicitly captures hierarchical dependencies among vegetation divisions and improves large-scale spatial continuity. By documenting both the construction and validation of these products, this data paper aims to provide an openly accessible, standardized dataset of hd for China. To our knowledge, this is the first dataset to characterize hd at a national scale using tree-based UAV LiDAR observations. This map is intended to support forest inventory, structural monitoring, carbon assessment, and habitat evaluation, providing a foundational dataset for ecological research and management.

UAV LiDAR data collection and processing

The UAV LiDAR data was collected over an eight-year period ending in 2023 and cover 1,117.76 km² across all major vegetation divisions of China, with the exception of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau alpine vegetation divisions (Table S1). Approximately 400 TB of point-cloud data were acquired using a uniform UAV LiDAR acquisition protocol (Table 1), forming the core reference dataset for deriving forest dominant height samples.

All point clouds were processed through a standardized workflow implemented in LiDAR360 (Version 6.0, www.lidar360.com), including resampling, denoising, ground point classification, and normalization. Individual trees were segmented using the Li et al.30 algorithm embedded in LiDAR360. To account for substantial variation in forest structure across China, segmentation parameters were optimized at the file level. For each LiDAR file, a representative local subset (typically 5 × 5 plots) was selected for parameter tuning. The key parameter, Sigma, which controls Gaussian smoothing, was tested from 0.2 to 2.0 at 0.2 intervals. Lower values were used to reduce under-segmentation, whereas higher values were used to reduce over-segmentation. The optimal Sigma value was determined by visual inspection of the subset results and then applied to the full file. For some early LiDAR datasets, where individual trees could not be reliably identified visually, Sigma selection was additionally informed by stand density or related structural information reported for the corresponding region. This procedure allowed the segmentation settings to be adapted to differences in vegetation structure while maintaining a consistent workflow for extracting the three tallest trees within each 30 × 30 m plot. Following the tree-level segmentation, the LiDAR data covering irregular large regions were divided into 30 × 30 m grid cells representing forest plots.

Only grid cells fully contained within the stand boundaries were retained as valid plot samples. Before plot generation, additional filtering was applied to ensure sample consistency and data quality. LiDAR data without valid geographic coordinates and non-representative urban or fragmented forest patches were excluded. For large LiDAR forest files, only internally complete 1 km × 1 km blocks were retained before subdivision into 30 m × 30 m plots, with incomplete edge and corner areas removed. Small LiDAR files that could not support a complete 30 m × 30 m plot were also excluded. This approach minimized edge effects and guaranteed that each plot represented complete and continuous LiDAR coverage. In total, 610,342 valid sample plots were generated.

Forest dominant height definition

Forest dominant height has been defined in several ways6,31,32, including: (1) the average height of the top 100 trees per hectare; (2) regression-based estimates corresponding to the quadratic mean diameter of the 20% thickest trees; and (3) the mean height of the three to six tallest trees in a plot. Consistent with conventional Chinese forest survey methods32, we applied the third definition in Eq. Feature data processing

To support nationwide modeling of hd, 30 predictors were extracted from multi-source remote sensing, classified into five categories: climatic, edaphic, topographic, Landsat-derived optical, and SAR predictors (Table 2). Climate predictors were sourced from the WorldClim 2.1 database (www.worldclim.org), which provides 19 bioclimatic predictors at 30-arc-second resolution. Edaphic attributes, including soil type, soil depth, and texture, were extracted from the Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD, Version 1.2), with the China component derived from the 1:1,000,000 Soil Map of China provided by the Institute of Soil Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Topographic metrics, including elevation, slope, and aspect, were computed from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) Version 3 (SRTM Plus) product. Vegetation conditions were captured through the annual maximum Normalized Difference Vegetation Index NDVI composite, extracted from Landsat data using Google Earth Engine (GEE). SAR variables, including VV and VH backscatter coefficients, were generated from Sentinel-1 observations. Additionally, a national forest type map distinguishing planted and natural forests33 and a forest age map for China in 202034,35 were incorporated. All layers were harmonized to 30-m resolution using nearest-neighbor resampling to maintain categorical integrity.

Data Records

The dominant height of China’s forests dataset (FDH-30C) generated is publicly available in Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18286069)38. The repository contains the main raster product, FDH-30C.tif, which stores the 2020 forest dominant height estimates for China at 30 m spatial resolution, together with associated metadata documentation. The raster is provided in GeoTIFF format and can be opened in standard GIS and remote sensing software.

The released raster contains one primary variable, forest dominant height, expressed in meters. Each pixel represents a 30 × 30 m ground area. Pixel values indicate the predicted dominant height of forest stands, while non-forest areas and unavailable pixels are encoded as NoData. The dataset provides national coverage for China and represents forest conditions for 2020. The product was developed using LiDAR-derived dominant height observations collected between 2015 and 2023 and a set of environmental predictor variables representing climatic, soil, topographic, vegetation, and SAR conditions. These predictor variables were used for model construction and mapping, but the released dataset itself contains the final forest dominant height layer rather than the full predictor stack.

The accompanying metadata file describes the dataset contents, spatial coverage, temporal reference, spatial resolution, variable definition, file format, and data usage information, thereby facilitating data reuse in forest monitoring, ecosystem assessment, and related large-scale ecological applications.

Forest dominant height dataset for China

The nationwide hd dataset revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity across China’s vegetation divisions (Fig. 3). The maximum hd reached 101 m in the subtropical evergreen broadleaf forest, followed by the tropical monsoon forest–rainforest (77 m) and temperate desert (52 m). Moderate values were found in the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau alpine vegetation (44 m), temperate steppe (39 m), temperate needleleaf–broadleaf mixed forest (36 m), cold temperate needleleaf forest (34 m), and warm temperate deciduous broadleaf forest (32 m) (Fig. 3, Table 8). In contrast, mean hd exhibited distinct patterns (Table 8). The tropical monsoon forest–rainforest had the highest mean hd (18.57 ± 5.62 m), followed by the subtropical evergreen broadleaf forest (15.71 ± 3.75 m), despite the latter hosting the tallest trees. The Qinghai–Tibet Plateau alpine vegetation recorded the lowest mean hd (12.34 ± 4.11 m).

Uncertainty analysis

As shown in Fig. 4, the uncertainty associated with hd at the 30 m pixel scale was assessed by aggregating three error components: measurement, product, and prediction errors. The overall uncertainty for mapping hd at the pixel scale stood at 22.73%. Nevertheless, the spatial distribution of uncertainty varied significantly across China, driven by regional forest heterogeneity, the quality of remote sensing inputs, and the spatial coverage and sample size of the reference data (UAV LiDAR data). Estimations across the country showed a variation of no more than 1%, indicating strong spatial consistency and robust model performance at the national scale.

Spatial distribution of pixel-level uncertainty in hd.

Vegetation divisions with sparse LiDAR coverage, such as Temperate desert and Cold temperate needleleaf forest, tended to show higher uncertainty (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S4). By contrast, Temperate steppe, which had the highest sampling proportion, showed comparatively lower uncertainty. In addition, vegetation divisions with more complex forest structure, such as Subtropical evergreen broadleaf forest and Tropical monsoon forest–rainforest, still showed moderate uncertainty despite relatively better sampling coverage, indicating that stand heterogeneity and vertical structural complexity may also contribute to uncertainty. Qinghai–Tibet Plateau alpine vegetation also showed elevated uncertainty (Supplementary Table S4). Because this division was not represented in the UAV LiDAR coverage statistics in Table 1, its elevated uncertainty is more likely associated with regional environmental complexity, such as terrain heterogeneity.

Usage Notes

The national wall-to-wall forest dominant height dataset provides substantial value for large-scale forest resource assessment and ecological research. hd is a key stand-level parameter that reflects site quality and long-term productivity potential. Because it is derived from the tallest trees within a stand, hd is largely independent of stand density, thinning intensity, or understory competition, and therefore exhibits stable age–height trajectories across diverse management regimes9,13,39,40. Owing to these properties, hd constitutes a fundamental variable for site index determination and is widely used in growth and yield modelling, biomass estimation, and a range of operational forest management applications.

At the national scale, continuous, wall-to-wall hd products derived from individual-tree detection remain extremely rare. To date, such datasets exist (Table S3) only in a handful of countries with near-complete airborne LiDAR coverage (e.g., Finland41, parts of Canada42, and selected U.S. regions43, and even there they are typically restricted to managed forests, specific species, or inconsistent methodologies that deviate from national forest inventory definitions. The present dataset advances beyond existing products by delivering the first nationwide 30 m hd map in China derived directly from individual-tree segmentation and detection using close-range UAV LiDAR data. Its key advantages include:

Data acquisition: Levering an unprecedented collection of > 1,117 km² of high-density UAV LiDAR data systematically acquired across all eight major vegetation divisions of the country, providing a nationally representative training and validation basis under diverse stand structures and species assemblages.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged to guide appropriate data use and future improvements. Its limitations include:

The spatial distribution of UAV LiDAR data is uneven at the national scale, with relatively sparse coverage in regions such as the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and parts of northwestern China. Coverage is also limited in some ecosystem types, particularly Temperate desert and Cold temperate needleleaf forest (Table 1). Although the mixed-effects modeling framework partially mitigates this imbalance by borrowing strength across ecozones, localized uncertainties may persist in under-sampled regions. The spatial distribution of UAV LiDAR data is uneven at the national scale, with relatively sparse coverage in regions such as the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and parts of northwestern China. Coverage is also limited in some ecosystem types, particularly Temperate desert and Cold temperate needleleaf forest (Table 1). Although the mixed-effects modeling framework partially mitigates this imbalance by borrowing strength across ecozones, localized uncertainties may persist in under-sampled regions. Accordingly, dominant height estimates in these under-sampled regions should be interpreted with caution, and expanded UAV LiDAR sampling in these areas will be important for improving future versions of the dataset.

The LiDAR datasets were acquired over a multi-year period using different sensors, which may introduce residual inconsistencies despite careful preprocessing and calibration. Temporal mismatches related to tree growth cannot be fully eliminated under current data availability but represent a common constraint in large-scale tree-based height mapping efforts.

An additional limitation is the lack of explicit tree species information for the tallest trees used to derive hd. Overall, this dataset unlocks a suite of previously unattainable national-scale applications, including site productivity and site index mapping, parameterization of regional and national growth and yield systems, improved forest biomass and carbon accounting, nationwide assessment of vertical forest structure, and robust benchmarking of current and future spaceborne LiDAR missions (e.g., GEDI, MOLI). By providing a standardized, reproducible hd product, the dataset offers a foundational data resource for forest structure analysis and model development at large spatial scales.
