SURE-Pipe: a pipeline to compare genomes and extract shared and unique regions
Authors: Infant Thomas, Abhishek B Kannur, Arya Sudheer, Debyani Samantray, Akshay Pramod Ware, Budheswar Dehury, Sandipan Chakraborty, Bobby Paul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentification of unique and shared genomic regions between organisms has substantial translational potential for the development of marker-based diagnostic assays and sequence homology-driven taxonomic classification. An automated pipeline capable of performing genome comparisons at both the intra- and inter-species levels with minimal computational requirements can significantly advance genome-driven translational research. Species-specific genomic regions are particularly valuable for sequence-based species identification and for developing DNA amplification- or hybridization-based diagnostic assays. Here, we present SURE-Pipe, an automated and flexible pipeline for genome comparison and extraction of unique and shared genomic regions (https://github.com/BPaul-bioinfoLAB/SURE-Pipe). Benchmarking of this pipeline using simulated datasets demonstrated high accuracy for shared and unique region identification. Using the pairwise genome comparison module, six genome pairs from diverse microorganisms were analysed, and identified the unique and shared regions. In addition, the multigenome comparison module was applied to 96 genomes representing 24 Bacillus species and identified species-specific genomic regions. These regions were highly conserved among four strains of a species (>98% sequence identity) and exhibit little to no similarity with other species. Species-specific primers designed for all 24 Bacillus species showed no off-target amplification in in-silico polymerase chain reaction analysis, indicating their specificity. Overall, SURE-Pipe provides a robust and multipurpose framework for comparative genomics, and the outcomes can be used for species identification and the development of genome-based diagnostic approaches.

Introduction

Bacterial species play diverse and crucial roles in human life, ranging from beneficial to pathogenic. Understanding their genomic characteristics with precision is essential for harnessing their benefits and mitigating associated risks [1, 2]. Historically, decoding bacterial genome complexity has posed significant challenges due to lack of advanced technologies and limited labour-intensive protocols. The advent of high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies has revolutionized microbial genomics by enabling whole-genome sequencing (WGS) at a relatively large scale [3, 4]. With sequencing costs continually declining, vast amounts of bacterial WGS data are now routinely accessible for translational research and genome mining. To analyse this wealth of genome data and translate it into information effectively, powerful computational tools and sophisticated analytical strategies are indispensable.

Approaches such as comparative genomics, pangenome analysis, and subtractive genomics have become key for exploring genomic diversity and identity [5–7]. Among these frameworks, comparative genomics is a sequence-level method that enables the systematic identification of similarities and differences among genomes, facilitating the detection of shared or lineage-specific and unique genomic regions [8, 9]. Core genomic regions are conserved across all strains of a species and may contribute to fundamental cellular processes, whereas shared or lineage-specific regions are variably present among some taxa, often contributing to ecological adaptation and phenotypic diversity [8]. On the other hand, unique regions are strain- or species-specific, and frequently encode traits associated with pathogenicity, antibiotic resistance, or environmental resilience [10].

Accurate identification of homologous regions, including both coding and non-coding elements, remains fundamental for understanding genome functional diversity [11]. From a biological perspective, gene-centric comparative genomics approaches have been widely used, with primary emphasis on protein-coding regions. Despite substantial progress, existing gene-based workflows primarily predict clusters of protein-coding sequences to define core and lineage-specific genomic regions for phylogenetic inference [12, 13]. Gene-centric frameworks that are effective for population-level analyses operate mainly on coding sequences. Therefore, non-coding, intergenic, or structurally unique regions tend to be overlooked. Unique genomic regions are of particular interest in species identification because of their high discriminatory potential [1]. The identification of species-specific genomic regions is well suited for the development of molecular markers and diagnostic assays [1, 14, 15]. Moreover, unaligned or low-similarity regions are frequently excluded, despite the representation of biologically meaningful sequences that may be absent from related taxa and valuable for diagnostic applications [16–18]. Tools such as BLAST [19] and MUMmer [20] are widely regarded as robust and effective for sequence similarity searches. These tools focus primarily on regions exhibiting detectable sequence similarity, whereas regions lacking significant alignment are not explicitly retained for downstream analysis. Although these unaligned regions are not directly reported, they may represent biologically meaningful information. Such regions are of particular interest as potential for the development of in silico or in vitro-based diagnostic assays and species- or strain-specific markers.

Bioinformatics tools such as UniqPrimer [21], GenMap v1.3 [22], KEC v1.1 [23], Unikseq v1.01 and Unikseq v2.01 [14, 15], and FUR v4.3 [24] can compare genomes and identify conserved or unique sequences. However, these tools exhibit some limitations. For example, Unikseq and KEC depend on k-mer size selection, which requires careful optimization. FUR does not support the simultaneous identification of both shared or lineage-specific regions and unique regions. UniqPrimer and GenMap have limitations in scalability or user-friendly automation, limiting their application to large genomic datasets [24, 25]. Moreover, the rigorous identification of truly unique genomic signatures requires alignment not only against target taxa but also against closely related non-targets, particularly when species share high interspecies sequence similarity. This is essential to ensure specificity for downstream diagnostic or therapeutic applications [26, 27].

To accurately identify the potential core and unique regions, we developed an automated pipeline named SURE-Pipe. This reference-anchored, coverage-based whole-genome comparison pipeline aggregates fragmented alignments to accurately identify conserved regions, while prioritizing specificity through systematic filtering against closely related non-target genomes for reliable marker discovery. A key feature of SURE-Pipe is the explicit incorporation of closely related non-target genomes, ensuring that the identified unique regions represent robust species- or strain-specific signatures rather than generic similarity gaps. The primary objective of SURE-Pipe is to discover candidate shared or lineage-specific and unique genomic regions. The identification of shared or lineage-specific and unique genomic regions has broad translational implications across biomedical, agricultural, and environmental domains. In infectious disease diagnostics, unique genomic regions enable the design of highly specific molecular assays such as LAMP, TaqMan qPCR, or microarray-based tests that can accurately distinguish pathogenic species or strains from closely related non-pathogens [1, 21, 28]. Conversely, conserved shared or lineage-specific genomic regions serve as reliable targets for genus or species complex-level detection, facilitating the development of broad-range screening assays and environmental DNA (eDNA) monitoring tools [14, 15, 29, 30]. Conserved regions underpin species identification and taxonomic classification, whereas unique regions provide highly specific targets for strain differentiation and potential therapeutic target discovery, particularly when associated with virulence or host-adaptation mechanisms [11, 31, 32]. Beyond diagnostics, conserved regions are valuable for eDNA monitoring, whereas unique regions represent promising targets for vaccine development and species-specific diagnostic assay development [31, 33–35]. We introduce SURE-Pipe, a flexible multipurpose platform technology designed to detect highly conserved regions shared between genomes, as well as unique genomic regions exclusive to an organism.

Materials and methods

We developed a pipeline named SURE-Pipe v1.1, which embeds Shell and Python scripts, with ease of execution, cross-platform compatibility, and options to customize the features and filters. The pipeline integrates essential dependencies such as BLAST+ v2.15.0 [19] and BEDTools v2.31.0 [36]. The BLASTN program enables pairwise sequence alignment, and BEDTools enables genomic coordinate operations such as merging, intersecting, joining, and subtracting aligned and unaligned regions. To streamline execution and scalability, the pipeline is implemented with the Nextflow v26.04.2 [37] architecture, which allows seamless integration of multiple scripts and efficient parallelization. The primary innovation of SURE-Pipe lies not in the creation of new alignment algorithm, but in its automation, workflow integration, and dual-region detection capability, which simultaneously identify both shared or lineage-specific and unique genomic regions within large-scale datasets. The pipeline is designed to process genomes that include plasmids, species with multiple chromosomes, and genome assemblies even in the draft stage. The genome of each species must be provided separately in one of the FASTA file formats (.fa, .fasta, .fna, .ffn, .frn). The input file can be either a single FASTA (containing only one complete chromosome) or a multi-FASTA (for genomes that include plasmids or multiple chromosomes, or are in the draft stage). However, highly fragmented assemblies may yield shorter regions due to contig boundaries, reflecting general assembly limitations rather than restrictions of the pipeline. Overall, the pipeline emphasizes both performance optimization and analytical utility, providing a scalable and reproducible framework for comparative genomic studies across diverse microbial taxa.

Workflow of the SURE pipeline

The pipeline is flexible enough to handle a variety of genomes, which makes this approach an invaluable resource for numerous biological research applications. SURE-Pipe offers a comprehensive genome comparison mode that flexibly supports both pairwise and groupwise comparisons. The overall workflow of the SURE-Pipe is presented in Fig. 1, which clearly outlines the stepwise execution. Comparison between two genomes (pairwise) or multiple genomes within two groups (groupwise) can be performed. The pipeline leverages customizable to set identity thresholds to identify unique and shared or lineage-specific genomic regions consistently across different dataset sizes. The pairwise comparison process begins with indexing of the genomes provided along with their genome size in base pairs. The coordinates of all the aligned regions were filtered based on percent identity and then saved in BED file format to identify regions shared (>98% identity) between the target and neighbour group genomes. The coordinates of overlapping alignments merge first, and then, the shared regions extract and save in FASTA file format. Similarly, the unique regions from the target and neighbour groups will be extracted into two separate files. This unified pipeline streamlines comparative genomics workflows by applying consistent principles and flexible parameters, accommodating various research needs with ease and reproducibility.

This schematic workflow illustrates the pipeline for identifying shared and unique genomic regions between two sets of genomes, designated the target group and the neighbour group. The genomes in the target group were aligned using BLAST to obtain core regions with >98% identity among the target group. These core genomes were then aligned by BLAST against genomes in neighbour group. The tool identifies core genomic regions as those that occur across all target genomes with high sequence identity (>98%). The core genome from the target group that is unaligned or shares less identity (<85%) with neighbour genomes will be extracted as unique regions. The core regions from the target group that share >95% identity with the neighbour group are extracted as shared regions.

The pipeline identifies candidate core, shared or lineage-specific, and unique genomic regions through the groupwise genome comparison mode. Groupwise genome comparison initiates by indexing the genomes in the target and neighbour groups using the faidx functionality of the SAMtools to ensure rapid retrieval of sequence coordinates. Each genome in the target group aligns against the neighbour genomes using BLASTN. The alignments are then filtered based on percent identity and aligned sequence length thresholds to retain only significant matches. These filtered genomic coordinates were converted into BED file format and then processed using the multiIntersect functionality of the BEDTools to distinguish core regions (shared across all target genomes). Optionally, these regions further screen against a set of genomes in neighbour group. The core regions from the target group then align against neighbour genome(s), and any matches above the identity threshold were filtered out as common or conserved elements, thus refining the detection of true unique regions. The final unique regions were subsequently obtained by subtracting shared or common segments from the reference genome, followed by sequence extraction using the subtract and getfasta functionalities of the BEDTools. The sequences will be further filtered by length constraints to retain only biologically relevant unique regions. In a similar manner, shared or lineage-specific genomic regions were identified by comparing core genomic regions against non-target genomes. This structured approach not only enhances precision and reduces false positives but also ensures that the identified unique regions are robust, genome-specific, and suitable for downstream applications such as diagnostic marker design and assay development.

SURE-Pipe benchmarking with simulated genomes

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of SURE-Pipe in identifying shared and unique genomic regions, we generated multiple sets of simulated genomes using a custom in-house simulation script that is based on the Stan framework [25, 38]. These simulation models show coalescent genealogy separate target and neighbour clades, followed by the introduction of point mutations and structural rearrangements. Each simulation included five target genomes and five neighbour genomes, with all the genomes sharing an overall average nucleotide identity (ANI) of > 97%, thereby representing a challenging scenario involving closely related genomes with subtle sequence variations. Within each target genome, five shared regions and five unique regions ranging in size from 200, 500, 1000, 2000, and 5000 bp were embedded to create a defined ground truth for benchmarking. Unique regions were included exclusively in the target genomes and were absent in the neighbour group. To incorporate realistic genomic complexity, random structural variations, including inversions (altering marker orientation) and duplications, were introduced. These modifications generate non-collinear genome structures that emulate natural evolutionary rearrangements, thereby testing the robustness of the pipeline in detecting shared and unique regions under realistic genomic conditions. To assess the adaptability of the SURE-Pipe, simulated datasets were generated with genome sizes of 100 kb, 1 Mb, 2 Mb, 3 Mb, 4 Mb, 5 Mb, 10 Mb, and 75 Mb, allowing evaluation across a wide range of genomic complexities. For accuracy evaluation, we conducted 150 independent simulations using 4 Mb-sized genomes (five target and five neighbour genomes per replicate). The outputs of SURE-Pipe v1.1, KEC v1.1 [23], and FUR v4.3 [24] were compared against the known ground-truth marker sets with defined settings by developers. Performance was evaluated at the nucleotide level using standard accuracy metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, precision, and accuracy, with additional evaluation using the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) for balanced performance assessment. The MCC was computed according to the previous studies [38, 39], by integrating true positives (TPs), false positives (FPs), true negatives (TNs), and false negatives (FNs) to provide a single scalar measure of prediction quality, ranging from −1 (inverse correlation) to +1 (perfect prediction) [40, 41].

Computational scalability assessment of SURE-Pipe

In addition to accuracy benchmarking, runtime, and memory efficiency were further evaluated using two additional simulation series with the default parameters of the tools.

Genome-size scaling: In the first assessment, datasets comprising five target and five neighbour genomes were generated, with genome sizes ranging from 0.1 Mb to 640 Mb. This series was designed to evaluate the ability of the pipeline to process genomes of progressively larger sizes while maintaining accuracy and computational efficiency.

Genome-number scaling: In the second assessment, the genome size was fixed at 5 Mb, while the total number of genomes increased in the target (T): neighbour (N) groups at a ratio of 1:4. The dataset combinations included 5T:20N, 10T:40N, 20T:80N, 40T:160N, 80T:320N, and 160T:640N genomes. This experiment aimed to assess how SURE-Pipe scales with the number of genomes analysed simultaneously, simulating increasingly large comparative genomics datasets. For all benchmarking experiments, runtime (wall-clock time) and peak memory usage (GB) were recorded using the /usr/bin/time command to evaluate computational performance. All analyses were executed on a Pop!_OS 22.04 LTS workstation equipped with an Intel® Xeon® E-2124G CPU @ 3.40 GHz × 4 cores and 16 GB RAM. These experiments demonstrated the scalability, adaptability, and computational efficiency of SURE-Pipe across a broad range of genome sizes and dataset complexities, while preserving high sensitivity and specificity in identifying unique and shared genomic regions.

The genome sizes range from 10.723 kb to 69.5 Mb, including small viral genomes to large eukaryotic fungal genomes. Selected genomes exhibited different GC content ranging from 27% to 76%. The details of the selected genomes and their selection criteria are provided in Table 1. A total of 12 genomes were selected for pairwise analysis. Guidelines for genome inclusion in groupwise comparisons

We suggest that the following guidelines be implemented for the groupwise comparison module. Genome data must be grouped and provided in two separate directories: target and neighbour. Genomes of four strains (>2 acceptable) belonging to a species can be considered for the target group, and the genomes of genetically related species of the target can be considered for neighbour group. An ANI of > 97% was suggested for the target group. Similarly, neighbour groups need to be prepared with reference genomes of other species belonging to the same genus or genomes of genetically related species of the target group. These pre-analyses facilitate the accurate categorization of genomes into target and neighbour groups, which enables precise and meaningful comparative analysis. Factors such as assembly completeness, contiguity, and potential sequencing or assembly errors may influence the downstream identification of genomic intervals. Accordingly, the use of high-quality, well-curated genome assemblies is recommended to ensure reliable results. Hence, BUSCO completeness (>95%) and CheckM contamination (<5%) should be measured to maintain minimal standards for the use of genome data [42].

Groupwise genome comparison for unique species-specific genomic regions

Previous studies have reported that several species within the genus Bacillus are genetically closely related, making them difficult to differentiate using conventional marker gene-based approaches. To overcome this limitation, we performed a groupwise comparative genomic analysis aimed at identifying species-specific genomic regions across representative Bacillus species. A total of 24 Bacillus species were selected based on the availability of at least four genome assemblies (assembly level: complete) per species in the Genome database (Table 2). To ensure taxonomic accuracy and eliminate potential misclassified genomes, pairwise ANI was computed. For each species, four genomes were included comprising one reference genome and three additional strains that shared >97% ANI with their respective reference genomes. The analysis excluded plasmid sequences from all target genomes, as plasmids may not be present in all the strains. However, the neighbour group included the strains harbouring plasmids. For species lacking an officially designated reference genome, four representative strains were chosen based on their assembly completeness, low contamination levels, and high ANI consistency within the species cluster. Similarly, a neighbour group was created with the reference genomes of 42 Bacillus species (Supplementary Fig. S5). To avoid self-alignment, the reference genome of the target species was removed from the neighbour group before groupwise genome comparison was initiated. This analysis facilitates the identification of intraspecies common regions, species-specific unique regions, and genomic segments shared with other species.

Species-specific primer design and in-silico validation

Following the identification of core unique genomic regions, sequence specificity was verified by performing BLASTN search against the NCBI RefSeq Genome database. This step showed that the identified regions were unique to the target species and not shared across other members of the genus Bacillus or other related taxa. Only regions showing exclusive alignment to the intended species and no significant similarity or less similarity (E-value > 1e-5 AND identity < 85%) to neighbour genomes were considered for primer design. The most appropriate candidate species-specific regions were then used for primer design using the Primer3 parameters optimized for polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based diagnostic assays. The designed primers were subsequently subjected to in-silico PCR using the Primer-BLAST to determine their specificity and amplification efficiency. In-silico PCR was performed against the NCBI RefSeq database, and only primer pairs that amplified exclusively the target species without producing cross-amplification in any neighbouring or non-target genomes were retained. This multistep in-silico validation ensured that the final primer sets were highly specific, reliable, and suitable for downstream experimental validation.

Results

The primary motivation behind the development of SURE-Pipe was to identify unique and shared genomic regions for applications in diagnostics, taxonomy, genome surveillance, and comparative genomics. The key features of the pipeline include: (i) automation for both pairwise and groupwise genome comparisons; (ii) a single workflow for the simultaneous identification and extraction of core, unique, and shared regions; (iii) reference-anchored filtering against closely related genomes to enhance specificity; and (iv) a scalable and reproducible workflow integrated within Nextflow architecture.

SURE-Pipe benchmarking with simulated genomes

To evaluate the accuracy of SURE-Pipe v1.1 in detecting shared and unique genomic regions, we tested with multiple simulated datasets. The simulated genome dataset contains realistic structural variations, including inversions and duplications, which were incorporated into the genomes to generate non-collinear genome structures resembling natural evolutionary rearrangements. Despite the high overall similarity between target and neighbour genomes, SURE-Pipe consistently identified both shared and unique genomic regions with high accuracy across all the genome sizes tested (100 kb to 75 Mb). With respect to unique region detection, SURE-Pipe achieved excellent sensitivity (0.8915–0.9983) and perfect specificity (1.0) for every dataset ≥100 kb (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S1), with precision remaining extremely high (≥0.9999), indicating minimal false-positive identifications. These performance trends are further illustrated in the ROC point plot (Fig. 2B), where unique regions cluster near the upper-left corner of the ROC space (TPR ≈ 1, FPR ≈ 0), including perfect classification. Similarly, for shared region detection, the sensitivity values ranged from 0.9924 to 0.9965, with a constant specificity of 1.0 for all genome sizes (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table S2). The corresponding ROC point plot (Fig. 2D) shows that shared regions maintain TPR values close to 1 with near-zero FPR across genome sizes, confirming robust and size-independent classification performance. The accuracy exceeded 0.9984 across all the datasets, and the MCC values remained consistently high (≥0.9922), demonstrating balanced and reliable performance across all the classification outcomes. Supplementary Figs S1 and S2 further summarize the nucleotide-level performance for unique and shared regions, respectively. In both cases, confusion matrices show consistently high true-positive counts, negligible false positives, and stable false-negative rates across genome sizes. True negative genome size correlate with actual genome length and not affected the classification balance. These results visually corroborate the metric-based evaluations, confirming that SURE-Pipe maintains robust, size-independent performance for both unique- and shared-region detection, even under structurally complex genome simulations.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) point plots showing classification performance for unique (B) and shared (D) genomic regions, with the true positive rate (TPR) plotted against the false positive rate (FPR). Unique regions achieve near-perfect classification across genome sizes, including complete sensitivity and specificity for the 75 Mb genome, while shared regions maintain consistently high accuracy with a TPR ≈ 1 and minimal FPR across all genome sizes.

Comparative benchmarking against KEC and FUR

To evaluate comparative performance, SURE-Pipe v1.1, KEC v1.1 and FUR v4.3 were benchmarked across 150 independent 4 Mb genome simulations, each containing five target and five neighbour genomes. Performance was assessed at the nucleotide level using sensitivity, specificity, and MCC. The accuracy metrics generated for the unique and shared regions predicted by FUR, KEC, and SURE-Pipe are provided in Supplementary Tables S3–S6. Across all simulations, SURE-Pipe demonstrated consistently high accuracy for both unique and shared genomic regions, with performance tightly clustered near the maximum possible values. For the simulated unique regions, SURE-Pipe achieved a mean sensitivity of 0.997, specificity of 1.000, and an MCC of 0.999, indicating highly reliable identification of species-informative regions. For the simulated shared regions, SURE-Pipe maintained similarly strong performance, with a mean sensitivity of 0.994 and an MCC of 0.997, reflecting robust detection of conserved sequences across target genomes. Although KEC detected the intended unique regions across most simulations, the predicted intervals frequently appeared as multiple short segments rather than a single continuous region. This pattern reflects the tool’s k-mer-based output structure: adjacent sequence matches are not merged, and small nucleotide differences interrupt otherwise contiguous intervals. As a result, KEC generally identifies the correct genomic locations; however, the output sequences were often fragmented rather than forming long contiguous regions. Importantly, nucleotide-level sensitivity remained high, but fragmentation reduced the MCC slightly when truncated alignments were introduced (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the current implementation accepts only a single target genome at a time. When multiple target genomes are provided as a multi-FASTA file, the method does not extract sequences that are truly conserved across all the targets, limiting its ability to identify conserved unique regions among the targets. FUR also produces performance profiles that are consistent with unique-region detection. While the specificity remained uniformly high, the sensitivity varied across simulations, and the resulting MCC values displayed wider dispersion relative to those of SURE-Pipe and FUR (Fig. 3A). In several replicates, only limited portions of the ground-truth unique regions were returned, which reduced the corresponding nucleotide-level performance metrics.

Shared-region benchmarking was conducted only for SURE-Pipe, as there were no other tools that identify and extract shared regions along with unique regions. SURE-Pipe includes a dedicated shared-region module with user-configurable identity and query-coverage thresholds, enabling the extraction of continuous conserved regions between target and neighbour groups under a range of filtering conditions. In contrast, FUR is designed exclusively for detecting unique genomic regions and does not provide functionality for identifying conserved or shared regions between groups. Likewise, although KEC offers an ‘include’ mode, the tool was developed primarily to locate unique diagnostic sequences among closely related bacterial species and is not intended for comparative extraction of shared genomic regions between predefined groups. Because neither FUR nor KEC is designed to produce shared-region outputs suitable for region-level evaluation between groups, a direct comparison with SURE-Pipe was not applicable. For this reason, shared-regions benchmarking was restricted to SURE-Pipe (Fig. 3B).

Computational performance and scalability

To evaluate computational scalability, we compared SURE-Pipe v1.1 with FUR v4.3 and KEC v1.1 under two benchmarking scenarios: increasing genome size and number of genomes. When the total genome size was expanded from 0.1 Mb to 6.4 Gb while keeping the same number of genomes, the three tools exhibited markedly different scaling behaviours in time and memory (Fig. 4A and B). FUR performs efficiently for small genomes, but its memory usage increases steeply with genome size due to the suffix-array construction step inherent to the FUR-DB module. At per-genome sizes above 80 Mb (corresponding to a total dataset size of 800 Mb), FUR repeatedly exceeded the available memory and terminated with core-dump errors, preventing any further analysis. This failure highlights the practical limitations of FUR for larger-sized genomes on computers with minimal configuration. KEC remained the most memory-efficient approach across all the genome sizes tested, requiring only a few megabytes of memory even for multi-gigabase datasets. Its runtime increased predictably with genome size, reflecting the linear complexity of its k-mer enumeration strategy. However, KEC performs only unique-region detection and typically produces fragmented sequences rather than long, contiguous genomic intervals, which limits its suitability for region-based comparative genomics. In large genomes (∼400 Mb), this tool often fails to produce longer stretches of common or unique regions unless the k-mer size is adjusted, indicating that parameter tuning is necessary to obtain meaningful results. SURE-Pipe showed highly stable memory usage across the entire range of genome sizes, beginning at ~0.5 GB memory for 1 Mb genomes and gradually increasing to 3.6 GB memory for 6.4 Gb genomic datasets. The runtime scaled linearly, with a genome size ranging from 8.92 s for 1 Mb to ~1626 s for 6.4 Gb. Notably, SURE-Pipe completed all analyses without memory failure, despite simultaneous extraction of both unique and shared genomic regions. Up to 400 Mb total input, the runtime profiles of KEC and SURE-Pipe were broadly comparable; differences became more pronounced only at the largest input sizes because SURE-Pipe performs two step BLAST process per comparison, whereas KEC executes only unique k-mer discovery. These features underscore the predictable trade-off between computational cost and the richer, region-level output produced by SURE-Pipe.

A complementary set of benchmarks was used to examine the impact of increasing the number of genomes while keeping the genome size fixed at 5 Mb to understand the performance of all three tools in terms of time and memory (Fig. 4C and D). In this scenario, FUR again showed escalating resource requirements: the runtime increased from ∼16 s for 25 genomes to >530 s for 800 genomes, whereas the peak memory increased from 1.8 GB to over 12 GB due to repeated suffix-array construction. In contrast, KEC maintained excellent memory efficiency (0.98–5.4 MB) and showed a steady increase in runtime up to 436.91 s for 800 genomes. SURE-Pipe exhibited a stable and predictable memory profile across the entire dataset range, maintaining 0.45–0.52 GB regardless of the number of genomes processed. The runtime scaled proportionally from 15.94 s for 25 genomes to 224.22 s for 800 genomes, demonstrating that SURE-Pipe can handle large comparative genomics datasets on modest hardware while performing both unique and shared region extraction. Taken together, these results show that SURE-Pipe achieves a balanced performance profile: its runtime remains within a practical range, its memory footprint is highly stable, and it reliably processes large genome collections without failure. In comparison, KEC is faster and uses very little memory, but it is limited to detecting unique regions and often reports highly fragmented outputs, even for small SNP-level differences. When scaling to large datasets, analyses involving ∼200 genomes may fail to produce FASTA sequences unless parameters (k-mer) adjusted, restricting its usefulness for region-based comparative genomics. FUR is well suited to small datasets but becomes memory-prohibitive for larger genomes and cannot complete analyses beyond ∼80 Mb per genome. Overall, SURE-Pipe provides the most robust and scalable solution for region-level comparative genomics while retaining the biological interpretability essential for downstream applications.

Pairwise genome comparison

The pairwise genome comparison yielded four sets of FASTA files. The common regions between two genomes that share >98% identity were categorized as candidate shared regions. The genomic regions that share <85% identity or are unaligned between two genomes were categorized as candidate unique regions. Using the pairwise comparison mode of the pipeline, we analysed 12 genomes, including small viral genomes and large eukaryotic fungal genomes (Table 1). Comparisons between Dengue virus type 1 (10 735 bp) and Dengue virus type 2 (10 723 bp) showed whole genome as unique region. These two genomes are genetically diverse; thus, the pipeline is unable to identify the shared regions (>98% identical). Further BLASTN-based whole-genome comparisons revealed that these two genomes share only 38% of their genome with 73.95% identity. The genome comparison between Spiroplasma helicoides (1.32 Mb) and Spiroplasma alleghenense (1.33 Mb) identified only one shared region (79 bp). Additionally, 14 candidate unique regions (1.31 Mb) were found in S. helicoides, and 15 unique regions (1.32 Mb) were found in S. alleghenense, indicating their limited genetic identity. The GC content of the S. helicoides and S. alleghenense genomes differed by 2.5%. Previous studies reported that a greater than 1% difference in GC content may be genetically different.

For Streptococcus species, SURE-Pipe identified 19 regions (19 355 bp) shared between Streptococcus ruminicola and Streptococcus halichoeri. Furthermore, 42 unique regions (1.87 Mb) were specific to S. ruminicola, and 59 regions (1.94 Mb) were unique to S. halichoeri, indicating moderate divergence between the species. The genome of Miltoncostaea marina showed no conserved regions with those of Brachyspira hyodysenteriae but presented two unique regions spanning the entire genome, suggesting high genomic dissimilarity between these two species.

Comparison between two strains of B. licheniformis showed strain ATCC 14580 shares 42 regions, totalling 4 Mb, with strain DSM 26543. Additionally, 46 unique regions (128 083 bp) were identified from strain ATCC 14580, and 61 unique regions (261 500 bp) were identified from strain DSM 26543, reflecting subtle genomic differences between the two strains of a species. In a comparison between F. oxysporum (69.5 Mb) and F. falciforme (53.6 Mb), the tool identified only 433 shared regions (37 924 bp). Despite this, extensive species-specific unique regions were observed. The pipeline identified 5000 unique regions (65.98 Mb) for the F. oxysporum genome and 2477 unique regions (52.32 Mb) for F. falciforme, demonstrating significant species divergence within the same genus.

Groupwise comparison for the identification of candidate species-specific genomic regions

Groupwise genome comparative analysis identified core, unique, and shared genomic regions from 24 distinct Bacillus species. Four strains belonging to a species (target group) shared >97% ANI (Supplementary Fig. S3), and they clustered together in phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4). The neighbour group consisting of a well-annotated, taxonomically validated reference genome of 42 distinct Bacillus species showed significant genetic differences (<95% ANI), except for 12 species. All these 12 Bacillus species shared >95% ANI but not >97%. The reference genomes of three Bacillus species, B. licheniformis, B. paralicheniformis, and B. haynesii, shared 95.1 to 95.9% ANI. Similarly, other closely related species, B. paranthracis, shared 95% to 96% ANI with B. anthracis and B. tropicus. Furthermore, B. cereus and B. thuringiensis shared 95.1% ANI, B. stercoris and B. subtilis shared 95.1% ANI, and B. mojavensis and B. halotolerans shared 95.86% ANI. The pairwise ANI of the neighbour group is provided in Supplementary Fig. S5.

The core, unique, and shared genomic region sizes vary for each species, and the details are provided in Table 2 and Table 3. The core genome size was a minimum of 1 913 007 bp found for B. siamensis and a maximum of 5 227 316 bp found for B. anthracis. The core genome of a species can serve as a fundamental genomic element for downstream comparative analysis, translational research, and the development of genome-based diagnostic approaches. Notably, relatively large number of unique genomic regions were found for B. cytotoxicus (2 338 130 bp) and B. atrophaeus (3 103 530 bp). Species-specific primers and in-silico PCR

In-silico validation confirmed that all species-specific genomic regions exhibited high sequence conservation (>98% identity) with the four representative genomes of each target species. Conversely, these regions showed low sequence homology (<85%) or no detectable sequence identity with other Bacillus species included in the neighbour group. These results demonstrate the ability of our comparative framework to resolve species-specific genomic signatures even among genetically closely related Bacillus members. To evaluate the translational utility of the identified candidate regions, species-specific primers were designed for all 24 Bacillus species and subsequently assessed by in-silico PCR (Table 4). The in-silico PCR analysis showed that all primer pairs produced amplification exclusively within designated target species genomes and did not generate any cross-amplification in neighbour or non-target Bacillus species. This multistep verification confirms that the predicted regions are suitable for diagnostic primer development and highlights the robustness of our approach for reliable species discrimination within the genus Bacillus.

Discussion

Significance of accurately identifying unique and shared genomic regions

Accurate extraction of unique and shared genomic regions is important for a wide range of biological and translational applications. Unique genomic signatures provide the basis for species‐specific molecular marker development [1, 43], identification of genes associated with adaptive traits, and diagnostic assay design [32]. In contrast, widely used marker-gene approaches such as 16S rRNA, 18S rRNA, and ITS offer limited discriminatory power for closely related taxa because these loci are often highly conserved and lack sufficient sequence divergence to resolve species or strain-level differences [44–46]. As a result, many genetically similar organisms remain indistinguishable when they rely solely on these canonical genes. Recent evaluations of tools such as GenMap v1.3, MACLE, and FUR v4.3 have further highlighted constraints in flexibility, accuracy across structurally variable genomes, and applicability beyond bacterial systems [47]. In this context, SURE-Pipe v1.1 was developed to provide a flexible, region-level comparative genomics workflow that accommodates both unique and shared region extraction across diverse genome types. By allowing fine-grained control over identity thresholds, query-coverage constraints, and interval-based filtering, SURE-Pipe adapts readily to a wide range of microbial and non-microbial genomes. Through extensive simulation-based benchmarking and a taxonomically broad case study involving 24 Bacillus species, we demonstrate that the pipeline performs robustly across genome sizes and architectures, supporting translational applications spanning diagnostics, taxonomy, and genome-based surveillance.

Biological and translational relevance of candidate shared and unique genomic regions

The species-specific and shared genomic regions provides significant biological insights. Unique genomic regions can encode traits linked to host adaptation, virulence, environmental resilience, or metabolic specialization, making them attractive targets for pathogen detection and species-level identification [43, 48]. Conversely, regions shared across phylogroups can serve as reliable anchors for molecular barcoding, universal primer design, and phylogroup-level surveillance [14, 15, 49, 50] . While several existing tools focus exclusively on identifying unique regions or candidate gene mining by comparing genomes, SURE-Pipe provides unified extraction of both regions from whole-genome resolution, independent of gene annotation. We retrieved the gene features from species-specific regions that were selected for primer design. Gene features indicate that these species-specific genomic regions, which encode hypothetical proteins, may be associated with the unique characteristics of each species trait [10, 11, 35, 51, 52]. The functional annotation of these genes provides a means to trace the genomic loci underlying distinctive phenotypic characteristics [11, 52, 53]. Furthermore, annotation of non-protein-coding regions residing in unique regions is essential for understanding their role in the structural integrity of the genome and the regulation of gene expression [54].

Interpretation of simulated benchmarking results

Across 150 independent simulations between target and neighbour genomes that shared >97% ANI, SURE-Pipe consistently produced high sensitivity, specificity, and MCC scores for both unique and shared regions (Fig. 3A and B). The narrow distribution of accuracy metrics demonstrates the stability of BLASTN-based alignment in recovering continuous, biologically interpretable sequence blocks, even in the presence of simulated inversions, duplications, and random structural rearrangements. Comparative benchmarking results align well with the methodological design of each tool. KEC, a k-mer exclusion tool, correctly identifies the intended unique loci but often reports them as discontinuous fragments due to its k-mer-based representation. FUR, designed primarily for unique-region detection in bacterial diagnostic contexts, showed variable sensitivity when reference genomes contained duplicated regions or when homologous sequences occurred in reverse orientation across target genomes. Because FUR does not treat reverse-complement sequences as equivalents, inverted segments were represented as dense blocks of ambiguous bases (Ns), which is consistent with previously reported behaviour [25]. Together, these observations confirm that the observed differences in sensitivity and output structure are inherent to each tool’s underlying computational strategy rather than artifacts of parameter choice or dataset composition.

Computational scalability and performance considerations

Runtime and memory usage were evaluated across increasing genome sizes (1 Mb–6.4 Gb) and number of genomes (20 to 800) to estimate the scalability. SURE-Pipe showed stable memory usage across all conditions, with peak consumption substantially lower than that of FUR-DB and comparable to or more efficient than KEC for most dataset sizes. The runtime increased predictably with dataset size and remained within practical limits for both small- and large-scale analyses. FUR-DB became unstable for very large genome sizes, terminating with core dumps beyond ~80 Mb per genome (total input size of ∼800 Mb), which is consistent with its reliance on memory-intensive suffix-array construction during database preprocessing. KEC and SURE-Pipe displayed similar runtime performance across moderate dataset sizes, diverging only at the largest scale tested (6.4 Gb), whereas SURE-Pipe required additional time because it extracted both shared and unique regions, whereas KEC evaluates only unique sequences. At these largest scales, KEC also failed to generate FASTA outputs without k-mer parameter optimization, reflecting its sensitivity to the k-mer configuration. Notably, SURE-Pipe completed all benchmarks up to 6.4 Gb of total genome size and 800 genomes without failure, while maintaining a consistently low memory footprint (<3.6 Gb for genome-size scaling and <600 Mb for genome-number scaling). This combination of predictable runtime scaling, stable memory usage, and biologically interpretable region-level output demonstrates that SURE-Pipe is well suited for large-scale comparative genomics workflows on standard workstation hardware.

Comparative genomics: real-world applications

To demonstrate the application of SURE-Pipe, the study analysed viral, bacterial, and fungal genomes and identified core, unique, and shared regions. In viral datasets, the pipeline correctly identified limited conservation across Dengue virus serotypes, matching known patterns of rapid RNA virus evolution. Among bacterial genomes, moderate conservation was identified within genera (e.g. Streptococcus), whereas distantly related genera (e.g. Miltoncostaea and Brachyspira) lacked shared regions, validating the discriminatory power of the tool. Intraspecies comparisons, exemplified by B. licheniformis, revealed large core genomic segments interspersed with strain-specific regions, indicating the replication of established patterns of bacterial genome plasticity. Groupwise comparisons of 96 genomes from 24 Bacillus species highlighted the ability of SURE-Pipe to recover species-specific marker regions with high precision, enabling robust taxonomic discrimination. These regions supported the successful design of species-specific primers, which were validated through comprehensive in-silico PCR against RefSeq databases. The presence of stable core species-specific regions aligns with documented Bacillus genomic diversity and supports downstream diagnostic applications [43]. Furthermore, by enabling both unique and shared region extraction, SURE-Pipe provides a pathway for developing hybrid marker sets suitable for metagenomic classification that integrate alignment-free (e.g. k-mer-based) and alignment-based strategies [55–57]. Its modular design accommodates organisms ranging from compact viral genomes to multi-megabase eukaryotic chromosomes, facilitating the identification of lineage-specific features, regulatory elements, or species-specific variations, analogous to observations in plant comparative genomics [54], where species-specific genomic regions underpin metabolic polymorphisms and functional diversification. Our approach highlights how comparative genomics can uncover lineage and species-specific features that are potentially associated with functional specialization across diverse organisms.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The primary strengths of SURE-Pipe include its transparent heuristic alignment framework, flexible filtering options, reproducible workflow structure, and applicability to a diverse array of genomic systems. Its ability to generate contiguous interval-level markers rather than fragmented k-mer sets provides biologically interpretable outputs that are well suited for marker discovery, diagnostic assay development, and taxonomic resolution. The scalability of the Nextflow implementation further ensures the usability of the standard computational infrastructure. In the context of Bacillus species, unique regions can serve as high-confidence molecular markers for species delineation. The presented pipeline holds significant potential for metagenomic studies, where rapid and accurate identification of unique and shared or lineage-specific sequences is essential for resolving community composition and detecting novel taxa. Furthermore, scalability and high precision in unique region detection make it a valuable resource for large-scale comparative genomics initiatives. However, few limitations remain. The reliance on BLASTN, while enabling precise interval-level alignments, introduces higher computational costs than alignment-free k-mer approaches do, and extremely large multi-gigabase datasets may require extended runtimes. Species-specific primers identified in the current study were validated only through in-silico PCR. However, our previous study confirmed species-specific genomic region amplification from Proteus mirabilis using conventional PCR [1]. Furthermore, few other studies also have reported that laboratory validation of in-silico predicted variants from genome data yields 99.965% accuracy [58–61]. Although experimental validation and standardization using laboratory techniques such as PCR or qPCR assays could further substantiate the performance of in-silico designed primers/probes under real wet-lab conditions. Future development of SURE-Pipe v1.1 will focus on enhancing more memory and time efficiency, incorporating faster search engines or alternative alignment algorithms, expanding support for metagenome-derived inputs, and integrating automated graphical visualization modules to facilitate intuitive interpretation of shared and unique genomic regions.

Conclusion

We present SURE-Pipe a reproducible, user-friendly, multipurpose, and flexible workflow to compare genomes and identify shared and unique genomic regions from diverse organisms. The pipeline accurately identified the unique and shared genomic regions from simulated genomes and the genomes of viruses, bacteria, and fungi. The outcome of SURE-Pipe highlights that the pipeline is efficient and accurate in identifying shared and unique genomic regions from the genome with considerable variability in size and complexity. Researchers, even those with minimal computing experience, can perform genome comparisons on computers with limited hardware configuration. Using this pipeline, we identified unique genomic regions for 24 Bacillus species, designed primers, and validated them using in-silico PCR, promising their species-specific amplification. Overall, SURE-Pipe platform technology can contribute significantly to the translation of genome data into knowledge and the development of computational and wet lab technologies.
