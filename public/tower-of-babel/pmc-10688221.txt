Comparison of Tiling Artifact Removal Methods in Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry Images
Authors: Sevvandi Kandanaarachchi, Wil Gardner, David L J Alexander, Benjamin W Muir, Philippe A Chouinard, Sheila G Crewther, David J Scurr, Mark Halliday, Paul J Pigram
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) imaging is used across many fields for the atomic and molecular characterization of surfaces, with both high sensitivity and high spatial resolution. When large analysis areas are required, standard ToF-SIMS instruments allow for the acquisition of adjoining tiles, which are acquired by rastering the primary ion beam. For such large area scans, tiling artifacts are a ubiquitous challenge, manifesting as intensity gradients across each tile and/or sudden changes in intensity between tiles. Such artifacts are thought to be related to a combination of sample charging, local detector sensitivity issues, and misalignment of the primary ion gun, among other instrumental factors. In this work, we investigated six different computational tiling artifact removal methods: tensor decomposition, multiplicative linear correction, linear discriminant analysis, seamless stitching, simple averaging, and simple interpolating. To ensure robustness in the study, we applied these methods to three hyperspectral ToF-SIMS data sets and one OrbiTrapSIMS data set. Our study includes a carefully designed statistical analysis and a quantitative survey that subjectively assessed the quality of the various methods employed. Our results demonstrate that while certain methods are useful and preferred more often, no one particular approach can be considered universally acceptable and that the effectiveness of the artifact removal method is strongly dependent on the particulars of the data set analyzed. As examples, the multiplicative linear correction and seamless stitching methods tended to score more highly on the subjective survey; however, for some data sets, this led to the introduction of new artifacts. In contrast, simple averaging and interpolation methods scored subjectively poorly on the biological data set, but more highly on the microarray data sets. We discuss and explore these findings in depth and present general recommendations given our findings to conclude the work.

Introduction

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) is a powerful surface analytical technique capable of elucidating the atomic and molecular composition of a wide range of organic and inorganic sample surfaces. In a typical ToF-SIMS experiment, a primary ion beam is rastered across the surface of a sample, causing the emission of secondary ions at each x and y position in the analysis area. These secondary ions are analyzed using a mass spectrometer, leading to hyperspectral imaging data in which each pixel in the image contains a complete mass spectrum.

ToF-SIMS data are information rich and highly complex, carrying enormous analytical potential if evaluated and understood appropriately. However, there is an inherent limitation in the size of the analysis area for a single ToF-SIMS experiment, and typically, images do not exceed 500 × 500 μm. In order to analyze larger areas, large area rastering (also called stage or macro rastering) is often used. With this approach, the total analysis area (which can be several millimeters across or larger) is divided into adjoining tiles, each of which is small enough to be rastered by the primary ion beam. Large area rastering has been shown to be critical for various sample types, such as polymer microarrays,1,2 biological tissue,3−5 and dielectric films.6

However, there are drawbacks to this approach, most notably the introduction of tiling artifacts. Generally, these artifacts manifest as gradients in secondary ion intensity across each tile and/or sudden changes in intensity between pixels in adjacent tiles.5 The artifacts are usually highly repetitive across some or all of the large area image. The exact causes of tiling artifacts are not well understood, but are thought to be related to sample charging, local detector sensitivity issues and misalignment of the primary ion gun.5 Other instrumental causes may also play a role.

Given the already complex nature of ToF-SIMS data, tiling artifacts present a considerable challenge as they can impair analysis and interpretation. Therefore, methods for correcting such artifacts are critically important and can be corrected either by modifying experimental conditions, such as sample preparation and instrument data acquisition parameters, or by using computational methods.5 Experimental approaches are important, as they attempt to mitigate the artifacts at the level of the physical systems that produce them. Computational methods, on the other hand, aim to reduce artifacts retrospectively using an algorithm-driven approach.5 While experimental approaches should be considered the gold standard, as they address the underlying causes of the artifacts, computational methods are also needed for two main reasons. First, as stated, the physical causes of these artifacts are not always clear, so it may not be possible to completely remove them experimentally. Second, experimental approaches offer no solution for existing data sets, which may contain important information, but might nevertheless be discarded or underutilized due to the presence of artifacts.

Gerber et al.5 explored computational methods for removing tiling artifacts in their work over a decade ago, culminating in various methods that have been applied a limited number of times since.7,8 They compared two methods: one based on linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and another on a generalized linear model (GLM). For the GLM approach, they divide the full region into two subregions: the region of the sample and the region of the background. For the LDA approach, such a classification was not done. While identifying these regions may be trivial when there is clear contrast between sample and background, such as for their mounted tissue section data set, manually classifying pixels into background or sample pixels is an additional task and may be considered a burden for some applications. Regardless, their work sparks interest in further investigation, especially in the use of other statistical and machine learning methods for tiling artifact removal.

In the current study, we build upon the findings of Gerber et al.5 and further explore the nature of tiling artifacts in ToF-SIMS. We consider not only LDA (which, to the best of our knowledge, is the only method of the two proposed by Gerber et al. that has been used in subsequent works), but we also explore a range of other methods, including a seamless stitching method developed for optical microscopy by Legesse et al.9 Our study comprises six diverse methods, described in the next section. We use four data sets from samples produced in biological and manufacturing domains in this study. One of these data sets, a sagittal section of a rat brain, was acquired using (OrbiSIMS), rather than ToF-SIMS. As we do not have the “true data” without tiling artifacts, we use two approaches to evaluate the methods: we perform a detailed statistical analysis, proposing a range of statistical metrics for the methods, and we conduct a survey asking participants to rank the methods on their effectiveness for artifact removal. Details of the survey and results of the statistical analysis are included in Section 3.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation and Analysis

Three ToF-SIMS data sets and one OrbiSIMS data set were acquired to explore tiling artifact removal. Briefly, we acquired ToF-SIMS stage raster imaging data from the following systems: an acrylate polymer microarray, resistors on a printed circuit board, and a dye microarray. OrbiSIMS imaging data were acquired from a sagittal section of a rat brain. In all cases, stage rasters were collected over large areas (from several to tens of mm2) with a range of patch sizes, pixel sizes, number of patches, etc. Data exporting and preprocessing varied slightly per data set (see SI), however, generally involved automatic peak detection using the summed spectra. Total peak intensity images were then exported. This resulted in between 1 and 2 thousand peaks per data set. Further preprocessing, such as normalization and (in some cases) spatial binning, were performed using in-house MATLAB scripts. Complete details of sample preparation, data acquisition, data analysis and data preprocessing are provided in the Supporting Information (SI) Sections SI-1.1 and SI-1.2.

Linear Discriminant Analysis Approach

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is a machine learning method used for classification, dimension reduction, and visualization. We perform LDA and subtract the first component from the data as it approximates the tiling artifacts. Our method is different from Gerber et al.5 mainly because we only use one iteration.

Seamless Stitching

We implemented a tile stitching method9 involving both an overall correction to each tile and a smoothing across tile boundaries. Each tile was multiplied by a correction map obtained by averaging all tiles, smoothing using Gaussian filters, and taking the multiplicative inverse.

Simple Averaging and Simple Interpolating

These are two simple methods that can be used as benchmarks. The simple averaging method averages the values on the border of each tile using values on either side, and the simple interpolation method interpolates the values across borders.

Of the methods discussed, tensor decomposition and LDA are applied to the full data set comprising the 3D array of I × J × K dimensions. The other methods can be applied to single hyperspectral slices of size I × J as needed.

Statistical Evaluation of Methods

It is challenging to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed tiling artifact removal methods on real data sets, because in these cases we do not have the “true” version of the data set without tiling artifacts. One approach is to add simulated tiling artifacts,5,9 though the usefulness of this approach depends on the effectiveness of the simulations, which is open to question. Therefore, we have taken two approaches to evaluate the effectiveness of these methods. One approach is to conduct a survey and get the input of end users as to which methods work well. The other approach is to report a set of statistical and information theoretical measures that are of relevance.

As statistical and information theoretic measures, we investigated mutual information, correlation coefficient and time series quantities13 such as seasonality of the data. Of these measures, the seasonality coefficients were more illuminating than the others. The complete analysis is given in the Supporting Information,SI-1.4.

where Var denotes the variance. If a time series is highly seasonal, then Var(Rt + St) is much larger than Var(Rt), making the strength of seasonality close to 1. If a time series is not seasonal, the numerator and denominator would be similar, making the seasonal strength close to zero. For a hyperspectral data cube, we consider a 2D array for a single spectral value and add up the values in x and y dimensions to come up with the time series. We note that it is not indexed in time and, as such, is not a time series in the strict sense of the term. But the concept of seasonality holds as evidenced by the periodicity of tiling artifacts. As an evaluation metric, we consider the reduction in seasonality by applying the tiling artifact removal method.

Survey and Evaluation

To get the input of end users, which in this case are researchers, we surveyed 65 research scientists at the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation, Australia (CSIRO). The study was reviewed by and received ethics clearance from the CSIRO Ethics Committee (076/23). The survey contained 8 questions about tiling artifact removal and one question on the participant’s familiarity with this type of data. We will refer to this final question as the familiarity question in our discussion. Responses to the familiarity question were used in our statistical analysis to explore whether researchers who are more familiar with these data ranked the methods differently from others. In each question on tiling artifact removal, participants were asked to rank the methods from best to worst. For each hyperspectral image, we asked two questions. One question asked the participant to rank the images on how well the information was retained after removing the tiling artifacts. The other question asked the participant to rank the images on how well the tiling artifacts are reduced. Supporting Information, Figures S3 and S4 show survey questions.

We use a preference/ranking method detailed in Hatzinger et al.14 to evaluate the survey responses. They use a pattern approach15 for ranking, where a pattern is a set of paired comparisons. Suppose there are J objects and let yjk denote 1 if object j is preferred to object k and −1 otherwise. Then they define the probability that a ranker will prefer j to k as

for certain πj, πk > 0, where the πs are called worth parameters. These parameters give the locations of objects on a preference scale. A ranking of J objects is transformed to a pattern by considering a vector , where yjk is defined as above. After multiple transformations and modeling choices, generalized linear models were used to fit the pattern models. The output of these models are the worth parameters, πi for i ∈ {1, ..., J}. The higher the worth is, the higher the overall preference. Preference/ranking models were fitted using the R package prefmod.16

Results and Discussion

To begin our systematic exploration and evaluation of the 6 artifact removal methods, we first present example results from two of the studied data sets. We chose to focus on the biological data set (OrbiTrapSIMS) and the dye microarray data set (ToF-SIMS) for maximum diversity. However, equivalent figures for the polymer microarray and resistor data sets are also included in the Supporting Information(SI), Section SI-2. Figures 2 and 3 show the results of the 6 methods on the dye and the brain data sets, respectively. We selected two hyperspectral slices (secondary ion images) from each data set for illustration. The specific slices were selected because they both exhibited prominent tiling artifacts, yet were somewhat different from each other, with regard to contrast, overall intensity, and spatial distribution. The left column in Figure 2 corresponds to m/z 41.04 and the right column corresponds to m/z 114.90. Several interesting observations can be drawn from Figure 2. In the bottom right region of the control image, we can clearly observe a criss-cross pattern. In the tensor output, this pattern is changed; the horizontal bars are emphasized and the vertical bars are greatly reduced. The seamless stitching output shows an interesting observation. While the criss-cross pattern on the bottom right has almost disappeared, several of the dark circular dots have light halos around some of their periphery. This can be explained as a consequence of the tile correction map. The halo effect of the seamless stitching method is caused by the regularity in the placement of dots across the tiles and exacerbated by the almost perfect matching of the tile size to the regular gap between dots. Aligning the tile size and the dot gaps has led the seamless stitching method to inadvertently introduce halo artifacts even while (very effectively) removing the tile boundary artifacts.

Figure 3 shows two hyperspectral slices corresponding to m/z 97.07 (left column) and m/z 281.25 (right column) from the brain data set. Again, we see some methods performing similarly and some methods performing quite differently. Both tensor and LDA exacerbate the tiling artifacts for both hyperspectral slices. We also see some similarities between the averaging and interpolation methods. Seamless stitching effectively removes tile boundaries and appears to retain important spatial features in the data. Therefore, this method appears to be the most promising, although it is important to note that this is a subjective evaluation. This method combines an overall correction, similar to the multiplicative linear correction, with an edge smoothing correction; its superior performance for this data set is thus likely due primarily to the smoothing across the edges. Further method comparison and ranking are left for the following sections.

The polymer data set is similar to the dye data set but is much less regular. Supporting Information, Figures S5 and S6 show two slices corresponding to m/z 45.04 and m/z 62.99 from the polymer data set. The circles have different sizes and colors, and the positioning is more casual. Again, we see that the methods repeat some already seen patterns. The seamless stitching method performs poorly on this data set with certain circles having a halo around them and some other circles almost being erased. However, the tiling artifacts are almost nonexistent when seamless stitching is used. The linear and tensor methods generally perform better for this data set.

Supporting Information, Figures S7 and S8 show two hyperspectral slices corresponding to m/z 27.02 and m/z 55.06 from the resistor data set. Unlike the previous data sets the tiling artifacts in this data set are less obvious. Generally, all methods perform similarly, and this is a unique characteristic of this data set; i.e., the choice of method does not have a significant effect on the output. One important issue with this particular data set is tile misalignment, which is not directly addressed using any of the methods used and is therefore outside the scope of this work. Thus, we consider this data set less representative of method performance but include it for further evaluation regardless.

Survey Results

As an additional evaluation to complement the statistical evaluation of the artifact removal methods, we also surveyed a group of research scientists to obtain subjective rankings for each method. We designed a series of survey questions aimed to identify how each method performed across the four data sets studied. The questions were designed to assess the capacity of the methods to remove the tiling artifacts, while also retaining as much information as possible from the original images. Conclusion

We have compared 6 diverse methods for tiling artifact removal on 4 hyperspectral SIMS data sets. At a superficial analysis of the images, it appears that Linear_Multi performed better than Seamless Stitching for the data sets with periodic characteristics, and Seamless Stitching performed better on biological data sets. However, this is an empirical study examining four data sets. We do not want to overinterpret any of our findings. Furthermore, from the survey we saw that, depending on the question asked, i.e., the objective of tiling removal, the “best” or most preferred method differed, even for the same data set, thus, there are nuances to perception preferences. While Linear_Multi and Seamless Stitching scored more highly on the subjective survey, for some data sets they introduced new artifacts. Other methods, such as simple averaging and interpolation methods, scored subjectively poorly on the biological data set, but more highly on the microarray data sets. As such, a key takeaway from this study is that the performance of each method (based upon our statistical and subjective evaluation methods) was dependent on the characteristics of the data set.

We also want to touch upon the misalignment of the beam and stage raster tiles; these would certainly influence the performance of the corrections, as they are not designed to target such artifacts. We do note, however, that Seamless Stitching does include an intensity correction step, which would help to correct the variation in the intensities between tiles. The statistical measures we have used to evaluate performance are not influenced by these artifacts, however, as they are designed to measure the relationship between intensity and within-tile position only.

To generalize, we propose that a method that is useful for one data set may be detrimental for another. Similarly, the best method may depend on the objective: we selected a specific evaluation procedure, whereas it is likely that this will depend on the analytical goals. For example, we clearly saw that methods that were subjectively good at retaining information were not necessarily ideal for removing tiling artifacts. This is an important insight. Often tiling artifact removal is a preprocessing step, and as such a single method may be employed for different data sets. We caution practitioners not to discount this preprocessing step and encourage them to explore different methods for tiling artifact removal. To this end, we make the code used in this project available at https://github.com/sevvandi/supplementary_material/tree/master/ToF-SIMS. The effect of different tiling artifact removal methods on downstream tasks, such as image segmentation, is a future research avenue.

Finally, we reiterate that the purpose of this work was not to understand and correct the physical origins of tiling artifacts in SIMS data. We leave such an investigation to future work, as it is outside the scope of this study. Rather, we focused here on computational methods to alleviate the artifacts, without requiring any knowledge of their cause. We propose such methods to be valuable when the origins of the artifacts are not clearly understood or when used retrospectively to improve the quality and usefulness of existing data.
