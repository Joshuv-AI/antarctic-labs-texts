Reduced Random Walks in the Hyperbolic Plane‽
Colin Defant, Mitchell Lee

Abstract
. We study Lam’s reduced random walk in a hyperbolic triangle group, which we view as a random walk in the upper half-plane. We prove that this walk converges almost surely to a point on the extended real line. We devote special attention to the reduced random walk in P ​ G ​ L 2 ​ ( ℤ ) PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) (i.e., the ( 2 , 3 , ∞ ) (2,3,\infty) triangle group). In this case, we provide an explicit formula for the cumulative distribution function of the limit. This formula is written in terms of the interrobang function , a new function ‽ : [ 0 , 1 ] → ℝ \textnormal{\textinterrobang}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R} that shares several of the remarkable analytic and arithmetic properties of Minkowski’s question-mark function.

1.1.
Random walks on hyperbolic triangle groups

Let
\Delta
be a triangle in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}
, possibly with ideal vertices. Assume that the angles of
\Delta
have measures
\pi/p
,
\pi/q
, and
\pi/r
, where
p,q,r\in\{2,3,4,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}
. Let
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be the reflections in the sides of
\Delta
. The isometries
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
generate a hyperbolic group
W
called the
(p,q,r)
triangle group, and it is a classical result that the triangles
w(\Delta)
for
w\in W
are distinct and form a tiling of the hyperbolic plane
[
25
, Chapter II]
. We refer to these triangles as
alcoves
, and we say that two alcoves are
adjacent
if they share an edge. We will generalize this setup in
Section

3
.

The
simple random walk on
W
starts at the fundamental alcove
\Delta
. At each step, it moves to an alcove chosen uniformly at random from the alcoves adjacent to the current one. Equivalently, the simple random walk on
W
is the right random walk on
W
driven by the uniform measure on
\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
.

Random walks on hyperbolic groups such as
W
have been studied for at least a century and have continued to attract considerable interest
[
27
,
26
]
. For a comprehensive treatment of the subject, see the 2023 book by Lalley
[
21
]
.

Minkowski’s
question-mark function

\?\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
has unexpectedly surfaced in the study of random walks on hyperbolic groups. In 2017, Letac and Piccioni studied a particular random walk on the hyperbolic group
PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. They proved that it almost surely converges to a point on the boundary of the hyperbolic plane and found an explicit formula for the cumulative distribution function of the limit in terms of Minkowski’s question-mark function
[
24
]
.

In this article, we consider instead the
(lazy) reduced random walk on
W
, which is the following variation on the simple random walk on
W
introduced by Lam in 2015
[
22
]
. Again, we start at the fundamental alcove
\Delta
. At each step, we again choose an alcove uniformly at random from the set of alcoves adjacent to the current one. However, in this model, we are not allowed to cross a line more than once. Thus, if moving to the new alcove would cause us to cross a line that we have already crossed, then we stay still at the current alcove; otherwise, we move to the new adjacent alcove.

In this article, we will be primarily concerned with the limiting behavior of the reduced random walk on
W
.

1.2.
Previous research on reduced random walks

The reduced random walk was originally defined more generally for any Coxeter group
W
. We will describe the generalization in
Section

2
.

For example, suppose that
W
is the Coxeter group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, where the simple reflection
s_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
) is the transposition that swaps
i
and
i+1
. In this case, the transitions in the reduced random walk have a simple combinatorial interpretation in terms of permutations. At each discrete time step, we choose an integer
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
uniformly at random, and we put the entries of the permutation in positions
i
and
i+1
into decreasing order (if they are already in decreasing order, we do nothing). This model is a discrete-time analogue of the
oriented swap process
, which was introduced by Angel, Holroyd, and Romik in 2009
[
3
]
and which has seen considerable recent interest
[
8
,
11
,
31
]
. With probability
1
, the reduced random walk eventually reaches the decreasing permutation, which is the unique absorbing state.

Lam studied the reduced random walk when
W
is an irreducible affine Weyl group
[
22
]
. In this setting, he proved that the limit of the reduced random walk has a finitely supported probability distribution on the boundary of the Tits cone. Lam found that this distribution is determined by an associated finite-state Markov chain. When
W
is the affine symmetric group
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, this finite-state Markov chain coincides with an instance of the
multispecies totally asymmetric simple exclusion process
(
multispecies TASEP
) on a cycle, which had already been studied extensively before Lam’s work
[
15
,
16
,
30
]
(see also the more recent works
[
12
,
5
,
7
,
6
]
). Lam’s seminal article has spawned a great deal of subsequent research
[
1
,
4
,
13
,
23
]
.

1.3.
Results

Let us now return to the setting introduced at the beginning of this article, where
W
is a hyperbolic triangle group. The reduced random walk in
W
can be viewed geometrically as a random walk
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
in the Poincaré upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
. We will prove (
Theorem

4.2
) that the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
almost surely converges to a point
\zeta
on the extended real line
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. We will also prove (
Theorems

4.4
and
5.1
) that the probability distribution of
\zeta
is uniquely determined by a simple equation.

We will then consider the special case
W=PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, which is a particularly notable example due to its myriad appearances in number theory. (See
Figures

1
and
4
.) We provide an explicit description of the limiting distribution of the reduced random walk via its cumulative distribution function
F_{\zeta}(x)=\Pr[\zeta\leq x]
(
Theorem

8.1
). We find that
F_{\zeta}
is continuous and satisfies
\lim_{x\to\infty}F_{\zeta}(x)=1
, which implies that the limiting distribution of
\zeta
is atomless. Our description uses an auxiliary function
\interro\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
that we call the
interrobang function
. This function, which we initially define on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
via a recurrence relation before extending its domain to all of
[0,1]
, shares many notable analytic and arithmetic properties with Minkowski’s question-mark function
\?\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
(see
Figure

5
). For example, it is strictly increasing and uniformly continuous (
Theorem

7.13
), it sends rational numbers to dyadic rational numbers (
Proposition

7.15
), and it sends quadratic irrational numbers to rational numbers (
Theorem

7.21
). As a consequence, we find that the cumulative distribution function
F_{\zeta}
sends rational numbers to dyadic rational numbers and sends quadratic irrational numbers to rational numbers (
Theorem

8.1
).

We also conjecture some additional properties of the interrobang function. On the analytic side, we conjecture that
\interro
is differentiable and has derivative
0
almost everywhere (
Conjecture

7.14
). On the arithmetic side, we conjecture that there exists a dyadic rational number
y\in[0,3/8]
such that
\interro^{-1}(y)
is irrational (
Conjecture

7.16
). In fact, we conjecture that
\interro^{-1}(1/8)
is transcendental (
Conjecture

7.17
).

2.
The reduced random walk on a Coxeter group

For a more complete introduction to the theory of Coxeter groups, see the 2005 textbook of Björner and Brenti
[
9
]
.

Let
I
be a finite index set. A
Coxeter matrix
is a function
m\colon I\times I\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
m(i,i)=1
for all
i\in I
, and
m(i,i^{\prime})=m(i^{\prime},i)>1
for all distinct
i,i^{\prime}\in I
. A
Coxeter system
with the Coxeter matrix
m
is a pair
(W,S)
, where
W
is a group,
S=\{s_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}\subseteq W
is a generating set, and
W
has the presentation

\langle S\,|\,((s_{i}s_{i^{\prime}})^{m(i,i^{\prime})})_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\rangle.

We refer to
W
as a
Coxeter group
, and we refer to the elements of
S
as
simple reflections
. It is well known that the simple reflections
s_{i}
are distinct and have order
2
.

The
length
of an element
w\in W
, denoted
\ell(w)
, is the minimum integer
\ell
such that
w
can be written in the form
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\ell}}
for some
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in I
. For
i\in I
and
w\in W
, we say that
i
is a
left descent
of
w
if
\ell(s_{i}w)<\ell(w)
. We say that
i
is a
right descent
of
w
if
\ell(ws_{i})<\ell(w)
. The set of left descents of
w
is denoted by
\mathrm{Des}_{L}(w)
, and the set of right descents of
w
is denoted by
\mathrm{Des}_{R}(w)
.

There is a unique associative binary operation
\star\colon W\times W\to W
such that for all
i\in I
and all
w\in W
, we have

\mathbbm{1}\star w=w\star\mathbbm{1}=w

and

(1)

s_{i}\star w=\begin{cases}w&\mbox{if $i\in\mathrm{Des}_{L}(w)$}\\
s_{i}w&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

The same binary operation
\star
also satisfies the dual equation

(2)

w\star s_{i}=\begin{cases}w&\mbox{if $i\in\mathrm{Des}_{R}(w)$}\\
ws_{i}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

We refer to
\star
as the
Demazure product
, although it is sometimes called the
greedy product
or
0
-Hecke product
. The Demazure product cannot decrease length; that is,
\ell(u\star w)\geq\max(\ell(u),\ell(w))
for all
u,w\in W
.

Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\ldots
be a sequence of indices chosen independently and uniformly at random from
I
. The
(lazy) reduced random walk

[
22
]
is the sequence of random variables
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
, where

u_{m}=s_{i_{1}}\star\cdots\star s_{i_{m}}.

In other words, the reduced random walk is the (right) random walk on
W
considered as a monoid with the Demazure product, where the step distribution is uniform on
S
. Thus, the reduced random walk fits into the more general field of random walks on semigroups
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

By construction, the reduced random walk is a Markov chain on
W
. Unlike the usual simple random walk in
\Cay(W,S)
, the reduced random walk is nondecreasing in length; that is,
\ell(u_{m})\leq\ell(u_{m+1})
for all
m\geq 0
, and equality holds if and only if
u_{m}=u_{m+1}
.

3.
Hyperbolic triangle groups

The material in this section can also be found in any reference work on hyperbolic geometry. For a more complete introduction, see the 2005 textbook of Anderson
[
2
]
.

3.1.
The hyperbolic plane

Let

\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}

denote the upper half-plane. Considering each element
z\in\mathbb{H}
as
z=x+iy
, where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
y>0
, we may equip
\mathbb{H}
with the conformal Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

It is well known that with this metric,
\mathbb{H}
is a surface with constant negative Gaussian curvature; we refer to
\mathbb{H}
as the
Poincaré half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane
or simply the
hyperbolic plane
. (There are many other ways of constructing the hyperbolic plane, including the
Poincaré disk model
, which we will use in
Section

5
.)

Denote the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}
by
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, which is homeomorphic to a circle. Denote the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
by
\overline{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}=\mathbb{H}\cup\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, which is homeomorphic to a closed disk.

A
(hyperbolic) line
in
\mathbb{H}
is a subset of the form

\{z\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,\operatorname{Re}(z)=x\}

or

\{z\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,|z-x|=r\},

where
x,r\in\mathbb{R}
with
r>0
. Every geodesic in
\mathbb{H}
is contained in a hyperbolic line.

The
ideal points
of a line
L\subseteq\mathbb{H}
are the elements of
\overline{L}\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\overline{L}
denotes the topological closure of
L
in
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
. Every line has two ideal points, and every two distinct elements of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
are the ideal points of a unique line.

For each hyperbolic line
L\subseteq\mathbb{H}
, there is a unique anticonformal isometry
t_{L}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
that fixes
L
. If
L=\{z\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,\operatorname{Re}(z)=x\}
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
t_{L}
is given by

t_{L}(z)=2x-\overline{z}.

On the other hand, if
L=\{z\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,|z-x|=r\}
for some
x,r\in\mathbb{R}
with
r>0
, then
t_{L}
is given by

t_{L}(z)=\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z}-x}+x.

We refer to
t_{L}
as the
reflection
in the line
L
; it extends uniquely to a continuous function
t_{L}\colon\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\overline{\mathbb{H}}
.

3.2.
Hyperbolic triangle groups

Fix lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
and a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
. Throughout the rest of this article, we make the following assumption.

Axiom 3.1
.

For all distinct
i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,2,3\}
, one of the following two conditions holds.

•

The lines
L_{i}
and
L_{i^{\prime}}
do not intersect in
\mathbb{H}
. Moreover,
z_{0}
lies between them; that is,
z_{0}
is in the connected component of
\mathbb{H}\setminus(L_{i}\cup L_{i^{\prime}})
whose boundary contains both
L_{i}
and
L_{i^{\prime}}
.

•

The lines
L_{i}
and
L_{i^{\prime}}
intersect at an angle with measure
\pi/m(i,i^{\prime})
, where
m(i,i^{\prime})\geq 2
is an integer. This angle contains the point
z_{0}
in its interior.

Define

\Delta=\{z\in\mathbb{H}\setminus(L_{1}\cup L_{2}\cup L_{3})\,|\,\text{$z$ and $z_{0}$ are on the same side of $L_{i}$ for $i=1,2,3$}\}.

Clearly,
\Delta
is an open neighborhood of
z_{0}
.

For example,
Axiom

3.1
holds if
\Delta\ni z_{0}
is the interior of a hyperbolic triangle with sides
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
L_{3}
and angle measures
\pi/m(1,2)
,
\pi/m(2,3)
,
\pi/m(1,3)
. However,
Axiom

3.1
is weak enough to allow for cases where two of the lines
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
L_{3}
do not intersect or even share an ideal point. In this case, the region
\Delta
bounded by
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, and
L_{3}
may be unbounded or even have infinite area; see
Figure

2
.

Figure 2.
An example of lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
and a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
satisfying
Axiom

3.1
. In this example,
m(1,2)=2
and
m(2,3)=m(1,3)=\infty
.

The reflections
s_{i}=t_{L_{i}}
generate a group
W
of isometries of
\mathbb{H}
called a
hyperbolic triangle group
. It is well known that
W
is infinite,
W
acts properly on
\mathbb{H}
, and
(W,S)
is a Coxeter system with Coxeter matrix
m
, where we take
m(i,i)=1
for all
i
and we take
m(i,i^{\prime})=\infty
if the lines
L_{i}
and
L_{i^{\prime}}
do not intersect
[
25
, Chapter II]
. The action of
W
on
\mathbb{H}
can also be extended uniquely to a continuous action of
W
on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
.

It is common in the literature to refer to
W
as the
(p,q,r)
triangle group
, where
p
,
q
,
r
are the values
m(1,2)
,
m(2,3)
,
m(1,3)
sorted in nondecreasing order. Note that
W
is determined up to isomorphism by
p
,
q
, and
r
.

The set
\Delta
is a fundamental domain for the action of
W
in the following sense: the sets
w(\Delta)
for
w\in W
are disjoint, and their closures cover
\mathbb{H}

[
25
, Chapter II]
. In other words, the generalized triangles
w(\Delta)
form a tiling of
\mathbb{H}
. We refer to the sets
w(\Delta)
as
alcoves
. For all
u,w\in W
, the alcoves
u(\Delta)
and
w(\Delta)
are adjacent (that is, they share an edge) if and only if
w=us_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

Also associated to the hyperbolic triangle group
W
is the following arrangement of lines:

\mathcal{H}=\{L\subseteq\mathbb{H}\,|\,\text{$L$ is a line and $t_{L}\in W$}\}.

Since
t_{wL}=wt_{L}w^{-1}
for all lines
L
and all
w\in W
, the set
\mathcal{H}
is invariant under the action of
W
. These lines partition the hyperbolic plane into the alcoves
w(\Delta)
.

It is well known that for all
w\in W
, the length
\ell(w)
is equal to the number of lines
L\in\mathcal{H}
that separate
z_{0}
from
w(z_{0})
.

4.
Reduced random walks in the hyperbolic plane

Use the setup of
Section

3.2
. We now show how the reduced random walk in the hyperbolic triangle group
W
can be seen as a random walk
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}
itself. In the remainder of the article, we will investigate the limiting behavior of
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
.

For
i=1,2,3
, define the
one-way reflection

\tau_{i}\colon\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\overline{\mathbb{H}}
by

\tau_{i}(z)=\begin{cases}s_{i}(z)&\mbox{if $z$ and $z_{0}$ are on the same side of $L_{i}$}\\
z&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Each one-way reflection is idempotent:
\tau_{i}^{2}=\tau_{i}
.

For all
w\in W
, define
\tau_{w}\colon\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\overline{\mathbb{H}}
by

\tau_{w}=\tau_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{i_{\ell}},

where
i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell}
is a reduced word for
w
(that is,
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\ell}}=w
and
w
has length
\ell
). By Matsumoto’s theorem
[
28
]
, the element
\tau_{w}
is well defined (i.e., does not depend on the choice of reduced word). We have
\tau_{w}(z_{0})=w(z_{0})
and
\tau_{u\star w}=\tau_{u}\circ\tau_{w}
for all
u,w\in W
.

Now, sample elements
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\ldots
uniformly and independently from
\{1,2,3\}
. Consider the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
, where

(3)

z_{m}=(\tau_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{i_{m}})(z_{0}).

By the properties above, we have
z_{m}=u_{m}(z_{0})
, where

(4)

u_{m}=s_{i_{1}}\star\cdots\star s_{i_{m}}.

Thus, we may think of
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
as a geometric interpretation of the reduced random walk. See
Figure

4
for an illustration.

The sequence of alcoves

u_{0}(\Delta),u_{1}(\Delta),u_{2}(\Delta),\ldots

that contain the points
z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots
respectively was informally described at the end of
Section

1.1
. To reiterate, these alcoves form a random walk that never crosses a given line in
\mathcal{H}
more than once. This implies the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

For each line
L\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists an integer
N
such that the set
{\{z_{m}\,|\,m\geq N\}}
lies entirely on one side of the line
L
.

We now state and prove our first main theorem about the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

The sequence
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is almost surely not eventually constant. Moreover, if it is not eventually constant, then
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
converges to some
\zeta\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

By (
4
), we have for all
m
that
u_{m+1}=u_{m}\star s_{i_{m+1}}
, so
u_{m}=u_{m+1}
if and only if
i_{m+1}
is a right descent of
u_{m}
. Because
W
is infinite, we have
{\mathrm{Des}_{R}(u_{m})\subsetneq\{1,2,3\}}
, so
u_{m}=u_{m+1}
with probability at most
2/3
. Since
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is a Markov chain, this implies that
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is almost surely not eventually constant.

Now, suppose that
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is not eventually constant. For all
m
with
u_{m}\neq u_{m+1}
, we have
\ell(u_{m})<\ell(u_{m+1})
. Therefore,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\ell(u_{m})=\infty,

so every
w\in W
appears in the sequence
(u_{m})_{m\geq 0}
finitely many times. Since
z_{m}=u_{m}(z_{0})
and
z_{0}
has trivial stabilizer in
W
, every
z\in\mathbb{H}
appears in the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
finitely many times.

Because
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
is compact, the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
has a subsequence converging to some
\zeta\in\overline{\mathbb{H}}
. The action of
W
on
\mathbb{H}
is proper, so
\zeta\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
does not converge to
\zeta
. Then
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
has a subsequence converging to some
\zeta^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathbb{H}}
with
\zeta^{\prime}\neq\zeta
. By the same argument as before,
\zeta^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

Our strategy will be as follows. We will find a small set
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of lines that divide the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}
into regions
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}
. By
Lemma

4.1
, there is some
j\in[k]
such that
z_{m}\in U_{j}
for all sufficiently large
m
. Hence,
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
are both in the topological closure of
W(z_{0})\cap U_{j}
, where
W(z_{0})
denotes the orbit of
z_{0}
under the action of
W
. By choosing the set
\mathcal{L}
of lines appropriately, we can ensure that for all
j\in[k]
, either the set
W(z_{0})\cap U_{j}
is finite or the topological closure of
U_{j}
does not contain both
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
. This will yield the desired contradiction.

Consider the unique line
M\subseteq\mathbb{H}
with ideal points
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
. We consider three cases.

Case 1.
Suppose that there is a line
L\in\mathcal{H}
such that
L\neq M
and
L\cap M\neq\emptyset
. Then
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
are on opposite sides of
L
, so we may take
\mathcal{L}=\{L\}
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
M\in\mathcal{H}
and
L\cap M=\emptyset
for all
L\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{M\}
. Then, the line
M
separates two alcoves
v_{1}(\Delta)
and
v_{2}(\Delta)
. We may take
\mathcal{L}
to be the set

\{v_{1}(L_{1}),v_{1}(L_{2}),v_{1}(L_{3}),v_{2}(L_{1}),v_{2}(L_{2}),v_{2}(L_{3})\}

of all the sides of
v_{1}(\Delta)
and
v_{2}(\Delta)
. (Note that this set has five elements because
M
is a side of both
v_{1}(\Delta)
and
v_{2}(\Delta)
.)

Case 3.
Suppose that
L\cap M=\emptyset
for all
L\in\mathcal{H}
. Then,
M
is contained in an alcove
v(\Delta)
. We may take
\mathcal{L}
to be the set

\{v(L_{1}),v(L_{2}),v(L_{3})\}

of the sides of
v(\Delta)
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Theorem

4.2
implies that the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
converges surely. Contrast this with the reduced random walk in an affine Weyl group, which converges to a point on the boundary of the Tits cone almost surely but not surely
[
22
, Theorem 1]
.

Since the pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable, the limit
\zeta=\lim_{m\to\infty}z_{m}
is a random variable. Our primary goal in the remainder of this article is to determine how
\zeta
is distributed in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. To do so, we will use the following key theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mu
denote the probability distribution of the limit

\zeta=\lim_{m\to\infty}z_{m}

in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

(5)

\mu=\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{2})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{3})_{*}\mu\right),

where
(\tau_{i})_{*}\mu
denotes the pushforward of
\mu
by the function
\tau_{i}
.

Proof.

For
m\geq 0
, let

z^{\prime}_{m}=(\tau_{i_{2}}\circ\tau_{i_{3}}\cdots\circ\tau_{i_{m+1}})(z_{0}).

This is the same as the definition of
z_{m}
given in (
3
), but using the sequence of indices
i_{2},i_{3},i_{4},\ldots
instead of
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\ldots
. Therefore, the sequences
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
and
(z^{\prime}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
are identically distributed, so the limit

\zeta^{\prime}=\lim_{m\to\infty}z^{\prime}_{m}

also has distribution
\mu
.

For all
m\geq 0
, we have
\tau_{i_{1}}(z^{\prime}_{m})=z_{m+1}
. Since
\tau_{i_{1}}
is continuous, we may take the limit as
m\to\infty
to conclude that

\tau_{i_{1}}(\zeta^{\prime})=\zeta.

Since
i_{1}
is uniformly distributed in
\{1,2,3\}
and independent from
\zeta^{\prime}
, the theorem follows.
∎

5.
Uniqueness of the stationary measure

In this section, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exists a unique probability measure
\mu
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
that satisfies the equation

\mu=\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{2})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{3})_{*}\mu\right)

from
Theorem

4.4
.

In some sense,
Theorems

4.4
and
5.1
completely answer the question of how the limit
\zeta=\lim_{m\to\infty}z_{m}
is distributed. Later, in
Sections

6
,
7
and
8
, we will explicitly describe the distribution of

in the important case

.

5.1.
The Poincaré disk model

In this section and only this section, it will be convenient to use a different model for the hyperbolic plane.

The
Poincaré disk model of the hyperbolic plane
is the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{z\,|\,|z|<1\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
in the complex plane. There is a biholomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{D}
known as the
Cayley transform
and given by

\varphi(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}.

Via the Cayley transform, we may transfer all the notions introduced in
Section

3.1
from
\mathbb{H}
to
\mathbb{D}
. Briefly:

•

There is a conformal Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=\frac{4(dx^{2}+dy^{2})}{(1-|z|)^{2}}

on
\mathbb{D}
, which makes
\mathbb{D}
into a surface with constant negative Gaussian curvature.

•

The boundary of
\mathbb{D}
is the unit circle
S^{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|z|=1\}
. Denote the closed unit disk by
\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\mathbb{D}\cup S^{1}
.

•

A
(hyperbolic) line
in
\mathbb{D}
is a subset of the form

\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,|\,z/c\in\mathbb{R}\}

or

\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,|\,|z-c|^{2}=|c|^{2}-1\}

for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
with
|c|>1
. The condition
z/c\in\mathbb{R}
defines a diameter of the unit circle, whereas the equation
|z-c|^{2}=|c|^{2}-1
defines a circle centered at
c
that is orthogonal to the unit circle.

•

For each hyperbolic line
L\subseteq\mathbb{D}
, there is a unique anticonformal isometry
t_{L}\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
that fixes
L
. If
L=\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,|\,z/c\in\mathbb{R}\}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
, then
t_{L}
is given by

t_{L}(z)=\frac{\overline{z}c}{\overline{c}}.

In the language of Euclidean geometry, this is the usual reflection in the line
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,z/c\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

On the other hand, if
\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,|\,|z-c|^{2}=|c|^{2}-1\}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
with
|c|>1
, then
t_{L}
is given by

t_{L}(z)=\frac{|c|^{2}-1}{\overline{z}-\overline{c}}+c.

In the language of Euclidean geometry, this is the inversion with respect to the circle centered at
c
with radius
\sqrt{|c|^{2}-1}
.

We refer to
t_{L}
as the
reflection
in the line
L
; it extends uniquely to a continuous function
t_{L}\colon\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

For details, see any reference text on hyperbolic geometry
[
2
]
.

We may also perform the setup of
Section

3.2
and
Section

4
in the Poincaré disk model
\mathbb{D}
rather than the Poincaré half-plane model
\mathbb{H}
. This yields a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
, three lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
, and three one-way reflections
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\colon\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We will prove
Theorem

5.1
in this equivalent setting.

By applying a suitable isometry of
\mathbb{D}
, we may make the additional assumption that
z_{0}=0
. Since
z_{0}
does not lie on any of the lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
, this implies that for
i=1,2,3
, the line
L_{i}
is of the form

\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,|\,|z-c_{i}|^{2}=|c_{i}|^{2}-1\},

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|c_{i}|>1
. Let us define
r_{i}=\sqrt{|c_{i}|^{2}-1}
. The one-way reflection
\tau_{i}\colon\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is then given by

(6)

\tau_{i}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{\overline{z}-\overline{c_{i}}}+c_{i}&\mbox{if $|z-c_{i}|>r_{i}$}\\
z&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

5.2.
The contraction properties

As in the statement of
Theorem

5.1
, let us now consider
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}
as functions
S^{1}\to S^{1}
on the boundary of the hyperbolic plane. We will prove that
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}
satisfy two contraction properties.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
x,y\in S^{1}
and all
i=1,2,3
, we have

|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|\leq\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}\right)\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|y-c_{i}|}\right)|x-y|\leq|x-y|.

Proof.

The second inequality is trivial, so it suffices to prove the first inequality. We have three cases.

Case 1.
Suppose that
|x-c_{i}|\leq r_{i}
and
|y-c_{i}|\leq r_{i}
. By (
6
), we have
\tau_{i}(x)=x
and
\tau_{i}(y)=y
, so

|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|=|x-y|=\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}\right)\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|y-c_{i}|}\right)|x-y|,

and the inequality holds with equality.

Case 2.
Suppose that
|x-c_{i}|>r_{i}
and
|y-c_{i}|>r_{i}
. Then, by (
6
), we have

\displaystyle|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left(\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{\overline{x}-\overline{c_{i}}}+c_{i}\right)-\left(\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{\overline{y}-\overline{c_{i}}}+c_{i}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{i}^{2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-c_{i}\right\rvert\left\lvert y-c_{i}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}\right)\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|y-c_{i}|}\right)|x-y|,

so the inequality holds with equality again.

Case 3.
Suppose that
|x-c_{i}|>r_{i}
and
|y-c_{i}|\leq r_{i}
. (The remaining case that
|x-c_{i}|\leq r_{i}
and
|y-c_{i}|>r_{i}
is analogous.)
By the intermediate value theorem, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<\lambda\leq 1
such that

\left\lvert\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y-c_{i}\right\rvert=r_{i}.

Let
z=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
; then, we have
|z-c_{i}|=r_{i}
and
|x-y|=|x-z|+|z-y|
.

By (
6
) and the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left(\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{\overline{x}-\overline{c_{i}}}+c_{i}\right)-y\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left\lvert r_{i}^{2}-(\overline{x}-\overline{c_{i}})(y-c_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left\lvert\left(r_{i}^{2}-(\overline{z}-\overline{c_{i}})(y-c_{i})\right)-(\overline{x}-\overline{z})(y-c_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left(\left\lvert r_{i}^{2}-(\overline{z}-\overline{c_{i}})(y-c_{i})\right\rvert+|x-z||y-c_{i}|\right).

Since
|z-c_{i}|=r_{i}
, we may rewrite
\overline{z}-\overline{c_{i}}
as
r_{i}^{2}/(z-c_{i})
to obtain

\displaystyle|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left(\left\lvert r_{i}^{2}-r_{i}^{2}\frac{y-c_{i}}{z-c_{i}}\right\rvert+|x-z||y-c_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left(r_{i}^{2}\frac{|y-z|}{|z-c_{i}|}+|x-z||y-c_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x-c_{i}|}\left(r_{i}|y-z|+|x-z||y-c_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}(|y-z|+|x-z|)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{i}|x-y|}{|x-c_{i}|}

\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}\right)\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|y-c_{i}|}\right)|x-y|,

as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a real constant
C<1
, depending only on
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
, such that for all
x,y\in S^{1}
, there exists
i\in\{1,2,3\}
with
|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|<C|x-y|
.

Proof.

Observe that for all
x\in S^{1}
, there exists
i\in\{1,2,3\}
such that
z_{0}
and
x
lie on the same side of the line
L_{i}
. That is, there exists
i\in\{1,2,3\}
such that
|x-c_{i}|>r_{i}
. Since
S^{1}
is compact, there exists a real constant
C<1
such that for all
x\in S^{1}
, there exists
i\in\{1,2,3\}
with
|x-c_{i}|>r_{i}/C
. (See
Figure

3
.)

Let
x,y\in S^{1}
, and choose
i\in\{1,2,3\}
such that
|x-c_{i}|>r_{i}/C
. By
Lemma

5.2
, we have

|\tau_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}(y)|\leq\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|x-c_{i}|}\right)\min\left(1,\frac{r_{i}}{|y-c_{i}|}\right)|x-y|<C|x-y|,

as desired.
∎

in

The following proof is adapted from an argument personally communicated to the authors by Amol Aggarwal.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

We use the Poincaré disk model; see
Section

5.1
. The fact that the measure
\mu
exists follows directly from
Theorem

4.4
. To show uniqueness, let
\mu,\nu
be two probability measures on
S^{1}
such that

(7)

\mu=\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{2})_{*}\mu+(\tau_{3})_{*}\mu\right)

and

(8)

\nu=\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\nu+(\tau_{2})_{*}\nu+(\tau_{3})_{*}\nu\right);

we will prove that
\mu=\nu
.

Let
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
be random variables. We wish to prove that
X
and
Y
are identically distributed. It suffices to prove that for every Lipschitz function
f\colon S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]

[
10
, Volume II, Theorem 8.3.2]
.

Consider the following Markov chain
(X_{m},Y_{m})_{m\geq 0}
valued in
S^{1}\times S^{1}
. Choose
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\ldots
independently and uniformly at random from
\{1,2,3\}
. Define
X_{0}=X
and
Y_{0}=Y
, and for all
m\geq 0
, define
X_{m+1}=\tau_{i_{m+1}}(X_{m})
and
Y_{m+1}=\tau_{i_{m+1}}(Y_{m})
. (Note that
X_{m}=(\tau_{i_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{i_{1}})(X_{0})
and
Y_{m}=(\tau_{i_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{i_{1}})(Y_{0})
;
contrast these equations with (
3
), where the
\tau_{i}
are composed in the opposite order.) Observe that by (
7
) and (
8
), we have
X_{m}\sim\mu
and
Y_{m}\sim\nu
for all
m\geq 0
.

Let
C<1
be the constant from
Lemma

5.3
, and let
m\geq 0
. By
Lemma

5.2
, we have
|X_{m+1}-Y_{m+1}|\leq|X_{m}-Y_{m}|
. Moreover, by
Lemma

5.3
, conditioned on any particular value of
X_{m}
and
Y_{m}
, there is a probability of at least
1/3
that
|X_{m+1}-Y_{m+1}|<C|X_{m}-Y_{m}|
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[|X_{m+1}-Y_{m+1}|]\leq\frac{C+2}{3}\mathbb{E}[|X_{m}-Y_{m}|].

It follows that

(9)

\mathbb{E}[|X_{m}-Y_{m}|]\leq\left(\frac{C+2}{3}\right)^{m}|X-Y|\leq 2\left(\frac{C+2}{3}\right)^{m}.

Let
K
be the Lipschitz constant for the function
f
. We compute

(10)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[f(X_{m})]-\mathbb{E}[f(Y_{m})]\right\rvert

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert f(X_{m})-f(Y_{m})\right\rvert]

(12)

\displaystyle\leq K\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert X_{m}-Y_{m}\right\rvert]

(13)

\displaystyle\leq K\lim_{m\to\infty}2\left(\frac{C+2}{3}\right)^{m}

(14)

\displaystyle=0.

In (
10
), we used the fact that for all
m\geq 0
, the random variables
X
and
X_{m}
are identically distributed, and the random variables
Y
and
Y_{m}
are identically distributed. In (
11
), we used the linearity of expectation and Jensen’s inequality, which implies that
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[Z]\right\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert Z\right\rvert]
for any random variable
Z
. In (
12
), we used the fact that
f
is Lipschitz. In (
13
), we directly applied (
9
). In (
14
), we used the fact that
C<1
. It follows that
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]
, as desired.
∎

\displaystyle L_{2}

3.1
with
m(1,2)=3

(2,3,\infty)

s_{1}

7.
The interrobang function

In this section, we define an auxiliary function
\interro\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, called the interrobang function
1
1

1

The symbol
\interro
is a ligature of a question mark and an exclamation point called an
interrobang
. It was invented in 1962 by Martin K. Speckter.
, which is related to Minkowski’s classical question-mark function
\mathbb{Q}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
. In
Theorem

8.1
, the interrobang function will allow us to explicitly describe the cumulative distribution function of
\mu
in the case
W\simeq PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
described in
Section

6
, and thus to provide a complete description of the limiting behavior of the reduced random walk in that case. We encourage the reader to read the statement of
Theorem

8.1
before proceeding.

Let us introduce some standard notation and definitions. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denote the fractional part of
x
. We say that a rational number is
dyadic
if, when written in lowest terms, its denominator is a power of
2
. A
quadratic irrational
is an irrational number that is a root of a nonzero quadratic polynomial with rational coefficients.

Definition 7.1
.

The
height function

\height\colon\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{N}
is given by

\height\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=\left\lvert a\right\rvert+\left\lvert b\right\rvert

for all integers
a,b
with
\gcd(a,b)=1
.

Remark 7.2
.

The function
\height
is slightly different from the classical height function
h(a/b)=\max\{\left\lvert a\right\rvert,\left\lvert b\right\rvert\}
commonly used in arithmetic geometry.

We also recall one recursive definition of Minkowski’s question-mark function on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
.

Definition 7.3

(
[
29
, Section 4]
)
.

Minkowski’s question-mark function

\?:[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\?(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $x=0$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\lfloor 1/x\rfloor}}\left(2-\?\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}\right)\right)&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

The interrobang function has a similar, but more complicated, definition. We start by defining the interrobang function on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
; we will later extend it to the whole interval
[0,1]
in
Theorem

7.13
.

Definition 7.4
.

The
interrobang function

\interro\colon[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined recursively as follows:

(15)

\interro(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $x=0$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{\lfloor 1/x\rfloor}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}\right)\right)&\mbox{if $0<x\leq\frac{1}{2}$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x)&\mbox{if $\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 1$}\end{cases}.

Note that this recursion is well founded by height. That is, for all
x\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
,

if
0<x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then
\height\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}\right)<\height(x)
; and

if
\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 1
, then
\height\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)<\height(x)
and
\height(1-x)<\height(x)
.

Thus, (
15
) defines a unique function
\interro\colon[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Minkowski’s question-mark function is known to have the following remarkable properties: it is strictly increasing, it can be extended to a continuous function on the real interval
[0,1]
, it maps rational numbers to dyadic rational numbers, and it maps quadratic irrational numbers to rational numbers
[
29
, Section 4]
. In what follows, we will prove that the interrobang function enjoys these properties as well.

A plot of the interrobang function.

7.1.
Basic properties

Lemma 7.5
.

We have
\interro(0)=0
and
\interro(1)=3/8
. Moreover,
\interro(1/n)=1/4^{n}
for all
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

This follows directly from
Definition

7.4
.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
x
be a rational number satisfying
{1/(n+1)\leq x\leq 1/n}
.

(1)

If
n>1
, then

\interro(x)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)\right).

(2)

If
n=1
, then

\interro(x)=\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x).

Proof.

If
1/(n+1)<x\leq 1/n
, then this is exactly (
15
). Otherwise, we have
x=1/(n+1)
, and the lemma easily follows from
Lemma

7.5
.
∎

7.2.
Analytic properties

In this subsection, we prove that
\interro
extends to a continuous, increasing function on the real interval
[0,1]
.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. For all rational numbers
x
satisfying
{1/(n+1)<x<1/n}
, we have
\interro(1/(n+1))<\interro(x)<\interro(1/n)
.

Proof.

We use strong induction on
\height(x)
. If
n>1
, then by the inductive hypothesis, we have

0<\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)<\interro(1)=\frac{3}{8}.

Hence, by
Lemma

7.6
, the function value

\interro(x)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)\right)

satisfies

\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\cdot\frac{3}{8}\right)<\interro(x)<\frac{1}{4^{n}}\cdot(1-2\cdot 0).

By
Lemma

7.5
, this simplifies to the desired inequality.

On the other hand, if
n=1
, then by the inductive hypothesis, we have

0<\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)<\interro(1)=\frac{3}{8}

and

0<\interro(1-x)<\interro\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{16}.

Hence, by (
15
), the function value

\interro(x)=\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x)

satisfies

\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{8}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{16}<\interro(x)<\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\cdot 0-\frac{1}{2}\cdot 0.

By
Lemma

7.5
, this simplifies to the desired inequality.
∎

Proposition 7.8
.

The function
\interro
is strictly increasing on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. That is, for all rational numbers
x,y
with
0\leq x<y\leq 1
, we have
\interro(x)<\interro(y)
.

Proof.

We use induction on
\height(x)+\height(y)
. If
x=0
or if there exists a positive integer
n
such that
x\leq 1/n\leq y
, then the proposition follows from
Lemma

7.7
.

Otherwise, there exists a positive integer
n
such that
1/(n+1)<x\leq y<1/n
. If
n>1
, then we have

\interro\left(\frac{1}{y}-n\right)<\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)

by the inductive hypothesis. Hence, by
Lemma

7.6
, we have

\interro(x)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)\right)<\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\frac{1}{y}-n\right)\right)=\interro(y),

as desired.

On the other hand, if
n=1
, then we have

\interro\left(\frac{1}{y}-1\right)<\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)

and

\interro\left(1-y\right)<\interro\left(1-x\right)

by the inductive hypothesis. Hence, by
Lemma

7.6
, we have

\interro(x)=\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x)<\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{y}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-y)=\interro(y),

as desired.
∎

Our next goal is to prove that
\interro
is uniformly continuous on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. We start by introducing the following definition.

Definition 7.9
.

Let
a\leq b
be real numbers, let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
. For
\epsilon>0
, we say that
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated
on
[a,b]
if there exist
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in T
such that

a=x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n}=b

and
\left\lvert f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert<\epsilon
for
0\leq i<n
.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
a\leq b
be real numbers, let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
. If
f
is monotone (increasing or decreasing) on
[a,b]\cap T
and
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
for all
\epsilon>0
, then
f
is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]\cap T
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
. We wish to prove that there exists
\delta>0
such that
\left\lvert f(x)-f(y)\right\rvert<\epsilon
for all
x,y\in[a,b]\cap T
satisfying
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert<\delta
.

By assumption,
f
is
(\epsilon/2)
-saturated on
[a,b]
, which means that there exist
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in T
such that
a=x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n}=b
and
\left\lvert f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert<\epsilon/2
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. We may assume, by replacing
\epsilon
with a smaller constant, that
n>1
. Let

\delta=\min\{x_{i+1}-x_{i}\,|\,0\leq i<n\}.

Then, for all
x,y\in[a,b]\cap T
with
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert<\delta
, there exists
i
such that
x,y\in[x_{i},x_{i+2}]
. Since
f
is monotone, this implies that

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(x)-f(y)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert f(x_{i+2})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert f(x_{i+2})-f(x_{i+1})\right\rvert+\left\lvert f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon,

as desired.
∎

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
a\leq b\leq c
be real numbers, let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
f,g\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
be functions. Let
\epsilon>0
, and let
C
be a real constant.

(i)

If
\left\lvert f(b)-f(a)\right\rvert<\epsilon
, then
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
.

(ii)

If
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
, then
f
is
\epsilon^{\prime}
-saturated on
[a,b]
for all
\epsilon^{\prime}>\epsilon
.

(iii)

If
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
, then
f+C
is also
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
.

(iv)

If
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
, then
Cf
is
(\left\lvert C\right\rvert\epsilon)
-saturated on
[a,b]
.

(v)

If
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on both
[a,b]
and
[b,c]
, then
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,c]
.

(vi)

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
h\colon U\to T
be a monotone (increasing or decreasing) function. If
f\circ h
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
, then
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[h(a),h(b)]
.

(vii)

Suppose that
f
and
g
are each either monotone increasing or monotone decreasing on
[a,b]\cap T
. Let
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. If
f
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[a,b]
and
g
is
\epsilon^{\prime}
-saturated on
[a,b]
, then
f+g
is
(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})
-saturated on
[a,b]
.

Proof.

We prove
Lemma 7.11vii
; the other parts are trivial. By
Definition

7.9
, there exist
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m},y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}
such that

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=x_{0}

\displaystyle<\cdots

\displaystyle<x_{m}

\displaystyle=b

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=y_{0}

\displaystyle<\cdots

\displaystyle<y_{n}

\displaystyle=b

and such that
\left\lvert f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert<\epsilon
for
i=0,\ldots,m-1
and
\left\lvert g(y_{j+1})-g(y_{j})\right\rvert<\epsilon^{\prime}
for
j=0,\ldots,n-1
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{p}
be the elements of the set
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{m},y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}\}
in increasing order. Then,

a=z_{0}<\cdots<z_{p}=b.

Moreover, for all
k
with
0\leq k<p
, there exists
i
such that
z_{k},z_{k+1}\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
, and there exists
j
such that
z_{k},z_{k+1}\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]
. Since
f
and
g
are monotone, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lvert(f+g)(z_{k+1})-(f+g)(z_{k})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert f(z_{k+1})-f(z_{k})\right\rvert+\left\lvert g(z_{k+1})-g(z_{k})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i})\right\rvert+\left\lvert g(y_{j+1})-g(y_{j})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime},

so
f+g
is
(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})
-saturated on
[a,b]
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 7.12
.

The function
\interro
is uniformly continuous on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

We claim that for every nonnegative integer
N
, the function
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
and
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
. We prove this claim by induction on
N
. In the base case
N=0
, the claim follows from
Lemma 7.11i
.

For the inductive step, suppose that
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
and
{(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})}
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
. We wish to prove that
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
and
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
. Note that the inductive hypothesis implies, by
Lemma 7.11ii
and
Lemma 7.11v
, that
\interro
is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1]
.

First, we prove that
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
. By
Lemma 7.11v
, it suffices to show that
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[0,1/(N+2)]
, as well as on
[1/(n+1),1/n]
for
2\leq n<N+2
. For the former,
Lemma

7.5
yields

\left\lvert\interro\left(\frac{1}{N+2}\right)-\interro(0)\right\rvert=\frac{1}{4^{N+2}}<\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N+1},

so
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[0,1/(N+2)]
by
Lemma 7.11i
. For the latter, recall from
Lemma

7.6
that for all
n\geq 2
and all
x\in[1/(n+1),1/n]
, we have

(16)

\interro(x)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)\right).

Since
\interro
is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1]
, it follows from
Lemma 7.11vi
that, as a function of
x
,

\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-n\right)

is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[1/(n+1),1/n]
.
By
Lemma 7.11iii
and
Lemma 7.11iv
and (
16
), it follows that
\interro
is
(2/4^{n}\cdot 4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[1/(n+1),1/n]
. Since
n\geq 2
, we have

\frac{2}{4^{n}}\cdot 4\cdot\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N}<\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N+1},

so by
Lemma 7.11ii
,
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[1/(n+1),1/n]
, as desired.

To complete the induction, it remains to prove that
\interro
is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N+1})
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
. Recall that by
Lemma

7.6
, we have

(17)

\interro(x)=\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x)

for all
x\in[1/2,1]
.
Since
\interro
is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1]
, it follows from
Lemma 7.11vi
that, as a function of
x
,

\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)

is
(4\cdot(7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
. Similarly, since
\interro
is
((7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
, it follows from
Lemma 7.11vi
that, as a function of
x
,

\interro\left(1-x\right)

is
((7/8)^{N})
-saturated on
[1/2,1]
.

By
Lemma 7.11iii
,
Lemma 7.11iv
and
Lemma 7.11vii
and (
17
), it follows that
\interro
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[0,1/2]
, where

\epsilon=\frac{3}{4}\cdot 4\cdot\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N}.

This equals
4\cdot(7/8)^{N+1}
, so the induction is complete, proving the claim.

Since

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}4\cdot\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{N}=0,

we conclude that
\interro
is
\epsilon
-saturated on
[0,1]
for all
\epsilon>0
. By
Lemma

7.10
,
\interro
is uniformly continuous on
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
, as desired.
∎

Theorem 7.13
.

The function
\interro
extends uniquely to a continuous, strictly increasing function
\interro\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the functional equation

\interro(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $x=0$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{\lfloor 1/x\rfloor}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}\right)\right)&\mbox{if $0<x\leq\frac{1}{2}$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x)&\mbox{if $\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 1$}\end{cases}

from (
15
) holds for all
x\in[0,1]
(not necessarily rational).

Proof.

This follows from
Propositions

7.8
and
7.12
and the fact that
[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
is dense in
[0,1]
.
∎

Dushistova and Moshchevitin proved in 2010 that Minkowski’s question-mark function is differentiable almost everywhere and that its derivative is zero almost everywhere
[
14
]
. We conjecture that the interrobang function has this same property.

Conjecture 7.14
.

For
x\in[0,1]
outside of a set of measure zero,
\interro
is differentiable at
x
and
\interro^{\prime}(x)=0
.

7.3.
Arithmetic properties

Like Minkowski’s question-mark function, the interrobang function maps rational numbers to dyadic rational numbers.

Proposition 7.15
.

For all
x\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
, we have that
\interro(x)
is an explicitly computable dyadic rational number.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
Definition

7.4
.
∎

Unlike Minkowski’s question-mark function, however, it does not appear that
\interro
maps rational numbers to dyadic rational numbers bijectively.

Conjecture 7.16
.

There exists a dyadic rational number
y\in[0,3/8]
such that
\interro^{-1}(y)
is irrational.

Using a computer search, the authors have verified that neither
\interro^{-1}(1/8)=0.61242994\ldots
nor
\interro^{-1}(1/4)=0.61834758\ldots
can be written as a fraction
A/B
, where
A
and
B
are integers with
0<A\leq B\leq 100000
. This provides some computational evidence for
Conjecture

7.16
. In fact, we conjecture the following stronger statement.

Conjecture 7.17
.

The number
\interro^{-1}\left(1/8\right)=0.61242994\ldots
is transcendental.

We now prepare to prove that if
x\in[0,1]
is a quadratic irrational, then
\interro(x)
is rational. For this, we will collect some properties of Minkowski’s question-mark function and recall the classical Levy–Desplanques theorem.

Lemma 7.18

(
[
29
, Section 4]
)
.

Minkowski’s question-mark function satisfies the following properties for all
x\in[0,1]
.

(i)

The functional equation

\?(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $x=0$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\lfloor 1/x\rfloor}}\left(2-\?\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}\right)\right)&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

from
Definition

7.3
holds (even if
x
is irrational).

(ii)

If
x
is a quadratic irrational, then
\?(x)
is an explicitly computable rational number.

(iii)

We have
\?(1-x)=1-\?(x)
.

(iv)

The function
\?
is strictly increasing and maps
[0,1]
to
[0,1]
bijectively.

Definition 7.19
.

Let
A
be a square matrix with real entries. We say that
A
is
diagonally dominant
if

\left\lvert A_{i,i}\right\rvert>\sum_{j\neq i}\left\lvert A_{i,j}\right\rvert

for all
i
.

Theorem 7.20

(Levy–Desplanques Theorem,
[
20
, Corollary 5.6.17]
)
.

If a matrix is diagonally dominant, then it is invertible.

Theorem 7.21
.

Let
x\in[0,1]
be a quadratic irrational. Then,
\interro(x)
is an explicitly computable rational number.

Proof.

By
Lemma 7.18ii
,
\?(x)
is rational. Let

be the denominator of
\?(x)
. For
0\leq i\leq n
, let

, which exists by
Lemma 7.18iv
. We know that
x\in\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Our strategy will be to construct a system of linear equations with rational coefficients that has
(\interro(x_{0}),\ldots,\interro(x_{n}))
as its only solution, thus proving that
\interro(x_{0}),\ldots,\interro(x_{n})
are rational and hence that
\interro(x)
is rational.

By
Lemma 7.18iii
, we have for
0\leq i\leq n
that

(18)

1-x_{i}=x_{n-i}.

By
Lemma 7.18i
, we have for

that

\frac{i}{n}=\?(x_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}}\left(2-\?\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x_{i}}\right\}\right)\right).

Rearranging this equation yields

\?\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x_{i}}\right\}\right)=\frac{2n-2^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}i}{n},

whence
0\leq 2n-2^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}i\leq n
and

(19)

\left\{\frac{1}{x_{i}}\right\}=x_{2n-2^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}i}.

18
) and (
19
) to find an equation for
\interro(x_{i})
. We have three cases.

•

If

, then

and hence

(20)

\interro(x_{i})=0.

•

If

, then by

, we have

. Therefore, by (

), we have

\interro(x_{i})=\frac{1}{4^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}}\left(1-2\interro\left(\left\{\frac{1}{x_{i}}\right\}\right)\right),

which, by (

•

If

, then by

, we have

. Therefore, by (

), we have

\interro(x_{i})=\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro(1-x_{i}),

which, by (
18
) and (

), simplifies to

\interro(x_{i})=\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3}{4}\interro\left(x_{2n-2i}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\interro\left(x_{n-i}\right).

Define the matrix

and the vector

\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{if $\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}<i\leq n$ and $j=n-i$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr 0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases},

\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{\lfloor 1/x_{i}\rfloor}}&\mbox{if $0<i\leq\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}&\mbox{if $\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}<i\leq n$}\end{cases}.

Then, the three equations (

), and (

) can be rewritten in the form

A\begin{bmatrix}\interro(x_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\interro(x_{n})\end{bmatrix}=b.

Let
D
be the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
diagonal matrix with

D_{i,i}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $0\leq i\leq\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}$}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr 4&\mbox{if $\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}<i\leq n$}\end{cases}.

It can be routinely checked that
AD
is diagonally dominant. By
Theorem

7.20
,
AD
is invertible, so
A
is invertible. It follows that

\begin{bmatrix}\interro(x_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\interro(x_{n})\end{bmatrix}=A^{-1}b,

so
\interro(x_{0}),\ldots,\interro(x_{n})
are all rational. Since
x\in\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, the result follows.
∎

8.
The limiting distribution for
PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

We are now ready to describe the limiting behavior of the reduced random walk in
PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. A plot of the cumulative distribution function
F_{\zeta}
is shown in
Figure

6
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
W\simeq PGL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the Coxeter group generated by the set
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
of simple reflections, where
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
are the following transformations of the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
:

\displaystyle s_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=-1-\overline{z}

\displaystyle s_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=1/\overline{z}

\displaystyle s_{3}(z)

\displaystyle=-\overline{z}.

Consider the sequence
(z_{m})_{m\geq 0}
, which was defined in
Section

4
as a geometric interpretation of the reduced random walk in
(W,S)
. The limit
\zeta=\lim_{m\to\infty}z_{m}
almost surely exists and is a real number. The cumulative distribution function
F_{\zeta}
is given by

(23)

F_{\zeta}(x)=\Pr[\zeta\leq x]=\begin{cases}\interro(-1/x)&\mbox{if $x\leq-2$}\\
1/4-\interro(-x-1)/2&\mbox{if $-2\leq x\leq-1$}\\
1/4+\interro(-1-1/x)/2+\interro(x+1)/2&\mbox{if $-1\leq x\leq-1/2$}\\
1/2-\interro(-x)/2&\mbox{if $-1/2\leq x\leq 0$}\\
1/2+\interro(x)&\mbox{if $0\leq x\leq 1$}\\
7/8+\interro(x-1)/4&\mbox{if $1\leq x\leq 2$}\\
1-\interro(1/x)/2&\mbox{if $2\leq x$}\end{cases}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\interro
is the interrobang function (
Theorem

7.13
). In particular,

(1)

F_{\zeta}
is continuous and strictly increasing;

(2)

for all
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have that
F_{\zeta}(x)
is an explicitly computable dyadic rational number;

(3)

for all quadratic irrationals
x
, we have that
F_{\zeta}(x)
is an explicitly computable rational number.

Proof.

Let
f(x)
denote the right-hand side of (
23
). It can be routinely checked using
Lemma

7.5
that
f
is well defined at the boundary points
-2,-1,-1/2,0,1,2
. By
Theorem

7.13
,
f
is continuous and strictly increasing on each of the intervals
(-\infty,-2]
,
[-2,-1]
,
[-1,-1/2]
,
[-1/2,0]
,
[0,1]
,
[1,2]
,
[2,\infty)
, so it is continuous and strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
. By
Proposition

7.15
, for all
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have that
f(x)
is an explicitly computable dyadic rational number. By
Theorem

7.21
, for all quadratic irrationals
x
, we have that
f(x)
is an explicitly computable rational number.

It remains to prove that
f=F_{\zeta}
. Since
f
is continuous and strictly increasing, and since we may easily compute that
\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=1
, there exists a probability measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution function
f
. By
Theorems

4.4
and
5.1
, it suffices to show that
\mu^{\prime}
satisfies the equation

(24)

\mu^{\prime}=\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\mu^{\prime}+(\tau_{2})_{*}\mu^{\prime}+(\tau_{3})_{*}\mu^{\prime}\right),

where
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\colon\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\overline{\mathbb{H}}
are the one-way reflections of
W
. Explicitly, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-1-x&\mbox{if $x>-1/2$}\\
x&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}-\left\lvert x+\frac{1}{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1/x&\mbox{if $|x|>1$}\\
x&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tau_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-x&\mbox{if $x<0$}\\
x&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert x\right\rvert.

Verifying (
24
) is routine; the method is to use
Lemma

7.6
to show that for all
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, both sides of (
24
) assign the same measure to the set
(-\infty,x]
. The computation involves casework depending on where
x
falls among finitely many intervals. We illustrate the case
x\in[-3,-2]
and omit the details for the other cases.

Suppose that
x\in[-3,-2]
. We have

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\frac{1}{3}\left((\tau_{1})_{*}\mu^{\prime}+(\tau_{2})_{*}\mu^{\prime}+(\tau_{3})_{*}\mu^{\prime}\right)((-\infty,x])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(\mu^{\prime}(\tau_{1}^{-1}((-\infty,x]))+\mu^{\prime}(\tau_{2}^{-1}((-\infty,x]))+\mu^{\prime}(\tau_{3}^{-1}((-\infty,x]))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(\mu^{\prime}([-1-x,\infty)\cup(-\infty,x])+\mu^{\prime}(\emptyset)+\mu^{\prime}(\emptyset)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(1-f(-1-x)+f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(1-\left(\frac{7}{8}+\frac{1}{4}\interro(-2-x)\right)+\interro\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}-\frac{1}{12}\interro(-2-x)+\frac{1}{3}\interro\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right).

Since
1/3\leq-1/x\leq 1/2
, we can apply
Lemma

7.6
with
n=2
to find that

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\frac{1}{24}-\frac{1}{12}\interro(-2-x)+\frac{1}{3}\interro\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}-\frac{1}{12}\interro(-2-x)+\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{16}\left(1-2\interro\left(-2-x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}\left(1-2\interro\left(-2-x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\interro\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=f(x)

\displaystyle=\mu^{\prime}((-\infty,x]),

as desired.
∎
