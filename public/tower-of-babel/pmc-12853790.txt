Challenges, merits, and demerits of curriculum co-creation in a private sector dental college: an interventional phenomenological study from Pakistan
Authors: Fatima Tu Zahra, Rehan Ahmed Khan, Rahila Yasmin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study explores co-creation practices in a Pakistani dental college to foster quality learner-teacher relationships and promote student empowerment. Co-creation emphasizes shared decision-making between students and faculty, aiming to create a more inclusive and equitable learning environment.ObjectivesThis study aims to identify the challenges, merits, and demerits faced during the co-creation process in a dental college, and how these factors influence learner-teacher relationships and student empowerment.MethodsThematic analysis was carried out using NVivo software. Using a qualitative interventional phenomenological research design, interviews were conducted with eleven participants, including six dental undergraduate students and five dental college faculty members who actively participated in the co-creation process.ResultsThe findings reveal that both teachers and students valued the cocreation process, recognizing its potential to enhance student engagement and motivation. However, challenges such as balancing creativity with academic rigor and managing power dynamics were identified.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of carefully managing co-creation initiatives to ensure their effectiveness in hierarchical educational settings like those found in Pakistan. This study contributes to the growing body of literature on co-creation in higher education by offering insights specific to the Pakistani context. It also provides a framework for new researchers to explore co-creation in similar cultural settings, emphasizing the need for contextual adaptation. Introduction

Co-creation is a collaborative process where students and teachers create instructional content [1, 2]. This concept is established in higher education and is part of a broader pedagogical approach that continuously questions and improves educational theories and practices. It involves students as equal partners in the educational process, encouraging their active participation in designing and implementing the curriculum [3].

The primary purpose of co-creation is to enhance student involvement in learning [4]. It is closely related to participatory design, which includes collaboration with all stakeholders to improve educational quality. Co-creation aims to engage students directly, while participatory design focuses on overall educational innovation for both teachers and learners [5].

The involvement of students in educational design can be seen as a ladder, with each rung representing increasing levels of engagement and responsibility. A learner-teacher partnership is a collaborative and reciprocal process where participants contribute equally to various aspects of education, such as curriculum development, decision-making, and analysis [6]. The most persuasive reason for developing a pedagogical partnership program is to empower participants and support their development [7].

For many institutions, embracing co-creation represents a significant cultural shift. This shift moves from a top-down approach, where administrators and faculty make decisions, to a collaborative approach, where students work as colleagues and partners [8]. This partnership model has demonstrated positive benefits, including empowering participants and enhancing educational outcomes [9].

Implementing co-creation can be challenging, particularly in cultures with hierarchical views of teacher-student relationships. For example, in Eastern cultures influenced by Confucianism, the hierarchical view of teachers as absolute authorities can hinder the adoption of partnership approaches [10]. However, reframing cultural values to emphasize that anyone can be a teacher can support the integration of co-creation [11].

Despite the growing interest in co-creation in educational settings globally, there is a notable lack of research examining this approach within the context of Pakistani higher education, particularly in medical and dental colleges [12]. The traditional hierarchical teacher-student relationship prevalent in Pakistan poses unique challenges to the implementation of co-creation [13]. These dynamics, coupled with cultural factors such as respect for authority and reluctance to challenge teachers, make it crucial to explore how co-creation can be adapted to and effective in this context [14, 15].

Internationally, studies have shown that co-creation can significantly enhance student engagement and motivation [1, 16]. However, research conducted in Asian contexts, such as China and Malaysia, suggests that cultural factors can influence the success of co-creation initiatives [10]. In Pakistan, the hierarchical nature of teacher-student relationships presents both challenges and opportunities for co-creation [13]. This study builds on these findings by exploring how co-creation can be adapted to the unique cultural and educational context of Pakistan, thereby contributing to the global discourse on participatory learning.

Methodology and methods

The study was conducted using an interventional phenomenological methodology, and the following steps were followed during the study.

Formation of the Course Designing Team (CDT)

The Course Designing Team (CDT) was formed by selecting students and faculty members based on specific criteria. For students, the criteria included academic excellence in relevant coursework, interest, willingness to engage in the redesign process, communication and collaboration skills, creativity, and critical thinking abilities. Faculty members were selected based on their subject matter expertise, teaching experience, commitment, and leadership qualities. Recruitment strategies involved emailing potential team members about the course redesign project and inviting them to participate.

Participants of the CDT

The CDT for this study included 16 participants, eight second-year BDS students, three third-year BDS students, three faculty members specialized in the research, one course coordinator from the first-year BDS program, and one faculty member from the Dental Education Department.

Orientation workshop

An orientation workshop was conducted for CDT members to familiarize them with medical and dental education co-creation principles. The workshop had two sessions:

Session 1: Explained the concept of co-creation, and its relevance to education, and encouraged discussions on collaborative decision-making and stakeholder involvement. Pre- and post-tests evaluated participant’s understanding.

Co-creation process for curriculum redesign

The curriculum redesign for the "Basics and Fundamentals of Research" course involved several meetings:

First meeting: Faculty and students gathered to initiate curriculum redesign. Using the Gibbs cycle, participants provided reflections that informed discussions. The focus was on redesigning the "Introduction to Research" topic, including refining learning outcomes, proposing interactive teaching methods, and reviewing assessment methods. Interviewer’s credentials

The interviewer who conducted the interviews has extensive experience in qualitative research, particularly in the field of medical education, and part of the dental faculty at the institute where the study was conducted. The interviewer was directly involved in the co-creation process with the participants, which helped in establishing rapport and trust. However, to ensure the objectivity of the research, she maintained a neutral stance during data collection, being mindful of the potential influence her dual role as both educator and researcher could have on the participants.

To minimize these biases, bracketing techniques were employed. This involved the researcher consciously setting aside her personal beliefs and experiences during the data analysis phase to ensure that the findings reflected the participant’s perspectives rather than her own.

Study population

All 16 participants of the co-creation project were invited for one-on-one interviews to share their insights and experiences. Six students and five faculty members accepted the invitation.

Sample size and selection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with six students and five faculty members of the CDT. Purposeful sampling was employed to select participants.

Participants and demographics

The study involved eleven participants, including six students and five faculty members from the dental college Course Designing Team (CDT). The participants were selected based on their active involvement in the co-creation process. The demographic details of the participants are as follows:

Students: The six student participants were in their second and third years of the Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) program. Their ages ranged from 20 to 23 years, with a gender distribution of four females and two males.

Faculty Members: The five faculty participants held qualifications in dental education and medical education, with their experience in teaching ranging from 5 to 15 years. Their ages ranged from 35 to 50 years, and the gender distribution included three females and two males.

Research instrument

The interview questions were developed following a thorough review of existing literature on co-creation and student–teacher partnerships. The process was guided by key sources such as Bovill et al. (2016), who discussed the challenges and benefits of co-creation, and Cook-Sather et al. (2019), who provided insights into participatory learning and teaching strategies.

The interview scheme used consisted of 19 questions for students and 18 questions for teachers. The interview inventory was validated by five medical educationists, and the pilot test was performed on one student and one teacher. Post-validation, the inventory was refined to 18 questions for both students and teachers.

Approval and duration

The study was conducted after receiving approval from the ethical review board of the Riphah Islamic Medical College Islamabad Pakistan and Dental College, HITEC Institute of Medical Sciences, Taxila, Pakistan.

Data collection procedure

The face-to-face interviews were conducted in a conference room within the Dental College, offering a comfortable and confidential environment for participants to openly share their experiences. Additionally, some interviews were conducted online via a Zoom video conferencing platform, accommodating participants' preferences and ensuring flexibility. Each interview, whether conducted in person or online, lasted between 30–45 min. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and interviews were audio-recoded with their permission to ensure accurate data collection. Interviews were conducted over six months, from March to August 2023.

In this study, data saturation was fully achieved after conducting interviews with six students and five faculty members. No new themes or insights emerged towards the end of the data collection process, indicating that the core experiences and perspectives of participants were thoroughly captured. This ensured that the data collected provided a comprehensive understanding of the co-creation process within the dental college context. The consistency of responses across participants further confirmed that the data was sufficient to address the research objectives effectively.

Data analysis

The data analysis followed a rigorous process that combined phenomenological reduction, bracketing, textural analysis, and structural analysis to identify themes representing the essence of participants' co-creation experiences. Audio recordings from the interviews were transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo software, where thematic analysis was carried out. The analysis began with open coding, creating nodes for emerging themes, which were then refined into sub-nodes to capture specific aspects of the data. NVivo tools were used to identify patterns and explore relationships between themes, with visual reports like word clouds and thematic maps generated to summarize the findings.

To ensure the trustworthiness of the data, several strategies were employed. Credibility was enhanced through triangulation and member checking, allowing participants to review and validate their transcripts. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study context, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to similar settings. Confirmability was achieved by maintaining an audit trail and using bracketing techniques to minimize researcher bias. Dependability was ensured through a consistent research protocol and peer debriefing sessions, which helped refine interpretations and strengthen the reliability of the findings. These comprehensive measures ensured the rigor and trustworthiness of the study’s analysis.

The study explored the experiences, challenges, and benefits of co-creation in the context of curriculum design for a "Basics and Fundamentals of Research" course in a 1st-year BDS undergraduate program. Both students and faculty members participated in designing the curriculum, and their insights highlighted several key themes.

Key themes

Perceived experience of teachers

Teachers expressed a positive attitude towards co-creation and valued student participation in the curriculum design process. They appreciated the opportunity to work collaboratively with students and learn from their perspectives. As one faculty member noted,

"We valued their perspectives and encouraged them to share their thoughts freely" (Participant 1, Faculty).

However, teachers also acknowledged the challenge of balancing student creativity with academic rigor. They recognized the importance of maintaining the academic standards of the course while incorporating innovative ideas from students. Another teacher noted:

"It was challenging to strike a balance between their ideas and the academic rigor required for the course" (Participant 3, Faculty).

Furthermore, teachers mentioned concerns about time management during co-creation activities. The collaborative nature of co-creation often involves multiple discussions and meetings with students, which could be time-consuming. A teacher shared their experience, saying:

"It took long, with all the different people involved and trying to reach an agreement and consensus" (Participant 5, Faculty).

Perceived experience of students

Students showed enthusiasm and engagement during the co-creation process. They appreciated the opportunity to actively contribute to their own learning experiences. One student expressed:

"It was a great chance to speak on behalf of the other students. I felt like an ambassador of the students" (Participant 2, Student).

However, some students felt hesitant to express their opinions fully in the presence of teachers. They might have perceived a power dynamic that inhibited their complete participation. As one student said:

"We felt a bit hesitant to express fully when faculty members were present" (Participant 3, Student).

Overall, students felt empowered and motivated by actively participating in co-creation. They valued their input's value and its impact on their learning journey.

Perceived merits from teachers

Teachers identified that co-creation enhanced student engagement and motivation. Students become more invested in their education when actively involved in the curriculum design process. As one teacher observed:

"Co-creation enhances student engagement and motivation" (Participant 3, Faculty).

Teachers also appreciated gaining a better understanding of students needs through co-creation. Working closely with students allowed them to see the learning process differently. A teacher commented:

"It's a great opportunity to make a positive difference in our education" (Participant 2, Faculty).

Moreover, co-creation fostered an enhanced sense of ownership and responsibility among students. Teachers noticed that students became more proactive in their learning when they had a say in the course content and structure. A teacher shared:

"Through this co-creation, so much autonomy is given that they feel more motivated to participate in academic activities" (Participant 4, Faculty).

Perceived merits from students

Students felt empowered and valued through their active participation in co-creation. They appreciated the openness of teachers to student input and the opportunity to shape their learning experiences. As one student noted:

"Co-creation gives students a sense of ownership and empowerment over their education" (Participant 1, Student).

Students also acknowledged the development of critical analysis and potential due to co-creation. Collaborating with teachers in the curriculum design process improved their critical thinking skills. A student expressed:

"It enhanced our capabilities and developed a sense of critical analysis in our capabilities" (Participant 4, Student).

Perceived demerits from teachers

Some teachers expressed resistance to change and innovative ideas during co-creation. They might prefer traditional methods of curriculum design and be hesitant to embrace new approaches. A teacher pointed out:

"Some teachers might be resistant to change and prefer traditional methods of curriculum design" (Participant 4, Faculty).

Teachers also mentioned concerns about imbalanced power dynamics in co-creation. Sometimes, students' input might not be fully valued, leading to unequal decision-making. A teacher said:

"Sometimes, they felt that they had the power to decide everything and did not value student input in the co-creation process" (Participant 1, Faculty).

Furthermore, teachers worried about compromising academic quality while trying out new ideas. They had to ensure that innovative elements did not compromise the educational standards of the course.

Perceived demerits from students

Students highlighted cultural barriers to challenging teachers' ideas during co-creation. In some educational settings, students might be hesitant to express disagreement openly with faculty members. A student shared:

“Some students seemed hesitant to speak up fully when faculty members were present" (Participant 5, Student).

Moreover, students expressed struggles in reaching consensus due to diverse perspectives. Co-creation involves collaboration between students with different backgrounds and ideas, leading to disagreements. A student explained:

"There were some disagreements, especially when it came to deciding the final content and structure of the curriculum" (Participant 6, Student).

Students also worried about not all voices being equally heard during the co-creation process. Ensuring the inclusivity and involvement of all students was a challenge. They wanted to make sure every student's input was given equal importance.

Perceived challenges from students

Students faced challenges in balancing creativity with academic requirements during co-creation. They aimed to integrate innovative ideas while adhering to the academic demands of the course. A student said:

“We had to figure out how to mix cool new ideas with the serious academic stuff" (Participant 6, Student).

Additionally, students worked on overcoming hesitancy to express their opinions fully. Creating an inclusive environment where all students felt comfortable contributing was a priority. A student expressed:

"Some students were hesitant to speak up initially, which affected the inclusivity of the process" (Participant 2, Student).

Moreover, ensuring the inclusivity and involvement of all students in the process was challenging. Students wanted to hear from everyone and ensure no one's voice was left unheard.

Perceived challenges from teachers

Teachers encountered challenges in balancing diverse perspectives and managing time during co-creation. Incorporating different ideas while maintaining a cohesive curriculum requires careful consideration. A teacher explained:

"It was challenging to strike a balance between their ideas and the academic rigor required for the course” (Participant 3, faculty).

Time management was a significant hurdle for teachers engaged in co-creation. Balancing multiple professional roles, personal commitments, and the co-creation process demanded effective time management. A teacher shared:

"It took a long time, with all the different people involved and trying to reach an agreement and consensus” (Participant 5, Faculty).

Furthermore, teachers navigated student resistance to faculty ideas during co-creation. Students' vocal feedback could be challenging to manage, especially when it is critical. A teacher noted:

"Sometimes the students become too vocal, especially when they feel they have the free leverage to criticize” (Participant 4, Faculty).

Co-creation brought valuable benefits to teachers and students, enhancing student engagement and motivation while providing teachers with deeper insights into student needs. However, addressing challenges related to power dynamics, cultural barriers, and time management would lead to more effective co-creation experiences for all stakeholders. Summary of the findings

The study indicated that co-creation in curriculum design can significantly enhance student engagement, motivation, and learning outcomes. However, it also highlighted the importance of addressing challenges related to time management, power dynamics, resistance to change, and cultural barriers. By fostering an inclusive and collaborative learning environment, educators can better meet the needs of students and create more effective educational experiences. These findings provided valuable insights for educators, administrators, and policymakers looking to implement co-creation practices in educational settings.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to explore the process and outcomes of co-creation in curriculum design for a fundamentals of research course within a first-year BDS undergraduate program. The study involved collaboration between students and faculty members, and the findings offer critical insights into the experiences, challenges, and benefits of co-creation in the specific context of Pakistani dental colleges. These insights highlight the potential for co-creation to transform the educational landscape while also revealing the complexities involved in its implementation.

The results highlighted that both teachers and students had limited familiarity with co-creation, pointing to the need for greater exposure and understanding of this approach within Pakistani medical and dental colleges. Despite this, the participants exhibited a positive attitude towards student participation in curriculum design. This suggests that co-creation can potentially bridge the power distance between students and educators, fostering a more collaborative and inclusive learning environment. Aligning with the study's objectives, these findings emphasize the importance of encouraging student voices and active listening from educators to make co-creation effective.

However, several challenges were identified that must be addressed to optimize the co-creation process. Time management emerged as a significant issue, with participants struggling to balance the demands of co-creation with their other academic responsibilities. This challenge is consistent with findings from studies which also observed difficulties in maintaining academic rigor while integrating innovative ideas in co-creation efforts [19]. To manage time constraints, it is recommended to implement structured timelines, clear objectives, and flexible scheduling that accommodates the commitments of both students and faculty.

Power dynamics also posed a challenge, particularly in a context where hierarchical relationships are deeply ingrained. These finding echoes similar challenges observed in studies from other Asian contexts, who noted that cultural factors significantly impact the success of co-creation initiatives [10]. Addressing these dynamics requires establishing clear roles and responsibilities and employing facilitated sessions led by neutral parties to ensure that all voices are heard equally. Additionally, training in collaborative leadership for both students and faculty could help mitigate these power imbalances and foster a more balanced and inclusive environment.

A significant finding of this study was the sense of empowerment and ownership experienced by students through their involvement in co-creation. This aligns with the broader literature on student-centered learning, where involving students in decision-making processes leads to increased engagement and motivation [1]. To enhance these outcomes, it is crucial to provide students with continuous opportunities to contribute meaningfully to their educational experiences.

Moreover, the study revealed a potential for enhancing learner-teacher relationships through co-creation, as faculty openness to student input was highly appreciated by students. This finding suggests that fostering a culture of mutual respect and collaboration can significantly improve the educational environment. Comparing these findings with those from Western contexts, it is clear that while co-creation is beneficial globally, its implementation must be tailored to local cultural contexts to address unique challenges effectively [20].

The study reflects key aspects of the theoretical framework, particularly in addressing student engagement, power dynamics, and stakeholder representation. While challenges like time management and power imbalances remain, our findings support the framework’s assertion that addressing these issues through structured timelines, neutral facilitation, and collaborative leadership can lead to a more successful co-creation process in clinical education. This alignment suggests that our study not only confirms the theoretical foundation but also provides practical insights into its application in a Pakistani dental college context.

Conclusions

Based on the study findings, it is concluded that both teachers and students had positive attitudes towards co-creation, valuing collaboration and the exchange of perspectives, although some teachers preferred traditional methods. Co-creation empowered students, increasing their motivation, engagement, and critical thinking skills. Teachers gained a deeper understanding of student needs, improving curriculum design and fostering better learner-teacher relationships. Balancing innovation with academic rigor was a challenge, as was managing the time investment required for co-creation. Power dynamics and cultural norms influenced student participation, necessitating efforts to ensure inclusivity and adapt practices to local contexts. Despite some resistance to change among faculty, co-creation has the potential to bridge power gaps, enhance student involvement, and improve educational quality.

The findings demonstrate that co-creation, as a pedagogical strategy, not only empowers students but also shifts the traditional power dynamics between learners and teachers, aligning with the theoretical framework of the study.

Recommendations

To enhance co-creation in higher education, conduct long-term impact assessments, provide institutional support and training, ensure diversity and inclusion, and recognize faculty engagement. Encourage student leadership, involve stakeholders, and develop robust assessment tools. Explore co-creation across various disciplines and cultural contexts. Promote faculty development, cultural sensitivity, technology integration, and interdisciplinary co-creation. Scale up initiatives and ensure sustainability.

To address the challenges identified in this study, several key strategies are recommended. First, implementing structured timelines and clear objectives can help manage time constraints, ensuring that the co-creation process progresses smoothly. Flexible scheduling that accommodates both faculty and student commitments is also crucial. Second, addressing power dynamics requires establishing clear roles and responsibilities, supported by facilitated sessions to ensure that all voices are valued equally. Providing training on collaborative leadership and communication can further promote an inclusive environment. Finally, given the cultural context, creating safe spaces for open dialogue is essential to encourage student participation without fear of repercussion, helping to reduce the perceived power distance between students and faculty.

Limitations of the study

The study's small sample size, contextual specificity, and potential biases limit external validity. The lack of a control group and subjective measures may affect findings. Future research should explore diverse settings, consider other stakeholders, and assess long-term effects on learning outcomes and faculty practices to better understand co-creation's potential.
