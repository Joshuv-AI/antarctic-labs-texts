Clinical-Epidemiological, Trichoscopic, and Light Microscopic Evaluation of Pediatric Hair and Scalp Disorders: A Cross-Sectional Study from a Tertiary Care Hospital
Authors: Taiba Trumboo, Yasmeen Jabeen Bhat, Obeid Shafi, Shafat Ahmad Tak, Saika Reyaz, Iffat Hassan Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric hair and scalp disorders encompass a diverse array of conditions, requiring a systematic approach for accurate diagnosis. Incorporating trichoscopic and light microscopic examinations can enhance diagnostic accuracy.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical-epidemiological, trichoscopic, and light microscopic features of hair and scalp disorders in the pediatric population.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted involving pediatric patients aged 0–12 years seeking medical attention for hair and scalp conditions. Trichoscopic and light microscopic examinations were used for diagnosis, and the agreement between these modalities was assessed with Cohen’s kappa coefficient.ResultsA total of 220 children were examined, with a median age of 7 years and male-to-female ratio of 0.9:1. The median duration of illness was four months, and median age at disease onset was 5.75 years. The most prevalent conditions observed were scalp infections, followed by non-infectious scaly scalp dermatosis and alopecia areata. Rare trichoscopic findings included “matchstick hair” in Netherton syndrome, trichoclasis in alopecia areata, trichoscopic features of transient neonatal hair loss, and new rare variants of tinea capitis, including alopecia areata-like, seborrheic dermatitis-like, and follicular pustular presentations. High agreement was found between trichoscopy and clinical diagnosis (Cohen’s kappa=0.917) as well as between trichoscopy and light microscopy (Cohen’s kappa=0.769).ConclusionThe findings underscore the significance of incorporating trichoscopy and light microscopy for diagnosing pediatric hair and scalp disorders, including rare genetic hair disorders. High agreement observed between trichoscopy, clinical diagnosis, and light microscopy emphasizes their utility as reliable noninvasive diagnostic tools in pediatric dermatology.Keywords: Pediatric dermatology, Trichoscopy, Light microscopy, Hair and scalp disorders, Trichogram

Introduction

Pediatric hair and scalp disorders present a complex clinical challenge and can serve as external markers for underlying systemic and psychological conditions, necessitating a comprehensive diagnostic and management approach. These disorders encompass a diverse spectrum, spanning congenital to acquired conditions, and often share overlapping clinical features, resulting in diagnostic uncertainty. Because invasive procedures such as scalp biopsy are limited in children due to age, cooperation, and parental concerns, reliance on noninvasive diagnostic modalities becomes essential [1].

Trichoscopy has emerged as a practical, accessible, and pediatric-friendly tool that enhances clinical evaluation by allowing visualization of characteristic patterns associated with infections, inflammatory scalp dermatoses, and hair shaft abnormalities [2]. Light microscopy complements the assessment, aiding in the evaluation of scalp infections, congenital or acquired hair shaft abnormalities, and hair cycle disturbances [3].

Despite the utility of trichoscopy and light microscopy, important knowledge gaps persist. The published literature rarely evaluates trichoscopic and microscopic correlates of rare pediatric genetic hair disorders, hair cycle disorders, early scarring alopecia, or atypical variants of common conditions such as tinea capitis. Infants and young children, particularly those under two years, remain underrepresented in existing studies despite having distinct developmental hair characteristics and a high burden of congenital and transient hair conditions.

Furthermore, no prior study has examined the concordance between trichoscopic findings, clinical diagnosis, and light microscopy in pediatric populations. This gap underscores the need for a systematic evaluation framework tailored specifically to children.

Objectives

Our study aims to investigate the clinical, epidemiological, trichoscopic, and light microscopic aspects of hair and scalp disorders in children aged 0–12 years presenting to a tertiary care center, and to assess the agreement among these diagnostic modalities.

Materials and Methods

This was a hospital-based observational, cross-sectional study carried out on consecutive pediatric patients aged 0–12 years who presented to the outpatient department of dermatology from June 2020 to February 2022 with various hair and scalp related complaints. Inclusion criteria were all consecutive children aged 0–12 years with hair or scalp concerns. This age range was selected because prepubertal patients demonstrate distinct developmental hair characteristics and pediatric-specific trichoscopic patterns. Exclusion criteria included lack of parental or guardian consent and children aged 13–18 years, whose post-pubertal scalp physiology and trichoscopic features more closely resemble adult patterns.

A predesigned form was utilized to document pertinent history and examination findings. General physical, systemic, hair, scalp, and mucocutaneous examination was carried out on each patient. In specific cases, a pediatric consultant conducted additional assessments. All patients were instructed not to wash their hair and scalp for 4–5 days before being examined at the trichology clinic, where they underwent a hair pull test, wherein a group of 50–100 hairs is gently pulled, and a proportion exceeding 10% is considered positive [4]. A hair tug test was performed by tugging hair at both ends in patients suspected of hair shaft disorders to determine fragility [5]. Woods lamp examination was done in suspected cases of tinea capitis to delineate the type of infection. Due to chances of false negative KOH and mycological cultures, diagnosis of tinea capitis was made based on the clinical morphological features as well as the specific trichoscopic signs. Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) and scalp psoriasis were diagnosed based on their specific vascular patterns observed on trichoscopy [6].

Trichoscopy was performed using an AM4515ZT Dino-Lite Edge video-dermatoscope in both non-polarized and polarized modes, with magnification ranging from 20x to 220x. A 70% alcohol solution served as the contact medium; however, for evaluation of surface scaling, non-contact (dry) trichoscopy was used to improve scale visualization. The sequence of trichoscopic evaluation was hair shafts, follicular, perifollicular, and interfollicular areas. Photographs and videos were captured, and trichoscopic findings were used to make a diagnosis and compared with the clinical diagnosis. Trichogram involved plucking 60–80 hairs with a rubber-sheathed Kocher forceps, cutting the shafts 1 cm above the root sheaths and arranging them side by side for evaluation [4]. Light microscopy was employed through either hair plucking or clipping. The former method was utilized to evaluate inflammatory and hair cycle disorders, and the latter for suspected hair shaft disorders and tinea capitis. For suspected fungal infections, wet mounts with a drop of potassium hydroxide were prepared to visualize fungal spores. Scalp biopsy was selectively performed in cases of cicatricial alopecia. Relevant laboratory investigations were carried out as per the diagnosis. Metabolic screening profile was done in patients suspected of hair shaft disorders to rule out inborn errors of metabolism. Ophthalmological and neurological opinions were sought in relevant genetic hair disorders. The clinical, trichoscopic, and light microscopic diagnoses were determined independently by multiple dermatologists, each blinded to the others’ findings.

Data were analyzed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency and percentages. Agreement between trichoscopy and clinical diagnosis and between trichoscopy and light microscopy was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, with a value between 0.41 and 0.60 considered moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 considered substantial agreement, and between 0.81 and 1.0 considered as almost perfect agreement.

Results

There were 220 children included in the study, of whom 104 were male (47.27%) and 116 were female (52.73%), with a male-to-female ratio of 0.9:1. The age at presentation ranged from 0 to 12 years, with a median age of 7 years (interquartile range [IQR] = 4–11 years). The median duration of various hair and scalp disorders was four months (IQR=2–12 months). The most frequently observed disorders were scalp infections (33%), noninfectious scalp dermatosis (18%), and alopecia areata (AA) (16%). A clear age-related distribution of disorders was observed. In infants (≤12 months), seborrheic dermatitis was the most common condition (56%), followed by transient neonatal hair loss (25%). Among toddlers (1–3 years), noninfectious scaly scalp dermatoses (27%) and scalp infections (18%) were most frequently identified, with alopecia areata accounting for 13% of cases. In preschool children (3–5 years), scalp infections comprised 42% of diagnoses, followed by alopecia areata (24%). Among school-aged children (6–12 years), scalp infections remained common (32.5%), while noninfectious scaly scalp dermatoses accounted for 18.5% and traumatic hair disorders for 12% of cases. Alopecia areata was observed in 11% of school-aged children.

The main presenting complaints included localized hair loss (26.82%), patchy hair loss (24.5%), scalp scaling (20.4%), and itching (20%). Out of 43 cases with tinea capitis, 21% exhibited blue-green fluorescence under Woods lamp examination. Positive wet mount (KOH) results were observed in 60.4% of cases, while culture positivity was seen in 76.6% of cases. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of pediatric hair and scalp disorders, which pose a significant clinical challenge and necessitate a holistic approach to diagnosis and management. Scalp infections, noninfectious scalp dermatosis, and alopecia areata were the most frequently observed disorders; however, their distribution varied meaningfully by age group, reflecting underlying developmental, biological, and behavioral factors. Infants most commonly presented with seborrheic dermatitis and transient neonatal hair loss, consistent with the surge in sebaceous activity in early infancy and the synchronized hair cycling and friction-related shedding typical of this age. Toddlers demonstrated higher frequencies of noninfectious scaly scalp dermatoses and early onset alopecia areata, patterns plausibly associated with epidermal barrier maturation, early atopic tendencies, and the known early childhood onset of autoimmune alopecia. Preschool children exhibited the highest burden of scalp infections, likely reflecting greater environmental exposure and close contact in group settings. Among school-aged children, scalp infections continued to be prominent, accompanied by an increase in traumatic hair disorders, which may be related to grooming practices, tight hairstyles, physical activity, and conscious or subconscious hair manipulation behaviors. Trichoscopy demonstrated strong concordance with both clinical diagnosis and light microscopy, underscoring its value as a noninvasive tool for accurately diagnosing and managing pediatric hair and scalp disorders. It was particularly helpful in differentiating conditions with overlapping presentations such as tinea capitis versus alopecia areata, hair shaft abnormalities versus hair cycle disturbances, and noninfectious scaly dermatoses such as seborrheic dermatitis and psoriasis.

Tinea capitis was the most common scalp infection, with a median onset age of 6 years (IQR=5–8 years). The predominant subtypes were gray patch, black dot, and kerion, which corroborated with a prior study, where noninflammatory tinea capitis was more common [1]. Other less common patterns like AA-like, SD-like tinea capitis, and follicular pustular variants were also observed in our study. We also noted specific trichoscopic features like corkscrew hairs (intensely coiled hair) in black dot variant and bent hairs, morse code hairs (hair shafts with alternating light and dark transverse bands), and zigzag (angularly bent) hairs in the gray patch variant. Trichophyton tonsurans was the most frequently isolated pathogen, consistent with prior regional patterns influenced by strain distribution, environment, and host immunity [7]. In addition, trichoscopy enabled identification of secondary cicatricial alopecia in cases of inflammatory tinea capitis, highlighting its value in detecting early scarring changes that may otherwise be missed clinically.

Median onset age of noninfectious scaly scalp dermatosis was 5 years (IQR=0.16–10 years). Trichoscopy differentiated SD and scalp psoriasis based on the specific vascular pattern [6]. Trichogram in scalp psoriasis revealed dystrophic and telogen hair roots, similar to findings described by Moodi F et al [8].

In patients with AA, patchy AA was the most common, followed by ophiasis, alopecia totalis, reticulate AA, alopecia universalis, and diffuse AA. Trichoscopic features in Table 1 align with previous findings [9]. Additional observations included coudability hair (long hairs with tapered proximal ends), Pohl-Pinkus constriction (localized narrowing of hair shaft), tulip hair (light colored hair with dark distal end), trichoclasis (greenstick fracture of the hair shaft), and the “craters of the moon sign” (multiple yellow dots), a marker of disease severity [10]. Trichogram findings of telogen and dystrophic anagen hairs, attributed to lymphocytic infiltration of anagen follicles, were consistent with prior studies [8].

Patients presenting with congenital hair complaints were evaluated for genetic hair shaft disorders. In Netherton syndrome (Figure 1A–B), trichoscopy revealed “matchstick hair,” a novel finding referring to a broken hair shaft with a bulging end, along with single follicular units (SFU), and interfollicular scales on a pinkish background. Most common trichoscopic features in ectodermal dysplasia (Figure 1C–D) were 180° twisting of hair shaft (pili torti), hypotrichosis, SFU, and heterogeneity in pigmentation, consistent with previous observations by Rakowska et al. [11]. Microscopy showed a predominance of telogen hairs and non-uniform shafts. In woolly hair (Figure 2A–B), microscopy confirmed shaft irregularities and a reduced anagen ratio of 5:1, compared to the 4.2:1 ratio observed by Gomes et al. [12]. In uncombable hair syndrome, microscopy revealed longitudinal grooves and flattened shafts (Figure 2C). In Griscelli syndrome type 3, trichoscopy showed lightly pigmented hair shafts and microscopy demonstrated irregular clumps of melanin (Figure 2D), similar to a prior report [13].

In traumatic hair disorders the median age at onset was 9.5 years (IQR=7–11.5 years) Traction alopecia was the most common due to the braids, clutchers, and head coverings in our patients. On trichoscopy, flambeau sign, referring to linear white tracks at the base of the hair shaft, was seen in 40% cases (Figure 3A). In trichotillomania, we observed trichoscopic signs as previously described by Rakowska et al. (Figure 3B) [14]. Trichogram showed distal end showing clear cut tip and trichoptillosis since the regrowing hair is blunt tipped instead of pointed, as described by Moodi F et al. [8]. In trichoteiromania, broom hairs (broken hairs with brush-like end) representing continuous frictional damage due to rubbing, considered as a trichoscopic feature specific to this condition, were observed (Figure 3C) [15].

Trichoscopic findings of transient neonatal hair loss (TNHL) have been described by Neri et al., who reported reduced hair density and diffuse shaft thinning [16]. In our cohort, we similarly observed vellus hairs and reduced density, consistent with their observations. In addition, we identified isolated yellow dots and dirty dots: findings that may reflect neonatal sebaceous activity or physiologic telogen shedding (Figure 3D) [17]. In patients suspected of loose anagen syndrome, trichogram revealed a ruffled cuticle resembling a “floppy sock” (ruffled, irregular cuticle at proximal hair shaft) and also an increased A:T ratio (9:1), indicating misshapen telogen bulbs (Figure 4A) [18]. Light microscopy confirmed the diagnosis of telogen effluvium by revealing predominantly telogen hairs (>25%) and reduced A:T ratio of 2:1, and that of short anagen syndrome by revealing predominance of telogen hairs with tipped ends, indicating that the hair had never been cut (Figure 4B) [8]. Anagen effluvium after chemotherapy showed trichoscopic findings of white hairs, tulipoid hair (normal colored hair with constricted distal end ending in a dark tip), tapered hair, and Pohl-Pinkus constrictions (Figure 4C). Trichogram showed dystrophic anagen hairs resulting from impaired hair follicle mitosis due to chemotherapy (Figure 4D) [19]. Trichoscopy in congenital triangular alopecia showed a carpet of vellus hairs (dense collection of short, thin, lightly pigmented vellus hairs), a feature that can help us to differentiate it from alopecia areata (Figure 5A–B). In our study, we did not observe the visible elongated hair bulbs in aplasia cutis congenita, due to crusts (Figure 5C–D) [20].

Trichoscopy’s reliability in comparison to clinical findings was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. The trichoscopic diagnosis showed a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.917, indicating almost perfect agreement with the provisional clinical diagnosis. Trichoscopy proved valuable in accurately diagnosing conditions with overlapping clinical features such as tinea capitis and alopecia areata, hair shaft disorders and hair cycle disturbances, and scalp psoriasis and seborrheic dermatitis. The Cohen’s kappa for trichoscopic and light microscopic diagnosis was 0.769, indicating substantial agreement. Light microscopy was particularly useful in diagnosing hair cycle disorders, while for hair shaft disorders, it was equally diagnostic as trichoscopy.

Based on our findings, several practical implications emerge for clinicians. First, trichoscopy should be considered a frontline tool for children presenting with patchy hair loss, scalp scaling, or suspected hair shaft disorders as it enables rapid differentiation between common mimickers. Second, light microscopy may be reserved for suspected hair shaft defects, hair cycle disturbances, or cases with inconclusive trichoscopic findings. Third, early detection of cicatricial alopecia (including secondary scarring in inflammatory tinea capitis) is critical as prompt treatment may prevent permanent hair loss and long-term cosmetic consequences. Early referral to dermatology is advisable when trichoscopy reveals uncommon patterns, hair shaft abnormalities suggestive of genetic disorders, or when scalp infections are severe or refractory Finally, a structured workflow (clinical assessment → trichoscopy → targeted light microscopy when needed) may help reduce diagnostic delays and optimize management in pediatric practice.

Limitations of this study include its single-center observational design, the limited numbers within individual diagnostic groups, the lack of a comparison cohort, and the absence of histological confirmation. Additionally, therapeutic response and follow-up patterns were not assessed, limiting inferences regarding long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

The trichoscopic and light microscopic features identified across pediatric hair and scalp disorders were highly informative and facilitated differentiation between conditions with overlapping clinical presentations. The strong agreement observed between trichoscopy, clinical diagnosis, and light microscopy highlights the reliability of these modalities as noninvasive diagnostic tools in pediatric dermatology. Given their diagnostic accuracy and non-invasiveness, trichoscopy and light microscopy can be preferred diagnostic modalities for pediatric hair and scalp disorders, offering significant utility in clinical practice.
