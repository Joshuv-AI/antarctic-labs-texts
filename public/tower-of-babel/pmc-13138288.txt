The minimally invasive grid approach for seizure localization: patient series
Authors: Megana Saripella, Hayley A Granberg, Hernan Gonzalez, Sabrina Chriqui, Kareem Khalifeh, Leena Kansal, Hoameng Ung, Shadi Dayeh, David Lee, Jerry J Shih, Arjun Khanna, Sharona Ben-Haim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEpilepsy surgery continues to advance new minimally invasive techniques for both seizure localization and treatment. Stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) is increasingly being used; however, some patients continue to require subdural electrodes for neocortical mapping. Traditional subdural grid electrodes involve medium to large craniotomies, relatively large incisions, and can be associated with morbidity. The authors describe a minimally invasive technique using a small, strip-like craniotomy and sequentially placed subdural strips to form a minimally invasive subdural grid (MIG) for seizure localization.OBSERVATIONSEight patients underwent electrode placement using a MIG in combination with stereotactic depth electrodes. Small craniotomies, typically positioned several centimeters away from the targeted region, allowed insertion of parallel strip electrodes under stereotactic navigation to create a grid-like array. Postoperative imaging confirmed electrode positioning and allowed for adjustments.LESSONSThe MIG technique successfully localized epileptogenic zones without major complications and was effectively used for stimulation-based mapping. No patient developed subdural fluid collection over the grid site, hemorrhage, or significant CSF leakage. The MIG technique significantly minimized the surgical incision and craniotomy size required for equivalent neocortical surface coverage. Epilepsy affects more than 50 million people worldwide and is one of the most common neurological diseases.1 While conventional antiseizure medications are effective for many, a significant portion of patients continue to experience seizures resistant to medical management.2 In certain cases of medically refractory epilepsy, resection, ablation, or modulation of the epileptogenic focus can eliminate or reduce overall seizure burden. Invasive intracranial electroencephalography (EEG), including subdural strip and grid electrodes for electrocorticography, is an important tool for definitive seizure localization and functional mapping.3 Subdural electrodes provide high-resolution seizure localization and allow for mapping of eloquent cortex, reducing postoperative neurological deficits.

Traditional cortical surface electrode grid placement typically requires craniotomies that are equal to or slightly larger than the size of the intended grid implant.4 In these larger approaches, the physical weight and rigidity of the electrodes can promote the creation of a potential space, leading to subdural fluid collections and hematomas, and in severe cases, these complications can lead to significant midline shift and/or neurological deficits requiring urgent surgical intervention.5,6 Larger incisions are also associated with higher rates of infection and significant CSF leakage, which are reported to occur in approximately 5%–10% of cases with this technique.6–15 The overall morbidity rates for this procedure can be substantial, ranging from 5% to 17%,6–15 particularly notable for a diagnostic surgical procedure.

More recently, many epilepsy centers in North America have shifted toward the stereo-EEG (sEEG) technique, which, despite including electrodes that are placed into the substance of the brain, is associated with lower overall morbidity and increased patient tolerability.3,9 Stereo-EEG is excellent for providing coverage in deep targets, including deeper cortical areas such as the archicortex of the mesial temporal lobe and insula, but remains more limited in providing neocortical surface coverage and is not optimal for performing intensive functional mapping.3,6,16–18

This case series introduces a minimally invasive grid (MIG) technique (or “neo-grid”) that utilizes a small, strip-like craniotomy to place a series of sequential subdural strips, effectively creating a grid-like framework. This approach aims to preserve the benefits of subdural EEG recordings and neocortical mapping while reducing incision and craniotomy size and decreasing the overall surgical morbidity associated with traditional large-grid craniotomies.

Study Description

Study Design and Patient Selection

A retrospective case series was conducted on 8 patients who underwent a combination of sEEG depth electrode and MIG placement at a single academic center.

Operative Technique: MIG Placement

We first proceed with placement of sEEG electrodes using the ROSA Brain Platform (Zimmer Biomet) in the typical fashion. Regions requiring subdural neocortical coverage are modeled in advance using the Brainlab software. In instances in which sEEG electrodes are to be placed in regions with subsequent planned grid coverage, attempts are made to plan the depth electrodes with cortical entry points away from the planned subdural array, and if this is not feasible, depth electrodes are planned in a sequential, linear fashion such that strips are placed around them (i.e., above and below).

Once depth electrode placement is completed, we obtain an intraoperative O-arm (Medtronic) scan for confirmation of the trajectories. Next, a curvilinear incision is made in the preplanned region, approximately 5 cm from the edge of the expected coverage regions. Incisions are planned typically either superior, posterior, or anterior to the region of coverage interest, depending on the final expected configuration of the electrodes. Burr holes are made with a matchstick drill, and a small, strip-like craniotomy is performed to accommodate the expected number of electrodes with a craniotome. The dura is subsequently opened in a linear fashion, and the underlying cortical surface is exposed. Stereotactic navigation is used to guide subdural strip electrode trajectories, when necessary. Strip electrodes are then placed in a sequential fashion using continuous irrigation, with attention to avoid regions of resistance. Electrodes are sometimes resized if distal regions of resistance are met. Attention is given to ensuring that the electrodes are placed side by side, when feasible, with the same or similar angles in an attempt to create a continuous, grid-like structure. Careful attention is given to their arrangement from anterior to posterior and medial to lateral, to optimize coverage of targeted brain regions. Intraoperative imaging is then performed with the O-arm to assess confirmation of coverage of target regions. If necessary, strip electrodes may be removed and realigned; further confirmation is performed with fluoroscopy localizer images (Fig. 1). Once placement is confirmed, the two edges of each electrode are sutured to the overlying dural leaflet with two 4-0 Nurolon sutures on each strip at the dural exit site to prevent migration. Electrodes are tunneled through the skin using a tunneler and secured with additional sutures at the skin surface.

Postimplantation radiographs showcasing depth electrode and MIG placement for patients 5 (A), 3 (B), and 4 (C).

The dura is then reapproximated, and a dural substitute (DuraGen Onlay PLUS) is placed over the dural closure under the bone flap. The craniotomy is then loosely plated and replaced, and the overlying skin is closed. A small Jackson-Pratt drain is often placed for part or all of the duration of the inpatient stay in an attempt to divert CSF and avoid CSF leakage.

Illustrative Cases

Eight patients with medically refractory epilepsy have undergone MIG placement at our institution since 2023 (Table 1). The first implant was performed in February 2023 (patient 1), and the most recent in October 2025 (patient 8). Four patients underwent cortical mapping. One patient (patient 3) subsequently received deep brain stimulation (DBS); 2 patients (patients 2 and 8) have been recommended for DBS but have not yet undergone the procedure. Patient 7 underwent expansion of his craniotomy on electrode removal for tailored cortical resection of the epileptogenic focus. Resection was offered to 1 patient (patient 1) following seizure localization, who had declined further surgery at the time of publication. Patient 5 was subsequently implanted with a responsive neurostimulation (RNS) system via the same incision site (case described below). Patient 4 underwent MR-guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT). Another patient (patient 6) is currently in the planning stages to undergo RNS via the same incision site as the MIG. All 8 cases underwent successful placement of side-by-side strip electrodes, with effective seizure localization, and functional cortical mapping, where applicable. No notable complications were noted in our series, including no instances of intracranial hemorrhage, infection, notable CSF leakage, or need for unplanned reoperation. Three of these patients (2 females, 1 male) are included in this illustrative case series.

Patient 5

Patient 5 is a 34-year-old right-handed female with migraine headaches and meconium aspiration at birth who presented with seizures at age 27. Her seizure semiology begins with an auditory aura of muffled sounds as well as a déjà vu sensation. Occasionally, she may have a visual scotoma. This may then progress to head and eye deviation to the left followed by secondary generalization. She reports at least one aura per week and may have a generalized tonic-clonic seizure every several months despite taking clobazam 10 mg twice per day (BID), lacosamide 150 mg BID, and lamotrigine 250 mg BID at the time of presentation.

Phase I presurgical evaluation included neuropsychological testing, MRI, PET, magnetoencephalography (MEG), and video-EEG. Neuropsychological testing showed possible right hemisphere dysfunction due to slightly worse visual memory performance. MRI of the brain was normal, although the PET imaging demonstrated subtle right lateral temporal hypometabolism. MEG captured epileptiform spikes predominantly over the left posterior superior temporal gyrus (STG) and left middle temporal gyrus with a second cluster over the right STG. Interictal EEG captured broadly distributed epileptiform sharp waves, maximal over the right middle temporal region, and ictal EEG was characterized by onset in the right centrotemporal and right middle temporal regions. Based on these findings, the presurgical hypothesis for the epileptogenic zone was a right temporal onset with possible bilateral or multifocal onset.

Phase II assessment involved placement of 14 sEEG electrodes—5 on the left side and 9 on the right. The patient subsequently underwent a small right-sided craniotomy, and strips were placed to cover the right lateral temporal neocortex using the MIG technique based on her phase I findings (Fig. 1A). Nine total cortical strips consisting of either 4-, 6-, 8-, or 10-contact electrodes were placed on the right side covering the inferior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, STG, inferior frontal gyrus, and occipital region. Phase II evaluation demonstrated multifocal independent interictal epileptiform discharges and multifocal seizures with independent ictal onsets in the left posterior STG, right hippocampal body, right frontotemporal region, and right superior posterior insula. The decision was made to ultimately proceed with implantation of two RNS devices (NeuroPace), which was performed several months later. On the right side, her previous MIG incision was reopened, and a craniectomy was performed in the region of her previous craniotomy. One 14-contact RNS strip electrode was placed subdurally into the right frontotemporal region where one of her previous MIG strips was placed, with no notable adhesions noted (Fig. 2). Another depth electrode was placed into the right mesial temporal region. We placed a second RNS system on the contralateral side with two depth electrodes, one in the STG (targeting the cortical portion and sulcal depth) and another in the right insular region. Placement of dual RNS systems is rare19 and was done in this case because of a clear denotation of four regions of focal onset and a combination of patient and epileptologist preference.

Patient 3

Patient 3 is a 21-year-old right-handed male with a history of in utero cytomegalovirus and toxoplasmosis exposure and autism spectrum disorder who developed seizures at age 13. His seizure semiology begins with an aura of rainbow colors in his right visual field that may progress to behavioral arrest, expressive aphasia, and forced head/eye version primarily to the right. Rarely, they may secondarily generalize. These seizures typically last 1–2 minutes, and he has roughly two or three seizures per week despite taking four antiseizure medications—clobazam 10 mg BID, oxcarbazepine 450 mg BID, lamotrigine 200 mg BID, and Epidiolex (cannabidiol) 2.5 mL BID at the time of presentation—and having previously trialed phenytoin, carbamazepine, levetiracetam, and zonisamide.

Phase I of the epilepsy surgical evaluation included neuropsychological testing, MRI, PET, MEG, and video-EEG. Neuropsychological testing suggested a dominant hemisphere malfunction with likely sparing of the mesial temporal regions. MRI noted a small focus of T2/FLAIR bright signal of unclear significance in the subcortical white matter of the left insula measuring 5 mm. An additional less prominent subtle T2/FLAIR bright signal was noted in the bilateral thalami along the right central sulcus near the left-hand locus as well as possible subtle decreased arterial spin labeling signal from the left posterior temporo-occipital region.

PET was nonlocalizing. MEG captured two clusters of epileptiform spikes in the left parieto-occipital lobe and the right middle temporal gyrus. Interictal EEG captured generalized spike-wave discharges, at times with left-sided predominance and lead-in. Ictal EEG captured multiple seizures characterized by focal rhythmic delta activity in the left posterior temporal region. Based on these findings, the presurgical hypothesis for the epileptogenic zone was the left posterior quadrant neocortex, although multifocal onset from bilateral hemispheres was not excluded.

His surgical plan included a combination of deep and surface neocortical coverage, including one 10-contact electrode strip and multiple 8-contact electrode strips, which were placed through a small craniotomy to create a MIG pattern, as seen in the postoperative posterolateral radiograph (Fig. 1B). A 4-contact strip served as a ground electrode in all cases. Stereo-EEG leads were simultaneously placed in various regions based on the preoperative hypothesis. Phase II evaluation captured more than 40 events with multifocal onset over the left posterior quadrant with potential epileptogenicity from the right hippocampal head, left hippocampal head and body, left insular lesion, right central sulcus lesion, and right middle temporal gyrus.

Patient 4

Patient 4 is a 41-year-old right-handed female with mild cognitive impairment who was diagnosed with epilepsy at age 22. Her seizure semiology begins with an aura of panic and occasionally a visual scotoma. This may progress to behavioral arrest, oral automatisms, left eye deviation followed by right head/torso version, and tonic extension of her right arm. She rarely has secondary generalization. Focal seizures occur at least 5 times per month, refractory to valproic acid 500 mg BID, clobazam 15 mg BID, and brivaracetam 100 mg BID at the time of presentation and failed prior trials of phenytoin, gabapentin, lamotrigine 200 mg BID, lacosamide 200 mg BID, and levetiracetam 500 mg BID.

Phase I evaluation consisted of neuropsychological testing, MRI, PET, and video-EEG. Neuropsychological testing was nonlateralizing. MRI demonstrated left mesial temporal sclerosis, corroborated by a PET scan showing left mesial temporal hypometabolism. Interictal EEG captured left posterior and left anterior temporal lobe epileptiform discharges, and ictal EEG captured onset in the left posterior temporal region. Based on these findings, the presurgical hypothesis for the epileptogenic zone was the left posterior mesial temporal lobe.

For her surgical plan, strip electrode placement was used to cover the left posterior temporal region (including Wernicke’s area/posterior STG) and the temporo-occipital junction. Five electrodes with either 4- or 6-contact strip electrodes were placed in the lateral direction, and three electrodes (two 6-contact and one 8-contact) were placed posteroinferiorly toward the posterior parietal and occipital region to create the pseudo-subdural grid pattern (Figs. 1C and 3). Phase II evaluation captured more than 300 electrographic seizures predominantly originating from the left hippocampus.

Discussion

Observations

Subdural electrode implantation has proven particularly beneficial in intracranial workup of neocortical seizures, but a challenge associated with this method is the relative invasiveness of the procedure as well as the morbidity. Particularly, there is an increased risk of fluid collection overlying the cortical surface and/or subdural hematoma, with one study15 showing a 10% complication rate of subdural hematomas in addition to occurrences of infection, epidural hematoma, and reoperation.8,14,15 In other studies, subdural grid placements are noted to have a mortality rate of 0.3% and a complication rate ranging from 5% to 17%.9,17 Typical craniotomy sizes for grid placements vary depending on the intended target region of coverage, with the mean being 28 ± 15 cm.5,6,15

These risks are partly attributed to the large craniotomy required to place these implants, as well as the potential space created by the placement of the grid directly on the brain. Large subdural collections, including hematomas, may increase intracranial pressure, cause neurological deficits, and lead to the need for additional surgical interventions.9 While the placement of subdural drains and close observation help mitigate these risks, they do not eliminate the potential for morbidity. In many cases, the potential space created by this approach prompts techniques such as minimally plating the craniotomy defect to avoid compression of the surface of the brain, and in some centers, the craniotomy flap is routinely removed during the duration of the study in an effort to avoid these complications.8,14,16

The adoption and evolution of the sEEG technique revolutionized epilepsy surgery and has gained popularity worldwide, particularly in light of stereotactic robotic assistance.20 While sEEG offers a minimally invasive option for the localization of an epileptogenic focus, it is sometimes necessary and advantageous to obtain dense and more widespread continuous coverage over the neocortical surface. This is often the case when the suspected seizure onset zone is thought to be neocortical, as continuous grid or neo-grid coverage can provide an accurate representation of the seizure onset zone, regions of immediate spread, and denote more precise margins. Surface electrode coverage is also advantageous in cases in which functional mapping over a neocortical region is deemed necessary, which is more limited with the sEEG technique.20,21 The MIG technique offers a potential solution to minimize morbidity and reduce the elevated complication rate associated with traditional subdural grids. By performing a small craniotomy away from the targeted area for placement of side-by-side subdural electrode strips individually into specific areas of interest, the MIG minimizes the space available for fluid accumulation (Fig. 4). Another study by Voorhies and Cohen-Gadol utilized a similar approach of placing electrode strips over the right frontal and temporal lobes (with possible extension to the occipital lobe) via burr holes, offering a minimally invasive alternative to achieve neocortical coverage.22

In applying this method to our current cohort, we did not note any subdural hematomas or strip migrations, and the common gap seen in traditional grid placements between the surface of the brain and craniotomy edge was not noted. These procedures are planned with the future therapeutic procedure in mind, with the incisions and craniotomies positioned in such a way that they can be extended and reopened for the purposes of a future resective procedure, or for the placement of an RNS device. Anecdotally, since introducing this procedure at our institution, we have had a significant increase in requests from our epileptology colleagues for neocortical coverage as part of our intracranial EEG monitoring plans.

Lessons

Although this technique may be suitable for certain patients, traditional craniotomy for grid placement remains the preferred approach in many cases due to the inherent limitations of this method. The incision and craniotomy will need to be enlarged in the case of a planned cortical resection, which may still be performed at the time of electrode removal. This technique is unlikely to be successful in patients who have had previous injuries or craniotomies over the site of interest, where meticulous dissection of scar over the cortical surface and direct placement of electrodes are often necessary.

Another potential complication involved in inserting sequential strips is the potential damage of cortical vessels, resulting in hemorrhage. While the incidence of this is low overall in data from much larger series evaluating complications associated with routine subdural strip electrode placement, meticulous technique and assessment of physical resistance when placing the individual strips is critical. As a consequence of possible adhesions or cortical vessels, subdural electrodes cannot always be placed directly side by side, resulting in some gaps between strips, unlike direct grid placement.

In this cohort, we did not have to make sacrifices for concurrent sEEG coverage with this technique (i.e., compromise sEEG electrode coverage by placing the MIG grid); however, the combination of techniques does require an extra level of planning, particularly where there is planned anatomical overlap. In our series, either sEEG trajectories were adjusted to accommodate placement of strips around the sEEG electrodes (i.e., above and below), or entry points for sEEG electrodes were planned to entirely avoid the craniotomy and region of neocortical coverage. While the MIG is a useful way to mimic the grid structure while decreasing morbidity associated with strip electrodes, surgical hypotheses that require coverage of very large neocortical surfaces may benefit from having a traditional grid. This technique also benefits from postplacement imaging for confirmation of the configuration of the electrodes, and reconfiguration of the strips may be necessary. Finally, the small patient sample size in this study restricts the generalizability of these results, and larger cohorts are necessary to determine true complication rates. The MIG technique offers an effective and minimally invasive alternative for localizing epileptogenic regions, potentially improving surgical outcomes for patients with refractory epilepsy. Further research is needed to validate these findings in larger cohorts.
