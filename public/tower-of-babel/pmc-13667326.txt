VERMONT μQFR: Diagnostic Accuracy of Murray Law‐Based 2D‐μQFR Compared to 3D‐μQFR and vFFR
Authors: Daniel Akrawi, Krishna Kadappu, James Xu, Tamer Yousef Naguib Badie, Oliver Gibbs, Hashim Kachwalla, Phong T D Nguyen, Rahul Kurup, Upul Premawardhana, Justyn Huang, Hao Tran, Kavie Soosapilla, Jim Basilakis, Aiden O'loughlin, Annemarie Hennessy, Giuseppe Femia, Sidney Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMurray law‐based quantitative flow ratio (μQFR) is an angiography‐derived physiological index that utilizes a single angiographic projection with artificial intelligence‐guided vessel segmentation.AimsTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and time efficacy of 2D‐μQFR against wire‐based fractional flow reserve (FFR ≤ 0.80), compare its performance with 3D‐μQFR and vessel‐FFR (vFFR), and assess feasibility in lesions excluded from vFFR analysis.MethodsThis sub‐study of the prospective VERMONT trial analyzed 2D‐ and 3D‐μQFR retrospectively using prospectively acquired angiograms, with wire‐based FFR as the reference standard.ResultsAmong 209 patients with 225 intermediate lesions, 205 lesions were analyzed. 2D‐μQFR demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for FFR ≤ 0.80 (AUC 0.95; 95% CI 0.93–0.98), with 93% sensitivity, 92% specificity, 88% positive predictive value (PPV), and 95% negative predictive value (NPV). Inter‐observer agreement was excellent (r = 0.94, p < 0.001), and 2D‐μQFR analysis was 15.1 min faster than wire‐based FFR (1.8 ± 0.7 vs. 16.9 ± 5.4 min, p < 0.001). Diagnostic performance was comparable to that of 3D‐μQFR, which achieved an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI 0.91–0.97), with 89% sensitivity, 90% specificity, 86% PPV, and 93% NPV. It was more specific than vFFR, which achieved an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.96), with 90% sensitivity, 79% specificity, 74% PPV, and 93% NPV. Single‐projection analysis was feasible in 85% of lesions excluded from vFFR due to image quality limitations.ConclusionSingle‐projection 2D‐μQFR provides accurate, rapid, and reproducible physiological assessment comparable to 3D‐μQFR with superior specificity to vFFR. These findings support 2D‐μQFR as a practical, wire‐free alternative to invasive FFR in routine clinical practice.Keywords: angiogram based fractional flow reserve, FFR, fractional flow reserve, Murray law‐based quantitative flow ratio, vessel fractional flow reserve, vFFR, μQFR

Introduction

Angiography‐derived fractional flow reserve (FFR) has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional wire‐based FFR for the functional assessment of coronary lesions [1, 2, 3]. A growing body of evidence supports its clinical validity, enabling physiology‐guided decision‐making without intracoronary pressure wires [1, 2, 3]. This approach facilitates broader adoption of coronary physiology, while avoiding the procedural risks, technical complexity, and additional costs associated with wire‐based FFR. This is reflected in the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, where quantitative flow ratio (QFR) carries a Class IB recommendation [4].

Our group recently published the VERMONT study, a single‐center, blinded, prospective observational study designed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of real‐time vessel fractional flow reserve (vFFR) against wire‐based FFR ≤ 0.80 [5]. vFFR derives coronary physiology through three‐dimensional vessel reconstruction from two orthogonal angiographic projections and computational fluid dynamics‐based pressure estimation with semi‐automated vessel contour tracing. vFFR demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.92), high sensitivity (90%), and a strong negative predictive value (NPV) (93%), supporting its role as a reliable physiological screening tool in routine practice [5]. However, limitations were observed, including the requirement for two optimized orthogonal angiographic views, resulting in an exclusion rate of 8.9%.

More recently, Murray's law‐based QFR (μQFR) has emerged as an alternate angiography‐derived physiological index. Unlike vFFR, 2D‐μQFR utilizes a single angiographic projection with artificial intelligence (AI)‐guided vessel contour delineation. Early validation studies have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance when compared with wire‐based FFR and conventional QFR [6, 7]. By reducing the requirement for dual high‐quality projections and minimizing operator‐dependent contour adjustment, μQFR may broaden patient applicability, particularly in cases where optimal orthogonal imaging is not feasible.

Accordingly, the VERMONT μQFR sub‐study was designed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and time efficiency of 2D‐μQFR, and to compare its performance with 3D‐μQFR and vFFR, using wire‐based FFR as the reference standard. A secondary objective was to determine whether lesions initially excluded from vFFR analysis could be successfully assessed using 2D‐μQFR (Central Illustration Illustration 1, 1).

Methods

Study Design and Population

The initial VERMONT trial was an investigator‐initiated, single‐center, blinded, prospective observational study designed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and time efficacy of real‐time vFFR in identifying physiologically significant coronary artery lesions, using wire‐based FFR ≤ 0.80 as the reference standard [5]. vFFR and wire‐based FFR were performed independently and simultaneously between February 2022 and August 2023 (Figure Illustration 1, 1).

The current VERMONT μQFR sub‐study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and time efficacy of 2D‐μQFR, using wire‐based FFR as the reference standard. Secondary objectives were to compare diagnostic performance with 3D‐μQFR and vFFR and to determine whether coronary lesions initially excluded from vFFR analysis due to suboptimal angiographic quality could be successfully assessed using 2D‐μQFR. μQFR analyses were performed retrospectively in August 2025 using the same prospectively acquired optimized angiographic images (Figure Illustration 1, 1).

In the original VERMONT trial, all patients with intermediate coronary artery disease (50%–70% stenosis) who underwent wire‐based FFR at Campbelltown Hospital (Sydney, Australia) during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Lesions were excluded if optimized angiographic projections demonstrated persistent vessel overlap, foreshortening, excessive tortuosity, or technical limitations precluding vFFR computation despite repeated acquisition attempts [5]. All patients included in the original VERMONT cohort were included in the μQFR sub‐study. Lesions excluded from vFFR analysis in the original VERMONT study underwent additional separate assessment using 2D‐μQFR.

Study Procedures

The detailed methodology of the initial VERMONT trial has been described previously [5]. In brief, patients with intermediate coronary lesions selected for physiological assessment underwent both vFFR and wire‐based FFR during the same coronary angiography procedure. Following guide catheter engagement and aortic root pressure recording, intracoronary glyceryl trinitrate (200 μg) was administered. A minimum of two angiographic projections were acquired at 15 frames per second from different angulations, with additional acquisitions performed as required to obtain two optimized projections suitable for vFFR analysis.

A pressure wire (PressureWire X; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA) was advanced distal to the target lesion, and wire‐based FFR was measured in accordance with contemporary guidelines, with maximal hyperemia induced using intravenous adenosine (140 μg/kg/min). The distal pressure‐wire sensor location during hyperemia was acquired and stored for reference. vFFR and wire‐based FFR were performed independently and concurrently, with the interventional cardiologist and vFFR analyst blinded to each other's results. All vFFR analyses were performed by the first author (D.A.), with a second blinded operator independently repeating vFFR measurements post‐procedure to assess inter‐observer variability.

For vFFR computation, two optimized orthogonal angiographic projections were transferred to the CAAS Workstation (version 8.2; Pie Medical Imaging). End‐diastolic frames were selected using electrocardiographic triggering, with manual adjustment when required. Semi‐automated vessel contour tracing was performed with manual refinement as necessary. Aortic root pressure was entered as the input boundary condition, and vFFR was derived using a computational fluid dynamics algorithm assuming linear tapering of the reference vessel diameter, with side branches excluded from analysis.

μQFR computation was performed retrospectively on all prospectively acquired optimized angiographic images by a single operator blinded to wire‐based FFR, vFFR results, and clinical outcome data. Images were analyzed using the Pulse Medical workstation (AngioPlus Core software, version 2; Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China). A single optimized angiographic projection was initially selected, followed by key‐frame identification. Projection selection was performed on a case‐by‐case basis to achieve the best overall balance of image quality and anatomical suitability for μQFR reconstruction. Priority was given to angiographic views with adequate contrast opacification throughout the vessel, particularly at the lesion site, to ensure reliable contour detection and stenosis assessment. Among projections meeting this requirement, preference was given to views with minimal foreshortening and vessel overlap at the lesion, followed by adequate visualization of side‐branch take‐off and bifurcation geometry for Murray‐law‐based reconstruction.

AI‐assisted vessel and side‐branch segmentation was performed, with minimal manual correction when required. Contrast flow velocity was automatically derived using TIMI frame count analysis and converted to a hyperemic flow velocity for boundary condition input. Two‐dimensional μQFR was calculated using stepwise reconstruction of reference vessel diameter based on Murray's bifurcation fractal law and computational fluid dynamics. A second optimized projection was subsequently incorporated to derive 3D‐μQFR. For all angiography‐based FFR analyses, including vFFR, 2D‐μQFR, and 3D‐μQFR, measurements were performed from the ostial vessel to the location of the distal wire pressure sensor during hyperemia, ensuring the same vessel segments were analyzed across all modalities.

To assess inter‐observer variability, a second independent observer, blinded to all preceding results, repeated both 2D‐ and 3D‐μQFR analyses. In addition, 2D‐μQFR analysis was attempted in lesions that were initially excluded from vFFR analysis as part of a separate exploratory assessment. μQFR, vFFR, and wire‐based FFR all used a threshold value of ≤ 0.80.

Study Endpoints

The primary outcomes were diagnostic accuracy and time efficiency of 2D‐μQFR compared with wire‐based FFR for intermediate coronary lesions, with FFR ≤ 0.80 defining hemodynamic significance. Secondary outcomes included comparisons of the diagnostic performance of 2D‐μQFR with 3D‐μQFR and vFFR and evaluation of the feasibility of 2D‐μQFR in lesions excluded from vFFR analysis.

Sample Size

Sample size calculations were based on data from the FAST II study, as outlined in the original VERMONT publication, with a minimum target enrollment of 165 patients [5, 8].

Statistical Analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using histogram inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and nonparametric variables as median with interquartile range (25th–75th percentile). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Relationships between 2D‐μQFR, 3D‐μQFR, and wire‐based FFR were assessed using scatter plots and Pearson's correlation coefficient (r), with agreement evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis and 95% limits of agreement. Inter‐observer variability for 2D‐μQFR and 3D‐μQFR was assessed using Pearson's correlation, scatter plots, Bland–Altman analysis, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Diagnostic performance for identifying FFR ≤ 0.80 was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and NPV were calculated for 2D‐μQFR and 3D‐μQFR at a threshold of ≤ 0.80. Analyses of lesions excluded from the original VERMONT study were performed and presented separately.

Mean 2D‐μQFR and 3D‐μQFR computation times were compared to wire‐based FFR and vFFR using paired two‐tailed t‐tests and illustrated using column graphs. Contour adjustment frequencies between the modalities were compared using chi‐square goodness‐of‐fit tests.

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 30; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All p values are two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Enrollment

All patients included in the original VERMONT study were included in the μQFR sub‐study. The original cohort comprised 209 patients with 225 lesions. Of these, 20 lesions (8.9%) were excluded from both the original VERMONT analysis and the primary analysis of the present study because they were unsuitable for 3D angiography‐based FFR assessment. Reasons for exclusion included extensive overlap (n = 10), technical issues including images acquired less than 30° apart (n = 7), foreshortening (n = 2), or tortuosity (n = 1) of the coronary artery in at least one of the two acquired projections required for 3D analysis (Figure Illustration 1, 1). The final analysis was based on 205 lesions (Figure Illustration 1, 1).

Baseline Characteristics and Procedural Data

Baseline characteristics and procedural data are listed in Table 1. The median age was 66 years (IQR 59−74), and 150 (73%) were male. The mean body‐mass‐index (BMI) was 29 ± 6 kg/m2, and 139 (68%) were referred for stable angina. Overall, 157 (77%) patients had hypertension, 173 (84%) had dyslipidemia, 78 (38%) had diabetes, and 109 (54%) were either current or ex‐smokers. The target vessels were the left‐anterior‐descending/diagonal (81%), the right‐coronary‐artery (12%), and the left‐circumflex/obtuse‐marginal (7%). Coronary lesions were characterized as being diffuse (63%), focal (36%), bifurcation (16%), ostial (11%), and moderate or severely calcified (24%). vFFR reference vessel diameter was 2.9 ± 0.6 mm, minimal lumen diameter was 1.6 ± 0.4 mm, and mean diameter stenosis was 44 ± 10%. μQFR reference vessel diameter was 3.2 ± 0.6 mm, minimal lumen diameter 2 ± 0.4 mm, and mean diameter stenosis 37 ± 8%.

Wire‐Based FFR

The mean wire‐based FFR was 0.81 ± 0.08 (Table 1), with values ≤ 0.80 observed in 40% of measurements (Table 1).

vFFR Diagnostic Performance

The diagnostic performance of vFFR has been previously reported in the original VERMONT study [5]. In summary, ROC curve analysis revealed that vFFR had excellent accuracy in predicting a wire‐based FFR of ≤ 0.80 (AUC 0.92; 95% CI: 0.89−0.96) (Figure 2). A vFFR threshold of ≤ 0.80 produced 90% sensitivity, 79% specificity, 74% PPV, 93% NPV, and 83% diagnostic accuracy (Figure 2).

2D‐μQFR Diagnostic Performance

The mean 2D‐μQFR was 0.82 ± 0.08, with 42% of lesions demonstrating a value ≤ 0.80 (Table 1). A good correlation was found between 2D‐μQFR and wire‐based FFR (R = 0.78, p < 0.001) with a mean bias of −0.0090 ± 0.0548 (Figure 3A). ROC curve analysis revealed that 2D‐μQFR had excellent accuracy in predicting a wire‐based FFR of ≤ 0.80 (AUC 0.95; 95% CI: 0.93−0.98) (Figure 2). A 2D‐μQFR threshold of ≤ 0.80 produced 93% sensitivity, 92% specificity, 88% PPV, 95% NPV, and 92% diagnostic accuracy (Figure 2). A strong inter‐observer correlation was found (R = 0.94, p < 0.001) with an ICC of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96−0.98) and a mean bias of 0.0012 ± 0.0294, indicating excellent agreement between the two blinded observers (Figure 4A).

3D‐μQFR Correlation and Diagnostic Performance

The mean 3D‐μQFR was 0.83 ± 0.07, with 42% of lesions demonstrating a value ≤ 0.80 (Table 1). A good correlation was found between 3D‐μQFR and wire‐based FFR (R = 0.76, p < 0.001) with a mean bias of −0.0211 ± 0.0537 (Figure 3B). ROC curve analysis revealed that 3D‐μQFR had excellent accuracy in predicting a wire‐based FFR of ≤ 0.80 (AUC 0.94; 95% CI: 0.91−0.97) (Figure 2). A 3D‐μQFR threshold of ≤ 0.80 produced 89% sensitivity, 90% specificity, 86% PPV, 93% NPV, and 90% diagnostic accuracy (Figure 2). A strong inter‐observer correlation was found (R = 0.95 p < 0.001) with an ICC of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96−0.98) and a mean bias of −0.0024 ± 0.0305, indicating excellent agreement between the two blinded observers (Figure 4B).

Time Efficacy and Contour Adjustment

μQFR analysis was significantly faster than both wire‐based FFR and vFFR. The mean analysis time was 1.8 ± 0.7 min for 2D‐μQFR and 2.6 ± 0.8 min for 3D‐μQFR, compared with 16.9 ± 5.4 min for wire‐based FFR and 3.1 ± 1.0 min for vFFR (Table 2). Paired‐sample t‐tests demonstrated that both 2D‐μQFR and 3D‐μQFR were significantly faster than wire‐based FFR (mean differences of 15.1 min [95% CI 14.4–15.9] and 14.4 min [95% CI 13.6–15.1], respectively; both p < 0.001) and vFFR (mean differences of 1.2 min [95% CI 1.1–1.3] and 0.45 min [95% CI 0.3–0.6], respectively; both p < 0.001) (Figure 5).

Manual contour adjustment was required far less frequently with μQFR than with vFFR. Following semi‐automated vessel contouring, 77% of vFFR lesions required manual adjustment, compared with 32% of 2D‐μQFR and 34% of 3D‐μQFR lesions after AI‐assisted vessel segmentation. A chi‐square test demonstrated a significant difference in the proportion of manual contour adjustment between vFFR and both 2D‐μQFR and 3D‐μQFR (p < 0.001).

2D‐μQFR Analysis of Excluded Lesions

Among the 20 lesions initially excluded from the original VERMONT study, 2D‐μQFR was successfully performed in 17 lesions (85%), each of which had one projection suitable for analysis. The remaining three lesions were unsuitable because of inadequate image quality in all projections. ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI 0.93–1.00), 89% sensitivity, 88% specificity, 89% PPV, 88% NPV, and 88% diagnostic accuracy.

Discussion

The VERMONT‐μQFR sub‐study demonstrates that 2D‐μQFR provides excellent diagnostic performance with high sensitivity and specificity, rapid computation, and strong inter‐observer agreement. Diagnostic accuracy was comparable to 3D‐μQFR and associated with improved specificity compared with vFFR [5]. Crucially, single‐projection analysis enabled reliable physiological assessment in most lesions previously excluded from vFFR due to image acquisition limitations, substantially increasing the real‐world applicability of angiography‐derived FFR. Collectively, these findings support 2D‐μQFR as a practical, wire‐free alternative to invasive FFR that reduces procedural complexity, time, cost, and patient discomfort, while facilitating broader adoption of physiology‐guided revascularization.

At an ischemic threshold of ≤ 0.80, 2D‐μQFR demonstrated excellent rule‐in and rule‐out performance (AUC 0.95), with high sensitivity (93%) and specificity (92%) and limited misclassifications (six false‐negatives and nine false‐positives). These results are consistent with prior validation studies, including the FAVOR II China cohort, where single‐view 2D‐μQFR achieved similarly high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 88%, specificity 96%) [6, 9, 10]. The marginal differences observed between cohorts likely reflect a more complex lesion profile in the present study, including more diffuse lesions, greater calcific burden, and a higher prevalence of hemodynamically significant stenoses [6]. Overall, the diagnostic performance observed exceeds that reported in a 2018 meta‐analysis of earlier angiography‐derived FFR techniques, which showed a pooled sensitivity of 89%, specificity of 90%, and an AUC of 0.84 for methods including QFR and FFRangio [3].

Despite incorporating additional geometric information, 3D‐μQFR did not confer a clear diagnostic advantage over 2D‐μQFR. A likely explanation is that the incremental value of a second angiographic projection depends critically on the quality of that projection. In routine angiography, at least one projection frequently contains residual vessel overlap, foreshortening, suboptimal lesion visualization, or inadequate separation of side branches. Rather than improving anatomical characterization, a suboptimal second view may introduce errors during 3D angiographic reconstruction, particularly in vessel contour detection, lumen sizing, and estimation of lesion geometry. These reconstruction inaccuracies could compromise the theoretical benefit of incorporating biplane geometric information.

By contrast, 2D‐μQFR relies on a single optimized projection in which the target lesion is adequately visualized. These findings suggest that Murray law‐based vessel reconstruction, combined with AI‐assisted segmentation and flow estimation, may be sufficient to derive a reliable physiological assessment without requiring a second projection, provided that lesion visualization is adequate. Although eccentric lesions or complex bifurcation anatomy may be less well characterized using single‐view analysis, the strong performance of 2D‐μQFR supports its use as a pragmatic solution in real‐world settings where optimal dual‐view imaging may not be achievable.

Compared with vFFR, 2D‐μQFR demonstrated similar rule‐out but superior rule‐in performance despite analysis of the same angiographic datasets. This likely reflects key methodological differences. vFFR assumes linear tapering of the reference vessel and does not account for side‐branch flow, whereas 2D‐μQFR applies stepwise vessel reconstruction based on Murray's bifurcation fractal law, explicitly incorporating side branches. Computational modeling has shown improved agreement with invasive FFR when side‐branch flow is localized to bifurcations rather than ignored, supporting the physiological plausibility of this approach [11]. In the present study, this was reflected in systematically larger reference vessel diameters with μQFR compared with vFFR (3.2 ± 0.6 vs. 2.9 ± 0.6 mm). In addition, μQFR utilizes AI‐assisted vessel and side‐branch segmentation, which was associated with substantially less manual contour adjustment than vFFR (32% vs. 77%), potentially reducing operator‐dependent bias. This likely led to a difference in minimal luminal diameter between μQFR and vFFR (2 ± 0.4 vs. 1.6 ± 0.4 mm). These findings suggest that physiologically informed vessel reconstruction and reduced contour manipulation may explain the improved rule‐in performance.

Time efficiency and procedural workflow represent major strengths of 2D‐μQFR. Mean analysis time was 1.8 ± 0.7 min and was significantly shorter than both wire‐based FFR and vFFR. Reliance on a single optimized angiographic projection reduces contrast use, radiation exposure, and acquisition time, and is particularly advantageous in vessels with overlap, foreshortening, tortuosity, or diffuse disease. In this context, single‐projection analysis enabled accurate assessment of most lesions previously excluded from vFFR, expanding the utility of angiography‐derived physiology. Incorporation of side‐branch physiology may further reduce the need for repeat image acquisition or re‐analysis in patients with bifurcation or multivessel disease. Excellent inter‐observer agreement supports the reproducibility of 2D‐μQFR, while inclusion of a broad spectrum of patient and lesion characteristics supports its scalability in routine practice.

Despite receiving a Class IB recommendation in the 2024 ESC guidelines, first‐generation QFR‐guided management failed to demonstrate non‐inferiority to wire‐based FFR in FAVOR III Europe [4, 12]. This was primarily driven by higher rates of myocardial infarction and unplanned revascularization, including a 21% relative increase in revascularization in the QFR‐guided group [12]. The modest inter‐observer reproducibility and variable intra‐observer reproducibility reported with first‐generation, semi‐automated QFR contouring in the QREP study may contribute to lesion misclassification and potentially unnecessary revascularization [13]. In contrast, the AI‐enhanced μQFR evaluated in the present study demonstrated improved reproducibility and required fewer manual corrections.

Recent outcome trials have provided encouraging evidence for newer angiography‐derived physiological techniques. In FLAVOUR II, an angiography‐derived FFR‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) strategy was non‐inferior to IVUS‐guided PCI for the composite of death, myocardial infarction, or revascularization at 12 months [14]. Similarly, FAVOR IV‐QVAS demonstrated that physiologically guided coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) using angiography‐derived FFR reduced the composite perioperative outcome compared with anatomically guided CABG [15]. Both studies predominantly employed single‐projection μQFR with AI‐enhanced vessel contouring.

These findings emphasize the importance of continued technological refinement and rigorous validation of angiography‐derived physiology. AI‐enhanced contouring and single‐projection techniques may reduce operator dependence and bias while improving reproducibility, workflow efficiency, and decision‐making in revascularization. By avoiding pressure‐wire manipulation and adenosine administration, 2D‐μQFR represents an attractive adjunct that could broaden access to physiology‐guided PCI and enable more selective use of pressure‐wire assessment. However, prospective, real‐time, outcome‐driven trials are required to define its optimal role in clinical decision‐making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions, patient populations, and catheterization laboratory workflows.

Second, vFFR analyses were performed in real time, whereas μQFR analyses were conducted retrospectively, which may have introduced systematic biases related to operator behavior and image selection. In particular, real‐time analysis may bias contour adjustment at the site of stenosis in an effort to avoid physiological underestimation. Although the distal analysis boundary was defined by the documented position of the pressure‐wire sensor during hyperemia to ensure anatomically matched vessel segments across modalities, the exact analyzed segment lengths were not available for all methods. Therefore, minor differences in segment definition between angiography‐based FFR analyses cannot be entirely excluded.

Third, both vFFR and μQFR analyses were performed by study investigators rather than an independent core laboratory. While this reflects routine clinical practice, it may introduce operator‐dependent bias, particularly in angiographic frame selection and manual contour refinement, despite blinding to wire‐based FFR results. Fourth, although vFFR, μQFR, and wire‐based FFR were performed independently, all clinical decisions were based exclusively on wire‐based FFR. This non‐randomized design precludes assessment of clinical outcomes or the prognostic value of an angiography‐derived FFR‐guided strategy.

Finally, the majority of lesions analyzed were located in the left anterior descending artery, which may limit extrapolation of these findings to right coronary or left circumflex territories.

Conclusions

The VERMONT‐μQFR sub‐study demonstrates that 2D‐μQFR provides excellent diagnostic accuracy with high sensitivity, specificity, rapid computation, and strong reproducibility. Diagnostic performance was comparable to 3D‐μQFR and showed improved specificity compared with vFFR, supporting the clinical suitability of single‐projection analysis, even in lesions initially excluded from vFFR analysis. These findings position 2D‐μQFR as a practical, wire‐free alternative to wire‐based FFR that simplifies workflow and may facilitate broader adoption of physiology‐guided revascularization.
