Drug resistance profile in postbiliary and gastrointestinal surgical-site infection cases from a tertiary care hospital
Authors: Deepjyoti Kalita, Madhab Chandra Rajbangshi, Sumi Deka, Ridip Kumar Sarma, Sangeeta Deka
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the developing world, surgical-site infection (SSI)-associated pathogens are becoming more drug resistant implicating higher morbidity and management cost. Here, we tried to analyze the drug resistance pattern in our SSI-related isolates. Over 2 years, 191 clinically diagnosed SSIs (postbiliary tract and postgastrointestinal surgery) were included, and wound swabs were processed by conventional aerobic and anaerobic culture. Antibiotic minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined by the Epsilometer testing. Multidrug-resistant phenotypes were detected as per guidelines. The rate of SSI was 5.3% with Klebsiella, Staphylococcus, and Pseudomonas, growing predominantly, and no anaerobe detectable. About 19.4% of Staphylococcus aureus were methicillin-resistant S. aureus, and one-third of them had raised macrolide MIC. About 58.2% of Enterobacteriaceae isolates were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producers. Isolates with raised meropenem MIC were observed. Antibiotic-resistant level in SSI cases is alarmingly high, and in susceptible strains, MIC is approaching the resistant level. Urgent corrective action is an emergency necessity.

Introduction

Surgical-site infection (SSI) is associated with increased patient morbidity, mortality, and health care-associated cost and is ironically considered preventable in more than 50% of cases.[1234] In low/middle-income countries, SSI is considered the most common health care-associated infections (HAIs), affecting up to 30% of patients undergoing surgery.[2] In advanced countries, SSI constitutes up to 20% of all HAIs and is ranked second among all HAIs.[5] A large multicentric study with data from six Indian cities reported the average SSI rate here to be 4.2%.[6] SSI-related bacterial isolates are predominantly derived from the hospital environment where drug resistance is common. Surgery-related factors such as emergency operation and prolonged surgical prophylaxis may be linked to drug resistance.[7] Overall resistant pathogens enhance the clinical and economic impacts of SSIs.[8]

Gram-positive organisms predominate SSI isolates in the Western world, and a considerable number of these are drug resistant.[5] Similar data from low- and middle-income countries are scarce.[345] Surgery-specific data, for example, biliary and gastrointestinal (GI) surgery, are even less reported. In the current work, we studied the profile of pathogens and pattern of drug resistance among SSI cases postbiliary and GI surgery (the two most common operations in the general surgery department) at our tertiary care hospital.

Methods

Patient selection

Subjects from the department of general surgery (situated at the main hospital complex as well as one annexed hospital 6 km away, within the city) were enrolled continuously. Inclusion criteria were those undergoing biliary and upper GI surgery (cholecystectomy, appendicectomy, gastrojejunostomy, duodenal ulcer repair, duodenal and intestinal perforation repair, choledochoduodenostomy, choledochal cyst removal, Whipple’s operation, and partial gastrectomy) and now presenting with evidence of SSI as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria.[1] After informed consent, relevant clinical, laboratory, and epidemiological data were recorded. Two swabs were collected from the visibly infected area, if present, and dipped in Amies transport media and then dispatched to the department of bacteriology laboratory as early as possible. In the laboratory, swabs were inoculated in standard culture media (blood agar and MacConkey agar) in two pairs for incubation in aerobic and anaerobic (using the GasPak technique) environments for 48 h (or till visible growth appears) as per standard guidelines.[9] Growth was identified, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed as a guideline (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [CLSI], USA).[10] Furthermore, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and drug-resistant phenotypes were checked using the Epsilometer test method (bioMérieux, USA, Durham).[10] Specific resistant phenotypes such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producers, carbapenemase producers, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) were checked as per recommendation.[10] The modified Hodge test (MHT) was employed to detect carbapenemase production in Enterobacteriaceae isolates.[10] The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the protocol vide letter no MC/119/2007/1128.

Sample size

We used the cross-sectional study to calculate the sample size, i.e. n (sample size) = Z (1−α)/2 2P (1 − P)/d2, where Z (1−α)/2 (standard normal variate) =1.96 (at 5% Type I error or α), P = 0.5 (as there was no published study/pilot study from our population, we presumed proportion of subject with drug-resistant isolate = 50%), and d = 7% (acceptable precision/absolute error).[11]

Calculated n = (1.96)2 × 0.5 (1−0.5)/(0.07)2 = 196.

Follow-up duration

The study was carried out from June 2014 to November 2015 (2½ years).

Objective

The primary objective of the work was achieved by identifying the pathogen profiles and drug-resistant patterns (in terms of major phenotypes) in the subjects.

Furthermore, patterns were analyzed in the backdrop of patient-specific epidemiological and clinical information in a spreadsheet, which was fed into SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) version 23 package (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were performed. Different groups associated with SSI and corresponding category values were analyzed.

Discussion

Our high culture positivity rate (69.6%) was similar to studies done earlier,[1314] which is attributable to the high rate of bacterial isolation in the presence of persistent drug resistance. Variation in culture positivity across different studies/regions may happen due to differences in the infection control practices and character of the population under study (the presence of comorbid illnesses, sex, and age). Negative isolation (30.4%) could be due to nonbacterial agents or fastidious bacteria.

Isolation in males (76.5%) was significantly higher than in females (61.8%) in tune with previous works.[1215] Higher smoking rates (a critical SSI risk factor), more incidence of emergency surgery (e.g., accident), and less compliance to the defined treatment protocol are commonly attributed to this.[16]

Pathogen isolation was significantly linked to the type of surgery (P = 0.00588), i.e. more in emergency surgery than in electives [Table 1]. This can be explained as in life-saving surgeries, (emergency procedures) compromise in the level of aseptic techniques as well as prolonged hospital stays (e.g., in complicated emergency cases) is expected.[1718] Similar to a few previous works, the isolation rate in our study was found to be significantly higher (P = 0.005218) in dirty and contaminated wounds compared to clean wounds [Table 1], which is attributable to higher bacterial load in contaminated wounds.[17]

A total of 136 isolates were detected, where 130 were single isolates, while three cases had two isolates in tune with the previous study.[14] Usually, clean surgical procedures are associated with monomicrobial isolates, while contaminated/dirty wounds are linked to polymicrobial isolates.[17]

We had predominantly Gram-negative isolates (60.3%), which agrees with previous works.[19] Gram-negative preponderance can be explained by the typical habit of this group of bacteria to colonize inanimate surfaces/environments in hospitals, express multidrug resistance, and easy contamination from the intestinal tract during surgery. In our study, Klebsiella species (27.9%) were overall second to [Table 2] S. aureus (33.1%), which is similar to many previous works.[617] The predominance of Klebsiella could be attributable to the fact that it is a common contaminant in operating room air and a fomite, including on medical equipment in hospitals.[712] Some studies have reported Pseudomonas as the dominant isolate. Such variation in dominant species could be due to multiple factors such as geographical location, season, differences in aseptic techniques, resistant patterns, and differences in the surgical procedures performed.[14] If internal organs especially lower gastrointestinal tract (esp.gut) are explored, Gram-negative gut flora may implicate SSI. Skin colonizers/exogenous bacteria usually predominate in clean procedures.[17]

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed 10 (19.6%) isolates of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus [Table 3]. Earlier, an Indian study found this rate at 30.3%, while another Mumbai-based work found this to be 44%.[1819] About 31.9% of S. aureus had macrolide resistance (MIC 8 μg/mL or more), with 3 (6.4%) isolates having very high macrolide resistance (MIC 64 μg/mL) [Table 3]. Similarly, 21.3% of S. aureus isolates showed a resistant range of ciprofloxacin MIC (8 μg/mL), out of which one isolate had very high resistance (MIC 32 μg/mL). No vancomycin or linezolid resistance was observed. However, in three isolates, linezolid MIC was found to be at the upper limit of sensitivity (i.e. 4 μg/mL), indicating a possible evolution of the resistance mechanism [Table 3]. All three (n = 3) enterococci isolates had vancomycin MIC below 1 μg/mL (upper limit of sensitivity = 4 μg/mL), implying the absence of VRE. Out of 55 Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 32 (58.2%) were found positive for pure ESBL production on phenotypic confirmatory testing (all were inhibited by ceftazidime/cefotaxime and clavulanate combination discs, with zone diameter difference [compared to ceftazidime/cefotaxime without clavulanate disc] of more than 5 mm).[9] ESBL production implicates multidrug resistance, for example, resistance to beta-lactams, including 3rd-generation cephalosporin and many other drugs.[9] About 10 (18.2%) Enterobacteriaceae isolates yielded mixed enzyme (lactamase) production. Such high resistance in Klebsiella and Escherichia coli isolates from SSI cases was reported by previous studies too.[17]

About three (one each of Klebsiella, E coli, and Citrobacter sp.) isolates were found to be positive in MHT for the detection of carbapenemase, and the same had considerably high MIC value of meropenem [Table 3]. One study from Saudi Arabia found 5.5% of Klebsiella isolates to be carbapenem-resistant, while another study found 67% of SSI-related Klebsiella to be resistant to this drug.[1820] About 31 (56.4%) and 27 (49.1%) of Enterobacteriaceae isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin and amikacin, respectively. Similar findings (especially for Klebsiella) were noticed in previous studies too.[17] Out of 24 Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, 8 (33.3%) had ciprofloxacin MIC of 4 μg/mL (resistant level), and 3 (12.5%) had MIC of 8 mμg/μL (double the resistant breakpoint), while about 12 (50%) had ceftazidime MIC 32–64 μg/mL (resistant breakpoint = 32 μg/mL). Three Acinetobacter sp. isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin (MIC 8 μg/mL), amikacin (MIC 64 μg–128 μg/mL), ceftazidime (MIC 64–128 μg/mL), and cefotaxime (MIC 64–128 μg/mL). El-Kholy et al. also found a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter sp. (70% carbapenem-resistant) and P. aeruginosa (67%) in their cohort of SSI cases.[20]

Conclusion

In SSI cases, the emergence of carbapenem resistance (with a 3-fold higher MIC than the CLSI standard for resistant level) is alarming. The significant presence of ESBL and resistant Pseudomonas in SSI cases of our hospital needs urgent attention. MRSA level was not that high compared to Western studies although 19.1% (9/47) of MRSA may trend toward a rising level. Our study finding of high antibiotic resistance or rising resistance suggests an urgent need for preventive measures, especially in surgical patients, including judicious antibiotic use, optimal surgical prophylaxis, antibiotic stewardship, and implementation of an effective infection control program.
