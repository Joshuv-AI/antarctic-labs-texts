Quarkonium in a QCD medium with momentum-dependent relaxation time
Sunny Kumar Singh, Samapan Bhadury, Ritesh Ghosh, Manu Kurian

Abstract
In this study, we explore the properties of quarkonia in a hot QCD medium using a newly proposed collision kernel that consistently incorporates the particle’s momentum dependence into the relaxation time scale of the medium. The longitudinal component of the gluon self-energy, along with the Debye screening mass, is computed within the one-loop hard thermal loop framework by incorporating non-equilibrium corrections. A modified kinetic theory with an extended relaxation time approximation is employed to model the non-equilibrium dynamics of the QCD medium. The sensitivity of the heavy quarkonia potential to the momentum dependence of the relaxation time is studied. Further, we studied the binding energy and thermal width of quarkonia states within this new kinetic theory. Sizable variations in the temperature behavior of these quantities are observed in comparison with the standard relaxation time approximation method due to the particle momentum dependence on the relaxation timescale of the QCD medium. Our findings highlight that accounting for the microscopic nature of the collision timescale is crucial for understanding the quarkonium behavior in a QCD medium.

Introduction

Experimental programs at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) and the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) are considered to be the primary laboratories for studying strongly interacting nuclear matter, under extreme conditions. Significant theoretical efforts have been dedicated to understanding the properties of the new phase of matter, Quark-Gluon Plasma (QGP). One of the notable breakthroughs is the success of relativistic dissipative hydrodynamics for describing the collective behavior of the QGP

;
Heinz and Snellings 2013

;

hard probes

;

;

Heavy quarkonia are bound states of heavy quark-antiquark pairs, and their suppression could act as an unambiguous signal for the existence of the QGP in the collision events. The pioneering work of Karsch, Mehr, and Satz laid the theoretical foundation for studying the properties of quarkonium in a QCD medium at a finite-temperature by utilizing potential models
Karsch et al. 1988
. Further, Matsui and Satz have demonstrated that the color screening in the QGP could lead to the dissociation of quarkonia
Matsui and Satz 1986
. Recent theoretical studies have explored the in-medium modifications of the heavy quark–antiquark potential, paving the way for a more realistic description of quarkonium suppression in the heavy-ion collision experiments
Dumitru et al. 2009a
;

;

The medium created at the collision experiments at the RHIC and LHC is not instantaneously thermalized. Instead, it undergoes a pre-equilibrium phase, which is characterized by momentum-space anisotropies and rapid longitudinal expansion. Such pre-equilibrium conditions can further the quarkonium potential and thereby its properties in the evolving medium. This sets the motivation for the present study. A comprehensive understanding of how non-equilibrium effects influence heavy quarkonium requires the proper knowledge of the system away from equilibrium. One natural way to model the non-equilibrium contribution is by solving kinetic theory. The most standard approach for this is the Anderson–Witting relaxation time approximation (RTA)
Anderson and Witting 1974

;
Dash et al. 2023
;
Singh et al. 2024b

;
Rocha et al. 2021

Dash et al. 2022

In the current analysis, we utilize the recently developed extended relaxation time approximation (ERTA)
Dash et al. 2022
;
Dash et al. 2023
to model the non-equilibrium corrections to the quarkonium properties. We begin by evaluating the non-equilibrium corrections to the longitudinal component of the gluon self-energy and the Debye screening mass within the framework of one-loop hard thermal loop (HTL) theory
Bellac 2011
;
Haque and Mustafa 2025

We then investigate the in-medium behavior of quarkonium states, focusing specifically on their binding energy and thermal width while accounting for non-equilibrium effects. Further, we perform a direct comparison of the ERTA corrections with the conventional RTA observations with a parameterized momentum-dependent form of the thermal relaxation time.

II
Formalism

II.1
Quarkonium potential in QCD medium

Understanding the dissociation of quarkonium states near the QCD crossover temperature requires a realistic treatment of the heavy quark-antiquark potential that includes non-perturbative effects. While the Cornell potential, comprising a short-range Coulomb interaction and a long-range linear confinement term, accurately captures quarkonium properties in vacuum, its extension to a thermal medium is far more complex and remains less well established. To model the medium modifications, we employ a framework in which the in-medium potential in coordinate space is derived through a Fourier transform of the momentum-space potential, modified by the medium’s dielectric response. This potential can be expressed as
Lafferty and Rothkopf 2020
;
Thakur et al. 2020
:

\displaystyle V(r)=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}-1\right)\frac{V_{\text{Cornell}}(p)}{\epsilon(p)},

(1)

where
\epsilon(p)
encodes the screening effects of the thermal medium. The momentum-space form of the Cornell potential takes the form as,

\displaystyle V_{\text{Cornell}}(p)=-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{p^{2}}-\frac{8\pi\sigma}{p^{4}}.

(2)

Here,
\alpha=C_{F}\alpha_{s}
with
\alpha_{s}
as the running coupling constant and
C_{F}=(N_{c}^{2}-1)/2N_{c}
with
N_{c}=3
as the number of color charges, and

\sigma
represents the string tension responsible for quark confinement. The dielectric permittivity
\epsilon(p)
plays a crucial role in describing medium effects on the heavy-quark potential. It is defined as
Thakur et al. 2014a
;
Thakur et al. 2020
:

\epsilon^{-1}(p)=\lim_{p_{0}\to 0}p^{2}\bar{D}_{11}(P),

(3)

where
\bar{D}_{11}(P)
denotes the longitudinal part of the ‘11’-component of the resummed gluon propagator in the real-time formalism. This relationship reflects how medium-induced screening alters the interaction between a heavy quark and antiquark ultimately influencing the stability and dissociation behavior of quarkonium states. The propagator
\bar{D}_{11}(P)
can be decomposed in terms of the retarded, advanced, and symmetric resummed propagators as
Dumitru et al. 2009b
;
Du et al. 2017
:

\bar{D}_{11}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{D}_{R}(P)+\bar{D}_{A}(P)+\bar{D}_{F}(P)\right).

(4)

Using this decomposition along with the relevant expressions for the propagators, the inverse dielectric function of an expanding QCD medium can be written as:

\epsilon^{-1}(p)=\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}+m_{D}^{2}}-i\frac{\pi Tp,m_{D}^{2}}{(p^{2}+m_{D}^{2})^{2}},

(5)

where
m_{D}
is the Debye screening mass.
The first term in Eq. (
5
) represents the medium-induced screening, while the second (imaginary) term accounts for the Landau damping.
Note that the real part of the screened quarkonia potential is obtained from the Fourier transform of
\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{D}_{R}+\bar{D}_{A}\right)
. In contrast, the imaginary component, associated with Landau damping, originates from the Fourier transform of the symmetric part of the gluon propagator
\bar{D}_{F}
.
Using Eq. (
5
) in Eq. (
1
), the screened (real) potential between heavy quarks in the medium becomes,

\displaystyle\text{Re\,}V(r)

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}-1\right)\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{p^{2}}+\frac{8\pi\sigma}{p^{4}}\right)\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}+m_{D}^{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\,m_{D}\left(\frac{e^{-\hat{r}}}{\hat{r}}+1\right)+\frac{2\sigma}{m_{D}}\left(1+\frac{e^{-\hat{r}}-1}{\hat{r}}\right),

with
\hat{r}=r\;m_{D}
and the imaginary part of the potential takes the form as,

\displaystyle\text{Im\,}V(r)=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}-1\right)\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{p^{2}}+\frac{8\pi\sigma}{p^{4}}\right)\frac{\pi Tp,m_{D}^{2}}{(p^{2}+m_{D}^{2})^{2}}.

(7)

Defining the following integrals,

\displaystyle\chi(x)=2\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{dz}{z(z^{2}+1)^{2}}\Bigg[1-\frac{\sin xz}{xz}\Bigg],

(8a)

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x)=2\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{dz\;z}{(z^{2}+1)^{n}}\Bigg[1-\frac{\sin xz}{xz}\Bigg],

(8b)

we can write the imaginary part of the heavy quarkonia potential as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\;V(r)=-T\alpha\phi_{2}(\hat{r})-2\frac{T\sigma}{m_{D}^{2}}\chi(\hat{r}).

(9)

In the Eq. (
9
) above, the first term is called the HTL part and the second term is called the String part of the imaginary potential. We note that the Debye screening mass plays a central role in determining both the real and imaginary parts of the in-medium quarkonia potential. Now, we proceed to calculate the Debye mass in the relevant thermal medium.

Gluon self energy and screening mass

In a thermal medium, the propagation of gluons is modified by the presence of thermal quarks and gluons, leading to the screening of color charges. This effect is quantified by the Debye mass, which governs the exponential suppression of long-range chromoelectric fields. The non-equilibrium effects due to the expansion of the medium can be captured by the screening mass. To consistently incorporate both equilibrium and non-equilibrium dynamics, we evaluate the self-energies and propagators using the well-known Keldysh real-time formalism
Carrington et al. 1999
;
Mrowczynski and Thoma 2000
, which is particularly suited for studying time-dependent phenomena in a thermal field theory, as discussed in Appendix
A
. Within this approach, the longitudinal component of the gluon self-energy receives contributions from gluon and quark loop diagrams. The longitudinal component of gluon self-energy with quark-loop is given as
Mrowczynski and Thoma 2000
;
Dumitru et al. 2009b
;
Nopoush et al. 2017
,

\displaystyle\Pi^{L}_{q,R}(P)

\displaystyle=-i2N_{f}g_{s}^{2}\int\frac{d^{4}K}{(2\pi)^{4}}(q_{0}k_{0}+\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}+m^{2})\Delta_{F}(K)\Delta_{A}(Q),

(10)

where we have employed the standard Dirac trace identities,

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\beta}]

\displaystyle=4[g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}+g^{\mu\beta}g^{\alpha\nu}-g^{\alpha\beta}g^{\mu\nu}],

(11a)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}]

\displaystyle=4g^{\mu\nu}.

(11b)

Using the definition of
\Delta_{F,A}
given in Eq. (
59
) and integrating over
k_{0}
, we can write Eq. (
10
)

\displaystyle\Pi^{L}_{q,R}(P)

\displaystyle=4\pi N_{f}g_{s}^{2}\int\frac{k^{2}dkd\Omega}{(2\pi)^{4}E_{k}}f\left\{\frac{2E_{k}^{2}-p_{0}E_{k}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{P^{2}-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}+\frac{2E_{k}^{2}+p_{0}E_{k}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{P^{2}+2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon}\right\}.

(12)

Here, we have made use of the relation

\displaystyle\delta(K^{2}-m^{2})=\frac{1}{2E_{k}}\bigg[\delta(k_{0}-E_{k})+\delta(k_{0}+E_{k})\bigg].

(13)

In the HTL framework, the internal particle momenta are considered to be much larger than the external ones, with typical scales set by
T
for internal lines and
g_{s}T
for external momenta. Then we can expand the terms in powers of coupling, for example,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P^{2}-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{P^{2}}{-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}-\frac{P^{2}}{(-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon)^{2}}.

(14)

We can express the leading order terms within the curly brackets from Eq. (
12
) as,

\displaystyle\frac{2E_{k}^{2}}{-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}+\frac{2E_{k}^{2}}{2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon}-\frac{2E_{k}^{2}P^{2}}{(-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon)^{2}}-\frac{2E_{k}^{2}P^{2}}{(2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{-p_{0}E_{k}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{-2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}-i\epsilon}+\frac{p_{0}E_{k}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{2E_{k}p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon}.

(15)

After integration over
d\Omega
, the first two terms cancel each other. The second and third terms contribute equally, and the same for the fourth and fifth terms.
After simplification and using the
d\Omega
integration, we get

\displaystyle\Pi_{q,R}^{L}(P)=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{f}g_{s}^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}\int\frac{k^{2}dk\,d\Omega}{E_{k}}f(k)\bigg[\frac{p_{0}\,E_{k}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{2E_{k}\,p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon}-\frac{2E_{k}^{2}\,P^{2}}{(2E_{k}\,p_{0}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}+i\epsilon)^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{f}g_{s}^{2}}{4\pi^{3}}\int\frac{k^{2}dk\,d\Omega}{E_{k}}f(k)\frac{\frac{E_{k}^{2}}{k^{2}}-(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{\left(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}+\frac{p_{0}+i\epsilon}{p\,k}E_{k}\right)^{2}},

(16)

where we consider the number of quark flavors as
N_{f}=3
. The quark contribution to the Debye mass in QCD is obtained as,

\displaystyle m_{D,q}^{2}=

\displaystyle-\Pi_{R}^{L}(p_{0}=0,p\rightarrow 0,m).

(17)

The gluon and ghost loop contributions to the Debye screening mass in an evolving QCD medium can be expressed within the HTL as,

\displaystyle m^{2}_{D,g}=\frac{2C_{A}g_{s}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,k\,dk\,f,

(18)

where
C_{A}
is the group factor
Bellac 2011
. For the system out of equilibrium, we have
f=f_{0}+\delta f
. In equilibrium, the screening properties are governed by the equilibrium distribution function
f_{0}
. However, in a system slightly away from equilibrium, such as the QGP produced in heavy-ion collisions, the distribution function acquires a non-equilibrium correction
\delta f
, reflecting the response of the system to external perturbations. The microscopic dynamics of the medium determine the precise form and encode essential information about its relaxation properties. One approach to model this is through kinetic theory. Below, we discuss the formulation of relativistic hydrodynamics from kinetic theory, based on the ERTA.

II.2
Relativistic hydrodynamics with ERTA

Before we obtain the expressions of the Debye mass and hence the potential, we would like to give a brief overview of the framework of ERTA hydrodynamics, based on which, the non-equilibrium corrections are calculated.

II.2.1
Relativistic hydrodynamics

As the system under consideration is made of massless chargeless particles, the only relevant conserved current is the energy-momentum tensor (associated with the translational invariance). Under the choice of Landau frame (
U_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu}=\varepsilon U^{\mu}
) the conserved energy-momentum tensor can be written as,

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\varepsilon\,U^{\mu}U^{\nu}-\mathcal{P}\Delta^{\mu\nu}+\pi^{\mu\nu}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}p}P^{\mu}P^{\nu}f(X,P),

(19)

where
\varepsilon
is the out-of-equilibrium energy density,
\mathcal{P}
is the isotropic pressure,
\pi^{\mu\nu}
is the shear viscous pressure, and
\Delta^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}-U^{\mu}U^{\nu}
is the projection operator orthogonal to the fluid four-velocity
U^{\mu}
. Additionally, the Landau matching choice requires that,
\varepsilon
be equal to the equilibrium energy density (
\varepsilon_{0}
), i.e.,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
. We can then express the macroscopic variables,
\varepsilon_{0}
,
\mathcal{P}
and
\pi^{\mu\nu}
as,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}

\displaystyle=U_{\mu}U_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}p}\left(U\cdot P\right)^{2}f_{0}(X,P),

(20a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{3}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}p}\left(P\cdot\Delta\cdot P\right)f_{0}(X,P),

(20b)

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}=\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}p}P^{\alpha}P^{\beta}f(X,P),

(20c)

where
\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}\Delta^{\nu}_{\beta}+\Delta^{\mu}_{\beta}\Delta^{\nu}_{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}\Delta^{\mu\nu}
is the doubly symmetric traceless projection operator that is orthogonal to fluid four-velocity.
The conservation law for energy-momentum tensor (
\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
) leads to the evolution equation for
\varepsilon_{0}
and
U_{\mu}
as,

\displaystyle\dot{\varepsilon}_{0}+(\varepsilon_{0}+\mathcal{P})\theta-\pi^{\mu\nu}\sigma_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0,

(21a)

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{0}+\mathcal{P})\dot{U}^{\alpha}-\left(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{P}\right)+\Delta^{\alpha}_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}\pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0,

(21b)

with
\theta\equiv\partial\cdot U
being the expansion, and
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}\left(\partial^{\alpha}U^{\beta}\right)
being the shear velocity tensor. The spacetime derivative has been split into components along and orthogonal to
U_{\mu}
, yielding
\partial_{\mu}=U_{\mu}D+\nabla_{\mu}
, where
D\equiv\left(U\cdot\partial\right)
is the co-moving derivative and
\nabla_{\mu}\equiv\Delta_{\mu}^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}
is the spacelike derivative. We have also used the shorthand notation,
\dot{A}=DA=U^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}A
for the co-moving derivative.
Using the definitions from Eqs. (
20a
)-(
20c
) into Eqs. (
21a
)-(
21b
), we obtain the evolution equations for temperature and fluid four-velocity as
Dash et al. 2022
,

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}=\frac{\beta}{3}\theta+\mathcal{O}\left(\partial^{2}\right),\qquad{\rm and,}\qquad\beta\dot{U}_{\mu}=-\nabla_{\mu}\beta+\mathcal{O}\left(\partial^{2}\right),

(22)

where
\mathcal{O}\left(\partial^{2}\right)
represents corrections from second and higher orders that have been ignored presently. The relations of Eq. (
22
) will be used later, to derive the non-equilibrium correction,
\delta f
.

II.2.2
The ERTA framework

It has been known for a long time that for a gas of classical particles, the particles with lower momenta attain the equilibrium distribution at an earlier time than those with higher momenta
Krook and Wu 1976
. Thus, it is natural to presume that the relaxation time for particles of different momenta will be different as well. Recent studies
Dusling et al. 2010
;
Teaney and Yan 2014
have also arrived at similar conclusions while considering the dynamics of the QCD system. These studies then inspired the development of several new kinetic theoretical frameworks where the relaxation time was allowed to be dependent on momenta
Rocha et al. 2021
;
Dash et al. 2022
;
Shaikh et al. 2025
;
Biswas et al. 2022
. In the present analysis, we employ the ERTA framework to determine the out-of-equilibrium distribution function up to first order in spacetime gradient. Below, we provide a brief overview of the first-order ERTA approach.

The relativistic Boltzmann equation, in the absence of any external force, under the RTA takes the form as
Anderson and Witting 1974
,

\displaystyle P^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f=-\frac{\left(U\cdot P\right)}{\tau_{\rm R}}\left(f-f_{0}\right),

(23)

where the equilibrium distribution function
f_{0}
is given by,

\displaystyle f_{0}(X,P)=\frac{1}{\exp\big[\beta\left(U\cdot P\right)+\alpha\big]+a},

(24)

with
\beta=1/T
being the inverse temperature,
U_{\mu}
as the fluid four-velocity, the dimensionless quantity
\alpha=\mu/T
being the ratio of chemical potential to temperature, and
a
taking the values
0,1
, and
-1
for particles following Maxwell-Boltzmann (MB), Fermi-Dirac (FD), and Bose-Einstein (BE) statistics, respectively.

For a system in an out-of-equilibrium state, the phase-space distribution function can be split into equilibrium and non-equilibrium parts by following the Chapman-Enskog expansion as,

\displaystyle f(X,P)=f_{0}(X,P)+\delta f(X,P),

(25)

where the condition
\frac{\delta f}{f_{0}}\ll 1
is imposed for the formulation of hydrodynamic theories. In such systems, the notions of temperature, chemical potential, and fluid velocity are ill-defined, and hence
T,\mu
and
U_{\mu}
correspond to some auxiliary variables that coincide with respective variables only at equilibrium. Consequently, the equilibrium state (
f_{0}
) used in Eq. (
25
) is an auxiliary equilibrium
Dash et al. 2022
. The out-of-equilibrium variables,
T,\,\,\mu
, and
U_{\mu}
(which we call
hydrodynamic variables
from this point onward) are fixed only through frame and matching conditions. However, in relativistic hydrodynamics, these field redefinitions are completely arbitrary, and hence the definitions of hydrodynamic variables are also a matter of choice. As a result of this ambiguity, one may expect that the equilibrium distribution function as described in Eq. (
24
) is not the actual equilibrium state of the system, as mentioned above. However, in the case of the RTA, one is forced to choose the Landau frame and matching conditions to ensure conservation law, as a result of which the hydrodynamic variables end up being the variables associated with the equilibrium state, provided the relaxation time is momentum-independent. When the relaxation time is momentum-dependent, the conservation laws can no longer be guaranteed, and this led to the proposal of the ERTA, which modifies the Boltzmann equation as,

\displaystyle P^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f=-\frac{\left(U\cdot P\right)}{\tau_{\rm R}}\left(f-f_{0}^{*}\right),

(26)

where
f_{0}^{*}
is given by,

\displaystyle f_{0}^{*}(X,P)=\frac{1}{\exp\big[\beta^{*}\left(U^{*}\cdot P\right)+\alpha^{*}\big]+a},

(27)

with
\beta^{*}=1/T^{*}
,
\alpha^{*}=\mu^{*}/T^{*}
. Here,
T^{*},\mu^{*}
, and
U_{\mu}^{*}
correspond to the temperature, chemical potential, and the fluid four velocity of the system at equilibrium. The starred quantities are called the
thermodynamic variables
, and consequently,
f_{0}^{*}
represents the true thermodynamic equilibrium of the system toward which the out-of-equilibrium system relaxes as
t\to\infty
. The advantages of this collision kernel in Eq. (
26
) are that (i) the relaxation time can now be taken to be momentum-dependent
Dash et al. 2022
, and (ii) one is free to choose any arbitrary frame and matching condition
Bhadury 2025
;
Mukherjee et al. 2025
. The thermodynamic variables and hydrodynamic variables are related to each other through the relation,

\displaystyle U_{\mu}^{*}=U_{\mu}+\delta U_{\mu},\qquad T^{*}=T+\delta T,\qquad\mu^{*}=\mu+\delta\mu,

(28)

where the correction terms (denoted with
\delta
in front) can be determined through frame and matching conditions
Dash et al. 2022
. Since the ERTA provides us with the expressions of the transport coefficients that are invariant under field redefinition, for the present work, we choose the Landau-Lifshitz version
Landau and Lifshitz 1987
of the frame and matching condition. Consequently, following the results of Ref.
Dash et al. 2022
, we can write,

\displaystyle\delta U_{\mu}=\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}\left(\nabla_{\mu}\alpha\right),\qquad\delta T=\mathcal{C}_{2}\,\theta,\qquad\delta\mu=\mathcal{C}_{3}\,\theta,

(29)

where the coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{C}_{3}
can be found in Refs.
Dash et al. 2022
;
Kumar Singh et al. 2025
. In the present study, the system under consideration consists of chargeless, massless particles and hence, we have
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{3}=0

II.2.4
Bjorken expansion

The non-equilibrium correction to the distribution function as given in Eq. (
31

\sigma_{\mu\nu}
as defined below Eq. (
21b

U^{\mu}=(t/\tau,0,0,z/\tau)
where
\tau=\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}}
is the proper time of the fluid element. In terms of Milne coordinates
(\tau,x,y,\eta_{s})

\eta_{s}=\tanh^{-1}(z/t)
is the space-time rapidity, the fluid velocity becomes
U^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)

g^{\mu\nu}=(1,-1,-1,-1/\tau^{2})

D=U^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\partial/\partial\tau

\theta=\partial_{\mu}U^{\mu}=1/\tau

\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\text{diag}\left[0,1/(3\tau),1/(3\tau),-2/(3\tau^{3})\right]

in Eq. (
21a

\displaystyle\frac{dT}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{d\varepsilon_{0}/dT}\left[\frac{4\eta}{3\tau^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\mathcal{P}}{\tau}\right],

(36)

where
\eta
is given by Eq. (
35

\varepsilon_{0}=3\mathcal{P}
. The temperature as a function of the proper time,
\tau

36

Next, we will use the information obtained so far to determine the gluon self-energy, screening mass, and other phenomenologically relevant variables to study the properties of quarkonium states in the fluid system under consideration.

II.3
Modified screening within the ERTA

The non-equilibrium corrections to the gluon self-energy from quark loop in the massless case can be obtained from Eq. (

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{R}(P)=\frac{N_{f}g_{s}^{2}}{4\pi^{3}}\int k\,{dk}\,d\Omega\,\frac{1-(\hat{\bf k}\cdot\hat{\bf p})^{2}}{\left(\hat{\bf k}\cdot\hat{\bf p}+\frac{p^{0}+i\epsilon}{p}\right)^{2}}\,\delta f.

Here, we have
\hat{\bf k}\cdot\hat{\bf p}=\cos\theta_{kp}

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{R}(P)=\frac{N_{f}g_{s}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}k\;dk\,\sin\theta_{kp}\;{d\theta_{kp}}\frac{1-\cos^{2}\theta_{kp}}{\left(\cos\theta_{kp}+\frac{p^{0}+i\epsilon}{p}\right)^{2}}\delta f\,.

(38)

In general, the non-equilibrium correction to the distribution function (

In this scenario, we can express the first-order correction to the distribution function as derived using momentum-dependent relaxation time within the ERTA framework given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\delta f\equiv\delta f_{(1)}=\frac{\beta\tau_{\rm R}}{\left(U\cdot K\right)}\frac{k^{2}}{3\tau}(1-3\cos^{2}\theta_{kp})f_{0}\tilde{f}_{0}.

(39)

A detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
B

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{R}(P)=\frac{N_{f}g_{s}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{t_{\mathrm{R}}}{3\tau}\frac{1}{T^{\ell+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{\ell+2}\,{dk}f_{0}\tilde{f}_{0}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}(1-3\cos^{2}\theta)d\theta\frac{\left(1-\cos^{2}\theta\right)}{\left(\cos\theta+\frac{p^{0}+i\epsilon}{p}\right)^{2}}.

Since the structure of

is the same for the gluon loop as well, we end up with,

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{R}(P)=\frac{g_{s}^{2}t_{\mathrm{R}}\beta^{\ell+1}}{6\pi^{2}\tau}(N_{f}I_{\ell}^{(q)}+N_{c}I_{\ell}^{(g)})\left[I_{\theta}(1,0)-3I_{\theta}(1,2)\right],

(41)

where the integrals in the above expression are defined as,

\displaystyle I_{\theta}(n,m)

\displaystyle=\int^{\pi}_{0}\sin^{n}\theta\cos^{m}\theta\frac{1-\cos^{2}\theta}{\left(\cos\theta+\frac{p^{0}+i\epsilon}{p}\right)^{2}}d\theta,

(42a)

\displaystyle I_{\ell}^{(q)}

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}k^{\ell+2}\frac{e^{k/T}}{\left(e^{k/T}+1\right)^{2}}dk,

(42b)

\displaystyle I_{\ell}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}k^{\ell+2}\frac{e^{k/T}}{\left(e^{k/T}-1\right)^{2}}dk.

(42c)

While the angular integrals are the same for both quark and gluon loop contributions to the gluon self-energy, the

integrals for quark and gluon contributions, denoted by
I_{\ell}^{(q)}
and
I_{\ell}^{(g)}
respectively, must be evaluated separately. The
I_{\ell}^{(q)}
and
I_{\ell}^{(g)}

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{R}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{s}^{2}t_{\mathrm{R}}T^{2}}{6\pi^{2}\tau}\left[N_{c}+N_{f}(1-2^{-(\ell+1)})\right]\Gamma(\ell+3)\zeta(\ell+2)[I_{\theta}(1,0)-3I_{\theta}(1,2)],\quad\ell>-1

where

is the Riemann Zeta, and the angular integrals,

\displaystyle I_{\theta}(1,0)=4\left[\frac{\left(p^{0}+i\epsilon\right)}{2p}\ln\left(\frac{p^{0}+p+i\epsilon}{p^{0}-p+i\epsilon}\right)-1\right],

(44a)

\displaystyle I_{\theta}(1,2)=-\frac{2\left(p^{0}+i\epsilon\right)}{p^{3}}\left[p^{2}-2\left(p^{0}+i\epsilon\right)^{2}\right]\ln\left(\frac{p^{0}+p+i\epsilon}{p^{0}-p+i\epsilon}\right)-\frac{8(p^{0}+i\epsilon)^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{4}{3}.

\displaystyle\delta m_{D}^{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{p^{0}=0,\,\,\bf{p}\rightarrow 0}I_{\theta}(1,0)=-4,

\displaystyle m_{D}^{2}=\frac{g_{s}^{2}T^{2}}{6}(N_{f}+2N_{c})+\frac{4g_{s}^{2}t_{\mathrm{R}}T^{2}}{3\pi^{2}\tau}\left[N_{c}+N_{f}(1-2^{-(\ell+1)})\right]\Gamma(\ell+3)\zeta(\ell+2),\quad\quad\ell>-1.

II.4
In-Medium properties of quarkonium states

We now focus on two additional physical observables essential for understanding the behavior of heavy quarkonium states in a thermal medium: the thermal width, which quantifies the dissociation rate, and the binding energy. These parameters provide valuable insights into the stability and longevity of heavy quark-antiquark bound states such as charmonium and bottomonium in hot and dense environments, like those created in heavy-ion collisions.

II.4.1
Binding energy

To compute the binding energy, we consider a simplified form of the quarkonia potential by focusing on its real part in the Coulombic limit. This approximation is valid at short distances, where the dimensionless variable
\hat{r}\ll 1
. In this regime, any terms that are independent of
\hat{r}
can be safely ignored, as they do not affect the potential due to translational invariance. The simplified potential captures the dominant interaction at small separations, which is essential for accurately modeling the bound states. Using this potential, we solve the corresponding Schrödinger equation to determine the discrete energy eigenvalues associated with the bound states. These eigenvalues directly reflect the binding energies of the quarkonium systems. The results offer important information about how strongly the heavy quark-antiquark pairs are held together under thermal conditions and can be used to assess how likely these states are to survive in the QGP.

By solving the associated Schrödinger equation with this simplified potential, we obtain the quantized energy levels for systems like charmonium and bottomonium as
Agotiya et al. 2009
,

\displaystyle E_{n}=-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{m_{Q}\sigma^{2}}{m^{4}_{D}}~,

(49)

where
m_{Q}
is the heavy quark mass and
m_{D}
is the Debye screening mass, whose explicit form is given in Eq. (
48
). The ground state (
n=1
) and first excited state (
n=2
) eigenvalues can be interpreted as the binding energies of the lowest and the next-to-lowest charmonium and bottomonium states, namely
J/\psi
and
\Upsilon
for
n=1
, and
\psi^{\prime}
and
\Upsilon^{\prime}
for
n=2
, respectively. In this study, we present the results for the ground state energy of the
J/\psi
meson. It is straightforward to derive similar results for other states as well, and is left as a future task.

II.4.2
Thermal width of quarkonium states

The thermal width (
\Gamma
) provides the measure of the in-medium decay or dissociation probability of a bound quarkonium state and can be evaluated from the imaginary part of the in-medium potential between the heavy quark and antiquark. Assuming knowledge of the spatial wave function
\Psi({\bf r})
of the quarkonium state, the thermal width
\Gamma
can be expressed as an expectation value of the imaginary part of the potential
\text{Im}\,V(r,T)
, given in Eq. (
9
), evaluated over the wave function profile. Specifically, it is given by
Nilima et al. 2023
;
Thakur et al. 2014a
;
Thakur et al. 2020
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(T)=-\int d^{3}{\bf r}\,\left|\Psi({\bf r})\right|^{2}\,\text{Im}\,V({\bf r};T)~,

(50)

where
T
denotes the temperature. The imaginary part of the potential accounts for the Landau damping and in-medium scattering processes that lead to the dissociation of the bound state. This formulation is derived using first-order quantum mechanical perturbation theory, where the unperturbed wave functions are taken to be the Coulombic eigenfunctions of the bound state system.

For our analysis, we consider the ground states of the charmonium system, corresponding to
J/\psi
. The radial parts of the Coulombic wave function for the
1s
state is given by,

\displaystyle\Psi_{1s}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi r_{B}^{3}}}\,e^{-r/r_{B}},

(51)

where
r_{B}=\dfrac{2}{\alpha_{s}m_{Q}}
is the Bohr radius of the quarkonium system with
\alpha_{s}
as the QCD strong coupling constant. The Bohr radius reflects the
spatial extent of the bound state and plays a
crucial role in determining the overlap with the
imaginary potential. Since the wave function

\Psi_{1s}(r)
is spherically symmetric, the
integral in Eq. (
50
) can be simplified
to a radial form. The magnitude of
\Gamma
thus
captures how the in-medium effects, encoded in
\text{Im}\,V(r,T)
, modify the stability of the quarkonium states.

IV
Summary and outlook

In this study, we explored the properties of quarkonia in a QCD medium within a newly proposed microscopic theory that incorporates particle-momentum dependence in the relaxation time. We begin by estimating the non-equilibrium corrections to the longitudinal component of the gluon self-energy within the one-loop HTL approximation. The non-equilibrium dynamics of the QCD medium are modeled within the ERTA-based modified kinetic theory. We analyzed the sensitivity of quarkonia potential to non-equilibrium effects arising from the expansion of the medium. The in-medium modifications to the potential are incorporated through the dielectric response of the QCD medium. To quantify these effects, we analyzed the binding energy and thermal width of quarkonia states. We observe that the bulk viscous corrections are minimal, whereas shear viscous corrections are non-negligible and significantly affect the binding energy and thermal width. We compared the ERTA-based calculations of the system of massless particles with those obtained from standard RTA results. Notably, we observe that the momentum dependence of the relaxation time has a sizable impact on the non-equilibrium corrections to the physical observables. Our findings highlight that incorporating the specifics of microscopic interactions on the collision timescale is crucial for the study of quarkonia in the QCD medium. This dependence has potential implications for phenomenological studies as well.

This work represents a step towards the realistic description of the quarkonia potential in the QCD medium. For a more realistic study, two aspects need further attention. First, it is necessary to incorporate effective modeling of thermal medium effects within the ERTA framework, which includes a consideration of the temperature dependence of the
\ell
-parameter
Mukherjee et al. 2025
. Although this extension poses mathematical challenges, it may reveal non-trivial contributions of bulk viscous corrections. Second, we need to consider a realistic 1+3D expansion of the QGP medium, where the medium may evolve according to the Israel-Stewart-like second-order hydrodynamic theories. We intend to explore these aspects in the near future.

Appendix A
Keldysh real-time formalism for non-equilibrium systems

In this appendix, we outline the essential framework employed to derive our results, presented concisely and self-contained. We revisited the Keldysh formalism in real time
Chou et al. 1985
;
Carrington et al. 1998
;
Dumitru et al. 2009b
;
Mrowczynski et al. 2017
;
Nopoush et al. 2017
. We need the special time contour in the Keldysh-Schwinger formalism to correctly formulate quantum field theory in real time for systems out of equilibrium, allowing us to compute causal correlation functions and track the evolution of the density matrix.

Figure 7:
The contour in the complex time plane
Mrowczynski et al. 2017
.

For the spinor field
\psi(X)
, the contour Green’s function is generally defined as

\displaystyle iS_{\alpha\beta}(X,Y)=\braket{T\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}},

(52)

where
\alpha,\beta
are spinor indices. For notational simplicity, we are not explicitly writing the color indices. The ensemble average denoted by
\braket{\dots}
is defined as
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\dots]
with
\rho
being the density matrix.
T
is the time-ordering operator:

\displaystyle T\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}=\Theta(x_{0}-y_{0})\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)-\Theta(y_{0}-x_{0})\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\psi_{\alpha}(X),

(53)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside theta function.
X^{\mu}\equiv(x_{0},\mathbf{x})
is the four vector.

In the Keldysh-Schwinger formalism, the contour-ordered propagator naturally splits into four components, each corresponding to a specific placement of the field operators along the two
branches of the closed time contour. These
components account for: (a) propagation entirely
along the forward (upper) branch, (b) propagation
from the forward to the backward (lower) branch,
(c) propagation from the backward to the forward
branch, and (d) propagation entirely along the backward branch. This decomposition elegantly captures all possible time orderings of field operators, making the formalism particularly suited for studying non-equilibrium quantum systems. The four Green’s functions are defined as

\displaystyle iS_{\alpha\beta}^{>}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)},

\displaystyle iS_{\alpha\beta}^{<}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\braket{\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\psi_{\alpha}(X)},

\displaystyle iS_{\alpha\beta}^{c}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{T^{c}\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}},

\displaystyle iS_{\alpha\beta}^{a}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{T^{a}\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}},

(54)

where
T^{c}
and
T^{a}
are time ordering and anti-ordering operators, respectively. They are defined as

\displaystyle T^{c}\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}

\displaystyle=\Theta(x_{0}-y_{0})\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)-\Theta(y_{0}-x_{0})\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\psi_{\alpha}(X),

\displaystyle T^{a}\{\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\}

\displaystyle=\Theta(y_{0}-x_{0})\psi_{\alpha}(X)\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)-\Theta(x_{0}-y_{0})\bar{\psi}_{\beta}(Y)\psi_{\alpha}(X).

(55)

In the Keldysh formulation, one can write the Green’s functions in the
2\times 2
matrix form as

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\
S_{21}&S_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S^{c}&S^{<}\\
S^{>}&S^{a}\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

In practical terms, four distinct Green’s functions can be introduced, each capturing a different type of time ordering based on where the field operators lie on the closed time contour:

S^{c}(x,y)
corresponds to both
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
lying on the forward (upper) branch,

S^{a}(x,y)
corresponds to both time arguments on the backward (lower) branch,

S^{>}(x,y)
describes the case where
x_{0}
on the lower and
y_{0}
on the upper branch, and

S^{<}(x,y)
describes the reverse configuration,
x_{0}
on the upper and
y_{0}
on the lower branch.

The retarded, advanced, and Feynman propagators are introduced as follows:

\displaystyle S_{R}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\Theta(x_{0}-y_{0})[S^{>}(X,Y)-S^{<}(X,Y)],

\displaystyle S_{A}(X)

\displaystyle=-\Theta(y_{0}-x_{0})[S^{>}(X,Y)-S^{<}(X,Y)],

\displaystyle S_{F}(X)

\displaystyle=S^{>}(X,Y)+S^{<}(X,Y),

(57)

respectively.
The form of the fermion propagator in momentum space is written in terms of
2\times 2
matrix as

\displaystyle S(k)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\not{K}}{K^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}&0\\
0&\frac{-\not{K}}{K^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\end{pmatrix}+2\pi i\not{K}\,\delta(K^{2}-m^{2})\begin{pmatrix}f(\mathbf{k})&-\Theta(-k_{0})+f(\mathbf{k})\\
-\Theta(k_{0})+f(\mathbf{k})&f(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix},

(58)

where
f(\mathbf{k})
corresponds to Fermi distribution function.
The form of the retarded, advanced, and Feynman propagators in momentum space can be written as
S_{R,A,F}(K)=\not{K}\Delta_{R,A,F}
with

\displaystyle\Delta_{R}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K^{2}-m^{2}+i\,\text{sgn}(k_{0})\,\epsilon},

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K^{2}-m^{2}-i\,\text{sgn}(k_{0})\,\epsilon},

\displaystyle\Delta_{F}(K)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\,\left(1-2f(\mathbf{k})\right)\delta(K^{2}-m^{2}).

(59)

Appendix B
First-order correction to distribution function
\delta f_{(1)}
in Bjorken expansion

Here, we show that the form of
\delta f_{(1)}
given in Eq. (
31
) can be reduced to Eq. (
39
) in a longitudinally expanding fluid that is boost-invariant. For this, we need to calculate the form of
K^{\mu}K^{\nu}\sigma_{\mu\nu}
in the local rest frame of the fluid using the cartesian coordinates to be able to express it in terms of
k
and the angle between the gluon and the particle momenta,
\theta_{kp}
. We begin with the components of the shear-velocity tensor,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}
in cartesian co-ordinates obtained by differentiating the fluid velocity
U^{\mu}=(t/\tau,0,0,z/\tau)
using its definition,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\partial^{\alpha}U^{\beta}

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{2z^{2}}{3\tau^{3}}&0&0&-\dfrac{2zt}{3\tau^{3}}\\

0&0&\dfrac{1}{3\tau}&0\\
-\dfrac{2zt}{3\tau^{3}}&0&0&-\dfrac{2t^{2}}{3\tau^{3}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{in Cartesian coordinates }(t,x,y,z).

(60)

Boosting to the local rest frame of the fluid via a Lorentz transformation
\Lambda^{\alpha}_{\,\,\beta}(\eta)
where
\eta=\tanh^{-1}(z/t)
is the space-time rapidity, the components of the
\sigma^{\mu\nu}_{\text{LRF}}
tensor become,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}_{\text{LRF}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&\dfrac{1}{3\tau}&0&0\\
0&0&\dfrac{1}{3\tau}&0\\
0&0&0&-\dfrac{2\tau}{3}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{in Cartesian coordinates }(t,x,y,z).

(61)

With this, we have,

\displaystyle k_{\mu}k_{\nu}\sigma_{\text{LRF}}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=k_{x}^{2}\sigma_{\text{LRF}}^{11}+k_{y}^{2}\sigma_{\text{LRF}}^{22}+k_{z}^{2}\sigma_{\text{LRF}}^{33},

(62)

where

are the components of particle momenta in the cartesian coordinates in the local rest frame of the fluid. Hence, we can express them as

\displaystyle K_{\mu}K_{\nu}\sigma_{\text{LRF}}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\tau}k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{kp}-\frac{2\tau}{3}k^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{kp}

Appendix C
Estimation of
I_{\ell}
integrals

We consider the form for the equilibrium distribution function,

\displaystyle f_{0}=\frac{1}{e^{k/T}+a},

\displaystyle f_{0}\tilde{f}_{0}=\frac{e^{k/T}}{\left(e^{k/T}+a\right)^{2}},

(64)

where
a=1,0,-1
for particles following Fermi-Dirac, Maxwell-Boltzmann, or Bose-Einstein statistics, respectively, and
\tilde{f}_{0}=1-af_{0}
. The
I_{\ell}
integral can be defined as,

\displaystyle I_{\ell}=T^{\ell+3}\int^{\infty}_{0}{dz}\,\frac{z^{\ell+2}e^{z}}{\left(e^{z}+a\right)^{2}},

(65)

where we have substituted
k/T=z
in Eq. (
42c
). By defining
u=z^{\ell+2}
and
v=-\frac{1}{e^{z}+a}
and using integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{I_{\ell}}{T^{\ell+3}}

\displaystyle=-\int^{\infty}_{0}v\frac{du}{dz}+uv|^{\infty}_{0}

\displaystyle=(\ell+2)\int^{\infty}_{0}{dz}\,\frac{z^{\ell+1}}{e^{z}+a}+\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{z^{\ell+2}}{e^{z}+a}-\lim_{z\rightarrow\infty}\frac{z^{\ell+2}}{e^{z}+a}.

(66)

The limits in the above expression become

since the exponential in the denominator increases faster than the polynomial in the numerator as

\displaystyle I_{\ell}=T^{\ell+3}(\ell+2)\int^{\infty}_{0}{dz}\,\frac{z^{\ell+1}}{e^{z}+a}.

(67)

for FD statistics (quarks) or if

C.0.1
Quark sector

The Dirichlet

\displaystyle\eta(s)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^{s}}=\frac{1}{1^{s}}-\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{3^{s}}-\dots

This is convergent for
s>0

\displaystyle\eta(s)=(1-2^{(1-s)})\zeta(s).

(69)

At

is canceled by the zero in the other factor, leading to a finite value of

. Integrals like the following can be expressed in terms of the

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}dz\frac{z^{s-1}e^{z}}{e^{z}+1}=\eta(s)\Gamma(s).

Using this, we can write

\displaystyle I_{\ell}^{(q)}=T^{\ell+3}(\ell+2)\Gamma(\ell+2)\eta(\ell+2),\quad\quad\ell>-2

Using the property

\displaystyle I_{\ell}^{(q)}=T^{\ell+3}\Gamma(\ell+3)(1-2^{-(\ell+1)})\zeta(\ell+2),\quad\quad\ell>-2

C.0.2
Gluon sector

The Riemann zeta function is defined as,

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\frac{1}{1^{s}}+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{3^{s}}+\dots
