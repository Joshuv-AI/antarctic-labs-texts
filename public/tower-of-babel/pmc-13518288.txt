Heterogenous Neuropathology in a Pedigree with RAB39B ‐Related Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Caitlin Latimer, Oswaldo Lorenzo‐Betancor, Dong‐Hui Chen, Kimmy Su, Marika Bogdani, Anna J. Park, Minsuh Kim, Joshua Weiss, Malia Callier, Ella H. Chiu, Sarah Fish, Jennifer L. Witt, Wendy H. Raskind, Marie Y. Davis, C. Dirk Keene, Cyrus P. Zabetian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2015, we reported a family with Parkinson's disease resulting from the RAB39B p.G192R (c.574G>A) variant. Since then, two affected brothers from the family have undergone autopsy.ObjectivesTo characterize neuropathological findings, assess intracellular distribution of RAB39B protein, and examine the effect of p.G192R on α‐synuclein and tau.MethodsDetailed neuropathological assessments were performed on both siblings. Dopaminergic neurons were generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from an affected and unaffected male in this kindred. Western blots were performed to measure RAB39B, α‐synuclein, phospho‐α‐synuclein, and phospho‐tau.ResultsThe younger brother had neocortical stage Lewy body disease (LBD) pathology and an unusual pattern and burden of four‐repeat (4R) tau pathology that included neocortical neurofibrillary tangles, pretangles, and neurites in the absence of β‐amyloid pathology. The older brother's brain showed a similar pattern of 4R tau pathology but had no LBD pathology. Robust RAB39B immunostaining was seen in p.G192R carriers and non‐carriers. In p.G192R iPSC‐derived neurons, RAB39B protein was reduced by ~50% and disproportionately diminished in peripheral cell processes compared with cell bodies. Aberrant forms of both α‐synuclein and tau were observed in p.G192R neurons.ConclusionsThe intrafamilial pathological heterogeneity observed here is unusual for monogenic parkinsonism and suggests that mechanisms underlying RAB39B‐related neurodegeneration might be complex. Our results from iPSC‐neurons provide additional support that RAB39B p.G192R promotes α‐synuclein and tau co‐pathology. Further work in model systems based on RAB39B p.G192R might offer important insights into the interplay between α‐synuclein and tau that are applicable to multiple neurodegenerative disorders. © 2026 The Author(s). Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder and the fastest‐growing neurological condition in the world. 1 Rare variants in the RAB39B gene cause X‐linked intellectual disability 2 and X‐linked dominant classic PD. 3 , 4 , 5 Previously, we described a kindred with typical PD in which the c.574G>A (p.G192R) RAB39B variant co‐segregated with disease. 6

RAB39B protein is part of the human Rab GTPase family, which regulates intracellular vesicular trafficking. RAB39B is neuron‐specific and plays a role in synapse formation and maintenance, 2 , 4 , 7 and α‐synuclein homeostasis. 8 The C‐terminus of Rab proteins contains a hypervariable domain of 35–40 amino acids that plays a major role in targeting each Rab to its appropriate intracellular location. 9 The p.G192R variant is within the hypervariable domain. In vitro studies have shown that mutant RAB39B protein is mislocalized, with less of it in neuritic processes and at the plasma membrane compared with wild‐type protein. 6

Here, we describe the clinical features and neuropathological findings of two affected brothers from this kindred. We characterize the intracellular presence and distribution of RAB39B both in vivo and in vitro. Finally, we examine the effect of p.G192R on the accumulation of aberrant forms of α‐synuclein and tau in vitro.

Methods

Subject Enrollment

The family was enrolled in the Parkinson's Genetic Research Study (PaGeR) at the University of Washington (UW) and VA Puget Sound Health Care System in Seattle. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at both sites and all participants provided written informed consent. Each individual underwent a full neurological examination performed by a movement disorder specialist.

Preparation of Brain Tissue and Immunohistochemistry

Consent for brain donation for research was obtained from the research participant and/or legal next of kin according to the protocols approved by the UW Institutional Review Board. At the time of autopsy, the entire brain was removed in the usual fashion and was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. After fixation, the cerebrum was sliced coronally, the brainstem was sectioned axially, and the cerebellum was sectioned parasagittally. Samples across the following brain regions were collected, processed, and subsequently paraffin‐embedded: middle frontal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, superior temporal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, primary visual cortex, cingulate cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, basal ganglia, thalamus, midbrain, pons, medulla, and cerebellum. A microtome was used to cut 5 μm‐thick tissue sections from the formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. Hematoxylin and eosin/Luxol fast blue (H&E/LFB) and Bielschowsky silver‐stained slides were prepared. Using previously optimized conditions, immunohistochemistry was performed using a Leica Bond III Fully Automated IHC and ISH Staining System (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Further details on immunostaining are provided in the Supplementary Material. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included with each antibody and with each run.

RAB39B Immunostaining

Three age‐ and sex‐matched healthy brain donors, and three age‐ and sex‐matched PD donors without RAB39B variants, were included for RAB39B immunostaining alongside the affected males (Fig. 1: individuals III.10 and III.13). FFPE slides (5 μm) were prepared from substantia nigra, striatum, and frontal cortex and underwent standard dewaxing and rehydration. For further details on tissue preparation and antibody staining see Supplementary Material. Images were captured using a Nikon Ti2 microscope and processed with NIS‐Elements imaging software.

Genetic Analysis

The original report of this family 6 was based on whole exome sequencing (WES) data from individuals III.2, III.6, III.8, III.9, and III.13. For this study we also performed WES on individual III.10 using methods previously described 6 and extracted data for APOE and 28 genes related to neurodegenerative parkinsonian disorders for further analysis: ATP13A2, ATP1A3, DNAJC13, DNAJC6, EIF4G1, FBXO7, GBA1, GCH1, GIGYF2, GRN, HTRA2, LRRK2, MAPT, PARK7, PINK1, PLA2G6, PRKN, RAB39B, SLC6A3, SNCA, SNCB, SYNJ1, TAF1, TARDBP, TH, TMEM230, VPS13C, and VPS35 (gene names, associated phenotypes, and gene coordinates based on genome build GRCh37 are provided in Table S1).

In Vitro Analyses

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of two members of this kindred, an affected male (Fig. 1; individual III.8) who carried the p.G192R variant and his similarly aged, unaffected brother (Fig. 1; individual III.12) who was wild‐type (WT). The iPSCs were generated by the University of Washington Institute for Stem Cell and Regenerative Medicine Ellison Stem Cell Core. All clones were verified by sequence analysis using the following primers: AGGGTGGGTGGGGTAGTTAG and CCTGGACCGCACCTAGAAC. All iPSC clones were screened for mycoplasma (Lonza #LT07‐118) and chromosomal abnormalities by karyotyping (Diagnostic Cytogenetics Incorporated, Seattle, WA, USA or Karyostat, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

iPSCs were differentiated into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and midbrain dopaminergic neurons using STEMCELL Technologies reagents and procedures specified in the Technical Manual of the STEMdiff™ SMADi Neural Induction Kit. Following differentiation, neurons were cultured for 8–10 days before fixation with 4% formaldehyde and antibody staining for various endolysosomal trafficking markers. See Supplementary Methods for details on immunocytochemistry, antibodies used, and quantification of confocal microscopy data. Western blot of iPSC‐neurons was performed per standard methods (see Supplementary Methods for details and antibodies used).

Individual III.10, the older brother, developed a mild postural and kinetic tremor of his hands in his late teens and was diagnosed with essential tremor. At age 53 years his tremor worsened and developed a resting quality. He was first seen by a neurologist at age 56 years who observed asymmetric resting tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. He underwent 123I‐ioflupane single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) dopamine transporter scan (DaTscan), which showed decreased radiotracer uptake in the striatum consistent with nigrostriatal dopaminergic denervation. He was diagnosed with PD and started on rasagiline. Pramipexole was later added, followed by carbidopa/levodopa with substantial improvement of motor symptoms. With disease progression, he developed motor fluctuations and dyskinesias, as well as several non‐motor symptoms including cognitive impairment, mood changes, psychosis with hallucinations, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder. He died at age 65 years.

Individual III.13, the younger brother, had a history of hypertension. He developed right‐hand resting tremor at age 29 years and was diagnosed with PD at age 31 years. He was started on carbidopa/levodopa with good response but later developed severe dyskinesias. He underwent bilateral pallidotomy at age 33 years, followed by bilateral subthalamic nucleus (STN) deep brain stimulation (DBS) implantation at age 47 years. With advancing disease, he struggled with significant dystonia, bradykinesia, dysarthria, cognitive and behavioral issues, and died at age 54 years.

RAB39B Expression and Localization

Double immunofluorescent staining of RAB39B and MAP2 in the substantia nigra of healthy controls, sporadic PD donors without RAB39B variants, and affected individual III.10 showed strong expression of RAB39B diffusely throughout the cytoplasm of MAP2‐positive neurons (Fig. 3). However, in individual III.13 RAB39B protein formed aggregates along the plasma membrane of nigral neuronal cell bodies and axons (Fig. 3).

Genetic Analyses

Other than RAB39B p.G192R, no mutations were found in the 28 genes related to neurodegenerative parkinsonism that were screened in individual III.10. The APOE genotype for individuals III.10 and III.13 was 2/3. Individual III.10 carried the H1/H2 MAPT haplotype while individual III.13 was an H1/H1 carrier.

In Vitro Analyses

To examine the effect of RAB39B p.G192R on a cellular level, iPSCs were generated from affected individual III.8 (p.G192R) and unaffected individual III.12 (WT). Dopaminergic neurons were successfully differentiated from p.G192R and WT iPSCs (Fig. 4A). RAB39B protein level was reduced by approximately 50% in p.G192R iPSC‐neurons compared with WT (Fig. 4B). We examined the subcellular localization of RAB39B in WT and p.G192R neurons, as prior studies in catecholaminergic cell lines indicated that p.G192R resulted in greatly reduced amounts of RAB39B protein in neuronal projections. We quantified the ratio of RAB39B antibody staining in the cell body in comparison to staining of the whole cell (Fig. 4C). In p.G192R iPSCs a significantly greater proportion of RAB39B protein was localized to the cell body rather than to peripheral neuronal projections. Because of the tau and α‐synuclein co‐pathology seen in individual III.13 we sought to determine if iPSCs with p.G192R accumulate abnormal forms of these two proteins. Western blots of Triton X‐100 insoluble lysates of iPSC neurons revealed high and low molecular weight aggregates of α‐synuclein (Fig. 4D), high and low molecular weight aggregates of phospho‐pSer129 α‐synuclein (Fig. 4E), and high molecular weight phospho‐tau (Fig. 4F) in p.G192R but not WT neurons.

Discussion

Here we describe the clinical characteristics and neuropathological findings of two siblings with RAB39B p.G192R‐related PD. These siblings experienced a different clinical onset but shared common clinical features suggestive of synucleinopathy. Individual III.10 initially exhibited features of essential tremor as a teenager until his early 50s, at which point he developed motor parkinsonism supported by an abnormal DaTscan, while his younger sibling (individual III.13) presented with motor parkinsonism in his late 20s. Both siblings had a robust response to levodopa, unilateral onset with continued asymmetry of motor symptoms, and in later stages of disease dyskinesias, motor fluctuations, cognitive decline, and psychosis.

Although the Lewy body pathology was discrepant between the two cases, cortical 4R tau pathology was a prominent feature in both siblings. The pattern, while overlapping with features of several well‐described tauopathies, was not definitive for any one entity. Tau pathology in Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by a mixture of 3R and 4R isoforms, and the absence of β‐amyloid pathology in both cases argues against Alzheimer's disease neuropathologic change (ADNC). Given the lack of β‐amyloid, primary age‐related tauopathy (PART) is a consideration. PART is defined as a medial temporal lobe‐predominant 3R/4R tauopathy corresponding to Braak stages I–IV in the absence (definite PART) or near‐absence (possible PART) of β‐amyloid pathology. 13 Although the older brother demonstrated mixed 3R/4R tau involving the hippocampus, which could reflect a component of PART, the younger brother showed predominantly 4R tau with greater neocortical than hippocampal involvement, an atypical pattern for classic PART.

Scattered foci of thorny astrocytes consistent with aging‐related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG) 14 were present in both siblings. However, glial tau lesions characteristic of other primary tauopathies, including tufted astrocytes, astroglial plaques, and oligodendroglial coiled bodies, were not identified. The overall distribution, particularly the prominent neocortical involvement, is atypical for classic argyrophilic grain disease (AGD), although an element of AGD cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, although a single perivascular sulcal depth lesion was identified in the older brother, in the setting of extensive age‐related and/or disease‐related tauopathy it remains uncertain whether this represents an incidental CTE‐type lesion, a nonspecific perivascular tau focus, or overlap pathology. Neither of the siblings had a history of even a single head injury (Table 1). Overall, the cortical 4R tau pathology does not fit neatly within a single defined tauopathy and may represent overlapping or mixed processes. The reason for the disparity in LBD pathology in the two siblings reported here is not clear. No additional variants in genes related to PD or related disorders were found in either individual. The fact that individual III.13 with both LBD and tau pathology had a much longer disease course than his sibling (III.10), who lacked LBD (25 vs. 12 years), suggests that RAB39B p.G192R‐related PD tauopathy might occur at an early stage coincident with nigral neuronal loss whereas α‐synucleinopathy could occur at later stages. In support of this hypothesis, there is some evidence that tau pathology might precede LBD in LRRK2‐related PD. Henderson and colleagues performed a detailed neuropathological assessment of nine PD brains with LRRK2 G2019S and found that only five had LBD, but all cases had some tau pathology. 15 The mean disease duration for the four cases that lacked LBD was substantially shorter (13 years) than the five LBD‐positive cases (20.8 years). Unlike the two siblings in our family, the tau pathology in all nine cases was consistent with AD‐type tau (using a conformation‐selective anti‐tau antibody that requires 3R and 4R isoforms for binding) and abundant β‐amyloid pathology was seen in most cases. For monogenic forms of neurodegenerative parkinsonism, a marked degree of intrafamilial heterogeneity in neuropathological findings among affected carriers is uncommon. In contrast, marked differences between unrelated individuals have often been reported for several genes including LRRK2 and PRKN, even for individuals who carry the same pathogenic variant. 16 , 17 For example, a case series of autopsied patients with LRRK2‐related PD found that 46% (17/37) of cases with any pathogenic variant, and 65% (11/17) of cases with p.G2019S, had LBD pathology. 17 Two autopsy series of PRKN‐related PD showed that 3/8 (38%) 18 and 2/5 (40%) 16 patients had LBD which was limbic stage or lower.

RAB39B‐related PD kindreds are extremely rare. 5 , 6 , 19 , 20 Only three families have been described worldwide excluding ours: an Australian family with a 45 kb deletion on the long arm of chromosome X that encompassed RAB39B, 3 a Wisconsin kindred with a missense mutation (c.503C>A; p.T168K) causing loss of function, 4 and a Chinese family with a frameshift mutation (c.536dupA; p.E179fsX48). 5 The clinical findings of our kindred differ from those of the three previously reported kindreds with RAB39B‐related parkinsonism. The affected individuals in these three kindreds displayed varying degrees of intellectual disability, seizures, and juvenile parkinsonism with variable response to levodopa. Unlike these three kindreds, the affected individuals in our family exhibited a classic PD phenotype without atypical features.

Neuropathological findings from only two patients with RAB39B‐related PD have been published to date, one from the Australian and one from the Wisconsin kindred described above. The individual from the Australian family underwent autopsy after death at age 48 years. 3 His brain showed neuronal loss and the presence of LBs and Lewy neurites in the substantia nigra and locus coeruleus and an abundance of cortical LBs. Tau pathology was observed but was limited to a few surviving pigmented substantia nigra neurons. The individual from the Wisconsin kindred, a RAB39B p.T168K carrier, underwent autopsy after death at age 51 years. 4 There was neuronal loss in the substantia nigra, and LBs and Lewy neurites were present in the substantia nigra, hippocampus, and thalamus. Tau pathology was only observed in the thalamus, in the form of neurofibrillary tangles. 4 Thus, the Australian and Wisconsin kindreds showed widespread LBD but very limited tau pathology, while the two affected siblings from our family both displayed unusual and extensive cortical and brainstem 4R tau pathology but were discordant for α‐synuclein pathology with LBD (neocortical stage) in one sibling but not the other.

The clinical and neuropathological differences between the Australian and Wisconsin kindreds and our family might be due in part to differences in the mechanism and degree to which each variant disrupts RAB39B expression and function. For example, whereas the variants in these two kindreds result in total or near‐total lack of protein expression, 3 , 4 p.G192R induces more modest reductions in RAB39B protein as shown by Western blots of iPSC‐neurons (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, in iPSC‐derived neurons expressing endogenous WT or p.G192R RAB39B we found an alteration in subcellular localization of the mutant protein. Expression of p.G192R RAB39B was increased in the cell body and decreased in the cell projections (Fig. 4C), while expression of WT RAB39B was distributed more evenly throughout the neuron. This is consistent with prior work in which PC12 and SK‐N‐BE(2)C cells transfected with p.G192R or WT constructs of human RAB39B displayed altered intracellular localization, with less expression of RAB39B in the neuronal cell projections and enrichment of expression in the cell body. 6 p.G192R is located within the RAB39B hypervariable domain that is responsible for proper intracellular localization. We speculate that disruption of the necessary effector protein interactions results in the protein mislocalization we observed here.

RAB39B dysregulation might also play a role in the pathogenesis of idiopathic LBD. Koss and colleagues examined banked brains from donors with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), AD, and controls. 21 RAB39B protein was reduced in the cytoplasmic pool of DLB cases and it co‐localized with LBs and β‐amyloid plaques. The authors hypothesized that progressive reduction of functional RAB39B due to sequestration within age‐associated aggregates impedes RAB39B‐associated functions such as α‐synuclein homeostasis and maintenance of synaptic integrity. This in turn leads to further LB formation and synaptic impairment.

There is a growing appreciation of the interplay between tau and α‐synuclein in driving neurodegenerative disease and RAB39B p.G192R‐related PD represents a convergence of these two pathologies. Studies in model systems have shown that α‐synuclein oligomers/fibrils enhance tau misfolding, and tau overexpression promotes α‐synuclein aggregation and toxicity. 22 , 23 This “cross‐seeding” has been proposed to create a feed‐forward cycle accelerating the spread of pathological aggregates and neurodegeneration. 23 One criticism of this work is that the models used were highly artificial, which might limit their translational relevance to human neurodegenerative diseases. 22 In vitro and in vivo studies of RAB39B p.G192R might elucidate shared mechanisms across α‐synucleinopathies and tauopathies in a more physiologically relevant manner without the need to introduce or overexpress exogenous or pathological forms of α‐synuclein or tau. We are embarking on studies in RAB39B p.G192R iPSCs to address several key questions including: (1) whether aberrant forms of tau begin to accumulate before abnormal α‐synuclein and (2) whether tau and α‐synuclein are trafficked through the endolysosomal system independently or are co‐localized with one another.
