There is substantial evidence that many Fano manifolds can be obtained by smoothing singular toric Fano varieties, or by smoothing toroidal crossing spaces, e.g.,

In order to identify the Fano manifold obtained from a smoothing, one needs suitable invariants and the intersection form on the second cohomology is a good candidate. For example, a Fano threefold hypersurface can be uniquely identified from its triple intersection form together with the index.
This work is part of a project to compute the 3-form on the second coholomology of all Fano threefolds as well as known higher dimensional Fano manifolds. We focus here on hypersurfaces in weighted projective spaces. Fanos of such form are of current interest
[
JK01
]
,
[

,
[

. Pieter Belmans kindly informed us that this intersection 3-form already featured in Mori—Mukai ”Classification of Fano 3-folds with
B_{2}\geq 2
”
[
MM81
]

and was used by Sergey Galkin
[
Gal18
]
.

It is straightforward to compute this 3-form on Fano manifolds which are hypersurfaces in ordinary projective space by using the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem
[
Mil63
]
. The hyperplane theorem states that, for a general hyperplane section of an
n
-dimensional projective variety, pullback under the inclusion map is an isomorphism for cohomology groups up to degree
n-2
, and a surjection in degree
n-1
. Using the Veronese embedding, one can consider a regular hypersurface as a hyperplane section. Therefore, we may apply the Lefschetz theorem and get that the 3-form on the hypersurface is the same as the intersection form of the ambient space just multiplied by the degree of the hypersurface.

However, the classic Lefschetz theorem does not hold for weighted projective spaces, because it requires the complement of a hypersurface to be smooth, but weighted projective spaces are singular.
Weighted projective spaces are toric varieties and Batyrev and Cox
[
BC93
]
generalized the Lefschetz theorem to hypersurfaces in toric varieties, but only for the field coefficients while we need the result for integer coefficients. A similar statement can be made about the work by Mavlyutov
[
Mav00
]
who considered complete intersections.

We provide an analogue of Lefschetz theorem for hypersurfaces in weighted projective spaces and cohomology with integer coefficients:

Theorem

(Theorem
6.1
below)
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}=\mathbb{P}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
be a weighted projective space with pairwise coprime weights. Let
X
be a
general hypersurface of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
of degree
d
which is divided by all
q_{i}
. And let
i\colon X\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be the inclusion map. Then for
k<n-1

(1)

H^{k}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if $k$ is even}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

for even
k=2r
the pullback map

i^{*}\colon H^{2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H^{2r}(X,\mathbb{Z})

is multiplication by
\frac{l_{r}\cdot l_{n-r}}{l_{n}}
, where

is the integer derived from weights
(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
(for precise definition see

As one can see, the pullback is no longer an isomorphism. This occurs because relative Poincaré duality — which underpins the Lefschetz theorem — requires field coefficients and smooth manifolds, where the cup product between cohomology groups in complementary degrees gives a perfect pairing. In our case, it does not. Instead, we use the extraordinary cup product for cohomology with support and Alexander duality with non-field coefficients.

We use this result to compute the intersection form on second cohomology of Fano hypersurfaces in weighted projective spaces of any dimension:

n

(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\to\frac{l_{n+1}^{n-1}\cdot d}{l_{n}^{n}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}

In dimensions 3, 4, and 5, the intersection form and index uniquely distinguish each hypersurface. However, in dimension 6, we exhibit an example of two hypersurfaces with different topology that share the same intersection form and index, indicating that these invariants are insufficient to uniquely identify Fano hypersurfaces in higher dimensions.

2.
Cohomology of weighted projective spaces

We will only deal with spaces over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. We will denote the multiplicative group of this field by
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

Definition 2.1

(Weighted projective space)
.

Let
q=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
with
q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and define the corresponding action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
by

\lambda\cdot(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=(\lambda^{q_{0}}x_{0},\ldots,\lambda^{q_{n}}x_{n}).

The quotient of this action is a
weighted projective space
. It is a projective variety. We will denote it as
\mathbb{P}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
or
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.

Example 2.2
.

\mathbb{P}(1,\ldots,1)
is the ordinary complex projective space.

As in ordinary projective space, we can use homogeneous coordinates
(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n})
and define a
hypersurface of degree

d
as the zero locus of a weighted homogeneous polynomial of a weighted degree
d
.

We are interested in integer cohomology of hypersurfaces in weighted projective spaces. We first need to understand the cohomology ring of the weighted projective space itself.

To describe the cohomology ring of a weighted projective space, we need to introduce one combinatorial definition.

Definition 2.3
.

For each subset
I=\{i_{0},\ldots,i_{k}\}
of
\{0,\ldots,n\}
denote

l_{I}=\frac{i_{0}\cdot\ldots\cdot i_{k}}{\gcd(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})}

Define
l_{r}
as

l_{r}=\lcm\{l_{I}\mid I\subset\{0,\ldots,n\}\text{ and }|I|=r+1\}

Example 2.4
.

l_{0}=1
,
l_{1}=\lcm\{q_{0},\ldots,q_{n}\}
,
l_{n}=q_{0}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{n}/\gcd(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})

With this definition in place, we now recall two theorems from
[
Al 97
]
, which completely describe the cohomology ring of weighted projective spaces.

Theorem 2.5
.

For a weighted projective space
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
we have

H^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\mathbb{Z})\simeq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if $i=2r$, $0\leq r\leq n$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof.

[
Al 97
]

∎

3.
Fano hypersurfaces in weighted projective spaces

Weighted projective spaces are toric varieties.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
RT13
]
)
.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be the basis of the lattice
N
and
(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
the set of positive integers. Consider the following n + 1 rational vectors

v_{0}=-\frac{1}{q_{0}}\sum e_{i},\quad v_{i}=\frac{e_{i}}{q_{i}}.

Let
N_{Q}
be the lattice generated by
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n}
and consider the fan
\Sigma_{Q}
generated by
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n}
. Then the toric variety corresponding to
\Sigma_{Q}
is the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
.

We are interested in smooth hypersurfaces in a weighted projective space. Therefore, we need the singular locus of
\mathbb{P}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
to be 0-dimensional, so that the generic hypersurface would not meet the singularities.

Proposition 3.2
.

\mathbb{P}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
have at worst isolated singularities if and only if weights
q_{0},\ldots,q_{n}
are pairwise coprime.

Proof.

If there are
q_{k},q_{l}
with
gcd(q_{k},q_{l})>1
then the
n-1
-dimensional convex cone spanned by
\langle v_{0},\ldots,\check{v}_{k},\ldots,\check{v}_{l},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle
is non-smooth. Therefore, the singularity has positive dimension. If there are no such pairs, then the only non-smooth cones are of dimension
n
.
∎

But this condition is not enough. For some degrees, any hypersurface of this degree will pass through a singular point of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
X
be a hypersurface of degree 3 in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(1,1,1,2)
. In homogeneous coordinates it is given by equation

F(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})+x_{3}\cdot G(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=0

where
F,G
are homogeneous polynomials of degrees 3 and 1, respectively. The unique singular point of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(1,1,1,2)
is
(0:0:0:1)
. Both
F(0,0,0)
and
G(0,0,0)
equal zero and therefore

F(0,0,0)+x_{3}\cdot G(0,0,0)=0

so any hypersurface of degree 3 passes through the singular point.

Proposition 3.4
.

For the surface not to pass through the singularities of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, the degree should allow monomials
x_{i}^{k}
for each non-trivial
q_{i}
. In other words, the degree should be divided by all weights
q_{i}
.

Some of those smooth hypersurfaces happen to be Fano.

Definition 3.5
.

A
Fano manifold
is a smooth projective variety whose anticanonical bundle is ample.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
JK01
]
)
.

Let
X
be a hypersurface of degree
d
in weighted projective space
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
which has at worst isolated singularities. It is Fano if and only if
d<\sum q_{i}
.

Let us now look at some examples. For 3-dimensional hypersurfaces in 4-dimensional weighted projective space combining Lemmas
3.2
,
3.4
and
3.6
we are left with 5 hypersurfaces: degree 2 and 4 in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,2)
, degree 3 and 6 in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,3)
, and degree 6 in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,2,3)
.
Note that the hypersurface of degree 2 in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,2)
and the hypersurface of degree 3 in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,3)
are just
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. So there are exactly three Fano threefolds of such form.

4.
Some homological algebra

To overcome difficulties related to the singularities of the weighted projective space, we will use sheaf cohomology. We quote lemmas and adapt the notations from Dimca’s ”Sheaves in topology”
[
Dim04
, chapter 1.4]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category.
Then let
C(\mathcal{A})
be the category of complexes,
K(\mathcal{A})
be its homotopy category,
D(\mathcal{A})
be the derived category.

Let
X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}\in D(\mathcal{A})
. Denote by
X^{\bullet}[n]
the shift by
n
, in other words
X^{k}[n]=X^{k+n}

Lemma 4.1
.

Then
\Ext^{n}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet})\simeq Hom_{D(\mathcal{A})}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}[n])
for all integers
n
.

Lemma 4.2
.

H^{n}(Hom^{\bullet}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}))\simeq Hom_{K(\mathcal{A})}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}[n])
for all integers n.

Denote the category of modules on the ring
A
as
mod(A)
. We will say that a complex
X^{\bullet}\in C(mod(A))
is free (injective) if every object in it is free (injective).

Condition 4.3
.

Assume that either:

(1)

X^{\bullet}\in C^{-}(mod(A))
and
Y^{\bullet}\in C^{+}(mod(A))
, or

(2)

X^{\bullet}\in C(mod(A))
and
Y^{\bullet}\in C^{b}(mod(A))
, or

(3)

one of (1) or (2) above holds when we interchange
X^{\bullet}
and
Y^{\bullet}.

Theorem 4.4

(Universal coefficients)
.

Let
A
be a principal ideal domain.

Let
X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}\in C(mod(A))
be complexes satisfying Condition
4.3
. If moreover
X^{\bullet}
is free or
Y^{\bullet}
is injective, then for any integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
we have the following short exact sequence:

0\to\bigoplus_{q-p=m-1}\Ext(H^{p}(X^{\bullet}),H^{q}(Y^{\bullet}))\to H^{m}(\Hom^{\bullet}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}))\to\\
\bigoplus_{q-p=m}\Hom(H^{p}(X^{\bullet}),H^{q}(Y^{\bullet}))\to 0.

In particular, if
A\to Y^{\bullet}
is a bounded injective resolution, then we have for any complex
X^{\bullet}
and any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
the following exact sequence:

0\to\Ext^{1}(H^{m+1}(X^{\bullet}),A)\to H^{-m}(\Hom^{\bullet}(X^{\bullet},Y^{\bullet}))\to\Hom(H^{m}(X^{\bullet}),A)\to 0.

Cohomology with supports in a closed subvariety

Let
Z
be a closed subvariety of
X
and denote
i\colon Z\to X
the corresponding closed immersion. Then one can define cohomology with supports in
Z
(sometimes called

cohomology).

Definition 5.1
.

Define
\Gamma_{Z}(X,\mathcal{F})
to be the set of sections of
\mathcal{F}
with support in
Z

\Gamma_{Z}(X,\mathcal{F}):=\{s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})|\Supp(s)\subseteq Z\}

Then
\Gamma_{Z}(X,-)
is a left exact functor.
Cohomology with supports in

Z
and coefficients in
\mathcal{F}

H_{Z}^{m}(X,\mathcal{F})

m

\Gamma_{Z}(X,-)

We will soon see how cohomology with supports in
Z

\Gamma_{Z}(X,\mathcal{F})\simeq Hom(i_{!}A_{Z},\mathcal{F})

5.2.
Alexander duality

We will recall the Verdier’s duality first.

Theorem 5.6

(Verdier’s duality)
.

For a continuous mapping
f\colon X\to Y
there is an additive functor of triangulated categories
f^{!}\colon D^{+}(Y)\to D^{+}(X)
, such that

RHom^{\bullet}(Rf_{!}\mathcal{F}^{\bullet},\mathcal{G}^{\bullet})\simeq RHom^{\bullet}(\mathcal{F}^{\bullet},f^{!}\mathcal{G}^{\bullet})

in
D^{+}(mod(A))
. In particular, we have

Hom_{D^{+}(Y)}(Rf_{!}\mathcal{F}^{\bullet},\mathcal{G}^{\bullet})\simeq Hom_{D^{+}(X)}(\mathcal{F}^{\bullet},f^{!}\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}).

In other words,
f^{!}
is right adjoint to the
Rf_{!}
.

Remark 5.7
.

Verdier’s duality is a more general form of Poincare duality and is compatible with the cup product defined with
\Ext
as above.

Denote
a_{X}:X\to pt
and
\omega_{X}=a_{X}^{!}(A)

\omega_{X}\simeq A_{X}[n]

(1)

H^{k}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if $k$ is even}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

for even
k=2r
the pullback map

i^{*}\colon H^{2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H^{2r}(X,\mathbb{Z})

is multiplication by
\frac{l_{r}\cdot l_{n-r}}{l_{n}}
.

Proof.

Consider the long exact sequence for the cohomology with support in
X
:

\to H^{k-1}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\to

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X
is affine so it has a homotopy type of an
n
-dimensional CW complex
[
K79
]
. Therefore, for all
k>n
we have
H^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})=0
and the map from
H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})
to
H^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})
is an isomorphism for
k>n+1
. Therefore

H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})\cong H^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})\text{ for }k>n+1

We required such set of weights and such a degree that generic hypersurface
X
is smooth and does not pass through the singularities of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. Therefore, we can replace
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with a smooth tubular neighborhood of
X
to compute
H^{k}_{X}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}

[
Ive86
]
[p.125].
It is an orientable manifold of dimension
2n
, so we can apply the Alexander duality Theorem
5.8
and obtain the exact sequence

(6.1)

0\to\Ext(H^{2n-k+1}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}}),\mathbb{Z})\to H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{2n-k}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}\to 0.

Let
k>n+1
.

If
k
is odd, then
H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})=0
, therefore
H^{2n-k}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}=0
and so
H^{2n-k}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})=0
.

If
k
is even, then
H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})=\mathbb{Z}
, therefore
H^{2n-k}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}
can be either
0
,
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
. Notice that
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
can not be dual of anything, so we are left with two options. So the exact sequence (
6.1
) can either be

0\to 0\to\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\to 0

or

0\to\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\to 0\to 0

For every finitely generated abelian group
A
,
\Ext_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,\mathbb{Z})
is torsion, so (
6.1
) is isomorphic to
0\to 0\to\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\to 0
and
H_{X}^{k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq H^{2n-k}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}
.
That proves the first part of the theorem.

Let us prove the second statement. For even
k
set
2r=k
. Due to Lemma
5.5
, there is a commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2r}(X,\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n-2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n-2r}_{X}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\underline{\mathbb{Z}})^{\vee}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

The map

is determined completely by the map from
H^{2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})
to
H^{2n-2r}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n})^{\vee}
. Using (
2.1
) one can see that it is multiplication by
\frac{l_{r}\cdot l_{n-r}}{l_{n}}
.

7.
Application: The intersection form on second cohomology of Fano manyfold

Consider a general hypersurface
X
of weighted degree
d
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
with at worst isolated singularities. It is a
2n
-dimensional manifold, so the cup product defines an
n
-form in the second cohomology group:

H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\otimes\ldots\otimes H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2n}(X,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}

Note that
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}
due to the theorem
6.1
, so this
n
-form would be simply

(7.1)

(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\to k\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\alpha_{n},

where
k
is some integer.

Corollary 7.1
.

For
X
a general smooth hypersurface of degree
d
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
, the
n
-form on the second cohomology induced by the cup product is given by:

(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\to\frac{l_{n+1}^{n-1}\cdot d}{l_{n}^{n}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}

Proof.

Recall the map
\phi\colon\mathbb{P}^{n+1}\to\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
sending
(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n+1})
to
\phi(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n+1})=(x_{0}^{q_{1}}:\ldots:x_{n+1}^{q_{n+1}})
from the Theorem
2.6
.

Denote
X^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}(X)
- the preimage of
X
under map
\phi
. Note that
X^{\prime}
is the hypersurface of degree
d
but in the ordinary projective space. Then the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}

leads to the commutative diagram in cohomology

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(X^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n}(X^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{*}}

where maps from
H^{2}
to

are described

We can compute some of the maps in the diagram:

•

are given by Theorem
2.6
;

•

the map

is multiplication by degree of a map
\phi
and equals the

due to Theorem
2.7
;

•

the map from
H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1})
to

is isomorphism due to the Lefschetz theorem;

•

the map from
H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1})
to

X^{\prime}

•

and the map

is multiplication by
\frac{l_{1}\cdot l_{n}}{l_{n+1}}
by the Theorem

.

Putting it all in the diagram above we have:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1})}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot\frac{l_{1}\cdot l_{n}}{l_{n+1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot l_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot l_{n+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot l_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha\to d\cdot\alpha^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha\to\alpha^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot d}

This information is enough to compute all remaining maps in the diagram, including the desired

-form.
