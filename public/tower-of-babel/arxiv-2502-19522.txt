Contrasting Cost-Agnostic and Cost-Sensitive Losses under Limited Model Capacity via ℋ \mathcal{H} -Consistency
Jessie Finocchiaro finocch@bc.edu, Sanket Shah sanketshah@g.harvard.edu, Milind Tambe tambe@g.harvard.edu

Abstract
There is a prevalent debate in machine learning about whether practitioners should train models to optimize a task-agnostic objective (e.g., cross entropy) or incorporate the downstream decision task into the optimization objective (e.g., weighted cross entropy). In ideal settings, like those with infinite data and infinite model capacity, the two approaches are statistically equivalent for the downstream decision task. In practice, however, incorporating the decision task into model training has been shown to empirically improve task-specific performance in certain real-world scenarios. The cause of these benefits has not been theoretically studied to date. Focusing on the setting with limited model capacity through the model class ℋ \mathcal{H} , we establish a strict performance gap between post-processing a model learned with a cost-agnostic objective (e.g., thresholding a risk score prediction) and models learned by optimizing a cost-sensitive model without a threshold search. In particular, we establish this gap when there is a hypothesis recovering the optimal decision boundary for the discrete task, but it does not align with the optimal cost-agnostic hypothesis, and give a simple example demonstrating the plausibility of this setting. With assumptions that are hard to verify in practice, we demonstrate that this gap generally exists on classification datasets from the UCI repository, particularly with very simple models.

Introduction

In training machine learning models for discrete prediction tasks, the practitioner faces a plethora of design decisions.
One of these choices is the loss function used as a training objective: should loss functions be hand-crafted for a “cost-sensitive” decision task at hand, or does it suffice to post-process a model trained on a generic loss like cross entropy?
The debate about this choice is longstanding; the former camp argues that, when faced with the inevitability of imperfect prediction, optimizing a custom surrogate will yield better predictions for the task at hand
(
Vapnik, 2000
;
Scott, 2012
;
Ramaswamy et al., 2016
;
Wilder et al., 2019
;
Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022
;
Shah et al., 2022
)
.
In contrast, the other camp argues that one can often adapt the model for the task at hand with clever post-processing while generalizing to unknown decision-makers
(
Dawid, 1982
;
Seidenfeld, 1985
;
Buja et al., 2005
;
Reid & Williamson, 2009
;
Reid & Williamson, 2011
;
Gopalan et al., 2022
)
.
As an example of such a cost-sensitive decision task, suppose the cost of a false positive is much higher than the cost of a false negative in a binary classification problem.
For example, an IT team coping with phishing attacks will probably prefer to cope with false positives (with consequences of some genuine e-mails being flagged as spam) than false negatives (leading to data breaches arising from successful phishing attacks) in a spam detection system.
In that case, one might have a threshold
\tau\in[0,1]
in mind where they want to flag an email as spam if a model predicts
\Pr[
spam
\mid
email contents
]\geq\tau
.

In “nice” settings where perfect prediction is possible, it is known that the two approaches are statistically equivalent
(
Frongillo & Kash, 2021
)
.
However, in practice, the decision-focused learning literature has empirically improved task-specific performance by designing custom loss functions
(
Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022
;
Wilder et al., 2019
)
.
We examine the gap induced by loss function choice in the setting of cost-sensitive classification from a theoretical perspective.
Here, cost-sensitive classification refers to a setting where “costs” for different types of errors (e.g., false positives and negatives) are written as a cost matrix
\ell
.
Our goal in this work is not to advocate for cost-sensitive surrogates over cost-agnostic ones generally, but rather to serve as a first step towards characterizing settings in which the choice of surrogate training loss affects the quality of decision-theoretic performance.
While we show positive results for cost-sensitive surrogates, we also note that cost-agnostic losses have their place.
For example, omnipredictors
(
Gopalan et al., 2022
)
enable post-processing of a model’s predictions for a broad class of decision-makers, which is a benefit sacrificed by selecting a cost-sensitive training loss.

We establish a gap between the performance of models trained on cost-sensitive vs agnostic losses in the setting of limited model capacity through the lens of
\mathcal{H}
-consistency.

\mathcal{H}
-consistency examines whether learning the best model in a
hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}
with respect to a training loss
L
implies making the best decision for a cost-sensitive target problem
\ell
.
We show that, for “small enough” model classes
\mathcal{H}
(relative to the data-generating distribution
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
), there are instances where a cost-sensitive surrogate
L^{cs}
is
\mathcal{H}
-consistent with respect to the target problem
\ell
while a canonical cost-agnostic surrogate
L^{ag}
is not
\mathcal{H}
-consistent,
regardless of post-processing choices
.

Concretely, we construct a counterexample giving some intuition for our setting in
Section

2
.
With this counterexample, we show in
Section

4
that a cost-agnostic surrogate (e.g., cross entropy, squared error) plus post-processing are not
\mathcal{H}
-consistent, and can fail to perform well for the cost-sensitive target.
In contrast, we evaluate the
\mathcal{H}
-consistency of a class of surrogates called embeddings
(
Finocchiaro et al., 2024
)
.
These task-specific losses in
Section

5
include piecewise linear losses like the weighted hinge.
By “corresponding exactly to the optimal decision for
\ell
,” we show these losses yield
\mathcal{H}
-consistency guarantees in the same setting as
section

4
.
In
Section

6
we analyze weighted analogs of canonical cost-agnostic surrogates (e.g., Weighted Cross Entropy, Squared Error, etc.), and show that even in binary outcome settings, stronger assumptions are needed to establish
\mathcal{H}
-consistency.
Our theoretical results establish a strict gap between the performance of small ML models trained on cost-sensitive surrogates and those trained on cost-agnostic surrogate losses, even when the cost-agnostic losses are equipped with a threshold search to post-process model predictions.
Finally, in
Section

7
, we empirically evaluate the existence of this performance gap when distributional assumptions cannot be verified.

2.1
Characterizing Limited Model Capacity

To clarify what we mean by “small” model classes or “limited model capacity” throughout our paper, we lean on
Example

1
.
The notion of “small enough” in our work leverages the separation that the
\ell
-optimal model is contained in
\mathcal{H}
, but the
L^{ag}
-optimal classifier is not contained in
\mathcal{H}
.

The general approach takes the following steps: (1) given a loss
L
, find its Bayes optimal classifier
h^{L}(x)\equiv\Gamma(p(x))
, through the property
\Gamma
elicited by
L
(
Section

3.2
).
(2) Diagnose if
h^{L}\in\mathcal{H}
, and
(3a) if
h^{L}\in\mathcal{H}
and
h^{L}=h^{\ell}
,
Steinwart (2007, Theorem 2.8)
proves that
\mathcal{H}
-calibration and
\mathcal{H}
-consistency are equivalent.
We can proceed with a pointwise analysis of the optimal decision via a sufficient condition called
\mathcal{H}
-calibration (
Appendix

B
).
(3b) If
h^{L}\in\mathcal{H}
but
h^{L}\neq h^{\ell}
, the path to
\mathcal{H}
-consistency still might hold through distributional assumptions (e.g.,
Assumption

3
and
Assumption

4
).
(3c) If
h^{L}\not\in\mathcal{H}
, analysis becomes complex, but previous results have bounded consistency rates in terms of the
minimizability gap
and proceeded with analysis in the manner of (3a)
(
Mao et al., 2025
;
Mao et al., 2024a
)
, folding in the minimizability gap as an additive regret term.
While some literature does proceed by assuming the conditions of Step (3a), we generally do not assume
h^{L}=h^{\ell}
, instead establishing that the condition holds for a class of piecewise linear surrogates.

To gain intuition for this approach, consider
Example

1
, with the class
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}
: the
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}
-optimal classifier for the
unweighted cross entropy
is
h^{ag}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x)=\rho(x_{1})
, which is independent of
\alpha
.
This hypothesis
h^{ag}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}
stands in contrast to the
\ell^{\alpha}
-optimal decision boundary with slope that is a function of
\alpha
.
With
h^{ag}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x)=\rho(x_{1})
, we claim there is no threshold
\tau
such that
\rho(x_{1})\geq 2\tau-1\iff\sigma(\frac{2x_{1}}{x_{2}})\geq\alpha
, and therefore we cannot recover optimal decisions via
h^{ag}
.

In contrast, consider the weighted hinge:
L^{cs}(r,y)=[(1-\alpha)\mathbf{1}(y=1)+(\alpha)\mathbf{1}(y=-1)]\max(1-ry,0)
.
The
L^{cs}
-optimal for weighted hinge in this case is
h^{cs}(x)=\mathrm{sign}(p(x)-\alpha)
.
Consider the linear score
h^{cs}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x)=2w_{1}x_{1}-\mathrm{logit}(\alpha)w_{1}x_{2}=2w_{1}x_{1}-\log(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha})w_{1}x_{2}
, so that
\rho(h^{cs}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x))\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}
(
Section

3.3
).
With some arithmetic manipulation, we obtain
\rho(h^{cs}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x))\to h^{cs}(x)
pointwise a.e. as

.
Moreover,
h^{cs}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x)\geq 0\iff\sigma(\frac{2x_{1}}{x_{2}})\geq\alpha\iff p(x)-\alpha\geq 0
, and therefore taking the sign of

yields the

-optimal decisions for this cost-sensitive problem.
The additional strength of this weighted hinge is that
\mathrm{sign}(h^{cs}_{{\mathrm{lin}}}(x))
coincides with the optimal decision for the cost matrix
\ell^{\alpha}
.

Finally, consider weighted cross entropy:
L^{\alpha}(r,1)=-(1-\alpha)\log(r)
and
L^{\alpha}(r,-1)=-\alpha\log(1-r)

h^{\alpha}(x)=\frac{(1-\alpha)\sigma(\frac{2x_{1}}{x_{2}})}{\alpha+\sigma(\frac{2x_{1}}{x_{2}})-2\alpha\sigma(\frac{2x_{1}}{x_{2}})}

3
Background and Related Work

We are primarily concerned with minimizing expected loss, where data is sampled from a distribution
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
over features
\mathcal{X}
and labels
\mathcal{Y}
, with
|\mathcal{Y}|
finite.
We often denote with
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
an arbitrary distribution over labels
\mathcal{Y}
.
When
P
is clear from context, we often let
p(x):=\Pr_{P}[Y|X=x]
denote the conditional distribution over labels given a feature vector
x
.
In binary settings, where
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
, we abuse notation and write
p(x)=\Pr_{P}[Y=1|X=x]
to denote the probability of a positive label.
Let
P_{X}
denote the marginal distribution of
P
over
\mathcal{X}
.
Throughout, we assume
P_{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{X}
when
\mathcal{X}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
; that is,
P_{X}(S)=0
for any Lebesgue-null
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.
This lets us treat lower-dimensional sets (e.g., decision boundaries, ties between reports) as
P_{X}
-null without further comment.

3.1
Decision and surrogate losses

In classification-like (discrete) prediction tasks, we can formalize the costs of a model’s decisions through a discrete loss
\ell:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
mapping a finite set of

, or
reports
,
\mathcal{R}
to a real-valued error when compared against the
true outcome
in
\mathcal{Y}
.
Equivalently, we often denote
\ell
by a
cost matrix

\mathbf{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|}
, where
\ell(r,y)=\mathbf{\ell}_{r,y}
.
This approach encapsulates broader classes of problems such as ranking
(
Ramaswamy & Agarwal, 2016
)
and classification with rejection
(
Ramaswamy et al., 2016
)
, though our primary motivation is cost-sensitive classification.

Unfortunately, discrete losses are often intractable to optimize directly

, which necessitates we instead optimize

.
These surrogates are then

via a link function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{R}
which maps real-valued surrogate predictions to discrete decisions.
Canonical links for classification take the argmax of scores corresponding to each class, and cost-sensitive analogs might take the argmax of a linear transformation of these scores.
For example, the “ideal” model
h^{*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
might have
h^{*}(x)\approx p(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
for all
x
.
In this case, using a link such as
\psi(u)=\argmax_{y}u_{y}
roughly corresponds to learning the most likely label conditioned on the feature vector
x
, which minimizes the 0-1 cost matrix
\ell=\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}-I
.
If the cost matrix was a generic loss matrix
\ell
, the conditional label predictor
h^{*}(x)=p(x)
can always be post-processed to obtain optimal decisions for
\ell
, as implied by results in property elicitation
(
Lambert & Shoham, 2009
;
Lambert, 2018
;
Frongillo & Kash, 2021
)
.

3.2
, specifically discussing “cost-sensitive” surrogates through the lens of property elicitation.

3.2
Cost-sensitive vs cost-agnostic surrogates, property elicitation

To state explicitly the relationship of a cost-agnostic surrogate and the cost-sensitive surrogate, we turn to
property elicitation
.

Definition 2

((Indirect) Property Elicitation)
.

Fix a set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
A property
\Gamma:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{R}
is a function mapping probability distributions over labels in
\mathcal{Y}
to reports in
\mathcal{R}
.
A loss
L:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

elicits

\Gamma
over
\mathcal{P}
if, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(p)\in\argmin_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}L(r,Y)~.

Moreover, we say
L
which directly elicits
\Gamma
,
indirectly elicits

\gamma:\mathcal{P}\to\hat{\mathcal{R}}
if there is a link
\psi:\mathcal{R}\to\hat{\mathcal{R}}
such that
\gamma=\psi\circ\Gamma
.
If
\mathcal{P}=\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, we omit “over
\mathcal{P}
.”

In what is to come, consider the property
\gamma
directly elicited by our cost matrix
\ell
.
We reason about cost-agnostic surrogates as those which indirectly elicit
\gamma
by using
\gamma
as the link.
That is, the cost-agnostic surrogate directly elicits the identity
p
, which is justified by the observation that since
\gamma
is a function of
p
, one can simply post-process
p
to obtain
\gamma(p)
.
In contrast, cost-sensitive surrogates also indirectly elicit
\gamma
, but through a different link such as a relabeling of reports.
Importantly, the cost-agnostic surrogate and cost-sensitive surrogates both indirectly elicit the property
\gamma
.

3.2.1
Cost-Agnostic Surrogates: Proper Scoring Rules

One special class of cost-agnostic surrogates to consider is
proper scoring rules

(
Savage, 1971
;
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
;
Brier, 1950
)
.
These are generally regarded as loss functions that ideally lead to the predictor of the conditional label distribution
h^{ag}(x)=p(x)
in ideal conditions.

Definition 3

(Proper scoring rule)
.

A loss
L:\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a
proper scoring rule
if, for all
p,q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}L(p,Y)\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}L(q,Y)~.

Moreover,
L
is
strictly proper
if the inequality is strict for all
p\neq q
.

Notably, all proper scoring rules (attaining their infimum) directly elicit the
identity property

\Gamma(p)=p
.
If a loss function directly elicits the identity, we call it
cost-agnostic
.
Examples of proper scoring rules include cross entropy, Brier score, and the Convolutional Fenchel-Young loss
Cao et al. (2026)
.

3.2.2
Cost-Sensitive surrogates

We discuss “cost-sensitive surrogates” as those which indirectly elicit a property of interest
\gamma
, but do not directly elicit the identity
\Gamma(p)=p
.
In particular, we consider two primary classes of cost-sensitive surrogates:
embeddings
and
weighted analogs
of proper scoring rules (for binary outcome settings).
While other cost-sensitive surrogates pervade the literature
(
Ramaswamy et al., 2015
;
Ramaswamy & Agarwal, 2016
;
Yu & Blaschko, 2018
;
Zhang & Agarwal, 2020
;
Khurana et al., 2025
;
Zhang, 2026b
;
Zhang, 2026a
)
, they often are constructed without the desired statistical guarantees, even with unlimited model capacity, or are arduously constructed with consistency guarantees for a specific
\ell
.

Embeddings

In
Section

5
, we study a class of piecewise linear and convex surrogates called
embeddings

(
Finocchiaro et al., 2024
)
, which are constructed directly from a given cost matrix
\ell
and yield a link function
\psi
to round predictions
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to discrete reports in
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The optimal reports minimizing these surrogates are “directly learning” the optimal decision for the loss matrix
\ell
.
For example, the hinge loss
L(u,y)=(1-uy)_{+}
is an embedding with labels
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
for the 0-1 cost matrix.
Unlike cross entropy, the minimizers of the expected (in the outcome) hinge are precisely at the reports
u^{*}\in\{-1,1\}
for (almost all) distributions on
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Informally, these piecewise linear cost-sensitive surrogates sacrifice a level of granularity in predictions present in cross entropy which can be overly burdensome in limited-model settings.
Instead of the optimal classifier returning
p(x)
like proper scoring rules, we have the optimal classifier
h^{cs}(x)=\gamma(p(x))=\mathrm{sign}\left(p(x)-\frac{1}{2}\right)
, minimizing the expected discrete loss, up to a relabeling of the minimizer by the link.
Importantly, in constructing these surrogates,
Finocchiaro et al.
also give a link construction, eliminating the need to conduct a threshold search for a link function.

Weighted analogs of proper scoring rules

We consider a loss function
L^{\alpha}
to be an
\vec{\alpha}
-weighted analog of a cost-agnostic surrogate
L
if, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we can write
L^{\alpha}(u,y)=\alpha_{y}L(u,y)
.
This permits cost-agnostic losses to be scaled by the outcome.
For example, in
Table

1
, we parameterize a false positive with weight
\alpha
and false negative with weight
1-\alpha
; one popular weighted analog in this cost-sensitive binary classification task is the
weighted
cross entropy, with
L^{\alpha}(u,1)=(1-\alpha)L(u,1)
and
L^{\alpha}(u,-1)=\alpha L(u,-1)
.
With binary outcome and report matrices, these weighted analogs can be parameterized without loss of generality by a single scalar, as in
Table

1
.
We let the binary case be our main focus, as generalizing results for these weighted analogs to multiclass settings is not straightforward as the number of parameters grows in
O(|\mathcal{R}||\mathcal{Y}|)
, though it poses an interesting line of future work.

3.3
\mathcal{H}
-consistency

We seek guarantees on surrogate and link pairs
(L,\psi)
which imply optimizing a surrogate loss
L
over a model class
\mathcal{H}
and applying the link function
\psi
to obtain discrete decisions yields the same discrete decisions as if one had optimized
\ell
directly.
This guarantee is formalized by the notion of
\mathcal{H}
-consistency in Definition
4
.
We let
\mathcal{H}
denote an abstract model class, and sometimes refer to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}^{d}:=\{h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ measurable}\}
denoting the set of all measurable hypotheses, and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{d}:=\{h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}|\exists w\text{ s.t. }h(x)=\rho(\langle w,x\rangle)\}
be the set of linear functions with a saturating activation
\rho
applied.
When clear from context, we omit

.
We write
\rho(z):=2\sigma(z)-1
for this activation, reserving
\sigma
for the logistic function itself, so that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}
has range
(-1,1)
rather than
(0,1)
.
The purpose of this choice is that the values at which members of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}
saturate then coincide with the embedded reports of the cost-sensitive surrogates studied in
Section

5
.
In the binary setting we study, nothing depends on the particular

, and we fix
\rho=2\sigma-1
only for concreteness.
Cost-agnostic surrogates and their weighted analogs, whose arguments are probabilities, are instead applied to the
probability coding

q(u):=\frac{u+1}{2}
.
Since
q(\rho(z))=\sigma(z)
, probability-coded expressions retain their usual logistic form; and because
q
is a fixed bijection, the two codings realize the same decision boundaries and differ only by a relabeling of the elicited property.

Agnostic learning aims to find a model
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that the expected loss of

is close to the best expected discrete loss possible in
\mathcal{H}
.
However, given the computational hardness of directly optimizing discrete losses,
\mathcal{H}
-consistency aims to guarantee that optimizing a

loss
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and applying a

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{R}
yields the desired agnostic learning guarantee.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and reports in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
can be mapped to discrete classes as appropriate.

Definition 4

(
\mathcal{H}
-consistency)
.

A surrogate

Distributional assumptions

In the literature, two common distributional assumptions are made:
realizability
and
minimizability
.
If a loss
L
is
P

\mathcal{H}
, then there exists a hypothesis
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}L(h^{*}(X),Y)=0
.
Notably, this means that

yields a deterministic labeling over outcomes, and there is no outcome uncertainty.
Even under these strict distributional assumptions,

;
Kuznetsov et al. (2014)
observe a gap between

-consistency and

-consistency, where (a) the cross entropy loss, which is
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
-consistent, is realizable

-consistent for multiclass classification.
This second result does not extend beyond the realizable setting.

Less stringent is the assumption that a loss

-minimizable
over
\mathcal{H}

However, its error may not be

3.4
Revisiting the main question

We now state our main question:
can we identify sufficient conditions for the existence of a strict gap between the performance of models trained on
cost-sensitive surrogate
loss functions and models trained on cost-agnostic surrogates combined with some
“clever” post-processing
?

Our results establish this gap in the setting of
small
models.

As discussed earlier,
cost-sensitive surrogates
refer to continuous loss functions
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
designed with the intention of emulating a discrete target problem
\ell:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Formally, we say cost-sensitive surrogates indirectly elicit the property
\gamma
elicited by
\ell
, and do not directly elicit the identity
\Gamma(p)=p
.
The cost-sensitive surrogates we use as a case study in
Section

5
are piecewise linear and convex, and constructed directly from the discrete target
\ell
.
In the cost-agnostic surrogate setting, we formalize
clever post-processing
by a search over possible link functions
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{R}
mapping surrogate predictions, such as risk scores, to discrete decisions, such as positive or negative classifications.
The most common example of this clever post-processing in binary outcome settings is through a threshold search to establish decision boundaries
\psi_{\tau}(h(x))=\mathrm{sign}(h(x)-\tau)
.
However, this threshold search is often difficult to scale beyond binary classification as the search space grows exponentially in the number of classes.

Our results show that when models are
small
, there is a strict gap between these two approaches.
In particular, we assume the model class
\mathcal{H}
is rich enough to contain the
\ell
-Bayes optimal decision boundary, but not so rich as to contain the
L^{ag}
-Bayes optimal hypothesis predicting the conditional label distribution
p(x)
for all covariates
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Our approach merges the perspectives of two fields: the work on surrogate loss design and the work on
\mathcal{H}
-consistency.
The surrogate loss design literature generally assumes that the model class
\mathcal{H}
is rich enough for any measurable hypothesis to be learned, ignoring the relationship between model class and learned hypothesis (e.g.,
Reid & Williamson (2011)
;
Reid & Williamson (2009)
;
Buja et al. (2005)
;
Bao (2023)
).
On the other hand, the
\mathcal{H}
-consistency literature has yet to provide results informing cost-sensitive surrogate design, instead providing regret bounds for a variety of standard surrogates for tasks such as adversarial learning
(
Awasthi et al., 2021a
;
Bao et al., 2021
;
Awasthi et al., 2021b
)
, regression
(
Mao et al., 2024a
)
, learning with deferral
(
Mao et al., 2024c
)
, multi-label learning
(
Mao et al., 2024b
)
, and the area under the ROC curve
(
Liu & Zhu, 2022
;
Liu, 2023
)
.
Most similar to our work is the contemporaneous work of
Mao et al. (2025)
, who derive
\mathcal{H}
-consistency guarantees for general metrics in binary outcome settings.
They implicitly make the observation that the decision boundary obtained from a cost-agnostic surrogate might differ from a cost-sensitive surrogate, and explicitly state that the approach does not extend to the single-variable parameterization of
\ell^{\alpha}
in
Table

1
.

In multiclass settings, the cost matrix instead requires

We have established a strict gap between cost-sensitive surrogate loss functions

under the assumption that

We view these experiments as a proof-of-concept supplementing our primary results in

Datasets

We evaluate the loss functions above on our example in

Metrics

The

7.1
Main Results

Our experiments use a randomly selected set of 500 samples from each dataset, intentionally keeping the sample size small. The models are trained on each loss function using gradient descent, and model selection is performed based on the validation loss. The results are averaged over 100 random seeds that control the choice of samples from the dataset and initial model weights. We summarize our main results in

•

Cost-Sensitive Losses Outperform Cost-Agnostic Losses + Post-Processing:

Unsurprisingly, we see that cross entropy maximizes the accuracy but not the cost-sensitive loss. 8
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we make one step towards characterizing when learning with task-specific surrogate losses differs from post-processing more generic, task-agnostic models.
We specifically focus on the setting when the model class is expressive enough to contain the optimal decision boundary, but not so expressive to contain a hypothesis yielding the conditional label distribution.
Notably, our gains come at the sacrifice of performance generalization and cost-agnostic accuracy: if the cost matrix
\ell
is unknown or varies across those receiving predictions, the omniprediction literature has established that post-processing models trained on cost-agnostic surrogates can perform well.
However, these results often assume access to very rich model classes, which is often not plausible in low-resource (e.g., edge computing) or high-frequency inference (e.g., ad auctions, blockchains) settings.
In these limited model settings, we establish a strict gap between the task-specific performance of models trained on cost-agnostic and cost-sensitive surrogates through the lens of
\mathcal{H}
-consistency.
To supplement our theoretical findings, we additionally contrast model performance on a handful of binary and multiclass cost-sensitive classification problems, generally finding that cost-sensitive surrogates outperform cost-agnostic losses with small models.

Future work

Many directions of future work are opened here: first, our positive results for
\mathcal{H}
-consistency of weighted analogs in the binary outcome setting require some heavy lifting from
Assumption

4
; understanding less stringent conditions under which
\mathcal{H}
-consistency holds remains one interesting area of future work.
We conjecture that some
\mathcal{H}
-consistency results for weighted analogs and other smooth surrogates might be attained by leveraging the notion of strong indirect elicitation from
Khurana et al. (2025)
.
Second, we restricted our analysis of weighted analogs to binary outcomes (
Section

6
); extending this to multiclass settings is not straightforward.
When
|\mathcal{Y}|>2
, a weighted analog requires aggregating an entire column of per-report costs into a single scalar weight per outcome, and this aggregation can be lossy: distinct target cost matrices can map to the same aggregated weight vector while eliciting different optimal decisions, so recovering
\mathcal{H}
-consistency (or even
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
-consistency) likely requires either a per-target threshold search or additional structure on the aggregation itself, possibly related to the approach taken by
Mao et al. (2025)
.
Formalizing when such an aggregation can still preserve
\mathcal{H}
-consistency guarantees remains open.
Finally, this work seems to have implications for the predict-then-optimize literature
(
Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022
;
Wilder et al., 2019
)
, but showing these connections merits careful consideration as the prediction task becomes much more complex and the outcome space becomes harder to define.

\mathcal{H}
-calibration

While
\mathcal{H}
-consistency yields agnostic learning guarantees, studying the expected loss over distributions on labels
and features
often poses practical difficulties.
To circumvent these, the notion of
\mathcal{H}
-calibration is often used to analyze these surrogates more tractably.

Definition 12

(
\mathcal{H}
-calibration)
.

A loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and link
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{R}
pair
(L,\psi)
is
\mathcal{H}
-calibrated with respect to a target loss
\ell:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
over the set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
if, for any
\epsilon>0
,
p\in\mathcal{P}
, and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}L(h(x),Y)-\inf_{h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}L(h^{*}(x),Y)<\delta

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\ell(\psi(h(x)),Y)-\inf_{h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\ell(\psi(h^{*}(x)),Y)<\epsilon

(6)

for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

Early works of
Bartlett et al. (2006)
;
Ramaswamy & Agarwal (2016)
show that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
-consistency and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
-calibration are equivalent in finite outcome settings.
However, when the model class
\mathcal{H}\neq\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
, two gaps emerge: first, the gap between
\mathcal{H}
-calibration and
\mathcal{H}
-consistency, and second, the gap between
\mathcal{H}
-consistency and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
-consistency.
When Bayes optimal classifiers match and both losses are
P
-minimizable,
Steinwart (2007, Theorem 2.8)
closes the gap between
\mathcal{H}
-calibration and
\mathcal{H}
-consistency.
However, the crucial observation that we make here is that the Bayes optimal classifiers for cost-agnostic surrogates are generally not matching their target loss, and therefore
(
Steinwart, 2007
, Theorem 2.8)
does not apply.

Appendix E

Loss Functions

•

Cross entropy (CE):
This is the most common loss function used for classification tasks and we use it as our baseline. In addition to simply training our models on cross entropy and using the
\argmax
link to predict the class with the highest score, we also emulate “clever” post-processing, computing a weighted
\argmax
where the weights are randomly sampled, and we use the weight yielding the best performance on the validation set.

•

Weighted Cross entropy:
This is a common cost-sensitive modification of the cross entropy loss used for classification in imbalanced data and cost-sensitive settings as studied in §
6
. The class scores are re-weighted in this loss based on the average misclassification cost from the cost matrix for that outcome.

•

Embeddings (
Finocchiaro et al. (2024)
):
This is the cost-sensitive surrogate loss function we analyze in
section

5
. It uses the cost matrix to create a piecewise linear and convex loss function that generalizes the hinge loss to cost-sensitive classification. In addition to the standard version of the loss, we also use a ‘softmax’ version of the loss function in which the scores of different classes are not directly predicted. Instead, the predicted point is parameterized as a convex combination of “embedding points,” one for each class. In this version, we predict the coefficients of the convex combination.
