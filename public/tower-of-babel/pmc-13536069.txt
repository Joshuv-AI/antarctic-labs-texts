Diagnostic accuracy of the passive mast cell activation test in neuromuscular blocking agent-induced perioperative anaphylaxis in a tertiary hospital in China: a diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Zhe Li, Xiaowen Liu, Huamei Cai, Tao Zhang, Jing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe passive mast cell activation test (pMAT) is a novel in vitro assay designed to complement existing tools like the skin test (ST) and basophil activation test (BAT) for allergen identification. This study aimed to validate the diagnostic accuracy of pMAT specifically for neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA)-induced perioperative anaphylaxis (POA).DesignA diagnostic accuracy study.SettingA tertiary hospital in Beijing, China.Participants44 patients with suspected POA were enrolled. Participants were stratified into groups based on ST and/or BAT. The NMBA-allergic group comprised 15 patients with confirmed NMBA allergy while the non-NMBA-allergic group consisted of 29 individuals without NMBA allergy.InterventionsAll participants underwent pMAT testing for relevant NMBA.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe diagnostic performance of pMAT for NMBA was assessed by measuring sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and the Youden index. An exploratory analysis evaluated its performance in diagnosing antibiotic-induced allergies within the same population.ResultsFor NMBA allergy, pMAT demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (90.9%; 95% CI 77.4% to 97.1%) with excellent sensitivity (93.3%; 95% CI 66.0% to 99.7%) and specificity (89.7%; 95% CI 71.5% to 97.3%). This was reflected in a high positive likelihood ratio (9.05; 95% CI 3.06 to 26.57), a low negative likelihood ratio (0.07; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.50), a Youden index of 0.83 and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.804 (indicating substantial agreement with the reference standard). In contrast, pMAT showed reduced performance for antibiotic allergy, with an accuracy of 79.5% (95% CI 64.3% to 89.7%), sensitivity of 66.7% (95% CI 30.9% to 91.0%), specificity of 82.9% (95% CI 65.7% to 92.8%) and a kappa of 0.378 (fair agreement).ConclusionspMAT demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for NMBA allergy, filling critical gaps in current POA diagnostic workflow. Its robust performance supports its clinical utility as a complementary tool alongside ST and BAT.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400084268.

Introduction

Perioperative anaphylaxis (POA) is an immediate hypersensitivity reaction occurring during anaesthesia, with mortality rates of 1.4%–6% and anoxic cerebral injury in approximately 2% of survivors.1–3 Its reported incidence varies widely from 1/353 to 1/18 600 due to differences in reporting methods, definitions and populations across countries.4 5 With an annual global surgical volume of over 300 million procedures, even low incidence estimates translate to a considerable number of affected patients, highlighting the clinical importance of POA.6 This represents a critical healthcare challenge and underscores the need for accurate allergen identification to prevent life-threatening recurrences in subsequent procedures.

However, current diagnostic strategies remain limited.7 While drug provocation testing is the diagnostic reference standard, its inherent risks and ethical constraints limit its clinical utility.8 Skin tests (ST) still remain the cornerstone of perioperative allergy evaluation but are limited by significant false-positive rates.9 10 In vitro alternatives also face constraints: specific immunoglobulin E tests are restricted by limited reagent availability for drug allergens, while basophil activation tests (BAT) demonstrate high specificity (>90%) but moderate sensitivity (40–60%), attributable to preanalytical basophil instability.11–16 These limitations collectively create a diagnostic gap, highlighting a pressing need for more reliable adjunctive testing methods.

The recent development of the passive mast cell (MC) activation test (pMAT) offers a promising alternative rooted in MC biology.17 18 Unlike BAT, pMAT accepts frozen plasma samples, allowing retrospective analysis and multicentre validation. Moreover, MCs have a mediator release capacity greater than that of basophils, which may enhance signal detection.19 Despite these advantages, pMAT has yet to undergo systematic clinical validation for neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs), which account for 50%–70% of POA cases.20 21

To address this gap, we conducted a diagnostic accuracy study in patients with suspected POA to evaluate the performance of an Laboratory of Allergic Diseases 2 (LAD2) cell-based pMAT protocol for identifying NMBA allergy. Given that antibiotics are another leading trigger of POA, a preliminary assessment of pMAT’s performance for antibiotic allergy was also performed.22 By evaluating this approach, our study provides preliminary evidence to support further validation of LAD2-based pMAT as a complementary tool within multimodal POA diagnostic workflows.

Materials and methods

Study overview and design

This prospective diagnostic accuracy study was conducted through a multicentre collaborative network encompassing 27 institutions previously engaged in our epidemiological investigations on POA. The anaesthesia department of each participating hospital designated a physician-investigator to systematically identify suspected cases of POA. Reported cases underwent blinded verification by two experienced anaesthesiologists using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible patients were subsequently enrolled in the study and invited to undergo allergen testing at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (CJFH) after written informed consents were obtained.

Patient recruitment was conducted between June 2024 and February 2025. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of CJFH, Beijing, China, on 27 October 2023 (Approval No. 2023-KY-247). The study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084268). The detailed protocol of this study has been peer-reviewed and published in BMJ Open, with additional details available in the online supplemental protocol.23 This manuscript adheres to the applicable Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines.

Case report

Case documentation was systematically collected via a secure online platform developed in accordance with security protocols. Authorised clinicians at each participating centre were given unique login details to enter standardised clinical data, which were then stored on encrypted servers with restricted access limited to the principal investigator team.

The clinical reporting protocol involved two sequential phases of data collection. Initial documentation focused on acute anaphylactic manifestations occurring within 15 min of anaesthetic administration, including detailed cardiovascular parameters, respiratory compromise and cutaneous/mucosal reactions. Site investigators were required to complete this preliminary documentation within 24 hours of identification of suspected POA.

The secondary analysis phase involved comprehensive abstraction of perioperative data, including surgical timelines, anaesthetic techniques and pharmacological interventions. Research fellows supplemented these records with structured chart reviews and telephone interviews, reviewing medication histories and resuscitation protocols. Particular attention was paid to temporal relationships between suspected triggers and clinical deterioration, with all chronologies cross-referenced with anaesthetic machine logs and nursing observations. Culprit-drug assessment was based on drug-reaction chronology, exclusion of plausible non-allergic causes and subsequent allergy testing of clinically relevant perioperative drugs. ST and/or BAT were performed for suspected agents, including NMBAs, antibiotics, hypnotics, opioids, benzodiazepines and other drugs when indicated. Patients positive for non-NMBA agents but negative for NMBAs were classified into the non-NMBA-allergic group for the primary analysis.

Skin tests

ST was conducted by consultant-level anaesthesiologists. Morphine (1 mg/mL) served as the positive control while 0.9% saline was utilised for negative controls. Skin prick tests (SPT) employing sterile lancets on the volar forearm surface were interpreted at 15 min, with ≥3 mm weal diameter constituting a positive result. Intradermal tests (IDT) with 0.02–0.03 mL injections were performed following negative/inconclusive SPTs, requiring ≥3 mm weal expansion from baseline at 20 min for positivity. All reactions were photographically documented using calliper-verified measurement protocols. The concentrations of the two NMBAs used for IDT in this study were consistent with the standards recommended by guidelines: 0.02 mg/mL for cisatracurium and 0.05 mg/mL for rocuronium.

Basophil activation tests

BAT was performed in strict accordance with the Flow CAST kit protocols (Bühlmann Laboratories AG, Schönenbuch, Switzerland), using flow cytometric analysis to quantify CD63 and CCR3 expression. Drug concentrations for basophil stimulation were selected based on previous guidelines and clinical studies and the research team’s extensive experience, as specified in the study protocol.23 To standardise interpretation of results, activation gate parameters were calibrated to maintain spontaneous basophil activation rates between 2.0% and 2.5%, in accordance with the manufacturer’s quality control specifications. Under these tightly controlled conditions, a positive BAT result was defined by the simultaneous fulfilment of two criteria: ≥5% activated basophils and a stimulation index greater than 2.

Culture of human MC LAD2

The human LAD2 MCs were kindly provided by A. Kirshenbaum and D. Metcalfe (National Institutes of Health, NIH, Bethesda, Maryland). Cells would be maintained in StemPro-34 medium supplemented with StemPro nutrient supplement, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, 2 mM L-glutamine and 100 ng/mL human stem cell factor in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37℃. The culture medium is half replaced every 7 days, and the cells were maintained at a density of 2–5×105 cells/mL.

Passive MC activation tests

Peripheral blood for pMAT was collected in EDTA-containing tubes and transported to the laboratory on ice. Plasma was separated by centrifugation after collection, aliquoted immediately to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles and stored at −80 °C until testing. Each aliquot was thawed only once for pMAT, and all samples were tested within 6 months of collection. For pMAT, LAD2 cells (5×10⁵ cells/mL) were passively sensitised with 20 µL of patient plasma overnight in a humidified CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 µL of sensitised LAD2 cells were preincubated with interleukin-33 (IL-33) (100 ng/mL; PeproTech) and IL-6 (100 ng/mL; ProMab) for 20 min at 37℃. The preincubated LAD2 cells were then stimulated with 100 µL of Tyrode’s buffer (Solarbio, Beijing) as a negative control, 100 µL of anti-IgE (final concentration: 10 µg/mL) as a positive control, and 100 µL of suspected allergen drugs for 20 min at 37℃. In this study, the final concentrations of the stimulants for cisatracurium, rocuronium, cephalosporins and levofloxacin were set at 20 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, 200 µg/mL and 25 µg/mL, respectively. The reactions were terminated by placing the cells on ice. After centrifugation (500×g for 5 min at 4℃), the supernatants were removed.

MCs were identified by double-positive staining using monoclonal anti-human CD117-APC (clone 104D2; BioLegend, California) and anti-human CD203c-PeCy7 (clone NP4D6; BioLegend, California). Activation of LAD2 cells was determined by staining with anti-human CD63-FITC (clone H5C6; BioLegend, California). To ensure reproducibility, MC activation tests with different drug concentrations were performed in duplicate. In cases of inconsistent results, a third test was conducted to confirm the findings.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California). Correct compensation settings for fluorochrome conjugated antibodies were performed using CytExpert (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California). Flow cytometric data were analysed using FlowJo V.10.8.1 software (BD Bioscience, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey). In this study, more than 1500 MCs should be counted per sample in case of system error according to the relative study. The gate is set based on the single-stained and fluorescent minus one sample.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.26.0. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were statistically described and compared between the NMBA-allergic and non-NMBA-allergic groups. For continuous variables, data conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean±SD and compared using independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance; otherwise, they were expressed as median (IQR) and compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages) and analysed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The validity and reliability of pMAT were evaluated across groups at different drug concentrations. Validity was appraised using sensitivity, specificity, false-positive rate, false-negative rate, likelihood ratios, accuracy and the Youden index, with 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity estimated using the Wilson score method and those for likelihood ratios estimated using the log method. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to explore the optimal cut-off value for pMAT positivity, defined as the point maximising the Youden index. Reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient to evaluate agreement between pMAT and the composite reference standard (ST/BAT). Kappa values were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch criteria: <0, poor; 0–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; 0.81–1.00, almost perfect.

Results

Patient enrolment and diagnostic results

The study enrolled 46 patients meeting inclusion criteria for perioperative allergic reactions, with two declining allergen testing, resulting in a final group of 44 participants (figure 1). Stratification by NMBA hypersensitivity status revealed 15 confirmed NMBAs-allergic cases and 29 non-allergic cases. Among NMBA-allergic patients, the median reaction-to-test interval was 6.0 months (IQR: 2.0–84.0), with six tested >12 months postreaction. Diagnostic stratification relied on concordant ST and BAT outcomes, with nine cases confirmed by positive ST alone, four by BAT positivity despite negative ST and two exhibiting dual positivity. Cisatracurium hypersensitivity predominated (n=9), followed by rocuronium (n=4), with two patients demonstrating cross-reactivity to both agents. The baseline characteristics of the two patient groups are summarised in table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed in age, gender or the severity of anaphylaxis classified by the Ring and Messmer grading system (p>0.05 for all comparisons). Notably, the NMBA-allergic group exhibited a significantly higher pMAT proportion for culprit NMBAs at 7.50% (IQR: 5.46%–9.34%) compared with the non-NMBA-allergic group’s activation proportion for non-culprit NMBA at 2.87% (IQR: 1.99%–4.21%), with a marked statistical difference (p<0.001).

pMAT gating strategy

The gating strategy for pMAT is detailed in figure 2. Initially, cellular debris was excluded, and intact cells were selected (figure 2A). Subsequently, doublets were removed to isolate single cells for further analysis (figure 2B). MCs were identified by double-positive staining for CD117 and CD203c (figure 2D), with unstained LAD2 cells provided for comparison (figure 2C). Activation of MCs was assessed via CD63 staining following incubation with patient plasma. A gating threshold was established using the negative control (figure 2E) and applied to other drug stimulations. A positive result was defined as an stimulation index (SI) ≥2 and an activation percentage>5%. Figure 2F demonstrates the increased activation percentage of LAD2 cells following stimulation with plasma from an NMBA-allergic patient. Diagnostic results for antibiotics

Among the 44 enrolled patients, 9 were allergic to antibiotics perioperatively, primarily cephalosporins and levofloxacin, while 35 showed no antibiotic allergy. The results of ST, BAT and pMAT for the allergic group are summarised in online supplemental table S2. pMAT was also performed for these antibiotics. Six of the nine antibiotic-allergic patients tested positive, while six of the 35 non-allergic patients showed false-positive results. Detailed pMAT results for all patients are also presented in the supplementary data. The diagnostic performance of pMAT for antibiotics was presented in table 3, with a sensitivity of 66.7% (95% CI 30.9% to 91.0%), a specificity of 82.9% (95% CI 65.7% to 92.8%), a PLR of 3.90 (95% CI 1.64 to 9.21), an NLR of 0.40 (95% CI 0.16 to 1.03), an overall accuracy of 79.5% (95% CI 64.3% to 89.7%), a Youden index of 0.50 and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.378.

Discussion

The optimised pMAT protocol demonstrated high diagnostic performance for NMBA allergy, with a sensitivity of 93.3% and specificity of 89.7%, significantly surpassing the typical range of BAT (40%–60%) and suggesting its potential to improve POA assessment. The positivity criterion (SI >2, corresponding to >5% activation) was validated by a preliminary ROC analysis, which identified an optimal threshold of 5.16% that closely aligned with our applied cut-off (online supplemental figure S1).25 26

Notably, significant discordance between ST and BAT results was observed in our NMBA-allergic group, with only two patients showing positivity in both tests. This limited overlap may be partly explained by the moderate sensitivity of BAT for NMBA allergy, which can be affected by basophil non-responsiveness, preanalytical basophil instability, the interval between reaction and testing and drug-specific activation thresholds. Therefore, a negative BAT result does not necessarily exclude NMBA allergy, particularly when the clinical history and ST results are suggestive. These inconsistencies underscore the inherent limitations of both ST and BAT in clinical practice.14 15 Patients with conflicting results face considerable diagnostic uncertainty and potential surgical risks if allergens are not definitively identified. In our study, two patients in the NMBA-allergic group demonstrated cross-reactivity to both NMBAs, with an incidence rate broadly concordant with prior literature.27 This highlights the potential value of pMAT as an adjunctive assay to support clinical interpretation when conventional tests are inconclusive or discordant, rather than as a replacement for established diagnostic methods.

However, the clinical translation of the pMAT faces technical hurdles.28 While the pioneering work of Elst et al established a pMAT system based on in vitro-differentiated human MCs (hMCs) for chlorhexidine and rocuronium allergy detection, the reliance on hMCs limits widespread use.17 18 To enhance clinical applicability, our study employed the LAD2 MC line, thereby avoiding complex cell differentiation procedures. We recognise that LAD2 cells have biological limitations, including attenuated IgE-FcεRI signalling and lower granule protease content compared with primary hMCs.29 30 Therefore, we optimised the pMAT protocol by reducing the patient plasma volume for LAD2 cell incubation from 100 µl to 20 µl to minimise background activation. In addition, based on the findings of Koren and Cop, to enhance the responsiveness of the FcεRI pathway, we supplemented the MC incubation with IL-33 and IL-6 in all samples (including the negative controls) to boost the FcεRI pathway responsiveness.31 32 Collectively, these adjustments improved discrimination between negative and positive controls, allowed clearer gating of activated cells, and likely contributed to the low false-positive rate (only 3/29 non-allergic cases) observed in our study.

The high sensitivity of pMAT observed in this study addresses a critical clinical need: mitigating the risk of false-negative results, which can lead to severe outcomes on re-exposure, as documented by Jeong et al.33 However, literature on re-exposure outcomes remains inconsistent, with larger cohorts sometimes reporting minimal reactions, discrepancies potentially arising from heterogeneous diagnostic protocols.34 This very inconsistency underscores the value of a highly sensitive confirmatory assay like pMAT. Integrating pMAT into a multimodal testing framework can thus better stratify re-exposure risks, particularly for patients with ambiguous standard test results. Establishing pMAT as a supplementary assay was, therefore, a key rationale for our investigation, aiming to enhance patient safety in subsequent procedures.

Given that antibiotics represent a major cause of POA, accounting for 40%–55% of cases in some European cohorts, we extended our primary focus on NMBA to include an exploratory analysis of pMAT’s performance for antibiotic allergy.22 35 36 The results revealed a comparatively lower diagnostic efficacy for antibiotics, a pattern consistent with trends observed in BAT.37 38 This performance discrepancy may originate from fundamental differences in antigen presentation. Like cephalosporins, as haptens, must conjugate with host proteins to form complete immunogens, with clinical and in vitro evidence pointing to the conjugated R1 side chain as the primary immunogenic determinant.37 The pMAT, however, tests the unconjugated drug form, which likely limits its ability to detect the true epitopes.39 Furthermore, the potential for non-IgE-mediated activation via MRGPRX2 must be considered. Given the high potency of levofloxacin for MRGPRX2 activation (EC50: 22.7 µg/mL).36 40 To minimise non-specific MRGPRX2-mediated activation in our pMAT, we applied a 200-fold drug dilution to approximate the EC50 concentration of levofloxacin, thereby reducing non-specific interference. However, this precaution introduces a trade-off: excessive dilution might underdetect true IgE-FcεRI-mediated responses. This issue warrants further investigation.

The MRGPRX2 pathway introduces a layer of complexity to POA diagnosis.40 This receptor, expressed on LAD2 cells, is activated by various cationic drugs like NMBAs and levofloxacin.36 41 42 In refining our pMAT protocol, we deliberately avoided blocking MRGPRX2 to preserve the pathophysiologically relevant potential for combined MC activation in vivo. However, the clinical interpretation of this pathway is challenged by patient-specific factors: functional genetic variants of MRGPRX2.43 44 It is quite possible that alleles that respond to much lower drug concentrations are expressed by some patients and may explain discrepant results in our study (ST+/BAT-/pMAT-). Additionally, MRGPRX2 expression levels vary tremendously among individuals.45 Crucially, pMAT cannot detect this antibody-independent pathway due to its use of standardised cells. While skin testing may indicate MRGPRX2 activation, unlike BAT.46 47 A direct test using a patient’s own MCs is theoretically ideal but pragmatically unfeasible. Consequently, reliably distinguishing hypersensitivity mechanisms remains a major diagnostic challenge, underscoring the pressing need to develop robust assays specifically for MRGPRX2-mediated reactions. Further research is essential to validate the role of this pathway.48

Our study has several limitations. First, LAD2 cells differ biologically from primary hMCs and have attenuated FcεRI signalling, which may have contributed to the modest activation observed in positive controls despite IL-33 and IL-6 prestimulation. Further optimisation and standardisation of the pMAT protocol are, therefore, needed. Second, pMAT currently requires continuous cell culture, flow-cytometric analysis, trained personnel and assay standardisation, limiting its feasibility in general clinical laboratories. Third, the single-centre design and the rarity of POA resulted in a limited sample size, particularly for antibiotic-allergic patients, reducing the precision of diagnostic estimates, the robustness of cut-off determination and the generalisability of the findings. Fourth, pMAT mainly assesses plasma-transferable IgE/FcεRI-associated MC activation and may not detect complement-mediated, MRGPRX2-related or other non-IgE mechanisms; therefore, a negative pMAT result should not definitively exclude NMBA culpability when the clinical history is strongly suggestive. Additionally, this study used a composite ST/BAT reference standard because drug provocation testing was ethically and practically infeasible. As both ST and BAT have recognised limitations, and quinolone skin testing may yield false-positive results, reference-standard bias may have affected the diagnostic accuracy estimates. Finally, a subset of patients with confirmed NMBAs allergy underwent allergen testing at a prolonged interval after POA, which may further compromise the reliability of assay results.

Conclusion

In summary, pMAT demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for NMBA-induced POA, with a sensitivity of 93.3% (95%CI: 66.0 to 99.7%) and specificity of 89.7% (95%CI: 71.5 to 97.3%). Although its performance was lower for antibiotic-induced allergic reactions, the exploratory findings suggest potential utility in broader allergen detection. With high diagnostic reliability, pMAT serves as a valuable adjunct to ST and BAT, especially in POA cases with inconclusive or discordant conventional test results. These findings support the further development of pMAT as a complementary tool in perioperative allergy diagnostics.
