Central limit theorems for martingales-III: discontinuous compensators Thanks: Partial funding in support of this work was provided by UAEU SRCL 2025 and the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada
Bruno Rémillard, Jean Vaillancourt

Abstract
. We propose a new weak convergence theorem for martingales, under gentler conditions than the usual convergence in probability of the sequence of associated quadratic variations. Its proof requires the combined use of Skorohod’s 𝒥 1 \mathcal{J}_{1} -topology and ℳ 1 \mathcal{M}_{1} -topology on the space of càdlàg trajectories. The emphasis is on those instances where the sequence of martingales or its limit is a mixture of stochastic processes with discontinuities. Alternative conditions are set forth in the special cases of arrays of discrete time martingale differences and martingale transforms. Examples of applications are provided, notably when the limiting process is a Brownian subordinator.

2.2.
Hypotheses and main results

Recall that
W_{n}:=M_{n}\circ\tau_{n}
, with compensator
\langle W_{n}\rangle=A_{n}\circ\tau_{n}
.

Hypothesis 2.1
.

All of the following hold:

(a)

A_{n}(t)=\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}\to\infty

(b)

(iii)

(c)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm E}\left[{A_{n}\circ\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)-A_{n}(t)}\right]=0

(d)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm E}\left[{\langle W_{n}\rangle_{t}}\right]=t

Remark 2.1
.

Hypothesis

The main change is Hypothesis

Note that

It then follows from Hypothesis

\displaystyle E\left\{|W_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)-M_{n}(t)|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{|M_{n}\circ\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)-M_{n}(t)|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left\{W_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)\right\}^{2}-\left\{M_{n}(t)\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\langle M_{n}\rangle_{\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)}-\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left[{A_{n}\circ\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)-A_{n}(t)}\right].

Therefore, Hypothesis

is almost a Brownian motion evaluated at its compensator. Furthermore, the first jump of

Asymptotic rarefaction of jumps hypotheses (stronger versions of Hypothesis

W_{n}
and
\tau_{n}
are
\mathbb{F}_{\tau_{n}}
-adapted,
\tau_{n}
has finite variation and

W_{n}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{\tau_{n}}
-martingale. Under Hypothesis
2.1
, the limit
(W,\tau)
is both
an
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-semimartingale and an inhomogeneous Lévy process.
As documented in
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
, previous approaches to the CLT exploited these features
of the limit
(W,\tau)
in order to achieve convergence, by way of the associated characteristic functions.
The intuitive conditions in both Theorem
2.3
and Corollary
2.5
are often easier to check for many
applications.

3.
Discrete martingale arrays and pure jump martingales

In this section, we consider the simplest sequences of real-valued discontinuous square integrable
\mathbb{F}
-martingales
M_{n}
,
namely those with step-valued trajectories arising from the weak invariance principle for arrays of discrete time martingale differences.
For each
n\geq 1
, let the
\{X_{n,j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be square integrable martingale differences with respect to
a filtration such that
\mathcal{G}_{n,k}\supseteq\sigma\{X_{n,j};j\leq k\}
.
Define
\displaystyle M_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}X_{n,j}
, with starting value
M_{n}(0)=0
, so

\displaystyle[M_{n}]_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}X_{n,j}^{2}
and
\displaystyle A_{n}(t)=\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}=V_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}
, with
[M_{n}]_{0}=A_{n}(0)=0
,
where
\displaystyle V_{n,k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}E\left(X_{n,j}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{n,j-1}\right)
.
By construction
(M_{n},[M_{n}],A_{n})
is
D
-valued and we only need to complete the filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{n,k})_{k\geq 1}
in order
to ensure that the set-up corresponds to our requirements. In Section
3.1
, we recall so early work on a CLT for a sequence (Proposition
3.1
) and we improve the result by proving a functional CLT. Next, in Section
3.2
, we state new hypotheses well-suited for partial-sum processes, and in Section
3.3
, we show some applications with discontinuous limits.

3.1.
Early work

In the special case where
A(t)=t
everywhere and all random variables are built on a common probability space,
the usual requirements for a martingale CLT to hold
(
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}W
with Brownian motion
W
as the limit)
are those in
McLeish, 1974
and
(
Aldous, 1978
, Theorem 3)
, namely,
A_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}t
,
plus a uniform boundedness condition on the second moment such as

\displaystyle\sup_{n}E\left(\max_{j\leq{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}X_{n,j}^{2}\right)<\infty
, in both cases for each
t\geq 0
.
CLTs involving explicit mixtures of laws date back to the same period — here is a sample result
(
Hall and Heyde, 1980
, Corollary 3.1)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that the
\sigma
-algebras are nested:
\mathcal{G}_{n,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n+1,k}
for all
n\geq 1
and all
1\leq k\leq n
.
Assume that

A_{n}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\eta
as
n\to\infty
, for some random variable
\eta
such that
P\{\eta\in(0,\infty)\}=1
.
Suppose also that, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\left\{X_{n,j}^{2}\mathbb{I}(|X_{n,j}|>\epsilon)|\mathcal{G}_{n,j-1}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
, as
n\to\infty
.
Then
M_{n}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}Z\sqrt{\eta}
, where
Z\sim N(0,1)
is independent of
\eta
.

In
Hall and Heyde, 1980
the conclusion is stated for (the stronger) stable convergence.
Here is an example where
\eta
is non deterministic, inspired by
Hall and Heyde, 1980
.

Example 3.1
.

Set
\{Y_{i}\}
and
\{\psi_{i}\}
iid with
\displaystyle P(Y_{i}=\pm 1)=P(\psi_{i}=\pm 1)=\frac{1}{2}
,
the two sequences being independent of each other. Let
\displaystyle B_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\;}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}Y_{i}\psi_{i}
,

\displaystyle M_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\;}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}Y_{i}Z_{i-1}
, where
\displaystyle Z_{i}:=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{Y_{j}}{j}\right)
and
Z_{0}:=1
.
For every
n\geq 1
, write
\mathcal{G}_{n,k}:=\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma\{Y_{i},\psi_{i};i\leq k\}
and

\displaystyle Z_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{Y_{i}}{i}\in\mathcal{F}=\sigma\{Y_{i};i\geq 1\}
. It follows that

\langle M_{n},B_{n}\rangle=[M_{n},B_{n}]_{t}=0
and

\displaystyle\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}=[M_{n}]_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}Z_{i-1}^{2}\to A(t)=tZ_{\infty}^{2}
, with
\displaystyle E\left(Z_{\infty}^{2}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}
.
As
|Z_{i}|\leq 1+\log{i}
,
\displaystyle J_{t}(M_{n})=\max_{1\leq i\leq{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}|Y_{i}Z_{i-1}|/\sqrt{n}\;\leq\left(1+\log{n}\right)/\sqrt{n}\;\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,
Theorem
2.2
yields
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}W\circ A
.
Proposition
3.1
also holds, as the Lindeberg-type condition ensues from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\log j)^{2}\mathbb{I}(\log j>\epsilon\sqrt{n})=0
for all
n
large enough. Note that our result is stronger than the result obtained by Proposition
3.1
.

Remark 3.1
.

Some measurability constraint on
\eta
or on the family of
\sigma
-algebras is necessary, otherwise the conclusion fails —
see
(
Dvoretzky, 1972
, Remark 6.1)
and
(
Hall and Heyde, 1980
, Section 3.3, Example 4)
for counterexamples.
These counterexamples all involve a limit
A
stochastically dependent on all of
\{X_{n,j}\}
, as does Example
3.1
.
The construction of a Brownian motion
B
rendering the limit
A
to be
\mathbb{F}_{B}
-measurable, with natural filtration

\mathbb{F}_{B}=\{\mathcal{F}_{B,t}:\;t\geq 0\}
, while keeping
A
independent of
W
, frees our approach from such constraints
on the martingale sequence itself. Focusing instead on the structure of the limit, expands the usefulness and applicability of the CLT.

3.2.
Hypotheses for pure jump arrays

Getting back to cases where
A
is discontinuous, Corollary
2.5
offers complementary versions with discontinuous limits,
of earlier CLTs with continuous limits — see
Merlevède et al., 2019
for a recent survey and Section
4
for some such versions. In the current discrete context, the following Hypothesis
3.1
implies Hypothesis
2.1
.

Hypothesis 3.1
.

All of the following hold:

(a)

V_{n,\infty}=\infty
almost surely for each fixed
n\geq 1
;

(b)

There is a
D
-valued process
A
started at
A(0)=0
, such that

(i)
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
;

(ii)
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left\{A_{n}(t)\right\}=E\left\{A(t)\right\}<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
;

(iii)
A(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
almost surely.

(c)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left\{X_{n,{\lfloor nt\rfloor}+1}^{2}\right\}=0
, for any
t\geq 0
;

(d)

E\left\{V_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)}\right\}\to t
as
n\to\infty
, for any
t\geq 0
.

(e)

Either of the following two conditions holds:

(i)
A
is an inhomogeneous Lévy process;

(ii)
\tau(t)=\inf\{s\geq 0;A(s)>t\}
is an inhomogeneous Lévy process such that

\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
.

Quick verifications confirm that: Hypothesis
3.1
a implies Hypothesis
2.1
a;

3.1
b is the same as
2.1
b;
3.1
c is equivalent to
2.1
c;

3.1
d is equivalent to
2.1
d;
3.1
e.i is the same as
2.1
e.i;
and
3.1
e.ii is the same as
2.1
e.ii, as
A_{n}(0)=0
.
Finally,
2.1
e.iii always fails for discrete martingale arrays, since
A_{n}(0)=0
and
V_{n,\infty}>0
together
imply
\tau_{n}(0)>0
for all
n\geq 1
, so the inverse mapping
A_{n}\mapsto\tau_{n}

may not be continuous at
A_{n}
in the
\mathcal{M}_{1}
-topology.
This is the case here even though the mapping
\tau_{n}\mapsto A_{n}
is continuous in the
\mathcal{M}_{1}
-topology.
Note also that Hypothesis
3.1
d holds if for any sequence of stopping times

\gamma_{n}\to\infty
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left(X_{n,\gamma_{n}}^{2}\right)=0
,
because of

V_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)}=A_{n}\circ\tau_{n}(t)\geq t\geq V_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)-1}=V_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)}-\,{\rm E}\left(X_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{n,V_{n}^{-1}(t)-1}\right),

where
V_{n}^{-1}(t)=\inf\{k\geq 0;V_{n,k}>t\}
.
Here
\displaystyle\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)=\frac{1+{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}\in\left(t,t+\frac{1}{n}\right]
.

Remark 3.2
.

In the frequent instance where
X_{n,j}^{2}>0
holds almost surely for all
j
and
n
, there ensues

J_{T}(\tau_{n})\leq 1/n
; under such circumstances,
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
implies

\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
, so
\tau
is continuous and
A
strictly increasing everywhere.
Thus strengthening
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
to
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau

in Hypothesis
3.1
e.ii invariably restricts
\tau
to be a continuous non-decreasing Lévy process and therefore deterministic.
Hence so is
A
. The more interesting limits here arise from instances when
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
fails.

Remark 3.3
.

Proving Hypothesis
3.1
b or
2.1
b is generally the heart of the matter and the hardest part of the verifications.
One can check the
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tightness using Theorem
D.2
and, separately, prove

A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
using the Cramér-Wold device,
which ensures the sufficiency of the convergence of linear combinations

(3.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\omega_{j}\{A_{n}(s_{j})-A_{n}(s_{j-1})\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\omega_{j}\{A(s_{j})-A(s_{j-1})\},

for every
\ell\geq 1
, all
(\omega_{j})_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and each choice of
0=s_{0}\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{\ell}\leq t
.
The same argument works for
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
.

To illustrate the choices made through the above hypotheses, consider their effect on a single sequence of real-valued discrete time martingales.

Example 3.2
.

Set
\displaystyle M_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\xi_{k}
, where
\xi_{k}
is a martingale difference sequence
with
E\left(\xi_{k}^{2}\right)<\infty
and
a_{n}\to\infty
. Then
M_{n}
is a martingale with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}=\mathcal{F}_{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}
,

\displaystyle\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}=A_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{a_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}E(\xi_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})
and

\displaystyle[M_{n}]_{t}=\frac{1}{a_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\xi_{k}^{2}
respectively.
Suppose that
A_{n}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\eta

and
a_{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}^{2}/a_{n}^{2}\to\ell(t)
, with
\ell
continuous, unbounded and started at
\ell(0)=0
.
Then
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
with
A(t)=\ell(t)\eta
, so Hypotheses
3.1
a and
3.1
b hold,
provided
\eta>0
almost surely and
E(\eta)<\infty
both hold.
Note that
\tau_{n}(0)=1/n
and
\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)=({\lfloor nt\rfloor}+1)/n>t
for all
t\geq 0
, so Hypothesis
3.1
c becomes

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{a_{n}^{2}}E(\xi_{n}^{2})=0
, because of
a_{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}^{2}/a_{n}^{2}\to\ell(t)
,
provided we assume
E(\xi_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})>0
to avoid trivialities.
Next, observe that
A_{n}\circ\tau_{n}(t)=A_{n}\left(\lceil nt\rceil/n\right)
.
Therefore, Hypothesis
3.1
d is equivalent to
\displaystyle A(t)=\frac{t\eta}{E(\eta)}
, which implies
\ell(t)E(\eta)=t
.
This suggests Hypothesis
3.1
d is only adapted to the single discrete martingale context when

A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
holds for
A
a straight line with an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable slope.
These hypotheses are to be compared with the classical ones for CLTs:

(L)

(Lindeberg-type condition)

(3.2)

\frac{1}{a_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}E\left\{\xi_{k}^{2}\mathbb{I}(|\xi_{k}|>a_{n}\varepsilon)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0\quad\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0;

(M)

(McLeish-type condition)

(3.3)

\sup_{s\leq t}|[M_{n}]_{s}-A_{n}(s)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0\quad\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0.

Keep in mind that (
3.2
) implies (
3.3
)
(
Hall and Heyde, 1980
, Theorem 2.23)

when
A_{n}(t)
is assumed to be tight for every
t>0
. More on these and related conditions can be found
in
Hall and Heyde, 1980
. We provide such a comparison next, in a simpler but still illustrative context.

Example 3.3
.

Pursuant to the notation of Example
3.2
, the
\{\xi_{k}\}
are now independent random variables
such that, for each
k\geq 1
,
P(\xi_{k}=\pm k^{\alpha/2})=1/(2k^{\beta})
while
P(\xi_{k}=0)=1-1/k^{\beta}
,
with
\beta\geq 0
and
\delta=\alpha-\beta+1\geq 0
. Set
a_{n}^{2}=n^{\delta}
if
\delta>0
and
a_{n}^{2}=\log{n}
if
\delta=0
.
When
\alpha\in[-1,0]
,
\sup_{k\geq 1}|\xi_{k}|\leq 1
holds and condition (
3.2
) in Example
3.2
is satisfied;
hence so is condition (
3.3
), no matter what the values of
\delta\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in[0,1]
are. We proceed with
\alpha>0
.
When
\delta=0
, condition (
3.2
) now reads

\displaystyle(\log n)^{-1}\sum_{k=\lfloor(\log n)^{1/\alpha}\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{n}k^{-1}\to 0
for any
\varepsilon>0
;
when
\delta>0
, condition (
3.2
) reads instead

n^{-\delta}\sum_{k=\lfloor n^{\delta/\alpha}\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{n}k^{\delta-1}\to 0
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Hence,
when
\alpha>0
, condition (
3.2
) holds if and only if both
\beta<1
and
\delta>0
.
When
\delta=0
, Hypothesis
3.1
.b.iii also fails, as
A_{n}(t)\to 1
for all
t>0
in this case;
but when
\delta>0
, Hypotheses
3.1
a and
3.1
b hold since it means that all of the following must hold:
using
\displaystyle A_{n}(t)=n^{-\delta}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}k^{\delta-1}
,

A_{n}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, for each fixed
n\geq 1
, plus

A_{n}(t)\to A(t)=\dfrac{t^{\delta}}{\delta}
as
n\to\infty
, for all
t\geq 0
,
and
A(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, which only holds provided
\delta>0
as well.
Hypotheses
3.1
a and
3.1
b are equivalent to
\delta>0
.
The behavior of
\displaystyle[M_{n}]_{t}=n^{-\delta}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}k^{\alpha}\mathbb{I}(\xi_{k}\neq 0)
is markedly different from that of
A_{n}(t)=E[M_{n}]_{t}

— for instance,
[M_{n}]_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
, for each
t\geq 0
, whenever
\beta>1
,
by an application of the Borel-Cantelli lemma to the sequence
\{\mathbb{I}(\xi_{k}\neq 0)\}
.
When
\delta>0
,
Z_{n}(t)=[M_{n}]_{t}-A_{n}(t)=n^{-\delta}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}k^{\alpha}\left\{\mathbb{I}(\xi_{k}\neq 0)-k^{-\beta}\right\}

is a martingale for each fixed
n
, and

\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}={\rm Var}\{Z_{n}(t)\}=\frac{1}{n^{2\delta}}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}k^{2\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{k^{\beta}}-\frac{1}{k^{2\beta}}\right),

satisfies, for each fixed
t>0
,
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to t^{2\alpha}/(2\alpha)
if
\beta=1
,
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to 0
if
\beta<1

and
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to\infty
if
\beta>1
; while
Z_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}-t^{\delta}/\delta
when
\beta>1
.
Condition (
3.3
) implies
Z_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
for each
t>0
, which fails as soon as
\beta>1
and
\delta\geq 0
;
while
\beta<1
and
\delta>0
together ensure
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to 0
for each

and therefore condition (
3.3
) is valid as well,
by way of an application of Lemma
B.2
.
Meanwhile, the conditions of the CLT in Theorem
2.2
boil down to
\delta>0
together with

\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\xi_{k}^{2}/a_{n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
.
Invoking Theorem
D.2
, they imply that
[M_{n}]
is
\mathcal{C}
-tight as well;
hence,
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to\infty
for fixed
t>0
is impossible and
the conditions of the CLT in Theorem
2.2
are not satisfied either when
\beta>1
.
If
\beta<1
then
\langle Z_{n}\rangle_{t}\to 0
holds and so do all the conditions of this CLT.
This leaves only the case
\beta=1
and
\alpha=\delta>0
: neither condition (
3.3
) nor
the CLT in Theorem
2.2
hold.
Summarizing: under
\alpha>0
and
\delta>0
, the Lindeberg condition (
3.2
), the McLeish condition (
3.3
)
and those for the CLT in Theorem
2.2
all hold when
\beta<1
but do not when
\beta\geq 1
.
For this example the three are equivalent.
The construction of a sequence of martingales
B_{n}
converging to a Brownian motion
B
with the desired properties
in order to apply Theorem
2.2
, has been sidestepped here.
Enlarge the space (if necessary) in order to use an iid Bernoulli sequence
\{\psi_{i}\}
with the right features.
Define the martingale
\displaystyle B_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\;}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\psi_{k}\sign(\xi_{k})
, with

\{\psi_{k}\}
iid with
\displaystyle P(\psi_{k}=\pm 1)=\frac{1}{2}
, independent of the whole sequence
\{X_{j}\}
.
That

holds for some Brownian motion

results from another application of

through the observations :
\langle M_{n},B_{n}\rangle=[M_{n},B_{n}]_{t}=0
,

\displaystyle\langle B_{n}\rangle_{t}=\frac{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}\to t
and the use of a further martingale
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\;}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\psi_{k}
.
Furthermore, while uniform convergence of

fails when

to
rescaled martingale
\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{n}(t)=\{\log{m(n)}\}^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor m(nt)\rfloor}\xi_{k}
with the

maintaining the requirement

and finding

with

and hence

a Brownian motion.
Note also that, for this rescaled martingale, when

,

and

,
illustrating the discontinuity of the map

for any fixed

, in general.
Finally, when

and

, the Lindeberg condition (
3.2
),

) and the conditions of the CLT in Theorem

however, Corollary

is applicable here since

.

3.3.
Applications

The following example, inspired from Mathematical Finance, shows that one can indeed get discontinuous limits.

Example 3.4
.

Let
\{\xi_{n,k}:k\geq 1,n\geq 1\}
be independent
{\rm Gamma}(1/n,1)
, independent of
\{Z_{k}:k\geq 1\}
which are iid
{\rm N}(0,1)
.
Set
\displaystyle M_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\xi_{n,k}^{1/2}Z_{k}
and define filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n,k}=\sigma\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\}\cup\mathcal{F}_{n,0}
,
with initial
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{n,0}=\sigma\{\xi_{n,k},k\geq 1\}
.
Then
M_{n}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{n,[n\cdot]}
-martingale with quadratic variation
[M_{n}]_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\xi_{n,k}Z_{k}^{2}

and predictable compensator
\displaystyle A_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\xi_{n,k}
. First there holds

A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
, where
A(t)\sim{\rm Gamma}(t,1)
.
We also have
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}M
,
where
M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{=}}W\circ A
, and
W
is a Brownian motion independent of
A
. In fact,
M(t)
has a Linnik distribution

(
Jacques et al., 1999
)
with characteristic function
E\left\{e^{i\lambda M(t)}\right\}=(1+\lambda^{2}/2)^{-t}
.
Since

4.
Non-stationary arrays with discontinuous limits

Given is an array
\{Y_{n,j}\}_{j,n\geq 1}
of random variables with mean
0

and finite variances, not necessarily all the same. No restriction is made on their dependency structure.
Put
\mathcal{G}_{n,k}:=\sigma\{Y_{n,j};j\leq k\}
and successively set
Z_{n,j}:=E\left(Y_{n,j}|\mathcal{G}_{n,j-1}\right)
,
X_{n,j}:=Y_{n,j}-Z_{n,j}
,

\displaystyle N_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Y_{n,j}
and
\displaystyle O_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Z_{n,j}
. Note that
\mathcal{G}_{n,k}=\sigma\{X_{n,j};j\leq k\}
as well.
The square integrable martingale of interest here is
\displaystyle M_{n}(t)=N_{n}(t)-O_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}X_{n,j}
,
with
A_{n}(t)=\langle M_{n}\rangle(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}E\left(X_{n,k}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{n,k-1}\right)
.
Empty sums are null, therefore in particular set
N_{n}(0)=O_{n}(0)=M_{n}(0)=A_{n}(0)=0
.

Merlevède et al., 2019
contains several CLTs of the form

N_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}N_{\infty}

when the limit
N_{\infty}
is the stochastic integral of a continuous function with respect to a Brownian motion.
Corollary
2.5
allows for the extension of several of their results to mixtures of stochastic
processes including some with discontinuities. Typically, Corollary
2.5
together with
Lemma
B.1
yield
N_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}N_{\infty}=W\circ A+O_{\infty}
with

W
is a Brownian motion independent of
A
, provided all conditions are verified of course, notably

O_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}O_{\infty}
with a continuous limit.
The following example gives a sample calculation of this type of asymptotic transfer.

Example 4.1
.

Consider an arbitrary array
\{Q_{n,j}\}_{j,n\geq 1}
of
bounded predictable random variables in the sense that each
Q_{n,j}
is
\mathcal{G}_{n,j-1}
-measurable.
Random variables
Y_{n,j}
still have mean
0
and finite second moment.
Set
\bar{N}_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Q_{n,j}Y_{n,j}
, a weighted version of
N_{n}
,

\displaystyle\bar{O}_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Q_{n,j}Z_{n,j}
and

\displaystyle\bar{M}_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Q_{n,j}X_{n,j}
.
The martingale transform
\bar{M}_{n}
of
\{X_{n,j}\}_{j,n\geq 1}
by
\{Q_{n,j}\}_{j,n\geq 1}

(
Burkholder, 1966

is another martingale, with compensator

\displaystyle\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}Q_{n,j}^{2}E\left(X_{n,j}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{n,j-1}\right)
.
Set
\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Q_{n,j}\mathbb{I}_{\{j/n\leq t<(j+1)/n\}}
,
\bar{W}_{n}:=\bar{M}_{n}\circ\bar{\tau}_{n}

and
\langle\bar{W}_{n}\rangle:=\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle\circ\bar{\tau}_{n}
.
Take notice that
\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}(s)dA_{n}(s)
and

\bar{O}_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bar{Q}_{n}(s)dO_{n}(s)

The following result provides a prototypical CLT obtained by such an asymptotic transfer.
In essence, it is a CLT for semimartingales. It is proved in Appendix

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that

2.5
.
Assume also all of the following:

with bounded variations on

b)

\displaystyle 0<\inf_{j,n\geq 1}|Q_{n,j}|\leq\sup_{j,n\geq 1}|Q_{n,j}|<\infty

c)

\bar{Q}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bar{Q}_{\infty}

and
\bar{W}
a Brownian motion independent of

Appendix A
Real-valued Lévy processes

An inhomogeneous Lévy process
A
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted
D
-valued process with independent increments (with respect to

\mathbb{F}
), without fixed times of discontinuity and such that
A_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}
. The inhomogeneity (in time) means that stationarity of the increments, usually required of Lévy processes, is lifted. This choice reflects the existence of weak limits exhibiting independent increments without homogeneity in time or space, in some applications. All processes are built directly on
D
, ensuring continuity in probability of the trajectories, another usual requirement.

The characteristic function of an inhomogeneous Lévy process
A
is given by

E\left[\left.e^{i\theta(A_{t}-A_{s})}\right|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=e^{\Psi_{t}(\theta)-\Psi_{s}(\theta)},\quad 0\leq s<t,

where
A
is
\mathbb{F}
-adapted with
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,

\Psi_{t}(\theta)=i\theta B_{t}-\frac{\theta^{2}C_{t}}{2}+\left\{\prod_{0<r\leq t}e^{-ir\Delta B_{r}}\right\}\int_{0}^{t}\int\left(e^{i\theta x}-1-i\theta x\mathbb{I}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\right)\nu(du,dx),

\Delta B_{r}=B_{r}-B_{r-}
, and
\nu
is a Lévy measure.
For details see
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.
The characteristics of
A
are defined to be the deterministic functions
(B,C,\nu)
.
The choice of
h(x)=x\mathbb{I}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}
is classical but in
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
,
it is rejected in favor of a continuous function
h
, a substitution which affects the value of
B
exclusively
— see
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.
One can instead make use of
h(x)=x\mathbb{I}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}+\sign(x)\mathbb{I}_{\{|x|>1\}}
, which is continuous.
A Lévy process is called homogeneous or stationary when
B_{t}=tB_{1}
,
C_{t}=tC_{1}
and
\nu([0,t]\times A)=t\nu([0,1]\times A)
,
so that
\Psi_{t}(\theta)=t\Psi_{1}(\theta)
ensues.
Important stationary Lévy processes include Brownian motion (
\nu\equiv 0
) and, for each
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
(non-Gaussian)
\alpha
-stable Lévy motions
(
Whitt, 2002
, Section 4.5)
, characterized by both
C_{t}\equiv 0
and

\displaystyle\nu(du,dx)=dudx\cdot|x|^{-1-\alpha}\left[c_{\alpha}^{+}\mathbb{I}_{\{x>0\}}+c_{\alpha}^{-}\mathbb{I}_{\{x<0\}}\right]
, when
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
,
with an alternative form for
\nu
when
\alpha=1
, the Cauchy process, which will not concern us in this paper.
Recall that
\alpha
-stable Lévy motions are either without negative jumps (
1\leq\alpha<2
) or non-decreasing (
0<\alpha<1
).

The following key result is taken from
Rémillard and Vaillancourt, 2025
.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose
A
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted inhomogeneous Lévy process with finite variation on any finite interval
[0,T]
and no Brownian component, i.e.,
C_{t}=0
for all
t\geq 0
. Then
A
is independent of any
\mathbb{F}
-Brownian motion
W
.
Further, if
A
is non-negative and non-decreasing everywhere, then the random rescaling property

(A.1)

W\circ A(t)-W\circ A(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{=}}(W_{t}-W_{s})\sqrt{\frac{A_{t}-A_{s}}{t-s}}

holds for every
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
and both sides define equidistributed inhomogeneous Lévy processes.

Appendix B
Topologies and weak convergence on
D

Equality in law is denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{=}}
,
convergence in law by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}
,
in probability by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Pr}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}
and
almost sure convergence by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}
.
Sequences of random processes are sometimes written
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
or
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
for clarity,
but usually just as
X_{n}
for brevity; similarly for all other types of sequences.

For either
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{J}
or
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{M}
, a (deterministic) sequence
x_{n}\in D
is said to

\mathcal{H}_{1}
-converge to
x\in D
if and only if

(i)

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}(t)=x(t)
holds pointwise for every
t
in an everywhere dense subset of
[0,\infty)
that includes the origin,

(ii)

for every
T>0
,

(B.1)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\omega_{\mathbb{H}}(x_{n},\delta,T)=0.

A subset
K\subset D
is
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-compact if and only if, for every
T>0
, there holds

\sup_{x\in K}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|x(t)|<\infty
and

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{x\in K}\omega_{\mathbb{H}}(x,\delta,T)=0
.

A sequence of
D
-valued processes
X_{n}
are
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tight if and only if

(i)

for every
t
in an everywhere dense subset of
[0,\infty)
that includes the origin, the marginal distributions of
X_{n}(t)
are tight,

(ii)

for any
\epsilon>0
, and
T>0
,

(B.2)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P\{\omega_{\mathbb{H}}(X_{n},\delta,T)>\epsilon\}=0.

Recall that
\mathcal{C}
-tightness is defined by setting
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{C}

with
\mathbb{C}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=|x_{3}-x_{2}|
in (
B.2
).
When (
B.2
) holds for any
T
, it remains so on the boundary
T=\delta
,
for each
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{J}
or
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{M}
.

Complete coverage of the
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-topology can be found in
Ethier and Kurtz, 1986
and
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
,
where
\mathbb{B}
is actually allowed be a Polish space; while
Whitt, 2002
is our main reference on the
\mathcal{M}_{1}
-topology.
Neither topology turns
D
into a topological vector space, that is to say, neither is compatible with the linear structure inherited
on
D
from the Banach space
\mathbb{B}
, which would have made the sum a continuous operator. Consequently, when considering vectors of processes valued in a product
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{B}_{i}
of Banach spaces, neither topology offers the equivalence
between
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-convergence of sequences of probability measures on
D
and coordinatewise
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-convergence,
an equivalence which holds for
\mathcal{C}
-convergence.
Similarly
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tightness cannot be treated coordinatewise; it does however ensue from the

\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tightness of each coordinate plus that of each pairwise sum of coordinates —

verifications are thus required.
This fact precludes immediate extensions of some results from the real line to higher dimensional spaces, but there are exceptions,
as will be seen in several of the proofs in Appendix
E
. One such exception is the following result, taken from
Rémillard and Vaillancourt, 2025
.

Lemma B.1
.

For any pair of functions
X,Y\in D
there holds, for every choice of
\delta>0
and
T>0
,

\omega_{\mathbb{H}}(X+Y,\delta,T)\leq\omega_{\mathbb{H}}(X,\delta,T)+\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(Y,\delta,T)
.
For sequences of
D
-valued processes, if
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tight and

Y_{n}
is
\mathcal{C}
-tight, then
X_{n}+Y_{n}
and
(X_{n},Y_{n})
are also
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tight.
Similarly if
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{H}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}X
,
Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}Y
, and

(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(X,Y)
then
X_{n}+Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{H}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}X+Y

and
(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{H}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(X,Y)
.

Deducing the
\mathcal{H}_{1}
-tightness of
D
-valued processes from that of dominating non-decreasing processes,
is achieved using Lenglart’s celebrated inequality, the proof of which can be found in

Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.
For any

and
T>0

\Delta f(t):=f(t)-f(t-)
and
J_{T}(f):=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\Delta f(t)|<\infty

Lemma B.2

(Lenglart’s inequality)
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted
D
-valued process. Suppose that
Y
is optional, non-decreasing,
and that, for any bounded stopping time
\tau

E|X(\tau)|\leq{\rm E}\left[{Y(\tau)}\right]
.
Then for any stopping time
\tau
and all
\varepsilon,\eta>0
,

a)

if
Y
is predictable,

(B.3)

P(\sup_{s\leq\tau}|X(s)|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}+P(Y(\tau)\geq\eta).

b)

if
Y
is adapted,

(B.4)

P(\sup_{s\leq\tau}|X(s)|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\eta+E\left\{J_{\tau}(Y)\right\}\right]+P(Y(\tau)\geq\eta).

Proving the stronger
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tightness generally involves the following lemma.

Lemma B.3

(Aldous’s criterion)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of

\left\{{\tau_{n}}\right\}_{n\geq 1}

\left\{{\delta_{n}}\right\}_{n\geq 1}

converging to

(A)

\mathcal{J}_{1}

if either of the two following conditions holds:

(1)

(2)

.

Proof.

See
Aldous, 1978
,
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003

.

.

Tightness

In this section the martingales take their values in
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}
.

Let
M
be a
D
-valued square integrable
\mathbb{F}
-martingale started at
M(0)=0
and
with quadratic variation
[M]
, predictable compensator
\langle M\rangle
, continuous part
M^{c}
,
purely discontinuous part
M^{d}
and largest jump
J_{T}(M)
up to time
T>0
.
The existence of
M^{c}
and
M^{d}
as orthogonal square integrable
\mathbb{F}
-martingales
such that
M=M^{c}+M^{d}
was established during the early development of martingale theory
and a proof can be found in
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.

Proposition D.1
.

Both the following hold almost surely:

\displaystyle[M]_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle M^{c}\rangle_{T}+\sum_{0<s\leq T}\{\Delta M(s)\}^{2};

\displaystyle\langle M\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle M^{c}\rangle_{T}+\sum_{0<s\leq T}E\bigl(\{\Delta M(s)\}^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s-}\bigr).

Proof.

The first statement is actually valid for any semimartingale
M

with
M^{c}
denoting its continuous martingale part
(
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
, Theorem I.4.52)
.
The second one is equivalent to stating that

N_{T}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq T}\Delta M^{2}(s)-\sum_{0\leq s\leq T}E\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right\}

is a martingale. This is indeed true. For if
t<T
, then

\displaystyle E(N_{T}|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{t}+E\left[\left.\sum_{t<s\leq T}\Delta M^{2}(s)-E\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right\}\right|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{t}+\sum_{t<s\leq T}\left[E\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}-E\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}\right]=N_{t},

because
E\left[\,{\rm E}\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right\}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=E\left\{\Delta M^{2}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}
for any
t<s
.
∎

Note that
\Delta\langle M\rangle_{s}={\rm E}\left[{\Delta[M]_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s-}}\right]

and
{\rm E}\left[{J_{s}(\langle M\rangle)}\right]\leq{\rm E}\left[{J_{s}([M])}\right]
, for further reference.

Let
M_{n}
be a sequence of
D
-valued square integrable
\mathbb{F}
-martingales started at
M_{n}(0)=0

with quadratic variation
[M_{n}]
and predictable compensator
\langle M_{n}\rangle
.

Now comes the main result about
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tightness, in the real-valued case
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem D.2
.

Let
M_{n}
be a sequence of
D
-valued square integrable
\mathbb{F}
-martingales
with predictable quadratic variation
A_{n}=\langle M_{n}\rangle
, such that
(M_{n}(0))_{n\geq 1}
is tight.

a)

If
A_{n}
verifies condition (A) in Lemma
B.3
, then so do both
M_{n}
and
[M_{n}]
.
If in addition
J_{t}(M_{n})
is tight for every
t>0
, then both
M_{n}
and
[M_{n}]
are
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight.
Finally, if
J_{t}(M_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, then both
M_{n}
and
[M_{n}]
are
\mathcal{C}
-tight.

b)

Assume
\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm E}\left[{J_{t}^{2}(M_{n})}\right]<\infty
for any
t>0
.
If
[M_{n}]
verifies condition (A) in Lemma
B.3
, then so do both
M_{n}
and
A_{n}
; all three are
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight.
If in addition
J_{t}(M_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, then all three are
\mathcal{C}
-tight.

c)

If
A_{n}
is
\mathcal{C}
-tight, then both
M_{n}
and
[M_{n}]
are
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight.

d)

Assume
\sup_{n}E\{J_{t}(M_{n})\}<\infty
for every
t>0
. If
M_{n}
is
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight then so is
[M_{n}]
.

Remark D.1
.

\mathcal{C}
-tightness implies condition (A) in Lemma
B.3
(Remark
B.1
).
Even the
\mathcal{C}
-tightness of
[M_{n}]
does not necessarily imply the tightness of
A_{n}(t)
for a single
t>0
without some
control over the growth of the latter — choose scaling
a_{n}^{2}=n^{2}
and any pair
(\alpha,\beta)
satisfying
\alpha-1>\beta>1

in Example
3.3
, where
[M_{n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
while
\lim_{n}A_{n}(t)=\infty
for every
t>0
.
When the increments of
M_{n}
are uniformly bounded, the tightness of
[M_{n}]_{t}
for all
t>0
and that of
A_{n}(t)
for all
t>0

are equivalent and each imply that of
M_{n}(t)
for all
t>0
as well
(
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
, Proposition VI.6.13)
.
For additional insight into the role Lemma
B.3

plays in the proof of
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tightness results, see
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.

Proof.

a) Set
X_{n}(s)=\left\{M_{n}(s+\tau_{n})-M_{n}(\tau_{n})\right\}^{2}
and

Y_{n}(s)=\langle M_{n}\rangle_{s+\tau_{n}}-\langle M_{n}\rangle_{\tau_{n}}
,
where
\tau_{n}
is a sequence of stopping times uniformly bounded by
T
for any
n
,
and
{\rm E}\left[{X_{n}(\tau)}\right]={\rm E}\left[{Y_{n}(\tau)}\right]
, for any bounded stopping time
\tau
.
Using Equation (
B.3
) from Lemma
B.2
, we have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\eta>0
and any numerical sequence
\delta_{n}\in(0,1)\to 0
,

P\left\{|M_{n}(\tau_{n}+\delta_{n})-M_{n}(\tau_{n})|\geq\varepsilon\right\}\leq P\left\{Y_{n}(\delta_{n})>\eta\right\}+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Choosing
\eta=\varepsilon^{3}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}P\left\{|M_{n}(\tau_{n}+\delta_{n})-M_{n}(\tau_{n})|\geq\varepsilon\right\}\leq\varepsilon

follows, since
Y_{n}(\delta_{n})
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
, showing that condition (A) of Lemma
B.3
is met by
M_{n}
as well.
When condition (1) is assumed,
M_{n}
is
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight.
Finally, if
J_{t}(M_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, then
\mathcal{C}
-tightness and
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tightness are equivalent for
M_{n}
.
The proof is similar for
[M_{n}]
, using
J_{T}([M_{n}])=J_{T}^{2}(M_{n})
.

b) Set
X_{n}(s)=\langle M_{n}\rangle_{s+\tau_{n}}-\langle M_{n}\rangle_{\tau_{n}}

and
Y_{n}(s)=[M_{n}]_{s+\tau_{n}}-[M_{n}]_{\tau_{n}}
instead, so there holds

E\{X_{n}(\delta_{n})\}=E\{Y_{n}(\delta_{n})\}
and

P(\langle M_{n}\rangle_{s+\tau_{n}}-\langle M_{n}\rangle_{\tau_{n}}\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\eta+E\left\{J_{\delta_{n}}(Y_{n})\right\}\right]+P(Y_{n}(\delta_{n}))\geq\eta),

using (
B.4
) of Lemma
B.2
, with
J_{\delta_{n}}(Y_{n})\leq Y_{n}(\delta_{n})
since
[M_{n}]
is a non-decreasing process.
Since both
Y_{n}(\delta_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm E}\left[{J_{t}([M_{n}])}\right]<\infty
hold,
uniform integrability yields
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E\left\{J_{\delta_{n}}(Y_{n})\right\}=0
and condition (A) is met by
\langle M_{n}\rangle
.
Proposition
D.1
implies
{\rm E}\left[{J_{t}(\langle M_{n}\rangle)}\right]\leq{\rm E}\left[{J_{t}([M_{n}])}\right]

so
J_{t}(\langle M_{n}\rangle)
is tight for every
t>0
.
Condition (1) of Lemma
B.3
is satisfied as well and
\langle M_{n}\rangle
is
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight.
A similar argument yields the results for
M_{n}
.

c) See
Rebolledo, 1979
.

d) See
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
.
∎

Appendix E
Proofs of the main results

E.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Hypothesis
2.1
d implies that
\langle W_{n}\rangle
is
\mathcal{C}
-tight, by Proposition
C.2
;
applying Theorem
2.2
to the martingale
W_{n}=M_{n}\circ\tau_{n}
,
taking
B_{n}
to be a Brownian motion independent of
M_{n}
, yields

(W_{n},\langle W_{n}\rangle)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(W,\langle W\rangle)
,
with
W
a standard Brownian motion since
\langle W\rangle_{t}=t
. Hypothesis
2.1
b.i states that

A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
, so
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
ensues.

In the case where Hypothesis
2.1
e.ii or
2.1
e.iii holds, so
\tau
is an inhomogeneous Lévy process,

W
is independent of the pair
(A,\tau)
. The argument runs as follows. Under Hypothesis
2.1
e.iii
and by Proposition
C.1
, the continuity of mapping
A_{n}\mapsto\tau_{n}

under the
\mathcal{M}_{1}
-topology on
D_{\uparrow}^{0}
ensures that
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
implies both

\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
and
(A_{n},\tau_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(A,\tau)
.
This proof remains unchanged if Hypothesis
2.1
e.ii holds instead, starting with

\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
— which implies
\tau_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
—
and proceeding similarly. Either way, since
(W,\tau)
is
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-adapted, and
\tau
is a non-decreasing
inhomogeneous Lévy process, it has no Brownian component, so it is independent of
W
by Lemma
A.1
.
Since
A(0)=0
guarantees the continuity of the reverse mapping
\tau\mapsto A
,
A
is also independent of
W

and so is the pair
(A,\tau)
as the image of Borel measurable mapping
\tau\mapsto(A,\tau)
.
The independence of
W
and
(A,\tau)
implies
(A_{n},\tau_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(A,\tau,W)

under either case of Hypothesis
2.1
e.ii or
2.1
e.iii. Since
W_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}W
holds,

(A_{n},\tau_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(A,\tau,W)
ensues by Lemma
B.1
.
If instead Hypothesis
2.1
e.i holds so
A
is an inhomogeneous Lévy process, then the independence of
A
from
W

follows from Lemma
A.1
. Therefore
(A_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(A,W)
ensues.
Next,
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
and
W_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}W
together imply
successively
(A_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(A,W)
and

(W_{n}\circ A_{n},A_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(W\circ A,A)
, since
(W,\langle W\rangle)
has continuous trajectories,
by the
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-continuity of the composition of functions
(
Whitt, 2002
, Theorem 13.2.2)
. It remains to show that
M_{n}
converges to
W\circ A
. First, note that under Hypothesis
2.1
.a,
0\leq\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)-t=\sup\{h\geq 0;A_{n}(t+h)=A_{n}(t)\}<\infty
holds everywhere,
along almost every trajectory, for any
t\geq 0
. Furthermore,

\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(t)=\sup\{u\geq t;A_{n}(u)=A_{n}(t)\}=\inf\{u>t;A_{n}(u)>A_{n}(t)\}

is an
\mathbb{F}_{t+\cdot}
-stopping time for every fixed
t\geq 0
, since the filtration is right continuous
and
A_{n}
is right continuous. Applying Lemma
B.2
in conjunction with (
2.2
) yields,
for any time
t>0
and all
\varepsilon,\eta>0
,

P(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|M_{n}\circ\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}(s)-M_{n}(s)|^{2}\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}+P(\langle M_{n}\circ\tau_{n}\circ A_{n}\rangle_{t}-\langle M_{n}\rangle_{t}\geq\eta).

Hypothesis
2.1
c then yields

M_{n}-W_{n}\circ A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
. As a result,

M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}W\circ A
and

(M_{n},A_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(W\circ A,A,W)
, proving the main statement of the theorem.
Finally, when
J_{t}(M_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
holds for any
t>0
,
then
M
is continuous and hence so is
A
, by
Ethier and Kurtz, 1986
.
Theorem
2.2
therefore implies

(M_{n},A_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(M,A,W)
.
One last appeal to Lemma
B.1
yields

(M_{n},A_{n},\tau_{n},W_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(M,A,\tau,W)
, completing the proof.
∎

E.2.
Proof of Corollary

The continuity away from the axes, on the complement of

\displaystyle\cup_{j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}}\{(s_{1},\cdots,s_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:A_{n}(s_{j})=0\}
, of the map

\{(M_{n},A_{n})(s_{1}),\cdots,(M_{n},A_{n})(s_{\ell})\}\mapsto\left\{\left(A_{n},\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{A_{n}}}\right)(s_{1}),\cdots,\left(A_{n},\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{A_{n}}}\right)(s_{\ell})\right\}

), together yield the

E.3.
Proof of Corollary
2.5

First, build a standard Brownian motion
\hat{B}
on a further enlargement of the stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},P)

so as to be independent of the whole sequence
M_{n}
. For time change
\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(t):=\frac{1-e^{-t}}{n}
,
the perturbed process
\hat{M}_{n}:=M_{n}+\hat{B}\circ\epsilon_{n}
is also a martingale (with respect to the product filtration),
but with both
[\hat{M}_{n}]
and
\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle
strictly increasing everywhere.
Writing
\hat{\tau}_{n}(s)=\inf\{t\geq 0;\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle_{t}>s\}
,
note that
\hat{\tau}_{n}
is continuous everywhere and started at
\hat{\tau}_{n}(0)=0
.
Furthermore
\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|\hat{M}_{n}(s)-M_{n}(s)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
holds for every choice of
T>0
.
Put
\hat{W}_{n}:=\hat{M}_{n}\circ\hat{\tau}_{n}
. Under the stated conditions of Corollary
2.5
,
the martingale
\hat{M}_{n}
also satisfies all Hypothesis
2.1
. In fact, Hypotheses
2.1
a,
2.1
b and
2.1
e.i are valid for
\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle
with the same
A
. Proposition
C.1
ensures the validity of

\hat{\tau}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
as a consequence of
\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f.d.d.}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
.
Hypothesis
2.1
c is valid for
(\hat{M}_{n},\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle,\hat{\tau}_{n})
,
irrespective of whether or not it does for
(M_{n},A_{n},\tau_{n})
, since
\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle
is strictly increasing, which implies

\hat{\tau}_{n}\circ\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle(t)=t
for all
t\geq 0
. By Lemma
B.1
,

A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
implies
\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A
.
Finally, Hypothesis
2.1
d ensues from Proposition
C.2
, since the inverse
\tau
of a strictly increasing
A

is continuous everywhere, so that
\hat{\tau}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{M}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
implies successively

\hat{\tau}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\tau
and

\langle\hat{W}_{n}\rangle=\langle\hat{M}_{n}\rangle\circ\hat{\tau}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}A\circ\tau

and

.

E.4.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Since
(M_{n},A_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(W\circ A,A)
holds with
W
a Brownian motion independent of
A
,
there ensues
N_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}N_{\infty}=W\circ A+O_{\infty}
, by condition a) of Theorem
4.1

and Lemma
B.1
. Applying Corollary
2.5
to
\bar{M}_{n}
yields the existence
of another pair
(\bar{W},\bar{A}_{\infty})
such that

(\bar{M}_{n},\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(\bar{W}\circ\bar{A}_{\infty},\bar{A}_{\infty})
follows,
provided all the following requirements are met:

(1)

\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle_{\infty}=\infty
almost surely for each fixed
n\geq 1
.

(2)

There is a
D
-valued process
\bar{A}_{\infty}
started at
\bar{A}_{\infty}(0)=0
, such that

(i)
\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bar{A}_{\infty}

\bar{A}_{\infty}
a

t\geq 0
;

(iii)
\bar{A}_{\infty}(\infty)=\infty
almost surely.

(3)

{\rm E}\left[{\bar{A}_{\infty}\circ\bar{\tau}_{\infty}(t)}\right]=t
for all
t\geq 0
.

(4)

are uniformly integrable for each fixed

.

4.1
. Since

,
proving Item (2.i) reduces to proving
\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bar{A}_{\infty}
.
Since
A_{n}
is a
\mathcal{J}_{1}
-tight sequence of non-decreasing processes, it is predictably uniformly tight,
in the sense of
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
. As
\bar{Q}_{n}
is predictable and uniformly bounded,

\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle

4.1
.
Furthermore, the continuity of
\bar{Q}_{\infty}
in condition c) of Theorem
4.1
means that the largest jumps of
\bar{Q}_{n}
and

\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle
go to

, since

\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|\Delta\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle_{s}|\leq A_{n}(T)\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|\Delta\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}(s)|.

There follows

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}(u-)dA_{n}(u)\bigr|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}0
.
By
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
there ensues

\displaystyle(\bar{Q}_{n}^{2},A_{n},\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\left(\bar{Q}_{\infty}^{2},A,\bar{A}_{\infty}\right)

Under condition b) of Theorem
4.1
, Item (2.ii) follows by dominated convergence
(

.
Items (2.iii) and (3) follow from conditions b) and c) of Theorem
4.1
, respectively.
Item (4) follows from conditions b) and c) of Theorem

, through

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{n}(s)\leq\tau_{n}\left(\frac{s}{\inf_{j,n\geq 1}Q_{n,j}^{2}}\right)
and therefore

\displaystyle\langle\bar{W}_{n}\rangle_{s}\leq\left(\sup_{j,n\geq 1}Q_{n,j}^{2}\right)A_{n}\circ\bar{\tau}_{n}(s)\leq\left(\sup_{j,n\geq 1}Q_{n,j}^{2}\right)\langle W_{n}\rangle\circ\left(\frac{s}{\inf_{j,n\geq 1}Q_{n,j}^{2}}\right)
.

2.5
yields

(\bar{M}_{n},\langle\bar{M}_{n}\rangle)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(\bar{W}\circ\bar{A}_{\infty},\bar{A}_{\infty})
.
Finally,
\bar{N}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}_{1}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bar{N}_{\infty}=\bar{W}\circ\bar{A}_{\infty}+\bar{O}_{\infty}
ensues from

B.1
again, through condition a) of Theorem
4.1
; indeed,

is a

-tight sequence of processes with bounded variation, it is also predictably uniformly tight and the argument

.
