TurboID‐based proteomic profiling reveals proxitome of the IRT1 metal transporter and new insight into IRT1 endocytosis
Authors: Liza Pellegrin, Steven Fanara, Bertrand Fabre, Carole Pichereaux, Nathalie Leonhardt, Valérie Cotelle, Grégory Vert, Julie Neveu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Iron Regulated Transporter1 (IRT1) is the major root iron transporter responsible for broad‐spectrum metal absorption in Arabidopsis thaliana root epidermal cells. Non‐iron metal substrates of IRT1 were recently shown to regulate IRT1 cell surface levels by endocytosis and vacuolar degradation.TurboID‐based proximity labeling was recently developed to detect protein–protein interactions and thus shed light on the intricate regulation of proteins in living cells. Although TurboID‐based proximity labeling technology has been successfully established in mammals, its application in plant systems remains limited, especially for highly hydrophobic multispan transmembrane proteins.Here, we established TurboID for proximity labeling of IRT1 and identified 494 IRT1‐specific proximal proteins, including the previously reported FYVE1 IRT1‐interacting protein. To showcase the biological relevance of identified IRT1 proximal proteins, we further characterized the NHX5 Na+(K+)/H+ antiporter and the RGLG2 E3 ubiquitin ligase. We validated both IRT1 proximal proteins as genuine IRT1 partners using several orthogonal assays. We also uncovered the contribution of NHX‐type antiporters and RGLG‐type E3 ligases in plant responses to non‐iron metal nutrition and IRT1 endocytosis.Overall, our work further highlights the power of TurboID to identify new interacting proteins and showcases its application to plant transporters. Keywords: Arabidopsis, degradation, endocytosis, IRT1, TurboID, ubiquitin

Introduction

Iron is essential for plant growth and development, playing fundamental roles in many cellular processes, including photosynthetic and respiratory electron transfer reactions. Conversely, overload of iron is also toxic, leading to oxidative stress. Soil iron bioavailability to plants is often limited, such as in calcareous soils where iron is present under the form of insoluble complexes (Briat et al., 1995). To maintain iron homeostasis, plants must tightly regulate iron absorption from the soil. In non‐graminaceous plants, including the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, iron absorption by root epidermal cells is achieved through the so‐called strategy I that requires three successive steps. First, soil ferric chelates are solubilized by local rhizosphere acidification, notably via the release of protons by the proton pump H+‐ATPase2 (AHA2). Solubilized Fe3+ ions are then reduced to Fe2+ by the Ferric Reduction Oxydase2 (FRO2) reductase and finally transported into the cell by the iron transporter Iron Regulated Transporter1 (IRT1; Palmer & Guerinot, 2009; Thomine & Vert, 2013; Jeong et al., 2017). Apart from the predominant role of IRT1, FRO2, and AHA2, another membrane protein called ATP‐Binding Cassette G37 (ABCG37/PDR9) was demonstrated to be involved in Arabidopsis iron acquisition by exporting coumarins in the rhizosphere under iron deficiency (Fourcroy et al., 2014). These excreted phenolic compounds chelate Fe3+ and facilitate iron availability for reduction by FRO2 (Fourcroy et al., 2016).

IRT1 is a major player in the regulation of plant iron homeostasis, as illustrated by the severe chlorosis and the lethality of irt1‐1 knockout mutant (Vert et al., 2002). As such, IRT1 is at the center of complex transcriptional regulatory networks aiming at integrating plant iron nutrition with several endogenous and exogenous cues. In Arabidopsis, the expression of iron uptake genes is activated under iron‐limited conditions through an intricate cascade of basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors and E3 ubiquitin ligases, culminating in the strong accumulation of IRT1 and FRO2 transcripts in root epidermal cells (Spielmann et al., 2023a).

Recently, much progress has been made in our understanding of IRT1 post‐translational regulation and function at the cell surface. IRT1 is present at the plasma membrane, where it associates with FRO2 and AHA2 to mediate iron uptake (Barberon et al., 2011, 2014; Dubeaux et al., 2018; Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020). IRT1 undergoes endocytosis from the cell surface and is found in the trans Golgi Network/early endosomes (TGN/EE) and is targeted to the vacuole for degradation (Barberon et al., 2011; Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020). IRT1 was shown to undergo constitutive endocytosis, using AP2 adapters and recognition of canonical Tyr‐based motifs within its regulatory loop, and cycling back to the cell surface (Barberon et al., 2011; Spielmann et al., 2025).

IRT1 is a broad‐spectrum transporter that takes up zinc, manganese, cobalt, and cadmium (Zn, Mn, Co, or Cd hereafter called non‐iron metal substrates) in addition to iron (Rogers et al., 2000; Vert et al., 2001, 2002). Non‐iron metals were shown to trigger IRT1 endocytosis and vacuolar degradation (Barberon et al., 2014; Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2022). IRT1 directly binds non‐iron metals using a histidine‐rich stretch located in IRT1's large cytosolic loop with the help of a nearby aspartic acid residue (Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2022; Cointry et al., 2025). Non‐iron metal binding to IRT1 allows the recruitment of Calcineurin B‐like (CBL)‐interacting serine/threonine‐protein kinase 23 (CIPK23) and subsequent phosphorylation of IRT1 (Dubeaux et al., 2018). This in turn disrupts the association of IRT1 with AHA2 and FRO2 (Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020) and allows lysine‐63 polyubiquitination of IRT1 by IDF1 and subsequent targeting to the vacuole (Dubeaux et al., 2018). Such elegant regulatory mechanism protects plants from overaccumulating readily available Zn2+, Mn2+, Co2+, and Cd2+ ions that do not need prior reduction by FRO2 to be transported. IRT1 is found in the outer plasma membrane domain facing the rhizosphere, which is necessary for proper radial transport of metals in the root (Barberon et al., 2014). IRT1 physically interacts with both the late‐endosome‐localized ESCRT‐associated FYVE1 (Barberon et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2014) and AP2 at the cell surface (Spielmann et al., 2025) to maintain its lateral polarity, pointing to a role of recycling and internalization in this process. IRT1 recycling from endosomes to the plasma membrane was also shown to require Sorting Nexin 1 (SNX1; Ivanov et al., 2014).

IRT1 was established as a model to study the post‐translational regulation of plasma membrane proteins and its functional relevance through the identification of IRT1 partners. To date, identifying partners of membrane proteins, including highly hydrophobic multispan transmembrane transporters and channels remains challenging. Affinity purification coupled to mass spectrometry (AP‐MS) fails to capture weak and transient interactors and requires membrane protein solubilization with mild detergents not to interfere with affinity purification, thus yielding many false positives. Yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) and split‐ubiquitin are tedious require the use of soluble fragments, are also prone to false positives, and suffer from the use of the yeast heterologous system. The recent development of proximity labeling, such as TurboID, to capture protein–protein interactions in planta overcomes many caveats from traditional approaches (Xu et al., 2023; Özmen et al., 2025). This is especially true for integral membrane proteins where proximity labeling allows to identify nearby and interacting proteins without the need of detergents to solubilize the membrane complex first. Biotinylation‐based TurboID proximity labeling uses a protein of interest (POI) fused to a promiscuous biotin ligase to catalyze biotinylation of amine groups on lysine residues from nearby proteins, usually within a radius of c. 10 nm. The biotinylated proximal proteomes (i.e. proxitomes) of the POI can be enriched using streptavidin‐based purification under stringent conditions followed by mass spectrometry‐based proteome identification. The use of TurboID approaches is rapidly rising in plants (Mair et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019, 2024; Arora et al., 2020; Mair & Bergmann, 2021; Lin et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023, 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Wallner et al., 2024; De Backer et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026), but its application to hydrophobic membrane proteins remains limited.

To shed light on new actors contributing to iron uptake and new regulatory proteins, we sought to identify the proxitome of an iron transporter. To this purpose, we established TurboID proximity labeling approaches for the integral multispanning IRT1 plant membrane protein. We uncovered the IRT1 proxitome under different metal regimes, and validated two candidate proximal proteins as new IRT1‐interacting proteins and players in plant metal homeostasis. The TurboID‐based proximity labeling strategy detailed in this study sets the stage to identify partners for other transporters and channels to better understand their role and regulation.

Materials and Methods

Plant material and constructs

All experiments were performed on Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0). The irt1 knockout mutant for the IRT1 gene (AT4G19690) in the Col‐0 background was generated by the CRISPR/Cas9 (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/CRISPR‐Associated Protein 9) technique and previously reported (Cointry et al., 2025; Neveu et al., 2025). Knockout T‐DNA insertion mutant lines were obtained from the Eurasian Arabidopsis Stock Centre (uNASC). The IRT1::IRT1‐mCit, PIN2::IRT1‐mCit, UBI10::IRT1‐mCit, 35S::Lti6b‐YFP expressing lines used in this study were previously reported (Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2022). T‐DNA knockout mutant alleles used in this work were the double nhx5nhx6 mutant (carrying nhx5‐2 (GABI_094H05) and nhx6‐2 (SALK_100042) alleles, respectively) and the complemented line nhx5nhx6/35S::NHX5‐YFP kindly donated by Bassil et al. (2011). The rglg1rglg2 double mutant, based on rglg1 (SALK_011892) and rglg2 (SALK_062384) mutants, was provided by Andreas Bachmair (Yin et al., 2007).

The TurboID‐FLAG fusion complementing irt1 chlorosis and used for TurboID analyses was inserted into IRT1 coding sequence between residues S73 and R74 in pDONR221 using primers listed in Supporting Information Table S1. The open reading frames of NHX5 (AT1G54370), RGLG2 (AT5G14420), and TurboID‐FLAG fusion were cloned into pDONR221 Gateway entry vector; the open reading frame of Lti6b (AT3G05890) was cloned into pDONR2R‐P3. Respective promoter regions and 5′UTRs (c. 2500 nucleotides upstream of the coding sequence) were selected and cloned into pDONRP4‐P1R entry vector using primers listed in Table S1. Other pDONRP4‐P1R vectors carrying IRT1, UBI10, and 35S promoters were previously described (Marquès‐Bueno et al., 2016). pDONRP2R‐P3 vectors carrying GFP, mCit, and mChe were previously described (Marquès‐Bueno et al., 2016). Final destination vectors were obtained by using MultiSite Gateway Three Fragment recombination system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), using pDONR vectors and gateway destination vectors (Karimi et al., 2007) to generate the following expression constructs: IRT1::IRT1‐TurboID, IRT1::Lti6b‐TurboID, UBI10::NHX5‐mCherry, NHX5::NHX5‐mCherry, RGLG2::GFP, UBI10::RGLG2‐mCherry, and UBI10::RGLG2CS‐mCherry. The list of constructs generated or used is listed in Table S2.

Generated constructs were transformed into wild‐type (WT) Arabidopsis by floral dipping or used in transient expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana. For all stable plant constructs, independent lines homozygous for the transgene were selected in T3. Confocal microscopy, phenotypic analysis, and protein extraction were performed on representative mono‐insertional homozygous T3 lines. Introgression of tagged constructs into genotypes of interest was done by crossing and selection of homozygous progeny.

To generate the final constructs for TriFC experiments, pDONR221 plasmids carrying the proteins of interest or ALFA NB (Neveu et al., 2025) were recombined with pDONR‐P2RP3 plasmids containing the N‐terminal (mCitN) or C‐terminal (mCitC) fragments of mCit (residues 1–155 and 155–238, respectively; Dolde et al., 2023) and with the pDONR P4P1R‐UBI10 promoter or pDONR P4P1R‐35S plasmids (Marquès‐Bueno et al., 2016), into the pK7m34GW or pH7m34GW pDEST vectors (Karimi et al., 2007).

Affinity purification of biotinylated proteins

Biotinylation and subsequent streptavidin‐based affinity purification protocol were done as described previously (Mair et al., 2019). irt1/IRT1::IRT1‐TurboID and the IRT1::TurboID‐Lti6b negative control transgenic lines were grown and treated as described above. After incubation with biotin, plants were washed three times with liquid ½MS medium lacking iron and biotin to stop the reaction, and roots were harvested. Roots were ground to fine powder in liquid nitrogen. One gram of roots was resuspended in 2 ml of RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40, 0.4% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM DTT, 1/100 protease inhibitor cocktail). Samples were then centrifuged twice at 3800 g at 4°C for 10 min, before being placed on rotor wheel at 4°C for 1 h and subjected to another centrifugation for 10 min at 10 000 g . PD‐10 desalting columns (Sephadex G‐25 resin, GE17‐0851‐01, Cytiva) were used to remove free biotin from samples and equilibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions (Gravity Protocol) using 150 mM NaCl RIPA lysis buffer. Two milliliters of crude extract on column were eluted by 2.5 ml of 150 mM NaCl RIPA. Few microliters were collected to measure protein concentration of the extracts before and after desalting by performing Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad protein assay, Microassay procedure). To capture biotinylated proteins, Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 (Invitrogen) were first equilibrated twice with 1 ml 150 mM NaCl RIPA lysis buffer for 1 min at RT. Seventy microliters of streptavidin beads were then added to 5 mg of proteins with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and incubated on a rotating wheel overnight at 4°C. Beads were then sequentially washed once with 1 ml of wash buffer 1 (2% SDS, 0.1% Triton), wash buffer 2 (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton), wash buffer 3 (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.5% NP‐40), and twice with wash buffer 4 (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP‐40).

To prepare samples for mass spectrometry analysis, beads were washed twice with 1 ml of 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, and transferred to a new tube and washed again with 1 ml Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 2 M urea. For the first tryptic digestion step, previous wash buffer is removed and 80 μl of Trypsin Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 1 M urea, 1 mM DTT, 0.4 μg trypsin) is added to the beads and incubated at 25°C with agitation. After 3 h, beads were transferred to a new tube and washed twice with 60 μl of 50 mM Tris–HCl, 1 M urea. Supernatants were collected to a new tube, DTT was added to a final concentration of 4 mM, and incubated at 25°C for 30 min with agitation. For alkylation of cysteine residues, 10 mM of iodoacetamide was added to the samples and incubated for 45 min at 25°C. For the final trypsin digestion step, 0.5 μg of trypsin was added to the samples and incubated overnight at 25°C. The following day, samples are acidified using 1% formic acid. Desalting of samples was carried out using OMIX C18 tips (10–100 μl; Agilent Technology, Santa Clara, CA, USA), previously activated and equilibrated twice using 200 μl Buffer B2 (0.1% formic acid, 50% acetonitrile) and four times using 200 μl Buffer A2 (0.1% formic acid). Samples were fixated with eight suction discharges and washed 10 times with 200 μl Buffer A2 before final elution with Buffer B2 in a new tube. Samples were completely dried using SpeedVac and then stored at −80°C. Four independent replicates were treated and analyzed.

TurboID nano‐LC‐MS/MS analyses

Biotinylated proteins were eluted from beads using biotin and digested with trypsin. Peptide samples were resuspended in 14 μl of 2% Acetonitrile, 0.05% TFA, vortexed and sonicated for 10 min before injection. Peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano‐LC‐MS/MS using nanoRS UHPLC system (Dionex, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) coupled to a Q‐Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Five microliters of each sample were loaded on a C18 precolumn (5 mm × 300 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 20 μl min−1 in 5% acetonitrile, 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid. After 5 min of desalting, the precolumn was switched on line with the analytical C18 column (15 cm × 75 μm; Reprosil C18 in‐house packed) equilibrated in 95% of solvent A (5% acetonitrile +0.2% formic acid in water) and 5% of solvent B (80% acetonitrile +0.2% formic acid in water). Peptides were eluted using a 5–50% gradient of B during 105 min at a 300 nl min−1 flow rate. The Q‐Exactive Plus was operated in data‐dependent acquisition mode with the xcalibur software. Survey scan MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap on the 350–1500 m/z range with the resolution set to a value of 70 000. The 10 most intense ions per survey scan were selected for HCD fragmentation, and the resulting fragments were analyzed in the Orbitrap with the resolution set to a value of 17 500. Dynamic exclusion was used within 30s to prevent repetitive selection of the same peptide.

Protein identification and quantification

Acquired MS and MS/MS data were searched with mascot (v.2.8.0.1, http://matrixscience.com) against a custom‐made database containing Turbo‐ID proteins and the TAIR database (release 20 221 110; 27 416 entries). The search included methionine oxidation, N‐ter acetylation, S/T/Y phosphorylation as variable modifications and carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification. Trypsin was chosen as the enzyme and 2 missed cleavages were allowed. The mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm for the precursor ion and to 20 mmu for fragment ions. Raw MS signal extraction of identified peptides was performed across different samples. Validation of identifications was performed through a false discovery rate set to 1% at the protein and peptide‐sequence match level, determined by target‐decoy search using the in‐house‐developed proline software v.2.1 (http://proline.profiproteomics.fr/) (Bouyssié et al., 2020). Raw MS signal extraction of identified peptides was performed with Proline across different samples. For all comparisons, statistical analysis was applied to the abundance values.

Proteins with a P‐value from t‐test < 0.05 and a ratio of average normalized area < 0.5 and > 2 were considered significant. Volcano plots were drawn to visualize significant protein abundance variations between two conditions: control and assay. They represent log10 (P‐value) according to the log2 ratio. All the mass spectrometry data were deposited with the MassIVE repository with the dataset identifier: MSV000100915.

Gene expression analyses

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription, digestion of genomic DNA, and cDNA synthesis were carried out using the RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCRs to monitor IRT1‐mCit, endogenous IRT1, or the EF1α loading control were carried out using GoTaq DNA Polymerase (Promega) with the primers listed in Table S1.

Localization/colocalization confocal microscopy analyses

Plants were transferred from plate to liquid ½MS medium with or without non‐iron metal provision for 3 h before observation. Plant samples were mounted in water and root tips were imaged on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope using the 488 nm, 514 nm, and 561 nm lasers to image GFP, mCit, and mChe, respectively. Laser intensity settings were kept constant in individual sets of experiments to allow for comparison of expression and localization of reporter proteins. Quantification of total fluorescence intensity in roots, at the plasma membrane, in intracellular compartments and colocalization analyses were performed using imagej/fiji (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) as described previously (Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2023b).

TriFC protein:protein interaction assay

Emissions of TriFC‐reconstituted mCit and the constitutively expressed MyrPalm‐mChe were collected in sequential mode. Mean fluorescence intensity was determined with imagej/fiji (Measure tool) for both mCit and mChe channels following automatic selection of the plasma membrane region. This selection was based on the MyrPalm‐mChe plasma membrane marker (pEAQ‐HT‐DEST1:MyrPalm‐mCherry (Sainsbury et al., 2009)). mCit/mChe fluorescence ratios were then calculated, and statistical analyses were performed using graphpad prism as described in the legends to figures.

Immunoprecipitation and western blot analyses

Immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments were carried out as previously described (Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020). Approximately 1 g of fresh roots or N. benthamiana leaves was collected from 14‐d‐old seedlings or 48‐h‐infiltrated N. benthamiana. Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in three volumes of cold solubilization buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% [w/v] n‐dodecyl‐β‐D‐maltoside (DDM), 1% Plant‐specific Protease Inhibitor Coktail (PIC)). Samples were centrifuged twice at 3800 g for 10 min at 4°C, and placed on rotor wheel for solubilization of membrane proteins for at least 1 h. Samples were then centrifuged 1 h at 20 000 g at 4°C for IP or 1 h at 100 000 g at 4°C for coimmunoprecipitation. mCit‐based IP was performed using μMACS GFP isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). RFP‐based IP was performed using RFP‐Trap_MA magnetic beads (Chromotek, Planegg‐Martinsried, Germany).

For protein detection, the following antibodies/probes were used: Monoclonal anti‐GFP horseradish peroxidase‐coupled (130‐091‐833, 1/5000; Miltenyi Biotec), anti‐ubiquitin P4D1 (05‐944, 1/2500; Millipore, Molsheim, France), anti‐IRT1 (AS11 1780, 1/5000; Agrisera, Vännäs, Sweden), anti‐FLAG M2 (F1804, 1/2500; Sigma‐Aldrich), anti‐RFP (AB34767, 1/5000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and avidin probe coupled to horseradish peroxidase (A3151, 1/5000; Sigma‐Aldrich).

Endosome quantification in N. benthamiana

Nicotiana benthamiana plants were grown for 4 wk in soil at 22°C with 16 h light and 55% humidity and then infiltrated with Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 strain.

Treatment with the control solution (infiltration solution without metals, standard conditions) or non‐iron metal solution (infiltration solution with −/+++ non‐iron metal conditions) was done 48 h after A. tumefaciens were infiltrated. Plants were analyzed by confocal microscopy after 3 h of treatment using SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), with the 63×‐oil‐immersion objective, and images were collected at 1024 × 1024 pixel resolution. Endosome analysis was done using ComDet plugin from the fiji software. Region of interests were drawn around each cell, and particles were detected with a maximum size of 4 pixels and an intensity threshold of 5.

Elemental analyses

Roots and shoots from 14‐d‐old plants grown in various non‐iron metal regimes were harvested separately. Roots were washed twice in ice‐cold desorption solution (1 mM MES‐KOH pH 7.5, 5 mM CaCl2) and twice with ddH2O for 10 min each. Aerial parts were washed twice with ddH2O for 10 min. Samples were then dried at 60°C for 3 d and digested using a closed‐vessel microwave digestion system (Multiwave 5000; Anton Paar) in a solution of concentrated HNO3 (85%) and ultrapure water (1/4, v/v). The digestion protocol consisted of a ramp to 200°C over 20 min, a hold at 200°C for 10 min and a cooling phase to 70°C. Digested samples were diluted to the appropriate volume with ultrapure water and analyzed by Inductively Coupled Plasma‐Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP‐OES; Agilent Technology 5800). Ytterbium (Yb) was used as an internal standard to correct for matrix effects and instrumental variability.

Statistical analyses

Statistical tests used, and numbers of biological replicates, sample size in each biological replicate, and significance are described in the legend to the figures. All statistical tests were performed and graphs constructed using graphpad prism 8.

Results

Implementing TurboID for the IRT1 metal transporter

To carry out proximity labeling approaches using IRT1, we first thought of generating a transgenic line expressing a functional fusion between IRT1 and the biotin ligase TurboID. TurboID has indeed emerged as the most efficient labeling enzyme in various plant systems (Branon et al., 2018; Mair et al., 2019). IRT1 is a highly hydrophobic multipass transmembrane protein with extracellular N‐ (after cleavage of the predicted signal peptide) and C‐termini, and the only functional fusion of IRT1 with a tag of significant size reported to date harbors the tag inserted in the first extracellular domain of IRT1 (Dubeaux et al., 2018). Since our goal was to use TurboID to identify intracellular partners, we screened for functional IRT1‐TurboID fusions in which the biotin ligase is located in the cytosolic regions of IRT1, while maintaining IRT1 metal transport function. To this purpose, we generated several IRT1‐TurboID fusions, where TurboID is inserted in different cytosolic loops, under the control of the IRT1 promoter in the irt1 mutant background (Fig. S1a,b). Several independent mono‐insertional homozygous lines for the different fusions were isolated. Among all the lines generated, the one carrying TurboID in the first cytosolic loop of IRT1 between transmembrane domain (TM) 1 and 2 (between residues S73 and R74) complemented the severe chlorosis of irt1 (Figs 1a,b, S1b), thus attesting that such fusion is still functional for iron transport. As negative control, we thought of using the generic plasma membrane protein Lti6b that has been previously shown to be present both at the plasma membrane and in endosomes where IRT1 is also found (Jaillais et al., 2007). We confirmed that Lti6b‐YFP fails to interact with IRT1, as observed by the lack of coimmunoprecipitation of endogenous IRT1 with Lti6b‐YFP (Fig. 1c). We therefore generated a TurboID negative control line expressing the Lti6b fused to TurboID under the control of the IRT1 promoter in the WT background.

To characterize these lines and ascertain that the IRT1‐TurboID cassette is expressed at levels close to endogenous IRT1, we first performed western blot analyses using total proteins extracted from roots. Since the IRT1‐TurboID and Lti6b‐TurboID cassettes carry a Flag tag, protein detection was performed using anti‐Flag antibodies. The IRT1‐TurboID protein nicely accumulated while Lti6b‐TurboID accumulated only to low levels (Fig. 1d, top panel). To compare IRT1‐TurboID levels to endogenous IRT1 levels from WT plants, protein extracts were probed with anti‐IRT1 antibodies. This proved that IRT1‐TurboID accumulated to similar levels to endogenous IRT1 protein (Fig. 1d, middle panel), pointing to the relevance of such a line to probe for IRT1 proxitome using TurboID.

The fact that the biotin ligase is inserted between two transmembrane domains in the IRT1‐TurboID line may potentially affect its ligase activity due to physical constraints. We therefore tested the labeling efficiency of TurboID in IRT1‐TurboID and Lti6b‐TurboID lines. Plants were exposed to 200 μM biotin or mock for 4 h, and total protein biotinylation was detected by immunoblot using avidin probes. Biotin addition greatly increased protein biotinylation in IRT1‐TurboID lines compared with both irt1 and WT plants, attesting to the functionality of the TurboID ligase (Fig. 1d, bottom panel). Interestingly, Lti6b‐TurboID showed strong biotinylation, although the corresponding fusion protein accumulated only at low levels, indicating that harboring TurboID at the protein extremity imposes less physical constraints and yields higher activity (Fig. 1d, bottom panel). Considering that biotin was previously reported to coordinate metals, we also ascertained that biotin treatment itself did not interfere with IRT1 expression and subcellular localization in response to metals (Fig. S2a).

We then assessed whether protein biotinylation impacted on the previously reported ubiquitination and endocytosis of IRT1 triggered by non‐iron metal excess (Dubeaux et al., 2018). This is all the more important considering that covalent binding of biotin to protein uses lysine residues that can also be targeted by ubiquitin, and already highlighted as an issue in TurboID approaches (Li et al., 2025). IRT1 ubiquitination profiles were therefore determined by immunoprecipitating IRT1‐TurboID from plants initially grown in the absence of iron (to activate IRT1 promoter) and non‐iron metals, and then subjected to simultaneous combinations of non‐iron metals and biotin, before detection with anti‐ubiquitin antibodies. When exposed to non‐iron metals, IRT1‐TurboID showed increased ubiquitination (Fig. S2b), as previously observed (Dubeaux et al., 2018). However, simultaneous biotin addition resulted in biotinylation of IRT1‐TurboID itself (Fig. S2b), preventing IRT1 ubiquitination likely due to the competition for target lysine residues. Alternatively, the presence of numerous lysine residues within ubiquitin that are likely biotinylated may also impair ubiquitin recognition by anti‐ubiquitin antibodies. To circumvent this caveat, we decided to subject plants to sequential treatments, with non‐iron metals for 2 h to trigger IRT1 ubiquitination followed by addition of biotin.

TurboID‐based analyses of IRT1 proxitome

IRT1 is known to be ubiquitinated and targeted for vacuolar degradation as early as 2 h after non‐iron metal excess (−/+++; Dubeaux et al., 2018). Since our goal was to better characterize IRT1's function at the cell surface and the early events driving IRT1 internalization to TGN/EE, we sought to lower non‐iron metal concentrations for resupply (hereafter called −/sub) to limit IRT1 sorting to late endosomal compartments and vacuolar degradation. Plants were therefore grown in the absence of iron and non‐iron metals, transferred to liquid growth conditions, and exposed to the low −/sub concentrations of non‐iron metals for 2 h before incubation with biotin (Fig. 2a). In this experimental setup, IRT1 is found both at the plasma membrane and in endosomes in both −/− and in −/sub conditions and is not subjected to degradation, as attested by the absence of drop in fluorescence that is otherwise observed at higher non‐iron metal concentration (−/+; Fig. S3a,b). Under such experimental conditions, Lti6b‐YFP also showed both plasma membrane and endosomal localization (Fig. S3c), therefore encompassing the subcellular compartments where IRT1 is found. Overall, these observations confirm the relevance of Lti6b as a negative control for TurboID analyses and indicate that TurboID analyses in −/− and −/sub conditions will capture both plasma membrane proteins and endosomal proteins.

To carry out TurboID analyses, IRT1‐TurboID and Lti6b‐TurboID control plants were grown as described above (Fig. 2a), and roots were collected before being subjected to protein extraction. Protein extracts were used for western blot analyses to confirm that IRT1‐TurboID and corresponding biotinylated proteins accumulated to a similar extent across the metal‐deficient and metal resupplied conditions, and that IRT1‐TurboID was still ubiquitinated (Fig. 2b,c). To identify IRT1 proximal proteins, we performed affinity purification of biotinylated proteins in four replicates. Biotinylated proteins bound to streptavidin beads were eluted using boiling SDS supplemented with biotin and analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS). Our analysis identified a total of 3042 proteins (Dataset S1). Among the identified proteins found in IRT1‐TurboID proxitome and absent from the TurboID‐Lti6b proxitome in −/− conditions, 416 were considered IRT1‐specific proximal proteins based on the threshold of P‐value ≤ 0.05 and fold change (IRT1/Lti6b) ≥ 2 (Fig. 2d; Dataset S2). Non‐iron metal addition (−/sub) allowed the isolation of 423 IRT1‐specific proximal proteins not present in the Lti6b‐TurboID dataset using the same criteria (Fig. 2e; Dataset S3). Among IRT1‐specific proximal proteins identified in both −/− and −/sub conditions was found the FYVE1 endosomal protein that was previously reported to interact with IRT1 and control intracellular sorting and polarity of IRT1 (Barberon et al., 2014), thus validating our TurboID approach. Comparison between IRT1 proximal proteins in non‐iron metal‐deficient (−/−) and non‐iron metal resupplied (−/sub) conditions identified 494 proteins enriched at least in one of the conditions compared with Lti6b (Fig. 2f; Dataset S4), including six specifically enriched in non‐iron metal‐depleted conditions (Fig. 2f; Dataset S5) and five enriched specifically in non‐iron metal resupplied conditions (Fig. 2f; Dataset S6). Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were carried out using all 494 IRT1‐specific proximal proteins to highlight biological functions associated. Three main families of GO terms were enriched in IRT1‐specific proximal proteins: lipid metabolism, protein localization and ion homeostasis (Fig. 2g). Of particular interest to our understanding of the dynamics of IRT1 and its post‐translational regulation by ubiquitination are several proteins related to the ubiquitination machinery, including several putative E3 ligases (among which RGLG2), ubiquitin‐binding proteins, or ubiquitin‐specific proteases. RGLG2 is found as a statistically significant IRT1 proximal protein in both non‐iron metal‐deficient (−/−) and non‐iron metal resupplied (−/sub) datasets, highlighting that non‐iron metals do not impact on the proximity between IRT1 and RGLG2, at least to the c. 10 nm resolution of TurboID. Considering the major role of ubiquitination in the control of IRT1 (Barberon et al., 2011, 2014; Shin et al., 2013; Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2022), and the previous connection established between RGLG2 and iron homeostasis (Pan et al., 2015), we decided to study RGLG2 in more detail. Several candidates from the IRT1‐specific proximal protein dataset are associated with ion homeostasis, including metal transporters from the ZIP and MTP families, as well as enzymes involved in coumarin biosynthesis. Among transport proteins is also found the NHX5 Na+(K+)/H+ antiporter that stands out as the most enriched protein among the IRT1‐specific proximal proteins upon non‐iron metal resupply. NHX5, and its NHX6 counterpart, are localized to the TGN/EE and late endosomes and have been shown to control endosomal ion homeostasis and TGN/EE trafficking (Dragwidge et al., 2019), thus also representing interesting candidates to go after.

Validation of NHX5 and RGLG2 as IRT1 proximal proteins and interactors

To showcase the ability of TurboID approaches of plasma membrane proteins to identify new partners and regulators, we decided to validate deeper the two proximal protein candidates RGLG2 and NXH5. Validation included the following steps: evaluation of protein–protein interaction between IRT1 and RGLG2 or NHX5, overlapping expression territories in root epidermal cells, and colocalization at the plasma membrane or in endosomal compartments.

To evaluate the ability of IRT1 to interact with its RGLG2 and NHX5 proximal proteins, we took advantage of the recently reported ALFA tag/ALFA nanobody (NB) technology and ratiometric tripartite functional fluorescence complementation assay (TriFC; Neveu et al., 2025). TriFC uses coexpression of the functional IRT1‐ALFA fusion, the ALFA NB‐mCitN fusion (where mCitN is the N‐terminal half of mCit), and mCitC‐tagged NHX5 or RGLG2 (where mCitC is the C‐terminal half of mCit) in N. benthamiana, allowing reconstitution of functional mCit upon interaction between IRT1 and NHX5 or RGLG2 (Fig. S4a). The IRT1:AHA2 interaction we previously uncovered (Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020; Neveu et al., 2025) was used as a positive control, and the omission of IRT1‐ALFA as a negative control. TriFC‐based reconstitution of mCit was observed at the plasma membrane for IRT1 and NHX5 and in intracellular vesicles (Fig. 3a,b), which are likely TGN/EE where both proteins have previously been found (Barberon et al., 2011; Dubeaux et al., 2018; Dragwidge et al., 2019). Ratiometric TriFC also allowed quantification of the reconstituted mCit fluorescence relative to constitutively expressed mCherry and further confirmed the IRT1:NHX5 interaction (Fig. 3b). This suggests that both proteins are not only proximal but also interact in plant cells. The closely related NHX6 Na+(K+)/H+ antiporter also interacted with IRT1 in TriFC approaches (Fig. S4b,c). Coexpression of TriFC components for IRT1 and RGLG2 likewise revealed mCit reconstitution at the plasma membrane, while no mCit signal could be observed for the negative control (Fig. 3a,b).

To back up TriFC protein:protein interaction evidence using an orthogonal assay, we performed coimmunoprecipitation using transient expression of IRT1‐mCit and NHX5‐mCherry (mChe) in N. benthamiana. NHX5‐mChe coimmunoprecipitated with IRT1‐mCit, while no signal could be observed for the plasma membrane MyrPalm‐mChe negative control (Fig. 3c). This confirms the ability of IRT1 and NHX5 to interact in plant cells. When challenged with non‐iron metals, IRT1‐mCit accumulated to lower levels but coimmunoprecipitated similar NHX5‐mChe levels (Fig. 3c), consistent with the enrichment observed upon non‐iron metal treatment in our TurboID dataset. Similar experiments were carried out with IRT1‐mCit and RGLG2‐mChe to confirm their physical interaction in live cells using transient expression in N. benthamiana. No interaction between RGLG2 and IRT1 could be observed in this assay (Fig. 3d). However, considering the notoriously weak and transient interaction between E3 ubiquitin ligases and their targets, we decided to test the ability of mutant versions of IRT1 and RGLG2 aiming at stabilizing such interactions. To this purpose, we used the non‐ubiquitinatable IRT12KR (Barberon et al., 2011; Dubeaux et al., 2018) and the RGLG22CS zinc finger mutant impaired in Zn2+ coordination to abolish ubiquitination by RGLG2 (Garcia‐Barcena et al., 2020). IRT12KR‐mCit failed to interact with RGLG2‐mChe (Fig. 3d). However, RGLG22CS‐mChe interacted with both IRT1‐mCit and IRT12KR‐mCit, supporting further the ability of IRT1 and RGLG2 to partner in live cells and stressing the edge of TurboID approaches to pick up weak or transient interactions.

Since both TriFC and coimmunoprecipitation protein:protein interaction evidences are based on overexpression, we next ascertained that both NHX5 and RGLG2 are found at least in root epidermal cells to support their capacity to physically interact with IRT1. We generated a complemented nhx5nhx6/NHX5::NHX5‐mCherry line that showed a WT phenotype, confirming the functionality of the NHX5‐mChe fusion (Fig. 3e). NHX5‐mChe driven by the NHX5 endogenous promoter was observed in the root epidermis (Fig. 3f), confirming that NHX5 and IRT1 coexist in similar cell types. To evaluate RGLG2 expression territories, we favored a transcriptional reporter line expressing mCit under the control of the RGLG2 promoter rather than a translational fusion since E3 ligases are notoriously unstable and difficult to detect due to self‐ubiquitination (Garcia‐Barcena et al., 2020). RGLG2 promoter activity was observed across all root cell types (Fig. 3g), suggesting that RGLG2 shares common expression territories with IRT1 and strengthening further the protein interaction data obtained from TurboID, TriFC, and coimmunoprecipitation.

NHX5 is localized to the TGN/EE (Dragwidge et al., 2019), where IRT1 transits through during endocytosis (Barberon et al., 2011; Dubeaux et al., 2018). To assess the possible subcellular overlap between IRT1 and NHX5, we generated transgenic plants expressing PIN2::IRT1‐mCit and UBI10::NHX5‐mChe. PIN2 promoter was chosen to drive IRT1‐mCit expression to (1) obtain constitutive IRT1‐mCit expression and thus focus later on post‐transcriptional events, and (2) image IRT1 endocytosis in epidermal cells at the root tip that are well suited to track the precise localization of plasma membrane proteins. We also favored the constitutive UBI10 promoter to drive NHX5 here to avoid possible interference of iron (Dinneny et al., 2008) and possibly non‐iron metal provision with NHX5 protein accumulation. Intracellular vesicles positive for both IRT1 and NHX5 were observed (Fig. 3h), pointing to the partial overlap of both proteins in TGN/EE. RGLG2 was previously reported to be myristoylated and associated with the plasma membrane (Yin et al., 2007). We evaluated the subcellular localization of RGLG2 using transient expression of a RGLG2‐mChe fusion in N. benthamiana and confirmed that, consistent with the literature, RGLG2 is mostly found at the plasma membrane where it colocalizes with IRT1‐mCit (Fig. 3i).

Altogether, the evidence gathered here confirmed the ability of IRT1 to interact with RGLG2 and NHX5 and their presence in overlapping territories and subcellular localizations, making of NHX5 and RGLG2 new IRT1 partner proteins.

NHX5 and NHX6 contribute to plant non‐iron metal responses

To better characterize the relationship between NHX5, IRT1, and responses to non‐iron metals, we first assessed the colocalization extent of PIN2::IRT1‐mCit and UBI10::NHX5‐mChe in stable transgenic lines exposed to various non‐iron metal provision. IRT1‐mCit showed increased internalization from the plasma membrane to TGN/EE with rising concentrations of non‐iron metals (−/− to −/+), as expected (Dubeaux et al., 2018). NHX5‐mChe displayed a punctate pattern consistent with its presence in the TGN/EE (Dragwidge et al., 2019). In agreement with the heightened localization of IRT1‐mCit in TGN/EE upon non‐iron metal provision (Dubeaux et al., 2018), its colocalization with the TGN/EE‐localized NHX5‐mChe increased with non‐iron metal addition, with a maximum observed in −/+ conditions (Fig. 4a,b).

Plants grown under iron starvation, thus expressing high levels of IRT1, and exposed to non‐iron metal excess have previously been shown to harbor reduced root growth (Dubeaux et al., 2018). To evaluate the genetic contribution of NHX5 and its NHX6 redundant counterpart in plant responses to non‐iron metals, we characterized root growth responses of the nhx5nhx6 double loss‐of‐function mutant (Bassil et al., 2011). To this purpose, WT and nhx5nhx6 plants were grown vertically for 7 d on plates containing iron and non‐iron metals (+/+) before being transferred to plates lacking iron and non‐iron metals (−/−), lacking iron and containing low concentrations of non‐iron metals (−/sub, as used for the TurboID), lacking iron and containing half MS physiological levels of non‐iron metals (−/+), or lacking iron and containing non‐iron metals excess (−/+++) as reported previously in Dubeaux et al. (2018; Fig. 4c). Root growth after transfer was measured and scored relative to control conditions (+/+), highlighting how nhx5nhx6 responds to non‐iron metals compared with WT plants. Under conditions where IRT1 is expressed and the corresponding protein is mostly found at the plasma membrane (−/− and −/sub), nhx5nhx6 appeared to be slightly less impacted than WT (Fig. 4c,d). Increasing non‐iron metal concentrations, conditions where IRT1 is less at the plasma membrane and is targeted to endosomal compartments (−/+) and the vacuole (−/+++), yielded a slight non‐iron metal hypersensitivity (Fig. 4c,d). Therefore, NHX5 and NHX6 functions seem to be conditional and differ between non‐iron metal provision and associated IRT1 localization.

Considering that the observed effects with nhx5nhx6 are mild and that the NHX gene family in Arabidopsis contains eight members, we conducted a gain‐of‐function approach using the previously reported nhx5nhx6/35S::NHX5‐YFP line (Bassil et al., 2011) to better characterize the involvement of NHX‐type antiporters in plant responses to non‐iron metals. NHX5 overexpression yielded increased resistance to non‐iron metals in conditions where IRT1 is gradually removed from the cell surface (−/+) and degraded in the vacuole (−/+++; Fig. 4e,f), further highlighting the negative genetic relationship between NHX‐type antiporters and responses to non‐iron metals. The mild impact on IRT1 intracellular distribution and plant responses to non‐iron metals observed upon alteration in NHX expression did not translate into significant change in zinc or manganese accumulation (Fig. S5a–h).

Loss of NHX5 and NHX6 impacts on IRT1 endocytosis

NHX5 and NHX6 were previously shown to be required for endosomal trafficking and recycling of endocytosed BRI1 to the cell surface, while not necessary for the recycling of PIN1 and PIN2 (Dragwidge et al., 2018, 2019). To further examine the role of NHX‐type antiporters in responses to non‐iron metals, we monitored the well‐established endocytosis and degradation of IRT1 triggered by non‐iron metals (Dubeaux et al., 2018). We therefore examined IRT1‐mCit localization driven by the PIN2 promoter in WT and nhx5nhx6 mutant in response to various non‐iron metal provisions. Our previous work confirmed that IRT1 undergoes metal‐dependent endocytosis even when driven by the PIN2 promoter (Dubeaux et al., 2018; Spielmann et al., 2022). Surprisingly, we observed that the metal‐induced degradation of IRT1 was enhanced in nhx5nhx6, highlighted by the lower plasma membrane:intracellular fluorescence ratio (Fig. 4g,h). Although this observation may be counterintuitive considering the delayed trafficking of FM4‐64 to the vacuole observed in nhx5nhx6 (Bassil et al., 2011), it may reflect a role of endosomal pH and NHX activity on protein recycling to the cell surface (Dragwidge et al., 2018).

RGLG2 and RGLG1 participate to non‐iron metal responses

Considering the reported redundancy between RGLG2 and RGLG1, we used the previously reported rglg1rglg2 double mutant (Yin et al., 2007) to evaluate its response to non‐iron metal provision. Root elongation of WT and rglg1rglg2 after transfer from +/+ to iron‐deficient conditions, combined with various non‐iron metal regimes, was scored. rglg1rglg2 appeared less impacted by non‐iron metal provision than WT plants (Fig. 5a,b). To strengthen the genetic analysis of rglg1rglg2, we generated the rglg1rglg2/UBI10::RGLG2‐mChe transgenic line that nicely complements the growth defect observed with rglg1rglg2 (Fig. S6). In contrast to rglg1rglg2, the complemented rglg1rglg2/UBI10::RGLG2‐mChe line failed to show reduced sensitivity to non‐iron metals (Fig. 5a,b). Taken together, these observations indicate that genetically, RGLG1 and RGLG2 act as positive regulators of plant non‐iron metal responses. Loss of RGLG1 and RGLG2 expression was associated with increased zinc or manganese content across some of the non‐iron metal conditions tested, further highlighting the perturbation in non‐iron metal homeostasis in this mutant background (Fig. S7a–d).

RGLG2 regulates IRT1 ubiquitination, endocytosis and degradation

Since RGLG2 was found in the proximal proteome of IRT1 and to interact with IRT1, we first sought to evaluate the impact of RGLG2 transient overexpression in N. benthamiana on IRT1 endocytosis in response to non‐iron metals. As controls, we used the non‐ubiquitinatable IRT12KR and the ubiquitination‐defective RGLG22CS. In the absence of non‐iron metal treatment (i.e. standard conditions), coexpression of IRT12KR and RGLG2, IRT1 and RGLG22CS, and IRT12KR with RGLG22CS all yielded a plasma membrane localization of IRT1 and no or little IRT1 endocytosis using endosome counts as proxy (Fig. S8a,b). In sharp contrast, coexpression of IRT1 and RGLG2 greatly reduced plasma membrane levels of IRT1 and increased endosome numbers (Fig. S8a,b), indicating that RGLG2 overexpression promotes IRT1 endocytosis. Treatment of N. benthamiana with non‐iron metals was previously shown to promote IRT1 endocytosis (Cointry et al., 2025). Consistently, IRT1 endocytosis was enhanced upon non‐iron metal exposure when coexpressed with the ubiquitination‐defective RGLG22CS (Fig. S8a,b). This illustrates the endogenous non‐iron metal response mechanism present in N. benthamiana leaves. Coexpression of IRT1 and RGLG2 did not show greater endocytosis of IRT1 upon non‐iron metal treatment compared with standard conditions, likely because RGLG2 overexpression already depleted IRT1 from the plasma membrane. Consistent with previous reports in Arabidopsis (Dubeaux et al., 2018), the ubiquitination‐defective IRT12KR variant failed to respond to metals. Altogether, this clearly highlights that overexpression of RGLG2 in N. benthamiana impinges on IRT1 endocytosis and levels.

To confirm these observations in Arabidopsis, IRT1 protein levels were monitored in WT and rglg1rglg2. To this purpose, we generated the rglg1rglg2/UBI10::IRT1‐mCit line constitutively expressing IRT1‐Cit (Spielmann et al., 2022) in the rglg1rglg2 mutant background. The UBI10 promoter was favored here to allow detection of IRT1‐mCit by western blot analyses, which is not possible when using the PIN2 promoter because of the limited expression at the root tip. In all non‐iron metal conditions tested, rglg1rglg2 accumulated higher levels of IRT1‐mCit protein compared with WT plants in independent transgenic lines (Figs 5c, S8c). This confirms the existence of a post‐transcriptional regulation of IRT1 by RGLG2 in Arabidopsis plants.

We validated further these observations by imaging PIN2::IRT1‐mCit in WT and rglg1rglg2. Metal provision lowered IRT1‐mCit plasma membrane levels in the WT background, as visualized by the decreased ratio between plasma membrane and intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 5d,e). By contrast, IRT1‐mCit remained significantly more at the cell surface upon non‐iron metal exposure in rglg1rglg2, indicating that the RGLG1 and RGLG2 E3 ubiquitin ligases directly regulate the metal‐dependent endocytosis of IRT1. We next investigated the impact of loss of RGLG1 and RGLG2 on IRT1 ubiquitination. IRT1‐mCit was immunoprecipitated from WT or rglg1rglg2 and immunoprecipitates probed with anti‐ubiquitin antibodies. Care was taken at that stage to compensate for differences in IRT1‐mCit protein levels in the different metal conditions and in rglg1rglg2. We confirmed the previously reported increase in IRT1 ubiquitination visualized as a high molecular weight smear in response to rising non‐iron metal concentrations (Fig. 5f; Dubeaux et al., 2018). rglg1rglg2, however, showed reduced ubiquitination of IRT1‐mCit in all non‐iron metal conditions tested (Fig. 5f).

Altogether, these observations point to RGLG1 and RGLG2 as new E3 ligases targeting IRT1 for ubiquitin‐mediated endocytosis and degradation in response to non‐iron metal excess.

Discussion

The main root iron transport machinery components and their associated regulatory networks were initially identified in Arabidopsis and nongrasses plants by forward genetic screens looking for mutants with altered macroscopic or molecular phenotypes (Yi & Guerinot, 1996; Ling et al., 2002; Colangelo & Guerinot, 2004), through reverse genetic approaches screening for phenotypes of interest (Shin et al., 2013), or using homology with nonplant organisms (Eide et al., 1996; Curie et al., 2000; Thomine et al., 2000; Ivanov et al., 2014). The search for interacting proteins based on Y2H screening, directed Y2H tests using candidate partners, and AP‐MS allowed a rapid expansion of our knowledge about the transcriptional networks and post‐translational regulatory mechanisms controlling iron transporters, and especially for IRT1 and NRAMP1 (Barberon et al., 2011, 2014; Dubeaux et al., 2018; Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2022). Here, we unveil new regulators of plant metal uptake by pioneering proximity labeling strategy for the IRT1 metal transporter.

Proximity labeling and TurboID approaches have been developed to identify proteins proximal to a target of interest within a certain radius in living cells, providing new insights into protein–protein interactions and subcellular localization (Xu et al., 2023). Most importantly, the stable labeling of proximal proteins holds the premise of picking up transient or weak interacting partners compared with traditional AP‐MS strategies, allowing the exploration of a new repertoire of regulators that was previously hardly accessible (Xu et al., 2023). Not surprisingly, the IRT1 proximal protein showed only minor overlap with the previously reported characterization of the IRT1 complex composition by AP‐MS (Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020; Fig. S9), keeping in mind that plant growth conditions used slightly differ between the two datasets. Regardless, that clearly illustrates the need for several orthogonal assays to draw the most comprehensive picture of the interaction networks for a protein of interest. It is noteworthy that the proteins specifically found in the IRT1 proxitome and absent from the AP‐MS‐based IRT1 complex contain many signaling proteins, such as kinases or E3 ubiquitin ligase (Datasets S7–S9), which are known to interact only weakly or transiently with their targets. Such observations also support the edge of proximity labeling strategies compared with AP‐MS.

Among IRT1 proximal proteins, the NHX5 Na+(K+)/H+ antiporter and the RGLG2 E3 ubiquitin ligase were selected for further analyses because they either represent proteins unrelated to metal nutrition (NHX5) or proteins that are difficult to identify using other protein:protein interaction strategies (RGLG2). For both, their ability to interact with IRT1 was confirmed using TriFC, coimmunoprecipitation, and their presence in overlapping expression territories and subcellular compartments with IRT1 was validated. Reverse genetic approaches using knockout mutants revealed their respective involvement in plant metal nutrition and/or responses to non‐iron metals. The fact that macroscopic phenotypes observed for nhx5nhx6 and rglg1rglg2 are rather subtle suggests that additional members of the corresponding families are likely playing a role in IRT1‐mediated metal nutrition as well. This is supported by the stronger gain‐of‐function phenotype obtained with NHX5 overexpression, for example. These observations also argue for the ability of TurboID to identify proteins that play minor roles in biological processes and that can hardly be identified through classical genetic approaches due to the absence of strong phenotypes.

The contribution of NHX5 and NHX6 to plant responses to non‐iron metals appears to be complex and depends on non‐iron metal provision, with a positive role observed for low concentrations and a negative role at elevated concentrations. The use of NHX5 overexpressor confirms the implication of NHX‐type antiporters in non‐iron metal nutrition and points to their negative role. NHX5 and NHX6 were previously shown to impact endosomal trafficking in a cargo‐specific manner (Dragwidge et al., 2018, 2019). Loss of NHX5 and NHX6 revealed faster endocytosis and degradation of IRT1 upon non‐iron treatment. This is reminiscent of the reduced PIN1 and PIN2 protein levels observed in nhx5nhx6 (Dragwidge et al., 2018). The fact that the cytosolic tail of NHX6 interacts with the retromer complex protein SNX1 (Ashnest et al., 2015), associated with the lower PIN abundance in nhx5nhx6, led to the assumption that NHX5 and NHX6 may assist retromer‐mediated retrieval of PINs. This would be consistent with the increased endocytosis of IRT1 observed in response to non‐iron metals. However, such a scenario is not consistent with the observed phenotypes derived from nhx5nhx6 or NHX5 overexpressor plants. Considering that NHX5 and NHX6 likely impact the trafficking of many plasma membrane proteins, determining the functional outcome of NHX5 and NHX6 loss is difficult. In addition, NHX5 and NHX6 mediate endosomal ion and pH homeostasis, with endosomal compartments being more acidic in nhx5nhx6 (Reguera et al., 2015). At the plasma membrane, IRT1‐mediated metal uptake requires H+ export by the IRT1 complex member AHA2 (Santi & Schmidt, 2009; Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020). One can speculate that IRT1 may also drive metal import into the cytosol from TGN/EE and that likely impacts plant metal nutrition. Future work evaluating pH in TGN/EE in response to non‐iron metals and the contribution of TGN/EE vATPase and NHX5/NHX6 will be necessary to better understand the phenotype of nhx5nhx6.

WT plants harbor bifurcated root hairs in response to iron starvation. rglg1rglg2 was found to show a constitutively high number of branched root hairs (Li & Schmidt, 2010). This points to a role of RGLG1 and RGLG2 in developmental responses to iron, although the underlying molecular mechanisms are unknown. The identification of RGLG2 as an IRT1 proximal protein and partner regulating IRT1 protein levels and endocytosis now raises new questions as to the biological function of RGLG2 and its close homolog RGLG1. The reduced phenotypic sensitivity of rglg1rglg2 to non‐iron metal provision contrasts with both the reduced ubiquitin‐mediated endocytosis of IRT1 and the tendency to accumulate zinc or manganese. RGLGs are involved in many pathways (auxin, immunity, drought, flowering, etc.; Yin et al., 2007; Cheng et al., 2012; Leitner et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2015; Bai et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and act both in developmental (branched root hairs; Pan et al., 2015) and cellular (IRT1 endocytosis, this study) responses to metals. This greatly complicates the interpretation of macroscopic phenotypes that result from many different input responses, likely explaining the discrepancies observed. Undoubtedly, RGLGs regulate IRT1 ubiquitination, cell surface levels, and overall protein abundance. To date, IDF1 was the only E3 ubiquitin ligase known to regulate IRT1 (Shin et al., 2013). IDF1 drives the ubiquitin‐mediated endocytosis and vacuolar degradation of IRT1 and thus contributes to iron and non‐iron metal homeostasis (Dubeaux et al., 2018). RGLG1 and RGLG2 are unlikely to act in a separate pathway from the non‐iron metal‐dependent ubiquitin‐mediated degradation of IRT1 since rglg1rglg2 is defective in the non‐iron metal‐induced ubiquitin‐mediated endocytosis of IRT1. Rather, RGLGs may act redundantly with the unrelated IDF1 E3 ligase to fine tune IRT1 levels. The rationale for having different E3s controlling IRT1 in response to the same cue is unclear, but may reveal time‐ or developmental stage‐specific ubiquitination of IRT1 in response to non‐iron metals. Alternatively, RGLGs and IDF1 may act sequentially during IRT1 ubiquitination. IRT1 was demonstrated to undergo K63 polyubiquitination in response to non‐iron metals (Dubeaux et al., 2018). The formation of K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains is under the control of the two UBC13‐type E2 ubiquitin‐conjugating enzymes, UBC35 and UBC36 in Arabidopsis (Romero‐Barrios et al., 2020). Structural analyses of the human K63 polyubiquitination machinery containing UBC13, the UEV1 E2 variant, and ubiquitin revealed interesting insight into K63‐linked chain formation. The active site cysteine of UBC13 is covalently bound to a first donor Ub, while UEV1A binds noncovalently to a second acceptor Ub, presenting residue K63 for chain assembly (Hodge et al., 2016). This suggests that a priming monoubiquitination on a substrate protein is necessary for UBC13 to catalyze K63‐linked chain extension (Soss et al., 2011; Mattiroli et al., 2012). RGLGs and IDF1 may therefore act at different steps along IRT1 ubiquitination. Since RGLG2 has previously been proposed to monoubiquitinate proteins, as described for the BIK1 cytosolic receptor (Bai et al., 2023), we favor the model where RGLG2 possibly catalyzes the priming IRT1 ubiquitination while IDF1 polymerizes K63 polyubiquitin chains to drive endosomal sorting and vacuolar degradation. The fact that IDF1 was not identified among IRT1 proximal proteins under conditions where internalization is promoted but not vacuolar targeting is consistent with this view. Regardless, this hypothesis will necessitate deep in vitro biochemical investigation of RGLG2, IDF1 and their cognate E2s to decipher their exact role.

In summary, our study identified numerous novel interactors of the IRT1 transporter, underscoring the central role of IRT1 in upholding metal homeostasis to sustain optimal plant growth and development. Unraveling the function of such IRT1 partners will enhance our understanding of integrated plant responses to metals. Our work on IRT1 proximity labeling also paves the way to applying TurboID to other transporters and channels, for which biochemical properties are still considered a hurdle. This will be necessary to further our global comprehension of transporter regulation and plant nutrition.
