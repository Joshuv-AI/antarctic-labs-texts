The ℳ \mathcal{M} -Operator and Uniqueness of Nonlinear Kinetic Equations
Ricardo Alonso, Maria Pia Gualdani, Weiran Sun

Abstract
. We introduce an ℳ \mathcal{M} -operator approach to establish the uniqueness of continuous or bounded solutions for a broad class of Landau-type nonlinear kinetic equations. The specific ℳ \mathcal{M} -operator, originally developed in [ 3 ] , acts as a negative fractional derivative in both spatial and velocity variables and interacts in a controllable manner with the kinetic transport operator. The novelty of this method is that it bypasses the need for bounds on the derivatives of the solution - an assumption typically required in uniqueness arguments for non-cutoff equations. As a result, the method enables working with solutions with low regularity.



1.
Introduction

The uniqueness of solutions with low regularity or, equivalently, with rough initial data is a central and challenging question in the analysis of nonlinear kinetic equations. There are several unsolved problems in the context of uniqueness; one of the most relevant is the uniqueness of renormalized solution for the Boltzmann equation
[
6
]
and uniqueness of entropic solutions (also called
H
solutions)
[
24
]
for homogeneous Boltzmann and Landau equations. Homogeneous equations are, in general, simpler to treat and give important insights about the inhomogeneous counterpart. In this setting, the most general results of uniqueness for homogeneous non-cutoff Boltzmann and Landau problems can be found in
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
, respectively, with an analysis based on the Wasserstein distance. In addition, the recent work
[
14
]
settles the question of uniqueness of solution to the homogeneous Landau-Coulomb equation for initial data in polynomially weighted
negative
Sobolev spaces. These techniques, however, are not easily adaptable to the inhomogeneous case. In the context of the inhomogeneous cutoff Boltzmann equation close to equilibrium, one of the most general uniqueness results can be found in
[
13
]
, in particular, one related to the uniqueness of bounded solutions. A counterpart in the context of non-cutoff Boltzmann or Landau equations is an open problem in the area. This is a key motivating factor that led to the present contribution.

In this paper, we will focus on inhomogeneous Landau-type equations and establish the uniqueness within the classes of continuous or bounded solutions. More specifically, we study two types of equations. The first is a toy model

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho(t,x)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}f\right),\qquad\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\,{\rm d}v,

(1.1)

and the second one is a viscous Landau-Coulomb equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A[f]\nabla_{v}f-f\nabla_{v}a[f]\right)+\nu\Delta_{v}f,

(1.2)

with
\nu>0
and
A[f],\;a[f]
defined as

\displaystyle A[f](x,v,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbb{P}(v-z)}{|v-z|}f(x,z,t)\;dz,\qquad\mathbb{P}(z):=\mathbb{I}d-\frac{z\otimes z}{|z|^{2}},

\displaystyle a[f](x,v,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|v-z|}f(x,z,t)\;dz.

The main results in this paper can be informally summarized as follows:

Main Theorem
.

(a) Let
\beta\leq 2
. Equation (
1.1
) has a unique solution that is continuous in
(t,x,v)
and has a fast enough polynomial decay tail in
v
.

(b) Let
\nu>0
be arbitrary. Equation (
1.2
) has a unique solution that is continuous in
(t,x,v)
with a fast enough polynomial decay tail in
v
.

(c) Let
\nu>0
be arbitrary and
k_{0}
large enough. Suppose
\left\lVert\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}f_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}
is small enough with its bound depending on
\nu
. Then (
1.2
) has a unique solution that is bounded in
(t,x,v)
with a sufficiently fast polynomial decay tail in
v
.

The precise statements can be found in Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.3
.

A common approach to proving the uniqueness of solutions to nonlinear diffusion equations is to identify a suitable function space in which some derivatives in
x
or
v
of the solution are bounded. Using (
1.1
) as an example, the difference between two solutions with same initial data,
w:=f-g
, satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+v\cdot\nabla_{x}w

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{f}(t,x)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}w\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{w}(t,x)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}g\right),

\displaystyle w|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=0,

where
\rho_{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\,{\rm d}v
and
\rho_{w}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w(t,x,v)\,{\rm d}v
. In a basic energy estimate, the last term would need a uniform bound in the variable
x
for
\nabla_{v}g
. Several uniqueness results for inhomogeneous kinetic equations present in the literature follow this approach. The most recent ones on the inhomogeneous Landau or non-cutoff Boltzmann equations are included in
[
2
,
7
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In
[
2
]
, it is shown that the non-cutoff Boltzmann equation with
2s
-singularity, has unique solution in
L^{\infty}_{t,x}H^{2s}_{v}
. This corresponds to
L^{\infty}_{t,x}H^{2}_{v}
for the Landau equation. In
[
7
]
, uniqueness of solution to the non-cutoff Boltzmann and Landau equations is shown in the class of functions near the global Maxwellian in the space
X=L^{1}_{k}L^{\infty}_{T}L^{2}_{v}
composed of the Wiener algebra. The space
X
is stronger than
C^{1/2}_{x}L^{\infty}_{T}L^{2}_{v}
, where
C^{1/2}_{x}
denotes the Hölder space of order
1/2
in
x
. In recent work
[
15
,
16
]
kinetic Schauder estimates have been employed to prove the uniqueness of solutions to the non-cutoff Boltzmann and Landau equations if the initial data have an arbitrary amount of Hölder regularity in both
x
and
v
. These results have recently been improved by
[
17
]
to initial data with Hölder regularity in
x
and logarithmic modulus of continuity in
v
.

In this paper, we take a different point of view and make use of negative Sobolev spaces. To illustrate the idea, we consider again the toy model (
1.1
) with
\beta=0
without transport term:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f=\Delta_{v}\left(\rho(t,x)f\right).

(1.3)

We now use
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f
as a test function. This is effectively equivalent to perform
H^{-1}
estimates on

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f=\rho(t,x)f,

for which derivative on
f
does not need to be bounded. Unfortunately, direct application of
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}
to the kinetic transport term no longer works, as its commuter with
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
induces differentiation in
x
.

To address this issue, we propose to use a pseudo-differential operator that plays the role of
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}
and commutes with
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
in a controllable way. This operator will feature a time-averaged
negative
Sobolev derivative with intermediate time. Operators with a time-averaged
positive
Sobolev derivatives already appeared in studies of the well-posedness of nonlinear kinetic equations (see, for example,
[
20
]
). The pseudo-differential operator we use here is inspired by the one introduced in
[
3
]
, used to prove the
L^{2}_{x}H^{-s}_{v}
to
L^{2}_{x}L^{2}_{v}
regularization estimates for the linearized Boltzmann equation. Let
\widehat{w}
be the Fourier transform of
w
in
(x,v)
. We define the Fourier multiplier operator with symbol
{M}

{\mathcal{M}}w:=({\mathcal{F}_{(x,v)\to(\xi,\eta)}})^{-1}({M}\widehat{w}),

with

\displaystyle{M}(t,T,\eta,\xi):=\frac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\;d\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}.

(1.4)

Here,
\epsilon
and
\delta
are positive constants that can be chosen as needed.
More precise properties for (
1.4
) are summarized in Section
2
. However, let us analyze the main characteristics. The commutator of
\mathcal{M}
with
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
generates a backward diffusion in
v
, whose strength is proportional to the parameter
\delta
:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{M},\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}]\sim-\delta(I-\Delta_{v})\mathcal{M},

(1.5)

This commutator term will be controlled by the diffusion on the right-hand side of (
1.1
) or by viscosity term of (
1.2
).
In the case of (
1.1
) the loss of weights (for
\beta<0
) can be overcome by building a negative velocity weight in
\mathcal{M}
: in Section
3
we introduce a dyadic decomposition in
M
to localize the velocity in each ring where
|v|\in[2^{n},2^{n+2}]
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Unfortunately, the dyadic decomposition approach is not yet applicable to anisotropic diffusion; therefore, in the proof of the uniqueness of solution to (
1.2
) we use the added viscosity
\nu\Delta_{v}
to control (
1.5
).

This work makes two main contributions. First, we introduce a novel approach to studying uniqueness properties of inhomogeneous kinetic equations through the use of pseudo-differential operators acting on negative Sobolev spaces. Second, we present two examples of nonlinear inhomogeneous equations for which the uniqueness of weak solutions can be established. In particular, while these two models possess certain structural advantages compared to the classical Landau or Boltzmann equations, current strategies based on energy methods or Schauder estimates do not seem to establish uniqueness within the same regularity classes considered in this paper.

Let us conclude by mentioning additional uniqueness results available for homogeneous kinetic equations. For the Landau-Coulomb equation,
[
11
]
shows that if the solution belongs to
L^{1}(0,T,L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
then it is unique. In addition, it is known that there exists a unique smooth solution if the initial data belongs to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p>d/2
, see
[
12
]
.
In the recent work of
[
14
]
, the uniqueness (together with the global existence) is shown for initial data in a polynomially weighted
H^{-1/2}\cap L^{1}\cap L\log L
space. The main techniques used in
[
14
]
include a careful analysis involving Littlewood-Paley type of estimates based on localization in the frequency space. The related pseudo-differential operators do not apply directly to the inhomogeneous case since they do not commute well with the transport operator. For hard potentials, uniqueness was established in
[
5
]
and, more recently, in
[
10
]
for more general initial data. For moderately soft potential
[
1
]
shows the uniqueness of smooth solutions. For the homogeneous Boltzmann equation refer to
[
9
]
and the references therein for uniqueness results in both the cutoff and non-cutoff setting. Analysis of inhomogeneous kinetic equations similar to (
1.1
) can be found in
[
4
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
21
]
.

1.1.
Notation

The bracket
\langle v\rangle
is a short notation for
(1+|v|^{2})^{1/2}
. We denote

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3};L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}_{x}L^{q}_{v}=\left\{f|\,\text{esssup}_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left|f(x,v)\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}v\right)^{1/q}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3};L^{\infty}_{k_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}_{x}L^{\infty}_{k_{0}}=\left\{f|\,\text{esssup}_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left|\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}f(x,v)\right|<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{3};L^{q}_{k_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}_{t,x}L^{q}_{v}=\left\{f|\,\text{esssup}_{t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left\lVert\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}f(t,x,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}<\infty\right\}.

Similar notations apply to
L^{2}_{t,x,v}
and
L^{2}_{T,t,x,v}
, where the subindices encode the information of the integration domains. For presentation purposes, we often suppress the volume elements
\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}t
or
\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}T
in long integrals. In such cases, the integration domains will be explicitly stated to avoid any ambiguity. We also use
\nabla
to denote
\nabla_{v}
, the gradient in the
v
-variable. The
x
-gradient is specified as
\nabla_{x}
.

2.
Properties of the
\mathcal{M}
-operator and technical lemmas

In this section, we state the main properties of the
\mathcal{M}
-operator, and prove several technical lemmas. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the Fourier transform in
(x,v)
. We define the Fourier multiplier
\mathcal{M}
with symbol
{M}
as

{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}w):={M}{\mathcal{F}}(w),

with

\displaystyle{M}(t,T,\eta,\xi):=\frac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\;d\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}},\qquad\epsilon,\delta>0.

In the next lemma, we summarize the key properties of
\mathcal{M}
and its symbol
M
. The Fourier transform of a function
h
in
x,v
is denoted as
\hat{h}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
h
be any smooth enough function; we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}h\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}\leq\|h\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(\partial_{t}h-v\cdot\nabla_{x}h)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\right)({\mathcal{M}}h)+[\mathcal{M},\partial_{t}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}]h,

(2.1)

where

\mathcal{F}([\mathcal{M},\partial_{t}h-v\cdot\nabla_{x}]h)=-\delta\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)\tfrac{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}}{1+\delta\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\;d\tau}{M}\mathcal{F}({h}),

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T_{0}}\langle\xi\rangle^{2}{M}^{2}(t,T,\eta,\xi)\,{\rm d}T\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon\delta},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle|\xi|^{2}|{M}\hat{h}|^{2}d\xi\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}T\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon\delta}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\hat{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}t.

(2.3)

Proof.

The first inequality is the consequence of the Plancherel identity and the fact that
M\leq 1
:

\|\mathcal{M}h\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}=\|M\hat{h}\|_{L^{2}_{\xi,\eta}}\leq\|\hat{h}\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}.

Product rule and direct calculations yield (
2.1
) since

\displaystyle{M}(\partial_{t}\widehat{h}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\widehat{h})=\partial_{t}({M}\widehat{h})-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\xi}({M}\widehat{h})-\widehat{h}(\partial_{t}{M}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\xi}{M})

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{M}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\xi}{M}=\delta\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)\frac{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}}{1+\delta\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau}{M}.

For the second estimate, we first notice that

\displaystyle 1+\delta\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\geq 1+\frac{1}{4}\delta\langle\xi\rangle^{2}(T-t),

which implies

\displaystyle\langle\xi\rangle^{2}{M}\leq\frac{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\delta\langle\xi\rangle^{2}(T-t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}},

and consequently

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T_{0}}\langle\xi\rangle^{2}{M}^{2}\,{\rm d}T\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon\delta}.

The last inequality follows from the previous one after switching the order of integration:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}\int|\xi|^{2}|{M}\widehat{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}T

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{h}|^{2}\int_{t}^{T_{0}}|\xi|^{2}|{M}|^{2}\,{\rm d}T\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon\delta}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm d}t.\qed

As a preparation for the commutator estimates, we recall the bounds of the derivatives of
\mathcal{M}
in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M(t,\eta,\xi)
be the symbol defined in (
1.4
) with
T<1
. Then for any
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
and
\beta\neq 0
, there exists a constant
C
that only depends on
\epsilon,\alpha,\beta
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}M(t,\eta,\xi)}{M(t,\eta,\xi)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(\langle\xi\rangle+(T-t)|\eta|)^{-\min\{|\alpha|,2\}},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nabla_{\eta}^{\beta}M(t,\eta,\xi)}{M(t,\eta,\xi)}\right|\leq CT

\displaystyle(\langle\xi\rangle+(T-t)|\eta|)^{-\min\{|\beta|,2\}},\qquad\beta\neq 0.

(2.5)

Proof.

The first inequality (
2.4
) is shown in Lemma 5.1 of
[
3
]
. The second inequality (
2.5
) follows from the observation that

\displaystyle(t-\tau)\nabla_{\xi}\left\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\right\rangle=\nabla_{\eta}\left\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\right\rangle,

which gives the extra
T
in (
2.5
).
∎

Next, we show three key commutator estimates of the
\mathcal{M}
-operator with smooth function in
x
or
v
or
(x,v)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\phi\in H^{5}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
. Then for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\phi f)-\phi\mathcal{M}f=\mathcal{A}_{\phi}\mathcal{M}f,

where the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\phi}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{\phi}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,v}\to L^{2}_{x,v}}\leq C\,T\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{H^{5}},

(2.6)

with
T
being the time in
\mathcal{M}
and
C
being a generic constant.

Proof.

We show the proof in the Fourier space. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the Fourier transform in
(x,v)
. Then

\displaystyle\quad\,\left(\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}(\phi f)-\phi\mathcal{M}f\right)\right)(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau\eta\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\phi_{k}f_{\eta-k}(\xi)-\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\phi_{k}f_{\eta-k}(\xi)\tfrac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\phi_{k}f_{\eta-k}(\xi)\underbrace{\left(\tfrac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau\eta\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}-\tfrac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}\right)}_{I(\xi,\eta,k)},

where
\phi_{k}
and
f_{\eta-k}(\xi)
are the corresponding Fourier mode. We will show that for each
\xi,\eta
,

\displaystyle I(\xi,\eta,k)=a(\xi,\eta,k)M(\xi,\eta-k),

where
a(\xi,\eta,k)
satisfies

\displaystyle|a(\xi,\eta,k)|\leq CT\left\langle k\right\rangle^{3},

(2.7)

where
C
is independent of
\xi,\eta,k,\delta,\epsilon
. To this end, write

\displaystyle I(\xi,\eta,k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}s}\left(\tfrac{1}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-(1-s)k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}\right)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{2\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\tau\left(\xi-\tau(\eta-(1-s)k)\right)\cdot k\,{\rm d}\tau}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-(1-s)k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{3}{2}+\epsilon}}\,{\rm d}s.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|M^{-1}(\xi,\eta-k)I(\xi,\eta,k)\right|\leq C\,T\,|k|\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}{\left(1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-(1-s)k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}\,{\rm d}s,

where
C
is a generic constant independent of
\xi,\eta,k,T,\delta
. By Corollary A.1 in
[
3
]
, the term in the integrand satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau}{1+\delta\int_{0}^{T-t}\left\langle\xi-\tau(\eta-(1-s)k)\right\rangle^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1+\delta\left(|\xi|^{2}+(T-t)^{2}|\eta-k|^{2}\right)(T-t)}{1+\delta\left(|\xi|^{2}+(T-t)^{2}\left|\eta-(1-s)k\right|^{2}\right)(T-t)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\frac{|\eta-k|^{2}}{1+|\eta-k+sk|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|k|^{2}),

where
C
is a generic constant. The last step holds since

\displaystyle\frac{|\eta-k|^{2}}{1+|\eta-(1-s)k|^{2}}\leq\begin{cases}4|k|^{2},&\text{if }|\eta-k|\leq 2|k|,\\[3.0pt]
5,&\text{if }|\eta-k|>2|k|.\end{cases}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left|M^{-1}(\xi,\eta-k)I(\xi,\eta,k)\right|\leq C\,T\left\langle k\right\rangle^{3},

or equivalently, (
2.7
) holds. By Plancherel Theorem,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{M}(\phi f)-\phi\mathcal{M}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\eta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\left|\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}(\phi f)-\phi\mathcal{M}f\right)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\eta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\left|\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\phi_{k}f_{\eta-k}I(\xi,\eta,k)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\,T^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\eta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\left|\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\left|\phi_{k}\right|\left|M(\xi,\eta-k)f_{\eta-k}\right|\left\langle k\right\rangle^{3}\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\,T^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\eta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}|\phi_{k}|^{2}\left\langle k\right\rangle^{10}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}(\mathcal{M}f)_{\eta-k}^{2}\left\langle k\right\rangle^{-4}\right)\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\,T^{2}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{M}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2},

which gives the desired bound in (
2.6
).
∎

For the commutator with
v
-dependent functions, we recall the following lemma from
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{R}
there exists an
L^{2}
-bounded operator
A(v,D_{v})
whose symbol belongs
to
S^{-1}_{0,0}
uniformly with respect to

such that

\displaystyle\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k}[\mathcal{M},\left\langle v\right\rangle^{-k}]=[\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k},\mathcal{M}]\left\langle v\right\rangle^{-k}=A(v,D_{v})\mathcal{M}

with

\displaystyle\|\left\langle v\right\rangle(1+|D_{v}|^{2})^{1/2}A(v,D_{v})g\|_{L^{2}_{v}}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{2}_{v}},\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

\displaystyle\|\left\langle v\right\rangle A(v,D_{v})(1+|D_{v}|^{2})^{1/2}g\|_{L^{2}_{v}}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{2}_{v}},\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

where

is independent of

.

In general, we have the following commutator estimate.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
h\in C^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then there exists an
L^{2}
-bounded operator

The rest of this section contains two technical lemmas.
First, we explain the dyadic decomposition and its properties applied to the velocity variable in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This technique is useful when dealing with weights in kinetic equations. The construction is classical, but we include the details here for the convenience of the reader. Denote
B(x,r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the open ball centered at
x
with radius
r
and
\overline{B(x,r)}
as its closure.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
V_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the dyadic rings defined by

\displaystyle V_{0}=B(0,4),\qquad V_{k}=B(0,2^{k+2})\setminus\overline{B(0,2^{k})},\qquad k\geq 1.

Then there exists a partition of unity
\theta_{k}(v)
such that for any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{k}\leq 1,\quad\text{Supp}(\theta_{k})\subseteq V_{k},\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta_{k}(v)=1,\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta_{k}^{2}(v)\geq\frac{1}{3},\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(2.9)

Moreover, there exist two constants
C_{1},C_{2}
independent of
k
such that

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{v}\theta_{k}\right|\leq C_{1}2^{-k},\qquad\left|\nabla_{v}^{2}\theta_{k}\right|\leq C_{2}2^{-2k},\quad\forall k\geq 0.

(2.10)

Proof.

We will choose
\theta_{k}
to be radial. To this end, denote
I_{k}
as the interval:

\displaystyle I_{0}=[0,4],\qquad I_{k}=[2^{k},2^{k+2}],\quad k\geq 1.

Let
\phi_{0}\in C^{\infty}[0,\infty)
satisfy that

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi_{0}\leq 1,\qquad\text{Supp}(\phi_{0})\subseteq[0,4],\qquad\phi_{0}(r)=1\,\,\text{for}\,\,0\leq r\leq 3.

Let
\phi_{1}\in C^{\infty}[0,\infty)
satisfy that

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi_{1}\leq 1,\qquad\text{Supp}(\phi_{1})\subseteq[2,8],\qquad\phi_{1}(r)=1\,\,\text{for}\,\,3\leq r\leq 7.

For any
k\geq 2
, let

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(r)=\phi_{1}(2^{-k+1}r).

Then
\text{Supp}(\phi_{k})\subseteq[2^{k},2^{k+2}]
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi_{k}(r)>0,\qquad\forall\,r\in[0,\infty).

Define

\displaystyle\theta_{k}(v)=\frac{\phi_{k}(|v|)}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j}(|v|)},\qquad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Then
\theta_{k}
is well-defined and the first three conditions in (
2.9
) hold. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta_{k}^{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\theta_{k-1}^{2}(v)+\theta_{k}^{2}(v)+\theta_{k+1}^{2}(v)\geq\frac{1}{3}\left(\theta_{k-1}(v)+\theta_{k}(v)+\theta_{k+1}(v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta_{k}(v)\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{3}.

Therefore, the last condition in (
2.9
) holds.

To bound the derivatives, we note that for any
v\in\text{Supp}(\theta_{k})
,

\displaystyle\theta_{k}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{k}(|v|)}{\phi_{k-1}(|v|)+\phi_{k}(|v|)+\phi_{k+1}(|v|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{1}(2^{-k+1}|v|)}{\phi_{1}(2^{-k+2}|v|)+\phi_{1}(2^{-k+1}|v|)+\phi_{1}(2^{-k}|v|)}=h(2^{-k+1}|v|),

where for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle h(y)=\frac{\phi_{1}(y)}{\phi_{1}(2y)+\phi_{1}(y)+\phi_{1}(y/2)},\qquad 2\leq y\leq 8,

and for
k=0
,

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(v)=\frac{\phi_{0}(|v|)}{\phi_{0}(|v|)+\phi_{1}(|v|)},\qquad|v|\in[0,4].

Therefore,
|\theta_{0}^{\prime}|\leq C
and for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\theta_{k}=h^{\prime}(2^{-k}|v|)2^{-k}\frac{v}{|v|},\qquad\nabla_{v}^{2}\theta_{k}=h^{\prime\prime}(2^{-k}|v|)2^{-2k}\frac{v}{|v|}\otimes\frac{v}{|v|}+h^{\prime}(2^{-k}|v|)2^{-k}\nabla_{v}\frac{v}{|v|},

where
|v|\in[2^{k},2^{k+2}]
.
By the boundedness of
h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain the desired bounds in (
2.10
). Moreover, due to the compact support of
h
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|h^{\prime}(2^{-k}|v|)|<2\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(2,8)}.\qed

The final technical lemma in this section concerns the classical uniform convergence of regularized functions via convolution.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose
h\in C([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3})
and
g\in C([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3};L^{\infty}_{k_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with
k_{0}>0
.

(a) Suppose
h\geq 0
. Then there exists
\phi_{a}(x)\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle h_{a}(t,x):=h*\phi_{a}\geq 0,\qquad h_{a}\to h\,\,\text{in}\,\,C([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3})\,\,\,\text{as $a\to 0$}.

(b) Let
\psi_{a}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a mollifier in
(x,v)
. Then

\displaystyle g_{a}(t,x,v):=g\ast\psi_{a}\to g\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,C([0,T_{0}]\times T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\,\,\,\text{as $a\to 0$.}

Proof.

(a) For positivity, we can choose
\phi_{a}
as a positive summability kernel such as the Fejér kernel. The proof of uniform convergence over the compact set
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
can be found in
[
8
]
Appendix C.

(b) Once again, the uniform convergence over a compact set
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times B(0,R)
follows from the standard argument in
[
8
]
. To obtain the uniform convergence on
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we make use of the decay tail of
g
. Note that

\displaystyle|v|^{k_{0}}\left|g\ast\psi_{a}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{k_{0}}|g(v-w)|\psi_{a}(w)\,{\rm d}w

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v-w|^{k_{0}}|g(v-w)|\psi_{a}(w)\,{\rm d}w+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|g(v-w)||w|^{k_{0}}\psi_{a}(w)\,{\rm d}w

\displaystyle\leq C\sup\left(|v|^{k_{0}}g\right)+C\sup|g|<\infty.

Hence for any
|v|>R
, we have

\displaystyle\left|g\ast\psi_{a}-g\right|{\boldsymbol{1}}_{|v|>R}\leq\left|g\ast\psi_{a}\right|{\boldsymbol{1}}_{|v|>R}+\left|g\right|{\boldsymbol{1}}_{|v|>R}\leq\frac{C}{R^{k_{0}}}\to 0\qquad\text{as $R\to\infty$,}

which combined with the uniform convergence on
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times B(0,R)
gives the uniform convergence on
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
∎

3.
A Toy Model

We start with a toy model to illustrate the idea. Consider the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho(t,x)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}f\right),\qquad\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\,{\rm d}v,

(3.1)

\displaystyle f|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=f_{0}(x,v),

where
\beta\leq 2
. This toy model mimics, to some extent, the Landau equation with both soft and hard potentials, with
\beta<0
being soft and
\beta>0
being hard. The key difference, when compared with the Landau equation, is that there is no weight gap in the upper and lower bounds of the diffusive term on the right-hand side. The uniqueness of solutions to this toy model suggests that whether the potential is soft or hard does not make an essential difference. It is possible that the condition
\beta\leq 2
is technical rather than structural.

Suppose the initial data of
f
satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq f_{0}\leq m_{0},\qquad\left\lVert f_{0}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}(L^{\infty}_{v}\cap L^{1}_{v})}<\infty,\qquad\rho(0,x)>c_{0}>0,

(3.2)

where
m_{0},c_{0}
are constants and
k_{0}>3+|\beta|
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose there exist
T_{\ast}>0
and
k_{0}>\max\{3+|\beta|,4\}
such that
\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}f\in C([0,T_{\ast}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a continuous solution to (
3.1
) with
initial data satisfying (
3.2
) and

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\left\langle v\right\rangle^{k_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}(L^{\infty}_{v}\cap L^{1}_{v})}<\infty.

Then
\rho
is uniformly continuous on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
and for
T_{0}
small enough,

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2},\qquad\forall(t,x)\in[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}.

(3.3)

Proof.

The uniform continuity of
\rho
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
follows from the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence theorem, given that
\left\langle v\right\rangle^{4}f\in C([0,T_{\ast}]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The bound (
3.3
) follows directly from continuity.
∎

A few words are in order to illustrate the main ideas. First, ideally, we want to commute the
\mathcal{M}
operator with
\rho\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}
. However, this commutation requires high regularity of
\rho
in
x
which is not known a priori. To overcome this difficulty, we decompose
\rho
into its regular and singular parts through convolution.

Second, equation (
2.1
) shows that the commutator
\mathcal{M}
with the transport operator introduces a backward diffusion with no weights in
v
. Hence, in the case of soft potentials where
\beta<0
, the regularity gained through diffusion is not sufficient to control the transport commutator. To address this, we incorporate the weight into the
\mathcal{M}
operator through the dyadic decomposition. It turns out that such a “weighted”
\mathcal{M}
is also needed in the hard potential case where
\beta>0
.

Let us now state the uniqueness theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
\beta\leq 2
and
f,g
are two continuous solutions to (
3.1
) satisfying the conditions in Lemma
3.1
.
Then
f=g
.

Proof.

First, we use the dyadic decomposition to localize the velocity variable. Let
\theta_{n}(v)
be the partition of unity in Lemma
2.6
. Let

\displaystyle h=f-g,\qquad w_{n}=\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}(f-g),\quad 3<m\leq k_{0},\quad m+\beta\leq k_{0}.

(3.4)

Then
\text{Supp}(w_{n})\subseteq B(0,2^{n+2})\setminus\overline{B(0,2^{n})}
and
w_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}w_{n}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{g}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}w_{n}\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{h}\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m+\beta}\nabla_{v}f\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,-\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)\cdot\rho_{h}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}f-\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)\cdot\rho_{g}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}h

\displaystyle\quad\,-\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{g}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)h\right),

(3.5)

\displaystyle w_{n}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=0,

where
\rho_{w}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w(t,x,v)\,{\rm d}v
. Let
\phi_{a}
be a non-negative kernel in Lemma
2.7
(a) and decompose
\rho_{g}
into its regular and singular parts

\displaystyle\rho_{g}=\rho_{g}\ast\phi_{a}+(\rho_{g}-\rho_{g}\ast\phi_{a}).

Rewrite equation (
3
) accordingly:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}w_{n}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\left(\rho_{g}\ast\phi_{a}\right)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}w_{n}\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\left(\rho_{g}-\rho_{g}\ast\phi_{a}\right)\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}w_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{h}\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m+\beta}\nabla_{v}f\right)-\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\rho_{g}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)h\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\quad\,-\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)\cdot\rho_{h}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}f-\nabla_{v}\left(\theta_{n}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{m}\right)\cdot\rho_{g}\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}\nabla_{v}h:=\sum_{i=1}^{6}J_{i},

where
a>0
is chosen to be small enough such that

\displaystyle\rho_{a}\ast\phi_{a}>c_{0}/4>0.

(3.7)

This is possible due to the uniform convergence in Lemma
2.7
(a).

Instead of using the generic
\mathcal{M}
-operator, we define the weighted version to accommodate the extra weight
\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}
: for each
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, define

\displaystyle M_{n}:=\frac{1}{1+\delta 2^{\beta n}\int_{0}^{T-t}\langle\xi-\tau\eta\rangle^{2}\;d\tau}.

Denote
M_{n}
as the symbol of the operator
\mathcal{M}_{n}
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}\right)=M_{n}\widehat{w_{n}}.

Apply the
\mathcal{M}_{n}
-operator to (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{6}\mathcal{M}_{n}J_{i}+R_{1},

where
R_{1}
is the transport commutator defined in (

\displaystyle\widehat{R_{1}}=\mathcal{F}\left([\mathcal{M}_{n},\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}]w_{n}\right)=\delta 2^{\beta n}\tfrac{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}}{1+\delta 2^{\beta n}\int_{t}^{T}\langle\xi+(t-\tau)\eta\rangle^{2}\;d\tau}\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}\right).

Perform an energy estimate for each

. We have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|w_{n}|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}t,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{n}w_{n}\geq\frac{1}{6}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{t,x,v}}^{2}.

and each

(3.10)

Similarly, by Lemma

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\rho_{g}\ast\phi_{a}\right)[\mathcal{M}_{n},\,\,\left\langle v\right\rangle^{\beta}]\nabla_{v}w_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,v}}

4.
Landau Equation with Viscosity

We consider a viscous approximation of the inhomogeneous Landau equation with Coulomb potential

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nu\Delta_{v}f+Q_{L}(f,f),

(4.1)

where

is the Landau operator, defined as

Q_{L}(f,f):=\textrm{div}_{v}\left(A[f]\nabla f-f\nabla a[f]\right),

with

\displaystyle A[f](x,v,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbb{P}(v-z)}{|v-z|}f(x,z,t)\;dz,

\displaystyle a[f](x,v,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|v-z|}f(x,z,t)\;dz,

\textrm{div}_{v}A[f]=\nabla_{v}a[f]

4.1
) is defined as follows:

Definition 4.1

A positive function
f
is a classical solution to (
4.1
) in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with initial data

4.1

.

The space

is an abbreviation for the kinetic representation of the classical Hölder spaces used for Schauder estimates
C_{t}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{x}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{3}}C_{v}^{2+\alpha}
.

We prove the following two theorems.

4.1

4.1
