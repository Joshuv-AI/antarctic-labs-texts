Conductivities and excitations of a holographic flavour brane Weyl semimetal
Haruki Furukawa, Sacha Ployet, and Ronnie Rodgers

Abstract
We compute the electrical conductivities at non-zero frequency in a top-down holographic model of a Weyl semimetal, consisting of 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 supersymmetric SU ⁡ ( N c ) \mathrm{SU}(N_{c}) Yang–Mills theory coupled to 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 hypermultiplets with mass m m , subject to an applied axial vector field b b . The model exhibits a first-order phase transition between a Weyl semimetal phase at small m / b m/b and an insulating phase at large m / b m/b . The conductivities develop peaks and troughs as functions of real frequency at low temperatures and for m / b m/b close to the phase transition. We compute the poles of the conductivities as functions of complex frequency, finding poles with small imaginary part at low temperatures and close to the phase transition.

Introduction

Weyl semimetals (WSMs) are a class of materials whose electronic bands intersect at an even number of points at or near the Fermi energy. The points in momentum space where the bands meet are called
Weyl nodes
. The defining property of WSMs is that the low energy excitations of electrons around the Weyl nodes are well described by an effective theory of relativistic Weyl fermions, with the Fermi velocity
v_{f}
playing the role of the speed of light. Half of the Weyl nodes are left-handed—meaning that they host left-handed Weyl fermions—while the other half are right-handed. Reviews of WSM physics may be found in refs.
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, for example.

One of the key features of WSMs is that they are topological materials; the degeneracy of the bands at the Weyl nodes is protected against small perturbations by non-trivial topology of the Berry curvature. The only way for Weyl nodes to disappear is for a left-handed and a right-handed node to be brought together in momentum space, where they can annihilate each another. A consequence of this topological nature is that WSMs with boundaries exhibit current carrying surface states called Fermi arcs
[
4
]
.

A WSM must necessarily break either inversion symmetry or time-reversal invariance; if both inversions and time-reversal are preserved then Weyl fermions must appear in degenerate pairs of opposite handedness, i.e. the low energy excitations must be Dirac fermions, not Weyl fermions. Known examples of WSMs include TaAs
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, which breaks inversions, and Co
3
Sn
2
S
2
, which breaks time-reversal
[
9
,
10
]
.

The vanishing or small density of states at the Fermi energy in a WSM causes the Coulomb interactions between electrons to be weakly screened. This makes it possible, but does not guarantee, that the electrons in a given WSM may be strongly coupled, leading to a breakdown in perturbation theory. For instance, the inversion breaking WSM Ce
3
Bi
4
Pd
3
exhibits evidence of strong coupling
[
11
,
12
]
.

One tool for modelling strongly coupled systems is the AdS/CFT correspondence, also known as holography
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, which relates strongly coupled quantum field theories (QFTs) to weakly coupled gravitational theories. A number of holographic models of strongly coupled WSMs have been constructed
[
16
,

,

,

, see ref.

[
27
]
, an odd viscosity connected to the breaking of time reversal
[
28
]
, and the effect of dislocations
[
29
]
. Existing holographic WSM models are of the type that breaks time-reversal, with the construction of a holographic inversion-breaking WSM remaining an open challenge.

One of the key advantages of holography is that it is well suited to the study of transport phenomena and hydrodynamics. The AC conductivities of the model of refs.
[

Many holographic WSM models are inspired by a simple free quantum field theory (QFT) model, consisting of a Dirac fermion
\psi
with mass
m
coupled to an external axial vector field
\vec{A}_{5}=(0,0,b/2)
for some constant
b
, where without loss of generality we have chosen to orient
\vec{A}_{5}
in the
z\equiv x^{3}
direction. The Lagrangian density of this model is
[
32
,
33
]

\mathcal{L}=i\bar{\psi}\left(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m+\frac{b}{2}\gamma^{3}\gamma^{5}\right)\psi.

(1)

The single particle Hamiltonian that follows from equation (
1
) has four eigenvalues which are functions of momentum
\vec{k}
. When the axial vector field is sufficiently strong,
b^{2}>4m^{2}
, two of these eigenvalues meet at
\vec{k}=(0,0,\pm\sqrt{(b/2)^{2}-m^{2}})
. These are the two Weyl nodes. When
b^{2}<4m^{2}
the Hamiltonian is gapped and the system is insulating. The transition at
b^{2}=4m^{2}
between the WSM and insulating phases occurs via the mechanism described above, in which two Weyl nodes are brought together in momentum space and annihilate each other.

One of the striking features of the model in equation (
1
) is that the separation of the Weyl nodes in momentum space induces an axial anomaly, with observable consequences for transport. If the model is in the WSM phase,
b^{2}<4m^{2}
, then thanks to the axial anomaly an electric field
\vec{E}
applied in a direction orthogonal to the
\vec{A}_{5}
will produce a vector current
\langle\vec{J}\rangle
in the direction orthogonal to both
\vec{E}
and
\vec{A}_{5}
, in addition to the usual current parallel to
\vec{E}
. Concretely, a uniform applied electric field produces a current

\langle\vec{J}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}&\sigma_{xy}&0\\
-\sigma_{xy}&\sigma_{yy}&0\\
0&0&\sigma_{zz}\end{pmatrix}\vec{E},

(2)

where rotational symmetry implies that
\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{yy}
, and the Hall conductivity, which is non-zero in the WSM phase due to the axial anomaly, is
[
34
]

\sigma_{xy}=\frac{\mathrm{sign}(b)}{4\pi^{2}}\sqrt{b^{2}-4m^{2}}\,\theta(|b|-2m),

(3)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function.

Roughly speaking, most existing holographic WSM models use gravity to describe a QFT with similar features to the free model in equation (

R_{0}\to 0
, respectively. Similarly, at non-zero temperature, black hole and Minkowski embeddings approach the critical embedding in the limits
r_{h}\to 0
and
R_{0}\to\rho_{h}
. Such near-critical solutions exhibit a discrete scale invariance with complex critical exponents, leading to a self-similarity of the solutions
[
49
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
23
]
. This self-similarity is connected to the first-order nature of the phase transition.

3
AC conductivities

3.1
On the holographic determination of the AC conductivities

In this section we compute the AC conductivities of the Weyl semimetal phase of our holographic model. A small time-dependent applied electric field
\vec{E}(t)
will induce a non-zero one-point function of the vector current
\langle\vec{J}(t)\rangle
. Ohm’s law relates the Fourier components
\langle\vec{J}(\omega)\rangle
and
\vec{E}(\omega)
of the current and electric field as

\langle J_{i}(\omega)\rangle=\sigma_{ij}(\omega)E_{j}(\omega),

(19)

where
\sigma_{ij}(\omega)
is the AC conductivity. We will obtain the AC conductivities from the Kubo formula (see e.g. ref.
[
56
]
)

\sigma_{ij}(\omega)=\frac{G_{ij}(\omega,0)}{i\omega},

(20)

where
G_{ij}(\omega,\vec{k})
is the retarded Green’s function of the vector current components
J^{i}
and
J^{j}
, computed in momentum space with frequency
\omega
and momentum
\vec{k}
.
6
6

6

Explicitly, the retarded Green’s function appearing in the Kubo formula is

G_{ij}(\omega,\vec{k})=-i\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{x}\,e^{i\omega t-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\,\theta(t)\,\langle[J^{i}(t,\vec{x}),J^{j}(0,0)]\rangle,

where
\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function.

Retarded Green’s functions are obtained holographically from linearised fluctuations of bulk fields subject to ingoing boundary conditions at the black brane horizon
[
41
,
57
,
58
]
, as follows.

Adopting a radial gauge
A_{r}=0
, we consider linearised fluctuations of the field
A_{i}
holographically dual to the vector current
J^{i}
about backgrounds of the form in equation (
9
). The
A_{i}
are the components of the D7-branes’ world-volume gauge field in the spatial directions
\vec{x}=(x,y,z)
of the dual QFT. Since the Kubo formula (
20
) contains the Green’s function at non-zero frequency but vanishing momentum, we allow for the gauge field fluctuations to depend on
t
and
r
. We then Fourier transform with respect to time, writing
A_{i}(t,r)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega t}A_{i}(\omega;r)
. The equations of motion for the Fourier modes
A_{i}(\omega;r)
, derived from the action (
8
), are

\displaystyle\left(\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{2}}\frac{f}{\sqrt{h}}\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}{1+R^{\prime 2}}}A_{x}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{6}}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{f}\sqrt{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}\sqrt{1+R^{\prime 2}}A_{x}

\displaystyle=-i\omega b\left(\frac{r^{4}}{\rho^{4}}\right)^{\prime}A_{y},

(21)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{2}}\frac{f}{\sqrt{h}}\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}{1+R^{\prime 2}}}A_{y}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{6}}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{f}\sqrt{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}\sqrt{1+R^{\prime 2}}A_{y}

\displaystyle=i\omega b\left(\frac{r^{4}}{\rho^{4}}\right)^{\prime}A_{x},

and

\left(\frac{r^{3}\rho^{2}f\sqrt{h}\,A_{z}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2})(1+R^{\prime 2})}}\right)^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{2}}\frac{h^{3/2}}{f}\sqrt{\frac{1+R^{\prime 2}}{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R}}\,A_{z}=0.

(22)

For non-zero
b
the equations of motion for
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
are coupled. However, they may be decoupled by the introduction of two new fields
A_{\pm}=A_{x}\pm iA_{y}
, the equations of motion for which are

\left(\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{2}}f\sqrt{h}\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}{1+R^{\prime 2}}}A_{\pm}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\frac{r^{3}}{\rho^{6}}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{f}\sqrt{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}\sqrt{1+R^{\prime 2}}A_{\pm}\pm\omega b\left(\frac{r^{4}}{\rho^{4}}\right)^{\prime}A_{\pm}=0.

(23)

We will use an index
n
to label the three components of
(A_{+},A_{-},A_{z})
.

As reviewed in section
2
, the WSM phase is dual to black hole embeddings, for which the solution for
R(r)
has the IR behaviour in equation (
15
) or (
16
) for
T>0
or
T=0
, respectively. The equations of motion for
A_{n}
then admit solutions obeying ingoing boundary conditions of the form

A_{n}(\omega;r)\approx\begin{cases}(r-r_{h})^{-i\omega/(2\pi T)},&\text{for }r\to r_{h}\text{ at }T\neq 0,\\
\dfrac{e^{i\omega/r}}{\sqrt{r}},&\text{for }r\to 0\text{ at }T=0.\end{cases}

(24)

We must impose that the fluctuations have the IR behaviour of equation (
24
) in order to obtain the retarded Green’s functions
[
41
]
.
7
7

7

The equations of motion also admit outgoing boundary conditions, which would correspond to the opposite signs in the exponents in equation (
24
) and would be appropriate for the computation of the advanced Green’s functions.

Near the boundary at
r\to\infty
, solutions to the equations of motion for the fluctuations behave as

A_{n}(\omega;r)=A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)\left[1+\frac{\omega^{2}}{4r^{2}}\log(r^{2}/\omega^{2})\right]+\frac{A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)}{r^{2}}+O(r^{-4}\log r).

(25)

where
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
are integration constants, fixed by the ingoing boundary conditions (up to an overall scaling, since the equations of motion for
A_{n}(\omega;r)
are linear). The integration constants determine the retarded Green’s functions, which in turn determine the conductivities through the Kubo formula (
20
). The result is that the non-zero conductivities are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\sigma_{yy}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{i\omega}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)}+\frac{A_{-}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{-}^{(0)}(\omega)}\right)+\frac{i\omega}{2}\right],

(26a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{xy}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\omega}\left(\frac{A_{-}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{-}^{(0)}(\omega)}-\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)}\right)+b\right],

(26b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{zz}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{2}{i\omega}\frac{A_{z}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)}+\frac{i\omega}{2}\right]

(26c)

Since
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{yy}
are equal, we will show results only for
\sigma_{xx}
. The derivation of equation (
26
) may be found in appendix
A
.

To summarise, the conductivities are obtained by solving the equations of motion for
A_{n}(\omega;r)
in equations (
22
) and (
23
) subject to the ingoing boundary conditions in equation (
24
). From the behaviour of the resulting solutions at
r=0
we then read off
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, which we substitute into equation (
26
). Usually this procedure must be carried out numerically. However, as we discuss in the next subsection, for massless hypermultiplets
m=0
the conductivities may be obtained analytically.

Reality of
A_{i}(t,r)
requires that the Fourier modes obey the condition
A_{\pm}(\omega;r)=A_{\mp}(-\omega;r)^{*}
and
A_{z}(\omega;r)=A_{z}(-\omega;r)^{*}
. This then implies that
A_{\pm}^{(0,2)}(\omega)=A_{\mp}^{(0,2)}(-\omega)^{*}
and
A_{z}^{(0,2)}(\omega)=A_{z}^{(0,2)}(-\omega)^{*}
. These conditions combined with equation (
26
) make manifest that the real parts of the conductivities are even functions of
\omega
, while the imaginary parts are odd functions of
\omega
. This is true even though non-zero
b
breaks time-reversal symmetry. For example, we may rewrite the real and imaginary parts of
\sigma_{xx}
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\omega}\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)}-\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(-\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(-\omega)}\right),

(27)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[-\frac{1}{\omega}\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)}+\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(-\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(-\omega)}\right)+\frac{\omega}{2}\right],

which are manifestly even and odd functions of
\omega
, respectively. Similar results hold for
\sigma_{xy}
and
\sigma_{zz}
. As a consequence, we will only show results for the conductivities at positive frequency.

The conductivities we present will be functions of four parameters, each with units of mass:
m
,
T
,
b
, and
\omega
. The conductivities themselves also have units of mass. In what follows, we will mostly measure dimensionful quantities in units of
b
, plotting
\sigma/b
as a function of
T/b
,
\omega/b
, and
m/b\sqrt{\lambda}
, the factor of
\sqrt{\lambda}
in the latter arising naturally from holography. The exception is when we present results at
m=0
, in which case the
b
-dependence of the conductivities drops out and we instead present results in units of
T
, and some occasions where we present the conductivities in units of their zero mass limits.

3.2
Conductivities of massless hypermultiplets

For
m=0
the embedding equation (
11
) is solved by
R(r)=0
. This greatly simplifies the equations of motion for the gauge field fluctuations; for
R(r)=0
equations (
22
) and (
23
) both become of the same form,

(r^{4}-\rho_{h}^{4})\left(\frac{r^{4}-\rho_{h}^{4}}{r}A_{n}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{r}(r^{4}+\rho_{h}^{4})A_{n}=0.

(28)

Since the equations of motion for
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
are identical at
m=0
, solutions obeying the same ingoing boundary conditions will have
A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)=A_{-}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)=A_{-}^{(2)}(\omega)
. The
A_{+}^{(2)}/A_{+}^{(0)}
and
A_{-}^{(2)}/A_{-}^{(0)}
terms cancel in the expression for
\sigma_{xy}
in equation (
26b
), leading to an AC Hall conductivity that is independent of both frequency and temperature, equal to the DC conductivity found in ref.
[
23
]

\left.\sigma_{xy}(\omega)\right|_{m=0}=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}b.

(29)

Further, since the equation of motion for
A_{z}
takes the same form as those for
A_{\pm}
the longitudinal conductivities are equal at
m=0
,
\left.\sigma_{zz}(\omega)\right|_{m=0}=\left.\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\right|_{m=0}
.

Since equation (
28
) is independent of
b
, its solutions are the same as for
b=0
. For
T=0
(corresponding to
\rho_{h}=0
) the solution obeying ingoing boundary conditions is

A_{n}(\omega;r)=e^{3\pi i/4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\omega}{2}}\frac{H_{1}^{(1)}(\omega/r)}{r},

(30)

where
H_{\alpha}^{(1)}
is a Hankel function of the first kind. Expanding this solution at large
r
we read off
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, which we then substitute into equations (
26a
) and (
26c
) to obtain the longitudinal conductivities at
m=T=0
,

\left.\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\right|_{m=T=0}=\left.\sigma_{zz}(\omega)\right|_{m=T=0}=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}+i\left(\gamma-\log 2\right)\right]\omega,

(31)

where
\gamma\approx 0.577
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

The solution to equation (
28
) obeying ingoing boundary conditions at non-zero temperature was found in ref.
[
59
]
. In our coordinate system it is

A_{n}(\omega;r)=\left(\frac{r^{2}-\rho_{h}^{2}}{r^{2}+\rho_{h}^{2}}\right)^{-i\mathfrak{w}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1-i}{4}\mathfrak{w},-\frac{1+i}{2}\mathfrak{w};1-i\mathfrak{w};\left(\frac{r^{2}-\rho_{h}^{2}}{r^{2}+\rho_{h}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right),

(32)

where we have introduced the short-hand notation
\mathfrak{w}=\omega/2\pi T
. Again expanding this solution at large
r
to find
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, we obtain the conductivities
[
59
]

\displaystyle\left.\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\right|_{m=0}=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4}T\biggl[\mathfrak{w}\frac{\sinh(\pi\mathfrak{w})+i\sin(\pi\mathfrak{w})}{\cosh(\pi\mathfrak{w})-\cos(\pi\mathfrak{w})}+

\displaystyle\frac{2i\mathfrak{w}}{\pi}\operatorname{Re}\psi\left(\frac{1+i}{2}\mathfrak{w}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{\pi}+\frac{i\mathfrak{w}}{\pi}\left(2\gamma-\log(2\mathfrak{w}^{2})\right)\biggr],

where
\psi
is the digamma function. This conductivity is plotted in figure
4
. It interpolates between the
m=0
value of the DC conductivity found in ref.
[
23
]
,
\left.\sigma_{xx}(0)\right|_{m=0}=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi}T
, at low frequencies and the zero temperature result in equation (
31
) for
\omega\gg T
.

3.3
Conductivities of massive hypermultiplets

For
m\neq 0
we must solve both the embedding equation (

) for
R(r)
and equation (
23
) for
A_{n}(\omega;r)
numerically. We have done so using two methods. The first is a shooting method, which works as follows. We use the IR asymptotics for
R(r)
written in equation (
15
) and (
16
) for some value of
r_{h}
or
\eta
, to set boundary conditions at small
r
, and integrate to large
r
using
NDSolve
in Mathematica. The value of
M

limit of the solution. We then tune the IR coefficient
r_{h}
or
\eta
to obtain the desired value of

. Then, we substitute the solution for
R(r)
into the gauge field equations (
22
) and (
23
) and solve these for
A_{n}(\omega;r)
with
NDSolve
, using equation (
24
) to set ingoing boundary conditions at small

. From the behaviour of these solutions near

we determine
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and

, which we then substitute into equation (

) to obtain the conductivities.

The other numerical method that we used is a pseudospectral collocation method, detailed in appendix
B.1
. The advantage of this method is that with it we can simultaneously impose boundary conditions in the IR and UV, eliminating the need to tune the IR coefficient
r_{h}
or

. The two numerical methods agree where we have checked them against each other. All of the plots we show of the conductivities use results obtained from the pseudospectral method, which we found was faster and converged better at large frequencies.

and

Features visible in both figures

and

are that the conductivities become independent of mass for
\omega/b\gg 1
, and that the real parts of the conductivities are independent of mass for
\omega/b\ll 1
. Moreover, the longitudinal conductivities are linear in frequency with the same slope in both limits. These behaviours arise due to the scaling behaviour of the black hole embeddings in the UV and the IR. From equation (
10
), we see that in the the far UV
r\to\infty
the induced metric on the D7-branes’ world-volume is that of
\mathrm{AdS}_{5}\times\mathrm{S}^{3}
with both AdS
5
and S
3
having radius of curvature
L
, independent of the hypermultiplet mass. The large frequency behaviour of the conductivities is controlled by the UV behaviour of the geometry, and hence is also independent of the hypermultiplet mass. Similarly, for zero-temperature black hole embeddings, the IR
r\to 0
limit of the induced metric is also
\mathrm{AdS}_{5}\times\mathrm{S}^{3}
with curvature radius
L
. The low-frequency limits of the imaginary parts of holographic Green’s functions—and therefore through the Kubo formula (
20
) the real parts of the conductivities—are determined by the IR geometry
[
60
,
61
,
62
]
.

The fact that the DC limit of
\sigma_{xy}
is independent of mass in the WSM phase was established in ref.
[
23
]
. One way to derive the low-frequency behaviour of the real parts of
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{zz}
is as follows
[
63
]
. Using the equations of motion (
22
) and (
23
), it is straightforward to show that the following quantities, which we shall refer to as fluxes, are independent of
r
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{+}(\omega)\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{r^{3}\sqrt{(r^{2}+R^{2})^{2}+b^{2}R^{2}}}{2i\omega(r^{2}+R^{2})\sqrt{1+R^{\prime 2}}}\left[A_{+}(\omega;r)\partial_{r}A_{-}(-\omega;r)-A_{-}(-\omega;r)\partial_{r}A_{+}(\omega;r)\right],

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{z}(\omega)\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{r^{3}(r^{2}+R^{2})}{2i\omega\sqrt{(r^{2}+R^{2})^{2}+b^{2}R^{2}}\sqrt{(1+R^{\prime 2})}}\!\left[A_{z}(\omega;r)\partial_{r}A_{z}(-\omega;r)-A_{z}(-\omega;r)\partial_{r}A_{z}(\omega;r)\right],

Evaluating these fluxes near the boundary, one finds

\mathcal{F}_{+}(\omega)=\frac{2}{\omega}|A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)}\right),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{z}(\omega)=\frac{2}{\omega}|A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{A_{z}^{(2)}(\omega)}{A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)}\right),

(35)

where we have made use of the fact that reality of
A_{x}(t,r)
and
A_{y}(t,r)
requires that
A_{+}(\omega;r)=A_{-}(-\omega;r)^{*}
, while reality of
A_{z}(t,r)
requires
A_{z}(\omega;r)=A_{z}(-\omega;r)^{*}
.

Using equations (
26
) and (
35
), we see that we may rewrite the real parts of the longitudinal conductivities in terms of the fluxes

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{+}(\omega)}{|A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{+}(-\omega)}{|A_{+}^{(0)}(-\omega)|^{2}}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{z}(\omega)}{|A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}}.

(36)

Evaluating the fluxes at small
r
, using the ingoing boundary conditions (
24
) and that for black hole embeddings we have
R\ll r
as
r\to 0
, one finds

\mathcal{F}_{+}(\omega)=\mathcal{F}_{z}(\omega)=1.

(37)

Substituting this into equation (
36
), we obtain the formulas

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}}+\frac{1}{|A_{+}^{(0)}(-\omega)|^{2}}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{|A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)|^{2}}.

(38)

So far no approximation has been made. We will now make a small frequency approximation, solving the equations of motion for the fluctuations at
\omega\ll b
in order to obtain
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
, and therefore determine
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}
and
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}
from equation (
38
).

At leading order at small non-zero
\omega
, one would be tempted to set
\omega=0
in the equations of motion (
22
) and (
23
) for
A_{n}
. However, this does not work because the coefficient of
\omega^{2}
in these equations diverges at
r\to 0
. Instead, the correct approach is to solve for the gauge field fluctuations using a matching method
[
60
,
61
,
62
]
; one solves the equations of motion for non-zero frequency close to
r=0
with the approximation
R(r)\approx 0
(since
R\ll r
for the black hole emebddings as
r\to 0
) and for zero frequency away from
r=0
, matching these two solutions in the region where both approximations are valid. The solution near
r=0
is the zero mass solution written in equation (
30
), while the zero frequency solution is
A_{n}(\omega;r)=A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
constant. Matching these two solutions, one finds

A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)=\lim_{\omega/r\to 0}e^{3\pi i/4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\omega}{2}}\frac{H_{1}^{(1)}(\omega/r)}{r}=\mathrm{sign}(\omega)e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\omega}}.

(39)

Substituting this into equation (
38
), we obtain the results

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{8\pi}\omega,\qquad\omega\ll b,

(40)

matching the real parts of
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{zz}
at
m=0
written in equation (
31
), as anticipated from the numerical results.

The imaginary part of
\sigma_{xy}
in equation (
26b
) may also be written in terms of
\mathcal{F}_{+}
,

\operatorname{Im}\sigma_{xy}(\omega)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{+}(-\omega)}{|A_{+}(-\omega)|^{2}}-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{+}(\omega)}{|A_{+}(\omega)|^{2}}\right).

(41)

The analysis of the preceding paragraphs then implies that
\operatorname{Im}\sigma_{xy}\to 0
as
\omega\to 0
, but does not tell us the rate that at which
\operatorname{Im}\sigma_{xy}
vanishes. Numerically we find
\operatorname{Im}\sigma_{xy}\propto\omega^{2}/b
at small
\omega/b
, with a coefficient that (as can be seen in figure
5
) depends on
m/b\sqrt{\lambda}
.

3.3.2
Non-zero temperature

We now turn to the conductivities at both non-zero temperature and non-zero hypermultiplet mass. First, we compute the DC conductivity
\sigma_{zz}(\omega=0)
. Unlike
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{xy}
, the DC value of
\sigma_{zz}
for the D3/D7 WSM was not computed in ref.
[
23
]
since it was not amenable to the Karch–O’Bannon method used there. However there is no problem computing
\sigma_{zz}(\omega=0)
using the appropriate limit of the Kubo formula.

One way to obtain the DC conductivity numerically would be to take the zero frequency limit in our numerical computation of the AC conductivities. However, it is also possible to derive a formula for the DC conductivity that depends only on the properties of the embedding
R(r)
. We will use the latter approach, since it allows for a more direct determination of the DC conductivity.

To derive this formula, one could use the same method of fluxes that we used to obtain the low-frequency behaviour of
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}(\omega)
at zero temperature. However a more transparent method is the membrane paradigm approach of ref.
[
64
]
, which works as follows. Using the holographic renormalisation results in appendix
A
, one finds that the radial canonical momentum conjugate to
A_{z}
(i.e. the derivative of the Lagrangian density with respect to
\partial_{r}A_{z}
) is

\Pi_{z}(\omega;r)=-\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{\rho^{2}r^{3}\,f\sqrt{h}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2})(1+R^{\prime 2})}}\partial_{r}A_{z}(\omega;r)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}A_{z}(\omega;r)\log(r^{2}/\omega^{2})\right],

(42)

where the term containing
\log(r^{2}/\omega^{2})
arises from a counterterm. Expanding for large
r
, one finds

\Pi_{z}(\omega;r)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(2A_{z}^{(2)}(\omega)-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)\right)+O(r^{-1}).

(43)

Comparing to the formula for
\sigma_{zz}
in equation (
26c
), we find that we may write

\sigma_{zz}(\omega)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\Pi_{z}(\omega;r)}{i\omega A_{z}(\omega;r)}=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\left[P_{z}(\omega;r)-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\log(r^{2}/\omega^{2})\right],

(44)

where on the right-hand side we have defined

\displaystyle P_{z}(\omega;r)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{4\pi^{2}}{N_{f}N_{c}}\left(\frac{\Pi_{z}(\omega;r)}{i\omega A_{z}(\omega;r)}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\log(r^{2}/\omega^{2})\right),

(45)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho^{2}r^{3}\,f\sqrt{h}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2})(1+R^{\prime 2})}}\frac{\partial_{r}A_{z}(\omega;r)}{A_{z}(\omega;r)}.

The key observation of ref.
[
64
]
is that in the limit
\omega\to 0
,
P_{z}(\omega;r)
becomes independent of
r
and the logarithmic term drops from equation (
44
), so that we may obtain the DC value of
\sigma_{zz}
by evaluating
P_{z}(\omega;r)
at the horizon. Indeed, using the equation of motion (
22
), one finds that
P_{z}(\omega;r)
obeys the equation of motion

\frac{\rho^{2}r^{3}\,f\sqrt{h}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2})(1+R^{\prime 2})}}\partial_{r}P_{z}(\omega;r)=i\omega\left(\frac{r^{6}h}{\rho^{4}h+b^{2}R^{2}}-P_{z}(\omega;r)^{2}\right).

(46)

We see explicitly that
\partial_{r}P_{z}(\omega;r)=O(\omega)
, and therefore to leading order at zero frequency,
P_{z}(\omega;r)
evaluated at the boundary is the same as
P_{z}(\omega;r)
evaluated at the black hole horizon. We can therefore write the DC limit of equation (
44
) as

\sigma_{zz}(\omega=0)=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{4\pi^{2}}\lim_{\omega\to 0}P_{z}(\omega;r_{h})=\frac{N_{f}N_{c}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{r_{h}^{3}}{\sqrt{2\rho_{h}^{4}+b^{2}(\rho_{h}^{2}-r_{h}^{2})}},

(47)

Where the right-hand side is evaluated by substituting the ingoing boundary conditions for
A_{z}(\omega;r)
in equation (
24
) and the near-horizon behaviour of
R(r)
written in equation (
15
). Note that we must still use numerics to determine how

corresponds to what value of

4
Quasinormal modes

As seen in the previous section, at low or zero temperature the AC conductivities exhibit peaks and troughs as functions of frequency. These features, particularly the peak in
\sigma_{xx}
, might be expected to arise due to the presence of poles in the retarded Green’s functions in the complex frequency plane that are close to the real axis at low temperatures and for masses close to the phase transition. In this section we investigate this intuition, finding that indeed there are poles close to the real axis, and arguing that their presence is related to the first-order nature of the phase transition.

Poles in retarded Green’s functions are holographically dual to the frequencies of quasinormal modes
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, the complex frequency eigenvalues for which one can have perturbations of the gravitational system that are ingoing at the black hole horizon and normalisable at the conformal boundary. The quasinormal modes of the pseudoscalar
\phi(r)
in the flavour brane WSM, dual to poles of the two-point function of a pseudoscalar operator in the dual QFT, were studied in ref.
[
65
]
.

In our context, the quasinormal modes dual to poles in the retarded Green’s functions of the charge current—and therefore dual to poles in the conductivities—are the values of
\omega
for which the gauge field fluctuation equations (
22
) and (
23
) admit solutions satisfying ingoing boundary conditions (
24
) in the IR and that are normalisable in the UV,
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)=0
. The quasinormal modes in the
A_{\pm}
channels are dual to poles in
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{xy}
, since from equation (
26
) we see that when
A_{+}^{(0)}(\omega)=0
or
A_{-}^{(0)}(\omega)=0
, both
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{xy}
will diverge. Similarly, the quasinormal modes in the
A_{z}
channel are dual to poles in
\sigma_{zz}
, since when
A_{z}^{(0)}(\omega)
vanishes, then according to equation (
26
),
\sigma_{zz}
will diverge.

For
m=0
and non-zero temperature, the quasinormal modes may be obtained analytically, from the near-boundary asymptotics of the solution for
A_{\pm}=0
in equation (
32
). They are
[
66
,
59
]

\omega=2\pi T(n+1)(\pm 1-i),\qquad n=0,1,2,\cdots\;.

(49)

Notice that these modes all collapse to
\omega=0
in the limit
T\to 0
. This is because the fluctuation equations are independent of
b
when
m=0
, and thus when
m=T=0
there is no dimensionful scale that can set the spacing between the quasinormal modes.

When
m\neq 0
, there can be quasinormal modes even zero temperature. We determine them numerically using a pseudospectral method, see for example refs.
[
67
,
68
]
. In this method, one approximates the solutions to the fluctuation equations (after removing the singular behaviour in the IR via a field redefinition) as a sum of the first
K
Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind. The fluctuation equations are then evaluated on an
N
-point grid, thus approximating the differential equation as an
K\times K
matrix equation, from which the quasinormal modes may be extracted as the eigenvalues. Details of our implementation of this method may be found in appendix
B.2
. To check convergence of our numerics, we evaluate the quasinormal modes for two different values of
K
, keeping only the modes that agree following the algorithm in ref.
[
67
]
.

We will only compute the quasinormal modes for
T=0
, leaving their determination at non-zero temperature to future work. The results of our numerical computation of the quasinormal modes for
T=0
and different sample values of
m/b\sqrt{\lambda}
are plotted in figure
10
. The modes were computed using a pseudospectral method, with grid sizes of
K=90
and
K=100
. As anticipated, in both channels we see quasinormal modes that are relatively close to the real frequency axis for the largest mass plotted,
m=0.073\,b\sqrt{\lambda}
, close to the phase transition. Decreasing
m/b\sqrt{\lambda}
, the modes move away from the real frequency axis.

The presence of quasinormal modes close to the real frequency axis may be roughly connected to the structure of the phase transition. As discussed in section

m/b\sqrt{\lambda}
correspond to different values of the IR parameter

appearing in equation (

R_{0}
in equation (

As the critical embedding is approached from either side, we expect the spectra of the black hole and Minkowski embeddings to become the same. Since Minkowski embeddings have normal modes, with vanishing imaginary part, this implies that the imaginary parts of the quasinormal modes of the black hole embeddings should vanish as
\eta\to\infty
.

See refs.
[

for results for the near-critical limit for

Thus, the existence of modes with small imaginary parts for

close to the phase transition can perhaps be understood as being due to black hole embeddings close to the phase transition being “close to” the critical embedding in some sense.

5
Discussion

In this work we have computed the AC conductivities in the holographic model of a WSM based on flavour D7-branes in a D3-brane background. We found that at zero or low temperature and for masses close to the transition to an insulating phase, the longitudinal conductivity
\sigma_{xx}
for directions orthogonal to the applied axial field exhibits a peak at frequencies
\omega\sim 0.1\,b
. Computing the poles of this conductivity, we argued that this peak arises from a pole that comes close to the real frequency axis due to the phase transition. We also found that the Hall conductivity and the longitudinal conductivity exhibit troughs as functions of frequency in roughly similar regimes.

There are a number of possible directions for future research. For example, our results for the real parts of the longitudinal conductivities scale linearly with frequency at both small and large frequency. The authors of ref.
[
30
]
have argued that experimental results for WSM conductivities are better approximated by conductivities scaling at small frequencies as
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}\propto\omega^{\beta}
and
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{zz}\propto\omega^{2-\beta}
with exponent
\beta\approx 0.14
. It would be interesting to try to construct a top-down holographic model that reproduces this exponent.

There are also many other channels in which one could study the holographic Green’s functions and quasinormal modes of the flavour brane WSM model, for example, for example one can study fluctuations of the embedding scalar
R
to compute the two point function of the QFT’s scalar operator
\mathcal{O}_{m}
, and its poles. One motivation to perform a more thorough analysis of the fluctuations of our model is to check the linear stability of D7-brane embeddings dual to the WSM phase.

The fluctuations of
R
and of the gauge field could also be studied at non-zero momentum. Due to the anisotropy introduced by the applied axial gauge field
\vec{A}_{5}
, the Green’s functions will depend differently on momentum parallel and perpendicular to
\vec{A}_{5}
. This leads to a richer hydrodynamic description than for Lorentz-invariant systems (see e.g. ref.
[
26
]
), which could be compared to the holographic Green’s function calculations at small frequencies and momentum. This would allow the determination of various transport coefficients, beyond the conductivities considered in this work.

As mentioned in the introduction, WSMs with a boundary have current-carrying surface states. Ref.
[
27
]
demonstrated the existence of surface currents in a bottom-up holographic WSM model, by constructing an interface between the WSM and a topologically trivial semimetal. One could repeat their analysis in the flavour brane model. This would involve the construction of an interface between the WSM and insulating phases of the flavour brane model, and determining whether one finds a current localised to said interface. It may also be interesting to study fermionic spectral functions in the presence of such an interface.

Appendix B
Numerical details

In this appendix we summarise the numerical methods used to generate the results shown in the main text. For both the conductivities and the quasinormal modes we must first numerically solve the embedding equation (
11
) for
R(r)
, subject to the boundary conditions that
R(r\to\infty)=M
and that
R(r)
behaves as a black hole embedding in the deep IR. The solution for
R(r)
is then substituted into the fluctuation equations (
21
), which must in turn be solved numerically.

B.1
Conductivities

To obtain the conductivities we solve both the embedding equation (
11
) and the fluctuation equations (
21
) using a pseudospectral collocation method based on Chebyshev polynomials. See ref.
[
67
]
for more technical details. The implementation of this method is slightly different for zero versus non-zero temperature, so we describe each case separately.

B.1.1
Zero temperature

To implement the pseudospectral method we define a new, dimensionless radial coordinate
\mathfrak{r}=(r-b)/(r+b)
which takes values in the range
\mathfrak{r}\in[-1,1]
, with the Poincaré horizon at
\mathfrak{r}=-1
and boundary at
\mathfrak{r}=1
. We solve the embedding equation by approximating
R
as a sum of Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
T_{k}
,

R(\mathfrak{r})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}c_{k}T_{k}(\mathfrak{r})

(57)

for some large positive integer
K
. We fix the coefficients
c_{k}
by solving equation (
11
) (transformed to the
\mathfrak{r}
) coordinate on the interior points of the Gauss–Lobatto grid,

\mathfrak{r}_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{K-1}\right),\qquad k=1,2,\cdots,K-2,

(58)

supplemented with the boundary conditions
R(\mathfrak{r}=-1)=0
and
R(\mathfrak{r}=1)=M
for the desired value of
M
.

It turns out to be convenient to solve not for the coefficients
c_{k}
(
57
), but rather the values
R(\mathfrak{r}_{k})
evaluated on the Gauss–Lobatto grid, which contain equiavalent information to the
c_{k}
(they are related by a discrete cosine transform). Differentiation identities for the Chebyshev polynomials may then be used to express
R^{\prime}(\mathfrak{r}_{k})
and
R^{\prime\prime}(\mathfrak{r}_{k})
as linear combinations of
R
evaluated on the rest of the grid. This is conveniently implemented in Mathematica using the differentiation matrices from
NDSolve
`
FiniteDifferenceDerivative
with the option
DifferenceOrder

\to

"Pseudospectral"
.

Since the equation of motion for
R(\mathfrak{r})
is non-linear, we solve for the
R(\mathfrak{r}_{k})
using the Newton procedure described in refs.
[
67
,
77
]
. This requires an initial seed
R_{0}(\mathfrak{r}_{k})
for the values of
R
on the grid points. We find that setting
R_{0}(\mathfrak{r}_{k})=0
works well.

Having obtained a numerical solution for
R(\mathfrak{r})
, we would then like to substitute this solution into the equations of motion for the gauge field fluctuations
A_{n}(\omega;r)
, which we would then solve by the same pseudospectral method to determine the near-boundary conditions
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, which in turn determine the conductivities through equation (
26
). However, we face two complications.

The first complication is that the ingoing boundary conditions (
24
) obeyed by the fluctuations are non-analytic at the Poincaré horizon
\mathfrak{r}=-1
, and therefore
A_{n}(\omega;r)
cannot be well approximated by a sum of Chebyshev polynomials. To solve this we define new fields

Z_{n}(\omega;r)=\left(1+\frac{b^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)^{-1}e^{-i\omega/r}A_{n}(\omega;r),

(59)

in terms of which the ingoing boundary conditions become
Z_{n}(\omega;0)=0
. The
Z_{n}
are therefore much more amenable to pseudospectral treatment.

The second complication comes from the determination of
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
. In the
\mathfrak{r}
coordinate, the near-boundary expansion of the rescaled fluctuation is

Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})=A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)\left[1+\frac{i\omega}{2b}(\mathfrak{r}-1)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{8b^{2}}(1-\mathfrak{r})^{2}\log\left(\frac{2b}{|\omega|(1-\mathfrak{r})}\right)\right]\\
+\frac{(1-\mathfrak{r})^{2}}{8}\left[\frac{2A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)}{b^{2}}-A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)\left(2+\frac{2i\omega}{b}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)\right]+O\left((1-\mathfrak{r})^{3}\log(1-\mathfrak{r})\right).

(60)

There is a logarithmic term that is more important as
\mathfrak{r}\to 1
than the term containing
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, which makes it difficult to reliably determine
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
from an approximation of
Z_{n}(\omega;r)
in terms of polynomials. One possible solution would be to explicitly subtract the logarithmic term from
Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})
, however we would still face the problem that
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
appears at a subleading order in the near-boundary expansion, so we would need to work at rather large precision to reliably extract
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)
, which would slow down numerics.

A more elegant solution is to define

p_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})=(1+\mathfrak{r})\left[\partial_{\mathfrak{r}}-\frac{i\omega}{2b}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{4b^{2}}(1-\mathfrak{r})\log(1-\mathfrak{r})-\frac{3\omega^{2}}{8b^{2}}(1-\mathfrak{r})^{2}\log(1-\mathfrak{r})\right]Z_{n}(\omega;r).

(61)

At leading order near the boundary we find

p_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})=(\mathfrak{r}-1)\frac{A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)}{b^{2}}-(1-\mathfrak{r})A_{n}^{(0)}\left[1+\frac{i\omega}{b}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{4b^{2}}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{2b^{2}}\log\left(\frac{|\omega|}{2b}\right)\right]+O((1-\mathfrak{r})^{2}).

(62)

We recast the second-order equation from
Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})
as a pair of coupled first-order equations for
(Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r}),p_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r}))
, which we then solve using the pseudospectral method, subject to the boundary condition
Z_{n}(\omega;1)=1
, corresponding to the choice
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)=1
,
10
10

10

We are free to choose any non-zero value of
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega;r)
since the fluctuation equations are linear, so given a solution with some non-zero
A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega;r)

A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)=1
. The conductivities are proportional to
A_{n}^{(2)}(\omega)/A_{n}^{(0)}(\omega)
so are invariant under this rescaling.
and
Z_{n}(\omega;-1)=0
. Differentiating the resulting solution for

\mathfrak{r}=1

. Following this procedure for each

\sigma_{zz}

B.1.2
Non-zero temperature

At non-zero temperature we use the same pseudospectral method to solve for the embedding and fluctuations. Compared to the zero temperature case, the range of the radial coordinate
r
and the ingoing boundary conditions obeyed by the fluctuations are both different, so we will need to perform different variable redefinitions.

At a given temperature
T
, corresponding to given horizon radius
\rho_{h}
, black hole embeddings are distinguished by
r_{h}
, the value of
r
at which they meet the horizon. Thus the radial coordinate for these embeddings takes values in the range
r\in[r_{h},\infty)
. We find it convenient to replace
r
with
\rho=\sqrt{r^{2}+R^{2}}
, which takes values in a range that is the same for all embeddings at a given temperature,
\rho\in[\rho_{h},\infty)
. We then map
\rho
to the finite interval
\mathfrak{r}\in[-1,1]
by defining

\mathfrak{r}=1-\frac{2\rho_{h}}{\rho},

(63)

where
\mathfrak{r}=-1
corresponds to the horizon, while
\mathfrak{r}=1
again corresponds to the conformal boundary. We also find it convenient to define a dimensionless variable
u(\mathfrak{r})=(1+\mathfrak{r})R(\mathfrak{r})/2b
, such that the IR boundary condition on becomes
u(\mathfrak{r}=-1)=0
.

In order to turn the ingoing boundary condition (
24
) into regularity boundary conditions, we define new fluctuation variables

Z_{n}(\omega;\rho)=\left(\frac{2\rho-i\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1+\rho^{4}/\rho_{h}^{4}}}{2\rho+i\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1+\rho^{4}/\rho_{h}^{4}}}\right)^{\frac{(1-i)\omega}{4\pi T}}\exp\left[\frac{i\omega}{2\pi T}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\rho/\rho_{h}}{\sqrt{1+\rho^{4}/\rho_{h}^{4}}}\right)\right]A_{n}(\omega;\rho).

(64)

This is not the simplest possible rescaling that would make

regular at the horizon. It contains additional factors that essentially amount to the exchanging the time coordinate

in equation (

Finally, in order to reliably extract

\Pi_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})=\frac{1+\mathfrak{r}}{2}\left[\partial_{\mathfrak{r}}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2\pi^{2}T^{2}}(1-\mathfrak{r})\log(1-\mathfrak{r})\right]Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r}),

B.2
Quasinormal modes

To compute the zero-temperature quasinormal modes using the pseudospectral method, we employ the same radial coordinate
\mathfrak{r}
as in section
B.1.1
. The quasinormal mode boundary conditions are that
A_{n}(\omega;r)
is ingoing at
r=0
(corresponding to
\mathfrak{r}=-1
) and
A_{n}^{(0)}=0
. In terms of the dependent variable
Z_{n}
defined in section
B.1.1
, these boundary conditions are
Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r}=\pm 1)=0
.

Near
\mathfrak{r}=-1
, solutions obeying ingoing boundary conditions behave as
Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})\propto(1+\mathfrak{r})^{3/2}
. It turns out to improve the convergence of the pseudospectral quasinormal mode method if we redefine the dependent variable to remove this non-analytic behaviour. Specifically, we define

\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})=\frac{Z_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})}{(1+\mathfrak{r})^{3/2}},

(67)

such that ingoing boundary conditions correspond to
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;r)
remaining finite in the limit
\mathfrak{r}\to-1
.

To determine the quasinormal modes, similar to section
B.1.1
we approximate
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;r)
as a sum of the first
K
Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, which we then use to evaluate the equation of motion for
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})
on the interior points of the
K
-point Gauss–Lobatto grid. We supplement this with the normalisibility condition
\mathcal{Z}(\omega;1)=0
, and the
\mathfrak{r}\to-1
limit of the equation of motion, which imposes that

16i\omega\partial_{\mathfrak{r}}\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r})|_{\mathfrak{r}=-1}=3(4i\omega-b)\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;-1).

(68)

This procedure approximates the equation of motion for
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
as a matrix equation of the forms

\left(P+\omega^{2}Q+\omega R\right)\vec{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}=0,

(69)

where the components of
\vec{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}
are
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\omega;\mathfrak{r}_{i})
, i.e. the dependent variable evaluated on the Gauss–Lobatto grid, while
P
,
Q
, and
R
are
K\times K
matrices. The quasinormal modes are the generalised eigenvalues
\omega
of equation (
69
), which we determine using
Eigenvalues
in
Mathematica
.
