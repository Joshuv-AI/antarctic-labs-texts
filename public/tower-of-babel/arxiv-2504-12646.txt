Replication Packages in Software Engineering Secondary Studies: A Systematic Mapping Journal: Information and Software Technology
Aleksi Huotala, Miikka Kuutila, Mika Mäntylä

Abstract
Context: Systematic reviews (SRs) summarize state-of-the-art evidence in science, including software engineering (SE). Objective: Our objective is to evaluate how SRs report replication packages and to provide a comprehensive list of these packages. Method: We examined 528 secondary studies published between 2013 and 2023 to analyze the availability and reporting of replication packages. Results: Our findings indicate that only 25.4% of the reviewed studies include replication packages. Encouragingly, the situation is gradually improving, as our regression analysis shows significant increase in the availability of replication packages over time. However, in 2023, just 50.6% of secondary studies provided a replication package while an even lower percentage, 29.1% had used a permanent repository with a digital object identifier (DOI) for storage. Conclusion: To enhance transparency and reproducibility in SE research, we advocate for the mandatory publication of replication packages in secondary studies.

Introduction

Systematic reviews (SRs) summarize state-of-the-art evidence in software engineering (SE). We argue that all SRs should include a replication package, as the true transparency of SRs is impossible without it. Transparency in systematic reviews is crucial for multiple reasons:

Reason 1: Replicability (Reproducibility or Repeatability).
Replication is a hallmark of science and an essential component of research integrity. Other researchers must replicate the review process to confirm its reliability and accuracy. These issues are promoted in open science

Reason 2: Credibility, Trust, and Bias Reduction.
Transparent SRs build trust in the integrity of the evidence. When the method, data, and process are fully available, readers can trust that the conclusions are based on a thorough and unbiased synthesis of the evidence.

Reason 3: Updates.
Replication packages are essential for updating SRs. Updating SRs ensures that the evidence remains current, preventing the research from becoming outdated or potentially misleading

Search process

We selected publications from 2013 to 2023, as key data repositories such as Zenodo and Figshare were launched in 2013 and 2011, respectively. 2.2
Study selection and quality assessment

We applied the following inclusion criteria:

1.

Study published between 2013–2023

2.

Study is a secondary study

3.

Study is related to SE

Title–abstract screening eliminated studies that were not secondary studies or related to SE. To see if the study contains a replication package, we full-text screened each research paper.

Research papers from “ACM Computing Surveys" and “Computer Science Review" journals were manually reviewed by the authors to select SE-related studies, as the two journals are not strictly limited to SE-related publications. After applying the inclusion criteria, 528 secondary studies remained.

2.3
Data extraction

First, we identified dedicated sections indicating the availability of replication packages. If not found, we also conducted a text search, which consisted of various words and sentences related to external data, e.g. “Replication", “Data is available", “External" and “Zenodo", “Figshare", and “http:" (for checking any outbound links).

RQ1: What proportion of secondary studies include a replication package and directly link to the data?

RQ1 gives an overview of the availability of replication packages in SE-related SRs and how the package is accessible. Ideally, every secondary study would include a replication package accessible through a direct link.
Results: In our dataset, 134 of the 528 (25.4%) reviewed secondary studies included a replication package. 130 out of the 134 (97.1%) secondary studies that included a replication package also contained a direct link to the data.

RQ2: Where are the replication packages stored?

RQ2 addresses the storage location for replication packages. Replication packages should be located in permanent storage with a digital object identifier (DOI). Links to personal or institutional web pages may stop working in a few years.
The use of a permanent repository (Zenodo, Figshare, or Mendeley Data) was observed to be present in less than half of the studies that included a replication package. To be precise, this was 56 out of 134 studies, amounting to 41.8% of secondary studies with a replication package while being 10.8% out of all papers (56 of 528).

RQ3: How is the availability of data stated?

RQ3 focuses on how the availability of replication packages is reported in research papers.
Out of the studies that included a replication package, 42 of 134 (31.3%) included a dedicated section that stated the availability of data or a replication package.

RQ4: How does publication year and the publication forum impact the availability of replication packages?

RQ4 answers how the publication forum and year impact the availability of replication packages.

Figure
1
shows a binary logistic regression model, which models whether a replication package is available, based on the publication year and journal.
For the modeling, we filtered out journals that had less than 10 publications.
For the reference category, which is the journal other journals are compared to, we chose “IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering", because of its esteemed reputation in the field of SE.

Figure
1
shows that the publication year, which was scaled and modeled as an ordered factor for more easily interpretable effect size measure, is a significant predictor of whether a paper includes a replication package. The models shows that for every 3 years (one standard deviation), the odds of the publication having a replication package increase by a factor of 2.17.

For publication venues, all coefficients are compared to the reference case, “IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering". Papers published in journals “Computer Science Review" and “Software: Practice and Experience" are far less likely to include a replication package, with the odds being approximately 7% respectively, compared to the reference journal.

4
Limitations

We excluded conference proceedings for two main reasons. First, most high-quality secondary studies are published in journals. Second, conference proceedings often lack ISSN numbers, and their quality can vary from year to year, with some years including colocated workshop papers and others not. While this decision may have caused us to miss some secondary studies, it is unlikely to have affected our high-level results.

We chose not to use multiple academic databases, as Scopus already includes metadata from all relevant databases. However, if our search had been focused on full-text content, using multiple academic databases would have been mandatory.

Only secondary studies published between 2013–2023 were included.
By narrowing down the publication year, we wanted to make sure that permanent repository providers, such as Zenodo and Figshare, are fully included.

5
Conclusion and Future work

Our regression model indicates that secondary studies in software engineering are increasingly adopting open data practices. In 2023, approximately 50.6% of secondary studies included a replication package, a figure we hope will approach 100% in the future. Only 41.8% of studies with replication packages used permanent repositories with DOIs, which represents another area for improvement. Fortunately, we are also seeing progress in this area, with the overall average for permanent repositories being just 10.8% in all SRs, while in 2023 it had already risen to 29.1%.

The importance of permanent repositories are highlighted, as links to non-permanent repositories can become inaccessible. In our study, even among papers published in 2023, 2 out of 19 links to non-permanent repositories were already dead.

Regarding 2023, the best adoption we saw in the papers’ structure itself; 58.2% of all papers contained a dedicated section stating the availability of data in 2023. Yet, we found that some papers with their "Data Availability" section simply stated that “no data was used" or something to a similar effect. Some papers mentioned that data is only “available upon request". Both of these approaches are alarming because secondary studies rely only on published academic studies, and should not encounter confidentiality limitations.

In the future, dedicated guidelines about publishing replication packages would increase the availability of data. Clearly stating how and where the data is shared would guide the readers in finding the data. Furthermore, the guidelines should standardize the replication package contents.

Currently, our recommendation to all entities publishing secondary studies is to have data availability section as it makes locating the replication package easier. Additionally, replication packages should be stored in a permanent repository with DOI’s to ensure long term accessibility.
