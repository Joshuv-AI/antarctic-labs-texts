Host cell entry and neutralisation sensitivity of H3N2 influenza A virus subclade K
Authors: Nicole Moor, Nianzhen Chen, Georg M. N. Behrens, Lu Zhang, Anna-Sophie Moldenhauer, Michael Winkler, Christine Happle, Sandra Steffens, Metodi V. Stankov, Karsten Lürken, Stefan Pöhlmann, Markus Hoffmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In 2025, a mutated H3N2 lineage, subclade K, emerged, showing high activity in many regions. Mutations in the hemagglutinin (HA) may affect cell entry and antibody-mediated neutralisation. Using pseudovirus particles, we show that subclade K-HA drives augmented entry into certain cell lines and displays significant antibody evasion. Both phenotypes were linked to mutation A186D. Influenza vaccination significantly boosted H3N2 subclade K neutralisation, suggesting that current vaccines may provide considerable protection.

Body

Seasonal H1N1 and H3N2 influenza A viruses (IAV) cause epidemics, leading to ~1 billion infections and >250,000 deaths annually1,2. Vaccination provides protection against severe disease, but rapid viral mutation drives antigenic drift in hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), requiring frequent vaccine updates. Historically, HA has been considered the exclusive target for antibody-mediated neutralisation of influenza viruses, but NA-specific antibodies can also prevent virus spread. While HA-specific antibodies, especially those targeting the head domain, exert classical neutralising activity, i.e., blocking of receptor engagement and subsequent host cell entry, the antiviral activity of NA-specific antibodies stems from distinct mechanisms. For instance, NA-targeting antibodies can interfere with NA’s enzymatic activity, thereby preventing sialic acid cleavage, which is required for mucus penetration by influenza viruses in the respiratory tract3 and for egress of progeny virion from infected cells4. Moreover, NA-specific antibodies may limit virus spread by cross-linking of viral particles5 and targeting infected cells for clearance by immune effector cells via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity6. Finally, a protective role of NA-specific antibodies in vivo has already been proven using animal models7,8 and epidemiological evidence links NA-specific antibodies to reduced virus spread and lower disease severity in humans9.

In 2025, a mutated H3N2 lineage, subclade K, emerged (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2), which showed high activity in many regions10, extended influenza seasons in Oceania11 and dominated the start of the influenza season in England12. The mutations in the HA head domain may affect cell entry and antibody-mediated neutralisation, potentially reducing vaccine effectiveness and highlighting the need for functional assessment. For our analyses, we used pseudovirus particles (pp), a well-established surrogate system for studying IAV entry and its inhibition13–19. HA and NA from three H3N2 clade 3C.2a1b.2a.2a.3a.1 strains were examined: two circulating in January (Early-2025) and October 2025 (Late-2025, subclade K), and the 2025 egg-based vaccine strain (A/Croatia/10136RV/2023, VAC-2025). These proteins were incorporated into vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV)-based pseudotype particles (Fig. 1C), yielding H3N2 Early-2025pp, VAC-2025pp, and Late-2025pp. Further, particles bearing VSV-G (VSV-Gpp) or Machupo virus GPC (MACV-GPCpp) served as controls. Pseudovirus titres were determined by quantitative PCR and found to be comparable for H3N2 Early-2025pp, VAC-2025pp, and Late-2025pp (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Cell line tropism was examined across nine human cell lines and a dog kidney cell line (MDCK) commonly used in influenza research (Fig. 1D). VSV-Gpp and MACV-GPCpp entered all cell lines efficiently. In contrast, entry mediated by H3N2 HA was cell line- and strain-dependent. Early-2025pp showed generally higher entry efficiency than VAC-2025pp and Late-2025pp. Differences between VAC-2025pp and Late-2025pp were observed for 293T (kidney-derived), NCI-H1299 (lung-derived), and RPMI-2650 (nasal epithelium-derived) cells, suggesting differences in receptor binding.

Influenza virus HA proteins are primed for membrane fusion by host cell proteases. We next compared HA cleavage by type-II transmembrane serine proteases and the proprotein-convertase furin20 to test whether H3N2 subclade K-specific HA mutations alter the spectrum of host cell proteases exploited for HA cleavage. However, no notable differences were observed for the different H3N2 strains (Supplementary Fig. S4A).

We further compared the sensitivity of particles bearing HA and NA proteins of the different H3N2 strains with respect to entry blockade by interferon-induced transmembrane (IFITM) proteins21. IFITM proteins, especially IFITM3, are determinants of host resistance against influenza virus infection22 and differences in IFITM-sensitivity among H3N2 strains may indicate differences in their pathogenic potential. As expected, MACV-GPCpp, which are insensitive to IFITM-mediated entry blockade21, were not inhibited by IFITM-expression. In contrast, cell entry of particles bearing HA and NA proteins of the different H3N2 strains was efficiently blocked and blockade could be partially or fully alleviated by amphotericin B, in agreement with a previous study showing that amphotericin B can rescue influenza viruses from IFITM-mediated entry inhibition23. However, no noticeable differences in IFITM-sensitivity among H3N2 strains were observed (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In addition, we investigated whether the HA proteins of the different H3N2 strains differ with respect to their pH optimum for driving membrane fusion, but found no differences (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

Finally, we assessed neutralisation of H3N2 subclade K in two cohorts directly before and at least 14 days after seasonal vaccination in November 2025 with Flucelvax 2025/2026 (cell culture-based), Influvac 2025/2026 (egg-based), or Efluelda 2025/2026 (egg-based) (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 and Supplementary Figs. S5–S8): healthcare professionals and dialysis patients. Before vaccination, both cohorts displayed neutralising activity against all strains, but neutralisation of Late-2025pp was ~3- to 4.5-fold lower than for Early-2025pp or the VAC-2025pp. Vaccination increased neutralising titres in both cohorts, yet activity against Late-2025 remained ~3.5- to 4.5-fold lower than against VAC-2025pp. Mutational analysis showed that mutation A186D in the HA of VAC-2025pp strongly enhanced cell entry (Fig. 3A, B) and was the main cause of reduced neutralisation, while mutations N158D and K189R contributed to neutralisation escape (Fig. 3C). Collectively, we found that the emerging H3N2 subclade K displays enhanced entry into some cell lines and significant antibody evasion. The latter finding is supported by studies that observed a reduction in neutralisation of authentic H3N2 subclade K viruses, using ferret and human sera12,24–28. In contrast, one study with matched human sera obtained before and within 6 weeks after vaccination only observed a minor difference between neutralisation of vaccine-matched H3N2 and H3N2 subclade K29. These findings suggest that there are differences in neutralisation escape of H3N2 subclade K with respect to human cohorts with different immunisation backgrounds. Nevertheless, our pseudovirus-based data and published live-virus data demonstrate that vaccination still boosters neutralising activity against this virus28–30, suggesting that current vaccines may provide considerable protection against H3N2 subclade K. Indeed, recent studies reported a vaccine effectiveness that is in the range of that observed for previous seasons12,24,27,31,32, underlining that vaccination should provide appreciable protection against H3N2 subclade K in the upcoming influenza season in the southern hemisphere. Altogether, our data obtained with pseudovirus particles correlate well with data obtained with authentic virus, supporting the use of pseudoviruses in assessing influenza A virus neutralisation and enabling the rapid analysis of the biological impact of emerging HA mutations.

Altered cell entry efficiency and increased antibody evasion of H3N2 were linked to mutation A186D in HA. The finding was not unexpected since amino acid 186 is located adjacent to the receptor-binding pocket and within the immunodominant antigenic site B and is known to be a determinant of receptor choice and antibody evasion. Introduction of the reverse mutation (D186A) into the HA proteins of H3N2 isolates circulating in early and late 2025 strongly reduced HA-driven cell entry, indicating that the impact of D186/N186 is context-dependent. Nevertheless, future studies on HA-mediated receptor binding and sialic acid distribution on target cells displaying differential susceptibility to particles bearing HA and NA proteins of the different H3N2 strains are warranted.

Our study has limitations. First, host cell entry and neutralisation of H3N2 influenza A viruses were analysed using immortalised cell lines and pseudovirus particles. Therefore, our data await confirmation with authentic influenza A virus and primary cell cultures. Second, additional research is needed to assess whether the observed differences in cell entry affect H3N2 spread within the infected host and disease severity. Third, the limited sample number of our two vaccination cohorts does not allow for the investigation of the impact of biological factors (e.g., age and biological sex) on neutralisation. Fourth, since all human post-vaccination plasma samples were collected 14–28 days after vaccination, changes in neutralisation efficiency after extended time periods post vaccination could not be studied. Fifth, the H3N2 infection status of the two cohorts is unknown, which is why additional studies are warranted to determine the impact of previous H3N2 infections on neutralisation efficiency of H3N2 subclade K. Finally, although a generalisation of our observations is tempting, additional neutralisation tests involving reference sera/plasma with defined immune background are needed considering the potentially complicated immune background of human plasma samples.

Calu-3 (human lung, male; ATCC catalogue no. HTB-55, RRID:CVCL_0609; kindly provided by Stephan Ludwig) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, pen/strep, 1% non-essential amino acid solution and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. A549 cells (human lung, male; ATCC catalogue no. CRM-CCL-185, RRID:CVCL_0023; kindly provided by Georg Herrler) were cultured in F-12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS, pen/strep, 1% non-essential amino acid solution and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. NCI-H520 (human lung, male; ATCC catalogue no. HTB-182, RRID:CVCL_1566; kindly provided by Kamila Kitowska), NCI-H1299 (human lung, male; ATCC catalogue no. CRL-5803, RRID:CVCL_0060; kindly provided by Christian Drosten), and BEAS-2B cells (human lung/bronchus, male; ATCC catalogue no. CRL-3588, RRID:CVCL_0168) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium (PAN-Biotech) supplemented with 10% FBS, pen/strep, 1% non-essential amino acid solution and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. HEK-293T cells stably expressing IFITM1, IFITM2, IFITM3, or CAT (chloramphenicol acetyltransferase) were generated by retroviral transduction and selection with puromycin. The cell lines used in this study were validated by STR analysis, amplification and sequencing of a cytochrome c oxidase gene fragment, microscopic examination, and/or their specific growth characteristics. Moreover, all cell lines were regularly screened for the absence of contamination by mycoplasma. For the transfection of HEK-293T cells, the calcium phosphate precipitation method was used. All cell lines were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Expression plasmids

Expression plasmids for VSV-G33, MACV-GPC (25256397), H3N2 Early-2025-HA, H3N2 Early-2025-HA-V5, H3N2 Early-2025-NA, H3N2 Early-2025-NA-FLAG (all codon-optimised: A/New York City/PV101188/2024; clade 3C.2a1b.2a.2a.3a.1; GISAID ID: EPI_ISL_19091264; shared by CEIRS Data Processing and Coordinating Center, Center for Research on Influenza Pathogenesis)34, TMPRSS235, and beta-galactosidase alpha- and omega-fragments36 have been described before. For the construction of expression plasmids for TMPRSS11A, TMPRSS11D, TMPRSS11E, TMPRSS13, hepsin and furin, the respective protease coding were amplified from existing plasmids37–39 or cDNA that was generated from isolated RNA of A549 cells (furin), and cloned into the pQCXIP vector. In addition, expression plasmids for codon-optimised (for codon-usage in human cells) HA and NA proteins of H3N2 VAC-2025 (X-425A, A/Croatia/10136RV/2023; clade 3C.2a1b.2a.2a.3a.1; EPI_ISL_19521051; Originating lab: Croatian Institute of Public Health; Submitting lab: Crick Worldwide Influenza Centre), and H3N2 Late-2025 (A/California/LACPHL-INF02035/2025, clade 3C.2a1b.2a.2a.3a.1; subclade K; EPI_ISL_20241922; Originating and Submitting lab: Los Angeles County Public Health Laboratories, Lab Microbial Pathogen Submission Group) were generated by Gibson assembly. For this, for each HA and NA sequence two overlapping DNA strings (obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific, sequences available upon request) were mixed with BamHI/XbaI-digested pCG1 plasmid (a kind gift of Roberto Cattaneo, Mayo Clinic College of Medicine, Rochester, MN, USA) and GeneArt™ Gibson Assembly HiFi Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples were incubated for 45 min at 50 °C. Next, mixtures were transformed into one-shot OmniMAX 2 T1 competent Escherichia coli bacteria (Thermo Fisher Scientific), plated onto LB agar plates containing 50 µg/ml of ampicillin, and incubated at 37 °C. The next day, bacterial colonies were screened for the presence of the desired insert by colony PCR, and positive clones were expanded for plasmid isolation. Mutations in HA protein coding sequences were integrated by overlap-extension PCR using overlapping primers harbouring the desired mutations. For the detection of HA and NA proteins in an immunoblot, expression plasmids coding for HA proteins harbouring a C-terminal V5-epitope tag (sequence: GKPIPNPLLGLDST) as well as NA proteins harbouring a C-terminal FLAG-epitope tag (sequence: DYKDDDDK) were generated by PCR using a reverse primer containing the respective epitope-coding sequence. Details on the cloning procedure and primer sequences are available upon request. The integrity of all sequences was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Microsynth SeqLab).

Pseudovirus particle production, cell entry studies and neutralisation assay

We employed a pseudovirus particle system that is based on a replication-restricted vesicular stomatitis virus. First, HEK-293T cells were transfected to express the respective HA and NA proteins (plasmid ratio = 2:1), MACV-GPC, VSV-G, or an empty pCG1 plasmid (to generate bald particles bearing no viral glycoprotein, negative control). For experiments addressing HA cleavage and activation by human proteases, HA/NA proteins were coexpressed with the respective proteases (plasmid ratio = 2:1:0.25). At 24 h posttransfection, VSV-G-transcomplemented VSV*ΔG(FLuc) (kindly provided by Gert Zimmer) was inoculated onto the cells at a multiplicity of infection of 3. Following 1 h of incubation, the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Next, cells received fresh culture medium supplemented with anti-VSV-G antibody (culture supernatant from I1-hybridoma cells; ATCC no. CRL-2700). Of note, cells transfected with VSV-G expression vector received medium without antibody. After an additional incubation period of 16–18 h, culture supernatants containing pseudovirus particles were centrifuged to remove cellular debris (4000 × g, 10 min), aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further use. Before use, particles bearing H3N2-HA + NA were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with bovine trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, 12.5 µg/ml final concentration) to cleave and activate the HA protein. Subsequently, excessive trypsin activity was blocked by adding soybean trypsin inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, 20 µg/ml final concentration). For cell entry studies, target cells were seeded into 96-well plates and grown to 50–75% confluence overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Next, identical volumes of pseudovirus particles, which were previously checked for comparable titres by quantitative PCR (see below), were added onto target cells and incubated for 16–18 h, before the culture medium was aspirated and PBS containing 0.5% Tergitol™ 15-S-9 (Carl Roth) was added. Following an incubation time of 30 min for complete cell lysis, lysates were transferred into white 96-well plates, mixed with firefly luciferase substrate (Beetle-Juice, PJK) and luminescence was measured with a Hidex Sense plate luminometer (Hidex). Of note, for experiments assessing the ability of amphotericin B to counteract the antiviral activity of IFITM-2 and -3 proteins, HEK-293T cells stably expressing IFITM1, IFITM2, IFITM3, or CAT were incubated for 1 h in the presence of 2.5 µM amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in culture medium, before pseudovirus particles were added. Neutralisation assays were performed using Caco-2 cells grown in 96-well plates as target cells. First, trypsin-activated (12.5 µg/ml, 30 min, 37 °C) pseudovirus particles harbouring H3N2-HA + NA were pre-incubated (30 min at 37 °C) with four-fold serial dilutions of heat-inactivated (56 °C, 30 min) human plasma (starting dilution = 1:12.5), before the mixtures were added to the cells. At 16–18 h postinoculation, luminescence was determined as detailed above and neutralisation efficiency was calculated based on the relative inhibition of pseudovirus entry. For this, entry of particles incubated in the absence of plasma was set as 0% inhibition. Next, a non-linear regression model was used for calculation of the plasma dilutions causing half-maximal inhibition (neutralising titre 50, NT50) and geometric mean titres for individual groups were determined. Of note, serum/plasma samples that yielded an NT50 value lower than 12.5 were considered negative and were assigned an NT50 value of 6.25 (50% of the lowest plasma dilution tested), while samples yielding NT50 values higher than 12,800 were assigned an NT50 value of 12,800 (4-times the highest plasma dilution tested).

Sequence analysis and protein modelling

Information on H3N2 strains was obtained from the GISAID (Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data) EpiFluTM database (https://gisaid.org/; accessed on 13.11.2025). 33 The HA protein structure (monomer) was generated by modelling the respective protein sequence of the vaccine strain A/Croatia/10136RV/2023 (VAC-2025) on the crystal structure PDB:2yp9.140. The protein model was further edited using the UCSF Chimera software version 1.17.341.

Determination of pseudovirus titres by quantitative PCR

Viral RNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturers protocol. Next, cDNA synthesis was done using the SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For this, 5 µl of RNA were mixed with 1 µl of random hexamers, 1 µl of 10 mM dNTP mix, and 6 µl of water. This mixture was incubated for 5 min at 65 °C and then placed on ice for 2 min. Next, 4 µl of 5x First-Strand buffer, 1 µl of 0.1 M DTT, 1 µl of RNaseOUTTM, and 1 µl of SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase (200 units/μl) were added to each tube, which were then placed in a thermocycler (Biometra) and incubated for 5 min at 25 °C, followed by 30 min at 50 °C, and 15 min at 70 °C. Finally, 1 µl of RNase H was added and samples were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C in order to remove residual RNA. Subsequently, quantitative PCR was conducted on a Rotorgene Q device (Qiagen) using the QuantiTect® SYBR® Green PCR kit. All samples were analysed in technical quadruplicates. Further, VSV-N plasmid standards containing 100–109 copies/µl were included to calculate copy numbers from cycle threshold values, while water instead of cDNA or plasmid standard was included as a negative control. The total reaction volume was 15 µl and consisted of 1 µl cDNA (or plasmid standard or water), 7.5 µl of x QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 1.5 µl of forward primer (VSV-N F, 5’-ATGACAAATGGTTGCCTTTGTATCTACTT-3’42), 1.5 µl of reverse primer (VSV-N R, 5’- ACGACCTTCTGGCACAAGAGGT-3’42), and 3.5 µl of water. Cycling conditions were as follows: (1) activation of the HotStarTaq DNA Polymerase by incubation at 95 °C for 15 min, (2) 50 cycles consisting of 15 s at 94 °C for denaturation, 30 s at 57 °C for primer annealing, and 30 s at 72 °C for primer extension. Finally, a melting curve analysis was initiated for temperatures spanning 60–90 °C (ramp = 1 °C/step).

Sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblot

For detection of HA/NA incorporation into pseudovirus particles by immunoblot, 1 ml of the respective particles bearing HA proteins with a C-terminal V5-epitope tag (HA-V5) and NA proteins with a C-terminal FLAG-epitope tag (NA-FLAG) was loaded on a sucrose cushion (20% w/v, 50 µl) and centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 90 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was removed except for the sucrose cushion, and the remaining volume was mixed with 2x SDS sample buffer (0.03 M Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 10% beta-mercaptoethanol, 0.1% bromophenol blue, 1 mM EDTA) and heated to 96 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, pseudovirus lysates were loaded on polyacrylamide gels, before proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Hartenstein). Next, the membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 and 5 g/l skim milk powder) for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated with primary antibody diluted in PBS/0.1% Tween-20 overnight at 4 °C. For this, membranes were probed either with anti-V5 (V5-tag Polyclonal antibody, rabbit, 1:2000, Proteintech, Cat: 14440-1-AP) or anti-FLAG (Monoclonal ANTI-FLAG® M2, mouse, 1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat: F1804) antibodies for the detection of HA-V5 or NA-FLAG, respectively. In addition, VSV-M (vesicular stomatitis virus matrix protein, loading control) was detected using an anti-VSV-M antibody (anti-VSV-M [23H12] antibody, mouse, 1:2000, Kerafast, Cat: EB0011). After incubation with the primary antibody, membranes were washed three times for 10 min at room temperature with PBS/0.1% Tween-20 and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with PBS/0.1% Tween-20 containing the secondary antibody, goat IgG anti-rabbit IgG (H + L)-HRPO (1:5000, Dianova, Cat: 111-035-003) or goat IgG anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-HRPO (1:5000, Dianova, Cat: 115-035-045). Finally, membranes were washed three times with PBS/0.1% Tween-20 for 10 min at room temperature, before being developed using a chemiluminescence substrate (Westar Antares, Cyanagen). For imaging, the Azure600 imaging system equipped with the AzureSpot Pro software (Azure Biosystems) was used.

Cell-cell fusion assay

The cell-cell fusion assay employed is based on a modified protocol established for analysis of membrane fusion driven by the spike protein of SARS-CoV-236. In brief, effector HEK-293T cells were transfected with an expression plasmid for the beta-galactosidase alpha-fragment and either an HA expression plasmid alone or in combination with the matching NA expression plasmid. In addition, target HEK-293T cells were transfected with an expression plasmid for the beta-galactosidase omega-fragment. After 18 h of incubation, effector and target cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in culture medium, and mixed in U-bottom 96-well plates before being co-cultured for 24 h. Next, cells were pelleted by centrifugation (600 × g, 5 min, room temperature) and the supernatant was carefully removed, before the cells were resuspended in PBS. After an additional centrifugation step, the cell pellets were resuspended in FBS-free medium containing bovine trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, 5 µg/ml) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Thereafter, the cells were washed with FBS-containing culture medium, before cell pellets were resuspended in PBS adjusted to different pH values (spanning pH 4.5 to 6.25) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Next, the cells were washed twice with PBS before the cell pellets were resuspended in fresh culture medium. Following an incubation period of 24 h, HA-driven membrane fusion was quantified. For this, beta-galactosidase substrate (Gal-Screen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the culture medium at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v) and incubated for 90 min in the dark at room temperature, before luminescence was recorded using a Hidex Sense plate luminometer (Hidex).

Human plasma samples

Human plasma samples were obtained from two cohorts before and at least 14 days after seasonal influenza vaccination in November 2025. Both belong to the COVID-19 Contact (CoCo) Study (German Clinical Trial Registry, DRKS00021152), a prospective observational study monitoring immune responses in healthcare professionals (HCP) and patients. Cohort 1: HCP (n = 59) were vaccinated by the occupational health service of Hannover Medical School. Age-stratified trivalent vaccines were administered: Fluclvax 2025/2026 or Influvac 2025/2026 for individuals below 60 years, and Efluelda 2025/2026 for those aged 60 years and above. Of the 59 participants, 17 provided paired pre- and post-vaccination samples, 24 provided pre-vaccination samples only, and 18 provided post-vaccination samples only. Forty-four percent reported underlying conditions, with cardiovascular diseases (22%, predominantly arterial hypertension) and hypothyroidism most prevalent; individual participants reported rheumatoid arthritis, axial spondyloarthropathy, multiple sclerosis, sarcoidosis, and breast cancer. Eleven percent reported intake of immunosuppressive medication (including natalizumab, methotrexate, bimekizumab, or upadacitinib), with antihypertensives, statins, and levothyroxine as the most frequently reported medications overall. Demographics are depicted in Supplementary Table S1. Cohort 2: Haemodialysis patients (n = 56, median age 72 years [IQR 22], 57% male) all received Efluelda 2025/2026, and only paired pre- and post-vaccination samples (day 0 and day 28) were analysed. Comorbidities were highly prevalent, including cardiovascular disease (55%), diabetes mellitus (36%), and obesity (30%); underlying kidney diseases included nephrosclerosis (29%), diabetic nephropathy (18%), and adult polycystic kidney disease (16%). Fourteen percent of dialysis patients received immunosuppressive medication, predominantly prednisolone-based regimens, with individual patients on tacrolimus/MMF (Prograf/Cellcept), rituximab, or siponimod. Demographics and laboratory values are depicted in Supplementary Table S2.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (included in the Microsoft Office Professional Plus software package, version 2016, Microsoft Corporation) and GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software). Statistical significance of normally-distributed data was assessed by one-or two-way analysis of variance, while statistical significance of non-normally-distributed data was assessed by the Kruskal–Wallis test or the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. The respective statistical test is stated in the figure legends. Only p values of 0.05 or lower were considered statistically significant (not significant, p > 0.05; *p ≤ 0.05; **p ≤ 0.01; ***p ≤ 0.001).
