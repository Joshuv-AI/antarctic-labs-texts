Intracameral steroids in pediatric cataract surgery: Clinical insights and the road ahead for sustained drug delivery
Authors: Suresh K Pandey, Vidushi Sharma
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with keen interest the article by Agarwal et al.[1] titled “Outcomes of pediatric cataract surgery: Intraoperative triamcinolone injection versus dexamethasone injection,” published in the January 2026 issue of the Indian Journal of Ophthalmology (IJO). The authors are to be congratulated for conducting a prospective, randomized, assessor-blinded contralateral eye study in a particularly challenging and vulnerable population: infants and young children undergoing cataract surgery.

Postoperative inflammation following pediatric (infantile) cataract surgery remains a formidable clinical challenge due to the immaturity of the blood–aqueous barrier and an exaggerated healing response, often leading to posterior synechiae, pigment deposition on the intraocular lens (IOL), and visual axis opacification (VAO).[12] The study by Agarwal et al. meaningfully contributes to this field by demonstrating that intracameral dexamethasone is non-inferior to intracameral triamcinolone in modulating postoperative inflammation, with comparable rates of inflammatory sequelae and without a significant rise in intraocular pressure (IOP) at three months.[1]

The contralateral eye design is a notable strength of this study, minimizing inter-individual variability and enhancing internal validity. Additionally, the inclusion of objective parameters such as IOP and central corneal thickness (CCT), along with clinically relevant outcomes like resurgery rates and VAO, strengthens the clinical applicability of the findings. The absence of significant IOP elevation in either group is reassuring, particularly in view of the known association between pediatric cataract surgery and secondary glaucoma.[3]

However, a few important considerations emerge that are elaborated in this Letter to the Editor. Although the authors report no statistically significant differences between the two groups, numerically higher rates of resurgery, IOL pigment deposition, and posterior synechiae were observed in the dexamethasone group.[1] Given the small sample size and pilot nature of the study, it may be underpowered to detect moderate or rare differences in outcomes. Longer follow-up is also crucial, as VAO and glaucoma may manifest beyond the three-month postoperative period. These limitations, appropriately acknowledged by the authors, highlight the need for larger, multicentric clinical trials with extended follow-up.

Another important aspect underscored by this study is the persistent challenge of postoperative steroid delivery in pediatric patients. Despite aggressive topical steroid regimens, non-compliance remains a major concern in infants and toddlers, where frequent drop instillation is often impractical, distressing for caregivers, and inconsistently administered.[24] In this context, the demonstrated efficacy of intracameral steroids, whether triamcinolone or dexamethasone, reinforces the paradigm shift toward intraoperative and sustained drug-delivery approaches.

The findings of Agarwal et al.[1] have important implications for the future of pharmacological innovation in pediatric (infantile) cataract surgery. Sustained-release drug-delivery systems offer a promising solution to overcome the limitations of topical therapy. Intracanalicular dexamethasone inserts, designed to provide controlled steroid release over several weeks, have already shown encouraging results in reducing postoperative inflammation and improving adherence in cataract surgery, including early pediatric experience.[4] Such approaches may significantly reduce caregiver burden while ensuring consistent anti-inflammatory coverage during the critical postoperative period.

Beyond canalicular inserts, advances in biodegradable intraocular drug-delivery systems are particularly relevant for pediatric patients. Novel sustained-release implants capable of delivering corticosteroids, alone or in combination with antibiotics, within the anterior chamber have demonstrated effective inflammation control with favorable safety profiles in experimental and early clinical studies.[5] Drug-eluting IOLs and bioresorbable polymer-based platforms are also under investigation and may further refine postoperative care by providing targeted long-term modulation of inflammation without reliance on topical drops.

Importantly, pediatric eyes are not simply smaller versions of adult eyes; they represent a distinct biological environment with heightened inflammatory reactivity and long-term vulnerability. Therefore, future innovations must prioritize safety, predictability, and biocompatibility while addressing the unique compliance challenges inherent to this population. The current study serves as an important stepping stone toward this goal by validating intracameral steroid drug delivery as a safe and effective strategy.

In conclusion, Agarwal et al.[1] deserve commendation for their thoughtful and clinically relevant contribution. Their work not only supports intracameral dexamethasone as an effective alternative to triamcinolone but also reinforces the growing need for sustained drug-delivery solutions in pediatric (infantile) cataract surgery. As pharmacological technologies evolve, integrating sustained-release systems into routine pediatric cataract IOL practice may represent the next major advance in optimizing outcomes and preserving vision in children.
