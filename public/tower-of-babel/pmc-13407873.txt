Prevalence and factors associated with coexisting forms of malnutrition among Malaysian children in the SEANUTS II Study
Authors: See Meng Lim, Giin Shang Yeo, Shoo Thien Lee, Jyh Eiin Wong, Ilse Khouw, Bee Koon Poh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoexisting forms of malnutrition (CFM), defined as multiple forms of malnutrition within the same child (individual-level), pose a greater risk to children than individual forms of malnutrition. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with individual-level CFM among children aged 0.5–12.9 years in Peninsular Malaysia. Between May 2019 and March 2020, a total of 2989 children were recruited for the SEANUTS II Malaysia study; after excluding 16 with implausible anthropometric data, 2973 were analysed. CFM prevalence was 6.8%, with the coexistence of underweight and stunting most common (5.2%). Associated factors included younger age (0.5 to 4.9 years; adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.58; 95% CI 1.01–2.48), low birth weight (< 2.5 kg; aOR = 2.12; 95% CI 1.25–3.59), Malay ethnicity (aOR = 2.58; 95% CI 1.47–4.50) and maternal height < 150 cm (aOR = 2.30; 95% CI 1.29–4.10). CFM affects approximately one in 15 children in Peninsular Malaysia and is associated with several childhood and maternal factors. As the impact of CFM on affected children is more severe than that of standalone malnutrition, interventions and policies should focus on improving child and maternal nutrition to effectively mitigate this problem in the region.Keywords: Children, Coexistence, Malaysia, Malnutrition, SEANUTS IISubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in an individual’s intake of energy and/or nutrients. Child malnutrition is a critical concern globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In 2022, 52% of stunted, 70% of wasted and 48% of overweight children under 5 years lived in Asia, while Africa had 43% of stunted, 27% of wasted and 28% of overweight children1. In recent years, increasing attention has been given to coexisting forms of malnutrition (CFM), which refer to the presence of more than one form of malnutrition within individuals2. While the term can also apply to households or populations, this study focuses specifically on individual-level CFM among children. Examples of individual-level CFM include the double burden of malnutrition, which refers to the simultaneous presence of undernutrition (e.g., stunting, wasting or underweight) and overnutrition (e.g., overweight or obesity), as well as the concurrent occurrence of multiple forms of undernutrition within the same child, such as stunting, wasting and underweight. The prevalence of CFM in children varies from country to country. For example, the prevalence of coexistence of overweight and stunting in children aged 0–4 years is between < 1% in Myanmar, Colombia, and Nepal and > 15% in Albania3.

Malnutrition is the leading underlying cause of death among children under 5 years. The health effects of malnutrition include impaired childhood development, increased susceptibility to infectious diseases, increased risk of extra visceral fat accumulation and increased risk of non-communicable diseases. The adverse effects of CFM in children are more severe than those of individual forms of malnutrition2. Children under 5 years who suffered from more than one form of malnutrition had a four to 12 times higher risk of mortality compared to non-malnourished children4. This highlights the urgent need to devote attention to CFM in children and implement targeted measures to combat it.

Most surveys currently report the prevalence of individual forms of malnutrition in children, with only a few studies reporting the prevalence of CFM in countries, including Pakistan5, Bangladesh6, China7,8, Cameroon9 and Ethiopia10. Similarly, most studies in Malaysia have investigated individual forms of malnutrition11 or household-level CFM, such as overweight mothers and underweight children12. Evidence on individual-level CFM among Malaysian children remains scarce. Only one study reported that one in five children under 5 years had at least one form of undernutrition13, but comprehensive assessment of multiple coexisting forms is lacking. Additionally, previous studies, including one in Malaysia13, have focused on narrower age ranges, such as children under 5 years5,6,9,10, under 7 years7 and 6 to 18 years8. As previous studies have shown that the prevalence of malnutrition in children varies by age14,15, therefore, it is crucial to investigate CFM across a wider age range to capture the full spectrum of nutritional challenges in children.

Child malnutrition results from a multifaceted interplay of socioeconomic, demographic, dietary, environmental and biological factors. Previous studies have identified several risk factors for CFM in children, including poor socioeconomic conditions, male sex, age between 12 and 59 months, low birth weight, acute respiratory infection, diarrhoea, malaria/fever, maternal underweight, maternal short stature and high parity16. However, the factors associated with CFM in Malaysian children remain insufficiently understood, limiting the development of targeted and context-specific interventions. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of individual-level CFM among children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years in Peninsular Malaysia. Additionally, we sought to identify the factors associated with CFM in this population. By identifying these factors, we hope to inform targeted interventions and policies to address the complex nutritional needs of this vulnerable population and ultimately improve their health and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and subjects

The South East Asian Nutrition Surveys (SEANUTS) II Malaysia is a cross-sectional study conducted from May 2019 to March 2020 in Central, Northern, Southern, and East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia. Detailed information on the study design, sampling protocol and subject inclusion/exclusion criteria have already been published17. In brief, a total of 2989 children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years were recruited from urban and rural areas using a multistage cluster sampling method. Sampling was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) randomly selected two districts, one urban and one rural, from different states within each region. Urban areas were defined as gazetted areas and their adjoining built up areas with a combined population of 10,000 and above, while rural areas were defined as gazetted or non-gazetted areas with a population of less than 10,000. In the second stage, a list of randomly selected enumeration blocks within these districts was provided by DOSM for participant recruitment. Children residing in households or educational institutions located within a 5 km radius of the selected enumeration blocks were eligible for inclusion. Children aged 0.5 to 6.9 years were recruited through a home-based approach (home visits) or in their nurseries or kindergartens, while children aged 7.0 to 12.9 years were recruited through primary school in the selected areas.

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (Reference no.: JEP-2018-569). This project is registered in the Dutch Trial Registry (Reference no.: NL7975). Permission to conduct the data collection was obtained from the relevant authorities. Written informed consent from parents or guardians and verbal assent from children were obtained prior to data collection.

Questionnaire

Information on the children’s sociodemographic, environmental and sanitation characteristics was collected using a self-administered questionnaire completed by their parents or guardians. The questionnaire was available in either Malay-English or Mandarin-English to cater for language preferences. The sociodemographic data included details such as sex, age, ethnicity, prematurity, birth weight, number of siblings, area of residence, monthly household income, monthly household food expenditure, mother’s age, mother’s employment status, parents’ education level and smoking status. The parents’ or guardians’ body weight status was determined by calculating body mass index (BMI) based on self-reported weight and height, with the classification based on the World Health Organization (WHO) 2000 classification of BMI categories18. Mother’s height was classified as < 150 cm and ≥ 150 cm, based on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s definition of idiopathic short stature, which is more than 2.25 standard deviations below the mean for age, corresponding to a height of < 150 cm for females19. The environmental and sanitation information was collected using questions on access to improved drinking water, improved cooking water, and improved sanitation facilities in the household. To assess household food security status, we used the 10-item Radimer/Cornell Hunger and Food Insecurity Instrument, which has been previously validated for use in the Malaysian context20. If all responses were negative, the household was categorised as food secure. Households were categorised as food insecure only at the household level if at least one response to items 1–4, but not to items 5–10, was positive; as food insecure for adults if at least one response to items 5–7 was positive; and as child hunger if at least one response to items 8–10 was positive.

Anthropometric measurements

Weight and height (or recumbent length) were taken to assess the nutritional status of the children. Detailed information on the measurement protocol is stated elsewhere21. These measurements, including sex and age, were used to determine anthropometric indicators, including BMI, BMI-for-age z-scores (BAZ), length/height-for-age z-scores (HAZ) and weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ). In this study, the WHO Child Growth Standards 2006 were used for children under 5 years22, while the WHO Growth Reference data 2007 were used for children aged 5–12.9 years23. Stunting was defined as HAZ of <− 2 SD. Underweight was identified in children aged up to 10 years with a WAZ of <− 2 SD. Wasting (< 5 years) or thinness (5–12.9 years) was determined in children with a BAZ of <− 2 SD. Although WHZ is conventionally used to assess wasting in children under 5 years of age, BAZ was applied in this study to ensure consistency of outcome definitions across age groups (up to 12.9 years) and to facilitate comparability of CFM within a single analytical framework. In addition, overweight and obesity were defined as BAZ > + 2 SD and > + 3 SD for children under 5 years, BAZ > + 1 SD and > + 2 SD for those aged 5-12.9 years, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). A complex sampling module was used, referring to the projected Malaysian population aged 0.5–12 years in 2019 as obtained from the 2010 Malaysian Census database24. Percentages and 95% confidence interval (CI) estimates were used to represent the proportions of children with different socioeconomic characteristics, household water sources and sanitation, household food security and nutritional status. Anthropometric measures were reported using mean and standard error, with comparison between age groups determined by general linear model t-tests. Categorical data on nutritional status was analysed using Pearson chi-square tests in relation to socioeconomic characteristics, home sanitation and household food security.

In this analysis, the dependent variables were the CFM among children, which were examined in two ways. First, the individual pairwise combinations of malnutrition were described, including the coexistence of stunting and wasting or thinness, underweight and wasting or thinness, stunting and overweight or obesity, stunting and underweight, as well as the coexistence of wasting or thinness, stunting, and underweight. Second, CFM was treated as a composite binary outcome variable, defined as the presence of two or more forms of malnutrition in the same child. This composite CFM outcome was used in the chi-square and logistic regression analyses. Children with at least two CFM were classified as having CFM, whereas those without any coexistence were classified as not having CFM.

Variables with p < 0.10 in the chi-square test were further examined using univariable logistic regression analyses. Those significant in univariable logistic regression tests were included in the final multivariable model, which was conducted using complete case analysis. No imputation was performed, as missingness for key covariates was below 10% and the available sample size remained sufficient for stable estimation. Variables that were significant only in logistic regression were retained as covariates to control for confounding. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed by examining variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values in the multivariable regression model. Results of VIF values of less than 5 and tolerance values greater than 0.1 were presented, indicating that multicollinearity was not present. The odds ratios (ORs) for CFM in relation to the independent variables were presented in two models: Model 1 (unadjusted) and Model 2, which was adjusted for identified confounding factors (paternal education level and paternal body weight status). Two-sided analysis tests were performed in this study, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Data from 2,973 children, representing 4.9 million children, were included in the analysis, after excluding 16 subjects with implausible z-score values (WAZ <-5SD or WAZ >5SD, HAZ <-6SD or HAZ >6SD, or BAZ <-5SD or BAZ >5SD)25. Table 1 shows that 860 (36.1%) were between 0.5 and 4.9 years and 2113 (63.9%) between 5.0 and 12.9 years. The study population comprised 51.5% boys, predominantly Malays (74.4%), residing in urban areas (73.4%), with 58.8% having a monthly household income of less than MYR 4850. Most children had a normal birth weight (81.6%) and were not born preterm (93.7%). Parents predominantly had secondary or tertiary education, 61.7% of mothers were employed, and 88.7% of mothers were ≥ 150 cm tall. About 44% of mothers and 38% of fathers had a normal body weight status. Half of the parents (both fathers and mothers) were non-smokers (52.7%). Environmental and sanitary conditions were generally favourable, with most children having access to improved drinking water (99.6%), improved cooking water (99.6%) and improved sanitation facilities (90.4%). Regarding food security, more than half of children lived in a food secure household (56.6%), but 16.7% experienced child hunger.

Figure 1 illustrates the nutritional status of children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years. Notably, 66.1% were not malnourished, 27.1% had one form of malnutrition, and 6.8% had two or more (CFM). Among children aged 0.5 to 4.9 years, 13.8% were stunted, a significantly higher proportion (p < 0.001) than the 6.2% of children aged 5.0 to 12.9 years (Table 3). Conversely, 26.4% of children aged 5.0 to 12.9 years were categorised as overweight or obese, significantly higher (p < 0.001) compared to 2.9% of children aged 0.5 to 4.9 years. In terms of CFM, the coexistence of stunting and underweight was the most prevalent, with 5.2% of children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years. Significantly (p = 0.015) more children aged 0.5 to 4.9 years (6.7%) were both stunted and underweight compared to children aged 5.0 to 12.9 years (3.9%).

Table 4 shows the distribution of nutritional status in children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years based on socioeconomic, parental, and environmental and sanitary characteristics. The younger age group (0.5–4.9 years; 8.9%) had a significantly higher proportion (p = 0.007) of CFM than the older age group (5.0–12.9 years; 5.5%). Children born weighing less than 2.5 kg (14.1%) had a significantly higher proportion (p < 0.001) of CFM than children with normal birth weight (6.1%). Malay children (8.1%) had a significantly higher proportion (p < 0.001) of CFM compared to children of other ethnicities (2.2–3.2%). Children from households with monthly food expenditure of less than MYR 783 (8.3%) had a significantly higher proportion (p = 0.026) of CFM compared to those with a monthly food expenditure of MYR 783 or more (5.6%). Several maternal characteristics had a significant impact on the proportion of children with CFM. Specifically, children of mothers aged 30 to 39.9 years, shorter than 150 cm and underweight had a higher proportion of CFM compared to children of mothers aged below 30 years or above 39.9 years (p = 0.004), height ≥ 150 cm (p < 0.001) and of normal or overweight/obese weight status (p = 0.04).

In multivariable analysis (Table 5), after adjusting for paternal education level and body weight status, factors associated with CFM included age 0.5–4.9 years [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.58; 95% CI 1.01–2.48], birth weight < 2.5 kg (aOR 2.12; 95% CI 1.25–3.59) and Malay ethnicity (aOR 2.58; 95% CI 1.47–4.50). Discussion

This study is the first to report on the prevalence of CFM in Peninsular Malaysian children aged 0.5 to 12.9 years. We found that about one-third of children in Peninsular Malaysia suffer from at least one form of malnutrition, with approximately one in 15 children affected by CFM. The most common form of CFM was the coexistence of stunting and underweight, while the least common was the coexistence of stunting and overweight/obesity. Risk factors for CFM included younger age (0.5–4.9 years), birth weight less than 2.5 kg, Malay ethnicity, and maternal height less than 150 cm. Conversely, maternal age of less than 30 years was associated with a slightly lower risk of CFM in their children.

The overall prevalence of CFM in children in Peninsular Malaysia was 6.8%, which was lower than other LMICs, such as 21.5% in Pakistan5, 38.9% in Bangladesh6, and 26.4% in Cameroon9. However, the prevalence of CFM in 110,491 children from nine cities in Northern, Central, and Southern regions of China was only 4.6%, which was lower than this study7. In Malaysia, a study of 15,331 convenience samples of children from 11 states and one territory reported that 25.0% children aged 1 to 5 years had at least one form of undernutrition13. The different prevalence reported in Lee et al. (2022)13 compared to our study is likely due to the fact that the study also included young children ‘at risk’ of undernutrition (stunting and underweight) and also used weight-for-height/length z-scores (WHZ) for the analysis. In contrast, our study focused exclusively on malnourished children and used BAZ instead of WHZ, as the latter is only applicable to children under 5 years whereas our study included children up to 12.9 years. In addition, their sample was recruited from children attending clinics for vaccinations or minor ailments; acute conditions such as fever, diarrhoea, or dehydration may cause transient weight loss, potentially inflating undernutrition estimates13. Despite the relatively small proportion of children affected by CFM, the finding of this study shows that future interventions and policies should simultaneously tackle both undernutrition and overnutrition in the population.

Although the coexistence of stunting and underweight was the most common CFM category at 5.2%, the prevalence of 6.7% among children aged 0.5–4.9 years in this study remains lower than in other LMICs, such as Pakistan (14.3%)5, Somalia (29%)26 and Tanzania (33% in children aged 2 years and below)27. In contrast, the 3.9% prevalence among children aged 5.0–12.9 years was higher than the 1.7% reported in Chinese children under 7 years7. A meta-analysis involving over 53,000 children under five in LMICs reported a pooled prevalence of stunting and underweight of 21.4%4, and highlighted that children affected by multiple forms of malnutrition, particularly wasting, stunting and underweight, have a more than 12-fold higher mortality risk than children without malnutrition. These findings emphasise the urgency of identifying and addressing CFM to prevent long-term developmental and health consequences.

The prevalence of coexistence of stunting and overweight/obesity was lowest among all CFM categories in this study, and was observed in only 0.2% of children overall, more specifically 0.1% in children aged 0.5–4.9 years and 0.2% in children aged 5.0–12.9 years. This rate is lower than for children under 5 years in other LMICs, including Kenya (1%)28, Pakistan (1.4%)5, Thailand (1.6%)29, and Ethiopia (2%)30. It is also lower in children over 5 years than in China (0.4% in children under 7 years)7 and Indonesia (7.5% in children aged 6–12 years)31. Additionally, previous studies have documented the prevalence of stunting and overweight in children under 5 years in 79 LMICs, with Guinea-Bissau having the highest (11.7%) and Senegal the lowest (0.3%) prevalence32. Therefore, the coexistence of stunting and overweight/obesity in children in Peninsular Malaysia is lower compared to most LMICs. Notably, due to the very low frequency of this phenotype (n = 5) in the current study, this specific combination could not be subjected to robust statistical significance testing. However, the presence of this ‘double burden’ of malnutrition at the individual level remains a critical policy concern in middle-income countries like Malaysia, as it necessitates integrated interventions that simultaneously address both undernutrition and overnutrition.

Younger age was identified as a significant risk factor for CFM in this study. Malnutrition, specifically undernutrition, is more common in younger children, especially those under the age of two6. This higher prevalence may be due to their reliance on breastfeeding and complementary foods, which may not always meet their nutritional needs, especially if the complementary foods are delayed and/or of poor quality33. In addition, low birth weight was another risk factor for CFM. Babies with low birth weight often have difficulty catching up on lost growth and are at higher risk for impaired development, including underweight or stunting in early life34. Poor maternal nutritional status is an important factor in low birth weight, as inadequate nutrition from mother to foetus can lead to growth restriction35. Children from the Malay ethnicity appear to be particularly vulnerable to CFM. According to the National Health and Morbidity Survey 2022, children of Malay ethnicity have the highest prevalence of undernutrition, including underweight, stunting, and wasting, compared to children of Chinese and Indian ethnicity36. These disparities may be influenced by income differences, as Bumiputra (including Malays) have been reported to have the lowest income levels37, which can impact dietary quality and the ability to adequately feed their children.

Maternal biological factors, including height and age, are closely associated with child malnutrition. In this study, CFM was positively associated with low maternal height (below 150 cm), reflecting previous research findings that low maternal height almost doubles the odds of all forms of CFM in children compared to children with normal height mothers38. The association between low maternal height and CFM may be partially explained by intergenerational factors, including both genetic and environmental influences. A genetic predisposition to shorter stature can be passed from mother to child, as certain gene variants involved in hormonal regulation, paracrine signalling, and extracellular matrix function have been linked to short stature39. Environmentally, short-statured mothers may have lower nutrient reserves, which leads to an insufficient supply of nutrients to the foetus and thus to intrauterine growth restriction40. In addition, short-statured mothers often have a narrower pelvis, which increases the risk of cephalopelvic disproportion and obstructed labour, which can create an unfavourable uterine environment for optimal foetal growth41.

Maternal age also plays an important role. In this study, mothers younger than 30 years were a protective factor compared to those aged 30 to 39.9 years. Although maternal age was measured at the time of data collection rather than at childbirth, it may still reflect broader life-stage factors influencing child nutrition outcomes. A previous study found that both younger (≤ 19 years) and older (≥ 35 years) mothers were associated with an increased risk of lower birth weight and preterm birth, both of which contribute to child undernutrition42. These patterns may contribute to the observed protective effect, though further studies using maternal age at childbirth are needed to confirm this hypothesis. Advanced maternal age is associated with poorer oocyte quality, placental issues and higher pregnancy risks, which can affect foetal growth and increase child malnutrition43. In contrast, young maternal age (≤ 19 years) often correlates with lower socioeconomic status, which leads to inadequate nutrition and limited prenatal care, increasing the risk of poor child nutrition outcomes44.

In this study, monthly food expenditure was significantly associated with CFM, whereas total household income was not. This suggests that direct spending on food may be a more proximal and sensitive indicator of nutritional risk in the Malaysian context than gross income alone. This interpretation is consistent with evidence from the Bank Negara Malaysia 2025 Annual Report45, which highlights that the burden of living costs is experienced unevenly across income groups. Specifically, lower-income (B40) households allocate a substantially higher proportion of their income to food (approximately 30%) compared with middle-income (M40; ~22%) and high-income (T20; ~16%) households. As a result, even when gross incomes appear stable, lower-income households remain more vulnerable to increases in food prices, which can directly affect dietary quality and diversity. This economic constraint likely explains why food expenditure, rather than total household income, emerged as a significant determinant of CFM in our analysis.

One of the strengths of this study is the large sample size used to estimate the prevalence of CFM. The data was collected in the Central, Northern, Southern and East Coast regions of Peninsular Malaysia, allowing the results to be generalised to these areas. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the observed factors and CFM. Second, some variables, such as birthweight, maternal weight and paternal characteristics, were based on self-reports from parents or guardians, which may introduce recall and reporting biases. However, as birthweight is recorded on the child’s birth certificate in Malaysia, parents or guardians were asked to verify this information prior to reporting.

In addition, this study utilised BAZ to define wasting across the entire age spectrum to maintain a consistent analytical framework for CFM. However, for children under 5 years, WHZ remains the WHO-recommended standard to define wasting. As BAZ and WHZ are not fully interchangeable, this approach may have introduced some misclassification of wasting in the younger cohort, potentially affecting comparability with studies that rely exclusively on WHZ. Moreover, underweight is defined only for children under 10 years of age, which reduced the effective sample size for analyses involving underweight related CFM and may have affected the statistical power of chi-square tests and associated significance levels. While missing data were reported in the descriptive tables, the use of a “missing” category in analyses rather than advanced imputation methods could influence the precision of some estimates. Finally, this study performed weighted analysis using the population information provided from DOSM. The weighting was derived from the 2019 population projections based on the 2010 census. Although this represented the most comprehensive data available at the time of analysis given that the 2020 census was delayed due to the COVID-19 pandemic, it may not fully capture changes in population distribution over the subsequent decade. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and risk factors of CFM in Malaysian children. Future studies should also include children from the East Malaysia (Sabah and Sarawak states) and consider micronutrient deficiencies to provide a comprehensive overview of this issue across the whole of Malaysia.

Conclusion

This study highlights that one in 15 children aged 0.5–12.9 years in Peninsular Malaysia are affected by CFM, with the coexistence of stunting and underweight being the most common form. Given that the impact of CFM is more severe than that of standalone malnutrition, urgent action is needed to address CFM among children in this region. The identified risk factors, such as younger age, low birth weight, Malay ethnicity, and maternal height, emphasise the importance of strengthening maternal and early childhood nutrition interventions. Policy efforts should prioritise improving maternal nutritional status before and during pregnancy, enhancing growth monitoring and early detection of multiple forms of malnutrition in young children, and implementing targeted nutrition programs for high-risk populations. Addressing CFM through coordinated, life course-based strategies are essential to reduce its burden and improve child health outcomes in this region.
