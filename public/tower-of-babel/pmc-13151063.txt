A Prospective Exposome-Based Gene–Environment Interaction Study on the Effects of Prenatal Environmental Exposure on Fetal Growth in the Shanghai Birth Cohort
Authors: Wen Jiang, Yun Huang, Hong Jin, Yuexin Gan, Qingli Zhang, Xiaoqing He, Ying Tian, Jun Zhang, for the Shanghai Birth Cohort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: Both environmental exposure and type 2 diabetes (T2D) genetic susceptibility affect fetal growth. However, most previous studies used single exposure rather than an exposome strategy to explore the association between environmental factors and fetal growth, and the interactions of environmental exposure with maternal and fetal genes were often overlooked. OBJECTIVES: We explore the associations between a broad range of prenatal environmental factors and fetal growth and further evaluate the effect of modification of maternal and fetal T2D genetic susceptibility on the identified exposures. METHODS: From 1,933 mother–neonate pairs from the Shanghai Birth Cohort, we estimated the associations between 70 prenatal exposure measures (including outdoor environment, residential environment, chemical exposures, lifestyle factors, and psychosocial status) and fetal growth, measured by birth-weight-for-gestational-age z-score (WAZ). Single-exposure analysis, elastic net regression, sparse partial least-squares regression, extreme gradient boosting, and random forest were applied jointly to screen for WAZ-associated exposures. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess the interactions of WAZ-associated exposures with maternal and fetal T2D polygenetic risk score (PRS). RESULTS: Sixteen prenatal exposures were associated with fetal growth, of which manganese, strontium, and residential greenspace showed a positive association, while bisphenol A (BPA), 2,4-dihydroxybenzophenone (BP-1), ethyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (EtP), 4-hydroxybenzophenone (4-HBP), artificial light at night, noise, nitrogen dioxide, rubidium, thallium, silver, and humidity had a negative association. Temperature had an inverse U-shaped association with WAZ. The interactions of BPA and silver with maternal and fetal T2D PRS and rubidium with fetal T2D PRS were statistically significant with more pronounced exposure effects in individuals with high T2D genetic risks. DISCUSSION: Our study identified several prenatal environmental exposures within the outdoor environment, phenols, and metal­(loid)­s that were associated with fetal growth. Mother–neonate pairs with a high T2D genetic susceptibility were particularly vulnerable to environmental insults.

Introduction

According to the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) theory, organ systems during the fetal stage are most elastic, because of the active proliferation and differentiation of progenitor cells. Perturbation at this time can permanently affect their structure and function, resulting in lifelong health effects. Birth weight is one of the most commonly used surrogates for fetal growth. It is not only an important factor for infant morbidity and mortality but also continuously associated with chronic disease risks in adulthood (e.g., cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes mellitus) in both extreme and normal weight ranges. − Thus, identifying influential factors for birth weight, especially the modifiable ones, may help generate intervention strategies for better short- and long-term health on individual and population scales.

A number of epidemiological studies focusing on a single exposure or exposure family have found some environmental factors, such as maternal diet and nutrients, air pollution (e.g., particulate matter and ozone), chemical exposures [e.g., phenols and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)], and psychological factors, to affect fetal growth. However, such studies may have produced biased results, as they hypothesize that exposures are linearly and independently associated with outcomes, ignoring the fact that humans are simultaneously exposed to a wide range of environmental factors.

The remedy lies in the widely accepted concept of exposome that emphasizes a complete environmental exposure assessment. So far, two studies from the Human Early-Life Exposome (HELIX) project have assessed the associations between prenatal exposome and birth weight: One included 131 exposure variables from multiple exposure families, using multivariable linear regression (MLR) models and deletion-substitution-addition (DSA) algorithm, found that birth weight was negatively associated with lead (Pb) and particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter of <2.5 μm (PM2.5) exposures but positively associated with paraben. The other, using the same models, focusing on 60 urban environment-related exposures, found positive associations of birth weight with greenspace, the presence of bus lines, and traffic density, and a negative association with land use Shannon’s Evenness Index. As the statistical models applied by these studies are somewhat limited in handling coexposure and nonlinearity, some important associations might have been underdetected. More exposome-based studies are warranted.

In addition to environmental factors, genetics also plays an important role in fetal growth. Evidence shows that type 2 diabetes (T2D)-related loci and polygenetic risk scores (PRS) in mother and offspring are associated with fetal growth but in different directions, suggesting both maternal and fetal genomes can influence birth weight through regulating glycemic status, and the effects may be antagonistic. It was hypothesized that the positive effect of maternal genetics is mainly achieved by blood-glucose-raising alleles regulating intrauterine nutrition supply, whereas the negative effect of fetal genetics is driven by insulin-secretion-reducing or insulin resistance-increasing alleles, both reducing fetal growth. , Moreover, the effects of environmental exposures on birth outcomes have been observed to differ by maternal glycemia traits. , These findings suggest a potential interplay of environmental exposures with maternal and fetal T2D genetic risks. However, much is unknown. As gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is prevalent globally and has shared genetic architectures with T2D, , filling the knowledge gap would help identify individuals at risk for environmental exposures in prenatal care for personalized interventions. For these reasons, we performed a prospective exposome study to identify prenatal exposures that may affect fetal growth and further explored their interactions with maternal and fetal T2D genetic predispositions across the genome.

Material and Methods

Study Population

The current study used data from the Shanghai Birth Cohort (SBC). This ongoing multicenter cohort enrolled 4,127 pregnant women and their husbands from six participating hospitals in Shanghai, China, during 2013–2016: Xinhua Hospital and Branch affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, International Peace Maternity and Child Hospital, Shanghai Children’s Medical Center, Fudan University Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital affiliated to Tong Ji University, and Maternal and Child Health Institute of the Yangpu District. Inclusion criteria of the SBC were (a) age 20 years or older, (b) at least one person in the couple was a registered Shanghai resident, (c) planning to seek prenatal care and give birth at the SBC participating hospitals, and (d) being willing to sign a consent form. More details on the cohort have been described elsewhere. Included in the current analysis were 1,933 singleton mother–neonate pairs with birth weight measures and maternal and fetal genome-wide genotype information.

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of all participating hospitals. All participating women signed informed consent forms for themselves and their children.

Prenatal Exposome Assessment

The SBC has assessed a wide range of prenatal and postnatal exposures for participants, of which 70 relevant prenatal exposure variables were included in the current study. These exposures cover eight exposure families, involving the outdoor environment, phenols, PFAS, metal­(loid)­s, residential environment, health behaviors, diet and nutritional supplements, and psychosocial status.

Outdoor Environmental Exposures

Data on outdoor environmental exposures (air pollutants, greenspace, noise, artificial light at night, solar irradiation, and meteorological factors) were obtained from spatial and remote sensing products and were assigned to pregnant women using ArcGIS (version 10.8) based on their geocoded residential addresses during pregnancy. The averages of all exposures over the entire pregnancy were used as the exposure metrics except for noise, for which the annual average during the year of conception was used.

Air pollutant concentrations (μg/m3), including PM2.5, particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter of <10 μm (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and ozone (O3), were estimated using pollutant-specific random forest models in 1 × 1 km and daily spatiotemporal resolutions. The R 2 (RMSE) of the predictions through 10-fold cross-validation (CV) for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3 were 0.84 (16.09 μg/m3), 0.87 (27.20 μg/m3), 0.80 (7.78 μg/m3), and 0.80 (20.93 μg/m3) at the daily level, respectively. − The concentration of particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter between 2.5 and 10 μm (PMcoarse) was calculated by using PM10 concentration minus PM2.5 concentration.

Greenspace exposure was assessed by using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) data of MOS13Q1 downloaded from the NASA Web site (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov), which has 250 × 250 m and 16-day spatiotemporal resolutions. The indicator is calculated from the visible and near-infrared light reflected by vegetation and takes a value between −1 and 1, with high positive values reflecting high vegetation coverage, while zero or negative values reflecting bare land or water bodies. We averaged NDVI within 500 m (NDVI-500 m) and 1,000 m (NDVI-1,000 m) buffers of the maternal residential address and used them as the exposure metrics.

Noise levels (dB­(A)) with 50 × 50 m and yearly spatiotemporal resolutions were derived from a land use regression model incorporating data on 40 min environmental noise measurement, land-use types, road networks, socioeconomic variables, and geographic information systems collected in Shanghai, China. The R 2 (RMSE) of the prediction through 10-fold CV was 0.72 (3.54 dB­(A)).

Data used to estimate the exposure level of artificial light at night (ALAN, nW/cm2/sr) was derived from the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) Day/Night Band (DNB) produced by the Earth Observation Group (https://eogdata.mines.edu/). All nighttime light maps exclude background noise, solar and lunar contamination, data degraded by cloud cover, and features unrelated to electric lighting (e.g., fires, flares, and volcanoes) and have 500 × 500 m and monthly spatiotemporal resolutions.

Solar irradiation (SI, nW/m2) data were obtained from a recently released global surface SI data set with 10 × 10 km and daily spatiotemporal resolutions. Data on meteorological factors, including daily mean temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%), were downloaded from the China National Weather Data Sharing System (https://data.cma.cn/). Data from the nearest meteorological station to the maternal residence was used to calculate the exposure metrics.

Chemical Exposures

Individual exposure levels of phenols, PFAS, and metal­(loid)­s were determined in the biological samples collected during the early pregnancy follow-up (<16 gestational weeks). All measurements were conducted at the Ministry of Education-Shanghai Key Laboratory of Children’s Environmental Health, Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

A volume of 0.2 mL of urine sample was used to quantify 18 phenols using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS; Agilent1290-6490, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA). The operation protocol has been described in detail elsewhere. The measured phenols include seven bisphenols [bisphenol A (BPA), bisphenol AF (BPAF), bisphenol AP (BPAP), bisphenol B (BPB), bisphenol P (BPP), bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol Z (BPZ)], six parabens [methyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (MeP), ethyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (EtP), propyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (PrP), butyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (BuP), benzyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (BzP), and heptyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (HeP)], four benzophenones [2,4-dihydroxy benzophenone (BP-1), 2,2′,4,4′-tetrahydroxy benzophenone (BP-2), 2,2′-dihydroxy-4-methoxy benzophenone (BP-8), 4-hydroxy benzophenone (4-HBP)], and triclosan (TCS). BuP, BzP, HeP, BPAF, BPZ, BP-2, and BP-8 had low detection rates (<50%) and, thus, were excluded from further analyses. The limit of detection (LOD) of the remaining phenols ranged from 0.0001 to 0.08 ng/mL (see supplemental Excel Table S1). To account for urine dilution, the phenol concentrations were adjusted for creatinine, which was measured using the enzymatic method on an automatic chemical analyzer.

A 100 μL plasma was used for PFAS quantification using HPLC-MS/MS (Agilent1290-6490, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA), following the process described by Wang et al. A total of 10 PFAS congeners were measured, including perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUA), perfluorohexanesulfonate (PFHxS), perfluorododecanoic acid (PFDoA), perfluorobutanesulfonate (PFBS), perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA), and perfluorooctane sulfonamide (PFOSA). PFOSA was not included in the study because of its low detection rate (19.0%). The LOD of the remaining PFAS ranged from 0.009 to 0.05 ng/mL (see supplemental Excel Table S1).

A 0.1 mL whole blood or plasma sample was used to detect twenty-two metal­(loid)­s using an inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7500ce, Agilent Technologies, USA), following the protocol reported elsewhere. Specifically, magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), arsenic (As), rubidium (Rb), selenium (Se), strontium (Sr), cadmium (Cd), and Pb were determined in the whole blood samples. Lithium (Li), boron (B), vanadium­(V), chromium (Cr), cobalt (Co), molybdenum (Mo), silver (Ag), tellurium (Te), tritium (Tl), and uranium (U) were measured in the plasma samples. The LOD of these metal­(loid)­s ranged from 0.005 to 810 mg/mL (see supplemental Excel Table S1).

Residential Environmental Exposures

Two residential environmental exposure indicators were included in the current study. The main traffic road exposure was assessed via the early pregnancy follow-up questionnaire, which is defined as the existence of a main traffic road within a five min walk distance of the maternal residence (yes/no). The moldy wall exposure was assessed via early- and mid-pregnancy (24–28 gestational weeks) follow-up questionnaires, defined as the emergence of moldy walls in the house during any period of pregnancy (yes/no).

Diet and Nutritional Supplements

Diet and nutritional supplements were assessed via a simplified food frequency questionnaire administered during early pregnancy. Four food groups (meat/egg and milk/fish and shell/fruit and vegetable) and two nutrition supplements (folate/vitamin) were generated accordingly. The frequency of food ingestion (≤three times per week/≥four times per week) and nutritional supplements (yes/no) were used as the exposure metrics.

Health Behaviors

Four indicators were generated for this exposure family. Smoking (active and passive) and alcohol drinking were assessed via questionnaires administered during each visit throughout pregnancy. A pregnant woman was defined as exposed to smoking if she reported active smoking behavior or was exposed to passive smoking at home or workplace in any visit. Similarly, a pregnant woman was defined as exposed to alcohol if she reported drinking during any visit. Sleep quality was assessed via the total score of the Pittsburgh questionnaire administered in midpregnancy. Physical activity was estimated in the early pregnancy follow-up using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, based on which metabolic equivalent tasks (MET) were estimated and used as the exposure metric.

Psychosocial Status

Four exposure indicators were generated in this category, including maternal anxiety, depression, stress, and social support status. Anxiety, depression, and stress status were evaluated during the midpregnancy visit using the total scores of the Zung self-rating Anxiety Scale, the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression Scale, and the Perceived Stress Scale, respectively. Social support was assessed during the early pregnancy visit using the ENRICHD Social Support Instrument. The scores of the first six items were summed and subtracted from the corresponding full score so that a higher score on the index means a poorer status. All of these scales have been calibrated with good validity in the Chinese population.

Outcome Assessment

Birth weight (in grams) was measured by midwives using electronic weight after delivery, following a standard method, and abstracted by research assistants from medical records. We transformed the original birth weight value into the sex-specific birth-weight-for-gestational-age z-score (WAZ) based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards (version 3.2.2) and used it as the main outcome.

Genotyping

The SBC used the TGuide Large Volume Blood Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, OSR-M104) to extract maternal and fetal DNA from venous blood during pregnancy and umbilical cord blood samples, respectively. Genotyping was done with the Illumina Global Screening Array v.3.0 (Illumina; San Diego, CA, USA), which contains 730,059 variants. Quality control was performed in PLINK (version 1.9) and King (version 2.2.8). Samples with discordant sex values, genotype call rates less than 97%, outlying heterozygosity for autosomal chromosomes [±3 standard deviations (SD) away from the sample mean), or an estimated kinship coefficient >0.088 were excluded. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a genotype call rate lower than 95%, a minor allele frequency less than 0.01, and that dissatisfy Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P < 10–5) were excluded. SNP imputation was performed using IMPUTE5 (version 1.1.5) based on the 1000 Genomes Project Phase III panel to maximize genetic coverage, and imputed SNPs with INFO < 0.6 were excluded.

T2D Genetic Risk Assessment

T2D genetics involves multiple variants regulating complex pathophysiological processes. Thus, we applied PRS rather than the candidate gene strategy to evaluate the T2D genetic risks in the study population. Specifically, we constructed T2D PRS for mothers and fetuses based on the latest genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data from the eastern Asian ancestry by the Diabetes Genetics Replication and Meta-analysis (DIAGRAM) Consortium. We set the P value threshold of 5 × 10–8 and clumped SNPs with linkage disequilibrium r2 > 0.1 within a 250 kb window. Ultimately, a total of 394 SNPs were included (see supplemental Excel Table S2), based on which the PRS was calculated as follows: PRSj=∑i=1nβiGijMj where n is the number of SNPs included, β i is the summary statistic for the effective allele of the i-th SNP, G ij is the number of the effective allele of the i-th SNP observed for the j-th individual, and M j is the number of alleles included in the PRS of the j-th individual.

Covariates

All exposome approaches applied in the study were adjusted for the same set of covariates that were determined by a directed acyclic graph (Figure S1), the characteristics of the study population, and evidence from previous literature. They include maternal age (continuous, years), body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy (continuous, kg/m2), weight gain during pregnancy (continuous, kg), parity (nulliparous/multiparous), education (high school or lower/college/master’s degree or above), self-reported financial status (very good/good/relatively poor), the season of conception (spring/summer/autumn/winter), the year of conception (2013/2014/2015), and neonatal sex (male/female).

Statistical analyses

Data Preprocessing

Chemical exposures below the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced by LOD/√2 (PFAS and metals) or LOD/2 (phenols) according to different skewness degrees to achieve more accurate estimates. The distribution of all continuous variables was checked, and proper transformation was performed on the skewed variables to approximate normality. Variables with a clear non-normal distribution after transformation were dichotomized. Missing data for exposure variables and covariates were imputed using the method of chained equations. Specifically, PFAS, phenols, and metal­(loid)­s were naturally log-transformed except for Ag, Li, and U, which were categorized into binary variables (high/low) by the medians of their distributions because of extreme skewness. Ambient environmental exposures and other continuous variables remained in the original format. Predictive mean matching, logistic regression, and multinormal regression were applied to impute continuous, binary, and categorical variables, respectively. Variables that correlated at a level >0.9 or <0.1 in absolute value or had missingness correlated at a level >0.4 in absolute value were not allowed to predict one another. No imputation was done on WAZ. Ten imputed data sets were generated, but only the first one was retained for the following analyses, given that no well-acknowledged method is available to combine the results of different imputation sets for the exposome approaches applied in this study, and repeating the analysis would significantly increase the already high computational burden.

After imputation, all continuous variables were standardized by their standard deviations. The pairwise correlations between continuous-continuous exposures, continuous–categorical exposures, and categorical–categorical exposures were estimated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients, the squared root of R-squared of logistic regression models, and Cramer’s V statistics, respectively.

Exposome-WAZ Association Assessment

In addition to single-exposure approaches, other statistical methods proposed for the exposome research have been categorized into three groups: variable selection, dimension reduction, and grouping of observations. As no single approach universally outperforms the others, we evaluated the popularity, interpretability, computational burden, and ability to deal with interaction and nonlinear associations of multiple models and ultimately chose elastic net regression (ENET) for variable selection, sparse partial least-squares regression (sPLS) for dimension reduction, extreme gradient boosting (XGBOOST) and random forest (RF) for grouping of observations, and conventional MLR model as the main methods to screen exposure variables associated with WAZ.

For MLR, a family wise error rate correction was used to account for multiple comparisons (0.05 divided by the effective number of tests), leading to a corrected P-value threshold of 7 × 10–4. Variables with P-values <0.05 and <7 × 10–4 were deemed nominally significant (important) and statistically significant, respectively. For other approaches, the variable importance score (VI) of each exposure, reflecting their contribution to the models, was generated and ranked. The top ten variables selected by each model were deemed important. If an exposure was identified as important by at least two models, then it was deemed to be associated with WAZ. A brief introduction of these approaches, except for MLR, and corresponding tuning parameters are given in Supporting Information Text 1. Besides, the Shapley value, a widely used index representing the average contribution of a feature value to the specific prediction in machine learning, was used to draw the association pattern between each identified exposure and WAZ.

Gene–Environment Interaction Analyses

To explore the gene–environment interaction on WAZ, we successively included a product term of each identified exposure and maternal/fetal T2D PRS in the MLR, where PRS was classified into a binary variable by the median of the distribution in the study population (high/low). Additionally, we conducted three-order interaction analyses of identified exposures, maternal/fetal T2D, and self-reported financial status to evaluate the effect modification by socioeconomic status, in which self-reported financial status was transformed into a binary variable (good and above/relatively poor) for easier comparison. The top ten genetic principal components were calculated and introduced to the model to account for the residual population structure.

Sensitivity Analysis

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the primary results. First, we repeated the MLR analysis in the data set without imputation and the data set excluding low birth weight (LBW, <2500 g) and macrosomia (>4,000 g). Second, we kept only one of the variables with pairwise correlation coefficients >0.80 and repeated ENET, sPLS, XGBOOST, and RF analyses. Third, we excluded mother–neonate pairs with a diagnosis of GDM following the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria and repeated the gene–environment interaction analysis. Fourth, we performed a stratification analysis on the associations between significant environmental exposures and WAZ by GDM diagnosis (case/non-GDM) to verify our gene–environment interaction findings. All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.2.1), using the mice, rexposome, glmnet, spls, xgboost, ranger, tuneRanger, and caret packages. A P value for interaction less than 0.10 was deemed statistically significant.

Environmental Exposures-T2D PRS Interactions on WAZ

Figure and Excel Table S9 show the interactions between significant exposures and mother–neonate pair T2D PRS. Given that temperature had an apparent nonlinear relationship with WAZ, we posteriorly recorded this variable into three categories (normal/low temperature/high temperature) according to Figure S5, using −1 and 1 as the cutoff points to better capture its interaction with PRS, where the combination of low T2D PRS and normal temperature was used as the reference group. For maternal T2D PRS, the environmental exposure effects on WAZ tended to be more pronounced in the high T2D genetic risk group. For instance, 4-HBP exposure showed a significant difference between the high and low T2D genetic risk groups, with β (95%CI) values of −0.06 (−0.12, −0.01) and −0.02 (−0.07, −0.04), respectively. In the sensitivity analysis, after excluding mother–neonate pairs with GDM (n = 298 pairs), only the interactions between exposures and fetal T2D PRS remained statistically significant despite the general trend found in the main analysis remaining unchanged (Figure S6). Discussion

Based on a comprehensive exposome analysis strategy, our prospective cohort study identified 16 prenatal exposures from the outdoor environment, phenols, and metal­(loid) families associated with WAZ. The further gene–environment interaction analysis showed that the exposure effects were more pronounced in mother–neonate pairs with high T2D genetic risks. Significant interactions between BPA, Ag and maternal and fetal T2D PRS, as well as between Rb and fetal T2D PRS, were found.

Exposure to Phenols and Fetal Growth

The adverse health effects of phenols, including bisphenols, benzophenones, parabens, and antimicrobials, have been a growing concern in the past decades because the group of synthetic chemicals is extensively disseminated in the environment (e.g., foods, personal care products, and commercial plastics) and widely detectable in human samples. Some epidemiological studies, but not all, have reported a negative association between prenatal exposure to phenols and birth weight. , The ignorance of coexposure to other environmental factors may have contributed to the inconsistency among different studies. In our exposome analysis, one bisphenol (BPA), one paraben (EtP), and two benzophenones (BP-1 and 4-HBP) were found to be negatively associated with WAZ. Although the underlying biomechanisms remain not fully understood, the activation of inflammatory and oxidative states in the maternal-fetal interface and disruption of metabolomic regulations and maternal hormones (e.g., thyroid and steroidal hormones) are believed to be involved.

Exposure to Outdoor Environmental Factors and Fetal Growth

Regarding outdoor environmental exposures, we found that WAZ was negatively associated with NO2, noise, ALAN, and humidity but positively associated with NDVI-500m and NDVI-1000m. An inverse U-shaped relationship between the temperature and WAZ was also observed. NO2 is an air pollutant that mainly originates from fuel combustion and traffic. It is the precursor of O3 and has independent harmful effects on human health. According to a recent umbrella review by Nyadanu et al., the existing evidence has shown a relatively consistent association between whole-pregnancy NO2 exposure levels and reduced birth weight, possibly by altering placenta hemodynamics, disturbing endothelial dysfunction, and triggering oxidative stress and inflammation, supporting our findings. It should be noted that PM2.5 was not identified as an influential factor in the current study, which has been listed as a contributor to low birth weight by the Global Burden of Disease 2019. One possible explanation may be that NO2 and PM2.5 are moderately correlated with each other (r = 0.69 in our study) and have similar emission sources, exposure pathways, and pathogenic mechanisms to impair fetal growth. As a series of measures have been adopted to reduce PM2.5 concentration in Shanghai, NO2 is growing to be the local main air pollutant and thus shows a more pronounced effect than PM2.5 in the coexposure models.

Noise and ALAN are two typical emerging environmental problems resulting from rapid urbanization. To date, only a handful of studies have explored the association between noise and birth weight, of which two reported a similar inverse relationship to our finding. , Noise is suspected of inducing neuroendocrine stress in pregnant women, which in turn affects the placental function and fetal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to decreased birth weight.

Only one previous study explored the association between ALAN exposure and birth weight while reporting no significant association, contrary to our findings. The observed negative ALAN-WAZ association is biologically plausible. One of the key effects of ALAN lies in its disruption of circadian rhythms and melatonin secretion in humans. Previous experimental evidence has overwhelmingly shown melatonin as a direct free radical scavenger and indirect antioxidant that can effectively protect the placenta, fetus, and gravidae from the damage of oxidative stress, consequently obviating placental dysfunction and ensuring proper fetal growth. Thus, we propose that ALAN exposure may affect birth weight through disturbing circadian rhythms and melatonin secretion. However, given that the existing evidence is limited and inconsistent, more studies are needed to verify our observations and explore the biological hypotheses.

Residential greenness has drawn increasing attention in recent years as it is shown to be beneficial for human health, possibly through encouraging physical activities, mitigating environmental hazards, relieving mental stress, and strengthening social interaction. We are not the first to observe a beneficial effect of NDVI on birth weight, but our exposome analysis further confirmed the relationship after simultaneously controlling for important confounders (e.g., noise and air pollutants) that were missed by many previous studies.

Our finding of the bidirectional effects of prenatal temperature exposure on birth weight is supported by some previous studies. , It has been suggested that anatomical and physiological changes happen during pregnancy, such as the change in surface area-to-mass ratio, higher-fat deposition, reduced systemic vascular resistance, and fetal metabolism, make gravidae less able to maintain normothermia and more vulnerable to extreme heat and cold exposure. The consequent hyperthermia or hypothermia can trigger oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions and reduce placental blood flow, contributing to the disturbance of fetal growth.

Exposure to Metal­(loid)­s and Fetal Growth

The independent and mixed effects of different metals on birth weight have been extensively studied, but the results are still inconsistent, probably due to a nonlinear dosage-response relationship. In the current study, five out of twenty-two metals were identified as the influential factors on WAZ, of which Ag, Rb, and Tl showed negative associations, whereas Mn and Sr had positive associations. The biochemical functions of these metals in regulating fetal growth are largely unclear. Only limited evidence suggested that Mn and Sr play a role in the development and formation of bone, oxidative damage defense, and metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, and lipids, which may partly explain their beneficial effects on birth weight. , In contrast, previous studies indicated that Tl can freely cross the placenta and disturb the mitochondrial function of fetal tissues, thus leading to low birth weight. However, more experimental studies are warranted to explore the exact biomechanisms of these metals.

Compared with the two exposome studies from the HELIX project, , residential greenspace is the only environmental factor that was consistently and positively associated with birth weight. The causes of the heterogeneity among studies are complex. Changes in environmental exposures over time and space may have resulted in different exposure levels and effects on populations. Differences in the assessed environmental factors and evaluation methods, especially for chemicals determined by various methods and biological samples and different statistical methods applied, could also affect the results. For instance, the HELIX project merely used MLR and DSA models, whereas we integrated five models involving XGBOOST and RF, allowing us to consider nonlinear exposure-outcome relationships and thus helping us capture the significant associations between abnormal temperature exposure and WAZ. Moreover, the study design, sample size, and covariates adjusted in different studies could also lead to observation discrepancies. For these reasons, larger-scale exposome studies based on uniform exposure assessment methods and advanced statistical models are warranted.

Gene–Environment Interactions on Fetal Growth

Although both T2D-related genes of mother-fetus pairs and environmental exposure can affect birth weight, no study has evaluated their interaction. Our gene–environment interaction analysis revealed that the effects of identified exposures on birth weight were generally more pronounced in mothers and fetuses with relatively higher T2D genetic risks. Particularly, the interactions of BPA and Ag with maternal and fetal T2D PRS and Rb with fetal T2D PRS were statistically significant (P for interaction <0.10). These findings are biologically plausible. As mentioned before, the causal effect of maternal T2D genetic risks on birth weight is mainly achieved through raising blood glucose. As a result, mothers with high T2D susceptibility tend to have higher blood glucose levels. The hyperglycemia status has been noted to cause endothelial dysfunction and increase arterial vascular resistance in the placental cotyledons, further leading to an adaptive development of the placenta to ensure appropriate nutrient and oxygen transport to the fetus. These changes may simultaneously lead to environmental exposures or related intermediate molecules crossing the placenta more from mother to fetus and thus aggravate the corresponding effects on birth weight. Besides, exacerbated inflammation is commonly characterized in individuals with hyperglycemia. Given that maternal inflammation is closely involved in fetal growth, the interaction between maternal T2D genetic risks and environmental exposures may also be a result of the combination of elevated systemic inflammation caused by hyperglycemia with environmental exposures. On the other hand, fetuses with high T2D PRS inherently tend to have a relatively lower birth weight due to reduced insulin secretion or increased insulin resistance. The fetal genetic effects may act synergistically with increased transplacental BPA, Rb, and Ag concentrations to decrease the birth weight further.

We found that the interactions of BPA and Ag with maternal T2D PRS were not statistically significant after excluding mother–neonate pairs from GDM, despite the trend remaining. Such weakened modification effects may be largely attributed to decreased heterogeneity in T2D genetic risks in GDM-free populations, as the two diseases have shared genetic etiology. Our stratification analysis by GDM diagnosis supported the hypothesis because the associations of BPA and Ag with WAZ significantly differed between GDM and non-GDM subgroups, which was more pronounced in the former. Taken together, these findings suggest that mother–fetus pairs with high T2D genetic susceptibilities may be more vulnerable to environmental insults. Meanwhile, considering GDM has overlapping genetic factors with T2D and is also characterized by hyperglycemia, which results in less insulin secretion by the fetus, environmental exposures may also interact with GDM in the same way as with maternal and fetal T2D genetic predisposition to affect fetal growth. Therefore, women with GDM need to be given more attention to reduce harmful environmental exposures.

Moreover, our three-order interaction analysis suggests socioeconomic status as a modifier of the fetal growth effects of environmental exposures and genetic susceptibility. Interestingly, for outdoor environmental exposures, such as ALAN, NO2, and greenspace, their interactions with T2D genetic predisposition tended to be stronger in the group of relatively poor financial status, while the interactions of phenols and metals (Ag and Rb) with genetics were more pronounced in mother–neonate pairs with good financial conditions. We hypothesize that mothers with different socioeconomic characteristics have different exposure sensitivities and spectra. It has been suggested that pregnant women with relatively poor financial status may be more vulnerable to harmful outdoor environmental exposures and could benefit more from exposure to greenspace. In contrast, consuming marine products, meat, packing materials, and cosmetics may increase exposure to phenols and some metals (e.g., Ag). Mothers with good financial status may have had more chances of such exposure and, thus, show stronger effects. Taken together, these findings suggest that social inequality could modify the combined effects of environmental exposures and genetic susceptibility on fetal growth and should be further explored in future studies.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths. The exposome data were collected prospectively and covered a variety of external and internal exposure families, avoiding the problems of recall bias and selective reporting in a single-exposure analysis. Besides, the analysis strategy took into account coexposure, potential interactions between exposures, and nonlinear relationships, and thus, can identify birth weight-associated exposure variables more efficiently. Moreover, we found significant effects of modifications of maternal and fetal genetic susceptibility on the association between prenatal environmental exposures and birth weight. These findings support that fetal growth is a result of both genetic and environmental factors, highlighting the importance of incorporating genetic factors in future exposome research to better understand the health impact of environmental exposures and identify high-risk genotypes or vulnerable populations.

Our study also has several limitations. First, restricted by financial resources and exposure measurement technology, not all exposures suspected of affecting fetal growth were included in the current study (e.g., polycyclic aromatic compounds and pesticides). , The exposure list is still far from optimal. Applying advanced technologies that can simultaneously detect a broader range of chemicals is encouraged to solve the problem. Second, exposure measurement bias in exposome research is inevitable, which may influence the effect estimations. For instance, the ambient exposures were estimated based on the residential address during pregnancy, ignoring the exact indoor time and movement; the chemical exposures were assessed using single-time-point biosamples, which failed to capture the variations across the whole pregnancy; the diet and nutrient status were assessed via simplified food frequency questionnaires (i.e., diet frequency) in the SBC, which may be unable to precisely reflect the accurate diet quality of complex Chinese food. Besides, quality heterogeneity in questionnaire data quality among the SBC participating hospitals may still exist despite the standard operation and quality control being implemented throughout the follow-up. As a result, interpretation and comparison of the association patterns among exposures warrant caution. Third, given that PRS was used as the genetic risk indicator for gene–environment interactions, our study could not further explore the exact biological mechanisms. Additionally, our gene–environment interaction analysis aims to generate useful hypotheses for future research. We, thus, selected a relatively relaxed threshold (P < 0.10) to define a significant interaction. However, this strategy might have increased the probability of false-positive results. Therefore, large genome-wide interaction studies based on cross-population cohorts are needed to validate our findings and to attempt deeper exploration. Fourth, statistical methods applied in this study, except for MLR, do not provide P values, not allowing us to control for the false discovery rate for all models at a similar level. We thus arbitrarily took the top 10 important variables in those models as candidates and identified the significant exposures as those repeatedly chosen by at least two models. This strategy may have a higher false negative risk. Last, although a diverse set of covariates was considered, our findings may still have been affected by other unmeasured confounders.

Conclusion

We identified several prenatal environmental exposures within the ambient environment, phenols, and metal­(loid) families that are associated with birth weight using a comprehensive exposome analysis strategy. The effects of BPA, Ag, and Rb tended to be more pronounced in pregnancies with a high mother–neonate T2D genetic susceptibility. Our findings may have important public health implications. For example, applying stricter regulations on the production and usage of phenols, lowering air pollution and noise, increasing city greenspace, providing a preferred temperature environment, and strengthening self-protection may be needed to achieve optimal fetal growth during pregnancy. Individuals with high T2D genetic susceptibility may warrant particular attention.
