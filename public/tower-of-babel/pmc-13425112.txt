Environmental footprint of a low-fat vegan diet and Mediterranean diet: a secondary analysis of a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Arathi Jayaraman, Brighid McKay, Laura Chiavaroli, Songhee Back, Ilana Fischer, Reagan Smith, Richard Holubkov, Neal D Barnard, Hana Kahleova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlant-based foods, compared with animal-based foods, have a smaller environmental impact.ObjectiveThis secondary analysis used a randomised, crossover trial to calculate and compare the environmental impact of two plant-forward diets: low-fat vegan and Mediterranean.Methods62 overweight adults were randomly assigned to a low-fat vegan or Mediterranean diet for 16-week periods, separated by a 4-week washout. 3-day dietary records were analysed, and greenhouse gas emissions (GHGEs) and cumulative energy demand (CED) were calculated for completers of the study. The calculated GHGEs and CED relationship to changes in body weight were analysed.ResultsTotal calculated GHGEs and CED were reduced on the low-fat vegan diet by 57%, or by −1451.7 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −1776.5 to −1126.9; p<0.0001), and 55%, or by −9306.7 kJ/person-day (95% CI −11022 to −7591.5; p<0.0001), respectively. Total calculated GHGEs in the Mediterranean diet reduced by 20%, or by −451.3 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −859.7 to −42.8; p=0.03), and no change in calculated CED. In both treatment groups combined, changes in body weight correlated with changes in total CED (r=+0.41; p=0.004), even after adjustment for changes in energy intake (r=+0.34; p=0.02). The correlation between changes in body weight and changes in GHGEs did not reach statistical significance (r=+0.26; p=0.07).ConclusionCollectively, a low-fat vegan diet demonstrated statistically significant reductions in GHGEs, CED and weight loss compared with the non-plant-forward and Mediterranean diets, suggesting that a low-fat vegan diet can improve both human and planetary health.Trial registration number NCT03698955 Keywords: Dietary patterns, Weight management, Agriculture

Introduction

The greatest anthropogenic influence on global environmental change comes from the food sector.1 Environmental change is quantified by several measures, including greenhouse gas emissions (GHGEs) and cumulative energy demand (CED). The commercial production of food by farming contributes to approximately 30% of total GHGEs, with animal-based foods producing greater GHGEs than plant-based foods.2 3 Plant-forward diets, such as the Mediterranean (including dairy, lean white meats and fish, along with more modest amounts of other animal products) and vegan (only plant-based food sources) diets, have been compared to evaluate both environmental changes and human health benefits. Both the Mediterranean and vegan diets improve cardiovascular health. However, a low-fat vegan diet (only plant-based food sources with the exclusion of high-fat plant-based foods such as avocados, added oils, nuts and seeds) was shown in a randomised, crossover, clinical trial to improve body weight and composition, plasma lipids, insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance, among other variables, significantly more compared with a Mediterranean diet.4

This secondary analysis assessed the environmental footprints of the non-plant-forward baseline diet, the low-fat vegan diet and the Mediterranean diet, hypothesising that the calculated GHGEs and CED would be lowest for the low-fat vegan diet.

Methods

Study design

The overall study methods have been previously described.4 This randomised, crossover trial occurred in 2019 in Washington, DC, followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials reporting guidelines. The trial has since closed and is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT03698955).

Participants

All the participants provided written informed consent. The inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously described.4 Demographic data, such as sex/gender and race/ethnicity, were self-reported and collected via paper forms. The respondent population was demographically diverse, reflecting the profile of the greater Washington, DC, area.

Randomisation and masking

62 overweight adults were randomly assigned, using a computer-generated random-number table, in a one-to-one ratio to a low-fat vegan or Mediterranean diet for 16-week periods in random order, separated by a 4-week washout. Since the assignment was done simultaneously within each replication, allocation concealment was unnecessary. Participants were assessed at weeks 0, 16, 20 and 36.

Procedures

The low-fat vegan group was instructed to follow a diet consisting of fruits, vegetables, grains and legumes, and to exclude added oils, avocados, nuts and seeds. The Mediterranean diet was based on the PREDIMED trial protocol,5 with the addition of using extra virgin olive oil (50 g daily). No instructions on caloric intake, GHGEs or CED were given to either group. A 3-day dietary record was given to each participant at weeks 0, 16, 20 and 36. These were analysed by a registered dietitian certified in the Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR).6

Outcomes

Primary outcomes, including body weight, body composition, plasma lipids, insulin sensitivity, glucose tolerance and resting and postprandial energy expenditure, have been previously described.4

Among the secondary outcomes were the GHGEs and CED. Three independent reviewers (AJ, BM and SB) calculated the GHGEs and CED for each NDSR category using the US Department of Agriculture Food Commodity Intake Database7 and the database of Food Impacts on the Environment for Linking to Diets,8 then applied the GHGEs and CED factors to the intakes from the dietary records. Linking accuracy was verified by a senior researcher (LC). These researchers were blinded to group assignment throughout the process.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis, including target sample size calculation and power analysis, for the primary outcomes has previously been described.4

Statistical analyses for the GHGEs and CED secondary outcomes were conducted for all participants who had complete data across all timepoints, which was 50 out of 62. Two participants were excluded because they did not submit their diet records. Six participants from groups one and two withdrew during the Mediterranean diet period, and four from group one during the low-fat vegan period (online supplemental figure 1).

A crossover ANOVA model was employed, incorporating both between-subject and within-subject factors, as well as their interactions. Factors including diet, subject, time and period were incorporated into the model. Period-specific estimates of treatment effects were also calculated for all outcomes. Within each intervention, paired comparison t-tests were performed to determine if the changes from week 0 to 16 and from week 20 to 36 were statistically significant. Due to a detected carry-over effect (difference in the magnitude of treatment effect based on the first diet each participant received—either low-fat vegan or Mediterranean) for total GHGEs and CED, a repeated measures ANOVA was additionally performed to assess the treatment effects from the first study period (week 0 to 16) alone, providing a conservative estimate of treatment effect. The statistician was blinded to group assignments. The results are reported as means with 95% CIs. The treatment effect represents the mean difference between outcomes on the low-fat vegan and the Mediterranean diet. Spearman correlations were used to examine the relationship between changes in GHGEs and CED and changes in body mass index across both diets.

Results

Total calculated GHGEs were reduced on the low-fat, vegan diet by 57%, or by −1451.7 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −1776.5 to −1126.9; p<0.0001), compared with a reduction on the Mediterranean diet by 20%, or by −451.3 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −859.7 to −42.8; p=0.03), treatment effect: −1000.4 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −1507.9 to −493; p=0.0002; figure 1; table 1). The low-fat vegan group can attribute this to reduced meat consumption with an effect size of −826.1 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI−1237.4 to −414.9; p=0.0002), followed by reduced dairy consumption with an effect size of −222 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −367.9 to −76.1; p=0.0036), and reduced egg consumption with an effect size of −97.7 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −154.6 to −40.8; p=0.0012).

Total calculated CED decreased on the low-fat, vegan diet by 55%, or by −9306.7 kJ/person-day (95% CI −11022 to −7591.5; p<0.0001), while there was no change on the Mediterranean diet: treatment effect −9541.3 kJ/person-day (95% CI −12740.3 to −6342.3; p<0.0001; figure 1; table 1). Similar to before, the reduced CED in the low-fat vegan group was attributed to a reduced meat consumption with an effect size of −8757.9 kJ/person-day (95% CI−11351.3 to −6164.5; p<0.001), followed by reduced dairy consumption with an effect size of −1218 kJ/person-day (95% CI −1989.4 to −446.7; p=0.0026), and reduced egg consumption with an effect size of −588.3 kJ/person-day (95% CI −931 to −245.6; p=0.0012).

There was a statistically significant carryover effect for calculated total GHGEs (p<0.001) and total CED (p=0.01), so an ANOVA for repeated measures was performed, conservatively evaluating treatment effect from the first study period (week 0 to 16) alone: GHGEs −1173.9 g CO2 eq/person-day (95% CI −2051.9 to −296; p=0.01) and CED −10703.5 kJ/person-day (95% CI −15438.4 to −5968.7; p<0.0001; online supplemental table 1).

In both treatment groups combined, the correlation between changes in body weight and changes in GHGEs did not reach statistical significance (r=+0.26; p=0.07). However, changes in body weight correlated with changes in total CED (r=+0.41; p=0.004), even after adjustment for changes in energy intake (r=+0.34; p=0.02).

Discussion

This randomised crossover trial demonstrated that total calculated GHGEs and CED decreased by 57% and 55%, respectively, on the low-fat, vegan diet, compared with a non-plant-forward diet. The Mediterranean diet, compared with a non-plant-forward diet, was associated with a 20% reduction in GHGEs and no change in CED. These findings are consistent with a systematic review of 35 studies comparing a Mediterranean diet with various other diets, which showed calculated GHGEs associated with a vegan (not low-fat vegan) diet as 2.65 kg CO2-eq/person-day, compared with 3.41 kg CO2-eq/person-day for the Mediterranean diet.1 Another study examined 18 environmental impact factors and compared these values to the vegan (not low-fat vegan) and Mediterranean diets; the results showed a 44% decrease in total environmental impact on the vegan diet compared with the Mediterranean diet.9 Additionally, the vegan diet has been shown to have the lowest total GHGEs (1.63 kg CO2-eq) of all commercial and non-commercial diets, including the Mediterranean diet (2.17 kg CO2-eq).10 11

When comparing the non-plant-forward diet to a low-fat vegan diet and a Mediterranean diet, there are significantly more reductions in high GHGE-producing foods and increases in low GHGE-producing foods in the low-fat vegan diet.

It has been previously demonstrated that a low-fat vegan diet, compared with both a non-plant-forward diet and a Mediterranean diet, resulted in significantly more weight loss and cardiovascular management, among other variables.4 12

The strengths of the current trial include a randomised, crossover design. The study also has limitations, such as self-reported diet records (which have measurement constraints), participants may not represent the general population (although they may represent the population of individuals seeking to lose weight), and relatively short (16-week) intervention periods. A carry-over effect was detected and addressed with an ANOVA for repeated measures in the first 16 weeks, as a conservative estimate of the diet effect. This secondary analysis is exploratory and was not powered for GHGE/CED. The findings should be interpreted as associations within a randomised crossover framework.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that the low-fat vegan diet is associated with greater weight loss in overweight adults and reduced environmental impact.
