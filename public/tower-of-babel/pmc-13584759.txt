Psychopharmacology Treatment of Schizoaffective Disorder
Authors: David N. Osser
Published: 2026

The diagnosis of schizoaffective disorder (SAD) was intended, in recent editions of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), to be used rarely.1-3 However, clinicians frequently encounter patients with psychosis and mood symptoms together, and use the diagnosis very often—up to 30% of admissions for psychosis.4 It seems, however, that there is overuse of this diagnosis, because the criteria (which will be described) are much more stringent than clinicians may appreciate. Regardless of how it is diagnosed, clinicians seem to have an idea of how to treat this condition. Whatever ideas they have, though, they seem not to be based on any substantial science. The evidence base for psychopharmacologic treatment of SAD is minimal,5 and what prescribers are doing seems to be almost exclusively based on guesses based on how the medications work in patients with schizophrenia and bipolar disorders, with the assumption that given the mixture of symptoms these patients have, combinations of schizophrenia and bipolar medications may offer the best approach. Naturalistic surveys of “real-world” medication use in patients diagnosed with SAD according to local diagnostic patterns demonstrate that this is what is being done.6,7 Practitioners have their favorite treatments for patients they are calling schizoaffective, as demonstrated in these population studies, and those that have proposed guidelines offer rationales that are at best speculative.8

Paliperidone extended-release and paliperidone palmitate long-acting injection have been extensively studied and are the only United States Food and Drug Administration–approved treatments for the acute and maintenance treatment of SAD.5,9 These studies and approvals were based on patients who met the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for SAD. However, the criteria changed significantly with DSM-5 in 2013 (and then stayed the same with DSM-5-TR in 2022), and there have been no randomized controlled treatment trials of any medications for SAD patients meeting the new criteria in the 12 years since they appeared. A recent update of the US Department of Veterans Affairs and the US Department of Defense Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Bipolar Disorder acknowledges that the criteria for schizoaffective disorder have changed significantly in DSM-5 and DSM-5-TR and that this may affect the generalizability of all prior treatment results.10

Users of the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Mental and Behavioral Disorders (e.g., ICD-11) will note that the criteria for schizoaffective disorder are quite different from those of the American Psychiatric Association’s DSM criteria. Industry-sponsored and other psychopharmacology research studies, and most journals, require that subjects be diagnosed using the DSM criteria, and consequently the psychopharmacology evidence base is most closely tied to these criteria, hence our particular interest in their use by clinicians.

A short narrative about the change from the DSM-IV to the DSM-5 SAD criteria is necessary at this point. The DSM-5 committee actually wanted to eliminate the diagnosis of SAD. This was because the evidence for the validity of the disorder, as defined by the criteria in DSM-IV, was quite weak.11-13 One consideration was that the DSM-IV SAD criteria were found to be unstable over time: long-term follow-up studies found that many patients would go on to meet the criteria for schizophrenia.14 Patients meeting DSM-IV schizophrenia criteria, by contrast, retained their diagnosis over time. Studies of familial transmission of psychotic disorders diagnosed by DSM-IV found that SAD probands do not have increased rates of SAD patients in their families, whereas proband patients diagnosed with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder do have significantly increased incidences of those disorders, respectively, in their families. The SAD probands do have increased rates of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in their families, but not as much of either as do the probands with those diagnoses. These familial data suggested that DSM-IV SAD does not exist as a distinct entity, but comprises a mixture of patients, some of whom are in the schizophrenia spectrum and some in the bipolar spectrum of disorders.

So, the initial drafts of the DSM-5 criteria did not include the diagnosis of SAD. However, this decision was criticized. To address these concerns but also to respect the evidence pertaining to the diagnostic validity of SAD, the new criteria were made even more stringent and more difficult to meet, compared to those in DSM-IV. Many patients who would have been diagnosed with SAD in DSM-IV would now be diagnosed with schizophrenia in DSM-5. The major change was in the quantity of time the patient could be in a mood syndrome. The DSM-IV schizophrenia criteria said a mood syndrome could be present, but for no more than a “brief” time compared to the total duration of all phases of the illness, acute and residual. In the text discussion of schizophrenia, an example of “brief” was given of someone having an episode for 5 weeks but had been ill for a total of 4 years. That would be 2.5% of the total duration of illness in a major depressive episode or mania. Reciprocally, in the DSM-IV schizoaffective criteria, anyone with more than this brief amount of time in a mood syndrome could have SAD.

This was changed. In the DSM-5 “D” criterion for schizophrenia, they made it so that the patient can be in a mood syndrome for “a minority” of the total duration of illness and still be diagnosed with schizophrenia. That means the patient could be in a mood syndrome for up to 49% of the time! Those spending 50% or more of the total time of their illness in a mood syndrome could (if they have the rest of the criteria) be diagnosed with SAD. Probably, there will not be many people who meet the new SAD criteria, which (like the older criteria) still require a 2-week period of having A criteria schizophrenia symptoms with no major mood episode. I rarely see patients meeting these criteria in my practice, which involves a lot of consulting on people with treatment-resistant bipolar disorder with psychosis. And, as noted above, there have been absolutely no published psychopharmacology randomized controlled trials with patients meeting the current criteria that came out in 2013.

With this background, I am now ready to tell you how I think SAD by the DSM-5 or DSM-5-TR criteria could be treated with pharmacotherapy. This is a working hypothesis. It will be brief, but at least it is consistent with the familial transmission data described briefly above.

After you have completed your careful diagnostic and biopsychosocial evaluation of the patient, and concluded that the patient meets the DSM-5 criteria for schizoaffective disorder, make a guess as to whether this patient’s overall presentation seems closer to something in the schizophrenia spectrum of disorders or something in the bipolar spectrum. Then consult your favorite evidence-derived practice guideline for either schizophrenia or bipolar (mania or depression, depending on the current prominent mood symptoms) and select a treatment. If the results are unsatisfactory after going through a few reasonable options, or steps of the algorithm if one is provided, consider that your guess about diagnosis may have been incorrect, and switch over to treating the patient with your best guideline for the other disorder. For example, if your guess is that the patient belongs in the schizophrenia spectrum, a schizophrenia psychopharmacology algorithm of employing 2 adequate monotherapy trials of antipsychotics followed by consideration of clozapine15 probably still applies for DSM-5-TR schizophrenia. Some of the observational data actually support antipsychotics being the most reliable treatments for variously diagnosed SAD, and that adding antidepressants, mood stabilizers, or benzodiazepines (as is commonly done) seemed “controversial” with respect to improving outcome.7
