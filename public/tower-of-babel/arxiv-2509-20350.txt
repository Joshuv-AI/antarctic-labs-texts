Nonlocal Games and Self-tests in the Presence of Noise
Honghao Fu, Minglong Qin, Haochen Xu, Penghui Yao

Abstract
Self-testing is a key characteristic of certain nonlocal games, which allow one to uniquely determine the underlying quantum state and measurement operators used by the players, based solely on their observed input-output correlations [ MY04 ] . Motivated by the limitations of current quantum devices, we study self-testing in the high-noise regime , where the two players are restricted to sharing many copies of a noisy entangled state with an arbitrary constant noise rate. In this setting, many existing self-tests fail to certify any nontrivial structure. We first characterize the maximal winning probabilities of the CHSH game [ CHSH69 ] , the Magic Square game [ Mer90a ] , and the 2-out-of- n n CHSH game [ CRSV18 ] as functions of the noise rate, under the assumption that players use traceless observables. These results enable the construction of device-independent protocols for estimating the noise rate. Building on this analysis, we show that these three games—–together with an additional test enforcing the tracelessness of binary observables—–can self-test one, two, and n n pairs of anticommuting Pauli operators, respectively. These are the first known self-tests that are robust in the high-noise regime and remain sound even when the players’ measurements are noisy. Our proofs rely on Sum-of-Squares (SoS) decompositions and Pauli analysis techniques developed in the contexts of quantum proof systems and quantum learning theory.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Nonlocal games are cooperative tasks involving multiple spatially separated players who are not allowed to communicate during the game.
Each player receives a question and must produce an answer, and their joint responses are evaluated according to a predetermined winning condition.
Such games provide a powerful framework for exploring the nonlocal correlations exhibited by entangled quantum systems
[
Bel64
]
, as demonstrated in canonical examples like the CHSH game
[
CHSH69
]
and the Magic Square game
[
Ara04
]
.

A particularly important property of certain nonlocal games is
self-testing
, which allow one to uniquely (up to local isometries) determine the underlying quantum state and measurement operators used by the players, based solely on their observed input-output correlations
[
MY04
]
.
It has been proved that the CHSH game can self-test one EPR pair
\left|\mathrm{EPR}\right\rangle=(\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
and single Pauli measurements
[
MYS12
]
,
and the Magic square game can self-test
\left|\mathrm{EPR}\right\rangle^{\otimes 2}
and tensor products of Pauli measurements
[
WBMS16
]
.
Self-testing provides a device-independent approach to certifying quantum behavior, with profound implications for quantum cryptography
[
MS16
,
VV19
]
, quantum computational complexity
[
NW19
,
JNV+20a
,
JNV+20b
]
, and foundational studies in quantum mechanics
[
CMV24
,
FMS25
]
.

A central feature of any self-test is its
robustness to noise
, i.e., its ability to tolerate deviations from the ideal quantum state and measurements.
For depolarizing noise, the CHSH game and the Magic square game can tolerate a small constant noise rate
[
MYS12
,
WBMS16
]
.
However, self-tests of
\left|\mathrm{EPR}\right\rangle^{\otimes n}
suffer from poor robustness, tolerating at most
1/\operatorname{polylog}(n)
noise strength
[
JNV+20a
]
.
Given that noise is inherent in physical quantum systems, these limitations pose a challenge for making self-tests more practical.

In the NISQ and early fault-tolerant era of quantum computing, detecting and characterizing noise is both practically important and theoretically intriguing. This motivates the study of nonlocal games in the high-noise regime, where self-testing may still be possible despite significant imperfections. Previous research on self-test assumes that players could share
any
state in
any
dimension. This assumption may be extravagant, especially given the current quantum technology.

In this paper, we initiate the study of self-testing under high noise. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first study of self-testing in the high-noise regime. We consider the following realistic scenario: the players’ devices cannot prepare arbitrary quantum states but can repeatedly generate many copies of a given noisy entangled state. However, their local measurements are assumed to be arbitrarily sophisticated. Noise may arise from imperfections in the state preparation or the quantum channels used to establish entanglement. Self-testing with this assumption falls short of the strongest notion of device independence. However, certain assumptions about the state and noise are unavoidable. To see it, non-locality could vanish for some shared states with high noise, allowing classical strategies to reproduce noisy quantum correlations, which makes any self-testing of measurement operators impossible without any restriction on the noise. For instance, consider a noisy CHSH game where the players share a
2n
-qubit (
n
pair of qubits) state
\Psi^{\prime}=(1-\delta)^{n}\left|\mathrm{EPR}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\mathrm{EPR}\right|^{\otimes n}+(1-(1-\delta)^{n})I/4^{n}
, and they employ the observables
(X,Z,(X+Z)/\sqrt{2},(X-Z)/\sqrt{2})
, which is the optimal strategy for a noiseless CHSH game, on one pair of states. Then the quantum correlation they generate contains no non-locality, as any pair of the qubits in
\Psi^{\prime}
contains no entanglement when
n
is sufficiently large. However, if the players share
\Psi^{\prime\prime}=(\rho\left|\mathrm{EPR}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\mathrm{EPR}\right|+(1-\rho)I/4)^{\otimes n}
with
\rho=1-4\delta/3
. Both
\Psi^{\prime}
and
\Psi^{\prime\prime}
has the same fidelity with noiseless EPR states, i.e,
F(\Psi^{\prime},\left|\mathrm{EPR}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\mathrm{EPR}\right|^{\otimes n})=F(\Psi^{\prime\prime},\left|\mathrm{EPR}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\mathrm{EPR}\right|^{\otimes n})
. However, the quantum correlation the players generate with the same observables is non-local if
\rho>1/\sqrt{2}
.

In fact, our assumptions remain weak. We do not fix either the number of copies or the noise level, yet we will show that we can still draw strong conclusions about the strategies of the players.
More specifically, in this work, we assume that the players share unbounded copies of depolarized EPR pairs.

The high-noise regime raises the following fundamental questions that we aim to answer:

Question 1.1
.

What are the winning probabilities of nonlocal games in the high-noise regime?

Question 1.2
.

Can we design nonlocal games or protocols that self-test quantum states and measurements under high noise?

While previous work has addressed aspects of

Main Results

\frac{17}{18}

Definition 1.3

(Noisy Nonlocal Games)

•

Maximum Winning Probability under Traceless Condition.

While
[
HHH95
]
has derived tight maximal CHSH violation sharing a fixed bipartite state
\psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, the optimal violation of the CHSH game sharing multiple copies of the depolarized EPR pairs remains open. We add the traceless condition to derive the first tight bounds for not only the noisy CHSH game but also the noisy Magic Square game and the noisy 2-out-of-
n
CHSH game, making it possible to design self-testing protocols.

Theorem 1.4
.

If the players use traceless observables, then

1.

The maximum winning probability of the noisy CHSH game is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\rho
(equivalently, the maximal violation is
2\sqrt{2}\rho
).

2.

The maximum winning probability of the noisy Magic Square game is
\frac{1+\rho}{2}
.

3.

The maximum winning probability of the noisy 2-out-of-
n
CHSH game is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\rho
.

The traceless condition is easily satisfied in experiments. In fact, we can design a trace test to enforce this condition, which will be introduced with the self-testing results.

These maximum winning probabilities can all be attained using the same optimal observables as in the noiseless cases. Since these results are proved for arbitrary
n
, more entanglement does not help achieve a higher winning probability in the noisy setting either. Moreover, we show that when the players use observables with normalized traces bounded by
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}
, the winning probabilities increase by at most
O\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}\right)
.

This theorem provides the noise tolerance bound for these three games: the noisy CHSH game and its 2-out-of-
n
version can tolerate single-qubit depolarizing noise with rate
\sqrt{1-\sqrt{2}/2}\approx 0.54
since

2\sqrt{2}\rho>2
implies that
\rho>1/\sqrt{2}
. The noisy Magic Square game can tolerate depolarizing noise of rate
\sqrt{1-17/18}\approx 0.23
on each 2-qubit register.

These results allow us to use these results to build
device-independent noise rate testers
.
When the noise rate of the shared EPR pairs is unknown, two spatially separated players can play these games repeatedly to learn the noise rate from the winning probability. One application scenario is in a quantum network, where multipartite entangled resource states are distributed and stored among the nodes.
These shared quantum resources can be affected by noise arising from imperfections in state preparation, memories, and measurements
[
MRD24
]
.
Then these device-independent noise rate testers can be applied.

Self-testing in the Presence of Noise

Having derived the maximum winning probabilities of the noisy nonlocal games under the traceless condition, we now analyze their robust self-testing properties.
Since the shared quantum state is known, our self-testing results focus on certifying the binary observables used by the players.
We prove that in the noisy setting, the CHSH game can self-test Pauli
X
and
Z

the Magic Square game can self-test Pauli
X
and
Z

n

X
and
Z

n

First, we introduce the
Trace Test

We assume these repetitions are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) trials. Passing this test ensures the observables are approximately traceless. Here, the i.i.d. assumption is both easily approximated in experiments and widely used in self-testing. When we need to certify that a certain strategy achieves a given winning probability, we need the players to repeat the protocol in an i.i.d. manner, which allows us to conclude the winning probability with a high level of confidence.

1.3
Proof Overview

Our approach employs Pauli analysis
[
MO10
,
NPVY24
]
, and the technique of sum-of-squares (SoS) decomposition.

Noisy CHSH Game

Let the Pauli expansion of the observable
Q
be

Q=\sum_{x\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}^{n}}\widehat{Q}\left(x\right)\sigma_{x},

where
\sigma_{x}=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{x_{i}}
and
\sigma_{0}=I,\sigma_{1}=X,\sigma_{2}=Y,\sigma_{3}=Z
are Pauli matrices.
If the shared state is
\Phi_{\rho}^{\otimes n}
, by analyzing the correlation matrix of noisy EPR pairs, we can show that the correlation can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(P\otimes Q\right)\Phi_{\rho}^{\otimes n}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(P\otimes Q^{\prime}\right)\mathrm{EPR}^{\otimes n}\right)=\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P^{T}Q^{\prime}\right).

Here, the noisy observable

Q^{\prime}=\sum_{x\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}^{n}}\rho^{\left|x\right|}\widehat{Q}\left(x\right)\sigma_{x},

and normalized trace
\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q\right)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Q\right)
.
This transformation allows us to work in the well-understood framework for perfect EPR pairs, but with modified observables.

From the definition of
Q^{\prime}
, the depolarizing noise attenuates higher-degree terms exponentially by a factor of
\rho^{\left|x\right|}
per Pauli term
\sigma_{x}
, where
\left|x\right|
is the degree of the term.
When
Q
is traceless, it does not contain Pauli terms of degree
0
. Consequently, every term will decay at least
\rho
times after applying noise on
Q
. By Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q^{\prime}\right)^{2}\leq\rho^{2}\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}Q^{2}.

(1)

Thus, for the noisy CHSH game using traceless observables, we can derive an upper bound of the violation using Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality:

violation

\displaystyle=\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{0}^{T}\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)\right)+\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{1}^{T}\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{0}^{T}\right)^{2}\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}+\sqrt{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{1}^{T}\right)^{2}\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left(\sqrt{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{0}+Q_{1}\right)^{2}}+\sqrt{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{0}-Q_{1}\right)^{2}}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{2}\rho.

Moreover this upper bound is attainable using canonical strategies
\left(Z,X,\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
.
Note that the traceless condition is crucial for
Eq.

1
and the violation proof.

For the self-testing of the noisy CHSH game, our proof proceeds in several steps.
First, we transform the analysis of players sharing noisy EPR pairs into an equivalent scenario where the players share ideal EPR pairs but apply
noisy observables
instead.
The key observatinon of our self-testing proof is that, if the players’ violation is close to
2\sqrt{2}\rho
, then their observables must concentrate on degree-one terms under the traceless condition.

We give a rigorous proof using a method similar to sum-of-squares (SoS) decomposition for the CHSH inequality.
In the noisy CHSH game, we can move the noise effect onto observables to get the following SoS decomposition:

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}\rho I-P_{0}\otimes\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)+P_{1}\otimes\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left(P_{0}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}\right)^{2}+\left(P_{1}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}\right)^{2}\right)+\sqrt{2}\rho I\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}.

Thus when the expectation of observable
C_{1}
is close to zero (i.e., the violation is close to
2\sqrt{2}\rho
), we can derive that the square terms in
C_{1}
are close to zero, and
Q_{i}^{\prime}
is close to
\rho Q_{i}
. These two conditions imply that
P_{0}\approx\frac{Q_{0}^{T}+Q_{1}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}},P_{1}\approx\frac{Q_{0}^{T}-Q_{1}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}}
. Direct calculation yields
\left\{P_{0},P_{1}\right\}\approx 0
.

Moreover, the condition
Q_{i}^{\prime}\approx\rho Q_{i}
also implies that we can truncate the Pauli terms with degree greater than one, causing only a small error.
Combining this observation with the fact
Q_{i}
is a binary observable, we can show that
Q_{i}
acts non-trivially on only one register.
In previous studies
[
MYS12
]
, swap isometries are used to extract Pauli observables. In contrast, here we only need unitaries that act on one qubit since the observables are concentrated on a single register.

Noisy Magic Square Game

Under the traceless requirement, we can show that the players can pass the consistency test with probability at most
\frac{1+\rho}{2}
using a similar Pauli decomposition method. Thus if the winning rate is close to this bound, the players’ strategies can also be self-tested. We prove that rigorously via a sum-of-square decomposition of the noisy consistency observable:

\displaystyle C_{1}=P\otimes Q^{\prime}=-\frac{\rho}{2}\left(P\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{Q^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}I+\frac{1}{2\rho}I\otimes Q^{\prime 2}.

Analysis of
C_{1}
yields two key properties for Alice’s observables:

1.

For any variable
s_{i,j}
,
P_{i,j}^{\mathrm{row}}\approx P_{i,j}^{\mathrm{column}}
. Thus we omit the superscripts of
P_{i,j}
.

2.

For any two variables
s_{i,j},s_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
of the same row or column,
\left[P_{i,j},P_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right]\approx 0
.

Here
P_{i,j}^{\mathrm{row}}
(resp.
P_{i,j}^{\mathrm{column}}
) is Alice’s observable on the
i
-th row,
j
-column variable
s_{i,j}
when receiving
r_{i}
(resp.
c_{j}
). The first property holds because both observables are close to
Q_{i}^{T}
. The second property is more technically involved.
In the noiseless setting, the measurements can be assumed to be projective using Naimark’s theorem, and in that case, commutativity follows naturally. However, in the noisy setting, since the form of the shared state is constrained, Naimark’s theorem is no longer directly applicable.
Instead, we express
P_{i,j}
and
P_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
in terms of the corresponding POVMs:

\displaystyle P_{i,j}\approx E_{0}-E_{1}+E_{2}-E_{3},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ P_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\approx E_{0}-E_{1}-E_{2}+E_{3},

where
\left\{E_{i}\right\}
are Alice’s correct POVMs associated with the common row or column shared by
s_{i,j},s_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
.
Since both
P_{i,j}
and
P_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
are binary observables, we can derive that
E_{0}+E_{2},E_{1}+E_{3},E_{0}+E_{3},E_{1}+E_{2}
are all approximately projective. Further matrix analysis shows that
E_{i}E_{j}\approx 0
for
i\neq j
and
E_{i}^{2}\approx E_{i}
. Thereby we justify the commutativity without invoking Naimark’s theorem.

Following previous self-testing proof of the Magic Square game, we can derive that both
\left(P_{1,1},P_{2,2}\right)
and
\left(Q_{1,1},Q_{2,2}\right)
almost anti-commute, act on the same 4-dimensional register, and can be extracted into Pauli matrices via simple unitaries.

Noisy 2-out-of-
n
CHSH Game

We adopt a similar proof framework as that in
[
CRSV18
]
. In the protocol, the referee randomly chooses two indices
i,j
, and sends both of the indices to Bob together with two CHSH questions
y,z
, and one of them (say
i
) to Alice together with one CHSH question
x
. On the shared index
i
, the players’ responses are tested by the CHSH predicate.

For any shared index
i
, the noisy CHSH self-testing
Theorem

1.5
forces
\left(P_{i,0},P_{i,1}\right)
to be anti-commuting and to be concentrated on single register.

To derive the commutation relations of observables for distinct indices, we consider two scenarios:

1.

Alice receives
\left\{\left(i,x\right)\right\}
, Bob receives
\left\{\left(i,y\right),\left(j,z\right)\right\}
;

2.

Alice receives
\left\{\left(j,x^{\prime}\right)\right\}
, Bob receives
\left\{\left(i,y\right),\left(j,z\right)\right\}
.

Bob who receives both indices cannot distinguish which one is tested with Alice. Thus his marginal strategies for indices

i
and
j
(obtained by summing POVMs over index
j
and

i
, respectively) must
simultaneously
form near-optimal strategies with Alice’s observables for the corresponding indices.
By similar proof techniques used in the noisy Magic Square game, we can prove that Bob’s POVMs are nearly projective, thus his two marginal strategies for indices
i
and
j
commute. The above will force:

\displaystyle\left[P_{i,x},P_{j,x^{\prime}}\right]\approx 0.

Moreover we have that
P_{i,x}
and
P_{j,x^{\prime}}
act on distinct registers. Otherwise,
P_{i,0}
and
P_{i,1}
anti-commute and
P_{j,x^{\prime}}
commutes with both of them, which is impossible within one register.

1.4
Discussion and Future Directions

Our characterization of the maximal winning probabilities in noisy nonlocal games represents a foundational step toward understanding the noise robustness of nonlocal games. These results also position nonlocal games as practical, device-independent tools for estimating the noise level in a shared quantum system. Furthermore, our self-testing results provide a precise description of the players’ binary observables, making them particularly useful for a verifier in a proof system, just like previous self-tests, and hence relevant for analyzing quantum proof systems in the presence of noise. Finally, our work highlights fundamental differences between self-testing in the noisy versus noiseless settings, revealing new structural and technical challenges unique to the high-noise regime.

Register Concentration.

In the noiseless setting, local isometries are necessary to isolate the observables onto a specific register. In contrast, in the presence of noise, we can
directly
prove that the players’ observables act non-trivially on only one register without extra operation.

Simple Unitary.

As a result of the first item, to extract a Pauli pair
\left(Z,X\right)
, we only need to apply a unitary on one qubit. In comparison, for the noiseless case, we use a swap isometry, which introduces an extra qubit and operates on the whole system. This result partly stem from fixing state, a feature different from previous setting, but not obvious since the provers can share arbitrarily many copies of noisy EPR pairs, i.e., the dimension can be arbitrarily large.

Measurement Projectivity.

Noiseless self-testing results can focus on projective measurements because Naimark’s theorem implies that for any quantum strategy with POVMs, there is another quantum strategy
with projective measurements that achieves the same winning probability. However, in the noisy setting, the shared state is fixed, and we cannot apply Naimark’s theorem. Thus, we use new insights and techniques to establish the projectivity of the measurements.

Noise Tolerance.

Previous work
[
Kan16
]
shows that for the CHSH game, if the violation exceeds
\frac{16+14\sqrt{2}}{17}\approx 2.11
, a non-trivial fidelity between the shared state and the EPR state can be inferred. In comparison, in the noisy CHSH game, if we compare to all classical strategies, our self-testing results for observables hold for any fidelity parameter
\rho\in\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},1\right)
. Moreover, any classical strategy that passes the trace test can succeed in the CHSH game with probability at most
\frac{1}{2}
. Since quantum strategies can succeed with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\rho}{4}
, the self-testing results hold for all
\rho\in(0,1)
when compared against such classical strategies.

Our result has connection with quantum complexity theory.
Self-testing is a central tool in question reduction for the compression of
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}
protocols, which is a crucial component in the proof of the seminal result
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}=\mathrm{RE}

[
JNV+20a
,
JNV+20b
]
. Extending self-tests to noisy settings is an essential step towards understanding the computational power of noisy
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}
protocols. We further hope that future work will realize such reductions via the noisy 2-out-of-
n
CHSH game, as the 2-out-of-
n
magic square game has been used in gapless compression of
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}
protocols
[
MNY22
]
.

Our result also raises some natural but intriguing questions. We list some of them below.

1.

What are the maximal violations for noisy CHSH and noisy Magic Square games without the traceless condition? In our paper we use a trace test to ensure that the observables have bounded trace.
It will complete the picture if the maximum winning probability can be derived and
similar self-testing results can be proved without the traceless condition.

2.

What are the maximum winning probabilities when players share other types of noisy quantum states?
In our paper we primarily focus on depolarizing noise and extend our analysis to more general types of noise. However, the question remains open: In the CHSH game where the players share multiple copies of a fixed state
\psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, what is the maximal achievable violation? Here we impose no additional constraints on
\psi
. Moreover, for the noisy Magic Square game, we analyze the winning probability when a depolarizing channel is applied to a 4-qubit system. Determining the optimal winning probability under qubit depolarizing noise and more general noise remains an open problem.

3.

Can we develop a communication-efficient self-testing protocol that is robust to constant noise on both the state and the observables? The 2-out-of-
n
CHSH protocol is communication efficient, as the questions are of length
O(\log n)
and the answers are at most 2 bits, but it is not robust to constant noise on the observables. Can we borrow ideas from the linearity test of
[
NV17
]
or the Pauli basis test of
[
JNV+20a
]

to design a simultaneous self-test of
n
anti-commuting Pauli operators that is also robust to constant noise on the observables?
Such a test might be useful when studying quantum proof systems in the noisy setting.

A quantum system

A

\sum_{i\in S}E_{i}=I
.

if each element

satisfies

for all

. When

, we have
\|R\|\leq 1
, and we say that the observable

is a

if

.
Naimark’s theorem states that any general POVM can be realized as a projective measurement on a larger Hilbert space by introducing an ancillary system and applying a unitary transformation followed by a standard projective measurement.
For convenience, we use

and

\displaystyle\left|\Phi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle\right),\qquad\Phi=\left|\Phi\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Phi\right|.

In fact, the 4-dimensional depolarizing channel acting on EPR states is equivalent to applying 2-dimensional (qubit) depolarizing channels independently on both subsystems of the EPR state. This equivalence holds because

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rho}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(I\otimes I+\rho X\otimes X+\rho Y\otimes\bar{Y}+\rho Z\otimes Z\bigr)=\Delta_{\rho_{1}}^{\left(2\right)}\otimes\Delta_{\rho_{2}}^{\left(2\right)}\left(\Phi\right),

for
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}=\rho
.

Fact 2.12
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{H}_{2^{n}}
be decomposed under the
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
bases:

P=\sum_{x\in[4]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\widehat{P}(x)\,\mathcal{A}_{x},\qquad Q=\sum_{x\in[4]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\widehat{Q}(x)\,\mathcal{B}_{x}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P\otimes Q)(\Delta_{\rho}(\Phi))^{\otimes n}\bigr)=\sum_{x\in[4]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\rho^{|x|}\widehat{P}(x)\widehat{Q}(x)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P\otimes Q^{\prime})\Phi^{\otimes n}\bigr)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P^{\prime}\otimes Q)\Phi^{\otimes n}\bigr).

Similarly, let
P,Q\in\mathcal{H}_{4^{n}}
be decomposed under
\mathcal{A}^{\otimes 2}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\otimes 2}
:

P=\sum_{x\in[16]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\widehat{P}(x)\,\mathcal{A}^{\otimes 2}_{x},\qquad Q=\sum_{x\in[16]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\widehat{Q}(x)\,\mathcal{B}^{\otimes 2}_{x}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P\otimes Q)(\Delta_{\rho}(\Phi^{\otimes 2}))^{\otimes n}\bigr)=\sum_{x\in[16]_{\geq 0}^{n}}\rho^{|x|}\widehat{P}(x)\widehat{Q}(x)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P\otimes Q^{\prime})\Phi^{\otimes 2n}\bigr)=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((P^{\prime}\otimes Q)\Phi^{\otimes 2n}\bigr).

One important and well-studied nonlocal game is the CHSH game introduced in

(CHSH game)

\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mu,V\right)

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\left\{0,1\right\}

In

Self-testing is a key property of certain nonlocal games. It certifies that when a strategy achieves a near-optimal violation, the shared state and measurements are close to the ideal strategy (up to local isometries). For the CHSH game, we have:

Theorem 2.17
.

[
MYS12
, Theorem 2]

For any quantum strategy with local dimension

and using observables

that attains violation

, there exists local isometries
V_{A},V_{B}:{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2d}
, such that:

•

\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\right)\left|\varphi\right\rangle\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left|\Phi\right\rangle\otimes\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle
,

•

\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\right)\left(P_{0}\otimes I\right)\left|\varphi\right\rangle\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(Z\otimes I\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle\otimes\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle
,

\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\right)\left(P_{1}\otimes I\right)\left|\varphi\right\rangle\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(X\otimes I\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle\otimes\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle
,

•

\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\right)\left(I\otimes Q_{0}\right)\left|\varphi\right\rangle\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(I\otimes\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle\otimes\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle
,

\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\right)\left(I\otimes Q_{1}\right)\left|\varphi\right\rangle\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(I\otimes\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle\otimes\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle

for some state
\left|\text{junk}\right\rangle\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

A standard proof of

first establishes anti-commutation relations between observables, and then constructs isometries via a swap method.

3
Self-testing via the Noisy CHSH Game

3.1
Robust Self-testing with Depolarized EPR Pairs

This section develops a self-testing protocol for the noisy CHSH game, which is the same as the CHSH game introduced in
Definition

2.16
except that the players share
n
copies of depolarized EPR pairs
\left(\Delta_{\rho}\left(\Phi\right)\right)^{\otimes n}
for a constant fidelity parameter
\rho\in\left(0,1\right)
.

The players’ strategies are denoted by
P_{0},P_{1},Q_{0},Q_{1}
. To self-test via the noisy CHSH game, the protocol comprises two phases:

1.

Repeated CHSH game executions.

2.

A
trace test
verifying unbiased measurement outcomes.

We assume independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) repetitions throughout.

Assumption 3.1
.

Alice and Bob use a fixed strategy and share new
n
copies of depolarized EPR pairs
\left(\Delta_{\rho}\left(\Phi\right)\right)^{\otimes n}
each repetition.

Protocol 3.2

(
Noisy CHSH Test
)
.

Set parameters
t
(minimum question repetitions per player-question pair),
\delta
(bias threshold) and
p
(the minimum passing probability) satisfying
\delta=\left(\frac{2\ln\left(2/p\right)}{t}\right)^{1/2}
first.

1.

CHSH Repetitions
: The referee conducts CHSH rounds for
t^{\prime}
times, ensuring each player
D\in\left\{A,B\right\}
receives each question
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}
for at least
t
times.

2.

Trace Test
: For any player
D\in\left\{A,B\right\}
and question
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, the referee rejects if
\left|\hat{p}_{D}\left(a|x\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right|\geq\delta
, where
\hat{p}_{D}\left(a|x\right)
is the frequency of player
D
answering
a
when asked question
x
, computed over the first
t
question-
x
trials. Otherwise the referee accepts.

It is easy to see that the expectation of the total repetition time
\bigE[t^{\prime}]=\Theta\left(t\right)
.

We first show that passing the trace test with high probability bounds the observables’ traces.
Define

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}=\max\left\{\left|\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{0}\right)\right|,\left|\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{1}\right)\right|,\left|\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{0}\right)\right|,\left|\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{1}\right)\right|\right\}.

(6)

Fact 3.3

(Hoeffding’s inequality)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
be independent random variables such that
a_{i}\leq\mathbf{X}_{i}\leq b_{i}
. Let
\mathbf{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Then

\Pr\left[\left|\mathbf{S}-\bigE[\mathbf{S}]\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

Proposition 3.4
.

If the players pass
3.2
with probability at least
p
, we have
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq 3\delta
for
\delta=\left(\frac{2\ln\left(2/p\right)}{t}\right)^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Given question
x
Alice answers
1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{x}\right)}{2}
and
-1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{x}\right)}{2}
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}=\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(P_{0}\right)
.
Suppose that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}>3\delta
. Let
\mathbf{X}_{i}
be the answer of Alice given question 0 for the
i
-th time, then
\bigE[\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}
. By
3.3
we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{Pass the Trace Test}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right|\leq 2\delta t\right]\leq\Pr\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbf{X}_{i}-t\bigE\mathbf{X}_{1}\right|\geq\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}-2\delta\right)t\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}-2\delta\right)^{2}t}{2}\right)<2\exp{\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}t\right)}.

For
t=\frac{2\ln\left(2/p\right)}{\delta^{2}}
then the passing probability will be less than
p
, contradiction.
∎

In the following, we provide self-testing results for players using trace-bounded strategies in the noisy CHSH game.

We first give an upper bound of the violation given that the traces of observables are bounded.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose the players share
\left(\Delta_{\rho}\left(\Phi\right)\right)^{\otimes n}
, then the violation of the noisy CHSH game is at most
2\sqrt{2}\rho+\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}}{\rho}
for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}
defined in
Eq.

6
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle C=

\displaystyle P_{0}\otimes\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)+P_{1}\otimes\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left(P_{0}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{Q_{0}^{\prime}+Q_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}\right)^{2}+\left(P_{1}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{Q_{0}^{\prime}-Q_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}\right)^{2}\right)+I\otimes\frac{\left(Q_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(Q_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\rho}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}P_{0}^{2}\otimes I+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}P_{1}^{2}\otimes I.

For each
j\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, the trace bound implies

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi^{\otimes n}\right|I\otimes Q_{j}^{\prime 2}\left|\Phi^{\otimes n}\right\rangle=\widebar{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(Q_{j}^{\prime 2}\right)=\hat{Q}_{j}\left(0^{n}\right)^{2}+\sum_{x\neq 0^{n}}\rho^{2|x|}\hat{Q}_{j}\left(x\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}+\rho^{2}.

(7)

Also
P_{0}^{2}\preceq I,P_{1}^{2}\preceq I
. By
2.12
, the violation is
\left\langle\Phi^{\otimes n}\right|C\left|\Phi^{\otimes n}\right\rangle
, upper bounded by
2\sqrt{2}\rho+\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}}{\rho}
.
∎

In particular, if the observables are all traceless, by
Theorem

3.5
the maximal violation is
2\sqrt{2}\rho
attained by
\left(Z,X,\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
.

The following theorem demonstrates that a near-optimal violation, combined with trace-bounded observables implies strong structural properties, which can be used to self-test observables.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose the players share
\left(\Delta_{\rho}\left(\Phi\right)\right)^{\otimes n}
, where
\rho\in\left(0,1\right)
. Assume the following:

1.

The players can succeed in
3.2
with parameters

\delta=\left(\frac{2\ln\left(2/p\right)}{t}\right)^{1/2}

p

2.

The players’ strategies attain a violation
2\sqrt{2}\rho-\epsilon_{v}
in a single noisy CHSH game.

Define parameters

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{1-\rho}>1,\qquad\epsilon=\epsilon_{v}+9\delta^{2}.

Then there exists a register index
k\in[n]
, satisfying:

•

For
D\in\left\{P,Q\right\},j\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, there exist 2-dimensional traceless observables
\tilde{D}_{j}^{\left(k\right)}
, such that

\displaystyle D_{j}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}\gamma}\tilde{D}_{j}^{\left(k\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{k\right\}}.

(8)

•

There exists unitaries
U_{A},U_{B}
acting non-trivially only on the
k

\displaystyle U_{A}P_{0}U_{A}^{*}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}\gamma}Z^{\left(k\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{k\right\}},

\displaystyle U_{A}P_{1}U_{A}^{*}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}\gamma}X^{\left(k\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{k\right\}},

\displaystyle U_{B}Q_{0}U_{B}^{*}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}\gamma}Z^{\left(k\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{k\right\}},

\displaystyle U_{B}Q_{1}U_{B}^{*}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon}\gamma}X^{\left(k\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{k\right\}}.

The first conclusion of
Theorem

implies that the observables are concentrated on a single register. In contrast, for the noiseless case, an optimal strategy may measure on multiple registers. That is, adding noise forces the players to use only one pair of states to play the game. The second conclusion shows that to extract Pauli
Z
and
X
, the unitaries act only on a single qubit, as opposed to the whole system in noiseless cases.

In both the noiseless (
Theorem

) case and noisy case with constant depolarizing level, when the winning violation is

\sqrt{\epsilon}
.
However for noiseless case, the extra requirements (register-concentration and small isometries) as listed in

are not satisfied as

. For example, the strategy using observables
\left(Z,X,\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{\otimes 3}
acting on three EPR pairs is also optimal, but violates the first item.

Lemma C.7
.

For a Hermitian matrix
A\in\mathcal{H}_{4}^{\otimes n}
, if
A^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}I
and
A
contains only degree-one terms:

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}O^{\left(i\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{i\right\}},

where
a_{i}\geq 0
,
O^{\left(i\right)}\in\mathcal{H}_{4}
is traceless, and
\|O^{\left(i\right)}\|_{F}=1
.
Then there exists
\ell\in[n]
such that
A\approx_{\epsilon}O^{\left(\ell\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{\ell\right\}}
.

Proof.

Let
\left(O^{\left(i\right)}\right)^{2}=I+B^{\left(i\right)}
, where
\mathrm{Tr}B^{\left(i\right)}=0
. We then have

\displaystyle A^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}\left(O^{\left(i\right)}\right)^{2}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{i\right\}}+\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}O^{\left(i\right)}\otimes O^{\left(j\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{i,j\right\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}I+\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}B^{\left(i\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{i\right\}}+\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}O^{\left(i\right)}\otimes O^{\left(j\right)}\otimes I^{[n]\backslash\left\{i,j\right\}}

From
A^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}I
and Parseval’s identity, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}1,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon^{2}}0.

Since
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}\left(1-a_{j}^{2}\right)\approx_{\epsilon}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}^{2}a_{i}^{2}
, we have
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}\left(1-a_{j}^{2}\right)\approx_{\epsilon}0
, which implies

\forall j,
either
a_{j}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}0
or
a_{j}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}1
. Combining this with
\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}1
, there exists one index
\ell\in[n]
such that
a_{\ell}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}1

(which implies
a_{\ell}\approx_{\epsilon}1
)
and
\sum_{j\neq\ell}a_{j}^{2}\approx_{\epsilon^{2}}0
. Then the conclusion holds.
∎

Lemma C.8
.

Let
A,B,C\in\mathcal{H}_{4}
be Hermitian and traceless matrices satisfying
A^{2}=B^{2}=C^{2}=I
. If
\left\{A,B\right\}\approx_{\epsilon}0,\left[A,C\right]\approx_{\epsilon}0,\left[B,C\right]\approx_{\epsilon}0
, then there exists a unitary

, such that

\displaystyle UAU^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}Z\otimes I,\qquad UBU^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}X\otimes I,\qquad UCU^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}I\otimes X.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
A=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&0\\
0&-I_{2}\end{bmatrix}
. From the condition
\left\{A,B\right\}\approx_{\epsilon}0
we have

\displaystyle B\approx_{\epsilon}\begin{bmatrix}0&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{*}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
B_{12}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
. By

we have
B_{12}^{*}B_{12}\approx_{\epsilon}I,B_{12}B_{12}^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}I
. Let the singular value decomposition of

be

\displaystyle B_{12}=V\Lambda W^{*},\qquad\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
B_{12}^{*}B_{12}\approx_{\epsilon}I
, we have
\Lambda^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}I
, thus

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{1}^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda_{2}^{2}-1\right)^{2}\approx_{\epsilon^{2}}0.

Since

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{1}-1\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda_{2}-1\right)^{2}\leq\left(\lambda_{1}-1\right)^{2}\left(\lambda_{1}+1\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda_{2}-1\right)^{2}\left(\lambda_{2}+1\right)^{2}\approx_{\epsilon^{2}}0,

we have
\Lambda\approx_{\epsilon}I
. Define
U_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}V^{*}&0\\
0&W^{*}\end{bmatrix}
, then we have

\displaystyle U_{1}AU_{1}^{*}=Z\otimes I,\qquad U_{1}BU_{1}^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}X\otimes I.

\left[U_{1}CU_{1}^{*},U_{1}AU_{1}^{*}\right]\approx_{\epsilon}0,\left[U_{1}CU_{1}^{*},U_{1}BU_{1}^{*}\right]\approx_{\epsilon}0
, we have
U_{1}CU_{1}^{*}\approx_{\epsilon}\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&0\\
0&C_{1}\end{bmatrix}
for some

B.6

. By calculation

∎
