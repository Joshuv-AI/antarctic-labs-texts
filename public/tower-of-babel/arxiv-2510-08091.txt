Everything is Plausible: Investigating the Impact of LLM Rationales on Human Notions of Plausibility
Shramay Palta Peter Rankel

Abstract
We investigate the degree to which human plausibility judgments of multiple-choice commonsense benchmark answers are subject to influence by (im)plausibility arguments for or against an answer, in particular, using rationales generated by LLM s. We collect 3,000 3,000 plausibility judgments from humans and another 13,600 13,600 judgments from LLM s. Overall, we observe increases and decreases in mean human plausibility ratings in the presence of LLM -generated PRO and CON rationales, respectively, suggesting that, on the whole, human judges find these rationales convincing. Experiments with LLM s reveal similar patterns of influence. Our findings demonstrate a novel use of LLM s for studying aspects of human cognition, while also raising practical concerns that, even in domains where humans are “experts” (i.e., common sense), LLM s have the potential to exert considerable influence on people’s beliefs. 1 1 1 Our data and code will be released upon publication.

Introduction

A typical framework for evaluating the commonsense reasoning abilities of
NLP
models is to present an everyday situation described in text (
A person drops a glass
), followed by a set of possible inferences (
The glass breaks
;
The glass bounces
; etc), and instruction to select the most plausible answer choice
(
Levesque et al. 2011
;
Roemmele et al. 2011
;
Mostafazadeh et al. 2016
)
. Unlike in logical or mathematical reasoning problems, these answer choices are rarely definitively true or false, but rather lie on a continuum of plausibility
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
, and require non-monotonic modes of reasoning to assess, such as defeasible
(
Rudinger et al. 2020
)
, abductive
(
Bhagavatula et al. 2020
)
, or counterfactual
(
Qin et al. 2020
)
reasoning. In this setting, the answer designated as “correct” should be the choice that human judges agree is most plausible. Indeed, human annotators are more likely to agree on a “correct” answer when the plausibility gap between answer choices is large
(
Palta et al. 2024
)
.

However, unless an answer choice is logically impossible, there exist circumstances under which even a relatively implausible distractor answer could be true. For example, one could argue that a glass is actually quite likely to bounce if dropped on a trampoline or rubber mat. By the same token, unless an answer choice is logically necessary, there exist circumstances under which an otherwise highly plausible outcome becomes much less likely: The drinking glass probably
won’t
break if it’s very sturdy or doesn’t fall far. Thus, any answer choice which is neither certain nor impossible can be subject to arguments for or against its plausibility. In principle, these types of (im)plausibility arguments or rationales do not introduce any new evidence to the situation; rather, they simply highlight possible circumstances which,
if true
, would impact an answer’s plausibility.

In this paper, we investigate whether an
LLM
can produce convincing arguments for the plausibility or implausibility of commonsense benchmark answer choices, as measured by their ability to impact the plausibility ratings of human judges. (We will refer to such plausibility or implausibility arguments as
PRO
or
CON
rationales, respectively.) Posed another way, this paper asks whether and how human plausibility judgments change in response to LLM-written
PRO
and/or
CON
rationales.

To answer these questions, our work builds on the prior study of
Palta et al. 2024
, who previously collected Likert-scale judgments from human annotators about the plausibility of individual question-answer choice pairs from two commonsense multiple choice benchmarks, Social IQA (
SIQA
;
Sap et al. 2019
) and CommonsenseQA (
CQA
;
Talmor et al. 2019
). These judgments were collected in the absence of any rationales or (im)plausibility arguments, which we will refer to as the
NO
rationale setting. We extend this work by sampling a subset of their annotated question-answer pairs, and using an
LLM
to generate
PRO
and
CON
rationales for or against the plausibility of each answer. Adopting a similar protocol, we then collect
3,000
new human plausibility ratings of answer choices, presented alongside either a
PRO
rationale of the answer, a
CON
rationale, or both (
PRO+CON
), as illustrated in Figure
1
. In a parallel set of experiments using the same stimuli, we prompt
17
different
LLM
s to also rate the plausibility of these answers, collecting an additional
13,600
judgments from
LLM
s.

With this data, we are able to make the following observations:

•

Both human and
LLM
plausibility judgments are significantly affected by the addition of
PRO, CON
, and
PRO+CON
rationales, relative to a
NO
rationale setting.

•

On average,
PRO
rationales raise mean plausibility ratings from humans and
LLM
judges alike, while
CON
rationales lower mean ratings.

•

These patterns hold across both gold and distractor answer choices, with one notable exception: mean plausibility ratings by humans surprisingly
drop
for gold answer choices when a
PRO
rationale is presented. In
LLM
s, mean plausibility ratings increase in response to
PRO
rationales, for gold and distractor answer choices alike.

•

PRO+CON
rationales yield mixed reactions from both humans and
LLM
s, with ratings decreasing for the gold label, but increasing for the distractor answer choice.

•

While OpenAI and Non-OpenAI models generally exhibit similar trends, we observe some differences. In particular,
CON
rationales appear to have a larger effect on the OpenAI models’ ratings compared to the human ratings and Non-OpenAI models’ ratings.

•

The initial plausibility rating of an answer choice has a strong anchoring effect: the higher the initial plausibility of an answer, the smaller the subsequent change in its rating after a rationale is provided.

SIQA
and
CQA
– from the set of questions used in
Palta et al. 2024

SIQA
is designed to evaluate the social and emotional intelligence in computational models. Each
SIQA

2.

: An explanation that best explains the
implausibility
of the answer choice for the given question and context (for
SIQA
).

These two rationales are then used to collect human and

2.1
Model Preference Study

Because the cost of human annotation is high ($1,000 USD for our full-scale study), we first ran a small-scale preference study to select the language model most likely to produce rationales convincing to humans for subsequent use in the full-scale study.

For this initial preference study, we prompted several state-of-the-art models:
GPT-4o
and
GPT-4o-mini

(
OpenAI et al. 2024c
)
, as well as
Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct
and
Llama 3.1-70B-Instruct

(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
to generate both
PRO
and
CON
rationales for a set of
40
randomly sampled
(q,a)
pairs from the
validation
split of
SIQA
. The prompts used for generating the
PRO
and
CON
rationales are presented in Prompts
A.5
and
A.5
in Appendix
A.5
.

The generated rationales, and their corresponding
(q,a)
pairs, were presented to
4
human annotators, who selected the rationale “that best explained the plausibility (for
PRO
) or implausibility (for
CON
) of the answer choice
a
, and was also the most believable”. We show an example of the interface presented to the annotators in
Figure 4
. Based on the annotators’ responses,
GPT-4o
received the most votes and was selected to generate the rationales in our study.
2
2

2

According to annotator feedback, selecting a single rationale from the four presented was challenging, as the generated rationales were quite similar to one another. Nevertheless,
GPT-4o
received the majority of votes.
The set of
(q,a)

We note that it is not the purpose of the preference study, nor the full-scale study, to determine
which

LLM
is
most

any

might produce rationales capable of swaying human plausibility judgment. A positive finding (that human judgments are indeed systematically swayed) can in principle be demonstrated with a single model, since it is not our claim that
all

LLM

LLM

3
Human Plausibility Ratings

To understand the impact of rationales on human (and LLM) plausibility judgments, we borrow the Likert scale introduced by
Zhang et al. 2017
, and also used in
Palta et al. 2024
, defined as
1-Impossible
,
2-Technically Possible
,
3-Plausible
,
4-Likely
and
5-Very Likely
.

Given a question
q
and possible answer
a
, judges are asked to assess the plausibility of
a
under each of the following four conditions:

•

NO
Rationale
: The
(q,a)
pair is presented alone. No rationale is provided.

•

PRO
Rationale
: The
(q,a)
pair is accompanied by an argument for the
plausibility
of
a
, i.e., a
PRO
rationale.

•

CON
Rationale
: The
(q,a)
pair is accompanied by an argument for the
implausibility
of
a
, i.e., a
CON
rationale.

•

PRO+CON
Rationale
: The
(q,a)
pair is accompanied by both a
PRO
rationale
and
a
CON
rationale.

For the
No
Rationale setting, we use the plausibility judgments released by
Palta et al. 2024
. For each of the three remaining rationale settings (
PRO
,
CON
, and
PRO+CON
) we collect
5
judgments, totaling 15 new judgments per selected
(q,a)
pair.

Annotators are recruited through Prolific, and compensated at
\$15
/hour. To ensure independence, each annotator could judge at most one
(q,a,r)
pair for a given question
q
for a rationale type
r
. Further details, including examples of the annotation interfaces (Figures
5
,
6
and
7
), are presented in Appendix
A.4
. We present the overall annotation counts in
Table 1
.

Critically,
we observe that the effect of
PRO
rationales on mean human judgments is bimodal
: on distractor answers, mean judgments rise, while for gold answers they fall. Thus, for implausible distractor answers,
PRO
rationales appear sufficiently persuasive to raise mean ratings. But why do ratings for gold answers drop? One possible explanation is that, for very likely answers, a mere
plausibility
argument actually “undersells” the likelihood of the outcome; that is, people may interpret the
PRO
rationales as arguing the answer is
at most
plausible, rather than
at least
plausible.

With
CON
rationales, both gold and distractor answers show drops in mean plausibility, albeit with much larger drops for gold answers. (Interestingly, the drop for gold answers with
CON
rationales is over one full Likert scale point on average, the largest changes observed for any setting.) While boundary effects likely contribute to the differences observed between changes for gold and distractor labels (e.g., there is a cap on how much an already high rating can rise), they cannot account for differences between answers of the
same
type (e.g. distractor) across different rationale settings. For example, we have seen that mean ratings for distractor answers rise with
PRO
rationales and fall with
CON
rationales; in our controlled experimental setup, the only non-random explanation for this difference is the rationale type itself. (Significance tests are described at the end of this section.)

For
PRO+CON
rationales, we observe an approximate averaging of the
PRO
and
CON
outcomes: for gold and distractor answers both, changes in mean ratings lie in between the changes observed in the
PRO
and
CON
settings, respectively.

We perform several Chi-squared tests of homogeneity to compare the rating distributions with and without rationales. For
SIQA
, p-values for the shifts in the distributions are
0.069
,
2.17E^{-9}
, and
0.001
for the
PRO
,
CON
, and
PRO+CON
settings, respectively; for
CQA
, they are
0.001
,
9.24E^{-7}
, and
0.0006
. In nearly all cases, adding rationales yields statistically significant distribution shifts.

Do Rationales Affect LLMs as well?

In an effort to understand whether
LLM

3

GPT-4o
generated the

and

LLM
s we study in our work into two groups, with membership as follows:

•

OpenAI Models
: GPT (3.5, 4, 4-turbo, 4o, 4o-mini, 4.5-preview, o1, and o3-mini)
(
OpenAI et al. 2024c
;

;
OpenAI et al. 2024b
)

•

Non-OpenAI Models
: LLaMA-3.1-Instruct (8B, 70B)
(

(

4.1
LLM Ratings Pre and Post Rationales

Paralleling our analysis in §
3.1
, we compare how mean plausibility ratings produced by LLMs (OpenAI and Non-OpenAI models) change in the presence of each rationale type, reporting results again in Tables
2
and
3
, for direct comparison with results from the human judges.

Overall,
PRO
rationales have a larger positive impact for both sets of models, with a large proportion of ratings increasing across both datasets with the inclusion of the
PRO
rationale. Similarly, adding
CON
rationales leads to a large drop in ratings across both datasets. The inclusion of
PRO+CON
rationales once again elicits a mixed response, similar to our observation with human judgments in
section

3.1
.

However, in sharp contrast to what we observed with human annotators, the introduction of
PRO
rationales leads to an increase in the mean plausibility ratings for
a_{gold-label}
across both
SIQA
and
CQA
for both OpenAI and Non-OpenAI models. Similar to the shifts in human ratings for
a_{gold-label}
and
a_{distractor}
, by adding
CON
rationales, both groups of models exhibit a decrease in mean plausibility ratings across both datasets.

Finally, when
PRO+CON
rationales are presented together with a
(q,a)
item, we observe the
LLM
s to also display mixed results, with plausibility ratings for
a_{gold-label}
dropping for both
SIQA
and
CQA
, but increasing for
a_{distractor}
. This behavior is similar to what was observed for the human ratings (
section

3.1
). One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that, if
PRO
rationales dampen responses at the low end of the plausibility scale, and
CON
rationales dampen responses at the high end of the plausibility scale, then
PRO+CON
rationales together may have the effect of pushing plausibility ratings to the center of the scale because neither extreme is being used for rating the answer
a
. This would have the effect of raising average scores for low-scoring distractors
(a_{distractor})
and lowering scores for high-scoring gold-label answers
(a_{gold-label})
, as is observed for both humans and
LLM
s.
Figures
2
and
3
show that this is not simply a case of reversion to the mean, as, for instance,
CON
rationales on average push mean scores of distractors (which are low to begin with) even lower.
Another compatible explanation is that, as observed in human judgments,
PRO+CON
rationales result in shifts in mean judgments that lie between the shifts observed in the
PRO
and
CON
settings.

To establish the significance of these shifts, we ran several Chi-squared tests of homogeneity to compare the ratings distributions with and without rationales within each setting. These shifts were found to be highly statistically significant across all settings, with all p-values
<0.0001
.

5
Why Did the Ratings Change?

We have now established that both humans and
LLM
s are sensitive to the inclusion of
PRO
,
CON
and
PRO+CON
rationales, and adjust their plausibility ratings accordingly. To better understand what factors contributed the most to the changes in the plausibility ratings, we perform ordinary least squares (
OLS
) regression on the collected data separately for
a_{gold-label}
and
a_{distractor}
using the rationale type (
PRO,CON,PRO+CON
) and the
NO
Rationale Plausibility Rating
4
4

4

Originally collected on the 1-5 Likert Scale as described in
Palta et al. 2024
. For the regression, we use a
[-2,2]
scale to center the ratings around
0
.
as features, with the dependent variable being the change
(\Delta)
in plausibility rating after a rationale is presented.

As detailed in
Table 4
, the
NO
Rationale Plausibility Rating is a statistically significant predictor (
p<0.05
) of the rating change for humans, OpenAI and Non-OpenAI models. The consistently negative coefficients reveal a strong anchoring effect: the higher the initial plausibility of an answer, the smaller the subsequent change in its rating after a rationale is provided. This suggests that all rater groups are less likely to raise their opinion if their initial assessment is already high. Additionally, this anchoring effect is more pronounced for distractors than for gold-label answers across all groups. This indicates that the initial rating of an incorrect answer choice has a much stronger influence on the final rating change than the initial rating about the correct one.

Among rationale types,
CON
rationales exert a stronger effect than
PRO
rationales, with large negative coefficients that sharply
reduce
plausibility for otherwise correct answers.
PRO
rationales do
increase
plausibility, but the magnitude of their coefficients is smaller, indicating that supporting reasons reinforce less than opposing reasons undermine.
PRO+CON
rationales typically carry small negative coefficients, indicating that when both supporting and opposing arguments are presented together, the opposing component tends to dominate, yielding a net plausibility decrease.

7
Conclusion

In this work, by collecting
3000
human and
13,600

LLM
ratings for
200

(q,a)
pairs, we are able to demonstrate that both human and
LLM
plausibility ratings shift when rationales either in favor, against, or presented together are included with a commonsense reasoning question
q
and answer
a
, highlighting the potentially persuasive nature of
LLM
-generated explanations, specifically for commonsense reasoning tasks. Our findings also highlight that humans and
LLM
s do not always react similarly when shown different rationales, with models showing a higher sensitivity to the rationales, underscoring the challenges of aligning AI-generated judgments with human intuition, particularly in subjective reasoning tasks. Finally, our findings highlight the potential for
LLM
s to aid humans by challenging users’ reasoning, stress-testing arguments, and introducing alternative perspectives, all potentially leading to more effective human-AI collaboration. However, this persuasive nature of
LLM
s could make them particularly effective tools for shaping opinions, potentially undermining autonomy and informed decision-making. Addressing these risks requires robust safeguards, including transparency, bias mitigation, and mechanisms to detect and counteract harmful manipulative uses.

Limitations

Our goal in this work was to determine whether
any

LLM
might produce rationales capable of swaying human (and other LLMs’) plausibility judgments. For that purpose,
GPT-4o
proved effective at creating convincing arguments which, we demonstrated, led to the changes in plausibility ratings. However, the persuasive power and style of
LLM
-generated rationales can vary significantly across different models and architectures. We hope that our findings inspire future works to look into the extent to which different models influence human and
LLM
plausibility judgments.

The scope of our investigation is confined to two English-language commonsense reasoning datasets, Social IQA and CommonsenseQA. The plausibility of an answer choice and the extent to which a rationale is persuasive might differ in other languages and domains, such as technical, scientific, or legal reasoning, where arguments may be more constrained by factual evidence. Future work may explore understanding the impact of
LLM
-generated rationales on domains beyond commonsense reasoning. Furthermore, our human annotators were recruited from a specific demographic (US-based, English-speaking users on Prolific). As common sense itself can be culturally situated, the impact of these rationales could vary across different cultural and linguistic groups.

While we employed rigorous annotation protocols and made sure each instance was annotated by at least
5
annotators, there may still be instances where ambiguity in interpretation arises and hence, the impact a rationale has on the plausibility ratings would be different.
