High-throughput specificity profiling of antibody libraries using ribosome display and microfluidics
Authors: Ellen K. Wagner, Kyle P. Carter, Yoong Wearn Lim, Geeyun Jenny Chau, Alexis Enstrom, Nicholas P. Wayham, Jessica-Mae Hanners, Chiann-Ling C. Yeh, Marc Fouet, Jackson Leong, Adam S. Adler, Jan Fredrik Simons
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn this work, we developed PolyMap (polyclonal mapping), a high-throughput method for mapping protein-protein interactions. We demonstrated the mapping of thousands of antigen-antibody interactions between diverse antibody libraries isolated from convalescent and vaccinated COVID-19 donors and a set of clinically relevant SARS-CoV-2 spike variants. We identified over 150 antibodies with a variety of distinctive binding patterns toward the antigen variants and found a broader binding profile, including targeting of the Omicron variant, in the antibody repertoires of more recent donors. We then used these data to select mixtures of a small number of clones with complementary reactivity that together provide strong potency and broad neutralization. PolyMap is a generalizable platform that can be used for one-pot epitope mapping, immune repertoire profiling, and therapeutic design and, in the future, could be expanded to other families of interacting proteins.

Introduction

As highlighted by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, monoclonal antibodies can be a crucial tool for preventing and controlling infectious disease in high-risk individuals. The exceptional specificity of antibodies, which provides safety and efficacy, also means that a diverse and evolving target can escape the therapeutic. For example, an early antibody developed by Lilly, bamlanivimab, lost almost all potency against severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) strains with the E484K mutation in the spike (S) protein, which occurred less than a year after the beginning of the pandemic.1 Instead, binary antibody cocktails,2 serum-derived polyclonal antibodies,3 and recombinant polyclonal libraries4,5 show improved longevity of binding and protection against this evolving pathogen.

The natural immune response is highly polyclonal and an ideal reservoir to mine for diverse binders. We and others have developed methods for capturing and sequencing the native heavy- and light-chain pairing of these diverse libraries,4,6,7,8 but there is no simple, high-throughput method to assess the binding specificity of the individual antibodies en masse. One approach, called LIBRA-seq,9 uses DNA-barcoded antigens to stain primary B cells, which are then taken through a single-cell sequencing workflow to obtain in-silico-linked antibody and antigen sequences. This method was adapted and used successfully to identify neutralizing CoV-2 antibodies,10 but a significant challenge is the need for soluble antigens that are individually expressed, purified, and barcoded. A different strategy is to exploit existing biological systems in which two components selectively fuse as a result of protein-protein interactions. For example, Alpha-seq11 involves encoding two interacting libraries on opposite yeast mating types, allowing them to mate, and then sequencing the diploid cells. ENTER-seq12 and RAPTR13 display separate libraries on lentivirus particles and mammalian cells, with transduced cells sequenced to identify interacting pairs. Though these methods have high selectivity and sensitivity, each fusion only provides information on a single pairing event, requiring the analysis of a large number of cells for mapping more complex interaction networks.

Ribosome display is an alternative display technology that enables genotype-phenotype linkage of a soluble protein.14 In its simplest form, the protein is translated from an mRNA lacking a stop codon, preventing the dissociation of the protein-ribosome-mRNA complex. The method has been further improved over time through the use of additional stall sequences15 and recombinantly expressed reagents.16 In a process deemed MIPSA,17 a human open reading frame (ORF) library was generated in the ribosome-display platform,18 paired with covalently linked DNA barcodes, and used to determine the antigen specificity of autoreactive antibodies from patient samples. This technology allows screening of an antigen library against an antibody library, but as the antibody sequences are not recovered, this is limited to a profiling method. SMI-seq19 uses a gel-immobilized ribosome-displayed, cDNA-barcoded protein library, which is incubated with a second barcoded library. Fluorescent sequencing is used to identify co-localized barcodes from interacting proteins; although the methodology is innovative, it is quite complex and of limited scalability.

We have developed a high-throughput library-by-library interaction platform, called PolyMap (polyclonal mapping), that utilizes cell-surface-displayed antigens and soluble ribosome-displayed antibodies. In contrast to previous work, the antigen library is expressed on the surface of mammalian cells, which supports the native structure of a wide range of human and viral proteins without the need for purification. The antibody library is expressed by ribosome display as soluble single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), barcoded through their complementarity-determining region 3 heavy-chain sequence (CDR3H), and used to stain the antigen-presenting cells in bulk. Single cells, each potentially bound by thousands of antibody-ribosome-mRNA (ARM) complexes, are encapsulated with uniquely barcoded RNA capture beads, and the barcoded cDNA is sequenced to reveal antibody-antigen pairings. PolyMap is compatible with synthetic library generation methods, enabling both profiling and protein engineering applications. In this manuscript, we describe the initial development and proof-of-concept testing of the PolyMap platform using single-cell sorting and a microfluidic platform. We then demonstrate how PolyMap data can be used to select CoV-2 targeting antibodies with unique antigen-binding specificities that can be combined to create an improved therapeutic.

Results

Design of the PolyMap workflow

The PolyMap platform allows one-pot interaction screening of an antibody library and an antigen library (Figure 1). The antibody library is expressed in a ribosome-display format, which uses a tethered mRNA to provide genotype-phenotype linkage of the soluble protein.14 Antigens are expressed at a high level on the surface of a mammalian cell, which can then be bound by the soluble ARM complex. Incubation of a library of ribosome-displayed antibodies with a library of antigen-expressing cells allows sampling of all possible antibody and antigen combinations in a single bulk incubation step. To elucidate antibody-antigen interactions, cells stained with ARM complexes are washed and individually encapsulated in microdroplets, which contain lysis reagents and a single uniquely barcoded bead that captures cellular and cell-bound ARM-associated mRNA. Using the Drop-seq paradigm,20 a series of molecular biology steps are applied to the isolated beads to generate barcoded cDNA linking antibody and antigen sequences for each individual cell. The barcoded transcripts are then read and quantified by deep sequencing and analyzed using bioinformatics to map antibody-antigen pairwise binding specificities.Figure 1Design of the PolyMap platformThe PolyMap workflow begins with a library of antigens expressed on the surface of a mammalian cell, which are incubated with a soluble library of antibody scFvs in ribosome display format. Establishing the antigen expression system

We first needed to establish a robust antigen surface display platform. In particular, we desired robust surface expression of a single antigen per cell, a unique barcode for antigen identification, and a simple and efficient cloning and cell line generation process amenable for use with libraries. We tested a series of mammalian expression vectors to determine the impact of different signal peptides and transmembrane regions on the surface expression of our chosen target, the SARS-CoV-2 S surface antigen (CoV-2 S; data not shown). We eventually chose to keep the native signal peptide and transmembrane region, as they performed at least as well as the alternatives. A 19 amino acid C-terminal truncation was implemented to remove an endoplasmic reticulum retention signal endogenous to CoV-2 S.21

Stable cell lines expressing CoV-2 S were generated with two different methods. First, we tested lentiviral transduction into Expi293 cells. Second, we used flippase (Flp)-recombinase-mediated integration into CHOZN cells engineered with a Flp recognition target (FRT) landing pad for single-site integration.4 After sorting or selecting the cell lines, respectively, staining for CoV-2 S surface expression revealed an over 3.5-fold higher relative signal from the CHOZN cell line compared to Expi293 (Figure S1A). We also evaluated a modified construct that included six stabilizing proline mutations and a furin site mutation to abrogate fusogenic activity of the S protein21,22 and found that these modifications further increased cell surface expression of the antigen (Figure S1A).

The final antigen display vector uses a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter with a translation-enhancing sequence element (2G UNic, ProteoNic) to drive efficient antigen expression, a signal peptide, a transmembrane domain for surface display, the aforementioned proline modifications, and a mutated furin site (Figure 2A). A 20-mer nucleic acid barcode, unique for each antigen, is included after the translational stop codon, followed by a common flanking sequence to allow PCR amplification and antigen identification with short-read sequencing. To generate the library of antigen-displaying cells, the antigen-encoding plasmid library was transfected along with a plasmid expressing the Flp recombinase into a previously engineered CHOZN cell line, with the FRT site integrated into a high-expressing genomic locus.4 This ensures single-copy integration and normalized expression levels across individual antigens. Successful integration of the plasmid results in the expression of glutamine synthetase, allowing selection in glutamine-free media.

For a proof-of-concept study, we selected the ancestral Wuhan CoV-2 S protein (termed wild type [WT] for this study) and three individual point mutants (K444T, E484K, F486K) known to significantly disrupt the binding of specific monoclonal antibodies in clinical development.23,24,25,26 The four S protein-coding sequences, including the native leader sequence, stabilizing mutations, native transmembrane region, and deleted cytoplasmic tail, were cloned into the platform vector with unique barcodes. In this instance, the plasmids were separately transfected into the platform CHOZN cells, which were then selected and stained for S surface expression using a cross-reactive antibody. Flow cytometry analysis showed high and consistent expression between the four CoV-2 S variants (Figure 2B).

Establishing the ribosome-display system

The ribosome-display construct includes the fundamental features reported earlier by others14 with some more recent enhancements and features specific to our application (Figure 2C). A T7 promoter drives the expression of the scFv gene, which is followed by a strep-tag II sequence. The absence of a stop codon and addition of a 17-amino-acid SecM stall sequence15 prevents the ribosome from dissociating from the mRNA, physically tethering the newly synthesized protein to its coding sequence. An encoded poly(A) tail allows subsequent capture onto beads for cDNA synthesis, barcoding, and sequencing. The CDR3H sequence provides a unique identifier for the antibody, and as was done with the antigen plasmid, common cut sites and flanking regions are included for library cloning, amplification, and sequencing. The whole cassette is maintained within a pUC-based plasmid.

To generate ARM complexes, a DNA fragment stretching from the T7 promoter through the transcriptional terminator region is amplified with PCR and used as a template to generate large amounts of RNA. While it is possible to use a circular plasmid as a transcriptional template, we found that a PCR fragment with a defined terminus was important for RNA uniformity, as otherwise significant terminator readthrough occurred even with constructs including improved terminator designs (data not shown). We found that using an in vitro expression system based on recombinant components16 and starting with a fixed input of purified RNA produced more consistent ARM complex yields than a coupled transcription-translation process, possibly due to varying transcriptional kinetics between variants. We also found that omitting the ribosome release factors from the in vitro translation reaction and using a short, 10-min translation time further improved the stability and specificity of our ARM complexes (Figure S1B).

For initial testing, we selected the clinical SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies bamlanivimab, casirivimab, and imdevimab2,27 with known susceptibilities to the S point mutations E484K, F486K, and K444T, respectively.23,24,25,26 We used bio-layer interferometry (BLI) to confirm the binding affinities (and the lack of binding to the specific point mutations) of these antibodies presented as scFvs to each S mutant expressed as a soluble receptor-binding domain (RBD)-Fc fusion (Table 1; Figure S2). The scFvs were then cloned into the ribosome-display construct, produced as ARM complexes, and individually incubated with antigen-expressing cells. Total RNA was isolated from the stained cells, which was then analyzed with RT-qPCR using probes targeting the ARM complex mRNA. Knowing how many cells were stained and assuming no cell loss through the process, we could estimate the average number of ARM complexes per cell. For each antibody, there was complete or partial loss of ARM complex binding to the expected S variants, consistent with the measured binding affinities, as measured by the estimated ARMs/cell (Figure 2D). Establishing Drop-seq-based single-cell barcoding

For proof-of concept testing, we made an equimolar mixture of five antibody mRNAs (bamlanivimab, casirivimab, and imdevimab plus negative controls ipilimumab [anti-CTLA-4] and pembrolizumab [anti-PD-1]) and used this to generate ARM complexes, which were then used to stain a mixture of the four antigen cell lines. Single cells were sorted into a 96-well plate and lysed, and antigen barcodes and antibody heavy chains were amplified with arrayed primers and sequenced.28 The distribution of antibodies bound to each cell was identified and compared to other cells of the same antigen identity (Figure S1C). There was high reproducibility between individual cells and very few reads for the negative controls (the anti-PD-1 pembrolizumab and the anti-CTLA-4 ipilimumab) and predicted dropout antigen variants (K444T, E484K, F486K). Visualization of the data in heatmap format (Figure S1D) clearly demonstrated the expected binding patterns.

For high-throughput analysis, we utilized the previously published microfluidic droplet technology Drop-seq, where single cells are co-encapsulated with barcoded beads (ChemGenes Corporation) in nanoliter droplets using a microfluidic chip and syringe pump setup.20 Upon encapsulation, cells are immediately lysed, releasing both the cellular mRNA (including the antigen mRNA) and the antigen-bound scFv RNA from the ARM complex. If the cell is co-encapsulated with a barcoded bead, then the RNA is subsequently captured by oligos on the bead. During cDNA amplification, the bead barcode is linked to the transcript. Since the barcodes are unique to each bead but shared by every oligo on that bead, the barcodes can be used to link each transcript back to the cell of origin. The same cell mixture as above was stained with the ARM complex mixture, then the Drop-seq workflow through the cDNA amplification step was performed as previously described (https://mccarrolllab.org/dropseq/). After amplifying the full cDNA library, antigen barcodes (different from the Drop-seq bead barcodes) and antibody heavy-chain sequences were further amplified using gene-specific primers with Illumina adapters for sequencing. Individual cells were first identified by the Drop-seq barcode, then the antigen identity was determined by the antigen barcode, and the number of sequencing reads from each antibody were counted. Ultimately, 610 cells were identified to map antibody-antigen interactions (Figures 2E and 2F). As with the cells sorted into individual wells, the negative control antibody sequences made up a low percentage of the total antibody reads for each cell, indicating little to no binding of those ARM complexes. Similarly, we observed few reads from bamlanivimab, casirivimab, and imdevimab associated with their respective dropout variants. Overall, the Drop-seq data were consistent with the single-cell sorting (Figures S1C and S1D), providing a method to analyze larger numbers of cells in a single experiment.

Profiling anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune repertoires from different donor sets

We previously immortalized the circulating antibody repertoire from the blood of 16 convalescent COVID-19 donors collected between April and July 2020. Two-donor pools were separately cloned into yeast-display vectors and enriched for binders to the ancestral S CoV-2 RBD protein, and a combination of these libraries was used to generate a recombinant polyclonal antibody therapeutic (GIGA-2050), which was good manufacturing practice (GMP) manufactured and showed potent neutralization against all tested RBD variants of concern.4,5,29 We repeated this process with three more convalescent donors collected in August 2023 and three donors vaccinated in December 2023. Each 3-donor set was pooled, cloned into yeast-display vectors, and enriched as above with either the ancestral RBD or a mixture of two Omicron RBD variants circulating at the time of suspected infection (XBB.1.16 and EG.5). Surprisingly, the Omicron-sorted samples did not yield any additional clones beyond those enriched using the ancestral RBD antigen (data not shown) and were not further pursued in this project. The ancestral-sorted libraries were reformatted into the ribosome-display backbone and sequenced (Figure 3A). The 16-donor library from 2020 showed good diversity, with 98% of the sequences coming from 279 unique clones and the top clone making up ∼9.3% of the library. As expected, the 3-donor libraries from convalescent and vaccinated patients in 2023 had reduced diversity, with 99% of the sequences coming from 19 and 20 unique clones and top clones of ∼35.4% and ∼50.6%, respectively. To generate a relevant antigen library, all publicly available CoV-2 S sequences (as of August 2023) were downloaded from GISAID.org, and 18 unique sequences representing phylogenetically distinct clades (nextstrain.org) were selected (Figure S3A). These sequences, along with non-target control antigens (CoV-1 S, CTLA-4, PD-1, and cytosolic blue fluorescent protein [BFP]), were individually synthesized with unique barcodes, cloned into the antigen plasmid backbone, and then pooled and transfected in bulk into the CHOZN landing-pad line. Genomic DNA was collected from the antigen line after selection and sequenced to confirm the antigen distribution (Figure 3B).

For Drop-seq-based microfluidic analysis of the larger libraries, the antigen cell line was stained with ARM complexes generated from either the 2020 library RNA or the pooled 2023 library RNA. Samples were separately processed as described previously, with each sample run in two independent experiments. After identifying antigen and antibody sequences with Drop-seq cell barcodes, we generated a merged list of every antibody and antigen associated with each cell barcode. We found that the antigen distribution recovered by Drop-seq was not as uniform as expected but correlated with RNA levels in the cell line (Figures S3B and S3C), suggesting some threshold expression level is required for sufficient capture and detection. Three antigens had <10 cells recovered and were excluded from further analysis. Ultimately, we analyzed 3,693 and 3,113 cells across two replicates for the 2020 donors and the 2023 donors, respectively, and characterized antibody binding to 19 different antigens.

To quantify antibody binding, we calculated a “PolyMap score” by counting the total antibody reads for each clone across all cell lines and then normalizing the percentage of reads for each antibody by the number of associated cells. A total of 129 antibodies were profiled from the 2020 library (Figures 3C and S4A), and 40 were profiled from the 2023 library (Figure 3D). As expected, the antibodies from the 2020 donors showed little reactivity to the highly mutated Omicron variants that emerged later in the pandemic. Many clones also showed reduced reactivity to the Beta and Gamma variants, which contain the common K417 and E484 mutations that disrupted many class I and class II clinical antibodies.30 In contrast, the antibodies from the 2023 donors showed a wider variety of binding patterns, and many clones showed binding to Beta, Gamma, and Omicron variants. For both samples, little to no signal was recovered for the control cells, and the replicates were consistent (Figures S4B and S4C).

Characterization of antibodies identified from PolyMap

We selected eight antibody clones (termed 20.1, 20.2, 20.5, and 20.7–20.11, Table S1) from the 2020 library with various antigen-binding patterns for validation. Additional sequencing on the input library was performed to identify the variable light-chain sequence, which was then combined with our previous variable heavy-chain sequencing data to assemble full-length, natively paired sequences for expression. Cell lines expressing individual S antigens were generated, stained with antibody-containing supernatant, and run on flow cytometry. A comparison of the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the stained cells and the PolyMap score from Drop-seq showed a preservation of key binding features (Figure 4A), even for antibodies with overall weaker binding (e.g., antibodies 20.1 and 20.10). Although these libraries were not explicitly designed for epitope mapping, we did notice several repeated dropout patterns that correlate with shared mutations in the RBD. For example, mutations in K417 are seen in Beta, Gamma, and Omicron (21K) onward (Figure S3A) and are a likely epitope for clones 20.1, 20.5, 20.7, and 20.10. The variants Beta, Gamma, Kappa, Eta, Iota, and Mu onward contain an E484 mutation, which is a likely epitope for a clone like 20.9. Clones like 20.8 and 20.11 show binding restricted to only the earliest variants and may recognize epitopes that include the above amino acids and others like L452 or N501. Finally, clone 20.2 shows broad binding and some reactivity to Omicron, which may bind to a more conserved epitope. Finally, we hypothesized that PolyMap data could be used to select mixtures of antibodies with improved potency. Based on complementary PolyMap profiles, additional clones from the 2020 and 2023 libraries were selected for monoclonal expression and purification (Table S1). Antibodies were equally pooled to generate binary (23.1 and 23.3), ternary (20.6, 23.5, and 23.7), and quaternary (20.4, 20.6, 23.4, and 23.5) mixtures, which were compared against a putative pan-binder (23.6) and a mixture of five high-frequency clones from each library (20.1–20.5 and 23.1–23.5). Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), a plasma-derived clinical product containing antibodies from a large number of donors, was used as a control to show the level of binding for an unenriched, low-potency antibody mixture. We ran all samples on ELISA to assess binding to purified S RBDs from diverse lineages covering the time period of our donor collections and one emerging variant (JN.1) (Figures 4B and S5A). As expected from the PolyMap data, the putative pan-binder and all of the mixtures were able to bind the S RBDs from each of the variants previously analyzed by PolyMap (EC50 of 0.004–0.021 ng/μL), where the ternary mixture binding 20H had the weakest binding. Not surprisingly, most combinations showed reduced binding to the RBD of the emerging variant JN.1, which contains an additional 7 mutations from previous Omicron variants and was not included in the PolyMap antigen library, though they all still showed strong binding relative to IVIG.

The pan binder, the binary mixture, and the mixture of top 10 clones were additionally tested for their ability to neutralize infection in a pseudoviral neutralization assay of the same variants (Figures 4C and S5B), which is a more rigorous test of function. The mixture of top clones was able to neutralize all variants tested (IC50 of 0.05–0.15 ng/μL). The binary mixture was able to neutralize most variants with a similar potency to the mixture of ten clones but was nearly 100-fold less potent at neutralizing JN.1 (EC50 = 10 ng/μL), indicating that a critical clone may be missing from this mixture. Surprisingly, the monoclonal 23.6, which was a pan-binder on ELISA, was only able to weakly neutralize most variants (IC50 of 8.61–54.84 ng/μL) and was unable to neutralize JN.1. It is possible this antibody targets a more conserved but unfortunately non-neutralizing epitope. While these data highlight that binding alone does not guarantee success, a single round of PolyMap was nevertheless able to identify diverse candidate antibodies and mixtures for further investigation.

Discussion

PolyMap is a library-by-library interaction platform that can generate binding profiles for hundreds of antibody clones against a library of mammalian cell-surface-expressed proteins in a single-pot reaction. A significant strength of the PolyMap platform as compared to previously developed platforms is a flexible antigen expression system tailored for the expression of membrane proteins. Unlike other published multiplexed mapping methods, which require purification,9,10 in vitro translation,18,17,19 or chimeras for yeast display,11 PolyMap uses full-length antigens displayed on the surface of mammalian cells. The robust quality control mechanisms in the cell ensure native structure and glycosylation of complex membrane-bound proteins, which is critical for many prominent therapeutic targets such as G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) or oligomeric viral antigens.31,32 A key component of the antigen expression system is the parental CHOZN cell line, which features an engineered FRT site at a highly expressed locus for recombinase-mediated integration. We have used this platform line extensively for one-pot transfections and manufacturing of thousands-diverse antibody libraries.4,29 Other methods for generating large antigen or antibody libraries in mammalian cells are also compatible with PolyMap, though uniform clonal distribution and even expression levels are beneficial.

We expressed our antibody library as scFvs in a ribosome-display format, which has been previously validated33 and has several advantageous features. First, the soluble antibody library allowed us to stain suspension cells in bulk, which, to our knowledge, has previously only been described for adherent cells.34 Second, although the ARM complex can potentially harbor several translating ribosomes, we used a short 19-amino-acid C-terminal linker to promote a single full-length scFv on each ribosome. We expect this functionally monovalent format to be critical for preventing cell aggregation and eliminating avidity effects to allow better differentiation between clones. Other advantages of ribosome display include ease of library generation, compatibility with extremely large library sizes, and readily available commercial reagents.

The PolyMap screening process is simple and scalable, and both antigen and antibody libraries can be reused indefinitely once generated. In PolyMap, each cell supports thousands of interactions (Figure 2D), meaning that the available screening capacity is mostly defined by the number of antigen-expressing cells that can be individually processed and identified. For studies including tens, hundreds, or more antigens, en masse transfection of a plasmid library into cells is convenient and presents an option not available with some alternative technologies. Drop-seq, as used in this work, can typically process up to 10,000 single cells per hour and is appropriate for studies involving dozens to potentially hundreds of unique antigens. Other single-cell and microfluidic sequencing technologies are also compatible with PolyMap and could be explored in future iterations.

We were initially unsure whether there was binding competition between antibodies and antigens, but we found a linear increase in the number of ARM complexes per cell at all feasible staining conditions (Figure S1E), indicating the staining is not at saturating conditions. As the antibody concentration is not critical, we can have confidence in the binding pattern even for clones with low proportions in the initial staining library. We determined the affinities of 5 randomly recovered clones, which ranged from ∼40 pM to ∼40 nM, suggesting that lower-affinity clones with an interesting binding profile could still be identified. Considering these features, we decided to calculate and report the PolyMap score in a way that emphasizes the binding profile of each individual antibody, rather than comparing across clones, to enable the selection of rare or lower-affinity clones that may nevertheless have unique potential. We found some correlation between the measured affinity and RNA recovery compared to the input stain (Figure S1F), which could be used to prioritize similar clones.

PolyMap is a powerful tool for profiling and comparing immune repertoires. In this work, we analyzed the immortalized natively paired antibody repertoire, enriched for RBD binders, from donors collected at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic (April–July 2020) and several years later (July–December 2023). We were able to see clear differences in the reactivity profiles between the samples, most notably a significant increase in the proportion of antibody clones that bind Omicron variants from the more recent samples. These antibodies were enriched using the original Wuhan RBD, and thus these sequences likely represent the reactivation and enrichment of cross-reactive clones that were already present, which may explain the reduced per-donor diversity.35,36 Although additional somatic hypermutation of original clones and induction of novel Omicron-specific clones has been documented, this was not seen until 5–6 months after breakthrough infection or vaccination,35 which is significantly later than our samples were taken. Consistent with this, an enrichment campaign using a mixture of Omicron RBDs circulating at the time of donor infection did not yield any additional clones.

Finally, we used PolyMap data to characterize and select antibodies for diverse functional properties. A critical strategy to generate potent therapeutics against highly mutable infectious pathogens is to target several non-overlapping epitopes,2 such that single escape mutations are not possible.37 Although our antigen library was not explicitly designed for epitope mapping, some of the PolyMap profiles were very clearly linked to specific mutations known to be critical to class I (K417) and class II (E484) S binders.30 We used PolyMap data to select several combinations of clones with complementary binding patterns and showed that these combinations were able to bind all profiled antigens and even showed some binding to the emerging and divergent JN.1 variant. Further functional characterization of a few of the combinations in a pseudoviral neutralization assay confirmed broad strain coverage, though the pan-binding monoclonal 23.6 showed very low potency despite showing broad binding ability. Though additional testing would certainly be required to find the optimal combination, these data help illustrate how PolyMap profiling can help rapidly identify the most interesting clones for further analysis.

Thoughtful selection of antibody and antigen libraries can enable a broad range of applications for the PolyMap platform. Pairing PolyMap with antibody-repertoire capture techniques6,7,8 can allow the profiling of an immune response across donors, treatments, or time. Libraries of human surface proteins or other target protein populations can be amplified from cDNA and generated in bulk for antibody-specificity profiling.17 As both the antigen and antibody platforms are compatible with any library generation technique, synthetic libraries can be generated for protein engineering studies. For example, a deep mutational scanning library of an antigen38 could be generated and screened with an antibody library for a massively parallelized epitope mapping platform. Targeted CDR mutagenesis and a panel of antigen-expressing cells could be used to evolve antibodies with either broad or very targeted specificity.39,40,41 An interesting extension of the current method could involve engineered cells with a ligand-induced activatable phenotype. In this instance, ARM stained and activated cells could be sorted based on marker activation and subsequently subjected to PolyMap for identification of the specific binding partners. Further, in principle, the technology could be adapted to any library of soluble proteins and their cognate binding partners expressed on the cell surface, such as cytokines and their cell-bound receptors, or immune checkpoint proteins.

Limitations of the study

The PolyMap platform relies on mammalian surface expression of antigen, and while this approach is compatible with complex membrane proteins or possibly modified soluble proteins, in this work, we only evaluated a viral protein in its native conformation. Additionally, while it is intuitive that relatively uniform antigen and antibody expression levels are beneficial, we did not determine a minimum RNA threshold for sequence capture in Drop-seq. Low cell recovery in Drop-seq limits the antigen library size that can be interrogated; further work could investigate alternative single-cell methods.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

All cell lines were cultured in a 37°C humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and were passaged every 2–3 days when they reached approximately 8 million cells per mL (CHOZN, Expi293) or 80%–90% confluence (HEK293Ta). CHOZN cells were cultured in EX-CELL CD CHO Fusion media (Millipore Sigma, 14365C). Expi293 cells were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14351-01). HEK293Ta cells were cultured in DMEM with Glutamax, non-essential amino acids, sodium pyruvate, and 10% FBS.

Method details

Antigen constructs and cloning

The amino acid sequence for wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2 S protein (UniProt P0DTC2) was modified for improved expression: 6 stabilizing proline mutations were added, the furin site was removed (RRAR>AGAG), and 19 amino acids were truncated from the C-terminus.21,22 The sequence was codon-optimized, synthesized, and cloned into the antigen expression vector (Figure 2A) along with an 18-mer barcode sequence. The expression vector includes a CMV promoter with a 2G-UNic translation enhancing element (ProteoNic) as well as an FRT site followed by a 2A ribosomal skipping motif and glutamine synthetase gene. Point mutations were introduced into the WT spike sequence by Gibson assembly and cloned with unique barcodes into the same expression vector backbone.

To generate the circulating spike variant library, we downloaded the protein sequences for all available spike variants from GISAID.org as of August 2023. We also downloaded the phylogenetic analysis for 4,217 SARS-CoV-2 genomes from nextstrain.org that were sampled between December 2019 and July 2023, which identified 30 distinct clades. We selected the most common sequence within each phylogenetic clade, and then filtered to remove sequences with <2 amino acid differences, resulting in 18 representative spike variants covering a diverse range of mutations (Figure S3A). These sequences, including the proline and furin mutations and the C-terminal truncation, were synthesized with unique DNA barcodes (HHSWNNHHCTGGNNHHSWHH) and cloned into the antigen expression vector. Wild-type sequences for CoV-1 S (P59594), human CTLA-4 (P16410), human PD-1 (Q15116) and mTagBFP242 were included as controls.

Stable cell line generation and analysis

Stable cell lines were generated by transfecting an equimolar mix of each antigen construct along with a plasmid encoding the Flp recombinase into CHOZN cells containing a matched FRT site and landing pad.4 Cells were electroporated (MaxCyte) using the default CHO protocol with 5 μg of a 4:1 mixture of antigen and recombinase plasmid per 106 cells. After recovery for 4 days in EX-CELL CD CHO Fusion media (Millipore Sigma, 14365C) supplemented with GlutaMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 35050061), cells were selected in the absence of GlutaMax for 18 days or until viability was >95%. After selection, gDNA and RNA was collected and the barcode region amplified using flanking primers containing sequencing adapters (Antigen BC P5 FWD, Antigen BC P7 REV; Table S2 for primer sequences). The DNA samples were quantified using qPCR and normalized to 1.8 nM. The library was diluted to a final concentration of 9 p.m., supplemented with 5% PhiX DNA and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq instrument using custom primers (M13 SEQ, BGH INDEX SEQ, BGH SEQ). Sequencing results were analyzed using Python scripts that calculated the number of times each unique antigen barcode appeared per library and the frequency relative to the total number of barcodes.

Flow cytometry

To assess spike protein expression, cells were stained with 10 μg/mL anti-COVID-19 and SARS-CoV-2 S glycoprotein antibody (clone CR3022, Absolute Antibody, Ab01680–10.0), followed by PE or APC anti-human IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 709-606-149). For binding validation of specific antibodies identified by PolyMap, spike variant expressing cell lines were stained with supernatant from CHO cells expressing the indicated antibody clone at 0.5 μg/mL, followed by APC anti-human IgG Fc Antibody (BioLegend, 409306). For checking spike surface expression, cells were stained with a dilution series of bamlanivimab followed by PE anti-human IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 709-606-149). After secondary antibody staining, cells were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; BioLegend, 422801). The samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the APC signal on live cells (DAPI negative) was determined in FlowJo (v10.6.1, FlowJo, LLC).

Sample collection, VL-VH pairing, and enrichment

50 mL of blood was collected from 6 donors two weeks past confirmed COVID infection or vaccination (August 2023 or December 2023, respectively). B cells were collected, pooled, and run through a microfluidic chip to capture and link heavy and light chain sequences as previously described.4 Linked sequences were cloned into a yeast-display platform and enriched for binders using 2 rounds of staining and sorting with 1200 nM Wuhan RBD.

Antibody constructs and cloning

Control antibody scFv sequences (VL-linker-VH) were assembled, codon optimized,and synthesized with overlaps for Gibson assembly into the ribosome display vector. Instead of a stop codon, the vector encodes a strep-tag II, an unstructured sequence derived from tolA (GGQKQAEE), and a secM stall sequence (FSTPVWISQAQGIRAGP).15 Transcription from the plasmid is controlled by flanking conventional T7 promoter and terminator elements.

Antibody libraries were amplified from the natively-paired antibody repertoires using a primer pool (scFv library FWD 1–5, REV 1–3; Table S2) designed to capture all germlines.4 These sequences were cloned in bulk into the linearized ribosome display plasmid using Gibson assembly. For sequencing, samples were prepared by amplifying the VH region directly from the plasmid library or from cDNA with universal primers binding to the linker and secM regions (scFv linker 1 P5 FWD, scFv P7 REV).

ARM complex generation and cell staining

The ribosome display plasmid was subjected to PCR to generate a fragment spanning the T7 promoter, scFv cassette, and the T7 terminator sequence (primers ARM complex FWD, ARM complex REV; Table S2). The PCR product served as template in an in vitro transcription reaction (HiScribe T7 Quick High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit, NEB, E2050S) to generate RNA which subsequently was DNase treated and purified. To generate ARM complexes, 450 nM of RNA template (based on 1100 bp length) was added to an in vitro transcription-translation kit without release factors (PURExpress ΔRF123, NEB, E6850S) and incubated at 37°C for 10 min. The reaction mix was immediately cooled on ice and diluted 1:5 or more with RBTH buffer (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, 0.05% BSA, 2.5 mg/mL heparin, 0.01% tween 20). 1×106 antigen-expressing cells were stained with 50 μL of ARM complex at 15–90 nM on ice for 30 min, then washed 3x with 1 mL of ice-cold RB (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 + 0.05% BSA) before further analysis.

RNA isolation and qPCR

For qPCR analysis of ARM complex binding after staining antigen-expressing cells (Figure 2D), we used the lysis solution and stop solution from the Cells-to-CT 1-Step Power SYBR Green Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A25599). In a 96-well plate, we lysed 10,000 stained cells per well in 20 μL lysis solution for 30 min at room temperature, then added 2 μL stop solution and incubated for 2 min at room temperature. The lysates were probed for secM (using primers ARM qPCR FWD, ARM qPCR REV, ARM qPCR PROBE; Table S2) in an RT-qPCR assay. We used the TaqMan RNA-to-CT 1-Step Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4392938) according to manufacturer’s instructions. We took 2 μL of each lysate into 30 μL reaction mixes (15-fold dilution); each reaction was aliquoted in quadruplicates into a 384-well plate, 6 μL per well. We included a 7-point standard curve using RNA of a known concentration, starting from 1 nM to 1 fM (10-fold serial dilutions). The average number of ARM complexes/cell was either estimated from the number of stained cells, assuming no loss (Figure 2D).

Drop-seq

The Drop-seq encapsulation, cDNA generation, and amplification steps were carried out as previously described20 (https://mccarrolllab.org/dropseq/), with some modifications. Since the cell barcodes allowed us to distinguish droplets containing multiple cells, we used a higher cell and bead density to maximize our chance of co-encapsulation. Cells were resuspended in RB buffer with 12% OptiPrep (Sigma-Aldrich, D1556-250ML) at 400 cells/μL (Figure 2E) or 200 cells/μL (Figures 3C and 3D), filtered through a 35 μm cell strainer, then a 20 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 30 x g for 1 min to remove any remaining large clumps of cells. The supernatant was carefully aspirated without disturbing the bottom ∼30 μL and loaded into a 1 mL syringe. Drop-seq beads (generated by and purchased from ChemGenes Corporation, Macosko-2011-10(V+)) were washed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and resuspended in 10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Tween at 440 beads/μL. Prior to encapsulation, the beads were resuspended in Drop-seq Lysis buffer (DLB, 200 mM Tris pH 7.5, 6% Ficoll PM-400, 0.2% Sarkosyl, 20 mM EDTA, 50 mM DTT) at 240 beads/μL (Figure 2E) or 440 beads/μL (Figures 3C and 3D).

Microfluidic chips for Drop-seq were purchased from FlowJEM. Pico-Surf 1 (Sphere Fluidics, C023) was used for the droplet generation oil. During encapsulation, syringe pumps were operated at flow rates of 900 μL/h for each aqueous suspension and 1600 μL/h for the oil. Approximately 400,000 (Figure 2E) or 200,000 (Figures 3C and 3D) cells were processed through the microfluidic chips for each sample. Droplet breakage was carried out as previously described,20 but 2 mL Pico-Break (Sphere Fluidics, C082) was used to break the droplets.

Reverse transcription and exonuclease treatment was carried out as previously described,20 except we used a modified template switch oligo (Table S2). After RT and exonuclease treatment, bead concentration was determined using a Fuchs-Rosenthal cell counter (Bulldog Bio, DHC-N01). Aliquots of 8,000 beads were PCR amplified in a volume of 50 μL using 1x HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems, KK2602), 0.4 μM each cDNA PCR FWD and cDNA PCR REV primers. For Figure 2E, cDNA from 164,000 beads was amplified; for Figures 3C and 3D, cDNA from 53,300 to 100,000 beads were amplified.

To prepare the samples for sequencing, 5 ng (Figure 2E) or 20 ng (Figures 3C and 3D) of cDNA library was PCR amplified in a volume of 50 μL using 1x Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (NEB, M0494S), 0.5 μM forward primer, and 0.5 μM reverse primer. We designed the forward and reverse primers to selectively bind and amplify the antibody and antigen sequences in the cDNA library. The primers used for antibody amplification have reversed P5/P7 Illumina adapters (Table S2): the reverse primers (Drop-seq P5 REV) contain the P5 Illumina adapter and a unique Illumina index for de-multiplexing; the forward primers (scFv linker 1 P7 FWD for Figure 2E and scFv linker 2 P7 FWD for Figures 3C and 3D) contain the P7 Illumina adapter. The primers used for antigen amplification were CoV-2 S FWD for Figure 2E and Antigen BC FWD for Figures 3B and 3C; the reverse primers were Drop-seq P7 REV with different Illumina indices for de-multiplexing.

The antibody and antigen samples were sequenced separately on the Illumina MiSeq using a 600 cycle MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina, MS-102-3003). For antibody sequencing, read 1 was 26 bp (Cell BC SEQ), the minimum requirement for good clustering on the MiSeq (bases 1–12 cell barcode, bases 13–20 UMI); index 1 was 150 bp to capture the antibody CDR3 (Strep-tag II SEQ); read 2 was 6 bp to read the Illumina barcode (Illumina BC SEQ). For antigen sequencing, read 1 was 270 bp (M13 SEQ primer) for Figure 2E and 76 bp for Figures 3C and 3D; index 1 was 6 bp to capture the Illumina barcode (Illumina BC SEQ); read 2 was 20 bp (bases 1–12 cell barcode, bases 13–20 UMI; Cell BC SEQ).

Protein expression

The receptor-binding domain of spike, with K444T, E484K, or F486K mutations, was cloned in-frame with the hinge and Fc of a murine IgG2a in a mammalian expression construct. Each plasmid was transiently expressed using the Expi293 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and purified using Protein A chromatography (PrismA, Cytiva). Size and purity were validated using SDS-PAGE and SE-HPLC.

Full VH sequences of select monoclonal antibodies were extracted from the previously-described sequencing runs. A second sequencing run of the input library, using primers that amplify the entire VL-linker-VH cassette, was performed to determine the full VL sequence. The CDRH3, which is read in both sequencing approaches, is used to identify the paired VL and VH domains. Sequences were codon-optimized and cloned into separate vectors containing the human IgG1 or human kappa constant regions, respectively. Plasmids were transiently transfected in HEK293 cells and the antibodies provided as harvested cell culture fluid (Twist Bioscience). Some monoclonal antibodies were cloned into a stable expression vector containing the FRT and GS gene compatible with our landing pad cell line. These were transfected and selected as above to generate stable antibody-expressing lines. A production run was performed as previously described4 and supernatant harvested for binding studies. Antibodies for functional testing were purified using Protein A chromatography (PrismA, Cytiva) and analyzed using SDS-PAGE and SE-HPLC.

Affinity measurement

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) kinetic affinity measurements were carried out on a GatorPrime Instrument (GatorBio). ScFv was generated using an in vitro transcription-translation kit (PURExpress, NEB, E6800). 5 μg/mL biotinylated strep-tag II capture antibody (GenScript, A01737) was bound at ≤50% saturation to Gator streptavidin coated probes (GatorBio, 160002) or Strep-Tactin XT probes (GatorBio, 160033) for 30, 60, or 120 s prior to loading 10-fold diluted scFv reaction mix for 600 s. Association and dissociation data were collected for each scFv against each RBD variant for 180 and 300 s, respectively. RBD antigen concentrations were titrated to 100, 33, 11, and 0 nM and run on a multicycle kinetic assay and kinetics were analyzed with a double reference strategy, as previously described5 and fitted to a global 1:1 kinetic model with Rmax Unlinked. 30 or 60 s baseline measurements proceeded the capture probe, scFv loading, and association steps.

Spike-binding ELISA

A set of 8 spike RBD variants covering diverse lineages and recent strains were selected and purchased as recombinant protein (Acro Biosciences). High-binding 96-well plates (BioLegend, 423501) were coated with 1 μg/mL of spike RBD overnight, then blocked with 1x PBST with 5% w/v nonfat dry milk (PBST-M) for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Antibody pools were diluted to 5 μg/mL in PBST-M, titrated 1:5 for a total of 8 points, and added to the washed plate. After incubation and washing, 0.2 μg/mL goat anti-human IgG-HRP (Southern Biotech, 2044-05) was added. After a final incubation and wash, plates were developed with TMB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34028), stopped with 450 nM stop solution (Abcam, ab171529), and imaged on a plate reader at 450 nm (SpectraMax i3x). Curve fits and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism.

Pseudoviral neutralization assays

The SARS-CoV-2 pseudotyped virus neutralization assay was performed in 96-well plates using ACE2 expressing HEK-293T target cells (Integral Molecular) transiently transfected with TMPRSS2 expression plasmid. GFP reporter virus particles (RVPs) expressing the same 8 Spike variants as the ELISA were purchased from Integral Molecular. The volume of RVP used per well was based on the manufacturer’s recommendation and diluted to 50ul with cDMEM +10% FBS.

Antibodies were prepared at 100 μg/mL and serially diluted 3-fold in cDMEM +10% FBS media in duplicate. 50 μL of antibody and 50 μL of diluted RVPs were incubated together at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 1 h, then 4 × 104 target cells were added to each well and incubated for an additional 72 h. Cells were trypsinized for 5 min at 37°C with TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12563029), quenched, stained with DAPI, and filtered before quantifying GFP+ cells using a Cytoflex LX (Beckman Coulter). Flow cytometry data were analyzed in FlowJo (BD Biosciences). Curve fits and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism.

Lentiviral transfection and stable cell line generation

The amino acid sequence for CoV-2 S WT (UniProt P0DTC2) was cloned with a human IgK signal peptide, c-myc tag, and PDGFR transmembrane region into a plasmid with the EF1α promoter and 5′ and 3′ lentivirus LTRs. This plasmid was transiently transfected along with the Rev, Gag/Pol, and VSV-G envelope plasmid into HEKTa cells for lentiviral packaging. Supernatant containing lentivirus was collected at 24 and 48 h, filtered, and added to Expi293 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final dilution of 1:4. Transduced cells were stained and sorted for c-myc expression.

In vitro translation optimization

A 1:1 mix of RNA encoding a positive (casirivimab) and control (ipilimumab) scFv was added at 450 nM to an in vitro transcription-translation kit with (PURExpress, NEB, E6800) or without (PURExpress ΔRF123, NEB E6850) ribosome release factors, and incubated at 37°C for 10 or 30 min to generate ARM complexes. These mixes were diluted to 90 nM and used to stain CoV-2 S WT CHOZN cells. Total RNA of the washed cells was collected and quantified using RT-qPCR (1-step RNA-to-Ct kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A Taqman probe to the CHO housekeeping gene Fkpb1a was used to normalize cell count, while probes specific for the unique CDRH3 sequences of casirivimab or ipilimumab were used along with standard curves to quantify the ARM RNA bound to the cell. The ratio of casirivimab RNA to ipilimumab RNA is reported (Figure S1B).

Single-cell sorting

An equal mix of 4 antigen lines was stained and washed as above, then single live (DAPI negative) cells were sorted into individual wells of a 96-well plate containing RNAse inhibitor and 2x RT-PCR master mix (SuperScript IV One-Step RT-PCR system, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After a freeze-thaw cycle, additional reagents were added: master mix, RT, and 2 pairs of primers that amplify the antigen barcode or antibody VH and add a universal PCR adapter (SCS scFv FWD, SCS scFv REV, SCS Antigen FWD, SCS Antigen REV; Table S2). 3 μL of the RT-PCR product is used in a second PCR (NebNext Q5 Ultra II, NEB) with forward primers that bind the adapter and add a plate and one of 8 row barcodes, and antigen- or antibody-specific reverse primers with one of 12 column barcodes (SCS P5 FWD, SCS scFv P7 REV, SCS Antigen P7 REV). The primers are arrayed across the plate so each well gets a unique combination of column and row barcode, allowing single-well identification.28 The resulting library was loaded at 1.8 nM onto a 500-cycle Reagent Nano Kit v2 (Illumina, MS-103-1003) and run on a MiSeq with a 255 x 255 read length scheme.

ARM staining dilution

An equimolar RNA mixture of 4 antibodies was used at 450 nM to generate ARM complexes, then diluted 1:2 and used to stain CoV-2 S WT cells. RNA was collected as previously described, then quantified with RT-qPCR using a probe for the SecM sequence and the CHO housekeeping gene Fkpb1a.43 Analysis was as previously described, with the number of cells quantified from the CHO housekeeping gene and a standard curve obtained for different cell concentrations.

Competitive ARM staining

An equimolar mix of RNA encoding 14 scFvs in ribosome display format was diluted 1:1 with RNA for an irrelevant library and used to generate ARM complexes. The mix was diluted to 45 nM positive ARMs and used to stain CoV-2 S WT CHOZN cells. Total RNA was collected, and the VH region amplified and sequenced. Bound ARMs were quantified by identifying reads with exact CDRH3 matches and quantifying the percentage of each clone. The recovery of each clone is calculated by dividing by the clone frequency in the input stain sample.

Quantification and statistical analysis

PolyMap sequencing analysis

For antibody sequencing, we performed sequencing analysis as previously described.6,7 Briefly, we calculated the expected number of errors (E) for a read from its Phred scores and discarded reads with E > 2. We processed IMGT44 immunoglobulin sequences to generate position-specific sequence matrices (PSSMs) to identify CDR3H for each sequencing read. Custom Python scripts were used to extract the Drop-seq cell barcode and unique molecular identifier (UMI), creating a dictionary linking these identifiers to the antibody CDR3H for each sequencing read. This cell barcode-UMI-CDR3H dictionary was then used to cluster antibody clones. We combined unique sequences if they had 1 amino acid difference for 5–6 amino acid long CDR3H, and 1–2 amino acid differences for >6 amino acid long CDR3H.

For antigen sequencing, we used custom Python scripts to identify unique DNA barcodes preassigned to each antigen, allowing for up to two mismatches. We generated a dictionary linking the Drop-seq cell barcode, UMI, and antigen barcode for every sequencing read. We further processed this cell barcode-UMI-antigen barcode dictionary in R. Duplicated UMIs within each cell barcode were removed. To distinguish cell barcodes associated with antigen-expressing cells from those associated with ambient RNA, we ranked all cell barcodes in decreasing order of read count and plotted their cumulative read fraction, producing a knee plot. The knee of the plot was identified using the kneedle function from the kneedle R package, and all cell barcodes after the knee were excluded. We determined the antigen identity for the remaining cell barcodes, requiring each cell barcode to be associated with ≥10 antigen reads and ≥90% single antigen reads to filter out droplets containing multiple cells.

We then merged the antigen dictionary with the antibody dictionary by cell barcode. For each antibody, we computed the total antibody reads across all cells. We then calculated the percentage of antibody reads associated with each antigen. We divided the percent antibody reads for each antigen by the number of cells expressing that antigen, resulting in normalized percent reads reflecting each antibody’s relative binding across different antigen variants. We next sought to identify antibodies with sufficient read counts to provide reliable and reproducible binding profiles across experimental replicates. To establish a minimum read count threshold, we first iteratively varied the minimum total antibody read count threshold from 0 to 5000. For each threshold value, we filtered the antibodies to include only those with total reads greater than or equal to the threshold, then calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient of the normalized percent reads for all antibody-antigen pairs between two experimental replicates. We identified the smallest threshold value at which the Pearson correlation coefficient reached at least 0.8. Antibodies with total reads below this threshold were excluded from further analysis. This process resulted in PolyMap binding profiles for 129 antibodies in the 2020 library and 40 antibodies in the 2023 library, respectively. The Pearson correlation coefficients for these PolyMap profiles between experimental replicates were 0.812 and 0.802, respectively (Figures S4B and S4C). The final PolyMap profiles were visualized as heatmaps using ggplot2 version 3.4.2.

Single-cell sorting sequencing analysis

Custom Perl scripts were used to identify the plate, row, and column barcodes, along with the antigen or antibody identity of each sequencing read from Fastq files. To assign the antigen identity of each well, only wells with ≥10 total antigen reads were considered. To exclude wells with multiple cells, we only considered wells where the antigen with the most reads represented ≥90% of the total antigen reads in a given well. We further filtered for wells with ≥600 total antibody reads. Percent antibody read for each antigen was visualized as heatmaps/scatterplots using ggplot2 version 3.4.2 in R.
