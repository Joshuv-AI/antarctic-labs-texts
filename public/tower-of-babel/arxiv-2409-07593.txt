Non-local dissipative Aw-Rascle model and its relation with Matrix-valued communication in Euler alignment
Nilasis Chaudhuri †, Jan Peszek †, Maja Szlenk †‡, Ewelina Zatorska

Abstract
We compare the multi-dimensional generalisation of the Aw-Rascle model with the pressureless Euler-alignment system, in which the communication weight is matrix-valued. Our generalisation includes the velocity offset in the form of a gradient of a non-local density function, given by the convolution with a kernel K K . We investigate connections between these models at the macroscopic, mesoscopic and macroscopic (hydrodynamic) level, and overview the results on the mean-field limit for various assumptions on K K .

Introduction

The purpose of this note is to explain the connection between the dissipative Aw-Rascle model and the Euler-alignment system with matrix-valued communication weight. We also review the well-posedness results for both of the models along with the literature concerning the derivation and the relations between their respective mico- meso- and macroscopic descriptions. The main novelty of the paper is the adaptation of results from
[
4
]
and
[
22
]
to the dissipative non-local Aw-Rascle model, which we employ to investigate the micro-to-macroscopic mean-field limit, cf. Corollary
4.3
and Theorem
5.1
.

To begin with, let us recall the classical Aw-Rascle-Zhang model (ARZ) of one lane road traffic,
[
3
,
27
]
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\varrho+\partial_{x}(\varrho u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\varrho w)+\partial_{x}(\varrho wu)=0,\\
&{u}={w}-P(\varrho).\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly to compressible pressureless Euler equations, the ARZ model is a system of two conservation laws. The difference between the systems is in the momentum equation, which for the ARZ model is associated with the the preferred velocity of motion
w
and not the actual velocity
u
. The two velocities are related by the density-dependent offset function
P(\varrho)
which models formation of congestion on the road.

The multi-dimensional extension of this model, called the
dissipative Aw-Rascle model
, was recently proposed in
[
1
]
to describe the flow of pedestrians, see also
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
20
]
for the relevant analytical results. It is again a system of conservation laws, where velocity vector fields
{\bf w}
and
{\bf u}
are related not by the scalar function
P(\varrho)
, but by the gradient of the offset function
\nabla p(\varrho)
.
In
[
1
]
a special singular function
p(\varrho)
was considered to take into account the volume exclusion effect and prevent overcrowding. However, the effects modelled by function
p(\varrho)
were of purely local character.

In this note we discuss the version of the dissipative Aw-Rascle model, in which the offset function is non-local and depends on the macroscopic density through convolution with the offset kernel
K(x)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf u})=0,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf w})+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf w})=0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle{\bf u}={\bf w}-\nabla K\ast\varrho.

(1.1c)

We will refer to this system as the dissipative
non-local
Aw-Rascle model, DNAR for short. We will review the mathematical results for the DNAR model and explain the connection with Euler-alignment model with matrix-valued communication weight (EAM) derived below.

On the level of classical solutions, both systems: DNAR and EAM, are equivalent. Indeed, let us assume for now that system (
1.1c
) has a classical solution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and that the kernel
K
is sufficiently smooth and symmetric
K(x)=K(-x)
.
Rewriting the momentum equation (
1.1b
) with indices, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho w_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{x_{i}}(\varrho w_{j}u_{i})=0,\;j=1,2,3,

with

w_{j}=u_{j}+(\partial_{x_{j}}K)\ast\varrho.

Then, using the continuity equation (
1.1b
), we rewrite the momentum equation:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho u_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{x_{i}}(\varrho u_{j}u_{i})+\partial_{t}(\varrho(\partial_{x_{j}}K)\ast\varrho)+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{x_{i}}(\varrho(\partial_{x_{j}}K)\ast\varrho\,u_{i})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\varrho u_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{x_{i}}(\varrho u_{j}u_{i})+\varrho\left(\partial_{x_{j}}K\ast\partial_{t}\varrho+\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}K)\ast\varrho\,u_{i}\right).

Again, with the help of the continuity equation and integration by parts, we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{j}}K\ast\partial_{t}\varrho

\displaystyle=-\int(\partial_{x_{j}}K)(x-y)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{y_{i}}(\varrho u_{i})\right){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}K)(x-y)\varrho(y)u_{i}(y){\rm d}{y}.

Consequently

\begin{split}&\partial_{x_{j}}K\ast\partial_{t}\varrho+\sum_{i=1}^{3}[(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}K)\ast\varrho]\,u_{i}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\int(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}K)(x-y)\left(u_{i}(x)-u_{i}(y)\right)\varrho(y){\rm d}{y}.\right)\end{split}

Therefore, coming back to the vector notation, we can rewrite system (
1.1c
) in the form of the EAM system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf u})=0,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})-\varrho\left(\int\left({\bf u}(y)-{\bf u}(x)\right)^{\intercal}\bm{\Psi}(x-y)\varrho(y){\rm d}{y}\right)=0,

(1.2b)

where

\begin{split}\bm{\Psi}=\nabla^{2}K=\left(\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}K\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}.\end{split}

(1.3)

Notice that for the classical solution of (
1.2b
) and for a given sufficiently regular
\bm{\Psi}
, satisfying (
1.3
), the steps leading to derivation of system (
1.2b
) can be inverted. In this sense the EAM model (
1.2b
) is equivalent to the DNAR model (
1.1c
). Because both systems describe the evolution of macroscopic quantities (averaged density and velocity) characteristic for the models of fluids, we refer to them as
hydrodynamic
or
macroscopic
models. We summarise the above argument in the form of the following proposition.

Proposition 1.1

Assume that
K
is sufficiently smooth and symmetric i.e.
K(x)=K(-x)
. Then classical solutions
(\varrho,{\bf w})
to the DNAR system (
1.1c
) are equivalent, through the transformation (
1.1c
), to classical solutions to the EAM system (
1.2b
).

Taking as a point of reference Figure
1
, we note that the microscopic equivalence between the DNAR and EAM systems follows by a direct differentiation in time of the former. In one space dimension, it has been the basis for cluster predictability for the Cucker-Smale model, see
[
14
,
13
]
. In the case of regular kernels
K
, the equivalence has been extended by Kim
[
18
]
to the kinetic and hydrodynamic levels in one space dimension. Choi and Zhang
[
8
]
used it as a means to establish the existence of solutions to the Cucker-Smale kinetic equation for weakly singular communication weight. In alignment dynamics, weakly singular communication weight is associated with the kernel
K(x)=|x|^{2-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, which translates to the singular communication weight
D^{2}K(x)\approx|x|^{-\alpha}
, on the level of the Cucker-Smale model. The equivalence relation between higher-dimensional Cucker-Smale-type models and their first-order reduction based on the DNAR dynamics was then studied by Kim
[
17
]
and extended to the kinetic models for smooth and strictly convex potentials
K
. In the case of singular communication (again,
K(x)=|x|^{2-\alpha}
) the equivalence was obtained by the second author and Poyato in
[
21
]
.
The derivation of the hydrodynamic DNAR model and the EAM model, as well as their equivalence, were never studied as far as we are aware. Also, the case of more general interaction potentials
K
is widely open. It should be noted however that the hydrodynamic Euler-alignment system (with classical scalar communication) has been derived rigorously, cf.
[
12
,
16
,
11
,
15
]
.

In this note, we focus only on the multi-dimensional models, because in
1D the matrix-valued communication weight becomes the usual scalar one, and the system (
1.2b
) reduces to relatively well-studied Euler-alignment system, see for instance
[

Most studies of the 1D Euler-alignment system rely to some extent on the quantity

\begin{split}e(t,x):=\partial_{x}u(t,x)+\int_{\Omega}\psi(x-y)(\varrho(t,x)-\rho(t,y)){\rm d}{y},\quad\psi(x)=\partial_{x}^{2}K(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}},\end{split}

where
\Omega
is either the whole line
\mathbb{R}
or the torus
\mathbb{T}
.
The key property of
e
is that it satisifes the continuity equation

\begin{split}\partial_{t}e+\partial_{x}(ue)=0.\end{split}

From the DNAR perspective, noting that
\partial_{x}w=\partial_{x}u+\partial_{x}^{2}K\ast\varrho
, it translates into the continuity equation

\begin{split}\partial_{t}(\partial_{x}w)+\partial_{x}(u\partial_{x}w)=0,\end{split}

at least on the support of
\varrho
, which is a natural consequence of the transport equation for
w
. In other words, the
e
quantity is nothing but the spacial derivative of the preferred velocity
w
.
The algorithm of obtaining higher order entropies was discussed in
[
9
]
, and it was used as a tool to propagate initial regularity of classical solutions (provided no vacuum has formed) instead of the classical energy method.
Equation (
1.1b
) can be therefore seen as a natural generalization of this concept to the multi-dimensional setting, for the entropy of order “-1”. Indeed, the (formally) derived equation for
\operatorname{div}{\bf w}
,

\begin{split}\partial_{t}(\operatorname{div}{\bf w})+\operatorname{div}({\bf u}\cdot\nabla{\bf w})=0,\end{split}

no longer has the structure of the continuity equation.
It is therefore not clear how to generate a strong solution to any of the multidimensional systems (
1.2b
) or (
1.1c
).

Notation

Throughout the paper by
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and by
{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
p\geq 1
– the space of probability measures with finite
p
-moment. We say that
\mu_{n}\to\mu
narrowly, if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,{\rm d}\mu_{n}\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,{\rm d}\mu

for all
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(i.e. all bounded-continuous functions
\phi
). By
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})-narrow
we denote the space of probability measures with the narrow topology; we specifically use the space
C([0,T];{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})-narrow)
as the space of all continuous mappings from
[0,T]
to the
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})-narrow
space.

For a Borel-measurable mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a probability measure
\mu
we define the
pushforward measure

F_{\#}\mu
by

F_{\#}(\mu)(B):=\mu\left(F^{-1}(B)\right)\quad\text{for Borel sets }B.

The main property of the pushforward measure, used throughout the paper, is the change of variables formula

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g\,{\rm d}(F_{\#}\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\circ F\,{\rm d}\mu,

whenever
g\circ F
is
\mu
-integrable.

We shall employ distances based on optimal transport commonly associated with the
{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
spaces. These include the bounded-Lipschitz distance

\begin{split}d_{BL}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,{\rm d}\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,{\rm d}\nu\right|:\quad\phi\mbox{ is Lipschitz continuous with }[\phi]_{Lip}\leq 1,|\phi|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\},\end{split}

where by
[\phi]_{Lip}
we denote the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. Another metric used throughout the paper is the Wasserstein distance

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,{\rm d}\gamma(x,y),

where the infimum is taken over the set
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
of admissible plans, i.e. over all probability measures
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
x
- and
y
-marginals equal to
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. In other words

\Gamma(\mu,\nu)=\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}):\quad(\pi_{x})_{\#}\gamma=\mu\mbox{ and }(\pi_{y})_{\#}\gamma=\nu\},

where
\pi_{x}
and
\pi_{y}
are projections to the first and the second component, respectively.

Throughout the paper we write

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)\mu(x,{\rm d}y)

to indicate that the integration above is taken only with respect to
y
. Integrating with respect to all available variables we use the simplified notation

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,y){\rm d}\mu.

From micro- to mesoscopic DNAR dynamics

In full analogy to the derivation of AR system from the Follow-the-Leader particle dynamics, one may try to connect the macroscopic equations (
1.1c
) with their microscopic counterpart.
For the dissipative AR system with offset function
p(\varrho)=(\varrho^{-1}-\varrho_{{\rm{max}}}^{-1})^{-\gamma}
the formal procedure was explained in
[
1
]
. In this section we discus various rigorous results concerning derivation of the kinetic variant of DNAR model from the system of interacting active particles, for which the offset function is non-local.

The microscopic analogue of the DNAR system, describing interactions of
N
agents with positions
x_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and velocities
v_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at time
t\geq 0
, is given by the ODE system:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=v_{i},

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}_{i}=0,

(3.1b)

\displaystyle v_{i}=\omega_{i}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla K(x_{i}-x_{j}).

(3.1c)

Here,
v_{i}
is the actual velocity of the
i
th particle, while
{{\omega}}_{i}
is the desired velocity of motion.
Note that, differentiating equation (
3.1c
) w.r.t.
t
, we can rewrite the system in the form of the
Cucker-Smale
-type system of alignment dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=v_{i},

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{j}-v_{i})\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(3.2b)

where
\bm{\Psi}
is related to
K
by (
1.3
). More specifically, (
3.2b
) is a variant of the Cucker-Smale model with matrix-valued communication as in
[
23
]
. In full analogy with the equivalence between the hydrodynamic models, cf. Proposition
1.1
, their microscopic counterparts, systems (
3.1c
) and (
3.2b
), are also equivalent as long as the existence classical solutions is guaranteed by the Cauchy–Lipschitz theory.

In the limit
N\to\infty
, one formally describes system (
3.1c
) by the probability
\mu_{t}(x,{{\omega}})
of finding agents with desired velocity
{{\omega}}
around point
x
at time
t
. It is subject to the kinetic equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu+\operatorname{div}_{x}(u[\varrho_{t}]\,\mu)=0,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle u[\varrho_{t}](x,\omega)=\omega-\nabla_{x}K\ast\varrho_{t}(x),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle\varrho_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{t}(x,{\rm d}\omega).

(3.3c)

It was shown in
[
22
]
that an appropriate weak measure-valued solution to (
3.3c
) is a gradient flow of the interaction energy

\begin{split}{\mathcal{W}}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}K(x-x^{\prime}){\rm d}[\mu\otimes\mu],\end{split}

(3.4)

under one of the following assumptions

\begin{split}K(x)&=K(-x)\mbox{ is lower semicontinuous and }\lambda-\mbox{convex for }\lambda>0,\quad\mbox{or}\\
K(x)&=\frac{1}{2-\alpha}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\alpha\in(0,1).\end{split}

(3.5)

In
[
22
]
kernel
K
in (
3.5
) is refereed to as “weakly singular” due to the singularity of the associated alignment communication kernel
\bm{\Psi}\approx|x|^{-\alpha}
in (
3.2b
).

Using the gradient flow interpretation of (
3.3c
) leads to a variety of results such as well-posedness, uniform-in-time contractivity. It is also used to rigorously derive system (
3.3c
) from the underlying particle dynamics governed by (

).
The definition of the solution and the statement of some main results from

are recalled below.

Consider any

4

From meso- to macroscopic DNAR dynamics

In order to derive the DNAR system (
1.1c
) from the corresponding kinetic equations (
3.3c
) we proceed as in the case of derivation of compressible Euler equations from Vlasov-type equations, i.e. by taking the
\omega
-moments of equation (
3.3a
).

Let us define the macroscopic variables

\begin{split}\varrho=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}}),\quad\varrho{\bf w}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{{\omega}}\;\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}}).\end{split}

We first integrate equation (
3.3b
) w.r.t.
{{\omega}}
to deduce that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u[\varrho_{t}]\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})=\varrho{\bf w}-\varrho\nabla K\ast\varrho.\end{split}

Next, integrating equation (
3.3a
) we obtain the continuity equation in the form:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\varrho+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf w}-\varrho\nabla K\ast\varrho)=0,\end{split}

and so, defining as in (
1.1c
)

\begin{split}{\bf u}:={\bf w}-\nabla K\ast\varrho,\end{split}

(4.1)

we derive the continuity equation (
1.1a
) of the DNAR system.

Next, we compute the first moment of (
3.3a
) in
{{\omega}}
, obtaining

\begin{split}\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf w})+\operatorname{div}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u[\varrho_{t}]\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})\right)=0.\end{split}

(4.2)

Equivalently, using (
3.3b
) and (
4.1
) subsequently, we can write

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u[\varrho_{t}]\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\omega-\nabla_{x}K\ast\varrho_{t}(x)\right)\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\omega\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})-\varrho_{t}(\nabla_{x}K\ast\varrho_{t}(x))\otimes{\bf w}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\omega\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}})+\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf w}-\varrho{\bf w}\otimes{\bf w}.\end{split}

As a consequence, we can rewrite (
4.2
) in the form

\begin{split}\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf w})+\operatorname{div}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf w})+\operatorname{div}\mathbf{\Pi}=0,\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Pi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}({{\omega}}-{\bf w})\otimes{{\omega}}\,\mu(x,{\rm d}{{\omega}}).\end{split}

(4.3)

In this sense, the DNAR system is similar to the compressible Euler equation, with transported velocity
{\bf u}
replaced by
{\bf w}
and the monokinetic ansatz, characterising the pressureless case, translated to

\begin{split}\mu(x,{{\omega}})=\varrho(t,x)\delta({{\omega}}-{\bf w}).\end{split}

Rigorous justification of hydrodynamic limit of the kinetic Cucker-Smale model to the pressureless Euler system with nonlocal alignment force was obtained by Figalli and Kang
[
12
]
. It was shown recently in
[
11
]
that in the strongly singular case, every weakly continuous and compactly supported solution to the kinetic Cucker-Smale equation is monokinetic, and its macroscopic variables satisfy the Euler-alignment system without any pressure term akin to (
4.3
) and with no necessity to take a hydrodynamic limit as in
[
12
]
.

The rigorous justification of this closure for general
K
will be the purpose of our study in the future. In this note we restrict ourselves to showing the following result for weakly singular case.

Proposition 4.1

Suppose that
K
is as in (
3.5
) and in the
\lambda
-convex case it is
C^{1}
. Let
0\leq\varrho\in C([0,T];{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})-\mbox{narrow})
and
{\bf w}\in C^{1}_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a weak solution to (
1.1c
) (tested with
C^{1}_{b}
functions). If

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}+|{\bf w}_{0}(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\varrho_{0}<+\infty

(4.4)

and

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{1-\alpha}{\rm d}\varrho_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}t<+\infty,

\begin{split}\mu_{t}(x,{{\omega}})=\varrho_{t}(x)\delta({{\omega}}-{\bf w})\end{split}

is a measure solution to (
3.3c
) in the sense of Definition
3.1

Proof.
Let us check all the assumptions required in Definition
3.1
. The fact that
\mu_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
follows from (
4.4
). To check the narrow continuity of
\mu
, let
\psi
be a bounded-Lipschitz function
2
2

2

Narrow convergence is defined as tested by bounded-continuous functions, but by Portmanteau theorem it is equivalent to testing with bounded-Lipschitz functions.
and suppose that
t_{n}\to t
. We have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\psi(x,{{\omega}})\,{\rm d}\varrho_{t_{n}}\delta({{\omega}}-{\bf w}_{t_{n}}(x))-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\psi(x,{{\omega}})\,{\rm d}\varrho_{t_{n}}\delta({{\omega}}-{\bf w}_{t}(x))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\psi(x,{\bf w}_{t_{n}}(x))-\psi(x,{\bf w}_{t}(x))|\,{\rm d}\varrho_{t_{n}}(x)+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x,{\bf w}_{t}(x))\,{\rm d}(\varrho_{t_{n}}(x)-\varrho_{t}(x))\right|=:I_{n}+II_{n}.

By the Lipschitz continuity of

and by the narrow continuity of

Since condition (

\begin{split}{\mathcal{R}}&:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Big(\partial_{t}\eta_{t}(x,\omega)+(\omega-\nabla K*\varrho_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{x}\eta_{t}(x,\omega)\Big)\,{\rm d}\mu_{t}\,{\rm d}t\\

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\eta}(t,x)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\eta}(t,x)+({\bf w}_{t}(x)-\nabla K*\varrho_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{\eta}(t,x)

Remark 4.2

Corollary 4.3

) and in the

For any bounded-Lipschitz function

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)d\varrho^{N}_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\varrho(t,x)dx\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\phi(x)d\mu^{N}_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\phi(x)d\varrho(t,x)\delta({{\omega}}-{\bf w})\right|

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}d_{BL}(\varrho^{N}_{t},\varrho(t,\cdot))\to 0.

Solutions to the DNAR system as a limit of particle dynamics

The purpose of this section is to prove that, under stronger assumptions on the potential
K
, the classical solutions to the hydrodynamic DNAR system (
1.1c
) might be obtained as a mean-field limit of the solutions to the microscopic formulation (
3.1c
). We aim to use the microscopic and macroscopic equivalence between the EAM and DNAR systems, see Proposition
1.1
and subsequent comments.
Then we modify the results obtained by Carrillo-Choi in
[
4
]
for the classical Euler-alignment system.

5.1

The mean-field limit

Another, currently better explored strategy follows the results of Carrillo and Choi
[
4
]
concerning the direct micro-to-macroscopic mean-field limit for the pressureless Euler-alignment system. Below, we generalize them to the case of sufficiently smooth, matrix-valued communication weight
\bm{\Psi}\in W^{1,\infty}
. The matrix-valued equivalent of the mean-field limit result from
[
4
]
(without the interaction potential) states the following.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\{(x_{i},v_{i})\}_{i=1,\dots,N}
solve the particle system (
3.2b
) and let

(\varrho,{\bf u})\in(C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty})),\quad\varrho>0,

be a classical solution to system (
1.2b
) subject to initial conditions
(\varrho,{\bf u})_{|_{t=0}}=(\varrho_{0},{\bf u}_{0})
.

Assume that the interaction matrix
\bm{\Psi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
belongs to
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies

\bm{\Psi}(-x)=\bm{\Psi}(x),\qquad{\bf{v}}^{T}\bm{\Psi}(x){\bf{v}}\geq 0\quad\forall_{{\bf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

Then, the following estimate is satisfied:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i}(t),t)-v_{i}(t)|^{2}+d_{BL}^{2}(\varrho^{N},\varrho)\leq C\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}_{0}(x_{i}(0))-v_{i}(0)|^{2}+d_{BL}^{2}(\varrho^{N}_{0},\varrho)\right),

(5.1)

where

\varrho^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}.

In consequence, assuming that the initial data satisfy

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}_{0}(x_{i}(0))-v_{i}(0)|^{2}+d_{BL}^{2}(\varrho^{N}_{0},\varrho)\right)=0,

(5.2)

the following convergence holds:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}(t)\delta_{x_{i}(t)}&\rightharpoonup^{*}\varrho{\bf u}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}(t)\otimes v_{i}(t)\delta_{x_{i}(t)}&\rightharpoonup^{*}\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}(t),v_{i}(t)}&\rightharpoonup^{*}\varrho\delta_{{\bf u}}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})).\end{split}

(5.3)

Sketch of the proof.
Derivation of the estimate (
5.1
) is based on Gronwall’s lemma. Differentiating the left-hand side in time (the dependence of time is omitted), we get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})\cdot(\partial_{t}{\bf u}(x_{i})+\nabla_{x}{\bf u}(x_{i})v_{i}-\dot{v}_{i}).

Next, using momentum equations (
3.2b
) and (
1.2b
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})^{T}\nabla{\bf u}(x_{i})({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})^{T}\cdot\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-y)({\bf u}(y)-{\bf u}(x_{i}))\varrho(t,y){\rm d}{y}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})(v_{j}-v_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}

For
I_{1}
we get

I_{1}\leq\|\nabla{\bf u}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}_{i}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}.

For the second part of
I_{2}
we write

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})(v_{j}-v_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})({\bf u}(x_{j})-v_{j}-{\bf u}(x_{i})+v_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})({\bf u}(x_{j})-{\bf u}(x_{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})({\bf u}(x_{j})-v_{j}-{\bf u}(x_{i})+v_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-y)({\bf u}(y)-{\bf u}(x_{i}))\;\mathrm{d}\varrho^{N}(y).

Using the above, we express
{I_{2}}
as

\displaystyle{I_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-y)({\bf u}(y)-{\bf u}(x_{i}))\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho-\varrho^{N})(y)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})^{T}\Psi(x_{i}-x_{j})({\bf u}(x_{j})-v_{j}-{\bf u}(x_{i})+v_{i}).

However, by the symmetry of
\bm{\Psi}
the second part of
I_{2}
equals

-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}-{\bf u}(x_{j})+v_{j})^{T}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-x_{j})({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}-{\bf u}(x_{j})+v_{j}),

which is negative by the fact that
\bm{\Psi}
is positive semi-definite, and thus, we arrive at

{I_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\Psi}(x_{i}-y)({\bf u}(y)-{\bf u}(x_{i}))\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho-\varrho^{N})(y).

Finally, applying Hölder’s inequality we obtain

I_{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}+\||\bm{\Psi}||{\bf u}|\|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2}d_{BL}^{2}(\varrho,\varrho^{N}).

From Proposition 2.2 from
[
4
]
, it follows that

d_{BL}(\varrho,\varrho^{N})\leq d_{BL}(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{0}^{N})+C\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}}.

Note that this estimate is derived only using the continuity equation for
\varrho
and the definition of
\varrho^{N}
, and thus remains the same for the matrix-valued communication weight. In conclusion

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}+d^{2}_{BL}(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{0}^{N})\right)

and the estimate (
5.1
) follows from Gronwall’s lemma.

From the estimate (
5.1
), one can easily deduce the desired convergence. Indeed, for any
\bm{\varphi}\in\left(W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\varphi}(x)\cdot(\varrho{\bf u})(x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\cdot\bm{\varphi}(x_{i})\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\varphi}(x_{i})\cdot({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\varphi}(x)\cdot{\bf u}(x)\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho-\varrho^{N})(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varphi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\|\bm{\varphi}\cdot{\bf u}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}d_{BL}(\varrho,\varrho^{N}).

Similarly, for any
\bm{\varphi}\in\left(W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)^{d\times d}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\varphi}:(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\varphi}(x_{i}):v_{i}\otimes v_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\varphi}(x_{i}):({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})\otimes({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\varphi}:({\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho-\varrho^{N})(x)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\varphi}(x_{i}):\Big({\bf u}(x_{i})\otimes({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})+({\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i})\otimes{\bf u}(x_{i})\Big),

and so

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\varphi}:(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})\;\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\varphi}(x_{i}):v_{i}\otimes v_{i}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varphi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}+\||\bm{\varphi}||{\bf u}|^{2}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}d_{BL}(\varrho,\varrho^{N})

\displaystyle+2\||\bm{\varphi}||{\bf u}|\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

In conclusion, assuming that the initial data satisfy (
5.2
), we have convergences (
5.3
) with respect to the bounded-Lipshitz distance.

On the other hand, for the empirical measure
\mu_{t}^{N}(x,v)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(x_{i}(t),v_{i}(t))}
, and a test function
{\varphi}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,v)\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho\delta_{{\bf u}}-\mu^{N})(x,v)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,{\bf u}(x))\;\mathrm{d}(\varrho-\varrho^{N})(x)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varphi(x_{i},{\bf u}(x_{i}))-\varphi(x_{i},v_{i}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}d_{BL}(\varrho,\varrho^{N})+(\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\|{\bf u}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\bf u}(x_{i})-v_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

and so

d_{BL}(\varrho\delta_{{\bf u}},\mu^{N})\to 0.

5.2

Existence of strong solutions

In this section we state the result for the existence of local in time classical solutions to the hydrodynamic DNAR system (
1.1c
). We again employ the observation from the introduction, that for classical solutions this system is equivalent with the EAM system (
1.2b
). The following statements generalize the results of
[
4
]
, to the case of matrix-valued communication weight.

Definition 5.2

Let

s>\frac{d}{2}+1

\varrho,{\bf u}
is a strong solution of (

and
\nabla^{2}{\bf u}\in C([0,T];H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

2.

(\varrho,{\bf u})

The analogue of the Theorem 4.1 from
[
4
]
in the case of matrix-valued communication weight reads as follows.

Theorem 5.3

Let
s>\frac{d}{2}+1
and
R>0
. Suppose that the communication weight
\bm{\Psi}

Remark 2

Appendix A

Disintegration Theorem

For the reader’s convenience we recall the disintegration theorem as stated in
[
2
, Theorem 5.3.1]
.

Theorem A.1
(Disintegration theorem)

For
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
denote projection onto the second componenent as
\pi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\right)
, define its projection onto second factor as
\nu:=(\pi_{2})_{\#}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
. Then there exists a family of probabilistic measures
\left\{\mu_{x_{2}}\right\}_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
, defined uniquely
\nu
almost everywhere, such that

1.

The map

\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\ni x_{2}\longmapsto\mu_{x_{2}}(B)

is Borel-measurable for each Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
;

2.

The following formula

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}\mu(x_{1},x_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}\mu_{x_{2}}(x_{1})\right){\rm d}\nu(x_{2})

holds for every Borel-measurable map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\longmapsto[0,\infty)
.
