Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Regarding Antibiotic Use and Resistance Among University Students in Mogadishu: A Comparative Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Walid Abdulkadir Osman, Khalid Abdukadir Osman, Hassan Muse Ahmed, Abdiwali Abdullahi Abdiwali, Yusuf Omar Hassan, Yahye Sheikh Abdulle Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimAntibiotic resistance is one of the greatest health threats in the world, particularly in Somalia, where the healthcare system is weak and information on antibiotic misuse is limited. University students are a crucial demographic, as they have the potential to influence future community behaviors through their knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to antibiotic use and resistance among students in Mogadishu, Somalia.MethodsA comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted among 321 undergraduate students at Mogadishu University using a stratified random sampling method from November 29 to December 17, 2025. Data were collected using a structured self‐administered questionnaire, with a 60% cutoff for classification of scores. Analysis was performed using SPSS version 26, which included descriptive statistics, t‐tests after confirming normality using the Shapiro‐Wilk test (p > 0.05 for all domains), and Spearman correlation analyses, with significance set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for mean differences.ResultsOnly 38.6% of all students had adequate knowledge, 24.9% held favorable attitudes, and 35.2% reported good practices. Health sciences students were significantly more likely than non‐health sciences students to have adequate knowledge (65.7% vs. 18.5%, p < 0.001), favorable attitudes (47.4% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001), and good practices (57.7% vs. 18.5%, p < 0.001). Female gender (p < 0.001 for all domains), upper academic year (p < 0.001 for knowledge; p = 0.001 for attitude; p = 0.004 for practice), and prior antibiotic training (p < 0.001 for knowledge; p = 0.010 for attitude; p = 0.005 for practice) were also significantly associated with higher KAP scores.ConclusionThere is a concerning lack of KAP regarding antibiotic use, especially among non‐health sciences students, indicating an urgent need for comprehensive antibiotic stewardship training across all university disciplines to combat antibiotic resistance.

Introduction

The discovery of antibiotics has indeed contributed to major advances in health, greatly reducing the rates of illness and death from bacterial infections and playing a key role in increasing life expectancy and enhancing quality of life [1, 2, 3]. However, the excessive and improper use of these agents has led to a major global concern of antibiotic resistance (ABR) [4, 5]. Antibiotic‐resistant infections pose serious health challenges, resulting in conditions that can be incredibly difficult, if not impossible, to treat effectively [4, 6]. ABR threatens global health and the economy as infections become harder to treat, leading to prolonged illness, longer hospital stays, rising healthcare costs, and increased mortality, which collectively strain the health system [7, 8]. This crisis is worsened by factors such as antibiotic self‐medication, easy access to non‐prescribed antibiotics, and high medical costs [9], and is further complicated by inadequate diagnostic tools and inappropriate prescribing practices [10, 11].

As a result, antibiotic resistance (ABR) is a critical global public health issue that poses significant challenges and leads to considerable socioeconomic effects [12]. A systematic review indicated that bacterial antibiotic resistance was directly associated with approximately 4.95 million deaths worldwide in 2019 [4]. Global bodies have warned that without urgent action, antibiotic resistance could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050, with severe economic damage [13]. The burden is particularly heavy in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where about one‐third (33.7%) of the population lacks a proper understanding of antibiotics and their function [14].

Research on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to antibiotics has been conducted among various populations, including university students [15, 16]. Findings indicate that while many students are aware of antibiotic resistance, significant knowledge gaps persist, leading to improper practices like self‐medication [17, 18]. University students are a crucial demographic; as future healthcare professionals, policymakers, and community leaders, their understanding and behaviors will significantly influence the trajectory of the antibiotic resistance crisis [19]. Studies from various regions confirm that students frequently misuse antibiotics, often due to a lack of knowledge and the prevalence of self‐medication practices [20, 21, 22]. The COVID‐19 pandemic has complicated the situation by increasing self‐medication and antibiotic use, driven by fears of contracting infections in hospitals, the convenience of accessing medications, and limited availability of healthcare [23, 24, 25].

In Somalia, antibiotic resistance presents a severe and escalating public health challenge, exacerbated by a fragile healthcare system, infrastructural deficiencies, and an ongoing humanitarian crisis [26]. The misuse of antibiotics in human and veterinary medicine has led to alarming resistance patterns in common pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus [27]. For instance, studies have documented high resistance rates to antibiotics like clindamycin, ampicillin, and vancomycin, highlighting a serious public health threat [28]. In the capital, Mogadishu, which hosts a large population and a growing youth demographic, the KAP of its educated youth regarding antibiotic use and resistance remains entirely unmeasured. Therefore, this study will fill the gap in evidence on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding antibiotic use and resistance among university students in Mogadishu, Somalia, with a focus on comparing health sciences and non‐health sciences students to understand the influence of academic background. The results will form the foundation for basic data to guide public awareness campaigns, university curricula development, and the creation of a national action plan on antibiotic resistance suitable for the local environment.

Methods

Study Design and Population

A comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted to assess students' knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding antibiotic use and resistance. This research was conducted among undergraduate students of the leading non‐state university in the capital, Mogadishu University. The university was selected purposively as it is one of the largest and most established higher education institutions in the country, with diverse faculties and a student population representative of the city's academic community. The data collection period was from November 29 to December 17, 2025. The study targeted two faculties, which are the Faculty of Health Sciences and the Faculty of Computer Science and Information Technology. The two faculties were purposively selected to represent Health Sciences and Non‐Health Sciences students, respectively. The total population from these two faculties was 1665.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Undergraduate students from the Faculty of Health Sciences and the Faculty of Computer Science and IT regularly attended classes and provided informed consent, while the postgraduate students, students from other faculties, or undergraduate students who were absent or refused to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample Size and Technique

The sample size for each faculty was calculated using a single population proportion formula: n = (Z2 × p × (1‐p))/d2 , assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. Due to the lack of previous local studies on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding antibiotic use and resistance, a proportion of 50% was estimated, resulting in a need for 384 participants. Since the total student population was less than 10,000, a finite population correction formula was applied: n f = n/(1 + n/N). This resulted in a revised sample size of 313 participants. To account for potential non‐responses, the sample size was increased by 10%, leading to a target sample size of 344 participants.

A stratified random sampling method was utilized for this research. The sampling frame was initially categorized by faculty (Health Sciences and Computer Science & IT) and then subdivided by year of study within each faculty. For each stratum, a complete list of registered students was sourced from the university registry. The predetermined sample size for each faculty was distributed proportionally across its respective year‐of‐study strata. Final participants were then chosen from these strata using a simple random sampling approach, ensuring that each student had an equal opportunity to be selected for the study.

Data Collection Tool and Technique

The data collection instrument was a structured, self‐administered questionnaire that was adapted from previous studies [29, 30, 31]. It was composed of four sections: first, socio‐demographic characteristics such as age, sex, marital status, faculty, year of study, average monthly personal expenditure, family connection to healthcare work, and participation in antibiotic use training; second, knowledge assessment (10 items), which included questions on the definition of antibiotics, effectiveness of antibiotics against infections, meaning of antibiotic resistance, its causes, and consequences; third, assessment of attitudes consisted of 10 items, which are related to antibiotic safety, efficacy, regulatory control, self‐medication practices, and adherence; fourth, assessment of practices, which consisted of 10 items, assessed pre‐use consultation, adherence (course completion, dose timing), medication management (saving, sharing, or discarding leftovers), and self‐medication tendencies.

Knowledge‐related questions were scored using a binary system. Correct answers received a score of 1, while incorrect answers or ‘don't know' responses received a score of 0. Therefore, the total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 10. For attitudes, responses to the 10 attitude statements were coded as 1 for a favorable response and 0 for an unfavorable or neutral response, depending on alignment with antimicrobial stewardship principles. The total attitude score also ranged from 0 to 10. Regarding practices, responses to the 10 practice questions were coded as 1 for self‐reported good practices and 0 for bad or neutral practices. The total practice score ranged from 0 to 10 as well.

For each domain, a binary classification was established using a 60% cutoff of the total possible score [32]. This benchmark was specifically chosen because it aligns with Mogadishu University's established academic passing standard. Participants who achieved a score of 6 or higher were classified as having ‘Adequate' knowledge, a ‘Favorable' attitude, and ‘Good' practices. Conversely, participants with a score of below 6 were categorized as having ‘Inadequate' knowledge, an ‘Unfavorable' attitude, and ‘Poor' practices.

Data Quality Management

The questionnaire was pre‐tested for reliability on 30 students from the Faculty of Medicine at Mogadishu University. This number was selected based on established recommendations for pilot studies [33]. These participants were excluded from the final study sample. The pre‐test was conducted to assess the clarity, comprehension, and time required to answer the questions. Certain wordings of a few questions were subsequently modified to improve understanding. The internal consistency (measured by Cronbach's Alpha) for the knowledge, attitude, and practice scales was found to be 0.738, 0.733, and 0.719, respectively, indicating acceptable reliability.

Data Analysis

The collected data were checked for completeness, coded, and entered into IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26 (released in 2019; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), for analysis. Frequencies and percentages were calculated to summarize the socio‐demographic characteristics and KAP scores. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test, which indicated approximate normality for knowledge scores (W = 0.989, df = 321, p = 0.212), attitude scores (W = 0.992, df = 321, p = 0.184), and practice scores (W = 0.991, df = 321, p = 0.201). The independent samples t‐test was used to assess the presence of significant differences among the sociodemographic variables in relation to the respondents' knowledge, attitudes, and practices after confirming approximately normal distributions. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for mean differences. The Spearman correlation coefficient was also used to evaluate the associations between the variables in the study. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical clearance from the Ethical Review Board of the National Institute of Health—Somalia (Ref: NIH/IRB/125/NOV/2025). An verbal informed consent form was obtained from each participant before filling out the survey. Written consent was not obtained because of concerns about potential stigmatization among university students. This approach was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee. Permission was also obtained from the administrative authorities of the selected faculties. The purpose and benefits of the study were explained to all participants. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. Anonymity and confidentiality were maintained through the research process as no personal identifiers were collected on the questionnaire.

Results

Participant Sociodemographic Profile

A total of 321 completed questionnaires were analyzed, resulting in a response rate of 93.3%. The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. The sample was predominantly young, with more than half (54.5%, n = 175) of the respondents being under 21 years of age. A notable disparity in sex distribution was observed between the faculties; the Health Sciences cohort was largely female (79.6%, n = 109), while the Non‐Health Sciences cohort was predominantly male (73.9%, n = 136). This distribution reflects the underlying gender composition of the respective faculties at the target university. The vast majority of all students (93.1%, n = 299) were unmarried. Non‐Health Sciences students were more often in their lower academic years (64.1%, n = 118), whereas Health Sciences students were more frequently in their upper years (61.3%, n = 84). Just over half of all participants (51.1%, n = 164) reported having a family member working in the healthcare field. Prior training on antibiotic use was reported by only a third of the total sample (33.3%, n = 107); however, this was more common among Health Sciences students (42.3%, n = 58) than among their Non‐Health Sciences counterparts (26.6%, n = 49). Finally, most students (76.9%, n = 247) reported a monthly personal expenditure of 100 US Dollars or less.

Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Regarding Antibiotic Use and Resistance

An assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding antibiotic use revealed concerning gaps, particularly among students from Non‐Health Sciences (NHS). A significant deficiency in fundamental knowledge was evident. Less than one‐third of participants correctly recognized that antibiotics are ineffective against fungi (31.8%, n = 102) and that they do not treat viral infections (31.5%, n = 101). While two‐thirds (67.9%, n = 218) understood that antibiotics should only be used with a prescription, essential concepts related to antibiotic resistance were understood by only about half of the students. For instance, only 172 (53.6%) recognized that misuse can lead to resistance, and 166 (51.7%) understood that missed doses increase this risk (Table 2). Attitudes were similarly mixed. Only 182 (56.7%) of the participants agreed that antibiotics should be sold by prescription only, and less than one‐third (33%, n = 106) disagreed with the statement that antibiotics are safe for common use. Additionally, less than half of the students (49.5%, n = 159) held the appropriate attitude that unused antibiotics should not be saved for later (Table 3).

Overall and Comparative KAP Levels

The overall assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) is presented in Table 5. Within the total sample, the level of adequate knowledge regarding antibiotic use was low, with only 124 (38.6%) of participants achieving this level, and a mean knowledge score of 4.77 ± 3.16. However, significant disparities existed between student groups; Health Sciences students demonstrated a markedly higher proportion of adequate knowledge (65.7%, n = 90) compared to Non‐Health Sciences students (18.5%, n = 34).

Performance was notably poorer in the attitudinal and behavioral domains (Table 5). Favorable attitudes were held by fewer than one‐quarter of all participants (24.9%, n = 80). A stark divide was evident, with 65 (47.4%) of Health Sciences students reporting favorable attitudes, compared to only 8.2% (n = 15) of Non‐Health Sciences students. Although the overall rate of self‐reported good practices was marginally higher than that for favorable attitudes, it remained concerningly low at 35.2% (n = 113). This domain again revealed a significant gap, with Health Sciences students reporting good practices more than three times as often as Non‐Health Sciences students (57.7%, n = 79 vs. 18.5%, n = 34).

Association Between Sociodemographic Characteristics and KAP Scores

In Table 6, the statistical analysis revealed several significant associations between sociodemographic factors and scores in the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) domains, with a p‐value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Faculty affiliation emerged as the strongest predictor for all three domains (p < 0.001). Health Sciences (HS) students consistently demonstrated higher mean scores in knowledge (6.84 ± 2.86), attitude (5.78 ± 3.12), and practice (6.10 ± 2.80) compared to Non‐Health Sciences (NHS) students, with mean differences being statistically significant and showing large effect sizes: knowledge (t = 12.22, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.13–1.61); attitude (t = 9.42, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.82–1.28); and practice (t = 10.18, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.90–1.37). Additionally, sex was significantly associated with all KAP scores, as female students reported higher mean scores in knowledge (5.88 ± 3.31), attitude (5.09 ± 3.38), and practice (5.36 ± 3.18) compared to male students (knowledge: t = 6.67, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.52–0.98; attitude: t = 5.87, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.43–0.88; practice: t = 6.38, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.49–0.94).

The academic year was another significant factor, with upper‐year students achieving higher mean scores than lower‐year students in knowledge (5.67 ± 3.25; t = 4.91, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.33–0.77), attitude (4.75 ± 3.18; t = 3.32, df = 319, p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.15–0.59), and practice (4.88 ± 3.03; t = 2.93, df = 319, p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.55). Prior antibiotic use training was significantly associated with higher mean scores across all three domains: knowledge (5.68 ± 3.01; t = 3.71, df = 319, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.21–0.67); attitude (4.77 ± 3.09; t = 2.59, df = 319, p = 0.010, Cohen's d = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.08–0.53); and practice (5.03 ± 3.20; t = 2.83, df = 319, p = 0.005, Cohen's d = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.56). Lastly, the presence of a family member working in healthcare was significantly associated only with higher knowledge scores (5.34 ± 2.90; t = 3.31, df = 319, p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.15–0.59). In contrast, age group, marital status, and monthly expenditure showed no statistically significant association with any of the KAP domains.

Correlation Between KAP Domains

A Spearman's rho test was performed to measure the strength of the correlation between the study domains of KAP (Table 7). The results displayed a significant strong positive correlation between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.706, p < 0.001), a significant strong positive correlation between knowledge and practice (r = 0.664, p < 0.001), and a significant strong positive correlation between attitude and practice (r = 0.670, p < 0.001). This suggests that an increase in knowledge level tends to strongly correlate with more positive attitudes and better practices, and that attitudes and practices are also closely linked.

Discussion

This study reveals critical deficits in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) concerning antibiotic use among university students in Mogadishu, Somalia. The findings illuminate a dangerous intersection of widespread misconceptions and significant disparities between student groups, directly contributing to the drivers of antibiotic resistance (ABR) in a resource‐constrained setting. The findings reveal that the overall level of KAP is suboptimal, but consistent with global trends where inappropriate antibiotic use is a primary driver of ABR, which is linked to poor KAP [34, 35]. Similar to studies in Ethiopia [36] and Iraq [37], a fundamental misunderstanding of antibiotic use was prevalent, underscoring a public health education gap that fuels unnecessary antibiotic demand. For instance, if a student believes a cold requires an antibiotic, they are more likely to seek one without a prescription.

The data demonstrates a profound “know‐do” gap, where even limited knowledge fails to translate into appropriate attitudes or behavior. While 67.9% knew antibiotics required a prescription, only 56.7% agreed with this as a necessary policy—a clear attitudinal disconnect. This gap between cognition and conviction is a recognized barrier in health behavior change. As Price et al. [38] argue in a systematic review of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) behavioral drivers, knowledge is a necessary but insufficient component; attitudes and social norms exert a stronger influence on practice. Our findings strongly support this model. The poorest performance was in the attitudinal domain, suggesting that deeply held beliefs about antibiotic safety (“safe for common use”) and convenience (saving leftovers) are significant, entrenched barriers. The deficiencies in knowledge and attitudes led to problematic self‐reported practices among the students. These findings suggest that, although a knowledge gap exists, the more critical deficits lie in the appropriate attitudes toward, and particularly the safe practices regarding, antibiotic use.

Crucially, health sciences (HS) students consistently scored significantly higher across all three domains compared to their non‐health sciences (HS) counterparts (p < 0.001 for KAP). This finding aligns with comparative studies from Thailand [39] and other regions [40, 41], which indicate that health‐related curricula provide students with greater exposure to concepts in microbiology, pharmacology, and infectious disease management. The exposure to advanced knowledge is a key differentiator in KAP scores, as observed in previous studies [39, 42]. A significant observation is that although HS students performed better than NHS students, 34.3% still showed insufficient knowledge, 52.6% had negative attitudes, and 42.3% indicated poor practices. This indicates that the existing training in health sciences may not be adequate to guarantee a thorough understanding and suitable behaviors among students. Lower‐year students score significantly worse than upper‐year students, suggesting that limited exposure to microbiology and pharmacology in early education contributes to their overconfidence in knowledge about antibiotic use. This partial understanding leads to a “false sense of clinical self‐competence,” encouraging students to self‐diagnose and avoid professional consultation [43]. Therefore, targeted interventions should be introduced early in health sciences training to address these issues.

The study identified gender as a significant factor influencing KAP scores. Female students demonstrated significantly higher average scores than their male counterparts across all three domains (p < 0.001). This association between being female and achieving higher KAP scores is supported by global research. A scoping review conducted by Pham‐Duc and Sriparamananthan [44] found consistent evidence indicating that women generally have better knowledge and more cautious attitudes, which may be linked to traditional gender roles in health management. Additionally, a study from the United Arab Emirates revealed that female students had higher KAP scores than male students, suggesting that female students possess a better understanding of, and more positive attitudes towards, antibiotic use and resistance [21]. This finding in Mogadishu is crucial for policy, suggesting that while the overall population requires intervention, educational campaigns may need to adopt gender‐specific strategies to effectively address the lower KAP levels observed among male university students. Furthermore, prior antibiotic use training was also significantly associated with higher mean scores across all three domains (KAP). The correlation between antibiotic training and improved outcomes is well‐documented, emphasizing the importance of structured education in shaping knowledge and behavior regarding antibiotic use. Research indicates that educational interventions significantly enhance students' understanding and attitudes towards antibiotics, which is crucial in combating antibiotic resistance [32, 45].

The influence of familial background on KAP scores provided another illuminating distinction between different types of knowledge acquisition. The statistical association between having a family member in healthcare and favorable KAP scores was found to be limited exclusively to the knowledge domain, with no significant impact on attitude or practice, consistent with a previous study conducted in Iraq [46]. This finding suggests that information acquired through informal family interactions primarily conveys descriptive and factual knowledge, such as drug names (for instance, referring to amoxicillin as “the two‐color pill”) and common treatment patterns for ailments, resulting in a more passive awareness [47]. This work builds on Miller's Pyramid of clinical competence (“knows,” “knows how,” “shows how,” “does”). It argues that moving from foundational knowledge (“knows”) to competent action (“does”) and professional identity requires structured, reflective training within a clinical community, which informal familial exposure alone cannot provide [48].

Strengths and Limitations

There are several strengths that enhance the relevance and validity of the research. First, a comparative cross‐sectional design was utilized to provide statistical comparisons between health sciences students and non‐health sciences students. Second, this study contributes to an important gap in the existing literature by being the first study to provide empirical KAP data from a Somali university where antibiotic resistance is a significant public health problem. Third, the use of a validated questionnaire and the application of a stratified random sampling technique contributed to the reliability of the data that were collected.

However, the findings do have some limitations. Firstly, because the study used a cross‐sectional design, it cannot determine causal relationships among demographic factors, knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Secondly, the research was limited to a single university and involved only two faculties, which could impact how representative and applicable the results are to other Somali universities or the wider student population. Thirdly, there was a significant gender imbalance between the faculties; although this reflects the actual gender breakdown of the student body at the university, it may restrict gender comparisons. Fourthly, self‐reported behaviors might be influenced by social desirability bias, which could result in the underreporting of negative behaviors. Lastly, the final sample size of 321 participants was slightly below the intended target of 344 due to logistical challenges in collecting data during exam periods; however, it exceeded the minimum required sample size of 313 and provided adequate statistical power to identify moderate effect sizes. Therefore, future studies should be performed on various disciplines and universities across Somalia.

Implications for Practice

The findings regarding antibiotic resistance (ABR) in Somalia present several urgent challenges that must be addressed through policy and practice changes. There is a clear need to enhance and standardize antibiotic stewardship education across health sciences programs at Somali universities. Additionally, making it mandatory for non‐health sciences students to take a credit‐bearing public health module that includes topics on ABR and the careful use of medications would be highly beneficial. Given the significant differences in understanding influenced by sex and academic year, it is essential to develop tailored messaging. Moreover, the fact that only 56.7% of students understand that antibiotics should only be used with a prescription indicates that many have normalized the practice of acquiring antibiotics without a prescription. This highlights the urgent need to enforce existing regulations on antibiotic sales and to improve public education about these laws through focused awareness campaigns.

Conclusion

This study provides the first empirical evidence of a significant antibiotic KAP crisis among Somali university students, characterized by a substantial deficit in appropriate knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards antibiotic use among university students in Mogadishu, with non‐health sciences students being disproportionately affected. The findings demonstrate that knowledge deficits are merely the surface of a deeper problem rooted in attitudes and social norms within this academic community, as evidenced by the substantially lower favorable attitude rates (24.9%) compared to adequate knowledge rates (38.6%). A holistic approach is required, combining enhanced, targeted educational programs with strengthened regulatory enforcement to curb misuse of antibiotics. Future research should explore the behavioral and contextual determinants of inappropriate practices using qualitative methods, evaluate the effectiveness of educational interventions, and assess KAP across multiple Somali universities to determine the generalizability of these findings. Educating all university students with correct antibiotic stewardship principles is an essential investment in Somalia's capacity to combat the growing threat of antibiotic resistance.
