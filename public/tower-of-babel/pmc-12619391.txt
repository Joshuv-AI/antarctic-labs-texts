Unraveling the causal association of epigenetic age acceleration with common oral diseases and its underlying mechanisms: findings from Mendelian randomization and integrative genetic analysis
Authors: Lianjie Peng, Shengzhe Nie, Yihua Sun, Shihan Wang, Yaying Wang, Zhichao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe epigenetic clock is recognized as a highly accurate predictor of biological aging, but the relationship between epigenetic age acceleration and oral diseases remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the causal associations between epigenetic age acceleration and oral diseases and identify the shared gene expressions.MethodsA two-phase study design was used: in phase 1, we conducted MR analysis to investigate the association between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases. In phase 2, we conducted transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) to identify gene expressions linked to phenotypes with positive outcomes. Subsequently, we performed summary-based MR (SMR) analyses integrating expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) datasets to ascertain whether these gene expressions could affect the phenotypes. Finally, we performed biological pathway enrichment analysis to investigate the potential mechanisms.ResultsMR analysis revealed significant causal relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and oral diseases. Specifically, GrimAge was associated with an increased risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.160, 95% CI 1.010–1.333, p = 0.036 in FinnGen cohort; OR = 1.120, 95% CI 1.000–1.255, p = 0.049 in GLIDE consortium). PhenoAge showed a significant association with stomatitis (OR = 1.062, 95% CI 1.007–1.120, p = 0.026). Intrinsic epigenetic age acceleration (IEAA) was linked to a higher risk of oral lichen ruber planus (OR = 1.128, 95% CI 1.036–1.230, p = 0.006). Reverse MR analysis identified a bidirectional causal relationship between oral lichen ruber planus and IEAA (OR = 1.127, 95% CI 1.006–1.263, p = 0.039). No significant associations were observed between epigenetic age acceleration and dental caries, recurrent aphthous ulcers, or oral leukoplakia (all p > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. TWAS and SMR identified eight genes associated with the effect of GrimAge on periodontitis, six genes associated with the effect of PhenoAge on stomatitis, four genes associated with the effect of IEAA on oral lichen ruber planus and seven genes associated with the effect of lichen ruber planus on IEAA.ConclusionsThis study revealed causal relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases, and explored the relevant mechanisms, highlighting the potential novel strategies for prevention.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02005-9.

Introduction

The global population distribution is increasingly shifting towards an older demographic, with the number and proportion of elderly individuals rising in various countries. According to World Health Organization (WHO) reports, by 2030, one-sixth of the world's population is projected to be aged 60 years or older. Moreover, by 2050, the number of individuals aged 60 years or older is expected to reach 2.1 billion, while the population aged 80 years or older will increase to 426 million [1]. The global aging crisis is unprecedented in severity. Given the decline in physiological function and increased susceptibility to diseases among the elderly, the global healthcare system is poised to face significant challenges [2]. Against this backdrop, elucidating the intrinsic connections between aging and disease is a crucial scientific foundation for addressing these challenges.

Chronological age serves as a conventional metric for assessing aging. However, substantial heterogeneity exists among individuals of the same chronological age in terms of physical appearance, health status, and overall functionality [3]. This disparity between chronological and biological age is defined as age acceleration. Scientifically, biological age provides a more accurate reflection of an individual's physiological state than chronological age. Various models have been developed to quantify biological aging, including stress load [4], telomere length [5], and more recently, epigenetic age [6], with varying predictive capacities for morbidity and mortality risks. Notably, epigenetic aging represents a crucial component of biological aging, characterized by specific epigenetic markers such as DNA methylation and histone modifications [7, 8]. Compared with traditional biological age indicators, epigenetic age demonstrates superior sensitivity in capturing the multifaceted biological changes occurring during aging [6, 9, 10]. Currently, the epigenetic clock, as a specific tool for quantifying epigenetic age, is regarded as the best available predictor of biological age [11]. The measurement of epigenetic clocks enables a direct quantification of the difference between biological age and chronological age, known as epigenetic age acceleration [12]. It is widely recognized that aging is a highly complex process, which cannot be adequately captured by any single biomarker. To this end, scientists have developed distinct epigenetic clocks, each with unique characteristics, with the aim of assessing epigenetic aging from different dimensions, thereby achieving a more precise quantification of biological [13]. The first-generation epigenetic clocks include the HannumAge and the DNAm HorvathAge [14]. HannumAge was developed based on DNA methylation sites from whole blood and is highly sensitive to biological aging processes reflected in blood samples [15], while DNAm HorvathAge was developed based on multi-tissue DNA methylation sites and demonstrates high accuracy in cross-tissue age prediction [8]. Subsequently, scientists developed IEAA (Intrinsic Epigenetic Age Acceleration) by eliminating the influence of age-related changes in blood cell composition from the DNAm HorvathAge, aiming to better characterize the intrinsic biological aging process of cells in tissues [16]. In recent years, second-generation epigenetic clocks have also been developed, such as PhenoAge and GrimAge. These clocks are constructed based on DNA methylation sites associated with mortality, risk factors like smoking, as well as a variety of clinical biomarkers, exhibiting greater proficiency in predicting age-related individual morbidity and mortality [12, 14, 17]. These distinct epigenetic clocks each assess biological aging changes in the organism from different perspectives. Therefore, their combined application allows for the leveraging of complementary advantages, thereby enabling more accurate evaluation of epigenetic age acceleration and facilitating a more systematic and comprehensive elucidation of the highly complex biological process—aging.

Oral health is an integral part of overall health. The prevention and treatment of oral diseases are of great significance for improving the quality of life [18–22]. However, an increasing body of evidence from animal models and human studies suggests that there may be a potential bidirectional relationship between aging and oral diseases. Age-related systemic changes may influence oral health [23–25], increasing susceptibility to oral diseases and potentially exacerbating their severity [26–28]. Poor systemic health may accelerate the development of oral diseases through mechanisms such as inflammation, immune dysregulation, and oxidative stress [29–32]. Conversely, existing research has also emphasized that oral diseases may affect systemic health through various mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, bacteremia, metabolic disorders, and impaired nutrient intake, thereby significantly accelerating biological aging of the organism [22, 33, 34]. However, prior investigations examining the association between aging and oral diseases have predominantly focused on specific conditions and were largely observational in design. These methodological limitations, including susceptibility to confounding variables, constrain their capacity to establish definitive causal relationships. Currently, our understanding of the relationship between biological aging and common oral diseases remains incomplete. Further research is needed to clarify the potential causal relationships, which may provide more effective strategies for the intervention of oral diseases and the mitigation of aging.

Mendelian randomization (MR), an epidemiological approach utilizing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs), enables causal inference between exposures and outcomes while minimizing confounding and reverse causation [35, 36]. In this study, we employed bidirectional MR analysis to investigate potential causal relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases. Subsequent transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted to explore underlying biological mechanisms.

Methods

Study design

As illustrated in Fig. 1, this study comprises two phases. In phase 1, we conducted bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to investigate the association between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases. In phase 2, we initially conducted a TWAS to identify gene expressions linked to phenotypes with positive outcomes. Subsequently, we performed summary data-based MR (SMR) analyses integrating expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) datasets to ascertain whether these gene expressions could affect the phenotypes. Finally, we performed biological pathway enrichment analysis to investigate the potential mechanisms associated with the mutual influence between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases.Fig. 1Design of this study

Design of this study

Data source

In this study, four genetic instruments were selected to measure epigenetic age, including GrimAge, HannumAge, Intrinsic Epigenetic Age Acceleration (IEAA), and PhenoAge [37]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data for common oral diseases were obtained from the FinnGen consortium and the GeneLifestyle Interactions in Dental Endpoints (GLIDE) consortium [38, 39]. Detailed datasets for each category are available in Table 1.

Selection of instrument variables

To enhance the reliability of the MR estimates, we applied stringent criteria to select eligible SNPs as instrumental variables (IVs) for exploring the relationship between oral diseases and epigenetic age acceleration. The specific screening criteria can be found in Supplementary Table S12.

To satisfy the correlation assumption, instrumental variables were chosen based on a genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) for their association with the exposure. This selection criteria was applied to all epigenetic age acceleration measures under study, namely GrimAge, HannumAge, IEAA, and PhenoAge. Given that the number of SNPs meeting this criterion was limited in some datasets, we relaxed the significance threshold to p < 5 × 10−6 to identify a suitable number of instrumental variables (IVs) [40]. This applied to all oral disease traits under study, including periodontitis, caries, DFSS (Decayed/Filled Surfaces Score), DMFS (Decayed/Missing/Filled Teeth Surfaces), stomatitis, recurrent aphthous stomatitis, oral lichen ruber planus, and oral leukoplakia. Subsequently, linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis (R2 < 0.001, clumping distance = 10,000 kb) was performed to ensure statistical independence, with palindromic SNPs excluded [41]. Furthermore, to avoid weak instrument bias of the IVs, the strength of the remaining SNPs was assessed using the F-statistic, computed as F = β2/SE2 [40]. SNPs with an F-value greater than 10 are considered strong genetic instruments that can explain phenotypic variation. These strong genetic instruments are chosen as IVs for the exposure phenotype and employed in MR analysis. The SNPs that met the requirements for MR analysis are in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2.

MR analysis

The primary analysis for assessing causal associations was performed using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) test. This test yields unbiased effect estimates of the exposure's impact on the outcome by weighting the regression of outcome effects on exposure effects, and serves as the primary statistical method for evaluating causal effects [40, 42].

Meanwhile, to validate our findings, MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode were employed to assess the robustness of the MR results.

Furthermore, scatter plots were used to delineate the association between the impact of SNPs on exposure and their impact on outcome [43].

Heterogeneity among SNPs was evaluated using Cochran's Q test. Pleiotropy was assessed via the MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test, where a P value greater than 0.05 indicated the absence of pleiotropy. Moreover, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the impact of individual SNPs on MR estimates by systematically removing each SNP and examining the resultant changes, thereby elucidating the influence of specific SNPs on outcomes [44, 45].

TWAS

For phenotypes yielding positive results in the MR analysis, we conducted a TWAS to identify genes significantly associated with both exposure and outcome.

In the TWAS, we utilized the FUSION method to integrate summary-level data from GWAS with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data obtained from European whole blood RNA-seq samples in the Genotype-Tissue Expression version 8 (GTEx v8) dataset [46]. We then used Fisher's method to combine the P values for exposure and outcome [47].

SMR

To further validate our findings, we applied SMR method to verify whether the genes identified in the TWAS analysis could exert an impact on the phenotypes. Then we used verified genes to conduct a biological pathway enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO) terms to uncover the mechanisms underlying the causality.

The SMR method was employed to evaluate the causal association between gene expression and phenotype by integrating summary statistics from GWAS and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analyses.

The eQTL summary data were obtained from the eQTLGen database [48]. Genome-wide significant SNPs were used as instrumental variables and a heterogeneity of causal instruments (HEIDI) test was conducted to separate causality or pleiotropy from linkage [49]. Significant gene expressions were confirmed if the P value for SMR analysis < 0.05 and P value for the HEIDI test > 0.01 [50, 51].

Results

Causal effect of epigenetic age acceleration on oral diseases

In our MR analysis, we found some potential causal associations of epigenetic age acceleration on oral diseases, the results are presented in Fig. 2.Fig. 2MR analysis of epigenetic age acceleration on oral diseases. MR analysis of epigenetic age acceleration on oral diseases. OR, odds ratio; CI, confidence interval; DFSS, Decayed/Filled Surfaces Score; DMFS, Decayed/Missing/Filled Teeth Surfaces; IVW, Inverse-Variance Weighted. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant

Our primary analysis, which used the IVW method, identified significant evidence of associations between epigenetic age acceleration and periodontitis, oral lichen ruber planus, and stomatitis. In MR analyses, GrimAge exhibited a significant and directionally consistent causal relationship with periodontitis in both the FinnGen cohort (OR = 1.160, 95% CI 1.010–1.333, p = 0.036) and the GLIDE consortium (OR = 1.120, 95% CI 1.000–1.255, p = 0.049). In addition, IEAA was significantly associated with an increased risk of oral lichen ruber planus (OR = 1.128, 95% CI 1.036–1.230, p = 0.006), whereas PhenoAge showed a significant association with stomatitis (OR = 1.062, 95% CI 1.007–1.120, p = 0.026). However, across all analyses, epigenetic age acceleration showed no significant causal association with dental caries (including the caries, DFSS, and DMFS phenotypes), recurring oral aphthaes, and oral leukoplakia (all p > 0.05).

To assess potential reverse causality between oral diseases and epigenetic age acceleration, we conducted a reverse MR analysis of oral diseases on epigenetic age acceleration, the results are presented in Fig. 3.Fig. 3MR analysis of oral diseases on epigenetic age acceleration. IVW analysis revealed a significant reverse causal association solely between oral lichen ruber planus and IEAA (OR = 1.127, 95% CI 1.006–1.263, p = 0.039). No significant reverse causal relationships were observed between any other oral diseases and epigenetic age acceleration (all p > 0.05).

Heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments

Heterogeneity testing via Cochrane’s Q statistic revealed no evidence of significant heterogeneity for the effect of GrimAge on periodontitis susceptibility, the effect of PhenoAge on stomatitis susceptibility, or the bidirectional associations between IEAA and oral lichen ruber planus (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, MR-Egger intercept tests and MR-PRESSO analyses indicated that these primary findings were not biased by pleiotropy (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses are detailed in Supplementary Figure S1–S4. Scatter plots indicated that the causal directions estimated by MR-Egger, weighted-median, simple-mode, and weighted-mode methods were concordant with those obtained via IVW, corroborating the robustness of the primary findings. Funnel plots corroborated the MR-Egger intercept test, demonstrating negligible pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analyses confirmed the absence of influential outliers, as the sequential exclusion of individual SNPs did not materially alter the effect estimates.

Genes concordantly expressed in GrimAge and periodontitis

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the impact of GrimAge on periodontitis susceptibility, we utilized whole blood RNA-seq data from GTEx, as well as GWAS data for GrimAge and periodontitis, to conduct a TWAS using FUSION. Thirteen overlapping genes were identified between GrimAge and periodontitis in the TWAS using data from the FinnGen database, while nine overlapping genes were identified using data from the GLIDE consortium (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Subsequently, we validated the effects of these genes on periodontitis using the SMR method. Seventeen genes were present in the eQTL dataset and were subjected to further SMR analysis. The SMR analysis revealed that eight genes (CENPV, RNF112, ZNF333, SCP2, DHX32, FAM151B, ECHDC2, and TFG) were significantly associated with periodontitis (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table S8). To elucidate the biological functions of these genes, we performed GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 4C). The enrichment results indicated that these genes are related to the activity of various enzymes and may be involved in the binding of fatty acids and other molecules. Discussion

Oral health is an essential component of systemic health and also influences the health of other body systems [22]. Although limited previous studies have suggested that the occurrence of certain oral diseases may be affected by aging, direct evidence comprehensively elucidating the causal relationship between aging and oral diseases remains lacking. In addition, most prior studies have used chronological age as an indicator for individual aging, neglecting the variability in biological aging rates influenced by multiple factors. In contrast, it is now widely recognized that biological age, as characterized by epigenetic markers, provides a more accurate reflection of an individual’s aging status [6, 9, 10].

In this study, we first employed MR to investigate the potential causal relationship between epigenetic age acceleration and oral diseases. The MR analysis revealed that epigenetic age acceleration was significantly and positively associated with an increased risk of periodontitis and stomatitis, while also demonstrating bidirectional causal relationships with oral lichen ruber planus. Subsequent sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness and reliability of these findings. Furthermore, through TWAS, SMR analyses, and GO functional enrichment, we elucidated potential mechanisms underlying the interplay between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases. These findings provide novel insights into the complex interactions between organismal aging and oral disorders.

In line with our study, a previous cross-sectional investigation revealed that the prevalence and severity of periodontitis escalate with increasing age [52]. This suggests a potential link between aging and periodontitis, though it remains unclear whether this reflects cumulative effects of aging or increased susceptibility to periodontitis in older individuals. Notably, several studies utilizing the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database reported a significant positive correlation between epigenetic aging acceleration and periodontitis, but failed to replicate this association in subsequent MR analyses [27, 53]. This discrepancy may stem from their limited use of epigenetic age acceleration metrics. Similarly, extensive clinical observational studies indicated that oral lichen ruber planus predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly populations, with median and mean diagnostic ages exceeding 60 years [54–56]. These findings partially align with our results. However, as observational studies, they failed to establish direct causal evidence between epigenetic aging acceleration and oral diseases. In contrast, our MR analysis leveraged multiple GWAS datasets on epigenetic age acceleration and common oral disorders, providing genetic evidence that accelerated aging increases the risk of both periodontitis and oral lichen ruber planus. Although no prior evidence linked aging to stomatitis, our study revealed a significant association between epigenetic aging acceleration and elevated stomatitis risk. Accumulated research indicated that aging individuals often experience a systemic chronic inflammatory state that progressively worsens. Given that the occurrence of stomatitis is largely influenced by the overall health status, it is reasonable to speculate that aging may promote the development of oral inflammation. Our study provides direct evidence for this hypothesis.

Aging is an inevitable biological process, typically accompanied by declining physiological function and reduced quality of life. Although humanity has long sought the secrets of longevity, current technology remains incapable of reversing aging. However, the rate of aging varies significantly among individuals due to genetic and environmental factors. Slowing the aging process may therefore represent the most viable strategy for extending lifespan. Disease is a well-established accelerator of aging. In this study, reverse MR analysis revealed a significant positive association between oral lichen ruber planus and IEAA, suggesting that oral lichen ruber planus may contribute to accelerated aging. While prior research on this relationship remains limited, existing studies demonstrated strong connections between oral lichen ruber planus and systemic health. Current evidence indicated that oral lichen ruber planus onset may be linked to anxiety/depression, sleep disorders, thyroid dysfunction, kidney disease [57–63], and elevated systemic inflammatory markers. Our findings provided genetic evidence linking oral lichen ruber planus to epigenetic aging acceleration, implying that effective oral lichen ruber planus management could potentially mitigate age-related decline. This positions oral lichen ruber planus treatment as a novel candidate strategy for aging intervention.

To further investigate the biological mechanisms underlying the mutual influence between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases, we conducted TWAS analysis to identify potentially relevant genes and used the SMR method to validate their impact on the target phenotypes. Subsequently, enrichment analysis was performed on these validated genes. The results showed that changes in the body's material metabolism, cell signaling, gene transcription, and cytoskeleton assembly may mediate the effect of epigenetic aging acceleration on the risk of periodontitis and stomatitis. Although this finding has been relatively underreported, it is, to some extent, supported by previous literature. For instance, the SCP2 gene encodes a nonspecific lipid-transfer protein, which plays a crucial role in the process of lipid metabolism [64]. Accumulating evidence has revealed a significant correlation between SCP2 and age-induced dysregulation of lipid metabolism [65]. Furthermore, the role of Lipid metabolism disorders in the pathogenesis of inflammatory conditions, including periodontitis, is well-established [66, 67]. Consistent with our findings, these reports suggest that SCP2-associated dysregulation of lipid metabolism may play a significant role in the increased risk of periodontitis associated with biological aging. Additionally, EPHB4 is a receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a critical role in cellular signal transduction [68]. Current research indicates that dysregulated signaling mediated by EPHB4 is significantly associated not only with aging but also with the onset of inflammatory diseases, thereby lending support to our hypothesis regarding its potential role in the increased risk of periodontitis associated with aging [68]. Meanwhile, epigenetic aging acceleration and oral lichen ruber planus may influence each other through biological processes such as transmembrane transport of substances, epigenetic modification, and cytoskeleton anchoring. This provides some references for further in-depth mechanistic research in the future. ABCC5 encodes a transmembrane transporter protein that plays a critical role in the cellular transport of various substances [69]. ABCC5 expression is significantly altered in aged individuals. The consequent dysregulation of its transport function is implicated as a key contributor to the pathogenesis of a range of age-related diseases [70]. Furthermore, studies have reported that ABCC5 is significantly upregulated in the peripheral blood of patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), an autoimmune disease [71]. And its expression level has also been correlated with the degree of immune cell infiltration in certain affected tissues [72]. As is well-known, oral lichen ruber planus is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease, with dysregulated immune cell infiltration being one of its pathological hallmarks [73, 74]. It is therefore plausible that ABCC5 plays a significant role in the complex interplay between aging and the pathogenesis of oral lichen ruber planus, which is consistent with our findings. In summary, while our findings currently receive only partial support from the published literature, they nonetheless lend considerable credence to the plausibility of the mechanistic hypothesis we have established.

It is noteworthy that, in this study, we observed that among the four selected epigenetic clocks, only GrimAge, PhenoAge, and IEAA exhibit a causal relationship with specific oral diseases, while HannumAge shows no significant association. This phenomenon has been almost universally observed in a large number of previously published similar studies related to epigenetic clocks [44, 75–79]. As we have previously outlined, aging is an immensely complex process. Different epigenetic clocks are grounded in distinct biological mechanisms and, consequently, capture only partial biological changes from specific aspects of the overall aging process. The heterogeneity in the biological aspects of aging, as captured by various epigenetic clocks, likely accounts for the observed inconsistencies in their associations with common oral diseases.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively explore the causal relationship between epigenetic aging acceleration and common oral diseases, revealing the potential connection between them from a genetic perspective. It has important clinical and public health significance. On the one hand, this research underscores the potential of epigenetic clocks like GrimAge, PhenoAge, and IEAA to act as new biomarkers. Their application in stratifying individuals at high risk for oral diseases including periodontitis, stomatitis, oral lichen ruber planus could facilitate early intervention and personalized preventive strategies, highlighting their significant clinical translational value. Moreover, there is increasing evidence positioning epigenetic modifications as feasible targets for therapeutic and preventive approaches [80]. This implies a potential strategy for disease prevention and therapy. For high-risk populations with significantly accelerated epigenetic aging, we might prevent or even reverse the onset of related oral diseases by controlling or reducing their level of epigenetic age acceleration. On the other hand, the bidirectional causal relationship between oral lichen ruber planus and IEAA suggests that patients are at a higher risk of accelerated aging. Therefore, managing oral lichen ruber planus should also involve addressing issues related to this accelerated aging, with the consideration of implementing anti-aging interventions where possible. This not only enhances the therapeutic efficacy for oral lichen ruber planus but also holds significant importance for identifying high-risk populations with accelerated aging and preventing aging-related diseases. Moreover, it suggests that effectively treating oral lichen ruber planus may not only improve oral health but also contribute to delaying systemic biological aging. This indicates that maintaining oral health could be a key strategy for humans to combat aging and extend healthspan, thereby providing new insights for anti-aging interventions. Finally, the key genes and pathways identified through our integrative genetic analysis have led to proposed mechanistic hypotheses for the interplay between biological aging and oral diseases. These findings provide valuable clues for the future development of novel therapeutic strategies for aging-related oral conditions, highlight potential therapeutic targets, and make it feasible to achieve efficient treatment by targeting the shared mechanisms underlying both oral diseases and aging.

However, our study failed to find evidence supporting causal relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and other oral diseases. Additionally, while this research provides valuable insights into the associations between epigenetic age acceleration and several common oral diseases, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study primarily relied on data from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although the four epigenetic clocks we selected capture distinct biological changes during aging and complement each other's limitations, they may still not fully capture the complexity of the aging process. Furthermore, it is important to note that aging is a systemic process, while oral diseases often present as localized manifestations. Given that prior research strongly suggests blood serves as a critical link between oral diseases and systemic health, we utilized large-scale and well-validated blood-derived eQTL data for our initial mechanistic exploration, as it represented the most robust and accessible resource among the limited options available [81–83]. However, although our TWAS and SMR analyses utilizing whole blood eQTL data have preliminarily identified candidate genes implicated in the aging-oral disease association and shed some light on their potential interacting mechanisms, these blood-based findings may still not fully capture the tissue-specific gene expression in the target tissues relevant to oral pathologies. Therefore, it remains essential to conduct further comparative validation using sequencing data from oral tissue-specific sources (such as periodontal tissues associated with periodontitis and oral mucosal tissues associated with oral lichen ruber planus), followed by experimental verification through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, the interconnections among these genes and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood and require further investigation.. Future studies should include more diverse ethnic populations and a broader range of epigenetic clocks to validate and extend our findings. Moreover, performing validation studies in sequencing data from oral tissue-specific sources and relevant oral tissue-specific in vivo and in vitro models represents a critical next step to substantiate our observations and uncover the mechanistic basis for these associations. Through such investigations, we may gain deeper understanding of the bidirectional interplay between aging and oral health, providing scientific foundations for developing novel prevention and treatment strategies to address the challenges posed by global population aging.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study employed MR to elucidate the complex relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and common oral diseases. Through integrated analyses including TWAS, SMR, and functional enrichment, this study preliminarily explored the underlying mechanisms. These findings are not only significant for the prevention and treatment of oral diseases and the development of anti-aging strategies, but also provide mechanistic hypotheses and direction for subsequent research. However, future studies are needed to further validate and explore these findings, in order to more profoundly elucidate the key mechanisms underlying their interaction and subsequently refine clinical prevention and treatment strategies based on these mechanisms.
