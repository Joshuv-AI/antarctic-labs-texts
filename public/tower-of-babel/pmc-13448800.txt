EZ-SPOTs: a simple, low-cost, high-throughput liquid handling platform applied to antimicrobial susceptibility testing of clinical isolates
Authors: Jon Albo, Shenghao Tan, Joee D. Denis, Rebecca Franklin-Guild, Samira Shiri, Kelsi M. Sandoz, Nate J. Cira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-performance liquid handling is essential for a wide range of experiments. Previously, we introduced the Surface Patterned Omniphobic Tiles (SPOTs) platform, which enables manipulation of hundreds to thousands of independent experiments without costly equipment or excessive consumable expenses. However, the SPOTs platform uses a custom coating that requires a chemical synthesis step. This coating is also easily damaged by handling and requires careful manipulation, making it challenging to transport and interface the platform with existing well plates. To overcome these challenges, here we introduce the EZ-SPOTs platform which can be created in an hour with common fabrication tools and three components: glass, a phobic coating, and plastic. EZ-SPOTs preserve many of the SPOTs platform's strengths including ease of use, the ability to handle a wide range of volumes, and scalability, but by adopting a robust, abrasion-resistant coating, EZ-SPOTs devices can be reused. Additionally, this improved robustness enables the platform to address the “chip-to-lab” obstacle by directly interfacing with 96-well plates. Here, we describe the fabrication of EZ-SPOTs and showcase how devices can be reused to test many isolates for antimicrobial susceptibility. These results quantitatively match current gold-standard assays, and the increased throughput provides substantially more information than standard approaches.

Introduction

Liquid handling is a routine task, critical to laboratory assays in life sciences, healthcare, and medical research. Manual pipetting is a versatile method for handling a broad range of liquids but is limited in throughput, requires large reaction volumes, has a high risk of human error, and requires substantial time commitment. Pipetting robots address the limitations of throughput and human error but require high initial and maintenance costs. A wide array of microfluidic techniques1–3 have their own advantages; however, they are often application-limited or require specialized expertise and equipment to fabricate and use. For example, emulsion-based droplet microfluidic techniques4,5 are highly efficient at rapidly generating and examining many thousands of individual reactions. However, they involve an immiscible oil phase which makes it challenging to modify or extract target droplets for downstream processes. A second example involves manipulating vertically stacked microwell and microchannel arrays, including Stacking Chip6 and SlipChip.7 This clever approach enables controlled droplet merging and splitting of sub-microliter volume through the relative movement of two micropatterned plates. While powerful, fabricating each new device layout requires a photolithographic microfabrication process, which can introduce complexity and limit accessibility for non-specialist users. A third example, droplet microarrays,8,9 offers multiplexing10,11 and sample recovery capabilities,9 but is restricted in compatible liquid types. These devices are often loaded by dipping the substrate into an aqueous solution,12 which requires a large dead volume, or rolling a bulk droplet over the surface,12 which lacks precise volume control. For more precise volume control, droplet microarrays can be loaded by printing using a non-contact liquid dispenser,13 though this requires tens of thousands of dollars in investment.

Previously, we introduced the Surface Patterned Omniphobic Tiles (SPOTs) platform,14 which builds on droplet microarrays and uses a superomniphobic coating15,16 that can repel liquids with low surface tensions. This platform allows for precise deposition of microliter to sub-microliter volumes of liquids using a passive loading device without the need for specialized fabrication equipment. We initially demonstrated the platform's versatility and ease of use through broad applications which included culturing bacterial cells and reagent manipulations for polymerase chain reaction (PCR). We then demonstrated the platform's throughput by screening thousands of drug combinations to discover novel synergistic pairs.17 Despite the advantages of the SPOTs platform, the custom-synthesized superomniphobic coating can be complicated to produce as it requires access and infrastructure to handle harmful chemicals and high-temperature ovens. Additionally, the coating used for the SPOTs platform can be damaged from contact, making repeated use and handling difficult. This lack of robustness significantly limits the platform's widespread adoption, as even minimal handling can cause severe degradation in performance, necessitating a new plate for each experiment. The fragility of the coating also limits the cross compatibility with commonly adopted consumables, such as well plates.14

In this paper, we introduce easy Surface Patterned Omniphobic Tiles (EZ-SPOTs), which improve upon the SPOTs platform by utilizing a commercially available coating to develop a robust, reusable, and easy-to-make solution for performing hundreds to thousands of simultaneous experiments. Abrasion resistance and ease of fabrication make this variation more accessible to laboratories across the world. We then demonstrate how these improvements enable a large number of antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) assays to be run with a small number of EZ-SPOTs devices.

Results and discussion

The EZ-SPOTs platform

The EZ-SPOTs platform consists of two components: 1) EZ-SPOTs plates that selectively repel and attract liquids in defined patterns (Fig. 1A), and 2) loading devices, referred to as loaders, which accurately deposit liquids by sliding across EZ-SPOTs plates (Fig. 1B). The platform can be used on its own or can be interfaced with well plates (e.g. standard 96-well plates).

Each scale bar represents 10 mm; D) schematics showing the important spacing characteristics of the loader indicating that the spacing element can be created directly when using a CNC mill; E) schematic indicating different methods for creating spacing elements when using a laser cutter to make a loader.

To ensure that the platform could be widely adopted by laboratories across disciplines, we established four key criteria for the coating: robust, readily obtained, easy to apply, and biocompatible. After testing numerous custom and commercially available coatings, we ultimately chose Ultra-Ever Dry (UED) bottom and top coatings (UltraTech International). The platform can be fabricated anywhere with adequate airflow and a laser cutter as it does not require the use of lithography masks, synthesis of custom molecules, specialized equipment, or high temperature heat treatment.

Fabrication of EZ-SPOTs plates and loaders

EZ-SPOTs plates can be fabricated with just a few materials and a laser cutter (Fig. 1A). The fabrication process involves cleaning the glass surface, masking the edges with tape for smooth loader sliding, applying UED bottom and top coatings, and laser ablating specific regions to create -philic circles for attracting liquid. The diameter of the ablated -philic circles can be varied across a plate to meter specific volumes for individual reactions.

EZ-SPOTs loaders can also be fabricated with just one or a few materials using CNC machining or laser cutting (Fig. 1B). The fabrication process involves cutting a loader from plastic stock, masking the edges with tape for smooth sliding on the EZ-SPOTs plates, applying UED bottom and top coatings, and optionally planarizing the loader with metal bars to alleviate any plastic deformation. Each loader has one or more reservoirs, consisting of a slot, multiple slots, or holes, to receive liquids and controllably deposit them onto the EZ-SPOTs plates. As an example, in Fig. 1B, the loader has six slots to load the same liquid in every two columns of the EZ-SPOTs plate (Fig. 1C).

Loaders are designed to slide over the EZ-SPOTs plates with a fixed “gap height” (between the bottom of the loader and the top of the plate) that ensures consistent volume deposition. The loader geometry and spacing elements are created together subtractively from a single piece of plastic when the loaders are fabricated by CNC machining (Fig. 1D) or are attached additively when the loaders are fabricated by laser cutting (Fig. 1E).

Performance of EZ-SPOTs

We benchmarked the performance of EZ-SPOTs by measuring deposited volumes and the range of compatible liquid surface tensions. Volume deposition was dependent on the size of the laser ablated -philic circular regions, the gap height between the coated loader and the coated plate, and the laser ablation parameters for the -philic regions. The latter two factors are kept constant to enable calibrating a consistent relationship between the size of the ablated region on the SPOTs plate and the metered volume. We first evaluated the consistency and precision of EZ-SPOTs, SPOTs, and pipettes for handling volumes spanning two to three orders of magnitude. Variation was characterized by a standard liquid handling metric, the coefficient of variation (CV), CV = σ/μ × 100%, where σ is the standard deviation and μ is the mean. We considered a single device as either a single SPOTs plate and loader, a single EZ-SPOTs plate and loader, or a single pipette and multiple devices as three SPOTs plates and loaders, three EZ-SPOTs plates and loaders, or three pipettes. For a single device (Fig. 2A and Tables S1–S3), both EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs had considerably lower CVs than a pipette and were able to maintain this consistency at much smaller volumes. Across multiple devices (Fig. 2B and Tables S4–S6), pipettes have extremely high CVs at small volumes while EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs maintained comparably much lower CVs. The low CV demonstrated by EZ-SPOTs is crucial for experiments that require precision. The EZ-SPOTs platform can meter volumes across a wide range, from 40 nL to 6 μL using -philic region-diameters of 0.5 mm to 4.5 mm (Fig. 2C and Table S4).

We assessed robustness by repeatedly interfacing EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs plates with 96-well plates (Fig. 3A). We designed the EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs plates to match the spacing of 96-well plates which allowed loaded liquids on our devices to be transferred to well plates. We performed multiple cycles that involved loading a dye on a SPOTs or EZ-SPOTs plate, and then interfacing with a 96-well plate containing 250 μL of water in each well. By mixing loaded dye into a consistent volume of water, we leveraged absorbance measurements to determine the volume of dye loaded, and we determined the consistency by measuring deposited volumes across multiple loading cycles.

The EZ-SPOTs antibiotic plate was rinsed, dried and reused for each isolate and the EZ-SPOTs cell plate was bleached for two minutes, rinsed, dried, and reused for each isolate.

During these cycles, the SPOTs or EZ-SPOTs plates are placed in direct contact with the wells on the 96-well plate, which can cause damage that manifests as changes to metered volumes in subsequent cycles. We measured the CV of eight -philic regions on the EZ-SPOTs or SPOTs plates after each cycle (Fig. 2D and Tables S7–S12). We found that with multiple uses, the SPOTs plates' CV rapidly increased to over 60%. This large increase in CV is due to the poor robustness of the SPOTs coating. In comparison, EZ-SPOTs had consistently low CVs, remaining under 5% for 100 cycles of use. This added robustness is primarily attributed to the UED bottom coating which keeps the UED top coating adhered despite abrasion. When reusing plates and sterilizing them after each reuse via bleaching or autoclaving, EZ-SPOTs had consistently low CVs for 15 cycles and at least 10 cycles, respectively (Tables S20–S23, Fig. S1 and S2). After numerous tests with this new platform, we did observe that the increased laser ablation power required to remove the UED coatings creates microcracks on the glass surface which can propagate as the number of uses increases (Fig. 2E). Fortunately, the cracks do not affect volume deposition.

Lastly, we assessed solvent compatibility by measuring the sliding angle of different percentages of ethanol solutions on the coated surface (Fig. 2F and Table S13). Compatible liquids with the platform roll off with a low sliding angle while unsuitable liquids pin or wet the coating when left stationary for less than 10 seconds. The SPOTs platform's superomniphobic coating is highly repellent to liquids with low surface tensions and repels up to 70% ethanol. The EZ-SPOTs platform (which uses Ultra-Ever Dry coating batch B24) can repel up to 50% ethanol which makes it compatible with liquids having surface tensions lower than water including DMSO, a PCR reaction mix, protein solutions, and many organic compounds. In comparison, devices made with NeverWet, a common superhydrophobic coating found in home improvement stores, only repel up to 30% ethanol, making them incompatible with low surface tension liquids used in many applications (Fig. 2F).

Using EZ-SPOTs for custom antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) panels

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) is widely used in clinical laboratories to identify effective antibiotics for treating bacterial infections.18,19 AST methods such as disk diffusion19 and broth microdilution19,20 rely on breakpoints standardized by organizations such as the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)21,22 to determine whether a bacterium is susceptible or resistant to a given antibiotic. The global rise of antimicrobial resistance,23 which continues to outpace the development of new, broadly effective antibiotics,24 contributes to over 100 000 deaths annually in the US due to multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs).25 With the extensive variation of resistance across MDROs, there is a need for more accessible, high-throughput platforms capable of accommodating drugs and concentration ranges not included in commercial panels.19,26

Recently, numerous microfluidic devices have been created27,28 which aim to address ease of use, scalability, time to result, and commercialization capacity of ASTs. Some specific advancements include channel-based devices for enhanced gas-exchange and rapid growth,29 self-loading devices for a simplified AST user interface,30 parallel inoculation for multiplexed ASTs,31 droplet arrays to easily create and merge AST dilution series,32 and digital microfluidic integration to automate the movement of droplets for ASTs.33 These innovations enable testing and assessing resistance trends in pathogens but require specialized expertise to use or make and lack easy means to directly interface with standard life science liquid handling formats.

Despite past microfluidic AST innovations, broth microdilution ASTs in clinical laboratories are typically performed in well plates with commercially available, preconfigured dried drug panels. The EZ-SPOTs platform offers greater flexibility in the selection of drugs and concentrations than preconfigured panels, without requiring specialized skills or tools. EZ-SPOTs are well positioned to evaluate MDROs by easily customizing drug selection and drug concentrations, a limitation of commercially available panels.34 One such MDRO is methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (MRSP), an emerging pathogen in humans and animals (primarily dogs).35–37 The ability of MRSP isolates to transmit between species35,38,39 makes it important to monitor resistance trends for a wide range of drugs and concentrations, including those not typically included on commercial AST panels. For example, for MRSP, linezolid is an emerging treatment option for highly resistant isolates, but is not currently available on commonly used commercial AST panels.40,41

We leveraged EZ-SPOTs to meter and transfer 12 clinical MRSP isolates and six antibiotics (at five concentrations, each in triplicate) into 12 separate 96-well plates (Fig. 3A–C). The reusability of EZ-SPOTs facilitated nearly 1200 experiments using just one plate–loader combination for drugs and one plate–loader combination for all isolates. To prevent cross-contamination, we soaked the plate and loader that were in contact with cells in bleach, which effectively killed all cells in the -philic regions and microcracks (Fig. 2E and SI).

Customizing our panel for a specific organism enabled testing of emerging drugs and an expanded concentration range, providing more resolution than standard panels regarding the antibiotic sensitivity of each isolate, which is important information to track for MDROs.42 Of the six antibiotics tested, five are included on the commercially available Thermo Fisher Sensititre™ panel and all drugs were tested here in a wider range of concentrations than the standard panel. The assay provides a facile way to obtain dose–response curves for numerous isolates at many drug concentrations (Fig. 4A and Table S14).

We distilled the dose–response results by identifying the MIC, defined here as the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that completely inhibits growth. This assay revealed that 10 out of the 12 isolates were MDROs as they were resistant to antibiotics from three or more classes43–48 (Fig. 4B and Tables S14 and S15). The isolates were found to be highly susceptible to rifampicin (RIF), susceptible to both vancomycin (VAN) and linezolid (LIN), and mostly resistant to clindamycin (CLI) and doxycycline (DOX), apart from one isolate each. All isolates tested were found to be resistant to oxacillin (OXA), as expected for methicillin-resistant clinical isolates. We found near perfect agreement of MICs for the 72 isolate/antibiotic combinations when comparing these data with data from the commercially available Sensititre panel obtained by Cornell's Animal Health Diagnostic Center (Fig. 4C and Table S15). Only four instances differ by a single two-fold dilution (shown in light green in Fig. 4C), which is within CLSI guidelines22 of an acceptable difference between assays. In numerous cases, the expanded concentration range provided additional information about antibiotic sensitivity than the standardized panel (asterisks in Fig. 4C).

To further demonstrate the applicability of the EZ-SPOTs platform to different clinically relevant species, we ran assays on four strains used for quality control benchmarking including Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212. Each strain was tested against eight antibiotics, at eight concentrations with three replicates. When comparing MICs between EZ-SPOTs, CLSI QC ranges,22 the isolates run on a Sensititre panel, and by pipetting, we found near perfect correlations with EZ-SPOTs and other methods (Tables S16–S19).

Materials and methods

Fabricating EZ-SPOTs plates

Borosilicate glass plates (127.76 mm × 85.48 mm) were first cleaned with acetone (VWR) and ethanol (VWR). Next, edges were masked (foam tape, 3.175 mm wide) based on the orientation of the loader to ensure a clean surface between the plate and the loader sliding on top. Then, the proprietary Ultra-Ever Dry (UED) bottom coat was applied with four light coats, and while the bottom coat was still wet, the tape mask was removed. After at least half an hour, the proprietary UED top coat (UltraTech International, batch B24) was applied with four light coats, and then the plates were left to dry at room temperature. We recommend testing each reagent on the coating prior to use with a similar test to the sliding angle measurements (as in Fig. 2F). The results in this paper are based on UED top coat batch B24, but correspondence with UltraTech International revealed that subsequent batches may have different repellency. Variations between batches may impact the repellency of liquids with lower surface tensions. If an application requires more repellency than the UED top coating batch provides, the SPOTs coating14 can be applied as the top coating instead with similar results to UED top coat batch B24.

Laser ablating the coating to create -philic regions

Specific regions of the coating were laser ablated using the rastering setting (Epilog Fusion Edge 40 W, 1 cycle at 15% speed, 30% power followed by 1 cycle at 15% speed, 10% power) based on the experimental design. The laser ablation settings should be kept consistent across a single experiment to ensure consistent volume deposition. Experiments were designed using spreadsheet software to plan desired volumes on each plate. These volumes were translated to ablation diameters using the volume characterization data (described in the “volume characterization” section). We then define the pitch between each ablated region in a MATLAB script which generates the plate print file in SVG format. Finally, we resize and fill objects in Inkscape, and then send the file to the laser to selectively ablate the plates.

Fabricating EZ-SPOTs loaders

When making a Delrin (acetal homopolymer polyoxymethylene, McMaster-Carr) loader, the loader was milled with the desired slot, slots, or holes from the bottom side to create the spacer feature (0.4 mm thick) and ensure a consistent gap height. After milling, all milled areas were deburred with a sharp blade.

When making a laser cut plastic (acrylic, 6.35 mm thick, McMaster-Carr) loader, the loader was laser cut with the desired slot, slots, or holes. Spacer elements (0.254 mm acrylic, 0.5 mm Teflon tape, 1 No. 4 or 2 No. 2 glass coverslips) were cut and adhered (dichloromethane for acrylic or super glue for glass) to the laser cut plastic.

The spacer feature or spacer element was masked (foam tape, 3.175 mm wide) to ensure a clean surface between the loader and the plate prior to applying the coating. Then, for both the milled and laser cut loaders, the UED bottom coat was sprayed with four light coats onto all outer and inner surfaces of the loader, and while the bottom coat was still wet, the tape mask was removed. After at least half an hour, six light coats of the UED top coat were sprayed onto all outer and inner surfaces of the loader, and the loader was left to dry for at least a few hours or overnight. Finally, the loader was planarized (optional) with metal bars (precision-milled stainless steel, McMaster-Carr), screws (Thorlabs), and nuts (Thorlabs) to eliminate any potential warping and create a consistent gap height to accurately deposit liquids of the desired volumes. The loader was then slid over the EZ-SPOTs plate to ensure that the sliding was smooth.

Interfacing EZ-SPOTs plates with well plates

Interfacing allows liquid to be moved from EZ-SPOTs plates to standard well plates (e.g. 96-well) and from standard well plates to EZ-SPOTs plates. We designed EZ-SPOTs plates to match the pitch of the standard 96-well plate layout. Then we loaded both plates with liquid. To mix liquid from EZ-SPOTs into well plates, the loaded EZ-SPOTs plates were aligned with the filled 96-well plates. Large binder clips were used to clamp plates together on two sides, and then the combined plates were flipped over and shaken until well-mixed (Fig. 3A). The combined plates were flipped back over and given several shakes to ensure that the liquid returned to the well in the 96-well plate, and the binder clips and EZ-SPOTs plate were removed. To move liquid from an EZ-SPOTs plate to an unloaded 96-well plate, the EZ-SPOTs plate can be aligned to the 96-well plate, taped together, and put in a plate centrifuge at 700–2300g for 30 seconds. To move liquid from 96-well plates to EZ-SPOTs, the plates can be brought into contact and shaken or centrifuged such that the liquid comes into contact with the EZ-SPOTs plate. The plates can then be separated, and the EZ-SPOTs plate will have “sipped” a small amount of liquid from each corresponding well.14 Through this interfacing method, cross-contamination between wells during transfer was below the detection limit of the assay (Table S24).

Considerations for maximizing experimental throughput

Experimental throughput is a function of the number of droplets per device and the number of devices. In experiments that interface EZ-SPOTs plates with standard formats (e.g. 96- or 384-well plates), the number of experiments per device is set by the standardized layout, as in the AST experiments demonstrated here. When using these devices as a stand-alone platform, various factors contribute to the number of experiments that can be simultaneously performed on one device including: the resolution of the patterning laser; the required dynamic range of droplet volumes; whether or not plates must be sandwiched for mixing; limits imposed by capillarity for dewetting discrete droplets; consideration of the evaporation speed for small volumes. Using the methods presented here, we maintain droplet diameters and spacing between droplets of at least 0.5 mm. Thus using this approach, the upper limit for the number of droplets per 127.76 × 85.48 mm plate is around 10 000, with the above-noted factors decreasing this in many experiments to several hundred to a couple thousand experiments per device. In addition to maximizing packing density, throughput can be increased by increasing device size and using multiple devices.

Volume characterization

To characterize the volume, 96-well plates were first loaded with 250 μL of deionized (DI) water. A dilution series was created from a 24 g L−1 fluorescein solution, and absorbance was obtained for four replicate series using a spectrophotometer (SpectraMax Plus 384 Microplate Reader, Molecular Devices) at 470 nm, 520 nm, and 530 nm. This was used to create “concentration calibration curves” relating absorbance to known fluorescein concentrations.

Next, the 24 g L−1 fluorescein solution was added to the EZ-SPOTs loader and loaded into the -philic regions of an EZ-SPOTs plate with 12 diameters ranging from 0.5 mm to 4.5 mm and eight replicates of each diameter. This was performed on three different EZ-SPOTs plates, repeated four times for each plate, and absorbance was obtained at 470 nm, 520 nm, and 530 nm using a spectrophotometer. These absorbance values were translated to concentrations using the concentration calibration curves described above. Each measured concentration corresponds to a specific volume metered by each of the differently-sized -philic regions. Together, these data establish a “reference curve” that relates volume to diameter. The reference curve is used to inform the laser ablation diameter needed to meter a specific volume of liquid. This process was repeated for each different style of the loader used.

In addition to the diameter of the -philic region, volume deposition was also dependent on the gap height between the coated loader and the coated plate as well as the laser ablation parameters used to create the -philic regions on the EZ-SPOTs plate.

Volume variations of EZ-SPOTs, SPOTs, and pipettes

To determine the variation of volume within or across devices, we measured the loaded volumes of pipettes, SPOTs platforms, and EZ-SPOTs platforms. We measured variability within a single device by utilizing a single SPOTs plate and loader, a single EZ-SPOTs plate and loader, or a single pipette. We measured variability across multiple devices using three different SPOTs plates and loaders, three different EZ-SPOTs plates and loaders, or three different pipettes. Each SPOTs and EZ-SPOTs plate contained 8 replicates of each volume. For each pipette run, each volume was pipetted 12 times for each device. Precision was captured using the coefficient of variation (CV), CV =σ/μ × 100% where σ is the standard deviation and μ is the mean. The data displayed for SPOTs and pipettes were shown previously,14 and here, we added the EZ-SPOTs volume variation. We performed this test by loading different volumes of a 24 g L−1 fluorescein solution into 96-well plates for each platform and then used the calibration curves described above to determine the loaded volume.

Reusing EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs plates

To mimic repeated use of both the EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs plates, we followed the volume characterization procedure and repeated the procedure 10 times for SPOTs and 100 times for EZ-SPOTs. Each cycle, the plates were rinsed with DI water and the SPOTs plate was dried with compressed air while the EZ-SPOTs plate was dried by blotting it on a paper towel. The SPOTs coating would be permanently damaged if it was dried by blotting on a paper towel. Eight replicates with a 3 mm diameter were used to compute the CV (Fig. 2D). CVs for other diameters can be found in the SI.

Sliding angle measurements

The sliding angle was measured by dropping 10 μL of ethanol–water mixtures 5 cm above a coated glass slide. For this measurement, we varied the ethanol percentages and the angle of the coated glass. We started with the lowest ethanol percentage and dropped 10 μL on the, initially level, surface. If the liquid rolled off, we kept the same angle and tested an increased ethanol percentage using the same procedure. If the liquid did not roll off, the angle was increased in one-degree increments and the same liquid was dropped again. The procedure was repeated until the liquid pinned to the surface.

The liquid must also be able to sit on the surface without wetting immediately for proper loading of EZ-SPOTs and SPOTs. Some of the high (>80%) ethanol percentages can bounce off when dropped on surfaces at a high angle but wet the surface with sustained contact. To account for this, we also tested to ensure that each concentration could sit on the surface for more than 10 seconds without wetting the coating.

Bacterial calibration curve

To relate optical density (OD) 600 nm (OD600) readings to bacterial cell concentration, three MRSP isolates (Cornell Animal Health Diagnostic Center, Bacteriology Laboratory) were grown with and without shaking at 37 °C in cation-adjusted Mueller Hinton broth (CAMHB) for 24 hours (Fig. S3). A spectrophotometer was used to measure the OD600 for each suspension. Then, serial dilutions were made for each isolate ranging from 10−3 to 10−7 and 100 μL of each of these was streaked on 5% sheep's blood in a tryptic soy agar plate (VWR) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. Finally, plates containing between 30 and 300 colonies were identified and counted to create a relationship between CFU mL−1 and OD600 (Fig. S3).

Cell isolates and antibiotics

Single colonies from 12 clinical MRSP isolates (Cornell Animal Health Diagnostic Center, Bacteriology Laboratory) were isolated, grown overnight in CAMHB (BD BBL) broth, and stored in freezer stocks. The day before the experiment, we inoculated the 12 clinical MRSP isolates from freezer stocks into CAMHB broth, grew them overnight, and then diluted them down the day of the experiment to 2.22 × 106 CFU mL−1 using an OD600 reading and the established bacterial calibration curve.

Concentrated antibiotic solutions were made in either water or DMSO and diluted in either CAMHB or CAMHB + 2% NaCl. Rifampicin (RIF) (Research Products International) was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 20 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB down to 44.4 μg mL−1. Vancomycin (VAN) (VWR) was dissolved in water at a concentration of 45 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB down to 88.9 μg mL−1. Linezolid (LIN) (AmBeed) was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 60 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB down to 88.9 μg mL−1. Clindamycin (CLI) (AmBeed) was dissolved in water at a concentration of 80 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB down to 177.8 μg mL−1. Doxycycline (DOX) (Sigma Aldrich) was dissolved in water at a concentration of 45 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB down to 177.8 μg mL−1. Oxacillin (OXA) (Research Products International) was dissolved in water at a concentration of 45 000 μg mL−1 and diluted in CAMHB + 2% NaCl down to 88.9 μg mL−1. Desired concentration ranges were determined by an initial experiment with a separate set of three MRSP strains.

EZ-SPOTs plates loading and use

We loaded antibiotics onto the EZ-SPOTs antibiotic plate with a six-slot loader, resulting in five volumes with three replicates for each antibiotic. We also included six regions that were not laser ablated as either a negative or positive control, depending on the corresponding location on the EZ-SPOTs cell plate. Mixing with media resulted in five two-fold antibiotic concentrations. For the EZ-SPOTs cell plate (Fig. 3B), we loaded one MRSP isolate with a single slot loader and used six regions in the bottom row for negative or positive controls.

AST experiment

12 96-well plates were first loaded with 100 μL of CAMHB in columns 1–10 and loaded with 100 μL of CAMHB + 2% NaCl in columns 11 and 12 to be consistent with CLSI guidelines. Next, antibiotics were loaded on the EZ-SPOTs antibiotic plate and were interfaced with a filled 96-well plate. After dilution into the 96-well plate, the concentration range for RIF was 0.125 to 2 μg mL−1, VAN, LIN, and OXA were 0.25 to 4 μg mL−1, and CLI and DOX were 0.5 to 8 μg mL−1. The EZ-SPOTs antibiotic plate was then rinsed with DI water, dried on a paper towel and was reused for each additional isolate. Similarly, the six-slot loader was rinsed with DI water, dried using compressed air and then reused for each additional isolate.

The 96-well plate containing media and antibiotics was interfaced with the EZ-SPOTs cell plate loaded with one of the MRSP isolates. This resulted in a 1 × 105 CFU mL−1 final cell concentration. The EZ-SPOTs cell plate was then soaked in undiluted bleach (Clorox) for two minutes, rinsed with DI water, dried on a paper towel, and reused for each isolate. Similarly, the single-slot loader was soaked in undiluted bleach (Clorox) for two minutes, rinsed with DI water, dried using compressed air, and reused for each isolate.

Notably, we only used a single EZ-SPOTs cell plate and loader and a single EZ-SPOTs antibiotic plate and loader to perform this entire experiment. This approach demonstrates how EZ-SPOTs can be sterilized and reused numerous times, to provide reproducible results across different isolates.

The resulting 96-well plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours to be consistent with CLSI guidelines. Each plate was then shaken and an OD600 reading was obtained using a spectrophotometer to determine cell growth within each well.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination and clinical breakpoints

After obtaining OD600 growth readings for each 96-well plate, we averaged replicates, plotted dose–response curves, and determined the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), which we defined as the lowest concentration of the antibiotic that completely inhibits bacterial growth in each well. Clinical breakpoints were determined based on CLSI guidelines22 of susceptibility tests for bacteria isolated from animals.

Commercially available panel and comparison

Aerobic bacterial cultures on canine specimens were performed using the standard procedures of AHDC's Bacteriology Laboratory. Samples were plated on Trypticase soy with 5% sheep blood agar (BAP), chocolate agar (CHOC), Levine eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar, and Columbia cephalothin nalidixic acid (CNA) agar by streaking for isolation. Culture plates were incubated overnight at 33–37 °C in 6% ± 1 CO2 and evaluated at 18–24 h and again at two days following plating. Isolated colonies resembling Staphylococcus species were identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry using a MALDI Biotyper® System (Bruker Scientific).

Susceptibility testing of Staphylococcus pseudintermedius isolates was performed using a Sensititre™ Automated AST System (Thermo Fisher) and their companion animal Gram positive panels (COMPGP1F), using procedures based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Isolates were prepared in Mueller Hinton broth according to manufacturer guidelines and inoculated into the 96-well susceptibility panel plates using a Sensititre AIM™ Automated Inoculation Delivery System. The panels were scanned into the database and loaded into an ARIS™ 2× automated incubator where they were incubated at 33–37 °C in ambient air for 24 hours. The panels were automatically read, and the results were transferred to the culture bench for reporting.

Isolates that were identified as methicillin-resistant based on their AST results were archived in 15% glycerol and stored at −80 °C. Twelve canine S. pseudintermedius strains were chosen from these archives for this study, with all isolates having unique animal identifications and originating from a variety of anatomical sites.

The susceptibility information obtained from COMPGP1F for each of these isolates was kept confidential until the experiment using the EZ-SPOTs plates was completed. We chose the same definition for MICs as the Thermo Fisher Sensititre software, allowing us to directly compare MICs.

Conclusion

We demonstrate a robust, easy-to-make, and low barrier-to-entry solution for performing hundreds to thousands of simultaneous experiments. Unlike many other microfluidic approaches, this platform does not require highly specialized equipment or expertise and can be easily created with just a laser cutter and a few readily available materials. We anticipate that this platform will find broader applications beyond ASTs, particularly in contexts that have traditionally lacked high-throughput options.

One significant advantage of this platform is its ability to reduce errors and enhance reproducibility, relative to manual approaches. By loading liquid simultaneously, the chance of accidentally pipetting into the wrong wells is minimized. Any human errors manifest uniformly across the entire plate, making them straightforward to identify and address, which significantly reduces time spent on troubleshooting non-reproducible results. Additionally, the use of print files as digital maps and cut plates as tangible maps further ensures that experiments are reproducible and retraceable.

The ASTs performed in this study would require thousands of pipette tips when conducted with traditional approaches, but by using EZ-SPOTs, we completed the entire experiment with fewer than one box of pipette tips. This reusability enhances cost-effectiveness and reduces biohazardous waste. We note that material costs for these devices (Table S25) are less than $5, also permitting single use, when required. Unlike commercially available AST platforms, these devices allow customized assay panels (S26). We chose to interface the platform directly with a 96-well plate in this study to directly compare results with a commercially available system. However, the platform can easily scale to 384 or 1536 wells to further reduce cost per experiment. Experiments can also be run entirely on the EZ-SPOTs plates, allowing for arbitrary layouts and avoiding the need for additional well plates.

We envision that this versatile platform will be used for many diagnostic and screening applications including drug discovery, sequencing library preparation, protein-based assays, cell-based assays, and other liquid-based reactions.
