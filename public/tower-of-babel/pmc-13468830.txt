Network toxicology and molecular docking identify BRCA1 as a functional target of the dietary carcinogen PhIP in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yaxing Deng, Chenglin Wang, Yingchun Hu, Hongying Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo clarify the molecular mechanism of PhIP-induced colorectal cancer and identify the potential of core regulatory targets as biomarkers for colorectal cancer.MethodsThe chemical structure of PhIP was obtained through the PubChem database and ProTox-3.0 platform, and its physicochemical properties and toxic effects were clarified. PhIP action targets were screened from the ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction databases, and pathogenic targets for colorectal cancer were acquired from the GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases. Intersection targets of PhIP and colorectal cancer were further screened. A compound-target regulatory network was constructed and subjected to functional enrichment analysis and PPI analysis. Core targets were further identified via CytoHubba, their expression differences in colorectal cancer were clarified, and their diagnostic efficacy and prognostic value were evaluated using ROC curves and Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Subsequently, the expression of core targets was validated through immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, and immune infiltration analysis was performed to clarify their correlation with immune cells. Finally, the interaction mechanism between PhIP and core targets was revealed at the molecular structural level through molecular docking technology.ResultsA total of 1088 PhIP action targets and 281 colorectal cancer-associated targets were screened, containing 35 intersection targets. Functional enrichment analysis found that these intersection targets were associated with biological processes or signaling pathways such as protein phosphorylation, regulation of cell cycle process, pathways in cancer, and estrogen signaling pathway. Target proteins like BRCA1, ESR1, and BCL2 were located at the center of the PPI network, and BRCA1 was identified as the core regulatory target based on Degree value. Expression difference analysis and immunohistochemistry results showed that BRCA1 was significantly highly expressed in colorectal cancer and had good diagnostic efficacy and prognostic value. Immune infiltration results indicated that BRCA1 was closely related to immune cells such as Th2 cells, T helper cells, Tcm, and iDC. Simultaneously, the gene mutation landscape suggested that mutations in genes like APC and TP53 also participated in colorectal cancer development. Finally, molecular docking results indicated strong binding activity between PhIP and BRCA1.ConclusionPhIP acts on the core target BRCA1 and participates in colorectal cancer development through mechanisms affecting immune cells, gene mutations, etc. BRCA1 has good diagnostic efficacy and prognostic value in colorectal cancer and is expected to become a new diagnostic and prognostic target for colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05334-0.Keywords: Heterocyclic amines, PhIP, Colorectal cancer, BRCA1, Network toxicology

Introduction

Heterocyclic Amines (HCAs) are a class of compounds possessing genotoxicity or carcinogenicity, primarily derived from protein-rich foods such as fish, meat, or poultry—particularly red meats like beef. During high-temperature cooking (e.g., grilling, frying) of these foods and related products, amino acids, sugars, creatine, or creatinine react with reducing sugars through complex chemical processes, forming potentially harmful substances like HCAs [1]. The formation mechanism of HCAs is intricate, influenced by factors including meat type, cooking duration, temperature, ratio of water-soluble substances to fat, cooking oil variety, and cooking methods (frying, oven baking, or grilling). Currently, the Strecker degradation of the Maillard reaction during cooking is widely recognized as the decisive factor in HCA formation [2]. The Maillard reaction, a non-enzymatic browning reaction, involves a series of chemical interactions between free amino acids and reducing sugars, ultimately generating melanoid compounds and volatile flavoring substances. These flavoring substances act as oxygen-active precursors, further forming carcinogenic aromatic organic compounds such as oxazoles, imidazoles, pyridines, thiazoles, pyrazines, and pyrroles [3]. Since their discovery, over 30 types of HCAs have been identified through extensive research. Based on structural features, HCAs are classified into two major categories [3, 4]: ① Aminoimidazoazaarenes (AIAs), also known as 2-amino-3-methylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoline (IQ) -type compounds or thermic HCAs, they primarily form during household cooking (100–300 °C). Common AIAs include: PhIP (2-Amino-1-methyl-6-phenylimidazo[4,5-b]pyridine), IQx (2-Amino-3-methyl-3 H-imidazo[4,5-f]quinoxaline), MeIQx (2-Amino-3,8-dimethylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoxaline), 4,8-DiMeIQx (2-Amino-3,4,8-trimethyl-3 H-imidazo[4,5-f]quinoxaline). ② Aminocarbolines (ACs), also termed non-IQ-type compounds or pyrolytic HCAs, they form via the combination of pyridines and amino groups at temperatures above 300 °C. Common ACs include: AαC (2-Amino-9 H-pyrido[2,3-b]indole), MeAαC (2-Amino-3-methyl-9 H-pyrido[2,3-b]indole), Harman (1-Methyl-9 H-pyrido[4,3-b]indole), Norharman (9 H-Pyrido[4,3-b]indole).

PhIP is the most abundant HCA formed during meat processing and cooking. Creatinine and phenylalanine serve as the primary precursors for PhIP formation, with formaldehyde acting as a key catalyst in this process [5]. Phenylalanine undergoes Strecker degradation to produce phenylacetaldehyde, which then interacts with creatinine via aldol reaction to generate intermediates such as the pyridine ring, ultimately forming PhIP [6]. Recent studies also indicate that under specific conditions, amino acids and lipid oxidation products can generate PhIP without carbohydrates, suggesting multiple pathways and mechanisms may be involved. High-temperature cooking methods like frying, grilling, or charbroiling significantly increase PhIP production. Epidemiological research reveals that long-term exposure to high levels of PhIP may elevate the risk of various cancers [7]. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) assessment report, PhIP is classified as “possibly carcinogenic to humans” (Group 2B) based on its carcinogenic potential [8, 9]. Similarly, the U.S. National Toxicology Program (NTP) lists PhIP as a “reasonably anticipated human carcinogen”.

With socioeconomic development, global meat consumption continues to rise annually. However, carcinogens like HCAs generated during meat cooking substantially increase the risk of malignancies such as colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and prostate cancer [7, 10]. Although numerous case-control and cohort studies have established strong associations between high intake of cooked meat products, elevated HCA exposure, and cancer incidence, the precise mechanisms remain inconclusive. This study employs big-data analysis, network toxicology, and molecular docking techniques to explore the potential targets and mechanisms by which the meat-derived carcinogen PhIP contributes to colorectal cancer at the genetic level. The findings aim to provide new directions for dietary guidelines and strategies for colorectal cancer diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Acquisition of PhIP molecular structure

The standard SMILES expression and 3D molecular structure of the compound “PhIP” were retrieved through the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [11], for subsequent toxicity analysis and target prediction.

Toxicity characteristic analysis

The ProTox-3.0 platform (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) was employed to predict parameters such as acute toxicity, carcinogenicity, and mutagenicity based on the SMILES structure of PhIP. This platform integrates multiple algorithmic models, providing key data support for compound safety assessment [12].

Screening of potential action targets

PhIP interaction targets were systematically mined through the following multi-source databases: ① ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/): Extract biologically active targets validated in the literature [13]. ② STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de): Analyze chemical-protein interaction networks [14]. ③ SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch): Predict potential protein targets [15]. Results from the three databases were merged and redundant targets were removed to construct the PhIP target dataset.

Integration of colorectal cancer-related targets

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) associated targets were retrieved from the following databases: ① GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/): Integrate multi-source gene-disease association data [16]. ② OMIM (https://omim.org/): Collect targets related to genetic diseases [17]. ③ TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/): Screen therapeutic target databases [18]. The union of targets from the three databases was taken and deduplicated to establish the CRC target library.

Identification of PhIP-CRC intersection targets

Venn analysis was performed on the PhIP target set and the CRC target set using the ggvenn package in R 4.4.2, and the intersection targets were screened as potential regulatory targets for PhIP-induced CRC.

Construction of compound-target regulatory network

Cytoscape 3.8.0 was applied to construct a ternary regulatory network of “PhIP-Intersection Targets-CRC”, visualizing molecular interaction relationships to reveal pathogenic mechanisms.

Enrichment analysis

Executed through the Metascape platform (https://metascape.org/): ① GO analysis: Including Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC). ② KEGG pathway analysis. Parameter settings: Min Overlap = 3, P < 0.05, Min Enrichment = 1.5 [19].

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

Intersection targets were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a PPI network (confidence score > 0.600), with isolated nodes hidden to display core interaction modules [20].

Screening of core targets

The CytoHubba plugin was used to calculate the Degree value of nodes in the PPI network, and core genes were screened by sorting in descending order.

Validation of core gene expression differences

Based on RNA-seq data (TPM format) from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) COAD/READ project, data was divided into cancer vs. non-cancer, and cancer vs. adjacent tissues [21]. Data preprocessing: log2(TPM + 1) transformation. Statistical methods: Unpaired samples used Welch t-test, paired samples used paired t-test. Visualization: ggplot2 package.

Clinical correlation analysis

Based on RNA-seq data (TPM format) and clinical data from the TCGA database’s COAD/READ project. Statistical method: Overall test (Welch one-way ANOVA test) + multiple hypothesis testing (Games-Howell post hoc test). Visualization was performed using R 4.2.1. The correlation of core genes with clinical indicators such as gender, age, and pathological stage was analyzed.

Diagnostic efficacy evaluation

The Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC curve) and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) are commonly used to evaluate the predictive accuracy of diagnostic tests. The closer the AUC is to 1, the better the diagnostic effect of the variable in predicting outcomes. The pROC package was applied to draw ROC curves, and AUC values were calculated to evaluate the ability of core genes to discriminate CRC.

Survival prognosis analysis

Survival curves are mainly used to describe the occurrence of events (survival or death/recurrence, etc.) in subjects or study objects over a period of time. Cox regression analysis was performed using the survival package, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were drawn using the survminer package (Log-rank test).

Protein expression validation

To further validate BRCA1 protein expression, immunohistochemistry results from normal colorectal tissues, rectal cancer samples, and colon cancer samples were obtained from the HPA (The Human Protein Atlas) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [22]. In normal colorectal tissues, BRCA1 expression was mainly analyzed in endothelial cells and glandular cells, and compared with colorectal cancer cells. Furthermore, based on the HPA database, immunofluorescence expression of BRCA1 in HAP1, Rh30, and U2OS cell lines was analyzed to clarify the expression level and localization of BRCA1 protein in cells, providing references for exploring its function and mechanism. The antibody selected for each cell line was HPA004840.

Immune infiltration feature analysis

Based on immune cell markers provided in the literature, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm in the R package-GSVA was used to calculate Spearman correlations between BRCA1 and different immune cells in colorectal cancer [23]. Correlation analysis was conducted on core targets and immune infiltration matrix data, and visualized using lollipop plots with the ggplot2 package in R 4.2.1, thereby elucidating the impact of core gene expression patterns on the infiltration degree of immune cells in CRC. To further explore the correlation between different immune cells and BRCA1 in CRC, tumor samples were divided into two groups based on BRCA1 expression (BRCA1 high/low expression group). Using the core algorithm CIBERSORT (analyzed by CIBERSORT.R script), immune infiltration in different groups was calculated through the 22 immune cell markers provided by the CIBERSORTx website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), obtaining the proportion of different immune cell types in each group.

Gene mutation landscape analysis

The TCGA database contains exome sequencing data from colorectal cancer tissue samples. Samples were grouped based on BRCA1 expression (BRCA1 high/low expression group). Gene mutation-related analysis was performed using waterfall plots to explore targets with the highest correlation between gene mutation rates and BRCA1 expression in colon and rectal cancers [24].

Molecular docking validation

The CB-Dock2 online platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/) was used to dock PhIP with core target proteins (from PDB). Binding activity determination: Vina score < -4.25 kcal/mol (weak binding); < -5.0 kcal/mol (medium binding); < -7.0 kcal/mol (strong binding) [25].

Discussion

Epidemiological studies have identified increased meat consumption as a significant environmental factor impacting human cancer development [4, 7, 26]. During meat processing and cooking, HCAs such as PhIP are generated. These compounds may contribute to malignancies like colorectal cancer through mechanisms including DNA adduct formation, oxidative stress, and inflammation [5, 27]. Cytochrome P4501A2 (CYP1A2) and N-acetyltransferase (NAT) isoenzymes serve as the primary bioactivators in the metabolic processing of HCAs [3]. The liver serves as the primary site for PhIP metabolism. During hepatic Phase I metabolism, CYP1A2 catalyzes N-hydroxylation of its exocyclic amino group. These hydroxylated intermediate metabolites possess high reactivity and migrate to sites like the intestine for Phase II metabolism, including processes such as O-acetylation or N-acetylation by NAT1/NAT2 or sulfation by sulfotransferases (SULTs), which enhance their DNA-binding reactivity to generate carcinogenic effects [28]. Simultaneously, a subset of CYP1A2 exhibits procarcinogenic activity by promoting N-acetyltransferase activity, thereby influencing colorectal cancer development. Additionally, upon entering the intestine, HCA metabolites interact with gut microbiota and may impact colorectal cancer progression by compromising the intestinal mucosal barrier and through other mechanisms [29].

Although extensive prior studies have substantiated the significant role of HCAs in cancer development, the genotoxicity and targets of diverse HCAs across different human cell types require further exploration due to their structural heterogeneity, complex influencing factors, and carcinogenic mechanisms. Therefore, this study employed network toxicology and molecular dynamics techniques to investigate PhIP’s targets and biological significance in colorectal cancer at the genetic level. The study first acquired the chemical structure of PhIP through the PubChem database and clarified its physicochemical properties and toxic effects. Subsequently, PhIP’s action targets were retrieved from three databases—ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction—and intersected with pathogenic targets of colorectal cancer from GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases. This yielded 35 intersection targets, which are potential key targets for PhIP-induced colorectal cancer. Through subsequent functional enrichment analysis, these targets were found to be primarily involved in: Biological Processes (BP): protein phosphorylation, regulation of cell cycle process, cellular response to lipid. Molecular Functions (MF): histone modifying activity, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, transcription factor binding. Cellular Components (CC): PML body, receptor complex, cell body. KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed participation in signaling pathways including: Pathways in cancer, Prostate cancer, HIF-1 signaling pathway, Estrogen signaling pathway. These results further illustrate their relevance to cellular metabolic regulation and tumorigenesis. In the PPI network, proteins including AKT1, BRCA1, ESR1, and BCL2 occupied hub positions, indicating their potential role as core targets within the regulatory network. Further topological analysis based on degree centrality specifically identified BRCA1 as the central regulatory target in PhIP-induced colorectal cancer. This study, utilizing network toxicology and molecular docking, predicted BRCA1 as a potential core target of the dietary carcinogen PhIP in colorectal cancer. Our analysis revealed that BRCA1 is significantly upregulated in CRC, correlates with specific clinical features and immune infiltration patterns, and possesses strong diagnostic and prognostic value.

The breast susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1) is recognized as a classical tumor suppressor gene [30]. The BRCA1 protein interacts with numerous key proteins involved in embryonic development and gene regulation, forming stable complexes critical for genomic stability and cellular homeostasis. The N-terminal zinc-binding RING finger domain (encoded by exons 2–5 of BRCA1) binds to BRCA1-associated RING domain protein 1 (BARD1) to form a stable heterodimer. The BRCA1-BARD1 heterodimer exhibits E3 ubiquitin ligase activity and plays essential roles in: DNA end resection and Late stages of homologous recombination repair [31]. Furthermore, BRCA1-BARD1-mediated PCNA ubiquitination mitigates the formation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) gaps during DNA replication, thereby promoting replication fork continuity and maintaining genomic stability [32]. Approximately 50% of pathogenic BRCA1 missense mutations occur within this domain, underscoring its critical role in DNA repair and tumor suppression [33]. The BRCT domains (encoded by exons 15–23) are highly conserved and recognize/interact with phosphorylated proteins including: CtIP (C-terminal binding protein interacting protein), BRIP1 (BRCA1 interacting protein C-terminal helicase 1), Abraxas (BRCA1 A Complex Subunit). These interactions are essential for BRCA1’s regulation of: Checkpoint activation, DNA damage signaling, Double-strand break (DSB) repair [34, 35]. The core region encoded by exons 11–13 similarly plays pivotal regulatory roles in DNA repair and cell cycle control [36]. The upregulation of BRCA1 mRNA observed in CRC tissues may be a compensatory response to PhIP-induced genotoxic stress and potential functional inhibition of BRCA1 proteins.

Recent studies demonstrate a close association between BRCA1 and colorectal cancer, with loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at the BRCA1 locus detected in nearly 50% of sporadic colorectal cancer patients [37, 38]. A prior retrospective study further revealed that BRCA1 mutation carriers from families with ovarian or breast cancer exhibit an elevated relative risk of developing colorectal cancer [39]. Multiple meta-analyses corroborate that BRCA1 mutations constitute a significant risk factor for colorectal carcinogenesis, positioning BRCA1 as a potential biomarker for early diagnosis and prognostic assessment of colorectal cancer [37, 40]. Therefore, based on RNA-seq data from the TCGA database, this study conducted differential expression analysis. The results revealed significantly upregulated BRCA1 expression in colorectal cancer samples compared to normal controls and adjacent non-tumor tissues, suggesting BRCA1 may function as an oncogene driving colorectal cancer. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, BMI, CEA level, pathological stage, and therapy outcome consistently demonstrated elevated BRCA1 expression across all colorectal cancer cohorts. The ROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.911, indicating strong diagnostic value of BRCA1 for colorectal cancer. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further showed that patients with high BRCA1 expression had significantly longer overall survival. The study by Sofia et al. revealed that high BRCA1 mRNA expression strongly correlates with colorectal cancer patients aged < 45 years, potentially constituting a key driver of younger-onset colorectal cancer [41]. Paradoxically, they observed poorer prognosis in colorectal cancer patients with low BRCA1 mRNA expression—contrasting with breast cancer patterns—suggesting tissue-specific impacts of BRCA1 dysregulation on clinical outcomes, though underlying mechanisms require further investigation. In colorectal cancer specifically, low or null BRCA1 mRNA expression associates with: early regional lymph node metastasis and advanced TNM stage. Consequently, this patient subgroup exhibits significantly increased recurrence risk and substantially reduced overall survival [42]. Experimental validation confirmed: ① Immunohistochemistry (IHC): Upregulated BRCA1 protein expression in CRC tissues versus normal colorectal mucosa. ② Immunofluorescence (IF): Predominant subcellular localization of BRCA1 to the nucleoplasm and nuclear bodies. The observed upregulation of BRCA1 mRNA in CRC tissues is likely a compensatory response to PhIP-induced genotoxic stress and potential functional inhibition of the BRCA1 protein.

HCAs such as PhIP also regulate tumor development through immune cells. Studies reveal that NAT2-metabolized HCAs modulate immune populations including T lymphocytes (CD3⁺), B cells (CD19⁺), and NK cells (CD56⁺). Concurrently, T lymphocytes interact with PhIP to generate substantial DNA adducts—processes dependent on CYP1A2 and NATs activation [3, 43, 44]. In essence, disruption of immune cell function during PhIP metabolism constitutes a plausible mechanism for its carcinogenic regulation. Therefore, this study investigated immune infiltration patterns of PhIP and its core target BRCA1 within the colorectal cancer tumor microenvironment (TME) [45]. Key findings include: significant positive correlations between BRCA1 and Th2 cells, T helper cells, Tcm, negative correlation with iDC. Indicating BRCA1 may exert tumor-regulatory functions by modulating these immune subsets. Additionally, PhIP induces GC→TA transversion mutations in human lymphocytes—a mutational signature consistent with TP53-mutant human tumors [46]. The BRCA1-associated gene mutation landscape further revealed high mutation rates of APC and TP53 in colorectal cancer. Rat models demonstrated that 50% of PhIP-induced colorectal tumors harbored APC mutations [47]. These findings indicate that beyond BRCA1, mutations in genes such as APC and TP53 likely contribute to PhIP-driven carcinogenesis. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed a distinctive immunomodulatory pattern of BRCA1 in colorectal cancer: positive correlations with Th2 cells and central memory T cells (Tcm), and a negative correlation with immature dendritic cells (iDC). Collectively, this pattern is likely to shape an immunosuppressive TME that facilitates CRC progression [48]. Th2 cells are known to secrete IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which promote M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, inhibit cytotoxic CD8 + T cell responses, and foster a pro-tumorigenic cytokine milieu. Although Tcm cells generally represent a memory population with potent antitumor potential, their accumulation in the TME—particularly in the context of BRCA1 upregulation—has been associated with an exhausted or dysfunctional state in several malignancies, characterized by reduced effector function and impaired tumor clearance. Moreover, the negative correlation between BRCA1 and iDC suggests compromised antigen presentation, as iDCs are critical for capturing tumor antigens and migrating to lymph nodes to prime naive T cells [49]. Reduced iDC infiltration may therefore limit the initiation of effective adaptive immune responses against CRC. In concert, the BRCA1-driven immune signature (Th2+, Tcm+, iDC-) contributes to an immune landscape dominated by regulatory elements and defective priming, ultimately promoting immune evasion and tumor progression [50]. These findings highlight that BRCA1 may exert its oncogenic functions not only through direct DNA repair-related pathways but also via remodeling the immune microenvironment. Critically, molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinity between PhIP and BRCA1, structurally elucidating the molecular mechanism underlying their co-carcinogenic interaction.

An unexpected finding in our study is that high BRCA1 expression in colorectal cancer is significantly associated with prolonged overall survival (Fig. 9B), despite its positive correlations with Th2 cells and Tcm and negative correlation with iDC, which collectively suggest a partially immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This apparent paradox can be reconciled by considering the dual functions of BRCA1. On one hand, as a classical tumor suppressor involved in DNA double-strand break repair and replication fork protection, high BRCA1 expression may enhance genomic stability and limit accumulation of oncogenic mutations, thereby slowing malignant progression. On the other hand, robust DNA damage response (DDR) driven by BRCA1 can increase the production of neoantigens and cytosolic DNA fragments, which serve as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that stimulate innate and adaptive immunity [51]. Consequently, tumors with high BRCA1 expression may exhibit higher immunogenicity, rendering them more susceptible to immune surveillance and cytotoxic T cell attack. This hypothesis is supported by the positive correlation we observed between BRCA1 and Tcm (central memory T cells), as Tcm cells are critical for long-term antitumor immunity and have been linked to favorable outcomes in several cancers when functionally intact. In contrast, the negative correlation with iDC may be compensated by other antigen-presenting cell subsets or by enhanced cross-presentation in the setting of DDR activation. Thus, we propose that high BRCA1 expression in CRC may reflect a state of enhanced DNA damage repair capacity and tumor immunogenicity, which collectively favors immune surveillance and outweighs the local immunosuppressive effects associated with Th2 enrichment. This interpretation provides a mechanistic rationale for the favorable prognosis of BRCA1-high CRC patients and highlights the potential of combining DNA damage response modulators with immunotherapies in this subgroup.

Based on toxicological and oncology data from multiple databases, this study identified BRCA1 as the central regulatory target in PhIP-induced colorectal cancer, established its potential as an early diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for colorectal cancer, and preliminarily explored mechanisms by which BRCA1 influences colorectal cancer through immune infiltration and gene mutations.

Conclusion

High-level PhIP exposure constitutes a risk factor for colorectal cancer. Acting through its core target BRCA1, PhIP participates in colorectal cancer development by modulating immune cells, gene mutations, and related mechanisms. BRCA1 demonstrates robust diagnostic efficacy and prognostic value in colorectal cancer, thus offering promising directions for early diagnosis, outcome prediction, and targeted drug development against this malignancy.
