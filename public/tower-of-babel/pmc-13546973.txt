Bone metastases from hepatocellular carcinoma: Guidance on the management of spinal and long bone lesions
Authors: Shanmei Lyu, Difan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone metastasis from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a marker of advanced disease and a potential cause of preventable functional loss. HCC skeletal lesions are commonly osteolytic and may be hypervascular, creating risks of pathological fracture, spinal instability, metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC), neurological deficit, and loss of ambulation. This structured narrative review integrates HCC-specific clinical evidence with established orthopedic and spine oncology frameworks to identify when specialist consultation should occur before irreversible skeletal or neurological deterioration. Retrospective HCC cohorts indicate that the spine is frequently involved, that surveillance-detected metastases were associated with a lower observed frequency of fracture or paralysis than symptom-detected metastases, and that hepatic reserve was associated with outcomes after skeletal surgery. Because direct HCC-specific comparative evidence remains limited, the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS), epidural spinal cord compression (ESCC)/Bilsky grading, the neurological, oncological, mechanical, and systemic (NOMS) framework, prognostic scores, Patchell's MSCC evidence, and Mirels score are used as communication aids rather than disease-specific operative thresholds. We propose a conceptual four-pathway triage model for neurological emergency, spinal mechanical failure, long-bone fracture risk, and pelvic or acetabular weight-bearing failure, followed by a systemic-fitness assessment incorporating liver reserve, hemostatic status, prognosis, rehabilitation potential, and patient goals. The framework is intended to accelerate multidisciplinary assessment, not to prescribe surgery.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Bone metastasis, Spinal metastasis, Metastatic spinal cord compression, Orthopedic oncology, Functional-risk triage

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant primary liver cancer, and liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1], [2]. HCC poses distinctive clinical challenges because prognosis and treatment are determined not only by tumor burden but also by underlying liver function and performance status [2]. Management is therefore stage-dependent: potentially curative options for selected patients with early-stage disease include surgical resection, liver transplantation, and local ablation; locoregional therapies play a major role in intermediate-stage disease; and systemic therapy, increasingly incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitor-based regimens, is central to advanced HCC [2], [3]. Although prognosis remains strongly dependent on disease stage and liver function, advances in imaging, locoregional therapy, systemic therapy, immunotherapy, and supportive care have improved outcomes for selected patients [2], [3].

As patients live longer with treated or advanced HCC, extrahepatic complications have become increasingly relevant in routine clinical practice. Bone metastasis is one of the most functionally consequential complications because it may convert systemic disease into pain, immobility, fracture, neurological injury, and loss of independence [4], [5].

Bone metastasis in HCC should therefore be viewed not only as a marker of disseminated disease but also as a potential cause of major functional deterioration. HCC-associated skeletal lesions are often osteolytic and may involve the spine, pelvis, ribs, and long bones. A vertebral lesion can progress from mechanical back pain to instability or metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC), whereas a lytic proximal femoral lesion can progress from weight-bearing pain to displaced pathological fracture. These events may permanently reduce ambulation, bladder and bowel function, eligibility for systemic therapy, and quality of life [5], [6].

Previous HCC bone-metastasis reviews have summarized epidemiology, biology, clinical manifestations, and treatment, while a recent musculoskeletal review placed bone metastasis within the broader context of skeletal and muscular alterations [4], [5], [6]. What remains less explicit is how clinicians outside orthopedic oncology should recognize a time-sensitive mechanical or neurological threat and route the patient before fracture, fixed neurological deficit, or loss of ambulation occurs. The orthopedic and spine question is therefore not whether every patient should undergo surgery, but when a lesion should trigger specialist assessment before irreversible functional loss. This review addresses that gap by linking HCC-specific evidence to four referral pathways: neurological emergency, spinal mechanical failure, long-bone fracture risk, and pelvic or acetabular weight-bearing failure. It also separates referral triggers from subsequent treatment selection, which may include radiotherapy, systemic therapy, analgesia, bracing, rehabilitation, minimally invasive procedures, surgery, or supportive care.

Clinical and biological features of HCC bone metastasis

The reported incidence and distribution of HCC bone metastasis vary with disease stage, imaging strategy, follow-up, and referral patterns. Large database studies suggest that bone metastases are present in approximately 3%–4% of patients at the initial diagnosis of HCC. In a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database analysis of 36,507 patients with newly diagnosed HCC, 4.29% had bone metastases at presentation [7], whereas a National Cancer Database study of 170,576 patients reported bone metastases in 3.1% at diagnosis [8]. These estimates primarily capture synchronous bone metastases and do not fully account for skeletal disease developing later in the clinical course. Among 459 patients with established extrahepatic metastatic HCC, bone metastases were present or subsequently developed in 32.9% [9].

In a 211-patient multicenter survey of established HCC bone metastasis, 59.7% had spinal involvement, 82.4% of lesions were osteolytic, and median survival after bone metastasis was 7 months [10]. A single-center cohort identified bone metastases in 20 of 1017 patients with HCC, including spinal involvement in 11 of the 20 [11]. These studies describe selected populations rather than population incidence, but they establish the prominence of osteolytic spinal disease and the short competing survival relevant to proportional intervention.

Radiographically, HCC bone metastases are commonly osteolytic and destructive [4], [5], [6]. Lesions may be expansile, may involve cortical disruption, and may be associated with soft-tissue mass formation [11], [12]. HCC metastases are also clinically important because they can be hypervascular. Hypervascularity is relevant not only to imaging and biopsy planning but also to orthopedic and spine surgery because tumor bleeding may complicate decompression, fixation, intramedullary nailing, or reconstruction [13], [14].

Pain is the most common clinical signal, but its phenotype matters. Localized tumor pain may respond to radiotherapy or systemic therapy, whereas pain provoked by movement, upright posture, weight bearing, or spinal loading suggests structural compromise. Neurological symptoms, gait disturbance, radicular pain, urinary retention, or bowel dysfunction raise concern for epidural extension or MSCC. Weight-bearing pain in the proximal femur, acetabulum, or pelvis may indicate impending structural failure. In a 76-patient HCC cohort, fracture or paralysis occurred in 3.0% of surveillance-detected cases and 20.9% of symptom-detected cases, supporting—without proving—a potential functional benefit of earlier recognition [15]. A 205-patient radiotherapy cohort further documented osteolytic expansile soft-tissue masses in 39.0% of patients and a median survival of 7.4 months [16].

The anatomical distribution of HCC bone metastasis includes the spine, pelvis, ribs, sternum, and long bones [5], [6]. Case-based literature has described severe neurological presentations and unusual skeletal sites, but these reports are most useful for diagnostic awareness rather than for estimating prognosis or treatment efficacy [17]. For orthopedic and spine clinicians, the most consequential sites are those in which local tumor growth threatens ambulation, spinal stability, neural elements, transfer ability, or independent self-care.

In this review, the term “function-threatening skeletal-related events” is used to distinguish major functional outcomes from broader oncologic skeletal-related event (SRE) definitions, which often include radiotherapy or surgery to bone as event categories. These function-threatening events include pathological fracture of weight-bearing bones, spinal instability, MSCC, paraplegia, bladder or bowel dysfunction, and permanent loss of ambulation. The distinction is important because the purpose of early orthopedic assessment is not merely to record skeletal events but to prevent avoidable loss of function.

Functional-risk scenarios in HCC bone metastasis

HCC bone metastasis is best approached through functional-risk scenarios rather than by asking generically how bone metastases should be treated. Four scenarios are especially important: spinal instability, epidural spinal cord compression or MSCC, impending pathological fracture of the proximal femur or other long bones, and pelvic or acetabular metastasis causing weight-bearing failure.

Spinal instability should be suspected when axial pain is mechanical, when imaging shows vertebral body collapse, junctional involvement, posterior element compromise, progressive deformity, or extensive osteolytic destruction. The Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS) provides a structured language for communicating instability risk, but it was developed for neoplastic spinal disease broadly rather than for HCC specifically [18], [19]. In HCC, SINS should be interpreted together with hypervascularity, liver reserve, expected survival, and the feasibility of radiotherapy, bracing, stabilization, or supportive care.

Epidural spinal cord compression and MSCC represent neurological risk scenarios. Epidural spinal cord compression (ESCC)/Bilsky grading supports structured assessment of epidural tumor extension, while mixed-cancer MSCC evidence suggests that selected patients may benefit from direct decompressive surgery plus postoperative radiotherapy [20], [21]. HCC-specific retrospective series also emphasize the importance of ambulatory status, neurological function, and careful patient selection [22], [23], [24]. The practical point is that high-grade epidural compression, progressive weakness, gait deterioration, urinary retention, or bowel dysfunction should not be managed as routine pain palliation.

Impending pathological fracture of the proximal femur or other long bones is a separate mechanical scenario. Mirels score can support discussion of fracture risk by integrating site, pain, lesion type, and lesion size, but it is not HCC-specific and should not be treated as an automatic operative rule [25], [26]. In HCC, an osteolytic proximal femoral lesion with weight-bearing pain has high functional consequences because a completed fracture may abruptly eliminate ambulation and force emergency surgery in a patient with impaired liver reserve, thrombocytopenia, or other hemostatic risk factors.

Pelvic and acetabular metastases can cause weight-bearing failure even without a classic long-bone fracture. Acetabular destruction may impair standing, transfer, sitting balance, and ambulation. Evidence specific to HCC pelvic reconstruction is sparse, so management should be individualized. The decision may include protected weight bearing, radiotherapy, cement augmentation, ablation, orthopedic reconstruction, or palliative care depending on pain, mechanical failure, liver reserve, expected survival, and patient goals.

Spine metastasis: SINS, ESCC, the neurological, oncological, mechanical, and systemic (NOMS) framework and surgical timing

Spine metastasis from HCC requires separate assessment of mechanical instability, epidural compression, local tumor control, and systemic fitness. HCC-specific evidence consists mainly of retrospective series and a systematic review dominated by case reports and small cohorts [13], [22], [23]. One 62-patient HCC spine cohort developed an internally assessed prognostic score based on general condition, albumin or Child-Pugh class, and bone-modifying therapy, but external validation is lacking [27]. Mixed-cancer evidence supplies structured systems for spinal instability and epidural compression [18], [19], [20]. NOMS and randomized MSCC evidence support multidisciplinary assessment and selected decompressive treatment [21], [28], [29]. Revised Tokuhashi and contemporary MSCC and radiotherapy guidance provide additional prognostic and treatment context [30], [31], [32]. These sources support structured communication but not an HCC-specific operative threshold.

SINS and ESCC/Bilsky separate mechanical stability from the degree of epidural compression [18], [19], [20]. NOMS adds oncological and systemic considerations, helping clinicians avoid treating all spinal metastases as pain-only lesions [28], [29].

Radiotherapy is central for pain palliation and local control, but it does not immediately correct mechanical instability or reconstruct a failed vertebral segment. Mechanical pain, a SINS score in the potentially unstable or unstable range, progressive collapse or deformity, posterior wall compromise, and suspected neural compression should prompt spine assessment. Urinary retention, bowel dysfunction, rapidly progressive weakness, or cauda equina features should trigger emergency magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and urgent multidisciplinary review. High-grade ESCC without a progressive deficit still requires prompt specialist assessment, but urgency and treatment depend on neurological trajectory, tumor radiosensitivity, mechanical stability, prior radiotherapy, and systemic fitness [20], [28], [29].

The NOMS framework is particularly useful as a communication model because it integrates neurological, oncological, mechanical, and systemic considerations [28], [29]. In HCC, the systemic component needs special attention because liver dysfunction, portal hypertension, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, and systemic therapy exposure may shift the balance away from extensive surgery even when mechanical or neurological risk is present. Conversely, a patient with preserved performance status, controlled liver disease, focal instability, and a realistic rehabilitation goal may deserve early spine consultation before neurological decline occurs.

Surgical timing should be framed around prevention of irreversible loss rather than rescue after complete deficit. Patchell et al. provide mixed-cancer randomized evidence supporting direct decompressive surgery plus postoperative radiotherapy in selected MSCC patients, but this evidence is not HCC-specific [21]. HCC-specific studies suggest that selected patients with spinal metastasis may benefit from multidisciplinary treatment, stabilization, decompression, or less invasive approaches, especially when ambulation and systemic fitness remain adequate [13], [22], [23]. Prospective mixed-cancer data and multidisciplinary spine-oncology guidance further support attention to neurological status, ambulatory function, and timely assessment, while retrospective timing data suggest better neurological recovery when surgery for MSCC is not delayed [33], [34], [35]. Case literature underscores that delayed recognition of spinal cord compression can lead to severe neurological deterioration [17].

Prognostic tools such as revised Tokuhashi and Tomita may help structure survival discussions, but they should be interpreted cautiously in modern HCC because survival depends on liver reserve, systemic therapy options, extrahepatic burden, and competing liver-related mortality [30]. Contemporary guidelines for MSCC and bone radiotherapy, including National Institute for Health and Care Excellence(NICE) and American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) guidance, can inform multidisciplinary care, but they are not HCC-specific validation studies [31], [32]. Postoperative stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) or stereotactic approaches may be considered in selected spine metastasis contexts, but structural instability and neural compression still require mechanical and surgical assessment rather than radiation planning alone [36], [37], [38]. Therefore, in HCC, SINS, ESCC/Bilsky, NOMS, and prognostic scores should be used as consultation triggers and communication frameworks rather than as validated operative thresholds.

Long-bone metastasis: Mirels score and prophylactic fixation

Long-bone metastasis becomes clinically urgent when it threatens weight bearing, transfers, or self-care. Direct HCC evidence is limited but not absent: a 42-patient appendicular surgical series included 30 pathological fractures and 12 lesions treated because of high fracture risk; median survival after surgery was 10 months, and Child-Pugh grade and number of skeletal metastases were independent prognostic factors [39]. Mixed-cancer evidence supports Mirels score and general appendicular metastasis principles [25], [26], [40]. Together, these data justify early orthopedic discussion while remaining insufficient to define an HCC-specific fixation threshold.

The proximal femur is the highest-consequence long-bone site because it bears weight, transmits high mechanical loads, and is central to standing, transfers, and ambulation. A completed proximal femoral fracture can abruptly eliminate walking ability and may require urgent fixation or reconstruction under suboptimal medical conditions. Humeral lesions are usually less directly related to ambulation, but they may still be functionally important when pain or fracture risk compromises transfers, walking aids, dressing, hygiene, or basic self-care. Rib lesions may be painful and clinically important, but they rarely create the same immediate mobility threat as proximal femoral or periacetabular disease.

Mirels score remains the most familiar tool for estimating impending pathological fracture risk in long bones. It incorporates anatomical site, pain, radiographic lesion type, and lesion size [25]. Jawad and Scully summarize its use and limitations, including the fact that it is a classification tool developed for metastatic long-bone disease broadly rather than for HCC-specific bone metastasis [26]. Its practical limitation is that it can be sensitive to fracture risk while remaining imperfectly specific; over-reliance may either encourage unnecessary prophylactic surgery or underestimate risk in lesions whose geometry, cortical destruction, or patient loading pattern is not fully captured. For HCC, Mirels score should be used as a communication aid together with computed tomography (CT) evaluation of cortical destruction, lesion location, pain with weight bearing, liver reserve, expected survival, and patient goals.

Prophylactic fixation may be more proportionate than waiting for a completed fracture in selected patients with focal, mechanically threatening lesions and sufficient systemic reserve. This is especially relevant for lytic lesions in the proximal femur, subtrochanteric region, femoral shaft, or humerus when pain occurs during use or weight bearing. The HCC surgical series supports feasibility in selected patients but is retrospective and cannot establish comparative benefit [39]. Broader appendicular metastasis literature shows that tumor type, visceral disease, metastatic burden, fracture status, expected implant durability, and survival all influence outcome [40].

Radiotherapy can palliate pain and contribute to local tumor control, but it does not immediately restore structural integrity in a bone at high risk of fracture. Conversely, fixation without adequate oncological planning may fail to control local tumor progression. Therefore, long-bone decision-making should integrate orthopedic oncology, radiation oncology, medical oncology, hepatology, anesthesia, rehabilitation, and palliative care. Protected weight bearing, bracing, radiotherapy, embolization, fixation, reconstruction, or supportive care may all be appropriate depending on mechanical risk and patient fitness. Therefore, in HCC, Mirels score and appendicular metastasis principles should be used as consultation triggers and communication frameworks rather than as validated operative thresholds.

Pelvic and acetabular lesions require a related but distinct logic. The major functional problem is often weight-bearing failure rather than a discrete shaft fracture. Periacetabular destruction can cause pain with standing, transfer failure, inability to sit or walk, and progressive dependence even when no long-bone fracture is present. Treatment options may include protected weight bearing, radiotherapy, ablation, cement augmentation, screw-cement constructs, reconstruction, or supportive care, but HCC-specific acetabular reconstruction evidence is limited. These options should therefore be discussed as extrapolated orthopedic oncology principles rather than disease-specific standards.

HCC-specific perioperative considerations

HCC-specific surgical decision-making differs from many other metastatic bone disease scenarios because skeletal metastasis often occurs in the setting of chronic liver disease. Preoperative assessment should distinguish hepatic reserve from hemostatic risk. Child-Pugh, the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD), and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade can describe liver dysfunction, whereas procedure-specific postoperative risk may be estimated with a validated cirrhosis model such as the Veterans Outcomes and Costs Associated with Liver Disease-Penn (VOCAL-Penn) score when applicable [41], [42], [43]. Hemostatic assessment should integrate procedure magnitude, platelet count, fibrinogen when clinically indicated, portal hypertension, acute kidney injury, infection or sepsis, acute-on-chronic liver failure, antithrombotic therapy, prior bleeding, and local capacity for hemostasis. Prolonged Prolonged prothrombin time/international normalized ratio in cirrhosis reflects hepatic synthetic dysfunction but, by itself, does not reliably predict procedural bleeding and should not be treated as an automatic correction target [44]. Nutrition, sarcopenia, renal function, anesthesia risk, prognosis, and realistic rehabilitation potential remain integral to candidacy.

Hypervascularity is a practical surgical issue. HCC metastases may bleed substantially during biopsy, decompression, curettage, intramedullary fixation, or reconstruction. Preoperative arterial embolization may be considered for selected hypervascular lesions after interventional radiology review, particularly when major blood loss would compromise safety. However, the available evidence does not support routine embolization for every HCC bone metastasis. A systematic review of preoperative embolization for long-bone metastasis reported that successful embolization was achieved in variable proportions of cases, complications were uncommon but present, and one level III study in HCC long-bone metastases did not show reduced perioperative blood loss or transfusion requirement [14]. The purpose of embolization is mainly to reduce bleeding from hypervascular metastatic tumors; its benefit depends on tumor vascularity, embolization completeness, lesion location, and the planned operation [14].

Systemic therapy timing also matters. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor-containing regimens raise bleeding and wound-healing concerns, tyrosine kinase inhibitors may affect hypertension, toxicity, and hepatic reserve, and immune checkpoint inhibitors may cause organ-specific toxicities relevant to anesthesia and recovery [45], [46], [47]. This review does not propose fixed interruption intervals. Perioperative cessation and resumption should follow the current product information, institutional protocols, procedure magnitude, wound-healing risk, hepatic reserve, and multidisciplinary judgment.

Bone-modifying agents may have supportive roles in selected cancer patients with bone metastases, but direct HCC-specific evidence remains limited. European Society for Medical Oncology(ESMO) bone-health guidance and MRONJ literature support careful attention to skeletal-event and dental risk [48], [49]. Randomized trials in broader solid-tumor populations additionally inform agent selection and dosing intervals [50], [51], [52]. These agents should not replace urgent evaluation of mechanical instability, MSCC, impending long-bone fracture, or pelvic weight-bearing failure.

The central perioperative principle is proportionality. A short-segment stabilization, minimally invasive approach, preoperative embolization, bracing strategy, or radiotherapy plan may be more appropriate than extensive reconstruction in a patient with limited liver reserve or rapidly progressive systemic disease. Conversely, a patient with controlled intrahepatic disease, preserved performance status, manageable bleeding risk, and a focal mechanically threatening lesion may deserve early surgical evaluation before irreversible functional decline.

Proposed conceptual decision framework

The framework starts by identifying the dominant functional threat and routes the patient through one of four clinical pathways. It separates the trigger for referral from the treatment chosen after specialist assessment.

Pathway 1: neurological emergency. Progressive weakness, gait deterioration, urinary retention, bowel dysfunction, or cauda equina features should prompt emergency MRI and urgent spine and radiation oncology review [21], [31]. MRI defines epidural disease and neural compression, and ESCC/Bilsky grading standardizes its description [20]. NOMS integrates neurological, oncological, mechanical, and systemic factors [28], [29].

Pathway 2: spinal mechanical failure. Mechanical back pain, progressive vertebral collapse or deformity, junctional disease, posterior-element involvement, or substantial osteolysis should prompt CT/MRI and SINS assessment. A SINS score of at least 7 is a consultation trigger rather than an operative mandate [18], [19]. Radiotherapy sequencing should account for instability because pain response does not reconstruct mechanical failure [28], [29].

Pathway 3: long-bone fracture risk. Functional or weight-bearing pain, a lytic proximal femoral lesion, progressive cortical destruction, or an actual pathological fracture should prompt radiography or CT, protected weight bearing when appropriate, Mirels-based communication, and early orthopedic oncology review [25], [39], [40]. Mirels does not replace assessment of lesion geometry, cortical involvement, expected loading, prognosis, and implant durability [26].

Pathway 4: pelvic or acetabular weight-bearing failure. Pain with standing or transfer, acetabular roof or column destruction, and loss of ambulation should prompt CT-based assessment of bone stock and orthopedic review [53], [54]. Potential options include protected weight bearing, radiotherapy, ablation, cement augmentation, screw-cement constructs, reconstruction, or supportive care; HCC-specific comparative evidence is sparse, and these principles are extrapolated from broader periacetabular metastasis literature [55], [56], [57].

All four pathways converge on a systemic-fitness gate. Hepatic reserve and procedure-specific cirrhosis risk should be considered separately from global hemostatic status [41], [42], [43]. Tumor vascularity and the feasibility of selective embolization require procedure-specific assessment [14], [58], [59]. Systemic disease control, recent therapy, infection, sarcopenia, expected survival, rehabilitation potential, and patient goals further shape proportional treatment selection [45], [46], [47]. Hemostatic interpretation should account for the limitations of conventional coagulation tests in cirrhosis [44]. Only after this gate should treatment be selected. Thus, urgent referral does not imply automatic decompression, fixation, or reconstruction.

Discussion

The principal clinical implication of this review is that, in patients with bone metastasis from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the threshold for orthopedic or spine specialist consultation should be lower than the threshold for surgery. The clinically relevant question is not simply whether skeletal metastasis is present, but whether a lesion threatens neurological function, mechanical stability, weight-bearing capacity, ambulation, continence, or independence. Progressive neurological compromise or suspected metastatic spinal cord compression requires particular attention [22], [23], [34]. Spinal mechanical instability represents a distinct functional threat [13], [18], [22]. Impending pathological fracture of a long bone may abruptly eliminate ambulation [25], [39], [60]. Pelvic or acetabular destruction can likewise threaten weight bearing and transfer ability [53], [56], [57]. Previous reviews have mainly summarized the epidemiology, biological mechanisms, clinical manifestations, radiotherapy, systemic treatment, and supportive management of HCC bone metastasis [4], [5], [6], whereas the present review addresses the narrower practical question of when a clinically relevant skeletal lesion should prompt specialist assessment before irreversible functional loss occurs. The proposed clinical logic is sequential: the dominant and potentially reversible neurological or mechanical threat should first determine referral urgency. Progressive weakness, gait deterioration, urinary retention, bowel dysfunction, or high-grade epidural compression should prompt urgent specialist assessment rather than observation while awaiting systemic treatment response [20], [23], [31]. Unstable vertebral collapse, a mechanically threatening proximal femoral lesion, or pelvic destruction impairing standing or walking should likewise prompt early mechanical assessment [15], [39], [56]. Once the immediate threat has been defined, HCC-specific factors—including hepatic reserve, portal-hypertensive complications, hemostatic status, tumor hypervascularity, systemic disease control, expected survival, rehabilitation potential, and patient goals—should determine whether and how intervention is proportionate [14], [41], [42]. This distinction is important because specialist consultation is a diagnostic and risk-stratification step rather than a commitment to surgery; it can clarify anatomy, neurological risk, mechanical instability, prognosis, treatment alternatives, and operative candidacy, whereas surgery requires a higher threshold in which the anticipated functional benefit must outweigh anesthesia risk, bleeding, wound complications, rehabilitation burden, interruption of systemic therapy, and competing mortality. Consequently, anatomically similar lesions may reasonably lead to different treatments in patients with different hepatic reserve, systemic disease trajectories, and rehabilitation potential. SINS, ESCC/Bilsky grading, and NOMS can support structured assessment and multidisciplinary communication [18], [20], [28]. Mirels, Tokuhashi, and disease-specific prognostic tools provide complementary fracture-risk and survival context [25], [27], [30]. None constitutes an HCC-specific operative rule or estimates the net benefit of a particular operation for an individual patient. Their appropriate role is to support triage and trigger expert review, particularly because the HCC surgical literature remains dominated by selected retrospective cohorts and is vulnerable to referral bias, confounding by indication, and survivor selection [22], [23], [39]. The proposed framework should not be interpreted as a population-screening strategy or as a mandate for routine skeletal imaging in all asymptomatic patients; its purpose is to reduce delayed referral once symptoms or imaging suggest a clinically meaningful neurological or mechanical threat. Accordingly, the most defensible conclusion is not that a particular symptom, score, or imaging feature mandates surgery, but that defined red flags warrant timely multidisciplinary assessment, while decisions regarding decompression, stabilization, fixation, radiotherapy, or supportive care remain individualized. Prospective multicenter studies should determine whether earlier specialist referral improves function-centered outcomes, including ambulation, neurological and sphincter function, weight-bearing pain, independence, rehabilitation, return to systemic therapy, and avoidance of subsequent emergency skeletal intervention, and existing decision tools require HCC-specific calibration. In summary, this framework does not define who must undergo surgery; it identifies who should not remain without timely specialist assessment. Neurological and mechanical threats should determine referral urgency, whereas hepatic reserve, bleeding risk, systemic disease status, expected survival, rehabilitation potential, and patient goals should determine the proportionality of treatment.

Limitations of this review

This review has four principal limitations. First, the structured narrative search was designed to identify clinically relevant evidence rather than to achieve exhaustive study capture, and complete historical database exports and stage-specific record counts were not prospectively archived. Accordingly, the illustrative search syntax provided in Supplementary Table S2 should not be interpreted as a search record compliant with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses or a fully reproducible systematic-review search record. Second, no formal risk-of-bias instrument or evidence-grading system was applied, although the directness of the evidence and the major limitations of the included study designs were summarized. Third, the proposed framework was developed by the authors without a formal Delphi process, external consensus panel, or prospective validation. Fourth, direct HCC-specific comparative evidence remains limited, particularly regarding prophylactic fixation, periacetabular reconstruction, preoperative embolization, and treatment sequencing; consequently, substantial parts of the framework rely on cautious extrapolation from mixed-cancer evidence. These limitations mean that the framework should be interpreted as a conceptual clinical triage aid rather than a validated guideline or a stand-alone treatment rule.

Conclusion

Bone metastasis from hepatocellular carcinoma should be regarded not only as a manifestation of advanced systemic disease but also as a potential cause of preventable functional deterioration [4], [10], [15]. Osteolytic and hypervascular skeletal lesions may threaten ambulation, spinal stability, neurological function, bladder and bowel control, and patient independence [5], [12], [13]. Mechanical pain, vertebral collapse, posterior wall compromise, epidural spinal cord compression, and progressive neurological symptoms should prompt timely spine specialist assessment [20], [23], [31]. Pain with weight bearing and high-risk lesions involving the proximal femur, pelvis, or acetabulum should likewise prompt timely orthopedic assessment [25], [39], [56].

SINS, ESCC/Bilsky grading, and NOMS can support structured communication regarding stability, epidural disease, and multidisciplinary treatment selection [18], [20], [28]. Mirels, Tokuhashi, and HCC-specific prognostic tools can provide complementary fracture-risk or survival context [25], [27], [30]. None constitutes an HCC-specific operative criterion. The central clinical distinction is between a deliberately low threshold for specialist consultation and a higher, individualized threshold for intervention. Treatment should be proportionate to hepatic reserve, hemostatic status, perioperative risk, tumor vascularity, systemic disease burden, expected survival, rehabilitation potential, and patient goals [14], [41], [47]. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether earlier pathway-based referral can preserve ambulation, neurological and sphincter function, independence, and access to systemic therapy without exposing patients to disproportionate procedural harm. The purpose of the proposed framework is not to define who must undergo surgery, but to identify who should not remain without timely specialist assessment.
