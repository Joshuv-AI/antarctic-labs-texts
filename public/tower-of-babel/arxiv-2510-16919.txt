A hitchhiker’s guide to first-order elliptic boundary value problems
Christian Bär, Lashi Bandara

Abstract
. To empower the mathematical hitchhiker wishing to use operator methods in geometry and topology, we present this user’s guide to first-order elliptic boundary value problems. Existence, regularity, and Fredholmness are discussed for general first-order elliptic operators on manifolds with compact boundary. The focus is on a very general class of elliptic boundary conditions, which contain those that are pseudo-local as a special case, yielding the relative index theorem. A new characterisation of a subclass of elliptic boundary conditions is also given.

Introduction

The index theorem of Atiyah-Patodi-Singer
[

This result, beyond its immediate value, highlighted nonlocal boundary conditions as the quintessential type in the study of first-order boundary value problems.
Their study has been a primary focus in the decades since, with a particular focus given to pseudo-local boundary conditions, arising as the range of a pseudo-differential projector of order zero.

Although it is beyond the scope of this paper to provide an exhaustive list of contributions,

A typical assumption in all of these papers is that the adapted boundary operator, which can be thought of as the trace of the operator to the boundary, can be chosen self-adjoint.
In particular,
[
8
]
provides a description of
all

That is, the boundary trace map is extended to the whole of the maximal domain.
Furthermore, regularity, Fredholmness, and index theory are discussed in a broadly applicable context.

In

In fact, results of

The methods employed in

Instead, modern

These techniques are considerably technical in nature.

Let us now arrive at the present paper, which we introduce with the following analogy.
The roadside hitchhiker, in order to travel to their desired destination, need not know about mechanical aspects of motor vehicles nor do they even need to know how to drive.
Much in the same way, the mathematical hitchhiker should be able to utilise results in

It is in this spirit that this “user’s guide” to first-order boundary value problems has been conceived.

1.
Setup and preliminaries

Throughout,
M
will be a smooth manifold with smooth boundary.
We write
{\rm C}^{k}(M;E)
to denote the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable sections of
E
,
{\rm C}^{k}_{\rm c}(M;E)
the subspace of compactly supported sections (possibly nonzero on the boundary), and
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm cc}(M;E)
the subspace of
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm c}(M;E)
whose sections are supported on the interior of
M
.

We fix a smooth measure
\mu
on
M
.
By this, we mean a smooth positive section of the density bundle of
M
.
Given a Hermitian vector bundle
(E,h^{E})\to M
, we naturally obtain the Hilbert space
{\rm L}^{2}(M;E)
of square integrable sections.

When
M
is compact and without boundary, the Sobolev spaces, with respect to
{\rm L}^{2}
, are denoted by
{\rm H}^{\alpha}(M;E)
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
These are Hilbert spaces.
For
\alpha<\beta
, there is a continuous embedding
{\rm H}^{\beta}(M;E)\hookrightarrow{\rm H}^{\alpha}(M;E)
.
In particular,
{\rm H}^{0}(M;E)={\rm L}^{2}(M;E)
and, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, the elements of
{\rm H}^{\alpha}(M;E)
are the sections whose distributional derivatives up to order
\alpha
lie in
{\rm L}^{2}(M;E)
.
For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, extending the
{\rm L}^{2}
-scalar product in one argument and restricting in the other, we obtain a perfect pairing
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{{\rm H}^{\alpha}\times{\rm H}^{-\alpha}}\colon{\rm H}^{\alpha}(M;E)\times{\rm H}^{-\alpha}(M;E)\to\mathbb{C}
.

We fix a first-order linear differential operator
D\colon{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;E)\to{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;F)
, where
(F,h^{F})\to M
is another Hermitian bundle.
There is a unique formal adjoint
D^{\dagger}\colon{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;F)\to{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;E)
.
The maximal and minimal extensions of
D
are defined by

D_{\max}:=\big(D^{\dagger}|_{{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm cc}}\big)^{\ast}\quad\text{ and }\quad D_{\min}:=\overline{\big(D|_{{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm cc}}\big)},

where
∗
denotes the
{\rm L}^{2}
-adjoint and
\overline{\phantom{x}}
the closure in
{\rm L}^{2}(M;E)
.
The domains
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
and
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\min})
are Banach spaces with respect to the graph norm
u\mapsto\|u\|_{D}=\|u\|_{{\rm L}^{2}}+\|Du\|_{{\rm L}^{2}}
.
Similarly, we define
D^{\dagger}_{\max}
an
D^{\dagger}_{\min}
by interchanging the roles of
D
and
D^{\dagger}
.
The principal symbol of
D
is denoted by
\upsigma_{D}(\xi)
, which is characterised by
D(fu)=\upsigma_{D}(df)u+fDu
.

The standard setup in which we work is the following:

(S1)

M
is a smooth manifold with compact smooth boundary
\partial M
;

(S2)

\mu
is a smooth measure on
M
;

(S3)

T\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;TM)
is an interior pointing vector field along
\partial M
;

(S4)

(E,h^{E}),\ (F,h^{F})\to M
are Hermitian vector bundles over
M
;

(S5)

D
is a first-order elliptic differential operator mapping sections of
E
to those of
F
;

(S6)

D
and
D^{\dagger}
are complete, i.e., compactly supported sections in
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
are dense in
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
with respect to the graph norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
and similarly for
D^{\dagger}
.

Note that
\partial M
is assumed to be compact but we do not assume that
M
is compact.
So the theory applies if
M
is the complement of a relatively compact smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for example.

The vector field
T
induces a covector field
\tau\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;T^{*}M)
characterised by the conditions
\tau(T)=1
and
\tau|_{T\partial M}=0
.

The measure
\mu
on
M
together with
T
induce a smooth measure
\nu
on
\partial M
given by
\nu(\cdots)=\mu(T,\cdots)
.

Example 1
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold.
The Riemannian metric induces a measure
\mu
on
M
and an interior pointing unit normal field
T
along
\partial M
.

Let
(E,h^{E}),\ (F,h^{F})\to M
be Hermitian vector bundles over
M
of the same rank.
A first-order differential operator
D
mapping sections of
E
to those of
F
is called a
Dirac-type operator
if its principal symbol satisfies the Clifford relations
\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}\upsigma_{D}(\eta)+\upsigma_{D}(\eta)^{*}\upsigma_{D}(\xi)=2g(\xi,\eta)\mathrm{id}_{E}
for all
\xi,\eta\in T^{*}_{x}M
and
x\in M
.
In particular,
\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}\upsigma_{D}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}\mathrm{id}_{E}
so that
\upsigma_{D}(\xi)
is injective for all
\xi\neq 0
.
Since the ranks of
E
and
F
are the same,
\upsigma_{D}(\xi)
is invertible.
Hence Dirac-type operators are elliptic.
Theorem
2
will show that
D
and
D^{\dagger}
are complete if the Riemannian metric of
M
is complete.

Example 2
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 3
.
Assume the setup of Example
1
and let
D
be a Dirac-type operator between
E
and
F
.
We define a Dirac-type operator
\mathscr{D}
between
T^{*}M\otimes E
and
T^{*}M\otimes F
by

\mathscr{D}(\eta\otimes e)=\eta\otimes De+\sum_{i}\nabla_{e_{i}}\eta\otimes\upsigma_{D}(e^{i})e.

Here
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection on
T^{*}M
and
\{e_{i}\}
is a local orthonormal tangent frame while
\{e^{i}\}
is its dual cotangent frame.
This definition is independent of the choice of frame and yields a well-defined first-order differential operator.
The principal symbol of
\mathscr{D}
is given by
\upsigma_{\mathscr{D}}(\xi)=\mathrm{id}\otimes\upsigma_{D}(\xi)
.
Thus,
\mathscr{D}
is also a Dirac-type operator.

Define

\displaystyle\gamma\colon T^{*}M\otimes E\to F,

\displaystyle\quad\gamma(\xi\otimes v)=\upsigma_{D}(\xi)v\text{ and}

\displaystyle\iota\colon F\to T^{*}M\otimes E,

\displaystyle\quad\iota(f)=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}e^{i}\otimes\upsigma_{D}(e^{i})^{*}f.

Straightforward computation shows

\displaystyle\gamma\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}_{F},

(1)

\displaystyle\iota^{*}=\tfrac{1}{n}\gamma.

(2)

Equation (
1
) shows that
\iota\circ\gamma\colon T^{*}M\otimes E\to T^{*}M\otimes E
is a projection onto the image of
\iota
.
By Equation (
2
), the projection
\iota\circ\gamma
is self-adjoint.
Since
\iota
is injective, the kernel of this projection is the same as the kernel of
\gamma
.
We define

E^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}:=\ker(\gamma)\subset T^{*}M\otimes E.

We have the orthogonal decomposition

T^{*}M\otimes E=\iota(F)\oplus E^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}.

There is an analogous orthogonal decomposition
T^{*}M\otimes F=\tilde{\iota}(E)\oplus F^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}
where

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\colon T^{*}M\otimes F\to E,

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{\gamma}(\xi\otimes f)=\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}f,\text{ and}

\displaystyle\tilde{\iota}\colon E\to T^{*}M\otimes F,

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{\iota}(v)=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}e^{i}\otimes\upsigma_{D}(e^{i})v\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle F^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\ker(\tilde{\gamma}).

The
Rarita-Schwinger operator

D_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\colon C^{\infty}(M;E^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})\to C^{\infty}(M;F^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})
is defined by

D_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}=(\mathrm{id}_{T^{*}M\otimes F}-\tilde{\iota}\circ\tilde{\gamma})\mathscr{D}|_{E^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}.

The Rarita-Schwinger operator
D_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}
is not of Dirac type but in Appendix
A Ellipticity of the Rarita-Schwinger operator
we show that it is elliptic.
Theorem
2
will show that
D_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}
and
D_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}^{\dagger}
are complete if the Riemannian metric of
M
is complete.

Imposing boundary conditions amounts to considering extensions of
D_{\min}
contained in
D_{\max}
.
To understand these extensions, it is necessary to define the boundary trace map on
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
as well as characterise
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\min})
.
This is provided by the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(The trace theorem
[
10
]
*Thm. 2.3 (i) and (ii))
.

Under the assumptions
(S1)
–
(S6)
,
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm c}(M;E)
is dense in
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
with respect to the graph norm and the restriction map to the boundary

u\mapsto u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\colon{\rm C}^{\infty}_{\rm c}(M;E)\to{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;E)

has a unique bounded extension

u\mapsto u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\colon\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})\to{\rm H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E).

The kernel of this extension is precisely
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\min})
.

For a closed subspace
B\subset{\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
we define

\mathrm{dom}(D_{B}):=\left\{u\in\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max}):u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\in B\right\}.

The restriction of
D_{\max}
to

.

2.
Verifying the completeness assumption
(S6)

In this section, we provide a useful geometric criterion for completeness of an operator and its formal adjoint.
Conceptually, any geometric operator on a complete Riemannian manifold satisfies completeness.

Theorem 2

(
[
11
]
*Thm. 2.1)
.

Assume
(S1)
–
(S4)
and let
D\colon{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;E)\to{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;F)
be a first-order differential operator.
Suppose

such that the principal symbol of

|\upsigma_{D}(\xi)|\leq C\cdot|\xi|_{g}

(3)

for all

.
Then
(S6)
holds, i.e.,

and
D^{\dagger}
are complete.

Remark 1
.

Note that there is no assumption here that
\mu

Although

depends on
\mu
, the principal symbol does not.

Example 3
.

For any Dirac-type operator

|\upsigma_{D}(\xi)u|^{2}=h\big(\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}\upsigma_{D}(\xi)u,u\big)=|\xi|_{g}^{2}\,|u|^{2}

Example 4
.

Let

be a Rarita-Schwinger operator.
From the computation of the principal symbol of

in (

Remark 2
.

A slightly more general version of Theorem

can be obtained by replacing the constant

3.
Elliptic boundary conditions

Regularity is a local question and interior regularity is furnished simply from the ellipticity of the operator
D
.
Given that we have defined the boundary restriction map on
\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})
, we are able to consider the question of regularity up to the boundary.

Theorem 3

(
[
10
]
*Thm. 2.4)
.

Under
(S1)
–
(S6)
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})\cap{\rm H}^{k+1}_{\rm loc}(M;E)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\{u\in\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max}):Du\in{\rm H}^{k}_{\rm loc}(M;E)\text{ and }u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\in{\rm H}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)\right\}.

(4)

By the Sobolev embedding theorem, we therefore have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})

\displaystyle\cap{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;E)

\displaystyle=\left\{u\in\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max}):Du\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(M;E)\text{ and }u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;E)\right\}.

In the next subsection, we will see that the condition
u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\in{\rm H}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
in (

) can be relaxed, see Theorem
4

(ii)

3.1.
Adapted boundary operators

To describe elliptic boundary conditions, we require the notion of adapted boundary operators.

Definition 1

(Adapted boundary operator)
.

Assume
(S1)
and
(S3)
–
(S5)
.
We say that a differential operator
A\colon{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;E)\to{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;E)
is an
adapted operator
for
D
if the principal symbol of
A
satisfies:

\upsigma_{A}(\xi)=\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{-1}\circ\upsigma_{D}(\xi).

(5)

Here we identify
\xi\in T^{*}_{x}\partial M
with its extension to
T_{x}M
which satisfies
\xi(T)=0
.

Remark 3
.

The construction of
\upsigma_{A}
and the notion of adapted operator are still meaningful if we restrict to a two-sided hypersurface
N\subset M
instead of
\partial M
.

Clearly
A
is a first-order elliptic differential operator.
Such an operator always exists.
Its spectrum is discrete.
The projectors
\chi^{\pm}(A)
projecting onto the eigenspaces for the eigenvalues with positive or non-positive real part, respectively, exist and act boundedly
\chi^{\pm}(A)\colon{\rm H}^{\alpha}(\partial M;E)\to{\rm H}^{\alpha}(\partial M;E)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 4

(
[
10
]
*Thm. 2.3 (iv) and Thm. 2.4)
.

Assume
(S1)
–
(S6)
.
Let
A
be an adapted boundary operator for
D
.
Then:

(i)

For all
u\in\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})\cap{\rm H}^{1}_{\rm loc}(M;E)
and
v\in\mathrm{dom}((D^{\dagger})_{\max})\cap{\rm H}^{1}_{\rm loc}(M;F)
,

\left\langle D_{\max}u,v\right\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}(M;F)}-\left\langle u,(D^{\dagger})_{\max}v\right\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}(M;E)}=-\left\langle u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M},\upsigma_{0}^{\ast}v{{\lvert}}_{\partial M}\right\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)}.

(ii)

The boundary regularity can be described in terms of
\chi^{+}(A)
by:

\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max})\cap{\rm H}^{k+1}_{\rm loc}(M;E)\\
=\left\{u\in\mathrm{dom}(D_{\max}):Du\in{\rm H}^{k}_{\rm loc}(M;F)\ \text{and}\ \chi^{+}(A)(u{{\lvert}}_{\partial M})\in{\rm H}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)\right\}.

Example 5
.

For a Dirac-type operator
D
on a Riemannian manifold we choose
T
to be the inward pointing unit normal vector field and its induced conormal field
\tau
along
\partial M
.
Then
\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{*}\upsigma_{D}(\tau)=|\tau|^{2}=1
and hence
\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{*}=\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{-1}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\upsigma_{A}(\xi)^{*}

\displaystyle=\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}\circ(\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{-1})^{*}=\upsigma_{D}(\xi)^{*}\circ\upsigma_{D}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{*}\circ\upsigma_{D}(\xi)=-\upsigma_{D}(\tau)^{-1}\circ\upsigma_{D}(\xi)=-\upsigma_{A}(\xi).

Moreover,
\upsigma_{A}
also satisfies the Clifford relations.
Thus,
A
is also of Dirac-type and can be chosen to be self-adjoint.

Example 6
.

The symbol
\upsigma_{A}
defined in (
5
) for the Rarita-Schwinger operator

is not skew-symmetric, see

*Sec. 3.3.
Thus the adapted operator

cannot be chosen self-adjoint and is not again a Rarita-Schwinger operator.

3.2.
General theory of elliptic boundary conditions

We identify a class of “good” boundary conditions for which we will obtain regularity up to the boundary.
We start by giving an abstract definition of elliptic boundary conditions.

Definition 2

(
\infty
-Elliptic boundary condition)
.

A closed subspace
B\subset{\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
is called an
\infty
-elliptic boundary condition
for
D
if

B=W_{+}\oplus\left\{v+gv:v\in V_{-}\cap{\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)\right\}

where

(i)

W_{\pm}
,
V_{\pm}
are mutually complementary subspaces of
{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)
such that

V_{\pm}\oplus W_{\pm}=\chi^{\pm}(A){\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E),

(ii)

W_{\pm}
are finite dimensional with
W_{\pm},\widetilde{W}_{\pm}:=(V_{+}\oplus V_{-}\oplus W_{\mp})^{\perp,{\rm L}^{2}}\subset{\rm C}^{\infty}(\partial M;E)
, and

(iii)

g\colon{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)\to{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)
is bounded linear with

\displaystyle g|_{V_{+}\oplus W_{+}\oplus W_{-}}=0,

\displaystyle g(V_{-})\subset V_{+},

\displaystyle g(V_{-}\cap{\rm H}^{s}(\partial M;E))\subset V_{+}\cap{\rm H}^{s}(\partial M;E)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle g^{\ast}(\widetilde{V}_{+}\cap{\rm H}^{s}(\partial M;E))\subset\widetilde{V}_{-}\cap{\rm H}^{s}(\partial M;E).

for all
s\geq\frac{1}{2}
, where
g^{*}\colon{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)\to{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)
is the adjoint map of
g
and
\widetilde{V}_{\pm}=(V_{\mp}\oplus W_{+}\oplus W_{-})^{\perp,{\rm L}^{2}}
.

From
g|_{V_{+}\oplus W_{+}\oplus W_{-}}=0
it follows that
g^{*}(\widetilde{V}_{+})\subset\widetilde{V}_{-}
.
If the decomposition
{\rm L}^{2}(\partial M;E)=V_{-}\oplus W_{-}\oplus V_{+}\oplus W_{+}
is orthogonal, then
\widetilde{W}_{\pm}=W_{\pm}
and
\widetilde{V}_{\pm}=V_{\pm}
.

Remark 4
.

If
B\subset{\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
is an
\infty
-elliptic boundary condition for
D
, then
D_{B}\colon\mathrm{dom}(D_{B})\to{\rm L}^{2}(M;F)
is a closed operator and
\mathrm{dom}\big(\big(D_{B}\big)^{*}\big)\subset{\rm H}^{1}_{\rm loc}(M;F)
.

Remark 5
.

In
[
10
]
a weaker notion of elliptic boundary condition was introduced.
Our notion of
\infty
-elliptic boundary condition is equivalent to that of “
\infty
-regular elliptic boundary condition” in
[
10
]
*Definition 2.11.

Remark 6
.

Ellipticity of a boundary condition
B\subset{\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
depends on
D
but is independent of the choice of adapted boundary operator
A
as one can see from Corollary
2
.

Example 7
.

If we put
W_{+}=W_{-}=0
and
g=0
, then
B=V_{-}=\chi^{-}(A){\rm H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial M;E)
is an
\infty
-elliptic boundary condition.
Then
B=:B_{\mathrm{APS}}(A)
is called the
Atiyah-Patodi-Singer boundary condition
.

Elliptic boundary conditions enjoy the best possible regularity properties as outlined in the following theorem.

Theorem 5

(
[
10
]
*Thm. 2.12)
.

Assume
(S1)
–
(S6)
.
Let
A
be an adapted boundary operator for

be an

Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
