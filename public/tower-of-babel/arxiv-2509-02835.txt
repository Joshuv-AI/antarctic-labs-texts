Graham conjectured (see, for example,
[
3
]
) that there are infinitely many integers
n
such that
\binom{2n}{n}
is coprime to
105=3\cdot 5\cdot 7
. By Kummer’s criterion, a prime
p
does not divide
\binom{2n}{n}
if and only if all of the base
p
digits of
n
are
<p/2
. Graham’s conjecture is therefore equivalent to the question of whether there are infinitely many integers such that in base
3
all digits are in
\{0,1\}
, in base
5
all digits are in
\{0,1,2\}
, and in base
7
all digits are in
\{0,1,2,3\}
. For example,

756=(1001000)_{3}=(11011)_{5}=(2130)_{7}.

This is a special case of a more general phenomenon, where we expect infinitely many
n
all of whose digits are small in multiple bases simultaneously, provided such bases are sufficiently large. To get an idea how large is necessary, we have the following heuristic of Pomerance
[
11
]
: note first that, if
N
is a power of
g
, then the number of
0\leqslant n<N
all of whose base
g
digits are
<g/2
is exactly
N^{\log_{g}(\lceil g/2\rceil)}
. Heuristically, therefore, the chance that
n
has all of its base
g
digits
<g/2
is
\approx n^{\log_{g}(\lceil g/2\rceil)-1}
. Assuming these events are independent for distinct bases
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
, we expect that the chance that
n\in[N,2N)
has all base
g_{j}
digits
\leqslant g_{j}/2
(for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
) is
\approx N^{\sum_{j}\log_{g_{j}}(\lceil g_{j}/2\rceil)-r}
. If this is
>N^{-1}
then it follows that there should exist at least one such
n\in[N,2N)
for all sufficiently large
N
. Therefore, provided the bases
g_{j}
are ‘independent’ and

\sum_{j}\log_{g_{j}}(\lceil g_{j}/2\rceil)-r=-\sum_{j}\log_{g_{j}}\left(\frac{g_{j}}{\lceil g_{j}/2\rceil}\right)>-1,

there should exist infinitely many
n
all of whose digits base
g_{j}
are
<g_{j}/2
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
simultaneously.

One obvious way that the digits between different bases
g_{j}
may not be independent is when one base is a power of another base. To rule out such dependencies, it is natural to insist that
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
(notice for example that this is certainly satisfied if the bases are pairwise coprime). Assuming this condition, and generalising the heuristic of Pomerance above, we are led to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
r\geqslant 1
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\geqslant 2
be integers such that
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
, with associated
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}\in(0,1]
such that

(1)

\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\log_{g_{j}}\left(\frac{g_{j}}{\lceil\kappa_{j}g_{j}\rceil}\right)<1.

There are infinitely many
n
such that, for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, all base
g_{j}
digits of
n
are
<\kappa_{j}g_{j}
.

For example, Graham’s conjecture is the case when
g_{1}=3
,
g_{2}=5
,
g_{3}=7
, and
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=1/2
, and indeed we calculate

\log_{3}(3/2)+\log_{5}(5/3)+\log_{7}(7/4)=0.974\cdots<1.

The main result of this paper establishes a weak version of this conjecture, weaker in two ways: we have a stricter condition (although of a similar shape) on how large the bases need to be, and (more significantly) we only obtain almost all of the digits being small.

Theorem 1
.

Let
r\geqslant 1
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\geqslant 2
be integers such that
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
, with associated
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}\in(0,1]
such that

(2)

\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\log_{g_{j}}(320r^{5}/\kappa_{j})<\frac{1}{2r}.

(Note in particular that this holds for all
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
sufficiently large in terms of
r,\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}
).

For any
\epsilon>0
there are infinitely many
n
such that, for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, all but at most
\epsilon\log n
of the base
g_{j}
digits of
n
are
<\kappa_{j}g_{j}
.

Indeed, the proof actually shows that such an
n
exists in any interval of the shape

[N,\exp(O(\epsilon^{-1-o(1)}))N]

assuming
N
is sufficiently large depending on
\epsilon,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r},\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}
(where the implicit constant depends on
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r},\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}
only). We stress, however, that Theorem
1
is ineffective, in the sense that, given
\epsilon,g_{1},...,g_{r},\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{r}
, it does not quantify how large the smallest
n
must be; by the above we can say that there exist

\gg\epsilon^{1+o(1)}\log N

many such integers
n\leqslant N
when
N
is sufficiently large (depending on
\epsilon
), but Theorem
1
gives no control on how large this needs to be.

Put another way, one could ask for a more quantitative result that allows for
\epsilon=\epsilon(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. A more technically complicated version of the argument in this paper should allow

\epsilon=\epsilon(n)\ll(\log n)^{-c}

for some constant
c>0
(depending on the
g_{i}
and
\kappa_{i}
). The limit of the method in this paper, even assuming a strong quantitative form of Schanuel’s conjecture, appears to be
\epsilon=(\log n)^{-1/2+o(1)}
. To avoid complicating an already technically involved proof, in this paper we will focus on establishing the qualitative statement of Theorem
1
, and defer further discussion about quantitative issues to Section
6
.

Kummer’s theorem implies that, for any prime
p
, if
p^{k}
is the highest power of
p
that divides
\binom{2n}{n}
then
k
is the number of base
p
digits of
n
that are
\geqslant p/2
. A trivial consequence is that, for any fixed primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
, for any integer
n
we can write
\binom{2n}{n}=n_{1}n_{2}
where
(n_{1},p_{1}\cdots p_{r})=1
and
n_{2}\leqslant n^{O(1)}
.

Theorem
1
immediately implies the following improvement, which can be viewed as a weak version of Graham’s conjecture.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
r\geqslant 3
and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
are primes, all sufficiently large depending on
r
only. For any
\epsilon>0
there are infinitely many
n
such that

\binom{2n}{n}=n_{1}n_{2}

where
(n_{1},p_{1}\cdots p_{r})=1
and
n_{2}\leqslant n^{\epsilon}
.

The quantitative improvement to Theorem
1
mentioned earlier would allow us to improve this to

n_{2}\leqslant\exp((\log n)^{1-c})

for some constant
c>0
.

On constants

The particular shape of the condition (
2
) should not be taken too seriously; while we have tried to produce an explicit bound, we have also compromised a little in the strength of this bound to make the proof easier to follow. To get an idea of what this threshold means in concrete terms, one can calculate that, when
r=3
and
\kappa_{j}=1/2
, the condition (
2
) is satisfied provided
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\geqslant 10^{94}
. In particular, Corollary
1
holds for any three distinct prime bases
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\geqslant 10^{94}
.

If one more carefully optimised the constants in this argument (at the cost of making the proofs and statements cosmetically messier) then a back of the envelope calculation suggests this threshold could be brought down to perhaps an order of magnitude closer to
\approx 10^{15}
, but achieving a more reasonable threshold (say
<100
) probably requires some new ideas.

Previous work

A weaker form of Theorem
1
was obtained in earlier work of the second author, Mousavi, and Schmidt
[
2
]
, who proved that, for any
\epsilon>0
and
r\geqslant 1
, if
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
are primes sufficiently large depending on both
\epsilon
and
r
, then there are infinitely many
n
such that all except
\epsilon\log n
of the base
p_{j}
digits of
n
are
<p_{j}/2
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
. In particular, note that
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
must be taken sufficiently large depending on
\epsilon
, not just
r
.

The argument of this paper is a strengthening of that of
[
2
]
, with the aim of removing this dependence on
\epsilon
. Many component lemmas of
[
2
]
have been improved both quantitatively and qualitatively; another significant change is that we avoid discretising and rather than work over
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
perform our Fourier analysis over
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Finally, instead of appealing to Parseval’s identity as in
[
2
]
, we introduce a new estimate, Lemma
6
, for the number of large Fourier coefficients of sets with small digits.

The case
r=2
was earlier studied by Erdős, Graham, Ruzsa, and Straus
[
4
]
, who proved a much stronger result.

Theorem 2

(Erdős, Graham, Ruzsa, and Straus
[
4
]
)
.

If
g_{1},g_{2}\geqslant 2
are integers and
1/g_{1}\leqslant\kappa_{1}\leqslant 1
and
1/g_{2}\leqslant\kappa_{2}\leqslant 1
are such that

(3)

\frac{\lceil\kappa_{1}g_{1}\rceil-1}{g_{1}-1}+\frac{\lceil\kappa_{2}g_{2}\rceil-1}{g_{2}-1}\geqslant 1

then there are infinitely many
n
whose base
g_{i}
digits are
<\kappa_{i}g_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

In particular, for any distinct primes
p_{1},p_{2}
there exist infinitely many
n
such that
\binom{2n}{n}
is coprime to
p_{1}p_{2}
, resolving the
r=2
analogue of Graham’s conjecture. Theorem
2
is almost the final word on the case
r=2
, except that the assumption (
3
) is stronger than the conjectured sufficient condition (
1
), which here is equivalent to

\frac{\log(\lceil\kappa_{1}g_{1}\rceil)}{\log g_{1}}+\frac{\log(\lceil\kappa_{2}g_{2}\rceil)}{\log g_{2}}>1.

(The condition (
3
) is indeed stronger since
\frac{\log x}{\log g}>\frac{x-1}{g-1}
for
1<x<g
.) It would be of interest even for
r=2
to prove that condition (
1
) is sufficient.

As a concrete challenge, can one prove the existence of infinitely many
n
whose digits are all
\in\{0,1\}
in both bases
3
and
5
simultaneously? Conjecture
1
predicts that this is true, since
\log_{3}(3/2)+\log_{5}(5/2)=0.938\cdots
, but this is not covered by Theorem
2
, since
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}=0.75<1
.

Structure of the paper

In Section
1
we will give an overview of the proof of Theorem
1
, in particular motivating the form of the main technical proposition which will be used in the semi-constructive proof. In Section
2
we will state this proposition precisely as Proposition
1
, and formally deduce Theorem
1
.

The proof of Proposition
1
will occupy the rest of the paper. In Section
3
we prove it using Fourier analysis, assuming further lemmas on the separation of power sums (proved in Section
4
) and the behaviour of large exponential sums over integers with small digits (proved in Section
5
).

The reader generally interested in additive problems concerning integers with small digits may like to jump immediately to Section
5
and peruse the lemma and proof therein, which we expect will be of wider use.

Finally, in Section
6
we will sketch how to prove a quantitative form of Theorem
1
, and discuss some connections with Schanuel’s conjecture.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
we write
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\in(-1/2,1/2]
for the distance from
x
to the nearest integer. We also write
\lfloor x\rfloor
for the greatest integer
n\leqslant x
and
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
for the fractional part of
x
.

We use the vector notation
\vec{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
(the dimension

\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and

We identify
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

\psi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})

1.
Sketch of the proof

We will prove Theorem
1
in a semi-constructive way. The idea is to begin with some candidate number and successively alter blocks of digits in each base to attempt to make the digits smaller, while avoiding affecting too many of the digits in other bases, or the digits in higher blocks in that same base.

This is an extension of the method used by Erdős, Graham, Ruzsa, and Straus
[
4
]
in the case
r=2
(see Theorem
2
) and of Croot, Mousavi, and Schmidt
[
2
]
.

To illustrate the method of Erdős, Graham, Ruzsa, and Straus when
r=2
, we will work with the bases
g_{1}=3
and
g_{2}=5
, and choose
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=1/2
. In other words, we seek integers
n
such that each of the base
3
digits are
0
or
1
and each of the base
5
digits are
0,1,
or
2
.

As mentioned above, we adopt a constructive approach, starting with a number like
3^{12}=(1000000000000)_{3}
, which has the form we seek in base
3
. Unfortunately, in base
5
this number is
(114001231)_{5}
(that is,

3^{12}=5^{8}+5^{7}+4\cdot 5^{6}+5^{3}+2\cdot 5^{2}+3\cdot 5+1
). Working from most to least significant digits, the first problematic digit here is the
4
(contributing the
4\cdot 5^{6}
). By adding a lower power of
3
to our initial
3^{12}
, we can make this digit smaller, at the expense of making the digit
1
corresponding to the
5^{7}
into a
2
. Specifically, since
3^{9}=(1112213)_{5}
, adding
3^{9}
to produce
3^{12}+3^{9}
we get

\displaystyle(1001000000000)_{3}

\displaystyle=(114001231)_{5}+(001112213)_{5}

\displaystyle=(120113444)_{5}.

Continuing in this manner, adding smaller and smaller powers of
3
to clear out large digits in the base
5
expansions of those numbers, we arrive at the number
(1001000101110)_{3}
, which has base
5
expansion
(120121111)_{5}
. It is not immediately obvious, of course, that such a procedure is always possible, i.e. that one can remove a large base
5
digit without creating large base
3
digits. That this is always possible is proved using elementary number theory in
[
4
]
(and makes essential use of the condition (
3
)).

When
r=2
(and condition (
3
) is satisfied) this constructive method always works. When dealing with three or more bases simultaneously, however, problems quickly arise: when fixing a problematic large digit in one base there are now at least two other bases to worry about, and the elementary counting argument of
[
4
]
no longer suffices to ensure that all the bases can be controlled simultaneously.

In the present work, instead of starting with some
g_{i}^{N}
, which has base
g_{i}
expansion
(1000\cdots 0)_{g_{i}}
, and then adding progressively lower and lower order (than
N
) base
g_{i}
digits so as to make the higher-order digits in the other bases small, we work in some large ‘universal’ base
L=\ell^{h}
, for some
h\geqslant 1
, where gcd
(\ell,g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{r})=1
. (There is no other restriction on
\ell
, and in particular one can simply take
\ell=2
when all
g_{i}
are odd.)

We will work relative to this large base
L
, and choose each new base
L
digit that we add to force long blocks of digits in the bases
g_{1},...,g_{r}
to be small simultaneously. A precise description of what is required is given in Proposition
1
: when choosing the base
L
digit corresponding to
L^{k}
we can ensure that all the digits
a_{i}
in a base
g_{j}
can be made to satisfy
a_{i}<\kappa_{j}g_{j}
so long as
g_{j}^{i}\in[CL^{k},C^{-1}L^{k+1}]
. The parameter
C
here will be ‘small’ compared to
L
, so that we can get almost all of the base
g_{j}
digits corresponding to a single base
L
digit to be
<\kappa_{j}g_{j}
. A significant complication arises from the fact that at the point we need to choose any base
L
digit all higher base
L
digits have already been fixed. This means that when choosing the current base
L
digit, with the goal of fixing the corresponding block of base
g_{j}
digits to be small, we are not starting from all zeros in base
g_{j}
but from some unknown ‘noise’, the result of the choices made for higher
L
digits.

This unknown noise is represented by the parameter
\beta
in Proposition
1
, and it is vital that this proposition should hold uniformly for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Roughly speaking then, Proposition
1
, the main technical result of this paper, states that (assuming various conditions), for almost all
n
, given any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we can shift
\beta
by some quantity
O(HL^{k})
(where
H
is some parameter slightly larger than
L
) such that almost all of the corresponding block of base
g_{j}
digits in
[CL^{k},C^{-1}L^{k+1}]
are small, for all bases
g_{j}
simultaneously. (Importantly, since we are only altering
\beta
by something
O(HL^{k})
almost all of the higher digits of
\beta
in every base
g_{j}
are unaffected, and hence once they are small they remain small throughout the process.)

It is worth mentioning where the exceptional set of at most
\varepsilon\log n
digits in Theorem
1
in each of the bases comes from. There are two sources, both coming from Proposition
1
. The first source is the fact that we have the interval
[CL^{k},C^{-1}L^{k+1}]
instead of an interval
[L^{k},L^{k}H]
, say, where that extra ‘padding’ factor
C
means we lose control over the digits with
g_{i}^{j}\in[L^{k},CL^{k})
and
(C^{-1}L^{k+1},L^{k}H]
– the number of digits we cannot control in this way is

\ll\frac{\log(CH/L)}{\log L}\log n.

The other main source of potential bad digits comes from the fact that we cannot ensure a suitable modification is possible for all base
L
digits, but only almost all. The bound on the number of possible bad
L
digits, where we cannot control anything, given in Proposition
1
is

\ll(\log L)^{O(1)}(L/H)^{1/r}\log n,

each such bad block contributing
O(\log L)
many bad base
g_{i}
digits.

The choices of
L
and
H
are therefore a little delicate; to ensure that the number of bad digits is at most
\epsilon\log n
in total we choose
H=L^{1+O(\epsilon)}
and
L=\epsilon^{-O(\epsilon^{-1}r)}
.

Finally, we conclude by discussing the proof of Proposition
1
. After dilating the scale under consideration so that we are working in
[0,1]
, and writing
\vec{x}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
for the rescaled
L^{k}
(with the direction
i
corresponding to the behaviour in base
g_{i}
) what we need to know is essentially whether a fixed
\vec{\beta}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
can be shifted by some constant multiple of
\vec{x}
to lie in
A+I
, where
A\subseteq[0,1]^{r}
is a finite subset, with the component in the
i
direction corresponding to those numbers with small digits in base
g_{i}
, and
I
is some small box around the origin.

This is proved by an application of Fourier analysis in
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
– the point is that the required statement is ‘generically true’, and the only exceptional
\vec{x}
must have some unusual correlations with large Fourier coefficients of the set
A
. By bounding the number of such exceptions we can control the number of potential ‘bad’
\vec{x}
, and hence the number of potential bad base
L
digits, and complete the proof of Proposition
1
.

Bounding the number of such exceptional
\vec{x}
requires a number of auxiliary lemmas, some of which have variants that appeared in
[
2
]
(although the versions in the present work are much more flexible and general).

The main new ingredient that allows us to get stronger bounds than this previous work is Lemma
6
, which gives an upper bound on the number of places where a certain exponential sum over integers with small base
g
digits is larger in magnitude than a given fraction

\eta
of its maximum possible value (where this maximum value is
|A|
) – that is, we have an exponential sum over numbers of the form
\sum_{0\leqslant i<R}c_{i}g^{i}
, where
0\leqslant c_{i}<t
. (For comparison the analysis in
[
2
]
bounded the number of such large exponential sums by an application of Parseval’s identity, and did not exploit the specific structure of the set
A
.) These bounds are proved using the fact that this exponential sum can be written as a Riesz product over geometric progressions. Analyzing the integers
k
where this product can be large in terms of the base
g
digits of that
k
, it is found that all but a small number of these digits must lie inside some small set. Bounding the number of such patterns of base
g
digits of these numbers
k
then gives the lemma.

Establishing control on exponential sums over sets of integers with restricted digits has also played a significant role in a number of other problems; we mention in particular the work of Fouvry and Mauduit
[
6
]
on the additive behaviour of sets of integers omitting specified digits, and the work of Maynard
[
10
,
7
]
on the existence of primes omitting specified digits. To the best of our knowledge, however, a result like Lemma
6
which controls the exponential sum over the set of integers with only
small
digits has not before appeared in the literature.

2.
Main technical result

We will now state our main technical result, and show how it implies Theorem
1
. The proof of Proposition
1
will take up the remainder of the paper.

Proposition 1
.

Let
r\geqslant 1
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\geqslant 2
be integers such that
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
, with associated
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}\in(0,1]
such that

\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\log_{g_{j}}\left(\frac{10g_{j}}{\lceil\tfrac{\kappa_{j}}{32r^{5}}g_{j}\rceil}\right)<\frac{1}{2r}.

Let
\ell\geqslant 2
satisfy
(\ell,g_{1}\cdots g_{k})=1
. There exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
\ell,g_{j},\kappa_{j}
) such that the following holds.

Let
H\geqslant L\geqslant C
, and suppose that
L
is a power of
\ell
. If
N
is sufficiently large (depending on
\ell,g_{j},\kappa_{j},H,L
) then, for all except at most
(\log L)^{C}(L/H)^{1/r}N
many
n\leqslant N
, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists an integer
s_{n,\beta}\in[1,H]
such that, for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, if

s_{n,\beta}L^{n}+\beta=\sum_{k}a_{k}g_{j}^{k}\quad\textrm{ with }\quad 0\leqslant a_{k}<g_{j}

then

0\leqslant a_{k}<\kappa_{j}g_{j}\quad\textrm{ for }\quad g_{j}^{k}\in[CL^{n},\tfrac{1}{C}L^{n+1}].

This statement is quite technical and difficult to parse. The point of the conclusion is that, for almost all ‘blocks’ of digits of length
\approx\log L
, any
\beta
can be shifted by some integer of scale
O(HL^{n})
to ensure that all of its base
g_{j}
digits in that block (except the fringes) are small, simultaneously for all
j
. Furthermore since we are shifting by an integer of size
O(HL^{n})
very few of the higher digits will be affected.

We can then work our way downwards along the blocks, beginning with the highest digits, repeatedly shifting to ensure that all of the digits except those in the fringes of each block (and perhaps all digits in any of the exceptional blocks) are small in all bases simultaneously.

Note that we need to take

smaller than

by at least

or else the conclusion is trivial. To get an idea about the scale of parameters, for our application we will choose

to be minimal such that

, and then choose

to be some large constant depending only on

and
L\approx H^{1-O(\epsilon)}
. With this choice of parameters the number of exceptional blocks

is
\ll H^{-O(\epsilon/r)}N\leqslant\epsilon N
for suitable choice of
H
.

The proof of Proposition
1
will take the majority of the paper. In this section we will show how it implies Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem

.

Choose

minimal so that

It suffices to construct some integer

Our final

3.
An application of Fourier analysis

Proposition
1
will be proved by the circle method – that is, using Fourier analysis and a careful study of where the relevant Fourier transforms can be large. In particular, we will use Fourier analysis to prove the following technical result and then explain how it implies Proposition

Proposition 2
.

Let
I_{1},\ldots,I_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}
respectively, and write
I=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{r}
and
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\delta_{i}

be a finite set such that
\vec{a}-\vec{b}\not\in\prod_{i}[-\delta_{i}/2,\delta_{i}/2]
for all
\vec{a}\neq\vec{b}\in A

\alpha=\delta\left\lvert A\right\rvert

be an integer.

For any
H\geqslant 1
and
\vec{x}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
, either

(1)

for any
\vec{\beta}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
there exists
1\leqslant s\leqslant H
such that
1
1

1

Here
hA
denotes the
h

A
, that is, all

of the shape
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}
for some

.

s\vec{x}+\vec{\beta}\in hA+I
, or

(2)

there exists some
\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\backslash\{0\}
such that

(a)

\lvert k_{i}\rvert\ll_{r}(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}\delta_{i}^{-1}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
,

(b)

\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})\rvert\gg_{r}\lVert\vec{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{-\frac{r+1}{h}}\left\lvert A\right\rvert,

and

(c)

\lVert\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}\rVert\ll_{r}\log(1/\alpha)H^{-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell\geqslant 1
be determined later, and let
H^{\prime}=\lfloor H/\ell\rfloor
. We fix
\vec{x}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
and suppose that the first alternative fails, so that there exists some
\vec{\beta}\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{r}
such that there is no
1\leqslant s\leqslant H
such that

\vec{\beta}
if necessary) we can assume

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
. Let
J\geqslant 1
be some integer parameter to be chosen later, and let

be a function supported on

such that

(1)

\widehat{\psi_{i}}(0)=\|\psi_{i}\|_{1}=1
,

(2)

0\leqslant\widehat{\psi_{i}}(k)\leqslant\min(1,(J^{2}/\delta_{i}k)^{2J})
for all

, and

(3)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\psi_{i}}(k)=\psi_{i}(0)\leqslant 4/\delta_{i}
.

(The existence of such a

is a standard construction in Fourier analysis; details are given in Appendix
A

\psi(\vec{x})=\psi_{1}(x_{1})\cdots\psi_{r}(x_{r})
. The basic theory of the Fourier transform implies that
\psi_{i}(x_{i})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\psi_{i}}(k)e(kx_{i})
and hence

\psi(\vec{x})=\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\widehat{\psi_{1}}(k_{1})\cdots\widehat{\psi_{r}}(k_{r})e(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x})=\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})e(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}),

say. Since the first alternative fails, and
\psi
is supported on

\psi(s\vec{x}+\vec{\beta}-\vec{a_{1}}-\cdots-\vec{a_{h}})=0
for all

and

0=\sum_{\vec{a_{1}},\ldots,\vec{a_{h}}\in A}\sum_{1\leqslant s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}\leqslant H^{\prime}}\psi((s_{1}+\cdots+s_{\ell})\vec{x}+\vec{\beta}-\vec{a_{1}}-\cdots-\vec{a_{h}}).

Inserting the above Fourier series for
\psi

\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})=\sum_{\vec{a}\in A}e(-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a})
,

0=\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})^{h}\sum_{1\leqslant s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}\leqslant H^{\prime}}e(\vec{k}\cdot((s_{1}+\cdots+s_{\ell})\vec{x}+\vec{\beta})).

The term
\vec{k}=(0,\ldots,0)

\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}(H^{\prime})^{\ell}

\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}(H^{\prime})^{\ell}\leqslant\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\backslash 0}\lvert\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\rvert\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})\rvert^{h}\lvert f(\vec{k})\rvert^{\ell}

and fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\backslash 0\\
\lvert k_{i}\rvert\geqslant M\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\rvert\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})\rvert^{h}\lvert f(\vec{k})\rvert^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}(H^{\prime})^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\backslash 0\\
\lvert k_{i}\rvert\geqslant M\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}4^{r}\delta_{i}\delta^{-1}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}(H^{\prime})^{\ell}\sum_{k\geqslant M}(J^{2}/\delta_{i}k)^{2J}.

The sum here is, using the bound
\sum_{k\geqslant M}k^{-2J}\leqslant 2J(2/M)^{2J-1}

If we take

then the total contribution from
\vec{k}

for some

is at most

.

Furthermore, since
\vec{a}-\vec{b}\not\in I

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\lvert\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\rvert\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})\rvert^{h}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h-2}\sum_{\vec{a},\vec{b}\in A}\sum_{\vec{k}}\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})e(\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{a}-\vec{b}))

\displaystyle=\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h-2}\sum_{\vec{a},\vec{b}\in A}\psi(\vec{a}-\vec{b})

\displaystyle\leqslant 4^{r}\delta^{-1}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h-1}=4^{r}\alpha^{-1}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h},

and hence since
\lvert f(\vec{k})\rvert\leqslant\min(H^{\prime},1/\lVert\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}\rVert)
, the contribution from those

such that

\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}(H^{\prime})^{\ell}

In particular, if

, say, then we can discard the contribution from those
\vec{k}
such that
\|\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}\|\geqslant 4/H^{\prime}=4/\lfloor H/\ell\rfloor
.

Therefore, if
\Gamma^{\prime}

\vec{k}\neq 0

\tfrac{1}{4}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{h}\leqslant\sum_{\vec{k}\in\Gamma}\lvert\widehat{\psi}(\vec{k})\rvert\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\vec{k})\rvert^{h}.

Finally, for any

and

with

Separation

In this section we study the following problem: given some

n\leqslant N
such that

\|\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{1}}\}}+\cdots\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{r}}\}}\|\leqslant\epsilon?

Suppose that we knew
1,\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{r}}
to be linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. An application of the classical Weyl’s theorem then implies that the vectors

\left(\left\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{1}}\right\},\ldots,\left\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{r}}\right\}\right)

are uniformly distributed in
[0,1)^{r}
as
n\to\infty
. In particular it suffices to bound, for some fixed parameter
\delta
, the number of
\delta
-separated
\vec{t}\in[0,1)^{r}
such that

\|\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{t_{1}}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{t_{r}}\|\leqslant\epsilon.

By counting along 1-dimensional ‘slices’ this in turn can be reduced to the problem of bounding the number of
t\in[0,1)
such that

\|\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{t}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{t}\|\leqslant\epsilon.

(Note the coefficients
\zeta_{i}
may be differ as we vary over the 1-dimensional slices, and hence some reasonable uniformity in the dependence on
\zeta_{i}
is required.)
Finally, we may give an upper bound on the number of such
t
by showing that in any large
\delta
-separated sequence of
t
there must be some value where
\sum\zeta_{j}\theta_{j}^{t}
is large, which in turn is proved by an elementary argument using induction on
r
and the mean value theorem.

There is, however, a complication in that it is not know how to prove that the
\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{j}}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
(although this is an immediate consequence of Schanuel’s conjecture). Surprisingly, however, this does not cause significant difficulties: we can simply take any possible linear dependencies into account, and apply the above argument to a maximal linearly independent subset of the
\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{j}}
, and apply Weyl’s theorem in a suitable lower-dimensional space.

We will now put this plan into action. The first step is the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}>0
be distinct real numbers with associated
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
. If
f(t)=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant r}c_{i}x_{i}^{t}
then for any
0<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{2^{r-1}}<1

\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{r-1}}\left\lvert f(v_{j})\right\rvert\gg_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}\Delta^{r-1}\max_{j}\left\lvert c_{j}\right\rvert,

where
\Delta=\min\left\lvert v_{k+1}-v_{k}\right\rvert
.

Proof.

We use induction on
r
. The case
r=1
is trivial. In general, suppose that
r\geqslant 2
and the statement holds for
r-1
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
\left\lvert c_{1}\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert c_{i}\right\rvert
for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant r
. Consider

g(t)=x_{1}^{-t}f(t)=c_{1}+\sum_{2\leqslant k\leqslant r}c_{k}(x_{k}/x_{1})^{t}.

Note that since
\left\lvert f(t)\right\rvert\geqslant\min(1,x_{1})\left\lvert g(t)\right\rvert
for all
t\in[0,1]
it suffices to prove

\max_{0\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{r-1}}\left\lvert g(v_{j})\right\rvert\gg_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}\Delta^{r-1}\max_{2\leqslant j\leqslant r}\left\lvert c_{j}\right\rvert.

By the mean value theorem there is some
w_{j}\in[v_{2j-1},v_{2j}]
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{r-2}
such that

\max(\left\lvert g(v_{2j})\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(v_{2j-1})\right\rvert)\geqslant\left\lvert g(v_{2j})-g(v_{2j-1})\right\rvert\geqslant\Delta\lvert g^{\prime}(w_{j})\rvert.

Note that
\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{r-2}}\lvert w_{j+1}-w_{j}\rvert\geqslant\Delta
so we are done by the inductive hypothesis applied to

g^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{2\leqslant j\leqslant r}(\log x_{j}/x_{1})c_{j}(x_{j}/x_{1})^{t}.

∎

We may now solve our analytic problem in the simplest one-dimensional case.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}>0
be distinct real numbers (all
\neq 1
) and
\delta>0
. Suppose that
\left\lvert\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{t}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{t}\right\rvert\leqslant Z
for all
t\in[0,1)
.

The number of
\delta
-separated
t\in[0,1)
such that

\left\lVert\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{t}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{t}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon

is

O_{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},r}(Z\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{1/r}(\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r}).

Proof.

It suffices to show that, for any integer
n
, the number of
\delta
-separated
t\in[0,1)
such that

\left\lvert\zeta_{1}\theta_{1}^{t}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}\theta_{r}^{t}-n\right\rvert\leqslant\epsilon

is
O(\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{1/r}(\max\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r})
.

Let the set of such
t\in[0,1)
be denoted by
T
. We can assume that
\left\lvert T\right\rvert\geqslant 2^{r+2}
, say, or else we are done. It follows that there are
2^{r}
many disjoint closed intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{2^{r}}\subseteq[0,1)
such that
T\subseteq\cup_{i}I_{i}
and
\left\lvert T\cap I_{i}\right\rvert\geqslant\lfloor\left\lvert T\right\rvert/2^{r}\rfloor
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2^{r}
. Let
f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\zeta_{j}\theta_{j}^{t}-n
and for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2^{r}
let
v_{i}
be a point in
I_{i}
where
f^{\prime}(t)
is minimised. Note that, since all points in
T
are
\delta
-separated, we have, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2^{r-1}
,

\left\lvert v_{2i+1}-v_{2i-1}\right\rvert\geqslant\delta(\left\lvert T\cap I_{2i}\right\rvert-1)\geqslant\frac{\delta}{2^{r+2}}\left\lvert T\right\rvert.

By Lemma
1
, therefore, there exists some
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2^{r-1}
such that

\min_{x\in I_{2i-1}}\left\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\right\rvert\geqslant\left\lvert f^{\prime}(v_{2i-1})\right\rvert\gg_{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},r}\left\lvert T\right\rvert^{r-1}\delta^{r-1}\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert.

We now use the general fact (which follows e.g. from the mean value theorem) that if
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
has
\left\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\right\rvert\geqslant\lambda>0
for all
x\in I
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in I
are
\delta
-separated points such that
\left\lvert f(t_{i})\right\rvert\leqslant\epsilon
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
then
\lambda\delta k\leqslant\epsilon
to deduce that

\left\lvert T\right\rvert^{r}\delta^{r}\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert\ll\epsilon,

and the bound follows after rearranging this.
∎

We will now deduce a more general form of this lemma, where the exponents are given by independent linear forms in some
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in[0,1)
. We need to be able to handle arbitrary linear forms in the exponents to handle the presence of possible linear dependencies between our
\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{j}}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
1\leqslant d\leqslant r
. Let
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}>1
be such that
\theta_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq\theta_{j}^{a_{j}}
for
i\neq j
and any
a_{i},a_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}\backslash\{0\}
. Let
Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}
be non-zero rational linear forms in
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}
. Suppose that

\left\lvert\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\zeta_{i}\theta_{i}^{t_{i}}+\sum_{d<j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}\theta_{j}^{Q_{j}(\vec{t})}\right\rvert\leqslant Z

for all
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in[0,1)
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta>0
. The number of
\delta
-separated points
\vec{t}\in[0,1)^{d}
such that

\left\lVert\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\zeta_{i}\theta_{i}^{t_{i}}+\sum_{d<j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}\theta_{j}^{Q_{j}(\vec{t})}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon

is

O_{r,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}}(Z\delta^{-d}(\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r}(\epsilon+\delta\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{1/r}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\delta=1/D
for some integer
D\geqslant 1
and (since changing a
t_{i}
by
\delta
can affect the size of the sum by at most
O_{r,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}}(\delta\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)
) it suffices to show that, for any
\epsilon>0
, the number of
\vec{t}\in\{0,\ldots,D-1\}^{d}
such that

(4)

\left\lVert\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\zeta_{i}(\theta_{i}^{1/D})^{t_{i}}+\sum_{d<j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}(\theta_{j}^{1/D})^{Q_{j}(\vec{t})}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon

is

O_{r,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}}(ZD^{d}\epsilon^{1/r}(\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r}).

Let
2\leqslant M\ll 1
be some integer to be chosen later. Any
\vec{t}\in\{0,\ldots,D-1\}^{d}
can be written as

(t_{1},Mt_{1}+u_{2},\ldots,M^{d-1}t_{1}+u_{d})

for some
u_{2},\ldots,u_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-M^{d-1}D,D)
.

It follows that exists some
U\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that

(1)

\left\lvert U\right\rvert\ll D^{d-1}
,

(2)

\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{\infty}\ll D
for all
\vec{u}\in U
, and

(3)

if
\vec{t}\in\{0,\ldots,D-1\}^{d}
then there exists some
\vec{u}\in U
such that

\vec{t}=(t_{1}+u_{1},Mt_{1}+u_{2},\ldots,M^{d-1}t_{1}+u_{d}).

It therefore suffices to show that, for any fixed
\vec{u}\in U
, the number of
t\in\{0,\ldots,D-1\}
such that

\left\lVert\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\zeta_{i}\theta_{i}^{u_{i}/D}(\theta_{i}^{M^{i-1}})^{t/D}+\sum_{d<j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}\theta_{j}^{Q_{j}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})/D}(\theta_{j}^{Q_{j}(1,M,\ldots,M^{d-1})})^{t/D}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon

is

O_{r,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}}(ZD\epsilon^{1/r}(\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r}).

This follows by an application of Lemma
2
, after choosing
M\ll 1
such that all the numbers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}^{M^{d-1}},\ldots,\theta_{r}^{Q_{r}(1,\ldots,M^{d-1})}
are all distinct and
\neq 1
, and noting that

\max(\lvert\zeta_{1}\rvert\theta_{1}^{u_{1}/D},\ldots,\lvert\zeta_{r}\rvert\theta_{r}^{Q_{r}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})/D})\gg_{r,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},Q_{d+1},\ldots,Q_{r}}\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert.

∎

We can now prove the goal of this section, for which we will require the following form of Weyl’s theorem.

Lemma 4

(Multidimensional Weyl’s theorem)
.

If
1,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
then the sequence

\left(\{n\theta_{1}\},\ldots,\{n\theta_{d}\}\right)

is uniformly distributed in
[0,1)^{d}
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\geqslant 2
be such that there are no solutions to
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
. Let
\ell\geqslant 2
satisfy
(\ell,g_{1}\cdots g_{r})=1
and let
L\geqslant\ell
be a power of
\ell
. Let
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
N
is sufficiently large the number of
n\in[N]
such that

\left\lVert\zeta_{1}g_{1}^{\{n/\log_{L}g_{1}\}}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}g_{r}^{\{n/\log_{L}g_{r}\}}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon

is

\ll_{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}}(1+\max\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)(\max\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{-1/r}\epsilon^{1/r}N

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{Q}(1/\log_{\ell}g_{1},\ldots,1/\log_{\ell}g_{r})
has dimension
1\leqslant d\leqslant r+1
, as a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that we have some
b_{ji}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\geqslant 1
such that, for
d\leqslant j\leqslant r
,

(5)

\frac{q}{\log_{\ell}g_{j}}=b_{j0}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\frac{b_{ji}}{\log_{\ell}g_{i}},

and that
1,1/\log_{\ell}g_{1},\ldots,1/\log_{\ell}g_{d-1}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. Note in particular that
d\geqslant 2
, or else
\log_{L}g_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
, contradicting
(L,g_{1})=1
. Similarly, the
b_{ji}
are not identically zero.

Let
L=\ell^{k}
, so that
\log_{L}g_{j}=\tfrac{1}{k}\log_{\ell}g_{j}
. We have

\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{j}}=\frac{kb_{j0}}{q}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\frac{b_{ji}}{q}\frac{1}{\log_{L}g_{i}}.

Multiplying by
n
and taking the fractional part it follows that, for any
n\geqslant 1
and
d\leqslant j\leqslant r
,

\left\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{j}}\right\}=\frac{c_{j}(n)}{q}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\frac{b_{ji}}{q}\left\{\frac{n}{\log_{L}g_{i}}\right\},

for some
c_{j}(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since the left-hand side is
\in[0,1)
we must have
\left\lvert c_{j}(n)\right\rvert\ll_{\ell,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}}1
.

In particular, if we write
t_{i}(n)=\{n/\log_{L}g_{i}\}
for
1\leqslant i<d
, then writing
Q_{j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d-1})=\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\frac{b_{ji}}{q}t_{i}
,

\zeta_{1}g_{1}^{\{n/\log_{L}g_{1}\}}+\cdots+\zeta_{r}g_{r}^{\{n/\log_{L}g_{r}\}}=\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\zeta_{i}g_{i}^{t_{i}(n)}+\sum_{d\leqslant j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}g_{j}^{\tfrac{c_{j}(n)}{q}}g_{j}^{Q_{j}(\vec{t}(n))}.

In particular, since there are
O(1)
many possible values for
c_{j}(n)
, there is some finite set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
of size
O_{\ell,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}}(1)
such that

(1)

\left\lvert\zeta^{\prime}_{i}\right\rvert\asymp\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert
for all
\vec{\zeta}^{\prime}\in U
and

(2)

if
\|\sum_{i}\zeta_{i}g_{i}^{\{n/\log_{L}g_{i}\}}\|\leqslant\epsilon
then there exists some
\vec{\zeta}^{\prime}\in U
such that

(6)

\left\lVert\sum_{1\leqslant i<d}\zeta_{i}^{\prime}g_{i}^{t_{i}(n)}+\sum_{d\leqslant j\leqslant r}\zeta_{j}^{\prime}g_{j}^{Q_{j}(\vec{t}(n))}\right\rVert\leqslant\epsilon.

Since
1,1/\log_{L}g_{1},\ldots,1/\log_{L}g_{d-1}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, by Lemma
4
the sequence
\vec{t}(n)
is uniformly distributed in
[0,1)^{d-1}
as
n\to\infty
. In particular, for any
\delta>0
, provided
N
is sufficiently large depending on
\delta
, the number of
n\in[N]
such that
\vec{t}(n)
lies in any fixed box in
[0,1)^{d-1}
of width
\delta
is
O(\delta^{d-1}N)
.

By Lemma
3
there are

\ll(1+\max\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)\delta^{1-d}\max\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert^{-1/r}(\epsilon+\delta\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert)^{1/r}

many boxes that contain some point
\vec{t}
satisfying (
6
) for some
\vec{\zeta}^{\prime}\in U
, and the lemma follows, choosing
\delta=\epsilon/\max_{i}\left\lvert\zeta_{i}\right\rvert
.
∎

5.
The large spectrum of integers with small digits

Finally, we will prove the spectral estimate that we require: namely that if
A
is the set of integers in
[0,g^{R})
with small digits in some base then we can bound the number of integers
k\leqslant M
such that the exponential sum
\sum_{n\in A}(nk/g^{R})
is large.

For context, we first consider what the ‘trivial’ bound is. Let

A=\left\{\sum_{0\leqslant i<R}c_{i}g^{i}:0\leqslant c_{i}<g/2\textrm{ for }0\leqslant i<R\right\}

and

\Delta(M)=\{k\leqslant M:\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\rvert\geqslant M^{-\delta}\lvert A\rvert\}.

By Parseval’s identity we have, when
M=g^{R}
,

\sum_{k\leqslant M}\left\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\right\rvert^{2}\ll\left\lvert A\right\rvert M

and hence, since
\left\lvert A\right\rvert\gg(g/2)^{R}=M^{1-\log_{g}(2)}
,

\left\lvert\Delta(M)\right\rvert\ll M^{1+2\delta}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-1}\ll M^{\log_{g}(2)+2\delta}.

As
\delta\to 0
and
g\to\infty
this is, of course, a great improvement over the very trivial bound of
\left\lvert\Delta(M)\right\rvert\ll M
. This only works, however, if
M\asymp g^{R}
.

We require stronger information however, and in particular for our application need an effective bound even when
M=(g^{R})^{o(1)}
. In such a regime the bound arising from Parseval’s identity becomes worse than trivial.

We will prove a bound that implies, in this situation, for any
M\ll g^{R}
,

\left\lvert\Delta(M)\right\rvert\ll M^{\log_{g}(20)+2\sqrt{\delta}}.

The exponent here is slightly worse than what Parseval’s identity delivers when
M
is comparable to
g^{R}
, but the key point is that we maintain a power type saving (for sufficiently large
g
) even when
M=(g^{R})^{o(1)}
.

The key observation is that, since the exponential sum over
A
can be factored as the product of
R
short exponential sums, each over a short interval, the size of the exponential sum is essentially governed by the base
g
expansion of
k
itself. In particular the exponential sum can only be large if many of the base
g
digits of
k
are large, which can only happen
M^{o(1)}
many times in
[1,M]
.

It is worth noting the duality here: the large spectrum of a set of integers with small digit requirements is itself a set of integers with small digit requirements. This is a generally useful heuristic; it is essentially the same phenomenon as the Fourier transform of a Gaussian being another Gaussian.

Lemma 6
.

Let
R\geqslant 1
and
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Let
g>t\geqslant 1
be integers. If

A=\left\{\sum_{0\leqslant i<R}c_{i}g^{i}:0\leqslant c_{i}<t\textrm{ for }0\leqslant i<R\right\}

then, for any
K\geqslant 1
,
the number of integers
0\leqslant k<g^{K}
such that

(7)

\left\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\right\rvert\geqslant\eta\left\lvert A\right\rvert

is at most

(10/t)^{\min(R,K)}\exp\left(2\sqrt{K\log t\log(1/\eta)}\right)g^{K}

In particular, if
X\geqslant 2
, for any
1\leqslant M\leqslant Xg^{R}
and
\delta>0
, the number of integers
0\leqslant k<M
such that

\left\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\right\rvert\geqslant M^{-\delta}\left\lvert A\right\rvert

is at most

X^{O_{g}(1)}M^{\log_{g}(10g/t)+2\sqrt{\delta}}.

Proof.

Note that the conclusion is trivial if
t=1
, and so we may henceforth assume that
t\geqslant 2
. Let
\Delta=\{0\leqslant k<g^{K}:\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\rvert\geqslant\eta\left\lvert A\right\rvert\}
. We have

\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})=\prod_{0\leqslant i<R}\left(\sum_{0\leqslant c<t}e(ckg^{i-R})\right).

Summing the geometric series yields, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left\lvert\sum_{0\leqslant c<t}e(c\theta)\right\rvert\leqslant\min(t,\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{-1}).

Suppose
k=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}a_{j}g^{j}
with
0\leqslant a_{j}<g
. Note that, for
0\leqslant i<R
,

\left\lVert kg^{i-R}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\sum_{0\leqslant j<R-i}a_{j}g^{j+i-R}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\frac{a_{R-i-1}}{g}\right\rVert+\delta_{i},

say, for some
\left\lvert\delta_{i}\right\rvert<1/g
. Writing
\tilde{a}
for the integer with minimal
\left\lvert\tilde{a}\right\rvert
such that
\tilde{a}\equiv a\pmod{g}
, it follows that

\left\lVert kg^{j-R}\right\rVert>\frac{\left\lvert\tilde{a}_{R-j-1}\right\rvert-1}{g}.

Let
C>1
be some parameter to be chosen later and let
J
be the set of those
0\leqslant i<R
such that
\lvert\tilde{a}_{R-i-1}\rvert\geqslant 2Cg/t
. For
j\in J
we have

\left\lVert kg^{j-R}\right\rVert>\frac{\left\lvert\tilde{a}_{R-j-1}\right\rvert}{2g}\geqslant C/t

and hence

\left\lvert\sum_{0\leqslant c<t}e(ckg^{j-R})\right\rvert\leqslant t/C.

For all
j\not\in J
we will bound this exponential sum trivially by
t
. It follows that (as
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=t^{R}
)

\left\lvert\sum_{n\in A}e(nk/g^{R})\right\rvert\leqslant C^{-\left\lvert J\right\rvert}\left\lvert A\right\rvert,

and hence for the left-hand side to be
\geqslant\eta\left\lvert A\right\rvert
we must have
\left\lvert J\right\rvert\leqslant\log_{C}(1/\eta)
.

Let
K^{\prime}=\min(R,K)
. We now note that the number of
k<g^{K}
where all except
r
of the digits from places
0\leqslant i<R
are inside some set
\subseteq\{0,\ldots,g-1\}
of size
T
is at most

\binom{K^{\prime}}{r}g^{K-K^{\prime}+r}T^{K^{\prime}-r}.

Applying this with
T=\left\lvert[-2Cg/t,2Cg/t]\right\rvert\leqslant 5Cg/t
and
r=\lfloor\log_{C}(1/\eta)\rfloor
, it follows that

\left\lvert\Delta\right\rvert\leqslant 2^{K^{\prime}}g^{K}(5C/t)^{K^{\prime}-r}\leqslant(10C/t)^{K^{\prime}}t^{r}g^{K}\leqslant(10/t)^{K^{\prime}}C^{K}t^{\frac{\log(1/\eta)}{\log C}}g^{K}.

In particular if we choose
C>1
such that
(\log C)^{2}=\frac{\log t\log(1/\eta)}{K}
then

\left\lvert\Delta\right\rvert\leqslant(10/t)^{K^{\prime}}\exp\left(2\sqrt{K\log t\log(1/\eta)}\right)g^{K}

as required. To deduce the second part, it remains only to note that without loss of generality we can assume that
M=g^{K}\leqslant Xg^{R}
, whence

(10/t)^{K^{\prime}}g^{K}\leqslant X^{O(1)}M^{\log_{g}(10g/t)}

and

\exp\left(2\sqrt{K\log t\log(1/\eta)}\right)=M^{2\sqrt{\delta\log_{g}t}}\leqslant M^{2\sqrt{\delta}}.

∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.

6.
Quantitative improvements

As already mentioned, it would be desirable to have a more quantitatively effective version of Theorem
1
. If one examines the proof, the only source of ineffectivity is the purely qualitative ‘for sufficiently large
N
’ in Proposition
1
, which in turn arises from the use of Weyl’s criterion (Lemma
4
). We recall that this is used at a critical stage in the proof of Lemma
5
, where one has some
1,1/\log_{L}g_{1},1/\log_{L}g_{2},...,1/\log_{L}g_{d-1}
linearly independent over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and then uses Weyl’s critertion to deduce that as
n\to\infty
the vector
(\{n/\log_{L}g_{1}\},...,\{n/\log_{L}g_{d-1}\})
is uniformly distributed in
[0,1)^{d-1}
.

To make our final result effective we need a quantified form of Weyl’s equidistribution theorem, such as the Erdős-Turán-Koksma inequality
[
8
]
. This would mean that, instead of using the fact that

{\mathbb{Q}}(1/\log_{L}g_{1},...,1/\log_{L}g_{r})
has dimension
1\leqslant d\leqslant r+1
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, we would use an approximate version of this. Specifically, would need to assume that (after some reordering) there exists some
1\leqslant d\leqslant r+1
such that there is no short integer linear combination of
1,1/\log_{L}g_{1},...,1/\log_{L}g_{d-1}
(i.e. linear combinations

m_{1}+m_{2}/\log_{L}g_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}/\log_{L}g_{d-1}
,
where the
m_{i}
satisfy some bound
|m_{i}|<M
, not all
0
, for an appropriate choice of
M
) too close to
0
in magnitude; furthermore, each of the remaining
g_{i}
will have the property that an approximate version of (
5
) holds.

There are a number of technical issues that present when attempting to implement this precisely, but heuristically this should give a version of Lemma
5
where the conclusion to that lemma now holds for
N\ll\epsilon^{-O(1)}
. Since
N\asymp\log_{L}n
and
\log L\asymp\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)
in the proof of Theorem
1
this in turn means that we may choose

\log n\asymp\epsilon^{-O(1)}.

Choosing
\epsilon>0
to satisfy this, and proceeding with the rest of the proof of Theorem
1
unchanged, would prove a quantitatively effective version of Theorem
1
in which one could replace
\epsilon\log n
by
(\log n)^{1-c}
for some constant
c>0
depending on the
g_{i}
and
\kappa_{j}
.

A more direct (albeit much more difficult) way that we could get effective bounds in Theorem
1
would be to prove a quantitative version of a special case of Schanuel’s conjecture
[
9
]
.

Conjecture 2

(Schanuel’s conjecture)
.

Given any
z_{1},...,z_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
that are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
the field extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r},e^{z_{1}},\ldots,e^{z_{r}})
has transcendence degree at least
r
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Taking
z_{1}=\log g_{1}
,
z_{2}=\log g_{2}
, …,

z_{r}=\log g_{r}
, we see that one consequence of this conjecture is that if

{m_{1}\over\log_{L}g_{1}}+\cdots+{m_{r}\over\log_{L}g_{r}}\ \in\ {\mathbb{Z}},

with
m_{1},...,m_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then

m_{1}=\cdots=m_{r}=0
. This would simplify the proof of Lemma
5

somewhat and give a stronger conclusion. To obtain a strong, effective bound in Theorem
1
, without working with approximate integer linear dependencies as described at the beginning of this subsection, then we would need to use the Erdős-Turán-Koksma theorem (as a quantitative version of Weyl’s lemma) and a quantitative statement like the following.

Conjecture 3
.

Let
g_{1},...,g_{r}\geqslant 2
be integers
such that
g_{i}^{a_{i}}\neq g_{j}^{b_{j}}
for all
i\neq j
and integers
a_{i},b_{j}\geqslant 1
. Then, for all
M\geqslant 2
,

\min_{m_{1},...,m_{r}}\left\|{m_{1}\over\log_{L}g_{1}}+\cdots+{m_{r}\over\log_{L}g_{r}}\right\|\ >\ {1\over M^{r(1+o(1))}},

where the minimum is taken over all integers
m_{1},...,m_{r}
satisfying
|m_{i}|\leqslant M
,
i=1,...,r
, and not all
m_{i}=0
.

This conjecture is based on the following heuristic. Assuming the
1/\log_{L}g_{i}
mod
1
behave like random real numbers in the interval
(0,1)
, the set of integer linear combinations
m_{1}/\log_{L}g_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}/\log_{L}g_{r}
mod
1
vary over
(2M+1)^{r}-1
numbers as the
m_{i}
vary over the integers in
[-M,M]
, not all
0
. If these linear combinations were themselves like random real numbers in
(0,1)
, we would expect none of them come closer than about
(2M+1)^{-r}
to
0
.

If one traces the consequences of this conjecture through the proof in this paper, it gives infinitely many integers
n
such that for all
i=1,...,r
, all but at most
(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}
base
g_{i}
digits of
n
are
<\kappa_{i}g_{i}
. All the implied constants would be effectively computable, provided the implied constants in Conjecture
3
are computable.

Appendix A
Construction of bump function

In this appendix we give details of the construction of the bump function used in the proof of Proposition
2
. Of course, bump functions with these properties are ubiquitous in Fourier analysis, but since we could not find an easy reference for the precise properties we required, we have elected to give a proof here for the benefit of the reader.

Lemma 7
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
J\geqslant 1
there exists a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on
[-\delta/2,\delta/2]
such that

(1)

\widehat{\psi}(0)=\|\psi\|_{1}=1
,

(2)

0\leqslant\widehat{\psi}(k)\leqslant\min(1,(J^{2}/\delta k)^{2J})
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

(3)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\psi}(k)=\psi(0)\leqslant 4/\delta
.

Proof.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{J}\geqslant 0
be constants to be chosen later with
\sum c_{j}\leqslant 1/2
and
\psi_{j}=(c_{j}\delta)^{-1}1_{[-c_{j}\delta/2,c_{j}\delta/2]}
. The Fourier transform of
\psi_{j}
is

\widehat{\psi_{j}}(k)=\frac{\sin(\pi c_{j}\delta k)}{\pi c_{j}\delta k}.

We choose
\psi=\psi_{1}\ast\psi_{1}\ast\cdots\ast\psi_{J}\ast\psi_{J}
. Note that
\psi\geqslant 0
pointwise and therefore

\widehat{\psi}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{1}=\prod_{j}\left\lVert\psi_{j}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}=1,

since each
\psi_{j}
was normalised to have
\left\lVert\psi_{j}\right\rVert_{1}=1
. Furthermore since
\sum_{j}c_{j}\leqslant 1/2
this is supported on
[-\delta/2,\delta/2]
as required, and

\widehat{\psi}=\prod_{j}\widehat{\psi_{j}}^{2}

and so in particular
\widehat{\psi}\geqslant 0
pointwise. Furthermore

\sum_{k}\widehat{\psi}(k)\leqslant\sum_{k}\widehat{\psi_{1}}^{2}=\|\psi_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=(c_{1}\delta)^{-1}.

We will choose
c_{1}=1/4
. It remains to show that

\widehat{\psi}(k)\leqslant(J^{2}/\delta k)^{2J}

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The left-hand side is

(\pi\delta k)^{-2J}\prod_{j}\frac{\sin(\pi c_{j}\delta k)^{2}}{c_{j}^{2}}.

If we choose
c_{j}=1/4j^{2}
then this right-hand side is

\leqslant 4^{2J}(\pi\delta k)^{-2J}(J!)^{4}.

Using
J!\leqslant J^{J}/e^{J}
and
4/\pi e^{2}<1
this is at most
(J^{2}/\delta k)^{2J}
as required.
∎
