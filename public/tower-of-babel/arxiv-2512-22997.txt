Generalised Entanglement Entropies from Unit-Invariant Singular Value Decomposition Preprint: YITP-25-193
Paweł Caputa, Abhigyan Saha, Piotr Sułkowski

Abstract
We introduce generalisations of von Neumann entanglement entropy that are invariant with respect to certain scale transformations. These constructions are based on Unit-Invariant Singular Value Decomposition (UISVD) with its right-, left-, and bi-invariant incarnations, which itself are variations of the standard Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) that remain invariant under (appropriate set of) diagonal transformations. These measures are naturally defined for non-Hermitian or rectangular operators and remain useful when the input and output spaces possess different dimensions or metric weights. We apply the UISVD entropy and discuss its advantages in the physically interesting framework of Biorthogonal Quantum Mechanics, whose important aspect is indeed the behavior under scale transformations. Further, we illustrate features of UISVD-based entropies in other representative settings, from simple quantum mechanical bipartite states to random matrices relevant to quantum chaos and holography, and in the context of Chern-Simons theory. In all cases, the UISVD yields stable, physically meaningful entropic spectra that are invariant under rescalings and normalisations.

Introduction

Quantum entanglement has become one of the most powerful and unifying concepts in modern theoretical physics. It underlies our understanding of correlations and information in quantum many-body systems

Traditional measures of entanglement rely on the Schmidt or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of a pure state or operator in a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
(see
31
;
19
;
46

However, when the system evolves non-unitarily, interacts with an environment, or the setup is described by transition rather than density matrices, the conventional SVD-based framework becomes ambiguous. In such cases, even defining an “entropy” that reflects physical, quantum correlations rather than normalisation or scaling artefacts can be subtle.

Related to these challenges, several generalisations of entanglement have recently been proposed, including pseudo-entropy
45
;
44
, SVD entropy
49
, and entropies in time
17
;
18
;
43
among them. These approaches use the singular values of transition matrices or non-Hermitian operators to construct Rényi-type quantities that extend entanglement to post-selected, time-dependent or general open quantum systems. Such ideas and tools already found applications across diverse areas, from dS/CFT correspondence
17
;
28
;
29
, renormalisation
23
, topological theories
47
;
12

11
.

However, a fundamental issue persists across all these constructions: the singular values themselves depend on the choice of units and scaling conventions in the subspaces involved. To see the problem concretely, consider even the simplest quantum mechanical example: a two-level system where one rescales the basis states in one subsystem by an arbitrary factor (a trivial change of normalisation or physical units). Although this transformation leaves the physical content unchanged, it modifies the singular values. As a result, the entropy constructed from those singular values varies under a mere redefinition of scale i.e. conventional SVD, while invariant under unitaries, is not invariant under such diagonal rescalings. Consequently, SVD-based entropic measures may conflate genuine quantum correlations with artefacts of normalisation, a subtle but crucial distinction, especially in field-theoretic or non-Hermitian settings where operator norms (choices of inner-products) or couplings carry non-trivial scaling behaviour (see e.g.
21
).

A mathematically rigorous resolution to this problem with SVD was recently provided by Uhlmann
61
. Following earlier works on construction of singular values invariant under more specific rescalings, he introduced the Unit-Invariant Singular Value Decomposition (UISVD) as a variation of the standard SVD that remains invariant under diagonal (unit) transformations acting on a given operator from both sides. The UISVD produces unit-invariant singular values that eliminate spurious dependencies on scaling conventions. In effect, UISVD isolates the physically meaningful, scale-independent structure of a linear map.

This property makes the UISVD particularly appealing for the above-mentioned physical setups, where operators often connect Hilbert spaces with different metric or normalisation structures present. Indeed, in these contexts, one may start from entanglement and information measures that are invariant under simple changes of physical units, and hope that classifying them will be easier and more robust, similarly to von Neumann entropies for pure states.

In this work, we introduce the UISVD framework into quantum information and field-theoretic contexts, proposing it as a new foundation for unit-invariant measures of entanglement. We construct UISVD-based analogues of von Neumann entropy and demonstrate the variation from familiar SVD-based quantities while removing their dependence on arbitrary scale choices. These measures are naturally defined for non-Hermitian or rectangular operators and remain robust when the input and output spaces possess different dimensions or metric weights. As a proof of concept, we apply UISVD-based entropies to several representative settings, from simple quantum mechanical bipartite mixed states to random matrices relevant to quantum chaos and holography. In all cases, the UISVD yields stable, physically meaningful entropic spectra that are invariant under rescalings and normalisations.

Beyond their practical advantages, our results suggest a deeper conceptual point: UISVD restores a natural physical covariance to operator-based entropies, ensuring that what we quantify as “entanglement” or “correlation” is independent of arbitrary normalisation or choice of units. In this sense, we hope that UISVD may contribute to providing a unifying, invariant language for quantifying quantum correlations in systems where conventional assumptions of unitarity or normalisation fail.

Unit-Invariant Singular Values

In this section we review the construction of various scaling invariants of matrices discussed in
61
. We call them left unit-invariant, right unit-invariant, and (full) unit-invariant singular values, and denote respectively LUI-, RUI-, and BUI-singular values. These invariants will play the key role in the construction of entropy measures introduced in next sections. In the following subsections we collect definitions of unit-invariant singular values, provide their geometric interpretation, and present explicit results for two-dimensional spaces.

2.1
Definitions of singular values

To start with, we quickly recall that the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is

A=U\Sigma V^{\dagger},

(2)

where
U\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0

\sigma_{i}\equiv\sigma_{i}(A)

\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}

\sigma_{i}(A)=\sigma_{i}(U^{\prime}AV^{\prime})

Left Unit-Invariant (LUI) singular values.

(3)

Normalisation.

Recall that the

\|A^{\mathrm{L}}\|_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathbb{A}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathbb{B}}}|A^{\mathrm{L}}_{ij}|^{2}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{min({d_{\mathbb{A}}},d_{\mathbb{B}})}(\sigma_{k}(A^{\mathrm{L}}))^{2}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{min({d_{\mathbb{A}}},d_{\mathbb{B}})}(\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(A))^{2},

(21)

where

d_{\mathbb{A}}=m,d_{\mathbb{B}}=n

\|A^{\mathrm{L}}\|_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathbb{A}}}\bigl\|A^{\mathrm{L}}(i,:)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathbb{A}}}1\;=\;{d_{\mathbb{A}}}.

(22)

Analogous statements hold for
A^{\mathrm{R}}

\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{\mathrm{L}})^{2}\;=\;d_{\mathbb{A}},\qquad\quad\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{\mathrm{R}})^{2}\;=\;d_{\mathbb{B}}.

(23)

This allows us to introduce the normalised left- and right-singular values as

\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}}{\sqrt{d_{\mathbb{A}}}},\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{\mathrm{R}}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\mathrm{R}}}{\sqrt{d_{\mathbb{B}}}}.

(24)

Unfortunately, we were not able to derive a simple expression (in terms of

\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{\mathrm{B}}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\mathrm{B}}}{||A^{\mathrm{B}}||_{\mathbb{F}}}=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\mathrm{B}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}}.

(25)

These normalised values will appear directly in our definitions of entropies based on the UISVD decompositions.

2.2
Geometric interpretation

It is worth invoking a geometric interpretation of the quantities introduced above. SVD has a well known representation in Euclidean space:
it takes the unit sphere in the input space and turns it into an ellipsoid in the output space. The right singular vectors are the input directions that land on the ellipsoid’s principal axes, the left singular vectors point along those axes in the output space, and the singular values are the axis lengths.

LUI-SVD and RUI-SVD keep this same sphere to ellipsoid story, but they first “re-unit” one side by a diagonal stretch of the coordinate axes chosen from the Euclidean row or column norms. In LUI-SVD one stretches or shrinks the output coordinate axes so the nonzero rows have equal size under that norm, which puts the output channels on a common scale before looking for the ellipsoid’s principal directions. In RUI-SVD one does the analogous axis rescaling on the input side so the nonzero columns have equal size, which makes the input coordinates comparable before looking for principal directions. After this one-sided axis standardisation, an ordinary SVD gives the ellipsoid geometry in the standardised coordinates, and the diagonal factor just converts that geometric description back to the original units.

BUI-SVD is the fully symmetric version. It diagonally stretches or shrinks both input and output axes until the matrix is balanced in a multiplicative sense, so the products of the nonzero entry magnitudes are equal across nonzero rows and columns, which one can think of as balancing typical entry magnitudes across rows and columns, or as centering log-magnitudes when no zeros are present. Once that balancing has fixed the arbitrary choice of units on both sides, SVD again reads off the same sphere to ellipsoid geometry, now in the balanced coordinate system, and the outer diagonal factors simply map the picture back to the original coordinates.

3
Unit-Invariant entanglement entropies

In this section we introduce various entanglement entropies based on unit-invariant singular values defined above. They generalise von Neumann, pseudo- or SVD entropies considered earlier in literature, whose definitions and properties we briefly summarise first.

3.1
Review of entanglement entropy

We start with the usual setup of a bipartite Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}},

(29)

with subsystems of arbitrary finite dimensions
d_{\mathbb{A}}
and
d_{\mathbb{B}}
respectively.
We consider bipartite pure state
\left|\psi\right>
and corresponding density matrix expressed in some fixed orthonormal basis as

\left|\psi\right>\;=\;\sum_{i,k}A_{ik}\,\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{B}},\qquad\rho=\left|\psi\right>\left<\psi\right|=\sum_{i,j,k,l}A_{ik}A^{*}_{jl}\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{B}}\left<l\right|_{\mathbb{B}}\otimes\left<j\right|_{\mathbb{A}},

(30)

with coefficient matrix
A
that does not have any all-zero rows or all-zero columns. The reduced density matrix of
\mathbb{A}
becomes

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}=\text{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}(\rho)=\sum_{i,j}(AA^{\dagger})_{ij}\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\left<j\right|_{\mathbb{A}}.

(31)

To compute entropies we will consider normalised density matrix
\text{Tr}(\rho_{A})=1
that we can write as operator
6
6

6

It is usually more natural to start this construction with normalising
\left|\psi\right>
so that
\text{Tr}(A^{\dagger}A)=1
, but we keep this discussion more general for later purposes.

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}=\frac{AA^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}(AA^{\dagger})}.

(32)

Then, in the standard computation of von Neumann entropy, we perform the singular value decomposition of
A

A=U\Sigma V^{\dagger},

(33)

with unitaries
U
and
V^{\dagger}
and diagonal matrix
\Sigma
with singular values
\sigma_{i}

\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots),

(34)

that leads to

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}=\frac{U\Sigma^{2}U^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}(U\Sigma^{2}U^{\dagger})}=\frac{U\Sigma^{2}U^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}(\Sigma^{2})}\,.

(35)

This way, the normalised eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}
are squares of the singular values of
A

\lambda_{k}=\sigma^{2}_{k}/(\sum_{l}\sigma^{2}_{l})
.
Recall that this procedure is equivalent to introducing a
particular basis
in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}}
through the Schmidt decomposition of
\left|\psi\right>
(often understood as a purification of

). Indeed, we can write the state
\left|\psi\right>
using the SVD decomposition (

\left|\psi\right>=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\left|j\right>_{\mathbb{B}}=\sum_{i,k,j}U_{ik}\Sigma_{kk}V^{\dagger}_{kj}\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\left|j\right>_{\mathbb{B}}\equiv\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{A}}\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{B}},

(36)

where we defined the basis vectors

\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{A}}=\sum_{i}U_{ik}\left|i\right>_{\mathbb{A}},\qquad\left|k\right>_{\mathbb{B}}=\sum_{j}V^{\dagger}_{kj}\left|j\right>_{\mathbb{B}},

(37)

that span the basis in
\mathbb{A}
(and its complement
\mathbb{B}
), and the singular values are also called the Schmidt coefficients.

Finally, from the singular values (Schmidt coefficients), we can evaluate a family of Rényi entropies indexed by an integer

(38)

The entanglement (von Neumann) entropy, which can also be obtained as the limit of
n\to 1
of the Rényi family, is defined as

S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathbb{A}}\coloneq-\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}\log(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}))=-\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\log(\lambda_{k})=-\sum_{k}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{k}}{(\sum_{l}\sigma^{2}_{l})}\log\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{k}}{(\sum_{l}\sigma^{2}_{l})}\right).

(39)

In what follows we generalise this construction to other decompositions of matrix
A
discussed in the previous sections.

Unit-Invariant SVD entropies for transition matrices

In turn, motivated by

4.1
Review of pseudo and SVD entropies

In this case, we generally consider
two independent, normalised pure states of the form (
30
) in
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}}
:
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
, with non-zero overlap
\langle\psi_{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle
. We define the
transition matrix
by

\tau^{1|2}=\frac{|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|}{\langle\psi_{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle},

(54)

which, in general, is a non-Hermitian operator on
\mathcal{H}
. From this matrix, we compute a partial trace over subsystem
\mathbb{B}
to obtain the reduced transition matrix operator on
\mathbb{A}

\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}\bigl(\tau^{1|2}\bigr).

(55)

Pseudo-entropy
45
is then defined as a generalisation of von Neumann entropy of this transition matrix

S^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})=-\text{Tr}(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}\log(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})).

(56)

Since the eigenvalues of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}
are in general complex, pseudo-entropy will also have real and imaginary parts. These features already found interesting applications in dS/CFT
17
, and in holographic CFTs, its gravity dual was found to be the minimal area in Euclidean time-dependent AdS spacetimes
45
. For some specific choices of the pre- and post-selected states, real and imaginary parts of pseudo-entropy were found to satisfy the Kramers–Kronig relations
11
. Nevertheless, the imaginary part of pseudo-entropy makes it hard to interpret it from the quantum information theoretical point of view and its operational meaning remains mysterious. For this reason its “improvement” was proposed in
49
that defined
SVD entropy
as von Neumann entropy computed from the singular values of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}
, which are real. More precisely, we first construct a density matrix

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}=\frac{\sqrt{(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}}}{\text{Tr}\left[\sqrt{(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}}\right]},

(57)

and define the SVD entropy of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}
and von Neumann entropy of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}

S^{\text{SVD}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})=-\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}\log(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})).

(58)

By construction, the singular values
\sigma_{k}
of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}=U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
, in
\Sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d_{\mathbb{A}}})
, are related to the normalised eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2}
by

\hat{\lambda}_{k}=\frac{\sigma_{k}}{\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}},

(59)

so that

S^{\text{SVD}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})=-\sum_{k}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\log(\hat{\lambda}_{k})\,.

(60)

By definition, SVD entropy is always non-negative and bounded,
0\leq S^{SVD}_{\mathbb{A}}\leq\log(d_{\mathbb{A}})
. Moreover, SVD entropy was given an operational meaning as the average number of Bell pairs distillable from intermediate states that appear between
\left|\psi_{1}\right>
and
\left|\psi_{2}\right>

49
. In the following, we will employ the UISVD values to provide one more generalisation of the pseudo- and SVD entropies.

The SVD values are again computed from

(65)

An analogous construction should be performed for

S^{\mathrm{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})\neq S^{\mathrm{I}}_{\mathbb{B}}(\tau_{\mathbb{B}}^{1|2})\,.

(67)

We will study some explicit examples of these quantities in the following sections.

Before we proceed, a few comments are in order.
First, let us pick one of the states, say the pre-selected state
\left|\psi_{1}\right>
, and act upon its left or right component with an arbitrary diagonal operator, denoted respectively
D_{\mathbb{A}}
or
D_{\mathbb{B}}

\left|\psi_{1_{\mathbb{A}}}\right>=D_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathbb{I}\left|\psi_{1}\right>\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\psi_{1_{\mathbb{B}}}\right>=\mathbb{I}\otimes D_{\mathbb{B}}\left|\psi_{1}\right>.

(68)

The corresponding transition matrices for such rescaled states are then also rescaled by diagonal factors, and we denote them by

\tau^{1_{\mathbb{A}}|2}_{\mathbb{A}}=\frac{D_{\mathbb{A}}}{\text{Tr}{(D_{\mathbb{A}}\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})}}\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{1_{\mathbb{B}}|2}_{\mathbb{B}}=\frac{D_{\mathbb{B}}}{\text{Tr}{(D_{\mathbb{B}}\tau_{\mathbb{B}}^{1|2})}}\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{B}}.

(69)

Thus, for pre-selected states, action of local scaling operators on one of the subsystems and tracing out the complement is equivalent to a scaling of the original transition matrix from the left. Consequently, left- and bi- UISVD entropies will be invariant under such transformations (but R-UISVD will detect them).

Secondly, for the case of post-selected states, any local scaling action results in a scaling on the right

\tau^{1|2_{\mathbb{A}}}_{\mathbb{A}}=\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}{{\frac{D_{\mathbb{A}}^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}{(D_{\mathbb{A}}^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathbb{A}}^{1|2})}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{1|2_{\mathbb{B}}}_{\mathbb{B}}=\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{B}}{{\frac{D^{\dagger}_{\mathbb{B}}}{\text{Tr}{(D_{\mathbb{B}}^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathbb{B}}^{1|2})}}}}.

(70)

Therefore RUISVD and BUISVD entanglement measures would remain invariant, while LUISVD entropy would detect it. Further, acting on the same subsystem of both pre- and post-selected states leads to the two-sided scaling of the original reduced transition matrix, upon which only BUISVD entanglement entropy remains invariant.

In the next section, as a proof of concept, we apply these definitions of unit-invariant entropies to various physical setups.

5.2
Unit-Invariant entropies in Chern-Simons theory

As another interesting and illustrative playground, we apply the entanglement measures introduced in
Section

3
to Chern–Simons theory, following the line of research that applies and tests ideas from quantum information theory in the realm of Chern-Simons and related systems
4
;
3
;
20
;
12
. In particular, in
12
, we observed that several entanglement entropy measures defined from transition matrices exhibit intricate connections with link complement states in Chern–Simons gauge theory. Specifically, the imaginary part of pseudo entanglement entropy
S^{\mathrm{P}}
, computed from the complex eigenvalues of the reduced transition matrices (
55
), provides a new tool to detect the chirality of the underlying links. This holds for any compact gauge group. For the special case of
U(1)
, it was further shown that the entanglement measure based on the singular values of the reduced transition matrix, called the
excess SVD entanglement entropy

\Delta S^{\mathrm{SVD}}
(constructed using
Eq.

58
), acts as a
pseudo metric
on the space of two-component links. These observations naturally lead us to ask: what is the physical interpretation (or a useful application) of the entanglement entropies derived from the remaining three invariants discussed in this work, when applied to link complement states?

To this end, consider a two-component link
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\bigcup\mathcal{K}_{2}
whose link complement state is given by
4
;
3

|\mathcal{L}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}V^{\mathcal{L}}_{ij}\,|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle,

(77)

where the coefficients
V^{\mathcal{L}}_{ij}
are
coloured polynomial invariants
(for any gauge group) associated with the link in representations
i
and
j
. In the matrix language, we represent these coefficients as the entries of the matrix

A=(V^{\mathcal{L}}_{ij}).

(78)

In this setup, we show that the operation of taking the connected sum and a framing change are respectively examples of diagonal and unitary operations, upon which appropriate measures of our interest are invariant (analogously as in
Eq.

1
).

Figure 3
:
Connected sum of left and right trefoil knots gives a “granny knot”.

We begin with the operation of connected sum. Recall that the
connected sum

\mathcal{K}\#\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
of two knots
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
is defined by first cutting a tiny piece out of each knot so that each one becomes an open string with two loose ends. Then we connect the two knots by joining an end from the first to an end from the second, and joining the remaining two ends, using short connections that don’t tangle with anything else. This results in a single closed loop denoted by
\mathcal{K}\#\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
. An example is shown in
Fig.

3
using left and right trefoil knots. Recently, this operation has also found a direct, experimentally realised counterpart in real materials
25
.

Suppose given a link
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\bigcup\mathcal{K}_{2}
, we perform a connected sum on the first component with a knot
\mathcal{K}
and on the second component with a knot
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, so that we obtain
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\coloneq(\mathcal{K}\#\mathcal{K}_{1})\bigcup(\mathcal{K}_{2}\#\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
. The coloured polynomials (normalised by the unknot) of these knots in representation
i
and
j
are given by
V^{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
and
V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{j}
respectively. We define the corresponding diagonal operators:

D=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(V^{\mathcal{K}}_{0},\,V^{\mathcal{K}}_{1},\,V^{\mathcal{K}}_{2},\,\dots\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad{D^{\prime}}^{\dagger}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{0},\,V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{1},\,V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{2},\,\dots\bigr).

(79)

Equivalently in
Eq.

79
the matrix
{D^{\prime}}
is constructed out of the coloured polynomial invariants of the
mirror image
of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

12
. The action of taking a connected sum on each component is equivalent to applying the local diagonal operator
D\otimes D^{\prime}
on the state:

(D\otimes D^{\prime})\,|\mathcal{L}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}V^{\mathcal{K}}_{i}\,V^{\mathcal{L}}_{ij}\,V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{j}\,|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle.

(80)

Thus, the new quantum state is in the matrix formulation
A^{\prime}=DAD^{\prime}

|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}A^{\prime}_{ij}\,|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle=\sum_{i,j}V^{\mathcal{K}}_{i}\,V^{\mathcal{L}}_{ij}\,V^{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}_{j}\,|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle=\sum_{i,j}V^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}_{ij}\,|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle.

(81)

This shows that connected sum with the knots
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
on the respective components is equivalent to acting on the initial state with the local operator
D\otimes D^{\prime}
, where each diagonal entry corresponds to the one-coloured polynomial of the knot in that representation. It follows that unit-invariant measures of our interest, which are invariant under diagonal rescalings of this form, are invariant under taking the connected sum.

In turn, we discuss local unitary operations. In the current setup, an interesting class of such operations are changes of
framing
of the component knots. A framing of knot can be interpreted as the linking number of two boundaries of a ribbon created by thickening that knot trajectory. Formally, a framing is a continuous, nowhere-vanishing normal vector field along the knot, effectively turning it into such a twisted ribbon.
The
framing number
is the integer that records how many times this field rotates as one traverses the knot once (with the sign determined by the ambient orientation).
In our link-complement states, increasing the framing by
+1
is implemented by a positive
Dehn twist
on the corresponding boundary torus. If we begin with a two-component link with state
\left|\mathcal{L}\right>
, and then vary the framing of the first component knot by
p
and of the second knot by

, then the new link
{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
encodes the state
4
;
36

\left|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\right>=\mathcal{T}^{p}\otimes\mathcal{T}^{q}\left|\mathcal{L}\right>,

(82)

where the Dehn-twist is effected by the matrices
\mathcal{T}
(e.g. of the form (
84
) in the case
SU(2)
Chern-Simons theory that we discuss below). These are exactly the relevant local unitary operators that we have been after. In fact, when we are dealing with compact gauge groups, these
\mathcal{T}
matrices are not just unitary but also diagonal, and a change of framing of each component knot leaves all entropies
S^{\mathrm{B}},S^{\mathrm{L}},S^{\mathrm{R}},S^{\mathrm{E}}
invariant.

5.3
UISVD entanglement entropy for Biorthogonal Quantum Mechanics

Finally, we study the bi-unit-invariant entanglement entropies in another natural setting: Biorthogonal Quantum Mechanics (BQM). BQM is a generalisation of standard quantum mechanics in which scale transformations act as physical symmetries
7
. It is therefore desirable to employ entanglement measures and other observables that remain invariant under such transformations. The unit-invariant measures developed above are ideally suited for this purpose.

We begin by briefly reviewing the essentials of BQM and, in particular, previously studied entanglement measures for biorthogonal states
7
, including the standard right–left (RL) construction
13
;
65
;
27
. We then adapt the formalism introduced in the earlier sections to define the BUISVD entanglement entropy in the biorthogonal setting. The key message of this section is that, whereas the conventional RL entanglement entropy is generally complex-valued, may take negative values, and can be unbounded, the BUISVD-based alternative is always real, positive, bounded, and fully compatible with the scaling symmetry of BQM.

Biorthogonal bipartite states.
As the name indicates, the defining feature of BQM is a biorthogonal character of its states
7
. We thus consider
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}}
and fix biorthogonal bases on each factor

\{|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\rangle\},\ \{\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}},\qquad\{|R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle\},\ \{\langle L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}},

(86)

satisfying

\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{ii^{\prime}},\qquad\langle L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}.

(87)

The corresponding product biorthogonal bases on
\mathcal{H}
are

|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle\coloneq|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\rangle\otimes|R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle,\qquad\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|\coloneq\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|\otimes\langle L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|.

(88)

A biorthogonal state is a pair
(|\Psi_{R}\rangle,\langle\Psi_{L}|)
with expansions

|\Psi_{R}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\Psi_{ij}\,|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle,\qquad\langle\Psi_{L}|=\sum_{k,\ell}\Lambda_{k\ell}\,\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{k}L^{\mathbb{B}}_{\ell}|,

(89)

subject to the normalisation condition

\langle\Psi_{L}|\Psi_{R}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\Lambda_{ij}\,\Psi_{ij}=1.

(90)

We collect the right and left coefficients into matrices

\Psi=(\Psi_{ij})
and
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{ij})
. The admissible BQM scale transformations are rescalings of the biorthogonal bases
21

|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\rangle\mapsto a_{i}\,|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\rangle,\quad\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|\mapsto a_{i}^{-1}\,\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|,\qquad|R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle\mapsto b_{j}\,|R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle,\quad\langle L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|\mapsto b_{j}^{-1}\,\langle L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|,

(91)

with all
a_{i},b_{j}\neq 0
. Under this scaling symmetry the coefficient matrices transform as

\Psi_{ij}\mapsto\Psi^{\prime}_{ij}=a_{i}^{-1}b_{j}^{-1}\,\Psi_{ij},\qquad\Lambda_{ij}\mapsto\Lambda^{\prime}_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}\,\Lambda_{ij},

(92)

i.e.

\Psi\mapsto\Psi^{\prime}=D_{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\Psi D_{\mathbb{B}}^{-1},\qquad\Lambda\mapsto\Lambda^{\prime}=D_{\mathbb{A}}\Lambda D_{\mathbb{B}},

(93)

with
D_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathrm{diag}(a_{i})
and
D_{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{diag}(b_{j})
.

5.3.1
Biorthogonal entanglement entropies

For a biorthogonal pure state
(|\Psi_{R}\rangle,\langle\Psi_{L}|)
the analogue of a pure-state density operator is
13
;
65
;
27

\rho^{\mathrm{R|L}}\coloneq|\Psi_{R}\rangle\langle\Psi_{L}|,

(94)

which reproduces BQM expectation values via

\langle O\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{\mathrm{R|L}}O)/\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho^{\mathrm{R|L}}
for operator
O
. From the perspective of our work, we will simply think about it as transition matrix (i.e. weak measurement) and extract its L-, R-, B- singular values the same way as we defined for transition matrices in
Section

4
.
The biorthogonal reduced operator on
\mathbb{A}
is defined by the partial trace over
\mathbb{B}
. In product biorthogonal basis

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}(O)=\sum_{j}\Bigl(I_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\langle L_{\mathbb{B}}^{\,j}|\Bigr)\,O\,\Bigl(I_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes|R_{\mathbb{B}}^{\,j}\rangle\Bigr).

(95)

Equivalently for matrix elements
\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}O\bigr)_{ik}=\sum_{j}\,\langle L_{\mathbb{A}}^{\,i}L_{\mathbb{B}}^{\,j}\,|\,O\,|\,R_{\mathbb{A}}^{\,k}R_{\mathbb{B}}^{\,j}\rangle
. Thus

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}\coloneq\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}\,\rho^{\mathrm{R|L}}=\sum_{i,j,k}\psi_{ij}\Lambda_{kj}|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\rangle\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{k}|,

(96)

or in the matrix form
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}=\Psi\,\Lambda^{\mathsf{T}}
. Under a BQM scale transformation the reduced operator transforms by diagonal similarity

\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}\mapsto\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}\,^{\prime}}=D_{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\,\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}\,D_{\mathbb{A}},

(97)

so its eigenvalues are invariant under the admissible rescalings.

Let
\lambda_{k}
denote the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}
.
With the normalisation
\langle\Psi_{L}|\Psi_{R}\rangle=1
one has

\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1
but, the same as for transition matrices
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
,
the
\lambda_{k}
’s can be complex because
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{RL}}
is non-Hermitian.
The standard “biorthogonal entanglement entropy” used in the BQM literature is the
RL entropy

13
;
65
;
27

S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{RL}}(\Psi)\coloneq-\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\log\lambda_{k}=S^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}),

(98)

which is simply the pseudo entropy
Eq.

56
of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}
.
This quantity is scale invariant (it depends only on the spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}\}
)
but generically complex and not bounded by
\log\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}
. A variant which we denote by
S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{TTC}}(\Psi)

60
;
64
, uses the same spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}\}
but replaces
\log\lambda_{i}
by
\log|\lambda_{k}|
in order to remove branch-cut ambiguities while retaining sensitivity to non-Hermiticity. This TTC entropy is again scale invariant but, like
S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{RL}}
, is not a non-negative, bounded entropy. In view of these deficiencies, we now see that the UISVD entanglement measures introduced in this paper may prove to be useful.

UISVD entropy in BQM.

We now evaluate the BUISVD entanglement entropy (
66
) directly for the reduced operator
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}
as

S^{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}})=-\sum_{k}\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\log\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{B}}_{k},\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}=\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}})}{\sum_{i}\sigma^{\mathrm{B}}_{i}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}})},

(99)

where
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
are bi-unit-invariant singular values (
16
) of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}
. Since
\sigma_{k}
are non-negative and not all zero, the
\hat{\sigma}_{k}
form a bona fide probability vector, so

0\leq S^{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}})\leq\log r,

(100)

where
r=\mathrm{rank}\,\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}\leq\text{min}(\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}},\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}})
.
Moreover,
S^{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}})
is invariant under all admissible BQM rescalings of the biorthogonal bases, in consequence of the invariance of the BUI singular values of
\rho_{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{R|L}}
, as seen in
Eq.

97
.
This quantity, however, is asymmetric in the indices and depends on the choice of subsystem to be traced out.

5.3.2
UISVD entropies for transition matrices

We now turn to BUISVD entanglement measures for
pre/post-selected pairs of biorthogonal states and the actual transition
matrices in BQM, following recent work

;
14

Biorthogonal transition operators.

On the same bipartite Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{B}}
with product biorthogonal bases

\{|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle\}
and
\{\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}L^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}|\}
as before, consider
two biorthogonal pure states

(|\Psi^{(1)}_{R}\rangle,\langle\Psi^{(1)}_{L}|),\qquad(|\Psi^{(2)}_{R}\rangle,\langle\Psi^{(2)}_{L}|),

(104)

each normalised so that

\langle\Psi^{(k)}_{L}|\Psi^{(k)}_{R}\rangle=1
for
k=1,2
.
In the product basis we write

|\Psi^{(1)}_{R}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\Psi^{(1)}_{ij}\,|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}R^{\mathbb{B}}_{j}\rangle,\qquad\langle\Psi^{(2)}_{L}|=\sum_{p,q}\Lambda^{(2)}_{pq}\,\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{p}L^{\mathbb{B}}_{q}|,

(105)

with coefficient matrices

\Psi^{(1)}=(\Psi^{(1)}_{ij})
and
\Lambda^{(2)}=(\Lambda^{(2)}_{pq})
.
The corresponding right-left transition matrix is

\tau^{1|2}=|\Psi^{(1)}_{R}\rangle\langle\Psi^{(2)}_{L}|.

(106)

The reduced transition matrix on
\mathbb{A}
is defined by the biorthogonal partial

\mathbb{B}
,

\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}\,(\tau^{1|2}),\qquad(\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}})_{ip}=\langle L^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}|\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}|R^{\mathbb{A}}_{p}\rangle=\sum_{j}\Psi^{(1)}_{ij}\,\Lambda^{(2)}_{pj},

(107)

and so as a matrix
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}=\Psi^{(1)}(\Lambda^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}},
obtained from the same biorthogonal partial trace rule as in the pure-state
case. Under the admissible BQM rescalings the coefficients transform exactly as for a single biorthogonal state. As in the pure-state case, the
\mathbb{B}
-scale cancels and the surviving
\mathbb{A}
-scale
acts on
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}
by diagonal similarity, i.e.
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}\mapsto\tau^{\prime}_{\mathbb{A}}=D_{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\tau_{\mathbb{A}}D_{\mathbb{A}}
, just like in

97
. In particular, the eigenvalues of

\tau_{\mathbb{A}}
are invariant under all admissible biorthogonal rescalings, and so are BUI-singular values of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}
.

Pseudo entropy.

To compute the pseudo-entropy
45
for the pre/post pair

(|\Psi^{(1)}\rangle,|\Psi^{(2)}\rangle)
along the bipartition
\mathbb{A}|\mathbb{B}
in BQM setting
14
, we
construct the transition-matrix (
107
), and denote the set of its normalised eigenvalues by
\{\hat{\lambda}_{k}\}

56
.

Because
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
transforms by diagonal similarity, the spectrum

\{\lambda_{k}\}
is invariant under all admissible BQM scales and

is diagonally scale free. However, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are
typically complex and not constrained to lie in

, so

is
generically complex and its real part is not bounded by
\log\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}
.

BUISVD entropy.

To obtain a real, non-negative, and bounded transition entropy that respects the BQM
rescaling symmetry, we replace the eigenvalues by the BUI
singular values of
\tau_{\mathbb{A}}
. Let

\sigma^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}(\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}})
denote the BUI singular values of
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
. These are invariant under arbitrary diagonal left and
right scalings. Then we compute the BUISVD entropy
Eq.

66
in BQM simply using the BUI singular values of
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
in
Eq.

107
.
By construction
S^{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathbb{A}}(\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}})
is invariant under all
admissible BQM scales on
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
, and satisfies the bounds

0\leq S^{\mathrm{B}}\leq\log r,

(108)

where
r=\mathrm{rank}\,\tau_{\mathbb{A}}\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Moreover
S^{\mathrm{B}}=0
if and only if

\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
has rank one. In particular, if the transition operator factorises
across the
\mathbb{A}|\mathbb{B}
with a rank-one
factor on
\mathbb{A}
, then
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
has rank one and
S^{\mathrm{B}}=0
.
In this sense
S^{\mathrm{B}}
is a diagonally scale-free analogue of the SVD-based entropies.

E(g)=\sqrt{1-4g^{2}}
of the two-qubit Hamiltonian, evaluated at
g_{1}

the states are the same, and thus

and BUISVD entropy

).

Example.

Consider two qubits with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, along with Pauli matrices
\sigma^{\alpha}
and define their actions on the two sites by

\sigma^{\alpha}_{1}=\sigma^{\alpha}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2},\qquad\sigma^{\alpha}_{2}=\mathbb{I}_{2}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha}.

(109)

An example of a PT-symmetric non-Hermitian XX Hamiltonian with a staggered imaginary field
34
;
56
is

H(g)=\sigma^{x}_{1}\sigma^{x}_{2}+ig\bigl(-\sigma^{z}_{1}+\sigma^{z}_{2}\bigr),\qquad g\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0,\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\}.

(110)

For each such
g
we biorthogonally
diagonalise
H(g)

H(g)\,|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g)\rangle=E_{n}(g)\,|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g)\rangle,\qquad\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g)|\,H(g)=\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g)|\,E_{n}(g),

(111)

which has the spectrum
E_{n}(g)\in\{\pm 1,\pm\sqrt{1-4g^{2}}\}
. If we choose any eigenbranch
n
, then from any two values of
g
we can obtain two eigenpairs

(|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g_{1})\rangle,\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g_{1})|)
and

(|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g_{2})\rangle,\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g_{2})|)
, with each eigenpair normalised so that

\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g_{i})|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g_{i})\rangle=1
. We regard these as the correlated pre-
and post-selected BQM states for each pair of
g
values. With the bipartition
\mathbb{A}|\mathbb{B}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is the first qubit and
\mathbb{B}
the second, we form the transition operator

\tau^{1|2}=|\Psi^{(n)}_{R}(g_{1})\rangle\langle\Psi^{(n)}_{L}(g_{2})|,

(112)

and its reduced transition matrix
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{B}}\,(\tau^{1|2})
.
The corresponding pseudo entropy

S^{\mathrm{P}}
and BUISVD transition entropy
S^{\mathrm{B}}
are obtained from the eigenvalues and BUI-singular
values of
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
respectively. Since this
\tau^{1|2}_{\mathbb{A}}
is a
2\times 2
matrix, the entropies can be calculated directly using
Table

1
, and they are plotted in
Fig.

7
.

6
Conclusions and outlook

The central aim of this work was to introduce unit-invariant singular values into quantum information theory and to use them to define generalised von Neumann–type entropies. After reviewing the basic construction and illustrating it with simple examples, we defined left-, right-, and bi-unit-invariant entanglement entropies for both quantum states and transition matrices. We computed these quantities explicitly and demonstrated their utility across a range of physically diverse and relevant examples, including random matrices, Chern–Simons link-complement states, and Biorthogonal Quantum Mechanics. Our selection of examples was deliberately non-exhaustive and intended primarily as a proof of concept.

We expect these mathematically well-defined tools and entanglement measures to admit many interesting and non-trivial applications in current developments across several areas of physics and mathematics. For instance, there has been significant recent interest in classifying phases and symmetries of general mixed states, notably via SymTFT-based approaches
53
. Since these constructions proceed through purifications and the study of their structural properties, our invariant-based entropies may offer a novel perspective on this problem. Moreover, it will be important to clarify the geometric interpretation of UISVD entropies in the context of (A)dS/CFT. As non-Hermitian quantum mechanics is beginning to play an increasingly prominent role in these settings, we anticipate that UISVD entropies will prove particularly useful there as well, see for example
26
. Further, interesting properties of other types of entropies based on rescaled singular values were discussed e.g. in
50
. On the other hand, various types of singular values underlying our work should be of interest in other contexts in random matrix theory. For example, the normalisations that arise in our diagonal scale functions are similar to those that arise in Sinkhorn algorithm and related bistochastic matrices
10
;
32
and deserve further scrutiny.

In many other settings, diagonal rescalings reflect a genuine freedom of units, normalisation, or residual gauge, rather than a numerical artefact. Such scalings appear across a broad range of problems: multi-channel and multiterminal transport and hydrodynamics
40
;
6
;
33
;
39
, tensor-network and string-net constructions where
F
-data is defined only up to gauge choices
37
;
35
;
22
, correlator-based spectroscopy where operator normalisations are conventional
15
;
5
, and calibration problems with per-sensor gain ambiguities
54
. Since the unit-consistent inverse is designed to respect diagonal changes of units, our diagonal-invariant UISVD entropy diagnostics provide a concise, convention-independent way to summarise “spectral” structure in these contexts. We leave analysis of all these ideas for future work.

Appendix A
Technical details on Unit-Invariant Singular Values

In this appendix we collect some explicit results used in computations in the paper. First, we derive unit invariant singular values, and for completeness also eigenvalues and ordinary singular values, for any complex
2\times 2
matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix},\qquad a_{i}\neq 0.

(113)

Note that for such matrices, the cycle ratio factor equals
\hat{a}\coloneq\big|({a_{1}a_{4}})/({a_{2}a_{3}})\big|^{\frac{1}{2}}
and determinant factor equals
\tilde{a}\coloneq|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}|^{2}
. We will also denote general
2\times 2
diagonal and unitary matrices by

\displaystyle D=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta_{1}&0\\
0&\delta_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad U=e^{i\alpha}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\beta}\cos(\theta)&e^{i\gamma}\sin(\theta)\\
-e^{-i\gamma}\sin(\theta)&e^{-i\beta}\cos(\theta)\\
\end{array}\right)\,,

where
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\displaystyle DAU=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta_{1}(a_{1}e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\cos(\theta)-a_{2}e^{i(\alpha-\gamma)}\sin(\theta))&\delta_{1}(a_{1}e^{i(\alpha+\gamma)}\sin(\theta)+a_{2}e^{i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos(\theta)\\
\delta_{2}(a_{3}e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\cos(\theta)-a_{4}e^{i(\alpha-\gamma)}\sin(\theta)&\delta_{2}(a_{3}e^{i(\alpha+\gamma)}\sin(\theta)+a_{4}e^{i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos(\theta))\\
\end{array}\right),

(122)

\displaystyle UAD^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta^{\prime}_{1}\left(a_{1}e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\cos(\theta)+a_{3}e^{i(\alpha+\gamma)}\sin(\theta)\right)&\delta^{\prime}_{2}\left(a_{2}e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\cos(\theta)+a_{4}e^{i(\alpha+\gamma)}\sin(\theta)\right)\\
\delta^{\prime}_{1}\left(a_{3}e^{i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos(\theta)-a_{1}e^{i(\alpha-\gamma)}\sin(\theta)\right)&\delta^{\prime}_{2}\left(a_{4}e^{i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos(\theta)-a_{2}e^{i(\alpha-\gamma)}\sin(\theta)\right)\end{array}\right),

(126)

\displaystyle DAD^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta_{1}\delta^{\prime}_{1}a_{1}&\delta_{1}\delta^{\prime}_{2}a_{2}\\
\delta_{2}\delta^{\prime}_{1}a_{3}&\delta_{2}\delta^{\prime}_{2}a_{4}\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Eigenvalues:

The eigenvalues of
A
are found from

\det(A-\lambda I)=(a_{1}-\lambda)(a_{4}-\lambda)-a_{2}a_{3}=0,

(130)

yielding

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{a_{1}+a_{4}\pm\sqrt{(a_{1}-a_{4})^{2}+4a_{2}a_{3}}}{2}.

(131)

Singular Values:

The singular values of
A
are the square roots of the eigenvalues of

A^{\dagger}A=\begin{pmatrix}|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}&\overline{a_{1}}a_{2}+\overline{a_{3}}a_{4}\\
a_{1}\overline{a_{2}}+a_{3}\overline{a_{4}}&|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(132)

Since

\operatorname{tr}(A^{\dagger}A)=|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2},\quad\det(A^{\dagger}A)=|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}|^{2},

(133)

the eigenvalues of
A^{\dagger}A
are obtained using
Eq.

131
as
\lambda^{\prime}_{\pm}
, which gives us the singular values of
A
as
\sigma_{\pm}=\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}_{\pm}}

\sigma_{\pm}=\sqrt{\frac{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}\pm\sqrt{(|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2})^{2}-4|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}|^{2}}}{2}}.

(134)

LUI-singular values:

The left diagonal scale function matrix in
Eq.

4
is defined using the Euclidean norm for each row

D^{\mathrm{L}}{(A)}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}\right).

(135)

The balanced matrix
A^{\mathrm{L}}
is given by

A^{\mathrm{L}}=D^{\mathrm{L}}\cdot A=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}}}&\frac{a_{2}}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}}}\\
\frac{a_{3}}{\sqrt{|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}&\frac{a_{4}}{\sqrt{|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}\end{bmatrix}.

(136)

The singular values of the balanced matrix
A^{\mathrm{L}}
, which are the left unit-invariant singular values
\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}_{\pm}
of
A
, are (using
Eq.

134
)

\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}_{\pm}=\sqrt{1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}|^{2}}{(|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2})(|a_{3}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2})}}}.

(137)

We can verify that these values are invariant under transformation (
122
). In fact, we can prove more generally that for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with any nonsingular diagonal
D\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and any unitary
U\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(A)=\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(DAU).

(138)

Proof:
Let
B\coloneq DAU
. Row
i
of
B
equals

B(i,:)=d_{i}\,A(i,:)\,U.

(139)

Now take Euclidean norms and use the facts that
\|cx\|_{2}=|c|\|x\|_{2}
and that
\|xU\|_{2}=\|x\|_{2}
for unitary
U
,

\|B(i,:)\|_{2}=\|d_{i}\,A(i,:)\,U\|_{2}=|d_{i}|\,\|A(i,:)\,U\|_{2}=|d_{i}|\,\|A(i,:)\|_{2}=|d_{i}|\,r_{i}.

(140)

So if
r_{i}>0
, then
(D^{\mathrm{L}}(B))_{ii}=1/(|d_{i}|r_{i})
. Using the diagonal expression above,

B^{\mathrm{L}}=D^{\mathrm{L}}(B)\,B=\mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{|d_{i}|r_{i}}\Big)\,\mathrm{diag}(d_{i})\,A\,U.

(141)

Factor the product of the two diagonal matrices

\mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{|d_{i}|r_{i}}\Big)\,\mathrm{diag}(d_{i})=\underbrace{\mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{d_{i}}{|d_{i}|}\Big)}_{\coloneq\Phi\ \text{diagonal unitary}}\;\underbrace{\mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{r_{i}}\Big)}_{=D^{\mathrm{L}}(A)},

(142)

and the
r_{i}=0
case is handled analogously with
(D^{\mathrm{L}}(B))_{ii}=1=(D^{\mathrm{L}}(A))_{ii}
from definition. Therefore

B^{\mathrm{L}}=\Phi\,D^{\mathrm{L}}(A)\,A\,U=\Phi\,A^{\mathrm{L}}\,U.

(143)

\Phi
is unitary (diagonal with entries of modulus
1
), and
U
is unitary, hence using property of usual singular values

\sigma(B^{\mathrm{L}})=\sigma(\Phi A^{\mathrm{L}}U)=\sigma(A^{\mathrm{L}}).

(144)

By definition
\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(A)=\sigma(A^{\mathrm{L}})
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(B)=\sigma(B^{\mathrm{L}})
.
Thus
\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(DAU)=\sigma^{\mathrm{L}}(A)
. An entirely analogous argument holds for RUISVD using columns instead of rows.

RUI-singular values:

The right diagonal scale function matrix
Eq.

9
is defined using the Euclidean norm for each column

D^{\mathrm{R}}{(A)}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}\right).

(145)

The balanced matrix
A^{\mathrm{R}}
is given by

A^{\mathrm{R}}=A\cdot D^{\mathrm{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}}}&\frac{a_{2}}{\sqrt{|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}\\
\frac{a_{3}}{\sqrt{|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}}}&\frac{a_{4}}{\sqrt{|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2}}}\end{bmatrix}.

(146)

The singular values of the balanced matrix
A^{\mathrm{R}}
, which are the right unit-invariant singular values
\sigma^{\mathrm{R}}_{\pm}
of
A
, are (using
Eq.

134
)

\sigma^{\mathrm{R}}_{\pm}=\sqrt{1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}|^{2}}{(|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2})(|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{4}|^{2})}}}.

(147)

We can verify that these values are invariant under transformation (
126
).

BUI-singular values:

Define

K=\log(\mathrm{Abs}[A])=\begin{bmatrix}\ln|a_{1}|&\ln|a_{2}|\\
\ln|a_{3}|&\ln|a_{4}|\end{bmatrix},\quad J_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},

(148)

where
J_{p}=\big[\frac{1}{p}\big]_{p\times p}
. So for
m\times n
matrix we compute

Q\;=\;J_{m}\,K\,J_{n}\;-\;K\,J_{n}\;-\;J_{m}\,K.

(149)

Here, dimensions
m=n=2
, and we set

P[A]\;=\;\exp(Q)\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}|a_{1}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}a_{2}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{3}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{4}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}|\quad\quad&|a_{1}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{2}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}a_{3}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{4}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}|\\[7.0pt]
|a_{1}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{2}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{3}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}a_{4}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}|\quad\quad&|a_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{2}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{3}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{4}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}|\end{bmatrix}.

(150)

Writing
P[A]
in the form
\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}
we
pick the top-left entry (index
(1,1)
) as the reference scale. Namely,

x_{1}=1,\quad x_{2}=\frac{P[A]_{2,1}}{P[A]_{1,1}},\qquad y_{1}=P[A]_{1,1},\quad y_{2}=P[A]_{1,2}.

(151)

Then

D^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(x_{1},\;x_{2}\bigr),\quad{D^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}}=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(y_{1},\;y_{2}\bigr).

(152)

A direct exponent check yields

D^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{\bigg|\dfrac{a_{1}\,a_{2}}{a_{3}\,a_{4}}}\bigg|\end{bmatrix},\quad{D^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}|a_{1}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}a_{2}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{3}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{4}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}|&0\\[3.0pt]
0&|a_{1}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{2}^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}a_{3}^{\tfrac{1}{4}}a_{4}^{-\tfrac{1}{4}}|\end{bmatrix}.

(153)

The balanced matrix
A^{\mathrm{B}}=D^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}AD^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}
is

A^{\mathrm{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\;|a_{1}|^{-3/4}\;\bigl|a_{4}/(a_{2}a_{3})\bigr|^{1/4}&a_{2}\;|a_{2}|^{-3/4}\;\bigl|a_{3}/(a_{1}a_{4})\bigr|^{1/4}\\[8.0pt]

(154)

The BUI-singular values of

\sigma^{\mathrm{B}}_{\pm}=\sqrt{\left(\sqrt{\bigg|\frac{a_{1}a_{4}}{a_{2}a_{3}}\bigg|}+\sqrt{\bigg|\frac{a_{2}a_{3}}{a_{1}a_{4}}\bigg|}\right)\ \pm\ \sqrt{\left(\sqrt{\bigg|\frac{a_{1}a_{4}}{a_{2}a_{3}}\bigg|}+\sqrt{\bigg|\frac{a_{2}a_{3}}{a_{1}a_{4}}\bigg|}\right)^{\!2}-\frac{\big|a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}\big|^{2}}{\big|a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}\big|}}}\,,

which further simplifies to

\sigma^{\mathrm{B}}_{\pm}=\left|\sqrt[4]{\frac{a_{1}a_{4}}{a_{2}a_{3}}}\ \pm\ \sqrt[4]{\frac{a_{2}a_{3}}{a_{1}a_{4}}}\right|,\qquad\text{ when }a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

A

Results from Random Matrix Theory

Here we collect known results from Random Matrix Theory and further prove certain Lemmas, which are required for proving
Theorems

2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
and
2.4
.

Notation and conventions.

For an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
M
with eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(M),\dots,\lambda_{n}(M)\in\mathbb{R}
, its
empirical spectral distribution (ESD)
is

\mathrm{ESD}(M)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(M)}.

(158)

We write
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
for weak convergence of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
.
For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, its
Stieltjes transform
is

m_{\mu}(z)\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{x-z}\,d\mu(x),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\coloneq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z>0\}.

(159)

For a Hermitian
M
, we write
m_{M}(z)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(M-zI)^{-1}
; then
m_{M}
is the Stieltjes transform of
\mathrm{ESD}(M)
. We use the Orlicz norms

\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}\coloneq\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}e^{|X|/t}\leq 2\},\qquad\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\coloneq\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}e^{|X|^{2}/t^{2}}\leq 2\}.

(160)

A (real) random variable is
sub-Gaussian
if
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
and
subexponential
if
\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}<\infty
.
For complex
X
, we interpret sub-Gaussian/subexponential in the same way with
|X|
(equivalently, it suffices that
\Re X
and
\Im X
are sub-Gaussian).
Throughout, for complex entries we use the convention
\mathbb{E}|X_{ij}|^{2}=1
when we say “variance
1
”. We write
A^{\dagger}
for the conjugate transpose of a complex matrix
A
, and
\|A\|_{\mathcal{O}}
for its operator (spectral) norm.
We write
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)
for probability.
For
\beta\in\{1,2\}
we use the shorthand
g_{\beta}
for a standard real/complex Gaussian:

g_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
g_{2}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
(circular, with
\mathbb{E}|g_{2}|^{2}=1
).

Lemma B.5
.

Let
T_{n}
be random
n\times n
matrices and
H_{n}\coloneq T_{n}T_{n}^{\dagger}
. If
\mathrm{ESD}(H_{n})\Rightarrow F

almost surely
with
\mathrm{supp}(F)\subset[0,b]
, then the empirical measure of singular values of
T_{n}
converges almost surely to the pushforward
F\circ f^{-1}
under
f(x)=\sqrt{x}
, supported on
[0,\sqrt{b}]
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1}(H_{n}),\dots,\lambda_{n}(H_{n})
be the eigenvalues of
H_{n}\coloneq T_{n}T_{n}^{\dagger}
.
The singular values of
T_{n}
are
\sigma_{i}(T_{n})=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(H_{n})}
.
Let
\mu_{n}\coloneq\mathrm{ESD}(H_{n})
and let
\nu_{n}
be the empirical measure of the singular values of
T_{n}
.
Then for every bounded continuous function
g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int g(s)\,d\nu_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(H_{n})})=\int g(\sqrt{x})\,d\mu_{n}(x).

(163)

If
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow F
almost surely and
x\mapsto g(\sqrt{x})
is bounded and continuous on
[0,\infty)
, then

\int g(\sqrt{x})\,d\mu_{n}(x)\to\int g(\sqrt{x})\,dF(x)
almost surely.
Hence
\nu_{n}\Rightarrow F\circ f^{-1}
almost surely where
f(x)=\sqrt{x}
, and
\mathrm{supp}(F)\subset[0,b]
implies
\mathrm{supp}(F\circ f^{-1})\subset[0,\sqrt{b}]
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let

where the entries
\{X_{ij}\}
of

are i.i.d., mean
0
, variance

, and sub-Gaussian.
Define the row and column Euclidean (

r_{i}\coloneq\|A_{n}(i,:)\|_{2},\qquad c_{j}\coloneq\|A_{n}(:,j)\|_{2}.

(164)

There exists a constant
C>0
(independent of
n

n

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\big|r_{i}-1\big|>C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-10},\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\big|c_{j}-1\big|>C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-10}.

(165)

Proof.

Fix
i

A_{n}=(1/\sqrt{n})X_{n}
, with
\mathbb{E}(X_{ij})=0

\mathbb{E}|X_{ij}|^{2}-|\mathbb{E}X_{ij}|^{2}=\text{Var}(X_{ij})=1

r_{i}^{2}=\|A_{n}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|X_{ik}|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[r_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|X_{ik}|^{2}=1.

(166)

Define centered variables
Z_{ik}\coloneq|X_{ik}|^{2}-1
so that

r_{i}^{2}-1=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{ik}.

(167)

Because
X_{ik}
is sub-Gaussian,
Z_{ik}
is
subexponential

(
63
, Lem. 2.7.4)
. Let
c_{0}\coloneq\|Z_{i1}\|_{\psi_{1}}
; since the

are i.i.d. in

\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\|Z_{ik}\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq c_{0}
. So

B.1
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|r_{i}^{2}-1\big|>u\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{ik}\right|>u\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c\,n\,\min\!\left(\frac{u^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}},\,\frac{u}{c_{0}}\right)\right].

(168)

We now choose the deviation level

u\coloneq c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},

(169)

with a constant
c_{1}>0
to be specified. For
n
large enough,
u/c_{0}\leq 1
, so

\min\!\left(\frac{u^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}},\frac{u}{c_{0}}\right)=\frac{u^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}.

(170)

Substitute this

into

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|r_{i}^{2}-1\big|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\left[-\,c\,n\cdot\frac{u^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right]\ =\ 2\exp\!\left[-\,\frac{cc_{1}^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}\,\log n\right].

(171)

Because constants are free to pick in a tail bound, we choose
c_{1}
large enough such that
8
8

8

For notational convenience we choose 12, can be any large
value.

\frac{cc_{1}^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}\geq 12\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|r_{i}^{2}-1\big|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ 2\exp(-12\log n)\ =\ 2n^{-12}.

(172)

Define events
E_{i}\coloneq\big\{|r_{i}^{2}-1|>c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\big\}
.
Then

\Big\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|r_{i}^{2}-1|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\Big\}\ =\ \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}.

(173)

By the union bound
\mathbb{P}\big(\cup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{i})
and the previous estimate,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|r_{i}^{2}-1|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}2n^{-12}\ =\ 2n\cdot n^{-12}\ =\ 2n^{-11}\leq n^{-10}

(174)

for all sufficiently large
n
. Now,

|r_{i}-1|=\frac{|r_{i}^{2}-1|}{\,\sqrt{r_{i}^{2}}+1\,}=\frac{|r_{i}^{2}-1|}{\,r_{i}+1\,}.

(175)

Therefore, always (since

|r_{i}-1|\ \leq\ |r_{i}^{2}-1|.

(176)

So we have the implication

\Big\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|r_{i}-1|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\Big\}\ \subseteq\ \Big\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|r_{i}^{2}-1|>c_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\Big\}.

(177)

Therefore, using
Eq.

174

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|r_{i}-1|>C\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ n^{-10}.

(178)

An entirely analogous argument, with rows replaced by columns, yields the bound for

Lemma B.8
.

(183)

Proof.

Let

. Then

DMD-M=(I+\Delta)M(I+\Delta)-M=\Delta M+M\Delta+\Delta M\Delta.

(184)

Hence, by the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity of

,

\displaystyle\|DMD-M\|_{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta M\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|M\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta M\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}

(185)

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|M\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|M\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|M\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}

(186)

\displaystyle=(2\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2})\,\|M\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

(187)

Finally,
\|M\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\|AA^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq\|A\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|A^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\|A\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}

Lemma B.9
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian and set
E\coloneq N-M
.
For
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
with
y>0
, define the resolvents
R_{M}(z)\coloneq(M-zI)^{-1}
,
R_{N}(z)\coloneq(N-zI)^{-1}
and their normalised traces
m_{M}(z)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}R_{M}(z)
,
m_{N}(z)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}R_{N}(z)
. Then

|m_{N}(z)-m_{M}(z)|\ \leq\ \frac{\|E\|_{\mathcal{O}}}{y^{2}}.

(188)

Consequently, if
\|E_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\to 0
and
m_{M_{n}}(z)\to m(z)
for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, then
m_{N_{n}}(z)\to m(z)
for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
as well. In particular,
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
have the same limiting ESD (by uniqueness of Stieltjes transforms).

Proof.

We use the resolvent identity. For
E\coloneq N-M
,

\displaystyle R_{N}(z)-R_{M}(z)

\displaystyle=(N-zI)^{-1}-(M-zI)^{-1}

(189)

\displaystyle=(M-zI)^{-1}\big((M-zI)-(N-zI)\big)(N-zI)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-R_{M}(z)\,E\,R_{N}(z).

Therefore

|m_{N}(z)-m_{M}(z)|=\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\big(R_{N}(z)-R_{M}(z)\big)\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}\,\big|\mathrm{Tr}\big(R_{M}(z)ER_{N}(z)\big)\big|.

(190)

Using the bound
\lvert\mathrm{Tr}(X)\rvert\leq n\|X\|_{\mathcal{O}}
and submultiplicativity of the operator norm,

\bigl|\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(R_{M}(z)ER_{N}(z)\bigr)\bigr|\leq n\,\|R_{M}(z)ER_{N}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq n\,\|R_{M}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\|E\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\|R_{N}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

(191)

Therefore

|m_{N}(z)-m_{M}(z)|\leq\|R_{M}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\|E\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\|R_{N}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

(192)

Diagonalise
M=U\Lambda U^{\dagger}
with
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then

R_{M}(z)=(M-zI)^{-1}=U(\Lambda-zI)^{-1}U^{\dagger},\quad\|R_{M}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{k}-z|}.

(193)

Because
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
with
y>0
and
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\lambda_{k}-z|\geq\Im z=y
, hence
\|R_{M}(z)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 1/y
. The same holds for
R_{N}(z)
. Therefore

|m_{N}(z)-m_{M}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{y}\,\|E\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{1}{y}=\frac{\|E\|_{\mathcal{O}}}{y^{2}}.

(194)

Finally, if
\|E_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\to 0
and
m_{M_{n}}(z)\to m(z)
for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
then the bound
Eq.

188
yields
m_{N_{n}}(z)\to m(z)
for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
as well.
By uniqueness of Stieltjes transforms,
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
have the same limiting ESD.
∎

.

(199)

where we set

Y
is subexponential (see, e.g.,
(

B.1
, for all

Remark B.2
.

If
X_{ij}

\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)
then

If

\,\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
(with

, as computed in Lemma

Lemma B.12
.

Let
G_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and let
G_{2}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
where
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
denotes the standard circular complex normal, i.e.
G_{2}=X+\mathrm{i}Y
with
X,Y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
(equivalently
\mathbb{E}|G_{2}|^{2}=1
).
Then

c_{\beta=1}\coloneq\mathbb{E}\log|G_{1}|=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\log 2),\qquad c_{\beta=2}\coloneq\mathbb{E}\log|G_{2}|=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma,

(213)

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Numerically,

c_{\beta=1}\approx-0.6351814227
and
c_{\beta=2}\approx-0.2886078324
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the gamma function and let
\psi(z)\coloneq\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
denote the digamma function.
Let
Y\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\theta)
with shape
\alpha>0
and scale
\theta>0
, i.e. with density

f_{Y}(y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^{\alpha}}\,y^{\alpha-1}e^{-y/\theta}\mathbf{1}_{y>0}.

(214)

Then

\mathbb{E}\log Y=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\log y)\,f_{Y}(y)\,dy.

(215)

Substituting
y=\theta t
gives

\mathbb{E}\log Y=\log\theta+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\log t\,dt=\log\theta+\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=\log\theta+\psi(\alpha).

(216)

where we used
\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\,dt
and (for
\alpha>0
) differentiation under the integral sign, justified by dominated convergence, to obtain

\Gamma^{\prime}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\log t\,dt
.
For
G_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, the change of variables
V\coloneq G_{1}^{2}
gives the density

f_{V}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}e^{-v/2}\mathbf{1}_{v>0},

(217)

which is
\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac{1}{2},2)
(shape
\tfrac{1}{2}
, scale
2
). Hence

\mathbb{E}\log|G_{1}|=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\log(G_{1}^{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\psi(\tfrac{1}{2})+\log 2\big).

(218)

For
G_{2}=X+\mathrm{i}Y
with
X,Y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, write

X=\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}
and
Y=\frac{Y_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}
with
X_{0},Y_{0}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Then

|G_{2}|^{2}=X^{2}+Y^{2}=\frac{X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}}{2}.

(219)

Since
S\coloneq X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}
has density
f_{S}(s)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-s/2}\mathbf{1}_{s>0}
(i.e.
\chi^{2}_{2}=\mathrm{Gamma}(1,2)
),
it follows that
|G_{2}|^{2}
has density
e^{-u}\mathbf{1}_{u>0}
, i.e.
|G_{2}|^{2}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1,1)
.
Therefore

\mathbb{E}\log|G_{2}|=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\log(|G_{2}|^{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi(1).

(220)

Using
\psi(1)=-\gamma
and
\psi(\tfrac{1}{2})=-\gamma-2\log 2

(
16
, §5.4(ii), Eqns. (5.4.12)-(5.4.13))
gives

c_{\beta=1}=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\log 2)
and
c_{\beta=2}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma
.
The numerical values follow from
\gamma\approx 0.5772156649

(
16
, §5.2(ii), Eq. (5.2.3))
.
∎

Lemma B.13
.

Let
A_{n}=(a_{ij})
be Wigner-
\beta
(GOE/GUE) with the
1/\sqrt{n}
scaling and set

r_{i}\coloneq\|A_{n}(i,:)\|_{2}
and
c_{j}\coloneq\|A_{n}(:,j)\|_{2}
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that, with probability at least
1-Cn^{-10}
,

\max_{i}\bigl|r_{i}-1\bigr|\;\leq\;C\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}},\qquad\max_{j}\bigl|c_{j}-1\bigr|\;\leq\;C\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}.

(221)

Proof.

We prove the row bound; the column bound is identical by symmetry. Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and write

r_{i}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{ik}|^{2}=\sum_{k\neq i}|a_{ik}|^{2}+|a_{ii}|^{2}.

(222)

For Wigner-
\beta
under the
1/\sqrt{n}
scaling, the off-diagonals
\{a_{ik}:k\neq i\}
are independent, mean zero, with

\mathbb{E}|a_{ik}|^{2}=1/n
; the diagonal has
\mathbb{E}|a_{ii}|^{2}=\nu_{\beta}/n
with
\nu_{\beta}=2
in GOE and
\nu_{\beta}=1
in GUE.
Hence

\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}=\frac{n-1}{n}+\frac{\nu_{\beta}}{n}=\begin{cases}1+\frac{1}{n},&\beta=1\text{ (GOE)},\\[2.0pt]
1,&\beta=2\text{ (GUE)}.\end{cases}

(223)

Define centered variables
Y_{ik}\coloneq|a_{ik}|^{2}-\mathbb{E}|a_{ik}|^{2}
for
k\neq i
and
X_{ii}\coloneq|a_{ii}|^{2}-\mathbb{E}|a_{ii}|^{2}
.
Since
a_{ik}
is sub-Gaussian with
\|a_{ik}\|_{\psi_{2}}=O(n^{-1/2})
, Vershynin’s inequality
(
63
, Lem. 2.7.4)
gives

\|Y_{ik}\|_{\psi_{1}}=O(n^{-1})
uniformly in
k\neq i
(and in
\beta
), and similarly
\|X_{ii}\|_{\psi_{1}}=O(n^{-1})
.
Therefore, applying Bernstein’s inequality
Theorem

B.1
to the
n
independent mean-zero variables

\{Y_{ik}:k\neq i\}\cup\{X_{ii}\}
yields, for
u\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigl|r_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}\bigr|>u\Big)\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\big(-cnu^{2}\big),

(224)

for some constant
c>0
.
Choose
u\coloneq C_{0}\sqrt{(\log n)/n}
with
C_{0}
large enough so that the RHS is
\leq n^{-11}
. On the event

\{|r_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}|\leq u\}
we get

\bigl|r_{i}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}}\bigr|\ =\ \frac{|r_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}|}{r_{i}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}}}\ \leq\ \,\frac{|r_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}|}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}}}\ \leq\ \,|r_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}|\ \leq\ u.

(225)

Hence

|r_{i}-1|\ \leq\ \bigl|r_{i}-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}}\bigr|+\bigl|\sqrt{\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}}-1\bigr|\ \leq\ u+\bigl|\mathbb{E}r_{i}^{2}-1\bigr|\ \leq\ C_{0}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}+\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\beta=1\}}}{n}.

(226)

Absorb the negligible
1/n
term into the
\sqrt{(\log n)/n}
term by increasing
C_{0}
if needed, to obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|r_{i}-1|>C_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ n^{-11}.

(227)

A union bound over
i=1,\dots,n
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{i}|r_{i}-1|>C\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\right)\ \leq\ Cn^{-10}\

(228)

for an adjusted constant
C>0
, proving the first part of
Eq.

221
. The column bound follows identically by
applying the same argument to
\{a_{ki}\}_{k}
.
∎

Lemma B.14
.

Let
A_{n}=(a_{ij})
be Wigner-
\beta
with the
1/\sqrt{n}
scaling, and define the row/column/grand log-means

m_{i}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log|a_{ik}|,\qquad n_{j}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log|a_{kj}|,\qquad\bar{m}\coloneq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\log|a_{ik}|.

(229)

Let
c_{\beta}
as in Lemma
B.12
. Then
\max_{i}\big|m_{i}-(c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n)\big|=O_{\mathrm{a.s.}}\!\big(\sqrt{(\log n)/n}\big)
, and similarly for
n_{j}
and
\bar{m}
.

Proof.

We give the row bound; the column bound is the same, and the grand average follows similarly. Set
\mu\coloneq c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n
. Fix
i
and split

m_{i}-\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\neq i}\bigl(\log|a_{ik}|-\mu\bigr)\;+\;\underbrace{\Big(\frac{1}{n}\log|a_{ii}|-\frac{\mu}{n}\Big)}_{\coloneq\,\Delta_{i}}.

(230)

For
k\neq i
we have
a_{ik}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}g_{\beta}/\sqrt{n}
, so, taking
Y_{ik}\coloneq\log|a_{ik}|
where
k\neq i
, we get

\mathbb{E}Y_{ik}=\mathbb{E}\log|g_{\beta}|-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n=c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n=\mu
.
The variables
\{Y_{ik}\}_{k\neq i}
are independent. Set

W_{ik}\coloneq Y_{ik}-\mu=\log|a_{ik}|-\Big(c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n\Big),\qquad k\neq i.

(231)

Following the steps of Lemma
B.11
, each
W_{ik}
is centered sub-exponential with parameters independent of
n
and
k
. Therefore, for any
u\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\neq i}W_{ik}\right|>u\right)\ \leq\ 2\exp(-cnu^{2}),

(232)

for some absolute
c>0
(depending only on
\beta
).
It remains to control the diagonal contribution
\Delta_{i}
,

\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\log|a_{ii}|-\frac{1}{n}\Big(c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n\Big).

(233)

Since
a_{ii}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\sqrt{\nu}_{\beta}g_{1}}/{\sqrt{n}}
with
\nu_{\beta}=2
for GOE and
\nu_{\beta}=1
for GUE,

\mathbb{E}\log|a_{ii}|=\mathbb{E}\log|\sqrt{\nu}_{\beta}g_{1}|-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n=\Big(\mathbb{E}\log|g_{1}|+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\nu_{\beta}\Big)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n.

(234)

Thus
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}\log|g_{1}|-c_{\beta}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\nu_{\beta}\Big)=O(1/n)
, and
\Delta_{i}
itself is sub-exponential with
scale
O(1/n)
(being
n^{-1}
times a sub-exponential variable). In particular, for
u\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|\Delta_{i}-\mathbb{E}\Delta_{i}|>u/3\big)\ \leq\ 2\exp(-c^{\prime}nu)\ \leq\ 2\exp(-c^{\prime}nu^{2}),

(235)

for some
c^{\prime}>0
(the last inequality since
u\in(0,1)
). Moreover,
\;|\mathbb{E}\Delta_{i}|=\frac{1}{n}\big|\mathbb{E}\log|a_{ii}|-\mu\big|=O(1/n)\leq u/3
for all large
n
when
u=K\sqrt{(\log n)/n}
.
Combining
Eq.

232
and
Eq.

235
by a union bound gives

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|m_{i}-(c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n)\big|>u\Big)\ \leq\ C_{1}\exp(-c_{1}nu^{2}).

(236)

A union bound over
i=1,\dots,n
yields the result. The case for
n_{j}
is identical. For the grand mean, decompose

\bar{m}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq k}\log|a_{ik}|+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log|a_{ii}|=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<k\leq n}\log|a_{ik}|+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log|a_{ii}|.

(237)

The variables
\{\log|a_{ik}|\}_{i<k}
are independent and identically distributed with mean
c_{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n
and are subexponential (as in Lemma
B.11
).
Applying
Theorem

B.1
to the sum over
i<k
gives a tail bound of the form

\mathbb{P}(|\cdot|>u)\leq 2\exp(-cn^{2}u^{2})
for
u\in(0,1)
.
The diagonal term is
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log|a_{ii}|
, which has
O((\log n)/n)
magnitude in expectation and subexponential fluctuations of scale
O(1/n)
, hence is negligible at the level
u_{n}\coloneq K\sqrt{(\log n)/n}
.
Summing the resulting bounds over
n
and applying Borel-Cantelli yields the stated almost sure control of
\bar{m}
.

\displaystyle\|(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n})A_{n}(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n})\|_{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle\geq\ \|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}-\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\big(\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\big)

(261)

\displaystyle-\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

On the high-probability event,

\|A^{\mathrm{B}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\ \geq\ e^{-c_{\star}}\sqrt{n}\,\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\Big(1-2\delta_{n}-\delta_{n}^{2}\Big)\ =\ 2e^{-c_{\star}}\sqrt{n}\,\big(1-o(1)\big),

(262)

since
\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\to 2
and
\delta_{n}\to 0
. Together with the upper bound,

\frac{\|A^{\mathrm{B}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \longrightarrow\ 2e^{-c_{\star}}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(263)

For the normalised matrices
T_{n}\coloneq n^{-1/2}A^{\mathrm{B}}_{n}
, rewrite as

T_{n}=e^{-c_{\star}}\,(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n})\,A_{n}\,(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}).

(264)

Set

H_{n}\coloneq T_{n}T_{n}^{\dagger},\qquad\widehat{H}_{n}\coloneq e^{-2c_{\star}}A_{n}A_{n}^{\dagger}.

(265)

Set

P_{n}\coloneq A_{n}(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}),\qquad\widetilde{H}_{n}\coloneq e^{-2c_{\star}}P_{n}P_{n}^{\dagger},

(266)

so that

H_{n}=e^{-2c_{\star}}(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n})\,P_{n}P_{n}^{\dagger}\,(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}).

(267)

Applying Lemma
B.8
with
D=I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}
and

gives

\|H_{n}-\widetilde{H}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq e^{-2c_{\star}}\bigl(2\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}\bigr)\,\|P_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}.

(268)

Next,

\widetilde{H}_{n}-\widehat{H}_{n}=e^{-2c_{\star}}\Big(P_{n}P_{n}^{\dagger}-A_{n}A_{n}^{\dagger}\Big)=e^{-2c_{\star}}A_{n}\Big((I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n})(I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n})^{\dagger}-I\Big)A_{n}^{\dagger},

(269)

hence

\|\widetilde{H}_{n}-\widehat{H}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq e^{-2c_{\star}}\bigl(\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}+(\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}\bigr)\,\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}.

(270)

Finally,
\|P_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\,\|I+\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}
, so combining the last two displays yields

\|H_{n}-\widehat{H}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq e^{-2c_{\star}}\,C\bigl(\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{L}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta^{\mathrm{B_{R}}}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)\,\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}

(271)

for all large
n
on the same event (absorbing quadratic terms into the constant

since

On the high-probability event,
Eqs.

255
,
256
and
242
give

\|H_{n}-\widehat{H}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\ =\ O\!\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\Big)\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ 0

on the high-probability event. By Lemma
B.9
,
\mathrm{ESD}(H_{n})
and
\mathrm{ESD}(\widehat{H}_{n})
have the same limiting law. By

B.2
,

The failure probabilities are

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Write
r_{i}\coloneq\|A_{n}(i,:)\|_{2}
and
c_{j}\coloneq\|A_{n}(:,j)\|_{2}
, and define
D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\coloneq\mathrm{Diag}(1/r_{1},\dots,1/r_{n})
,

D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}\coloneq\mathrm{Diag}(1/c_{1},\dots,1/c_{n})
so that
A_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}=D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}A_{n}
and
A_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}=A_{n}D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}
. Let
\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\coloneq\max_{i}|r_{i}-1|
and
\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}\coloneq\max_{j}|c_{j}-1|
. Then

\|D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}-I\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\max_{i}\bigg|\frac{1}{r_{i}}-1\bigg|=\max_{i}\frac{|1-r_{i}|}{r_{i}}\leq\frac{\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}}{\min_{i}r_{i}},\qquad\|D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}-I\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq\frac{\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}{\min_{j}c_{j}}.

(273)

By Lemma
B.13
, for each
n
we have
\mathbb{P}(\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}>\tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{or}\ \delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}>\tfrac{1}{2})\leq Cn^{-10}
.
Since
\sum_{n}Cn^{-10}<\infty
, the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that
\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}},\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
for all sufficiently large
n
almost surely; hence

\min_{i}r_{i},\min_{j}c_{j}\geq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
and therefore

\|D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}-I\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 2\,\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}},\qquad\|D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}-I\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 2\,\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}=O\!\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{n}}\Big).

(274)

By Borel–Cantelli this holds almost surely eventually.
Set
\Delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\coloneq D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}-I
and
\Delta_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}\coloneq D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}-I
.
Consider

M_{n}\coloneq(A_{n}^{\mathrm{L}})(A_{n}^{\mathrm{L}})^{\dagger}=D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\,A_{n}A_{n}^{\dagger}\,D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}},\qquad N_{n}\coloneq A_{n}A_{n}^{\dagger}.

(275)

By Lemma
B.8
,

\|M_{n}-N_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\;\leq\;\bigl(2\|\Delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\|_{\mathcal{O}}+\|\Delta_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}\bigr)\,\|N_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

(276)

Theorem

B.4
implies
\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\to 2
almost surely, hence
\|N_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\|A_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}\to 4
.
Therefore
\|M_{n}-N_{n}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\to 0
almost surely. By Lemma
B.9

M_{n}
and
N_{n}
have the same limiting ESD.
Since the non-zero eigenvalues of
N_{n}
(resp.
M_{n}
) are the squares of the singular values of
A_{n}
(resp.
A_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}
),
the singular-value ESDs of
A_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
A_{n}
have the same limit. Finally, by
Theorem

B.4
, the eigenvalue ESD of
A_{n}
converges to semicircle on
[-2,2]
,
hence its singular-value ESD converges to the quarter–circle on
[0,2]
(pushforward by
x\mapsto|x|
; Lemma
B.5
).
The right-balanced case
A_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}
is identical, replacing
D_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}
by
D_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

and define the row/column/grand log-means

By Proposition

By Lemma
