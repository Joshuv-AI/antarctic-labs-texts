Unconventional Superconductivity Mediated by Nematic Fluctuations in a Multi-Orbital System - Application to doped FeSe
Kazi Ranjibul Islam, Andrey Chubukov*

Abstract
We analyze superconductivity in a multi-orbital fermionic system near the onset of a nematic order, using doped FeSe as an example. We associate nematicity with a spontaneous polarization between d xz d_{\text{xz}} and d yz d_{\text{yz}} orbitals (a Pomeranchuk-type order) and analyze the pairing mediated by soft nematic fluctuations. Such a pairing gives rise to a highly anisotropic gap function whose structure strongly varies with temperature, and leads to strongly non-BCS behavior in thermodynamics, spectroscopy and transport. We compute the specific heat and its directional variation with a magnetic field, magnetic susceptibility, density of states, tunneling conductance, Raman intensity, superfluid stiffness and penetration depth without and with impurity scattering and for the latter computed also optical conductivity and T c T_{c} variation. We find good agreement with the existing data for FeSe 1-x S x and FeSe 1-x Te x and suggest new experiments.

Model Hamiltonian and Pairing Interaction

II.1
Model

The electronic structure of pure/doped FeSe in the tetragonal phase consists of two non-equal hole pockets, centered at
\Gamma
, and two electron pockets centered at
X=(\pi,0)
and
Y=(0,\pi)
in the
1
FeBZ. The hole pockets are composed of
d_{\text{xz}}
and
d_{\text{yz}}
fermions, the X pocket is composed of

d_{\text{yz}}
and
d_{xy}
fermions, and the
Y
pocket is composed of
d_{\text{xz}}
and
d_{xy}
fermions.
The inner hole pocket is quite small and likely does not play much role for nematic order and superconductivity. We assume that heavy
d_{xy}
fermions also do not play much role and consider an effective two-orbital model with a single
d_{\text{xz}}/d_{\text{yz}}
circular hole pocket, and mono-orbital electron pockets (
d_{\text{yz}}
X-pocket and
d_{\text{xz}}
Y-pocket). We define fermionic operators for mono-orbital Y and X pockets as
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively
(
f_{1,\mathbf{k},\sigma}=d_{xz,\mathbf{k}+Y,\sigma}
,
f_{2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}=d_{yz,\mathbf{k}+X,\sigma}

The band operator for the hole pocket is
[
42
]

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}=\cos{\theta_\bk}d_{yz,\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\sin{\theta_\bk}d_{xz,\mathbf{k},\sigma}.

(1)

The kinetic energy is quadratic in fermionic densities and have the following expression

\displaystyle H_{\text{Kin}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\xi_{h}(\mathbf{k})\,h_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}h_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\xi_{Y}(\mathbf{k})f_{1,\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}f_{1,\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\xi_{X}(\mathbf{k})f_{2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}f_{2,\mathbf{k},\sigma},

(2)

where

is isotropic dispersion of the band index

There are
14

-symmetric interactions involving low-energy

orbital states near
\Gamma,X
and

pockets. We present these interactions in Appendix

can be separated into density-density, exchange and pair-hopping interactions. In the band basis, density-density interactions are the ones with a small momentum transfer within a hole or an electron pocket, while the exchange and pair-hopping interactions are the ones with momentum transfer comparable to the distance between hole and electron pockets. For our analysis of superconductivity near the end point of a

\displaystyle H^{\text{den}}_{\text{int}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\rho_{j}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{q})V_{i,j}^{\text{den}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q};\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}),

is the density operator for the

) and the interaction vertices

\displaystyle V_{h,h}^{\text{den}}=

\displaystyle+\tilde{U}_{4}\Big\{\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}+\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}\Big\}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\tilde{U}}_{4}\Big\{\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}+\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}\Big\}

\displaystyle+\bar{U}_{4}\Big\{\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}+\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{p}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}}\Big\},

(4)

\displaystyle V_{h,Y}^{\text{den}}

\displaystyle=U_{1}\,\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}+\bar{U}_{1}\,\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}},

(5)

\displaystyle V_{h,X}^{\text{den}}

\displaystyle=U_{1}\,\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}+\bar{U}_{1}\,\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}},

\displaystyle V_{X,X}^{\text{den}}

II.2
Nematic susceptibility

We associate the nematic order with the d-wave Pomeranchuk order. In the orbital basis, this order is an orbital polarization (densities of
d_{\text{xz}}
and
d_{\text{yz}}
fermions split). In the band basis, we introduce two
d-
wave order parameters on hole and electron pockets:
\phi_{h}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\langle h_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}h_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\rangle\cos 2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
and

\phi_{e}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle f_{2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}f_{2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}\rangle-\langle f_{1,\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}f_{1,\mathbf{k},\sigma}\rangle=\phi_{x}-\phi_{y}
, where
\phi_{x/y}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle f_{1/2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}f_{1/2,\mathbf{k},\sigma}\rangle
. We introduce an infinitesimally small
Z_{2}
symmetry breaking external perturbation to the interaction Hamiltonian in the form of
\phi_{0}\,\cos 2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,h^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}h_{\mathbf{k}}
and define nematic susceptibility as
\phi_{h}=\chi_{\text{nem}}\,\phi_{0}
. The onset of the nematic order is signaled by the divergence of
\chi_{\text{nem}}
. Within the mean field analysis, we find a set of two coupled self-consistent equations for
\phi_{h}
and
\phi_{e}
(see Appendix
B
for the details),

\displaystyle\phi_{h}

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}-\phi_{h}\,U^{d}_{h}\,\Pi_{h}^{d}-\phi_{e}\,U^{d}_{he}\,\Pi_{e},

(8)

\displaystyle\phi_{e}

\displaystyle=-\phi_{e}\,U^{d}_{e}\Pi_{e}-2\phi_{h}\,U^{d}_{he}\Pi_{h}^{d}.

(9)

Here

U_{h}^{d}=(U_{4}-2\tilde{U}_{4}+\tilde{\tilde{U}}_{4})/2,~~U_{e}^{d}=U_{5}-2\tilde{U}_{5},~~U_{he}^{d}=(U_{1}-\bar{U}_{1})

(10)

are the d-wave components of the intra-pocket and inter-pocket density-density interactions for fermions near hole and electron pockets, and

\Pi_{h}^{d}=-\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits G_{p}^{h}\,G_{p}^{h}\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{p}},~~\Pi_{e}=-(1/2)\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle p$}}$}}\nolimits\left(G_{p}^{X}\,G_{p}^{X}+G_{p}^{Y}\,G_{p}^{Y}\right)

(11)

are the polarization bubbles for fermions at hole and electron pockets. As defined, the polarization bubbles are positive
\Pi_{h}^{d},\Pi_{e}>0
. Eq. (
9
) is found from two other equations:
\phi_{y}=-\phi_{y}\,U_{5}\,\Pi_{e}+\phi_{h}\,U_{he}\,\Pi_{h}^{d}-2\,\phi_{x}\,\tilde{U}_{5}\,\Pi_{e}
and
\phi_{x}=-\phi_{x}\,U_{5}\,\Pi_{e}-\phi_{h}\,U_{he}\,\Pi_{h}^{d}-2\,\phi_{y}\,\tilde{U}_{5}\,\Pi_{e}
. Combining Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
), and evaluating further the nematic susceptibility at a small but finite momentum
q
, we obtain the expression for the nematic susceptibility,

\displaystyle\chi_{\text{nem}}(q)=\dfrac{1}{\left(1+U_{h}^{d}\,\Pi_{h}^{d}(q)\right)\left(1+U_{e}^{d}\,\Pi_{e}(q)\right)-2U_{he}^{2}\Pi_{h}^{d}(q)\Pi_{e}(q)},

(12)

where
\Pi_{h,e}^{d}(q)
is the polarization bubble with momentum
q
. As stated before the onset of the orbital order is set when

\chi_{\text{nem}}(0)
diverges.
This can happen when
U^{d}_{he}
is strong enough, specifically when
2\,(U_{\text{he}}^{\text{d}})^{2}\geq U_{\text{h}}^{\text{d}}\,U^{d}_{e}

[
43
]
assuming all interactions are repulsive. In this case, nematic order (known as
d^{\pm}
) with different signs of
\phi_{h}
and
\phi_{e}
develops. This is quite similar to sign-changing
s^{+-}
order, which develops when pair hopping exceeds intra-pocket repulsion in the particle-particle channel.

II.3
Pairing interaction

The analysis of the pairing interaction mediated by soft nematic fluctuations requires care as these fluctuations are at small momentum transfer and for this reason they do not affect the pair hopping term between hole and electron pockets, which is the key element for spin-mediated superconductivity.
Also, the bare pairing interaction at small momentum transfer is repulsive, and if we just dress it by nematic fluctuations, we find that the repulsion only gets stronger. Despite all this, the pairing interaction
V_{\text{eff}}(k,-k;p,-p)
, mediated by nematic fluctuations, does become attractive near
x_{\text{c}}
, although for a rather special reason.
Namely, as we just said, the driving force for a
d-
wave Pomeranchuk order is d-wave density-density interaction between hole and electron pockets,
U^{d}_{he}
.
By itself,
U^{d}_{he}
does not contribute to pairing, however taken at the second order, it produces an effective attractive interaction between fermion on the same pocket. The full pairing interaction, proportional to the susceptibility for a
d-
wave Pomeranchuk order, is obtained by collecting ladder and bubble diagrams, which contain d-wave polarization bubbles at a small momentum transfer. This can be done analytically (see Refs.
[
44
,
45
]
for detail). Because
\mathbf{q}
is small, the dressed pairing interactions are between fermions on only hole pocket or only electron pockets:
V_{\text{eff}}^{\text{h}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=V_{\text{eff}}^{\text{h}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2,-(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2);\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2,-(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2)
,
V_{\text{eff}}^{\text{e}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=V_{\text{eff}}^{\text{e}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2,-(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2);\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2,-(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2)
. We find

\displaystyle V_{\text{eff}}^{\text{h}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=A_{h}^{(0)}-A_{h}^{(1)}(U^{d}_{he})^{2}\,\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\chi_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{q})+...

\displaystyle V_{\text{eff}}^{e}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=A_{e}^{(0)}-A_{e}^{(1)}(U^{d}_{he})^{2}\,\chi_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{q})+...,

(13)

where
A_{h}^{(0)}=\frac{U^{d}_{h}}{1+U^{d}_{h}\Pi^{d}_{h}(\mathbf{q})}
,
A_{e}^{(0)}=\frac{U^{d}_{e}}{1+U^{d}_{e}\Pi_{e}(\mathbf{q})}
,
A_{h}^{(1)}=\dfrac{\Pi_{e}}{1+U_{h}^{d}\,\Pi_{h}^{d}(\mathbf{q})}
and
A_{e}{(1)}=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\Pi_{h}^{d}}{1+U^{d}_{e}\,\Pi_{e}(\mathbf{q})}
.
The dots stand for other terms which do not contain
\Pi_{h}^{d}(\mathbf{q})
and
\Pi_{e}^{d}(\mathbf{q})
and are therefore not sensitive to the nematic instability. We see that each interaction contains two terms. The first is the dressed intra-pocket pairing interaction.
It does get renormalized, but remains repulsive and non-singular at the nematic instability. The second term is the interaction, induced by
U^{d}_{he}
. It is attractive and scales with the nematic susceptibility.
We emphasize that the attraction is induced by inter-pocket density-density interaction, despite that relevant nematic fluctuations are with small momenta and the pairing interactions involve fermions from the same pocket. Because the nematic susceptibility diverges at
x_{\text{c}}
, the attractive component, induced by nematic fluctuations, necessary exceeds the bare intra-pocket repulsion in some range around
x_{\text{c}}
, i.e., the full intra-pocket pairing interaction
becomes attractive. This attractive interaction is, however, rather peculiar on the hole pocket because it inherits from
U^{d}_{he}
the
d-
wave form-factor

A_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}}=\cos^{2}{(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\theta_{\mathbf{p}})}
, where
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\theta_{\mathbf{p}}
specify the location of the fermions. A similar pairing interaction has been earlier suggested for one-band models on
phenomenological grounds
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
. We emphasize that for our microscopic derivation, the presence of hole and electron bands is the necessary condition
for a nematic-mediated superconductivity.

II.4
Gap Structure

To simplify the discussion, we neglect
A^{(0)}_{h}
and
A^{(0)}_{e}
in Eqs. (
13
), i.e.,
keep only the components of the pairing interaction proportional to the nematic susceptibility and
also assume that
A^{(1)}_{h}>A^{(1)}_{e}
, in which case the strongest pairing interaction is for fermions on a hole pocket.
We also treat
\chi_{nem}(q)
as static. The full analysis, which we leave for further study, should involve the
dynamical nematic
\chi_{nem}(\mathbf{q},\Omega_{m})
, like it was done for a phenomenological one-band model in Refs.
[
49
,
50
]
.

For fermions on the hole pocket the pairing interaction is

V_{h}(\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k};\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p})=V_{h}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=-\cos^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\theta_{\mathbf{p}})\tilde{\chi}_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})
,
where

\theta_{\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{p})}
is the angle between

\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{p})
and the direction towards electron pockets (
\Gamma-X
or
\Gamma-Y
in 1Fe Brillouin zone).
and
\tilde{\chi}_{\text{nem}}=A_{h}\left(U_{\text{he}}^{\text{d}}\right)^{2}\chi_{\text{nem}}
.
A superconducting order parameter
\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{k},T)
at temperature
T
is obtained by solving the non-linear gap equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{k},T)

\displaystyle=-N_{0}\,\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\Lambda$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\Lambda$}}$}}\nolimits d\xi_{\mathbf{p}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\,V_{h}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})\,\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{p},T)\dfrac{\tanh\beta E_{\mathbf{k}}/2}{2\,E_{\mathbf{k}}}

(14)

where
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+|\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{k},T)|^{2}}
is the quasi-particle excitation energy,
N_{0}
is the density of states at the Fermi energy and
\Lambda
is the ultra-violet cutoff for the pairing interaction.
The presence of the
\cos^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\theta_{\mathbf{p}})
in the pairing interaction
V_{h}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
combined with the fact that
\tilde{\chi}_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})
is peaked at
\mathbf{k}\approx\mathbf{p}
makes the gap structure rather exotic. We keep the momenta
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}
on the Fermi surface (
|\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{p})|=k_{F}
) and model the nematic susceptibility as a Lorentzian
\tilde{\chi}_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})=2V_{0}\,\delta/\left(\delta^{2}+4\sin^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}-\theta_{\mathbf{p}})/2\right)
, where
\delta
is the distance to a nematic critical point in units of Fermi momentum. For this form,
\int\tilde{\chi}_{\text{nem}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}
tends to a finite value at
\delta\to 0
.

The pairing potential
V_{h}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\theta_{\mathbf{p}})=-2V_{0}\,\delta\,\cos^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\theta_{\mathbf{p}})/\left(\delta^{2}+4\sin^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}-\theta_{\mathbf{p}})/2\right)
is attractive in all
pairing channels associated with the irreducible representations of the
D_{4h}
group for the square lattice (one-dimensional
A_{1g}

B_{1g}
,
B_{2g}
and two-dimensional
E
, each with an infinite number of eigenfunctions).
Furthermore, at small
\delta
, the pairing interaction is nearly identical for a large set of eigenfunctions within a given representation, and the interplay between these eigenfunctions makes the gap function highly anisotropic and strongly peaked near particular points on the Fermi surface

1

1

There is a certain similarity between the present case and pairing near hot spots in a model of pairing mediated by soft antiferromagnetic fluctuations.
.

At vanishing
\delta
, the non-linear equation for

(

\displaystyle 1=g\,\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.22911pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\Lambda$}}$}}\nolimits d\xi\dfrac{\tanh\Bigr(\dfrac{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+|\Delta_{h}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|^{2}}}{2T}\Bigr)}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+|\Delta_{h}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|^{2}}}.

(15)

where

III
Fingerprints of NFMS

To highlight novel features associated with NFMS, we chiefly focus on the system behavior right at the nematic quantum-critical point (QCP), where at an finite
T
the gap on the hole pocket,

\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)
is non-zero in a finite range around
\theta_{k}=n\pi/2,n=0,1,2,3
(we drop the subscript
h
from
\Delta
onward). We also assume a parabolic hole dispersion and weak coupling. We analyze below thermodynamic, spectroscopic and transport properties of the NFMS. In Appendix
E
, we compare these properties with those of ordinary s-wave and d-wave superconductors.

III.1
Thermodynamic Properties

III.1.1
Specific Heat

The electronic contribution to a specific heat
C_{v}(T)
of a superconductor at temperature
T
is given by
[
51
]

\displaystyle C_{v}(T)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2N_{0}}{T}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\xi_{k}d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)}\Bigr(-\dfrac{\partial\,n_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial E_{\mathbf{k}}}\Bigr)\left[E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\dfrac{T}{2}\dfrac{\partial|\Delta(\mathbf{k},T)|^{2}}{\partial T}\right]

(18)

where
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi^{2}_{k}+|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|^{2}}
is the quasi-particle energy and
n_{\mathbf{k}}=1/(\exp(\beta E_\bk)+1)

with
\beta=1/k_{B}
is the Fermi function. Because
\dfrac{\partial\,n_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\,E_{\mathbf{k}}}=-\dfrac{1}{4\,T}\sech^{2}\bigr(\beta\,E_{\mathbf{k}}/2\bigr)
is peaked near the Fermi surface, we split the momentum integration into
integration over
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
and keep the density of states equal to

N_{0}
at the Fermi surface. We introduce dimensionless variables

t=T/T_{c}
,
\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)=\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)/T_{c}
, and dimensionless specific heat coefficient
\gamma(t)=C_{v}(t)/(TN_{0})=(C_{v}(t)/t)(1/T_{c}N_{0})
. The expression for
\gamma(t)
is

\displaystyle\gamma(t)=\dfrac{1}{t^{3}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dx\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\sech^{2}\Bigr(\dfrac{\sqrt{x^{2}+|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)|^{2}}}{2\,t}\Bigr)\Bigr[x^{2}+|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)|^{2}-\dfrac{t}{2}\dfrac{\partial|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)|^{2}}{\partial t}\Bigr].

(19)

In the normal state
\gamma(t)=2\,\pi^{2}/3
is temperature independent.
Immediately below the transition point (at
t=1^{-}
) the gap
\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)
is infinitesimally small but its temperature derivative is not, and
\gamma(t)
is generally supposed to jump by

\displaystyle\Delta\gamma=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},t)|^{2}.

(20)

Our case is special in this respect as
the gap opens up at
T_{c}
only at
4
points on the Fermi-surface. In this situation,
\Delta\gamma
vanishes,
i.e., there is no jump in the specific heat at
t=1^{-}
. (see Fig.
2
).
At
t<1
, we split the specific heat coefficient into two contributions:
\gamma^{\text{N}}
from the part of the Fermi surface where there is no gap, and
\gamma^{\text{SC}}
from the gapped region. The total

\gamma(t)=\gamma^{\text{SC}}(t)+\gamma^{\text{N}}(t)
. The two contributions are

\displaystyle\gamma^{\text{N}}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{8}{t^{3}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\theta_{0}(T)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\pi/4$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dx\sech^{2}\Bigr(\dfrac{x}{2\,t}\Bigr)\,x^{2}=\dfrac{8\,\pi}{3}\Big[\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\theta_{0}(T)\Bigr]

(21)

and

\displaystyle\gamma^{\text{SC}}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{8}{t^{3}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dx\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\theta_{0}(t)$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\sech^{2}\Bigr(\dfrac{\sqrt{x^{2}+|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})|^{2}}}{2\,t}\Bigr)\Bigr[x^{2}+|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})|^{2}-\dfrac{t}{2}\dfrac{\partial|\bar{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})|^{2}}{\partial t}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{\pi}}\,\gamma_{s}(t)\,\text{Erfi}[\sqrt{|\log t|}],

(22)

where
\gamma_{s}(t)
is the specific heat coefficient for an ordinary
s-
wave superconductor
and
\text{Erfi}(z)
is the imaginary error function (see Appendix
C
for the derivation of Eq. (
22
) and other details).
Near
t=1
,
\gamma^{\text{N}}(t)\approx 2\pi^{2}/3-4\pi\sqrt{g}\sqrt{1-t}/3
, while
\gamma^{\text{SC}}(t)\approx 2\,\sqrt{g}\,\gamma_{s}(1)\,\sqrt{1-t}/\pi
. Summing up the two contributions, we find that the specific heat coefficient increases continuously at
T\leq T_{c}
, with a
\sqrt{T_{c}-T}
temperature dependence.
At smaller
T
,
\gamma(t)
passes through a maxima, present because

\gamma^{\text{SC}}(t)
in Eq. (
22
) is the product of a monotonically decreasing
\gamma_{s}(t)
and a monotonically increasing
\text{Erfi}(\sqrt{\log t})
.
At low temperatures,
\gamma_{s}(t)
is exponentially small, such that
\gamma(t)
chiefly comes from the normal part and behaves as

\gamma(t)\approx 4\,\pi/(3\,\sqrt{|g\,\log t|})
.
The specific heat coefficient does vanish at
t=0
, but very weakly, as
1/\sqrt{\log(t)}
.
We plot
\gamma(t)
in Fig.
2
. We see that the
1/|\log t|^{1/2}
downturn of

\gamma(t)
occurs only at very low
t\sim 10^{-2}
. Extrapolating from higher
t
one would obtain instead a constant offset, expected when some zero energy fermionic states remain at
T=0
.

Change of a Specific Heat in a Magnetic Field

An important probe aimed to detect whether a superconductor posses nodes, and if yes, where the nodes are located and how the gap structure varies near these nodes, is to measure specific heat at low temperatures in the presence of an external magnetic field. H_{c1}

III.2.1
Dynamical density of states and tunneling conductance

The dynamical single particle density of state
N(\omega,T)=\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\delta(\omega-E_{\mathbf{k}}(T))
in a superconductor is given by

\displaystyle N(\omega,T)=N_{0}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)^{2}}}\right].

(30)

The tunneling conductance
G(V,T)
at a bias voltage
V
and temperature
T
is related to
N(\omega,T)
as
[
51
,
59
]

\displaystyle G(V,T)=-\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt-\infty$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits d\omega\,N(\omega,T)\,\dfrac{\partial n(\omega-V)}{\partial\omega},

(31)

where
n(x)=1/(\exp(x/T)+1)
is the Fermi Function. We will analyze two quantities:
(1)
tunneling conductance at zero temperature which is the zero temperature density of states, and
(2)
zero bias tunneling conductance as a function of temperature.
The first coincides with
N(\omega,T=0)
:

\displaystyle G(\omega,0)

\displaystyle=N_{0}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)^{2}}}\right]

(32)

where, we remind,
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)=\Delta_{0}\,\exp(-\tan^22\theta_\bk/g))
, and the
second is

\displaystyle G(0,T)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2T}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits d\omega\,N(\omega,T)\,\sech^{2}\dfrac{\omega}{2T}.

(33)

At
T=0
and
\omega\ll\Delta_{0}
, we have analytically

\displaystyle G(\omega,0)=8\,N_{0}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\theta_{0}(\omega)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\pi/4$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)^{2}}}\approx 8\,N_{0}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\theta_{0}(\omega)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\pi/4$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}=8\,N_{0}\left(\pi/4-\theta_{0}(\omega)\right)\propto 1/\sqrt{g\log\dfrac{\Delta_{0}}{\omega}}.

(34)

where
\theta_{0}(\omega)
is determined by
\Delta(\theta_{0}(\omega),0)=\omega
and at the smallest
\omega
is approximately

\pi/4-1/\sqrt{g\log{\dfrac{\Delta_0}{\omega}}}
. Close to
\omega=\Delta_{0}
, the contribution to
G(\omega,0)
comes from frequencies near the gap maxima at
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}=n\,\pi/2,n=\{0,3\}
. Expanding
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)
as
\Delta_{0}(1-4\theta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}/g)
, we find that
G(\omega,0)
diverges as
\log(1-\omega/\Delta_0)
. We show the result of full numerical evaluation of
G(\omega,0)
in Fig.
6
(a). We show the numerical result for the zero bias tunneling conductance
G(0,T)
in Fig.
6
(b). The behavior is similar to that for the susceptibility, but note that the extrapolation from small but finite
T
yields a finite value, which is almost half of the normal state
G(0,T)
.

III.2.2
Superfluid Density

Superfluid density
\rho_{s}(T)
is related to the current-current correlation function at
\omega=0+
and is
expressed as
[
51
,
60
]

\displaystyle\dfrac{\rho_{s}(T)}{\rho_{s}(0)}=\pi\,T\,\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\dfrac{|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|^{2}}{(|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2})^{3/2}}=1-\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dE\,\dfrac{\sech^{2}\dfrac{E}{2T}}{\sqrt{E^{2}-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)^{2}}}\dfrac{E}{2T}.

(35)

Here,
\rho_{s}(0)
is superfluid density at zero temperature, equal to the total fermion density
n
, and
\omega_{n}=(2\,n+1)\,\pi\,T
is the Matsubara frequency. Above
T_{c}
, the first term in the r.h.s. of Eq. (
35
) cancels the contribution from the second term, and
\rho_{s}=0
. Below
T_{c}
, we split the integral in Eq. (
35
) into two parts: superconducting part for
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\in[0,\theta_{0}(T))
and the normal part for
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\in[\theta_{0}(T),\pi/4]
, where
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
vanishes. Combining the two terms, we get,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\rho_{s}(T)}{n}

\displaystyle=1-8\left[\dfrac{\frac{\pi}{4}-\theta_{0}(T)}{2\pi}+\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\theta_{0}(T)$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)|$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dE\dfrac{\sech^{2}\dfrac{E}{2T}}{\sqrt{E^{2}-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},T)^{2}}}\dfrac{E}{2T}\right]=\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\theta_{0}(t)$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}f_{\rho}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})

(36)

where

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})=8\left(1-\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\tilde{\Delta}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\infty$}}$}}\nolimits dE\dfrac{\sech^{2}\dfrac{E}{2t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}}}{\sqrt{E^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})^{2}}}\dfrac{E}{2t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}}\right).

(37)

Here we introduced
t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}=T/T_{c}({\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})
,
\tilde{\Delta}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})=\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})/T_{c}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
, and
T_{c}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=T_{c}\exp{-\tan^2 2\theta_\bk/g}
. Because

\tilde{\Delta}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})
is not explicitly angle dependent (see Appendix
C
where this is explained in detail), the scaling function
f_{\rho}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})
is equal to
8
times the superfluid density of an s-wave superconductor,
\rho_{s}^{\text{s-wave}}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})
at an angle dependent temperature
t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}
. This gives

\displaystyle\dfrac{\rho_{s}(t)}{n}=\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\theta_{0}(t)$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}f_{\rho}(t_{\theta_{\mathbf{k}}})=\dfrac{\sqrt{g}}{\pi}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861ptt$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits dx\dfrac{\rho_{s}^{\text{s-wave}}(x)}{x\,\left(1+g\,\log\dfrac{x}{t}\right)\,\sqrt{\log\dfrac{x}{t}}}\approx\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{\pi}}\,\rho_{s}^{\text{s-wave}}(t)\,\text{Erfi}[\sqrt{g|\log t|}].

(38)

where
t=T/T_{c}
.
Near the transition point (
t\approx 1
),
\rho_{s}^{\text{s-wave}}\propto(1-t)
and
\text{Erfi}[\sqrt{|\log t|}]\approx\sqrt{1-t}
. Combining, we find that near
T_{c}
, the superfluid density increases as
\rho_{s}(t)\propto(1-t)^{3/2}
. At low temperature
t\ll 1
,
\rho^{\text{s-wave}}\approx 1
, while
\text{Erfi}[\sqrt{|\log t|}]\approx 1/\sqrt{\log t}
. As a result,
\rho_{s}(t)
approaches
n
such that
1-\rho_{s}/n\sim 1/\sqrt{\log(t)}
. To find the full temperature dependence of
\rho_{s}(T)
at
T<T_{c}
, we numerically integrate Eq. (
35
). We show the result in Fig.
7
(a,b). We clearly see
(T_{c}-T)^{3/2}
behavior near
T_{c}
and rapid increase of
\rho_{s}
towards
n
at the lowest
T
. At the same, if one extrapolates
\rho_{s}(T\to 0)
from small but finite
T
, one would obtain
\rho_{s}(T\to 0)
at a fraction of
n
. The London penetration depth
\lambda(T)
is expressed via
\rho_{s}(T)
as
\lambda(T)=1/\sqrt{\rho_{s}(T)}
. We plot
\Delta\lambda(T)=\lambda(T)-\lambda(T=0)
in Fig.
8
for
t<0.4
.
The black solid line shows a power-law fit
\Delta\lambda(T)\sim T^{a}
. We find that
a\approx 1.5
captures the low temperature behavior of
\Delta\lambda(T)
quite accurately.

A word of caution. In the calculations above we assumed the validity of local electrodynamics. It has been pointed out
[
61
]
that in the case of a nodal gap, there is a range near the nodes where the angle-dependent coherence length
\xi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=v_{F}/\pi|\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})|
becomes larger that London penetration depth
\lambda_{0}=(mc^{2}/(4\pi ne^{2})^{1/2}
. Once this happens, the electrodynamics becomes non-local and the temperature dependence of the penetration depth changes. This certainly happens in our case as the gap near cold spots is exponentially small. However, we argue that the temperature below which non-local effects become relevant is very small. The reasoning is that, at zeroth-order approximation, our gap function can be viewed as discontinuous – existing in finite ranges around hot spots, where it is weakly angle-dependent, and vanishing in the regions near cold spots. In the case, the temperature dependence of the penetration depth is comes from the ranges where
\xi<<\lambda_{0}
. Beyond zero-order approximation,
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
is a continuous function of the angle at
T=0
, and
\xi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})>\lambda_{0}
in cold regions. However, the value of
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
is very small in the whole cold region, and the crossover from cold to hot regions is rather sharp. As a result, we expect that
non-local electrodynamics will be relevant only at very low
T
, in the range where in Fig.
7
(a)

\rho_{s}
rapidly goes up and in Fig.
8

\Delta\lambda(T)
dives down.

Raman Intensity

Raman intensity
I(\Omega)
is related to the dynamic density-density like correlation function with finite frequency
\Omega
and vanishing momenta
\mathbf{q}
, and weighted with symmetry related form factors a.k.a Raman vertices. We compute Raman intensity with B
1g
vertices (
\cos 2\theta
) which at zero temperature takes the following expression (we ignore vertex correction in B
1g
channel)
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]

\displaystyle I(\Omega)=-\dfrac{N_{0}}{\Omega}\text{Im}\left[\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi}\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\dfrac{\Delta^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)\sin^{-1}\dfrac{\Omega}{2\,\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)}}{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)-\left(\dfrac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}}}\right]=\dfrac{4N_{0}}{\bar{\Omega}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\theta_{0}(\bar{\Omega}/2)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\pi/4$}}$}}\nolimits d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\dfrac{\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}f^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})}{\sqrt{\bar{\Omega}^{2}-4f^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})}}

(39)

where we remind that
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},0)=\Delta_{0}\,f(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
is the zero temperature superconducting gap with angular dependence
f(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=\exp(-\tan^22\theta_\bk/g)
,
\bar{\Omega}=\Omega/\Delta_{0}
and the lower integration cut-off
\theta_{0}(x)
is defined in Eq. (
16
). We numerically integrate Eq. (
39

.
At low frequency

\theta=\pi/4
and define
\delta\beta=\pi/4-\theta_{0}(\bar{\Omega}/2)\approx 1/2\sqrt{\log 2\Delta_{0}/\Omega}
. This gives us analytically (let’s take

\displaystyle I(\Omega)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{4\,N_{0}}{\bar{\Omega}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt\theta_{0}(\bar{\Omega}/2)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\pi/4$}}$}}\nolimits d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\dfrac{\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,f^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})}{\sqrt{\bar{\Omega}^{2}-4f^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})}}\approx\dfrac{16N_{0}}{\bar{\Omega}^{2}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\delta\beta$}}$}}\nolimits d\tilde{\theta}\tilde{\theta}^{2}\exp^{-1/2\tilde{\theta}^{2}}\propto\left[\log(\dfrac{2 \, \Delta_0}{\Omega})\right]^{-5/2}.

(40)

We note that the power
5/2

B_{1g}
vertices. For
A_{1g}
channel (ignore the vertex correction to consider the particle number conservation), we would get

. Close to

39
) comes from the gap maxima (hot spots:

).
We expand the gap function near these points where
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=\Delta_{0}(1-4\,\theta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})
and
\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\approx 1
and find

\displaystyle I(\bar{\Omega})\propto\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle W$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\Omega}-2(1-4\,\theta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})}}\propto\log\left(\dfrac{\Omega}{2\Delta_{0}}-1\right)

(41)

where
W=O(1)
is some arbitrary upper cut-off. At large frequency

IV
Effects of Disorder

In this Section we study the effects on NFMS from scattering by non-magnetic impurities.
We analyze how the impurity scattering affects the transition temperature, gap structure and superfluid density.
We also compute optical conductivity.
We consider a uniform distribution of
n_{\text{imp}}
impurity centers with a static s-wave scattering potential
U_{\text{dis}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})=U_{0}
, and treat electron-impurity scattering within the self consistent Born approximation
[
60
]
.
To simplify the formulas, we again write
\Delta(\mathbf{k},T)
as
\Delta(\mathbf{k})=\Delta(\theta_{k})
.
The translationally invariant normal (
G
) and anomalous (
F
) Matsubara Green’s functions, averaged over
the impurity positions, have the form

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{i\,\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(i\,\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})\right)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\left(\Delta(\mathbf{k})+\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Delta(\mathbf{k})+\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})}{\left(i\,\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})\right)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\left(\Delta(\mathbf{k})+\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})\right)^{2}},

(42)

where
\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})
and
\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})
are normal and anomalous components of the impurity-induced self energy.
Both are calculated self-consistently:
\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})=-n_{\text{imp}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}|U_{\text{dis}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})|^{2}\,G(\mathbf{p},\omega_{m})
and
\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})=-n_{\text{imp}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}|U_{\text{dis}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})|^{2}\,F(\mathbf{p},\omega_{m})
.
Using the expressions for the Green’s functions (
42
), and approximating
\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}
by
N_{0}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits d\xi_{\mathbf{p}}
,
we obtain the standard expressions

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{i\,\tilde{\omega}_{m}(\omega_{m})}{\sqrt{\tilde{\omega}_{m}^{2}(\omega_{m})+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}}

(43)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{\tilde{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}{\sqrt{\tilde{\omega}_{m}^{2}(\omega_{m})+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}}

(44)

where
\Gamma_{0}=n_{\text{imp}}\,N_{0}\,U_{0}^{2}\,\pi
is the impurity scattering rate, and
\tilde{\omega}_{m}
and
\tilde{\Delta}
are the dressed Matsubara frequency and the gap function
i\,\tilde{\omega}_{m}(\omega_{m})=i\,\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})
and
\tilde{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})=\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Sigma_{2}(\omega_{m})
, which have to be computed self consistently. The gap equation in the presence of impurities becomes

\displaystyle\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=-T\pi N_{0}\sum_{\omega_{m}=-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\,V(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\theta_{\mathbf{p}})\dfrac{\tilde{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}{\sqrt{\tilde{\omega}_{m}^{2}(\omega_{m})+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}}

(45)

where, we remind, that
V(\theta_{k},\theta_{p})
is the pairing interaction, and
\Lambda
is the upper cut-off for the theory.
It is convenient to define an auxiliary function
\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})=\omega_{m}\,\dfrac{\tilde{\Delta}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})}{\tilde{\omega}_{m}(\omega_{m})}
and re-express Eqs.(
43
-
45
) as a set of two self-consistent equations for
\psi
and
\Delta
:

\displaystyle\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Gamma_{0}\,\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}{\sqrt{\psi^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})+\omega_{m}^{2}}}}{1+\Gamma_{0}\,\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\psi^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega)+\omega^{2}}}},

(46)

\displaystyle\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=-T\,\pi\,N_{0}\sum_{\omega_{m}=-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\,V(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\theta_{\mathbf{p}})\dfrac{\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}{\sqrt{\omega_{m}^{2}+\psi^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}},

(47)

For an isotropic s-wave gap, transition temperature and gap function are unaffected by the impurity scattering
[
68
]
. For this case,
V(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\theta_{\mathbf{p}})=-V_{0}
, and
\psi_{s}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=\Delta_{s}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=\Delta_{s}
. This make the gap equation (
47
) invariant of scattering rate
\Gamma_{0}
. However, for a highly anisotropic NFMS gap, this is not the case as one can’t ignore the angular integration.
Below we solve Eqs. (
46
-
47
) for NFMS for various values of the scattering rate
\Gamma_{0}
and use the solutions,
\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})
and
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
to compute the self energy, spectral function, superfluid density and optical conductivity.

IV.1
Transition Temperature

As before, we focus on the pairing right at the nematic QCP, when the pairing interaction

V(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\theta_{\mathbf{p}})=-(g/N_{0})\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}-\theta_{\mathbf{p}})
.
We discuss the effect of impurity on the transition temperature and whether
the impurity scattering breaks the degeneracy between
s,p
, and
d-
channels and uniquely determine the gap function. We label the superconducting transition temperature for the clean and disorder cases as
T_{c}^{0}
and
T_{c}
, respectively, and denote their ratio as
\eta=T_{c}/T_{c}^{0}
. To compute
T_{c}
, we linearize Eq.(
46
) in
\psi
and plug it into Eq.(
47
) for
\Delta
as
\psi(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega_{m})=\left(\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Gamma_{0}\,\langle\Delta\rangle/|\omega_{m}|\right)/\left(1+\Gamma_{0}/|\omega_{m}|\right)
where
\langle\Delta\rangle=\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta_{\mathbf{p}}}{2\pi}\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{p}})
is the gap function averaged over the Fermi surface. This gives

\displaystyle\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=2T_{c}\pi g\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\sum_{\omega_{m}>0}^{\Lambda}\,\dfrac{\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Gamma_{0}\,\dfrac{\langle\Delta\rangle}{\omega_{m}}}{\omega_{m}+\Gamma_{0}}=\cos^{2}2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})\,I_{1}(\eta,\bar{\Gamma}_{0})+\langle\Delta\rangle\,I_{2}(\eta,\bar{\Gamma}_{0})\right],

(48)

with

\displaystyle I_{1}(\eta,\bar{\Gamma}_{0})

\displaystyle=2T_{c}g\pi\sum_{\omega>0}^{\Lambda}\dfrac{1}{\omega+\Gamma_{0}}=g\left[\Psi\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{\bar{\Gamma}_{0}}{\eta}+\dfrac{\bar{\Lambda}}{\eta}\right)-\Psi\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{\bar{\Gamma}_{0}}{\eta}\right)\right]\approx g\left[\Psi\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{\bar{\Lambda}}{\eta}\right)-\Psi\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{\bar{\Gamma}_{0}}{\eta}\right)\right]

(49)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\eta,\bar{\Gamma}_{0})

The normal and anomalous self energies

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=i\,\Gamma_{0}\,\omega\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-2.04861pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d\theta\mathbf{p}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\omega_{m}^{2}+\psi^{2}(\theta_{\mathbf{p}},\omega_{m})}}

IV.4
Spectral Function at zero temperature

With the self-energy
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
at hand, we are in position to compute the spectral function,
A(\mathbf{k},\omega)-\dfrac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}G^{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)
as a function of momentum
\mathbf{k}
and real frequency
\omega
. We keep the momentum on the Fermi surface,
|\mathbf{k}|=k_{F}
and vary
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
along the Fermi surface. The retarded Green’s function is

\displaystyle G^{R}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\omega+\Sigma_{1R}}{(\omega+i\,\delta+\Sigma_{1R}(\omega))^{2}-(\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Sigma_{2R}(\omega))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\dfrac{1}{(\omega+i\,\delta+\Sigma_{1R}(\omega))-(\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Sigma_{2R}(\omega))}+\dfrac{1}{(\omega+i\,\delta+\Sigma_{1R}(\omega))+(\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Sigma_{2R}(\omega))}\right].

(66)

In the clean case,
\Sigma
vanishes and the spectral function
A(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\delta(\omega-\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}))
. In the presence of impurity scattering the spectral function broadens and the frequency dependence of the self-energy becomes important. For a dirty
s-
wave superconductor,

\displaystyle A(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)=A(\omega)=\Theta(|\omega|-\Delta_{s})\dfrac{\Gamma_{0}\,\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\Delta_{s}^{2}}\,\left(\omega^{2}-\Delta_{s}^{2}+\Gamma_{0}^{2}\right)}

(67)

where
\Theta(x)=1
for
x>0
.
In our case, the behavior of
A(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)
is more complicated. We computed the spectral function numerically using the results for the self-energy. We plot
A(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)
in Fig. (
13
)
as a function of frequency for a set of angles and
for two values of
{\bar{\Gamma}}_{0}
, corresponding to weak and strong disorder.
We list several characteristics of the spectral functions:
(i) The width of
A(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)
expectedly increases with
{\bar{\Gamma}}_{0}
. However, unlike the s-wave case, the width strongly depends on the position of
{\bf k}
on the Fermi surface. The width is the largest at hot spots (blue curve) and the smallest at cold spots (red curve) implying that single particle excitations live longer near cold spots; (ii) For larger disorder strength, the excitations become more isotropic along the Fermi surface –
the distance between the peaks of
A(\theta_{\mathbf{k}},\omega)
at hot and cold spots decreases with increasing
{\bar{\Gamma}}_{0}
; (iii) The emergence of a gap in the excitation spectrum, which we discussed above, is clearly visible in the spectral function, particularly at larger
{\bar{\Gamma}}_{0}
, see the inset to Fig.
13
(b). The threshold frequency is determined by the value of the auxiliary function
\psi
at the cold spot;

The position of the peak of the spectral function,
\omega=\omega^{*}
, at a given point on the Fermi surface is determined by (
\omega^{*}>0
)

\displaystyle\omega^{*}=\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})+\Sigma_{2R}^{\prime}(\omega^{*})-\Sigma_{1R}^{\prime}(\omega^{*}).

(68)

Using the results for the self-energies, we find that for
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
close hot spots, the contribution from the self energies is smaller than
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
and to first approximation can be ignored, i.e.,

\omega^{*}\approx\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
. The situation changes as one gets close to the cold spot. In this range of angles
\Delta(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
is small and can be ignored compared to the self-energies.
As a result, the peak position is determined
\Sigma_{2R}^{\prime}-\Sigma_{1R}^{\prime}
and become independent of
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
, i.e., the excitations above the minimum gap are flat.

We calculate the optical conductivity,

where

and

are the charge and mass of the electron,
\rho_{s}(T)
is the superfluid density and

is the retarded current-current correlation function given by

\displaystyle\Pi(\Omega_{m},T)=\dfrac{2\,e^{2}}{m^{2}}\,T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\dfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathbf{k}_{x}^{2}\left[G(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m}+\Omega_{m})\,G(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m})+F(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m}+\Omega_{m})\,F(\mathbf{k},\omega_{m})\right],

(70)

where

and

are given by
Eqs.(
42
).
We assume that relevant
\mathbf{k}
are near the Fermi surface and replace
\int d^{2}k/(2\pi)^{2}
by

,

by
k^{2}_{F}/2=n\pi
, and impose the upper cutoff of the integration over
\xi_{k}
at
|\xi_{k}|=\Lambda
. Within this scheme, the superfluid density,

is related to
\Pi
as
[

,

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(T)

\displaystyle=n+\dfrac{m}{e^{2}}\Pi(0,T),

(71)

where

is the electron density. One can easily make sure that in the normal state

\dfrac{m}{e^{2}}\Pi(0,T)=-n
and
\rho_{s}=0

The retarded current-current correlation function,

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we analyzed in detail the behavior of observables in a superconductor with pairing mediated by soft charge-nematic fluctuations. We argued that such a pairing is difficult do realize in a one-band/one-orbital system, but it develops naturally near the onset of a nematic order in a multi-orbital/mulri-band system. We used doped FeSe as an example. The electronic structure of FeSe consists of hole and electron pockets, and excitations near a hole Fermi surface are made out of
d_{xz}
and
d_{yz}
orbitals.
A pure FeSe develops a nematic order at
85K
and superconductivity at
8K
. Upon doping by either
S
or
Te
, the onset temperature of a nematic order decreases an vanishes at some critical doping, but superconducting
T_{c}
remains finite. Numerous experimental data cited above have demonstrated that superconducting state near and above critical doping is very different from the one in pure and weakly doped FeSe and probably has different origin.

The point of departure for our study is the assumption that while the pairing in pure and weakly doped FeSe is mediated by spin fluctuations, much like in Fe-pnictides, the pairing mechanism near the end point of the nematic order is the exchange of soft charge nematic fluctuations. Such a mechanism is difficult to realize in a one-band/one-orbital system, but we showed that it can be realized in a multi-band/multi-orbital FeSe near a nematic quantum critical point under the assumption that the nematic order is a spontaneous polarization between
d_{xz}
and
d_{yz}
orbitals and the polarization changes sign between hole and electron pockets. Even though all the interactions are repulsive, we showed (following the earlier work
[
40
]
) that the renormalized pairing interaction mediated by soft d-wave nematic fluctuations is attractive. This interaction is, however, a rather unconventional one as for fermions on a hole pockets, where, we argued, the pairing interaction is the strongest, its strength depends on the location of a fermion on a Fermi surface. This leads to highly anisotropic gap function and to a highly unconventional thermal evolution of the gap structure below
T_{c}
.
Specifically, we found that right at the nematic quantum-critical point, the gap opens up at
T_{c}
only at
4
points on the hole Fermi surface, along the direction towards electron pockets. As
T
decreases, the range where the gap is non-zero widens, but there are still regions on the Fermi surface where the gap vanishes. At
T=0
,
the gap opens everywhere except special points, but remains highly anisotropic, creating hot and cold regions.
Symmetry-wise, such a gap can be either
s-
wave, or
p-
wave or
d-
wave - the condensation energies for all three cases are the same. Away from a critical point and/or in the presence of impurity scattering the degeneracy is broken and
s-
wave gap function wins. In our analysis we assumed
s-
wave gap, but we caution that by adding extra interactions, which we neglected, one can tip the balance towards
p-
wave.

In the bulk of this work we analyzed the effects of the non-trivial gap structure on the behavior of thermodynamic, spectroscopic and transport properties: the specific heat and its directional variation with a magnetic field, magnetic susceptibility, density of states, tunneling conductance, Raman intensity, superfluid stiffness and penetration depth. We found clear signatures of strong gap anisotropy and its unconventional thermal evolution in all observables that we calculated. In general terms, there is a strong non-BCS behavior near
T_{c}
because the gap only develops in narrow momentum ranges, and the behavior at
T<T_{c}
indicates the presence of un-gapped portions of the Fermi surface. In our theory, such a behavior holds down to very small
T~10^{-2}T_{c}
as the gap in some momentum ranges remains exponentially small even at
T=0
. We compared the theory with the existing data and found a reasonably good agreement. We made several predictions for future experiments, e.g., for measurements of a directional variation of the specific heat under an applied magnetic field (a Volovik effect).
We also studied the effects how superconductivity mediated by nematic fluctuations is affected by an s-wave scattering by non-magnetic impurities. We analyzed the evolution of the behavior of the same observables that we studied in the clean limit, and also computed optical conductivity and the variation of
T_{c}
. We found that with increasing impurity scattering, the gap becomes less anisotropic, but this process holds differently at different temperatures and frequencies. We again found a good agreement between our theory and the existing data and made suggestions for future experiments.

As we wrote in the Introduction, another theoretical proposal for superconductivity in doped FeSe is an exotic pairing that creates a Bogoliubov Fermi surface within a superconducting state
[
38
,
39
,
29
]
. It would be interesting to compare direct predictions for the observables within our and Bogoliubov Fermi surface scenarios and compare both with the experiments.
