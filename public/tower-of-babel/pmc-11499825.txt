Spatiotemporal distribution of organochlorine pesticides in the upper La Antigua watershed, Veracruz Mexico
Authors: Carolina Valdespino, Zoila G. Guillen-Guillen, Sergio Albino-Miranda, Jaime Rendon von Osten, Gabriela Vázquez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Even though the use of organochlorine pesticides has been prohibited since the last century their presence is still being recorded around the world. In the upper La Antigua watershed, Veracruz, Mexico we have reported concentrations of these contaminants in different compartments of the riverine ecosystems. This is of relevance due to their potential disruptive endocrine effect on the vertebrates drinking the water or feeding in the riverine area. In this study we evaluated concentrations of organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) in streams running throughout mountain cloud forest and two different types of land use, pasture lands and coffee plantations. Water and periphyton samples were collected during the wet and the dry seasons from streams located in independent micro-basins. Spatial explicit analyzes show that the concentrations measured are independent of the distribution of the streams in the geographical area sampled. However seasonal differences were found during the dry season in the water and during the rainy season in periphyton. While OCPs in water support the idea that micro-basins can be used as independent monitoring units, OCPs in periphyton can be indicators of the effect of different land use. Some concentrations of all the OCPs registered had values above the limits for tap water.

A recent review of land use transformation in Veracruz, Mexico, reports that there is only 23% of the natural vegetation left1 with only 8.6% of this amount corresponding to primary vegetation. One of the most threatened vegetation types investigated is the Mountain Cloud Forest2 with a transformation rate of 10.23% between 1989 and 2003 and 5.45% between 2003 and 2015. Land use change is mainly due to the conversion of natural vegetation to pasture lands and coffee plantations.

Besides natural vegetation lost, land use transformation has different effects, one of which is chemical contamination due to agriculture practices and pest’ control. Chemical contaminants that are washed out during the rainfall from the plantations and the animals sprayed during pest control practices, enter the run-off water that gets to the streams and rivers.

Watersheds are geomorphological landscape units within which nature cycles like those of the water and the chemical compounds in dissolution (nutrients, solid matter, etc.) take place. It is because of their localization within watersheds3, that the chemical effect of land use transformation can be measured as chemical concentrations in the water. The movement of exogenous compounds such as chemicals associated with agriculture, tree plantations, animal production, can as well be measured, followed and reported using watersheds as a study unit.

At the landscape level, we have previously reported differences in the bioaccumulation of organochlorine pesticides by bats using riverine forests located within micro-basins (those delimited by the convergence of two separated streams before the convergence with another stream), of forested (> 70% green cover) and transformed (< 70% green cover) areas4. In these cases, pasture lands represented the most common land use type in the landscape matrix of the transformed areas. Different types of land use are normally found in the riverine area of watersheds contributing in different proportions to the different types of organochlorine pesticides transported in the water of the rivers. However, drain-off is not the single source of the water of a river and some information indicates that samples taken from a river may be several years old as a result of infiltration and saturation of the water table5.

Periphyton, the biofilm surrounding the bed rocks in a river, has been used as a bio-indicator of water quality due to its sensitivity and rapid response to changes in the water6,7. In this paper we report the amounts of organochlorine pesticides in periphyton samples and water of rivers with different type of land uses taking place in the basins that they drain-out. Because seasonal differences seem to be the norm, we compared concentrations measured during the rainy and the dry season using spatio-temporal discriminant approaches to determine differences that might depend on the type of land use taking place in the basin.

Materials and methods

Eleven streams (Fig. 1) located in the area of cloud forest, where land use comprises shaded coffee plantations and pasture lands were sampled during the dry season (in April-May) and the period right after the rainy season (in October-November) of 2019. Precipitation during the dry season would be less than 10 mm and the annual precipitation ranges between 1390–2000 mm1. These second order streams are part of the upper (between 1500 and 2000 m a.s.l.) La Antigua basin, Veracruz, Mexico. This is a 2,623 km2 basin that drains part of the states of Puebla and Veracruz (19°050’ and 19°340’N; 96°060’ and 97°160’W) in eastern Mexico, discharging into the Gulf of Mexico8. General characteristics of the study site have been previously reported9.

Streams running throughout cloud forest, had dense riparian vegetation of either natural or secondary growth forest. In the case of coffee plantations, trees belonging to the natural vegetation could be found interspersed with the coffee trees (i.e. shaded coffee plantations). Pasturelands were for cattle grazing and, in some cases, scattered riparian trees were present. In order to investigate the effect that the different types of land use have on the presence of OCPs in the water and the biofilms, we selected streams where two different land use types could be found, each one covering a distance of at least 100 m length of the rivershore of the micro-basin.

Field work

Water sampling

Water samples were collected in 4 L clear glass laboratory bottles and processed in situ before moving to the next sampling point to avoid further reaction and changes in organochlorine pesticides concentrations. 4 L of water from the streams were obtained avoiding air getting into the bottle locating the bottle against the flow of the river. A sequence of hexane, dichloromethane-hexane and dichloromethane (30 mL each) was added to the collected water to extract the organochlorine contaminants10. Hexane allowed separation of fraction F1 containing Heptachlor, pp´DDT and Aldrin. The combination of hexane and dichloromethane (50:50) separated fraction F2 with Lindane and DDT. Finally, dichloromethane fractioned Dieldrin and Endrin11. Each solvent was added separately, the bottle with the sample capped with a Teflon lined cap and moved during 2 min to allow mixing with the water column. Bottles were uncapped and the solvent phase removed using a 5 mL Pasteur pipette with a safety filler. The solvent phase was then poured into an amber glass 120 mL flask before adding the next solvent. After the combination of the 3 extracts ambar flasks were cap with Teflon lined caps. Recoveries ranged from 72 to 93%11 Extracts were transported to the lab in a field cooler with refrigerants and kept in refrigeration once in the lab.

Periphyton sampling

Three or four stones covering an area of 150 cm2 of the bottom of the river were collected, protected with aluminum paper in a plastic bag and maintained in a cooler with refrigerants for transportation to the lab. Samples were kept in a freezer (-40⁰ C) until processing. The biofilm was separated from the stones using a metal brush and 10 mL of acetonitrile and kept in solution under refrigeration in 30 mL amber flasks. Further processing was done using a microwave oven (MARS-CEM laboratories) as previously reported10.

Laboratory work

The lab, purification of samples was done using glass columns packed with glass fiber, silica gel, alumina and Na2SO412. In order to identify and quantify the OCPs, the SUPELCO 47426-U CLP Organochlorine Pesticide Mix was used. Limits of detection (LOD) were 0.0018 µg/mL, αHCH & Endosulfan sulfate; 0.0021 µg/mL, βHCH & Aldrin; 0.0017 µg/mL, γHCH & p,p DDE; 0.0015 µg/ml, δHCH; heptachlor & heptachlor epoxide; 0.0013 µg/ml, Dieldrin; 0.0045 µg/ml, Endrin; 0.006 µg/ml, endrin aldehyde; 0.0035 µg/ml, Endrin ketone; 0.0023 µg/ml, Endosulfan I (α); 0.0041 µg/ml, Endosulfan II (β); 0.0031 µg/ml, o,p’DDE; 0.0017 µg/ml, p,p’DDE; 0.0025 µg/ml, o,p’DDD; 0.0026 µg/ml, p,p’DDD; 0.007 µg/ml, o,p’DDT & metoxychlor; 0.0064 µg/ml, p,p’ DDT; 0.003 µg/ml, Trans Chlordane & Cis Chlordane and 0.0015 µg/ml, Heptachlor & Heptachlor Epoxide.

Recovery rates of the OCPs were: 100.6 ± 2.0, γ-HCH; 99.4 ± 2.8, δ-HCH; 90.1 ± 8.1, Aldrin; 91.6 ± 7.4, Dieldrin; 97.8 ± 0.4, Endrin; 97.4 ± 1.9, Endrin aldehyde; 96.0 ± 3.4, Endrin ketone; 92.7 ± 7.6, Endosulfan I (α); 94.5 ± 5.6, Endosulfan II (β); 96.5 ± 1.2, Endosulfan sulfate; 98.5 ± 1.5, o, p’DDE; 98.1 ± 2.4, p, p’DDE; 99.8 ± 2.2, o, p’DDD; 101.0 ± 2.3, p, p’DDD; 99.51 ± 2.7, o, p’DDT; 97.2 ± 0.7, p, p’DDT; 95.0 ± 4.4, Trans Chlordane; 97.2 ± 3.9, Cis Chlordane; 98.1 ± 5.0, Methoxychlor; 97.1 ± 3.5, Heptachlor and 95.9 ± 3.4, Heptachlor Epoxide. The concentration values of OCPs are presented and analyzed as families (∑HCH, ∑DDT, ∑Drines, ∑Heptachlor, ∑Endosulfan and ∑Chlordane) and the results are reported as µg L− 1. We used a TSQ 8000 Evo™ Gas Chromatogram equipped with a Mass Spectrometer and a Thermo Scientific™ TRACE 1310 gasses detector with a 15 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 m SQC, to analyze the samples11.

Statistical analyses

GPS locations were taken at each sampling collection site. This geographic id allowed to use spatial explicit analyses whilst concentrations measured during the dry and the wet season, permitted the temporal differentiation. In the case of periphyton samples, a censured approach was followed to calculate values under the limit of detection of the chromatogram. Analyses were performed using the NADA, survival, MASS, geoR and gstat libraries of the software R, version 3.2.413.

The spatial explicit analyses indicated that there is an effect of the season on the concentrations of ΣHCH, ΣHeptachlor, ΣEndosulfan and ΣChlordane in the water, with higher concentrations during the dry season. However, the geographic distance between sites, did not have an effect on the concentrations measured. This is the fluctuation of concentrations is independent of the distance separating the rivers sampled.

Discussion

In this study we report concentrations of OCPs measured in periphyton and water of streams crossing lands with different types of use (cloud forest, coffee plantations and pasturelands). Significant differences could not be found among land use types in the case of the water however, as previously reported higher concentrations of some of the OCPs were recorded during the dry season as shown in Fig. 2. In contrast, concentrations of the same OCPs in periphyton biofilms were higher during the rainy season and differed among some of the land use types, depicted in Fig. 4.

OCPs in the water

Concentrations of both, short-term (ΣHCH and ΣHeptachlor) and long-term (ΣEndosulfan and ΣChlordane) persistent OCPs, were significantly higher during the dry season in streams. The finding of a seasonal effect supports our previous reports in other streams within the upper section of La Antigua basin10 where concentrations are responding to changes in the volumes of water of the streams and corroborates seasonal variations of other chlorinated pesticides measured in other regions of the world13,18.

Studies that evaluate OCPs during different seasons are not common. However, higher concentrations of OCPs were recorded during the rainy season in the Owan River in Nigeria16 (information in Table 1). In our study area, an important part of the preparation of the land including application of chemicals to attain good products, takes place during the dry season. Whilst application of parasite-control products to the animals may take place several times during the year19 both activities may incorporate OCPs to the streams during the low-level season resulting in higher concentration of the pesticides that we reported in our study.

In the water highest OCPs concentrations were recorded in coffee plantations represented by shaded coffee farms characterized by a complex vegetation structure comparable to that of natural forested areas14,20. This result implies that the presence of vegetation with complex structures will play an important role in the maintenance of OCPs within the ecosystems as we previously suggested4. A vegetation with a complex structure allows natural cycling of nutrients, of organic matter and ecosystem processes, as part of riverine forest stripes, retain chemicals and contaminants during longer periods before they get to the water21. Shaded coffee plantations are similar to natural forested areas in that they have tree species characteristic of the cloud forests promoting persistence of those cycles and maintaining during a longer period the OCPs that have been incorporated in the trophic webs. Because this takes place by the streams that drain the coffee plantations, run-off of the OCPs applied to the coffee plants will result in higher concentrations of the OCPs in the water10.

OCPs in biofilms

Both, short (ΣHCH and ΣHeptachlor) and long-term (ΣChlordane) persistent OCPs showed higher concentrations in biofilms during the rainy season which differs from the pattern found in the water, where concentrations were higher during the dry season. The contrasting display may relate to the characteristic of periphyton as an effective bioindicator responding rapidly to anthropogenic toxicants and stimulants19,22 and that will bioaccumulate the elevated dry-season concentrations of OCPs in the water.

River biofilms are able to remove different pesticides with rates ranging from 99.6 to 41.2%5,20,23. Concentrations of ΣEndosulfan measured in periphyton in the upper section of La Antigua basin, are lower than those registered in the North Florida Bay (2.25 ng g− 1)22. However, ΣDrines, ΣChlordane, ΣDDT, ΣHeptachlor and ΣHCH, that were registered in La Antigua, were not recorded in Florida.

An important contribution of this paper is that spatial explicit models allowed taking into consideration the distance separating the streams sampled and the OCPs measured in each one. A random pattern was found in the distribution of OCPs in the space. This finding adds to the already described characteristic of watersheds working as independent units where OCPs concentrations depend on processes associated to the activities taking place within them. An isotopic study of the food webs characterizing the streams that were studied, indicates that the resources used by the aquatic organisms that conform these webs (e.g. periphyton biofilms) have an allochthonous origin9. This means that the material that they consume originates in offshore sources, in the different land use types that the rivers drain-out. This finding supports the results of the spatial models, as each of the streams will have OCPs concentrations associated with the watershed activities taking place in the lands that they drain.

Also, in each stream, crossing the two land uses within a micro-basin, OCPs concentrations measured in the water will not show differences but the periphyton biofilm characterizing each type of land use will bioaccumulate differential concentrations of ∑Chlordane, ∑HCH, ∑Heptachlor and ∑DDT. Because water from the rivers is used in farm activities in some cases, comparison with established regulatory levels of contaminants seemed important. Several of the ΣDrines, ΣHeptachlor, ΣHCH and ΣChlordanes punctual concentrations recorded in this study were higher than the permissible concentrations for tap water21,24. OCPs have been reported in vegetables22,25, milk23,26, meat24,27, blood of inhabitants25,28 and pregnant women26,29 of Coatzacoalcos and Cordoba, in the state of Veracruz. All these places are located in lower parts of different watersheds of the State of Veracruz, Mexico, where our study took place.

A study of OCPs measured in prey (fish and crustaceans) and eliminated in the feces of the Neotropical otter (Lontra longicaudis) corroborates the presence of these compounds in the middle and lower parts of the watersheds in the state of Veracruz27,30. Animals in the wild will drink the water, and the organochlorine compounds in dissolution28–33. Due to the endocrine disruptive effect of these contaminants, their presence in the water of the upper part of La Antigua watershed was of concern. A description of the distribution of OCPs in the compartments (water, sediments, soil, invertebrates and bats) of rivers characterized by the presence of riparian corridors in the upperpart of the watershed, allowed establishing the relationship of concentrations in the environment and the amounts bioaccumulated by the animals10.

Compared to the OCPs concentrations reported in this study, those that we found in the study reported in 20224 (ΣDrines = 0.0005 µg L− 1, ΣHCH = 0.0002 µg L− 1and ΣEndo = 0.0004 µg L− 1) were lower. Riparian corridors may be functioning as a filter for OCPs running into the stream as previously proposed4,10. However, because these studies were done in different years, this conclusion should be taken with caution. Remediation technologies31,34 could be used to “clean” the water both for human and domestic animals use in coffee plantations and pasture lands activities with an improvement side effect to wildlife using the same lands.
