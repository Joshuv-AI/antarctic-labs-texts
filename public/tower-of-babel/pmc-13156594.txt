Atypical presentation of intraosseous plasmacytoma in a patient with POEMS syndrome
Authors: Jude Kolodisner, Matthew Scarsbrook, Gabriella Mamo, Tyson Chadaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
POEMS syndrome is an uncommon paraneoplastic syndrome featuring polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal gammopathy, and skin changes. POEMS syndrome in conjunction with plasmacytoma is a particularly rare finding, mostly featured only in case reports and small case series. We present a case of a 52-year-old female with POEMS syndrome with a biopsy-proven solitary bone plasmacytoma of the scapula demonstrating atypical imaging features. While plasmacytomas are typically considered locally aggressive lesions, the plasmacytoma in this patient with POEMS syndrome exhibited stability in size and local invasion over a period of almost a year despite lack of treatment. This case demonstrates that radiographic stability of these lesions, while uncommon, can occur and should not exclude the diagnosis of plasmacytoma.

Introduction

POEMS syndrome is an uncommon paraneoplastic syndrome which stands for Polyneuropathy, Organomegaly, Endocrinopathy, Monoclonal Gammopathy, and Skin Changes [1]. Its incidence is estimated at approximately 0.3 cases per 100,000 individuals [2]. Diagnosis per the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) requires both mandatory criteria, at least one major criterion, and at least one minor criterion [3]. Mandatory criteria include 1) Polyneuropathy and 2) Monoclonal plasma cell-proliferative disorder. Major criteria include Castleman disease, sclerotic bone lesions, or elevated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Minor criteria include organomegaly, extravascular volume overload, endocrinopathy (excluding diabetes/hypothyroidism for certain classifications), skin changes, papilledema, or thrombocytosis/polycythemia.

Plasmacytomas are clonal proliferations of plasma cells that may arise in either the bone or soft tissues. Plasma cell dyscrasia drives overproduction of pro-angiogenic and inflammatory cytokines, most notably VEGF, which leads to the multi-system manifestations and characteristic sclerotic bone reaction [3]. Solitary bone plasmacytomas (SBP) arise within the marrow of a single skeletal site, most commonly of the axial skeleton [4]. In contrast, extramedullary plasmacytomas (EMP) originate within soft tissue, most commonly the upper aerodigestive tract. SBPs have a higher likelihood of progression to multiple myeloma compared to EMP, which more often tend to remain localized [5].

POEMS syndrome and multiple myeloma are both types of plasma cell dyscrasias which can present with bone lesions. POEMS syndrome is caused by an underlying clonal plasma cell disorder and features sclerotic bone lesions. Multiple myeloma is a malignant plasma cell cancer and classically features lytic bone lesions. Multiple myeloma is characterized clinically by hypercalcemia, renal failure, and anemia, among others. POEMS syndrome patients uniquely have low incidences of anemia, hypercalcemia, and renal insufficiency [6,7]. Patients with POEMS syndrome also have a higher survival rate and experience less burden of disease compared to those with multiple myeloma [6].

While SBPs can be seen on radiographs and computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior evaluation. On MRI, lesions often demonstrate low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high fluid signal intensity with homogenous enhancement and overlying partly preserved or sclerotic cortical bone [7]. Large plasmacytomas may show necrosis, invasion, and destruction of adjacent structures [4]. While imaging is important for the diagnosis and staging of plasmacytomas, definitive diagnosis requires biopsy. Radiotherapy remains the optimal treatment for these tumors [4].

This is a unique case of a patient with POEMS syndrome with a biopsy-proven SBP of the left scapula demonstrating atypical imaging features. This case highlights the significance of radiology-pathology correlation in situations in which imaging features are atypical. The case also calls attention to the importance of evaluating patients holistically, by considering and evaluating multiple organ systems as well as the patient’s clinical history, as a constellation of different symptoms and physical exam findings may not necessarily be unrelated.

Case report

This case describes a 52-year-old female with past medical history of hypothyroidism who presented to an outpatient ophthalmology clinic with complaints of painless acute vision loss, constant eye floaters for three days that had been intermittent for several months, and a pressure-like sensation behind her right eye. She was found to have bilateral optic disc edema and was subsequently advised to go to the emergency department for concerns of bilateral papilledema to evaluate for possible brain mass and optic neuritis.

In the emergency department, the patient reported a history of a left scapular lesion. She reported being managed conservatively outside the conventional medical system by a naturopathic provider for the past several months with ivermectin and fenbendazole. She described a four-year history of progressive extremity weakness.

Physical exam was pertinent for sluggish pupils bilaterally, right inferior nasal vision loss, and bilateral foot drop, and hypertrichosis. The patient exhibited symmetric sensorimotor polyradiculoneuropathy in both the distal upper and lower extremities. Inpatient ophthalmology consultation revealed a right branch retinal artery occlusion and severe optic disc edema. An urgent stroke workup and neurological imaging was recommended.

Prior images were reviewed from an outside imaging center, including a left shoulder radiograph, which demonstrated a large, expansile, lucent bone lesion involving the scapular body, coracoid process, glenoid fossa, and acromion (Fig. 1). An initial chest radiograph obtained in the emergency department confirmed the left scapular lesion, which appeared grossly similar compared to the prior radiograph approximately 8.5 later. Computed Tomography (CT) angiogram of the head and neck demonstrated bilateral long segment vascular wall thickening along the cervical and intracranial internal carotid artery with complete occlusion beyond the left cavernous segment and severe stenosis in the right supraclinoid segment. There was occlusion of the origin of the left ophthalmic artery with distal reconstitution of flow. Also visualized was mild to moderate diffuse extraocular muscle enlargement bilaterally, particularly of the inferior and medial recti, in a pattern suggestive of thyroid orbitopathy. Ultrasound-guided tissue sampling of the left scapular mass was subsequently obtained by interventional radiology, in which pathology results were consistent with a plasma cell neoplasm with 100% of plasma cells expressing lambda light chain.

There were also mixed sclerotic and lytic lesions seen in the pelvic bones and sacrum, as described on the concurrent MRI of the lumbar spine. Laboratory analysis exhibited the presence of two M-spikes on SPEP, including IgA lambda and IgG kappa, elevated TSH, and polycythemia (elevated hematocrit of 49.1% and 5.53 M/uL red blood cells). Opening pressure on lumbar puncture was not elevated, however, CSF analysis demonstrated elevated protein of 104 mg/dL with normal nucleated cell count. Serum calcium and renal function were within normal limits (calcium 8.8 mg/dL and estimated glomerular filtration rate 111 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Bone marrow analysis was normocellular (approximately 40%) with trilineage hematopoiesis and no increase in blasts. Plasma cells were mildly increased at 5.67% by morphology. They were approximately 10% by CD138 immunohistochemistry and demonstrate polyclonal light chain expression by in situ hybridization. Flow cytometry analysis (FC25-13876) showed no monotypic B-cell population or abnormal increase in plasma cells. The peripheral blood showed no rouleaux formation or plasma cells. Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) Multiple Myeloma panel was normal.

Given the constellation of these findings, the patient was subsequently diagnosed with POEMS syndrome.

The patient was ultimately not interested in undergoing local systemic therapy (suggested given the presence of multiple sclerotic lesions for POEMS), which was recommended to her by oncology, but instead preferred to continue treatment under her naturopathic practitioner due to concerns with toxicities and financial constraints. Local radiation therapy to the shoulder plasmacytoma was a reasonable alternative was suggested as a reasonable and more alternative treatment, however, the patient ultimately denied treatment.

Discussion

POEMS syndrome is a rare paraneoplastic condition caused by an underlying plasma cell dyscrasia, most commonly associated with sclerotic osseous lesions and SBPs [3]. Based on the IMWG criteria, the patient in this case met both of the mandatory criteria for POEMS syndrome, including polyradiculopathy and monoclonal plasma cell proliferative disorder (including two M spikes, IgA lambda and IgG kappa, and biopsy-proven plasmacytoma). She also met one of the required major criteria of sclerotic bone lesions, which were present within the sacrum, pelvis, C7, and L1-L3. Only one minor criterion is required for diagnosis of POEMS syndrome, however our patient exhibited multiple: organomegaly (in the form of splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy), skin changes (hypertrichosis), optic disc swelling, extravascular volume overload (mild anasarca), and polycythemia.

Although multiple osseous lesions and dual M-protein spikes were present, the overall clinical, radiologic, and laboratory evaluation did not meet criteria for multiple myeloma (eg, absence of features such as hypercalcemia, renal failure, or anemia), and the findings were interpreted in the context of POEMS-associated plasma cell dyscrasia [8]. Additionally, Bone marrow analysis revealed normocellularity with mildly increased plasma cells (∼5–10%) demonstrating polyclonal light chain expression, no monotypic B-cell population, and a normal myeloma FISH panel — findings consistent with POEMS syndrome, in which a plasma cell clone is detected in only two-thirds of patients and the marrow burden is typically small with concomitant polyclonal plasma cells [9,10].

Despite imaging features suggestive of intracranial hypertension, the normal opening pressure suggests a more complex process. In POEMS syndrome, papilledema may occur independently of elevated intracranial pressure, potentially mediated by VEGF-induced increased capillary permeability at the optic nerve head or by pachymeningeal thickening impairing cerebrospinal fluid dynamics [[11], [12], [13]].

The ICA wall thickening and occlusion observed on CTA is a rarely reported but significant vascular manifestation of POEMS syndrome, possibly mediated by VEGF-induced endothelial proliferation or chronic inflammation [9]. Differential considerations included vasculitis or accelerated atherosclerosis, though the distribution and systemic context point toward a POEMS-related vasculopathy [14].

SBPs are typically very locally aggressive with a tendency for progressive growth, local destruction, and soft tissue extension if left untreated [4]. Imaging is therefore routinely recommended to assess local spread and monitor progression to systemic disease. However, the SBP described in our case demonstrated minimal interval change over an 8.5-month period, despite the absence of treatment such as radiation or chemotherapy. Specifically, the scapular lesion measured approximately 13.4 × 6.8 × 7.3 cm (transverse × craniocaudal × anteroposterior) on the current examination, compared to 7.1 × 6.1 cm (anteroposterior × craniocaudal) on prior imaging obtained 8.5 months earlier, demonstrating minimal interval change in size over this period. This stability is extremely atypical for a plasmacytoma of this size, making this an unusual presentation. Long-term assessment of untreated plasmacytomas is poorly characterized in the literature, as most cases undergo early local treatment with radiation therapy following diagnosis. One prior study showed significant improvement following radiation of SBTs, even including resolution of POEMS syndrome features [15].

While the scapular lesion appeared aggressively lytic—mimicking metastasis or primary bone sarcoma—its radiographic stability over nearly a year is highly atypical for such a large plasma cell tumor. In the context of POEMS, bone lesions are more commonly sclerotic; however, when lytic lesions occur, they may represent a more rapid progression of disease [9,16].This case serves as a reminder to radiologists that stability in a 'malignant-looking' lesion does not preclude a plasma cell dyscrasia, especially when paraneoplastic symptoms are present [17].

This unique case highlights several important considerations. Plasmacytomas should be included within the differential diagnosis in patients with destructive, lytic bone lesions, even when they appear radiographically stable over time. Radiographs and MRI are useful tools for evaluating these lesions and narrowing down the differential diagnosis. However, due to the variable imaging appearance of many of the osseous lesions, histopathology remains an important tool for definitive diagnosis and appropriate clinical management.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case highlights that while plasmacytomas are typically considered aggressive lesions, it is possible for them to rarely demonstrate radiographic stability over time; thus, they should not be ruled out as a differential diagnosis. If this tumor is also supported by clinical, laboratory, and histopathology findings of POEMS syndrome, this should also be a consideration. There is limited information within the literature regarding the imaging appearance of untreated or minimally treated plasmacytomas, thus future research may focus on characterizing the course of these lesions over time.
