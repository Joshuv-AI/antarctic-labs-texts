Monitoring nutritional consequences of obesity treatment on long-term health of women and their offspring: protocol of a multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: Rebecca Marie Schipper, Laura Heusschen, Lara Schepers, Femke Fleur Verstraete, Eric J Hazebroek, Ellen Kampman, Agnes A M Berendsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOver the past years, the number of people receiving surgical or pharmacological obesity treatment has been increasing globally. The majority of these patients are female and of reproductive age. Although these treatments are effective in reducing body weight and obesity-related comorbidities for most patients with obesity, they also compromise nutrient status, which may negatively impact the long-term health of women and their offspring born after obesity treatment. Hence, there is a need to investigate the nutritional consequences of obesity treatment and their long-term health effects on women and their offspring.Methods and analysisThe MOnitor the NUtritional Consequences of Obesity (MONUCO) study is a multicentre prospective cohort study with an integrated pregnancy and birth cohort. At least 1150 women living with obesity, undergoing either surgical or pharmacological obesity treatment, aged 18–55 years, will be included. Assessments will be performed pretreatment and post-treatment at 3 months, 6 months and yearly up to 10 years. Dietary intake, nutrient status, lifestyle factors and parameters related to musculoskeletal, reproductive, endocrine, gastrointestinal and mental health will be assessed. Data will be collected using physical measurements, questionnaires or biological samples, including urine, blood and faeces. In the pregnancy and birth cohort, pregnant women with a history of obesity treatment will be followed throughout and after pregnancy. In this cohort, additional information will be collected on nutrient status during pregnancy, foetal growth, child development and human milk composition. This observational study will provide comprehensive insights into the consequences of both surgical and pharmacological obesity treatment on nutrient status and dietary intake and consequent musculoskeletal health, maternal health and foetal growth. Based on these findings, more evidence-based nutrition recommendations to optimise long-term health for individuals treated for obesity can be developed.Ethics and disseminationThe MONUCO study is approved by the Medical Ethical Committee Oost-Nederland, Nijmegen, the Netherlands (NL86647.091.24). The results of this study will be disseminated in the form of scientific abstracts, posters, oral presentations at conferences and publications in peer-reviewed journals. Additionally, results may be made available to study participants and the general public via other platforms.Trial registration numberNCT06945016. Registration was perfromed in the pre-results stage.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex disease characterised by excessive adiposity that poses a major risk to health.1 Obesity has a multifactorial aetiology that includes interactions between genetic, behavioural, environmental, physiological, social and cultural factors that lead to an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure during a prolonged period of time.2 In 2022, approximately 16% of adults were living with obesity globally, which mirrors the prevalence of obesity in the Netherlands.1 3 Treatment options for obesity include combined lifestyle interventions, pharmacotherapy by means of obesity management medications (OMM) and metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS). The recommended treatment approach depends on the severity of obesity, which is assessed based on body mass index (BMI), waist circumference and the presence of obesity-related comorbidities and complaints.4 In 2023, approximately 540 000 primary MBS procedures were performed globally.5 In the UK alone, 1.6 million adults reported using glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) in 2025.6

Current evidence indicates that both MBS and OMM can result in sustained clinically relevant weight loss and remission of obesity-related comorbidities.7–9 However, these treatments can also negatively impact nutrient status due to the intended reduction in food intake, altered digestion and reduced absorption of nutrients.10 After MBS, nutrient deficiencies have been reported to develop or persist and exacerbate; these include deficiencies of protein (2.2%–5.9% of patients), iron (12.5%–76.7%), vitamin D (6.2%–70%) and vitamin B12 (6.2%–50%).11 Their prevalence is influenced by the type of MBS, occurrence of adverse effects (eg, nausea, vomiting), compliance with dietary and supplement recommendations, food intolerances and changes in taste and eating patterns post-treatment.10 12 13 When it comes to OMMs, data on the prevalence of nutrient deficiencies before and during treatment are still limited. However, nutrient intake below the recommended dietary allowance has been reported among individuals on GLP-1 RAs, for multiple micronutrients such as iron (88.4%), vitamin D (98.6%), and magnesium (89.9%).14

The nutrient deficiencies induced by obesity treatment can have clinical manifestations. Examples are anaemia and neuropathy, which occur in 26% and 16% of MBS patients, respectively.15 16 However, as nutrients are essential for many processes in the body, the impact of a low nutrient status on the overall health of patients likely goes beyond the known clinical manifestations. There is evidence that low protein intake and low vitamin D status during obesity treatment might play a role in the loss of fat-free mass, which is considered an adverse effect of obesity treatment.17–19 Additionally, due to their influence on depression and anxiety symptoms, low vitamin B12 and vitamin D status could limit improvements in mental health after obesity treatment or even worsen pre-existing psychological symptoms.20 21 To date, studies investigating the role of nutrient intake and nutrient status on a wide range of health outcomes after obesity treatment are limited.22

Additionally, in women who have received obesity treatment, impaired nutrient status during pregnancy may be detrimental for foetal growth and child development. A higher prevalence of intrauterine growth restriction and small-for-gestational age (SGA) has been observed in infants born after maternal MBS compared with infants of non-operated women with obesity.23–27 Currently, it is not completely understood how maternal obesity treatment may impact foetal growth, programming and children’s long-term health and development.28–30 However, as suggested by the ‘Barker hypothesis’ and results from the Dutch famine cohort, maternal undernutrition and correlated neonatal growth restriction have been linked to health consequences for the child later in life.31–34 This suggests that the consequences of obesity treatment for maternal nutrient status could result in transgenerational health effects. Further investigation of the role of maternal obesity treatment and nutrient status on foetal growth and child development is needed to identify and prevent potential adverse transgenerational effects of obesity treatment.

Given the described gaps in our knowledge on the role of nutrient intake and status in improving health outcomes for current and future generations after obesity treatment, we aim to address these gaps with the MONUCO study, in which we MOnitor the NUtritional Consequences of Obesity treatment on the health of women and their offspring. The primary objective is to study the impact of obesity treatment on women’s dietary intake, nutrient status and consequent musculoskeletal health—including muscle mass, muscle strength and bone mass—and to investigate associations between maternal nutrient intake and status with birth outcomes such as foetal growth, birth weight and child development. As a secondary objective, the impact of the nutritional consequences of obesity treatment on gastrointestinal health, immune function, reproductive health and mental health will be studied.

Methods and analysis

This protocol is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement for STROBE. The Medical Ethical Committee Oost-Nederland, Nijmegen, the Netherlands (2024–17240) approved the MONUCO study protocol (NL86647.091.24). The MONUCO study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with identifier NCT06945016.

Study design

The MONUCO study is a multicentre prospective longitudinal cohort study with an integrated pregnancy and birth cohort. Dutch women eligible for surgical or pharmacological obesity treatment are included and followed for at least 10 years. Within the pregnancy and birth cohort, pregnant women with a history of obesity treatment are followed during and after pregnancy. Their children are followed up to 4 years of age (figure 1). Recruitment for the study started in January 2025. Taking into account a recruitment phase of 4 years, it is expected that data collection for the study will be completed in 2040.

Study population of the MONUCO study

The study population of the MONUCO study consists of women undergoing surgical or pharmacological obesity treatment or both. Participants are recruited from multiple obesity centres, covering a broad area of the Netherlands. Recruitment started in January 2025. Women who are approved for obesity treatment in one of the participating obesity centres are eligible for participation if they meet the eligibility criteria outlined in table 1. All participants in the MONUCO study participate in a basic set of study procedures, including digital questionnaires, physical measurements and collection of biological samples. At inclusion, participants can choose to participate in subgroups with additional measurements, including a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and MRI scan. For these subgroups, additional exclusion criteria apply (table 1). The subgroups are embedded within the cohort as illustrated in figure 2.

Study population of the MONUCO pregnancy and birth cohort

Participants who become pregnant during follow-up of the MONUCO study are asked to continue their participation within the integrated pregnancy and birth cohort. Additionally, all other pregnant women with a history of obesity treatment are eligible to participate in the pregnancy and birth cohort. Both participants who already participate in the MONUCO study and new participants need to meet the pregnancy and birth cohort eligibility criteria outlined in table 1. For the pregnancy and birth cohort, recruitment is extended to obstetricians and gynaecologists of the participating centres and midwifery practices in the surrounding regions.

Study size

In line with the primary aim of the MONUCO study, the study size was determined with a dietary factor as the exposure and a musculoskeletal parameter as the outcome. Disproportionate loss of fat-free mass—defined as >30% fat-free mass loss of total weight loss—was selected as the outcome to base the initial study size of the MONUCO study on, while inadequate protein intake was selected as the exposure. Previous studies reported a prevalence of disproportionate fat-free mass loss of 12% and a prevalence of inadequate protein intake of 41%.35 36 Taking these prevalences into account, it was determined that a sample size of 915 participants is required to detect a risk ratio between 1.5 and 1.7, with 80% power and a two-sided 95% CI, for disproportionate fat-free mass loss in patients with an inadequate protein intake compared with an adequate protein intake at 2 years of follow-up. This range represents a scientifically and clinically relevant risk. In a previous comparable cohort study, the loss to follow-up was 13% at 2 years follow-up.37 Due to some additional study visits, the loss to follow-up in the MONUCO study is estimated to be 25% (n~229) at 2 years of follow-up, resulting in a required total study size of at least 1150 participants.

For the pregnancy and birth cohort, the aim was to include as many participants as was deemed feasible. Based on the aimed study size of 1150 for the MONUCO study, it was estimated that it would be feasible to achieve a sample size of at least 385 participants in the pregnancy and birth cohort. This number was rounded up to 400 participants.

Study procedures

Potential participants for the cohort receive the participant information brochure via their care providers, after which they can decide to provide informed consent during a face-to-face information session or after a phone call with a member of the research team. Participants who have signed the informed consent are invited to the first study visit and receive more instructions about the study procedures.

Study visits take place before the start of obesity treatment, and at 3 and 6 months after obesity treatment, followed by annual measurements (online supplemental table 1). To reduce participants’ burden, these visits are combined with hospital check-ups as part of standard care practices, whenever possible. Due to variation in the timing of medical check-ups between hospitals, the two follow-up visits within the first year may be reduced to only one visit between 3 and 6 months. Around the time of a study visit, participants complete online questionnaires and collect biological material at home. During the study visit, physical measurements are performed, including anthropometrics, bioimpedance analysis (BIA) and muscle function tests. Within the subgroups, a DXA scan, an isokinetic strength assessment, and an MRI scan are performed. At the healthcare facility, venous blood samples are collected and processed into whole blood, plasma and serum aliquots. Subgroup participants also wear an accelerometer-based device for 7 consecutive days.

Participants in the pregnancy and birth cohort complete online questionnaires during each trimester, at birth, and 2 months, 1 year and 4 years postpartum. Subgroup participants have an in-person visit for a quantitative ultrasound measurement. They also collect urine during the first and third trimester, faeces at delivery and 2 months postpartum and human milk samples 2 months postpartum. Venous blood samples are collected during blood draws scheduled for standard care in each trimester, at 2 months postpartum and 1-year postpartum. During delivery, umbilical cord blood samples as well as an additional venous blood sample are collected from the mother. All biological samples, collected in the cohort, are stored at −80°C until analysis.

Study parameters

An overview of all study parameters as discussed below is provided in online supplemental table 1 for the MONUCO study and online supplemental table 2 for the integrated MONUCO pregnancy and birth cohort.

Study population characteristics

Demographic, medical and treatment information is retrieved from the electronic health record (EHR) and by means of a questionnaire. Height, weight, waist circumference and hip circumference are measured during study visits. For the pregnancy and birth cohort, general information on obstetric history, pregnancy course (eg, development of complications like gestational diabetes) and other birth outcomes is retrieved from medical files and a questionnaire.

Dietary intake and nutrient status

A web-based comprehensive food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) is used to assess habitual dietary intake,38–41 while actual dietary intake is assessed with a 3-day food record using a mobile app (Traqq).42 A questionnaire is used to gather information on temporal eating patterns, including the timing, frequency and regularity of meals and snacks. Dietary supplement intake is also assessed using a questionnaire. Information on nutrient status is collected from the EHR and assessed in venous blood samples (eg, ferritin, vitamin B12, folic acid, vitamin D).

In the pregnancy and birth cohort, a short FFQ is used to assess diet quality in pregnant women.43 The short FFQ was used as it is especially developed for pregnant women and takes less time to complete than the comprehensive FFQ. Foetal nutrient status is determined by biochemical parameters in venous cord blood collected at delivery (eg, iron, vitamins A, B11, B12 and D). Additionally, human milk samples are analysed for nutritional composition (eg, energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, vitamin A).

Musculoskeletal health

The primary musculoskeletal parameters assessed in the MONUCO study are fat-free mass, measured with BIA and bone mineral density, measured in both hips with a DXA scan. To characterise musculoskeletal health beyond mass and density, parameters for muscle function and composition, including handgrip strength, functional and isokinetic lower limb strength, and muscle and bone marrow fat fraction, are also assessed. Additionally, bone turnover markers (procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and c-telopeptide cross-link type 1 collagen (CTX)) are measured in the venous blood samples.

Foetal growth and child development

Information on (estimated) foetal growth and development outcomes is retrieved from the medical records and includes parameters from standard foetal ultrasounds as well as birth weight in grams, sex and weight-for-age percentile. SGA will be defined as a birthweight <10th percentile of the Dutch Perined birth weight charts stratified for sex and gestational age at delivery.44 Information on child growth and development until 4 years of age is retrieved from general development questionnaires as well as from standard visits to the child health clinics

Endocrine and reproductive health

Endocrine and reproductive health is assessed with a custom questionnaire about the menstrual cycle and menopause as well as through hormonal levels measured in venous blood and urine (eg, oestrogen, progesterone, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)). Within the pregnancy and birth cohort, pregnancy symptoms are assessed with the Pregnancy Symptom Inventory questionnaire.45

Gastrointestinal health

Parameters to represent gastrointestinal health are assessed by various questionnaires. These parameters include abdominal complaints assessed with the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale,46 irritable bowel syndrome-related complaints assessed with the RRome IV criteria,47 dumping syndrome symptoms assessed using the Sigstad score48 and the Arts dumping questionnaire,49 food intolerances assessed using the quality of alimentation questionnaire50 51 and stool pattern assessed with the Bristol stool chart.52 Additionally, the collected stool samples are used to assess pH as a marker of fermentation and microbiome composition.

Mental health

Mental health-related parameters include depression symptoms assessed with the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression scale,53 global self-esteem assessed with the Rosenberg Self-Esteem scale54 and symptoms of eating disorder assessed with the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire.55 Health-related quality of life is assessed using the Obesity Quality of Life Questionnaire (OBESI-Q), which is a questionnaire specifically developed for patients with obesity.56 The OBESI-Q includes a set of six selected subscales of the BODY-Q that have been separately validated: eating behaviour, social function, psychological function, physical function, body image and sexual function. In the pregnancy and birth cohort, additional risk factors for postpartum depression are assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.57

Sleep and physical activity

Physical activity outcomes and sleep quality are retrieved from the Short Questionnaire to Assess Health-enhancing Physical Activity58 and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index questionnaires,59 respectively. More detailed information on sleep duration and physical activity is collected in the subgroups using the Actigraph wGT3X-BT, an accelerometer-based device, which is worn for 7 consecutive days.

Data handling, analysis and interpretation

Data monitoring and quality assurance

To ensure the quality of the collected data, all study parameters are assessed by trained personnel using standardised protocols. The collected data are systematically recorded in an electronic case report form, Castor EDC (Castor EDC, Amsterdam, the Netherlands), and the source data are stored to refer back to, if needed. Validated questionnaires are used, which will be scored according to the guidelines provided by the publishers. Questionnaire responses are reviewed by members of the research team, and potential mistakes are verified with the participant.

Data analysis plan

For all analyses, R software (V.4.5.1 or newer; https://www.r-project.org) will be used, and a double-sided α of 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Continuous variables will be expressed as means±SD on normally distributed data, or as medians with IQRs in case data are not normally distributed. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies or proportions. Descriptive statistics will be used to explore the measured outcomes at different time points. Differences over time in the various outcomes will be explored using linear mixed models for repeated measurements. To address missing data, multiple imputation will be used when appropriate and feasible. We will analyse the risk of disproportionate fat-free mass loss (>30% of total weight loss) based on protein intake (sufficient (≥60 g/day) vs insufficient (<60 g/day)) either using Poisson or Hazard regression modelling with robust SEs, while adjusting for potential confounders to obtain a risk ratio with a 95% CI. A similar approach will be used to explore associations in the pregnancy and birth cohort, such as the risk of delivering an SGA infant and maternal nutrient status. Additionally, we will study linear relationships between continuous exposures and continuous outcomes using linear regression models, while accounting for potential confounding variables. Suitability of non-linear models, such as fractional polynomials or restricted cubic splines, will be explored to study non-linear relationships. Demographic variables, medical conditions, use of medications, lifestyle factors and anthropometric measures will be used as descriptive variables and inspected for being confounders if appropriate. Potential confounders and effect modifiers will be identified through literature research, inspecting correlations between these variables and the exposure and outcome, and evaluating changes in the effect measures in adjusted compared with unadjusted models. Potential confounders for the association between disproportionate fat-free mass loss and protein intake include age, pretreatment BMI, presence of comorbidities (eg, diabetes, thyroid dysfunction) and physical activity. For the association between delivery of an SGA infant and maternal nutrient status, potential confounders include maternal age, gestational weight gain, pregnancy-induced hypertension, gestational diabetes and maternal smoking status. For both analyses, treatment type will also be assessed as a potential confounder or effect modifier and subgroup analyses will be performed when appropriate.

Patient and public involvement

During the study design process, MBS patients were involved in the writing of the information pamphlet and informed consent. Furthermore, throughout the study, participants are invited to participate in focus group discussions on how to improve the study procedures. Additionally, at any time, participants are encouraged to reach out to the research team via email or by telephone with questions or suggestions. There are currently no plans for public involvement in this study.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice, the Dutch Agreement on Medical Treatment Act (WGBO) and the European General Data Protection Regulation. The MONUCO study protocol has been approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (METC) Oost-Nederland. Study measurements could result in unexpected findings that are a concern to the participant’s health. With the informed consent, participants approve that these unexpected findings are reported to their primary care doctor or medical specialist.

The results of this study will be disseminated in the form of scientific abstracts, posters, oral presentations and publications in peer-reviewed journals. Additionally, information may be made available to study participants via a newsletter and to the general public, for example, through a website(page) about the cohort.

Discussion

With the MONUCO study, we aim to provide valuable insights into changes in nutrient intake and status, and consequent health of women in response to obesity treatment, with a focus on musculoskeletal health and the role other lifestyle factors can play in optimising health after treatment. Additionally, with the results from the integrated pregnancy and birth cohort, we aim to create an evidence base for prenatal and postnatal nutritional care for women who have received obesity treatment. The insights from this study will, therefore, not only contribute to improved care and better treatment outcomes for future patients but will also support the health of future generations.

A major strength of the MONUCO study is the long-term follow-up compared with other observational studies with a similar focus. In the case of MBS, the follow-up of 10 years will allow us to look at changes in health far beyond the initial 2 years in which a nadir weight is reached. For OMMs, we can provide insights into the effects of the long-term use of weight loss medications. Additionally, the long-term follow-up also allows us to observe changes in health, nutrient status and diet as the participants age. To minimise loss-to-follow-up during the several years of follow-up, we meet with our participants during the face-to-face visits, organise focus groups to improve study procedures, update them about the study via newsletters and provide them with a personal results report.

Another strength of the MONUCO study is the use of practical measurements in a large sample (eg, anthropometrics and BIA), while additionally using more accurate and resource-intensive measurements in a subgroup of this sample (eg, DXA and MRI). This allows us to investigate how results from more practical measurements compare to results of the resource-intensive measurements. Based on this, we can provide guidance on when and how these practical methods might be used for monitoring, screening or diagnosis in usual medical care.

Finally, due to the relatively large sample size required, the multicentre design and the inclusion of both MBS and treatment with OMMs, we have the opportunity to gather a study population representative of all Dutch women who receive treatment for obesity. To ensure the representativeness of our study population, we will monitor the representation of different demographic groups during the recruitment phase. If needed, we will adjust the recruitment strategies and materials and study procedures to ensure the inclusion of demographic groups who are insufficiently represented.
