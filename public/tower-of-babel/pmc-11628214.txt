The Association of Prenatal Alcohol Exposure With Brain Development During the First 1000 Days of Life: A Systematic Review
Authors: Marin Pielage, Melek Rousian, Sem van Heteren, Irene A. L. Groenenberg, Manon H. J. Hillegers, Eric A. P. Steegers, Hanan El Marroun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrenatal exposure to alcohol (PAE) can impact short‐ and long‐term offspring health. However, knowledge on PAE and brain development in early life is limited. This systematic review investigated associations between PAE and brain development during the first 1000 days of life, and was registered in PROSPERO at CRD42022355144. The literature search was performed from inception until February 2024 in EMBASE, MEDLINE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Library. Studies investigating PAE and brain development during the first 1000 days using ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) were included. Our search initially identified 3618 articles, of which 16 were included. The findings suggest inconsistent associations between PAE and early brain development. Ultrasound studies on PAE and brain size report no clear relationship. Some postnatal MRI studies reported smaller thalami, amygdalae and hippocampi in alcohol exposed neonates. Postnatal DTI studies (n = 5) examining network integrity and connectivity reported bidirectional results in multiple brain networks. Our results highlight the need for further research on first trimester brain development, timing and quantity of alcohol exposure using a core set of validated instruments. Longitudinal assessments and standardized procedures for neuroimaging are crucial to understand the impact of PAE on early brain development.

Introduction

The first 1000 days of life are described as a crucial window during which environmental exposures can influence cell differentiation and tissue development, impacting both short‐ and long‐term offspring health [1]. One of the exposures forming a significant health concern is prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE), with a global prevalence of 10% [2]. In literature there is no clear definition of PAE, and it varies from one drink at any time during pregnancy to heavy alcohol consumption of several drinks per day. PAE has been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage, stillbirth, and premature birth [3, 4, 5]. Furthermore, the well‐known consequences of PAE are cognitive and behavioural problems, known as foetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) [6]. Foetal alcohol syndrome (FAS) is the most severe variant, characterized by facial features and central nervous system abnormalities [7]. However, milder forms of FASD manifest as impaired cognitive functioning, learning disabilities, hyperactivity, inattentiveness, poor memory, and language difficulties, symptoms that may result from morphological brain changes [7]. Currently, knowledge on PAE and brain development during the first 1000 days is scarce.

A previous systematic review of FASD diagnosed individuals aged 5–22 years reported smaller total brain volumes and smaller grey and white matter volumes [8]. Additionally, neuroimaging studies in children and adolescents prenatally exposed to alcohol or having FASD have reported thinning, malformation or agenesis of the corpus callosum and volume reduction of the hippocampus, cerebellum, basal ganglia and thalami [6, 8]. These studies are valuable but were not performed in the first 1000 days of life. Gaining insights into PAE and brain development can contribute to early detection, monitoring, prevention strategies and treatment. Hence, the objective of the current systematic review was to provide a comprehensive overview of existing studies that investigated associations between PAE and brain development during the first 1000 days of life.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Study Selection

The literature search was performed by author M.P. and a librarian experienced in health‐related systematic reviews in Embase, MEDLINE, Web of Science, PsycINFO and the Cochrane Library (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cochrane Methodology Register) from inception to February 26, 2024. The search strategy included prenatal alcohol exposure, foetal or infant brain development, and neuroimaging related keywords (Table S1). This systematic review was registered (PROSPERO 2022: CRD42022355144), available from (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=355144).

We excluded animal studies, in vitro studies, studies in children above 2 years of age, studies investigating other substances (without any alcohol use), functional MRI studies, book chapters, dissertations, congress proceedings, and studies with incomplete information. Additionally, unrelated systematic reviews were excluded.

Figure 1 depicts the selection process of studies included in this systematic review. The search strategy resulted in 5187 records, of which 3618 records remained after removing duplicates. The complete list of titles and abstracts (n = 3618) was independently screened by authors M.P. and S.H. to identify studies eligible for full‐text screening. In total, 3588 articles were excluded. Overall, there was a high agreement between both raters, and a few abstracts were discussed, resulting in the exclusion of fourteen articles (Table S3). Finally, sixteen original articles were eligible for full text screening, after which thirteen articles met all inclusion criteria. Cross reference checking for relevant articles on PAE and early brain development within included articles and related systematic reviews (n = 112) resulted in three additional inclusions [9, 10, 11].

Flowchart of study selection.

A renewed search due to the time lag between the initial search and manuscript drafting yielded 459 new records, with one article eligible for inclusion [11]. In total, sixteen articles were included in this systematic review. Furthermore, during the screening process, 20 case reports containing relevant information were identified (Table S6).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Authors M.P. and S.H. independently read the full text articles, and performed the data extraction. Study information was collected in a custom‐made data extraction form including study period, study population, study design, methodology, exposure information, imaging type (ultrasound, MRI or DTI), brain measurements and study results. M.P. assessed the quality of the included studies using an adapted ErasmusAGE quality score (Table S2) [12, 13]. The quality score was based on five items covering study design and size, description and investigation of exposure and outcome, and possible adjustments in analyses. For each study a total quality score varying from 0 to 10 was obtained, with 10 representing the highest quality. Case reports were not scored based on quality. Because of the heterogeneity of outcomes across studies, it was not possible to perform a meta‐analysis.

Brain Development During Pregnancy

In total, eight studies investigated brain development during pregnancy, including size and growth measurements. All were performed in second and third trimesters using either ultrasound or MRI. Quality scores ranged between 4 and 9 with a mean of 6.5 (Table S4).

Brain Development Investigated With Ultrasound—Standard Biometric Measurements

Figure 3 illustrates the ultrasound measurements used to assess brain development in the included studies. In seven studies, standard biometric measures including head circumference (HC), biparietal diameter (BPD) or occipitofrontal diameter (OFD) were investigated [9, 10, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. In these studies, alcohol consumption was mostly assessed with the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) (n = 5) and the Tolerance‐Worried‐Eye opener Amnesia and (K) Cut‐down (TWEAK) (n = 4) [19, 20], with some using multiple instruments. Structured and unstructured face‐to‐face interviews, postal questionnaires and urinalysis (to test for agreement with self‐reports) were used [9, 14, 17, 18].

Overview of ultrasound measurements performed during pregnancy in reviewed studies. Overview of ultrasound measurements performed in included studies. BPD, biparietal diameter; CCD, caval‐calvarial distance; CM, cisterna magna; FTD, frontothalamic distance; HC, head circumference; LV, lateral ventricle; OFD, occipitofrontal diameter; TCD, transverse cerebellar diameter.

Two studies (n = 31 and n = 223) reported negative associations of PAE with foetal HC in second trimester or longitudinally measured HC/abdominal circumference ratio in second and third trimester [10, 16]. Two studies (n = 31 and n = 166) reported a smaller BPD size in second or third trimester [15, 16]. Three other studies reported no association of PAE with HC or BPD size or growth [9, 14, 18]. Two of these studies had small sample sizes (n = 31 and n = 233) and one investigated a large cohort (n = 7333) [9]. The OFD is the foetal head diameter from the external occipital protuberance to the frontal bone midline [14]. The majority of studies (n = 4) reported no associations of PAE and OFD [10, 14, 15, 17]. One study (n = 31) reported that PAE was associated with a smaller OFD in mid‐pregnancy [16]. Summarized and as shown in Figure 2, negative associations between PAE and foetal HC, BPD and OFD were reported in some studies (box 1, 2 and 8), but results were inconsistent (box 3–5 and 7).

Brain Development Investigated With Ultrasound—Structure Specific Measurements

Six studies (from n = 31 to n = 233) investigated associations between PAE and structure specific brain measurements, including the frontal lobe, cerebellum, corpus callosum, lateral ventricles and cisterna magna [10, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Figure 3 illustrates two frontal lobe measurements: the caval‐calvarial distance (CCD) measured from the inner frontal bone (calvarium) to the posterior cavum septum pellucidum margin; and the frontothalamic distance (FTD) measured from the calvarium to the posterior thalamic margin [14].

In all studies, PAE was assessed using self‐reports, except for one study using a structured interview method [18]. The most commonly used self‐report methods were AUDIT (n = 4) and TWEAK (n = 4) [19, 20]. In addition to self‐reports, two studies used the Timeline Follow back method to quantify PAE [15, 17]. While most studies adjusted their analyses for smoking as a confounder, two studies also considered additional substance use (e.g., cannabis, cocaine or methamphetamine) [10, 18], while one study did not adjust their analysis [14].

Two studies reported that PAE was associated with a smaller CCD and FTD in second or third trimester [15, 18], whereas other studies reported no associations [16, 17]. One study reported a larger FTD in occasional drinkers (n = 14, EtG level more than 100 ng/mL in maternal urine) with no association of PAE with the CCD [14].

In total, six studies (sample sizes ranged from n = 31 to n = 233) investigated the cerebellum by measuring the transverse cerebellar diameter (TCD, Figure 3) or the cerebellar volume in three‐dimensional (3D) US images [10, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. One study performed longitudinal measurements, investigating cerebellar growth, whereas the other studies measured cerebellar size once [10]. Most studies (n = 5) reported no associations between PAE and cerebellar size or growth [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. However, one study reported that heavy alcohol consumption (i.e., > 5 drinks/drinking day) was associated with a smaller TCD [10].

Further, no associations were reported for PAE with lateral ventricle or cisterna magna measurements, corpus callosum size or vermis [10, 14, 16]. Summarizing, although some associations between PAE and frontal lobe measures were reported (Figure 2, box 2 and 5), results varied across studies (Figure 2, box 1, 3 and 4). Associations between PAE and TCD were less inconsistent, with most reviewed studies reporting no associations (Figure 2, box 1–5). Lastly, no associations of PAE with lateral ventricle or cisterna magna size, corpus callosum, cerebellar volume or vermis were reported (Figure 2, box 1, 4 and 8).

Brain Development Investigated With MRI—Volumetric Brain Measurement

One study used MRI to examine corpus callosum and periventricular zone volume. Alcohol consumption was assessed in 76 women using the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitor System (PRAMS) and Tolerance‐Annoyed Cut down Eye opener (T‐ACE) self‐reports [21, 22]. In this study, analyses were corrected for multiple testing but not for potential confounders [11]. PAE was associated with a larger corpus callosum volume and smaller periventricular zone volume (Figure 2, box 6) [11].

Brain Development and Connectivity During Infancy

In total, eight studies examined associations between PAE and brain development from birth to 2 years of age using either trans fontanel ultrasound or MRI, as discussed below. The quality scores ranged between 3 and 8 with a mean of 5 (Table S5).

Brain Development Investigated With Ultrasound—Infant Brain Structure

Three studies examined brain development using trans fontanel ultrasounds, measuring the corpus callosum, and volumes of grey matter, thalamus, hippocampus and amygdala [23, 24, 25]. One study is a pilot study including a subset of participants (n = 18) who were also included in the original article (with a total sample size of n = 44) [23, 24]. PAE was assessed with self‐reported information using the World Health Organization's Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST), a drinking calendar or an interview method [26].

Bookstein et al. reported an association between PAE and altered distribution of corpus callosum splenium angle coordinates [24], confirming a previous pilot study [23]. The confirmation study, with a sample of n = 44, included 23 PAE cases. A third study (n = 349) found an association between PAE and brain haemorrhage or white matter damage in preterm infants [25]. Summarizing, Figure 2 (box 9–11) shows potential associations between PAE and corpus callosum development, brain haemorrhage, and white matter damage. However, these results were reported in only three studies.

Brain Development Investigated MRI or DTI—Infant Brain Size and Connectivity

Five studies used MRI techniques to investigate brain development during infancy [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. In three studies, PAE was investigated using ASSIST questionnaire self‐reports [26], while two studies quantified PAE with the Timeline Follow back method.

Two studies used MRI modalities (T1 and T2 weighted images) to investigate associations between PAE and brain size, adjusting for maternal smoking/substance use or excluding participants using substances [27, 29]. Donald et al. (n = 73) reported that PAE was associated with smaller volumes of the total brain, grey matter, amygdala, left thalamus and left hippocampus [27]. Additionally, PAE was associated with a smaller corpus callosum area between 6 and 40 days of age [29].

The three remaining studies used DTI techniques to investigate potential associations of PAE with newborn brain size [30, 31, 32]. One study (n = 20) investigated associations between PAE, total brain volume and five major white matter tracts (i.e., corpus callosum and corona radiata; left and right hemispheric projection fibres; left and right hemispheric association fibres) divided into 8–12 local regions of interest (ROI) per tract. Taylor et al. observed an association of PAE with reduced total brain volumes, mean diffusivity and axial diffusivity in all five major white matter tracts. Additional analysis showed the strongest associations in the medial and inferior regions [31]. Another study (n = 56) reported associations between PAE and reduced axial diffusivity in the right superior longitudinal fasciculus, a major white matter bundle connecting frontal, temporal and parietal cortices [28]. Lastly, Roos et al. (n = 25) studied associations between PAE and structural connectivity. Structural connectivity was estimated as nodal betweenness centrality at minimum density, which was described in detail elsewhere [33]. Alcohol exposed neonates had higher structural connectivity in frontal, temporal and occipital regions compared with controls. In contrast, controls showed higher structural connectivity in parietal regions compared to alcohol‐exposed neonates [30].

In summary, limited studies investigated PAE in relation to volumetric brain measurements. Results suggest associations with reduced total brain, grey matter, amygdala, left thalamus, left hippocampus volumes, and a smaller corpus callosum area (Figure 2, boxes 13, 14 and 16).

Studies investigating PAE and network integrity and connectivity investigated multiple regions of interest. They reported an association between PAE and lower mean and axial diffusivity, especially in medial and inferior white matter, and reduced connectivity in parietal regions (Figure 2, boxes 14 and 16). In contrast, other studies suggest associations of PAE with increased frontal, temporal and occipital regional connectivity (Figure 2, box 15). Since these studies investigated different regions of interest, drawing strong conclusions based on these data is challenging.

Case Reports and/or Case Series

Although describing case reports/series on PAE and offspring brain development was not the main aim of this systematic review, these can be valuable in clinical practice. Table S6 presents a detailed overview of these cases.

In total, 21 reports/series were identified, describing over 200 cases with PAE. All foetuses and newborns were exposed to high alcohol consumption (i.e., multiple standard drinks per day). In 20 cases with chronic maternal alcohol abuse, 16 subjects with FAS were described [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Other studies (n = 4) reported five cases of holoprosencephaly, including cyclopia and agnathia [43, 44, 45, 46], while two studies mentioned microcephaly [35, 36].

Common structure‐specific anomalies included cerebellar hypoplasia (n = 7) [34, 35, 38, 39, 41, 47], brainstem hypoplasia or an absent pons (n = 6) [34, 35, 38, 42, 48], lateral ventricle abnormalities (n = 11) [36, 37, 38, 39, 48, 49, 50, 51], corpus callosum hypoplasia or agenesis (n = 13) [38, 40, 44, 45, 51, 52, 53], fusion of basal ganglia, thalami or frontal lobes (n = 3) [38, 44, 47], and intracranial haemorrhages [38, 45, 54]. In 10 cases pregnant women co‐used marijuana, tobacco, cocaine, methadone, painkillers, sedatives or benzodiazepines [38, 46, 47, 48, 49]. In summary, various anomalies have been observed in prenatally exposed foetuses or infants. Similar to the original articles included in this review, these case reports used different methods to assess PAE. The described cases highlight the complexity of investigating the consequences of PAE on brain development and challenges of disentangling the causal effect in severely affected children, especially when considering the co‐use of other substances.

Discussion

Main Findings

This systematic review investigated the associations of PAE with early brain development. We found that the reported results are inconsistent. Some studies during pregnancy have reported associations between PAE and biometric measures (i.e., HC and BPD) and frontal lobe measurements although results varied. Most studies performed during pregnancy used ultrasound imaging for foetal brain examination, except for one study using MRI. This study reported positive associations of PAE with corpus callosum and periventricular zone volume. Ultrasound studies during infancy suggest an association of PAE with brain haemorrhage, and white matter damage. Furthermore, MRI studies have reported negative associations between PAE and volumes of the total brain, grey matter, amygdala, left thalamus, left hippocampus, and corpus callosum size or area in infants. Additionally, postnatal MRI studies have reported bidirectional associations between PAE and network connectivity in the infant brain. Inconsistent findings in the reviewed studies limit the ability to draw strong conclusions about PAE and early brain development.

Strengths and Limitations

The major strengths of this review include an extensive literature search across multiple databases with cross‐reference checking, resulting in comprehensive literature coverage. Also, a predefined protocol with clear inclusion and exclusion criteria and a quality assessment of included studies is beneficial.

There are some limitations, as discussed in the following section. Studies during pregnancy had a mean quality score of 6.5, while post‐partum studies had a mean quality score of 5. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution. First, the included studies used self‐reported PAE measures, mostly assessed once during pregnancy, thus depending on recall of multiple exposure periods and possibly leading to recall bias and misclassification of PAE [55]. Moreover, PAE definitions varied across studies, with 14 distinct questionnaires used. Few studies differentiate between exposure periods (e.g., each trimester), often lacking details on the quantity and timing of alcohol use. This variability likely contributed to inconsistencies in results across the included studies. Frequently used questionnaires such as TWEAK and T‐ACE primarily identify hazardous drinking patterns, leading to the exclusion of women with less severe but potentially harmful drinking patterns during pregnancy [20, 21, 56]. The use of multiple instruments across the included studies further highlights the lack of a clear and consistent definition of PAE. To improve future research, we recommend a core set of validated instruments that can be used individually or in combination, capturing both quantity and timing of PAE. The validated Timeline Follow (TLFB) method provides detailed information on daily alcohol consumption using memory aids to enhance recall accuracy [57]. Additionally, the TLFB method can be administered both by an interviewer and by computer and is time‐efficient, taking only 10–15 min to complete a 90‐day recall [58]. The validated, short and efficient AUDIT‐questionnaire assesses alcohol and other substance use in 10 questions [19]. The AUDIT is effective for identifying drinking behaviours in the past year. The Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) assesses health behaviour before and during pregnancy, including timing and quantity of both alcohol use and co‐use of other substances [22]. This method allows longitudinal data collection, which is useful for understanding trends in PAE as well as the impact of interventions.

Second, comparison of results of included studies is complicated by variations in imaging techniques. Most studies during pregnancy used ultrasound imaging, except for one study using MRI. Postpartum studies used either ultrasound or MRI, but focussed on different brain regions. Additionally, standard biometric measures (i.e., HC and BPD) limit the ability to draw conclusions about the sensitivity of specific brain areas to PAE. Furthermore, drawing conclusions on associations of PAE with longitudinal brain growth is complicated since this was only investigated in two studies [9, 10].

Third, alcohol consumption often co‐occurs with other substance use, including tobacco or recreational drugs [59]. However, some included studies did not correct for other substance use, introducing bias in published results [11, 14, 30]. Substance co‐use can confound the association between PAE and brain development, as both alcohol and other substances may influence foetal outcomes. Therefore, distinguishing the specific influences of alcohol from other substances is challenging. Excluding participants using multiple substances may introduce selection bias, while adjusting for co‐use could mask consequences of PAE. Furthermore, investigating these interactions in a controlled experimental setting is not feasible due to ethical concerns, complicating the ability to isolate influences of PAE on foetal brain development. Consequently, future studies should implement comprehensive measures of substance use, including standardized questionnaires capturing co‐use behaviours, and consider the use of biomarkers to objectively identify exposure to multiple substances. As a result, future studies could more accurately isolate the effects of PAE on foetal brain development.

Lastly, most reviewed studies during pregnancy had small sample sizes (ranging from n = 31 up to n = 233). The only study investigating n = 7333 foetuses, studied a single ultrasound measure (HC) and investigated PAE with postal questionnaires. Similarly, postnatal studies had small sample sizes (ranging from n = 18 up to n = 73), expect for one study including n = 349 subjects [9, 25].

Interpretation

Future Research Recommendations

This systematic review identified a research gap regarding associations between PAE and first trimester brain development. Longitudinal brain growth during the first 1000 days was also understudied. Existing studies lack details on the quantity and timing of PAE, as well as details on substance co‐use, complicating the differentiation between influences of alcohol or other substances. Future studies should collect a comprehensive dataset including these details to help unravel the influences of PAE on the developing brain. This could facilitate the identification of vulnerable brain structures, and improve the future detection of brain abnormalities in relation to PAE.

To overcome identified challenges, future studies should standardize the time of observation (e.g., preconception and each trimester) and methodology of investigating PAE. Accurate, quantitative and prospective identification of PAE combined with biomarker analyses could overcome underreporting or misclassification of PAE [57, 60]. This approach increases knowledge about the quantity of PAE, providing the ability to distinguish low or moderate drinking behaviour from hazardous drinking behaviour. Future studies during pregnancy may benefit from standardised measurements of brain structures. A stepwise approach, in which biometric measures are followed by targeted neurosonography, as described in the ISUOG neurosonography guidelines, would be beneficial [61, 62]. Since separate brain structures have unique growth potentials, it is necessary to identify structure‐specific alterations in the brain after PAE [63]. A longitudinal approach with multiple ultrasounds during pregnancy combining volumetric and cortical folding measurements provides more details on longitudinal brain development [64, 65, 66]. Advanced imaging technology including non‐invasive 3D ultrasound or virtual reality could improve image quality and measurement accuracy [67, 68]. Finally, artificial intelligence (deep learning) might be a time‐saving solution for image alignment and measurements [69, 70].

Clinical Implications

We cautiously suggest an association between PAE and early brain development although establishing causality from the results of this systematic review is not possible. Despite obstetric guidelines advising alcohol abstinence during pregnancy, PAE has a global prevalence of 10% [2]. Since brain development measurements in alcohol exposed foetuses are not incorporated in standard obstetric care, intervening solely based on abnormal brain ultrasounds is not advised. Nonetheless, previous studies indicate harmful effects of PAE on pregnancy outcomes, early development and offspring health. Therefore, this review does not change any existing guideline recommendations, and we recommend alcohol abstinence during pregnancy. Early identification of altered brain development could facilitate timely intervention and support before FASD‐related problems emerge in childhood. To draw stronger conclusions, it is necessary to establish scientific evidence regarding associations between PAE and alterations in early brain development through large longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

We identified a research gap regarding PAE and first trimester brain development as well as longitudinal brain growth data during the first 1000 days, both limiting evidence on associations of PAE and brain development. Therefore, future studies should preferably be longitudinal in nature, performed in a large cohort, investigate the timing and quantity of PAE, and use a standardized neuroimaging approach. Although results remain inconclusive, health care providers should be attentive about PAE, actively discuss maternal alcohol consumption, advise abstaining from alcohol use during pregnancy, and intervene early in pregnancy if possible.
