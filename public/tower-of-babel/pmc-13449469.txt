ROS-mitochondria-NLRP3/STING axis mediates the enhanced anti-coronavirus activity of ZIF8 nanoparticles
Authors: Haojie Ge, Mengjuan Liao, Yaoxin Shen, Yong Zhao, Baochao Fan, Jizong Li, Min Sun, Shanshan Yang, Li Mao, Xinmei Geng, Rongli Guo, Bin Li, Mi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coronaviruses (CoVs) pose serious threats to both animal and human health, underscoring the urgent need for broad-spectrum antiviral strategies that can provide rapid and immune-independent protection beyond vaccination. In this study, we developed zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF8) as an innovative zinc-based anti-coronavirus nanoagent and revealed that ZIF8 exhibits markedly enhanced antiviral activity relative to the commonly used zinc oxide (ZnO). Using porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) as a model α-coronavirus, mechanistic studies demonstrated that ZIF8 alleviates excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and preserves mitochondrial homeostasis, while simultaneously promoting cytosolic mtDNA release through moderate opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) and coordinately regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation and STING signaling, thereby enhancing host antiviral responses. Consistent with this mechanism, ZIF8 nanoparticles displayed broad-spectrum inhibitory effects against representative β-, γ-, and δ-coronaviruses, including bovine coronavirus (BCoV), infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), and porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV). Moreover, in a PDCoV-infected mouse model, ZIF8 provided effective in vivo protection by reducing viral replication and preventing intestinal damage under both preventive and therapeutic regimens. Collectively, these findings establish the ROS-mitochondria-NLRP3/STING axis as a key mechanistic basis for ZIF8-mediated antiviral activity and provide new insights for the rational design of nanomaterial-based broad-spectrum antiviral therapeutics.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04618-5.

Introduction

Coronaviruses (CoVs) are a diverse family of RNA viruses that infect humans and a wide range of animal species [1]. Over the past two decades, several outbreaks caused by emerging CoVs have led to severe public health crises and substantial economic losses worldwide [2, 3]. Based on viral taxonomy, CoVs are divided into four genera: α-, β-, γ-, and δ-coronaviruses, represented by porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV, α), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2, β), bovine coronavirus (BCoV, β), infectious bronchitis virus (IBV, γ), and porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV, δ), respectively [4–6]. They cause severe respiratory and enteric diseases with high morbidity and, in some cases, substantial mortality, drive pandemics and epizootics, and impose major economic losses [7, 8]. Despite the availability of vaccines, the development of antiviral agents remains essential to provide broad-spectrum, rapid, and immune-independent protection against viral transmission and mutation.

Compared with conventional agents, nanomaterials may offer advantages in antiviral potential, which can be attributed to their nanoscale size, increased surface reactivity, and tunable physicochemical properties [9]. Multiple metal oxides such as copper oxides, titanium dioxide tungsten trioxide, and zinc oxide, have shown activity against diverse viruses, including influenza A (H1N1/H3N2), herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1), adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rotavirus, norovirus, and coronaviruses such as HCoV-229E, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2 [10–12]. Among them, ZnO is the most extensively studied [13], whose antiviral effects arise from a combination of ion release, surface-mediated reactive oxygen species that oxidize viral proteins/RNA, and strong virus adsorption [14, 15].

Recently, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), a type of crystalline porous materials, have emerged as promising candidates for developing protective strategies against infectious diseases [16]. Among them, zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF8), which is a kind of zinc based MOFs, may confer antiviral advantages by leveraging its high surface-area porous framework and positive surface to adsorb virions and its pH-triggered Zn²+ release may more effectively trigger immune signaling pathways [17]. ZIF8 has been studied predominantly for antibacterial applications such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis [18]. By contrast, antiviral reports on ZIF8 are still lacking, and current evidence only indicates that it can inactivate HSV-1 virus [19]. Therefore, the development of ZIF8 as an antiviral nanoagent and compared against traditional ZnO in terms of their antiviral efficacy, mechanisms, and biocompatibility would be of great significance for elucidating how the structural evolution from simple metal oxides to metal-organic frameworks influences antiviral performance, providing insights for the rational design of next-generation antiviral nanoagents.

In this study, PEDV, an enteric α-coronavirus with a well-characterized infection mechanism and viral life cycle, was used as a model to investigate the antiviral effects and underlying mechanisms of ZIF8 and ZnO. The underlying mechanisms responsible for the antiviral effects of the two nanoparticles (NPs) were subsequently investigated by comparing their impacts on reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, mitochondrial homeostasis, mtDNA synthesis and release, and the activation of the innate immune signaling. Finally, to determine the broad-spectrum antiviral potential of these NPs, their inhibitory activities were further evaluated against β-, γ-, and δ- representative coronaviruses, including BCoV, IBV and PDCoV. Our findings reveal that ZIF8 NPs exert antiviral activity by modulating ROS-mitochondria-NLRP3/STING signaling axis. Compared with ZnO, ZIF8 exhibits enhanced broader antiviral efficacy against multiple coronaviruses. Given the pronounced antiviral efficacy observed in PDCoV cell-based assays and the high mortality caused by PDCoV in neonatal piglets, which results in substantial economic losses to the swine industry [20], we next evaluated the in vivo protective effect of ZIF8 NPs using a PDCoV-infected mouse model. The results showed that ZIF8 confer effective in vivo protection against PDCoV infection by reducing viral replication and preventing virus-induced intestinal damage under both preventive and therapeutic regimens. These results provide new insights into the structure-antiviral function relationship of nanomaterials and offer promising strategies for the rational design of antiviral agents.

Methods

Cells, viral strains and specific reagents

Vero, PK-1, CEK, and HRT-18 cell lines were obtained from the Institute of Veterinary Medicine, Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Vero cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Vazyme). PK-1 and HRT-18 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Biological Industries). CEK cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. The PEDV AH2012/12 strain (GenBank accession no. KU646831), PDCoV CZ/2020 strain (GenBank accession no. OK546242), cpCoV/AHFY2302G strain (GenBank accession no. OR077309.1), and IBV CK/CH/WJ215 strain (GenBank accession no. PP212853.1) were maintained in our laboratory. DMEM and DMEM/F12 (1:1) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanpei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Trypsin-EDTA solution was obtained from Nanjing Shenghang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The RNA extraction kit, HiScript II One Step RT-PCR Kit, and ChamQ SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix with low ROX premix were obtained from Nanjing Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Monoclonal antibodies against PEDV-N, PDCoV-N, and IBV-N proteins, and rabbit polyclonal antibodies against bovine coronavirus, were provided by the Institute of Veterinary Medicine, Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, goat anti-rabbit IgG, and goat anti-chicken IgG secondary antibodies were purchased from Master Biological Technology. DCFH-DA ROS fluorescent probe, CoCl2·6H2O, Calcein-AM, cyclosporin A (CsA), and 1,6-bismaleimidohexane were purchased from Solarbio (China). DAPI dye, JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit, CCK-8 cell viability assay kit, BCA protein assay kit, RIPA lysis buffer, PMSF, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), and mitochondrial isolation kit were obtained from Beyotime (China). Zinc acetate was purchased from Kermel (China). Zinc oxide was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. INF39 and C-176 were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (U.S.A) and used as inhibitors of NLRP3 and STING signaling, respectively. BAX-IN-1, rotenone, and VBIT-4 were purchased from Henghuibio. The 1 M Tris-HCl buffer was purchased from Yuanye (China). EGS was obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Primary antibodies against NLRP3, Caspase-1, GAPDH, IL-1β, STING, TBK1, p-TBK1, β-actin, BAX were rabbit antibodies purchased from ABclonal (China).

Preparation and characterization of ZIF8 NPs

ZIF8 NPs were synthesized according to the method of Abdelhamid et al. [21]. Briefly, 30 mmol of zinc acetate was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water, and 30 mmol of 2‑methylimidazole was dissolved in another 20 mL of deionized water. A small portion of each solution was diluted with water and mixed together under stirring for 40 min. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the product, which was then dried in a vacuum freeze-dryer for 24 h to obtain white ZIF8 nanoparticle powder. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to analyze the chemical bonds and functional groups present in the NPs. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the crystal structure and phase composition. The surface morphology and size distribution of the NPs were examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), while Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was performed to assess the hydrodynamic size, particle size distribution, and zeta potential.

Quantitative assessment of Zn²⁺ release from ZIF8 NPs

Briefly, 1 mg of ZIF8 or ZnO NPs was dispersed in 1 mL of PBS and acetate buffer (pH 5.5) and mechanically shaken at 37 °C. Samples were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were collected, diluted 25-fold to a final volume of 25 mL prior to measurement, and the concentration of Zn²⁺ was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; PQ 9000E, Analytik Jena GmbH, Germany).

Evaluation of cell viability

Vero, PK-1, HRT-18, CEK cells were cultured in 96-well plates to 80–90% confluence and then treated with ZIF8, ZnO, zinc acetate, H2O2, rotenone at specific concentrations, in the corresponding culture medium for each cell type. Each concentration was tested in three replicates, with 100 µL per well. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for the following durations: 12, 24, and 36 h for ZIF8, ZnO, and zinc acetate; 2, 4, and 6 h for H₂O₂; and 2, 6, and 24 h for rotenone. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of CCK-8 solution (prepared as 10% CCK-8 in DMEM containing 1% PSF) was added to each well. Cells incubated with CCK-8 solution without treatment served as the control group, while wells without cells were used as the blank group. After 1–2 h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was calculated using the following formula:

Antiviral effect assay

Vero cells and PEDV (MOI = 0.1) were preincubated with ZIF8 or ZnO at 10, 20, 40, and 100 µg/mL (the nonlinear selection of these doses was based on potential feedback regulation at higher concentrations), or with zinc acetate at corresponding equimolar Zn²⁺ concentrations, for 1 h at 37 °C to allow these agents to act on cellular susceptibility to infection and/or directly interact with viral particles. The pretreated virus was then added to the cells and allowed to adsorb for 1 h, facilitating viral attachment to the cell surface. After viral adsorption, the supernatant was removed to eliminate unbound virus particles, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were subsequently incubated in medium containing the corresponding concentrations of ZIF8, ZnO, or zinc acetate until 24 h post-infection to allow continued inhibition of post-entry stages of the viral life cycle. The antiviral effects were evaluated by immunofluorescence assay (IFA), Western blot, and qRT-PCR. Similarly, the antiviral activities against BCoV, IBV, and PDCoV were evaluated in HRT-18, CEK, and PK-1 cells, respectively, using the same procedure.

Virus adsorption assay

Vero cells were precooled at 4 °C for 30 min and then infected with PEDV (MOI = 1) in the presence of NPs at 4 °C, to allow viral attachment to the cell surface while preventing internalization. After 1–2 h of adsorption, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed twice with precooled serum-free DMEM to eliminate unbound virus and residual NPs. Cells were then lysed, and total RNA was extracted for subsequent qRT‑PCR analysis to determine the levels of cellular viral RNA.

Virus entry assay

Vero cells were precooled at 4 °C for 30 min and then infected with PEDV (MOI = 0.1) at 4 °C for 1–2 h. Following adsorption, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed twice with precooled serum-free DMEM to remove unbound virus. Cells were subsequently incubated with culture medium containing NPs for 1 h at 37 °C to initiate cell entry. After two additional washes with serum-free DMEM, cells were then lysed, and total RNA was extracted for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis to determine the levels of cellular viral RNA.

Virus replication assay

PEDV (MOI = 0.1) was added to Vero cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following viral adsorption, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed twice with serum-free DMEM to remove unbound virus particles. Cells were subsequently incubated with NPs for 10–16 h. After two additional washes with serum-free DMEM, cells were then lysed, and total RNA was extracted for subsequent qRT‑PCR analysis to determine the levels of cellular viral RNA.

Virus release assay

PEDV (MOI = 0.1) was added to Vero cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After adsorption, cells were washed twice with serum-free DMEM to remove unbound virus and cultured for 16–24 h. The supernatant was then discarded, and cells were washed twice with serum-free DMEM to remove extracellular virus particles. Cells were subsequently incubated with NPs. After incubation for 60 min, the supernatant and cell lysate were collected separately to determine the levels of cellular viral RNA.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

All samples were first fixed onto glass slides with 4% paraformaldehyde and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5–10 min to allow antibody access. After blocking with 5% BSA to reduce nonspecific binding, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies specific to the target antigen, followed by washing to remove unbound antibodies. Subsequently, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied to bind the primary antibodies. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and after a final series of washes, the slides were mounted with antifade medium. Fluorescence signals were then visualized and imaged under CLSM (LSM880; ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany).

Western blot analysis

All the samples were washed and lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail on ice to extract protein. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit was used to determine the protein concentration. Proteins in equivalent amounts were then separated on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to the PVDF membrane, followed by blocking in PBST solution containing 5% skim milk or 5% BSA (for phosphorylated proteins) for 2 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against target proteins. Then, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 h at room temperature, and the protein blots were visualized via X-ray film. GAPDH was employed as the protein loading control.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells with the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC112, Nanjing, China) and was reverse transcribed to cDNA using HiScript II QRTSuperMix (Vazyme, R223-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ChamQ Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02) was used for real-time PCR. The reactions were run on QuantStudio 6 Flex machine (ABI, Thermo Fisher, USA) with the following steps: (i) 30 s at 95 ˚C and (ii) 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 ˚C and 30 s at 60 ˚C, followed by melting curve analysis. The primers used for the qRT-PCR analysis are listed in S1 Table. Levels of mRNA were normalized to GAPDH using the formula Δ CT (GAPDH)-ΔCT (mRNA ×) Detection of ddhCTP in cell lysates.

Determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Treatment was performed using 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime). Vero cells were exposed to PEDV, H₂O₂, or rotenone at specified concentrations for defined durations (PEDV at 0.1 MOI for 24 h, 300 µM H₂O₂ for 2 h, and 10 µM rotenone for 6 h), either in the presence or absence of NPs. Then, cells were washed twice with DMEM and incubated with DCFH-DA (5 µΜ/mL) for 30 min. After two washes with Opti-MEM, fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope to evaluate the relative levels of ROS production. And ROS levels were quantitatively analyzed by flow cytometry.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

Vero cells were seeded in 12-well plates and grown to confluence. Cells were washed twice with serum-free DMEM, and PEDV (MOI = 0.1) was pre-incubated with ZIF8 or ZnO NPs (40 µg/mL) for 1 h. The treated virus was used to infect the cells for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After adsorption, unbound virus was removed by PBS washes, and cells were maintained in medium containing the corresponding NPs. Virus-only and untreated cells served as positive and negative controls, respectively. After 24 h, mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 assay kit. A normal control and a CCCP-treated positive control (10 µM, 37 °C for 20 min) were included. Cells were washed with PBS, then incubated with 0.5 mL complete medium plus 0.5 mL JC-1 working solution at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. After two washes with JC-1 buffer, cells were resuspended in 1 mL complete medium. JC-1 aggregates were imaged by confocal microscopy, and red fluorescence intensity was quantified by flow cytometry to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential.

Determination of total and cytosolic mtDNA

Vero cells were inoculated into 24 well plates and treated according to the conditions above. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were washed twice with serum-free medium. Then, the cells were digested with trypsin and collected, and mtDNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit. To quantitatively detect the level of cytoplasmic mtDNA, Vero cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated according to the above conditions. The mitochondrial isolation kit was used for isolation. After extracting cytoplasmic DNA according to the instructions of the DNA extraction kit, the obtained DNA was identified by qPCR using TERT gene and B2M gene as internal parameters, ND5 gene and CYTB gene as mtDNA indicators.

Confocal analysis of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and mitochondria

Vero cells were inoculated into 24-well plates and treat them according to the above conditions. After discarding the supernatant, wash the cells twice with serum-free medium. Subsequently, the cells were stained with dsDNA and TOM20, and observe their co-localization using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

mPTP opening assay

mPTP opening was assessed using the Calcein-AM/CoCl₂ fluorescence quenching method. After 24 h of infection with PEDV, the cells of each group were washed with phenol red-free HBSS and maintained in the same buffer. Cells were then incubated with Calcein-AM (1 µM, dissolved in DMSO) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, CoCl₂·6 H₂O was added at a final concentration of 1 mM Co2+ and incubated for an additional 20–30 min to quench cytosolic calcein fluorescence. Cells were subsequently washed 1–2 times with HBSS and analyzed by flow cytometry to measure intracellular calcein fluorescence intensity. A decrease in calcein fluorescence indicates increased mPTP opening.

VBIT-4 and CsA inhibitory assay

Cells were pretreated with VBIT-4 (10 µM) or CSA (1 µM) for 1 h, followed by 24 h of infection with PEDV at 0.1 MOI in cells treated with NPs. After treatment, the culture medium was removed and cells were washed once with HBSS. Total RNA and cytosolic mtDNA were than collected and examined.

Detection of BAX oligomerization

After 24 h of infection with PEDV at 0.1 MOI in cells treated with NPs, mitochondrial fractions were isolated from cells using a commercial mitochondrial isolation kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The fractions were incubated with freshly prepared 1,6-bismaleimidohexane (BMH) for BAX cross-linking. BMH was prepared as a DMSO stock and diluted to 1 mM before incubation at room temperature for 30 min. The cross-linking reaction was terminated by adding Tris-HCl buffer to a final concentration of 20–50 mM and incubating for 5–10 min at room temperature. Mitochondrial fractions were lysed in 1% CHAPS buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. BAX oligomerization was subsequently analyzed by Western blotting.

PDCoV diarrhea models in suckling mice

BALB/c mice were purchased from Yangzhou University Laboratory Animal Center (Yangzhou, China) and housed under SPF conditions with free access to food and water. Suckling mice were obtained by mating adult BALB/c males and females. Three-day-old BALB/c suckling mice were used to establish PDCoV diarrhea models. To induce PDCoV diarrhea, 5 × 10⁸ TCID₅₀ of the PDCoV CZ2020 strain in 40 µL PBS was orally inoculated using a pipette tip. Mice were randomly assigned to six groups: ZIF8 preventive, ZIF8 therapeutic, ZnO preventive, ZnO therapeutic, infection control, and mock, with six mice per group. In the preventive groups, mice received oral administration of 1 mg NPs per mouse every 12 h, starting 24 h before viral challenge and continuing until sacrifice. In the therapeutic groups, mice received the same dose every 12 h, starting 6 h post-challenge. Infection control mice were inoculated with PDCoV and treated with PBS, while mock mice received PBS only without viral infection. Preventive and therapeutic group mice were sacrificed at 1 day post-challenge. All mice were humanely euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. Intestinal tissues, including duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, and rectum, were collected for viral load quantification, histopathological analysis (H&E staining) to assess the intestinal pathology.

Statistics analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for statistical analysis and figure generation. All data were expressed as the mean ± SD. Differences between groups were examined for statistical significance using a mixed-effects analysis or a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison post-test. The asterisks in the figures indicate significant differences, with ns, not significant; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001.

Results

Comparative antiviral efficacy of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against PEDV infection

Firstly, ZIF8 was synthesized and characterized of its physicochemical properties as well as its zinc ion release behavior in comparison with ZnO. FTIR and XRD were applied to cnfirm the successful sythesis of ZIF8 (Fig. S1). Its morphology and particle size were characterized by SEM and DLS, respectively (Fig. S2), revealing that its particle size showed no significant difference compared with commercially purchased ZnO. ICP-MS was then used to characterize the release of Zn²⁺ from ZIF8 and ZnO under physiological conditions and acidic conditions simulating the endosomal environment (Fig. S3). Zn²⁺ release from ZIF8 and ZnO NPs increased over time and gradually reached a plateau. Acidic conditions markedly accelerated Zn²⁺ release compared with pH 7.4. ZIF8 exhibited higher Zn²⁺ release than ZnO under both conditions, indicating its degradation behavior after cellular uptake.

Prior to conducting antiviral effect assays, Vero cells were incubated with varying concentrations (10, 20, 40, and 100 µg/mL) of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs for 12 h, 24 h and 36 h to assess cytotoxicity. Across all tested concentrations and time points, cell viability was generally maintained above 80% (Fig. S4), indicating excellent biocompatibility of both NPs [22].

As shown in the schematic representation of the experimental protocol (Fig. 1A), we evaluated the antiviral effects of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs, as well as their impact on different stages of the viral life cycle, including adsorption, entry, replication, and release. Vero cells were infected with PEDV (MOI = 0.1) and treated with increasing concentrations of NPs (10, 20, 40, and 100 µg/mL). The overall antiviral effects of the NPs were evaluated using IFA, qRT-PCR, and Western blot. IFA results revealed intense PEDV N-protein signals in the untreated control, whereas both ZIF8 and ZnO treatments reduced fluorescence intensity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Notably, ZIF8 achieved a more pronounced viral reduction than ZnO at equivalent doses. Consistent with the IFA results, qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 1C) showed that ZIF8 treatment markedly decreased PEDV N-gene copy numbers compared with the control, whereas ZnO induced a more moderate reduction Western blot analysis (Fig. 1D) further confirmed this trend, showing diminished PEDV N-protein band intensity following ZIF8 exposure compared with ZnO. Together, these findings indicate that ZIF8 NPs exert superior antiviral activity compared with ZnO.

To further elucidate the specific stages at which ZIF8 and ZnO NPs interfere with PEDV infection, we assessed their effects on different stages of the viral life cycle. At the adsorption stage (Fig. 1E), treatment with ZIF8 markedly reduced viral binding to the cell surface compared with ZnO. Zeta potential analysis also indicated that PEDV exhibited a more pronounced change in surface charge upon binding with ZIF8 (Fig. S5), which may be due to the porous structure and high specific surface area of ZIF8, enabling it to adsorb a larger number of viral particles and thereby enhance binding efficiency [23]. During the viral entry stage, there was no significant difference between the two NPs (Fig. 1F). Based on previously reported PEDV growth curves in Vero cells [24], samples were collected at 10 h (Fig. 1G), 12 h (Fig. S6A), 16 h (Fig. S6B) post-infection to evaluate the effects of NPs on viral replication, and at 16 h, 24 h post-infection to assess their impact on viral release (Figs. 1H and I, Fig. S7). Both types of NPs inhibited viral replication and release, with ZIF8 exhibiting notable inhibitory effects on viral replication, suggesting that its antiviral activity may involve intracellular regulatory mechanisms.

In addition, since ZIF8 is pH‑responsive and releases Zn²⁺ differently from ZnO, we included zinc acetate at equimolar Zn²⁺ concentrations to compare the antiviral effects of free zinc ions with those of ZIF8 and ZnO. Within the tested concentration of zinc acetate and treatment durations, overall cell viability was well maintained (Fig. S4). We then compared the antiviral activities of ZIF8, ZnO, and zinc acetate at the corresponding equimolar Zn²⁺ concentrations. At all tested concentrations, zinc acetate exhibited weaker antiviral activity (Fig. S8). These findings indicate that the enhanced antiviral activity of ZIF8 cannot be attributed solely to Zn²⁺ release.

ZIF8 alleviate PEDV-induced oxidative stress by reducing intracellular ROS generation

During viral infection, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated as a consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic stress [25]. To quantify the antioxidant effects of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs during PEDV infection, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. As shown by the IFA images and quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity (Figs. 2A and B), the PEDV-infected control group exhibited substantial ROS accumulation. And treatment with ZIF8 or ZnO NPs markedly reduced ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner. Quantification of flow cytometric analysis of intracellular ROS levels following treatment with varying concentrations of NPs demonstrated a consistent trend across all experimental groups (Fig. 2C). In addition, intracellular ROS accumulation was examined at different post-infection time points, including 2, 4, 6, 12, 24 h post-infection (Figs. 2D and E). The results showed that NPs effectively attenuated PEDV-induced intracellular ROS accumulation as early as 2 h post-infection.

To determine whether the reduction in ROS is attributable to decreased viral load, we used non-viral ROS stimuli, including H2O2 and rotenone, to induce ROS and assessed the ability of NPs to scavenge intracellular ROS. Based on CCK8 results (Fig. S9), we selected treatment with 300 µM H2O2 for 2 h and 10 µM rotenone for 6 h as the stimulation conditions. The results showed that ZIF8 treatment effectively reduced ROS accumulation induced by these non-viral stimuli (Fig. S10), suggesting that ZIF8 can directly alleviate intracellular oxidative stress, at least in part independently of intracellular viral load.

ZIF8 maintains mitochondrial homeostasis while enhancing cytosolic mtDNA release during PEDV infection

In recent years, the role of mitochondrial dynamics in viral infection has attracted increasing attention. Accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key contributor to the pathogenesis of many viral infections, and that restoration of mitochondrial function may have therapeutic potential [25]. Given that ZIF8 was observed to reduce ROS, which is a reactive molecule generated by mitochondria during energy metabolism that also functions as a key signaling molecule regulating mitochondrial function and signal transduction [26], we further investigated the effects of ZIF8 on mitochondrial function including mitochondrial membrane potential and mtDNA synthesis during viral infection.

In the PEDV-infected cell model, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h post-infection was assessed using JC-1 staining, and CCCP, a mitochondrial uncoupler that disrupts membrane potential, was used as a positive control. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that nanoparticle treatment markedly preserved ΔΨm starting from 2 h post-infection with stronger JC-1 aggregate signals and increased red fluorescence compared with the untreated group (Fig. 3A), suggesting effective maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis. Consistently, JC-1 immunofluorescence analysis at 24 h post-infection showed similar results (Fig. 3B).

The level of mtDNA synthesis, which reflects mitochondrial biogenesis and homeostasis, was further analyzed. Tracking total and cytosolic mtDNA levels at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h post-infection revealed that, compared with the other groups, cytosolic mtDNA levels in the ZIF8-treated group began to increase at 4–6 h and gradually accumulated thereafter, whereas total intracellular mtDNA levels increased later, primarily at 12–24 h post-infection (Fig. 3C). At 24 h post-infection, CLSM images of TOM20 and dsDNA revealed that viral infection led to mitochondrial damage, as indicated by weakened TOM20 signals and fragmented mitochondrial structures. In contrast, TOM20 integrity in the ZIF8-treated group was comparable to that in the mock control (Fig. 3D). Moreover, cytosolic dsDNA exhibited markedly increased punctate fluorescence in the ZIF8 group, further suggesting that ZIF8 treatment promotes mtDNA release into the cytosol while maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis.

ZIF8 promotes cytosolic mtDNA release via moderate mPTP opening

Increasing evidence suggests that cytosolic mtDNA release can occur even in the absence of severe mitochondrial damage through several mechanisms, including VDAC1 oligomerization [27, 28], BAX/BAK oligomerization [29], mPTP opening [30, 31]. We therefore examined these indicators to determine the mechanism by which ZIF8 induces mtDNA release into the cytosol.

To examine whether ZIF8-induced mtDNA release depends on VDAC1 oligomerization, cells were pretreated with 10 µM VBIT-4, a VDAC1 oligomerization inhibitor, for 1 h before cytosolic mtDNA analysis. As shown in the Fig. S11, VBIT-4 treatment did not alter the overall trend of cytosolic mtDNA release at 24 h, with the highest level still observed in the ZIF8 group. These results suggest that ZIF8-induced mtDNA release is not mediated by VDAC1 oligomerization. We examined BAX oligomerization in different treatment groups (Fig. S12). The results showed that PEDV infection induced BAX dimerization; however, no further increase in BAX oligomerization was observed in the ZIF8 treated groups. These findings suggest that ZIF8-induced cytosolic mtDNA release is not dependent on BAX/BAK oligomerization.

Next, we performed a calcein-AM staining assay to determine whether mPTP opening is involved in ZIF8-induced cytosolic mtDNA release. Flow cytometry results showed that PEDV infection alone did not markedly induce mPTP opening, whereas ZIF8 treatment promoted mPTP opening, as indicated by reduced calcein-AM fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3E). Previous studies have suggested that moderate mPTP opening can increase mitochondrial inner membrane permeability and facilitate cytosolic mtDNA release while maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, whereas excessive mPTP opening may cause mitochondrial damage [32]. In our study, the proportion of calcein-AM-positive cells in the ZIF8-treated group decreased by approximately 5% compared with the control group, indicating a moderate mPTP opening.

To further validate this mechanism, cells were treated with 1 µM Cyclosporin A (CsA), an inhibitor of mPTP opening, and cytosolic mtDNA release was then assessed. The results showed that CsA treatment abolished the increasing trend of ZIF8-induced cytosolic mtDNA release (Fig. 3F), suggesting that ZIF8-promoted cytosolic mtDNA release may depend on moderate mPTP opening.

ZIF8 activates NLRP3 inflammasome and STING signaling pathways during PEDV infection

As the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm can subsequently activate multiple cellular signaling pathways, particularly those involved in innate immunity and inflammatory responses [33]. IFA staining was first performed at 24 h post-infection, when cytosolic DNA release was pronounced, to examine the colocalization of mtDNA with NLRP3 and STING. (Figs. 4A and B). Next, we tracked the expression levels of NLRP3 and STING at different time points post-infection (Figs. 4C and D). The time-course analysis of NLRP3 and STING mRNA expression showed an increasing trend beginning at 4 h post-infection, with the highest levels observed at 12–24 h post-infection. This pattern was consistent with the kinetics of cytosolic mtDNA release. Notably, spearman correlation analysis between absolute viral RNA copy numbers at each time point and NLRP3 or STING expression revealed that the regulatory effect of NPs on NLRP3/STING expression is essentially independent of viral RNA levels (Figs. 4E and F). To further elucidate pathway activation, the expression of key mRNAs and proteins associated with the NLRP3 inflammasome and STING pathways was also evaluated by Western blot (Figs. 4G and H) and qRT-PCR (Figs. 4I and J). Notably, since Vero cells are deficient in type I interferon production and are therefore not suitable for study of interferon induction. PK-1 cells were applied as the cell model [34, 35]. Based on the CCK-8 results, a concentration of 5 µg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments in PK-1 cells (Fig. S13).

The results showed that PEDV infection alone initially triggered activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and STING signaling pathways, and treatment with ZIF8 or ZnO NPs further enhanced this activation. This was evidenced by increased expression of NLRP3, the effector enzyme caspase‑1, and downstream cytokines IL‑1β and IL‑18, as well as elevated STING expression levels and upregulation of IFN‑β, ISG15, IFIT1, and MX1, leading to increased production of inflammatory cytokines and interferons and thereby potentiating antiviral effects.

Inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome and STING signaling attenuated the antiviral effect of ZIF8

To further confirm the roles of the NLRP3 and STING pathways in ZIF8-mediated anti-PEDV effects, specific inhibitors INF‑39 (NLRP3 inhibitor) and C‑176 (STING inhibitor) were introduced. Their concentrations were chosen based on cytotoxicity evaluations. As shown in Fig. S14, treatment with INF-39 (10–50 µM) exhibited no detectable cytotoxicity after 24 h, whereas C-176 caused mild toxicity only at concentrations above 20 µM. Accordingly, a concentration of 20 µM was selected for subsequent experiments to ensure effective pathway inhibition without compromising cell viability.

Under NLRP3 inhibitor INF-39 pretreatment conditions (PEDV + INF-39), qRT-PCR analysis showed that the transcriptional levels of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β in Vero cells treated with ZIF8 and ZnO NPs were markedly reduced (Fig. 5A). The transcriptional level of IL-18 did not exhibit a notable change before or after inhibitor treatment. IFA assay demonstrated that the antiviral effect of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against PEDV was weakened after pretreatment (Fig. 5B). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis showed that pretreatment with the NLRP3 inhibitor INF-39 moderately attenuated the antiviral effect of ZIF8, with the relative PEDV N gene level increasing from 39.2% to 66.1% of the control, whereas ZnO treatment resulted in a smaller increase from 74.0% to 80.7%. (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis further confirmed this trend, showing that pretreatment with C-176 attenuated the antiviral activity of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against PEDV (Figs. 5D).

Under pretreatment with the STING inhibitor C-176 (PEDV + C-176), the transcriptional levels of STING, IFN-β, ISG15, and IFIT1 in ZIF8-treated PK-1 cells were significantly reduced compared with the corresponding groups without inhibitor treatment (Fig. 5E). MX1 expression showed no significant difference before and after inhibitor treatment, which may be attributed to the ability of PEDV to induce MX1 via STING-independent signaling pathways. After pretreatment with the STING inhibitor C-176, IFA assay demonstrated that the antiviral effects of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against PEDV were significantly attenuated following pretreatment with C-176. qRT-PCR analysis showed that, in the ZIF8-treated group, the relative PEDV N gene level increased from 39.2% to 90.9% of the control, whereas the antiviral effect of ZnO was almost completely abolished (Figs. 5F and G). Consistently, Western blot analysis further confirmed this trend, demonstrating that C-176 markedly attenuated the antiviral activity of both ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against PEDV (Fig. 5H).

Together, these results suggest that ZIF8 NPs exert antiviral effects against PEDV by activating innate immune pathways mediated by NLRP3 inflammasome and STING signaling.

Broad-spectrum antiviral properties of ZIF8 NPs

To assess the broad-spectrum antiviral potential of ZIF8 NPs, their effects were evaluated against representative coronaviruses from different genera [36], including β-coronavirus (BCoV) in HRT-18 cells, γ-coronavirus (IBV) in CEK cells, and δ-coronavirus (PDCoV) in PK-1 cells. Prior to antiviral testing, the cytotoxicity of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs was assessed in HRT-18 and CEK cells after 12, 24, and 36 h of incubation (Figs. S15 and S16). Based on the results, a concentration of 10 µg/mL was used for IBV in CEK cells and BCoV in HRT-18 cells, while the previously mentioned 5 µg/mL was used for PDCoV in PK-1 cells.

qRT-PCR, Western blotting and IFA were performed to evaluate the antiviral effects of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs against BCoV, IBV, and PDCoV. qRT-PCR analysis (Figs. 6A, D and G) revealed that ZIF8 treatment significantly reduced viral N gene expression for all three viruses, with the most pronounced inhibition observed for PDCoV, followed by IBV, while the effect against BCoV was moderate yet still significant compared with untreated controls. ZnO exhibited weaker inhibitory effects across all three viruses. Consistently, Western blot analysis (Figs. 6B, E and H) confirmed that ZIF8 markedly decreased protein levels of BCoV N, IBV N, and PDCoV N, with the strongest suppression in PDCoV-infected cells and comparatively less effect on IBV, whereas ZnO treatment showed a more modest reduction. IFA assays further supported these findings (Figs. 6C, F and I), showing substantially lower viral N protein signals in ZIF8-treated cells for all three viruses, while ZnO-treated cells showed a less pronounced decrease. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ZIF8 NPs exert potent broad-spectrum antiviral effects against β-, γ-, and δ-coronaviruses, with the strongest efficacy against PDCoV and the weakest, though still significant, effect against BCoV, surpassing the inhibitory activity of ZnO NPs.

ZIF8 and ZnO NPs inhibit PDCoV infection in suckling mice

PDCoV is an enteric coronavirus that causes acute watery diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration, and death in neonatal piglets, resulting in significant economic losses to the global swine industry [37]. Given the potent antiviral activity of ZIF8 against PDCoV observed in in vitro cell-based assays, we further evaluated its efficacy in a neonatal mouse model, established based on previously reported mouse models [38]. The NPs were orally administered to suckling mice infected with the CZ2020 strain of PDCoV, in both preventive and therapeutic regimens (Fig. 7A). Mice were euthanized at designated time points. The intestines of mice that did not receive any treatment appeared distended with lesions, whereas this phenomenon was not observed in mice treated with either type of nanoparticle (Fig. 7B). To further assess antiviral effects, and intestinal tissues including the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and rectum, were collected for viral load quantification via qRT-PCR (Figs. 7C-F). Both ZIF8 and ZnO, in preventive and therapeutic settings, reduced viral RNA levels compared with the infection control group. ZIF8 treatment exhibited more pronounced inhibition than ZnO, particularly in the jejunum and ileum.

Histopathological analysis further supported these findings. PDCoV infection in control mice caused pronounced villous denudation and thinning of the intestinal wall, indicative of severe mucosal damage (Fig. 7G). In contrast, intestinal tissues from ZIF8- and ZnO-treated groups showed intact villous structure, preserved cellular density, and normal morphology comparable to the mock group, indicating effective protection against virus-induced intestinal injury. These results demonstrate that both ZIF8 and ZnO NPs effectively protect against PDCoV infection in vivo, reducing viral replication and preventing intestinal damage, with both preventive and therapeutic administration demonstrating remarkable antiviral effects.

Discussion

Coronaviruses (CoVs) remain major pathogens for humans and animals, whose outbreaks causing substantial health and economic losses [1, 8]. Their high mutation and recombination rates contribute to immune escape and cross-species transmission, posing ongoing challenges for control. Although various coronavirus vaccines including inactivated, live-attenuated, subunit, and mRNA-based formulations, have been developed and proven effective in preventing infection and reducing disease severity [39], the continuous emergence of viral variants and the limited breadth of immune protection highlight the ongoing need for the development of effective antiviral therapeutics [40].

Conventional antiviral drugs, such as acyclovir, remdesivir, favipiravir, oseltamivir, and lopinavir/ritonavir, typically act through molecular targets to inhibit viral replication, protein processing, or release [41]. While these agents have shown efficacy against specific viruses including herpesviruses, influenza virus, and coronaviruses, their clinical performance is often constrained by drug resistance, short biological half-lives, and off-target toxicity. In contrast, nanomaterials provide unique physicochemical and biological advantages that enable specific antiviral effects [42, 43]. Depending on their composition and surface chemistry, NPs can directly inactivate virions, block viral adsorption and entry, or modulate intracellular signaling pathways. These attributes underscore the potential of nanomaterials as promising next-generation antiviral platforms.

In this study, the antiviral effects of ZIF8 and ZnO NPs were firstly evaluated using PEDV as a model α-coronavirus. The antiviral mechanisms of ZIF8 have been only rarely investigated, whereas the antiviral mechanisms of Zn2+ have been studied much more extensively. Elevated intracellular Zn²⁺ has been reported to inhibit viral polymerase activity [44, 45], including that of RdRp, reverse transcriptase, and viral DNA polymerase. Moreover, Zn²⁺ may interfere with viral polyprotein cleavage and processing [46]. In addition, zinc may also exert indirect antiviral effects through the modulation of host-related pathways, such as NF-κB activity [47] and metallothionein-dependent responses [48]. Since ZIF8 can release Zn2+ within the intracellular environment, the aforementioned mechanisms cannot be entirely ruled out. However, our comparative experiments with free zinc ions showed that the antiviral activity of ZIF8 does not rely solely on the release of free zinc ions.

Most antiviral metal nanomaterials currently investigated are based on their oxide forms, such as ZnO, CuO/Cu2O, TiO₂, MgO, Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄, and et al. These oxides have demonstrated broad-spectrum antiviral efficacy against both enveloped and non-enveloped viruses, including SARS-CoV-2, influenza A/B viruses, herpes simplex virus (HSV-1), and noroviruses [49–51]. These metal oxides typically act directly on the virus and exhibit antiviral activity through mechanisms, including the generation of ROS via intrinsic or photoinduced catalytic reactions, which oxidatively damage critical structural components of coronaviruses, such as the viral envelope and proteins, thereby leading to viral inactivation [52–55]. Herein, We found that the MOF form of Zn2+ exhibited superior antiviral efficacy. It achieved this by modulating the ROS-mitochondria-innate immunity axis [44, 56–59], thereby more effectively reducing virus-induced ROS accumulation, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, and promoting the synthesis and cytosolic release of mitochondrial DNA to activate the NLRP3 and STING signaling pathways [60, 61].

Current broad-spectrum antivirals include host-directed agents such as interferons, nitazoxanide, polymerase-targeting nucleoside analogues such as ribavirin, favipiravir, remdesivir, molnupiravir. While these agents can cover multiple RNA viruses, they face notable limitations. Although they exhibit certain efficacy, resistance can emerge for direct-acting agents, systemic toxicities constrain dosing. Compared with the broad-spectrum antiviral drugs noted above, nanomaterials can simultaneously target multiple stages of the viral life cycle and reduce the risk of resistance [41, 42]. Elucidating the structure–antiviral effect relationship of NPs is crucial for identifying the structural features that determine effects and potency. This supports the design of locally effective materials with a low risk of resistance while improving comparability and reproducibility across experimental systems [62, 63]. More importantly, it provides a guidance for broad-spectrum antiviral platforms that can be rapidly adapted to emerging or variant pathogens.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that ZIF8 is an effective zinc-based nanoagent with potent and broad-spectrum antiviral activity against coronaviruses. By modulating the ROS-mitochondria-NLRP3/STING axis, ZIF8 preserves mitochondrial homeostasis and promotes cytosolic mtDNA release through moderate mPTP opening, enhances innate immune signaling, and effectively suppresses viral replication. Importantly, ZIF8 confers significant in vivo protection in a PDCoV-infected mouse model under both preventive and therapeutic regimens, highlighting its translational potential. Together, these findings not only elucidate a previously underappreciated mechanism underlying nanomaterial-mediated antiviral immunity but also provide a rational framework for the design of next-generation nanomaterial-based broad-spectrum antiviral therapeutics.
