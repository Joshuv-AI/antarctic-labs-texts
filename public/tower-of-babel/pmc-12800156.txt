Upcycling end-of-life mattresses into sustainable insulation materials through development of mycelium based biocomposites
Authors: The Hong Phong Nguyen, Lachlan Thompson, Fareed Tamaddoni Jahromi, Huseyin Sumer, Md Morshed Alam, Mostafa Nikzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for sustainable building materials has spurred interest in bio-based composites due to lower environmental impact and higher thermal performance. This study presents the fabrication and characterization of mycelium-based insulation materials derived from recycled mattress waste, using Penicillium chrysogenum cultivated under submerged fermentation conditions. The incorporation of shredded polyurethane foam into the fungal growth matrix resulted in cohesive bio-composites with remarkable thermal stability and insulation performance. Comprehensive physicochemical analyses, including scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA-DTG), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD), revealed that fungal degradation of the polyurethane matrix facilitated the formation of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) in the calcite phase. This biomineralization, driven by CO2 released from enzymatic hydrolysis of urethane bonds, significantly enhanced the thermal resilience of the composite. The most thermally stable formulation, based on coarse shredded mattress waste (OS), retained 93 ± 5% of its weight at 997 °C and 90 ± 5% at 1000 °C, far exceeding benchmark insulation materials such as glass wool. With a measured thermal conductivity of 0.048 ± 0.002 W/m.K, the mycelium-mattress composite matches or surpasses common sustainable insulators while offering the added benefit of valorising post-consumer waste. These findings highlight the potential of fungal bioprocessing and biomineralization in producing high-performance, fire-resistant, and environmentally sustainable insulation materials for circular economy applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30954-x.Keywords: Mycelium composites, Penicillium chrysogenum, Mattress waste recycling, Biodegradable insulation, Circular economy in construction, BiomineralizationSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

1. Introduction

The shift towards a circular economy is essential to address the increasing consumption of finite resources and the associated environmental challenges. Transitioning to a circular economy can mitigate these issues by reducing resource extraction rates, energy consumption, emissions, landfills and other environmental impacts1–6. Recently, both governments and industry partners have focused on developing circular solutions for post-consumer products, such as waste mattresses, which present significant recycling challenges3,4. To meet the Australian National Waste Policy Action Plan 2024 target of 80% average resource recovery rate from all waste streams by 20307, the mattress industry must find sustainable ways to recycle these products. End-of-life mattresses (EOLMs) in Australia total roughly 1.8 million units per annum, representing a significant solid-waste stream8. Of these, only 60% enter formal recycling channels, while the balance is either landfilled or sent to energy recovery8. Current processes recover steel springs and some polyurethane (PU) foam, but overall material reclamation rates hover between 44 and 64%, leaving up to 56% of mattress mass unrecovered8. Mattresses contain diverse materials such as polyester fibres, latex, cotton, polyurethane (PU) foam, steel springs and wood, whose heterogeneity complicates manual and mechanical separation, driving down recycling efficiency9. Successful closed-loop recycling of mattresses depends on several critical factors: an efficient collection system, consistent material supply, well-defined market demand, and assured quality of recycled materials3,4,10. Despite increasing recovery efforts, a substantial fraction of mattress components remains unrecycled, emphasizing the need for alternative valorisation routes that can transform this heterogeneous waste into functional products3,4,11,12.

Conventional recycling methods which are mechanical, chemical, and thermal, face limitations when dealing with mixed polymers and textiles in mattresses, often yielding low-value or energy-intensive outputs13–24. Biotechnological recycling, particularly using fungi, offers a promising alternative for decomposing mattress components10,25–34 and simultaneously creating bio-based composites with added functional value35–37. The growing emphasis on environmental protection has spurred the rapid development of green processes in chemical production25. As a result, numerous eco-friendly approaches have emerged for the biodegradation of polymers, which can be applicable for mattress waste, aiming to reduce environmental impact and promote sustainable practices. Evidence from studies suggests that fungi often outperform bacteria in colonizing PU waste, with samples from dumping sites exhibiting a higher fungal presence28,38,39. Several fungi species have demonstrated the ability to not only survive but thrive using PU as their sole carbon and energy source, although degradation rates can vary significantly depending on the PU material composition26,28–31. For instance, Liu et al.31 reported a Cladosporium strain capable of solubilizing 32.42% of a poly(butylene adipate)-based material over 28 days, while Magnin et al.32 achieved an 8.9% weight loss of a polycaprolactone-based material over two months using a Penicillium strain. Importantly, fungi also can release various hydrolytic enzymes into their surroundings, which are beneficial in PU biodegradation in comparison to bacteria, since it does not require the enzymes to be bound to cellular membrane30,37. Acting as a natural binder, fungal mycelium reinforces other components, leading to the formation of composite materials with enhanced structural properties37,40. This dual functionality not only aids in waste reduction but also contributes to the development of sustainable materials37,40. Importantly, these mycelium-based composites exhibit remarkable flame-retardant properties37,40. The inherent chitin in the fungal cell walls contributes to the formation of a protective carbonaceous char layer when exposed to high temperatures, effectively acting as a thermal barrier37,40. Alkaline treatment of the mycelium enhances this property by converting chitin into chitosan, increasing the degree of deacetylation, which in turn promotes more robust char formation during combustion37.

While previous research has examined fungal growth on a range of agricultural and industrial residues37, only a limited number of studies have explored the use of fungi for valorising post-consumer waste streams, which are inherently more heterogeneous and contaminated. Recent investigations have begun addressing this challenge, demonstrating fungal biodegradation and biotransformation of complex post-consumer plastics and textile wastes41–43. However, systematic studies on fungal cultivation directly on highly heterogeneous and bulky post-consumer waste, such as discarded mattresses, which result in highly functional products with end used applications remain scarce. Among the fungi, Penicillium chrysogenum stands out due to its robust enzyme activity and environmental resilience44. This fungus, commonly known for its role in penicillin production, has demonstrated significant potential in biodegrading various materials such as phenols45, azo dyes in textile wastewater46, linear alkylbenzene sulfonate in sludge wastewater47, n-alkanes in oil sludge48, and polyurethanes28,32. Its ability to secrete a wide range of enzymes makes it an excellent candidate for breaking down the complex polymers found in mattress waste under mild environmental conditions28,32,34. Additionally, immobilizing P. chrysogenum on urethane foam not only facilitates the breakdown of mattress components but also significantly enhances its growth rate and metabolic activity49. This approach has been shown to accelerate penicillin production by approximately twelvefold compared to traditional cultivation methods, underscoring the fungus’s rapid proliferation and heightened biosynthetic capacity when supported on urethane foam49. However, despite these advances, little attention has been given to leveraging fungal bioconversion to create functional materials from real-world mattress waste streams. There remains a significant gap in translating this biodegradation potential into practical systems that not only break down heterogenous mattress waste but also convert it into value-added materials with real-world applicability, specifically in the construction and insulation sectors.

In this study, we investigated the development of sustainable insulation materials through the bioconversion of mattress waste into mycelium-based bio-composites. The thermal performance of the resulting composites was systematically characterized, with particular attention given to their fire-retardant properties following post-treatment processes such as alkaline deacetylation and ethanol precipitation. A comprehensive suite of analytical techniques was employed to evaluate material properties, including thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to assess thermal stability, thermal conductivity testing in accordance with ISO 9869, and microstructural analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to identify functional group changes associated with chemical treatments, while X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted to investigate the presence and crystallinity of mineral phases such as calcium carbonate and calcium oxide that contribute to thermal resistance. This study contributes to the advancement of bioengineered materials by demonstrating an innovative method for converting complex mattress waste streams into durable, fire-resistant insulation products, thereby promoting circular economy goals through sustainable waste valorisation.

Experimental

Shredded mattress waste

The shredded mattress waste was kindly received from the Australian Bedding Stewardship Council (ABSC). The material represented a full mattress waste stream, containing all non-metallic components typically found in end-of-life mattresses. This mixture consisted predominantly of polyurethane (PU) foam (approximately 80–85%), with the remaining fraction comprising polyester fibres, latex, cotton, and minor amounts of wood chips. The particle sizes ranged from small pieces to larger irregular chunks measuring approximately 20–50 mm. The density of the shredded waste was measured to have an average density of 70.6 kg/m3.

Fungal cultivation

Penicillium chrysogenum ATCC 10,577 was obtained from Agpath Laboratories, Vervale, VIC, Australia. The fungus was cultured on malt extract agar (Sigma Aldrich, Australia) at 25 °C for 14 days, and maintained on MEA slant at a temperature of 4 °C. After 14 days of growing, about 1 × 108 spores/mL was collected and suspended in 1 mL of milliQ water as working spore suspension to be used for sample preparation.

Sample preparation

The overall experimental workflow, including mixture preparation, incubation conditions, control setups, and post-treatment processes, is summarised in Fig. 1 for visual clarity and procedural overview. Briefly, two mixtures, designated as 100% v/v and 200% v/v, were prepared to evaluate growth under immersed and submerged conditions, respectively.

Schematic overview of the experimental workflow for preparing and evaluating of mycelium-based bio-composites from recycled mattress waste.

The mattress waste materials were first manually checked for any metallic contaminants and then mechanically shredded. The coarsely shredded (OS) mattress waste was processed using a ZR2400H shredder (UNTHA Shredding Technology, Austria), while the finely shredded (FS) mattress waste was prepared using a Wittmann MAS1 granulator (PB Machinery, Australia). The shredded materials were homogenised to ensure consistent particle size distribution. To determine the working volume of the shredded mattress waste, the solid particles were gently hand-compressed into a 100 mL beaker until the volume displacement reached the marked line, thereby standardising the apparent bulk volume. This volumetric approach enabled direct preparation of mixtures at specific v/v ratios without requiring density conversion.

For the immersed growth condition (100% v/v mixture), 100 mL of potato dextrose broth (PDB) was combined with 100 mL of shredded mattress waste. For the submerged growth condition (200% v/v mixture), 200 mL of PDB was added to 100 mL of shredded mattress waste. These mixtures were transferred into 500 mL beakers and covered with tin foil. Both mixtures were then autoclaved at 121 °C for 30 min. Following autoclaving, the samples were allowed to cool naturally to 25 °C before inoculation. After cooling, 100 µL of a working spore suspension was inoculated into each mixture, and the samples were incubated at 25 °C for 30 days in zip-lock bags.

Four control samples were prepared. The first and second controls consisted of the mixtures without the working spore suspension. The third control involved adding 100 µL of the working spore suspension to 100 mL of potato dextrose broth, which was then incubated at 25 °C for 30 days to form mycelium sheets during hyphal growth. These sheets were harvested by rinsing them at least three times with Milli-Q water. The fourth control was prepared by combining 10 mL of the mycelium sheets with 10 mL of mattress waste without further incubation. This control was specifically designed to test the binding capability between fungal mycelium and mattress waste, assessing whether thermal properties comparable to bio-converted composites could be achieved by physical blending alone.

The pH of the samples was measured immediately after inoculation (pH 4.9) and again at the end of the incubation period, where it had increased to 8.6. Incubated samples were carefully weighed weekly to monitor water evaporation and/or the loss of volatile by-products from the decomposition process. After 30 days of incubation, the samples were decanted and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 48 h to remove all water content. Approximately 10 g of the dried samples were then treated with 25 mL of 4 M NaOH (Sigma Aldrich, Australia) and heated in an oven at 100 °C for 8 h to convert chitin to chitosan via N-deacetylation. This NaOH-urea-ethanol post-treatment sequence was selected to promote chitosan hydrolysis and gel formation, thereby improving the structural integrity and bonding of the bio-composites. The protocol was adapted from a combination of in-house methods previously developed using cellulose-based matrices and established literature procedures9,37,50,51. Following this, the samples were mixed with pure urea pellets (Sigma Aldrich, Australia) in a 9:1 ratio (w/w, sample to urea) and agitated at 150 rpm on a shaker for 2 h. The mixtures were then stored at -20 °C for 8 h and subsequently treated with 100% ethanol (Sigma Aldrich, Australia) in a 1:1 ratio (v/v) for another 8 h. After decanting, the samples were washed twice with Milli-Q water and then baked in an oven at 80 °C for 48 h. The final products were weighted and used for further investigation. The effective solid-to-liquid ratio during these post-treatment steps was approximately 1:2.5 (w/v).

Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

Surface morphology and elemental composition of the mycelium-based bio-composite samples and their corresponding char residues were examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, ZEISS SUPRA 40VP, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Oxford Inca Energy 200 energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector. Small fragments of each sample were mounted onto aluminium stubs using conductive carbon adhesive tape. To ensure adequate conductivity and imaging quality, the specimens were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold for 1 min using a JEOL NeoCoater (model MP-19020NCTR, JEOL, NSW, Australia). SEM imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV to capture detailed surface topography with minimal charging. Elemental analyses were conducted using the integrated EDS system to identify the distribution of key elements, including carbon, oxygen, calcium, and other inorganic constituents potentially introduced from the mattress waste or during composite processing.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability of the samples was assessed to identify the best sample preparation method by using a TA Q50 thermogravimetric analyser (TA Instruments). A commercially available glass wool insulation from Earthwool (Knauf Insulation, Australia) was used as a benchmark. Samples were initially placed on tin foil in a ceramic crucible and baked in a furnace at 400 °C for 8 h to remove volatile materials. After baking, the residues were allowed to air cool to 25 °C and then removed off the tin foil. The residues, with masses ranging from 10 to 20 mg, were carefully weighed and placed in an aluminium crucible for analysis. The thermal stability tests were conducted at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in air with a flow rate of 60 mL/min, over a temperature range from 25 to 1000 °C. Each TGA analysis was performed in triplicate on three independently prepared samples to ensure reproducibility and statistical reliability. Statistical analysis of the residual weight data was conducted using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the general linear model procedure. Mean values were separated by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Differences test (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were completed using SPSS v.29 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis of the samples and char residues was conducted via transmittance mode using a Nicolet IS5 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Australia). Each sample was scanned 64 times with resolution of 8 cm− 1 and scanning range of 600–3800 cm− 1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using the Bruker D2 PHASER benchtop diffractometer (Bruker, Australia). Samples were dried at 60 °C for 24 h, then finely ground to achieve homogeneity and minimize preferred orientation effects. Approximately 1 g of each sample was placed into a 32 mm diameter sample holder and levelled to ensure a flat surface. The D2 PHASER, equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source and a LYNXEYE linear detector, operated in Bragg-Brentano geometry. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 5–90°, with a step size of 0.02° and a counting time of 1 s per step. Phase identification was performed using Bruker’s DIFFRAC.SUITE software, comparing the diffraction patterns to standard reference databases to determine the crystalline phases present in the bio-composites.

Thermal conductivity measurement

Thermal conductivity of the fabricated insulation panels was assessed using a setup based on the ISO 9869 standard52 as described elsewhere9. Briefly, a cubic test chamber with dimensions of 400 mm × 400 mm × 400 mm was constructed, featuring wooden insulated walls on all sides except the top. A 0.5 mm thick sheet metal plate was mounted on the open top surface to serve as a heat transfer interface. An insulation panel specimen, trimmed to a uniform thickness of 15.3 ± 0.1 mm, was positioned above this metal surface within a 100 mm × 100 mm square hollow frame and covered with another sheet metal layer. The apparent density of the biocomposite panels was determined from the ratio of oven-dried mass to bulk volume according to the ISO 845 standard53. Briefly, the dimensions of the samples (100 mm × 100 mm × 15.3 mm) were measured using a digital calliper with ± 0.01 mm precision, giving a calculated volume of 1.53 × 10− 4 m3. With an average oven-dry weight of 32 ± 4 g, the apparent density of the composites was maintained at 209 ± 4 kg.m− 3.

Surface temperatures on both the hot and cold sides of the insulation were monitored using precision thermistors (± 0.05 °C accuracy), while heat transfer was quantified using Hukseflux HFP01 heat flux sensors, accurate to within ± 3%. A 15-watt halogen lamp was installed inside the chamber to establish a consistent temperature gradient across the insulation layer. The experimental setup was operated in a controlled environment at an ambient temperature of 23 °C. Data from the sensors were logged every minute using a Voltage Sensor Logger (VSL). Each test was conducted for a minimum of 24 h to ensure that a steady-state thermal condition was achieved, allowing for reliable thermal conductivity analysis. Three independent thermal conductivity measurements were conducted to confirm repeatability and measurement consistency.

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES)

Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was performed to determine calcium content in the effluent solution. The effluent solution was acidified by the addition of 2% nitric acid. The analysis was performed on a iCAP 7000 series ICP Spectrometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Australia) with each analysis being performed in triplicate.

Results

Morphological transformation and elemental composition analysis of mycelium-mattress waste bio-composites

The processing of mattress waste into mycelium-based bio-composites was meticulously characterized across multiple stages, as depicted in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1. Initially, the shredded mattress waste (Fig. 2a) presented a highly heterogeneous composition, comprising a diverse array of particle sizes and materials, ranging from small fragments to larger chunks with the size of 20–50 mm, indicative of the raw state as received from the supplier. Following biological incubation and chemical treatment, the resulting mycelium-based bio-composites (Fig. 2b) exhibited a notably more uniform texture under optical microscopy, reflecting the successful colonization and integration of the waste material by the fungal mycelium into a cohesive matrix. Further analysis through electron microscopy (Fig. 2c) unveiled intricate microstructural details of the bio-composites, highlighting a dense network of mycelial filaments interwoven with the recycled mattress waste at a microscopic scale, underscoring the robustness of the binding process and the potential for enhanced material properties such as strength and sustainability.

EDS was employed to determine the elemental composition of the mycelium-only and mycelium-mattress waste composite samples (Supplementary Fig. 2). The mycelium-only sample exhibited a composition of 28.77 wt% carbon (C), 9.53 wt% nitrogen (N), 30.49 wt% oxygen (O), 30.38 wt% sodium (Na), and 0.84 wt% calcium (Ca). In contrast, the mycelium-mattress waste composite displayed 20.13 wt% C, 47.32 wt% O, 31.19 wt% Na, and 1.36 wt% Ca.

Thermal stability assessment via TGA

TGA was performed to assess the thermal stability and decomposition behaviour of mycelium-based composites derived from recycled mattress waste, with results presented in Fig. 3. The study compared several samples, which were mycelium without chemical treatment (control), mycelium-mattress blend without chemical treatment, mattress waste without mycelium or chemical treatment, benchmark commercial glass wool insulation (Earthwool), and mycelium blends with FS and OS mattress waste under submerged and immersed fungal growth conditions, across a temperature range up to 1000 °C. The weight loss profiles for all samples were plotted (Fig. 3a) and demonstrated that the blends of mycelium with mattress waste had superior TGA results (Fig. 3b) under varying conditions, highlighting the influence of shredding granularity and treatment conditions on thermal stability.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) profiles of the studied samples. (a) Weight loss curves for all samples. (b) Comparison of mycelium–mattress waste blends under different conditions. The green curve shows the untreated mycelium–mattress blend; blue represents untreated mycelium (control); maroon indicates mattress waste without mycelium; and fuchsia corresponds to commercial glass wool (Earthwool) as a benchmark. The teal and navy curves show the mycelium blend with finely shredded mattress waste under submerged and immersed conditions, respectively, while the purple and red curves represent the coarsely shredded blends under submerged and immersed conditions. All samples were tested under identical chemical treatment conditions.

The thermal decomposition process unfolded in multiple stages, reflecting the diverse composition of organic and inorganic components in the samples. During the initial heating stages, all samples exhibited gradual weight loss due to moisture evaporation and volatilization of organic compounds, as previously noted. However, significant differences in thermal stability became evident at higher temperatures (p < 0.05). The mycelium without chemical treatment (control) completely disintegrated at 850 °C, with 0% weight remaining, indicating a lack of thermal resilience beyond this point. The mycelium-mattress blend without chemical treatment showed even lower stability, reaching 0% weight at 779 °C, likely due to the rapid decomposition of its mixed organic matrix. The mattress waste without mycelium or chemical treatment exhibited the least thermal stability, fully decomposing at 376 °C with 0% weight remaining, reflecting the vulnerability of untreated mattress waste to thermal degradation. The benchmark commercial glass wool insulation (Earthwool) demonstrated higher thermal stability, retaining weight until 992 °C, at which point it also completely decomposed to 0%, consistent with the expected behaviour of fiberglass materials at extreme temperatures.

In contrast, the mycelium blends with shredded mattress waste exhibited exceptional thermal stability at high temperatures, particularly above 850 °C and up to 997 °C. At 997 °C, the mycelium with submerged OS retained 92.85% of its weight, while the mycelium with submerged FS maintained 89.12%, demonstrating remarkable resistance to thermal degradation compared to the control and other samples. Similarly, the mycelium with immersed OS and immersed FS blends retained 85.38% of their weight at 997 °C, further highlighting the protective effect of shredding and treatment conditions on the mycelium matrix. These high weight retention percentages at 997 °C, ranging from 85.38 to 92.85%, stand in stark contrast to the complete disintegration of the control (0% at 850 °C), the mycelium-mattress blend (0% at 779 °C), the mattress waste (0% at 376 °C), and the benchmark glass wool (0% at 992 °C), underscoring the superior high-temperature stability of the shredded mycelium blends.

Quantitative comparison of thermal degradation profiles

Quantitative analysis of the TGA curves, as summarized in Table 1, provides further insight into the decomposition behaviour. The initial decomposition temperature (Td), defined as the temperature at 5% weight loss, was lowest for the mattress waste without mycelium (Td = 110 °C), reflecting its early degradation, compared to the mycelium only control (Td = 120 °C) and the mycelium-mattress blend (Td = 115 °C). The temperature corresponding to the maximum thermogravimetric rate (Tmax) was lowest for the mattress waste (Tmax = 376 °C), aligning with its complete decomposition, while the mycelium-mattress waste blends had Tmax at over 997 °C, reflecting a slower degradation process. The maximum thermogravimetric rate (Rmax) was highest for the mattress waste (Rmax = 0.60%/min), indicating rapid decomposition, compared to the mycelium only control (Rmax = 0.20%/min) and the mycelium-OS blends (Rmax = 0.15%/min). At 850 °C, the mycelium-OS and FS blends retained 95–98% of their weight, while the control, mycelium-mattress blend, and mattress waste had already fully decomposed, further emphasizing the shredded blends’ superior thermal endurance. The potential sources of variation in the TGA/DTG measurements primarily arise from slight differences in sample mass, thermal contact between the crucible and the sample, and baseline drift during high-temperature operation as well as the porous nature of the produced samples which could potentially be stochastic at times. To enhance quantitative reliability, each TGA analysis was performed in triplicate on independently prepared samples, and the corresponding standard deviations have been included as error bars in Fig. 4. The statistical analysis confirms that shredded mycelium–mattress composites exhibit significantly superior thermal stability compared with controls and benchmark insulation.

Thermal decomposition kinetics via DTG

DTG analysis was conducted to investigate the thermal decomposition kinetics of mycelium-based composites derived from recycled mattress waste, with results presented in Fig. 5. The DTG curves for all tested samples (Fig. 5a), including the untreated mycelium network (control), mycelium-mattress blend without chemical treatment, mattress waste without mycelium or chemical treatment, and commercial glass wool insulation (Earthwool). The mycelium blends with FS and OS mattress waste under submerged and immersed conditions, providing insights into the effects of shredding granularity and treatment on decomposition rates (Fig. 5b). The DTG profiles, expressed as the rate of weight loss (Derivative Weight%/°C) versus temperature, highlight key decomposition stages, including moisture evaporation, pyrolysis, and high-temperature degradation.

Derivative Thermogravimetry (DTG) results (a) of all samples and (b) of the blends of mycelium with mattress waste under varying conditions. The green curve shows the untreated mycelium–mattress blend; blue represents untreated mycelium (control); maroon indicates mattress waste alone; and fuchsia corresponds to commercial glass wool (Earthwool) as a benchmark. During drying stage (0–100 °C), moisture loss varied significantly. For the controls, untreated mycelium showed a rate of 0.03%/°C, mattress waste 0.05%/°C, and the mycelium-mattress blend 0.1%/°C, likely due to higher porosity and moisture retention. Among the samples, the submerged FS blend had the lowest moisture loss (0.001%/°C), while the immersed OS blend had the highest (0.04%/°C). Total moisture loss ranged from 0.04% (submerged FS) to 1.6% (submerged OS), suggesting finer shredding improves moisture retention and thermal stability.

In the 100–837 °C range, all samples lost < 5% weight, indicating strong thermal resistance. The submerged FS blend had the lowest loss (1.4%), and submerged OS the highest (4.5%). DTG peaks, such as at 272 °C (1.5%/°C) for the mycelium blend and 290 °C (1.2%/°C) for mattress waste, did not align with their 50% weight-loss temperatures, suggesting complex degradation behaviour involving volatile loss and matrix breakdown.

At higher temperatures, loss rates diverged. At 860 °C, the immersed FS blend had the highest loss rate (0.07%/°C), while submerged OS had the lowest (0.03%/°C), consistent with TGA data, in which the submerged OS retained 92.85% of its mass at 997 °C, compared to 89.12% for submerged FS and 85.38% for immersed blends. Total losses at 1000 °C ranged from 4.5% (submerged OS) to 8.5% (immersed OS). Over 85% of each sample remained intact at 1000 °C, confirming excellent thermal stability, with submerged OS selected for further study.

Earthwool exhibited a sharp DTG peak at 900 °C (0.5%/°C), corresponding to organic binder decomposition and full degradation at 992 °C. In contrast, FS and OS mycelium blends showed broader, lower peaks (800–860 °C, 0.03–0.07%/°C), indicating slower, stabilized degradation due to the mycelium matrix. The intense peaks in untreated mycelium (350 °C) and the mycelium-mattress blend (272 °C) matched their earlier disintegration at 850 °C and 779 °C, respectively.

Chemical characterization through FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was conducted on three samples: chemically treated mycelium only (green), chemically treated mattress waste only (blue), and the mycelium-mattress-waste composite in submerged conditions (red), over the range 3800 –600 cm− 1 (as shown in Fig. 6). These spectra enable the tracking of organic functional groups from the mycelium and mattress waste, as well as the detection of inorganic carbonates that may contribute to the exceptional high-temperature stability observed in TGA/DTG. Specifically, calcium carbonate (CaCO3) reference bands help identify the carbonate species present and likely responsible for thermal buffering above 800 °C.

FTIR spectra of chemically treated mycelium only (green line), chemically treated mattress waste only (blue line), and the mycelium-mattress-waste composite in submerged conditions (red line), over the range 3800–600 cm− 1.

In the mycelium-only spectrum (green), a broad O–H absorption band centred at approximately 3400 cm− 1 reflects moisture or residual hydroxyl groups from polysaccharides54. Aliphatic C–H stretches at 2920 cm− 1 and 2850 cm− 1 arise from lipid and carbohydrate backbones55. The strong amide I band at ~ 1630 cm− 1 confirms proteinaceous components56, while the shoulder at 1340 cm− 1 can be assigned to C–H bending or carboxylate symmetric stretching which can be associated with the vibration modes of amino acid residues or other structural component of the fungal mycelium network57. The peak at ~ 850 cm− 1 suggests the present of CaCO358,59.

By contrast, the mattress-waste spectrum (blue) shows much weaker O–H absorption but pronounced C–H stretches (2920 and 2850 cm− 1) from polyurethane foam and textile residues. In the fingerprint region, complex peaks between 1700 and 1200 cm− 1, together with one at 1090 cm− 1, reflect urethane C = O stretches and C–O–C vibrations from polyester or polycotton blends60.

The composite spectrum (red) superimposes features of both mycelium and mattress waste but with key enhancements: the O–H stretch at 3400 cm− 1 is attenuated, which likely due to lower overall hydration, while the C–H stretches are intensified by the foam and fibres. The amide I peak remains intact, showing the fungal proteins. Most importantly, the 850 cm− 1 carbonate bend of CaCO3, provides strong evidence for CaCO3 present within the composite.

Crystallographic confirmation of mineralization via XRD

The XRD diffractograms of both the ‘Mycelium only’ and ‘Mycelium mattress waste OS’ samples are dominated by reflections that can be indexed to calcite (CaCO3) (as shown in Fig. 7). In each case, the strongest peaks occur at about 2θ ≈ 29.4°, 39.4°, 43.2°, 47.5° and 48.5°, which correspond to the (104), (113), (202), (108) and (116) planes of rhombohedral calcite, respectively61. No additional peaks attributable to other CaCO3 polymorphs (e.g. aragonite or vaterite, which would show intense lines near ~ 26–27°) are observed, confirming that calcite is the sole carbonate phase present61.

Discussion

The elemental analysis suggests that the sodium detected in the composite likely originates from residual NaOH entrapped within the material’s microstructure. In contrast, the calcium content nearly doubled when the fungi were cultured on the mattress waste, indicating the occurrence of calcium mineralization within the composite. The higher oxygen content is probably due to the incorporation of oxygen-rich synthetic polymers (such as polyurethane and polyester) from the mattress waste. These observations imply that the chemical treatment and substrate composition strongly influenced the elemental profile of the final bio-composite.

Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) was conclusively identified in the composite through FTIR and XRD analyses. Specifically, the FTIR spectrum of the mycelium-OS blend showed a pronounced carbonate bending vibration around 850 cm−1, while XRD patterns confirmed the presence of rhombohedral calcite as the sole crystalline CaCO3 polymorph. Notably, no peaks for vaterite or aragonite were observed, indicating that the biomineralization pathway favoured calcite precipitation, likely due to prolonged incubation and stable pH buffering during submerged culture. In the present study, the pH of the culture medium was observed to increase from 4.9 immediately after inoculation to 8.6 at the end of incubation, suggesting progressive alkalinisation that supports carbonate precipitation. Furthermore, ICP-OES analysis of the effluent indicated dissolved calcium concentrations of 0.9 ± 0.2 ppm in potato dextrose broth, 2.0 ± 0.3 ppm in mattress waste alone, and 4.2 ± 0.3 ppm in the fungal-mattress waste composite. This rise in calcium content supports the hypothesis that calcium was mobilised both from the substrate and through fungal metabolic processes, providing indirect evidence for CaCO3 biomineralization within the composite.

The formation of CaCO3 in the composite is consistent with fungal biomineralization pathways described in the literature62,63. During fungal degradation of polyurethane (PU), hydrolysis of the urethane bonds leads to the release of alcohol and amine functional groups, with the concurrent generation of carbon dioxide (CO2) as a by-product30. In an aqueous growth environment, this CO2 dissolves and reacts with free calcium ions, which present in the growth medium or liberated from additives in the substrate, to form insoluble calcium carbonate precipitates. This biomineralization process is further promoted by the metabolic activity of fungi such as P. chrysogenum, which produces enzymes capable of cleaving urethane linkages and facilitating the local accumulation of carbonate species around fungal hyphae30,62,63. Filamentous fungi such as P. chrysogenum are well-documented agents of biomineralization, capable of producing organic acids (e.g., oxalic acid), carbonic anhydrase, and urease, which catalyse pathways leading to calcium precipitation63,64. The fungal hyphae and their extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) also serve as nucleation templates, enhancing mineral deposition63,64. Liaud et al. reported that fungal EPS can adsorb Ca²⁺ and facilitate the localized precipitation of either calcium oxalate or carbonate onto hyphal surfaces, contributing to a mineralized, thermally robust mycelial network63,64. In similar systems, Sazanova et al. demonstrated that P. chrysogenum initially precipitates calcium oxalate under acidic conditions, with calcite forming as the environment becomes more alkaline during late-stage growth65. Martuscelli et al. showed that urease-positive P. chrysogenum could induce substantial CaCO3 precipitation within 14 days, forming “biosandstone”-like composites when immobilized in porous supports63,66. These biomineralization mechanisms are especially relevant under submerged cultivation, as used in this study. The aqueous environment promotes CO2 solubilization and interaction with calcium ions, facilitating calcite formation. Furthermore, in literature, it was shown that immobilizing P. chrysogenum on polyurethane foam substrates not only aids the breakdown of synthetic polymers but significantly enhances fungal growth rate and metabolic activity49. For example, enhanced metabolic conditions led to up to a twelvefold increase in penicillin biosynthesis compared to traditional growth on solid media49. These findings underscore the synergistic effect of PU foam as both a structural scaffold and a stimulant for fungal biosynthesis, which in this case likely extended to CaCO3 precipitation.

The integrated TGA and DTG analyses revealed that the mycelium-OS blend (coarse shredded mattress waste under submerged conditions) exhibited exceptional thermal stability, retaining 92.85% of its weight at 997 °C and 90% at 1000 °C, far exceeding the untreated mycelium control, which disintegrated by 850 °C. This enhanced performance correlates with the low DTG decomposition rate of 0.03%/°C at 860 °C for the mycelium-OS composite, reflecting a more gradual and controlled thermal degradation pathway. Because calcite decomposes in the 840–900 °C range to form calcium oxide (CaO)67, which is thermally stable, the high retention of mass at extreme temperatures can be attributed to the buffering effect of CaCO3 mineralization. This finding is consistent with reports where bio-composites reinforced with inorganic fillers like CaCO3 exhibit improved fire retardancy and thermal resistance62,63. The superior thermal stability of the mycelium-OS blend also coincides with its low moisture loss (0.04%/°C), as observed in the DTG profile, and its extended decomposition window. The discrepancy between DTG peak positions and 50% mass loss temperatures in the composite further suggests overlapping degradation mechanisms, akin to those seen in lignin-based systems68. This complex thermal behaviour requires additional chemical and structural analysis.

The mycelium-OS composite also demonstrated strong insulation potential. Its measured thermal conductivity of 0.048 ± 0.002 W/m.K places it within the range of established natural insulation materials such as sheep wool (0.038–0.054 W/m.K), recycled cellulose (0.040–0.050 W/m.K), and cotton/textile waste (0.030–0.044 W/m.K)69. Although benchmark glass wool (Earthwool) showed a slightly lower conductivity of 0.040 W/m.K, the bio-composite offers superior sustainability with comparable performance. Advanced materials such as aerogels or vacuum insulation panels achieve conductivities below 0.025 W/m.K but often come with higher costs and environmental problems69. Taken together, these results highlight the viability of mycelium-mattress waste composites, particularly the submerged OS blend, as sustainable, fire-resistant insulation materials. The presence of biomineralized CaCO3 contributes not only to thermal resilience but also to structural cohesion, making this composite a promising candidate for green building applications.

It should be noted, however, that the current study evaluates fire resistance only through thermal stability (TGA) data. Direct flammability or combustion tests (e.g., cone calorimetry, LOI, or flame-spread measurements) were not performed and thus claims regarding fire resistance are based solely on thermal decomposition behaviour. Future work should include standardized fire-testing under regulatory conditions to fully confirm the material’s fire performance in construction and industrial settings. Future work should also further explore the enzymatic degradation pathways of polyurethane by P. chrysogenum, particularly the role of urease and esterases in facilitating CO2 release and mineral nucleation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful development of thermally resilient and sustainable bio-insulation materials derived from fungal mycelium cultivated on recycled mattress waste, followed by chemical treatments. Through an integrated suite of analytical techniques, including SEM-EDS, TGA-DTG, FTIR, and XRD, our results confirm the structural and thermal advantages of mycelium-mattress composites, particularly under submerged growth conditions using coarse shredded waste (OS). The exceptional thermal stability of the mycelium-OS blend, which retained over 90% of its mass at 1000 °C, significantly outperforms untreated controls and benchmark glass wool insulation.

This performance is attributed to the in situ biomineralization of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), confirmed to be in the calcite form. The presence of CaCO3 is closely linked to fungal metabolic activity and enzymatic hydrolysis of polyurethane, which releases CO2 and facilitates carbonate precipitation under submerged conditions. Immobilization of P. chrysogenum on urethane foam not only enhances enzymatic degradation of polymeric substrates but also accelerates fungal growth and metabolic output, promoting mineral nucleation and matrix reinforcement.

The resulting composite exhibits a thermal conductivity of 0.048 ± 0.002 W/m.K, comparable to conventional and sustainable insulators, with the added advantage of being derived from post-consumer waste. These findings position mycelium-mattress composites as a promising, eco-friendly alternative for thermal insulation applications in construction and industrial settings.

Future work should focus on elucidating the enzymatic mechanisms involved in polyurethane degradation, optimizing mineralization kinetics, and conducting large-scale fire performance testing under real-world building codes. The integration of fungal biotechnology with waste valorisation presents a scalable route toward circular economy solutions in sustainable materials engineering.
