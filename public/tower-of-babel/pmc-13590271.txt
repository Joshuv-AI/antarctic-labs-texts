An Exploratory Analysis of Potential Core Targets and Signaling Pathways Linking Dexmedetomidine to Diabetes Insipidus: Integration of FAERS Pharmacovigilance Data, Network Toxicology and Clinical Transcriptomics
Authors: Shaopeng Ming, Zhouyan Wu, Jingjing Li, Yicheng Su, Jianyou Yu, Hongtao Liu, Yanzhuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDexmedetomidine is widely used for clinical sedation, while clinical data suggest a potential correlation between its administration and diabetes insipidus. This exploratory study aimed to characterize molecular correlates linking dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus via multiomics and pharmacovigilance analysis.MethodsFAERS data (2004–2024) were mined for disproportionality analysis to screen suggestive association signals. Network toxicology predicted shared targets of dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus, followed by GO/KEGG enrichment, PPI network construction and molecular docking. Transcriptome sequencing of 10 treated patients preliminarily validated bioinformatic correlations.ResultsA strong suggestive association signal was detected (ROR=471.47). A total of 105 overlapping targets were screened, among which IL6, IL10, INS, IL1B, AKT1 and IFNG served as core correlated hub genes. Docking confirmed stable binding between dexmedetomidine and these proteins. Enrichment revealed enriched MAPK cascade, kinase activity and PI3K/AKT pathways, which may correlate with abnormal AQP2 function. Transcriptomics identified differential expression of inflammation-immune genes after infusion, consistent with predicted molecular correlations.ConclusionThis study combined FAERS pharmacovigilance analysis, network toxicology, molecular docking and transcriptome sequencing to explore potential mechanisms of dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus. FAERS data mining uncovered a robust adverse signal, indicating a strong correlation between dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus for clinical reference. Six hub genes and PI3K/AKT, MAPK pathways may be associated with this side effect. These findings facilitate high-risk population management and individualized sedation, and supply molecular candidates for future validation studies.Keywords: dexmedetomidine, diabetes insipidus, network toxicology, molecular correlation, FAERS database

Background

Dexmedetomidine, a selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, can effectively reduce the dosage of opioids when used adjunctively in clinical practice. Additionally, this drug can alleviate symptoms of respiratory depression, reduce sympathetic nervous system tone, and mitigate stress responses induced by anesthesia and surgical procedures. Meanwhile, it can decrease the incidence of adverse events such as hypotension, delirium, tremor, and postoperative nausea and vomiting.1,2 Therefore, dexmedetomidine is widely applied in intensive care units (ICU), emergency departments, anesthesia departments, endoscopic procedures, and radiological operations.3

With the widespread clinical application of dexmedetomidine, the number of reported adverse events associated with its use has been on the rise, with common ones including hypotension, hypertension, and bradycardia4 However, during the actual implementation of clinical trials, issues such as insufficient follow-up duration, discrepancies in screening criteria for the study population, and limited sample sizes may arise, leading to the inability of observed adverse reactions in clinical trials to accurately reflect real-world conditions. Currently, research on adverse events associated with dexmedetomidine primarily focuses on the cardiovascular system5 However, in animal experiments, it has been observed that dexmedetomidine exerts a diuretic effect by inhibiting the antidiuretic action of vasopressin in the collecting ducts6 In recent years, multiple clinical case reports and retrospective studies have documented secondary diabetes insipidus following dexmedetomidine administration. This adverse event occurs sporadically among critically ill patients receiving sedation, while large-sample epidemiological data remain unavailable.7–9 Existing literature indicates that dexmedetomidine ranks as the second most frequently reported agent contributing to drug-induced diabetes insipidus and exhibits the strongest association with the onset of this disorder10 Preliminary basic studies have suggested that dexmedetomidine may modulate aquaporin 2 (AQP2) trafficking in renal collecting ducts, arginine vasopressin (AVP) signaling, and systemic inflammatory imbalance.11–14 Relevant investigations are mostly limited to animal experiments or case analyses, and the comprehensive molecular regulatory network underlying dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus has not been systematically elucidated. Therefore, it is critical to investigate safety data associated with dexmedetomidine-related diabetes insipidus and explore the underlying mechanisms.

The FAERS database provides a rich resource for real-world data analysis regarding drug safety and efficacy, facilitating the identification of potential drug-disease associations and the prediction of drug reaction risks15 Literature reports have indicated a strong association between dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus as reported in the FAERS database16 However, systematic research on the specific role of dexmedetomidine in regulating the pathological mechanisms of diabetes insipidus is still lacking, and its underlying mechanism of action remains unclear. Network toxicology, grounded in molecular interaction networks and bioinformatics, enables high-throughput identification of key genes, proteins, and signaling pathways associated with drugs and diseases, providing theoretical support for exploring intervention targets for complex diseases17 The present study combines FAERS-based pharmacovigilance disproportionality analysis, network toxicology target prediction and clinical transcriptome sequencing to sequentially realize adverse event signal mining, potential molecular mechanism prediction and preliminary exploration with human samples. Such a multi-dimensional analytical system efficiently minimizes the bias caused by monomodal approaches and offers a robust research paradigm to unravel the mechanisms of adverse drug reactions.

Methods

Mining of FAERS Database

In this study, post-marketing adverse drug reaction (ADR) data of dexmedetomidine from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2024 were retrieved from the FAERS database, covering standard FAERS sub-tables including demographic information, drug information, adverse reaction events, patient outcomes, reporting sources, medication therapy information and invalid deleted cases, with the unified time window consistent with the previously studied remimazolam to guarantee baseline data comparability; all raw data were imported into MySQL 15.0 for standardized management, and rigorous data cleaning procedures were performed, including screening dexmedetomidine-targeted valid records, removing duplicate cases and excluding records with core missing information to ensure dataset integrity and eliminate analytical bias. Prior to statistical analysis, all adverse drug event (ADE) descriptions were standardized and classified based on the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA 25.0), with System Organ Class (SOC) and Preferred Term (PT) adopted as the classification indicators, and only reports labeling dexmedetomidine as the primarily suspected drug were included for subsequent signal mining. Three classical disproportionality analysis methods were applied to comprehensively screen and verify ADR signals using Microsoft Excel 2016 for statistical calculation: Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), and Empirical Bayes Geometric Mean (EBGM), with consistent variable definitions for all three algorithms (a: target ADE reports of dexmedetomidine, b: non-target ADE reports of dexmedetomidine, c: target ADE reports of other drugs in FAERS, d: non-target ADE reports of other drugs in FAERS). The calculation formulas were defined as ROR = (a/b)/(c/d) and PRR = [a/(a+b)]/[c/(c+d)], while EBGM was adopted to correct statistical deviation of sparse and low-frequency ADE data and reduce false-positive signals. The unified positive signal threshold was set as an index value ≥2 with the lower limit of 95% confidence interval >1, and a valid dexmedetomidine-related ADR signal was confirmed only when ROR, PRR and EBGM simultaneously met the positive threshold criteria to improve the accuracy and reliability of signal identification.

Network Toxicology

Identification of Functional Targets of Dexmedetomidine

The canonical SMILES string for dexmedetomidine was retrieved from the PubChem database. Following this, the corresponding SDF file was submitted to the ChEMBL,18 STITCH19 and SwissTargetPrediction20 databases to facilitate in silico target prediction. The target profiles generated by these three distinct platforms were subsequently integrated, and redundant entries were systematically eliminated through deduplication to establish a definitive, non-redundant set of potential targets for dexmedetomidine.

Retrieval of Disease-Associated Targets

Genes implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetes insipidus were retrieved from the Genecards21 and OMIM22 databases. Following retrieval, the target gene datasets derived from these two repositories were consolidated, and redundant entries were systematically eliminated through deduplication to generate a curated, non-redundant set of pathogenic genes associated with diabetes insipidus.

The Overlap of Pharmacological Targets and Disease-Associated Molecular Targets

To visualize the overlapping targets between pharmacological agents and disease-associated molecular entities, the VennDiagram23 package implemented in the R statistical environment was employed to generate a Venn diagram. Using this approach, we obtained the intersection results between the relevant targets of dexmedetomidine and the pathogenic genes associated with diabetes insipidus.

Construction of Pharmacological Target-Pathway Interaction Network

The drug-target-pathway network elucidates how drugs regulate specific pathways through their core targets, and highlights the interactions between key targets and drugs, so as to identify the pivotal targets and pathways. The intersection dataset derived from the prior analysis in The Overlap of Pharmacological Targets and Disease-Associated Molecular Targets was uploaded to Cytoscape 3.10.124 to construct the drug-target-pathway network.

GO and KEGG Pathway Enrichment Analysis

To elucidate the potential biological roles and molecular pathways underlying candidate therapeutic targets associated with dexmedetomidine-triggered diabetes insipidus, “ClusterProfiler”25 was utilized to conduct GO term26 and KEGG biological pathway27 analyses. GO enrichment analysis was performed from the perspectives of molecular function, biological process and cellular component. Terms satisfying the criteria of P<0.05 and Q<0.05 were retained and exhibited in bubble plots. Meanwhile, significantly enriched KEGG pathways were screened with identical cut-off values and visualized in the same graphical form.

PPI Network

The intersecting genes obtained above were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org), and a PPI network28 was constructed with a confidence threshold set at 0.7. The results were exported in TSV format and visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.1. The genes were ranked based on their degree values within the software, and the top six genes with the highest degree values were selected as hub genes.

Molecular Docking

The three-dimensional (3D) structures of active compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database. The receptor structures of key target proteins corresponding to hub genes were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). Molecular docking simulation was performed to predict binding conformations and binding affinities, so as to further characterize the intermolecular interactions between dexmedetomidine and the hub target proteins screened in this study. Briefly, molecular docking analysis was implemented using the CB-Dock2 web server29(https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php). Subsequently, PyMOL 3.130 and Discovery Studio 2019 software31 were utilized for visual characterization of docking complexes to quantitatively assess the binding capacity of dexmedetomidine toward candidate hub targets, which further validated the results derived from network pharmacology analysis.

Transcriptome Sequencing

Ethical Approval

This prospective self-matched paired trial was performed in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Liuzhou Workers’ Hospital (Ethics Approval No. KY2025584). The trial has been formally registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2500105507). Written informed consent was acquired from all enrolled patients and their family members before surgical intervention.

Study Design and Patient Cohort

This prospective self-matched paired trial enrolled 10 perioperative patients to perform transcriptomic validation of network toxicology predictions. Sample size was calculated via the paired mean comparison formula for self-controlled design:

Two-sided α=0.05 and power=0.8 were adopted, and the minimal required sample size was calculated as 5; we enrolled 10 participants to improve sequencing reliability. Eligible subjects were aged 18–55 years with ASA I–II physical status; individuals with severe organ dysfunction, uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment, dexmedetomidine allergy or recent related drug exposure were excluded. All patients received standardized dexmedetomidine infusion (1 μg/kg loading over 15 min, maintained at 0.5 μg/kg/h). Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was collected at baseline, 30 min and 60 min after infusion for transcriptome sequencing. Conventional hemodynamic rescue drugs were available for critical bradycardia or hypotension according to clinical routine.

Database Building Method and Sequencing

Total RNA was subjected to mRNA enrichment using magnetic beads conjugated with oligo(dT) to capture mRNA containing poly(A) tails. The enriched mRNA was fragmented with fragmentation buffer, followed by reverse transcription using random N6 primers to synthesize the first-strand cDNA, and second-strand cDNA was further synthesized to generate double-stranded DNA. Subsequently, the termini of double-stranded DNA were blunted, and 5′ ends were phosphorylated. A single “A” base was added to the 3′ ends to form sticky ends. Bubble adapters with a protruding 3′ “T” were ligated to the DNA fragments. The ligation products were amplified via PCR using specific primers. The purified PCR products were thermally denatured into single-stranded DNA, which was circularized with bridge primers to construct single-stranded circular DNA libraries. All constructed libraries underwent quality inspection. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ platform with a PE150 read length strategy. All raw transcriptomic sequencing data were anonymized prior to analysis. Unique random identification codes replaced patient name, admission ID and other identifiable information, and all clinical metadata were de-identified to eliminate personal identifiers. Raw sequencing files are stored in encrypted institutional servers with restricted access only for authorized researchers, in compliance with local clinical data privacy regulations.

Bioinformatics Analysis of Sequencing Results

Quality control and alignment quantification were performed on the raw data, ultimately generating a gene expression data matrix (read count matrix) for multiple samples. High-quality clean reads were mapped to the reference genome via Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.3)32 Gene expression abundance was subsequently quantified with RSEM (v1.3.1),33 and a clustering heatmap illustrating the gene expression patterns among different samples was constructed using the pheatmap package (v1.0.8)34 DEGs were identified by DESeq2 (v1.4.5),35 with the threshold set as Q value ≤ 0.05 or false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.001. Volcano plots were generated using the Dr. Tom Multi-Omics Data Mining System (https://biosys.bgi.com). DEGs were intersected with dexmedetomidine-related pathogenic targets identified in The Overlap of Pharmacological Targets and Disease-Associated Molecular Targets to obtain overlapping genes shared by transcriptome sequencing and network toxicology analysis. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed with the filtering thresholds set at P < 0.05 and Q < 0.05, to explore biological functions and signaling pathways associated with dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus. Results

Results from the FAERS Database

The results revealed that among various adverse events, the reporting odds ratio (ROR) value of diabetes insipidus reached 471.47 (95% CI: 385.78–576.19), the proportional reporting ratio (PRR) was 463.20 (95% CI: 380.29–564.19), and the empirical Bayes geometric mean (EBGM) was 441.03 (95% CI: 360.87–538.99). All values substantially exceeded the predefined thresholds, indicating a robust and statistically significant adverse event signal. To further explore the clinical characteristics of this adverse reaction and its association with dexmedetomidine, detailed clinical information of all patients with reported diabetes insipidus was extracted (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), and demographic characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The analysis revealed that a total of 102 patients reported adverse events of diabetes insipidus associated with dexmedetomidine use over the more than 20-year monitoring period. Diabetes insipidus represents one of the frequently reported adverse reactions of dexmedetomidine, suggesting a strong correlational relationship between them.

Potential Targets of Dexmedetomidine-Induced Diabetes Insipidus

A total of 4865 potential target genes of dexmedetomidine were identified through integrated analysis of the GeneCards, OMIM, and Therapeutic Target Database databases, as shown in Figure 2A. Similarly, integrated mining of the GeneCards and OMIM databases yielded 360 candidate pathogenic targets for diabetes insipidus (Figure 2B). Venn analysis uncovered 105 common targets between dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus (Figure 2C). Additionally, a drug-target pathway network depicting dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus was constructed using Cytoscape, as shown in Figure 2D.

The top six genes identified were Interleukin-6 (IL6), Interleukin-10 (IL10), Insulin (INS), Interleukin 1 beta (IL1B), RAC-alpha serine/threonine-protein kinase (AKT1), and Interferon gamma (IFNG), as indicated in Figure 3B.

Molecular Docking

Figure 5A–F illustrate molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinity between dexmedetomidine and its core targets. For the IL1B target (Figure 5A), the C3 binding pocket was identified as the optimal binding site, with a Vina score of −5.8 and a cavity volume of 112 Å3. Dexmedetomidine formed stable binding interactions around the coordinate (−18, 5, 6), with relevant data summarized in Table 1. For IFNG (Figure 5B), the C2 pocket displayed the most favorable binding activity at the coordinate (7, 35, 23), with a Vina score of −5.7 and a cavity volume of 996 Å3, as detailed in Table 2. In general, a binding energy value below 0 kcal/mol signifies effective binding capacity, whereas an energy threshold lower than −5.0 kcal/mol indicates strong binding affinity. Notably, lower binding energy corresponds to stronger molecular interaction. The binding energies of dexmedetomidine with four key target molecules—IL6, IL10, AKT1, and INS—were determined to be −5.8 kcal/mol, −6.8 kcal/mol, −7.3 kcal/mol, and −5.2 kcal/mol, respectively, as detailed in Tables 3–6. The results demonstrated that dexmedetomidine exhibits strong binding affinities toward six key target molecules, suggesting their spontaneous association and indicating their critical roles in the molecular mechanism underlying dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus.

Analysis of Transcriptome Sequencing Results from Clinical Samples

Differential expression analysis between pre-treatment and 30 minutes post-treatment identified 188 DEGs, comprising 148 upregulated and 40 downregulated genes (Figure 6A and C). Differential expression analysis between pre-treatment and 60 minutes post-treatment identified 3139 DEGs, comprising 1888 upregulated and 1251 downregulated genes (Figure 6B and D). Transcriptomic analysis at 30 minutes post-dexmedetomidine administration identified three upregulated genes associated with dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus, while no downregulated targets were detected in the differential expression profiling (Figure 7A and B). Transcriptomic analysis at 60 minutes post-dexmedetomidine administration identified 13 upregulated genes associated with dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus, including IFNG and IL1B, while 8 downregulated targets were detected in the differential expression profiling (Figure 7C and D). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of differentially expressed targets at 60 minutes post-treatment revealed that upregulated genes were primarily involved in the regulation of kinase activity and the IL-17 signaling pathway. Additionally, the downregulated target genes were primarily involved in immune cell regulation, as shown in Figure 8A–D.

Discussion

As a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, dexmedetomidine is widely administered for clinical sedation and anesthesia. Its advantages of slight respiratory inhibition, satisfactory stress relief and favorable hemodynamic stability have driven a steady annual rise in its clinical dosage. In this context, safety monitoring of dexmedetomidine is particularly valuable. Herein, we utilized the FAERS database, a major public pharmacovigilance resource, to explore real-world evidence and provide references for its standardized clinical use. Through analysis of adverse event reports involving dexmedetomidine in the FAERS database, we identified that in addition to cardiac disorders (the most frequently reported with the strongest signal intensity), diabetes insipidus also demonstrated a notably strong safety signal. Combining findings from the FAERS database, we employed network toxicology analysis to explore the mechanistic pathways underlying dexmedetomidine-associated diabetes insipidus, providing novel insights into its pharmacological mechanisms.

We first mined real-world spontaneous adverse event reports from the FAERS database spanning 2004–2024 to objectively quantify the clinical risk of diabetes insipidus following dexmedetomidine administration. The disproportionality analysis yielded an extremely high ROR value of 471.47 accompanied by 102 clinical case reports, which robustly confirmed a strong correlation linking dexmedetomidine to polyuria complications across large population-based real-world data. Unlike single-center small-sample clinical observations limited by inclusion criteria and short follow-up durations, FAERS aggregates global spontaneous adverse reports and captures rare postoperative adverse events such as drug-induced diabetes insipidus that are easily overlooked in routine clinical trials. This prominent pharmacovigilance signal laid a solid clinical foundation for our subsequent systematic mechanistic exploration.

Based on 105 overlapping targets associated with dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus, a PPI network was constructed. Degree value was adopted as the core topological indicator to quantitatively screen hub genes. The Degree value reflects the number of interactive connections between a single protein and other proteins within the network; a higher value indicates that the corresponding molecule acts as a core signaling node in the drug-disease regulatory network. Finally, the top six genes ranked by Degree, including IL6, IL10, INS, IL1B, AKT1 and IFNG, were selected as candidate hub genes. Molecular docking analysis revealed that the binding free energies between dexmedetomidine and all six proteins were lower than −5.0 kcal/mol, indicating stable and spontaneous binding interactions. These findings suggested that dexmedetomidine may directly target these proteins to exert regulatory effects.

In perioperative patients, repeated renal biopsy is clinically invasive and infeasible, while peripheral blood can minimize the capture of systemic inflammatory signals triggered by general anesthesia, and provide easily detected biomarkers for routine clinical monitoring. In order to further explore the findings of network toxicology analysis, we performed transcriptome sequencing. Subsequent transcriptome sequencing results partially validated the predictions derived from network toxicology. Differential expression analysis identified 3139 DEGs at 60 min after drug administration, which was markedly more than the 188 DEGs detected at 30 min. The far larger number of DEGs at 60 min indicates cumulative transcriptional remodeling upon sustained dexmedetomidine exposure. Transient weak gene perturbations at 30 min represent early, unstable signaling fluctuations, while robust transcriptional shifts at 60 min correspond to elevated polyuria risk. Therefore, transcriptomic data obtained at 60 min post-administration were used for subsequent analyses. Among the 3139 DEGs identified at 60 min, 1888 genes were upregulated and 1251 genes were downregulated. Upregulated genes, such as IFNG and IL1B, were significantly enriched in pathways related to kinase activity regulation and the IL-17 signaling pathway. Meanwhile, downregulated genes were mainly involved in the modulation of immune cell function, implying potential immunomodulatory effects of dexmedetomidine.

Previous studies have demonstrated that dexmedetomidine exerts immunomodulatory effects, attenuating perioperative stress and inflammatory responses while preserving immune function in surgical patients14 The immunomodulatory effects of dexmedetomidine may indirectly influence ADH secretion via activation of inflammatory pathways. IFNG, IL1B, IL6, and IL10 encode the canonical pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10, respectively. Accumulating evidence indicates that IL-10 participates in the regulation of glial cell in situ activation, gliosis, infiltration, and immune cell phenotypic modulation following central nervous system injury36 In the lysophosphatidylcholine-induced demyelination model, IFN-γ and IL-1β upregulate downstream inflammatory mediators (eg, TNF-α, IL-6) by activating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis, thereby triggering systemic inflammatory responses36 Accumulating evidence has indicated that chronic inflammation is tightly linked to the pathogenesis and progression of multiple cerebral disorders, including hydrocephalus, intracerebral hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, and traumatic brain injury. Furthermore, a single-center study revealed that preoperative peripheral blood inflammatory biomarkers may serve as potential predictors for the risk of postoperative diabetes insipidus in patients with cerebral palsy.37–40 In traumatic brain injury models, neuroinflammatory responses have also been found to potentially damage the thalamic region of the brain, leading to endocrine system dysfunction41 Sequencing analysis revealed that the upregulated differentially expressed targets primarily participate in the IL-17 signaling pathway, which induces the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, with IL-1β and IL-6 playing key roles in this process. Accordingly, we speculate that dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus may be associated with inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, direct evidence confirming the involvement of inflammatory reactions remains absent. Further experimental investigations are required to elucidate the relationships among the three factors in future studies.

AKT1 is a member of the AKT family and serves as a pivotal hub within the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, participating extensively in the modulation of cell proliferation, survival and metabolism42 In renal tissues, AKT1 modulates sodium and water reabsorption in renal tubular epithelial cells through activation of the PI3K/AKT cascade, thereby maintaining fluid balance43 During renal ischemia-reperfusion injury, AKT1 inhibition is observed, which promotes the progression of kidney injury. AKT1 is likely a decisive factor influencing tubular cell death.44,45 Naief Dahran et al found that in a mouse model of renal injury, significant polyuria developed concurrently with decreased AKT expression46 Additionally, AKT can influence renal tubular water reabsorption by regulating the expression and trafficking of AQP247 Our enrichment analyses consistently indicate that dexmedetomidine participates in the biological processes of kinase activity regulation and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Accordingly, we propose a tentative speculative cascade: dexmedetomidine-associated transcriptional changes in AKT1 signaling might indirectly disturb downstream AQP2-mediated water reabsorption, which could plausibly contribute to polyuria. This AKT1-AQP2 regulatory axis remains a speculative hypothesis requiring dedicated cellular and animal functional assays to verify causality.

The INS gene encodes preproinsulin and is closely related to the regulation of insulin synthesis and secretion48 Numerous studies have demonstrated that insulin exerts multifaceted effects on renal tissues ranging from glomeruli to tubules by regulating glomerular filtration, gluconeogenesis, natriuresis, glucose uptake, ion transport modulation, and anti-apoptotic protection49 The tubular structures of the kidney are subject to regulation by multiple hormones, among which insulin plays a critical role in modulating renal tubular transport. Currently, the antidiuretic effect of insulin has been confirmed in humans50 However, a secondary effect of dexmedetomidine is the reduction of pancreatic insulin release. A clinical trial observed that low-dose dexmedetomidine administration decreased plasma insulin concentrations in healthy fasting individuals.51,52 We conjecture that altered INS transcription following dexmedetomidine exposure may diminish insulin biosynthesis and secretion, which could secondarily attenuate insulin’s tubular antidiuretic effects and potentially correlate with polyuria. In addition, clinicians in the operating room and ICU who apply dexmedetomidine clinically, especially in patients with diabetes, should monitor the urine volume per hour, plasma and urine osmotic pressure during long-term infusion of dexmedetomidine, so as to early identify polyuria and drug-induced diabetes insipidus. In case of abnormal permeability index, it is recommended to reduce the dose and adjust the body fluid.

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus, we performed enrichment analysis. GO enrichment analysis indicated that dexmedetomidine primarily affects biological processes such as activation of the MAPK signaling cascade and enhancement of kinase activity. Our sequencing results revealed that dexmedetomidine participates in diabetes insipidus regulation mainly through positive modulation of kinase activity. The study found that central diabetes insipidus is one of the most common symptoms of Erdheim–Chester disease, and the pathogenesis of this disease is closely related to pathogenic variants in the MAPK signaling pathway53 In a mouse model of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, rotenone treatment significantly downregulated MAPK pathway activation, thereby modulating AQP2 trafficking and reducing urinary sodium excretion54 Accumulating evidence has confirmed that the MAPK pathway is tightly correlated with AQP2 trafficking, and the modulation of AQP2 is reliant on the cAMP‑PKA signaling axis. Dexmedetomidine indirectly regulates AQP2 function by modulating AVP expression, thereby influencing downstream AQP2 activity13 KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for dexmedetomidine-mediated regulation of diabetes insipidus revealed that the drug primarily exerts its effects through the PI3K/AKT and AMPK signaling pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that AMPK activation induces plasma membrane localization of AQP2 in mice, thereby promoting water reabsorption, suggesting that AMPK may represent a promising therapeutic target for diabetes insipidus.55,56 Moreover, the AMPK and PI3K/Akt pathways are not only involved in oxidative stress responses but also closely associated with inflammation and apoptosis.57,58 Previous studies have confirmed that dexmedetomidine can suppress oxidative stress and inflammatory injury via PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby attenuating organ damage.59,60 Prior preclinical work reported correlational associations between MAPK signaling and AQP2 membrane trafficking, yet we lacked direct measurement of renal AVP or AQP2 protein levels in patient samples. We therefore hypothesize that dexmedetomidine-induced perturbations in MAPK cascade activity might indirectly interfere with AVP-dependent AQP2 function. Sequencing results revealed that downregulated targets are involved in immune cell regulation, suggesting that dexmedetomidine may participate in diabetes insipidus regulation via immune cell modulation. However, the role of immune cells in this interaction remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Although existing investigations have largely concentrated on the signaling mechanisms of dexmedetomidine in neurological disorders and liver/lung injuries, this study comprehensively explores its network regulatory mechanisms in water metabolism and diabetes insipidus pathology. This study integrated large-scale biological databases and employed rigorous topological analysis, significantly enhancing the reliability and reproducibility of our conclusions. In addition, molecular docking simulations verified that dexmedetomidine could stably bind to the proteins encoded by six core genes, providing robust in silico validation for our proposed mechanistic hypothesis. Collectively, the multidimensional modulation of key signaling pathways by dexmedetomidine provides a theoretical foundation for identifying its molecular targets in diabetes insipidus therapy. However, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, disproportionality analysis based on the FAERS database can only identify statistical correlations between dexmedetomidine and diabetes insipidus rather than causal relationships. Second, network toxicology and molecular docking are purely in silico predictive approaches, and the predicted target genes and signaling pathways require further experimental verification. Third, transcriptome sequencing was performed using clinical samples from only 10 patients receiving dexmedetomidine, representing a small exploratory cohort. In addition, blood samples were collected merely at two time point (30 min and 60 min after drug administration), lacking multi-timepoint dynamic expression data. These factors restrict the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, the molecular regulatory mechanism proposed herein is merely a preliminary hypothesis. In the future, it is still necessary to carry out experiments, expand the sample size and set up continuous multi time point sampling to confirm the results of this study.

Conclusion

This study integrated real-world pharmacovigilance data from FAERS, network toxicology, molecular docking, and transcriptome sequencing to explore the mechanisms underlying dexmedetomidine-induced diabetes insipidus. FAERS data mining revealed a robust adverse signal, reminding clinicians that dexmedetomidine has a strong correlation with the occurrence of diabetes insipidus. Six hub genes (IFNG, IL1B, AKT1, IL6, IL10, and INS), along with the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, may be closely implicated in dexmedetomidine-triggered diabetes insipidus. This work provides practical guidance for anesthesiologists to stratify high-risk populations, strengthen urine output monitoring, and implement individualized sedation protocols. Meanwhile, it offers prioritized molecular candidates for subsequent large-cohort validation and targeted.
