A low-complexity subtraction-based weighting coefficient for synthetic aperture ultrasound imaging
Authors: Roya Paridar, Babak Mohammadzadeh Asl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A major challenge in medical ultrasound beamforming is developing algorithms capable of producing high-resolution images. The minimum variance (MV) algorithm achieves higher resolution than the delay-and-sum (DAS) approach but suffers from high computational cost, limiting its practical use. Recently, the null subtraction imaging (NSI) algorithm has been proposed, offering high resolution with significantly lower complexity than MV. However, its main drawback is the loss of background speckle information. In this paper, a novel method is introduced to simultaneously achieve high resolution and preserve background speckle. The proposed technique reconstructs a high-resolution image while maintaining speckle characteristics comparable to those of DAS. Appropriate weights are determined based on a subtraction process between beamformed images corresponding to two transmit sub-arrays. Moreover, the computational burden of the proposed method is low, making it suitable for real-time applications. Simulated and experimental evaluations demonstrate that the proposed technique outperforms the MV and NSI algorithms in terms of resolution enhancement. Furthermore, among existing methods, it uniquely achieves simultaneous improvement in image resolution, contrast, and background speckle preservation.

Introduction

Ultrasound imaging, due to its valuable advantages such as non-ionizing, non-invasive, high-speed, and low-cost properties, is among the most popular imaging methods in medicine1. The conventional B-mode technique, in which the imaging process is done by scanning the imaging region line by line, suffers from low frame rate and poor resolution. In contrast, the synthetic transmit aperture (STA) method offers improved lateral resolution, and when using sparse transmit elements, a higher frame rate can also be achieved2,3. Basically, in the STA method, only one element is employed to transmit acoustic energy into the tissue. The backscattered echoes of the mentioned transmit element are then acquired using all of the elements. That is, after the transmission process of each single transmit element, all of the elements act as the receive elements. Each transmit element undergoes this process in sequence, and the aggregated received signals result in a high-resolution reconstructed image4. The image reconstruction algorithm is crucial for determining both the image resolution and the system’s frame rate. This topic has generated significant interest among researchers, leading to numerous studies in this area to date. A summary of the relevant publications is provided below.

The well-known delay-and-sum (DAS) algorithm is commonly employed to generate the reconstructed image, as this beamformer is simple and doesn’t impose much computational complexity on the system (O[N], with the consideration of N elements). However, the resolution of the DAS approach is not well enough. The non-linear delay-multiply-and-sum (DMAS) algorithm was proposed by Matrone et. al., and used in different studies where the received signals from the elements are mutually correlated5,6. While the DMAS method enhances image resolution and contrast, it also introduces increased processing load because of the multiplication operations involved (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O[N^2]$$\end{document}). One should note that several studies have proposed reformulations that reduce the computational complexity to O[N] while maintaining equivalent results7–9. Despite these improvements, DMAS-based approaches still involve additional non-linear processing steps compared to DAS, which increases implementation complexity. Moreover, by using the DMAS algorithm, the reconstructed image contains dark artifacts, and the background speckle information is less accurately maintained compared to the DAS approach (as reference case).

The adaptive minimum variance (MV) beamformer was proposed in10,11, which can be considered a breakthrough in improving resolution in medical ultrasound12. In the high-resolution MV algorithm, estimating and inverting the covariance matrix of the received signals is necessary to derive the adaptive weighting coefficient. This, in turn, leads to a considerable increase in computational complexity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O[N^3]$$\end{document}) compared to DAS. As a result, the MV algorithm cannot be used in real-time imaging applications. Research efforts have been made to lower the processing load of the MV algorithm; for instance, in13, a set of pre-defined weighing coefficients was designed in order to prevent the calculation of the time-consuming process of the weighing coefficients that are used in the conventional MV algorithm. Also, in14, the Toeplitz structure was considered for the covariance matrix to reduce the complexity of its inversion process using the Trench method. Furthermore, in15, the beamspace algorithm was introduced to reduce the dimensionality of the covariance matrix and, consequently, accelerate the processing. In16, a high-resolution method known as the transmitting-receiving beamformer was suggested where the data matrix is converted into a virtual array vector. Afterwards, the MV algorithm was implemented on the extracted vector. A comprehensive set of methods for reducing the processing load of the MV method can be found in17.

The coherence factor (CF) was also proposed in18, as an adaptive weighing factor in which the ratio of the coherent to non-coherent energy is obtained and applied to the beamformed image as a weighing factor. Some other improved versions of the CF method were also introduced to improve the image quality, such as generalized CF, sign CF, phase CF, etc.19–24. The dual apodization with cross-correlation (DAX) was suggested in25 to improve the image contrast by suppressing the clutter. In26, the DAX method was combined with a sub-beamformer to improve the image quality with respect to both resolution and contrast. In27, a new combinatorial-based algorithm was introduced, where two weighing coefficients are involved, one for improving the image resolution, and the other for better suppressing the noise level inside the cystic regions. In28, a high-resolution method was proposed utilizing the signal range-standard deviation factor combined with multi-phase apodization with cross-correlation. In29, the sub-array beamforming (SAB) with applying beam steering in reception was used to obtain an improved resolution image while the computational complexity is reduced. The compressive sensing method has also been used to process the ultrasound data to either achieve an improved reconstructed image or speed up the imaging process (e.g.,30–33). In recent times, a novel algorithm called the null subtraction imaging (NSI) method was introduced in the context of plane wave imaging to enhance image resolution comparable to, or even better than, MV34,35. In this method, the beamformed images obtained from different pre-defined weighing coefficients are incoherently combined to achieve an improved resolution image. Due to its high resolution and low computational complexity, the NSI algorithm has attracted a lot of attention, and studies have been conducted on it to further improve its performance36,37. It should be noted that the NSI method struggles to effectively maintain background speckle information.

The primary focus of this paper is to obtain a high-resolution image, comparable to the NSI algorithm, while the background speckle information is also well preserved. To this end, a new low-complexity method is proposed; inspired by26, a new weighing factor is suggested in this paper, which is based on the subtraction of two beamformed images, each of which is obtained from the received signals from half the total number of transmit elements. Then, the corresponding output passes through some processing stages, including an adaptive thresholding process, to obtain the final improved weighting coefficient. It is shown that the proposed subtraction-based weighting coefficient (SC) either reaches an improved image resolution or preserves the background speckle information, similar to the NSI and DAS algorithms, respectively. The computational complexity of the suggested SC technique is comparable to that of the DAS beamformer, indicating its potential application in a real-time imaging scenario.

In the following, the DAS, DMAS, MV, and NSI algorithms are briefly introduced in Section Background, as they are going to be compared with the proposed method. Then, a comprehensive description of the suggested SC method is provided in Section Proposed Method. Next, the performance of the suggested technique is evaluated, and the results are presented in Section Results. Finally, the paper wraps up with a discussion and final conclusions presented in Section Discussion and Conclusion.

Proposed method

In this paper, a low complexity weighting coefficient, known as subtraction-based weighting coefficient (SC), is proposed to be employed on the DAS beamformed data in order to achieve a high-quality image characterized by excellent resolution and contrast. In the suggested technique, the transmit array is partitioned into two sub-arrays. Then, the DAS beamformed images produced by these two sub-arrays are obtained and subtracted. More precisely, we have:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y_{Sub}(n)&=y_{DAS1}(n)-y_{DAS2}(n)\nonumber \\&=\sum _{i=1}^{N/2}x_{s,i}(n) - \sum _{i=N/2+1}^{N}x_{s,i}(n). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}By doing so, the mainlobe amplitude of the resulting beampattern is canceled out, and its sidelobe level potentially remains; the mainlobe of the beampattern corresponding to the first transmit sub-array (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{DAS1}(n)$$\end{document}) is in the same direction as the one corresponding to the second transmit sub-array (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{DAS2}(n)$$\end{document}). Hence, through subtraction of the sub-array outputs, the mainlobe information will overlap, resulting in a reduction of the signal’s primary energy amplitude. For instance, point targets (as well as background speckle) exhibit higher values in the beamformed data. Also, the sidelobe levels of point targets (as well as inside the anechoic cystic regions) contain much lower values compared to point targets. Therefore, it can be found that in the resulting output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{Sub}(n)$$\end{document}, lower values indicate the mainlobe information of point targets (which should be maintained), and higher values correspond to the sidelobe levels (which should be suppressed). This can be efficiently used to assign appropriate weighting coefficients to the DAS beamformed data to enhance the image resolution while the information of the background speckle is also preserved. To this end, the absolute value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{Sub}(n)$$\end{document} is considered to keep the amplitude information. Then, it is normalized to keep the weighting coefficient in the range of [0, 1]:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} a(n)=\mathbb {N}\left[ |y_{Sub}(n)|\right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The resulting factor is denoted as a(n), as shown in the above equation. According to the earlier explanations, it can be predicted that the appropriate weighting coefficient is obtained from the reciprocal of the factor a(n). One should note that it is desired to apply a thresholding process prior to the reciprocal process; as mentioned before, lower values contain the information of point targets’ mainlobe and background speckle. To put it simply, the information corresponding to point targets and background speckle falls within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,\tau _0]$$\end{document}. By considering a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document}, it can be guaranteed that the information of the point targets and background speckle remains amplified after the reciprocal process of a(n). Therefore, the thresholding process is applied as below:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} a^{\prime }(n)=\text {min}(a(n),\tau _0) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To achieve a better resolution in deep regions, it is proposed to adjust an adaptive threshold value; the intensity of the desired signal, which is backscattered from deeper areas, is lower compared to the one that is backscattered from the shallower areas. In particular, the received signal corresponding to a point target in a deeper imaging region has a lower amplitude compared to the one that is located in a shallower region. This leads to the amplitude of the on-axis portion of the signal being close to the amplitude of the off-axis component. By considering a constant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document} for all imaging depths, off-axis components will also pass the threshold value, and therefore, the width of the point targets will be increased in the reconstructed image, that is, the image resolution will be degraded at deeper regions. The key point is that by reducing the threshold value at deeper regions, the on-axis component of the signal will be better separated from the off-axis component, which considerably prevents resolution degradation. To this end, the imaging region is proposed to be divided into P parts in the depth direction, and for each part, the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \tau =\tau _0/P$$\end{document} is subtracted from the current threshold value:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \tau _p = \tau _{p-1}-(p-1)\Delta \tau \\ \Delta \tau =\frac{\tau _0}{P} \\ \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Therefore, depending on the imaging depth, the threshold value is reduced proportionally. The initial threshold value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document} depends on the data type and should be adjusted experimentally. In general, the greater the value of the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document}, the better the performance of the proposed method in terms of background speckle preservation. However, beyond a certain value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document}, where the proposed method reaches its best performance in terms of speckle preservation and contrast improvement, further increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document} no longer leads to additional contrast improvement. Meanwhile, the image resolution degrades as the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document} increases. Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _0$$\end{document} should not be initialized with a high value. Experimentally, it has been found that an initial value of 0.2 can be considered an appropriate upper bound for this parameter. This observation is supported by the evaluations presented in Section Results. Also, the value of the parameter P depends on the depth of the imaging tissue as well as the data type (speckle generating or non-speckle generating).

According to the above explanations, the thresholding process is updated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a^{\prime }(n)=\text {min}(a(n),\tau _p)$$\end{document}. Then, the reciprocal process is performed to achieve the proposed weighting coefficient (SC):17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} SC(n)=\frac{1}{1/\tau _p\times a^{\prime }(n)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The reconstructed image is ultimately created by applying the coefficient SC to the DAS beamformed image:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y_{Proposed}(n)=SC(n)\times |y_{DAS}(n)|. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The schematic illustrating the processing steps of the suggested method, shown in Fig. 1, complements the above explanations.Figure 1Schematic of the proposed method.

Quantitative performance indicators

To assess the image resolution, full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) is used in this study. Also, for quantitative evaluation of the images in terms of contrast, contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and generalized CNR (gCNR) metrics are used:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} CNR&=20\log _{10}\left( \frac{|\mu _i-\mu _o|}{\sqrt{\sigma _i^2+\sigma _o^2}}\right) ,\end{aligned}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} gCNR&=1-\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\min \left\{ p_{i}(x),p_{o}(x)\right\} dx, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _{i/o}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{i/o}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i/o}(x)$$\end{document} demonstrates the mean inside/outside the cyst, the standard deviation inside/outside the cyst, and the probability density function of inside/outside the cyst, respectively. Also, to assess the algorithms’ ability to preserve background speckle, the speckle signal-to-noise ratio (sSNR) metric is employed, expressed as below:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} sSNR=\frac{\mu _o}{\sigma _o}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To quantitatively evaluate speckle preservation, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test is also performed38; a homogeneous background region is selected from the beamformed envelope image, and the empirical intensity distribution is compared with the theoretical Rayleigh distribution expected for fully developed speckle. The KS statistic measures the maximum distance between the empirical and theoretical cumulative distribution functions. A smaller KS value indicates that the speckle statistics more closely follow the Rayleigh distribution, and therefore, the beamformer better preserves the natural speckle characteristics.

Discussion and conclusion

This paper suggests a low-complexity weighting coefficient to enhance the image quality. More precisely, the suggested technique improves the image resolution better than the MV and NSI methods. Also, by using the suggested SC weighting method, the background speckle information is prevented from being diminished in contrast to the NSI and DMAS algorithms. Note that the MV method is also capable of preserving the background speckle. However, the first point is that the processing load of the MV algorithm is much higher in comparison with the suggested technique. Although several approaches have been proposed to decrease the computational demands of the MV method, most of them are based on some approximations that negatively influence the algorithm’s performance. The second point is that, regardless of the background speckle preservation capability, the suggested technique achieves better resolution in comparison with the MV method. The main distinction between the suggested technique and the NSI algorithm is that the information of the background speckle is well preserved by utilizing the suggested technique. In the NSI algorithm, the bias parameter affects the performance of the algorithm in terms of speckle preservation, such that higher values for the parameter bias result in a better contrast and better speckle information preservation. However, a balance must be struck between image resolution and contrast in this algorithm. Moreover, even for higher values of the parameter bias, the ability of the NSI algorithm to preserve the speckle information cannot reach the DAS beamformer. In contrast, the suggested SC weighting method can either improve the resolution or preserve the background speckle information as well.

In the suggested technique, the subtraction of the beamformed images corresponding to two transmit sub-arrays is used to obtain the appropriate weighing coefficients. Then, an adaptive thresholding process followed by an reciprocal process is done to calculate the weighting coefficient. More precisely, in the proposed method, the transmit array is divided into two sub-arrays, and two DAS beamformed images are formed independently. The mainlobe from both sub-arrays remains highly coherent as they correspond to the same physical scatterers and propagation paths. Therefore, the two beamformed images exhibit similar spatial patterns for point targets and speckle structures. In contrast, sidelobe artifacts are more sensitive to aperture configuration and beampattern variations. Using different sub-arrays results in a more significant change in the sidelobe responses compared to the mainlobe responses. Consequently, subtracting the outputs of the two sub-arrays tends to suppress the coherent background speckle components while emphasizing regions where the beam responses differ, which are more likely associated with sidelobe artifacts. The output obtained from the subtraction process is then used to derive a weighting factor that reduces the contribution of these artifacts when applied to the conventional DAS beamformed image.

The results demonstrate that the suggested technique can successfully improve the image quality. In particular, it can be observed from the reconstructed images corresponding to the ats-wires and geabr-0 datasets demonstrated in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively, that the suggested technique better separates the point targets and improves the image resolution in comparison with other algorithms. Quantitative findings from Table 2 also show that the CNR value of the suggested technique is improved by about 2.89 dB and 2.6 dB in comparison with DMAS and NSI algorithms, respectively, for the ats-wires dataset. Also, it can be seen from the Tables 2 and 3 that the smallest FWHM value belongs to the suggested SC weighting method, confirming the superior resolution performance of the suggested technique. Furthermore, by using the suggested technique, the gCNR evaluation metric, which exhibits greater robustness to dynamic range variations compared to CNR, is similar to the one that is calculated for the DAS algorithm for the geabr-0 dataset. This evaluation metric is also improved by about 0.02, 0.44, and 0.02 compared to DMAS, NSI, and MV algorithms, respectively, by utilizing the suggested method.

To better improve the image resolution in deep imaging regions, we proposed to use an adaptive thresholding method instead of considering a constant threshold value. In the suggested technique, the threshold value decreases by increasing the imaging depth; since the amplitude of the desired signal decreases by increasing the imaging depth, a smaller threshold value is desired to be considered to successfully pass the information corresponding to point targets (and also, background speckle). Evaluations performed in Fig. 8 demonstrate the superiority of the adaptive thresholding compared to the case where a constant threshold value is considered for all imaging depths (i.e., the case where P=1 is considered). One should note that by considering a constant threshold value, the resolution of the resulting image still outperforms the MV method. Using the adaptive thresholding process provides much better resolution, even better than the NSI algorithm, as demonstrated in Fig. 7, as an example.

However, after a certain point, i.e., when the contrast as well as the speckle preservation capability of the suggested technique reaches its best, increasing the threshold value will no longer improve the image contrast, but the resolution will worsen. In particular, the CNR improvement saturates for threshold values above 0.2 (as reported in Table 4). Therefore, we recommend not to set the initial threshold value beyond 0.2, as higher values do not yield additional performance improvement. As mentioned earlier, the computational complexity of the proposed method is low enough to enable its use in real-time applications; its computational complexity is in the order of O[N], which is comparable to that of the DAS and NSI methods. Also, compared to the adaptive MV algorithm, the computational complexity of which is in the order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O[L^3]$$\end{document}, the proposed SC method requires significantly less processing time while still producing high-quality images. To evaluate the computational efficiency of the investigated methods quantitatively, the processing of each method is measured using the tic and toc functions in MATLAB. All algorithms are implemented on a PC with an Intel core i7-3630QM CPU and 8 GB memory. The measured processing times for DAS, DMAS, NSI, MV, and the proposed SC method are 27.46 sec, 46.72 sec, 36.49 sec, 1005.86 sec, and 27.51 sec, respectively, for the simulated phantom. The results show that the proposed method requires substantially lower processing time than the adaptive MV algorithm, and it is close to the one corresponding to the conventional DAS beamformer, as expected.

Finally, one should note that although the experiments in this study were conducted using the STA method, the proposed framework is not restricted to this imaging scenario. The proposed method relies on constructing two beamformed images from two different subsets of transmit data and exploiting their difference to estimate artifact-related components. Therefore, similar processing can be applied to other imaging strategies. For instance, in plane-wave or diverging-wave imaging, the emissions can be divided into two groups based on the transmit angles, and two beamformed images can be reconstructed separately and processed in the same manner described in the proposed SC algorithm. Likewise, for focused transmit imaging, the set of transmit events can be divided into two groups and processed in a similar manner. Because the proposed method mainly consists of DAS beamforming and pixel-wise operations, its computational complexity remains low and largely independent of the imaging scenario, making it compatible with high frame rate imaging approaches.
