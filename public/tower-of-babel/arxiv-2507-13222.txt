Computational-Statistical Tradeoffs from NP-hardness
Guy Blanc, Caleb Koch, Carmen Strassle, Li-Yang Tan

Abstract
A central question in computer science and statistics is whether efficient algorithms can achieve the information-theoretic limits of statistical problems. Many computational-statistical tradeoffs have been shown under average-case assumptions, but since statistical problems are average-case in nature, it has been a challenge to base them on standard worst-case assumptions. In PAC learning where such tradeoffs were first studied, the question is whether computational efficiency can come at the cost of using more samples than information-theoretically necessary. We base such tradeoffs on 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hardness and obtain: ∘ \circ Sharp computational-statistical tradeoffs assuming 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} requires exponential time: For every polynomial p ⁡ ( n ) p(n) , there is an n n -variate class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} with VC dimension 1 1 such that the sample complexity of time-efficiently learning 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is Θ ⁡ ( p ⁡ ( n ) ) . \Theta(p(n)). ∘ \circ A characterization of 𝖱𝖯 \mathsf{RP} vs. 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} in terms of learning: 𝖱𝖯 = 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{RP}=\mathsf{NP} iff every 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -enumerable class is learnable with O ⁡ ( VCdim ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) ) O(\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})) samples in polynomial time. The forward implication has been known since (Pitt and Valiant, 1988); we prove the reverse implication. Notably, all our lower bounds hold against improper learners. These are the first 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hardness results for improperly learning a subclass of polynomial-size circuits, circumventing formal barriers of Applebaum, Barak, and Xiao (2008).



1
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the relationship between two fundamental resources in learning: runtime and samples. Naturally, we would like algorithms that are both time-
and
sample-efficient. However, such algorithms have proven elusive even for simple learning problems, suggesting the existence of inherent tradeoffs between the two resources. Are there problems for which any sample-efficient algorithm must be time-inefficient? Are there problems for which more samples provably lead to faster algorithms?

The possibility of such computational-statistical tradeoffs was first raised in an early landmark paper of Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler, and Warmuth
[
BEHW89
]
. Their paper proved that the Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension
[
VC71
]
characterizes the sample complexity of PAC learning, a foundational result now known as the “Fundamental Theorem of PAC Learning”. While undoubtedly important, this characterization is crucially statistical in nature and does not take time complexity into consideration. The upper bound is realized by empirical risk minimization, a naive implementation of which involves iterating over all possible hypotheses. The authors themselves noted this and concluded their paper as follows:

[C]onsiderable further research remains to be done in this
area. In particular, there may be interesting general trade-offs between the sample
size required for learning and the computational effort required to produce a
consistent hypothesis that are yet to be discovered. These issues are important if this theory of learnability is to find useful applications.

Existing tradeoffs and their assumptions.

The first such tradeoffs were obtained by Decatur, Goldreich, and Ron
[
DGR00
]
who constructed a concept class
\mathcal{C}
with small VC dimension—and is therefore information-theoretically learnable with a small number of samples—and yet is such that, under cryptographic assumptions, a much larger number of samples is both necessary and sufficient to
time-efficiently
learn
\mathcal{C}
.

Computational-statistical tradeoffs carry both a negative and a positive message. On one hand, they say that time-efficient learning is impossible even with many more samples than information-theoretically necessary. The size of this gap corresponds to the width of the yellow region in
Figure

1
. On the other hand, they also say that just a small increase in sample complexity—the transition from the yellow to the green region in
Figure

1
—leads to substantial speedups in the runtimes of algorithms.

This research has led to a rich theory of average-case reductions and an improved understanding of restricted classes of algorithms. However, connections to worst-case complexity—and specifically
\mathsf{NP}

Is PAC learning
\mathsf{NP}
-hard?

This ties into a major effort in PAC learning that dates back to its inception: basing the intractability of learning on minimal complexity assumptions. As Valiant already observed in
[
Val84
]
, runtime lower bounds for learning follow straightforwardly from cryptographic assumptions: The very definition of a pseudorandom function
[

Such intractability results are incomparable to computational-statistical tradeoffs. See

2.2

\mathsf{NP}

A basic fact about the computational complexity of learning is that a broad swath of concept classes
can
be time- and sample-efficiently learned if
\mathsf{RP}=\mathsf{NP}
. A concept class
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-enumerable
if there is an efficient algorithm that, given access to an
\mathsf{NP}

\mathcal{C}

\mathsf{NP}
-enumerable.
Pitt and Valiant observed the following:

Fact 2.1

(
[
PV88
]
)
.

If
\mathsf{RP}=\mathsf{NP}
, every
\mathsf{NP}
-enumerable class can be learned in polynomial time with
O(\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C}))

The proof of
2.1

\mathsf{NP}
-enumerable classes if
\mathsf{RP}=\mathsf{NP}
.

As noted in
[
PV88
]
, the significance of this fact is that short of proving
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, lower bounds against
\mathsf{NP}
-enumerable classes have to be conditioned on complexity-theoretic assumptions—ideally on
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, matching
2.1
. Their paper initiated research on such lower bounds.

Representation-dependent vs. -independent hardness.

This effort has proven fruitful in the representation-
dependent
setting where the learner is restricted to output a hypothesis of a certain form (e.g. proper learning). By now, there is a large body of results showing that it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to learn a certain concept class
\mathcal{C}
with a certain hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
. See the survey
[
Fel16
]
and the textbook
[
SSBD14
, Chapter 8]
.

However, representation-dependent results leave open the possibility that the learning task at hand becomes tractable for learners that are allowed to return more expressive hypotheses.

In fact, many representation-dependent hardness results are shown for problems that
can

Much of the power of learning algorithms comes from their ability to return any hypothesis of their choosing, and so representation-

Basing the hardness of representation-independent learning on

[

Learnability beyond polynomial-size circuits.

The PAC model requires learners to output an efficient hypothesis, i.e. a polynomial-size circuit. Without this restriction, every concept class is time- and sample-efficiently learnable by offloading all the computation to the hypothesis (see e.g. Exercise 2.4 in
[
KV94b
]
). Given this restriction, Schapire
[
Sch90
]
used boosting to show the impossibility of learning any strict
super
class of polynomial-size circuits. For example, if
\mathsf{NP}/\mathsf{poly}\neq\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{poly}
then
\mathsf{NP}/\mathsf{poly}
is not learnable.

The focus of much of learning theory, including our work and the
[
ABX08
]
barrier, has therefore been on learning
sub
classes of polynomial-size circuits. In this setting, lower bounds are more difficult to establish. While Schapire’s result is representational in nature, concerning the impossibility of representing a superclass of polynomial-size circuits as polynomial-size circuits, our result can no longer rely on representational impossibility. We are instead concerned with the computational difficulty of finding a good hypothesis even with the promise that one exists.

2.3
Extensions to other settings

Our proof extends to give analogous tradeoffs in other well-studied settings.

Uniform-distribution learning.

The focus of our exposition is on Valiant’s original model of distribution-free PAC learning, where learners are expected to succeed with respect to all distributions. However, our construction and proof can be modified so that they also give tradeoffs for
uniform-distribution
PAC learning. In fact, in the modified construction the upper bounds are witnessed by learners that succeed with respect to all distributions, and the lower bound holds even if the learner only has to succeed with respect to the uniform distribution. See
Theorem

3
.

There is a sense in which uniform-distribution learning is “more average-case” in nature than distribution-free learning. This makes lower bounds, especially those based on worst-case assumptions, more difficult to prove. In the distribution-free setting hardness reductions can (and often) produce distributions that place their mass on a few carefully chosen inputs, but the uniform-distribution setting does not admit this degree of freedom.

Online learning.

Two of the most standard theoretical models for supervised learning are the PAC model (batch learning) and Littlestone’s mistake bound model (online learning)
[
Lit88
]
. We also give analogous tradeoffs for the latter, where Littlestone dimension takes the place of VC dimension and mistake bounds take the place of sample complexity. See
Theorem

4
.

There has long been a focus on time-efficient online algorithms with optimal mistake bounds. Indeed, there are two parts to Littlestone’s original paper: the first proves that Littlestone dimension characterizes the optimal mistake bound
if runtime is not a concern
(exactly analogously with VC dimension and sample complexity), and the second gives a time-efficient algorithm,
Winnow
, that achieves the optimal mistake bound for simple concept classes. Our work gives the first example of a concept class that, assuming
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, does not admit any such algorithm.

3
Technical Overview

Our approach is simple. For each language
L\in\mathsf{NP}
, we define a learning problem that inherits its time vs. sample complexity tradeoffs from the
time vs. nondeterminism
tradeoffs for
L
. Crucial to this connection is a view of nondeterminism as a quantitative resource
[
KF77
,
GLM96
]
. While conjectures such as
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
and randomized ETH concern the distinction between algorithms with no access to nondeterminism versus those with unlimited access to nondeterminism, we will be concerned with the finer-grained distinction between algorithms with access to a few bits of nondeterminism versus those with access to more.

Time vs. nondeterminism tradeoffs for
\mathrm{SAT}
.

For concreteness, consider
L=\mathrm{SAT}
. Instances of
\mathrm{SAT}
over
n
variables can be decided in polynomial time with
n
bits of nondeterminism: these
n
bits specify a satisfying assignment. The conjecture that
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
posits that the time complexity of
\mathrm{SAT}
is superpolynomial for (randomized) algorithms with no access nondeterminism, and similarly, randomized ETH posits that it is exponential for such algorithms.

What about algorithms with access to
b(n)
bits of nondeterminism for
0<b(n)<n
? Surely giving algorithms access to more bits of nondeterminism can only lead to faster runtimes, but what is the rate of speedups? In other words, we are interested in how the quantity:

\mathrm{SAT}(b(n))\coloneqq\text{Time complexity of $\mathrm{SAT}$ given $b(n)$ bits of nondeterminism}

behaves as
b(n)
ranges from
0
to
n
.
We call this the
time vs. nondeterminism tradeoff curve
for
\mathrm{SAT}
.

3.1
A learning problem based on
\mathrm{SAT}

We now define a learning problem that inherits its time vs. sample complexity tradeoffs from the curve
\mathrm{SAT}(b(n))

\phi

\mathrm{SAT}
over
n

\mathrm{Sol}(\phi)\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\phi

f_{\phi}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{Sol}(\phi))_{i}&\text{if $x=(\phi,i)$}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
is the encoding function of an efficient error correcting code with constant rate and distance. Let us refer to inputs
x
whose prefix match
\phi
(i.e.
x=(\phi,i)
for some
i

useful
and all other inputs as
useless

f_{\phi}
reveals a coordinate of
\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{Sol}(\phi))
on useful inputs and always outputs
0

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}
and its basic properties.

Now consider the concept class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}
comprising of
f_{\phi}
for all instances
\phi

\mathrm{SAT}

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}

1(i)
and
1(iii)

\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}})=1

by brute force, thereby learning

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}
is time-efficiently learnable with
O(n)
samples. This is because with that many samples, the learner will have seen most coordinates of
\mathrm{Enc}(\mathrm{Sol}(\phi))
and can therefore efficiently produce an accurate hypothesis. More work is needed to make these proofs formal (the last sentence for example assumes that the distribution over examples places significant weight on useful ones), but this description captures the essential ideas.

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}
is therefore learnable in exponential time with

samples. As in the case of

above, we are now interested in how the quantity

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}(s(n))\coloneqq\text{Time complexity of learning $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SAT}}$ with $s(n)$ samples.}

s(n)
ranges from

Remark 3.2

3.3
Details of the
[
ABX08
]
barrier and why we evade it

[
ABX08
]
considered
nonadaptive Turing reductions
from an
\mathsf{NP}
-hard language
L
to the learning of a concept class
\mathcal{C}
. These are reductions that map an instance
z
to multiple, say
k
, learning instances such that the hypotheses for these instances allow one to efficiently determine if
z\in L
. In more detail, they considered reductions that produce generators
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
of distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}
. On a random seed
\bm{r}
, the
i
-th generator
G_{i}(\bm{r})
outputs a labeled example
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. We associate
G_{i}
with the function
f_{i}
whose labeled examples it generates, and the corresponding learning task is to output an accurate hypothesis
h_{i}
for
f_{i}
if
f_{i}\in\mathcal{C}.
The reduction is allowed to produce
G_{i}
’s whose corresponding
f_{i}
is not in
\mathcal{C}
, but in this case the learner is off the hook and there are no requirements on its performance.
[
ABX08
]
further required the reduction to decide if
z\in L
by accessing the
h_{i}
’s only as blackboxes. They proved that such a nonadaptive blackbox Turing reduction can be used to collapse the polynomial hierarchy to the second level. Since the polynomial hierarchy is believed to be infinite, this suggests that such a reduction does not exist.
2
2

2

[
ABX08
]
also established barriers for more general types of reductions (constant-round Turing reductions) and showed that their barriers even hold in the setting of agnostic learning (where
f_{i}
only has to be close to
\mathcal{C}
), but it is this result that is most relevant to us.

Our proof of
Theorem

1
can be cast as a nonadaptive blackbox Turing reduction and therefore falls within
[
ABX08
]
’s framework. However, we evade their barrier because our reduction only works for learners that access a certain number of samples from the
G_{i}
’s. Our reduction actually produces
G_{i}
’s for which
none
of the corresponding
f_{i}
’s belong to
\mathcal{C}
. Crucially, though, since we are dealing with learners with a bound
m
on their sample complexity, it suffices for us to show that we are likely to “get lucky” in the sense of having one of the
G_{i}
’s produce a batch of
m
labeled examples that happen to coincide with some function in
\mathcal{C}
. This guarantee no longer holds, and consequently our overall reduction breaks, for learners that are allowed
\gg m
samples—necessarily so, as otherwise the
[
ABX08
]
barrier would apply.

4
Discussion and Future Work

Understanding the relative strengths of cryptographic, average-case, and worst-case hardness assumptions, the subject of Impagliazzo’s Five Worlds survey
[
Imp95
]
, is a central goal of complexity theory. Pinning down the minimal assumptions necessary for computational lower bounds in learning has been challenging because there is
both
a worst-case and an average-case aspect to the PAC model: the learner has to succeed with respect to a worst-case concept in the concept class, and yet its hypothesis only has to achieve high accuracy with respect to a distribution over examples.

It has long been noted
[
IL90
,
BFKL93
]
that cryptographic assumptions, which are fundamentally average-case in nature, yield learning lower bounds that are “stronger than necessary”: not only do they show that a
worst-case
concept is hard to learn, they even give a
distribution
over concepts under which learning is average-case hard. Quoting
[
BFKL93
]
, “This state of affairs naturally leads one to wonder
if intractability results for learning in fact
require

cryptographic assumptions.” On the other hand, as formalized in
[
ABX08
]
, the distribution over examples makes it challenging to base learning lower bounds on worst-case assumptions.

Our work makes progress towards resolving this tension by showing that it
is
possible to obtain computational lower bounds for learning from
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness—if sample complexity is also taken into account. Sample-efficiency is as important a desideratum as time-efficiency, and so it is of interest to understand the limitations of time- and sample-efficient algorithms.
One avenue for future work is to see if our techniques could apply to concept classes with long-conjectured computational-statistical tradeoffs (e.g. decision lists
[
Blu90
]
). Another is to understand if the same is true for the many other settings, beyond PAC learning, in which such tradeoffs are being studied.

5.2.1

5.2.2
Complexity classes involving nondeterminism

Definition 5.6

(Nondeterministic time and nondeterministic time with bounded certificate size)

.

Definition 5.7

(

A language

and uses proofs of length

from Merlin. Formally, an

protocol is a Turing machine

(i)

(ii)

An important difference between

\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon^{\star}
using
\delta p(n)
samples.

(iii)

There is an algorithm that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
whose sample complexity and runtime are both
O(p(n)/\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

We apply
Theorem

2
with
p(n)
and
m(n)=\delta p(n)
for a small constant
\delta>0
specified later. To prove the second part of the corollary, suppose for contradiction that there is a
2^{\delta p(n)}
-time algorithm that can learn
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon^{\star}
using
\delta p(n)
samples. Then,
Theorem

2
implies that
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n))\subseteq\mathsf{RTIME}(2^{O(\delta p(n))})
which, for sufficiently small
\delta
, contradicts our randomized time lower bound for
L
.
∎

9
Extensions to other settings

9.1
Uniform Distribution

In this section, we extend
Theorem

2
to hold for
uniform-distribution
learning.

Theorem 3

(Extension of
Theorem

2
to the uniform distribution)
.

For every time constructible growth function
p(n)\geq n
, there is a concept class
\mathcal{C}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that the following holds.

(i)

We have
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})\leq n
and
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n)\log p(n),p(n))
-enumerable. There is an algorithm that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
using
O(n/\varepsilon)
samples in time
2^{O(p(n))}
.

(ii)

Let
\varepsilon^{\star}
be the constant in
Definition

5.8
. If there is an
m(n)
-sample, time-
t(n)
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon^{\star}/100

over the uniform distribution
, then

\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n))\subseteq\mathsf{RTIME}\left(2^{O(m(n))}t(n)\log t(n)\mathrm{poly}(p(n))\right).

(iii)

There is an algorithm that for every
\varepsilon>0
learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
whose sample complexity and runtime are both
O(p(n)/\varepsilon)
.

For example, under the assumption that there is a language in
\mathsf{NTIME}(n^{100})
that requires
2^{\Omega(n^{100})}
time on randomized Turing machines,
Theorem

3
gives a concept class that,

(i)

Can be learned inefficiently with
O(n)
samples, over any distribution.

(ii)

Cannot be learned efficiently with
c_{1}\cdot n^{100}
samples for an appropriate constant
c_{1}
even over just the uniform distribution.

(iii)

Can be learned efficiently with
c_{2}\cdot n^{100}
samples for appropriate
c_{2}>c_{1}
over any distribution.

The main difference between
Theorem

3
and
Theorem

2
is that the collapse in part (ii) of
Theorem

3
holds even if the algorithm only learns
\mathcal{C}
over the uniform distribution. This comes at the cost of increasing the VC dimension of the concept class.

Recall that in the proof of
Theorem

2
, the concepts in
\mathcal{C}
are
sparse
. Every concept
c\in\mathcal{C}
is highly biased toward
0
. This makes it easy to learn
\mathcal{C}
over the uniform distribution with
0
samples. So, to achieve the collapse in
Theorem

3
for the uniform distribution, we will have to modify the concept class. Additionally, the concepts
c\in\mathcal{C}
from
Theorem

2
have some inputs which are “useless” and some which are “useful” (recall
Definition

6.1
). The distribution we construct to prove part (ii) of
Theorem

2
is uniform over useful examples. To generalize to the uniform distribution, we will want every example to provide the same amount of information as all the others. Given these considerations, we define the following variant of the concept class in
Definition

6.1
.

Definition 9.1

(Definition of the concept class
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
)
.

Let
L\in\mathsf{NTIME}(t(n),p(n))
for growth functions
t(n),p(n)\geq n
and let
V
be a verifier for
L
running in time
t(n)
whose certificate size is bounded by
p(n)
. Let
(\mathrm{Enc},\mathrm{Dec})
be the error-correcting code from
Definition

5.8
with rate
1/c
for constant
c>1
. For
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define a concept
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}:\{0,1\}^{\log(cp(n))+n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows. If
z\not\in L
, then
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
is the constant
0
function. Otherwise, let
w^{\star}\in\{0,1\}^{p(n)}
be the lexicographically first certificate such that
V(z,w^{\star})=1
and define
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
as

\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}(i,x)=\mathrm{Enc}\left(w^{\star}\right)_{i}

where
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log(cp(n))}
is interpreted as an integer index in the range
\{1,\ldots,cp(n)\}
. Finally, the concept class
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
is defined to be the set of all
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
for
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

It is straightforward to verify the analogues of
6.2
,
Corollaries

6.8
and
6.3
for the class
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
:

(i)

There are at most
2^{n}
distinct concepts
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
and so
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{U}_{L,V})\leq n
by
5.3
. Also, following the proof of
6.3
,
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
is
\mathsf{NTIME}(t(n),p(n))
-enumerable. Therefore, by
5.4
, there is an algorithm that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
using
O(n/\varepsilon)
samples in time
2^{O(p(n))}\cdot O(t(n))
.

(ii)

Each concept
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
is a
\log(cp(n))
-junta over the first
\log(cp(n))
bits of input. In particular, the junta is trivially
O(p(n))
-sparse and therefore can be learned to error
\varepsilon
via
6.7
using time and sample complexity
O(p(n)/\varepsilon)
. This gives an
\varepsilon
-error hypothesis over the marginal distribution of
\bm{i}
where
(\bm{i},\bm{x})\sim\mathcal{D}
. By evaluating the junta hypothesis on the first
\log(cp(n))
bits of an input, we obtain an
\varepsilon
-error hypothesis for
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
over
\mathcal{D}
.

It remains to prove the extension of
Corollary

7.3
to learning algorithms for
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
over the uniform distribution.

Claim 9.2

(Uniform distribution extension of
Corollary

7.3
)
.

Let
L\in\mathsf{NTIME}(t(n),p(n))
, let
V
be a time-
O(t(n))
verifier using size-
O(p(n))
certificates, and let
\varepsilon^{\star}
be the constant from
Definition

5.8
. If there is a algorithm for learning
\mathcal{U}_{L,V}
over the uniform distribution which uses
m(n)
samples, runs in time
T(n)
, and outputs a hypothesis with error
\varepsilon^{\star}/100
, then we have that
L\in\mathsf{RTIME}(2^{O(m(n))}T(n)\log T(n)\mathrm{poly}(t(n)))
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the learner for
\mathcal{C}_{L,V}
. Let
\varepsilon^{\star}>0
be the constant from
Definition

5.8
which represents the fraction of errors our decoding algorithm can decode. Following the proof of
Lemma

7.2
, we first design a randomized
\mathsf{AM}
protocol
M
for
L
. On input
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
M
does the following.

(i)

Draw
m=m(n)
uniform random examples
\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(m)}
where
\bm{y}^{(j)}=(\bm{i}^{(j)},\bm{x}^{(j)})
.

(ii)

Ask Merlin for the
m
labels,
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
, of the examples
\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(m)}
.

(iii)

Run the learner
A
on the sample
\bm{S}=\{(\bm{x}^{(1)},w_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}^{(m)},w_{m})\}
with error parameter
\varepsilon/100
and obtain a hypothesis
\textsc{Hyp}:\{0,1\}^{n+\log(cp(n))}\to\{0,1\}
.

(iv)

Pick a uniform random
\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
and query
Hyp
on all points
(i,\bm{x})
for
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log(cp(n))}
to obtain a string
y\in\{0,1\}^{cp(n)}
which is a noisy version of
\mathrm{Enc}(w^{\star})
.

(v)

Apply the decoding algorithm to
y
to obtain
\tilde{w}=\mathrm{Dec}(y)
.

(vi)

Run the verifier
V
on
z
using certificate
\tilde{w}
and output the result, i.e. output
V(z,\tilde{w})
.

Runtime.

The learner
A
runs in time
T(n)
and therefore the hypothesis that it outputs can simulated on a query in time
O(T(n)\log T(n))
. So obtaining
y
takes time
O(p(n)T(n)\log T(n))
. The decoding algorithm runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(p(n))
. Finally, running the verifier
V
takes time
t(n)
. So the overall runtime of
M
is
O(p(n)T(n)\log T(n)+\mathrm{poly}(p(n))+t(n))=O(T(n)\log T(n)\mathrm{poly}(t(n)))
since
p(n)\leq t(n)
.

Correctness.

We prove the completeness and soundness of this protocol separately.

Completeness.

If
z\in L
, then for every choice of examples
y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(m)}
, Merlin can respond with the true labels given by
\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}
. The sample
\bm{S}
is then a size-
m
sample from the uniform distribution and with probability
0.99
, the learner outputs a hypothesis
Hyp
satisfying
\mathrm{error}(\textsc{Hyp},\textsc{UnifCert}_{z})\leq\varepsilon^{\star}/100
. We can rewrite this error as

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left[\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\{0,1\}^{\log(cp(n))}}[\textsc{Hyp}(\bm{i},\bm{x})\neq\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}(\bm{i},\bm{x})]\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{100}

which, by Markov’s inequality, implies that with probability at least
0.99
over a random
\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
, we have

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\{0,1\}^{\log(cp(n))}}[\textsc{Hyp}(\bm{i},\bm{x})\neq\textsc{UnifCert}_{z}(\bm{i},\bm{x})]\leq\varepsilon^{\star}.

Therefore, with high probability, the string
y
has at most
\varepsilon^{\star}\cdot cp(n)
errors relative to
\mathrm{Enc}(w^{\star})
. The decoder then returns
w^{\star}
. Finally, since
w^{\star}
is a certificate for
z
, the output of
M
is
1=V(z,w^{\star})
.

Soundness.

If
z\not\in L
, then for every certificate
\tilde{w}
,
V(z,\tilde{w})=0
. Therefore,
M
always outputs the correct answer regardless of Merlin’s responses.

Since
M
is an
\mathsf{AM}
protocol for
L
with perfect soundness, uses
m(n)
bits of nondeterminism, and runs in time
O(T(n)\log T(n)\mathrm{poly}(t(n)))
, then by
Proposition

7.4
, we get
L\in\mathsf{RTIME}(2^{O(m(n))}T(n)\log T(n)\mathrm{poly}(t(n)))
as desired.
∎

We can now combine our observations to give a proof of
Theorem

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

This proof is identical to that of
Theorem

2
except that we replace the concept class
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
with
\mathcal{U}^{\star}\coloneqq\mathcal{U}_{L^{\star},V^{\star}}
where
L^{\star}
and
V^{\star}
are as in
Theorem

2
. As we have already observed,
\mathcal{U}^{\star}
has
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{U}^{\star})\leq n
and is
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n)\log p(n),p(n))
-enumerable and there is an algorithm that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
using
O(n/\varepsilon)
samples in time
2^{O(p(n))}
. Also, it can be learned to error
\varepsilon
over any distribution with time and sample complexity
O(p(n)/\varepsilon)
. Finally, point (ii) of the theorem follows from
9.2
and the fact that
L^{\star}
is
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n))
-hard.
∎

9.2
Online Learning

In this section, we extend
Theorem

2
to the setting of online learning. An efficient online learner is one that must label examples efficiently; in other words, we bound the algorithm’s runtime for each round of online learning. This quantity is analogous to the overall time complexity of a PAC learning algorithm.
Similarly, the mistake bound in online learning is analogous to sample complexity in the PAC model. Thus, the analogue of time-sample tradeoffs in the PAC model is time-mistake tradeoffs in the online learning model.

Definition 9.3

(Littlestone dimension)
.

The
Littlestone dimension
of a concept class
\mathcal{C}
is the largest number
M
such that every online learning algorithm for
\mathcal{C}
must make at least
M
mistakes. We denote this quantity
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C})
.

The standard definition of Littlestone dimension is combinatorial in nature. However, we only use the optimal mistake bound definition and it is a well known fact that the two are equivalent
[
Lit88
]
.

Theorem 4

(
Theorem

2
for Online Learning)
.

For every time constructible growth function
p(n)\geq n
, there is a concept class
\mathcal{C}
such that the following holds.

(i)

We have
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C})=1
and
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n)\log p(n),p(n))
-enumerable. Consequently, there is an algorithm that online-learns
\mathcal{C}
with at most 1 mistake and takes time
2^{O(p(n))}
in each round.

(ii)

If there is an online learning algorithm that makes at most
m(n)
mistakes and takes time
t(n)
in each round, then
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n))\subseteq\mathsf{RTIME}\left(2^{O(m(n))}T(n)\log(T(n))\mathrm{poly}(p(n))\right)
where
T(n)=t(n)m(n)\log(m(n))
.

(iii)

There is an algorithm that online-learns
\mathcal{C}
with at most
O(p(n))
mistakes and takes time at most
O(n\log(p(n))+\log^{2}(p(n))
in each round.

Note that when setting parameters, we generally have
m(n)=\log(t(n))
. Therefore,
Item

4(ii)
and
Item

2(ii)
achieve the same parameters save extra
\log
-factors. Since we set
t(n)
to be polynomial or even exponential in
n
to achieve
Theorem

2
’s relevant corollaries, this log factor can safely be ignored.

Ingredients for the proof of
Theorem

4
.

To prove
Theorem

4
, we will use the same concept class as in the proof of
Theorem

2
(see
Definition

6.1
). We must prove the two upper bounds
Item

4(i)
and
Item

4(iii)
essentially from scratch. However, the analogous bounds in the PAC setting,
Item

2(i)
and
Item

2(iii)
, were relatively easy to show, and the proofs are even simpler in the online model. The two claims
9.4
and
9.5
will establish these two upper bounds.

On the other hand, we can use the more complicated lower bound
Item

2(ii)
as a black box to establish
Item

4(ii)
. This follows from a classical result
9.7

[
Lit89
]

that online learning is at least as hard as PAC learning.

Claim 9.4

(
6.2
for Online Learning)
.

Let
L\in\mathsf{NTIME}(t(n),p(n))
and let
V
be a time-
O(t(n))
verifier for
L
using size-
O(p(n))
certificates. Then,
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C}_{L,V})=1
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C})\geq\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})
for all concept classes
\mathcal{C}
, it follows from
6.2
that
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C}_{L,V})\geq\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C}_{L,V})\geq 1
.
To show that
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C}_{L,V})\leq 1
, we provide an algorithm
A
that makes no more than 1 mistake.
A
labels all examples 0 until the adversary labels an example
(z,i)
with 1.
As soon as this happens,
A
makes a single mistake. However, the only concept in
\mathcal{C}_{L,V}
consistent with this labeling is
\textsc{Cert}_{z}(x)
.
A
runs
V(z,w)
with all possible
w\in\{0,1\}^{O(p(n))}
to determine the lexicographically first
w^{*}
such
V(z,w^{*})=1
. With knowledge of
w^{*}
, it is trivial to determine the labeling of all future examples. Therefore,
A
makes no further mistakes.
∎

Claim 9.5

(
6.7
for Online Learning)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that every function
f\in\mathcal{C}
is
p(n)
-sparse. Then there is an algorithm that online-learns
\mathcal{C}
with at most
p(n)
mistakes and takes time at most
O(n\log(p(n)))
in each round.

Proof.

We provide an algorithm
A
that achieves these efficiency guarantees.

A
works by keeping a sorted list
\ell
of all examples that have been labeled 1. Upon receiving a new example
x
,
A
checks if
x\in\ell
. If it is,
A
returns the label 1, if not it returns the label 0. If
A
errs, learning that
x
is labeled 1, it will update
\ell
by inserting
x
in the relevant position.

We now analyze
A
’s runtime and mistake bound.
Each round,
A
must search and potentially insert into a sorted list. Using a standard method like binary search, this takes time
O(n\log(p(n)))
.

Note that every time
A
makes a mistake, the length of
\ell
increases by 1. Therefore,
A
makes
\leq|\ell|
mistakes. Since
\ell
can only contain examples labeled with 1, by assumption,
|\ell|\leq p(n)
.
A
therefore achieves the desired mistake bound.
∎

By definition, each function
\textsc{Cert}_{z}\in\mathcal{C}_{L,V}
is
cp(n)
-sparse. Since
\mathcal{C}_{L,V}
has examples over
\{0,1\}^{n+\log(cp(n))}
,
9.5
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 9.6

(
Corollary

6.8
for Online Learning)
.

Let
L\in\mathsf{NTIME}(t(n),p(n))
and let
V
be a time-
O(t(n))
verifier for
L
that uses size-
O(p(n))
certificates. Then, there is an algorithm that online-learns
\mathcal{C}_{L,V}
with at most
O(p(n))
mistakes and takes time at most
O(n\log(p(n))+\log^{2}(p(n))
in each round.

Fact 9.7

(Online learners imply PAC learners
[
Lit89
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class that is online-learnable by an algorithm that makes at most
m(n)
mistakes and takes time at most
t(n)
in each round. Then, there exists an algorithm that uses
M(n)\coloneqq O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(m(n)+\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))\right)
samples and
T(n)\coloneqq O(t(n)M(n)\log(M(n)))
time and PAC-learns
\mathcal{C}
to error
\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
.

9.2.1
Putting things together: Proof of
Theorem

4

We use the same concept class
\mathcal{C}^{\star}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{L^{\star},V^{\star}}
as in the proof of
Theorem

2
. We show that it fulfills the three requirements of
Theorem

4
.

(i)

By
9.4
,
\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal{C}^{\star})=1
and by
6.3
, the class is
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n)\log(p(n)),p(n))
-enumerable. An
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n)\log(p(n)),p(n))
oracle can be simulated deterministically in time
2^{O(p(n))}
and therefore all concepts in
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
can be enumerated in time
2^{O(p(n))}
. It follows that there is an online learning algorithm that makes
\leq 1
mistake and takes time
\leq 2^{O(p(n))}
in each round.

(ii)

By
9.7
, if there is an online learner for
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
that makes at most
m(n)
mistakes and takes time at most
t(n)
in each round, then there there is an
O(m(n))
-sample, time-
O(t(n)m(n)\log(m(n)))
algorithm that PAC-learns
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
to error
\varepsilon
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
. Since
Theorem

2
holds for the concept class
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
, we can apply
Item

2(ii)
to conclude that
\mathsf{NTIME}(p(n))\subseteq\mathsf{RTIME}\left(2^{O(m(n))}T(n)\log(T(n))\mathrm{poly}(p(n))\right)
where
T(n)=t(n)m(n)\log(m(n))
as desired.

(iii)

By
Corollary

9.6
, there is an algorithm that online-learns
\mathcal{C}^{\star}
with at most
O(p(n))
mistakes and takes time at most
O(n\log(p(n))+\log^{2}(p(n))
in each round.
