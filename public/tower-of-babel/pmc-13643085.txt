Risk factors associated with moderate and severe traumatic brain injury mortality in admitted older adults: a retrospective, single-center study at a level II trauma center
Authors: Michael Sawyers, Katherine Graham, John Elliott, Jeff Hubartt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeApproximately one in four adults aged ≥65 years experiences a fall each year, and over one-third of these events require medical care. Older adults are at higher risk of complications because of comorbidities. Most prior studies have focused on incidence rather than outcomes, and few have evaluated the association between age and traumatic brain injury (TBI) mortality. This study assessed risk factors and risk-adjusted mortality and morbidity among older adults with moderate or severe TBI.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of data from a single-center trauma registry. Inclusion criteria were age ≥65 years, admission ≥3 days (2019–2024), Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) head region score ≥1, and no documented advance directive limiting care. Patients were stratified by Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) severity as moderate (GCS 9–12) or severe (GCS <9). Mortality was defined as in-hospital death or discharge to hospice (including transition to comfort care). Morbidity was defined as discharge to a skilled nursing facility or inpatient rehabilitation. Analyses included t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-tests for continuous variables, chi-square tests with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for categorical variables, and logistic regression to identify predictors.ResultsAmong 199 participants, 92 had moderate and 107 had severe TBI. Advanced airway placement was strongly associated with mortality (OR, 5.7; 95% CI, 2.3–14.4; P<0.001). Higher AIS head region scores predicted morbidity (OR, 1.9; 95% CI, 1.4–2.7; P<0.001). In the moderate TBI cohort, diabetes and advanced airway placement were significant predictors of mortality, and longer length of stay was associated with increased morbidity. In the severe TBI cohort, age and ventilator days predicted mortality, whereas activation level and length of stay predicted morbidity.ConclusionsPredictors of outcomes differed by TBI severity, underscoring the importance of comorbidities, airway management, and hospitalization-related factors in older adults.

INTRODUCTION

Background

Patients who sustain a head injury may develop a traumatic brain injury (TBI), which is classified as mild, moderate, or severe. Classification depends on injury severity and associated symptoms, including clinical deterioration within 72 hours after injury or the identification of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) [1]. TBIs encompass any physical insult to the head resulting from traumatic events such as falls, motor vehicle crashes, or penetrating injuries [2]. These injuries can be life-altering for patients and caregivers, limit functional status, and contribute to a cascade of chronic conditions, particularly among older adults [3]. The American College of Surgeons (ACS) defines individuals aged ≥65 years as “geriatric” [4]. The objective of this single-center retrospective review was to assess risk factors and risk-adjusted mortality and morbidity among patients aged ≥65 years who sustained moderate or severe TBI.

Most existing studies emphasize incidence rather than mortality and morbidity outcomes and provide a limited assessment of the potential effects of age on TBI mortality [5]. Consequently, an evidence gap remains regarding risk factors associated with mortality among hospitalized adults aged ≥65 years. Older adults are at increased risk of in-hospital complications related to polypharmacy, physiologic vulnerability, and chronic comorbidities [6]. The first 72 hours have been identified as a key period for mortality among patients with ICH, with a substantial proportion of adverse events occurring during this interval; however, fewer studies have evaluated factors associated with mortality or morbidity beyond 72 hours in patients with moderate or severe TBI [6,7].

Objectives

The objectives were to describe mortality rates of patients ages 65 or older who sustain a moderate (GCS 9–12) or severe (GCS <9) TBI and are admitted at least 3 calendar days after injury. To determine the risk factors associated with moderate (GCS 9–12) or severe (GCS <9) TBI mortality and morbidity based on demographics, age, prior medical history, current anticoagulant or antiplatelet agent use, coagulopathy defined as abnormal serum coagulation laboratory results.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of OhioHealth Riverside Methodist Hospital (No. FY25-146) and was conducted under a waiver of informed consent and a waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization. Because this was a retrospective review of routinely collected clinical data, risk to participants was minimal, and the waivers did not adversely affect participants’ rights or welfare. A retrospective approach was used to reduce observer and selection bias. In addition, some patients may have been unreachable, making it impractical to obtain informed consent and HIPAA authorization, particularly given the sample size.

Study design and study population

This retrospective cohort study used historical data from the hospital trauma registry to identify an initial sample of patients with an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) head region score ≥1. Eligible encounters occurred between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024, reflecting the available archived trauma registry data. The investigator reviewed and validated case lists to confirm that all encounters met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the final analytic cohort. This study was not hypothesis-driven or statistically powered; rather, it was designed to develop predictive models.

To identify the study population, we queried TraumaBase for all patients within the 6-year period who had an AIS head region score, yielding 58,952 charts. This represented the total number of patients with coded AIS head region injuries. We then applied the following inclusion criteria to define the final cohort: (1) admission for a minimum of 3 calendar days at the trauma center and (2) age ≥65 years at the time of admission [4,7]. This sample was reduced to 200 patients who met the inclusion criteria and then refined to 199 after excluding 1 patient with duplicate hospital encounters. The final cohort was stratified into moderate TBI group (n=92) and severe TBI group (n=107). Sixty-nine patients in the moderate group and 55 in the severe group were included in the morbidity analysis. Fig. 1 shows the inclusion and exclusion of the study population.

The final cohort was aggregated, and patients with a documented advance directive limiting care (do not resuscitate order) before the encounter were excluded, because mortality was not considered an outcome of interest in this context according to ACS Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) reporting guidelines. Patients were dichotomized into moderate and severe TBI groups based on the initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score: moderate TBI was defined as GCS 9–12, and severe TBI as GCS <9 [2].

Measures

Outcomes

The outcomes evaluated in this study were mortality and morbidity, analyzed separately within the moderate and severe TBI groups. Mortality was defined as in-hospital death or transition to end-of-life care (discharge to hospice). In contrast, morbidity was defined as discharge to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) or inpatient rehabilitation (IPR), which served as a proxy for limited ability to function independently at discharge [8]. Morbidity analyses were restricted to survivors from the mortality cohort (n=124). Discharge to “home” or “home with home health care” was used as a proxy for independent function without continuous nursing or therapeutic care after discharge [8,9]. A discharge disposition of “other” (e.g., disposition not available or leaving against medical advice) was coded and excluded from morbidity analyses. Predictor variables included demographic, injury, clinical, and radiographic factors and were assessed for both mortality and morbidity.

Clinical factors

AIS region scores range from 1 to 6, with higher scores indicating more severe injury and lower predicted survival [10]. A score of 6 indicates a fatal injury with no possibility of survival [10]. Postal code distance (defined as the difference between the patient’s residential postal code and the hospital postal code) was used as a proxy for travel distance. Laboratory values were collected at two time points: “arrival” (obtained at presentation) and “72 hours” (the first sample obtained ≥72 hours after arrival). Differences in laboratory values were calculated only for patients with results available at both time points.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based platform designed to support data capture for research studies [11]. Data quality procedures included the use of a standardized abstraction instrument (REDCap), validation of data-pull parameters and query terms, and review of entered data for completeness, duplication, and statistical anomalies. Demographic, clinical, and social variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. Patients with missing data for a given model were excluded from that specific analysis (complete-case analysis). For mortality comparisons within moderate and severe TBI groups, continuous variables were analyzed using two-tailed t-tests and/or Mann-Whitney U-tests, depending on distributional assumptions. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for mortality and morbidity outcomes and compared between the moderate and severe groups. Logistic regression was used to evaluate predictors of mortality and morbidity for continuous and categorical variables. Stepwise logistic regression with backward elimination used a retention threshold of P<0.1 [12].

Relevant medical conditions and histories were collected as dichotomous variables and analyzed within TBI groups (moderate vs. severe). A prior medical condition was considered present only if the diagnosis was documented before admission. Diagnoses documented after admission or with an unspecified diagnosis date were not classified as preexisting conditions. “Current smoker” status was based on the documented history of tobacco use.

RESULTS

Demographics

The moderate and severe TBI groups had similar sex distributions (male and female), and most participants were White and non-Hispanic (Table 1). The severe group had a higher proportion of participants reported as African American/Black (7.5% vs. 3.3%), although this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.634). In the severe group, most participants were married (52.3%), whereas participants in the moderate group were most commonly married (38.5%) or widowed (31.9%). Most participants in both groups were insured at hospitalization; however, insurance type (e.g., private insurance, Medicare, or multiple policies) was not differentiated. Mean age was similar between groups (78.1 years in the moderate group vs. 76.6 years in the severe group). There were no statistically significant differences between groups in medical history, including current smoking, prior myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, diabetes (type 1 or type 2), hypertension, or a primary bleeding disorder (hemophilia, von Willebrand disease, or factor V Leiden). Use of anticoagulants and/or antiplatelet agents was also similarly distributed between groups, including when stratified by medication class and concurrent use. Medication adherence was not assessed.

Clinical factors

The first component of the analysis compared the moderate and severe groups to evaluate differences in mortality and morbidity. We then assessed clinical factors, injury mechanisms, radiologic findings, and care processes (Table 2). Patients with severe TBI had 2.63-fold higher odds of in-hospital mortality after 3 hospital days than those with moderate TBI (OR, 2.63; 95% CI, 1.44–4.82; P=0.002). Head injury severity, independent of TBI group, was assessed using the maximum AIS head region score. The severe group had higher mean AIS head region (3.7) and Injury Severity Score (ISS; 18) values (P=0.004 and P=0.016, respectively). The moderate group had a higher incidence of concurrent extremity injury, which may have influenced mean ISS because of how ISS is calculated (27.2%, P=0.034) [12]. Falls were the most common mechanism: 70.7% of moderate group, compared with 50.5% in the severe group (P=0.004). The second most common mechanism was “found down,” and these patients had higher odds of severe TBI (OR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.08–3.77; P=0.026). “Found down” implies a prolonged period of unresponsiveness or immobility before presentation, which may increase the interval between injury and initiation of treatment [2,13]. Nearly all injuries were classified as blunt; one patient in the severe group sustained a penetrating injury after being impaled by a fence post during a motor vehicle crash (P=0.353).

Trauma activation level differed significantly between groups. In the severe group, 65.4% received the highest activation level (level 1) at emergency department arrival, whereas a larger proportion of the moderate group were activated as level 2 trauma or as trauma consults (level 3) (P<0.001). Severe TBI was also associated with greater initial resource use: 99.1% were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), and 92.5% required advanced airway placement (endotracheal intubation and/or subsequent tracheostomy) (both P<0.001). However, most patients in the moderate group were also admitted to the ICU (68.5%). Among patients who received an advanced airway, ventilator days were similar between groups (P=0.213).

Laboratory values at arrival and at 72 hours were evaluated to assess differences that may have been associated with outcomes. Tests included hematology, metabolic, and coagulation measures. We also calculated the change between arrival and ≥72-hour values to assess whether within-patient differences were associated with outcomes, independent of reference ranges. Hemoglobin values were similar between groups at arrival; however, significant differences were observed at 72 hours and in the calculated change. The severe group had a lower mean 72-hour hemoglobin (10.1 g/dL) and a larger mean decline (−2.3 g/dL) than the moderate group (mean, 11.9 g/dL; mean decline, −0.7 g/dL; P=0.005 and P=0.020, respectively). International normalized ratio (INR) values differed at arrival, with a higher mean INR in the severe group than in the moderate group (2.4 vs. 1.2, P=0.028).

Toxicology screening was performed for most patients in each group and was dichotomized as positive or negative. The severe group had a higher proportion of positive screens than the moderate group (41.0% vs. 23.2%, P=0.011). Central nervous system (CNS) depressants were detected in 24.3% of the severe group and 13.0% of the moderate group (P=0.044). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the screening does not differentiate illicit substance use from medications administered for treatment [13,14].

Most patients underwent initial head computed tomography (CT); only three had no documented initial head imaging. Imaging was assessed for ICH, skull fracture, and midline shift (MLS). All patients in the severe group underwent repeat imaging to assess interval change, as did all but two patients in the moderate group. Skull fracture incidence was similar between groups (9.0% in the moderate group vs. 16.2% in the severe group, P=0.136). ICH was present in 80.0% of the moderate group and 74.5% of the severe group (P=0.364), and the maximum ICH size was also similar (16.4 mm vs. 20.5 mm, P=0.101). MLS presence was similarly distributed; however, the severe group had a larger mean MLS size than the moderate group, with an absolute difference of approximately 5 mm (P=0.297 for presence, P<0.001 for size).

Two logistic regression models were developed to evaluate unadjusted predictors of mortality and morbidity, with final models derived using stepwise procedures. Advanced airway placement was the strongest predictor of mortality in the overall sample (moderate and severe), with nearly sixfold higher odds of mortality after controlling for GCS category (OR, 5.7; 95% CI, 2.3–14.4; P<0.001). The AIS head region score was the strongest predictor of morbidity after controlling for GCS category; higher AIS scores were associated with higher odds of morbidity (OR, 1.9; 95% CI, 1.4–2.7; P<0.001).

Moderate TBI

In the moderate TBI group, we compared clinical characteristics between patients with mortality and those with morbidity outcomes (Table 3).

Mortality

Patients with a prior diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes had nearly fourfold higher odds of mortality than those without diabetes (OR, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.39–10.9, P=0.008). Admission to the ICU was associated with higher in-hospital mortality (P=0.007) and a longer ICU length of stay among those who died than among survivors (P=0.001). Platelet transfusion was more common among patients who died than among survivors (30.4% vs. 8.8%, P=0.010), although the mean number of units transfused did not differ significantly (P=0.324). Overall, 31.5% of patients in the moderate group experienced a National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) event during admission; NTDB events were more frequent in patients who died than in survivors (60.9% vs. 21.7%, P<0.001).

Initiation of the mean arterial pressure (MAP) protocol for blood pressure management was associated with higher mortality in the moderate TBI group; among moderate TBI patients who experienced mortality, 95.7% were placed on MAP protocol (P=0.024). Because MAP protocol initiation may reflect injury severity and the need for aggressive management, this association should not be interpreted as causal. Notably, 79.1% of the moderate group were placed on the MAP protocol overall. Neurosurgical intervention (craniotomy or craniectomy, or placement of an intracranial drain or monitor) was associated with nearly fourfold higher odds of in-hospital mortality (OR, 3.99; 95% CI, 1.40–11.4; P=0.007). Overall, 23.1% of patients in the moderate group underwent neurosurgical intervention. MLS was present in 33.3% of the moderate group and was associated with higher mortality (52.2% vs. 26.9% among survivors) and approximately threefold higher odds of mortality (OR, 2.97; 95% CI: 1.11–7.92; P=0.026).

Patients who died had lower hemoglobin levels at 72 hours (10.1 g/dL) than survivors (12.4 g/dL) and a larger mean decline from arrival to 72 hours (−2.3 g/dL; P=0.023 and P=0.010, respectively). A larger change in serum sodium was also associated with mortality (mean difference, 5.3 mEq/L; P=0.006). Coagulation indices differed between groups. Prothrombin time at 72 hours was higher in patients who died (16.3 seconds) than in survivors (13.7 seconds), indicating longer clot formation time (P=0.002) [15,16]. INR values were also higher among patients who died at both arrival and 72 hours (both 1.4; P=0.005 and P=0.002, respectively). However, these differences may have limited clinical significance because both values fall within the typical reference range [16]. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was higher in patients who died than in survivors (33.5 seconds vs. 29.7 seconds, P=0.010).

Stepwise logistic regression identified two predictors of mortality in the moderate group: prior type 1 or type 2 diabetes and advanced airway placement. The final model accounted for 15.2% of the variation in mortality (McFadden R2, P<0.001). Advanced airway placement was the stronger predictor (P<0.001) than diabetes (P=0.081).

Morbidity

Residential distance was estimated using the postal code distance as a proxy, calculated from the study hospital postal code. Patients who experienced morbidity lived farther from the hospital than those discharged home (45.1 miles vs 15.5 miles, P=0.047). Injury severity also differed: the morbidity group had higher mean maximum AIS head region and ISS values (AIS, 3.3; ISS, 15.5) than the nonmorbidity group (AIS, 1.9; ISS, 6.5; both P<0.001). Hospital utilization was greater in the morbidity group, which had a longer mean length of stay (12.8 days vs. 5.5 days) and more ICU days (6.3 days vs. 2.6 days) than the nonmorbidity group (P<0.001 and P=0.013, respectively).

Differences in neurosurgical care and imaging findings were also statistically significant. Neurosurgical intervention occurred in 21.2% of the morbidity group (craniotomy/craniectomy procedures, n=9; intracranial pressure monitor placements, n=2; P=0.044). This comparison was limited because no patients in the nonmorbidity group underwent neurosurgical intervention. ICH was present in 84.3% of patients with morbidity, and 33.0% had MLS, compared with 56.3% and 6.3%, respectively, in the nonmorbidity group (P=0.019 and P=0.033, respectively). Although ICH and MLS were more common in the morbidity group, ICH size and MLS size did not differ significantly between groups.

Stepwise logistic regression was conducted to identify potential predictors of morbidity. The initial model included nine variables and was reduced to a final model in which the postal code distance and length of stay were retained as predictors. The final model explained 28.4% of the variance in morbidity (McFadden R2, P<0.001). A greater distance was associated with a higher morbidity probability; however, this had marginal statistical significance (P=0.053). Length of stay was a stronger predictor, with longer hospitalization associated with higher morbidity probability (P=0.005). Severe TBI

In the severe TBI group, we compared clinical characteristics between patients with mortality and morbidity outcomes (Table 4).

Mortality

Patients who died in-hospital were older on average than survivors (79.4 years vs. 74.1 years, P<0.001). Survivors had a higher maximum systolic blood pressure (SBP) during hospitalization (197.4 mmHg vs. 185.6 mmHg, P=0.015). In addition, MAP protocol use was more frequent in the mortality group than in the survival group (86.0% vs. 68.4%, P=0.032). This difference may reflect injury severity and clinical course rather than a causal effect and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the method of using the single highest SBP value during hospitalization [17]. As in the moderate group, AIS head region and ISS values differed: survivors had lower mean AIS and ISS scores than patients who died (AIS: 3.4 vs. 4.0, P=0.042; ISS: 15.6 vs. 20.3, P=0.034). Concurrent neck/cervical spine injury was more common among patients who died than among survivors (20.0% vs. 7.0%, P=0.047). Most patients in the severe group received level 1 activation, including 78.0% of the mortality group and 54.4% of the survival group. The remaining survivors were distributed between level 2 activation (19.3%) and trauma consult (level 3 activation, 26.3%). Nearly all patients were admitted to the ICU (all but one), including 106 in the mortality group. Among patients who died, total length of stay was shorter (9.4 days vs. 13.4 days, P=0.041), but ICU days were greater than the survivors (7.2 days vs. 5.9 days, P=0.029). Ventilator duration was longer in the mortality group (7.2 days vs. 5.4 days, P<0.001).

ICH and MLS incidence differed significantly between patients who died and survivors; however, ICH size and MLS size did not differ significantly. ICH was present in 86.0% of the mortality group and 64.3% of survivors (P=0.010), and MLS was present in 52.0% and 30.4%, respectively (P=0.023). Although ICH size did not differ statistically, the mortality group had a larger mean ICH size than survivors (23.8 mm vs. 16.8 mm), which may be clinically relevant (P=0.196) [18].

Two differences were observed in complete blood count measures. Hemoglobin levels at 72 hours differed between groups (10.6 g/dL in the survival group vs. 9.5 g/dL in the mortality group, P=0.017), whereas arrival hemoglobin did not (12.7 g/dL vs. 12.1 g/dL, P=0.263). Platelet counts were lower in the mortality group at arrival than in the survival group (208.4×109/L vs. 235.9×109/L, P=0.026); at 72 hours, platelet counts did not differ significantly (193.1×109/L vs. 184.3×109/L, P=0.173).

Stepwise logistic regression identified two predictors of mortality in the severe group: age and ventilator days. The final model explained 27.4% of the variance (McFadden R2, P<0.001). Both predictors were significant, with higher age and longer ventilator duration associated with higher mortality probability (both P<0.001).

Morbidity

Among 107 patients with severe TBI, 55 were included in the morbidity analysis. Two differences in preadmission history were observed: current smoking and atrial fibrillation. Current smoking was more common among patients discharged home (33.3%) than among those with morbidity (11.1%); however, this finding is based on a small subgroup and should be interpreted cautiously (P=0.048). Atrial fibrillation was present in 22.2% of the morbidity group and in none of the nonmorbidity group (P=0.030).

AIS head region and ISS values were higher in the morbidity group than in the nonmorbidity group (AIS: 3.7 vs. 2.9, P=0.034; ISS: 18.2 vs. 11.4, P=0.023). Activation level also differed: 59.5% of the morbidity group received level 1 activation, 10.8% level 2, and 29.7% trauma consults, whereas the nonmorbidity group was more evenly distributed (38.9% level 1, 38.9% level 2, and 22.2% trauma consults; P=0.050). Length of stay and ICU days were greater in the morbidity group than in the nonmorbidity group (length of stay: 16.1 days vs. 7.2 days, P<0.001; ICU: 6.6 days vs. 3.9 days, P=0.011). Ventilator duration was also longer in the morbidity group (6.7 days vs. 2.6 days, P=0.031). Anticoagulant reversal was more common in the morbidity group, whereas none of the nonmorbidity group received reversal (P=0.033). MAP protocol use was also more frequent in the morbidity group (78.4% vs. 50.0%, P=0.033). ICH incidence did not differ, but MLS was more common in the morbidity group (40.5% vs. 11.8%, P=0.034). Repeat head CT was performed in 91.9% of the morbidity group and 61.1% of the nonmorbidity group. Repeat imaging, MLS presence, and MAP protocol use may reflect greater clinical severity rather than causal drivers of morbidity; larger studies are needed to evaluate these relationships within designs that prespecify treatment-related aims [18].

Laboratory differences were observed in hemoglobin at arrival and 72 hours and in arrival APTT. Hemoglobin values were lower in the morbidity group at arrival and at 72 hours than in the nonmorbidity group (at arrival: 12.1 g/dL vs. 13.9 g/dL, P=0.012; at 72 hours: 10.2 g/dL vs. 11.8 g/dL, P=0.018). Arrival APTT was higher in the morbidity group than in the nonmorbidity group (34.9 seconds vs. 19.4 seconds, P=0.021). Overall toxicology screen positivity did not differ between groups; however, CNS depressants were more frequently positive in the nonmorbidity group, which was statistically significant despite the small number of positive cases (P=0.001). NTDB events were more common in the morbidity group (P=0.005), including a higher proportion coded as “other” (P=0.007). NTDB events reflect reportable in-hospital events during admission; additional work is needed to identify which specific event types contribute most to morbidity in this population [14].

Stepwise logistic regression identified two predictors of morbidity in the severe group: trauma activation level and length of stay. The lowest activation level served as the reference category. Compared with the reference level, level 2 activation was associated with a lower probability of morbidity (P=0.021). Longer length of stay was associated with higher morbidity probability (P=0.004). The model explained 34.0% of the variance in morbidity probability (McFadden R2, P<0.001). Additional outcome details of the severe group are shown in Table 4.

DISCUSSION

The initial analyses described demographic and clinical characteristics associated with moderate and severe TBI in older adults, including preinjury factors, injury classification, treatment processes, clinical measures, and outcomes of mortality and morbidity.

Three injury classification measures differed significantly between the moderate and severe groups: mean GCS, ISS, and AIS head region scores. The GCS is a clinical assessment tool used to rapidly assess cognitive status and level of consciousness at initial evaluation [8,14]. Differences in mean GCS were expected because GCS was used to dichotomize patients into the moderate and severe groups. The severe group had higher AIS head region scores, indicating more severe head injury, which may be inversely related to GCS category [15,19]. However, a dedicated study would be needed to evaluate the strength of association between GCS and AIS head region score. The moderate group had a higher proportion of concurrent extremity injury than the severe group (27.2% vs. 15.0%). This difference may reflect survival bias introduced by the inclusion criterion of hospitalization for ≥3 days. Severe TBI is frequently accompanied by multisystem trauma and may be associated with earlier mortality than moderate TBI [15]. Survival bias may also contribute to the lower observed incidence of concurrent chest and abdominal injuries, because combined head, chest, and abdominal injuries are associated with higher mortality and shorter time from injury to death [20].

Two injury mechanisms were prominent in this cohort: falls and “found down.” Falls accounted for 59.8% of moderate and severe TBIs combined. In an American Association for the Surgery of Trauma study, falls accounted for 69.7% to 87.5% of TBIs among hospitalized older adults [21]. “Found down” represented the next highest proportion (30.7%) of TBI cases in this cohort; however, this mechanism is difficult to classify and study because events preceding treatment are often unknown.

Trauma activation level differed between groups, with most patients in the severe group receiving level 1 activation. This finding is consistent with institution-specific criteria recommending the highest activation level for patients presenting with GCS ≤8. Mortality and morbidity were then evaluated separately within the moderate and severe groups.

Mortality

Moderate TBI

Mortality-associated factors differed between the moderate and severe groups, with implications for future research and clinical care. The moderate group had a mortality rate of 25.0%, compared with 46.7% in the severe group. In the moderate group, two predictors were retained in regression analyses: preexisting diabetes and advanced airway placement. Older adults with moderate TBI and diabetes had higher odds of mortality than those without diabetes (OR, 2.5; 95% CI, 0.89–7.10; P=0.081). Evidence remains limited regarding the association between metabolic disease and TBI incidence or outcomes; however, diabetes-related pathophysiology may increase fall risk and reduce physiologic reserve for recovery [22]. Peripheral neuropathy, a common diabetes complication, reduces sensation in the extremities and has been associated with higher fall incidence [22]. In a study by Schellenberg et al. [14], diabetes was associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality among patients with mild TBI (OR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.13–1.42). That study included adults aged ≥18 years and did not stratify diabetes by age group.

Advanced airway placement was associated with substantially higher mortality odds in the moderate group (OR, 6.1; 95% CI, 2.3–16.1; P<0.001), independent of ventilator days. The proportion of patients receiving an advanced airway in this study (39.6%) was higher than that reported in another study of older adults stratified by age (18.0%–37.7%) [23]. Prior work has similarly identified advanced airway placement as a predictor of mortality (OR, 5.8) [24].

Severe TBI

In the severe group, two factors were retained as predictors of mortality: level 2 trauma activation and length of stay. Patients with level 2 activation had lower odds of mortality than those with level 1 activation (OR, 0.04; 95% CI, 0.002–0.612; P=0.021), whereas level 1 activation was not significantly associated with mortality relative to the reference category (OR, 1.088; 95% CI, 0.220–5.386; P=0.918). These differences should not be interpreted causally; they likely reflect clinical presentation and potential variability in how activation criteria are applied by emergency department clinicians [23,25]. Patients activated as level 2 may have had less severe injuries and fewer intracranial imaging findings. Length of stay was the strongest predictor, with longer hospitalization associated with higher odds of mortality (OR, 1.3; 95% CI, 1.1–1.6; P=0.004). The mean length of stay was 13.2 days, corresponding to an estimated mortality probability of 74.6% (P=0.004).

Morbidity

Moderate TBI

In the moderate group, postal code distance was a marginally significant predictor: greater distance between the hospital and the patient’s residence was associated with a higher probability of morbidity. This pattern may reflect longer transport times or added complexity in discharge planning, including coordination of home health services outside the local region and placement in a SNF or inpatient rehabilitation [19]. Higher morbidity associated with longer length of stay and more ICU days may reflect greater injury severity and the need for prolonged care [8,26]. Longer hospitalization may also reflect logistical delays related to placement and acceptance by receiving SNF/IPR facilities [26]. Patients discharged to SNF or IPR had higher AIS head region and ISS values, consistent with poorer functional outcomes in more severe head injury and with the higher incidence of ICH and MLS in this group [9,18]. Similarly, patients who underwent neurosurgical intervention had a longer length of stay and higher morbidity [8].

Severe TBI

In the severe group, patients discharged to SNF or IPR had higher AIS head region and ISS values, reflecting greater injury severity across the head and other regions [20]. Most patients with level 1 activation also experienced higher morbidity, consistent with greater injury severity at presentation as reflected by AIS and ISS values [26]. Patients with higher morbidity also had longer hospital stays and more ventilator days, which may reflect injury severity and the need for more invasive interventions to enable discharge planning and placement [27]. Longer ventilator duration may also reflect tracheostomy placement and subsequent ventilator dependence at discharge, a common care trajectory in severe TBI [8]. Higher morbidity may further relate to MLS incidence and subsequent neurosurgical interventions. Patients undergoing craniotomy or craniectomy may require prolonged, specialized nursing care because of limited postoperative function [9,28]. Lower arrival hemoglobin in the morbidity group may reflect hypovolemic anemia from blood loss [28]. The lower hemoglobin at 72 hours may reflect intraoperative blood loss or hemodilution from fluid resuscitation [28]. Although hemoglobin decreased at both time points, this finding may have limited clinical significance because values remained within the reference range and did not require immediate intervention [9].

Patients in the morbidity group had higher arrival APTT values and a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation. Elevated APTT is associated with increased bleeding risk, including medication-related coagulopathy [15]. Atrial fibrillation may confound this relationship because these patients are more likely to be receiving anticoagulant therapy [15]. Higher APTT in the morbidity group may therefore reflect anticoagulant exposure and/or more extensive intracranial bleeding and is consistent with the higher proportion receiving anticoagulant reversal agents [15].

A larger proportion of patients placed on the MAP protocol were discharged to SNF or IPR and underwent repeat head CT imaging [17]. These findings likely reflect injury severity and clinical course rather than a causal relationship with morbidity [17].

There was a statistically significant difference between groups in CNS depressant positivity, with lower morbidity among patients who screened positive. One potential explanation is the “vices paradox,” which posits that certain substance use patterns may be associated with lower mortality in some trauma populations [29]. However, this finding was not a prespecified aim of the study and should be interpreted cautiously; evaluation in larger, dedicated cohorts would be needed. Higher current smoking prevalence may also confound these findings because tobacco use commonly co-occurs with substance use [29].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the analyses relied on historical registry data, which may be affected by variability in data validity and accuracy related to abstraction practices by trauma registrars. In addition, retrospective designs can be subject to period effects across the study timeframe; however, to the best of our knowledge, no major changes in clinical practice or abstraction procedures occurred during the 6-year period. Second, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power, and findings should be interpreted cautiously. Third, the single-center design limits generalizability; however, results may be most applicable to similarly sized trauma centers with comparable patient demographics. Fourth, the small sample size and aggregation of some measures may limit clinical interpretability and contribute to wider CIs. Differences in mortality and morbidity probabilities across predictors should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The study definition of mortality aligned with the ACS TQIP definition, recognizing that not all trauma centers participate in ACS TQIP. Finally, restricting inclusion to patients hospitalized for ≥72 hours may introduce survival bias, because patients who died or were discharged earlier were not captured; this may have influenced observed injury severity, mechanisms, demographics, and outcomes. Future studies could address these limitations by increasing sample size to improve generalizability and statistical power.

Conclusions

This study aimed to assess demographic and clinical factors associated with poor outcomes among older adults with moderate and severe TBI and to serve as a hypothesis-generating analysis for future single-center work on mortality and morbidity. The analyses were designed to evaluate predictors aligned with ACS TQIP outcome definitions, which rely on retrospective data collection. Predictors of morbidity and mortality differed between the moderate and severe groups, including differences related to injury severity, coagulation measures, and hospitalization-related care processes.
