Mutation-derived lentil genotypes confer better tolerance against post-emergence herbicides
Authors: Maria Ghaffar, Muhammad Jawad Asghar, Muhammad Shahid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWeed control is a major constraint to lentil production worldwide. Although post-emergence herbicides are commonly used to manage weeds, their application can reduce crop yield. Developing herbicide-tolerant lentil varieties through different breeding methods offers an effective solution to minimize such losses. In this study, 60 diverse lentil genotypes, developed through mutation, hybridization, and a combination of both, along with three standard checks, were evaluated against eight post-emergence herbicides to identify tolerant genotypes and determine which breeding method was most effective. The genotypes were planted in an augmented design and evaluated over two growing seasons. Abiotic stress indices were calculated, and data were analyzed using standard augmented design procedures. ANOVA was performed, and multivariate analyses such as PCA, hierarchical clustering, and the multi-trait genotype–ideotype distance index (MGIDI) were used to identify herbicide-tolerant genotypes. ANOVA results showed a highly significant genotype × treatment interaction for all measured traits, indicating that genotypes responded differently to the various herbicide treatments. PCA and clustering analyses revealed that most of the high-performing genotypes were mutants, a finding further confirmed by MGIDI, highlighting the effectiveness of mutation breeding for inducing herbicide tolerance. Based on MGIDI results, the best genotypes for each herbicide were G45 (T1), G32 (T2), G41 (T3), G30 (T4 and T5), G17 (T6), G9 (T7), and G4 (T8). These genotypes represent valuable sources of herbicide tolerance and can serve as donors in breeding programs, as controls in research studies, or as candidates for variety development following further adaptation trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26602-z.Keywords: Herbicide tolerance, Lentil, Mutation induction, MGIDI, Cross-resistance, iPASTICSubject terms: Plant breeding, Plant stress responses

Introduction

Lentil is one of the oldest cultivated and important Rabi crops, cultivated extensively in Canada, Turkey, Syria, Nepal, Bangladesh, India and China for its protein (25%)1. It is highly valued in cereal-based cropping systems for its nitrogen-fixing ability, which enhances soil fertility and supports sustainable agriculture2. The crop is highlighted as significant due to its rich nutritional content, boasting ample dietary proteins, fiber, minerals, and carbohydrates3. This makes it a crucial player in tackling malnutrition, particularly in developing nations4. Furthermore, growing lentils has several benefits for crop rotation with cereal crops, including symbiotic nitrogen fixation, carbon sequestration, and pest control5. However, lentil yields are typically low, primarily due to issues in management and the plant’s sensitivity to various biotic and abiotic stresses i.e. major abiotic stresses like drought, heat, cold, and salinity, while major biotic stresses include bacterial, fungal, viral, insect attacks, weeds etc.4,6. In 2023, 5.6 million hectares of lentils were grown worldwide, yielding 7.06 million tonnes of output7. During past decade, the lentil cultivation area in Pakistan has reduced from 19 thousand hectares in 2013 to only 7.4 thousand hectares in 20237. Among many stresses, weeds competition is considered one of the most detrimental. Lentil is a fragile crop and is considered a poor competitor of weeds because of its weak seedling vigor, short height, slow growth and lack of protective canopy development8. Weeds compete with lentil for space, nutrients, moisture9. Moreover, nitrogen fixing ability of root nodules in lentil provides a nourishing environment for many competitor weeds thus adversely affecting lentil growth and yield.

Weed control is one of the most important limitations of lentil growth worldwide10. Weed infestation can lead to yield reductions in lentil crops ranging from 20 to 80%11,12 which depends on the severity of weeds infestation and the type of weeds. Critical period for weed control in lentil is 30–60 days post sowing13, therefore, weed control at an early stage is necessary to maintain good vigour and production of the crop14. Most commonly observed weeds in lentil crop are Fumaria indica, Convolvulus arvensis, Chenopodium album, C. murale, Rumex dentatus, Anagalis arvensis and Vicia sativa etc.15,16.

Given the diversity and abundance of these weed species, effective and timely management strategies are essential, as relying solely on manual removal becomes increasingly challenging with larger cultivation areas. In developed countries, the primary strategy for managing weeds in lentil farming involves the use of herbicides, a method that is also becoming more prevalent in many developing nations17. At present, a range of effective post emergence herbicides are commercially available, but the legume crops are highly sensitive to most of the herbicides due to their sensitive physiology, potential disruption of nitrogen-fixing symbiosis, and high herbicide uptake, with tolerance levels varying across species and cultivars18. Managing the sensitivity of lentils to herbicides is essential because it is challenging to select herbicides that exclusively target weeds. Developing and registering new herbicides for minor crops like lentils can be difficult due to the high costs involved, which are disproportionate to the industry’s size. A viable alternative is to focus on increasing the crop’s tolerance to existing herbicides, particularly when there is insufficient selectivity between the crop and the weeds19. Scientists have attempted seed mutagenesis to create herbicide-resistant crops because leguminous crops are not very responsive to transformation20. A variety of mutagens, such as gamma irradiation and chemical treatments like sodium azide, ethyl methanesulfonate, N-nitroso-N-methylurea, and ethylnitrosourea, are used for seeds mutagenesis in various crops. Since lentils are sensitive to broad-spectrum herbicides such as lipid inhibitors (acetyl-CoA Carboxylase enzyme), amino acid synthesis inhibitors (acetolactate synthase enzyme), and growth regulator inhibitors (phenoxyacetic acid and benzoic acid), it is necessary to screen for selective and effective herbicides14.

Mutation induction has proved a potential method for creating targeted herbicide tolerance in lentil and other crops21. A large number of genotypes can be screened to take advantage of natural variation, or variation induced through mutations can to identify highly tolerant genotypes22. Many studies have reported the identification of herbicide-tolerant mutants by selection from spontaneous mutation23,24. Herbicide tolerant mutant plants have been discovered in many crops like lentil25, wheat26 and rice27. It has been reported in other crops that the induced mutations were also efficient in the creation of herbicide-tolerant genotypes14,20,23,24,28. A common method for creating crop resistance to herbicides is seed mutagenesis followed by herbicide selective pressure29.

The first report of pesticide resistance was made by Melander30, who identified populations of San Jose scales (Quadraspidiotus perniciosus) with evolved resistance to sulfur-lime31. In the case of synthetic herbicides, resistance was first reported by32 in wild carrot (Daucus carota L.) surviving 2,4-D applications. Since then, herbicide tolerance studies are mostly done in weeds, however, in addition to weeds, the important crops can also depict tolerance to the herbicides.

Given the urgent need for sustainable weed management in lentil, this study aims to identify herbicide-tolerant and susceptible genotypes. It further evaluates the most effective genetic variability induction method for developing such tolerance, providing a pathway to reduce yield losses, lower production costs, and enhance lentil adaptability in modern cropping systems.

Experimental design & Herbicide Treatments

The experiment was conducted at Nuclear Institute for Agriculture and Biology (NIAB), Faisalabad (3103N latitude, 73003E longitudes) during Rabi season of 2020–21 & 2021–22. The experiment was laid out in augmented design where eight herbicides were applied (Table 2). The unit plot size was 4 m×0.6 m. Seeds were sown at a depth of 2 cm in soil with 10 cm plant to plants distance at the rate of 10 kg/ha. Two rows/genotype were sown during third week of November at row spacing 30 cm. The land was fertilized with recommended doses of NPK (50 kg acre−1) and SOP (25 kg acre−1), uniformly applied as a basal dose during land preparation before sowing.

The experiment comprised 08 herbicide treatments (T1–T8) along with a non-weedy control (T0: weeds removed manually with no herbicide treatment). A knapsack sprayer fitted with a flood jet nozzle was used to apply the herbicide at a spray volume of 3.8 (L/ha), ensuring uniform coverage of the target area. The spraying was done with a calibrated walking speed of 4 km/h to achieve the desired application rate. Herbicides were applied at 60 days after sowing (BBCH growth stages 19) as post-emergence. All experimental procedures and collection of plant material were conducted in accordance with institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Data collection & statistical analysis

Data on lentil survival %, yield and biomass (g/plot) was recorded. Following the standard procedure for augmented design analysis, the adjusted means for these traits were derived. The adjusted yield data from control and treatments were utilized to calculate abiotic stress tolerance indices using iPASTIC package from R33 (Table 3). Best-fit mixed effect model with keeping year as random was applied. Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite’s approximation was used to estimate degrees of freedom in mixed models and to assess the interactions between genotypes, treatments, and years. Considering the fact that the year effect was non-significant for the traits presented in this study, so the years were treated as replications. However, Genotype: Treatments interaction was highly significant for all the studied parameters.

Multivariate analysis like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation coefficients was performed for all treatments to reduce the dimensionality of data and to study the correlation among traits, respectively. To identify the tolerant and susceptible genotype per treatment, Multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) was also computed on basis of measured traits using the method proposed by Olivoto et al.34. Index was computed considering selection intensity (SI) of ~15.8% by R software using metan package35. Based on the ideotype concept, the traits were classified as high, low and medium depending on the desirability of each trait, which facilitates to define the ideotype. In the present investigation, PYR, TOL and SSI were selected as traits in which a decrease value was more desirable, while for rest of the traits increased value was desirable. The genotype with the lowest MGIDI value is closer to the ideotype and therefore presents desired values for most of the measured traits.

where MGIDI is the multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index for the ith row/genotype/treatment; ij is the score of the ith row/genotype/treatment in the j th factor (i = 1; 2; :::; g; j =1; 2; :::; f), being g and f the number of rows/genotypes/treatments and factors, respectively; and j is the j th score of the ideotype. Cluster analysis using complete Euclidean distance method was used to group the genotypes and traits to compare the response of different genotypes against different herbicides. Clustering also helped in validating the selection of genotypes on MGIDI basis. The data were statistically analysed using R software (version 4.4.2) packages; augmentedRCB, doebioresearch, Ime4, ggplot2, factoextra, clv, cluster, pheatmap, metan, corrplot and agrocolea.

Results

ANOVA-III results revealed that the genotype × treatment interaction was highly significant for all the parameters which showed that genotypes response was different against different herbicide treatments (Supp Table).

Climate effect on study

The minimum and maximum temperature during the first growing season was recorded 5 and 30.6 °C in January and April, respectively. In the second growing season, the maximum (32 °C) and minimum (7 °C) temperature were recorded in April and January, respectively (Fig. 1). During the first growing season, 12 mm of average rainfall was recorded, while in the second growing season, the average rainfall was 11 mm. Moreover, the same pattern was observed for relative humidity, on average it was 57.8 and 53.7 in first and second season, respectively. These patterns and data showed a non-significant year effect for the studied traits, as the difference in weather conditions between two growth seasons was not pronounced.

The degree of association among the traits was determined by their correlation coefficients. Pearson correlation analysis (p <0.05 and <0.01) revealed relationship among observed traits (Fig. 2). Ys was negatively correlated with TOL and PYR in all treatments, while a positive significant correlation with MP, GMP, HM, STI was observed. In T1, T2 and T7, Ys was negatively correlated with Yp. BIO was negatively correlated with Yp, TOL and PYR in most of the treatments.

Principal component analysis

The principal component analysis (PCA) was computed for all herbicide treatments separately on the data set of 11 traits of 63 lentil genotypes with aim of reducing dimensionality and see how different genotypes are responding to different treatments. For all the treatments, the first two components (PCs) with eigenvalues ≥ 1 accounted for more than 80% of the total variance (Supp Fig. 1). Since the first two PCs showed the highest proportion of variance, therefore biplots were developed using these two components. Moreover, the genotypes were colour coded in PCA to see how the genotypes with different method of genetic variance responded to different treatments. The sharp angle (<90°) means a positive correlation between the measured parameters, the obtuse angle (>90°) depicts negative, while the right angle (90°) means no correlation between the parameters. It was observed that for all treatments except T8, the genotypes that were closer to the positive desired traits were mostly mutants>hybrid-mutant>hybrids. However, the response of cultivars (standard checks) was different for each treatment. The contribution of traits to the PC1 and PC2 was variable for treatments, although the major response was that traits where higher value was desired, were positively correlated to each other and contributed more towards PC1 (Fig. 3a, b).

Discussion

Weeds are among the most significant biotic stresses affecting lentil productivity, causing yield losses of up to 80% in South Asian regions i.e. Pakistan, Nepal, India14,36,37. Due to their shallow root system, poor early vigor, and slow vegetative growth, particularly in the cool season, lentils face intense competition from weeds throughout their growth cycle38. Various weed management strategies, including mechanical and manual weeding, soil sterilization, and high seed rate density, have been employed1,39, but these approaches are often inefficient or costly40. Pre-emergence herbicides help control weeds during the early stages of crop growth; however, weeds that emerge post-crop establishment, particularly at the pre-flowering stage, pose a major challenge to lentil production41. Post-emergence herbicides such as imazethapyr and metribuzin have proven effective in managing weeds in several legume crops, including lentils14,25, chickpeas41, and soybeans42. These herbicides can control a broad spectrum of annual and parasitic weeds43, but their phytotoxicity limits their use on existing lentil genotypes. Hence, developing herbicide-tolerant lentil cultivars with stable yields under diverse conditions is essential14,25,38,44. However, to ensure their suitability for large-scale commercial farming, these cultivars must demonstrate wide adaptability, necessitating multi-environment testing to assess their yield stability45.

In our study, 60 lentil accessions were evaluated for their response towards different post-emergence herbicides. The aim was to identify genotypes that are tolerant/resistant to the individual herbicides and those which were susceptible to each herbicide. Results showed that response varied depending on the type of the herbicide, and the nature of the genotype. Based on MGIDI, the top three genotypes that showed tolerance towards each herbicide: G45>G32>G33 (T1: Bromoxynil 20%+MCPA 20%), G32>G42>G37 (T2: Metribuzin), G41>G44>G13 (T3: 15%Pendimethaline+30%Metolachlor+5% Oxyfluorfen), G30>G53>G16 (T4: Lactofen), G30>G16>G55 (T5: Oxyfluorfen). Similarly, the genotypes G17>G33>G41 (T6: Flumetsulam), G9>G32>G16 (T7: Imazethapyr) and G4>G9>G60 (T8: Metazachlor) were top performer. The variation in response may be attributed to the fact that herbicide resistance arises largely from pre-existing genetic variation within populations, with differential responses influenced by the diverse chemical groups of herbicides used in this study46. However, a number of selected genotypes were also found tolerant against more than one herbicide i.e. G41 (Fig. 6). Some of herbicides shared the herbicide-classification group, for instance, utilized. G37, G9 and G41 are common tolerant genotypes against T6 (Flumetsulam) and T7 (Imazethapyr), both belong to same group:02 (ALS inhibitors). So, we can infer that the mutations induced in these genotypes has led to their tolerance against ALS-inhibitors. Tolerant genotypes to a particular herbicide may also tolerate others within the same classification group due to a shared target site, a phenomenon known as cross-resistance47, originally reported in weed species48–50. This phenomenon was also observed in field crops like lentil51,52, mungbean53, soybean54, Lactuca serriola55. Cross-resistance arises from complex target-site and non–target-site mechanisms influenced by environmental and application timing factors56,57. By interacting with biological pathways, these factors can amplify or diminish herbicide effectiveness. To validate this hypothesis, further research is needed to identify mutations in the selected genotypes.

In order to see which method to create genetic variability produced more potentially tolerant/resistant genotypes against each herbicide, the treatments were ranked using MGIDI. Our findings showed that the mutation induction was more potent method for most of the treatments. The top performing genotypes selected per herbicide i.e. G45 (T1), G32 (T2), G41 (T3), G30 (T4 & T5), G17 (T6) and G9 (T7) were all derived using induced mutation or a combination of hybridization followed by mutation. Moreover, the genotype G1 that appeared to be susceptible against all herbicides, except T8, was a hybrid. This shows that induced mutation has great potential to prompt changes in genome that can lead to stress tolerance in legumes. Mutagenesis is a well-established breeding tool that complements existing germplasm by enhancing specific traits, particularly useful for developing herbicide-tolerant genotypes in crops with limited natural diversity for this trait58,59. Several studies support these findings, demonstrating that mutant genotypes exhibit enhanced tolerance to herbicides. In order to assess the sensitivity of lentils to post-emergence herbicides, Singh et al.60 employed mutation breeding by subjecting seeds of genotype LL1203 to gamma radiation. Metribuzin-tolerant mutants with other desirable features were identified from the M₂ generation, highlighting their breeding potential. RH44 is a imidazolinone-tolerant lentil mutant (also a registered patent) derived through EMS-induced mutagenesis. Seeds from various cultivars were treated in batches and planted, followed by whole-plant selection, resulting in the identification of the tolerant mutant RH4420. Similar studies were also done by other researchers, and they identified herbicide tolerant mutants in lentil20,21,61, chickpea22,62, lupin63, mungbean64 and other legumes.

This study identified the potential tolerant and susceptible lentil genotypes. Moreover, mutation breeding proved to be the most effective method to develop genotypes with better genetic variation, especially in crops with bottle-neck effect i.e. Lentil. The identified genotypes can be used as a source for herbicide tolerance in hybridization and other breeding techniques i.e. gene and linked-markers identification. The presence of herbicide-tolerant donor lines, along with extensive genomic resources, will facilitate the genomics-driven development of herbicide-tolerant lentil varieties in the near future. These genotypes can also be used as controls (positive and negative) in herbicides tolerance studies in lentil. In addition, the lentil accessions identified as tolerant may be introduced as a candidate variety after some more adaptation trails and studies. However, the genotypes that depicted multiple tolerance against herbicides needs to be evaluated further at the genomic level. Most of the genotypes with multiple tolerance trait are mutants, which again emphasizes the impact of mutation breeding for creation of new desirable variations in crops.
