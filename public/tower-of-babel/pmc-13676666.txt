A deep neural network-enhanced multi-scale coupled model of patient-specific acoustic wave propagation for mechanotransduction stimulation of healthy and osteoarthritic knee articular cartilage
Authors: Nuno A. T. C. Fernandes, Betina Hinckel, Ana leal, Óscar Carvalho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a multi-scale computational framework to quantify how externally applied acoustic waves propagate through the knee joint and induce mechanotransductive strain at the level of the chondrocyte nucleus. Patient-specific knee geometries (n = 8) were used to simulate acoustic wave transmission through layered tissues, while a representative volume element modelled the coupled solid–shell–acoustic behaviour of the chondrocyte, pericellular matrix, and extracellular matrix. Modal and frequency–response analyses identified the spheroidal s3 mode as the dominant contributor to nuclear deformation. Strain amplification was found to occur within defined frequency bands rather than at a single frequency, with healthy patients exhibiting a broader mid-frequency amplification region (50–170 kHz) and diseased patients showing a shifted low-frequency (30–85 kHz) dominance due to hypertrophy and altered stiffness. Macro-to-micro coupling demonstrated that, despite anatomical variability, consistent transmissibility trends were preserved across patients, with ideal frequencies for healthy and OA patients. Time-explicit simulations comparing linear and nonlinear acoustic formulations revealed negligible harmonic generation at the applied pressure levels, indicating that linear acoustics adequately describe propagation within the mechanotransductive strain range considered. Overall, the results demonstrate that cartilage stimulation is frequency-band dependent and disease-sensitive, and they provide a mechanistically grounded basis for optimizing ultrasound excitation parameters based on cellular-scale mechanics rather than empirical frequency selection.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most pressing public-health issues of our time: globally it affects over 500 million people (7% of the world population) and places massive strain on healthcare systems and societies (Leifer et al. 2022). Knee OA is a degenerative joint disease that develops when the articular cartilage and/or menisci progressively degenerate, leading to friction, inflammation, direct bone-to-bone contact within the knee joint and symptoms like pain, stiffness and reduced mobility. This process results in varying levels of pain and functional limitation, often impairing the patient’s ability to perform daily activities (). In terms of economic impact, OA’s annual cost (direct and indirect) in countries with market economies can amount to 1–2.5% of gross national product, with individual annual costs ranging from about US$700–US$15 600 (Leifer et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 1990). Despite this burden, therapeutic options remain mostly symptomatic and invasive (including joint replacement) and there is a distinct lack of truly non-invasive treatments that protect cartilage without accelerating degeneration (Fernandes et al. 2022). If demonstrated to be clinically effective, ultrasound stimulation could contribute to reducing this burden by preserving cartilage integrity, slowing disease progression, maintaining joint function, and potentially delaying the need for invasive procedures and long-term symptomatic treatment. However, the magnitude of this economic benefit remains uncertain and will require confirmation through clinical and health-economic studies.

Recent work has increasingly explored the use of ultrasound waves as a general biological stimulation therapy, and more specifically as a non-invasive mechanostimulation strategy for cartilage repair and OA. In vitro studies on both healthy and OA chondrocytes and cartilage explants have shown that ultrasound can enhance matrix synthesis (e.g., proteoglycan and type II collagen production), promote cell proliferation and migration, and suppress catabolic and inflammatory pathways (such as NF-κB, JNK, p38) in OA cells while activating anabolic signalling (PI3K/Akt, ERK) in healthy cells (Uddin et al. 2016; Oliveira et al. 2023, 2022, 2025; Korstjens et al. 2008). These findings suggest that ultrasound may convert acoustic pressure waves into mechanical strain at the cell boundary and within the pericellular matrix, thereby triggering mechanotransduction in chondrocytes. However, while the biological evidence is promising, detailed mechanistic understanding (particularly of how the mechanical stimulus is transmitted through the ECM/PCM–cell system) and the optimal ultrasound parameters remain under investigation (Oliveira et al. 2023; Sekino et al. 2018).

According to vibration and acoustic wave propagation theory, each structure exhibits characteristic resonance frequencies that depend on its geometry, boundary conditions, and material properties (Rao xxxx). When the excitation frequency approaches one of these natural frequencies, energy transfer into the structure is significantly amplified, resulting in higher local deformation and stress fields. In the context of ultrasound stimulation of chondrocytes, identifying such resonance frequencies could enable maximization of mechanical energy delivery to the cell and its substructures (e.g., plasma membrane, cytoskeleton, and nucleus). This resonance-based optimization may enhance mechanotransduction efficiency by amplifying membrane tension, ion channel activation, and intracellular strain transmission, all of which are implicated in chondrocyte biosynthetic responses (Louw et al. 2013). Therefore, performing a parametric modal analysis of the chondrocyte– extracellular matrix (ECM) system provides a physically grounded approach to determine the optimal ultrasound frequency range for therapeutic stimulation.

Despite its potential, accurately modelling ultrasound propagation and resonance within biological tissues remains a significant challenge. The highly heterogeneous and anisotropic nature of cartilage, comprising the ECM, pericellular matrix (PCM), and chondrocytes, leads to complex scattering, reflection, and refraction phenomena that affect acoustic energy transmission. Moreover, the nonlinear viscoelastic behaviour of both the solid and fluid phases introduces amplitude-dependent responses and damping effects that attenuate wave propagation (Rudenko 2022; Peng 2021). These factors make it difficult to predict how mechanical energy is distributed at the cellular level, especially under realistic geometrical and boundary conditions. Consequently, achieving a reliable numerical representation requires the coupling of acoustic–structure interaction models with nonlinear material laws and frequency-dependent damping formulations to capture the true dynamic behaviour of cartilage under ultrasound excitation (Fernandes et al. 2025a).

The objective of this study is to develop a realistic computational framework to simulate and analyse ultrasound-induced wave propagation from the skin surface to the knee cartilage. To achieve physiological accuracy, patient-specific MRI data are used to reconstruct the geometry of the knee joint, allowing the model to capture individual anatomical variation. The mechanical and acoustic properties of the biological tissues are defined using previously characterized and experimentally validated datasets (Fernandes et al. 2025a), ensuring that the numerical model reflects the real mechanical behaviour of soft tissues. A multiscale modelling framework is adopted to couple the macroscopic propagation of ultrasound through layered joint tissues with the microscopic mechanical environment surrounding chondrocytes within the cartilage. At the microscale, representative volume elements (RVEs) composed of the extracellular matrix, pericellular matrix, and chondrocyte are used to investigate local mechanical responses. Within this context, a modal analysis in the frequency domain is performed to determine the natural resonance frequencies of chondrocytes, which are hypothesized to correspond to optimal ultrasound stimulation frequencies that maximize local strain and mechanotransductive efficiency. To evaluate the influence of physiological variability at the microscale, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in conjunction with surrogate modelling based on a deep neural network (DNN). The sensitivity analysis was used to quantify the contribution of the investigated geometrical and material parameters to the variability of the predicted mechanical response, thereby identifying the parameters with the greatest influence on the numerical outputs. The most relevant variables were subsequently incorporated into the surrogate model, which enabled the parameter space to be explored at a substantially higher resolution than would be computationally feasible using repeated finite-element simulations alone. This combined approach provided a more detailed assessment of the effects of biological variability while reducing the computational cost associated with extensive parametric analyses.

To fully characterize the dynamic behaviour of the system, simulations are conducted across three complementary regimes. In the frequency domain, harmonic analysis provides steady-state responses and resonance identification; in the linear transient domain, time-dependent responses to impulsive or chirped excitations are examined; and in the nonlinear time-explicit domain, viscoelasticity, damping, and nonlinear wave propagation are incorporated to capture the realistic behaviour of cartilage and surrounding tissues. This comprehensive, experimentally grounded, and multiscale approach enables the identification of frequency ranges and excitation conditions that enhance mechanical energy transfer to the chondrocytes, offering new insights into the optimization of non-invasive ultrasound therapies for osteoarthritis.

Mechanical stimulation becomes biologically meaningful only when intracellular and intranuclear deformations exceed specific thresholds known to trigger mechanotransductive signalling. Multiple studies have quantified how much nuclear deformation is required to trigger mechanotransduction pathways. For instance, in the landmark study by Driscoll et al. found that mesenchymal stem cells subjected to a 3% global substrate stretch (1 Hz cyclic) exhibited a Lagrangian nuclear strain of approximately 5–10%, and it was clear only when the nucleus was appropriately deformed via the cytoskeleton that the mechanosensitive transcriptional co-activator YAP/TAZ mechanism translocated to the nucleus (Driscoll et al. 2015). More broadly, Kalukila et al. outlines in their review report that nuclear deformations in the range of 1–10% (and sometimes up to 30% for larger structural changes) suffice to alter chromatin conformation, nuclear pore transport dynamics and gene expression (Kalukula et al. 2022). Although the exact threshold varies with cell type, loading mode and duration, a working range is that 1–10% strain is biologically meaningful for mechanotransduction via nuclear deformation.

Therefore, the purpose of coupling the acoustic wave equations (Eqs. 1–4) with the elastic solid equations (Eqs. 6, 7) is to determine how an externally applied acoustic or mechanical wave propagating from the patient’s skin all the way through cartilage ultimately produces deformation at the nuclear scale. The fluid–solid interface conditions ensure that acoustic pressure in the ECM is transmitted into stresses within the cell body, while the Navier–Cauchy equation governs how these stresses generate compressional (P-wave) and shear (S-wave) deformation patterns inside the cytoplasm, PCM, and nucleus. In osteoarthritic tissue, the chondrocyte radius was assumed to increase as a consequence of hypertrophic changes (Chawla et al. 2022). The nucleus was taken to be approximately half the size of the cell (Chan and Marshall 2010), a proportionality reported in healthy cartilage and considered to remain consistent even under hypertrophy (Chawla et al. 2022). Although Pascarelli et al. reported enlarged and morphologically altered nuclei in osteoarthritic chondrocytes, they did not quantify nuclear and cytoplasmic volumes, and therefore do not establish preservation of the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic volume ratio (Pascarelli et al. 2015). Because no reliable measurements of plasma-membrane thickness in chondrocytes (particularly in OA patients) were identified in the literature, a standard reference value for mammalian cells was adopted (Campos Muñiz and Fernández Perrino 2023). The PCM thickness, however, was assumed to decrease in OA due to the well-documented structural degradation of this matrix (Guilak et al. 2018). Regarding chondrocyte spacing, healthy adult hyaline cartilage typically exhibits intercellular distances on the order of tens of micrometres, commonly between 40 and 80 µm depending on the depth zone (Nahian and Sapra 2025). In OA tissue, this spacing becomes more variable: some regions exhibit increased separation due to cell loss, whereas others form clusters (chondrons) in which cells are positioned closer together, leading to a broader estimated range of 50–100 µm (Sandell and Aigner 2001; Pettenuzzo et al. 2023).

Chondrocyte spacing and spatial periodicity were incorporated into the model, with their implementation detailed in Sect. 2.3, “Mesh and Boundary Conditions.”

MRI data in the coronal plane were used to reconstruct the 2D macroscale knee models employed in the acoustic simulations. The MRI datasets were obtained from the Open Knee(s)/3DTechniques database, which provides specimen-specific knee imaging data suitable for anatomical reconstruction and computational modelling. The imaging protocol included a 3D T1-weighted sequence without fat suppression for general anatomical reconstruction (TR = 20 ms, TE = 6 ms; spatial resolution: 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm). Cartilage imaging was based on a 3D T1-weighted sequence with fat suppression (TR = 29 ms, TE = 5.3 ms; spatial resolution: 0.35 mm × 0.35 mm × 0.7 mm). Connective tissues were imaged using three orthogonal acquisitions in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, based on a proton-density sequence (TR = 10,000 ms, TE = 9.7 ms; spatial resolution: 0.35 mm × 0.35 mm × 2.8 mm) (Chokhandre et al. 2023).

For the 2D macroscale model, the coronal slice was selected to represent the central load-bearing tibiofemoral region, where the femoral condyles, tibial plateau, articular cartilage, menisci, and surrounding soft tissues could be clearly identified. This selection allowed the model to retain relevant subject-specific anatomical features, including cartilage thickness, joint geometry, limb alignment, and soft-tissue distribution, while maintaining a computationally tractable domain for acoustic propagation analysis.

The anatomical regions visible in the selected coronal slice were segmented and discretized into separate computational domains corresponding to the principal tissue components. For the 3D macroscale model, several voxel-threshold-based segmentation procedures were performed in 3D Slicer using the available MRI images to isolate the desired anatomical structures. The segmented volumes were then exported as surface geometries and imported into Blender, where smoothing, surface correction, and Boolean operations were performed to improve geometric continuity. The processed geometries were subsequently imported and rebuilt in COMSOL Multiphysics. Geometry-cleaning tools were applied to remove nonconforming features, correct surface inconsistencies, and ensure mesh-domain conformity before discretization.

Mechanical and acoustic material properties used for the healthy and OA chondrocyte microenvironment

The material parameters of the cellular domains were adapted according to current evidence on osteoarthritic alterations. For healthy chondrocytes, the cytoplasmic density was adopted from Rohrbach et al. (Rohrbach et al. 2017). For osteoarthritic chondrocytes, the density was estimated to be approximately 62% of the healthy value, based on the alterations reported by Bush and Hall (Bush and Hall 2003). The Young’s modulus values for the healthy and OA cytoplasm were obtained from Haudenschild et al. (Haudenschild et al. 2011), while the Poisson’s ratios were taken directly from experimental measurements reported in the literature (Trickey et al. 2006). The cytoplasmic damping ratio was calculated by converting the dissipative parameter measured by Rohrbach et al. into an equivalent damping ratio (Rohrbach et al. 2017).

Because direct measurements of chondrocyte nuclear density are not available, the nuclear density was assumed to be approximately 99% of the corresponding cytoplasmic density, meaning that the nucleus was considered approximately 1% less dense than the cytoplasm. The same relative density relationship was applied to healthy and OA chondrocytes because no evidence currently supports an OA-specific alteration in the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic density ratio (Dahl et al. 2008; Schlüßler et al. 2022).

The nuclear Young’s modulus was defined relative to the cytoplasmic modulus. Based on the in situ nuclear deformation analysis reported by Ofek et al., the nucleus was considered to be approximately 1.4–2.0 times stiffer than the surrounding cytoplasm (Ofek et al. 2009). A factor of 1.4 was adopted for the healthy nucleus, while a factor of 2.0 was considered for the OA nucleus to represent a possible increase in relative nuclear stiffness associated with OA-related changes in nuclear structure. This assumption is supported qualitatively by reports of increased lamin A expression and chromatin condensation in OA chondrocytes, both of which may contribute to greater nuclear mechanical resistance. However, because no direct measurements of the Young’s modulus of OA chondrocyte nuclei are currently available, the higher OA stiffness ratio should be interpreted as an evidence-informed modelling assumption rather than a directly measured material property (Attur et al. 2012).

A Poisson’s ratio of 0.42 was assigned to the nucleus in both healthy and OA conditions, based on experimentally determined values for chondrocyte nuclei. The same value was retained for both conditions because the available literature does not demonstrate a significant OA-related change in nuclear compressibility (Leipzig and Athanasiou 2008).

Direct measurements of nuclear dissipation at ultrasonic frequencies are also unavailable. Therefore, the nuclear damping ratio was estimated from the relationship between the loss factor and damping ratio for a lightly damped viscoelastic material. To date, direct measurements comparing the density, Young’s modulus, and Poisson’s ratio of isolated plasma membranes from healthy and osteoarthritic chondrocytes have not been reported. Although osteoarthritic chondrocytes have been associated with increased membrane cholesterol content and reduced membrane fluidity, these alterations cannot be directly translated into quantitative changes in elastic modulus, density, or Poisson’s ratio. In particular, membrane fluidity is not mechanically equivalent to stiffness, and the influence of cholesterol on membrane elasticity depends on the composition and organization of the surrounding lipid bilayer (Arai et al. 2019). Consequently, the same plasma membrane properties were assigned to the healthy and osteoarthritic conditions.

Based on the compositional characteristics reported by Julkunen et al. (Julkunen et al. 2009), the density of the pericellular matrix was assumed to be close to that of water. Osteoarthritis is associated with marked PCM softening, structural deterioration, expansion of the pericellular region, and increased tissue hydration. To represent these alterations, the effective solid fraction was assumed to decrease to approximately 6%, resulting in an estimated OA PCM density of 1.02 g/cm3 (Wilusz et al. 2013). The Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio assigned to the PCM were obtained directly from the experimental measurements reported by Alexopoulos et al. (Alexopoulos et al. 2003, 2005).

Because reliable experimental measurements of PCM energy dissipation at ultrasonic frequencies are not currently available, the viscoelastic parameters were defined using the generalized Maxwell formulation adopted by Nguyen et al. (Nguyen et al. 2010). The corresponding deviatoric viscosity was derived from an assumed mechanical loss factor evaluated at the operating frequency. In the absence of evidence supporting an OA-specific modification of this parameter, the same viscoelastic properties were assigned to the healthy and osteoarthritic PCM.

The ECM surrounding the chondrocyte–PCM complex was represented as a continuous, homogeneous acoustic medium. The material properties assigned to the healthy microenvironment were obtained directly from the experimental study conducted by Rohrbach et al. (Rohrbach et al. 2017). Under osteoarthritic conditions, the ECM density was reduced to 1.08 g/cm3 to account for collagen network disruption, progressive matrix loss, and increased tissue hydration (Nieminen et al. 2004), thereby shifting the effective density towards that of water. In addition, based on the findings of Joiner et al. (Joiner et al. 2001), the speed of sound was decreased by 2%, while the frequency-dependent acoustic attenuation was increased by 25% to represent OA-associated matrix degeneration.

Acoustic and mechanical properties of the biological tissues included in the macro-scale knee model

For fluid-like soft tissues, wave propagation was described using density, speed of sound, nonlinearity parameter (B/A), and a frequency-dependent sound diffusivity to account for viscous and thermal attenuation. The sound-diffusivity values adopted in the present simulations were obtained from previously published experimental attenuation measurements performed on biological tissue samples over an ultrasonic frequency range. Their derivation and calibration were therefore not repeated in the present study, and the previously established tissue-specific parameters were directly implemented in the numerical mode (Fernandes et al. 2025a). In contrast, bone was modelled as a solid elastic medium, requiring separate longitudinal and shear wave speeds rather than a single acoustic velocity, reflecting its ability to support both compressional and transverse elastic waves. Although the selected tissue properties were derived from porcine and bovine models (Fernandes et al. 2025a), previous studies indicate that the acoustic impedance of animal soft tissues, typically ranging from 1.33 to 1.71 MRayl (Koch et al. 2011; Liu 2006) have got very similar values to that is comparable to the values reported for human soft tissues, which range from 1.33 to 1.72 MRayl (Chen et al. 2022). For cortical bone, the calculated acoustic impedance was 5.9 MRayl, which lies within the commonly reported range of 3.5–6.0 MRayl (Raum et al. 2002). Bone structures were represented as homogeneous effective domains in the present macroscale model. This simplification was adopted because the available MRI data did not provide sufficient anatomical contrast to reliably distinguish cortical and trabecular bone layers throughout the knee geometry. Although cortical and trabecular bone have different acoustic properties, at the investigated frequencies the acoustic wavelength is considerably larger than the characteristic trabecular microarchitecture and local cortical-layer thickness. Therefore, bone is expected to act primarily as a high-impedance reflecting boundary relative to the surrounding soft tissues, with the dominant contribution to the macroscopic pressure field arising from the bone–soft tissue impedance contrast and global joint geometry. Nevertheless, this approximation may influence the local magnitude of reflected waves and the detailed spatial distribution of pressure near bone interfaces. Future work should investigate multilayer bone representations using imaging modalities capable of resolving cortical and trabecular compartments, such as CT or high-resolution MRI.

Mesh and boundary conditions

Representative volume element meshing

The RVE was constructed to capture the coupled mechanical and acoustic behaviour of an individual chondrocyte embedded within its local extracellular environment while ensuring that the simulated domain reproduced the conditions of an infinite periodic tissue. Within the RVE, the nucleus, cytoplasm, and PCM were all modelled as solid, linearly elastic continua, allowing the propagation of both compressional and shear waves throughout the intracellular and pericellular regions. The plasma membrane separating the cytoplasm from the ECM was represented as a thin structural shell, and its interaction with the cytoplasm was enforced through a structure–shell interface condition, ensuring continuity of displacements and tractions between the membrane and the underlying solid domains. Surrounding the cell, the ECM was treated as an acoustic pressure medium, supporting only longitudinal wave propagation and interacting with the solid regions via standard acoustic–structure boundary conditions that impose equality of normal accelerations and balance of normal stresses.

Boundary conditions are defined to replicate physiological and open-domain behaviour. A soft sound boundary condition is applied at external tissue interfaces to minimize artificial reflections and approximate free-field radiation. Perfectly matched boundaries are implemented at the outer boundaries of the computational domain to enforce domain continuity and simulate an unbounded acoustic medium, allowing outgoing waves to be absorbed without reflection. An added mass of 55 kg was added, taking into account the average human body weight and removed the weight below the knee, as has been previously studied (Fernandes et al. 2025b).

To quantify cartilage stimulation, a surface integration of the acoustic pressure field is performed over the cartilage domain, yielding an averaged pressure value representative of the effective mechanical stimulus transmitted to the tissue.

Simulation setup

Modal analysis setup

Modal analysis at the cellular scale was performed in COMSOL Multiphysics using a fully coupled multiphysics formulation that incorporated the solid mechanics, shell mechanics, and acoustic pressure domains relevant to the chondrocyte microenvironment. The nucleus, cytoplasm, and PCM were modelled as linear elastic solids governed by the Navier–Cauchy equation, enabling both longitudinal and shear elastic wave modes to develop within the intracellular space. The plasma membrane was represented as a thin structural shell, whose deformation was coupled to the cytoplasm through a structure–shell interface enforcing continuity of displacement and traction. Surrounding the cell, the ECM domain was modelled using the Pressure Acoustics, Frequency Domain interface, allowing propagation of acoustic pressure waves governed by the Helmholtz equation.

To correctly capture the interplay between these domains, COMSOL’s Acoustic–Structure Boundary conditions were applied at each solid–fluid interface, ensuring that normal accelerations and stresses remained continuous. This allowed pressure fluctuations in the ECM to drive elastic deformation in the solid regions, and conversely, solid vibrations to radiate pressure waves back into the fluid. The shell mechanics interface was similarly coupled to both solid domains to preserve mechanical continuity across the plasma membrane.

A Modal Analysis study was then executed on the fully coupled system, solving for the natural frequencies and eigenmodes of vibration of the composite cell–PCM–ECM structure.

Time-explicit simulations setup

Time-domain simulations were performed using an DG explicit time-integration scheme to resolve the transient propagation of acoustic and elastodynamic waves through the coupled fluid–structure system. This formulation was selected due to its numerical robustness for high-frequency wave propagation, its ability to accurately capture short-wavelength phenomena, and its suitability for modelling strongly coupled interfaces between acoustic media and deformable solids.

Both linear and nonlinear acoustic formulations with dissipation were considered. In the linear regime, wave propagation in fluid domains was governed by the classical linear acoustic wave equation (Eq. (1)), while solid domains (cells, cartilage, and bone where applicable) were described by linear elastodynamics. To evaluate whether finite-amplitude effects become relevant at the applied excitation levels, a nonlinear formulation based on the Westervelt equation (Eq. (4)) was additionally implemented in the fluid domains, incorporating quadratic pressure nonlinearity and frequency-dependent attenuation. By directly comparing linear and nonlinear responses under identical loading conditions, the necessity of including nonlinear effects at the target pressure amplitudes could be systematically assessed.

To prevent artificial wave reflections at the computational boundaries, perfectly matched boundaries were replaced by absorbing layers in the time-explicit framework. These absorbing regions were implemented with gradually increasing attenuation to mimic an unbounded domain and ensure stable dissipation of outgoing waves across the entire frequency range of interest. This approach is particularly well suited for transient simulations, where broadband signals and multiple reflections can otherwise contaminate the response.

Pressure excitation was applied as a transient input, allowing the system’s full temporal response to be captured. The resulting pressure fields, displacement fields, and strain histories were monitored within the extracellular matrix as well as at the cellular and nuclear levels. To excite a finite frequency band while preserving temporal localization, the pressure input was prescribed as a Gaussian-modulated tone burst, defined as Eq. The training and validation of the used models is available in appendix A. New combinations of the two dominant parameters were supplied directly to the surrogate, which returned simultaneous predictions for the outputs. Because neural-network inference required only matrix operations, the computational time per evaluation was substantially lower than that required for a complete finite-element analysis. The surrogate was used only for interpolation within the parameter ranges represented in the training dataset; predictions outside these limits were not considered reliable without additional COMSOL simulations and subsequent model retraining.

At lower excitation conditions, the acoustic field remains relatively diffuse, with modest pressure amplitudes primarily confined to the soft tissue layers and gradually decreasing towards the cartilage interfaces. The energy transmission appears broadly distributed, indicating that wave propagation is governed primarily by bulk tissue impedance and attenuation.

As the excitation parameters vary, localized amplification zones become more evident, particularly along tissue interfaces and near the cartilage surfaces. These regions of higher RMS pressure suggest constructive interference and impedance-related reflections at structural boundaries, especially at transitions between soft tissues and bone. The spatial concentration of pressure near the joint space indicates that the geometry of the knee plays a significant role in guiding wave energy towards cartilage.

While the overall propagation pattern remains consistent across the conditions shown, the magnitude and localization of peak pressures increase in specific configurations. This indicates that certain parameter combinations enhance energy focussing within the joint cavity. Nevertheless, the general propagation pathway remains structurally preserved, suggesting that the wave transmission mechanism is robust with respect to moderate variations in excitation parameters.

The RMS maps confirm that acoustic energy reaches the cartilage region with spatially heterogeneous distribution, influenced by tissue interfaces and geometric confinement. These results support the transmissibility findings in the frequency-domain analysis, demonstrating that the macro-scale propagation pathway can effectively deliver acoustic energy to the cartilage while exhibiting localized amplification effects governed by anatomical structure.

The 2D and 3D models produced qualitatively different acoustic fields. In the 2D representation, the pressure distribution was comparatively smooth and spatially confined, with energy concentrated around the joint space and along selected tissue interfaces. The highest local values approached \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.3\bullet {10}^{3}$$\end{document}1.3∙103 Pa. By contrast, the 3D model exhibited a more heterogeneous field characterized by multiple spatially distributed regions of constructive and destructive interference. Its maximum pressure was lower, reaching approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.0\bullet {10}^{2}$$\end{document}5.0∙102 Pa.

The greater amplitudes obtained in 2D can be attributed primarily to dimensional confinement. Because out-of-plane propagation is excluded, the acoustic energy remains restricted to the modelling plane, leading to stronger superposition of incident and reflected waves. The 3D geometry permits lateral and out-of-plane spreading, diffraction, and interaction with a larger set of anatomical interfaces. These mechanisms redistribute acoustic energy throughout the joint and reduce the localized amplitudes observed in any individual section.

The maximum pressures identified in both acoustic fields should not be interpreted as the pressure effectively delivered to the articular cartilage. The global maxima occurred within localized regions of acoustic-wave trapping, where repeated reflections and constructive interference caused pressure accumulation between tissue boundaries or within confined anatomical regions. These hotspots therefore describe local features of the overall propagation field rather than direct acoustic impingement on the cartilage surface. Evaluation of cartilage exposure should instead rely on pressure values sampled or spatially integrated specifically within the cartilage domain.

Discussion

The findings in this study demonstrate that acoustic stimulation of cartilage is frequency-band dependent, disease-sensitive (because it will mechanically alter the condition of the chondrocytes (Nahian and Sapra 2025; Chawla et al. 2022)), and predominantly linear under clinically relevant pressures.

At the cellular scale, modal analysis demonstrated that chondrocytes exhibit spheroidal and torsional vibration modes analogous to elastic sphere behaviour. Among these, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{3}$$\end{document}s3​ spheroidal mode produced the most significant nuclear deformation pattern, making it the primary mechanotransductive target. Parametric analyses showed that chondrocyte radius is the dominant geometric factor governing modal frequency shifts, while PCM thickness has a secondary effect and plasma membrane thickness and intercellular spacing have negligible influence. In OA cells, modal frequencies shift downward due to hypertrophy and altered stiffness, confirming disease-dependent dynamic behaviour. Although the OA nucleus was assigned a higher elastic modulus, the corresponding reduction in resonance frequency does not contradict the classical stiffness–frequency relationship. The chondrocyte is a coupled multilayer system, and its natural frequencies are governed by the global eigenvalue problem, rather than by the stiffness of an individual cellular component. In the OA model, the decrease in cytoplasmic stiffness, combined with the substantial increase in the relative volume of the cytoplasm, reduces the effective stiffness of the dominant coupled modes. This effect outweighs the localized increase in nuclear stiffness, leading to lower predicted natural frequencies. Frequency–response analysis revealed that nuclear strain amplification occurs within specific frequency bands rather than at a single discrete frequency. Healthy chondrocytes exhibited a broader mid-frequency amplification band, whereas OA cells showed a dominant low-frequency peak with reduced high-frequency sensitivity.

At the macroscale, acoustic wave propagation through the layered knee tissues demonstrated that energy reaches the cartilage with spatially heterogeneous distribution governed by impedance mismatches and geometric confinement. Beyond a certain transducer diameter, transmissibility stabilizes, indicating limited sensitivity to source placement. Despite the amplitude differences, both formulations (2D and 3D) confirmed that acoustic energy reached the joint region. The 3D results provide a more physically complete representation of this process by accounting for energy redistribution in all spatial directions. As such, the 2D model remains useful for identifying general propagation tendencies and conducting broad parametric analyses, but it may overestimate localized pressures and cannot fully reproduce the complex interference patterns present in the three-dimensional anatomy.

Multi-scale coupling of macro-scale pressure to micro-scale nuclear strain confirmed that disease state significantly alters pressure-to-strain transmissibility. Healthy cartilage supports broader and higher-frequency amplification (50–170 kHz), whereas OA tissue concentrates transmissibility in a narrower low-frequency band (30–85 kHz). The comparison demonstrates that OA-related alterations enhance and shift macro-to-micro coupling towards lower frequencies while simultaneously narrowing the frequency range over which substantial nuclear deformation is maintained. Cellular hypertrophy, PCM softening, and changes in the mechanical properties of the intracellular components may collectively reduce the effective dynamic stiffness of the chondrocyte–PCM system, thereby increasing its low-frequency compliance. Conversely, the broader response of healthy chondrocytes suggests more distributed modal participation and greater preservation of higher-frequency coupling. The predicted differences remain dependent on the adopted geometrical and constitutive assumptions and should therefore be interpreted as model-based trends pending experimental validation.

Time-explicit simulations comparing linear and nonlinear acoustic formulations demonstrated negligible harmonic generation and no significant nonlinear spectral growth at the applied pressure levels. This indicates that, within the mechanotransductive strain range considered, wave propagation is adequately described by linear acoustics.

The ranges reported in this study are markedly different from the stimulation frequencies typically employed in the cartilage and OA literature. Conventional therapeutic ultrasound therapies (continuous or pulsed) typically have a representative carrier frequency of 1–3 MHz (Matthews and Stretanski 2023). For continuous therapeutic ultrasound RCTs report that the frequency used across is 1 MHz, with intensities commonly in the 1–2 W/cm2 range and session times ~5–10 min (Dantas et al. 2021). Long-duration ultrasound treatment can also be applied at 3 MHz at 132 mW/cm2 for 4 h daily (Winkler et al. 2021). Regarding Low-Intensity Pulsed Ultrasound (LIPUS), Miller et al. note that the vast majority of in vitro and in vivo cartilage restoration protocols rely on empirically derived LIPUS regimens at 1.0–1.5 MHz without explicit tuning to cellular resonances (Miller et al. 2017), and similarly Zhou et al. describe standard LIPUS devices operating at 1–3 MHz, most commonly around 1.5 MHz (Zhou et al. 2024). Similarly, Sekino et al. apply pulsed ultrasound at 1.5 MHz with intensities <100 mW/cm2 (Sekino et al. 2018). For cartilage-directed approaches, a randomised trial of focussed LIPUS (FLIPUS) for knee OA reported a 0.6 MHz carrier frequency (600 kHz) with a low-intensity pulsed regime and described this as enabling more direct cartilage stimulation than earlier unfocussed approaches (Jia et al. 2022).

In fact, the chosen frequencies for chondrocyte stimulation directly contradict general therapeutic ultrasound teaching: higher-frequency ultrasound attenuates more rapidly and is commonly used for more superficial effects, while lower frequency should be used for deeper heating (Matthews and Stretanski 2023). This is in part corroborated by current findings in the literature. For conventional therapeutic ultrasound in knee OA (typically 1 MHz), a GRADE systematic review assessment found small, statistically significant improvements in pain versus sham controls, but judged the overall certainty of evidence as low, with substantial limitations across trials (Dantas et al. 2021). A large sham-controlled trial using LIPUS-type device (1.5 MHz carrier) reported no clinically meaningful improvement in symptoms or cartilage thickness over a lengthy treatment period (48 weeks) (Sawitzke et al. 2022). A comparative review of multiple major OA guidelines reported insufficient evidence to recommend therapeutic ultrasound (along with several other passive modalities), even while exercise, weight management strategies are more consistently supported across guidelines (Overton et al. 2022). These findings might also be partially explained due to the fact that authors often primarily report symptom-management claims rather than confirmed disease-modification, and the modality’s mechanism is commonly framed in the “deep heating/diathermy over hours” regime (Winkler et al. 2021), suggesting that high frequency protocols may be inherently inefficient at producing the nuclear deformation necessary.

On the other hand, vibration-based approaches for cartilage and OA typically use very low frequencies, either whole-body vibration in the 5–20 Hz range in animal OA models (Lützenberg et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023), or direct mechanical vibration of chondrocytes at hundreds of hertz, with enhanced proliferation reported above 350 Hz but still well below kilohertz regime identified in our model (Kaupp and Waldman 2008). Taken together, this suggests that the predicted optimal bands of 30–85 kHz (OA) and 50–170 kHz (healthy) occupy an underexplored frequency region between classical low-frequency mechanical loading and conventional MHz LIPUS, and that future experimental work explicitly targeting these bands could test whether resonance-based stimulation at cell- and disease-specific frequencies yields more efficient mechanobiological responses than the empirically chosen frequencies currently used in the literature.

The suggested range is already applied in transdermal ultrasound/sonophoresis literature, with 20–200 kHz being a standard definition of “low frequency ultrasound”(Marathe et al. 2024). A clinical prototype for chronic wounds has been developed to operate in the 20–100 kHz range, explicitly aiming for non-thermal, non-inertial-cavitational exposure (with peak pressure limited and extended wearability) (Ngo et al. 2019).

This study presents a novel multi-scale computational framework linking macro-scale acoustic wave propagation in the knee to mechanotransductive strain at the level of the chondrocyte nucleus. A primary strength of the work is that it is, to our knowledge, the first study to explicitly relate ultrasound-based stimulation strategies to nuclear strain at the single-cell level within a patient-specific joint geometry. Although previously articles have previously studied the modal analysis and harmonic responses of individual cells (Ahmed Mubeen et al. 2020), or macro-scale acoustic wave propagation (Fernandes et al. xxxx; Sharma et al. 2025), the integration of modal analysis, frequency–response characterization, and macro-to-micro transmissibility within a unified computational environment represents a significant methodological advance. The multi-scale coupling strategy, connecting tissue-level acoustic fields to intracellular strain amplification, provides a mechanistically grounded basis for frequency selection and highlights disease-dependent differences between healthy and osteoarthritic cartilage. Additionally, the systematic comparison between linear and nonlinear acoustic formulations strengthens the modelling assumptions by demonstrating that, under the investigated pressure ranges, nonlinear propagation effects are negligible.

Experimental evidence increasingly indicates that cartilage degeneration produces measurable alterations in both acoustic and mechanical behaviour. Knee-generated acoustic signals, analysed using multiscale signal-processing and machine-learning methods, have shown potential for distinguishing different stages of cartilage damage and supporting non-invasive OA assessment (Machrowska et al. 2025). In living human knees, intraoperative ultrasonic measurements demonstrated that increasing cartilage degeneration grade was associated with reduced acoustic signal intensity and cartilage thickness, confirming that disease-related structural changes are reflected in the measured ultrasonic response (Nakagawa et al. 2023). Similarly, quantitative high-frequency ultrasound measurements in an ovariectomized rat model identified site-dependent changes in cartilage thickness, surface roughness index, and reflection coefficient following OA-related degeneration (Wang et al. 2016). Ultrasound-based elastography has also been shown to distinguish alterations in cartilage stiffness under experimentally induced OA conditions, demonstrating that acoustic-wave propagation is sensitive to changes in cartilage mechanical properties (Georgas et al. 2024) More broadly, ultrasound can function not only as a diagnostic modality but also as a mechanical stimulus capable of regulating mechanotransductive pathways, supporting the investigation of frequency-dependent acoustic stimulation for musculoskeletal regeneration and therapy (Qin 2025). Collectively, these studies support the biological and mechanical plausibility of using acoustic responses to characterize tissue degeneration and motivate the experimental evaluation of the candidate frequency bands predicted in the present work. Nevertheless, because the cited studies employed different excitation mechanisms, frequencies, experimental models, and outcome measures, they provide contextual support rather than direct validation of the present numerical results.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study relies entirely on computational modelling, and the predicted nuclear strain levels and optimal frequency bands require experimental validation. Material properties, particularly high-frequency viscoelastic and attenuation parameters at the cellular scale, are derived from available literature and may not fully capture in vivo behaviour. Medical phantoms with controlled acoustic and mechanical properties could be explored as a reproducible alternative to biological tissues for preliminary experimental validation of wave propagation and pressure-to-strain transmission (Santos et al. 2025; Mencarelli et al. 2026; Fernandes et al. 2025c). Furthermore, biological mechanotransduction involves complex biochemical feedback mechanisms that are not represented in the present mechanical model. Future work should therefore include controlled experimental measurements, both at the tissue level and at the cellular/nuclear level, to validate the predicted strain magnitudes and frequency-dependent responses. Interspecies differences in bone microarchitecture, density, porosity, and mineralization may affect local acoustic reflection and transmission, particularly near bone–soft tissue interfaces. These differences may influence the absolute magnitude of the localized pressure amplification factor, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|{H}_{pp}|$$\end{document}|Hpp|, although the overall amplification trends are expected to remain representative because the adopted impedance values lie within physiological ranges reported for human tissues. Despite these limitations, the proposed framework establishes a quantitative foundation for designing ultrasound stimulation protocols grounded in cellular-scale mechanics.

Although the macroscopic propagation framework and tissue-specific dissipative parameters were previously calibrated using experimentally measured acoustic transmission through ex vivo biological tissues, the complete knee-specific model presented here has not yet been directly validated using an anatomically representative knee phantom or cadaveric specimen. Directly comparable experimental datasets describing frequency-dependent pressure transmission through the heterogeneous knee joint remain scarce, particularly within the frequency range investigated here. Consequently, the predicted resonance bands should be interpreted as computationally derived candidates intended to guide subsequent experimental studies rather than as clinically validated stimulation frequencies. Future work will focus on knee-specific phantom and ex vivo validation of the predicted acoustic transmission profiles and resonance behaviour.

Articular cartilage was represented as a homogeneous effective medium in the macroscopic model. Although native cartilage exhibits depth-dependent anisotropy, the wavelengths associated with the investigated frequencies, which extend up to 200 kHz, are substantially larger than the characteristic dimensions of its individual structural zones. The homogenized approximation was therefore considered appropriate for evaluating whole-joint acoustic transmission. Nevertheless, zonal variations may affect local pressure amplitudes, attenuation, and phase and should be investigated in future multilayer or anisotropic models.

Several unanswered questions remain and provide clear directions for future research. First, while the model predicts frequency-dependent nuclear strain amplification, the direct relationship between predicted strain magnitudes and specific biological outcomes, such as gene expression, matrix synthesis, or inflammatory modulation, has not yet been experimentally established. The precise mechanotransductive thresholds for chondrocytes under high-frequency acoustic loading remain insufficiently characterized, particularly in osteoarthritic conditions. Additionally, the present framework assumes homogeneous and isotropic tissue properties, whereas cartilage exhibits depth-dependent structure and anisotropy that may influence wave propagation and energy focussing. It would also be worthwhile to adopt the framework developed by Girón and Lelis (Gómez Girón and Rodríguez Lelis 2025), to model chondrocytes, rather than representing the entire domain as a multi-layer homogeneous continuum. In particular, the cytoskeleton could be explicitly discretized using truss elements, allowing the structural contribution of the filament network to be captured more realistically. The resulting model could then be validated against experimental data available in the literature, thereby improving the accuracy and physical representativeness of the RVE formulation.

The use of infinitesimal-strain linear elasticity does not imply that the predicted cellular deformation is biologically insignificant. It indicates that the strain amplitude is assumed to remain sufficiently small for a linear constitutive approximation. Mechanotransduction may occur under small cyclic deformation through repeated loading of the cell membrane, cytoskeleton, nucleus, and extracellular matrix attachments, particularly when excitation occurs near a mechanically responsive mode. Under these conditions, modal amplification may increase local cytoplasmic or nuclear strains relative to off-resonance stimulation, even when the externally imposed acoustic displacement is small. A strain range of approximately 1–10% has been reported as a useful biological reference for mechanically induced cellular and nuclear responses; however, this should not be interpreted as a universal threshold, since the response also depends on frequency, exposure duration, number of cycles, cell type, and loading configuration. The present model therefore identifies frequencies that are mechanically favourable for deformation but does not directly predict downstream biochemical signalling.

Periodic boundary conditions were applied to the microscale RVE to represent a chondrocyte embedded within a continuous tissue environment and to minimize artificial boundary effects associated with isolated finite domains. This approximation is appropriate for evaluating the controlled influence of selected geometrical parameters on the local chondrocyte–PCM–ECM response. Chondrocyte spacing was explicitly included as a parametric variable, allowing the influence of cellular spatial periodicity on the predicted resonant behaviour to be assessed. Within the investigated range, the dominant changes in natural frequency were primarily associated with chondrocyte radius and PCM thickness, whereas chondrocyte spacing produced comparatively smaller variations.

Nevertheless, the periodic RVE should be interpreted as an idealized microscale representation rather than a complete description of severely degraded OA cartilage. Advanced OA tissue may contain irregular chondrocyte clusters, disrupted PCM regions, fissures, and spatially heterogeneous matrix properties that are not fully represented by a single periodic unit cell. Consequently, the present model does not explicitly resolve nonperiodic cluster-to-cluster stress interactions. Future studies should incorporate stochastic or image-based multi-cell RVEs to determine how OA-induced clustering and local matrix heterogeneity affect cellular-scale acoustic strain transfer.

The long-term biological consequences of repeated or chronic acoustic stimulation, including potential cumulative effects or adaptation mechanisms, also remain unexplored. Future work should integrate experimental validation at multiple scales, incorporate more detailed tissue heterogeneity, and potentially couple mechanical models with biochemical signalling pathways to bridge mechanics and cell biology. The broader implications of this work extend beyond cartilage stimulation: the proposed multi-scale strategy could inform the design of targeted ultrasound therapies for other mechanosensitive tissues, enabling patient-specific optimization of stimulation parameters grounded in cellular-scale mechanics rather than empirical frequency selection.

Conclusion

This study addressed the need for a mechanistic understanding of how externally applied acoustic waves used in cartilage stimulation translate into biologically relevant deformation at the level of the chondrocyte nucleus. Current ultrasound protocols are largely empirical and do not explicitly account for multi-scale mechanical transmission from the joint to the cell. To resolve this gap, a patient-specific multi-scale computational framework was developed, coupling macro-scale acoustic wave propagation in the knee with micro-scale cellular mechanics through a representative volume element approach. The results demonstrated that nuclear strain amplification occurs within defined frequency bands rather than at a single discrete frequency, with the spheroidal s3 mode playing a dominant role in nuclear deformation. Osteoarthritic alterations were shown to shift the optimal frequency band towards lower frequencies and reduce higher-frequency sensitivity (Healthy: 50–170 kHz, OA: 30–85 kHz). Despite anatomical variability across patients, consistent transmissibility trends were observed, and time-explicit simulations confirmed that, at the applied pressure levels, nonlinear acoustic effects are negligible. It should nevertheless be acknowledged that the proposed framework relies on several simplifying assumptions regarding the geometrical representation, material properties, and mechanical behaviour of the biological tissues. These assumptions were necessary to reduce model complexity and maintain computational feasibility, but they may limit the extent to which the numerical predictions reproduce the full physiological response of the knee joint. Further experimental validation is therefore required to confirm the accuracy of the predicted acoustic fields, modal behaviour, and tissue-level mechanical responses. The key takeaway is that effective cartilage stimulation should be frequency-band targeted and disease-sensitive, and that linear acoustic modelling is sufficient within mechanotransductive strain ranges relevant for therapy. The proposed framework provides a quantitative basis for rational selection of ultrasound parameters grounded in cellular-scale mechanics rather than empirical tuning.
