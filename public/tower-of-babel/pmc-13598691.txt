US Pediatric Hospitalizations Complicated by Acute Kidney Injury and Dialysis From 1997 to 2022
Authors: Alexander J. Kula, Shan Sun, Ndeah Terry, Jeanne Frisby-Zedan, Scott M. Sutherland, Deirdre Bartlett, Sanjana Venkat, Shina Menon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHas the number and rate of US pediatric hospitalizations complicated by acute kidney injury (AKI) and AKI requiring dialysis (AKI-D) changed between 1997 and 2022?FindingsIn this national cohort study of more than 26.3 million pediatric hospitalizations, both the yearly total and annual rate of AKI diagnoses and AKI-D have increased over time.MeaningThese findings suggest pediatric health systems must prepare to address the large, and growing, number of hospitalizations complicated by a diagnosis of AKI and AKI-D.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs frequently in hospitalized patients across the age spectrum, particularly those who are critically ill. It is associated with increased risk of mortality as well as adverse short- and long-term renal and systemic complications.1,2 Likewise, neonatal and pediatric AKI is associated with increased length of stay, requirement for mechanical ventilation, and mortality.3 After discharge, patients who experienced AKI are at higher risk for repeated episodes of AKI and increased health care utilization.4,5 The worst outcomes are observed in youth with severe AKI requiring dialysis (AKI-D).

The published incidence of AKI in children and young adults varies greatly depending on the specifics of the patient population, health system, and study time frame. A prospective multicenter study from 4 centers in China reported that the AKI incidence varied from 6.8% to 25.7% depending on the definition used and how the baseline creatinine level was estimated.6 A 2023 meta-analysis7 showed that the incidence of AKI in hospitalized children worldwide was 26% and that of moderate to severe AKI was 14%. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of existing studies makes defining longitudinal trends in neonatal and pediatric AKI far more difficult.

Evidence from adult studies suggests the documented rates of AKI and AKI-D have grown with time. Hsu et al8,9 reported across 2 studies that the incidence of non–dialysis requiring AKI increased from 322.7 to 522.4 per 100 000 person-years between 1996 and 2003 and the rate of AKI-D increased by 10% per year from 2000 to 2009 across the United States. A study in adults admitted to an intensive care unit in Canada showed a 4-fold increase in the incidence of AKI-D from the 1990s to 2010.10 Similar longitudinal data on non–dialysis requiring AKI are not available for pediatric patients. Using the provincial health care administrative databases from Ontario, Chanchlani et al11 evaluated nearly 1400 patients from 29 days to 18 years of age with AKI-D from 1996 to 2015. The incidence of AKI-D among hospitalized children increased from 0.58 per 1000 person-years in 1996 to 0.65 per 1000 person-years in 2015 (P = .01).11

We aimed to describe longitudinal trends of AKI and AKI-D over a 25-year period (1997-2022) in the United States in youths admitted to pediatric care. These data will address a knowledge gap with significance for both patients and health systems. We hypothesized that the rates of AKI and AKI-D increased over the past 25 years but in-hospital outcomes for these patients improved with time.

Methods

Data Sources

The study examined inpatient admissions in 1997, 2000, 2003, 2006, 2009, 2012, 2016, 2019, and 2022 using the Kids’ Inpatient Database (KID), Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.12 KID is an all-payer, inpatient care database for patients admitted to pediatric hospitals or units in the United States that contains information included in a typical discharge abstract. KID is made available every 3 years, and this study included all discharge data. Depending on the year, each hospital record contains up to 40 International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) (1997-2012) and International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) (2016-2022) discharge diagnosis codes and 25 ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM procedure codes. The KID also contains all patient-refined, diagnosis-related group (DRG) data, which allow for patients across similar diagnostic groups to be compared from a case complexity standpoint. Data are collected at the hospitalization level, so patients readmitted within the same year would be included as 2 unique cases. A preliminary analysis was performed in 2023 that informed the analytic design of the present analysis performed between June 2025 and February 2026.13 All authors completed the required data use agreement training required by HCUP to work with KID. Because no state-specific data were requested or used, KID did not meet the criteria for human participant research and was determined exempt by the institutional review board at Lurie Children’s Hospital (IRB No. 2022-5199). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.14

Cohort optimization is shown in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1. We excluded uncomplicated births, patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and patients with history of kidney transplant. Uncomplicated births were excluded using the HOSPBRTH = 1 and UNCBRTH = 1 terms, as they represent healthy newborns admitted to a maternity ward or newborn nursery. Newborns experiencing complications were not excluded. Given that individuals older than 18 years may receive care at pediatric centers due to their underlying medical condition, we did not exclude patients older than 18 years from our analysis. Patients with ESKD were identified using the ICD-9-CM codes 585.5 (chronic kidney disease [CKD] stage 5) and 585.6 (ESKD) and procedure codes relating to presence of an arteriovenous fistula (39.27, 39.42, 39.43, and 39.93) and excluded. For ICD-10-CM, ESKD was identified using the diagnosis code of N18.6 (ESKD). Patients who had received a kidney transplant were excluded because this is a population at high risk for unique causes of AKI and graft dysfunction. The ICD-9-CM codes used for kidney transplant included V42.0 (kidney replaced by transplant) and 996.81 (complications of transplanted kidney), and the ICD-10-CM codes were Z94.0 (kidney transplant status) and procedure codes for transplantation of kidney (0TY00Z0 and 0TY00Z1).

AKI and Dialysis Definitions

AKI was identified using ICD-9-CM codes 584.5 to 584.9: 584.5 (acute kidney failure [AKF] with lesion of tubular necrosis), 584.6 (AKF with lesion of renal cortical necrosis), 584.7 (AKF with lesion of renal medullary [papillary] necrosis), 584.8 (AKF with other specified pathologic lesion in kidney), and 584.9 (AKF unspecified).15 ICD-10-CM codes for AKI were N17.0 (AKF with tubular necrosis), N17.1 (AKF with acute cortical necrosis), N17.2 (AKF with medullary necrosis), N17.8 (other AKF), N17.9 (AKF, unspecified), and N99.0 (postprocedural AKF). The use of dialysis was identified using the codes V45.1 (postsurgical renal dialysis), V56.0 (encounter of extracorporeal dialysis), V56.1 (fitting of an extracorporeal dialysis catheter), 39.95 (hemodialysis), and 54.98 (peritoneal dialysis). For ICD-10-CM, dialysis was identified by Z49.0 (preparatory care for renal dialysis and/or catheter placement), Z49.01 (fitting and adjustment of dialysis catheter), 5A1D00Z (performance of urinary filtration, single), 5A1D60Z (performance of urinary filtration, multiple), 5A1D70Z (performance of urinary filtration, <6 h/d), 5A1D80Z (performance of urinary filtration, 6-18 h/d), 5A1D90Z (performance of urinary filtration, continuous).16

Covariates

ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnoses were categorized into diagnosis groups using the pccc package from R statistics.17 The All Patient Refined–DRG (APR-DRG) risk of mortality (ROM) and severity of illness (SOI) scores were available starting in 2003. APR-DRG ROM uses data on demographic characteristics, diagnoses, and procedures to place hospitalizations into 1 of 4 categories from minor to extreme. Likewise, the APR-DRG SOI uses a similar process to categorize severity of hospitalizations to the 4 same categories. In-hospital mortality was included in the KID datasets starting in 2003.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means along with 95% CIs, and categorical variables were presented as counts with proportions. Characteristics associated with hospitalizations were presented for hospitalizations with AKI only and the complete cohort after applying exclusion criteria. All results were stratified by year. National estimates were calculated using the discharge-level sampling weights provided by KID. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of KID by incorporating discharge weights, hospital-level clustering, and stratification variables. Survey-weighted estimates and proportions were generated using the survey package in R, with appropriate adjustment for clustering and stratification to produce nationally representative estimates and valid variance estimates.18 Unweighted counts are presented for descriptive purposes; weighted estimates were used for national incidence calculations.

Joinpoint regression models were fitted to the log-transformed annual rates. Candidate models with 0, 1, 2, and 3 joinpoints were evaluated to identify the optimal segmentation of temporal trends. The optimal model was selected based on the bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower BIC values indicating a better balance between model fit and complexity. For each time interval defined by the selected joinpoints, the annual percentage change (APC) was calculated from the slope estimates of the segmented log-linear regression model using the formula APC = (exp[β] − 1) × 100. Corresponding 95% CIs were derived from the SEs of the slope estimates. Grouped binomial logistic regression models were used including AKI status, year, and AKI status × year to test change in mortality rates over time between hospitalizations with no AKI, AKI, and AKI-D. Statistical significance was set at P < .05, and all tests were 2-tailed. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Hospitalization Characteristics

A total of 26 357 519 discharges were reported during the study period, and 24 344 876 met final inclusion criteria (estimated mean age, 6.9 [95% CI, 6.8-6.9] years; weighted 46.9% male) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Using these data, we estimated that 253 138 and 15 069 hospitalizations were complicated by AKI and AKI-D, respectively. Between 1997 and 2016, the mean age at hospitalization in patients with AKI steadily increased from 6.2 (95% CI, 5.8-6.6) years to 12.2 (95% CI, 11.9-12.6) years, after which it slightly decreased to 11.7 (95% CI, 11.4-12.0) years by 2022. There was a male predominance in hospitalizations with AKI across all years. Characteristics of hospitalizations complicated by AKI, stratified by study year, are described in Table 1, and all included hospitalizations are described in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. In 1997, the 3 most common primary diagnosis for hospitalizations with AKI were malignancy (476 of 6318 [7.5%]), cardiovascular (425 [6.7%]), and hematology or immune (139 [2.2%]) (Figure 1; eTable 2 in Supplement 1). By 2022, the most common primary diagnoses were cardiovascular (2119 of 62 923 [3.3%]), malignant neoplasm (1538 [2.4%]), and complex medical technology dependence (855 [1.3%]). The proportion of AKI hospitalizations admitted to teaching hospitals and hospitals of large size increased over time.

Yearly Incidence and Rates of AKI and AKI-D

The estimated yearly incidence of hospitalizations with AKI increased every year, from 6318 hospitalizations in 1997 to 63 923 hospitalizations in 2022 (Figure 2 and Table 2). This translated to an increase in the rate of hospitalizations with AKI from 15.7 (95% CI, 14.3-17.1) per 10 000 admissions (0.15%) in 1997 to 168.7 (95% CI, 156.9-181.0) per 10 000 (1.68%) in 2022. Joinpoint analyses identified 3 segments of growth. There was significant growth in rates of AKI with an APC of 8.8% (95% CI, 7.7%-9.9%) between 1997 and 2006, 12.8% (95% CI, 11.3%-14.3%) from 2006 to 2019, and 5.1% (95% CI, 1.8%-5.4%) after 2019 (Figure 2A).

Similar to AKI, the yearly incidence of hospitalizations with AKI-D increased between 1997 and 2022, from 981 to 2075. The rate of hospitalizations with AKI-D grew between 1997 and 2022 from 2.4 (95% CI, 2.0-3.0) per 10 000 hospitalizations to 5.5 (95% CI, 4.8-6.2) per 10 000 hospitalizations. The APC was significantly positive and varied from 11.2% (95% CI, 7.5% to 15.0%) between 1997 and 2000, then 0.9% (95% CI, −0.8% to 2.6%) until 2012, and increased back to 3.2% (95% CI, 2.2% to 4.2%) until 2022 (Figure 2B).

APR-DRG Associated With AKI and AKI-D

At all time points, a greater proportion of hospitalizations with AKI were classified as extreme for APR-DRG SOI and ROM compared with hospitalizations without AKI (Figure 3; eTables 3 and 4 and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). The proportion of AKI hospitalizations classified as extreme for APR-DRG SOI and ROM decreased from 59.7% (SOI) and 45.0% (ROM) in 2003 to 32.6% (SOI) and 27.5% (ROM) in 2022. Conversely, the proportion of AKI-D in the extreme category increased from 66.5% (SOI) and 49.7% (ROM) in 2003 to 73.3% (SOI) and 64.4% (ROM) in 2022.

Outcomes

Hospital length of stay was longer for hospitalizations with AKI compared with the complete cohort at all years. In hospitalizations with AKI, the mean length of stay decreased from 22.7 (95% CI, 19.9-25.5) days to 15.2 (95% CI, 14.3-16.1) days from 1997 to 2022. Mortality rates for hospitalizations with no AKI, AKI, and AKI-D are described in Figure 3 and eTable 3 in Supplement 1. Between 2003 and 2022, the mortality rates for hospitalizations without AKI decreased from 57.8 per 10 000 hospitalizations to 44.3 per 10 000 hospitalizations, and the mortality rate for those with AKI decreased from 1814.0 per 10 000 hospitalizations to 643.4 per 10 000 hospitalizations. Model-based analyses showed that the odds of mortality declined over time in both groups, with a greater annual decline among hospitalizations with AKI (OR per year, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.94-0.94) compared with those without AKI (OR per year, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.98-0.99) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). In contrast, mortality among hospitalizations with AKI-D remained stable over time (OR per year, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.99-1.01), with mortality rates of 2467.6 per 10 000 hospitalizations in 2003 and 2577.8 per 10 000 hospitalizations in 2022 (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this analysis of 24 344 876 admissions to pediatric care, we found that the yearly incidence and rates of hospitalizations with AKI and AKI-D have significantly increased over the past 25 years. The mortality rate in hospitalizations with AKI is improving, although the rate in AKI-D has remained stable over time. The increasing rates of AKI and AKI-D diagnoses reflect a multitude of factors. The trends observed in this study have implications for the future of pediatric health care at both patient and population levels.

Existing evidence describing longitudinal trends in AKI primarily comes from studies in adults. Trajectories of non–dialysis requiring AKI in hospitalized adults has been variable. Depending on the included population and study era, there are conflicting reports about whether rates of non–dialysis requiring AKI have grown or remained stable.9,19 Comparable longitudinal data from pediatric centers are limited. In 1 study of pediatric patients aged 1 to 17 years receiving care within an integrated health care delivery system (Kaiser Permanente Northern California) from 2008 to 2016,20 the population-level age- and sex-adjusted incidence of AKI did not change significantly over time, ranging from 0.64 (95% CI, 0.58-0.71) per 1000 person-years in 2008 to 0.75 (95% CI, 0.69-0.82) per 1000 person-years in 2016.20 Among the subset of hospitalized children, however, age- and sex-adjusted risk of AKI increased significantly from 6.0 (95% CI, 5.4-6.6) cases per 100 hospitalizations in 2008 to 8.8 (95% CI, 8.0-9.6) cases per 100 hospitalizations in 2016.20 There is a more consistent signal that rates of AKI-D have been increasing in adults.8,21 Both our findings, and existing pediatric data, mirror the adult literature. A large study using provincial records by Chanchlani et al11 observed the incidence of pediatric AKI-D increased significantly between 1996 and 2015 in Ontario. Our findings also mirror a study by Elgendy et al16 that demonstrated an increase in the yearly incidence of dialysis in neonates in the United States between 2000 and 2017.

The evolving population of youth cared for at pediatric centers may partly explain some of the observed increase in AKI incidence. The increase in complexity of patients admitted to children’s hospitals over time coupled with improving survival of critically ill children has increased the denominator of youths at risk for AKI.22 For example, the risk for AKI in neonates increases at younger gestational age and lower birth weights. Over the past 25 years, survival has increased for those born prematurely, notably for those born extremely preterm (<28 weeks) and with low and extremely low birth weight.23 Outcomes for youths with congenital heart disease continue to improve, yet this population remains at high risk for AKI.24,25,26 Lastly, hematopoietic stem cell transplants and more recently, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies have transformed survival for pediatric patients with cancer, and these therapies are also associated with high rates of AKI.27,28 In this study, malignant neoplasms, cardiovascular conditions, and use of complex medical technology were among the most common underlying diagnoses, reflecting the complex population admitted.

The increasing rates of AKI could also reflect improved recognition of AKI. The development of formalized definition of AKI helped provide a standardized way to diagnose AKI.29,30 These include the Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss, End Stage (RIFLE) classification, pediatric modification of RIFLE, Acute Kidney Injury Network, and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) definitions published in 2004, 2007, 2007, and 2012, respectively.29,30,31,32 Our joinpoint analysis detected that the APC of AKI increased from 8.8% to 12.8% starting in 2006, possibly reflecting the effect of these guidelines on clinical recognition. In addition to standardized definitions, increasing awareness of AKI has also likely led to improving documentation.33,34,35 It is also possible that some of the increase in growth is driven by increased recognition of mild AKI. While KID does not classify AKI by severity, there may be circumstantial evidence. We observed a decrease in mortality rate and DRG severity scores, suggesting greater presence of hospitalizations with lower complexity and risk among patients with AKI. Improvement in mortality rates may in part reflect optimizing care for AKI, but it also likely reflects a growing proportion of mild AKI. In the study from the Kaiser Permanente Northern California,20 which used the KDIGO serum creatinine criteria to diagnose AKI, the increase in AKI incidence over time was largely driven by a significant increase in stage 1 AKI with the combined incidence of severe AKI (stages 2 and 3) remaining mostly unchanged.

While increase in recognition and documentation could partly explain an increase in the incidence of AKI, this would not be the case for dialysis. The increase in AKI-D incidence may be a reflection of changes in the wider availability of dialysis devices and protocols suitable for infants or changes in physician practice patterns regarding willingness to initiate dialysis in increasing numbers of patients with AKI.36 It could also be secondary to an increase in the severity of AKI stemming from an increase in the disease severity, multiple organ dysfunction, and requirement for technology use in hospitalized pediatric patients.22 Despite growing AKI-D hospitalizations with a DRG ROM score of extreme, mortality in AKI-D remained stable at approximately 25%. This mortality rate is consistent with previous studies of pediatric AKI-D.11

We noted other interesting observations comparing the AKI cohort with the larger study population. The mean age of patients with AKI increased with time, while the mean age of the entire cohort remained stable. This may reflect a few things. In 1997, patients older than 18 years represented only 6% of the cohort with AKI, whereas since 2009, 30% or more of those with AKI are young adults. Older patients with long-standing chronic disease may also accumulate repeated renal insults, making them more prone to AKI. And finally, some studies suggest that AKI is better recognized in older children.37 There was a male predominance in hospitalizations with AKI. The hormonal milieu associated with female sex is postulated to be protective against AKI.38 Hospitalizations complicated by AKI also increasingly consolidated in large pediatric hospitals and centers, teaching institutions, and involved care for patients with public health insurance. AKI does not follow geographic margins, so attention must be paid to ensure the expertise for recognition and treatment surrounding AKI is not limited to those in large academic centers.

Our findings have implications for health systems. Just more than a quarter of a million hospitalizations were complicated by AKI during the 9 years of active data collection. This includes 63 923 hospitalizations in 2022 alone. The population of children and young adults who have experienced AKI is sizable and continuing to expand. As recognition and rates of AKI continue to grow, the question of who will provide appropriate care both in the inpatient setting and after discharge becomes important. AKI is associated with significant health care risks in the short and long term. In the inpatient setting, some programs in adults have shown improved outcomes after AKI with the establishment of acute care nephrology programs and early nephrology consultation.39,40 A recent study using provincial administrative datasets from Ontario reported that after a median follow-up of 9.6 years after pediatric AKI-D, 2% developed ESKD, 16% developed CKD, and 17% developed hypertension.41 While it is not known whether follow-up with a nephrologist can ameliorate some of these risks, it is clear that these patients need follow-up with a health care professional who understands the long-term risks of AKI.42 The KDIGO 2026 Clinical Practice Guideline for AKI recommends a holistic approach for AKI follow-up, incorporating predischarge education and planning, comprehensive discharge summaries, and patient education and considering patient-specific risk factors and characteristics of the AKI episode, including the degree of recovery of kidney function.43 Additionally, given the high frequency of comorbid conditions and the need for multidisciplinary outpatient care, it would be important to include other subspecialists in such follow-up care with clear guidance on when to refer back to nephrology.43 Nonetheless, the increased demand for pediatric kidney care stands in stark contrast to the shrinking number of pediatric nephrologists available to provide such care.44

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has strengths and limitations. We benefited from the high-quality data provided by KID. The rigor applied in producing KID allows for accurate estimations on the national scale. By examining results over a 25-year span, we could better illustrate the growth of hospitalizations with AKI and AKI-D. However, given that KID did not include patient-level data, there is a possibility that some hospitalizations within a survey year were readmissions of the same patient. That is why we focused our analysis on the burden of AKI and AKI-D rather than patient-level risk factors for these outcomes. Future research should focus on testing the association of demographic and/or hospitalization covariables with AKI and AKI-D. Because laboratory data were not available in KID, we used diagnosis codes to identify AKI. Use of diagnostic codes for AKI is associated with low sensitivity but high specificity.45 Also, the desire to enhance patient complexity for billing reasons may have contributed in part to increased diagnosis of AKI. Even so, this still reflects an episode of incident AKI that occurred during hospitalization.

Conclusions

In this retrospective study of the KID, the absolute number and rate of pediatric hospitalizations complicated by AKI and AKI-D increased significantly between 1997 to 2022. Pediatric health systems must be prepared to handle the short- and long-term impacts of pediatric AKI. Since pediatric AKI is associated with higher rates of hypertension and CKD, there may be a sizable and growing population of children transitioning to adult care at risk for these outcomes. AKI should remain a priority for ongoing research and therapeutic innovation.
