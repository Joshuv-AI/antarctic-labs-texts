Potential biological application of silver nanoparticles synthesized from Citrus paradisi leaves
Authors: Naseem Akhter, Musarat Batool, Asma Yaqoob, Muhammad Shahid, Faqeer Muhammad, Jallat Khan, Muhammad Ahmad Mudassir, Majeeda Rasheed, Sana Javed, Dunia A Al Farraj, Inshad Alzaidi, Rashid Iqbal, Urszula Malaga-Toboła, Marek Gancarz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract Developing sustainable and eco-friendly methods for nanoparticle (NP) production in an era of environmental consciousness is crucial. This study introduces a novel approach to synthesizing silver (Ag) NPs using Citrus paradisi leaves extract (CPLE) as a green precursor at optimum conditions of the AgNO3 (2 mM) with CPLE in 1:3 ratio, at pH 14 and 80 °C temperature for reaction time of 4 h. The CP@AgNPs were formed and stabilized by Naringen, a major Citrus paradisi component. CP@AgNPs were thoroughly characterized through ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) imaging techniques. CP@AgNPs demonstrated SPR peak at 450 nm, face cubic crystal structure, the average size of 8 nm, rod-shaped particle adsorbed on quasi-spherical shaped agglomerated NPs, significantly impacting both environmental and biomedical fields. In the catalytic degradation experiment, an application for environment pollutant reducer, CP@AgNPs, achieved an impressive 85% degradation efficiency of the methyl orange (MO) dye, showcasing their potential as a sustainable solution for wastewater treatment. Additionally, CP@AgNPs exhibited potent anti-biofilm properties, with half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 0.13 and 0.12 mg/ml against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), respectively, indicating their promise in addressing biofilm-related issues in healthcare and industrial settings. CP@AgNPs also displayed exceptional antioxidant potential with IC50 values of 2.02, 0.07, and 0.035 mg/ml for CPLE, CP@AgNPs, and ascorbic acid, respectively, in scavenging DPPH radical, suggesting their utility in biomedical applications for mitigating oxidative stress. Notably, the bio-activity results of CP@AgNPs surpassed those of CP leaf extract, highlighting the enhanced properties achieved through this green synthesis approach. Introduction

Nanoscale materials have ignited extensive discussions in recent years due to their extraordinary properties, particularly their larger surface area in contrast to their larger-scale counterparts1. Among the array of nanomaterials, NPs composed of noble metals, especially Ag, have gained immense popularity in research laboratories2 and industries3. It is well-documented that Ag NPs exhibit harmlessness to humans while exerting substantial antimicrobial effects on microbes and other eukaryotic microorganisms, even at low concentrations4. This unique attribute positions them as promising antimicrobial agents for various applications, including fabrics5, cosmetics6, household7, medical devices8, food preservation9, and more10.

The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effect is exhibited by noble metal NPs, Such as gold and Ag11, when interacting with light. This effect results from the collective oscillation of conduction electrons at the metal’s surface, excited by incident light of a specific wavelength12. For Ag NPs, the SPR effect is pronounced and plays a crucial role in their optical properties and applications13. It occurs when the frequency of incident light matches the natural frequency of the surface electron oscillating against the restoring force of positive nuclei. The resonance leads to strong light absorption at specific wavelengths and depends on various factors, including the NPs’ size, shape, and dielectric environment. For Ag NPs, SPR results in a distinct absorption peak spectrum at around 400‒450 nm in the visible spectrum. The prominent SPR in Ag NPs is responsible for unique optical properties: intense color, strong scattering, and light absorption utilized in several applications. The application due to this effect may include sensing, where changes in SPR can indicate the presence of specific molecules, and biomedical applications, where SPR can be exploited for imaging and therapeutic purposes.

Ag NPs are revered for their exceptional bio-compatibility, environmental friendliness, active binding sites, and remarkable catalytic efficiency14. Their utility extends to environmental remediation, particularly in purifying water contaminated by industrial discharges, such as plastics, cardboard, food residues, steel, painting pigments, and ammunition, all posing severe environmental threats15. Additionally, Ag NPs have piqued the curiosity of researchers in the biomedical field for their remarkable anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antimicrobial, biofilm inhibition, and antioxidant characteristics when dealing with a broad spectrum of pathogenic strains16.

Nevertheless, it is crucial to emphasize that surface-capped Ag NPs can also act as oxidant antagonists. Oxidants induce cell damage, and natural or artificial antioxidants can mitigate or delay such damage17. Moreover, numerous investigations have highlighted the promise of NPs to enhance the antioxidant properties of bio-molecules, underscoring the opportunity to boost Ag NPs’ antioxidant capabilities through NPs18.

Literature studies show that NPs can enhance the overall efficiency of wastewater treatment processes by preventing biofilm formation of harmful bacteria that hinder the water cleaning systems/equipment process due to their slime nature19. Similarly, the pollutants in wastewater release different free radicals in the water, so reducing oxidative stress can enhance the overall efficiency of wastewater treatment processes20. This may lead to better removal of pollutants, increased lifespan of treatment equipment, and a more stable and reliable wastewater treatment system21.

Dye and bacterial contaminants are significant environmental pollutants that pose severe risks to the ecosystem and human health. Industrial effluents from textile, paper, and plastic industries often contain high concentrations of synthetic dyes. These dyes are visually unappealing but also toxic, carcinogenic, and resistant to biodegrade. Their persistence in aquatic environments leads to reduced light penetration and oxygen levels, adversely affecting marine life and disrupting the photosynthesis in marine plants. On the other hand, bacterial contamination originates from various sources, including untreated sewage, agricultural runoff, and hospital waste. Water bodies’ pathogens can cause waterborne diseases, posing significant public health risks. Antibiotic-resistant bacteria further complicate this issue, making it crucial to develop effective strategies for bacterial degradation. Recent studies have highlighted nanomaterials’ potential in catalyzing the degradation of these pollutants. For instance, Ag NPs synthesized through green methods have shown promise in degrading various dyes and exhibiting significant antibacterial activity. These nanomaterials offer dual functionality, making them ideal candidates for addressing the dual challenges of dye and bacterial contamination. Traditionally, Ag NPs are synthesized using physical and chemical methods, including electrochemical and photo-/chemical reduction approaches22. Many of these techniques involve numerous steps, substantial energy consumption, limited material conversion, intricate purification processes, and the employment of unsafe chemicals23. The chemical synthesis process can also introduce harmful chemical species onto the NPs’ surface, which could have adverse effects24. To address these concerns and in light of the environmental challenges faced by our planet, green chemistry has emerged as a sustainable alternative, advocating for eco-friendly materials and processes25.

Furthermore, plant-based synthesis of NPs offers distinct advantages over other biological methods that are often time-consuming, such as cell culturing26. Among these approaches, using plant extracts has surfaced as a promising avenue for the environmentally friendly production of Ag NPs with unique attributes. Various researchers have documented the successful generation of Ag NPs using different extracts of Citrus (C) members, such as fruit extract of C. grandis27, C. reticulata28, and C. sinensis29, leaves extract of C. aurantifolia30, C. aurantium31, C. medica32, C. sinensis33, peel extract of C. aurantium34, C. clementina35, C. limetta36, C. limon37, C. medica38, C. sinensis39, C. unshiu40, and fruit peel extract C. aurantifolia41, C. limetta42, C. limon, C. sinensis, C. tangerina43, C. macroptera44, C.reshni, C. latifolia, and C. paradisi41.

Most studies have focused on utilizing peels, fruits, or other parts of plants for NP formation. Specifically, the biosynthesis of Ag NPs using Citrus paradisi (grapefruit) peel extract has been reported45. However, this study represents the first attempt to explore and utilize Citrus paradisi leaf extract (CPLE) for green synthesis of Ag NPs. The novelty lies in using CPLE as a green precursor, an area previously unexplored in the literature. Furthermore, it only confirms the successful formation of CP@AgNPs through detailed characterization (FTIR, UV−vis, XRD, and FE-SEM) and explores its unique multifunctional applications. The application includes catalytic activity, anti-biofilm properties, and antioxidant potential. In the catalytic activity, the CP@AgNPs demonstrated remarkable efficiency in degrading MO dye at 85%, underscoring their potential for sustainable wastewater treatment. In the anti-biofilm activity, CP@AgNPs exhibited potent anti-biofilm against E. coli and S. aureus, offering a promising approach for combating biofilm-associated issues in both medical and industrial sectors. In antioxidant activity, the CP@AgNPs possess superior antioxidant properties compared to CPLE alone, highlighting their enhanced biofilm efficacy. These findings emphasize the novelty of using Citrus paradisi and the broad-spectrum potential of synthesized NPs for environmental and biomedical applications. This study introduces a new sustainable pathway for the synthesis of Ag NPs, advancing the field of green nanotechnology.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Preparation of Citrus paradisi leaves extract (CPLE)

The Citrus paradisi (CP) leaves were soaked in distilled water, dried, and snipped. The 20 g of chopped fresh leaves were immersed in 100 milliliters of hot pure water for 20 min. The resultant solution was filtered utilizing filter paper (Whatman no. 1). The filtrate, CPLE, was placed in the refrigerator at 4 °C and used in subsequent procedures.

Condition optimizations for synthesis of CP@AgNPs

The synthesis of CP@AgNPs was performed by optimizing temperature conditions, reaction time, molar concentration of AgNO3 solution, metal precursor solution/CPCE ratios, and pH of the reaction mixture. The reaction was carried out for 1 h for each optimization (except time optimization), and the synthesized Ag NPs were studied using electronic absorption. The optimization study of temperature, time, and concentration of Ag precursor solution, precursor solution/CPLE ratio, and pH for the synthesis of CP@AgNPs was conducted in ranges of 60‒90 °C (Fig. 3A), 1‒5 mM (Fig. 3B), 1:1‒1:4 ratio (Fig. 3C), 1‒5 h (Fig. 3D), 6‒14 pH value (Fig. 3E), pH variation was performed by NaOH (5%) solution without use of any buffer solution. The formation of CP@AgNPs at all reaction conditions was monitored using a UV−vis spectrophotometer. The summarized conditions of optimizations for synthesizing CP@AgNPs are expressed in Table 2.

Biosynthesis of optimized CP@ AgNPs

20 ml of AgNO3 (2 mM) was added to the 60 ml CPLE using optimized reaction conditions (obtained from the 2.2.3 section). The pH of the reaction mixture was maintained at 14, and the resultant mixture was heated at 80 °C for 4 h. The color change from orange to dark brown gradually, observed by the naked eye, confirmed the formation of CP@AgNPs. After diluting with distilled H2O, the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to isolate the pelleted CP@AgNPs from the supernatant. The CP@AgNPs pellet was removed and dried at 60 °C. The aqueous suspension of the CP@AgNPs had a brownish tinge. Figure 2 depicts the schematic diagram of the synthesis of CP@AgNPs.

Schematics illustrating the synthesis procedure of CP@AgNPs.

Characterization

The CP@AgNPs formation was preliminary verified using the Agilent Cary 60 UV−vis spectrophotometer. The functionalities accountable for bioreduction and stabilization were analyzed using the Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer. The physical features and morphology of CP@AgNPs were visualized using the NovaNano FE-SEM. The crystalline nature and crystallite size of CP@AgNPs were determined by D2 Phaser Bruker X-ray diffractometer with CuKα radiations (λ = 1.54 Å, 45 kV, 40 mA) in 20–90° range (scan rate, 0.02 min‒1).

Applications

Catalytic degradation of MO

The catalytic effectiveness of CP@AgNPs in degrading MO dye was tested using NaBH4. A clean quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length was filled with a mixture containing 2 ml of MO (0.1 mM) and 0.5 ml of 10 mg CP@AgNPs/5 ml distilled water solution. 0.5 ml of fresh NaBH4 (0.5 M) solution was introduced and carefully mixed to begin the catalytic process. Subsequently, UV−vis spectra were recorded at an interval of 4 min for 20 min in the 300–550 nm range at an ambient temperature51.

Biofilm inhibition analysis

The nutritional broth (100 ml), test sample (100 ml), and 20 ml of inoculation bacterial solution (20 ml) were added to a cleaned 96-well flat-bottom plastic tissue cultivation plate. The negative control wells were enriched with nutritional broth. The plates were left to incubate (24 h) at 37 °C. Pure phosphate buffer (220 ml) was used thrice to rinse the items inside all wells. Additionally, the plates were violently agitated to eliminate any bacteria that were not adhering. The biofilm was fixed by pouring 99% methanol (220 ml) into each well. The plates were then emptied and left to dry for 15 min. The crystal violet 50% solution (220 ml) was used to dye the plate wells for five min. Any surplus stain was eliminated through a rinsing process. The plates were left to dry naturally, and the adhered dye was subsequently dissolved again using glacial acetic acid (220 ml) with a concentration of 33% (v/v). A microplate reader (BioTek, USA) was employed to determine the optical density at 630 nm. Each bacterial strain underwent three tests, with the results reported as a mean52.

Antioxidant activity assessment

The radical capturing capacity of CP@AgNPs was assessed using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH). The CP@AgNPs solution (3ml) was combined with the freshly made 0.004% DPPH methanolic solution (1ml). Subsequently, it was kept in a dark environment at room temperature for 30 min. Then, at 517 nm, the absorbance shift was observed. The same procedure was employed to assess the antioxidant potential of CPLE. As a positive control, ascorbic acid was used, which had not been exposed to the test sample. The majority of the trials were run in pairs. The percentage of DPPH inhibition was determined as Eq. 1.

The graph of inhibition percent versus log concentration of the sample was used to calculate 50% inhibition (IC50) at a particular concentration53.

Results and discussion

Preparation of optimized CP@AgNPs

The synthesis optimization of CP@AgNPs was performed by varying the temperature condition, reaction duration, AgNO3 solution concentration, metal precursor solution to CPLE extract ratio, and pH. The transition in color, shifting from a light-yellow shade to a deep brown, was noted after increasing the temperature from 60‒90 °C with reaction time (1 h), AgNO3 solution (1 mM), metal precursor solution to CPLE extract ratio (1:1) and non-adjusted pH in each experiment. The color change is mainly owed to SPR due to the simultaneous oscillation of valance-shelled electrons of CP@AgNPs in resonance with the corresponding UV−vis waves54. The synthesis at 90 °C has a sharper peak and less width with a lower baseline, indicative of narrow size distribution and a more stabilized colloidal solution. The temperature of 80 °C was chosen to optimize for the formation of stable CP@AgNPs with dark brown color (Fig. 3A)55 due to practical constraints such as equipment limitation, cost, and energy efficiency, comparison of previous studies, ensuring stability and reproducibility, which was challenging to control at 90 °C.

The color change was also observed with the changing AgNO3 concentration (1‒5 mM), making other parameters constant, such as reaction temperature (80 °C), reaction time (1 h), metal precursor solution to CPLE extract ratio (1:1), and non-adjusted pH for each experiment. Since the color turned black after using > 2 mM AgNO3 solution, 2 mM was chosen as the optimized concentration (Fig. 3B)56. Likewise, the metal precursor solution and CPLE were combined in different ratios (i.e., 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4)57 maintaining constant reaction temperature (80 °C), metal precursor solution (2 mM), reaction time (1 h), and non-adjusted pH for every experiment. Consequently, the higher concentrations of CPLE resulted in higher peak absorbance in the UV−vis spectra. Since this relationship was not linear using a 1:4 ratio, 1:3 was selected as the optimum ratio (Fig. 3C). Likewise, by increasing the time period of the reaction from 1 to 4 h, making other parameters constant for each reaction, reaction temperature (80 °C), metal precursor solution (2 mM), CPLE ratio (1:3), and non-adjusted pH, the absorption intensity of UV−vis spectra went on increasing (Fig. 3D). The formation of CP@AgNPs also appeared to be influenced by pH, where the changing coloration with rising pH made other conditions constant for each reaction. Reaction temperature (80 °C), metal precursor solution (2 mM), CPLE ratio (1:3), and reaction time (1 h) indicated the size variation of the CP@AgNPs58. In this way, the formation of highly-stable CP@AgNPs was achieved at pH 14 (Fig. 3E). Considering optimized values of reaction conditions, the AgNO3 (2 mM) was allowed to react with CPLE in 1:3 ratio at pH 14 and 80 °C for 4 h (Fig. 3A)56. This resulted in a reduced Ag ion and formed stable Ag NPs with an appreciable size of 8 nm with a dark brown color.

Characterization

The UV−vis spectrum of synthesized CP@AgNPs prepared at optimized reaction conditions displayed a characteristic SPR peak around 450 nm. This peak is typical for Ag NPs and serves as solid evidence of the reduction of Ag ions in Ag NPs, confirming the formation of CP@AgNPs (Fig. 4A). The broadness in peaks represents polydispersity with a wide range of particle size distribution, which could be attributed to bio-molecules present in leaf extract acting as capping agents. These bio-molecules reduce reducer ions and stabilize the stabilizers, preventing aggregation. Additionally, the inset image of the colloidal solution indicates the formation of AgNPs through the visible change in color from yellow (CPLE) to brown. The synthesized Ag NPs were found stable when monitored for 15 d (Fig. S1 of supplementary data).

UV−vis spectrum (A), FTIR spectra (B), and XRD pattern (C) of CP@AgNPs.

The FTIR spectrum supports the synthesis of Ag NPs by involving bioactive compounds in CPLE. In the FTIR spectrum of CP@AgNPs, the significant peaks at 3474.6 cm‒1, 1640 cm‒1, 1399cm‒1, and 1048 cm‒1 were associated with OH, C=O, C–O, C–O–C stretching vibrations, respectively (Fig. 4B). The broad O–H stretching band suggests that the hydroxyl group reduces Ag ions. In comparing the CP@AgNPs spectrum to pure CPLE, the shifts in peak positions after Ag NP formation indicate strong interactions between the NPs and these functional groups, confirming their dual role in reducing and stabilizing the Ag NPs. The FTIR peaks demonstrated that naringin, a flavonoid, the main component in Citrus paradisi, might be responsible for the synthesis and stabilization of CP@AgNPs59.

The XRD pattern of synthesized CP@AgNPs reveals four distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 38.1°, 44.2°, 64.7°, and 77.6° which corresponds to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes, confirming the face-centered cubic crystal (FCC) structure of Ag (lattice parameter: α = 4.08 Å)60. The sharp peaks demonstrate the crystalline nature of Ag NPs. The most intense peak at 38.1° is associated with the (111) plane, indicating that the Ag NPs predominantly orient in this plane, a common feature observed in Ag NP synthesis. Additionally, broader peaks and smaller peaks with low intensities may suggest the presence of organic molecules from leaf extract. These organic molecules might aid NP formation and contribute to functional properties like antimicrobial activity. The average size of CP@AgNPs was found using the Debye–Scherrer equation (D = kλ/βcosθ). By calculating the width of the (111) lattice plane, the average crystalline size of the CP@AgNPs was estimated to be 8 nm (Fig. 4C).

Figure 5A shows a high-magnification view of CP@AgNPs, where the NPs appear densely packed and agglomerated. The particle exhibits a quasi-spherical shape with a somewhat uneven surface, suggesting a high degree of interaction between individual NPs. This agglomeration could be due to the inherent adhesive properties of CP extract with metal, which facilitate the binding of Ag NPs. Figure 5B provides a medium magnification image, revealing a more extensive view of CP@AgNPs distribution. The image depicts a network-like structure, where rod-shaped particles are observed to be adsorbed onto the agglomerated NPs. This indicates that CP extract constituents not only support the dispersion of Ag NPs but also enable the adsorption of rod-like particles, potentially enhancing the material’s overall surface area and reactivity. Figure 5C shows lower magnification images highlighting the broader texture and surface morphology. The image suggests a fibrous and porous structure. The fibrous structure may contribute to the material’s mechanical stability and provide a site for NP adsorption. Figure 5D presents a histogram of particle size distribution, which was determined using Image J software. The data indicates that the average size range of CP@AgNPs is approximately 5‒25 nm with a diameter of 10‒15 nm. This range is consistent with crystallite size obtained from XRD, confirming the successful synthesis of NPs within the expected nanometer scale. The red curve overlying the histogram represents a Gaussian fit to size distribution data, illustrating the NPs’ relatively narrow size distribution and uniformity.

SEM images at scale bar of 500 nm (A), 50 µm (B), and 10 µm (C) and particle size distribution (D) of CP@AgNPs.

Applications

Catalytic reduction of MO dye

Owing to its frequent use as an indicator in several analyses and severe health risks, MO was used as a model to test the dye degradation potential of CP@AgNPs. Without CP@AgNPs, the MO dye reduction operation stalled after up to several hours of testing61. Also, the reducing agent NaBH4 could not efficiently and independently complete the reduction. The MO absorption spectrum showed no change because of the prolonged gradual decrease in dye concentration or the ineffectiveness of NaBH462,63. Interestingly, a sharp drop in absorbance was immediately after adding the CP@AgNPs as the nanocatalysts64. The adsorption phenomena of MO on the catalyst’s surface (CP@AgNPs) play a crucial role in the catalytic reduction of MO. It maintains the local dye concentration at the catalyst’s surface, enhances the dye’s contact with the dye and activates the catalyst site, facilitates electron and hydrogen transfer between makes, and makes N=N of dye more susceptible to reduction by weakening and cleavage of the azo bond. Furthermore, it stabilizes the intermediates on the catalyst surface, preventing them from reacting with other species in the solution, thus promising the reduction of dye to complete. As a result of MO degradation, the absorption intensity drops significantly and becomes near after 20 min (Figs. 6A, 5B), when the percentage reduction of MO was 85% (Fig. 6C). The degradation efficiency was calculated by a formula expressed in Eq. 265. The reduction of MO is a pseudo-first-order reaction with a slope of 0.17596.

Antibiofilm activity

The antibiofilm activity of the synthesized Ag NPs (CP@AgNPs) was assessed against E. coli and S. aureus biofilms, with IC50 values compared to those of plant extract (CPLE) and the standard antibiotics, ciprofloxacin (Fig. 7A). The results demonstrate that CP@AgNPs exhibit significant antibiofilm activity, with a low IC50 of 0.13 mg/ml for E. coli and 0.12 mg/ml for S. aureus. These values are close to those of ciprofloxacin (0.07 mg/ml for E. coli and 0.08 mg/ml for S. aureus), indicating that the Ag NPs synthesized from CPLE have strong potential as antibiofilm agents. In contrast, the CPLE alone exhibited much weaker antibiofilm properties, with IC50 values of 2.55 mg/ml for E. coli and 0.46 mg/ml for S. aureus. The enhancement of biofilm inhibition by CP@AgNPs compared to CPLE underscores the role of the NPs in improving antimicrobial properties.

Antibiofilm (A) and antioxidant (B) activity.

The antibiofilm efficacy of CP@AgNPs is particularly pronounced against S. aureus, where the IC50 value of 0.12 mg/ml is comparable to ciprofloxacin and reflects an inhibition percentage of 42.59%. This efficacy is significantly higher than the 38.33% inhibition observed against E. coli, where the inhibition was as low as 1.96%. The difference in efficacy between CP@AgNPs and CPLE is substantial, with the NPs being 19.56-fold more effective in inhibiting E. coli biofilm formation and 3.91-fold more effective against S. aureus. This suggests that the bio-synthesized Ag NPs enhance the inherent biofilm-inhibitory properties of plant extract and demonstrate superior efficacy against S. aureus.

The improved antibiofilm activity of CP@AgNPs can be attributed to the synergistic effects of Ag ions and bioactive compounds in CPLE. The Ag NPs disrupt biofilm formation by penetrating the biofilm matrix and interacting with microbial cells, destabilizing the extracellular polymeric substance and hindering bacterial adhesion. The enhanced inhibition against S. aureus may be due to differences in the structural composition of biofilms formed by Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria like S. aureus typically produce a thicker peptidoglycan layer, which may be more susceptible to the action of Ag NPs and bioactive compounds from CPLE. The disruption of quorum sensing, inhibition of matrix production, and generation of reaction oxygen species by CP@AgNPs are likely contributing factors to this enhanced biofilm inhibition.

While ciprofloxacin remains a highly effective antibiofilm agent, the close performance of CP@AgNPs suggests that the NPs could serve as a potent alternative, particularly in cases where antibiotic resistance is a concern. The similar IC50 values and inhibition percentages indicate that CP@AgNPs could provide comparable therapeutic benefits with the added advantage of being synthesized via a green, sustainable process. Moreover, the slightly better performance of CP@AgNPs against S. aureus suggests that these NPs might be particularly valuable in treating biofilm-related infections caused by Gram-positive pathogens.

Antioxidant assay

The antioxidant properties of the synthesized CP@AgNPs were evaluated and compared to crude CPLE and a standard antioxidant (ascorbic acid) using the DPPH assay, which measures the radical scavenging ability. As shown in Fig. 7B, the IC50 values, representing the concentration required to scavenge 50% of DPPH radicals, were determined for all tested materials.

The CP@AgNPs exhibited an IC50 value of 0.07 mg/ml, which is significantly lower than the 2.02 mg/ml observed for CPLE. This indicates that CP@AgNPs possess markedly higher antioxidant activity, approximately 50.5 times stronger than CPLE. Remarkably, potential as a powerful antioxidant agent.

The enhanced antioxidant activity of CP@AgNPs can be attributed to the role of phytochemicals, such as naringin, adsorbed on the surface of the NPs. These phytochemicals aid in stabilizing the Ag NPs and enhance their capacity to act as electron donors (Fig. 8), hydrogen atom donors, and singlet oxygen quenchers66. This dual role likely contributes to the superior radical scavenging ability of CP@AgNPs compared to the CPLE alone. The transfer of single electron and hydrogen atoms between the Ag ions and these phytochemicals further promotes the reduction of free radicals, highlighting the synergy between the NPs and bioactive compounds in the plant extract. Additionally, the potent antioxidant performance of CP@AgNPs suggests potential applications in mitigating oxidative stress in environmental contexts, such as wastewater treatment. By neutralizing free radicals generated by organic pollutants, these NPs could play a crucial role in reducing oxidative damage, thereby improving the overall efficiency of wastewater treatment processes and protecting aquatic ecosystems from pollutant-related harm17,67,68.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully demonstrates the green synthesis of Ag NPs using Citrus paradisi leaves extract (CPLE), forming CP@AgNPs with remarkable properties. The synthesis optimization, distinguished by varying temperature, reaction duration, AgNO3 concentration, precursor solution to CPLE ratio, and pH, revealed the optimum conditions for producing stable CP@AgNPs are 2 mM AgNO3, 1:3 precursor to extract ratio, pH 14, and a reaction temperature of 80 °C for 4 h. These conditions yielded NPs with a consistent size of 8 nm, confirmed by UV−vis spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, and SEM analysis. The CP@AgNPs exhibited a characteristic SPR peak at 450 nm, indicating successful formation, which plays a crucial role in the synthesis and stabilization of the NPs. XRD analysis confirmed the face-centered cubic crystal structure of Ag NPs, while SEM images corroborated the particle size distribution and morphology. CP@AgNPs demonstrated an impressive catalytic reduction capability, achieving 85% MO dye degradation, showcasing their potential in sustainable wastewater treatment. Their potent biofilm activity was evident with low IC50 values against E. coli and S. aureus biofilms, indicating their significant promise for health care and industrial applications. Moreover, the antioxidant activity of CP@AgNPs, with IC50 of 0.07 mg/ml, was markedly superior to that of CPLE, underscoring their potential in biomedical applications to mitigate oxidative stress. Overall, this study highlights the efficacy of an environmentally benign synthesis method for Ag NPs, presenting CP@AgNPs as a versatile and potent solution for environmental remediation and healthcare challenges. The results pave the way for applying CP@AgNPs in various fields, emphasizing the importance of sustainable practice in NP synthesis. Future research should focus on scaling up the green synthesis process for industrial applications and exploring CP@AgNPs interactions with various pollutants and pathogens. Further investigation into their antibacterial and antioxidant mechanisms will optimize their use in healthcare. Studies on bio-compatibility and therapeutic applications in living organisms are also essential, along with exploring another plant extract to enhance NP properties and broaden the scope of green synthesis.
