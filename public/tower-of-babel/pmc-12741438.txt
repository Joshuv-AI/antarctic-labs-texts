An unusual case of primary coronary angioplasty using the right humeral artery as a rescue vascular access: a case report and literature review
Authors: Mohamed Sarsari, Oualid Kerrouani, Anass M'ghari, Omar Nafii, Chaimae Aboueddahab, Ouassima kihoul, Jamila Zarzur, Mohamed Cherti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains pivotal in managing ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), enabling prompt revascularization. While radial and femoral accesses are conventionally preferred, the brachial (humeral) artery can represent a valuable alternative, particularly when punctured proximally in the upper arm (high brachial/humeral access). We present a 70-year-old hypertensive patient with recent ischemic stroke and end-stage renal disease on dialysis, who developed an inferobasal STEMI complicated by slow atrial fibrillation and cardiogenic shock. Radial and femoral accesses were not feasible due to diffuse arterial disease, including iliac occlusion, radial thrombosis, and a thrombosed arteriovenous fistula. Surgical exposure of the right high brachial (humeral) artery enabled successful PCI with drug-eluting stent implantation, without immediate complications. This report highlights humeral/brachial access as an effective rescue option, and to our knowledge, represents one of the rare descriptions of surgical humeral access for primary PCI in STEMI with cardiogenic shock.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the standard of care for STEMI due to its efficacy in rapid coronary reperfusion. The transradial approach (TRA) has gained widespread preference owing to reduced bleeding and vascular complications compared to the transfemoral approach (TFA) [1, 2]. However, when both radial and femoral routes are unavailable, the brachial (humeral) artery remains a valuable fallback access [3]. In interventional cardiology, ‘humeral access’ generally refers to a higher puncture of the brachial artery at the upper arm, while ‘brachial access’ often designates the antecubital fossa site. In this manuscript, we specifically refer to a proximal high brachial (humeral) approach obtained via surgical cut-down.

The novelty of this case lies in the fact that surgical humeral (high brachial) access was used as a last resort in a patient with STEMI, cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation with bradycardia, and severe peripheral vascular disease, conditions that severely limited conventional access options. This case therefore provides valuable insights into alternative vascular access strategies in extreme clinical scenarios.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man with a past medical history notable for long-standing arterial hypertension and a right sylvian ischemic stroke three weeks earlier, which resulted in dysarthria and left hemiparesis. He had also been followed in nephrology for four years for end-stage chronic kidney disease of undetermined etiology, managed with peritoneal dialysis.

The patient was initially admitted to the nephrology department for management of a displaced peritoneal dialysis catheter. On the seventh day of hospitalization, he developed chest pain suggestive of acute myocardial infarction.

On clinical examination, he was found to be in hemodynamic shock, with a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg and a heart rate of 37 beats per minute. The electrocardiogram demonstrated atrial fibrillation with a slow ventricular response (37 bpm), ST-segment elevation in the inferior and basal leads, and reciprocal changes in the high lateral leads (Fig. 1A). A diagnosis of inferobasal ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by bradyarrhythmic atrial fibrillation and cardiogenic shock was established.

(A) An electrocardiogram showing ST-segment elevation in the inferobasal region with a mirror image in the high lateral region, and slow atrial fibrillation with a heart rate of 37 bpm. (B) An electrocardiogram showing the recovery of a sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 60 beats per minute.

Resuscitation measures were promptly initiated, including placement of a left femoral central venous line and a right radial arterial line. The patient received loading doses of clopidogrel and aspirin, and intravenous isoprenaline with vasopressor and positive inotropic agents was started. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed akinesia of the basal and mid-inferior and inferolateral walls, without mechanical complications. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved with good RV function.

He was transferred to the cardiac catheterization laboratory approximately five hours after the onset of symptoms. A temporary pacing lead was inserted and set at 60 bpm. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was indicated. However, multiple vascular access attempts failed: Arteriography demonstrated complete occlusion of the left common iliac artery and a severe, uncrossable stenosis of the right external iliac artery. Although the right iliac stenosis was clearly visualized during the procedure, no fluoroscopic image was saved for documentation (Fig. 2). In addition, a thrombosed arteriovenous fistula was present at the left elbow flexure (Fig. 3), and the right radial artery used for hemodynamic monitoring was also thrombosed.

Angiographic image showing complete occlusion of the left common iliac artery (arrow). A severe, uncrossable stenosis of the right external iliac artery was also demonstrated during the procedure, but unfortunately no fluoroscopic image was recorded at that time.

AV fistula at the bend of the left elbow, thrombosed.

Systemic thrombolysis was contraindicated in view of the ischemic stroke that had occurred less than four weeks earlier. An invasive approach remained the only option. Consequently, the right humeral artery was selected as a rescue access site.

In our patient, surgical exposure of the high brachial (humeral) artery was deliberately chosen instead of a percutaneous puncture. This decision was based on several factors: the patient was in profound cardiogenic shock with severe hemodynamic instability, requiring immediate and reliable arterial access; the vessels were heavily diseased and calcified, which increased the risk of percutaneous puncture failure or uncontrolled bleeding; surgical cut-down allowed direct visualization and precise arterial control, thereby minimizing the risk of complications such as hematoma, arterial rupture, or distal ischemia. For these reasons, surgical access was considered the safest and most effective option in this high-risk emergency context.

Surgical exposure of the right humeral artery was performed in the catheterization laboratory under local anesthesia. A 6F radial sheath was successfully introduced (Fig. 4). Coronary angiography demonstrated a thrombotic occlusion of the mid-right coronary artery. Primary PCI was carried out, with implantation of a drug-eluting stent, achieving optimal angiographic results (Fig. 5). Arterial closure was secured by surgical suturing without complications.

(A) Image of coronary angiography showing an occlusion of the right coronary artery at the level of the proximal knee. (B) Angiographic image after the recanalization of the right coronary artery and stent implantation.

The post-procedural course was favorable. The patient regained a spontaneous cardiac rhythm eight hours after intervention (Fig. 1B), and the temporary pacing lead was removed 24 hours later. After three days in the cardiac intensive care unit, he was transferred back to the nephrology service for ongoing care.

Discussion

This case illustrates the complexity of performing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in the setting of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by severe hemodynamic compromise. Our patient presented with an inferobasal STEMI associated with slow atrial fibrillation and cardiogenic shock, requiring immediate reperfusion therapy. In such critically unstable patients, timely access to the coronary arteries is essential, yet conventional vascular routes were unavailable due to diffuse arterial disease. The successful use of a surgical high brachial (humeral) access in this context underscores the importance of mastering alternative strategies for coronary revascularization in life-threatening scenarios.

From a diagnostic perspective, the initial differential diagnosis of acute chest pain in this patient included several possibilities. Pulmonary embolism can mimic STEMI, particularly in dialysis patients with multiple thrombotic risk factors, but was ruled out by the characteristic ST-segment elevation and angiographic findings. Acute pericarditis could also produce ST-segment changes, yet the presence of regional wall motion abnormalities and the absence of pericardial effusion made this unlikely. Given the context of prior stroke, an aortic dissection extending to the coronary ostia might have been considered, but no clinical or imaging evidence supported this. The combination of ECG changes, echocardiographic wall motion abnormalities, and angiographic thrombotic occlusion of the right coronary artery confirmed the diagnosis of STEMI.

The radial approach is now the preferred route in most centers due to superior safety and patient comfort. In the United States, radial access increased from ~ 20% of PCIs in 2013 to nearly 58% in 2022 [1]. Radial PCI in STEMI reduces mortality and major bleeding compared with femoral PCI [2, 4]. Randomized trials such as SAFARI-STEMI confirmed non-inferiority in terms of ischemic outcomes, with less bleeding [5].

Radial access is predominant in STEMI, whereas femoral access remains relevant in cases requiring larger-bore catheters (e.g. mechanical circulatory support) or in anatomically unsuitable radial arteries [2, 4].

Although rarely used, high brachial (humeral) access is valuable when radial and femoral routes are unavailable. It is particularly useful in patients with polyvascular disease, diabetes, or advanced renal failure on dialysis, where vascular access is often compromised [3, 6]. In our patient, iliac occlusion, radial thrombosis, and a thrombosed arteriovenous fistula rendered conventional access impossible, thereby justifying humeral access.

Compared with other published cases [3, 6], our report is distinctive because surgical exposure was performed instead of percutaneous puncture. The decision was guided by the patient’s unstable hemodynamic condition, the need for secure arterial control, and the presence of severely diseased, calcified vessels that increased the risk of bleeding or failed puncture. This approach, though rarely reported, demonstrates that surgical high brachial access can be a safe and effective alternative in carefully selected situations.

Other alternatives such as axillary and carotid access remain exceptional and are usually reserved for extreme cases. Comparatively, radial access is the safest and most widely adopted, femoral access is still indispensable for complex or large-bore procedures, and humeral access represents a rare but valuable rescue route [7, 8].

In summary, this case adds novelty to the existing literature by showing that surgical high brachial (humeral) access can enable lifesaving primary PCI in the setting of STEMI with cardiogenic shock and extensive peripheral vascular disease. It highlights the need for interventional cardiologists to remain proficient with alternative access techniques and to tailor their strategy according to patient-specific anatomical and clinical challenges.

Conclusion

The right humeral (high brachial) artery can serve as a feasible and effective rescue access for primary PCI in patients with STEMI when conventional radial and femoral approaches are unavailable. Our case is distinctive in that surgical exposure of the humeral artery was performed in the context of cardiogenic shock and extensive peripheral vascular disease, allowing successful revascularization where no other access route was possible.

This report underscores two key messages:

Maintaining proficiency with alternative vascular access techniques, including high brachial (humeral) exposure, is essential for interventional cardiologists faced with anatomically challenging or high-risk patients.
