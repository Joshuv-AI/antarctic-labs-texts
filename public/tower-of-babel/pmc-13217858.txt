A city-scale optimization framework for biomass-to-clean-energy transitions: The KAMPALA-TIMES model
Authors: Ismail Kimuli, John Baptist Kirabira, Francis Mujjuni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRapid urbanization in Sub-Saharan African cities is accompanied by persistent reliance on traditional biomass for household energy, posing challenges for climate mitigation, public health, and sustainable development. This study develops the KAMPALA-TIMES model, a city-scale, bottom-up optimization framework that integrates localized energy data with IPCC AR6 SSP-RCP pathways to evaluate long-term energy transition strategies for Kampala, Uganda, from 2022 to 2060. Four scenarios (business-as-usual, enhanced biomass utilization, mixed renewable integration, and policy-driven carbon reduction) are assessed. Results show that efficiency improvements alone stabilize emissions but do not significantly reduce them, whereas electrification combined with renewable expansion substantially lowers system emissions. The policy-driven carbon reduction pathway achieves the deepest mitigation, reducing emissions to approximately 3.4 MtCO2e by 2060 and nearly eliminating household cooking emissions by 2040. These findings demonstrate that coordinated fuel substitution, infrastructure investment, and policy alignment are critical for urban decarbonization and provide a transferable framework for energy planning in rapidly growing, data-constrained cities.

Introduction

Urban centers in developing countries such as Kampala face rapidly growing energy demand alongside escalating environmental and public health challenges.1,2,3 In Kampala, more than 85% of households rely on traditional biomass, primarily charcoal and firewood, for cooking, resulting in substantial carbon emissions, deforestation, and declining urban air quality.4,5 Evidence from ESMAP and global health studies further indicates that this dependence intensifies indoor air pollution, particularly affecting women and children, and contributes significantly to respiratory illness and greenhouse gas emissions.6

Despite expanding electrification initiatives and policies promoting cleaner fuels, inefficient biomass combustion technologies remain deeply rooted in urban household energy systems due to affordability constraints, fuel availability, and behavioral inertia.7 These ongoing socio-technical barriers underscore the need for analytical frameworks that can diagnose biomass reliance and assess scalable pathways toward sustainable household energy transitions.

To address this challenge, the present study employs the KAMPALA-TIMES model, a bottom-up engineering optimization framework designed to analyze urban energy system dynamics and long-term decarbonization pathways.8 The model incorporates technical, economic, and environmental variables while embedding global scenario architectures, specifically the IPCC AR6 and shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs),9,10 within a localized urban modeling environment. Four SSP-RCP-aligned scenarios are evaluated: business-as-usual (BAU); enhanced biomass utilization (EBU), emphasizing modern combustion and efficiency technologies11; mixed renewable integration (MRI), incorporating solar, hydropower, and sustainable biomass resources; and policy-driven carbon reduction (PDCR), aligned with SSP1 and including carbon pricing, fuel-switching incentives, and regulatory reforms.

Positioning these scenarios within a unified computational framework allows for a comparative analysis of Kampala’s potential energy futures. This highlights trade-offs among costs, emissions, and technology adoption, while capturing multisector interactions across households, commerce, industry, agriculture, and transport. Incorporating global decarbonization frameworks into a city-scale optimization model strengthens ongoing debates on climate-aligned urban energy planning in rapidly growing African cities and provides a scalable method for integrating socio-technical complexity into energy systems analysis.12,13,14 In this context, the lack of city-scale optimization frameworks that simultaneously address biomass dependence, urban sectoral dynamics, and climate-aligned policy pathways motivates this analysis.

Accordingly, this study addresses four key questions: What role does biomass play in Kampala’s carbon emissions? How effectively can the KAMPALA-TIMES framework assess viable decarbonization pathways? What emission reductions are possible under different policy scenarios? And which policy interventions most effectively support a clean and equitable household energy transition? By linking a computational modeling pipeline with policy design,15,16 the study promotes practical strategies for sustainable biomass use and urban carbon reduction, helping to advance broader efforts to meet long-term energy and environmental goals.17

This research leverages a city-scale computational pipeline (the KAMPALA-TIMES model) to examine household biomass dynamics in Kampala. Unlike national or regional energy system models that often overlook urban diversity, KAMPALA-TIMES explicitly models city-specific demand profiles, fuel-use patterns, and policy options, filling a significant gap in Sub-Saharan Africa’s urban energy planning literature.2,4,9 The framework incorporates IPCC AR6–aligned SSP-RCP pathways within a localized optimization structure and evaluates four integrated transition scenarios (BAU, EBU, MRI, and PDCR) that jointly explore sustainable biomass use, LPG transition, renewable electrification, and policy tools such as carbon pricing and subsidies.10,11 Its modular design allows for future enhancements such as participatory planning, behavioral calibration, and informal-sector energy modeling, essential in low-income urban settings.1,3 By examining the biomass-energy-carbon nexus at the household level, the study uncovers cross-sector interactions while maintaining practical relevance and empirical grounding.12,14 Overall, the framework offers a scalable and adaptable city-scale modeling approach that enhances the science-policy interface and can be applied to rapidly growing cities across the Global South.16,17,18

Within this context, existing research on sustainable biomass use and urban energy transitions highlights both opportunities and structural challenges in household energy transformation. Biomass remains the dominant household energy source across much of Sub-Saharan Africa because of its accessibility, affordability, and cultural familiarity.19,20,21 However, its traditional use in open fires and rudimentary stoves leads to substantial inefficiencies, harmful emissions, and environmental degradation.22,23 Indoor air pollution from biomass combustion disproportionately affects women and children, intensifying respiratory disease burdens and broader household health risks.24

Recent studies highlight the potential of advanced biomass technologies and cleaner fuels, such as biogas, ethanol, and LPG, to reduce emissions and improve efficiency.25,26 However, widespread adoption relies on supportive policy environments, access to technology, financing options, and behavioral change strategies.7,27 Successful biomass transitions require coordinated supply-chain development and policy alignment.

Urbanization further complicates these transitions. Kampala, whose population exceeds 4.5 million and is projected to double by 2050,28 faces rapidly increasing energy demand that strains infrastructure, elevates emissions, and degrades environmental quality.29 Household energy use remains dominated by charcoal and firewood, with consumption averaging approximately 1.4 kg of charcoal per capita per day and generating more than 3.1 MtCO2e annually.30,31 This dependence reinforces indoor air pollution, forest degradation, and health risks, particularly for women and children.32 Transitioning toward LPG, electricity, and modern biomass technologies is therefore both a development and climate priority.21,33

Comparable studies from other African cities reveal similar structural challenges. SSP-based modeling in Dar es Salaam demonstrates persistent biomass dependence and emissions lock-in,34 while evidence from Nairobi, Accra, and Lusaka highlights continuing energy poverty, institutional fragmentation, and limited access to clean fuels.35,36,37 These shared dynamics underscore the broader relevance of Kampala’s energy transition challenges for rapidly growing cities across Sub-Saharan Africa.

At the same time, Kampala offers significant opportunities for a fair and sustainable energy transition. Emerging solutions include efficient cookstoves, biogas systems, solar-electric hybrids, and decentralized energy planning, with modern biomass technologies such as gasifiers and bioethanol stoves showing particular promise for low-income households.38 Electrification programs and off-grid solar initiatives are also growing, though they remain modest in scale.

A central challenge, however, lies in understanding how to scale these technologies under realistic socio-economic and policy conditions. Addressing this requires analytical frameworks that integrate localized energy data, urban sectoral dynamics, and global climate scenarios within a coherent modeling architecture. Although TIMES-VEDA has been widely used in national and metropolitan energy planning, its application to urban biomass transitions in rapidly growing African cities remains limited. Existing studies often overlook spatial heterogeneity, institutional fragmentation, and behavioral complexity within informal household energy systems, producing models that are insufficiently granular or transferable.

This study addresses this gap by developing a city-specific TIMES-VEDA framework for the Kampala Metropolis that combines localized data, SSP-RCP scenario structures, and bottom-up technology details. The model offers insights into urban energy transitions in data-limited settings while establishing a versatile computational approach applicable across the Global South.

Recent research increasingly emphasizes the roles of artificial intelligence (AI), financial development, and digital governance in speeding up renewable energy deployment and reducing energy poverty. It highlights how algorithmic optimization, institutional capacity, and human capital influence sustainability outcomes. While such studies show how AI can improve grid efficiency, mobilize green finance, and better resource allocation, they usually focus on macroeconomic or cross-country levels and often overlook the complex, technology-rich dynamics of urban energy systems.

In contrast, the current study uses a bottom-up engineering optimization approach based on the TIMES-VEDA framework, in which algorithmic decision-making is embedded within a transparent linear programming architecture. Instead of viewing AI as an external influence, KAMPALA-TIMES implements algorithmic governance directly by optimizing systems that minimize costs within clear resource, technology, and policy constraints. This difference is important: It shows that computational optimization can be separate from broader AI-finance ideas and can produce policy-relevant, structurally sound decarbonization pathways, even in environments with limited data.

By demonstrating how a city-scale optimization framework integrates biomass dependence, electrification pathways, and policy-driven decarbonization in a rapidly urbanizing African context, this study offers a transferable analytical approach for assessing sustainable household energy transitions in data-constrained cities across the Global South.

The rest of the paper presents the modeled transition pathways generated by the KAMPALA-TIMES framework. The Results section describes trajectories of total primary energy supply (TPES), electricity generation, sectoral energy demand, household fuel transitions, carbon emissions, and energy system costs across the four scenarios. The Discussion section interprets these findings by examining the main drivers of urban energy transitions, including electrification processes, clean-cooking pathways, and multisector policy coordination. The paper also summarizes key insights for sustainable urban energy planning, discusses the study’s limitations, and highlights directions for future research. Detailed documentation of the modeling framework, data sources, and scenario logic is provided in the STAR Methods section.

Results

The KAMPALA-TIMES model generates a comprehensive set of energy system indicators, including TPES, electricity generation disaggregated by fuel type, aggregate final energy demand for the Kampala metropolis, household energy demand disaggregated by sector and fuel type, sectoral and household carbon emissions, and annual system expenditures. All energy quantities are reported in petajoules (PJ), where 1 PJ = 1015 J. Carbon emissions are expressed in million tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (MtCO2e), and energy system costs are reported in billion U.S. dollars (Bn US$). Scenario outcomes are evaluated across the four modeled pathways, BAU, EBU, MRI, and PDCR, to identify structural differences in energy supply, fuel substitution, emissions trajectories, and investment dynamics.

In contrast, the MRI pathway moderates TPES growth, reaching 244.81 PJ by 2060. This outcome reflects a structural shift toward electrified end uses powered by renewable electricity, thereby reducing upstream conversion losses and lowering primary energy requirements per unit of final energy service. Increased deployment of solar photovoltaic and hydropower technologies contributes to this transition by replacing solid fuels and liquid hydrocarbons with lower-loss electricity-based supply chains.40,41

The PDCR scenario produces the lowest TPES requirement (211.82 PJ), reflecting the combined effects of electrification, efficiency improvements, and economy-wide carbon pricing. The carbon constraint shifts the least-cost solution toward lower-emission technologies and accelerates substitution toward renewable electricity across multiple sectors.42

Across all scenarios, raw biomass remains a major contributor to TPES, accounting for approximately 120 PJ in the baseline configuration, consistent with observed regional energy-use patterns.43 However, the MRI and PDCR scenarios significantly reduce charcoal consumption, reflecting greater reliance on electricity-based energy services and more efficient conversion technologies.

Discussion

Structural drivers of urban energy transition

The scenario outcomes indicate that differences in energy and emissions trajectories across pathways arise primarily from structural transformations in the energy system rather than from incremental efficiency improvements alone. Under BAU and EBU, continued reliance on biomass-based household energy and fossil-fuel-based transport leads to sustained growth in energy demand and emissions, underscoring the persistence of carbon-intensive infrastructure and consumption patterns when fuel substitution does not occur.

By contrast, the MRI and PDCR pathways show that structural carrier substitution and electrification significantly reshape the urban energy system. Rapid expansion of solar photovoltaic generation is a key mechanism for emissions reduction in the model, reflecting both the favorable solar resource base and declining technology costs. However, the feasibility of high shares of renewable electricity depends on complementary investments in grid reinforcement, system flexibility, and institutional coordination, as well as land-use planning and financing conditions that influence deployment dynamics.41

The strongest mitigation performance occurs under the PDCR scenario, in which economy-wide carbon pricing and efficiency constraints reinforce renewable deployment and accelerate structural shifts in TPES. Similar patterns have been reported in global decarbonization analyses, which show that coordinated policy frameworks are necessary to sustain long-term energy transitions.75,76

Stakeholder engagement also played a key role in contextualizing the model results, particularly in refining assumptions about biomass dependence in the BAU pathway and the policy feasibility of the PDCR scenario. Participatory approaches like these have been shown to enhance the legitimacy and usability of energy system models for policy development.77

These transition pathways also align with broader international climate objectives, including the Paris Agreement and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 7, 11, and 13). Reduced dependence on biomass and increased reliance on hydropower and solar photovoltaics may yield co-benefits, including improved air quality, public health, and employment opportunities in emerging renewable-energy value chains.44

Electricity system transformation and electrification dynamics

Electricity generation is central to the broader urban energy transition. Hydropower remains the structural backbone of the modeled electricity system, providing dispatchable generation capacity that supports the integration of variable renewable technologies, such as solar photovoltaics. Similar complementarities between hydropower and variable renewables have been observed in several renewable-dominated electricity systems.43,45,46,47,48,49

The rapid increase in solar photovoltaic generation under the MRI and PDCR scenarios reflects declining technology costs and the growing role of electricity as a substitute for direct fuel combustion in end-use sectors such as cooking and transportation. Under the PDCR pathway, carbon pricing further accelerates this transition by making low-carbon electricity generation more competitive.52,53

Together, these results suggest that scaling hydropower and solar PV in combination can provide the electricity backbone required for electrification-driven urban decarbonization, particularly in rapidly growing cities where energy demand is expected to expand substantially over the coming decades.78

Sectoral transformation and demand dynamics

Sectoral demand results indicate that reductions in total energy demand are driven primarily by structural carrier substitution and electrification, rather than by the suppression of energy services. Electrified technologies and higher-efficiency energy carriers reduce the energy intensity of economic activity while maintaining or expanding energy services.54

Industrial demand remains substantial across all scenarios, underscoring the central role of manufacturing and process heat in urban economic development. This suggests that long-term decarbonization strategies must prioritize cleaner industrial fuels, electrified process heat, and efficiency improvements, rather than relying solely on demand contraction.79

Transport electrification also emerges as an important mechanism for moderating urban energy demand. Electric mobility technologies deliver higher end-use efficiency than internal combustion engines, reducing energy consumption per unit of mobility service.80

The residential sector illustrates that improvements in household welfare and access to modern energy services may increase energy demand even as emissions decline. Similar patterns have been observed in studies of energy transitions in developing urban contexts, where improved living standards coexist with declining emissions intensity.81,82,83

Stakeholder consultations with the Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA), the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Development (MEMD), industry associations, and transport unions helped validate the plausibility of these sectoral trajectories and informed assumptions about technology adoption and infrastructure constraints.

These findings indicate that reducing urban energy demand requires structural change across sectors, including electrifying transportation and industry, upgrading residential energy services, and implementing coordinated policies that promote low-carbon technologies.84

Household energy transitions and clean cooking

The household demand results indicate that cooking energy services dominate residential energy consumption across all modeled pathways. This structural pattern is typical of many urbanizing regions in Sub-Saharan Africa, where cooking accounts for most household energy demand.55,83

The limited reduction observed under the EBU scenario suggests that efficiency improvements alone cannot fundamentally transform household energy systems if reliance on traditional biomass remains widespread.56

In contrast, the MRI and PDCR scenarios indicate that larger reductions occur when households switch to electric cooking technologies and modern energy services, which have higher conversion efficiencies and lower upstream energy losses.57

These results also show that improvements in household welfare, such as increased use of appliances, lighting, and refrigeration, can occur alongside reductions in emissions intensity when energy services are supplied by lower-carbon energy carriers.85

Stakeholder consultations with women’s groups, clean cookstove entrepreneurs, and community organizations highlighted several practical constraints to adopting clean cooking, including affordability barriers, cultural cooking practices, and appliance accessibility. These insights underscore the need to complement system-level energy transitions with targeted social and financial mechanisms to accelerate the adoption of modern cooking technologies.86

Household fuel transitions and clean cooking pathways

The household fuel demand results indicate that reductions in residential emissions depend primarily on substituting away from solid biomass, rather than on reducing total household energy demand.59

The significant increase in LPG consumption across several scenarios reflects its role as a transitional clean cooking fuel, capable of displacing traditional biomass where electrification remains constrained by limited infrastructure or high appliance costs.60

Simultaneously, the growing share of electricity in MRI and PDCR reflects a longer-term trend toward electric cooking technologies and appliances, which offer higher efficiency and lower emissions intensity.61

These results suggest that household decarbonization depends largely on replacing high-emission fuels, such as charcoal and fuelwood, with modern fuels and electricity rather than on reducing energy service demand.62

Stakeholder consultations with households, fuel distributors, and local authorities highlighted several barriers to rapid fuel switching, including LPG cylinder availability, affordability constraints, and limited familiarity with modern cooking technologies. These socio-technical constraints help explain the gradual transition patterns observed in the modeled pathways.87

Under the PDCR scenario, the combination of carbon pricing, electricity expansion, and accelerated LPG adoption produces the most pronounced shift away from biomass. These transitions may also yield co-benefits, including improved indoor air quality, reduced pressure on deforestation, and enhanced energy access.88

Emissions drivers and urban decarbonization pathways

The emissions trajectories show that differences across scenarios stem primarily from structural changes in the energy system, rather than incremental efficiency gains. Under BAU and EBU, continued reliance on biomass and fossil fuels leads to sustained growth in carbon emissions, illustrating the persistence of carbon lock-in when structural fuel substitution does not occur.

The stronger performance of MRI demonstrates the emissions benefits of expanding renewable electricity and electrifying end uses. However, residual emissions in transport and industry indicate that technology-driven transitions alone may not achieve deep urban decarbonization without complementary policy measures.54,64

The PDCR scenario delivers the most substantial emissions reductions because the carbon constraint reshapes investment decisions across sectors, thereby accelerating electrification and clean-fuel adoption.65,89

Transport remains the dominant source of emissions across most scenarios, underscoring the importance of mobility transitions. Electrified transport systems and mass rapid transit can substantially reduce emissions in dense urban environments with high vehicle-kilometer demand.90,91

Residential emissions decline rapidly when traditional biomass use is replaced by modern cooking technologies and cleaner fuels. These results underscore the importance of clean cooking transitions for reducing emissions in urban household energy systems.66,67,92

Stakeholder consultations with KCCA, MEMD, and civil society organizations helped refine assumptions about transport electrification, charcoal phase-out timelines, and adoption constraints. These insights improved the scenario’s realism and supported the interpretation of the model results.

Overall, the results suggest that coordinated multisector policy interventions, including electrified transportation and clean household energy transitions, are central to achieving sustained reductions in urban emissions.93,94

Household emissions and clean cooking transitions

The household emissions results indicate that reductions in residential carbon emissions are closely linked to fuel substitution away from solid biomass rather than to reductions in total household energy demand. Under BAU and EBU, continued reliance on woody biomass and charcoal leads to relatively high emissions, underscoring the persistence of carbon-intensive household energy practices.

The stronger performance of MRI demonstrates the emissions benefits of increased electrification and the adoption of modern fuels. However, the persistence of biomass use under this pathway suggests that technology diffusion alone may not fully eliminate dependence on solid fuels without complementary policy support and infrastructure development.69

The PDCR scenario delivers the largest emissions reductions by accelerating the transition to electricity and LPG and by reducing reliance on traditional biomass.70 This pattern underscores the central role of clean cooking transitions in reducing residential emissions in rapidly urbanizing contexts.

Stakeholder consultations with community groups, utilities, and LPG distributors helped refine assumptions about affordability constraints, safety perceptions, and infrastructure limitations. These insights informed the design of plausible fuel-switching pathways in the modeled scenarios.

Investment dynamics and energy transition financing

The expenditure results illustrate how different decarbonization pathways alter the composition of energy system costs. Under BAU and EBU, expenditures remain dominated by fuel purchases, reflecting continued reliance on biomass and petroleum fuels.71

In contrast, MRI and PDCR shift expenditures toward capital investments in renewable generation and electrified technologies, thereby reducing long-term fuel dependence. This pattern reflects the cost-minimizing logic of the optimization model, which favors higher upfront investments when they reduce discounted lifetime system costs by improving efficiency and lowering fuel use.94

The stronger investment signal under PDCR reflects the influence of carbon constraints and electrification policies that accelerate the deployment of renewable electricity technologies and efficient end-use systems.74

Stakeholder consultations with MEMD, KCCA, UEGCL, development finance institutions, and private-sector actors underscored the importance of financing mechanisms, including blended finance and public-private partnerships, in enabling capital-intensive energy transitions.

These results indicate that reallocating expenditures from recurrent fuel consumption to capital investment in clean technologies can support long-term energy system transformation while reducing exposure to fuel price volatility and environmental externalities.95,96,97

Equity considerations and just transition implications

The transition pathways identified in this study also have important implications for equity and energy access in rapidly urbanizing cities. The PDCR scenario shows that economy-wide policy instruments, particularly carbon pricing, electrification, and clean fuel substitution, can simultaneously achieve substantial emissions reductions and expand long-term access to modern energy services.98

However, the transition to cleaner fuels may also create short-term affordability pressures for households that remain dependent on traditional biomass fuels. Without appropriate policy safeguards, rapid decarbonization measures may impose disproportionate costs on lower-income households that face financial, informational, or infrastructure barriers to adopting modern energy technologies.

While emerging research highlights AI and digital financial systems as accelerators of renewable energy deployment and energy-poverty reduction, the Kampala case shows that algorithmic optimization alone is insufficient without coherent infrastructure, regulatory alignment, and affordability safeguards. Recent studies demonstrate how AI can enhance the efficiency of renewable investment through financial deepening,99 strengthen the institutional and equity dimensions of sustainability transitions,100 improve carbon-efficiency outcomes through nonlinear technological pathways,101 and alleviate energy poverty via digitally enabled diffusion of renewables.102 However, the TIMES-VEDA engine used in this study, though itself an algorithmic optimization system solving intertemporal resource allocation via linear programming, produces outcomes that remain bounded by institutional feasibility, informal market dynamics, infrastructure readiness, and socio-political constraints. This reveals a critical boundary condition: advanced computational pipelines, whether AI-driven or optimization-based, cannot substitute for governance capacity. Their effectiveness depends on the alignment of policy instruments, financial structures, infrastructure investment, and distributional safeguards. In this respect, KAMPALA-TIMES complements AI-centered sustainability scholarship by demonstrating how transparent, systems-level optimization can generate structurally grounded transition signals in contexts where digital infrastructure, financial development, and institutional capacity remain uneven across the Global South.

To ensure that urban decarbonization pathways remain socially inclusive, complementary policy instruments may be necessary. These could include lifeline electricity tariffs, targeted clean-cooking subsidies, consumer financing for appliances, improved access to LPG cylinders, and stronger enforcement against illegal charcoal supply chains. Such measures can reduce barriers to adoption and enable the environmental and public health benefits of modern energy transitions.

These findings underscore that successful urban decarbonization strategies must integrate technological innovation with institutional and social policies, ensuring that climate mitigation efforts also promote equitable energy access and improved living standards.

System-level implications for urban energy transitions

Overall, the results show a consistent pattern at the system level across energy supply, demand, and emissions outcomes. Pathways focused on incremental efficiency improvements within existing fuel structures, such as the EBU scenario, yield only limited system-wide changes. In contrast, pathways that enable carrier substitution and structural electrification, such as MRI, achieve much larger reductions in primary energy use and emissions.

The introduction of economy-wide carbon pricing in the PDCR scenario further strengthens these dynamics by guiding investment choices, technology selection, and end-use behaviors toward lower-carbon outcomes. This pattern indicates that urban decarbonization relies less on the performance of individual technologies and more on the alignment of system-wide incentives, infrastructure development, and policy coordination.

Overall, the analysis shows that urban energy transitions are driven more by structural changes in fuel sources, infrastructure investment, and policy coordination than by incremental efficiency gains. The scenario comparison reveals that pathways focused primarily on improving efficiency within existing fuel systems have limited mitigation impact, whereas strategies centered on electrification, expanded renewables, and coordinated policies yield much greater reductions in energy demand growth and carbon emissions. Notably, the PDCR pathway demonstrates how carbon pricing, deployment of renewable electricity, and electrification of end uses can align investments and technology adoption with low-carbon urban energy systems. Future research could enhance this method by incorporating income-specific demand patterns, behavioral adoption factors, and macroeconomic interactions to better understand the distributional and institutional aspects of urban energy transitions.

Limitations of the study

The KAMPALA-TIMES framework relies on the standard TIMES-VEDA assumption of intertemporal cost minimization with perfect foresight. While this approach effectively identifies cost-efficient transition pathways, it does not fully capture behavioral dynamics in real-world household energy systems. In fast-growing urban areas such as Kampala, energy decisions are shaped by social and technical factors, including fuel stacking, informal biomass markets, liquidity constraints, safety concerns about LPG, and trust in electricity reliability, which can slow the adoption of modern energy technologies. Therefore, some scenario results should be viewed as indicators of structural transition rather than precise behavioral predictions. For example, BAU and EBU may underestimate biomass persistence, while MRI and PDCR might serve as optimistic upper bounds for fuel switching. Additional uncertainty could arise from rebound effects, in which improved energy access increases demand for electricity and modern fuels. Future updates to the KAMPALA-TIMES framework will include income-differentiated households, an explicit model of informal markets, and hybrid behavioral modules to better address uncertainties surrounding fuel adoption, spending, and emissions trajectories.

Method details

Study design and analytical framing

This study employs a mixed-methods design grounded in a critical realism paradigm, integrating quantitative modeling and qualitative insights to understand the socio-technical complexity of biomass use and carbon reduction in Kampala’s urban households.103 This approach helps interpret how structural conditions, institutional constraints, and human agency interact within urban energy transitions.

Quantitatively, the analysis focuses on the KAMPALA-TIMES model, a bottom-up engineering optimizer that examines long-term energy system evolution over a 38-year planning horizon (2022–2060) under technological, economic, and environmental constraints.104,105,106 The model represents the full energy chain, from primary supply through conversion, transmission, and end use across households, commerce, agriculture, industry, and transport, and includes a dedicated electricity production module. Its modular architecture enables scenario comparison through a unified computational pipeline.

Scenario development is grounded in the IPCC AR6 and the SSP–RCP scenario architecture, producing four policy-relevant pathways: Business-as-Usual (BAU), Enhanced Biomass Utilization (EBU), Mixed Renewable Integration (MRI), and Policy-Driven Carbon Reduction (PDCR). These scenarios differ in policy ambition, fuel substitution, and technology deployment, enabling a structured assessment of transition trajectories.

Model inputs were compiled from national statistics, peer-reviewed and gray literature, and targeted, field-informed consultations. Stakeholder engagement with utilities, non-governmental organizations, regulators, and community actors informed parameter selection, validated scenario narratives, and strengthened policy relevance through co-creation. This integrative approach improves empirical robustness and enhances the model’s suitability for analyzing scalable biomass-to-clean-energy transitions in rapidly urbanizing contexts.

Computational workflow of the KAMPALA-TIMES model

The KAMPALA-TIMES model is a city-scale adaptation of the TIMES-VEDA family of bottom-up engineering optimization tools.54 Like other TIMES implementations, it represents the full energy chain, from primary resource extraction through conversion, infrastructure, and end-use services, and operates under explicit resource, technology, and policy constraints.107 TIMES models remain among the most widely used frameworks for long-term decarbonization analysis in both developing and industrialized contexts.38,108

KAMPALA-TIMES operates within the ETSAP modeling architecture, using linear programming to minimize the total discounted system cost over the 2022–2060 horizon under a perfect-foresight assumption. The model determines least-cost energy pathways through intertemporal decision-making across the residential, commercial, transport, industrial, agricultural, and electricity-generation sectors. Local specificity is captured through disaggregated household energy data harmonized with national statistics and IPCC Tier 1 emission factors. Input processing and solution management were performed using VEDA-FE and VEDA-BE, with model compilation in GAMS and optimization with the CPLEX solver.

To align the framework with the realities of urban systems, the computational workflow comprised four interconnected stages. First, model inputs were preprocessed, including fuel prices, technology costs, efficiency values, projected service demands, emissions factors, and primary energy supply profiles. Second, scenario assumptions were encoded using IPCC AR6 SSP–RCP structures to implement the four pathways (BAU, EBU, MRI, and PDCR). Third, a detailed Reference Energy System (RES) was developed to map primary fuels, conversion technologies, and end-use options across sectors. Fourth, intertemporal optimization runs were conducted to determine least-cost technology portfolios and associated energy flows, followed by post-processing and diagnostic validation to assess internal consistency, calibration accuracy, and alignment with stakeholder-informed assumptions.

Decarbonization scenario logic

Four decarbonization scenarios were developed within the KAMPALA-TIMES framework using a structured SSP–RCP-aligned design to ensure methodological rigor and cross-scenario comparability. Each scenario represents a distinct policy and technology narrative reflecting different levels of fuel switching, renewable deployment, efficiency improvements, and regulatory ambition. The following table summarizes the modeled archetypes and their alignment with global SSP–RCP pathways.

The Business-as-Usual (BAU) scenario represents a continuation of current trends and serves as the reference case. The Enhanced Biomass Utilization (EBU) scenario focuses on advanced biomass technologies and clean-cooking improvements. The Mixed Renewable Integration (MRI) scenario involves accelerated electrification and a broader expansion of renewable energy sources. The Policy-Driven Carbon Reduction (PDCR) pathway models an ambitious transition aligned with the Paris Agreement and deep decarbonization goals.

By integrating local urban energy drivers with SSP–RCP global narratives, the scenario set provides a consistent computational foundation for analyzing cost-effective technology combinations, energy flows, and emissions pathways. This approach enables the model to identify distinct yet comparable mitigation and computational patterns across Kampala’s growing urban system and to evaluate the feasibility, trade-offs, and policy needs of differentiated decarbonization strategies.

The BAU scenario assumes that Kampala’s energy system continues on its current trajectory, with no additional energy or climate policies beyond 2022. It aligns with SSP2–4.5/SSP3–7.0 (middle-of-the-road) or regional-rivalry futures and assumes the continued dominance of fossil fuels and traditional biomass, particularly charcoal and firewood, in the household sector. Integration of other renewables remains minimal, limited to pre-existing hydropower and a small number of planned solar installations. Energy efficiency improves only through natural technological turnover rather than targeted policy measures.8 BAU therefore serves as the analytical baseline against which more ambitious transition pathways are evaluated.

The EBU scenario is a biomass-focused mitigation pathway aligned with SSP2–4.5 (regional sustainability), highlighting biomass modernization as a transitional decarbonization option under moderate policy ambition. In this pathway, sustainability is achieved through regulated feedstock sourcing, improved conversion efficiency, and stronger governance of the biomass supply chain. The scenario assumes managed biomass sources and more efficient, lower-emission conversion technologies, including improved cookstoves, biomass boilers, biogas systems, and biomass-based combined heat and power, along with minimal monitoring and certification systems to reduce leakage from unregulated biomass harvesting. Within the modeled system, modern biomass serves a dual purpose by reducing end-use fuel demand and providing a dispatchable renewable supplement to variable generation. Although bioenergy with carbon capture and storage is not explicitly modeled, the scenario captures partial mitigation gains through biomass modernization and efficiency improvements. EBU thus serves as a transitional benchmark, demonstrating the mitigation potential of biomass-focused reforms with moderate institutional capacity while highlighting their limitations in the absence of broader electrification and economy-wide policy tools.

The MRI scenario is a technology-driven, supply-side transition pathway aligned with SSP1–2.6 (sustainability-driven mitigation), emphasizing the accelerated deployment of renewable electricity as the primary mitigation lever. This pathway combines the expansion of solar photovoltaic generation, hydropower, and limited modern biomass with the growing electrification of cooking and transport. Renewable uptake is driven by improving technology economics and supportive policies, such as feed-in tariffs and the gradual reform of fossil-fuel subsidies. The scenario produces substantial decarbonization of the electricity sector, but its implementation depends on enabling conditions beyond generation capacity alone, including grid reinforcement, storage or demand-side flexibility, institutional coordination, land availability for utility-scale PV, and access to finance for distributed systems. MRI demonstrates the technical feasibility of renewable-led urban decarbonization under favorable investment and governance conditions, while also showing that residual non-electric fossil fuel use may persist without stronger cross-sector policy constraints.

The PDCR scenario outlines a high-ambition decarbonization pathway aligned with SSP1–1.9 and the IPCC AR6 Paris-compatible mitigation logic. It applies an economy-wide carbon price that rises from USD 20 tCO2e−1 in 2025 to USD 100 tCO2e−1 by the end of the planning horizon, thereby internalizing carbon externalities and driving cost-effective system transformation. In response to this increasingly binding carbon signal, the model endogenously shifts investment toward rapid electrification of end-use sectors, accelerated deployment of renewable electricity, and associated system-support infrastructure, including grid reinforcement and flexibility measures. Traditional biomass combustion declines sharply, and remaining biomass use is redirected to lower-carbon, regulated applications, while fossil fuel use contracts across the system. PDCR achieves the deepest emissions reductions among all scenarios and therefore serves as the highest-ambition reference case for scalable, multisector urban decarbonization.

To ensure transparency in scenario operationalization, all policy, technology adoption, and resource availability assumptions were translated into explicit model-level parameter changes. The full mapping from narrative assumptions to modeled parameters is provided in Table S1. Because long-term urban transitions are sensitive to techno-economic uncertainty, the perturbation ranges used to test scenario stability are documented in Table S2.

Stakeholder engagement

Stakeholder engagement was incorporated into model development as a systematic process to refine assumptions, test scenario plausibility, and enhance empirical validity. Engagement took place iteratively during model scoping, baseline calibration, and scenario creation, involving city authorities, national energy agencies, utilities, transport planners, industry representatives, civil society organizations, and community-linked actors.

Stakeholder input informed key quantitative and narrative elements of the model, including sectoral demand trajectories, technology adoption ceilings, policy timing, and infrastructure deployment constraints. For example, consultations on transport and household energy highlighted behavioral, institutional, and supply chain barriers, leading to more conservative assumptions about electric mobility uptake and LPG adoption than initially specified. Similarly, input from forestry-related institutions and non-governmental organizations informed sustainability constraints on biomass supply under the EBU scenario. These adjustments were incorporated ex ante into the optimization structure rather than introduced only as a post hoc interpretation.

To ensure transparency and traceability, the translation of stakeholder insights into modeling decisions is documented in Table S3, which links stakeholder categories to specific parameter changes and scenario updates. Stakeholder perspectives also serve as a qualitative plausibility check for near-term BAU projections, comparing them with recent sectoral developments and institutional expectations, thereby strengthening confidence in the reference baseline.

Stakeholder engagement was therefore used not only for contextual validation but also for structural calibration of transition pathways. Constraints related to institutional feasibility, adoption rates, and infrastructure sequencing, all derived from stakeholder engagement, were directly incorporated into the model as implementation limits. This approach enhanced policy realism while maintaining the internal consistency of the optimization logic and ensuring that simulated transitions were practically implementable within institutions rather than solely representing techno-economic optima.

Sensitivity analysis

A deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted on the calibrated BAU reference case to assess the robustness of the model’s least-cost solution under uncertainty in key techno-economic assumptions. Key parameters influencing long-term investment and fuel substitution decisions, specifically technology capital costs, fuel prices, and the social discount rate, were varied individually within plausible ±10% bounds relative to baseline values.

Across all perturbations, the model showed a consistent structural response: traditional biomass remained the primary household energy source, and total emissions continued to rise over the projection period. Although the variations affected overall system costs and led to minor changes in technology adoption, they did not cause fundamental shifts in the optimal energy mix or the long-term emissions path. This suggests that baseline results are driven primarily by structural factors, affordability issues, and infrastructure lock-in rather than by uncertainties in individual parameters. Quantification and statistical analysis

The KAMPALA-TIMES framework is a deterministic linear optimization model and therefore does not employ inferential statistical testing in the traditional experimental sense. Quantification relies on model-generated outputs derived from explicitly defined assumptions and constraints. The main outputs evaluated in this study include total primary energy supply, electricity generation by fuel type, sectoral final energy demand, household energy demand, household fuel demand, sectoral carbon emissions, household carbon emissions by fuel type, and annual energy system costs.

All energy quantities are reported in petajoules (PJ), and carbon emissions are reported in MtCO2e using IPCC-based coefficients. Scenario outcomes were compared across the four modeled pathways to identify differences in fuel substitution, technology adoption, system costs, and emissions trajectories. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using one-at-a-time deterministic parameter variations around the calibrated BAU reference case. Results are interpreted as structural cost-optimal scenario outcomes under the stated assumptions rather than probabilistic forecasts.
