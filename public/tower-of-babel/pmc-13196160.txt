Prevalence, diagnosis gap, and treatment patterns of hypertension in a primary care electronic health record cohort in Qatar: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Melis Dorter, Ahmad Eid, Naela Darwish Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a major global public health concern and a leading contributor to cardiovascular and renal morbidity and mortality. In Qatar, diverse expatriate populations constitute a large proportion of Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC) service users, yet evidence on nationality- and region-specific hypertension burden and care gaps in primary care remains limited. This study aimed to estimate hypertension prevalence and assess disparities in diagnosis and treatment by nationality and World Health Organization (WHO) region.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using electronic health record (EHR) data from the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC) in Qatar. Adults aged ≥ 18 years with sufficient blood pressure data to derive a reliable summary measure (defined as the median of the most recent three readings) were included. Hypertension was defined using a composite operational definition incorporating blood pressure measurements, recorded diagnosis, and antihypertensive medication use. Undiagnosed hypertension was defined as elevated blood pressure without a recorded diagnosis, and absence of recorded treatment was defined as no antihypertensive prescription documented during the study period among individuals meeting hypertension criteria. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify associated factors.ResultsAmong 1,006,122 registered adults, 644,055 with sufficient blood pressure data were included. Mean age was 39.9 ± 13.6 years, and 53.3% were female. Overall hypertension prevalence was 26.0% in the analytic cohort. Among individuals meeting hypertension criteria, 70.2% had no recorded antihypertensive prescription during the study period, while 29.8% had at least one recorded prescription. Undiagnosed hypertension was observed in 8.3% of individuals meeting hypertension criteria. Hypertension prevalence varied across nationality groups (22.5% in Qataris to 36.7% in Filipinos) and WHO regions (23.6% in the Eastern Mediterranean Region vs. 36.2% in the Western Pacific Region). Increasing age and cardiometabolic comorbidities were strongly associated with hypertension.ConclusionsHypertension remains common in this large primary care EHR cohort, with substantial proportions of individuals lacking recorded diagnosis or treatment. These findings should be interpreted in the context of EHR-based data capture and may reflect gaps in documentation as well as care. Strengthening hypertension identification and management within routine primary care settings may improve cardiovascular risk reduction.

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most common chronic conditions managed in primary care and represents a major driver of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. In recent years, the prevalence of hypertension has declined and its treatment has improved substantially in some countries, such as Canada, South Korea, Germany, and Chile. Despite advances in clinical guidelines, more than half of individuals with hypertension globally remain either undiagnosed or untreated [1–3]. Because hypertension management—including diagnosis, risk stratification, medication titration, monitoring, and lifestyle counseling—takes place predominantly in primary care, primary care–based epidemiological data are essential for understanding real-world disease burden and identifying gaps in care [4–7]. Such data provide insight into population-level patterns that cannot be captured through hospital-based cohorts, which often reflect more severe or selective patient groups.

Understanding variation in hypertension across population subgroups is increasingly recognized as a priority within global public health strategies. Studies from diverse health systems have shown that ethnicity and nationality are significant determinants of hypertension prevalence and treatment patterns [8–12]. These differences likely reflect a combination of biological, lifestyle, and healthcare-related factors. Sub-nationality analyses are therefore crucial for identifying high-risk groups and designing culturally appropriate, equity-oriented primary care interventions. Despite this growing recognition, evidence from the Middle East—particularly from Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries—remains limited and often derived from small samples, hospital-based populations, or studies lacking detailed primary care–level demographic stratification, restricting generalizability to routine clinical practice [13–15].

Qatar represents a unique context for exploring nationality-specific hypertension patterns, given that expatriate populations constitute the majority of its residents and originate from diverse regions including South Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and Southeast Asia [16]. This demographic heterogeneity, combined with universal access to standardized primary care services delivered by the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC), provides an opportunity to examine variation in hypertension burden and management across nationality groups within a unified healthcare model. PHCC’s integrated electronic health record system captures routine clinical measurements and chronic disease management data across multiple health centers, enabling large-scale population-level analyses [17].

Few large-scale studies have analyzed hypertension epidemiology across different nationality groups within Qatar’s primary care system. Previous studies in Qatar have been limited by relatively small sample sizes, hospital-based populations, or lack of detailed primary care–level demographic stratification, limiting their generalizability to routine clinical practice [15, 18]. Large primary care cohorts capturing routine clinical practice are therefore essential to address this gap and assess disparities across population subgroups.

To address this evidence gap, the present study aimed to estimate hypertension prevalence and examine patterns of recorded diagnosis and treatment in a large primary care cohort in Qatar, with a specific focus on variation across nationality groups and World Health Organization (WHO) regions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using routinely collected electronic health record (EHR) data from government-funded primary health care centers in Qatar.

Study population

Among adults (≥ 18 years), individuals without sufficient blood pressure measurements to derive a reliable summary measure (defined as the median of the most recent three readings) were excluded from BP-based analyses. The final analytic cohort therefore included 644,055 adults with valid blood pressure data. Individuals excluded from analysis may have had incomplete or insufficient BP recordings, rather than a complete absence of BP measurements.This exclusion may introduce selection bias, as included individuals may be older and have a higher comorbidity burden. Consequently, hypertension prevalence may be overestimated, and findings may not be fully generalizable to the entire registered primary care population. A comparison between included and excluded individuals was not feasible due to lack of available clinical and demographic data for individuals without BP recordings. Individuals included in the analytic cohort may differ systematically from those excluded, as inclusion required sufficient blood pressure measurements to derive a reliable summary measure. This may introduce selection bias, potentially leading to overestimation of hypertension prevalence and limiting generalizability to the broader primary care population.

Blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure measurements were not standardized for research purposes and may include variability in timing, frequency, and measurement conditions. Use of the median of the most recent three readings was intended to reduce the influence of transient elevations and outliers; however, misclassification due to variability in measurements cannot be excluded. The number and timing of BP measurements varied across individuals depending on clinical encounters.

This composite definition was selected to improve case detection in routine EHR data; however, it may include individuals with treated or previously diagnosed hypertension whose current blood pressure levels are below threshold values. To enhance transparency, a sensitivity analysis using a stricter blood pressure–only definition (≥ 140/90 mmHg) was also performed.

Absence of diagnosed hypertension

Undiagnosed hypertension was defined among individuals meeting hypertension criteria and refers to the absence of a recorded diagnosis in the EHR despite meeting analytic criteria for hypertension.

Absence of recorded treatment

Absence of recorded treatment was defined as no documented antihypertensive prescription during the study period among individuals meeting hypertension criteria. This definition reflects prescriptions recorded within the EHR and may not capture medications obtained outside the system or initiated prior to the study period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages.

The prevalence of defined hypertension, undiagnosed hypertension, and untreated hypertension was calculated overall and stratified by nationality and WHO region. Group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the most recent available clinical data for each individual within the 2023–2025 study period.

Given the large sample size, statistical significance was interpreted alongside effect sizes and clinical relevance, and emphasis was placed on the magnitude of associations rather than p-values alone. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31). Missing data were minimal for key variables included in the analysis, and complete-case analysis was performed. Individuals without sufficient blood pressure data to derive the median of the most recent three readings were excluded from BP-based analyses, as described above.

Continuous variables, including age, were modeled as linear predictors after assessment of their distribution and clinical interpretability. Model assumptions were evaluated using standard diagnostic measures, including assessment of multicollinearity and goodness-of-fit. Given the large sample size and centralized care structure, clustering at the facility level was not explicitly modeled; however, this may represent a potential source of residual correlation.

Ethical considerations

The study used anonymized routinely collected health data. Individual patient consent was waived due to the retrospective study design and the use of de-identified data. Ethical approval was obtained from the appropriate institutional review board.

Results

Study population

A total of 1,006,122 individuals were identified during the study period. After exclusion of individuals younger than 18 years and those without sufficient blood pressure measurements to derive a reliable summary measure, 644,055 adults were included in the analytic cohort.

The mean age of the study population was 39.9 ± 13.6 years, and 53.3% were female.

Baseline characteristics of the study population, overall and stratified by defined hypertension status, are shown in Table 1. Individuals with hypertension were substantially older than those without hypertension (51.6 ± 13.4 vs. 35.6 ± 10.9 years, p < 0.001). The proportion of females was similar between groups (53.5% vs. 53.2%, p = 0.24).

Cardiometabolic comorbidities were markedly more prevalent among individuals with hypertension. Diabetes mellitus was present in 44.6% of individuals with hypertension compared with 9.5% of those without hypertension (p < 0.001). Similarly, dyslipidemia (43.6% vs. 11.3%), cardiovascular disease (15.5% vs. 1.9%), and chronic kidney disease (7.1% vs. 0.5%) were significantly more common among those with hypertension (all p < 0.001). Among adults with a recorded hypertension diagnosis, the mean age at diagnosis was 49.7 ± 11.9 years, with similar values observed among females and males (Supplementary Table S1).

Hypertension prevalence and treatment status

Overall, 26.0% (n = 167,746) of the analytic cohort met the criteria for defined hypertension. Among individuals with hypertension, 70.2% had no recorded antihypertensive prescription during the study period, while 29.8% (n = 49,981) were receiving at least one antihypertensive agent.

Characteristics of adults with hypertension according to treatment status are presented in Table 2. Treated individuals were older than individuals without a recorded antihypertensive prescription (56.2 ± 13.0 vs. 49.7 ± 13.1 years, p < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease were more prevalent among treated individuals, whereas absence of recorded treatment was more common among those without these comorbidities. Among adults with hypertension who were receiving antihypertensive treatment (n = 49,981), calcium channel blockers were the most commonly prescribed medication class (62.2%), followed by angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (39.3%) and diuretics (18.0%). Combination therapy involving two or more antihypertensive classes was observed in 11.7% of treated individuals (Supplementary Table S2).

Factors associated with hypertension

Results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis examining factors associated with hypertension are presented in Table 4. Increasing age was strongly associated with hypertension (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.09 per year, 95% CI 1.09–1.10). Diabetes mellitus (aOR 2.19, 95% CI 2.15–2.22), cardiovascular disease (aOR 3.15, 95% CI 3.06–3.25), chronic kidney disease (aOR 5.96, 95% CI 5.63–6.31), and dyslipidemia (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.58–1.64) were all independently associated with higher odds of hypertension (all p < 0.001).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with hypertension

Adjusted odds ratios were obtained from a multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and World Health Organization (WHO) region. After adjustment for covariates, WHO region remained a significant predictor of hypertension. Compared with the Eastern Mediterranean Region, higher odds of hypertension were observed in the South-East Asia Region (aOR 2.15, 95% CI 2.11–2.18) and the Western Pacific Region (aOR 3.62, 95% CI 3.55–3.69). Sex was not independently associated with hypertension.

In the sensitivity analysis using the stricter blood pressure–based definition, hypertension prevalence was lower (14.9%) compared with the primary analysis. However, the overall pattern of associations remained broadly consistent. Increasing age remained strongly associated with hypertension, and most cardiometabolic factors showed similar directional associations, although the magnitude of associations differed for certain comorbidities (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

In this large primary care–based study, we describe hypertension prevalence, patterns of recorded diagnosis and treatment, and variation across nationality groups and World Health Organization (WHO) regions within a diverse primary care population in Qatar. Using a composite definition of hypertension, our findings provide insights into variation in hypertension burden within routine primary care settings.

Overall hypertension prevalence in context

The overall prevalence of hypertension (as defined above) in our cohort was 26.0%, which is broadly consistent with recent global estimates reported by the World Health Organization. The WHO Global Report on Hypertension (2023) estimated that approximately one in three adults worldwide has hypertension, with prevalence ranging between 25% and 35% across regions [19]. Kario et al., in their commentary on the WHO report, emphasized that despite regional variation, hypertension remains highly prevalent even in countries with expanding healthcare coverage [20].

Our observed prevalence is slightly lower than the global pooled estimate of approximately 32% reported by Zhou et al. among adults aged 30–79 years [21]. This difference may plausibly reflect the younger age structure of our primary care population (median age 39.8 years) and the inclusion of adults from 18 years of age. Data from the May Measurement Month global screening initiative similarly demonstrated lower prevalence in younger and more mobile populations, supporting the plausibility of our findings [22].

Primary care perspective

Primary care–based analyses are essential for understanding hypertension epidemiology in routine clinical practice. A systematic review by Abegaz et al. highlighted that hypertension prevalence and management indicators derived from primary care settings often differ from population-based surveys, reflecting routine clinical practice rather than structured screening campaigns [23]. By applying an analytic composite definition incorporating blood pressure measurements, recorded diagnoses, and medication use, our study captures both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension within routine primary care settings, highlighting the central role of primary care in chronic disease detection and management.

Nationality-level differences and South Asian populations

A key finding of our study was the pronounced heterogeneity in hypertension prevalence across nationality groups, particularly among South Asian populations. Among the most represented nationalities, prevalence ranged from 22.5% among Qatari nationals to 36.7% among Filipino nationals, with consistently elevated prevalence observed among Indian, Bangladeshi, Pakistani, and Sri Lankan populations.

These findings are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating heightened cardiometabolic risk among South Asian populations. Misra and Shrivastava reported that South Asians exhibit a higher burden of dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and central obesity at lower body mass indices, contributing to elevated cardiovascular risk [24]. Similarly, Joshi et al. demonstrated that South Asians experience myocardial infarction at younger ages and with a higher prevalence of hypertension compared with other ethnic groups [25]. A systematic review by Gasevic et al. further confirmed persistent ethnic differences in cardiovascular risk profiles even after migration to high-income countries [26].

Our findings extend this literature by showing that these elevated risks translate into higher hypertension prevalence within a standardized primary care system, suggesting that biological susceptibility and sociocultural factors may contribute to observed differences. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as differences across nationality and WHO region may reflect residual confounding and unmeasured factors, including socioeconomic status, occupation, duration of residence, and healthcare utilization patterns. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference.

Filipino population and the Western Pacific Region

Hypertension prevalence was highest among Filipino nationals in our cohort (36.7%), consistent with patterns reported in other healthcare systems. Gordon et al., using electronic health record data in the United States, documented a high prevalence of hypertension among Filipino adults across the lifespan, exceeding that of several other ethnic groups [27]. WHO reports from the Western Pacific Region similarly describe a substantial burden of hypertension and related noncommunicable diseases, particularly among migrant and urban populations [28].

In multivariable analyses, individuals from the Western Pacific Region had the highest odds of hypertension compared with the Eastern Mediterranean Region (aOR 3.62), reinforcing the consistency of this finding across geographic and healthcare contexts.

The consistently higher burden of hypertension, undiagnosed disease, and lack of treatment observed among individuals from the Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions may reflect differences in cardiometabolic risk profiles and healthcare engagement patterns. Previous studies have highlighted that migrant workers from these regions often experience delayed care-seeking, fragmented follow-up, and competing work-related priorities, which may hinder continuity of hypertension management. Our findings suggest that uniform care pathways may not fully address population-specific barriers within highly diverse primary care settings.

Gender differences in hypertension

Unlike many population-based studies reporting higher hypertension prevalence among men, we observed minimal sex differences, with prevalence of approximately 26% in both females and males. After adjustment for age, comorbidities, and region, sex was not independently associated with hypertension.

This observation aligns with evidence suggesting that sex differences in hypertension prevalence may narrow in healthcare settings with equitable access. Du et al. reported that although men often exhibit higher prevalence in population surveys, gender gaps in awareness and treatment may be attenuated in structured care systems [29]. Consensus statements on hypertension management further emphasize that observed sex disparities are often context-dependent rather than biologically fixed [30].

Undiagnosed hypertension and missed opportunities

Among adults meeting the analytic criteria for hypertension, 8.3% had undiagnosed hypertension, with significant variation across WHO regions. The proportion of undiagnosed hypertension was lowest in the Eastern Mediterranean Region (5.0%) and highest in the Western Pacific Region (15.0%).

Global analyses by the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration have highlighted that undiagnosed hypertension remains a major contributor to cardiovascular disease burden worldwide, particularly among younger and migrant populations [31]. Studies of opportunistic screening in clinical settings have demonstrated that elevated blood pressure is frequently detected but not consistently acted upon [32]. Sheppard et al. further described missed opportunities for cardiovascular prevention in primary care, even in well-resourced systems [33].

Our findings support these observations and suggest that system-level access alone does not eliminate diagnostic gaps, particularly in demographically diverse primary care populations.

Absence of recorded treatment and global comparisons

Despite universal access to government-funded primary care services, a substantial proportion of individuals meeting hypertension criteria had no recorded antihypertensive prescription within the study period. However, prescription data were limited to medications recorded within the PHCC system and may not capture medications obtained outside the system or initiated prior to the study period. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Even when restricted to individuals with a recorded hypertension diagnosis, 65.4% had no antihypertensive prescription documented within the study period, suggesting that recorded treatment rates may be underestimated.

Large multinational analyses have consistently demonstrated that hypertension treatment remains suboptimal even in settings where healthcare access is available. Mills et al. reported substantial global disparities in antihypertensive treatment coverage, particularly in rapidly urbanizing and middle-income contexts [34]. Similarly, Egan et al. described stagnation in treatment gains over time despite widespread availability of effective pharmacologic therapies [35]. The PURE study further showed that awareness and treatment rates remain disproportionately low in many non–high-income populations, highlighting structural and systemic barriers beyond healthcare availability alone [36].

Importantly, prior research has also suggested that mobile and migrant populations may experience fragmented continuity of chronic disease care, leading to interruptions in pharmacologic management despite nominal access to primary care services [34–36]. In highly expatriate populations, occupational mobility, variable healthcare engagement, and cross-border medication access may contribute to inconsistent longitudinal follow-up. Within this context, opportunistic blood pressure detection during clinical encounters may not consistently translate into sustained treatment initiation and monitoring.

Our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating that meaningful treatment gaps can persist even within a unified, universal-access primary care system serving a predominantly expatriate population. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions such as opportunistic blood pressure screening, electronic health record–based alerts for elevated readings, structured follow-up systems, and culturally tailored patient education strategies to improve hypertension detection and management.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study leveraged an exceptionally large, nationally representative primary care cohort, enabling precise prevalence estimates and detailed nationality- and region-specific analyses. The use of routinely collected EHR data reflects real-world clinical practice and enhances external validity. Qatar’s unique demographic composition allowed focused examination of South Asian and Filipino populations, which are often underrepresented in epidemiological studies. Standardized primary care delivery across PHCC minimizes confounding related to healthcare access. Sensitivity analyses using an alternative blood pressure–only definition of hypertension yielded lower prevalence estimates, as expected, but demonstrated broadly similar patterns of association. Differences observed for certain comorbidities likely reflect the exclusion of diagnosis- and medication-based criteria in the stricter definition. These findings support the overall robustness of the main results while highlighting the influence of operational definitions in EHR-based studies.

Limitations

Approximately one-third of registered individuals were excluded due to insufficient blood pressure data to derive a reliable summary measure (median of the most recent three readings), rather than a complete absence of BP measurements. This reflects the requirement for multiple BP readings to improve measurement reliability and reduce misclassification. Individuals with insufficient BP data may differ systematically from those included, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability.

This approach prioritizes measurement validity over inclusiveness, which is particularly important in EHR-based studies where single BP readings may be subject to variability. In addition, the use of routinely collected EHR data may be subject to incomplete recording of diagnoses and prescriptions, which may result in misclassification. Data on important potential confounders, including body mass index, smoking status, and socioeconomic factors, were not available and may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusions

Hypertension was common in this large primary care EHR cohort, with substantial proportions of individuals lacking recorded diagnosis or antihypertensive prescriptions. These findings should be interpreted in the context of EHR-based data capture and may reflect incomplete documentation as well as potential gaps in care. Strengthening hypertension identification and management within routine primary care settings may improve cardiovascular risk reduction.
