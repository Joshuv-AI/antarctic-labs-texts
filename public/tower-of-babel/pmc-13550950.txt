Second‐to‐Fourth Digit (2D:4D) Ratio as A Risk Factor for Osteoporosis and Osteoarthritis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Özlem Elvan, Birgül Vural Doğru, Emine Kaplan Serin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe second‐to‐fourth digit (2D:4D) ratio is an established marker of prenatal sex hormone exposure and has been associated with various health outcomes across the lifespan. Increasing evidence suggests that developmental factors reflected by the 2D:4D ratio may contribute to susceptibility to musculoskeletal disorders, including osteoarthritis (OA) and osteoporosis (OP).AimThis systematic review aimed to synthesize the available evidence regarding the association between 2D:4D ratio and the risk of OA and OP.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261439840). A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus between April and May 2026. Observational studies investigating the relationship between 2D:4D ratio and OA or OP related outcomes were included. Study selection, data extraction, and methodological quality assessment were independently performed by three reviewers. Methodological quality was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools.ResultsNine studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising eight studies on OA and one study on OP. Most studies reported that lower 2D:4D ratios or Type III finger patterns were associated with increased OA risk, greater radiographic severity, and a higher likelihood of severe knee OA requiring joint replacement. Associations were particularly evident for knee and hand OA, with several studies reporting stronger associations in women than in men. In contrast, evidence regarding OP was limited, as only one eligible study specifically investigated OP. Methodological heterogeneity was observed across studies regarding digit ratio assessment methods and disease definitions.ConclusionCurrent evidence suggests that lower 2D:4D ratios are associated with OA‐related outcomes, particularly in knee and hand OA, with several studies indicating stronger associations in women. These findings suggest that variation in 2D:4D may reflect developmental and sex‐dependent patterns of musculoskeletal health rather than a causal relationship. However, evidence regarding OP remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions. Further large‐scale prospective studies using standardized assessment methods are needed to clarify these associations and their underlying biological mechanisms.

Introduction

The second‐to‐fourth digit (2D:4D) ratio, defined as the ratio between the lengths of the index and ring fingers, is widely recognized as an indirect marker of prenatal sex hormone exposure. Lower 2D:4D ratios are generally associated with higher prenatal testosterone levels and lower prenatal estrogen exposure, whereas higher ratios are thought to reflect the opposite hormonal profile (Manning et al. 1998; Manning and Bundred 2000; Iljin et al. 2022). The 2D:4D ratio is established early during fetal development and remains relatively stable throughout life, making it a potential marker of developmental influences on adult health and disease (Manning et al. 1998; Kalichman et al. 2018).

Osteoarthritis (OA) and osteoporosis (OP) are among the most prevalent chronic musculoskeletal disorders worldwide and represent major causes of pain, disability, reduced quality of life, and healthcare utilization. Osteoarthritis is characterized by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling, and joint dysfunction (Tang et al. 2025), whereas OP is characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, resulting in increased fracture susceptibility (Compston et al. 2019). Osteoporotic fragility fractures constitute a substantial public health burden because they are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, hospitalization, rehabilitation requirements, and long‐term healthcare expenditures (Compston et al. 2019).

Growing evidence suggests that prenatal hormonal and developmental factors may contribute to susceptibility to musculoskeletal disorders later in life (Zhang et al. 2008; Ab Rahman et al. 2021; Hwang et al. 2022). According to the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease hypothesis, environmental and hormonal influences during fetal life can have long‐lasting effects on skeletal development and adult disease risk. Maternal nutrition, intrauterine growth, vitamin D exposure, and early‐life developmental programming have all been associated with bone mineral density and fracture risk in adulthood (Wood et al. 2015). As an indirect marker of prenatal hormonal exposure, the 2D:4D ratio may therefore provide insight into developmental pathways influencing both joint and bone health.

Previous studies have demonstrated associations between lower 2D:4D ratios and a variety of sex‐dependent physiological and pathological conditions, including cardiovascular disease, reproductive outcomes, behavioral traits, and musculoskeletal disorders (de Kruijf et al. 2014). Since OA exhibits marked sex differences in prevalence and progression, particularly among women after menopause, prenatal hormonal influences have been proposed as a potential contributor to OA susceptibility (de Kruijf et al. 2014).

Several observational studies have investigated the relationship between 2D:4D ratio and OA phenotypes, including knee, hip, hand, and trapeziometacarpal OA (Zhang et al. 2008; Ferraro et al. 2010; Haugen et al. 2011; Kalichman et al. 2018; Hwang et al. 2022; Mustapha et al. 2024). In particular, lower 2D:4D ratios and Type III finger patterns (2D < 4D) have been associated with increased OA risk, greater radiographic severity, and symptomatic disease in several populations (Zhang et al. 2008; Ferraro et al. 2010; Mustapha et al. 2024). In contrast, considerably fewer studies have explored the relationship between digit ratio and OP‐related outcomes, such as bone mineral density and fracture risk (Arazi and Eghbali 2019; Uzeli et al. 2026).

However, findings regarding the association between 2D:4D ratio and musculoskeletal disorders remain inconsistent. While several studies have reported significant associations between lower 2D:4D ratios and OA‐related outcomes, others have failed to demonstrate such relationships, particularly when different OA definitions, measurement techniques, and outcome assessments were used (de Kruijf et al. 2014). Furthermore, evidence regarding OP remains limited and fragmented. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to synthesize the available evidence regarding the association between the 2D:4D ratio and musculoskeletal degenerative diseases, specifically OA and OP, and to examine whether variation in digit ratio may provide insight into developmental and sex‐dependent patterns of musculoskeletal health. We hypothesized that lower 2D:4D ratios would be consistently associated with increased risk of OA, whereas evidence regarding OP would be less consistent because of the limited number of available studies.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews statement (Moher et al. 2015). The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No: CRD420261439840). To enhance methodological transparency and minimize selection bias, all stages of the review process, including literature screening, eligibility assessment, data extraction, and quality appraisal, were performed independently by three reviewers (ÖE, BVD, and EKS).

Literature Search Strategy

A systematic search of the literature was conducted between April and May 2026 using the PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases. The search strategy was designed to identify studies examining the relationship between the 2D:4D ratio and musculoskeletal outcomes related to OA and OP. The core search strategy consisted of the following terms: (“2D:4D” OR “digit ratio” OR “finger length ratio”) AND (“osteoarthritis” OR “osteoporosis”).

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) employed an observational design (cross‐sectional, case–control, or cohort); (2) included adult human participants; (3) assessed the 2D:4D ratio as an exposure variable; (4) evaluated OA and/or OP‐related outcomes using clinical, radiological, anthropometric, or fracture‐risk measures; and (5) were published between January 2006 and May 2026 in English in peer‐reviewed journals.

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, case reports, experimental animal studies, or publications lacking original data. Articles were also excluded when digit ratio measurements were not reported, when OA or OP outcomes were absent, or when full‐text access was unavailable. Consistent with the predefined focus on peer‐reviewed observational evidence, gray literature and trial registries were not included as formal search sources, and citation/reference‐list snowballing was not prespecified as an additional search method.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

The identification and selection of studies followed the PRISMA framework and predefined PICOS criteria (Table 1). After the removal of duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Full‐text articles were subsequently reviewed to determine final eligibility. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

The database search yielded 441 records. The titles and abstracts of the articles were examined and evaluated. Then, articles for which the full text was available were reviewed according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. After this stage, nine articles that met all eligibility criteria were deemed suitable for inclusion in the systematic review. A structured extraction template was developed before data collection. Information extracted from each study included author details, year of publication, country of origin, study design, sample characteristics, methods used to determine the 2D:4D ratio, OA and/or OP assessment approaches, and principal findings. Given the methodological and clinical heterogeneity across the included studies, a meta‐analysis was not considered appropriate; therefore, the findings were synthesized narratively.

Quality Assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklists appropriate for each study design (Moola et al. 2024, 2020). Cross‐sectional studies were assessed using the eight‐item JBI checklist, case–control studies using the 10‐item checklist, and the prospective cohort study using the 11‐item JBI checklist. Each criterion fulfilled received one point, while criteria rated as “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable” received zero points. Higher scores reflected greater methodological quality and lower risk of bias. Assessments were independently performed by three reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

Results

This systematic review identified nine studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria (Table 2). Three studies included only female participants (Ab Rahman et al. 2021; Mustapha et al. 2024; Uzeli et al. 2026), whereas six studies involved both male and female participants (Zhang et al. 2008; Ferraro et al. 2010; Haugen et al. 2011; Hussain et al. 2014; Kalichman et al. 2018; Hwang et al. 2022). Of the nine included studies, eight investigated OA and one investigated OP. Sample sizes ranged from 118 to 14 511 participants. Four studies employed a cross‐sectional design, four utilized case–control designs, and one was conducted as a prospective cohort study.

For studies reporting categorical digit patterns (Zhang et al. 2008; Ferraro et al. 2010; Mustapha et al. 2024), the 2D:4D ratio was classified into three categories according to the relative lengths of the second digit (index finger) and fourth digit (ring finger). Type I was defined as an index finger longer than the ring finger (2D > 4D), Type II as equal finger lengths (2D = 4D), and Type III as an index finger shorter than the ring finger (2D < 4D). These patterns are commonly referred to as the female, intermediate, and male finger patterns, respectively. The methodological quality of the included studies was generally high. Cross‐sectional studies achieved JBI scores of 7–8 out of 8, all case–control studies achieved 10 out of 10, and the prospective cohort study achieved 11 out of 11. Overall, the included studies demonstrated good methodological quality across the study designs evaluated.

Zhang et al. (2008) conducted a case–control study involving 3172 participants and reported that the Type III finger pattern was associated with an increased risk of OA, particularly knee OA (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.54–2.44). The association was stronger in women (OR = 3.05, 95% CI: 2.08–4.47) than in men (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.08–1.95). A dose–response relationship was observed whereby lower phalangeal and metacarpal 2D:4D ratios were associated with progressively greater risk of knee OA. Associations with hip OA were inconsistent.

Ferraro et al. (2010) evaluated 478 participants and found that the Type III finger pattern was significantly associated with radiographic knee OA (OR = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.54–4.37). The association was stronger in women (OR = 4.40, 95% CI: 2.62–7.38) than in men (OR = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.34–5.00). Lower phalangeal and combined 2D:4D ratios also demonstrated dose–response relationships with OA risk.

Haugen et al. (2011) evaluated 1020 participants and found no significant associations between low 2D:4D ratio and radiographic knee OA, severe symptomatic knee OA, or MRI‐defined meniscal lesions. However, women with lower phalangeal 2D:4D ratios had an increased likelihood of hand OA (adjusted OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.11–2.93), whereas men with lower phalangeal ratios reported a higher prevalence of previous knee injury (adjusted OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.02–3.10).

Hussain et al. (2014) conducted a prospective cohort study of 14 511 Australian adults with a mean follow‐up period of 10.5 years. During follow‐up, 580 participants underwent total knee replacement and 499 underwent total hip replacement due to OA. Higher right‐hand and mean 2D:4D ratios were associated with a reduced incidence of total knee replacement (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.84–0.99). Participants in the lowest average 2D:4D tertile exhibited an increased risk of total knee replacement (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05–1.59). No significant association was identified for hip OA.

Kalichman et al. (2018) investigated 1540 adults from a Russian Chuvash population and reported that women with a Type III finger pattern had significantly higher radiographic hand OA scores and greater numbers of affected joints after adjustment for age and body mass index. No significant associations were observed among men.

Ab Rahman et al. (2021) examined the association between 2D:4D ratio, WOMAC osteoarthritis scores, and body mass index in women with primary knee OA. No significant associations were observed between 2D:4D ratio and WOMAC total score (p = 0.383), pain (p = 0.360), stiffness (p = 0.560), or physical function (p = 0.464). However, 2D:4D ratio was significantly associated with body mass index (p = 0.015), and body mass index showed a moderate positive correlation with WOMAC total score (r = 0.643, p < 0.001).

Hwang et al. (2022) evaluated 604 elderly Korean adults and found that lower metacarpal 2D:4D ratios were independently associated with trapeziometacarpal joint OA (adjusted OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99). In addition, severe trapeziometacarpal OA was associated with the Type II finger pattern (adjusted OR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.13–8.33).

Mustapha et al. (2024) conducted a case–control study including 480 women with primary knee OA and 480 controls. The Type III finger pattern was significantly more prevalent in the OA group (48.8%) than in controls (27.9%), whereas the Type I pattern was more common among controls (46.3%). The distribution of 2D:4D patterns differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001).

In contrast to the relatively broader evidence available for OA, evidence regarding the relationship between 2D:4D and OP remains particularly limited, as only one eligible study specifically addressing OP was identified in the present review. Uzeli et al. (2026) investigated 127 postmenopausal women, including 66 women with OP and 61 controls. No significant differences were observed in right‐ or left‐hand 2D:4D ratios between OP and control groups. Likewise, no significant associations were found between right‐hand 2D:4D ratio and bone mineral density or FRAX‐derived fracture risk estimates. However, left‐hand 2D:4D ratio demonstrated weak negative correlations with major osteoporotic fracture risk (r = −0.269, p = 0.029) and hip fracture risk (r = −0.257, p = 0.037). Left‐hand dominance was associated with an increased risk of OP (OR = 3.584, 95% CI: 1.224–10.494).

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the evidence regarding the relationship between 2D:4D ratio and musculoskeletal degenerative diseases, particularly OA and OP. The strongest evidence was observed for knee and hand OA. Zhang et al. (2008) reported that the Type III finger pattern was associated with an increased risk of knee OA, particularly among women (Zhang et al. 2008). Similarly, Ferraro et al. (2010) demonstrated that individuals with a Type III finger pattern had significantly greater odds of radiographic knee OA after adjustment for age, sex, and body mass index (Ferraro et al. 2010). Mustapha et al. (2024) further confirmed that the Type III pattern was significantly more prevalent among women with primary knee OA than among healthy controls (Mustapha et al. 2024). Consistent with these findings, Hussain et al. (2014) showed that lower 2D:4D ratios were associated with an increased long‐term risk of severe knee OA requiring total knee replacement (Hussain et al. 2014). Consistent with the findings of the included OA studies, additional evidence from the AGES‐Reykjavik study showed that individuals with a Type III finger pattern had a significantly increased likelihood of total knee replacement due to OA, whereas no significant associations were observed for hip replacement or hand OA (Sigurjonsdottir et al. 2013). Collectively, these findings suggest that prenatal hormonal influences reflected by the 2D:4D ratio may contribute to OA susceptibility, particularly in relation to knee OA.

Evidence regarding hand OA was similarly supportive. Women with a Type III finger pattern exhibited significantly greater radiographic hand OA severity and a higher number of affected joints in the study by Kalichman et al. (2018). Likewise, lower metacarpal 2D:4D ratios were independently associated with trapeziometacarpal joint OA in elderly adults (Hwang et al. 2022). In contrast, Haugen et al. (2011) did not observe significant associations between digit ratio and knee OA, although lower phalangeal 2D:4D ratios were associated with hand OA among women (Haugen et al. 2011).

Interestingly, Ab Rahman et al. (2021) found no significant relationship between 2D:4D ratio and WOMAC‐derived pain, stiffness, or physical function scores in women with knee OA. However, the authors reported a significant association between digit ratio and body mass index while body mass index was positively correlated with WOMAC scores (Ab Rahman et al. 2021). These findings suggest that prenatal hormonal influences may affect OA outcomes indirectly through anthropometric characteristics rather than symptom severity itself.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed associations. The 2D:4D ratio is widely accepted as a marker of prenatal androgen exposure and reflects hormonal influences during critical periods of fetal development (Manning et al. 1998; Manning and Bundred 2000). Experimental evidence suggests that androgens may influence chondrocyte proliferation, cartilage maturation, and skeletal patterning through pathways involving HOX gene expression (de Kruijf et al. 2014; Arazi and Eghbali 2019). Consequently, prenatal hormonal environments may affect both digit development and long‐term susceptibility to degenerative joint disease (de Kruijf et al. 2014).

Previous population‐based evidence may also help explain the heterogeneity observed among the included studies. de Kruijf et al. (2014) reported that the Type III finger pattern was associated with symptomatic knee OA but not with radiographic knee OA, suggesting that associations with 2D:4D may vary according to OA definition and outcome assessment. In the same study, the Type III finger pattern was also associated with chronic joint pain, raising the possibility that developmental hormonal influences reflected by 2D:4D may be related to pain‐related phenotypes rather than structural OA alone (de Kruijf et al. 2014).

Beyond the single OP study included in the present review, previous research has explored the relationship between 2D:4D and skeletal health. Arazi and Eghbali (2019) reported positive associations between 2D:4D ratio and BMD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck in young women, with lower digit ratios observed among participants classified as osteoporotic. (Arazi and Eghbali 2019). In contrast, evidence from a separate study of postmenopausal women showed no significant association between 2D:4D ratio and BMD at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, or total hip, further illustrating the inconsistency of the available evidence concerning digit ratio and bone health (Yaman et al. 2024). Similarly, Uzeli et al. (2026) reported no significant differences in digit ratios between OP and control groups, although lower left‐hand 2D:4D ratios were associated with higher FRAX‐derived fracture risk estimates (Uzeli et al. 2026).

The OP findings may also be interpreted within the framework of the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease hypothesis. Environmental influences during fetal life and early childhood may exert long‐lasting effects on skeletal development and future disease susceptibility. Maternal nutritional status, intrauterine growth, vitamin D exposure, and early‐life developmental trajectories have all been associated with peak bone mass and fracture risk later in life. Therefore, the observed associations between 2D:4D ratio and OP‐related outcomes may reflect the influence of early developmental programming on adult skeletal health (Wood et al. 2015).

The findings of the present review may also be relevant to the broader multidisciplinary perspective on musculoskeletal health. Although the 2D:4D ratio cannot currently be recommended as a diagnostic, screening, or prognostic tool, its associations with OA‐related outcomes may contribute to understanding developmental and sex‐dependent variation in musculoskeletal health. From a multidisciplinary perspective, established strategies for reducing the burden of OA and OP include patient education, lifestyle and weight management, nutritional counseling, fall‐prevention strategies, and interventions aimed at maintaining mobility and physical function (Compston et al. 2019; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE] 2022). However, the current evidence does not support tailoring these interventions according to an individual's 2D:4D ratio.

Methodological evidence from previous research may also help explain the inconsistencies observed among the included studies. Robertson et al. (2008) reported that phalangeal 2D:4D measurements may be influenced by osteoarthritic deformities and previous finger trauma, whereas metacarpal measurements appear less susceptible to such biases. The authors further noted that osteoarthritic changes affecting finger morphology may introduce measurement bias, particularly in older populations. Moreover, substantial bilateral symmetry of digit ratios and minimal effects of hand dominance were observed, suggesting that hand selection alone is unlikely to explain divergent findings across studies (Robertson et al. 2008).

Additional descriptive evidence is provided by Duruöz et al. (2022), who reported a mean 2D:4D ratio of 0.96 ± 0.057 in a large multicenter cohort of patients with hand OA. Although the study did not include a control group and did not directly evaluate OA risk, it highlighted previous evidence linking lower digit ratios with greater hand OA severity (Duruöz et al. 2022). These observations support the hypothesis that the 2D:4D ratio may represent a lifelong marker of developmental influences affecting musculoskeletal health.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, the majority of the included studies employed cross‐sectional or case–control designs, limiting the ability to establish causal relationships between 2D:4D ratio and OA or OP outcomes. Second, considerable methodological heterogeneity was observed across studies in terms of participant characteristics, disease definitions, outcome measures, and 2D:4D assessment techniques, which may have affected the comparability of findings. In particular, Robertson et al. (2008) reported that phalangeal 2D:4D measurements may be influenced by osteoarthritic deformities and previous finger trauma, whereas metacarpal measurements appear less susceptible to such biases. Consequently, variation in measurement methodology and the potential effects of hand OA on finger morphology may have contributed to the inconsistent associations reported across studies (Robertson et al. 2008). Third, evidence regarding OP was particularly limited, as only one eligible study specifically investigated osteoporosis, precluding firm conclusions regarding the relationship between 2D:4D and OP‐related outcomes. Finally, only English‐language publications were included, which may have introduced language bias and resulted in the exclusion of potentially relevant studies published in other languages. Therefore, future research should employ standardized 2D:4D measurement protocols, larger prospective cohorts, and diverse populations to further clarify the developmental and sex‐dependent associations between digit ratio and musculoskeletal health.

Conclusion

This systematic review indicates that lower 2D:4D ratios are associated with OA‐related outcomes, particularly in knee and hand OA, with several studies suggesting that these associations may be more pronounced in women. However, given the predominance of cross‐sectional and case–control studies, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship between prenatal hormonal exposure and OA development. In contrast, evidence regarding OP remains insufficient, as only one eligible study specifically addressing OP was identified, precluding any firm conclusions regarding the association between 2D:4D and OP‐related outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that variation in the 2D:4D ratio may provide insight into developmental and sex‐dependent patterns linking the prenatal endocrine environment to adult musculoskeletal health. Rather than serving as a diagnostic or prognostic marker, the 2D:4D ratio may therefore be more appropriately considered an anthropometric indicator of developmental variation relevant to musculoskeletal health. Future large‐scale prospective studies using standardized digit‐ratio assessment methods and comprehensive musculoskeletal outcomes are needed to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these associations, particularly their sex‐specific patterns.
