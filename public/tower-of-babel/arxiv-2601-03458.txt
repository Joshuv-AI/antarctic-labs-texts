Automated Feedback Generation for Undergraduate Mathematics: Development and Evaluation of an AI Teaching Assistant
Aron Gohr, Marie-Amelie Lawn, Kevin Gao, Inigo Serjeant, Stephen Heslip

Abstract
Intelligent tutoring systems have long enabled automated immediate feedback on student work when it is presented in a tightly structured format and when problems are very constrained, but reliably assessing free-form mathematical reasoning remains challenging. We present a system that processes free-form natural language input, handles a wide range of edge cases, and comments competently not only on the technical correctness of submitted proofs, but also on style and presentation issues. We discuss the advantages and disadvantages of various approaches to the evaluation of such a system, and show that by the metrics we evaluate, the quality of the feedback generated is comparable to that produced by human experts when assessing early undergraduate homework. We stress-test our system with a small set of more advanced and unusual questions, and report both significant gaps and encouraging successes in that more challenging setting. Our system uses large language models in a modular workflow. The workflow configuration is human-readable and editable without programming knowledge, and allows some intermediate steps to be precomputed or injected by the instructor. A version of our tool is deployed on the Imperial College London mathematics homework platform Lambda Feedback . We report also on the integration of our tool into this platform.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

1.1.1
Need for scalable feedback solutions

Exercises are at the heart of university-level mathematics education. However, for students transitioning from high-school mathematics, it is often unclear whether they have solved a university-level assignment successfully. Unlike schoolwork, university problems require the construction of rigorous mathematical proofs—something most students have little or no prior experience with. They must tackle problems that are far more abstract and unfamiliar, often with only a vague sense of what is expected. Even when students arrive at correct solutions, many lack the experience to write them with the clarity and precision that university standards demand. Feedback at this stage is essential: without it, many students will struggle silently or fail to develop the ability to reliably check their own work.

Despite its importance, providing timely, detailed feedback on proofs remains challenging, particularly as class sizes grow and resources become increasingly limited. Traditionally, feedback is given manually by teaching assistants (TAs), typically more advanced students familiar with the course material. This approach is labour-intensive, with turnaround times often stretching to one or two weeks—assuming resources for regular homework evaluation are available at all. Even then, feedback quality can be inconsistent, and the traditional model scales poorly. Instructors and TAs must prioritise grading and administrative tasks, leaving minimal time for individualised feedback beyond general comments or standardised solutions. Moreover, those most in need of feedback—students struggling with material or time management—may be the least likely to seek it out, risking the amplification of existing equity gaps.

Existing feedback mechanisms, such as answer keys or model solutions, offer limited support. Without immediate, adaptive feedback, students are often left guessing at the causes of their mistakes, allowing misunderstandings to persist and accumulate between assessments. Even when answer keys explain correct solutions, they rarely address the specific reasoning errors individual students make, leaving significant gaps in understanding and potentially undermining future learning and motivation.

Finally, mathematics feedback requires a particularly precise combination of technical and pedagogical elements. It must address not only the correctness of the answers, but also the logical structure, notation, and step-by-step reasoning that underlie the students’ solutions. Constructive feedback involves identifying common errors, suggesting alternative strategies, and clarifying misconceptions — each of which demands significant time and specialised expertise. Although mathematics departments have access to highly qualified teaching staff capable of providing such feedback, the volume of students makes it impractical to deliver personalised, detailed feedback at scale.

Automatic feedback generation systems that produce high-quality, helpful feedback with short turnaround times and low cost could offer a solution to these challenges. The goal of this work is to provide evidence that such systems can be built using current AI capabilities and to discuss evaluation methodology.

1.1.2
Importance of mathematical rigour

Rigour turns intuition into reliable knowledge. By requiring that every statement follow transparently from clear definitions, students learn to trace errors, think clearly, generalise ideas, and recognise links across topics. It also concerns presentation: proofs must be not only correct, but readable—and, where possible, elegant. Because this mindset differs sharply from the procedure-oriented habits fostered in school, early undergraduates need explicit guidance. Any effective feedback system must therefore flag logical gaps, imprecise language, and needless complexity, steering students toward the standards of university mathematics.

1.1.3
Potential and limitations of LLM-based systems in mathematics education

Until recently, building a system that could provide such mathematical feedback would have seemed impossible. The combination of natural language understanding, mathematical reasoning, and pedagogical insight required would have been beyond the capabilities of any automated system. However, recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in the form of Large Language Models (LLMs), have opened up new possibilities for addressing these challenges.

LLMs are AI systems trained on vast amounts of text data that can process and generate human-like text.

1.2
Related Work

We now review prior work on automated feedback generation in mathematics and the natural sciences. While not exhaustive, this overview highlights systems and approaches conceptually related to our setting.

1.2.1
Early Intelligent Tutoring Systems

Expert-system-based Approaches

Early systems used expert systems —collections of rules that model a tutor’s reasoning in a given domain—to deliver timely, personalized feedback. Traditional Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) of this kind were developed over decades and applied in various fields, including mathematics. Examples include Andes
[
10
,
34
]
and Cognitive Tutor
[
18
]
, which guided students through step-by-step problem solving in physics and algebra, respectively.

Among these, Andes is most relevant to our work. Designed for undergraduate physics courses at the US Naval Academy, it combined a rule-based expert system, a graphical interface for solution entry, and a computer algebra system for verifying steps. It also checked units and displayed immediate error messages. On demand, it could diagnose mistakes or suggest next steps.

An early version (Andes 1) included a Bayesian student model to estimate conceptual understanding, but this was later removed because it provided little actionable information.

Andes was extensively evaluated and shown to improve learning outcomes
[
34
]
. However, expert-system-based ITSs proved difficult to scale due to several limitations:

•

High development and maintenance costs.

•

Adding new problems required both domain experts and knowledge engineers.

•

Each change demanded regression testing to ensure newly introduced rules did not have unintended side-effects
[
34
]
.

•

Dependence on structured graphical interfaces to constrain user input.

•

Like other expert systems, these systems tended to be brittle. Edge cases had generally to be foreseen by the designer to be handled correctly.

•

They were also unable to deal with free-form input.

1.2.2
Mathematical Proof Assistants

Another approach to teaching mathematical proof writing involves formal verification tools, which check completed proofs. We use the term
proof assistant
for those that also support proof construction.

We first outline their capabilities and limitations, and then survey concrete educational applications.

Formal Verification

Proof assistants were originally developed for the
formal verification
of mathematical results, not education. Verification translates theorems and proofs into a formal language where each step can be reliably checked by a computer program. This process requires detail far beyond standard exposition—akin to implementing an algorithm in a low-level language—but offers practically absolute certainty of correctness, provided that the claim to be proven has been correctly formalised.

Despite the high effort required, several major results have been formalised, including the Feit–Thompson theorem
[
11
]
and the Kepler conjecture
[
12
]
. Formal methods are also used in software verification, for example in the verification of the seL4 microkernel
[
17
]
.

Limitations for Feedback Generation

Despite their strengths, proof assistants remain rare in mathematics instruction.

Major limitations include:

•

They require formal syntax and offer little support for natural mathematical writing.

•

The level of detail exceeds that used even by professional mathematicians.

•

Most instructors lack the expertise to use them.

•

They check correctness but not clarity, simplicity, or elegance.

•

For some exercises in undergraduate mathematics, obtaining a version of the question that
can
even be formalised
already constitutes a large part of the conceptual work
. In our test set, this is the case for instance for some of the geometry questions. These are natural-language questions that present a mathematical problem in the form of a text task, and the student has to use their understanding of the situation at hand to construct a purely mathematical question that can then be solved. Proof assistants cannot check the first step, which can be highly nontrivial.

These limitations affect pedagogical value:

•

Learning the formal verification language creates an additional barrier of entry.

•

Proofs may be formally correct but fail to convey insight, while insightful but flawed ones receive no recognition.

•

Overly complex but valid proofs receive no critique on efficiency or clarity.

•

Partial solutions with promising ideas receive little or no positive feedback.

•

Built-in tactics may solve subgoals students cannot do by hand.

Some mitigations exist: for example, instructors can restrict tactic use or limit background results. However, generally it is fair to say that the commonly used mitigations do not address all concerns, in particular the lack of guidance on clear expression of mathematical ideas.

Existing Educational Applications

Despite these issues, proof assistants remain a promising tool for feedback generation. One approach is to create constrained, gamified environments with a limited set of tactics and background results tailored to the course. A well-known example is the

by Buzzard
[
5
,
6

Massot
[
20
]
proposes a version of Lean tailored for undergraduate education, featuring: (1) a syntax layer close to natural mathematical writing, and (2) a curated tactic library suitable for undergraduate-style proofs.

Bartzia et al. compare six proof assistants (Lean, Isabelle, Coq, Lurch, Edukera, D
\forall\exists
duction) by solving the same elementary set theory exercise in each, analysing the process and reflecting on different purposes of proof.

Iannone and Thoma
[
14
]
explored Lean (v3) in a first-year proof course. Students were introduced via a voluntary workshop; 99 completed surveys and 37 participated in interviews. Student responses were mixed. Some found that Lean enhanced their understanding of rigour. Others cited syntax difficulties, unnatural proof style, and time constraints as barriers to engagement.

A follow-up study by the same authors
[
33
]
analysed student proof writing in more detail. They found improved understanding overall, though it was unclear whether this was due to Lean itself or the increased time spent practising.

1.2.3
Large Language Models

Preliminaries

Brown et al.
[
3
]
showed that LLMs trained on text prediction do more than replicate statistical patterns—they exhibit surprising capabilities in zero- and few-shot learning. This means they can solve novel tasks from minimal examples or natural language instructions.

GPT-3, introduced in that work, produced human-like responses across many domains, including basic programming and simple mathematics. Given that such models are trained on diverse educational content and can follow complex instructions, it is natural to ask whether they could support teaching. However, one problem with LLMs is that they have a tendency to make up a plausible-sounding answer when they cannot reliably answer. This made early LLMs largely unsuitable for educational applications.

1.2.4
Our Approach

Our work builds on these developments by designing an AI teaching assistant specifically for undergraduate mathematics. Like the previously mentioned intelligent tutoring system Andes, our system is intended to be minimally invasive—requiring minimal changes to students’ or teachers’ existing workflows. We aim to support a broad range of mathematical reasoning tasks, similar to proof assistants, but without requiring formalised input.

Unlike prior systems, we focus not only on helping students construct correct solutions, but also on improving the clarity and structure of their exposition. In this way, we aim to overcome key limitations of earlier approaches, which were often difficult to maintain, tied to rigid interfaces, restricted to narrow domains, or burdened with steep learning curves.

1.3
Main Contributions

Overview

This paper makes five core contributions: (1) we discuss design criteria for an ITS aimed at undergraduate mathematics; (2) we introduce an evaluation methodology to assess whether a system meets these criteria; (3) we design a modular, reconfigurable ITS based on large language models; (4) we empirically evaluate our tutoring system both on a realistic early undergraduate problem and answer dataset we constructed and on a set of stress testing questions designed to test the limits of the system’s capabilities; (5) we report lessons learned from integrating the tutoring system into a live learning platform. We briefly summarise each below.

Design Criteria for an Undergraduate Mathematics ITS

We propose a set of minimal design criteria for intelligent tutoring systems aimed at undergraduate mathematics. These are derived from practical design goals.

We define design criteria as properties testable at development time. They are minimal in that some goals cannot be evaluated pre-deployment and do not include advanced capabilities like multimodal input or long-turn dialogue. Our formulation allows flexibility in how each criterion is assessed.

Design of a Modular Feedback Generation System

We implement a modular feedback system based on LLMs, designed to meet the above criteria for undergraduate-level mathematics. To ensure accuracy, feedback generation is decomposed into discrete tasks that the LLM can execute reliably. The system is reconfigurable through editable JSON files containing English-language instructions.
4
4

4

We expect this would generalise to other languages, but have not tested this.
Some criteria (e.g. safety, scalability) are satisfied directly by design; others depend on configuration and are assessed through evaluation.

Evaluation of the Feedback Generation System

We evaluate the system across multiple configurations (4 LLMs and 5 workflows) using two new datasets developed for this work. Our primary evaluation approach translates system feedback into numerical grades and correlates these with human expert grades. To ensure robustness, we employ a mildly adversarial grading methodology: multiple lightweight LLMs grade each piece of feedback, and we report the worst-case performance. We also evaluate feedback conciseness through word counting and conduct qualitative analysis through manual inspection. Some design goals (safety, reconfigurability) we try to meet by design rather than empirical testing. We discuss quantitative and qualitative results as well as methodological limitations. In our assessment, the system meets the design criteria when paired with a strong LLM and suitable configuration and when used in a context that is not mathematically too advanced.

Integration into a Feedback Platform

We report on integrating our tool into a live feedback platform originally designed for fixed-format responses (e.g. multiple choice). Several challenges emerged:

1.

Customisation Challenges.
Because mathematical notations and conventions vary, the system needs to know what conventions apply to each question. This requires giving the model context that students would have from the course. We embedded additional context into each question as needed, but of course this means that the question as it is seen by the model differs from what is displayed to the student, and crucially also to the instructor unless they specifically look at the question configuration.

2.

Technical Integration.
The platform assumed fixed-format responses; our prototype used free-form inputs. In our trial deployment, we used this to our advantage by fixing the question part of our inputs and letting a reasoning LLM generate model answers, which improved the accuracy of feedback without additional online inference cost.

3.

No Image Support.
As not all students were fluent in

System Design

2.1
Design Goals

In this subsection, we explain the design goals of our system. Subsequently, we will explain how we try to meet each of these goals, and perform evaluations where possible. Our justification for the design goals comes partly from our understanding of both success and failure modes of mathematical teaching in humans, and partly from working with early versions of our tutor itself and observing the ways
it
fails or succeeds.

2.1.1
User Interaction

Ease of use

Students often begin their mathematical learning without previously encountering
L
a
T
e
X
, hence it would be restrictive to immediately require these students to correctly typeset solutions. As such, the system should be resilient to receiving free-form plain text inputs, despite this leading to ambiguity in notation. At the same time, an ability to process handwriting and other forms of multimodal input (e.g. drawings, speech) would be highly desirable. These goals should be achieved while keeping the user interface simple and intuitive.

Understanding malformed content

The system should make at least a human-equivalent effort to understand the thinking behind poorly expressed submissions. Ideally, a poorly written submission that contains correct thinking should still receive critical feedback – and if it is unclear to the extent that further thinking would be required, may receive critical feedback that is almost as harsh as a solution that is completely wrong.

2.1.2
Dealing With Knowledge Gaps

Feedback given should be mathematically correct

Hallucination or failures of logical reasoning are a known problem of LLMs, especially of non-reasoning models.
5
5

5

To be more precise, reasoning models are certainly better at logic than non-reasoning models. For resistance to hallucination, depending on the type of question asked, an advantage for reasoning models is, however, not as clear-cut
[
26
]
.
While an argument
could
be made that there is a productive type of tutor error (for instance where a tutor gives erroneous feedback, the student catches the error, and learns to think critically), most feedback that states mathematical falsehoods is in our view not desirable. We therefore aim to minimise erroneous feedback.

Advice given should not be hypercritical

On the other hand, not all errors are created equal. Clerical errors have a different level of significance than conceptual errors, and even when there are multiple real errors in a submission, it seems reasonable to think that sometimes a student will be better served by a tutor who does not highlight
every
problem they have found in the submission, but who identifies the
main
or
most fundamental
conceptual problem in the submission and helps the student avoid at least that type of error in the future. In short: feedback should be as concise as possible, and prioritise appropriately.

Feedback should not be authoritative when the system has no reason to be sure

When the system does not understand a question, is unable to say whether a solution is correct, or is unable to solve a question itself, it would be desirable if the system said so.

2.1.3
Maintenance Considerations

Reconfigurability

Teachers should be able to reconfigure the system, at least within limits, without needing computer programming skills. The type of reconfiguration that a teaching academic should be able to perform on their own includes use cases like the following:

1.

Supply context from the course that tells the system what tools the student is allowed to use and what level of detail is required.

2.

Adjust choice of models in workflows to improve mathematical skill level or reduce cost.

3.

Adjust some instructions within the analysis workflow to adapt the system to their use cases.

Other steps, for instance integration of the tutor into a pre-existing e-learning platform that may exist at a given institution, or integration of specialised tools into workflows, are expected to be more complicated. We expect that these steps will require deeper technical expertise than the reconfiguration options mentioned above.

Costs

The system should allow tracking and limiting of API costs, to the extent that models on paid APIs are used.

Model agility

The system should be kept flexible with regards to the models used, both to allow for reconfiguration but also to allow switching to locally run models if a deployment requires it (for instance, for privacy reasons).

2.1.4
Safety

Moderation of harmful content

Filter mechanisms should be used to recognise harmful content or prompting that tries to elicit harmful content and moderate outputs appropriately.

Constructive behaviour

Criticism should always remain factual and be offered constructively, ideally even if the student were to try to provoke the system into giving non-constructive feedback.

2.2
Architecture

2.2.1
System Overview

Our automated feedback system is built around a modular architecture designed to be reconfigurable and to encourage the design of efficient workflows. In this paper, we describe two main instances of our system:

1.

The research prototype, which was used for the majority of the experiments reported.

2.

A version of the system that we deployed experimentally with the
Lambda Feedback
system.

The research prototype is a stand-alone Python application that manages all stages of the feedback generation process. It allows the user to submit a question and associated answer, both in text or image format, and to receive feedback on the submission. In the version we used in the work on this project, it was able to run either in a graphical user interface or process batch inputs from a directory of questions and answers. The database format for batch processing was modelled after the format of the GHOSTS dataset
[
8
]
. The work here reported uses only the batch processing functionality, but a version of the graphical user interface is still available in the codebase published on github.
6
6

6

Our repository is available at
https://github.com/agohr/math_tutor
.

The system is driven by configuration files that define the workflow as well as the models to use for each step. The configuration is stored in a human readable JSON file, which contains directives written in natural language (English, in our tests).

The deployed version reuses the same processing core and configuration system, but does not use the stand-alone user interface and instead connects to
Lambda Feedback
. In this version, we changed the workflows used in the research prototype in one significant respect: we restricted the question submission to the fixed questions set by the instructor (i.e. students could only get feedback on homework questions assigned in their course), and precomputed exemplary solutions for each question using a reasoning model (o1-mini) to both reduce the cost of the system and improve the quality of the feedback. However, to keep configuration simple, we kept this preprocessing step completely automated, i.e. we did not edit the precomputed exemplary solutions.

The system follows a pipeline approach, decomposing the complex task of mathematical feedback generation into discrete, manageable stages that can be individually optimised and reconfigured.
At a high level, the research prototype consists of:

1.

Graphical User Interface
: Manages interaction with the user via a web interface. Interaction in the research prototype was originally limited to single-turn question-answer interactions, although we later added the ability to engage in follow-up conversations; in this paper, we focus on the single-turn version only.

2.

Input Processing Module
: Handles input, including both text and images, and converts them into a unified text representation.

3.

Core Analysis Pipeline
: Processes student submissions and synthesises analysis results into coherent feedback through configurable workflows.

4.

Integration Layer
: Connects the system to learning platforms.

5.

Configuration Management
: Enables workflow customisation without code changes.

The architecture does not inherently achieve our key design goals outlined in Section
2.1
: to what extent it does so depends to a large extent on the configured workflows, and on the models used in each step. The
Lambda Feedback
integration additionally adds preprocessing scripts that reads a question set supplied in
L
a
T
e
X

2.2.2
Input Processing

The research prototype accepts inputs in multiple formats:

•

Text submissions
: Free-form text containing mathematical notation.

•

Image transcription
: Conversion of handwritten or typeset mathematics to processable text.

•

Contextual knowledge
: Course-specific information and constraints.

For image inputs, we utilise Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to transcribe mathematical notation into
L
a
T
e
X
or plain text representations. This transcription is then combined with any manually entered information to create a description of the student’s submission. The system also performs a preliminary completeness check to identify truncated submissions. This is an important usability feature, since processing of a submission takes around a minute for most of our workflows, and of course the feedback is not useful if the submission is incomplete.

2.2.3
Core Analysis Pipeline

The core of the system is a configurable pipeline implemented as a series of directive-driven processing steps. Each step can:

1.

Take the output of previous steps as input.

2.

Apply an LLM-based transformation.

3.

Produce a new output for subsequent steps.

The directives system gives the user a simple way to express small workflow graphs with steps expressed in natural language. As each LLM call has some cost in terms of both waiting time and tokens, we expect that typical workflows will be small and that they will not need more complex control flow than our system provides; in particular, we have not implemented loops or conditionals. More complex control flow could be useful in future work, but in a setting like ours, where the system is expected to answer in almost real-time, it would only be useful if LLM inference was very fast or if some of the steps were done by components much less computationally expensive than an LLM, like a proof verifier or a computer algebra system. We think such settings should be explored in future work, but our system has only used pure-LLM based workflows and the LLMs used had response times that allow only a few steps to be executed if a return is required within the order of one or two minutes.

In many educational settings, we expect that systems such as ours would be used to give feedback on student work on fixed questions set by the instructor. In such settings, it is possible to implement some fairly deep workflow graphs while maintaining fast response times. This is the case when most of the nodes of the workflow can be precomputed because they are independent of the student submission.

2.2.4
Integration Capabilities

The architecture supports integration with existing educational platforms by offering the core functionality of executing configurable workflows while accepting configurable input fields through a Python class interface. For our deployment with the
Lambda Feedback
system (see Section 5), we implemented adapters that convert between the platform’s fixed-format question structure and our free-form feedback system.

Integration with a pre-existing platform required some adaptation of our expected input and output formats; we also removed image inputs for deployment, because
Lambda Feedback
had a different, already established workflow for image inputs. We also needed to add logic to handle user rate limits to avoid exceeding API budgets for the test, but this was straightforward as the platform provided a way to see how many requests had already been made by the current student user.

3
Experimental Methods

3.1
Datasets

This section outlines the construction of the dataset used to evaluate our prototype, including the educational context, question selection, and preparation of student responses for testing.

3.1.1
Context of Questions

Introduction to University Mathematics
(IUM) is a compulsory four-week module for all first-year mathematics students at Imperial College London. It introduces formal reasoning, logical structure, and mathematical writing—skills essential for undergraduate study. Building directly on the pre-arrival material, it serves as a unified foundation for the rest of the first-year curriculum, with an emphasis on axiomatic methods and rigorous proof techniques.

The IUM exercises are well suited to our investigation: they are self-contained, proof-based, and carefully designed to develop logical precision, while also involving substantial bridging-level computation. Their selection supports students’ transition from school mathematics to the standards of university-level proof and abstraction, making them an ideal testbed for evaluating automated feedback.

3.1.2
Content of Questions

The IUM problem set contains over 60 exercises, rated from one to three stars in difficulty. For this study, we selected a subset of challenging and conceptually rich problems from the first two topics:

•

Topic 1:
Number systems. This covers the construction of the number systems, starting with the Peano Axioms and the natural numbers, and proceeding through the formal construction of the integers and rationals, ending with the axioms of the real numbers. Students re-derive properties they have previously taken for granted, such as associativity of addition or trichotomy of order. These exercises are intentionally stripped of visual aids or heuristic shortcuts—students must reason strictly from axioms, definitions, and formal deduction. The result is a deliberate break from intuition and a rigorous introduction to abstract reasoning.

•

Topic 2:
Vector Geometry and Vector Calculus. This topic is more applied, but equally foundational. Students work with lines, planes, and distances in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, constructing geometric arguments in vector notation. The emphasis shifts from axiomatic reasoning to bridging geometric intuition with algebraic formulation, preparing students for later modules in linear algebra and multivariable calculus.

3.1.3
Generation of Solutions

The question set was divided among three members of the team. These were experienced students who had previously completed the IUM and are co-authors of this paper. Two were in their second year of study at the time of the active part of the project, and one in their third. Each independently wrote solutions to a subset of the questions. While most of the resulting answers were of high quality and reflected strong proof attempts, they naturally varied in style, structure, and accuracy. Some solutions included inadvertent mistakes, while others were deliberately written to contain common misconceptions or conceptual errors, simulating the kinds of responses a struggling student might submit. In total, 65 solutions were generated.

3.1.4
Constructing a Dataset for Marking and Feedback

Our main dataset consists of four components: the questions, the student-written solutions, numerical grades from 0 to 5 assigned by three human markers (two of whom graded half the dataset and one provided an independent grading run for the whole dataset), and short written feedback aligned with undergraduate teaching standards. These were used as the benchmark for evaluating LLM-generated feedback and grading accuracy. Grades were for the final version of the dataset reconciled between the two independent human grading runs. The reconciliation process was done by one of the authors based on manual review of all disagreements.

Validating the Grader

First, we needed to validate the idea that our feedback could be used to infer grades. In that way, we could test whether at least feedback
identical
to the human feedback would produce high grade correlations with the human grades. This turned out to be the case.

Human feedback was used in test runs where
gpt-4o
was asked to assign a grade based solely on the feedback, with or without access to the original question. These tests assessed whether
gpt-4o
could infer a grade from realistic tutor-style feedback. As such, the feedback needed to be both informative enough to imply a mark and concise enough to remain authentic in the sense that a real tutor could afford to comment at that level of detail on the work of
every
student.

Evaluation revealed that minor changes in phrasing led to significant variation in LLM-assigned grades. For example, using “correctly identifies” resulted in lower grades, even when the proof was accurate, whereas rephrasing as “proves” or “constructs” produced appropriate marks (e.g. a shift from 0 to 5). Accordingly, some feedback entries were reworded to test how linguistic cues influenced the model’s interpretation. However, overall correlation between LLM-assigned grades and human grades was quite high, showing that
gpt-4o
was to a large extent able to infer grades from human feedback.

Marking Scheme:

To ensure consistency, the marking scheme was improved over several iterations:

•

Mark Scheme 1
was the initial version. It described each mark level qualitatively, using phrases such as “some correct elements” or “significant mistakes”. However, it included overlapping criteria, which made the distinction between adjacent scores ambiguous.

•

Mark Scheme 2
addressed these ambiguities by introducing mutually exclusive criteria for each mark. It distinguished clearly between “major” and “minor” errors and provided both positive and negative indicators for each grade level. It also included representative examples to aid interpretation.

•

Mark Scheme 3
was a more concise formulation. It reduced each mark level to one defining strength and one limitation, improving interpretability for the LLM while preserving rigour. This version was found to improve performance in grading tasks.

•

Mark Scheme 4
further simplified the format. It retained the structure of one positive and one negative feature per grade level, resulting in short but clear instructions for each of the six marks from 0 to 5. This version was used for the final evaluation.

3.2
Evaluation Methods

Our evaluation uses different approaches to check different design goals. In the sequel, we briefly discuss our methods to evaluate how well a particular configuration of our system hits the goals discussed.

Design Goals Not Tested

Some of the design goals are difficult to test outside of practical deployment. We list these goals briefly below and mention to what extent we nonetheless made an effort to test for them or to make sure that they are satisfied:

1.

Safety can ultimately only be judged in large deployments with monitoring of potentially unsafe inputs and outputs, or alternatively in adversarial testing, i.e. using jailbreaking studies. The deployment setting we target (tutoring in an undergraduate mathematics context) is in our judgment relatively low-risk to begin with. Our design decisions to allow only single-turn interactions and to filter inputs through OpenAI’s standard moderation endpoints should further lower the likelihood of outputs that are directly harmful to the student or inappropriate. In our test deployment, we further reduced these problems by making the tutor available only for submission of answers to fixed homework questions set by the course instructor, and with a query limit per student.

2.

Reconfigurability can likewise only be tested in deployment, ideally outside test deployments where developers can directly troubleshoot problems. The directive system does allow reprogramming the tutor in natural language, although the use of JSON to encode the directives still represents a barrier to entry.

Overall Evaluation

Our main tool for overall quantitative evaluation of tutor performance is comparison of tutor feedback with the
grading
of the answers submitted returned by human experts. The main rationale behind this is as follows:

1.

Grades implicitly contain many of the judgments that also go into feedback generation: for instance, both contain an assessment of mistakes made in a submission, of their relative severity, and also of aspects of a submission that are not strictly speaking mathematical mistakes, like unclear expression.

2.

Given a dataset of submissions, grades and short written feedback can be collected once from human experts and can then be reused when evaluating different system configurations. By contrast, direct evaluation of the feedback itself by human experts would probably provide a more reliable measure of feedback quality, but implies a large manual work factor that has to be repeated for every iteration of the system.

3.

We also wanted to be able to make use of the pre-existing dataset by Frieder et al.
[
8
]
, which contains a broad set of questions and solutions that roughly resemble mathematical undergraduate homework in terms of mathematical depth, but which contains only grades and not detailed feedback on the solutions.

Therefore, we extend all of our feedback generation workflows by a
grading
step. The grading step takes the feedback and is instructed to derive a numerical grade for the submission purely from the feedback. The grading step was optimised by manually trying a small number of grading prompts and testing them on the human-generated feedback on our test submissions. Grader performance was optimised by simply picking the grading prompt that produced the highest Pearson correlation between human grades and automatically generated grades based on the human feedback.

This preliminary step of optimising the grader used the full dataset, but we do not think that this creates a data contamination risk for the experiments subsequently run because grader optimisation only used the human-generated feedback as input. This feedback is not used in the subsequent evaluation pipeline, and we settled on the final grading prompt after trying fewer than 20 possibilities, so we did not exert high optimisation pressure on the grading mechanism.

Additionally, we evaluate the conciseness of the feedback by simple word counting. A word here is just a sequence of characters separated by whitespace or punctuation marks.

Methodology Details for the Grading Step

The grading step is in our study performed by an additional workflow step that is run after the feedback generation step.

The translation of feedback to a grade is an important step in our quantitative evaluation. As such, it is important to realise that it comes with some choices that must be made and which contain non-obvious tradeoffs. We briefly discuss these choices and the choices we made in our experiments here.

First, we need to decide on the rubric to use, i.e. on the general instructions that the model will follow when grading. We chose a rubric by trying a few variants and selecting among the tested variants the one that gave the most accurate translation of
human-written feedback
to human grades. The rubric we chose gives short but clear instructions for each of the six grade levels from 0 to 5.

Second, we need to decide what information will be available to the model when grading. One can make a case both for just showing the grader the feedback, and for showing the grader the question and the feedback. Sometimes it is useful for the grader to know this context, because without it the importance of any problems mentioned in the feedback may be unclear; on other hand, showing the grader the question and the feedback in principle allows an extremely simple “feedback generator” that just copies the student submission verbatim and leaves evaluation entirely to the grader, which could be an optimal strategy if feedback generation is much harder than grading. We opted to accept this risk, but are overall agnostic on which variant would be methodically superior for the present paper. In no case did we show the grader the student submission, because this would allow the grader to ignore the feedback and assess the student’s answer on its own.

Third, we need to decide
which model
will be used to grade the feedback. There are two natural choices: either use the same model that was used to generate the feedback, or use a different model. We opted for the latter, because we are trying to assess the quality of the feedback generation system, and using different grading steps for each model/workflow combination could bias evaluation towards models that are capable of performing accurate grading while ignoring the feedback.

Concretely, we used a grading step that is
mildly adversarial
to the feedback generation system. Essentially, after a particular model/workflow combination has generated feedback, we use a range of different grading models to grade the feedback, compute our metrics for grading accuracy with respect to reconciled human grades, and use as the true score of the model/workflow combination the
worst-performing
grading model. Our selection of grading models spans a range of predominantly lightweight models:
gpt-4o
,
gpt-4.1-nano
,
gpt-5-nano
,
gpt-5-mini
, and
gpt-4o-mini
.

The rationale for doing this is that good feedback must be robust against being misunderstood by a student who struggles with the material presented. We propose that using a range of different, relatively lightweight models in the grading step captures this robustness requirement, albeit in a very rough way: most of the models used in the grading step are unlikely to be good enough at mathematics to perform the grading task without the aid of the feedback, and if they misunderstand the feedback, the likelihood is high that students might do so as well. If, on the other hand, feedback generated can be reliably translated to grades that correspond well to the judgments of human experts even by a model such as
gpt-4.1-nano
, then it is likely that students will likewise understand it correctly.

In total, we used eight different feedback generation models (
gpt-4o
,
gpt-4.1
,
gpt-4.1-mini
,
gpt-4.1-nano
,
gpt-5
,
gpt-5-mini
,
gpt-5-nano
), five different workflows, and five grading models, for a total of 200 different model/workflow/grading model combinations and 40 model/workflow combinations. Apart from the three best workflows from our workflow development, two variations of a basic workflow were included in the evaluation: both just see the question and the student submission and
directly
generate feedback. The two basic workflows differ from each other in that one includes an instruction to limit feedback length to 200 words, while the other does not.

Results

4.1
Overview of Results

In this section, we present the quantitative and qualitative results of our study. We first discuss quantitative results, then qualitative results, and finally, we discuss the lessons learned from our study.
The subsection on quantitative results will first discuss grade correlations between human and LLM grades, and finally the coverage and conciseness of the feedback as discussed in the previous section.

The subsection on qualitative results will discuss the findings from manual testing of the feedback generator and manual inspection of feedback generated in the automated evaluation. It will also cover a manual comparison of human and LLM feedback. Of necessity, the results of the qualitative testing will be more anecdotal than the quantitative results, as it is difficult to turn these observations into clear metrics. We do however believe that the qualitative results give important insights into the behaviour of the feedback generator, on the one hand by exercising edge cases, and on the other hand by capturing aspects of the feedback that may be important for practical teaching deployment, but hard to capture otherwise.

The subsection on lessons learned will discuss what we learned from the study in terms of design choices for the feedback generator, for the process of optimising such a system, and for evaluating these systems in the small sample size regime our study operates in.

4.2
Quantitative Results

Our quantitative results are all based on measuring the correlation between grades derived from the feedback our tutoring system produces in various configurations and the human grades in our dataset.

We then manually reviewed all disagreements between the two human graders and reconciled them. The resulting grades were used as the human baseline against which all LLM graders were compared.

gpt-4o

gpt-4o
grades. worst

4.3
Qualitative Results

4.3.1
Feedback on exemplary solutions

We evaluated our LLM-based feedback tool (running, for these tests, with
o4-mini
as the underlying language model and with a simple solve/feedback/grade workflow) on a set of exemplary solutions from a first-year mathematics course. We had two reasons for doing this: one, we wanted to test another use case of our tool, in this case proofreading for technical accuracy, that is similar but distinct from giving feedback to students; two, our tool is designed to provide critical feedback, and as such, it is natural to ask whether it is prone to giving negative feedback even when there is nothing to be critical about.

In the majority of cases, we found the tool’s feedback was substantively correct. Most flagged issues stemmed from missing problem context: for instance, whether prime-factorisation characterisations of
gcd/lcm were permitted (Item 1), which Peano axiom numbering was being used (Item 4), or whether prior lemmas/cancellation rules were available (Items 6, 11–12).

We found that these “false positives” are largely avoidable by explicitly giving the allowed assumptions as context. We note that this context problem is difficult to address in a general chat platform, because different teaching settings differ in this regard even when the material taught is the same.

The tool was also effective at detecting genuine mismatches and errors. It correctly identified solutions proving the wrong statement (Items 2 and 7), a misleading substitution that did not yield the target claim (Item 9), and a computational mistake (Item 14). In several cases it improved exposition without altering mathematical content (Items 8, 13, 15), and it supplied missing steps when the submitted solution appealed to earlier results not shown (Items 3, 11–12). The only clearly unhelpful intervention was Item 10, where the critique hinged on treating
\pm\infty
as numbers, contrary to the intended (non-extended) real setting. This is arguably also a context issue: in an introductory Analysis course, the ambient domain is always
\mathbb{R}
, not the extended reals, and no one would ordinarily consider

\pm\infty
as numbers.

Our main takeaways are as follows. The overall performance on this early undergraduate problem set at proofreading exemplary solution was very good; several problems were found, most of them clerical. Providing minimal, machine-readable context, such as the allowed axioms, prior results, and notation/conventions, substantially reduces spurious objections. The remaining failure modes are chiefly due to convention sensitivity (for example, axiom labels) and domain specification (for instance,working in
\mathbb{R}
versus the extended reals).

Solution:
Since nobody ever dies, and since everyone has at most two children, and since the gender ratio is 1:1, every generation will have exactly 1000 births (since each child has two parents). Therefore, initial growth is 1000 births every 30 years, which is a growth rate of
1000/30\approx 33.33
births per year. After a billion years, the population will be roughly
1000\times 10^{9}/30\approx 3.33\times 10^{10}

\approx 3.33

Comment:
The solution is designed to show a failure mode that is plausible for human reasoning. It fails to properly model the growth process as a stochastic process. The limiting factor for growth is the size of the male or female population who have had less than two children as yet, and this quantity should be modelled by a random walk where each birth increases either males or females by one, and deducts one from the other gender. This random walk will eventually hit the boundary where one gender has no members with remaining reproductive capacity, and the population is subsequently eternally stable.

4.4
Lessons Learned and Practical Guidance

This section gives a summary of the lessons we learned from both our experiments and our work deploying the tutoring application in
Lambda Feedback
(a pre-existing feedback generation application at Imperial College London). The results we discuss here are mostly qualitative in nature; quantitative results are reported in Subsection
4.2
and Appendix
A
.

Prompting and workflow

In our testing, a modular workflow was essential for obtaining acceptable feedback, especially when working with
non-reasoning
LLMs. When asking the model directly to generate feedback to a given question and solution, output was frequently too verbose and/or at least partially wrong. Non-reasoning models in this setting are forced to simultaneously figure out the solution and the feedback, and do so without revealing the solution via their chain-of-thought to the student. Nonetheless, if a separate grading step is added, non-reasoning models are able to produce good grade-correlations with direct feedback (see Section
4.2
) if a relatively strong model is used for the grading step. However, this could plausibly be due to the grading model ignoring the feedback and grading based on the question and solution alone. In principle, this could be easily tested by running the grading step in isolation while randomly permuting the feedback and the question/solution pairs to be graded; if the grade-correlation is still high, this would very strongly suggest that the feedback is being ignored. We leave such tests for future work.

Decomposing the task also opens up the possibility of performing parts of the task (for instance, generation of model solutions or detailed grading rubrics) in a one-time precomputation step, which saves cost and allows the use of the most powerful models available for this part. This is very relevant for deployment settings where the use case is not to process
arbitrary
questions, but rather to give feedback on questions assigned to the student by a teacher. Precomputation of such steps also has the advantage that human teachers can directly edit the precomputed steps to improve accuracy or pedagogical quality or to otherwise tune the system to produce better feedback. More generally, task decomposition greatly simplifies debugging of the tutoring system, because each step can be independently audited.

We also experimented with varying the model’s
persona
, by which we mean the role the model is instructed to play when performing the various steps of a given workflow. Adopting a
teaching-assistant
persona—clear, supportive, and non-hedged—consistently produced more actionable feedback for first-year proofs than either neutral or overly cautious voices. Experiments with making the LLM model (in this case,
gpt-4o
) act as a specific expert in mathematics (in this case, Terence Tao) did not yield noticeably better results either along our quantitative metrics or in subjective assessment than the teaching-assistant persona.

For non-reasoning models, one parameter that is a natural target for tuning is the
temperature
used in the inference process.
7
7

7

For reasoning models, sampling temperature is commonly not exposed by the model API.
Roughly speaking, increasing the temperature flattens the distribution of tokens selected for output, while lowering it makes the model more deterministic. The models offered by OpenAI do not seem to be deterministic at zero temperature. For steps that can be run in precomputation, it is very plausible that results can be improved and sanity-checked by running the step multiple times and storing a majority vote or flagging significant disagreements for human review. However, in our experiments, we did not perform such best-of-N runs.

Data augmentation

We attempted to augment the dataset with more answers generated automatically to match a given target grade. This yielded very unsatisfactory results, as the models we tried seemed unable to generate wrong answers with natural mistakes on command. It would certainly have been possible to make language models generate wrong answers to the given questions, for instance by choosing a sufficiently weak language model, but such answers would likely not have produced natural human error patterns and it would have been impossible in advance to predict the severity of the mistakes.

Failure modes and mitigations

We now discuss some common failure modes and mitigations that we found useful in our testing:

•

Feedback is often still verbose. This is especially true for feedback on correct solutions. It is possible to mitigate this problem by explicitly limiting the length of the feedback, but this needs to be balanced against the need to provide the student with all of the information they need to learn from the feedback; in our opinion, neither the human feedback nor our better LLM-generated workflows hit the mark on this, with the human feedback tending towards too much concision and the LLM-generated feedback being too verbose. Further experimentation and user testing will be needed to find a good balance.

•

Feedback on correct solutions is often padded with suggestions for stylistic improvements. Qualitatively, we have seen this less with reasoning models than with non-reasoning models, which for the question sets considered in this work is one of the few tangible benefits of using reasoning models instead of sufficiently capable non-reasoning models.

•

When the tutoring application is integrated into a pre-existing e-learning platform, compatibility issues can arise. For instance, traditional intelligent tutoring systems rely on dedicated algorithms to check student answers. These dedicated algorithms cannot process the question in the natural-language format that the student is shown, and indeed frequently do not even have to know what the question is (instead, it is sufficient for them to know what the expected
answer
is). A consequence for systems such as ours is that the platform will have separate storage for reference solutions and questions, and a connection between the two cannot be expected to be automatic. This can easily lead to misconfigurations and problems with the human review of question setup on the platform. Automated setup scripts are a mitigation, but conversion from natural-language mathematical problem sheets to the format required for storage on
Lambda Feedback
for instance proved nontrivial to automate fully. In principle, dedicated online platforms for ITS systems that process problems posed in natural language should probably make the question setup directly visible to the tutoring system.

•

Supplying context from the course about notations, conventions, and allowed results is very important to allow the system to correctly assess student answers. The underlying models have otherwise no way of knowing these course-specific details. It should be possible to automatically extract this context from lecture notes or to guess plausible defaults from the automatic processing of problem sheets, but human review of such assumed defaults is necessary and the teaching platform has to support it.

•

Deployments must balance processing cost with accuracy of generated feedback. For mathematically more complex questions, it is likely beneficial to use reasoning models for the feedback generation step, even though the present work does not show any benefit from doing so. To allow for this, deployments should allow model defaults to be overridden on a per-question or even per-workflow-step basis. Additionally, automated processing of user feedback could be used to escalate to stronger models if the user either requests it or the system detects that they are having trouble with the feedback.

•

For questions that overwhelm the system’s mathematical reasoning abilities, our stress testing showed instances of very poor output. We expect that some of our stress testing questions would also be wrongly graded by mathematically competent but not specifically prepared human graders. Still, in settings with harder questions, our stress testing shows that selecting underlying language models with strong reasoning capabilities is important. Additionally, when parts of a workflow can be run without knowing the student submission and the results of those workflow steps can be reused across many submissions, it makes sense to use the strongest available models to run these steps and/or to review or modify these steps using human expertise.

Overall, we found that the system presented is quite robust in the sense that a wide range of errors in the submitted solutions are correctly identified and the feedback is helpful. Nonetheless, there is certainly still room for improvement, which we hope to achieve in future work.

4.5
Open Questions and Future Work

The current paper presented work on the construction and evaluation of a modular feedback generation system for undergraduate university-level mathematics. Unlike other work we are aware of, our work focused to a large extent on the evaluation of feedback correctness. We hope to have shown that the system as presented is already a useful tool for feedback generation and related tasks (such as proofreading of exemplary solutions) for higher mathematics education. It should open up various possibilities for follow-up work. We will briefly discuss some of these in this section.

Integrating Large Language Models with Traditional Intelligent Tutoring Systems

In the context of deploying our mathematics tutor on
Lambda Feedback
, we did a significant amount of work to automatically convert problem sheets presented in their traditional format to the format required by the platform and by our system. This work naturally lends itself to the follow-up question of whether similar automations could be made available to other types of tutoring systems. Essentially, this would involve developing an intelligent interface that allows a teacher to give their online platform their problem sheets, and have applicable questions converted to the format required by an e-learning platform that uses specialised algorithms to check student answers.

Integrating Formal Theorem Provers or Other Mathematical Tools

It could be interesting to develop interactive teaching environments similar to ours that support natural-language mathematical problem solving and interaction with the teaching system, but which connect to computer algebra systems or formal theorem prover backends to perform complete verification of the student’s solution.

In the setting where an LLM would be working in tandem with Lean or another proof assistant, it would be expected to handle on the one hand autoformalisation (and this has been studied for instance in LeanTutor
[
28
]
), but could also comment both on logical gaps in the student’s argument that become apparent after autoformalisation, or on high-level strategy and presentation issues. We expect that this could be very useful in some mathematics teaching contexts, for instance where having a computational sandbox to run experiments in is beneficial.

Better Evaluation of Feedback Quality

The methods used for the evaluation of feedback quality in the present work are relatively limited. The end goal of teaching is to teach mathematics to students who do not yet know it, and of course at the other end of the evaluation methods spectrum, practical longitudinal studies of student learning are the gold standard, but they are also very expensive to run, need ethics review, and are therefore often limited in scope. It would be very interesting to develop automated proxies for pedagogical quality of feedback. For instance, one idea could be to use the in-context learning capabilities of language models to simulate human learners solving the same questions as the students in the dataset, and to then compare the solving ability of a particular model on new questions before and after receiving feedback on questions attempted.

More Intuitive Workflow Configuration

The workflow configuration interface used in our prototype requires only knowledge of a natural language (with the tested language being English, but other widely spoken languages expected to work as well), and a basic understanding of how to use a JSON file. However, in many settings, this still leaves some room for improvement. Ideally, teaching staff should be able to set and experiment with new workflows just by
drawing
a workflow graph similar to the one shown in Figure
1
. We expect that this should be achievable; possibly even with something as simple as an LLM workflow of the type configurable in our system when it is using a vision language model and prompted to output a JSON configuration file instead of feedback on mathematical homework.

We hope to study these ideas in future work.
