Achieving Irrational Correlated Equilibria without Mediator
Shitong Wang

Abstract
This paper investigates the implementation and performance of a decentralized information transmission mechanism in game with complete or incomplete games. We propose a mechanism that realizes irrational correlated equilibria or irrational communication equilibria through a finite rounds of cheap talk without mediator. Designed for games with at least five players, the core procedure involves three players decomposing and encrypting the equilibrium, while two other players securely randomize and deliver the encrypted recommended strategies to designated players. Our results demonstrate that all irrational correlated equilibria or irrational communication equilibria can be achieved through this mechanism, which is both strategically robust and practically implementable. Keywords: Mechanism, Irrational correlated equilibria, Irrational communication equilibria, Cheap talk, Encryption

Introduction

The concept of
cheap talk
—costless, non-binding, and non-verifiable communication—was formally introduced by Crawford and Sobel (1982), who established the canonical model of strategic information transmission. They demonstrated that when players’ preferences are not perfectly aligned, communication can still credibly convey partial information through partition equilibria, in which the sender coarsely groups private information into intervals. Building on this foundation, Farrell (1987, 1995) explored cheap talk in coordination games, such as the
battle of the sexes
and
stag hunt
, showing that pre-play communication can facilitate Pareto-superior equilibria when interests are sufficiently aligned, though residual coordination failures persist under conflicting incentives.

Subsequent research expanded the scope of the theory. Krishna and Morgan (2004) further formalized two-way, multi-round communication, revealing how conversational exchange can increase informativeness and improve welfare outcomes. Similarly, Aumann and Hart (2003) analyzed long cheap talk in two-player games with one-sided incomplete information, showing that extended conversations can yield strictly higher equilibrium payoffs than one-shot communication.

Another strand of literature addressed the limits of cheap talk under significant preference divergence. Battaglini (2002) demonstrated that in multi-dimensional settings, even when one-dimensional communication fails, credible information can be transmitted through relative comparisons across dimensions. Chen et al. (2008) proposed the No Incentive to Separate (NITS) refinement, which, under monotonicity, uniquely selects the most informative equilibrium, offering a robust equilibrium selection criterion.

More recent work has examined implementation and practical challenges. Abraham et al. (2018) studied the feasibility of replacing mediators with asynchronous cheap talk, providing boundaries for its applicability in distributed mechanism design. Experimental studies, such as Charness (2000, 2006) and Kenkel (2019), have tested the effectiveness of cheap talk in laboratory and field settings, showing that while non-binding communication can substantially improve coordination and efficiency, its success depends critically on the degree of common interest and the structure of the game environment.

Together, these contributions chart the evolution of cheap-talk theory from its foundational model to its multi-stage, multi-dimensional, and implementation-oriented extensions, providing both theoretical and empirical insights into when and how costless communication can meaningfully influence strategic outcomes. In this paper, we attempt to design a cheap-talk mechanism to replace the mediator in correlated equilibrium or communication equilibrium.

To provide a theoretical foundation for our mechanism design, we first present a brief review of the literature on correlated equilibrium.

In game theory and information economics, Nash (1950) equilibrium is a cornerstone in game theory, providing a fundamental concept of strategic stability where no player has an incentive to unilaterally deviate. A common criticism of this equilibrium is its overly demanding assumptions, particularly in ‘‘reasonable’’ situations where players are required to choose their strategies independently, which often leads to inefficiencies in coordination. To address this, Aumann (1974) introduced the concept of correlated equilibrium and developed (1987) it through bayesian rationality and common prior. The visual process of achieving correlated equilibrum is that players engage in pre-play communication to coordinate their strategies based on signals they receive, and then they separate into isolated rooms, each receiving a signal from a ‘‘signaling device’’

The above papers all constructed sophisticated mechanisms to replace signaling devices. The core idea of simulating a signaling device is to uniformly draw an element from a finite set, with each element mapped to a strategy profile in the correlated equilibrium. This process is implemented through complex permutations and information transmission among players, ensuring that each player privately receives the strategy recommended for them in the selected profile.

These mechanisms, however, are effective only for rational correlated equilibria, since uniform randomization over a finite set can generate only rational probabilities. As a result, this method applies exclusively to correlated equilibria whose probability distributions assign rational numbers to all strategy profiles i.e., to rational correlated equilibria.

In this paper, we aim to construct a new mechanism that implements any correlated equilibrium—including irrational ones—as the outcome of a Nash equilibrium via a finite number of rounds of cheap talk.

2.1
Irrational correlated equilibrium in game with complete information

Gerardi (2004) proposed a method to construct irrational correlated equilibria as finite convex combinations of rational correlated equilibria, provided that the payoff of every strategy profile is rational for all players. For example, consider a game in which players aim to implement a correlated equilibrium whose distribution over strategy profiles is given by
\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)
. This distribution can be decomposed into two rational correlated equilibria with distributions
\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)
and
\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}\right)
, weighted by probabilities
\frac{2\sqrt{2}-1}{2}
and
\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{2}
respectively. That is,
\frac{2\sqrt{2}-1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)+\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}\right)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)
.

While this approach is computationally tractable, it introduces a critical limitation: decomposing the original game into subgames may break ex-ante equivalence. That is, players become aware of which rational correlated equilibrium they are participating in, leading to variations in their expected payoffs across different realizations.

We now turn to the design of a mechanism for mediator-free games in which all stages must preserve players’
interim
expectations—not merely their ex-ante payoffs—in order to maintain fidelity to the original game structure.

Before that, we formally give a strict definition of correlated equilibrium:

Consider a finite strategic-form game with complete information, where the set of players’ indices is
N=\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
R_{i}
denotes the
i
-th player. The strategy set of
R_{i}
is
A_{i}
, and the payoff function of

is

u_{i}:A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}\to\mathbb{R}.

Let
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}
denote the set of all pure strategy profiles.

A

is a probability distribution
p\in\Delta(A)
over strategy profiles such that, for every player
i\in N
and for all

\sum_{a_{-i}\in A_{-i}}p(a_{i},a_{-i})\,u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})\ \geq\ \sum_{a_{-i}\in A_{-i}}p(a_{i},a_{-i})\,u_{i}(a_{i}^{\prime},a_{-i}),

where
A_{-i}=\prod_{j\neq i}A_{j}
and

denotes the strategies of all players except

In an actual game, such a distribution

can be implemented via a mediator: before the game begins, the mediator privately recommends to each
R_{i}
a strategy
a_{i}
drawn according to
p
. The above inequalities ensure that, given their recommendation, no player can gain by unilaterally deviating to any alternative strategy.

Proposition:

For any finite strategic-form game with complete information and at least five players, any correlated equilibrium can be implemented as a Nash equilibrium through a finite number of rounds of cheap talk.

2.2
Irrational correlated equilibrim in game with incomplete information

We now turn to
irrational correlated equilibrium
in games with incomplete information. Forges (1993) identifies five legitimate definitions of
correlated equilibrium
in such games: the
Strategic Form Approach
,
Agent Normal Form Approach
,
Communication Equilibrium
,
Partial Bayesian Approach
, and
Universal Bayesian Approach
, the last of which is not considered in this paper.

The
Strategic Form Approach
maintains structural consistency with the correlated equilibrium concept in complete-information games. The
Agent Normal Form Approach
, by definition, assumes that the mediator is fully informed of each player’s type and is therefore incompatible with our mediator-free setting. The
Partial Bayesian Approach
, on the other hand, does not require a real mediator. Consequently, we adopt the framework of
Communication Equilibrium
to illustrate our mechanism for realizing
irrational correlated equilibrium
without a mediator.

Before that, we introduce the concept of communication equilibrium. In a game with incomplete information, a communication equilibrium is defined as follows: each player observes their private type and sends a message to a mediator; based on the reported messages, the mediator then privately recommends strategies to the players. The equilibrium condition requires that no player can improve their expected payoff by misreporting their type or deviating from the recommended strategy. When this condition holds, the resulting outcome is called a communication equilibrium.

Now, we formally give a strict definition of communication equilibrium:

Consider a game with incomplete information where each
R_{i}
has a private type
t_{i}
drawn from a commonly known distribution over the type space
T_{i}
. A
communication equilibrium
consists of:

A messaging function for each player,
m_{i}:T_{i}\to M_{i}
, where
M_{i}
denotes the message space of player
i
;

A recommendation function by the mediator,

\sigma:M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{n}\to\Delta(A),

which maps the vector of messages to a distribution over strategy profiles.

These satisfy the following incentive compatibility condition: for every player
i
, every type
t_{i}\in T_{i}
, every alternative message
m_{i}^{\prime}\in M_{i}
, and every alternative strategy
a_{i}^{\prime}\in A_{i}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[u_{i}(t_{i},a_{i},a_{-i})\mid m_{i},m_{-i}\big]\geq\mathbb{E}\big[u_{i}(t_{i},a_{i}^{\prime},a_{-i})\mid m_{i}^{\prime},m_{-i}\big],

where
a_{i}
is drawn from the distribution
\sigma(m_{i},m_{-i})
recommended by the mediator, and
u_{i}
is player
i
’s utility function.

By the revelation principle, the messaging functions
m_{i}
can be taken as self-map, meaning that each player’s message space coincides with their type space. Thus, the incentive constraints can be equivalently written as:

\mathbb{E}\big[u_{i}(t_{i},a_{i},a_{-i})\mid t_{i},t_{-i}\big]\geq\mathbb{E}\big[u_{i}(t_{i},a_{i}^{\prime},a_{-i})\mid t_{i}^{\prime},t_{-i}\big].

In other words, no player can gain by misreporting their type
t_{i}
as
t_{i}^{\prime}
, nor by deviating from the recommended strategy
a_{i}
.

Before the game begins, all players agree in advance on a correlated equilibrium for every type profile in
T=T_{1}\times\cdots\times T_{n}
. For each
t\in T
, let
p[t]
denote the strategy profile distribution corresponding to the type profile
t
. Here, if at least one
p[t]
is irrational, then we say that the communication equilibrium is irrational communication equilibrium.

Proposition:

For any finite strategic-form game with incomplete information and at least five players, any communication equilibrium can be implemented as a Bayesian Equilibrium through a finite number of rounds of cheap talk.

3
Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel mediator-free mechanism that enables the implementation of irrational correlated equilibria through a finite sequence of cheap talk. Unlike previous approaches restricted to rational equilibria due to their reliance on finite randomizations, our construction leverages layered decompositions, interval partitioning, and encryption-based recommendation protocols to overcome the limitations of rational support. Crucially, the mechanism ensures that all players’ strategies remain secure, private, and indistinguishable under execution, preserving both strategic robustness and informational integrity.

Our results demonstrate that, in any finite strategic-form game with complete information and at least five players, any correlated equilibrium—rational or irrational—can be faithfully implemented as a Nash equilibrium; in any finite strategic-form game with incomplete information and at least five players, any communication equilibrium—rational or irrational—can be faithfully implemented as a Bayesian equilibrium. This expands the scope of implementability in mechanism design, bridging the gap between theoretical feasibility and practical realization. Future research may explore relaxing the player-number requirement, improving communication efficiency, or extending the mechanism to dynamic or continuous-action environments.
