Multitype 𝚲 \bm{\Lambda} -coalescents and continuous state branching processes
Adrián González Casanova, Noemi Kurt, Imanol Nuñez Morales, José Luis Pérez

Abstract
. We provide new connections between multitype Λ \Lambda -coalescents and multitype continuous state branching processes via duality and a homeomorphism on their parameter space. The approach is based on a sequential sampling procedure for the frequency process of independent CSBPs, and provides forward and backward processes for multitype population models under Λ \Lambda -type reproduction. It provides some insight on different approaches to generalise Λ \Lambda -coalescents to the multitype setup. Keywords. Multitype Λ \Lambda -coalescent, continuous state branching process, duality MSC2020 subject classification 60J90, 60J80



1.
Introduction

The genealogy of continuous-state branching processes (CSBPs) has attracted significant attention over the past few decades. Various approaches have been employed to describe them and relate them to other processes, such as coalescents. Le Gall and Le Jan
[
25
]
, inspired by previous work where a discrete height process encodes the genealogy of a Galton–Watson process (see, for instance,
[
24
]
), proposed a method to encode the genealogy of a CSBP through a real-valued random process also termed the height process. This approach was further developed by Duquesne and Le Gall
[
16
]
, who reformulated and solved problems about the genealogy of CSBPs in terms of spectrally positive Lévy processes. Later, Bertoin and Le Gall
[
3
]
introduced a new perspective, suggesting that the genealogy of CSBPs could be studied using flows of subordinators. Specifically, they demonstrated that the genealogy of Neveu’s CSBP corresponds to the Bolthausen–Sznitman coalescent. In a series of works
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, Bertoin and Le Gall defined the
\Lambda
-Fleming–Viot process, proving its moment duality with the
\Lambda
-coalescent introduced by Pitman
[
29
]
and Sagitov
[
31
]
. Pitman characterized the
\Lambda
-coalescent as the exchangeable and consistent coalescent where separate coalescent events cannot occur simultaneously. In the final paper of the series
[
6
]
, Bertoin and Le Gall established that locally, the
\Lambda
-Fleming–Viot process behaves similarly to a CSBP. This connection allowed them to link the notion of “coming down from infinity” for
\Lambda
-coalescents to the extinction of a corresponding CSBP. However, their proof was analytical, and they conjectured the existence of a deeper, probabilistic link between
\Lambda
-coalescents and CSBPs.

Birkner, Blath, Capaldo, Etheridge, Möhle, Schweinsberg and Wakolbinger
[
7
]
took a different approach. Using the lookdown construction of Donnelly and Kurtz
[
13
,
14
]
, they defined the genealogy of a CSBP and a measure-valued process simultaneously. Birkner et al. showed that the genealogy derived from the lookdown construction could be time-changed to be a Markov process if and only if the CSBP is
\alpha
-stable, in which case the time-changed genealogy corresponds to a
\beta
-coalescent. This result generalizes the findings of Bertoin and Le Gall
[
3
]
which corresponds to
\alpha=1
. Furthermore, if
X
and
Y
are two independent realizations of a CSBP, the frequency process
R=X/(X+Y)
can be time-changed to be the dual of the block-counting process of a
\Lambda
-coalescent if and only if the branching mechanism is
\alpha
-stable. Building on earlier ideas
[
14
,
7
]
, Berestycki et al.
[
2
]
constructed a coupling between a
\Lambda
-coalescent and an associated CSBP, addressing the conjecture posed by Bertoin and Le Gall in
[
6
]
.

Caballero et al.
[
12
]
, motivated by studying genetic dynamics in populations with two types of individuals and potentially distinct reproduction mechanisms, introduced the sequential sampling procedure, referred to as “culling” in their work and inspired by Gillespie’s framework
[
19
]
. They showed that, if
X
and
Y
are two independent CSBPs, the sequential sampling procedure discretizes the frequency process
R
to derive an autonomous frequency process. When
X
and
Y
share the same reproduction mechanism, this autonomous frequency process is dual to the block-counting process of a
\Lambda
-coalescent, with
\Lambda
explicitly determined by the branching mechanism’s parameters. Caballero et al. constructed a homeomorphism between CSBP parameters and the driving measure
\Lambda
of
\Lambda
-coalescents, deepening the connection between these processes. If we call this homeomorphism Gillespie’s transformation, an alternative and equivalent definition of the
\Lambda
-coalescents is the image of the CSBP under Gillespie’s transformation.

The previously mentioned processes naturally generalize to the multitype set up. Multitype branching processes are a well-established area of research, and there are many examples of multitype aspects in coalescent processes. For instance, structured coalescents where blocks have different types and interact depending on their types are a prominent class. The two-island model is the simplest example of this category. More recent examples include the seed bank coalescent with simultaneous switching
[
9
]
, and the nested coalescent
[
8
]
. A natural question arises: What is a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent? Extending Pitman’s characterization to the multitype case yields a reasonable definition: A multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in the Pitman sense is a multitype exchangeable and consistent coalescent where separate coalescent events cannot occur simultaneously. It can be shown that the examples mentioned above are multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents in the Pitman sense.

Recently, Johnston and Lambert
[
22
]
used a Poissonization technique to investigate the genealogy of multitype CSBPs. While they showed that the genealogy of a CSBP is not generally Markovian, they proved that it can locally be represented as a
\Lambda
-coalescent. For multitype CSBPs, the local genealogy corresponds to the newly introduced multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent by Johnston, Kyprianou and Rogers
[
21
]
. The definition in
[
21
]
of multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent states that a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent is a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in the Pitman sense with the additional condition that simultaneous transitions of multiple blocks are not permitted. This excludes other exchangeable and consistent multitype coalescents, such as the seed bank coalescent with multiple switches
[
9
]
and the nested coalescent
[
8
]
.

In this work, we characterize the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents that can be constructed from multitype CSBPs using Gillespie’s transformation. We show that the Gillespie-multitype-
\Lambda
-coalescent aligns with the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent introduced in
[
21
]
. Thus, the Johnston, Kyprianou and Rogers notion of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent naturally extends the
\Lambda
-coalescent in the context of multitype CSBP genealogy. Furthermore, we introduce the multitype sequentially sampled processes as the moment duals of the block counting process of multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents. This partially extends recent results by Möhle
[
28
]
by introducing forward in time processes to the genealogies. Following Caballero et al.
[
12
]
, we construct an autonomous multitype frequency process via sequential sampling. We prove that this autonomous frequency process is in moment duality with the block-counting process of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent. This result establishes a homeomorphism between the parameter spaces of multitype CSBPs and multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents.

While the Gillespie-
\Lambda
-coalescent agrees with the Pitman-
\Lambda
-coalescent in one dimension, our results demonstrate that this is not the case in the multitype setting. Furthermore, we show that the Gillespie-multitype-
\Lambda
-coalescents correspond to the class characterized by Johnston et al. This leads to two natural open problems for future research, namely the characterisation of the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in the Pitman sense, and determining the superset of multitype CSBPs that is mapped to the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in the Pitman sense via Gillespie’s homeomorphism.

2.
Model and main results

In this section, we briefly review the main mathematical objects of our study, namely multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents and multitype continuous state branching processes (CSBPs).
Then we go on to study the frequency process of our CSBPs via a
sequential sampling procedure
(sometimes also called culling method in the literature).
We derive a duality with the block counting process of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent, that serves as a stepping stone in deriving our main result, the homeomorphism between the state of multitype CSBPs and multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents.
For now we fix some notation that will be used throughout the paper in order to deal with the multitype setting.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
we consider
|x|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}
.
Moreover, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
we put
[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
[d]_{0}=\{0\}\cup[d]
.
With this notation, if
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we set
[n]=[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]
and
[n]_{0}
in an analogous way.
Now, for
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we set

(2.1)

r^{n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}^{n_{i}}

Following this multi-index notation, we also consider that for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
k\in[n]_{0}
,

(2.2)

\binom{n}{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\binom{n_{i}}{k_{i}}.

2.1.
Multitype
\mathbf{\Lambda}
-coalescents

\Lambda
-coalescents or coalescents with multiple collisions have famously been introduced by Pitman
[
29
]
and Sagitov
[
31
]
. They may be characterised as being Markov processes taking values in the partitions of
\mathbb{N},
with transitions consisting of mergers of finitely many blocks, satisfying three conditions:

•

Exchangeability
, that is invariance under permutation of the blocks of a partition,

•

Consistency
, meaning that the restriction of the process to partitions of
\{1,...,n\}
is again Markov for any
n\in\mathbb{N},

•

No
simultaneous
occurrence of mergers (
asynchronity
).

The well-known result of Pitman states that such processes are uniquely characterised by a finite measure
\Lambda
on
[0,1]
, from which the rates of merging a
k
-tuple out of
b
distinct blocks to one new block are calculated as

(2.3)

\lambda_{b,k}=\int_{[0,1]}x^{k-2}(1-x)^{b-k}\Lambda(dx).

Equivalently, the previous expression can also be written as

(2.4)

\lambda_{b,k}=\Lambda(\{0\})1_{\{k=2\}}+\int_{(0,1]}x^{k-2}(1-x)^{b-k}\Lambda(dx),

where it becomes explicit that the mass of
\Lambda
at
0
only contributes to pairwise mergers.

Recently, Johnston, Kyprianou and Rogers
[
21
]
have generalised this to multitype coalescents.
They consider processes taking values in
d
-type partitions of
\mathbb{N}
, meaning that every block in the partition has one out of
d
different types (or colours).
Blocks may change type, and finitely many blocks (of same or different types) may merge to form a single new block (of one type).
We may equivalently think of a spatial structure, with types corresponding to islands and colonies, and type changes corresponding to migration.
In
[
21
]
it is proved that the rates of
d
-type coalescent processes which are exchangeable and consistent, and where no multiple
transitions
of blocks occur simultaneously are exactly the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents defined by a generalisation of the condition (
2.3
).
More precisely, according to
[
21
]
, a
multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent
, which we will denote simply by
\Pi
, is characterized by the pair

(2.5)

\bigl(\{\rho_{ij}:i,j\in[d]\},\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}:i\in[d]\}\bigr),

where
\rho_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for all
i,j\in[d]
and for each
i\in[d]
,
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is a measure on the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
, without mass at the point
\{0\}
, satisfying the integrability condition

(2.6)

\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)<\infty.

Given that there are
b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
blocks, the rate at which
k\in[b]_{0}
blocks are involved in a merger where the resulting block is of type
i
is

(2.7)

\lambda_{b,k}^{i}=\rho_{ii}1_{\{k=2e_{i}\}}+\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\rho_{ij}1_{\{k=e_{j}\}}+\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k}(1-u)^{b-k}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du).

For
i,j\in[d]
,
\rho_{ij}
is then the rate at which a block of type
j
becomes a block of type
i
when
i\neq j
, corresponding to migration, while
\rho_{ii}
is the rate at which two blocks of type
i
merge into a block of type
i
.

It has been noted that there is a slight discrepancy in this generalisation of
\Lambda
-coalescents to a multitype setting:
While in
[
21
]
asynchronity is required for
any
type of transition, that is, both for type changes and mergers, it is possible to define multitype coalescent processes that are exchangeable and consistent with asynchronous
mergers
of two or more blocks, but
simultaneous
type changes, that may equally well be considered multitype generalisations of
\Lambda
-coalescents.
A simple example is given by the so-called seedbank (
\Lambda
-)coalescent with simultaneous switches of
[
9
]
.
This shows that the question of the most natural generalisation of
\Lambda
-coalescents to the multitype setting should be treated with some care in this respect, since they don’t coincide.
The main results of our paper shows that actually the approach of
[
21
]
is natural, as the space of those multitype multiple merger coalescents are dual and homeomorphic to certain multitype CSBPs.

2.2.
Continuous state branching processes

Continuous state branching processes (CSBPs) are Markov processes with values in
[0,\infty]
that are the continuous space and time versions of Galton-Watson processes. A CSBP
X
is described in terms of its infinitesimal generator given, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, by

(2.8)

\mathcal{L}_{X}f(x)=xbf^{\prime}(x)+x\frac{c}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)+x\int_{(0,\infty)}(f(x+w)-f(x)-w1_{(0,1]}(w)f^{\prime}(x))\mu(dw),\qquad x\geq 0,

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma
and
c
are non-negative numbers, and
\mu
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying the integrability condition
\int_{(0,\infty)}(1\wedge w^{2})\mu(dw)<\infty
.

We can think of
b
as a drift or mass production term,
c
as a diffusion or variance term, and
\mu
is associated to large reproduction events.

This class of processes have the branching property, meaning that the sum of independent copies of the process started at
x
and
y
respectively has the same law as the process started at
x+y.
According to Lamperti
[
23
]

X
can also be obtained from a Lévy process with nonnegative jumps and is characterised via its Laplace-exponent.

In what follows we will consider
multitype continuous state branching processes
, which are strong Markov processes
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
with values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, that generalise the above setup.
We will think of
d
different types, or equivalently of a system of
d
colonies, with interacting CSBPs for each type or colony.

For a formal definition we follow Duffie et al.
[
15
]
(see also
[
1
,
34
]
). Let
U_{d}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
e_{i}
be the
i
-th canonical vector.
Moreover, consider

•

B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d\times d}
,
where
\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d\times d}
is the set of
d\times d

matrices such that
b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
i\neq j
. We interpret
b_{ij}
as the rate at which mass in colony
i
is produced according to the mass in colony
j
;

•

c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
a diffusion (or variance) term.

•

Let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d}):U_{d}\to[-1,1]^{d}
be the continuous truncation function given by

\xi_{i}(x)=(1\wedge|x_{i}|)\frac{x_{i}}{|x_{i}|}.

•

For
i\in[d]
,
\mu_{i}
is a Borel measure on
U_{d}
such that

(2.9)

\int_{U_{d}}\Bigl(\xi^{2}_{i}(w)+\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{j}(w)\Bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)<\infty,

governing reproduction resp. large reproduction events in colony
i
.

Under these assumptions, the operator
\mathcal{L}
acts on functions
f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
by

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f(x)={}

\displaystyle\langle Bx,\nabla f(x)\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}x_{i}\partial_{ii}f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(x+w)-f(x)-(1\wedge w_{i})\partial_{i}f(x)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw),

is the generator of a Markov process
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
that we call the
d
-type continuous state branching processes
with characterising tuple
(B,c,\mu)
.

2.3.
Multitype population model and the sequential sampling procedure

We now turn to the setup in which we investigate the connections between multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents and multitype CSBPs.
We consider two independent realisations
X
and
Y
of the
d
-type continuous state branching process defined in Section
2.2
, that we may think of as two populations.
We first study the frequency process of one population, and apply a sequential sampling procedure that leads to a Markov process, for which we obtain a duality.
Then we will reach the aforementioned homeomorphism between
d
-type CSBPs and
d
-type
\Lambda
-coalescents in the sense of
[
12
]
, which is our main result.

Throughout this section we will fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and will take
X
and
Y
as two independent realisations of the multi-type branching process described in Section
2.2
.
The generator of the process
(X,Y)
,
\mathcal{A}
, acts on functions

f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
, and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x,y)={}

\displaystyle\langle Bx,\nabla_{x}f(x,y)\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}f(x,y)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(x+w,y)-f(x,y)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{x_{i}}f(x,y)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle+\langle By,\nabla_{y}f(x,y)\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}y_{i}}f(x,y)

(2.11)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(x,y+w)-f(x,y)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{y_{i}}f(x,y)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw).

Consider the frequency process
R=(R_{i}(t))
and the total mass process
Z=(Z_{i}(t)),

i\in[d]
,
t\geq 0
defined by

(2.12)

R_{i}(t)=\frac{X_{i}(t)}{X_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{i}(t)=X_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t),\quad i\in[d],

for
t\in[0,\kappa)
, where

(2.13)

\kappa:=\min_{i\in[d]}\inf\bigl\{t\geq 0:Z_{i}(t)\in\{0,\infty\}\bigr\}.

It readily follows that
(R,Z)
is Markovian due to being in a one-to-one correspondence to
(X,Y)
and the fact that the latter process is Markovian due to
X
and
Y
being independent Markov processes.
Then some computations allow us to obtain its infinitesimal generator through a martingale problem and Itô’s formula, see Proposition 1 in
[
12
]
for the unidimensional case.
For completeness, we state the corresponding result below in our multidimensional setting.
The frequency process
(R(t))
in general isn’t Markovian. However, as in
[
12
]
we can apply a
sequential sampling procedure
that goes back to ideas of Gillespie (where it was called “culling”,
[
19
]
). The idea is to keep the total population size constant on a dense set of times, and let the frequency process run independently, but with a fixed constant population size.

We now give an introduction to the sequential sampling method.
Fix
r\in[0,1]^{d}
and
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
.
Consider
\varepsilon,L>0
such that
0<\varepsilon<\min_{i\in[d]}z_{i}\leq\max_{i\in[d]}z_{i}<L
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we will construct a pure jump Markov process
\overline{R}^{n}
with values in
[0,1]
, using the following scheme:

(1)

Set
\mathcal{R}^{(n)}(0)=r
.

(2)

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
let
(R^{(m)},Z^{(m)})
be a realisation of
(R,Z)
started at
(\mathcal{R}^{(n)}(m-1),z)
.

(3)

Put

\mathcal{R}^{(n)}(m)=R^{(m)}\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\wedge\tau^{(m)}\biggr)\,,

where

\tau^{(m)}:=\min_{i\in[d]}\inf\{t\geq 0:Z_{i}(t)\notin(\varepsilon,L)\}\,.

(4)

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, set
\overline{R}^{n}(t):=\mathcal{R}^{(n)}(N(nt))
, where
N
is a Poisson process of rate
1
.

By construction, the discrete approximation of the sequentially sampled process
\overline{R}^{n}
is a pure jump Markov process with exponential jump times of rate
n
, and whose transition kernel
\rho_{z,n}:[0,1]^{d}\times\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d})\to[0,1]
is given by

(2.14)

\rho_{z,n}(r^{\prime},A):=\mathbb{P}_{r^{\prime},z}\Bigl(R(n^{-1}\wedge\tau)\in A,Z(n^{-1}\wedge\tau)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\Bigr)\,,

where
\tau=\min_{i\in[d]}\inf\{t\geq 0:Z_{i}(t)\notin(\varepsilon,L)\}
, and
P_{r^{\prime},z}
is the law of the process
(R,Z)
started at
(r,z)
.
Moreover,
\overline{R}^{n}
has infinitesimal generator whose action on functions
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]^{d})
is given by

(2.15)

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(z)}f(r^{\prime})=n\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\bigl(f(u)-f(r^{\prime})\bigr)\rho_{z,n}(r^{\prime},du),\quad r^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d}\,.

Note that in this construction, we are sampling whole realisations of the process
\{(R(t\wedge\tau),Z(t\wedge\tau)):t\geq 0\}
, which have in common the starting value of the second entry, to construct the skeleton chain
\mathcal{R}^{(n)}
.
By construction,
\overline{R}^{n}
evolves as the first coordinate of the process
(R,Z)
but, as
n
tends to infinity, the fluctuations of
Z
around
z
become increasingly smaller, since the times between jumps converge to zero as
n
increases to infinity.
This last remark implies that as
n
tends to infinity, the election of
\varepsilon
and
L
becomes irrelevant, in the sense that the limit of the jump processes will not depend on these constants.
See also
[
12
]
, Section 4.2.

As in
[
12
]
, for
z\in(0,\infty)^{n}
fixed, one can prove convergence of this sequence of processes to a process
R^{(z,r)}
that we call the
sequentially sampled process
.
We sketch the general idea, the technical details will be given in Section
4
.
As we are interested in the frequency process
R
with
Z
fixed, we write the generator
\mathcal{A}
formally acting on functions that depend only on
r\in[0,1]^{d}
, although we stress the dependence on the total mass in each of the colonies, given by
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, by writing
\mathcal{A}^{(z)}
.

By looking closely at (
2.11
), the action of this simplified generator on a test function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]^{d})

is formally given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(r)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}b_{ij}\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}(r_{j}-r_{i})\partial_{i}f(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}}r_{i}(1-r_{i})\partial_{ii}f(r)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}r_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(f(r+(1-r)\odot u)-f(r)-(1-r_{i})u_{i}\partial_{i}f(r)\bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)

(2.16)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(f(r-r\odot u)-f(r)+r_{i}u_{i}\partial_{i}f(r)\bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du),

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product of componentwise multiplication.
The strategy of the proof will consist in proving the existence and uniqueness of a process solving a system of SDEs given in (
2.18
) below that corresponds to this simplified generator, and then prove that the approximating process converges to this solution by showing convergence of the generators.
For this we will use the following notation.
Given
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and and a measure
\mu
over
U_{d}
, let
\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu
be the pushforward measure of
\mu
under the mapping
T_{z}:U_{d}\to[0,1]^{d}
given by

(2.17)

T_{z}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})=\biggl(\frac{w_{1}}{w_{1}+z_{1}},\ldots,\frac{w_{d}}{w_{d}+z_{d}}\biggr).

Consider the system of SDEs
1

1

Formally
all of the terms in (

) should be multiplied by

2.4.
Main results: Duality and homeomorphism

From the SDE (
2.18
) and Theorem
2.1
it is straightforward to check that the generator of the sequentially sampled frequency process
R^{(z,r)}
is given by (
2.16
), and by standard methods one can derive a dual process.
See Section
4
for details.

Theorem 2.2
.

Fix
z=(z_{1},...,z_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}
.
For any
r\in[0,1]^{d}
, every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and any
t>0
we have

(2.19)

\mathbb{E}_{r}\Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{d}(R_{i}^{(z,r)}(t))^{n_{i}}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}^{N_{i}(t)}\Bigr],

where
N
is a Markov process on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and transition rates (for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
)

(2.20)

q_{nm}=\begin{cases}2\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}}\binom{n_{i}}{2}&if $m=n-2e_{i}+e_{i}$,\\
n_{j}b_{ji}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}&if $m=n-e_{j}+e_{i}$,\\
z_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\binom{n}{k}u^{k}(1-u)^{n-k}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)&if $m=n-k+e_{i}$ with $k\in[n]_{0}\setminus\{0,e_{i}\}$,\\
0&otherwise.\end{cases}

Observe that for fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d},
the process
N=(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
is exactly the block counting process of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in the sense of
[
21
]
.
If there are currently
n_{i}
blocks of type
i
, there are pairwise mergers of blocks of type
i
at rate
2c_{i}z_{i}^{-1},
type switch from
j
to
i
of single blocks at rate
b_{ji}z_{i}z_{j}^{-1},
multiple mergers of blocks at rates governed by
\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}
. Thus the duality provides both an a posteriori justification of the sequentially sampled process as an object related to the original population model, and a nice connection between multitype CSBPs and multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents generalising previous results,
[
7
,
12
,
22
]
. This connection is strenghtened by our next result, which shows that the parameter sets of multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents and multitype CSBPs are in a one to one correspondence, which is even a homeomorphism for topologies on these spaces that are natural in the sense of convergence of the corresponding processes.

In order to state the homeomorphism, we need to define suitable topologies on the parameter spaces of the coalescents and the CSBPs.
This is rather straightforward in the case of the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents.
Write
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
for the space of vectors
(\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{d})
of measures on
[0,1]^{d}
with no mass on
\{0\}
that satisfy

(2.21)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)<\infty\,.

The characterizing pair (
2.5
) of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent takes values in
\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d\times d}\times\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
The topology on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d\times d}
we will be the usual one, given by the Frobenius norm defined by
\lVert A\rVert_{F}:=\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lvert a_{ij}\rvert^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}
.
In the case of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
, equip it with the topology given by the weak convergence topology of the measures
u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)
for each
i,j\in[d]
.
This is, in the specified topology,
(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{d}^{(n)})\to(\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{d})
as
n\to\infty
if and only if
u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(n)}(du)\to u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)
weakly, as
n\to\infty
, for any
i,j\in[d]
.
Finally, consider the product topology on
\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
We will show below that convergence in this space implies weak convergence of the corresponding multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents in the Skorokhod space of càdlàg functions.

For the parameter space of multitype CSBPs we need to be a little more careful.
Recall from section
2.2
that we can characterize the CSBPs by the tuple
(B,c,\mu)
, where
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})
is a vector of measures over
U_{d}
.
We follow the assumptions on
B
,
c
and
\mu
established in section
2.2
.
Writing
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
for the set of tuples of measures
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})
over
U_{d}
such that for any
i\in[d]
,
\mu_{i}
satisfies (
2.9
). We note that
(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}:=\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
In
\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
we consider the usual topologies defined by the Frobenius and
L_{2}
norm respectively.
On the other hand, we equip
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
with the topology of weak convergence of the measures
(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)
for each
i,j\in[d]
.
Similar to the topology used for
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
, this means that
\mu^{(n)}\to\mu
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
, as
n\to\infty
, if and only if
(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)
weakly as
n\to\infty
.
We equip
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
with the product topology.
In this case we will prove that convergence in this space implies the convergence of the corresponding CSBPs in the Skorokhod space of càdlàg functions.

Note that by applying the sequential sampling procedure at the total mass level
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
to the process
(R,Z)
, we get that the infinitesimal generator of the resulting frequency process
\overline{R}
is given by (
2.16
) and its moment dual
N
is described in Theorem
2.2
.
The diagonal of the matrix
B
plays no role in the rates of the dual process
N
, so for the homeomorphism we will consider a subspace of
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
For
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
define

(2.22)

\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}:=\bigl\{(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}:b_{ii}=a_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[d]\bigr\}\,,

and equip it with the subspace topology.
Additionally, note that because
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and the definition of
T_{z}
, as in (
2.17
),
\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}([0,1]^{d}\setminus[0,1)^{d})=0
for all
i\in[d]
.
Thus, we will also consider the subspace

(2.23)

\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}:=\Bigl\{(\rho,\mathcal{Q})\in\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}:\mathcal{Q}_{i}\bigl([0,1]^{d}\setminus[0,1)^{d}\bigr)=0\text{ for all }i\in[d]\Bigr\}

of
\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
equipped with the subspace topology.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
fixed.
The spaces
\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
are homeomorphic. More precisely, given
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
, we can define a explicit homeomorphism
H_{z}:\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}\to\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
that maps a multitype CSBP with triplet
(B,c,\mu)
to the mutitype
\Lambda
-coalescent with pair
(\rho,\mathcal{Q})
given as

(2.24)

\rho_{ii}=2\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}},\quad\rho_{ij}=b_{ji}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Q}_{i}=z_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in[d]\text{ and }j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}.

Its inverse
H_{z}^{-1}:\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}\to\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
maps a pair
(\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
to
(B^{\prime},c^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
with

(2.25)

c_{i}^{\prime}=z_{i}\frac{\rho_{ii}^{\prime}}{2},\quad b_{ij}^{\prime}=\rho_{ji}^{\prime}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\mathbf{T}_{z}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}\quad\text{for }i\in[d]\text{ and }j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}.

This result is a multidimensional extension of Theorem 5 in
[
12
]
, which establishes that the space of
\Lambda
-coalescents with no atom at
\{1\}
is homeomorphic to the space of CSBPs. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. We will first discuss in some more details the homeomorphism, providing some insight into the choice of topologies and prove Theorem
2.3
. The proof of the convergence result for the sequential sampling scheme (Theorem
2.1
) and the duality (Theorem
2.2
) will be provided after that in Section
4
, since those proofs are more straightforward, even though quite technical.

3.
Proof and discussion of the homeomorphism

We now present the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
The proof is structured as follows: First, we establish a lemma stating that the function
H_{z}
, as defined in Theorem
2.3
, is a homeomorphism between
\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Next, we discuss the spaces of multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents and multitype CSBPs, showing that the topologies we defined on the parameter spaces are natural, in the sense that convergence in the parameter space imply convergence of the corresponding processes.
Finally, we combine these results to complete the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

3.1.
Homeomorphism between parameter spaces of CSBPs and multitype
\mathbf{\Lambda}
-coalescents

From Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
we know that the block counting process of a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent is the moment dual of the frequency process associated to a multitype CSBP.
The homeomorphism that we give in Theorem
2.3
essentially sends a CSBP to the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent in moment duality to the frequency process obtained through the sequential sampling procedure, which can be interpreted as describing the genealogy of the multitype CSBP.

Specifically, recall that the transition rates of the moment dual to the sequentially sampled frequency process of a multitype CSBP
X
with characteristic triplet
(B,c,\mu)
are given by (
2.20
).
Fix
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
.
Note that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, each
i\in[d]
and each
k\in[n]_{0}\setminus\{0,e_{i}\}
we can rewrite
q_{nm}
, with
m=n-k+e_{i}
, in (
2.20
) as

(3.1)

q_{nm}=\binom{n}{k}\biggl(\frac{2}{z_{i}}c_{i}1_{\{k=2e_{i}\}}+\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}b_{ji}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}1_{\{k=e_{j}\}}+\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k}(1-u)^{n-k}z_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)\biggr).

Notice that the expression within parentheses is a particular case of (
2.7
), where

(3.2)

\rho_{ii}=2\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}},\ \rho_{ij}=b_{ji}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}\text{ and }\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)=z_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)

for each
i\in[d]
and
j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}
.

By the previous remark, for a given
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
, we map a CSBP
X
with characteristic triplet
(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
to a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent where the pair
(\{\rho_{ij}:i,j\in[d]\},\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}:i\in[d]\})\in\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
is given by (
3.2
).
This is the underlying idea behind the homeomorphism
H_{z}
established in Theorem
2.3
.
We now state and prove that
H_{z}
is a homeomorphism between the parameter spaces
\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed and
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
given.
The function
H_{z}:\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}\to\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
as defined in (
3.2
) is a homeomorphism between
\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
whose inverse
H_{z}^{-1}
is given explicitly in (
3.5
).

Proof.

The proof is done in three steps.
We first prove that
H_{z}
is a well defined function.
After that, we show that
H_{z}
is a bijection.
Finally, we prove that
H_{z}
and
H_{z}^{-1}
are continuous, i.e. that
H_{z}
is a homeomorphism.

Consider a triplet
(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
, and let

(\rho,\mathcal{Q})
be defined by (
3.2
).
Then, it is clear, because
B\in\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d}
, that
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d\times d}
.
On the other hand, for each
i,j\in[d]
, by definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)

\displaystyle=z_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(\frac{w_{j}}{z_{j}+w_{j}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{z_{i}}{(1\wedge z_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}}\int_{U_{d}}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)\,.

By condition (
2.9
) it follows that
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Moreover, it is straightforward that
\mathcal{Q}_{i}([0,1]^{d}\setminus[0,1)^{d})=0
for all
i\in[d]
.
As a consequence
(\rho,\mathcal{Q})\in\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
, so
H_{z}
is well defined.

Let us prove that
H_{z}
is one-to-one.
Assume that
H_{z}(B^{(1)},c^{(1)},\mu^{(1)})=H_{z}(B^{(2)},c^{(2)},\mu^{(2)})
.
By (
3.2
) it is immediate that
B^{(1)}=B^{(2)}
and
c^{(1)}=c^{(2)}
.
Now fix a measurable function
f:U_{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
By definition of
\mathbf{T}_{z}
, for every
i\in[d]
we get

\displaystyle\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}^{(1)}(dw)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}f\biggl(\frac{z_{1}u_{1}}{1-u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{z_{d}u_{d}}{1-u_{d}}\biggr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}^{(1)}(du)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}f\biggl(\frac{z_{1}u_{1}}{1-u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{z_{d}u_{d}}{1-u_{d}}\biggr)\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(1)}(du)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}f\biggl(\frac{z_{1}u_{1}}{1-u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{z_{d}u_{d}}{1-u_{d}}\biggr)\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(2)}(du)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}f\biggl(\frac{z_{1}u_{1}}{1-u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{z_{d}u_{d}}{1-u_{d}}\biggr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}^{(2)}(du)=\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}^{(2)}(dw)\,.

Subsequently
\mu_{i}^{(1)}=\mu_{i}^{(2)}
for every
i\in[d]
.
This proves that
H_{z}
is injective.

To show that
H_{z}
is surjective consider
(\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})\in\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Let us define
(B^{\prime},c^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
by setting, for
i\in[d]
and
j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}
,

(3.5)

c_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{z_{i}}{2}\rho_{ii},\ b_{ii}^{\prime}=a_{i},\ b_{ij}^{\prime}=\frac{z_{i}}{z_{j}}\rho_{ji}\text{ and }\mu_{i}(dw)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\mathbf{T}_{z}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(dw)\,,

where
\mathbf{T}_{z}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is the pushforward measure of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
under
T_{z}^{-1}
.
We need to prove that
(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
By (
3.5
), it is obvious that
B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{(+)}^{d\times d}
with
b_{ii}=a_{i}
for
i\in[d]
, and that
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
.
Thus, it suffices to prove that
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
, for which we will prove that the integrability condition

(3.6)

\int_{U_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime}(dw)<\infty\quad\text{holds for each $i\in[d]$.}

This in turn follows from the inequality

1\wedge(Lv/(1-v))\leq(1+L)v
valid for any
L>0
and

v\in[0,1)
.
Certainly, by applying this inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\biggl(1\wedge\biggl(\frac{z_{j}u_{j}}{1-u_{j}}\biggr)\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1+z_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)<\infty\,.

Then (
3.6
) is fulfilled because of the
equality

(3.7)

\int_{U_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime}(dw)=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\biggl(1\wedge\biggl(\frac{z_{j}u_{j}}{1-u_{j}}\biggr)\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du).

In light of this
H_{z}
is onto and therefore a bijection, with inverse

H_{z}^{-1}
.

Let us prove that
H_{z}
is continuous.
Suppose that
(B^{(n)},c^{(n)},\mu^{(n)})\to(B,c,\mu)
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathbf{\Psi}_{a}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
From (
3.2
), it is obvious that
\rho^{(n)}\to\rho
as
n\to\infty
, so if suffices to prove that
\mathcal{Q}^{(n)}\to\mathcal{Q}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Consider
f\in C_{b}([0,1]^{d})
and
i,j\in[d]
given.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(u)u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(n)}(du)

\displaystyle=z_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(\frac{w_{j}}{z_{j}+w_{j}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}f\circ T_{z}(w)\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)

\displaystyle=z_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(\frac{1}{z_{j}+w_{j}}1_{\{w_{j}<1\}}+\frac{w_{j}}{z_{j}+w_{j}}1_{\{w_{j}\geq 1\}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}f\circ T_{z}(w)(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\,.

Noting that

w\mapsto\biggl(\frac{1}{z_{j}+w_{j}}1_{\{w_{j}<1\}}+\frac{w_{j}}{z_{j}+w_{j}}1_{\{w_{j}\geq 1\}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}f\circ T_{z}(w)

defines a bounded continuous function from
U_{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
, the assumption
\mu^{(n)}\to\mu
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
implies

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(u)u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{(n)}(du)\to\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(u)u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)\,.

As
i,j\in[d]
were arbitrary, it follows that
\mathcal{Q}^{(n)}\to\mathcal{Q}
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Thus,
H_{z}
is continuous.

To prove that
H_{z}^{-1}
is continuous suppose that
(\rho^{\prime(n)},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime(n)})\to(\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
By (
3.5
), it is immediate that
B^{\prime(n)}\to B^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime(n)}\to c^{\prime}
as
n\to\infty
.
We need to show that
\mu^{\prime(n)}\to\mu^{\prime}
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
Now consider
f\in C_{b}(U_{d})
and
i,j\in[d]
fixed.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{U_{d}}f(w)(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime(n)}(dw)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\biggl(\frac{z_{j}}{1-u_{j}}1_{\{u_{j}<1/(1+z_{j})\}}+\frac{1}{u_{j}}1_{\{u_{j}\geq 1/(1+z_{j})\}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}f\circ T_{z}^{-1}(u)u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime(n)}(du)\,.

To obtain the convergence

(3.8)

\int_{U_{d}}f(w)(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime(n)}(dw)\to\int_{U_{d}}f(w)(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime}(dw)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty

note that the mapping

u\mapsto\biggl(\frac{z_{j}}{1-u_{j}}1_{\{u_{j}<1/(1+z_{j})\}}+\frac{1}{u_{j}}1_{\{u_{j}\geq 1/(1+z_{j})\}}\biggr)^{1+\delta_{ij}}f\circ T_{z}^{-1}(u)

from
[0,1]^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
is almost everywhere, with respect to
\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}
, continuous and bounded. This last assertion follows because
\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}([0,1]^{d}\setminus[0,1)^{d})=0
.
Thus, the convergence in (
3.8
) is attained by applying the assumption

u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime(n)}\to u_{j}^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}
as
n\to\infty
weakly.
This proves that
(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime(n)}\to(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{\prime}
as
n\to\infty
weakly, entailing the continuity of
H_{z}^{-1}
.
∎

3.2.
The topological space of multitype
\mathbf{\Lambda}
-coalescents

We now show that the topology on the parameter space of the multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents is reasonable since it corresponds to a natural topology on the space of these processes. So far we have only worked with the block counting process of a multitype

\Lambda
-coalescent.
We now turn to study the process itself, starting by introducing its state space, the
d
-type partitions over
\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
\bm{\pi}=\{(\pi_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}

is a
d
-type partition of
\mathbb{N}
if

\pi=\{\pi_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a partition of
\mathbb{N}

and for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{t}_{i}\in[d]_{0}
is the type
of block
\pi_{i}
, where
\mathfrak{t}_{i}=0
if and only if
\pi_{i}=\varnothing
.
We assume that the elements of
\pi
are arranged by their minimal elements;
i.e.
\min\pi_{i}\leq\min\pi_{j}
for
i<j
, with the convention

\min\varnothing=\infty
, where there is strict inequality if

\pi_{i}\neq\varnothing
.
Let
\mathbf{P}_{d}
be the collection of all
d
-type partitions of

\mathbb{N}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

\bm{\pi}\in\mathbf{P}_{d}
we put

\bm{\pi}|_{[m]}:=\{(\pi_{i}|_{[m]},\mathfrak{t}_{i}1_{\{\pi_{i}|_{[m]}\neq\varnothing\}}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}

for the restriction of
\bm{\pi}
to
[m]
,
where
\pi_{i}|_{[m]}:=\pi_{i}\cap[m]
.

Multitype
\Lambda
-coalescents can be defined as partition valued processes, which we will denote by
\Pi
, in this framework.
By Theorem 1.5 in
[
21
]
, the restriction
\Pi|_{[M]}
of
\Pi
to
[M]
is a Markov chain with a finite state space.
This chain can jump from the state
\bm{\pi}
with
b_{j}
blocks of type
j
, for
j\in[d]
, to the state
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
if and only if there exist
k_{j}\in[b_{j}]_{0}
for each
j\in[d]
such that
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
can be constructed by merging
k_{1}
blocks of type 1,
k_{2}
of type 2, etc. and assigning the resulting block the type
i\in[d]
.
In this case, for
k\in[b]_{0}\setminus\{0,e_{i}\}
, the jump rate from
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
is given by

(3.9)

\tilde{\lambda}_{b,k}^{i}=\rho_{ii}1_{\{k=2e_{i}\}}+\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\rho_{ij}1_{\{k=e_{j}\}}+\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k}(1-u)^{n-k}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)\,,

with
(\rho,\mathcal{Q})\in\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
given.

Note that given a
d
-type partition
\bm{\pi}\in\mathbf{P}_{d}
we can
define a function
\vartheta_{\bm{\pi}}:\mathbb{N}\to[d]

by

\vartheta_{\bm{\pi}}(i):=\mathfrak{t}_{\mathfrak{j}(i)}
,
where
\mathfrak{j}(i)\in\mathbb{N}
is the index such that

i\in\pi_{\mathfrak{j}(i)}
.
We now define a distance
d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}}
on
\mathbf{P}_{d}
by

d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\pi}^{\prime})=\Bigl(\max\bigl\{M\in\mathbb{N}:\pi|_{[M]}=\pi^{\prime}|_{[M]}\text{ and }\vartheta_{\bm{\pi}}(i)=\vartheta_{\bm{\pi}^{\prime}}(i)\text{ for all }i\in[M]\bigr\}\Bigr)^{-1},\quad\bm{\pi},\bm{\pi}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{P}_{d},

where
\pi|_{[M]}
denotes the restriction of
\pi
to
[M]
.
This distance takes into account both the partition structure and the types of
the blocks of
d
-type partitions.
We observe that the topology induced by this metric coincides with the topology induced by the identification of
\mathbf{P}_{d}
with a subset
\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[1]}\times\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[2]}\times\cdots
, via the mapping
\bm{\pi}\mapsto(\bm{\pi}|_{[1]},\bm{\pi}|_{[2]},\ldots)
, equipped with the inherited topology of
\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[1]}\times\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[2]}\times\cdots
with the product of discrete topologies.

Now consider
A\subset\mathbf{P}_{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
, and denote

A^{\varepsilon}
to be the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
, defined by

A^{\varepsilon}:=\bigcup_{\bm{\pi}\in A}\bigl\{\bm{\pi}\in\mathbf{P}_{d}:d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\pi}^{\prime})<\varepsilon\bigr\}\,.

It is readily seen that
A^{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{P}_{d}
for
\varepsilon>1
, and

A^{\varepsilon}=A^{1/\lfloor 1/\varepsilon\rfloor}
for

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Thus, if we define

\mathcal{D}_{M}^{i}:=\bigl\{\bm{\pi}\in\mathbf{P}_{d}:\mathbb{N}\setminus[M]\in\pi\text{ and }\vartheta_{\bm{\pi}}(M+1)=i\bigr\},

for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in[d]
, it follows that

\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{D}_{M}^{i}
is a
countable dense set in
\mathbf{P}_{d}
.
Consequently
(\mathbf{P}_{d},d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}})
is separable.
This enables us to employ the Prohorov metric to study the convergence of stochastic
processes whose trajectories are in
(\mathbf{P}_{d},d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\{\Pi^{(n)}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence of multitype

\Lambda
-coalescents such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Pi^{(n)}
is characterized by the pair
(\rho^{(n)},\mathcal{Q}^{(n)})\in\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
Furthermore let
\Pi
be a multitype
\Lambda
-coalescent characterized by

(\rho,\mathcal{Q})\in\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
.
If
(\rho^{(n)},\mathcal{Q}^{(n)})\to(\rho,\mathcal{Q})
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{coal}}
, then

(3.10)

\Pi^{(n)}\Rightarrow\Pi\text{ as $n\to\infty$}

weakly in
D([0,T],(\mathbf{P}_{d},d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}}))
for all
T>0
.

Proof.

Let
\Pi|_{[M]}
(
\Pi^{(n)}|_{[M]}
resp.) be the restriction of
\Pi
(
\Pi^{(n)}
resp.) to
[M]
.
By the definition of the topology imposed on
\mathbf{P}_{d}
, it suffices to prove that for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.11)

\Pi^{(n)}|_{[M]}\Rightarrow\mathbf{\Pi}|_{[M]}\text{ as $n\to\infty$}

weakly in
D([0,T],(\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[M]},d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}}))
for all
T>0
.

Putting
\ell:=\min\{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}:k_{j}>0\}
we note that the integral in the rates given in (
3.9
)

(3.12)

\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k}(1-u)^{n-k}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)=\begin{cases}\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k-2e_{i}}(1-u)^{n-k}u_{i}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)&if $k_{i}\geq 2$,\\
\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u^{k-e_{\ell}}(1-u)^{n-k}u_{\ell}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(du)&if $k_{i}<2$.\end{cases}

Using this last expression, it is clear that the assumption of convergence
(\rho^{(n)},\mathcal{Q}^{(n)})\to(\rho,\mathcal{Q})
as
n\to\infty
implies the convergence of the transition rates of the processes
\{\Pi^{(n)}|_{[M]}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
to those of the process
\Pi|_{[M]}
, as
n\to\infty
, for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.

(3.13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\lambda}_{b,k}^{i,(n)}=\tilde{\lambda}_{b,k}^{i}.

By the fact that
\Pi^{(n)}|_{[M]}
and
\Pi|_{[M]}
are continuous-time Markov chains with finite state space, the convergence of the transitions imply the weak convergence

(3.14)

\Pi^{(n)}|_{[M]}\Rightarrow\Pi|_{[M]},\quad\text{ as $n\to\infty$,}

in the space of càdlàg paths from
[0,T]
to

(\mathbf{P}_{d}|_{[M]},d_{\mathbf{P}_{d}})
endowed with the Skorokhod topology
for any
T>0
.
∎

3.3.
The topological space of multitype CSBPs

Recall that in section
2.4
we defined a topology on the parameter space
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
of CSBPs.
We now provide a result that justifies its definition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{X^{(n)}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence of
d
-type CSBPs such that for each

n
the characteristic triplet of
X^{(n)}
is
(B^{(n)},c^{(n)},\mu^{(n)})\in\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
In addition, consider a
d
-type CSBP
X
with characteristic triplet

(B,c,\mu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
.
If

(3.15)

(B^{(n)},c^{(n)},\mu^{(n)})\to(B,c,\mu)\text{ as $n\to\infty$ in $\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}$,}

then
X^{(n)}\Rightarrow X
as
n\to\infty
, weakly on the space of càdlàg
paths from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
[0,\infty]^{d}
with respect to the Skorokhod
topology if the branching mechanism of
X
is nonexplosive, and with
respect to the uniform Skorokhod topology if the branching mechanism is
explosive.

In the proof of the previous result we will consider
d
auxiliary Lévy processes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from which the multitype CSBP can be obtained through a multiparameter Lamperti time-change.
From
[
15
]
, a multitype CSBP
X
with characteristic triplet
(A,\gamma,\nu)\in\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
can be characterized by the Laplace transform of its semigroup, which satisfies that for each
x,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
,

\log\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-\langle X(t),\lambda\rangle}]=-\langle u(t,\lambda),x\rangle,

where
u(t,\lambda)=(u_{1}(t,\lambda),\ldots,u_{d}(t,\lambda))
satisfy a system of Riccati equations.
Said Riccati equations are given, for
i\in[d]
, by

\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,\lambda)=-\varphi_{i}(u(t,\lambda)),\qquad\text{$\lambda\geq 0$},

with

\varphi_{i}(\lambda)=\gamma_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}-\langle Ae_{i},\lambda\rangle+\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(e^{-\langle\lambda,w\rangle}-1+\lambda_{i}(1\wedge w_{i})\bigr)\nu_{i}(dw),\qquad\text{$\lambda\geq 0$}.

For each
i\in[d]
we will consider a Lévy process
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with characteristic exponent

(3.16)

\varphi_{i}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)=-\gamma_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\langle Ae_{i},\lambda\rangle+\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\lambda,z\rangle}-1-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{i}(1\wedge z_{i})\bigr)\nu_{i}(dz),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

or, equivalently, characteristic triplet
(Ae_{i},\Gamma_{i},\nu_{i})
, where
\Gamma_{i}=(\gamma_{i}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ik})_{j,k\in[d]}
, which is different from the characteristic triplet of the CSBP
X
.
Then, according to Theorem 1 in
[
11
]
, the
d
-type CSBP
X
with characeristic triplet
(A,\gamma,\nu)
can be seen as a multiparameter time-change of the
d
Lévy processes
\mathcal{Y}_{i},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{i}
.

An important property that we will use in the proof of Proposition
3.2
is that the weak convergence of the auxiliary Lévy processes implies the weak convergence of the CSBPs.
Thus, before proving Proposition
3.2
we will prove an auxiliary lemma, which essentially states that the convergence of the parameters in
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\mathrm{branch}}
implies the convergence of the associated Lévy processes through the multiparameter Lamperti time-change.
The latter convergence, of Lévy processes, will be proved through the pointwise convergence of their associated characeristic exponent, as in Theorem 8.7 in
[
32
]
.

To prepare the way for the auxiliary lemma, note that if we define
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by setting its
(i,j)
-th entry, with
i,j\in[d]
, as

\tilde{a}_{ij}=a_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\int_{U_{d}}\Bigl((1\wedge w_{i})-w_{i}\bigl(1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq 1\}}+(2-\lVert w\rVert)1_{\{1<\lVert w\rVert\leq 2\}}\bigr)\Bigr)\nu_{i}(dw)\,,

then we can rewrite (
3.16
) as

(3.17)

\varphi_{i}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)=-\gamma_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\langle\tilde{A}e_{i},\lambda\rangle+\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(e^{\mathrm{i}\langle\lambda,z\rangle}-1-\mathrm{i}z_{i}\lambda_{i}\upsilon(z)\bigr)\nu_{i}(dz),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\upsilon(z):=1_{\{\lVert z\rVert\leq 1\}}+(2-\lVert z\rVert)1_{\{1<\lVert z\rVert\leq 2\}}
is a continuous function with compact support commonly used in the representation of the characteristic exponent (see section 8 in
[
32
]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
\{(B^{(n)},c^{(n)},\mu^{(n)}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
(B,c,\mu)
are as in Proposition
3.2
.
For each
i\in[d]
let
\varphi_{i}^{(n)}
and
\varphi_{i}
, constructed as in (
3.16
), be the characteristic exponents of the Lévy processes
Y_{i}^{(n)}
and
Y_{i}
, respectively.
Under condition (
3.15
) we get

(3.18)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{i}^{(n)}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)=\varphi_{i}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)

for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
i\in[d]
.

Proof.

By (
3.16
), for each
i\in[d]
,
Y_{i}^{(n)}
and
Y_{i}
are Lévy processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with characteristic triplets
(B^{(n)}e_{i},G_{i}^{(n)},\mu_{i}^{(n)})
and
(Be_{i},G_{i},\mu_{i})
respectively, where
G_{i}^{(n)}=(c_{i}^{(n)}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ij})_{j,k=1}^{d}
and
G_{i}=(c_{i}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ij})_{j,k=1}^{d}
.
Considering the alternative representation (
3.17
) of
\varphi_{i}^{(n)}
and
\varphi_{i}
, with
\tilde{B}^{(n)}
and
\tilde{B}
instead of
B^{(n)}
and
B
respectively, it will suffice to prove that the conditions of Theorem 8.7 in
[
32
]
hold for each
i\in[d]
under condition (
3.15
) to deduce (
3.18
).
Namely, we will prove that for each
i\in[d]
:

(1)

\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}(dw)
as
n\to\infty
for every
f\in C_{b}(U_{d})
such that
f=0
in a neighborhood of
0
;

(2)

for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}{\limsup}_{n\to\infty}\Bigl\lvert z_{i}^{2}c_{i}^{(n)}-z_{i}^{2}c_{i}+\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\Bigr\rvert=0;

(3)

\tilde{b}_{ij}^{(n)}\to\tilde{b}_{ij}
as
n\to\infty
.

We will first prove (1).
For each
j\in[d]
define the open subset
V_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}:x_{j}>1/\sqrt{2d}\}
of
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\lVert x\rVert=1\}
, and note that
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}\subset\bigcup_{j\in[d]}V_{j}
.
By Theorem 2.13 in
[
30
]
, there exists a collection of functions
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{d}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d})
such that
\sum_{j\in[d]}\chi_{j}\equiv 1
, and for each
j\in[d]
both
0\leq\chi_{j}\leq 1
and
\operatorname{supp}\chi_{j}\subset V_{j}
hold.
For a function
f\in C_{b}(U_{d})
such that
f=0
in a neighborhood of
0
,

\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{U_{d}}\chi_{j}\biggl(\frac{w}{\lVert w\rVert}\biggr)\frac{f(w)}{(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\,.

Note that

w\mapsto\chi_{j}\biggl(\frac{w}{\lVert w\rVert}\biggr)\frac{f(w)}{(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}}

defines a function in
C_{b}(U_{d})
.
Then, for each
j\in[d]
we deduce, by the hypothesis
(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)
as
n\to\infty
weakly, that

\int_{U_{d}}\chi_{j}\biggl(\frac{w}{\lVert w\rVert}\biggr)\frac{f(w)}{(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to\int_{U_{d}}\chi_{j}\biggl(\frac{w}{\lVert w\rVert}\biggr)\frac{f(w)}{(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}}(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)\text{ as }n\to\infty\,,

which entails

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)=\int_{U_{d}}f(w)\mu_{i}(dw)\,.

Thus, (1) is proved.

Having proved (1), we can now show that (3) holds.
Indeed, it suffices to note that

w\mapsto(1\wedge w_{i})-w_{i}\bigl(1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq 1\}}+(2-\lVert w\rVert)1_{\{1<\lVert w\rVert\leq 2\}}\bigr)

defines a function in
C_{b}(U_{d})
that is equal to
0
in a neighborhood of
0
.
Therefore, (3) follows by (1) and the hypothesis
b_{ij}^{(n)}\to b_{ij}
as
n\to\infty
for each
j\in[d]
.

Let us finish the proof by showing that (2) holds.
In this direction, note that by elementary properties of the limit superior,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Bigl\lvert z_{i}^{2}c_{i}^{(n)}-z_{i}^{2}c_{i}+\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}z_{i}^{2}\lvert c_{i}^{(n)}-c_{i}\rvert+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\,.

As
c_{i}^{(n)}\to c_{i}
,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}z_{i}^{2}\lvert c_{i}^{(n)}-c_{i}\rvert=0
, so it will suffice to show that

(3.19)

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}{\limsup}_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)=0\,.

To prove this, write
\mathcal{D}_{i}:=\{\varepsilon>0:\mu_{i}(\{\lVert w\rVert=\varepsilon\})>0\}
for the set of radii
\varepsilon>0
for which the sphere of radius
\varepsilon
has positive measure under
\mu_{i}
.
As
\varepsilon\mapsto\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)
is an increasing function from
(0,\infty)
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we can consider, without loss of generality,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{D}_{i}
.
By considering
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)={}

\displaystyle z_{i}^{2}\int_{U_{d}}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}(1\wedge w_{i})^{2}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}z_{j}\int_{U_{d}}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{d}(1+\delta_{ik})z_{k}w_{k}\Bigr)1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}(1\wedge w_{j})\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\,.

Now, if
\varepsilon\in(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{D}_{i}
, the assumption
(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to(1\wedge w_{j})^{1+\delta_{ij}}\mu_{i}(dw)
as
n\to\infty
weakly entails that, as
n\to\infty
,

\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)\to\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}(dw)\,,

so for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{D}_{i}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert\varepsilon\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}^{(n)}(dw)=\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}(dw)\,.

Finally, note that by Dominated Convergence we deduce

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{U_{d}}\langle z,w\rangle^{2}1_{\{\lVert w\rVert\leq\varepsilon\}}\mu_{i}(dw)=0\,,

which allows us to obtain (
3.19
), finishing the proof.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every

, let

be a Lévy process on

, where

.
Then, Lemma

,
along condition (

), imply that

Y_{i}^{(n)}\Rightarrow Y_{i}\text{ as $n\to\infty$ for each $i\in[d]$, }

where

,

It then transpires, by the continuity of multiparameter time changes, see

, that

weakly on the space of càdlàg paths from

∎

To finish the section we present the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

By Lemma
3.1
we know that
H_{z}
defines a homeomorphism between

\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}^{\mathrm{coal}}

Propositions

4.
Frequency process and convergence the sequential sampling procedure

In this section we will provide the proof of Theorem
2.1
. We will first show pathwise uniqueness of the solution to (
2.18
) followed by existence, and then show that the sequence of processes obtained from the culling procedure converges to this limit and has the desired properties. We will start by providing an auxiliary result that describes the dynamics of the process
(R,Z)
defined in (

)
as the solution to a martingale problem.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any fixed
f\in C_{c}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
, the process

M(t\wedge\kappa):=f(R(t\wedge\kappa),Z(t\wedge\kappa))-f(r,z)-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\kappa}\mathcal{A}f(R(s),Z(s))ds,

is a local martingale, where
\kappa
is defined in (
2.13
), and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(r,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}z_{i}\biggl(\frac{(1-r_{i})r_{i}}{z_{i}^{2}}\partial_{r_{i}r_{i}^{2}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{ij}\biggl(\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}(r_{j}-r_{i})\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+z_{j}\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}r_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(f\bigl(r+(1-r)\odot u,z+T_{z}^{-1}(u)\bigr)-f(r,z)-(1-r_{i})u_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(T_{z}^{-1}(u))\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\Bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(f\bigl(r-r\odot u,z+T_{z}^{-1}(u)\bigr)-f(r,z)+r_{i}u_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(T_{z}^{-1}(u))\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\Bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du),

recalling that
\odot
represents the Hadamard product.

Proof.

Let us recall that given
x,y\in U_{d}
,
r\in[0,1]^{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}

are defined through

r_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+y_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad z_{i}=x_{i}+y_{i}\quad\text{for $i\in[d]$.}

Moreover,
x=z\odot r
and
y=z-x
.
By this correspondence and the chain rule we get, for any function
f\in C_{c}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
and for each
i\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z),

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}f(r,z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}^{2}}(\partial_{r_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z)-\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z))+\frac{(1-r_{i})^{2}}{z_{i}^{2}}\partial_{r_{i}r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z),

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{i}}f(r,z)={}

\displaystyle-\frac{r_{i}}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z),

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{i}y_{i}}f(r,z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{2r_{i}}{z_{i}^{2}}(\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\partial_{r_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z))+\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{z_{i}^{2}}\partial_{r_{i}r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z).

Hence, we can compute
\mathcal{A}f(r,z)
by considering

f(r,z)=f\circ g(x,y)
, where
g(x,y)=(r,z)
,
and the action of
\mathcal{A}
over
f\circ g
, given by (
2.11
).
Therefore, by definition of
(R,Z)
and Dynkin’s formula, see Proposition IV.1.7 in
[
17
]
, we deduce that

M(t):=f(R(t),Z(t))-f(r,z)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}f(R(s),Z(s))ds,

defines a local semimartingale.
To obtain Proposition
4.1
it remains to compute
\mathcal{A}f(r,z)
.

Note that

\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{y_{i}}f(r,z)=\frac{(1-2r_{i})}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+2\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z),

Hence, by using the identities
x_{i}=z_{i}r_{i}
and
y_{i}=z_{i}(1-r_{i})
for each
i\in[d]
, we obtain

(4.1)

\begin{split}\langle Bx,\nabla_{x}f(r,z)\rangle+\langle By,\nabla_{y}f(r,z)\rangle&=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\biggl[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{ij}z_{j}r_{j}\biggl(\frac{(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{ij}z_{j}(1-r_{j})\biggl(\frac{r_{i}}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\biggr]\end{aligned}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{ij}\biggl(\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}(r_{j}-r_{i})\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)+z_{j}\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\end{split}

On the other hand, we note that

r_{i}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}f(r,z)+(1-r_{i})\partial_{y_{i}y_{i}}f(r,z)=\frac{r_{i}(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}^{2}}\partial_{r_{i}r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z),

and therefore

(4.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\bigl(x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}f(r,z)+y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}y_{i}}f(r,z)\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}z_{i}\biggl(\frac{r_{i}(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}^{2}}\partial_{r_{i}r_{i}}f(r,z)+\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr).

We now turn to the part concerning

.
In this case, for

, we note that

maps

to

Straightforward computations yield

and

\frac{x_{i}+w_{i}}{x_{i}+y_{i}+w_{i}}=r_{i}\frac{z_{i}}{z_{i}+w_{i}}+\frac{w_{i}}{z_{i}+w_{i}}=r_{i}+(1-r_{i})\frac{w_{i}}{z_{i}+w_{i}}=r_{i}+(1-r_{i})T_{z}(w)_{i},

for

.
Thus,

\displaystyle x_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(\tilde{r}_{w},\tilde{z}_{w})-f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle=z_{i}r_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(f(r+(1-r)\odot T_{z}(w))-f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\frac{(1-r_{i})}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\mu_{i}(dw).

Similarly, if we denote by

(x,y+w)
under

(x,y)\mapsto(r,z)

\displaystyle y_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(\hat{r}_{w},\hat{z}_{w})-f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle=z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(f(r-r\odot T_{z}(w))-f(r,z)+\xi_{i}(w)\frac{r_{i}}{z_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\mu_{i}(dw).

Before applying a change of variable that will give us the pushforward measure
\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}
in the previous expressions, note that condition (

\int_{U_{d}}\left|\frac{1}{z_{i}}\xi_{i}(w)-T_{z}(w)_{i}\right|\mu_{i}(dw)\leq\frac{1}{z_{i}^{2}}\int_{U_{d}}w_{i}^{2}1_{\{w_{i}\leq 1\}}\mu_{i}(dw)+\frac{3}{1\wedge z_{i}}\mu_{i}(\{w_{i}>1\})<\infty.

Hence, by adding and substracting

we get the first equality in

\displaystyle x_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(\tilde{r}_{w},\tilde{z}_{w})-f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)+y_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\bigl(f(\hat{r}_{w},\hat{z}_{w})-f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{x_{i}}f(r,z)\bigr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle=z_{i}r_{i}\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(f(r+(1-r)\odot T_{z}(w))-f(r,z)-(1-r_{i})T_{z}(w)_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle+z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{U_{d}}\biggl(f(r-r\odot T_{z}(w))-f(r,z)+r_{i}T_{z}(w)_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(w)\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\biggr)\mu_{i}(dw)

\displaystyle=z_{i}r_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(f\bigl(r-r\odot u,z+T_{z}^{-1}(u)\bigr)-f(r,z)-(1-r_{i})u_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(T_{z}^{-1}(u))\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\Bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)

\displaystyle+z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(f\bigl(r-r\odot u,z+T_{z}^{-1}(u)\bigr)-f(r,z)+r_{i}u_{i}\partial_{r_{i}}f(r,z)-\xi_{i}(T_{z}^{-1}(u))\partial_{z_{i}}f(r,z)\Bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du),

Proposition 4.2
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Pathwise uniqueness holds for the solution of (

Proof.

For
x,u\in[0,1]^{d}

we define

\displaystyle m_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\biggl(b_{ik}\frac{z_{k}}{z_{i}}+z_{k}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{k}(du)\biggr)(x_{k}-x_{i}),

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\biggl(z_{k}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{k}(du)\biggr)(x_{k}-x_{i}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2c_{i}}{z_{i}}x_{i}(1-x_{i})},

\displaystyle g_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)

\displaystyle|g_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)-g_{i}^{j}(y,u,v)|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl(2z_{j}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)\biggr)\bigl(|x_{i}-y_{i}|+|x_{j}-y_{j}|\bigr).

If

\zeta(0)=0
, and the result follows from
Gronwall’s lemma and the right continuity of
t\mapsto\zeta(t)
.
∎

4.2.
Existence of a strong solution

Having proved that the pathwise uniqueness holds for (
2.18
)
we now turn to the question of the existence of a strong solution, which

a fortiori
will be unique.
In fact, we only need to ensure that a weak solution exists due to the Yamada–Watanabe Theorem, see for instance

[
33
, p. 104]
.
This can (and will) be proved by showing that for any function

f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
,

(4.12)

f(R(t))-f(R(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(R(s))ds,\quad t\geq 0,

is a martingale, which is the final step in proving the existence part of Theorem
2.1
.

Before turning to the proof, note that (
2.16
) may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(r)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\biggl(b_{ij}\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}+z_{j}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)\biggr)(r_{j}-r_{i})\partial_{i}f(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}}r_{i}(1-r_{i})\partial_{ii}f(r)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}r_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(f(r+(1-r)\odot u)-f(r)-\langle(1-r)\odot u,\nabla f(r)\rangle\bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)

(4.13)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(f(r-r\odot u)-f(r)+\langle r\odot u,\nabla f(r)\rangle\bigr)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du).

Certainly, as
\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)<\infty

for
i,j\in[d]
with
i\neq j
, to see the equivalence between
(
2.16
) and (
4.13
) it suffices to note that the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}z_{j}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)(r_{j}-r_{i})\partial_{i}f(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}z_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{j}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)(r_{i}-r_{j})\partial_{j}f(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}r_{i}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}(1-r_{j})u_{j}\partial_{j}f(r)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}(1-r_{i})\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}r_{j}u_{j}\partial_{j}f(r)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du).

Let us now finish the existence part of Theorem
2.1
.

Existence of a strong solution.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
R(0)
is deterministic.
We start by stating that for any
i\in[d]
and
x\in[0,1]^{d}

we have the simple bound

\displaystyle|m_{i}(x)|+\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(g_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)^{2}+h_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)^{2})dv\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)

(4.14)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}\biggl(b_{ik}\frac{z_{k}}{z_{i}}+z_{k}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{k}(du)\biggr)+\frac{c_{i}}{2z_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}2z_{j}\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}u_{i}^{2}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du).

By a straightforward generalization of Proposition 4.1 in
[
26
]
,
this bound gives us that a càdlàg process is a weak solution (hence a strong solution) to
(
2.18
) if and only if (
4.12
) is a
locally bounded martingale.

We now consider the sequences
\{W_{n}\}
and
\{V_{n}\}
of non-decreasing sets defined
by

W_{n}=\{|u|>1/n\}\subset[0,1]^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{n}=W_{n}\times[0,\max z_{i}+n]\subset[0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Then
(\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}\times\mathrm{Leb})(V_{n})<\infty
, where
\mathrm{Leb}
is the Lebesgue measure,
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in[d]
.
Moreover, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i,j\in[d]
, the mappings

x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto\iint_{V_{n}}g_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)dv\quad\text{and}\quad x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto\iint_{V_{n}}h_{i}^{j}(x,u,v)\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)dv

are seen to be Lipschitz, cf. (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and (
4.6
)—the latter holds for
j=i
when replacing
[0,1]^{d}
for
W_{n}
.
Hence, by results on continuous-type SDEs,
see Theorem 2.2 in Chapter 4 of
[
20
, p. 169]
,
there is a weak solution,
R^{(n)}(t)=(R_{1}^{(n)}(t),\dots,R_{d}^{(n)}(t))
, to

(4.15)

\displaystyle R_{i}^{(n)}(t)={}

\displaystyle R(0)+\int_{0}^{t}m_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))dB_{i}(s)

(4.16)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{V_{n}}(g_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s),u,v)+h_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s),u,v))\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)dvds,

with values in
[0,1]^{d}
.
Pathwise uniqueness holds for the SDE (
4.15
)
due to Proposition
4.3
, so it follows that
there is a strong unique solution for (
4.15
) by the
Yamada–Watanabe Theorem.

Then, by an easy modification of Proposition 2.2 in
[
18
]
,
there exists a unique strong solution
\{R^{(n)}(t):t\geq 0\}
to

\displaystyle R_{i}^{(n)}(t)={}

\displaystyle R(0)+\int_{0}^{t}m_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))dB_{i}(s)

(4.17)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{V_{n}}g_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s-),u,v)\widetilde{N}_{1}(ds,du,dv)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{V_{n}}h_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s-),u,v)\widetilde{N}_{2}(ds,du,dv).

Indeed, due to the manner in which we chose
V_{n}
and
W_{n}
, the Poisson point processes that appear in
the SDE (
4.17
) have finite intensity.
Consequently, the number of jumps in any compact interval of time is finite,
allowing us to order the jumps of the Poisson processes by
0<S_{1}<S_{2}<\cdots
.
Doing this, we can now construct the solution in a pathwise manner.
First consider a solution
\mathcal{R}^{(0)}=(\mathcal{R}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{R}^{(0)}_{d})
to (
4.15
), and define

R(t)=\mathcal{R}^{(0)}(t)
for
t\in[0,S_{1})
.
Define
r^{(1)}
as the random vector whose
i
-th entry is given by

\displaystyle r_{i}^{(1)}={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}^{(0)}(S_{1}-)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\{S_{1}\}}\iint_{V_{n}}g_{i}^{j}(\mathcal{R}^{(0)}(s-),u,v)\widetilde{N}_{1}(ds,du,dv)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\{S_{1}\}}\iint_{V_{n}}h_{i}^{j}(\mathcal{R}^{(0)}(s-),u,v)\widetilde{N}_{2}(ds,du,dv).

With the same Brownian motion used to construct
\mathcal{R}^{(0)}
now
consider a solution
\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
to the SDE

\displaystyle R_{i}^{(n)}(t)={}

\displaystyle r^{(1)}+\int_{0}^{t}m_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{i}(R^{(n)}(s))dB^{(i)}(S_{1}+s)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{V_{n}}(g_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s),u,v)+h_{i}^{j}(R^{(n)}(s),u,v))\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{j}(du)dvds

and define
R(t)=\mathcal{R}^{(1)}(t-S_{1})
for
t\in[S_{1},S_{2})
.
By proceeding recursively we obtain a strong solution to (
4.17
).

Thus, by Itô’s formula, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, the process

f(R^{(n)}(t))-f(R^{(0)})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{(n,z)}f(R^{(n)}(s))ds,\quad t\geq 0,

is a locally bounded martingale,
where
\mathcal{A}^{(n,z)}
is given by replacing the integrals over

[0,1]^{d}
with integrals over
W_{n}
in (
4.13
).
As
\{R^{(n)}(t):t\geq 0\}
takes values in
[0,1]^{d}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
for every,
t\geq 0
,
\{R^{(n)}(t):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a tight
sequence of random vectors.
In addition, by (
4.14
) we can use an analogous argument to that
given in the proof of Lemma 4.3 in
[
18
]

to show that Aldous’ criterion holds, and hence

\{R^{(n)}(t):t\geq 0\}
is actually tight in
D(\mathbb{R}_{+},[0,1]^{d})
.

The existence of a strong solution follows from

\mathcal{A}^{(n,z)}f(x_{n})\to\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(x)
if
x_{n}\to x

as
n\to\infty
,
which is a consequence of a straightforward extension of the proof of
Lemma 4.2 in
[
26
]
.
∎

To continue we will show that (
2.16
) is indeed the
infinitesimal generator of the solution to (
2.18
), and
that this solution can be obtained as a weak limit of pure jump Markov processes
obtained through sequential sampling.

4.3.
Sequential sampling

We are left with proving that (
2.16
) is
actually the infinitesimal generator of the solution to
(
2.18
), as well as the Feller property.
Throughout this subsection we assume that
z\in(0,\infty)^{d}
is given and fixed.
Note that from (
4.11
), putting
\zeta=R^{(z,r)}-R^{(z,s)}
, Gronwall’s lemma gives

(4.18)

\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert R^{(z,r)}(t)-R^{(z,s)}(t)\rVert\big]\leq K(t)\lVert r-s\rVert.

Proposition 4.4
.

The strong solution
R^{(z,r)}
of (
2.18
) is Feller and its
infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}^{(z)}
is given by
(
2.16
).

Proof.

For
t\geq 0
and
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]^{d})
, we define the operator
T_{t}
by

T_{t}f(r):=\mathbb{E}[f(R^{(z,r)}(t))]
.
Then
\{T_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is the semigroup associated to
R
.
By (
4.18
) and a Taylor expansion of
order 1, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1]^{d})
,
r,s\in[0,1]^{d}
,

\left|T_{t}f(r)-T_{t}f(s)\right|\leq\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\lVert R^{(z,r)}(t)-R^{(z,s)}(t)\rVert]\leq K(t)\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\lVert r-s\rVert,

which gives us the continuity of
r\mapsto T_{t}f(r)
for

f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1]^{d})
.
By a density argument and dominated convergence, this implies the continuity of

r\mapsto T_{t}g(r)
for
g\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]^{d})
;
this is
T_{t}(\mathcal{C}([0,1]^{d}))\subset\mathcal{C}([0,1]^{d})
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, Itô’s formula gives us the equality

f(R^{(z,r)}(t))=f(r)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(R^{(z,r)}(s))ds+M_{t}^{f},

with
M^{f}
a local martingale.
In fact,
M^{f}
is a locally bounded martingale,
which follows easily from the bound

(4.19)

\sup_{r\in[0,1]^{d}}|\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(r)|\leq K,

for some
K>0
, from where we get

|M_{t}^{f}|\leq Kt+2\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
The bound given in (
4.19
) is a consequence of

f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]^{2})
and the bounds that entail (
4.14
).
Then, as

\mathbb{E}[f(R^{(z,r)}(t))]-f(r)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(R^{(z,r)}(s))ds\biggr],

we deduce both that

\sup_{r\in[0,1]^{d}}|T_{t}f(r)-f(r)|\leq Kt\to 0\quad\text{as }t\to 0,

and that, for any
r\in[0,1]^{d}
,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{T_{t}f(r)-f(r)}{t}=\mathcal{A}^{(z)}f(r).

The former conclusion shows that
R^{(z,r)}
is Feller,
while the latter lets us conclude that (
2.16
)
is the infinitesimal generator of
R^{(z,r)}
due to
Theorem 1.33 in
[
10
, p. 24]
.
∎

By a simple modification of the proof of Theorem 1 in

[
12
]
for
d=1
one obtains that for any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
T>0
,

\overline{R}^{n}\Rightarrow R^{(r)}
as
n\to\infty
in
D([0,T],[0,1]^{d})
,
where
R^{(r)}
is the solution to (
2.18
) with initial
condition
R(0)=r.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

To conclude the section we will prove the moment duality of the sequentially sampled limit and the block counting process.

We will first work at the level of infinitesimal generators, applying (
2.16
) to polynomials of the form
r^{n}
for
r\in[0,1]^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, where
r^{n}
is understood as in (
2.1
).
In this direction we will use the identities

\displaystyle(r_{j}-r_{i})n_{i}r^{n-e_{i}}

\displaystyle=n_{i}(r^{n-e_{i}+e_{j}}-r^{n}),

\displaystyle r_{i}(1-r_{i})n_{i}(n_{i}-1)r^{n-2e_{i}}

\displaystyle=2\binom{n_{i}}{2}(r^{n-e_{i}}-r^{n}),

along the equalities

\displaystyle r_{i}\Bigl[\bigl(r+(1-r)\odot u\bigr)^{n}-r^{n}-(1-r_{i})u_{i}n_{i}r^{n-e_{i}}\Bigr]+(1-r_{i})\Bigl[\bigl(r-r\odot u\bigr)^{n}-r^{n}+r_{i}u_{i}n_{i}r^{n-e_{i}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{0,e_{i}\}}\binom{n}{k}u^{k}(1-u)^{n-k}(r^{n-k+e_{i}}-r^{n}).

where in the las sum we are using the multiindex notation as in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).
Then, by considering the function

H:[0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined
as
H(r,n)=r^{n}
,
the previous expressions entail

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(z)}H(r,n)

\displaystyle={}\sum_{i=1}^{d}2\frac{c_{i}}{z_{i}}\binom{n_{i}}{2}(r^{n-e_{i}}-r^{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}n_{i}b_{ij}\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}(r^{n-e_{i}+e_{j}}-r^{n})

(4.20)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k\in[n]_{0}\setminus\{0,e_{i}\}}z_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\binom{n_{j}}{k_{j}}\lambda_{n,k}^{i}(r^{n-k+e_{i}}-r^{n}),

where for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
k\in[n]_{0}
and
i\in[d]

we define

\displaystyle\lambda_{n,k}^{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{k_{j}}(1-u_{j})^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\mathbf{T}_{z}\mu_{i}(du),

whenever the quantities are finite.
Expression (
4.20
) and a straightforward modification of the proof of Theorem 4 in
[
12
]
complete the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
