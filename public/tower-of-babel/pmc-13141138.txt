Randomised controlled trial comparing antibiotic cement bead pouch versus negative pressure wound therapy for the management of severe open tibia fracture wounds: Beads versus VAC (BvV) protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarly open fracture management aims to minimise the risk of complications. For the most severe open fracture wounds, multiple irrigation and debridement surgeries are required to overcome severe wound contamination, to reassess the evolving tissue injury or to temporise and plan further surgery. When multiple irrigation and debridement surgeries are needed, uncertainty remains about how the open fracture wound should be managed to best minimise complications. The primary aim of this trial is to compare the antibiotic cement bead pouch vs negative pressure wound therapy in the management of patients with severe open tibia fracture wounds.Methods and analysisBvV is a multicentre, pragmatic, parallel arm randomised controlled trial that aims to enrol 312 adult patients admitted to a participating centre with a severe open tibia fracture requiring multiple irrigation and debridement surgeries. Participants will be randomly allocated on a 1:1 basis to either antibiotic cement bead pouch or negative pressure wound therapy. The primary outcome will be a composite outcome to evaluate clinical status 6 months after randomisation. Using the win ratio approach, we will hierarchically assess the composite outcome in the following order: (i) all-cause mortality, (ii) injury-related amputation of the lower extremity, (iii) unplanned reoperation to manage wound complications, an infection or promote fracture healing and (iv) clinical fracture healing assessed using the Functional IndeX for Trauma (FIX-IT) instrument.Ethics and disseminationThe BvV trial has been approved by a central institutional review board (IRB) (Advarra) for clinical sites in the USA, the ethics board at the coordinating centre at McMaster University (Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board), and participating sites not using the central institutional IRB (Fraser Health Research Ethics Board, The University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board, Newfoundland and Labrador Health Research Ethics Board, University of Manitoba Biomedical Research Ethics Board). Additional clinical sites who are in the start-up phase, as well as any new selected clinical sites, will obtain local approvals prior to initiating trial activities. This will include a clinical site in the UK who is in the process of obtaining the necessary approvals. Recruitment began in November 2023. Both interventions are frequently used to manage severe open fracture wounds, ensuring that the trial results can be easily transitioned into clinical practice. Introduction

Burden of open fractures

Open fractures represent some of the most severe orthopaedic injuries. Severe open fractures are commonly caused by blast, ballistic and high-velocity trauma, such as motor vehicle collisions.1,4 These injuries can lead to infection in up to 50% of grossly contaminated fractures and have lasting consequences.1 5 If an open fracture becomes infected, its treatment requires prolonged antibiotic administration, multiple surgeries and, in the worst-case scenarios, amputation of the injured limb or death. As a result, fracture-related infections lead to fracture healing difficulties, poor function and low health-related quality of life.6

Treatment principles for early management of open fractures

The overarching objective of early open fracture management is to minimise the risk of fracture-related infections, as these are associated with significant morbidity.7 Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics are routinely administered shortly after the injury, before each fracture surgery and post-operatively during a patient’s hospitalisation. Intraoperatively, the initial surgical management of an open fracture focuses on thoroughly irrigating and debriding the injured extremity. This entails cleaning the open fracture wound by removing all foreign debris and surgical excision of non-viable skin, muscle and bone that may harbour micro-organisms and ultimately lead to infection. For the most severe open fracture wounds, more than one irrigation and debridement surgery may be required to overcome severe wound contamination, to reassess the evolving tissue injury or to temporise and plan appropriate soft tissue coverage with a skin graft or muscle flap. When multiple irrigation and debridement surgeries are needed, uncertainty exists about how the open fracture wound should be managed between procedures.

Current approach: negative pressure wound therapy and wound vac

Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), commonly known as the wound vac, is currently the preferred method of temporary open fracture wound management for most surgeons.8 The wound vac is a sealed dressing with an occlusive plastic wound dressing connected to a vacuum pump, tubing and a canister to collect fluid. With NPWT, there are no local antibiotics or biomaterials carrying antibiotics that are used.9

Alternative treatment: antibiotic bead pouch

Alternatively, some surgeons use antibiotic bead pouches to manage the open fracture wound. An antibiotic bead pouch is created during the surgical procedure using polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement and a mixture of heat-stable antibiotic powder. Vancomycin and tobramycin are two common antibiotics mixed with cement to form a bead pouch.10,13 These antibiotics are typically used in combination to ensure adequate prophylaxis against gram-positive and gram-negative organisms. Once the antibiotics are mixed with the cement, they are typically placed on a suture string and implanted into the open fracture wound under an occlusive dressing. The beads subsequently release antibiotics with the highest elution in the first 4 days after placement, followed by continued elution at therapeutic concentrations for weeks to months.14 Prior work has demonstrated the efficacy of the antibiotic bead pouch in open tibia fractures.10 15

Controversy in open fracture wound management

Published in 2018, the Wound Management of Open Lower Limb Fractures (WOLLF) trial, a randomised controlled trial of 460 open fracture patients evaluated NPWT versus non-vac wound management strategies after the first surgical debridement of the wound.16 The authors found no statistically significant between-group differences in patients’ Disability Rating Index scores at 12 months or in the occurrence of deep surgical site infections. The authors concluded that the findings of their trial do not support the use of NPWT for open fractures; however, the control group was a mix of sealed dressings, some of which included antibiotics and some did not. Building on this research, we conducted a propensity score analysis using data from the international multicentre Fluid Lavage of Open Wounds (FLOW) trial.1 Unexpectedly, our recently published data showed that the odds of developing a deep infection requiring operative management were over four times higher in patients who received NPWT than those who received a non-vac wound dressing, perhaps indicating the difficulty in determining the effects of these interventions in non-randomised data.17 Similarly, a recent Cochrane review concluded that there is moderate certainty evidence for no apparent difference between NPWT and non-vac care on the proportion of wounds healed at 6 weeks in open fractures and that there is moderate certainty evidence that NPWT is not a cost-effective treatment for open fracture wound management.18 Despite the current literature, NPWT remains the most common wound management strategy for severe open fractures.8

Objectives

The primary aim of BvV is to compare the antibiotic bead pouch to NPWT for temporary wound management in severe open tibia fractures. We hypothesise that the antibiotic bead pouch will reduce the risk of open fracture complications (a composite outcome of mortality, injury-related amputations of the lower extremity, unplanned reoperations and clinical fracture healing) compared with NPWT. The secondary objectives are to determine if the antibiotic bead pouch, compared with NPWT, (i) reduces mortality, (ii) reduces the frequency of injury-related amputation of the lower extremity, (iii) reduces the frequency of unplanned reoperations and (iv) improves clinical fracture healing.

Methods and analysis

Trial design and setting

BvV is a multicentre, pragmatic, parallel arm randomised trial recruiting patients with a severe open tibia fracture requiring multiple irrigation and debridement surgeries. For the purpose of this trial, ‘severe’ fractures are those open tibia injuries requiring more than one irrigation and debridement surgery, as these are the injuries necessitating wound temporisation. This trial will enrol patients in trauma centres throughout the USA and Canada, with the possibility of expanding enrolment to clinical sites in the UK. The Department of Surgery at McMaster University will serve as the Methods Centre and the Department of Orthopaedics at the University of Maryland School of Medicine will serve as the administrative centre. The trial initiated participant enrolment in November 2023, and it is anticipated that enrolment will take 3–4 years.

Patient and public involvement

The trial’s design and plans for implementation received feedback from patient partners, including best practices for obtaining informed consent and encouraging participation in the trial. Additionally, the trial’s patient partners played an integral role in determining outcomes of interest.

Patient screening and recruitment

The initial screening will occur in the emergency room before going to the operating room for the first irrigation and debridement of the open fracture and wound. Once a patient is deemed eligible, written informed consent will be obtained from the patient or their legally authorised representative. The process of obtaining and documenting informed consent forms will be completed in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and local policies and regulations (online supplemental material). Participants may withdraw their consent at any time during the trial.19 20 The final eligibility determination will occur in the operating room when the surgeon determines if the patient will require subsequent irrigation and debridement procedures to manage the open injury. If a potentially ineligible participant is enrolled, the Adjudication Committee will review the participant’s medical record and determine eligibility. This decision will be based on the information present at the time of randomisation.

Randomisation

In the operating room, the surgeon will conduct the final screening for eligibility to determine if the participant will require subsequent irrigation and debridement procedures to treat the soft tissue damage. If a participant is deemed ineligible in the operating room, they will not be randomised or included in the trial.

After confirmation of eligibility in the operating room, participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive either an antibiotic bead pouch or NPWT. Each participant will be randomised using a centralised randomisation system built into the electronic data capture (EDC) system accessed via an internet-enabled device and available 24 hours per day, 7 days per week. The treatment allocation will be stratified by clinical site and open fracture severity (Gustilo-Anderson type I, II or IIIA vs type IIIB or IIIC).21

Interventions

Polymethyl methacrylate antibiotic bead pouch

Participants randomised to the bead pouch treatment group will have medical grade PMMA bone cement mixed with antibiotic powder, formed into beads, and placed in their open fracture wound. The bead recipe will consist of 2 g of vancomycin, 2.4 g of tobramycin and 40 g of PMMA cement. The safety data and clinical experience supporting the rationale for this recipe have been previously reported.22,43 The beads will be handmade and approximately 10 mm in diameter, in the shape of a sphere, following standard practice. The number of beads placed in the wound will be at the discretion of the treating surgeon based on the recipient’s wound size. After the surgical procedure, the wound will be covered with an occlusive dressing. If able, the wound may be temporarily closed over the beads instead of using an occlusive dressing at the treating surgeon’s discretion. As commonly practised, the antibiotic beads may be exchanged for new beads at each subsequent irrigation and debridement surgery. The brand and supplier of the cement, tobramycin and vancomycin will be left to the discretion of the clinical site; however, the Methods Centre will confirm that the chosen supplier is acceptable.

Negative pressure wound therapy

The NPWT will be applied per the manufacturer’s instructions following the initial irrigation and debridement. Participants allocated to this treatment group may have a portion of their surgical wound closed at each debridement; however, the NPWT device must be applied to the remaining open fracture wound bed. The brand and supplier of the NPWT components will be left to the discretion of the clinical site; however, the Methods Centre will confirm that the chosen supplier is acceptable.

The recommended NPWT device setting is 125 mm Hg of continuous negative pressure; however, the treating surgeon may adjust these settings as clinically necessary.

The frequency and timing of NPWT changes will also be at the surgeon’s discretion but are expected to primarily coincide with the timing of repeat debridement, typically every 24–72 hours until definitive management has occurred. The NPWT settings, date of application, adjustments, re-applications and removals will be recorded.

Investigational drug status

While both treatments are commonly used to manage open fracture wounds, the antibiotic bead pouch elution is an off-label delivery route for vancomycin and tobramycin. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the Investigational New Drug (IND) use of the antibiotic bead pouch in this protocol (IND 164944). Additionally, an application and subsequent no objection letter for the use of the bead pouch in this trial has been provided by Health Canada, allowing for the completion of the investigation at Canadian centres.

Each clinical site will be responsible for obtaining and preparing the investigational medical product (ie, antibiotic cement bead pouch) described above for use in their operating rooms. All product storage, labelling and trial-related record-keeping will comply with applicable regulatory requirements.

Intervention adherence

Adherence to the treatment arms will be collected and reported. Adherence will be defined as the participant receiving the allocated intervention for temporary wound management at the end of surgical debridement of the open tibia fracture, with planned definitive wound management at a subsequent date. If a participant receives multiple surgical debridements, adherence will be met if the participant receives the allocated intervention at least once and does not receive the comparator intervention before definitive wound management. To be classified as adherent when assigned to the antibiotic bead pouch arm, participants must also receive the prescribed dose of vancomycin and tobramycin powder in the antibiotic beads. To be classified as adherent in the NPWT arm, the participant must receive at least one wound vac application. To maximise pragmatism as it relates to the interventions, adherence will not depend on the sustained function of the intervention. If a wound vac dressing fails to maintain suction, is removed due to malfunction, or the bead pouch leaks or is removed before the subsequent planned surgery, the treatment will still be considered adherent.

Perioperative adjuvant interventions

To reflect current practices and maintain a pragmatic approach, we will document key details of adjuvant interventions known to influence open fracture outcomes. The use of prophylactic systemic antibiotics and their duration will be recorded. Additionally, we will document intrawound adjuvant antibiotics used after the completion of the interventions. The trial protocol does not permit additional intrawound antibiotic use during the intervention period, which ends at the final wound debridement.

Outcomes

Trial outcomes

Our primary outcome will be a composite outcome to evaluate clinical status 6 months after randomisation. Using the win ratio approach, we will hierarchically assess the composite outcome in the following order: (i) all-cause mortality, (ii) injury-related amputation of the lower extremity, (iii) unplanned reoperation to manage wound complications, an infection or promote fracture healing and (iv) clinical fracture healing assessed using the FIX-IT instrument (figures1 2).

All reported deaths, amputations and unplanned reoperations will be reviewed by an adjudication committee. Members of the committee will review relevant clinical documentation and determine if each outcome meets the criteria of a study event. The committee will reach a consensus on all reviewed outcomes. The FIX-IT assessment provides a simple, standardised approach for measuring weight-bearing and pain assessment on a score ranging from zero to 12 points in two domains: the ability to bear weight (maximum of six points) and pain at the fracture site (maximum of six points).

The secondary outcomes will be each component of the primary composite outcome, also assessed 6 months after randomisation. Serious adverse events will be reported by treatment group.

Data collection and participant follow-up

After obtaining informed consent, research personnel will record the participant’s baseline data from the participant or proxy, the participant’s medical chart and the participant’s treating physicians. Detailed information will be collected regarding the procedures performed for surgical management of the open fracture. Information regarding the severity of the injury will be recorded (Gustilo-Anderson and Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen and the Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) Open Fracture Classification). For participants who receive NPWT, the date of application, adjustments, re-applications and removals will be documented. Details regarding antibiotic bead use will be recorded, including the date of bead placement, the number of beads placed and the date of bead removal. Research personnel will also document the same details for replacement beads. Research personnel will collect surgical data, including the timing of irrigation and debridements, the method and timing of temporary and definitive fixation, the details regarding the final wound closure, the use of local antibiotics at the fracture site, and whether PMMA cement spacers (with or without antibiotics) are used for planned staged bone grafting of large bone defects.

Follow-up visits will occur at regularly scheduled clinic visits 6 weeks, 3 months and 6 months post-randomisation. Clinical site personnel will complete all trial activities and assessments at each follow-up visit as detailed in table 1. Several strategies will be encouraged to maximise follow-up, including: (1) before hospital discharge, each participant will be asked to provide their telephone number and the name and phone numbers of three alternate contacts at different addresses with whom the participant does not live and who are likely to be aware of the participant’s whereabouts, (2) participants may receive information on fractures, their complications and the importance of adherence with follow-up, (3) participants may receive a reminder card on discharge for their next follow-up visit by local site personnel, (4) follow-up will coincide with the routine fracture clinic visit and (5) if a participant is unable to return for the follow-up assessment, clinical site personnel will determine their status with regard to trial outcomes by telephone, email or text contact with the participant or the provided alternate contacts. Using these techniques, we expect greater than 90% follow-up at 6 months, as our group has reached this success rate in similar trials.

Blinding

The orthopaedic team (including the study coordinators) and trial participants cannot be blinded to the treatment allocation as both treatments are visually distinguishable. The data analysts will be blinded to the trial interventions.

Data management

The data management plan includes the development of the case report forms (CRFs), the management and oversight of the data management system, data monitoring and the oversight of data as it moves from the sites into the trial database. The Methods Centre personnel will provide the clinical sites with the trial CRFs prior to initiation of enrolment. Research personnel at each clinical site will submit the required data, as detailed on the CRFs, to the Methods Centre using an EDC system. Clinical site personnel will receive a unique login and password for the EDC system and will be able to view and modify data for participants recruited at their clinical site.

All eCRFs will be completed by designated, trained site personnel. At the end of the trial, each local site investigator will receive participant data for their local clinical site in a readable format that must be kept with the trial records. Acknowledgement of receipt of the data is required.

Data and safety monitoring committee

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) is responsible for monitoring the collected interim safety data as the trial progresses. The DSMC will review all adverse events and safety analyses. A DSMC Charter will describe the committee’s procedures, meeting schedule and processes for safety reporting to clinical sites, ethics committees and regulatory agencies.

Statistical analysis

The analysis and reporting of the results will follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for reporting randomised controlled trials (RCTs).44 Participants’ enrolment and flow throughout the trial will be summarised using a flow diagram. Participant demographics and baseline outcome variables will be summarised using descriptive summary measures expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables depending on the distribution, and counts with percentages for categorical variables.45 Details of the primary and secondary outcome analyses, as well as for the subgroup and sensitivity analyses, are listed below. We will not adjust for multiple testing; therefore, we will interpret all secondary and sensitivity results as exploratory.

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle, analysing participants in the groups to which they were randomly assigned. All confidence intervals (CIs) will be 95% and two-sided.

For the trial’s primary outcome, we will hierarchically assess mortality, followed by injury-related amputation of the lower extremity, unplanned reoperation and clinical fracture healing (FIX-IT score) at 6 months post-randomisation using the win ratio method. The win ratio method is based on the principle that each participant in the clinical trial is compared with every other participant assigned to the alternative treatment in a pairwise manner. In our approach, we give higher importance to higher-ranked components of the composite outcome. The pairwise comparison proceeds hierarchically, starting with all-cause mortality, followed by injury-related amputation of the lower extremity, unplanned reoperation, and then the FIX-IT score when participants cannot be differentiated based on a higher-ranked comparison. The all-cause mortality, injury-related amputation of the lower extremity and unplanned reoperation components will be included as time-to-event analyses, assuming an earlier event is worse than a later occurrence. Comparisons of unplanned reoperations will also allow for more than one reoperation, with the model assuming more reoperations are worse. Participants with an unknown status of a dichotomous hierarchical outcome will have each outcome censored at its last known observation. Missing FIX-IT data will be imputed using multiple imputations. Finally, the treatment groups are assigned a win, loss or tie in each pairwise comparison. The win ratio is the number of wins in the antibiotic bead groups divided by the number of wins in the NPWT group, with a 95% CI calculated using the methods described by Bebu and Lachin.46 The corresponding p value will be interpreted for superiority.

The components of the primary composite outcome will be analysed separately as secondary outcomes. We will compare all-cause mortality and injury-related amputation of the lower extremity between treatment groups using a Cox proportional hazards model, with treatment and stratification factors as covariates. We will use a frailty model to analyse unplanned reoperation and account for the possibility of multiple events using a random effect for distinct patients and stratification factors as covariates.47 48 For participants with an unknown outcome status for one of the binary secondary outcomes, the Cox proportional hazards and frailty models will censor participants at their last known observation. Between-group differences in FIX-IT scores will be compared using multivariable linear regression with treatment and stratification factors as covariates. The linear regression model will assume missing outcome data to be missing at random.

A limited number of a priori subgroup analyses will be performed. These subgroups include analyses for wound contamination and severity of the fracture. We hypothesise that the effect will differ by subgroup, and the antibiotic bead pouch will be even more effective for more severely contaminated wounds and the highest Gustilo-Anderson injury types. For each subgroup analysis, we will stratify the sample based on the binary subgroup and perform the win ratio analysis for each stratum. We will compare the win ratios and 95% CIs for each subgroup strata to explore the heterogeneity of treatment effects. The interpretation of subgroup effects will follow the Instrument to assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN) criteria.49

Sample size determination

The primary outcome will be clinical status 6 months after randomisation as determined by a hierarchically assessed composite outcome consisting of four mutually exclusive categories as described previously. The WOLLF trial and 2018 Cochrane Review concluded that NPWT did not reduce infection or disability from open fractures within 12 months of the fracture.16 18 Results from Morgenstern et al and a review of over 800 patients from our institution suggest antibiotic beads may reduce the odds of infection and unplanned reoperations by more than 50%.50

Using 1000 simulations, we determined that complete primary outcome data for 140 participants per arm would provide 90% power to detect an overall clinical benefit for antibiotic beads compared with NPWT with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. The hypothesised benefit for each component of the hierarchically assessed composite outcome is provided in figure 1 and corresponds with a win ratio estimate of 1.70. The benefits in the binary components assume a 25% relative reduction and an effect size of d=0.66 for the continuous FIX-IT. These hypothesised differences are conservative compared with the benefits reported in Morgenstern et al’s review and in unpublished data from our institution.50 We have inflated our overall sample size to 312 to account for up to 10% attrition of participants. Clinical sites will be added as needed to achieve the target sample size of 140 participants per treatment arm. If a clinical site is no longer able to participate, an alternate site may be selected depending on the status of the trial.

Ethics and dissemination

The protocol, including the written informed consent forms, was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB) at McMaster University, the FDA, Health Canada and Advarra (central institutional IRB) and/or the local IRB/REB at each participating centre not using the central institutional IRB (Fraser Health Research Ethics Board, The University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board, Newfoundland and Labrador Health Research Ethics Board, University of Manitoba Biomedical Research Ethics Board). Additional clinical sites who are in the start-up phase, as well as any new selected clinical sites, will obtain local approvals prior to initiating trial activities. This will include a clinical site in the UK who is in the process of obtaining the necessary approvals to execute the trial. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05615844). The first participant was enrolled on 5 November 2023.

Any amendments to the trial protocol that may affect the conduct of the trial, the potential safety of, or benefits to participants (eg, changes to the trial objectives, trial design, sample size or trial procedures) will require a formal amendment to the protocol. Any protocol amendments will be approved by the Principal Investigators and may require approval by all applicable ethics committees and regulatory agencies.

Summary

Open tibia fracture complications are debilitating for patients. NPWT is the current standard of care for temporary wound management of severe open fractures requiring multiple debridements; however, emerging evidence suggests that the antibiotic bead pouch may significantly reduce the risk of infection compared with NPWT. The BvV trial will fill this critical knowledge gap as the only comparative randomised trial comparing the bead pouch to NPWT. BvV has been designed to be pragmatic and generate findings readily available for implementation in clinical practice. The results of the trial will be submitted for publication regardless of whether there are significant findings.
