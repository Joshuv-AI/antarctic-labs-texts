Sweetening the Odds: Can Newer Glucose-Lowering Drugs Bend the Arc of Epileptogenesis?

ABSTRACT
Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitors and the Risk of Late Onset Epilepsy: A Real-World Cohort Study Lin BH, Huang HM, Lin HA, Lin SF. Epilepsia. 2026;67(7):3444–3456. doi:10.1002/epi.70239.Objective: Late onset epilepsy (LOE) is associated with substantial morbidity. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) may exert neuroprotective effects. This study evaluated the association between SGLT2i and risk of LOE among older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Methods: This retrospective cohort study was conducted between 2013 and 2025 with a 3-year follow-up using the TriNetX global network. Patients ≥ 60 years old with type 2 diabetes mellitus were classified into two cohorts—new SGLT2i users and new dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors users—following a 6-month washout of other antihyperglycemic agents except metformin. Patients with major neurological diseases or contraindications to SGLT2i were excluded. Propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics between two cohorts. The primary outcomes were incident LOE, status epilepticus, and initiation of antiseizure medications. Unadjusted Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CIs). Results: A total of 1435648 patients were identified. After matching, 60203 patients were included in the SGLT2i cohort (mean age = 67.7 years, 42.3% female) and 60203 in the control cohort (mean age = 67.7 years, 41.7% female). SGLT2i use was associated with lower risk of LOE (HR = .55, 95% CI = .44–.68), status epilepticus (HR = .38, 95% CI = .21–.69), and antiseizure medication initiation (HR = .63, 95% CI = .58–.69). SGLT2i reduced LOE risk in patients with stroke (HR = .69, 95% CI = .42–.88) and dementia (HR = .44, 95% CI = .25–.78) but not in those with traumatic brain injury or brain tumors. Significance: SGLT2i use was associated with reduced risk of LOE among selected patients, supporting its role in an etiology-specific therapeutic approach for older adults at risk of epilepsy. Semaglutide and Risk of Adult-Onset Seizure: A Target Trial Emulation Eun Y, Bong S, Koh HY, Trousdale RK, Cho YM, Jang Y, Lee ST. Neurology. 2026 Jul 14;107(1):e218174. doi: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218174. Epub 2026 Jun 17. PMID: 42308439; PMCID: PMC13278381.Background and objectives: Adult-onset seizure reflects the burden of acquired brain insults, but established disease-modifying preventive strategies are limited. We evaluated whether semaglutide initiation is associated with a lower incidence of adult-onset seizure compared with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and other glucose-lowering drugs (GLDs) in adults with type 2 diabetes. Methods: Using the All of Us Research Program, we emulated a population-based target trial in new-user, active-comparator cohorts from January 2018 to October 2023. We compared semaglutide vs other GLDs and semaglutide vs SGLT2i. Incident epilepsy or seizure was identified from diagnostic codes. Effects were estimated using inverse probability of treatment weighting with weighted Cox models and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses with multiple outcome definitions and analytic approaches were conducted to assess the robustness of findings. We evaluated mediation through hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and body mass index (BMI) using the longitudinal Vansteelandt framework. Results: We analyzed 10,213 patients in the semaglutide (n = 2,586, mean age, 60.1 years; 56.8% female) vs other GLDs cohort (n = 7,627, mean age, 63.7 years; 54.2% female) and 8,605 patients in the semaglutide (n = 2,814, mean age, 60.5 years; 66.2% female) vs SGLT2i cohort (n = 5,791, mean age, 64.4 years; 47.3% female). Semaglutide was associated with a lower risk of adult-onset seizure compared with other GLDs (weighted HR 0.44 [95% CI 0.25-0.79]; 4-year risk difference -1.78% [95% CI -2.58 to -0.98]) and SGLT2i (weighted HR 0.48 [95% CI 0.27-0.85]; 4-year risk difference -1.46% [95% CI -2.41 to -0.51]). TMLE estimated risk differences per 1,000 persons of -14.20 (95% CI -18.33 to -10.07) vs other GLDs and -7.62 (95% CI -11.65 to -3.60) vs SGLT2i, corresponding to numbers needed to treat of 70 and 131, respectively. Mediation was minimal for HbA1c (2.4% vs other GLDs; 6.5% vs SGLT2i) and BMI (0% vs other GLDs; 0.7% vs SGLT2i). Discussion: Semaglutide initiation was associated with a lower risk of adult-onset seizure compared with SGLT2i and other GLDs in patients with type 2 diabetes, independent of glycemic and weight effects. Residual confounding, low event counts, and shorter follow-up limit causal interpretation. Classification of evidence: This study provides Class II evidence that semaglutide use was associated with a lower risk of adult-onset seizures compared with other GLDs and SGLT2i.

Commentary

For all its advances, epilepsy care still waits for the disease to announce itself before acting. We hold our fire until the first seizure, then titrate antiseizure medications (ASMs) against a disorder that has already taken hold, managing what we might once have prevented. This gap is especially consequential in later life, when seizure incidence is highest.1-3 Against this landscape, two recent studies pose a question that would have bordered on fanciful a decade ago: could glucose-lowering drugs bend the arc of epileptogenesis?4,5

Lin and colleagues used the TriNetX global network to compare sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP4i) among adults aged ≥ 60 years with type 2 diabetes. 5 Among 60,203 propensity score-matched pairs, SGLT2i use was associated with 45%, 62%, and 37% lower hazards of incident late-onset epilepsy, status epilepticus, and ASM initiation, respectively. Eun and colleagues approached from the other side of the metabolic aisle. 4 Using All of Us data, they emulated a trial comparing semaglutide with SGLT2i and other glucose-lowering drugs. Semaglutide initiation was associated with half the hazard of adult-onset seizure: hazard ratios of 0.44 versus other agents and 0.48 versus SGLT2i. 4 Read together, the studies converge on a provocative thesis yet disagree instructively.

The convergence first. Both studies deploy rigorous causal-inference methodology: an active-comparator, new-user design that aligns eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up at a common time zero, addressing biases that have undermined earlier pharmacoepidemiologic claims. 6 They also extend the emerging literature: a prior meta-analysis found fewer late-onset seizures with newer glucose-lowering drugs 7 and a Chinese target-trial emulation found a 29% lower incidence of epilepsy with SGLT2i than with DPP4i. 8 Concordant findings across independent populations and drug classes give the hypothesis weight no single study could bear.

Do these findings place semaglutide atop a therapeutic hierarchy for epilepsy prevention? Placed side by side, the studies suggest an alluring but premature gradient: DPP4i, then SGLT2i, then semaglutide. However, these were not three arms of one randomized trial: Lin et al included diabetics aged ≥ 60 years, whereas Eun et al included diabetics aged ≥ 18 years and analyzed the ≥60-year subgroup secondarily.4,5 Differences in databases, eligibility, outcomes, exposure definitions, covariates, follow-up, and comparator composition preclude a direct therapeutic ranking. The studies therefore provide convergent evidence of potential neuroprotection, not a league table of antiepileptogenic efficacy.

Yet the disagreement is where the intellectual action lies. For Lin, SGLT2i is the protagonist, the neuroprotective agent that outperforms DPP4i. 5 For Eun, SGLT2i is the comparator, and semaglutide pulls ahead, while SGLT2i shows inconsistent and largely nonsignificant associations in sensitivity analyses. 4 Lin chose DPP4i precisely because it lacks established neuroprotective effects; Eun began from the premise that some glucose-lowering drugs may be neuroprotective and others may not.4,5 If SGLT2i is genuinely antiepileptogenic, using it as the reference would bias the semaglutide comparison toward the null and understate semaglutide's effect. Conversely, if the observed SGLT2i association is artifactual, Lin's headline is imperiled. Observational comparisons cannot settle this. Comparator choice is not a neutral methodological detail but a hidden assumption about which drugs are inert.

Biological plausibility is nevertheless compelling. Epilepsy in the aging brain often arises at the intersection of vascular injury, inflammation, and neurodegeneration. 9 The most arresting finding belongs to Lin and colleagues. 5 The stronger associations observed among those with stroke or dementia than among those with brain trauma or tumors raise the possibility that metabolic therapies act preferentially on neurovascular or neurodegenerative substrates. 5 More intriguingly, among people with established epilepsy, SGLT2i use was associated with a lower risk of dementia, but not with functional decline or mortality. 5 Eun et al offer a resonant echo: their protective association was stronger in the late-onset subgroup. 4 Together, these observations hint at a differential pharmacology of epileptogenesis: some acquired epilepsies may prove more metabolically tractable than others. However, this interpretation remains provisional. Subgroup event counts were small, and the absence of statistically significant associations in other etiologic groups does not establish treatment-effect heterogeneity. A drug that prevents stroke could reduce poststroke epilepsy without directly altering epileptogenesis.

What unites the two studies more durably than any hazard ratio is their shared verdict on mechanism: whatever protection exists, it does not appear to run through glucose. Eun et al used a longitudinal mediation framework to show that changes in HbA1c and BMI accounted for little of semaglutide's association with seizures. 4 Lin et al reached a similar inference by another route: post-treatment HbA1c was nearly identical between matched groups despite divergent epilepsy risk. 5 If not glycemia, then what? Both invoke pleiotropic CNS effects, including attenuation of neuroinflammation and microglial activation, reduction of oxidative stress, and, for SGLT2i, mild ketogenesis and reduced neuronal sodium influx that may stabilize membrane excitability.4,5,10 Yet these mechanisms are inferred rather than observed: neither database measured neuroinflammation, electrophysiology, or tissue-level drug action. Mediation analyses also require strong assumptions, and incomplete mediator ascertainment and unmeasured time-varying factors preclude firm conclusions about direct biological effects.

Eun et al further complicate a class-effect narrative. 4 The association appeared with semaglutide but not with other GLP-1 receptor agonists, suggesting a possible drug-specific rather than class-wide association. Semaglutide's prolonged receptor engagement and potential CNS penetration offer a plausible explanation.4,11 However, differences in uptake, indication, prescribing, and follow-up could also produce apparent within-class heterogeneity. Likewise, the observed benefit of semaglutide in men but not in women is an intriguing lead for future studies rather than evidence of an established sex-specific effect.

Several inherent design limitations temper enthusiasm.4,5 Seizure outcomes were identified from diagnostic codes without clinical adjudication, EEG, or semiology. Event counts were low, especially in the study by Eun et al and in subgroup and mediation analyses. ASM initiation is an imperfect surrogate because these drugs are also prescribed for pain and mood disorders. 12 Neither database captured medication dose, adherence, discontinuation, or crossover reliably. Residual confounding by healthcare access, clinician preference, and indication therefore remains possible. The study by Eun et al also spanned the COVID-19 pandemic, while the study by Lin et al did not explicitly account for death as a competing event, an important consideration in older adults. An intention-to-treat approach may distort associations when treatment changes are common. Despite these limitations, the findings remain promising but insufficient to alter clinical practice.

What should clinicians do come Monday morning? Not prescribe semaglutide or an SGLT2i solely to prevent epilepsy. But the findings may reframe how these drugs are considered. If a medication already indicated for diabetes or cardiovascular disease also biases the odds against epileptogenesis, neurologic vulnerability may inform selection between otherwise reasonable therapies, perhaps for an older adult with diabetes after stroke. A small, uncertain neurologic benefit cannot be detached from adverse effects, and epilepsy prevention should not become a backdoor justification for exposing non-diabetics to drugs not studied for this purpose. The question is not whether to create a new indication from observational evidence, but whether an ancillary benefit might someday help choose among treatments a person already needs.

The next step is independent replication in datasets using shared protocols, adjudicated outcomes, active comparators, competing-risk methods, as-treated analyses, and prespecified negative controls, followed by prospective studies incorporating medication exposure, MRI, and inflammatory biomarkers. Ultimately, pragmatic randomized trials in neurologically enriched populations must test whether these agents can serve as disease-modifying preventive strategies, with incident seizures as a prespecified outcome. Mechanistic studies must distinguish true antiepileptogenesis from prevention of precipitating brain injuries or better overall health.

A century of epilepsy therapeutics has been seizure-centric. Lin and Eun gesture toward a different principle: the insult preceding the seizures and the metabolic milieu in which epileptogenesis unfolds, especially in the aging brain. A diabetes drug may alter that terrain. Observational neurology can now pose the question of epilepsy prevention; randomized trials must test it, and mechanistic studies must explain it. We have spent generations asking how to quiet seizures. Lin and Eun invite a more ambitious question: how to keep them from ever beginning?
