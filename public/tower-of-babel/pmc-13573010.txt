Association Between Diastolic Dysfunction and Postoperative Heart Failure in Non-cardiac Surgeries
Authors: Sheizaf Gefen, Ofer Havakuk, Lior Zornitzki, Shir Frydman, Michal Laufer-Perl, Daniel Karlsberg, Yoav Granot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cardiovascular complications are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality following non-cardiac surgery. Although the association between systolic dysfunction and perioperative outcomes is well recognised, the prognostic significance of diastolic dysfunction remains underexplored and is not incorporated into risk models. This study investigated whether preoperative diastolic dysfunction is associated with adverse in-hospital outcomes following non-cardiac surgery.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included 3,882 adults who underwent non-cardiac surgery between 2011 and 2021 and had preoperative echocardiographic assessment of diastolic function. Diastolic dysfunction was graded per 2016 American Society of Echocardiography guidelines and categorised as normal/grade 1 (normal left-sided filling pressure [NLFP]) or grade 2/3 (elevated left-sided filling pressure [ELFP]). The primary outcome was a composite of in-hospital mortality and acute heart failure.Results:The primary outcome occurred in 22.3% of patients with ELFP and 11.6% of patients with NLFP (OR 2.1; 95% CI [1.7–2.6]; p<0.001). In-hospital mortality was 9.1% in the ELFP group and 4.2% in the NLFP group (OR 2.2; 95% CI [1.6–3.0]). Pulmonary oedema (3.8% versus 0.7%; OR 4.9; 95% CI [2.9–8.4]), furosemide use (12.9% versus 5.5%; OR 2.5; 95% CI [1.9–3.2]) and vasoactive medication administration (7.9% versus 5.4%; OR 1.5; 95% CI [1.1–2]) were more frequent in the ELFP group. These associations persisted after adjustment for echocardiographic and clinical parameters. Stratified analysis showed the association was strongest in intermediate- to high-risk surgeries.Conclusion:Diastolic dysfunction is independently associated with adverse postoperative outcomes in non-cardiac surgery. Incorporating diastolic parameters into preoperative risk assessment may improve perioperative outcomes.

The global increase in the volume of non-cardiac surgeries has highlighted the need for improved preoperative risk stratification to enhance postoperative outcomes.1 Cardiovascular complications remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality following non-cardiac surgery.2 Although left ventricular systolic function is a well-established determinant of cardiac risk, studies have suggested that diastolic function also plays an important role as a marker of perioperative outcomes.3–7

Diastolic dysfunction, characterised by impaired ventricular relaxation and increased filling pressures, is observed in approximately 27–28% of patients undergoing echocardiographic evaluation in the general population.8,9 The severity of diastolic dysfunction can be graded based on various echocardiographic parameters, including E, A and e′ waves and their respective ratios, left atrial enlargement and tricuspid regurgitation.10,11

Various diastolic parameters have been associated with adverse outcomes in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. Cho et al. showed that preoperative elevations in E/e′ and pulmonary artery systolic pressure were associated with postoperative pulmonary oedema and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients undergoing low- or intermediate-risk non-cardiac surgeries.12 Higashi et al. reported a higher incidence of postoperative heart failure in intermediate-risk surgeries among patients with elevated preoperative E/e′.13 Zhou et al. showed an association between preoperative grade 3 diastolic dysfunction, as classified by the 2009 guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE), and postoperative MACE.14,15 Additional echocardiographic parameters have also been correlated with poor postoperative outcomes.16–18

Despite its potential significance, the relationship between diastolic function and non-cardiac surgical outcomes remains underexplored. Current perioperative risk models, including the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2022 guidelines on cardiovascular assessment and management of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery and the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI), largely omit diastolic function parameters, potentially overlooking a critical marker of complications.19,20

The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between diastolic dysfunction, as assessed by echocardiographic parameters, and postoperative heart failure in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.

Methods

The study population for this retrospective analysis consisted of consecutive adult (age ≥18 years) patients who underwent non-cardiac surgery at Tel Aviv Medical Center between March 2011 and March 2021 and had a preoperative echocardiographic examination that included diastolic parameters. Patients were excluded if they had severe aortic or mitral valve disease. All types of non-cardiac surgeries were included, encompassing both elective and urgent or emergency procedures, across all surgical departments. Data were obtained from electronic medical records. Comprehensive demographic information, comorbid conditions, laboratory findings and inpatient medication regimens were systematically documented for all patients.

Each patient underwent a detailed transthoracic echocardiogram within 7 days before surgery. Echocardiography was performed within this 7-day window to ensure clinical relevance to the planned surgical procedure and to reflect the patient’s current haemodynamic status as part of the formal preoperative evaluation. The timing of echocardiography (within 7 days before surgery) was predefined for inclusion in the study; however, the clinical indication for performing preoperative echocardiography was determined at the discretion of the consulting cardiologist or anaesthesiologist, as part of the routine preoperative evaluation, based on clinical judgement, comorbidities and physical examination. Surgical data, including American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, the RCRI score, the type of procedure, surgical risk (according to the 2022 ESC guidelines on cardiovascular assessment of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery) and duration, were also collected.19–21 If a patient had undergone multiple surgeries, only details for the first surgery were recorded. The decision to perform the echocardiographic examination was at the discretion of the referring physician conducting the patient’s preoperative evaluation.

This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tel Aviv Medical Center (TLV-0111-18; approved 28 December 2023). The committee waived the requirement for informed patient consent because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Echocardiographic Examination

Echocardiograms were performed according to the 2019 guidelines from the ASE for performing comprehensive transthoracic echocardiographic examination in adults.22

Left ventricular diameters were measured as recommended.23 Measurements of mitral inflow included late diastolic filling (A wave) and peak early filling (E wave) velocities, the E/A ratio and the deceleration time of early filling velocity. Early diastolic septal and lateral mitral annular velocities (e′) were measured in the apical four-chamber view.10 Left atrial volume was calculated using the biplane area–length method at end-systole in both four- and two-chamber views.

Patients were stratified into grades of diastolic dysfunction according to the 2016 ASE recommendations for the evaluation of left ventricular diastolic function by echocardiography.24 A stepwise algorithm was used, with separate assessment in patients with preserved (≥50%) and reduced (<50%) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

In patients with preserved LVEF (≥50%), diastolic function was first assessed using four variables: mean E/e′ ratio >14, septal e′ velocity <7 cm/s or lateral e′ velocity <10 cm/s, tricuspid regurgitation velocity >2.8 m/s and left atrial volume index (LAVI) >34 ml/m2. Each variable was classified as positive or negative according to these predefined cut-off values. Diastolic function was considered normal (grade 0) if more than half the available parameters were negative. Diastolic dysfunction was diagnosed if more than half the variables were positive. If exactly half the available variables were positive, diastolic function was classified as indeterminate.

Patients classified as indeterminate at this stage were not assigned a diastolic dysfunction grade based on the initial assessment; instead, they underwent further evaluation according to the grading algorithm described below, with final classification determined on the basis of these subsequent criteria.

Patients with diastolic dysfunction and preserved LVEF, as well as all patients with reduced LVEF (<50%), underwent grading of diastolic dysfunction based on mitral inflow parameters, following the ASE algorithm. An E/A ratio ≤0.8 with peak E velocity ≤50 cm/s was classified as grade 1 (impaired relaxation). An E/A ratio ≥2 was classified as grade 3 (restrictive filling). In patients with an E/A ratio ≤0.8 and peak E velocity >50 cm/s, or with an E/A ratio between 0.8 and 2, additional parameters were evaluated (mean E/e′ >14, tricuspid regurgitation velocity >2.8 m/s and LAVI >34 ml/m2). If at least two of these parameters were positive, patients were classified as grade 2; if at least two were negative, patients were classified as grade 1. If only one parameter was available, or if two parameters were available with one positive and one negative result, diastolic grade was considered indeterminate and the patient was excluded from the study.

Eventually, patients were divided into two groups based on diastolic grade: grade 0 or 1, normal left-sided filling pressure (NLFP); and grade 2 or 3, elevated left-sided filling pressure (ELFP).

Postoperative Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of clinically adjudicated acute heart failure or mortality during postoperative hospitalisation. Acute heart failure was defined according to predefined clinical criteria and required the presence of new or worsening signs and symptoms consistent with heart failure, together with objective evidence of pulmonary congestion. This included the diagnosis of pulmonary oedema documented in the electronic medical record, the use of IV furosemide for the treatment of suspected heart failure or the administration of vasoactive medications at any dosage for haemodynamic support in the setting of suspected cardiac dysfunction.

Pulmonary oedema was diagnosed based on compatible clinical signs and symptoms (e.g. dyspnoea, hypoxaemia, rales on auscultation), in conjunction with radiographic evidence of pulmonary congestion on chest imaging when available, and the treating physician’s documentation supporting a diagnosis of cardiogenic pulmonary oedema.

Secondary outcomes included each component of the primary outcome (i.e. in-hospital mortality, pulmonary oedema, use of IV furosemide and vasoactive medications), as well as the length of hospital stay after surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using visual inspection of quantile–quantile plots, histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous normally distributed parameters are presented as the mean ± SD and were compared using t-tests. Non-normally distributed parameters were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data are presented as rates and percentages, and were compared between groups using the χ2 test. Variables included in the multivariable analysis were age, sex, comorbidities, surgical risk and echocardiographic parameters, selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance and associations with perioperative and cardiovascular outcomes, in order to account for potential confounding. All statistical tests were two-sided and p<0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29 (IBM).

The sample size was calculated using a significance level of 1% and a power level of 90%. Overall, 1,006 subjects were needed to identify a small effect (effect size W=0.1) of the primary outcome.

Results

Study Population

The cohort consisted of 4,353 consecutive patients who underwent non-cardiac surgery and had a preoperative echocardiogram performed within 7 days before the procedure. Of these patients, 82 were classified as having indeterminate diastolic function, 345 were excluded due to missing diastolic parameters and 44 were excluded due to severe aortic or mitral valve disease. Consequently, the final cohort comprised 3,882 patients.

Postoperative pulmonary oedema was diagnosed in 1.4% of all patients. The incidence of postoperative pulmonary oedema was markedly higher in the ELFP than NLFP group (3.8% versus 0.7%, respectively; OR 4.9; 95% CI [2.9–8.4]; p<0.001).

IV furosemide was administered postoperatively to 7.1% of all patients. In the ELFP group, 12.9% of patients received IV furosemide, compared with 5.5% in the NLFP group (OR 2.5; 95% CI [1.9–3.2]; p<0.001).

In all, 232 patients (6%) required vasoactive medications postoperatively, with 74% receiving norepinephrine, 14.7% receiving epinephrine and 9.5% receiving dopamine. The use of vasoactive medications was higher in the ELFP than NLFP group (7.9% versus 5.4%, respectively; OR 1.5; 95% CI [1.1–2.0]; p=0.007).

The median total length of hospital stay was 12.8 days. Patients in the ELFP group had a significantly longer median length of hospital stay than patients in the NLFP group (15.4 versus 12 days, respectively; p<0.001).

Multivariable Analysis

Outcomes were adjusted for age, sex, medical history, surgical risk and echocardiographic parameters, including LVEF, LVEDD, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, moderate aortic or mitral valve diseases and right ventricular dysfunction (Table 5). The combined outcome remained significantly higher in the ELFP group (OR 1.6; 95% CI [1.2–2.0]; p>0.001). (Note, ORs in this section are multivariable-adjusted.) Multivariable analysis of the secondary outcomes, using the same variables, remained significant for in-hospital mortality (OR 1.5; 95% CI [1.1–2.1]; p=0.03), pulmonary oedema (OR 2.0; 95% CI [1.1–3.7]; p=0.024) and IV furosemide (OR 1.7; 95% CI [1.3–2.4]; p<0.001).

Furthermore, the primary outcome was stratified according to the surgical risk (Figure 1). In the low-risk surgical group, the primary outcome occurred in 12.1% of patients in the ELFP group and in 7.6% of patients in the NLFP group. After adjusting for the previously mentioned echocardiographic parameters, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.173). In the moderate-risk group, the primary outcome occurred in 25.6% of patients in the ELFP group and 11.4% of patients in the NLFP group, a statistically significant difference that remained after adjustment (p<0.001). In the high-risk group, the primary outcome was observed in 32% and 23.7% of patients in the ELFP and NLFP groups, respectively, with an adjusted p-value of 0.29. When the moderate- and high-risk groups were combined, the overall incidence of the primary outcome was 26.3% in the ELFP group and 13.1% in the NLFP group. This difference was statistically significant after adjustment for echocardiographic parameters (p<0.001).

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study provides compelling evidence that diastolic dysfunction, as assessed through comprehensive echocardiographic parameters, is independently associated with adverse outcomes in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. Patients with elevated left-sided filling pressure (grade 2 or 3) had higher rates of acute heart failure, in-hospital mortality, postoperative pulmonary oedema and longer hospital stays. Most of these differences, including the combined outcome, remained significant after adjusting for echocardiographic characteristics. After stratifying by surgical risk, the differences remained significant for moderate- to high-risk surgeries but not for low-risk surgeries. These findings underscore the importance of preoperative evaluation of diastolic function as a critical component of perioperative risk assessment.

Comparison With Previous Literature

Our findings align with previous studies that have demonstrated the prognostic importance of diastolic function in surgical populations. Elevated E/e′ ratios, an increased LAVI and higher pulmonary artery systolic pressures, which were more prevalent in patients with high-grade diastolic dysfunction, likely reflect elevated left ventricular filling pressures and impaired compliance. These pathophysiological alterations may predispose these patients to volume overload and haemodynamic instability during the perioperative period, increasing the risk of complications such as pulmonary oedema and acute heart failure.

Our results build on the work of Cho et al. and Higashi et al., who identified the association between elevated preoperative E/e′ and pulmonary pressures and postoperative complications.12,13 However, the inclusion in the present study of a large, diverse cohort undergoing various non-cardiac surgeries enhances the generalisability of these findings. In addition, our use of the 2016 ASE guidelines for the grading of diastolic dysfunction allows for precise stratification and comparison across studies.

Although Zhou et al. highlighted the association between grade 3 diastolic dysfunction and MACE, the present study expands this understanding by demonstrating significant differences even at grade 2 diastolic dysfunction.15 This highlights the need for closer surveillance of patients with intermediate grades of dysfunction.

Importantly, our findings align with contemporary perioperative cardiovascular risk frameworks, including the 2022 ESC guidelines on the cardiovascular assessment and management of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery and the 2024 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) perioperative guideline update, both of which emphasise structured cardiac risk assessment but do not explicitly incorporate diastolic grading into routine risk models.19,25 Our results extend these frameworks by suggesting that echocardiographic markers of diastolic dysfunction, particularly indicators of elevated left ventricular filling pressures, may provide incremental prognostic value beyond traditional clinical risk indices. Therefore, incorporating diastolic parameters into existing guideline-based risk stratification algorithms could enhance the identification of patients at increased perioperative risk, particularly among those with preserved LVEF.

Similarly, the 2022 AHA/ACC/Heart Failure Society of America heart failure guidelines recognise elevated left ventricular filling pressures and structural abnormalities such as left atrial enlargement as central features of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a condition highly prevalent in the surgical population.26 Our data suggest that preoperative identification of elevated filling pressures, even in the absence of overt heart failure, may identify a vulnerable HFpEF phenotype at increased perioperative risk.

In addition, our findings may be interpreted in the context of altered ventricular–arterial coupling, a key mechanism in HFpEF. As highlighted by Li et al., impaired coupling may limit cardiovascular reserve and contribute to reduced tolerance to perioperative haemodynamic stress, potentially explaining the association between elevated filling pressures and adverse outcomes in our cohort.27

Implications for Perioperative Management

The observed differences in mortality rates further emphasise the clinical significance of diastolic dysfunction. The nearly twofold increase in in-hospital mortality in patients with ELFP compared with those with NLFP suggests that these patients may benefit from tailored perioperative management strategies aimed at mitigating cardiac stress.

These findings suggest several practical implications for perioperative management. First, incorporating diastolic function parameters into existing perioperative risk models, such as the RCRI, may improve the identification of high-risk patients. This approach is consistent with and may complement current ESC and ACC/AHA guideline frameworks, in which integration of imaging-derived markers could refine risk stratification beyond conventional clinical variables.19,25 Second, patients with high-grade diastolic dysfunction may benefit from individualised perioperative care, including meticulous fluid management, the early use of diuretics and closer haemodynamic monitoring. Third, routine preoperative echocardiographic assessment may be particularly beneficial in patients scheduled for moderate- or high-risk surgeries.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective observational design limits control over exposure and outcome assessment and introduces potential information bias and missing data. The decision to perform preoperative echocardiography was clinician-dependent, which may have led to selection bias and the inclusion of patients at higher perceived cardiovascular risk. Importantly, the study cohort was restricted to patients who underwent echocardiography within 7 days before surgery, and this investigation was not performed routinely for specific surgical categories. Rather, echocardiography was obtained at the discretion of the consulting cardiologist, typically in patients with suspected or known structural heart disease or in those requiring reassessment of cardiac status. This selection process introduces potential confounding by indication, because patients referred for echocardiography are more likely to have underlying cardiovascular disease, symptoms or higher baseline risk, which may have influenced both diastolic function classification and postoperative outcomes, potentially amplifying the observed associations. Furthermore, echocardiographic measurements were extracted from clinical reports rather than centrally reanalysed and interobserver variability was not formally assessed, which may introduce measurement variability and potential misclassification.

Second, although our findings demonstrate strong associations, causality cannot be established. Residual confounding is likely, because unmeasured factors such as perioperative management and haemodynamics may have influenced both diastolic classification and outcomes.

Third, outcome misclassification is possible. Although predefined clinical criteria were applied to define postoperative heart failure, these criteria, including pulmonary oedema, the administration of intravenous diuretics and the use of vasoactive medications, may lack specificity and could overlap with non-cardiogenic conditions such as postoperative fluid overload or sepsis-related haemodynamic instability. Although pulmonary oedema required supporting clinical features and physician documentation consistent with a cardiogenic aetiology, objective confirmation with standardised diagnostic criteria, such as natriuretic peptide levels or systematic echocardiographic reassessment, was not uniformly available. Furthermore, although outcome classification was based on prespecified definitions applied across the cohort, adjudication was performed through structured medical record review rather than by an independent blinded clinical events committee, which may introduce variability in outcome classification.

Fourth, the cohort included a heterogeneous mix of elective and urgent surgeries, and patients with conditions such as sepsis were not excluded. Perioperative factors, including surgical urgency, anaesthesia type and intraoperative haemodynamics, may have influenced outcomes; however, these data were not consistently available and could not be included in the analysis, introducing potential residual confounding.

Finally, the cohort was derived from a single tertiary-care centre, which may limit external validity. Differences in patient populations and perioperative care may restrict generalisability to other settings.

Future Directions

Future prospective studies should aim to validate these findings in larger, multicentre cohorts and explore the potential benefits of targeted perioperative interventions.

Conclusion

Diastolic dysfunction is associated with perioperative complications, including acute heart failure and mortality in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.
