The (mis)use of evidence in contested rights: commentary on the UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women and girls’ report on “prostitution and violence”
Authors: Susana T Fried, Alice M Miller, Rupsa Mallik, Ivana Radačić, Esteban Restrepo-Saldarriaga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractReaders of Sexual and Reproductive Health Matter are no strangers to interrogating evidence in all its forms, assessing which claims it can support, and about challenges and uncertainties in international norms in the fields of sexual and reproductive rights and health. Questions of evidence, positionality and the role of testimony are particularly live in the context of sex work and human rights. As an exploration about good and bad practices in research and evidence, in this Commentary we highlight the errors, mistakes and wrongly shaped conclusions arising in the recent report by the UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women and girls about prostitution law, sex worker health and rights, and the status of international human rights law on sex work and trafficking. We do this not only to reinforce more accurate information about the status of human rights law, public health evidence and the needs of people of all genders in the sex sector, but also as an opportunity to remind us of the principles around evidence, transparency, and self-determination. We are conscious of the current vulnerability of global rights and health systems. Our Commentary seeks to contextualise our criticisms to this current moment of rights and health systems' fragility and multi-pronged attacks on the emancipatory potential of rights for persons in the sex sector as workers especially as they intersect with racist stereotypes. Readers of Sexual and Reproductive Health Matters are no strangers to interrogating evidence in all its forms, assessing which claims it can support, and grappling with challenges and uncertainties in international norms in the fields of sexual and reproductive rights and health. Questions of evidence, positionality and the role of testimony are particularly live in the context of sex work and human rights. This is why we now write a commentary on the role and misuse of evidence in the new report issued by the UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women and girls (SRVAW), entitled “Prostitution and Violence against Women and Girls”.1 This report is one of the more troubling interventions into this universe. We engage with this SRVAW report in order to engage with the practices of a UN human rights mechanism at a time when these practices are central to the making of international human rights standards. Importantly, new standards will be of immense importance to a uniquely vulnerable population – in this case, people in the sex sector.*

The SRVAW and her reporting practices are part of the United Nations Human Rights Council’s (HRC) Special Procedures,† which encompass special rapporteurs, independent experts and working groups. These independent human rights experts are appointed by the President of the UN Human Rights Council with mandates to report and advise on human rights violations from a thematic or country-specific perspective. They are independent: the structure of their mandate means they should not be coerced by states, nor be intimidated by disagreement with civil society, but civil society plays a key role in providing information to these experts.

Their main functions are three-fold: submitting annual reports‡ to the HRC and (for most) to the UN General Assembly, conducting country visits and communicating with the states with respect to complaints. These mechanisms have been critical tools of advancement of, inter alia, sexual rights, health rights, anti-racism and post- and de-colonial rights claims.2–4 Notably, while any independent expert cannot “make” or claim to authoritatively “find” what the international law is in a disputed domain, they contribute progressively to clarify the law. Although this kind of work has been called “soft law” (in contrast with the “hard law” of state-adopted treaties, such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women), it has been used widely, including at national level such as by courts to interpret and guide their implementation of law.5–7

As such, they have an important function in the areas where the international human rights law standards are not fully developed or around which there are debates over application.§ Independent experts can (and do) have disagreements over contested issues, mirroring disagreements in wider human rights/feminist communities. One of these areas, where there is ideological disagreement that impacts development of human rights norms, is sex work/prostitution.

Here, in the name of making our positionality and the foundation of our claims transparent, we note that we believe our position – that sex work is work and should be fully decriminalised – is backed by a preponderance of evidence. From our perspective, the bulk of good quality evidence shows that the criminalisation of sex work** is causally linked to a wide range of harms, including increasing (risk of) violence against sex workers, raising sex workers’ vulnerability to contracting HIV, reducing sex workers’ access to justice (especially, migrant sex workers), and contributing to the limits on their ability to work legally, among other harms.8–11 We flag in this context, the diversity of robust evidence that we think is of good quality, produced by a wide range of public health, human rights, women’s rights and sex worker-led organisations, all of whom have conducted research about the health and rights impacts of a range of policies related to sex work.12–16

As an exploration about good and bad practices in research and evidence, in this commentary we highlight the errors, mistakes and wrongly shaped conclusions arising in the recent SRVAW report about prostitution law, sex worker health and rights, and the status of international human rights law on sex work and trafficking. We do this not only to reinforce more accurate information about the status of human rights law, public health evidence and the needs of people of all genders in the sex sector, but also as an opportunity to remind us of the principles around evidence, transparency, and self-determination. These principles matter intensely now, when claims in the context of health and rights, and sexual and reproductive rights in particular, are under threat at global norm-setting institutions, including but not limited to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the UN Joint Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS).17–25

We started writing this commentary on 21 June 2024, the day that the SRVAW engaged in an interactive dialogue†† with states and civil society at the Human Rights Council.1 We begin our analysis with the “dialogue”, because it is one of the sites for advocacy by both states and NGOs regarding the shape of international legal frameworks. The process of making this report also included a call for submissions, another form of interaction between the independent experts, civil society and states. Submissions are materials prepared for review by the expert after a public call: States, UN agencies and civil society can all submit materials for review and these submissions can be cited by experts in their reports as part of their evidence.

A number of us submitted materials to the SRVAW report. This commentary is therefore a critique from within the world of feminist health and human rights practice, and allyship with the sex workers’ rights movement. It focuses on the roles, uses and misuses of evidence that can arise when challenging and trying to change existing international human rights legal frames and when there is disagreement within and amongst advocates and nation states.

We are also aware of the many concerns about this report raised by sex worker movements, many human rights practitioners and a range of feminists and queer advocates globally, as well as the calls by some states to reject the claims and findings of this report.26 There are others within the women’s rights movement that support the SRVAW’s position. As we point out below, while there is a growing recognition of the rights of sex workers and the need to decriminalise sex work (including by other special procedure mandates) there is as yet no agreement on the full scope and content of international (“hard law”) legal obligations on states vis a vis the buying and selling of sex. There are international obligations when coercion, force, or abuse of authority in the sex and other labour sectors arise (see discussion below); and an emerging set of soft law norms that engage with protecting the rights of those who work in the sex sector as workers, as well as address the need to remedy harms that arise in this sector. Increasingly these soft law norms treat criminalisation of both seller and buyer of sex as components generating harm. Thus, the disputes and debates over what are the emerging and best standards for state obligations and for whom in the diverse sex sectors around the world, are matters with great impact.

The interplay of evidence and advocacy is fundamental to the work of the various independent mechanisms of the UN human rights system, which includes the SRVAW.27 The SRVAW’s evidence and citation practices in this report present a fierce challenge to all of us as researchers, scholars of human rights law, and advocates for gender justice and sexual and reproductive health. In taking the position that “prostitution is violence” and calling on Member States to further criminalise the sex sector, the report risks reversing over a decade of UN norms and standards development including by the Special Rapporteur’s colleagues (see below). These, in turn, were based on significant human rights and public health evidence that support the contention that criminalisation of sex work fosters and provides impunity for violence.‡‡

Human rights address state responsibility for respecting, protecting and fulfilling rights. Expert reports in the past have included not just exposés of harms to individuals, but also highlighted the commitments of states, who bear formal rights obligations, to refrain from violating rights and act to prevent, identify, investigate and remedy harms as a matter of systems, policies, institutions, laws and programmes.35–37 Human rights reports thus build the argument for structural/state accountability. Over the last three decades, public health evidence is increasingly used as part of the grounds for analyses pinpointing the structural causes of health issues to inform law, policy and practice.38 It is common in UN reports to mix evidence of individual cases of harm with structural analyses, and call attention to categories of specific laws and practices in national laws or international human rights norms and standards that fail to meet rights obligations. The use of multiple sources of information (testimony, public health evidence, human rights reporting, etc.) does not, however, constitute a fact-free zone: good practice in advancing SRH rights regarding better law requires careful assembly of texts as evidence of interpretive trends and meaning making in law and policy.27,39

Sexual and Reproductive Health Matters has a lengthy history of work that has focused on advancing evidence in controversial or neglected sectors of sexual and reproductive rights development. We are conscious of the current vulnerability of global rights and health systems. Therefore, our commentary seeks to contextualise our criticisms to this current moment of rights and health systems’ fragility, i.e. the specific risks and multi-pronged attacks on the emancipatory potential of rights for persons in the sex sector as workers (whose practices defy conservative and/or binary gender and sexual norms), especially as they intersect with racist stereotypes. These attacks on rights arise at the same time as there is agreement on the lack of effective responses to those coerced into or exploited in the sex sector. Practices of deploying evidence matter for rights advocacy: its legitimacy as well as its efficacy depend on good practices.

In this commentary, we highlight three categories of evidentiary errors made by the SRVAW in this report: errors of professional practice, errors of deployment of evidence, and epistemological missteps. The first arises regarding how UN experts participate in building the content of human rights standards in the context of existing interpretations of the relevant law; the second mistake touches upon opaque citation forms and instrumentalisation of submitted evidence; and the third error relates to what we refer to as epistemic injustice and the silencing of or disregard of evidence. We call this “conspicuous invisibilisation”.

How UN experts participate in building the content of human rights law

There are generally accepted practices used in the process of developing UN human rights reports in relation to the role of evidence (including good state practices), prior UN reports, statements, consensus documents, conventions and treaties (including by other human rights mechanisms and UN agencies) and using the expertise and input from civil society and affected persons, particularly in the form of “submissions”. In relation to using submission material, good citation practice (i.e. who submitted what to what end) and a recognition of the diversity of sources of information are of particular importance. Transparent and clear methods allow readers to investigate the basis for the experts’ findings.

First, this report by the SRVAW does not follow the practice of canvassing and citing work done on this topic by other independent mechanisms, e.g. previous SRVAWs, Special Rapporteurs on trafficking, Special Rapporteurs on the Right to Health and the Working Group on Discrimination against Women and Girls, Independent Expert on sexual orientation and gender identity,40–46 as well as reports and guidance from UN Agencies and leading global human rights and public health NGOs.47–49 These reports, with robust analysis based on fact finding, academic and civil society citations, concluded that protecting health and rights in the sex sector requires the decriminalisation of both selling and buying sex.42–45

Regarding the role of law in promoting or impeding health and rights in the sex sector, they have also called on states to ensure protections against fraud, coercion, discrimination, and force in entering the sex (or any other labour sector).42–45 Citation of these reports would provide context for the SRVAW propositions, which can then be evaluated by readers (including states). Contestation over interpretation of human rights norms is not a free-for-all: there are rules and principles that guide interpretive issues and the assertion of what counts as valid law. Many claims in the SRVAW’s report fall foul of those rules and principles, and the commitments and vision of human rights.

Another example of falling foul of good practice arises in the mischaracterisation of evidence of the status of legal interpretation of the international laws on prostitution and trafficking. While there is certainly room for disagreement about where human rights law should go in response to complex needs, experts in the UN procedures in general are expected to clarify accurately the state of law and the grounds for contestation. Different independent experts might have varying views on emerging or controversial topics, reflecting also the difference of views in wider communities of which they are a part (in this case, feminist and human rights). However, in this report, the SRVAW calls for adherence to the 1949 Convention for the Suppression of the Traffic in Persons and of the Exploitation of the Prostitution of Others (the 1949 Convention) but without representing the interactions between that treaty and a later treaty on trafficking with many more States Parties (182).50 The 1949 Convention, ratified by only 82 states, states that “prostitution” is “incompatible with the dignity and worth of the human person”.51 The International Commission of Jurists, in their submission to the SRVAW, contend that “States’ aversion towards criminalizing ‘prostitution’ per se was one of the main reasons for the limited number of States Parties.”52 The 1949 Convention also does not reference any human rights protections.

Many scholars aver that the most pertinent international framework on the crime of trafficking into any labour sector is the 2000 UN Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons Especially Women and Children, supplementing the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, which focuses on state obligation to criminalise force, fraud, and coercion, and the exploitation of any person, in any labour sector (different rules apply for people and materials and for those under the age of 18). Many argue that this treaty supersedes the 1949 Convention.53–56 Moreover, the SRVAW slides between wording extracted from one convention to the other, sometimes shifting phrases and dropping out words that are central to the point being made: a prime example is the shifting between naming the harm as the “exploitation of the prostitution of another” (which is how the UN Protocol and CEDAW name the harm) vs “prostitution” per se as the harm (the position of the 1949 Convention).§§

Another example of failure of good practice arises in the missed opportunity to build an argument about the status and scope of norms on state accountability. This SRVAW report reads more as an account of men's crimes against women,1 with some state action added at times, for example under the anthropomorphising language of “pimp states”.*** The report rarely engages evidence of harmful state practice or the failures of specific agents of the state or laws, despite evidence provided in numerous submissions.††† As a result, the report stands on a weak footing to make claims on states. While the recommendations at the close of the report do begin with a call to states to guarantee rights, by its conclusion the recommendations primarily focus on increased criminalisation. Paradoxically, although the report does call for states to increase services for persons selling sex,12 it does not sufficiently engage with the systemic abuses of the sex workers by the justice and law enforcement sectors: as many submissions from civil society asserted, police officers are one of the main perpetrators of violence against sex workers, leading to both fear and distrust of the state as a site of protection.52,58 The SRVAW report also does not take up the tension raised between her uncritical call for increased judicial and police action and the evidence that persons will not access services from a state they do not trust.‡‡‡59

Finally, the SRVAW consistently slides between the words “prostitution” and “trafficking”, as if these were the same. Many scholars have written about the many elisions and opacities in analyses of and evidence about sex work and trafficking, particularly the call to a moral panic embedded in the use of the nineteenth and twentieth-century “trafficking” language, and especially, the inadequacy of the language of trafficking in the context of gendered and racialised movement of labour in the twenty-first century.§§§62,63 Importantly, vis a vis the kind of evidence looked for and relied on, the trafficking discourse tends to take an “over inclusive approach” that includes all women (and only women) engaged in sex work, including voluntarily, and “under inclusive” when it comes to addressing trafficking for other purposes.64 In addition, the report asserts the unsupported equation that all prostitution = human trafficking. Such elisions have been subjected to very sound and rigorous critique that the SRVAW simply ignores.65

What evidence is used and how it is deployed

The report, in our view, makes a range of errors in good practice around evidentiary standards including using evidence of radically different methodological approaches and rigour without proper reference to their origin (what form of study, what methods of data production were used; what are the indicia of validity), and raises ethical questions by deploying evidence from submissions in a manner directly contrary to the goals of those submissions (taking materials out of context).**** Feminist work in sexual and reproductive rights and health has sought to both expand the scope of meaningful evidence in innovative ways (narrative and storytelling) as well as improving and deploying traditional epidemiological methods. We also stress the importance of feminist and human rights theory and practice’s shared commitments to intersectional analyses and modes of gathering evidence while, at the same time, avoiding the tendency to oversimplify, generalise or “essentialise”66,67 the diverse and complex realities of individuals and their unique and context-specific experiences and identities, in the sex sector and more generally.

For example, certain statements are presented as factual (evidence based), rather than positional (argumentative), without references (or with incomplete references) to the sources or broader contextual claims of the information. We specifically note that this report: (1) problematically uses testimony; (2) mischaracterises information from submissions, including misleadingly taking evidence of the harms of criminalisation to support contrary claims; (3) makes strong claims without verifiable sources of evidence (for instance, generic citations to “expert consultations”) about prevalence, incidence and causality of harms of “prostitution” and (4) refers to the scope of various national and international laws, without verifiable sources of evidence (again, with generic citations to “expert consultations”).

Use of testimony

Feminists, human rights activists, and other advocates for structurally excluded people, have long struggled to make clear the importance of self-representation, voice and narrative to inform policy and challenge oppressive laws and practices.68,69 At the same time, while narrative and testimony is part of our work, it is not the sole basis of rights claims: certainly, it is important to ensure that those who have experienced harm have access to rights protection and public attention.70 However, we are concerned by the SRVAW’s methodology, i.e. the move to use one set of stories as the exclusive basis of advocating for a certain law and policy and using these voices to silence others. However emotionally affecting and deserving of response, individual testimonies cannot alone be the basis of policy. Describing and understanding the harms named and experienced by diverse populations and devising the best policies to prevent these harms and respond to them should be based on listening carefully to the voices of many differently situated persons and using a diverse set of methodologies and empirical methods that will produce the analyses and knowledge necessary to support this task. For example, because the submission process is usually public, we know that information about the harms of criminalisation of sex work was submitted by many sex workers in narrative forms and with reference to a range of studies produced by quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods, including on structural determinants of health and health and rights outcomes research.71–73 Yet these narratives are not cited, and the Rapporteur places certain individual stories as self-explanatory “chunks” of evidence in her report while dismissing others.††††

Unverified claims about prevalence, incidence and causality of harms

Among the bold claims made by the report is the suggestion that countries like Switzerland, Germany, Austria and Uruguay, are among the countries that “[profit] from the prostitution of others and all commercial sex establishments through administrative or territorial laws,” and are, as a result, called “pimp States” in the report.1 Beyond this claim of profit, the report provides no data on industry profits, sources or analysis to support this claim.

Notably, the report synthesises claims about the harms of prostitution with claims about the harms of pornography, without any effort to distinguish the practices or even elucidate the nature of their overlap. We cannot undertake a more detailed analysis here about this totalising set of claims but include it as a flag to the lack of evidence, masked by the emotive weight of the claims. For example, the report forcefully discusses “the perceived right of men to purchase sexual acts” as “normaliz[ing] systematic violence against women and girls and blur[ring] the line between consensual sex and sexual violence.”1 The report goes on to claim that the normalisation of prostitution [here, assumed to include pornography as filmed prostitution] “creates harmful sexual expectations and undermines the safe and equal participation of women and girls in society.”1 Good evidentiary practice would have called for some acknowledgement that research and literature about the links and differences between sex work and pornography, is vast, complex and multifaceted, and governed by distinct branches of law, including freedom of speech and expression laws. There is, of course, significant debate within the activist, academic and policy-making communities about the best approaches to address issues related to sexual exploitation, autonomy, and public health.13,74–76

Finally, there is the odd use of generic and opaque citations to make apparently factual assertions. For example, in the report there is a statement that “Many women lose custody of their children as they are labelled bad mothers. Children of women in prostitution also suffer from violence, even while they are in their mothers’ womb. Many children are conceived because of a violent act of prostitution”. No citation is provided for these claims.1 The assertion that “[t]hey [children of sex workers] are also often stolen, drugged or sold in trafficking networks regularly, used for sexual abuse or rape, and often are killed too,” is supported by a citation to “Expert consultation”.1 No further information is provided: which expert, on what date, on what basis is the claim made, in what country or specific context, for verifying these claims or understanding the scope of state obligation to respond to them.

Use of evidence of violations submitted by civil society out of context

The SRVAW received many submissions by sex workers, feminist, human rights and public health organisations, activists and academics who used their materials to elaborate their position on the harms of criminalisation and argue for full decriminalisation. Many of these submissions provided analyses of various forms of abuse in the sex sector as a product of criminalisation of the sex sector. However, the SRVAW used these submissions in support of her position of client criminalisation, in direct opposition to what these submissions call for, without acknowledgement of their positions. For example, she cites the statement of Trans Gender Europe for evidence that a range of intersectional factors, including gender identity, are “factors that exacerbate the risk of entry into prostitution”, while ignoring TGEU’s broader analysis centring on “widespread structural and institutional discrimination”, and explaining “how the criminalization of sex work puts sex workers and especially trans sex workers in unnecessary danger.”77 The submission of CREA and the Global Health Justice Partnership (GHJP) on precarity in conditions of life for sex workers because their form of livelihood is criminalised, is cited twice: once in support of a statement that many women and girls in the sex trades “are homeless, housing insecure, often evicted,” and then again in relation to the disproportionate and discriminatory impact on women and girls of criminalisation. While our submission strongly linked these situations to criminalisation and therefore advocated for full decriminalisation, the SRVAW used it to argue for criminalisation of sex workers, contrary to the submission’s explicit recommendations.

In addition, important and authoritative submissions countering the Rapporteur’s claims are not cited in her report. For example, the International Commission of Jurists’ submission is ignored in detriment of the possibility of readers to access the ICJ’s analysis on relevant international law and, especially, its work on the The 8 March Principles, which apply “ … the general principles and legal standards of international law to the criminalisation of conduct associated with ‘prostitution’”.78 In an opening footnote the SRVAW justifies her selection of materials by explaining: “Owing to the word limit, the Special Rapporteur was unable to cite all the documents reviewed.”

Epistemic injustice and the silencing of or disregard of evidence

We use the broad frame of epistemic injustice, (i.e. injustice related to power, knowledge, and the role of knowledge practices, including but not limited to scholarly work, as causes and consequences of exclusion)79 here to capture the SRVAW report’s practices as silencing of or disregarding evidence proffered by those with whom she does not agree. This injustice is created in part by a practice of “conspicuous invisibilisation”: this concept addresses the way in which the report’s frame solicits evidence that fits only the SRVAW’s chosen claim (i.e. that sex work is a form of violence, and all sex workers are victims/survivors). In her report, “prostitutes” are almost exclusively portrayed as (cis-gender) women‡‡‡‡ victims/survivors of exclusively male clients’ violence. However, human rights approaches require acknowledgment of and research into the distinct experiences and realities of differently gendered people and with diverse sexual orientations, selling and buying sexual services.§§§§

While the SRVAW has a mandate to focus on women, a human rights approach requires acknowledging the diversity of people selling and buying sexual services as recognised in the UN Protocol on trafficking, the work of other independent experts and UNAIDS guidance documents.80,81 The report’s focus on portraying cis-gender, heterosexual men as buyers and cis-gender women as sellers renders “prostitution” exclusively as a single axis/male-female gender equality issue, neglecting how other forms of discrimination (race, caste, ethnicity, migration and citizenship status) impact gender construction and affect persons in the sex sector.1

Moreover, even in relation to women (the key population of her mandate), the report focuses predominantly on those who define themselves as victims and survivors, thus obscuring the voices of those who define themselves as sex workers.***** Repeatedly conflating sex work and trafficking, and misreading and misquoting existing international law on prostitution, rape, torture and trafficking, not only inaccurately depicts the past and current law, it also creates a silencing of other viewpoints that do not fit the mould of the privileged-victim viewpoint. By using such a limiting frame, those whose experience differs are rendered both invisible and thereby excluded from participation in shaping the laws and policies that will govern their lives. Moreover, by claiming that anyone in sex work is always also a victim of trafficking, the current report duplicates the trend of some service providers, prosecutors, and law enforcement, among others, who routinely disregard the voices of those who do not fit a victim narrative, thereby excluding them from care as persons undeserving of protection.82,83

The long-standing commitment of health and rights advocates to insist on the inclusion of diverse voices among the most affected, and the key role of narrative as part of the evidence in feminist (and queer) theories on “gendering” human rights, undergird this component of our critique. While the report uses narrative evidence and seems to give voice to the most affected, the selective and partial inclusion of only some of those most affected, renders others’ experiences invisible.84–86

A commitment to this inclusive approach put many of us in a constrained position even as to participating in providing evidence via submissions to a report whose drafting process appeared biased from the start. As the SRVAW’s call for submissions broadcasted,87 the report explicitly equated prostitution with violence, pornography and trafficking, raising the question as to what use would countervailing evidence be in this kind of process? Out of a commitment to the more interactive, multivocal process of submitting evidence as part of the development of new norms in human rights, many groups did send in submissions: the Count Me In (CMI) consortium put together a compendium of sex worker-led and allied submissions,52 in which some of us (Miller, Fried) were involved. As we noted above, these submissions were either not used and/or selectively cited to inform the findings and recommendations of the report.

As was suggested in the call for submissions, the report comes out strongly in favour of criminalisation with a view toward the abolition of sex work (what has come to be called the “Nordic” or “Swedish” model), proposing to criminalise the entire sex sector, except for the seller of sex themselves. We note here that the evidence about the value of this model is widely disputed.††††† While some studies suggest that it has reduced incidence of sex work, public health evidence suggests that criminalisation puts sex workers’ health and well-being at risk.10,52,89

Conclusion

This commentary on the SRVAW’s report on “Prostitution and Violence” is an example of re-focusing careful attention to the evidence and research practices as part of the process of developing human rights claims. We have sought to bring this general concern about evidence to the ideologically contested areas of state regulation of sexuality, specifically sex work. Analysis and documentation have been critical in revealing how the universal application of rights has been frustrated by policies and structures that exclude certain people and groups, thereby deepening the stigma and discrimination they face. The presentation of new facts and material realities necessitates nuanced and intersectional, rather than homogenising, approaches to norm building about rights and state obligations.7

The SRVAW report and this commentary arise at a particularly fraught moment with tensions over sexuality mobilised as part of a larger anti-gender and anti-rights mobilisation. Therefore, the work of the SRVAW, and debates it generates, have the potential for greater impact. As we have argued, the report falls well short of good evidentiary practices developed across different expertise, disciplines and approaches. We emphasise that this report uses evidence (or fails to engage with evidence) in a manner which both reduces trust in UN mechanisms and impedes discussion and exploration in ways that also unfairly represent the diversity of positions and sources of disagreements. The attacks and weakening, as well as co-option, of human rights mechanisms, can occur as much through acts as from within, as well as by external actors. There are material impacts at stake and goals of justice and dignity that are served by principles, standards and rigour in human rights practice.

Human rights have always advanced in multiple and not only in fully consistent ways: we fully endorse the independence of the various experts in the UN’s human rights system. Our call here is to ensure that actors that seek to advance policy positions do so in ways that are accurate, transparent, inclusive and accountable: to not claim or advocate rights in ways that harm the rights of others, to follow the fundamental principles of human rights law as a legal practice, with accountability not just to rights-related policy norms of the past but to the future needs of rights (teleological principle).90,91

These concerns have larger resonance: while the SRVAW’s claims and conclusions in her report run counter to the positions of most UN agencies and independent experts, the report aligns with, and offers a form of human rights veneer to, the growing trend of criminalisation (or recriminalisation) of gender, sexuality, identity, (im)migrant status, health status, and forms of work, among others. Among other concerns, it must be noted that the positions laid out in the SRVAW’s report and held by some feminists and women’s rights activists, can be used in the service of retrogressive, fundamentalist and authoritarian governments and the attacks on rights that they command.92–99 However, the cross-movement mobilisation to advance sex workers’ rights can be seen as another call to action: to understand the ways that criminalisation of all forms of identity, practice or dissent affects all human rights-promoting justice movements and to take collective action. In this call to action, the role of evidence – testimonial and narrative, experiential and academically derived, must play a central, formative and constructive role. Health and rights, as a field of practice, deserve no less.
