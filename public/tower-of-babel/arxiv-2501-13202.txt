Subtree Distances, Tight Spans and Diversities
David Bryant, Katharina T. Huber, Vincent Moulton, Andreas Spillner

Abstract
We characterize when a set of distances d ⁡ ( x , y ) d(x,y) between elements in a set X X have a subtree representation , a real tree T T and a collection { S x } x ∈ X \{S_{x}\}_{x\in X} of subtrees of T T such that d ⁡ ( x , y ) d(x,y) equals the length of the shortest path in T T from a point in S x S_{x} to a point in S y S_{y} for all x , y ∈ X x,y\in X . The characterization was first established for finite X X by Hirai (2006) using a tight span construction defined for distance spaces , metric spaces without the triangle inequality. To extend Hirai’s result beyond finite X X we establish fundamental results of tight span theory for general distance spaces, including the surprising observation that the tight span of a distance space is hyperconvex. We apply the results to obtain the first characterization of when a diversity – a generalization of a metric space which assigns values to all finite subsets of X X , not just to pairs – has a tight span which is tree-like.

Introduction

A classical result of metric geometry is that a
metric space
(X,d)
can be embedded in a tree if and only if it satisfies the
four-point condition:

d(x,y)+d(w,z)\leq\max\{d(w,x)+d(y,z),d(x,z)+d(w,y)\}

(1)

for all
w,x,y,z\in X

[
10
,
28
]
.

Hirai
[
17
]
proved an appealing generalization of this result
for finite
distance spaces

(X,d)
,
where
X
is a finite, non-empty set
and
d:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is symmetric, vanishes on the diagonal but need not satisfy the
triangle inequality.
Distance spaces are also known as semi-pseudometrics
[
30
, p. 300]
and smetrics
[
29
]
.

A collection of subtrees
\{S_{x}\}_{x\in X}
of an edge-weighted tree determines a distance
d
on
X
where, for all
x,y\in X
,
d(x,y)
equals the length of the shortest path in the tree between
S_{x}
and
S_{y}
.
If an arbitrary distance
d
on
X
can be represented in this way, we say that the collection
\{S_{x}\}_{x\in X}
is a
subtree representation
of
d
.
Hirai proved that a finite distance space
(X,d)
has such a subtree representation if and only if it satisfies the
extended four-point condition
:

d(x,y)+d(z,w)\leq\max\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}d(x,y),\ d(w,z),\\
d(w,x)+d(y,z),\ d(x,z)+d(w,y),\\
(d(x,y)+d(y,z)+d(z,x))/2,\\
(d(x,y)+d(y,w)+d(w,x))/2,\\
(d(x,z)+d(z,w)+d(w,x))/2,\\
(d(y,z)+d(z,w)+d(w,y))/2\end{array}\right\}

(2)

for all
w,x,y,z\in X
. The distances between subtrees in a tree do not, in general, obey the triangle inequality, and two distinct subtrees can have distance zero (see Figure
1
for an example). Condition
2
reduces to the classical four-point condition when
(X,d)
is a metric space.

The main tool that Hirai uses to prove the characterization is the
tight span
. The tight span of a metric space, otherwise known as the injective or hyperconvex hull, is a fundamental object in metric space theory.
It was introduced by Aronszajn and Panitchpakdi
[
1
]

and Isbell
[
22
]
, and studied extensively
by Dress et al.
[
9
,
10
]
.
It has applications in areas such as combinatorial optimization
[
20
]
, tropical geometry
[
7
]
, and group theory
[
26
]
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{X}
denote the set of real-valued functions on
X
and define

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):=\sup\{|f(x)-g(x)|:x\in X\}

(3)

for
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
, noting that this supremum can be infinite.
The tight span
T_{\rho}
of a metric space
(X,\rho)
consists of the point-wise minimal elements in the set

P_{\rho}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:f(x)+f(y)\geq\rho(x,y)\mbox{ for all }x,y\in X\}.

(4)

The pair
(T_{\rho},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space. It includes, for each
x\in X
, the function

h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:y\mapsto\rho(x,y).

(5)

The map
\kappa:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{X}
taking
x
to
h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
is an isometric embedding of
(X,\rho)
into
(T_{\rho},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
, known as the
Kuratowski embedding
.

Though the theory of tight spans was developed for metric spaces,
various approaches have been presented to extend the theory to distance spaces.
In
[
17
,
18
]
Hirai develops tight span theory for finite distance spaces, an
approach that was built upon in for example
[
16
]
(see also
[
8
, Chap. 5]
).
An alternative definition for injectivity of distance spaces is studied in
[
29
]
.

In this paper, as in
[
17
]
, the
tight span
T_{d}
of a distance space
(X,d)
is defined as the pointwise-minimal elements in the set

P_{d}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:f(x)+f(y)\geq d(x,y)\mbox{ for all }x,y\in X\},

(6)

just as for metric spaces.
The absence of a triangle inequality implies that the function
h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(d)}}
need not be an element of
T_{d}
, nor of
P_{d}
. Instead,
\kappa
is defined as the map taking
x\in X
to the
subset
of
T_{d}

\kappa(x):=\{f\in T_{d}:f(x)=0\}.

(7)

Hirai proves that, when
X
is finite,

d(x,y)=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):f\in\kappa(x),\,g\in\kappa(y)\}

(8)

for all
x,y\in X
. This result is central to the characterization of subtree distances.

One of our main contributions is to extend Hirai’s results from finite
X
to general
X
. As we shall see (Section
2.4
), even basic results about the tight span of a distance space fail when
X
is not finite. There are cases when the tight span of a distance space is empty, and others where there are
f,g\in T_{d}
with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
. We show that Hirai’s extended four-point condition for subtree representations holds for general distance spaces
(X,d)
, not just finite ones (Theorem
3.22
). To prove this, we establish fundamental properties of the distance tight span as well as pathological cases when the construction breaks down. Like Hirai, we make use of the distance tight span to characterize subtree representations, though follow quite a different strategy.

Our original motivation for studying tight spans of distances came from diversity theory
[
4
]
. A
diversity

(X,\delta)
, formally defined in Section
4
, can be regarded as generalization of a metric space
(X,d)
, where
\delta
assigns values to all finite subsets of
X
, not just to pairs. The axioms for diversities parallel those for metric spaces, as do the theorems and applications. The mathematics of diversity tight spans
[
4
]
extends that for metric tight spans to a surprising extent. As we shall
see, tight spans of diversities are closely related to tight spans of distance spaces (Theorem
4.26
, Theorem
4.29
).
As an application of our main result, we shall present
a characterization of those diversities whose tight span is a tree (which we call

arboreal diversities
) using the results on subtree representations (Theorem
5.37
).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows:

Section 2
develops the theory of tight spans for general, possibly infinite, distance spaces. The starting point is tight span theory for metric spaces, so we begin with a concise summary of relevant results for metrics, many of which reappear later in a different form.

A key tool for working with the tight span of a distance space
(X,d)
is the set of metric spaces which dominate it, that is, metric spaces
(X,\rho)
for which
d(x,y)\leq\rho(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
. There are useful connections (e.g. Proposition
2.6
) between the tight span of a distance function and the (metric) tight spans of its dominating metrics. We use these relationships to prove Theorem
2.9
, which describes how a distance space embeds into its tight span. The result generalizes Theorem 2.4 in
[
17
]
to general
X
and plays a key part of the subtree representation proof.

The tight span of a metric space
(X,\rho)
is exactly the minimal hyperconvex metric space into which
(X,\rho)
can be embedded
[
12
]
. Hyperconvex metric spaces have many important characteristics and properties and it is surprising that so much structure emerges from such a simple and general tight span construction. It is even more surprising that such a structure emerges even when we start with a distance space rather than a metric space. It is proved in Theorem
2.10
, that the tight span of a distance space is hyperconvex (with caveats). Our proof draws heavily on a characterization by Descombes and Pavón
[
6
]
of hyperconvex subsets of
\ell_{\infty}
-spaces.

The hyperconvexity result is useful for the subtree representation theorem, however it also reveals deeper properties of the distance tight span. We show, for example, that the
distance
tight span is equal to the
metric
tight span of the metric space formed from the union of sets
\kappa(x)
with which we embed the distance space into the tight span. We also show (Proposition
2.15
and Theorem
2.16
) that minimal metric spaces dominating a distance space, as well as their tight spans, can be embedded in the distance tight span.

We note that many of the results in this section come with an implicit caveat. In some cases, the tight span of a distance space can be empty. In others, the tight span can be non-empty but there exist elements at infinite distance from each other. In Section
2.4
, we provide examples of these and some characterizations. There is scope for more direct necessary and sufficient conditions of when a distance space has a non-trivial tight span.

Section 3
generalizes Hirai’s subtree representation theorem for arbitrary distance spaces using real trees. Our approach differs quite significantly from that used by Hirai. For a start, we need to demonstrate that a distance space satisfying the extended four-point condition has a non-empty tight span that is a metric space, something which is not needed for finite distance spaces.

A key observation we make use of is that if a distance space satisfies the extended four-point condition (
2
) then so does its tight span and, as the tight span is a metric space, it follows that the tight span satisfies the classical four-point condition and is therefore a real tree. With that established, the main result (Theorem
3.22
) follows once we establish that the sets
\kappa(x)
are indeed closed (and geodesic) subtrees, something we prove as a consequence of hyperconvexity.

We strengthen Hirai’s result further by showing that the representation provided by the distance tight span is minimal, in the sense that it can be embedded into any other subtree representation of the same distance space (Theorem
3.23
).

Section 4
switches from metric spaces to
diversities
. A diversity is a generalization of a metric to a function defined on finite subsets, rather than just pairs. Diversities have only been introduced fairly recently
[
4
]
and yet have already exhibited a rich theory and ‘remarkable’ analogies between hyperconvexity theory for diversities and metric spaces
[
24
]
. In particular there is a well-developed theory of diversity tight spans and hyperconvexity, briefly reviewed at the beginning of Section 4.

This section develops tools for working with diversity tight spans which make direct use of our results for distance tight spans. Given a diversity on
X
we define an associated distance space on the set of finite subsets of
X
and then demonstrate that we can embed a diversity tight span into the corresponding distance tight span (Theorem
4.26
). In general, this embedding is not bijective, but there are situations where it is and we determine those in Theorem
4.29
.

In
Section 5
we describe the problem which was, for us, the catalyst for the work on distance tight spans and subtree representations. We determine necessary and sufficient conditions for when the tight span of a diversity is a tree (when considered as a metric space). The analogous questions for metric spaces was answered by
[
10
]
, and indeed led to a host of applications of related theory to computational biology
[
2
,
3
,
21
]
. The characterization we prove (Theorem
5.37
) is based on the distance space introduced in Section 4 and the main equivalency theorem for subtree representation (Theorem
3.22
).

2
Tight spans of distance spaces

2.1
Background

A pair
(X,d)
is a
distance space
if
X\neq\emptyset
and
d:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a symmetric map that vanishes on the diagonal. The space is
non-null
if
d(x,y)>0
for some
x,y\in X
. A
metric space

(X,\rho)
is a distance space which, in addition, satisfies the triangle inequality

\rho(x,z)\leq\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z)

(9)

for all
x,y,z\in X
. Note that, for convenience, we do not require separability, so that we can have
\rho(x,y)=0
even when
x\neq y
.

For
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
we write
f\preceq g
if
f(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in X
. The following theorem summarizes key properties of the tight span
(T_{\rho},d_{\infty})
of a metric space
(X,\rho)
. We continue to use the notation introduced in Section
1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
10
]

Let
(X,\rho)
be a metric space. For each
x\in X
define
\kappa(x)=h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
.

1.

For all
x\in X
,
\kappa(x)\in T_{\rho}
.

2.

If
f\in T_{\rho}
and

then
f=\kappa(x)
.

3.

f\in T_{\rho}
if and only if

f(x)=\sup\{\rho(x,y)-f(y):y\in X\}

x\in X
.

4.

For all
f,g\in T_{\rho}

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\sup\{\rho(x,y)-f(x)-g(y):x,y\in X\}.

5.

For all

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,\kappa(x))

\displaystyle d(x,y)

\displaystyle=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(\kappa(x),\kappa(y)).

6.

There exists a map

f,g\in P_{\rho}

Dress

10

defines a metric space as

if it is isometric to its tight span. The same concept has two important and equivalent formulations. Let

\rho(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})\leq r_{\alpha}+r_{\beta}

in
\Gamma

[

12
]
.

A map

is

\rho(f(y_{1}),f(y_{2}))\leq\rho_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})

\rho(f(y_{1}),f(y_{2}))=\rho_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})
for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
. We say that

is

(Y,\rho_{Y})

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
12
]
)

(i)

A metric space

is fully spread if and only if it is hyperconvex, if and only if it is injective.

(ii)

If there is an embedding from

Property (ii) justifies the terms

Hyperconvex metric spaces have several geometric properties, two of which now we recall.
We say that a metric space
(X,\rho)
is

if for all

there is an isometric embedding

[0,\rho(x,y)]

Proposition 2.3
.

(
[

A hyperconvex metric space is complete and geodesic.

2.2
Distance tight spans and embeddings

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space. Recall that, analogous to the metric case,

P_{d}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:f(x)+f(y)\geq d(x,y)\mbox{ for all }x,y\in X\},

and
T_{d}
is the set of elements of
P_{d}
which are minimal under
\preceq
.
Clearly, if a distance space happens to be a metric space then the ‘distance’ tight span is the same as its ‘metric’ tight span.

As we now show, some of the basic properties of the metric tight span carry directly over to the distance case.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space.

1.

For all
f\in P_{d}
there is
g\in T_{d}
such that
f\preceq g
.

2.

For
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
we have
f\in T_{d}
if and only if for all
x\in X
,

f(x)=\sup\{d(x,y)-f(y):y\in X\}.

(This is Lemma 5.1 in
[
8
]
)

3.

For
f,g\in T_{d}
,

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\sup\{d(x,y)-f(x)-g(y):x,y\in X\}.

Proof.

(i) Let
\mathcal{H}=\{h\in P_{d}:h\preceq f\}
. For each
x
the set
\{h(x):h\in\mathcal{H}\}
is closed and any descending chain
h_{1}\succeq h_{2}\succeq\cdots
in
\mathcal{H}
has a lower bound
\ell\in\mathcal{H}
given by the pointwise infinum

\ell(x)=\inf\{h_{i}(x):i=1,2,\ldots\}.

Let
g
be a minimal element of
\mathcal{H}
, which exists by Zorn’s lemma.

(ii) For all
x,y
we have
f(x)\geq d(x,y)-f(y)
so
f(x)\geq\sup\{d(x,y)-f(y):y\in X\}
. As
f
is minimal, for all
\epsilon>0
there is
y
such that
d(x,y)\leq f(x)+f(y)<d(x,y)+\epsilon
, giving
f(x)<d(x,y)-f(y)+\epsilon
. Taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives the result.

(iii) For all
x,y\in X
,

\displaystyle f(x)-g(x)

\displaystyle=f(x)+f(y)-f(y)-g(x)

\displaystyle\geq d(x,y)-f(y)-g(x),

so
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)\geq\sup\{d(x,y)-f(x)-g(y):x,y\in X\}
. This holds even when the supremum is infinite.

For the reverse inequality, we consider two cases. First suppose

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)<\infty.

Let
\epsilon>0
. From the definition of
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt
there is
x\in X
such that

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)-\epsilon<|f(x)-g(x)|\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g).

(10)

Without loss of generality we assume
f(x)\geq g(x)
. As
f
is minimal, there is
y
such that

0\leq f(x)+f(y)-d(x,y)<\epsilon

and so

-\epsilon<d(x,y)-f(x)-f(y)\leq 0.

(11)

Adding (
10
) and (
11
) with
f(x)\geq g(x)
gives

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)-2\epsilon<d(x,y)-f(y)-g(x)\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g).

Taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives the result.

For the second case, suppose
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Given any
K\in\mathbb{R}
there is
x
such that (switching
f
and
g
if necessary)
f(x)-g(x)>K+\epsilon
. Assume
f(x)>g(x)
. There is
y
such that
d(x,y)\leq f(x)+f(y)<d(x,y)+\epsilon
, or
d(x,y)-f(x)-f(y)>-\epsilon
. It follows that
d(x,y)-g(x)-f(y)>K
, so that

\sup\{d(x,y)-f(x)-g(y):x,y\in X\}=\infty.

∎

[f(w),f(x),f(y),f(z)]
.

Given two distance functions (or metrics)
d
and
p
on the same set
X
we write
d\preceq p
if
d(x,y)\leq p(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
. For a distance space
(X,d)
we define the set

M(d):=\{\rho\succeq d:(X,\rho)\mbox{ is a metric space}\}

of metrics dominating the distance function
d
, noting (see Example
2.17
) that this set can be empty.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space. If
M(d)
is empty then so are
P_{d}
and
T_{d}
. Otherwise

\displaystyle P_{d}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\rho\in M(d)}P_{\rho}

\displaystyle T_{d}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{\rho\in M(d)}T_{\rho}.

(12)

Proof.

If
P_{d}
is non-empty then so is
T_{d}
, by Theorem
2.4
(i), and if
f\in T_{d}
then the metric
\rho
defined by
\rho(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)
for all distinct
x,y
is an element of
M(d)
. Suppose then that
M(d)
is non-empty.

If
f\in P_{\rho}
for some
\rho\in M(d)
then
d(x,y)\leq\rho(x,y)\leq f(x)+f(y)
for all
x,y
, so
f\in P_{d}
. Conversely, if
f\in P_{d}
then the metric space
(X,\rho)
given by
\rho(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)
for all
x\neq y
satisfies
\rho\in M(d)
and hence
f\in P_{\rho}
.

For the second part we have
T_{d}\subseteq P_{d}
so
f\in T_{d}
implies
f\in P_{\rho}
for some
\rho\in M(d)
. Minimality of
f
then implies
f\in T_{\rho}
.

∎

The inclusion in (
12
) can be strict, even when the union is restricted to minimal metrics
\rho\in M(d)
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
X=\{w,x,y,z\}
and let
d
be the distance on
X
with
d(x,y)=4
and all other pairs at distance
0
.
Let
\rho
be the metric given by

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}\rho&w&x&y&z\\
\hline\cr w&0&1&3&2\\
x&1&0&4&3\\
y&3&4&0&1\\
z&2&3&1&0\end{array}.

Then
\rho
is minimal in
M(d)
. The function

h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
given by
h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}(u)=\rho(w,u)
for all
u\in X
is in
T_{\rho}
.

Define
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
h(w)=h(z)=0
,
h(x)=1
and
h(y)=3
. Then
h\in P_{d}
,
h\preceq h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
and yet
h\neq h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
. Hence
h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
is not minimal in
P_{d}
and

h_{w}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}\in\left(\bigcup_{\rho\in M(d)}T_{\rho}\right)\setminus T_{d}.

∎

We make use of the following technical lemma about
M(d)
.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
M(d)
is non-empty and
x,y\in X
then there is
\rho\in M(d)
such that
\rho(x,y)=d(x,y)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p\in M(d)
and
p(x,y)>d(x,y)
. Define
\rho
by

\displaystyle\rho(x,y)

\displaystyle=d(x,y)

\displaystyle\rho(x,u)

\displaystyle=p(x,u)+p(x,y)-d(x,y)\geq p(x,u)

\displaystyle\rho(y,u)

\displaystyle=p(x,u)+p(x,y)\geq p(y,u)

\displaystyle\rho(u,v)

\displaystyle=p(u,v)

for all
u,v\in X\setminus\{x,y\}
, see Figure
3
. Then
\rho\in M(d)
and
\rho(x,y)=d(x,y)
.
∎

Figure 3:
Editing a metric space
(X,p)
to give the metric space
(X,\rho)
: we remove
y
and attach it to
x
with a line segment of length
p(x,y)
and then move
x
along that segment until it is distance
d(x,y)
from
y
. The resulting metric space satisfies
\rho(x,y)=d(x,y)
and
\rho(u,v)\geq p(u,v)
for all other pairs
u,v\in X
.

For a distance space
(X,d)
and
x\in X
, Hirai
[
17
]
defines the set

\kappa(x):=\{f\in T_{d}:f(x)=0\}.

By Proposition
2.6
, for each
f\in\kappa(x)
there is a metric
\rho\in M(d)
such that
f\in T_{\rho}
, in which case
f=h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
by Theorem
2.1
(ii). As
g\in T_{d}
and
g\preceq f
implies
g(x)=0
we have that
\kappa(x)
consists of all minimal elements in

\{h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}:\rho\in M(d)\}
. As a consequence, if
(X,d)
is a metric space,
\kappa(x)
contains the single function
h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(d)}}
.

The following theorem generalizes Theorem
2.1
(v) from metric spaces to distance spaces and is central to our characterization of subtree distance spaces later in the article.

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose that
M(d)
is non-empty. For all
x,y\in X
and
f\in T_{d}
we have

1.

f(x)=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):g\in\kappa(x)\}

and

2.

d(x,y)=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):f\in\kappa(x),\,g\in\kappa(y)\}.

Proof.

(i) For all
g\in\kappa(x)
we have
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)\geq f(x)-g(x)=f(x)
, so

f(x)\leq\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):g\in\kappa(x)\}.

By Proposition
2.6
there is
\rho\in M(d)
such that
f\in T_{\rho}
. Then
h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}(x)=0
and, by Theorem
2.1
(v) we have
f(x)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}})
. As
h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}\in P_{d}
there is
g\in T_{d}
such that
g\preceq h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
. Then
g\in\kappa(x)
and
g(y)\leq\rho(x,y)
for all
y
.

Suppose that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)>f(x)
, so there is
y\in X
such that
|g(y)-f(y)|>f(x)
. Since

g(y)-f(y)\leq h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}(y)-f(y)\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}})=f(x)

it follows that

f(y)-g(y)>f(x).

Let
\epsilon=f(y)-g(y)-f(x)>0
. Since
f\in T_{d}
there is
z
such that
f(y)+f(z)<d(y,z)+\epsilon
and

\displaystyle g(y)+g(z)

\displaystyle\leq g(y)+\rho(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq g(y)+f(x)+f(z)

\displaystyle=g(y)+f(x)+f(z)+(f(y)-g(y)-f(x))-\epsilon

\displaystyle=f(y)+f(z)-\epsilon

\displaystyle<d(y,z),

contradicting
g\in T_{d}
.

(ii) Let
x,y\in X
. First, note that if

f\in\kappa(x)
and
g\in\kappa(y)
, then
f(x)=0
,
g(y)=0
and

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)\geq g(x)-f(x)\geq d(x,y)-g(y)=d(x,y).

It remains to show that there exist
f\in\kappa(x)
and

g\in\kappa(y)
with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)\leq d(x,y)
.

By Lemma
2.8
there exists some
\rho\in M(d)
such that
\rho(x,y)=d(x,y)
. Let
h=h_{y}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
, so
h\in T_{\rho}\subseteq P_{d}
. By Theorem
2.4
(i), there is some
f\in T_{d}
with
f\preceq h
. Hence,
f(y)=h(y)=0
and
f(x)\leq h(x)=\rho(x,y)=d(x,y)
. Thus
f\in\kappa(y)
, and it follows from (i) that there is some
g\in\kappa(x)
with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=f(x)\leq d(x,y)
, as required.

∎

2.3
Distance tight spans are hyperconvex

When
(X,d)
is a metric space, the tight span
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex, in fact it functions as a minimal
hyperconvex hull
for
(X,d)
. When
(X,d)
is a distance space, its tight span
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is not necessarily a metric space: sometimes
T_{d}
is empty, and other times we can have
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
for some
f,g\in T_{d}
. Our next result shows that if
(X,d)
is a distance space and the tight span
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
of
(X,d)
is a metric space then
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex. This is surprising: a distance space is such a general concept, while hyperconvex metric spaces are full of structure. Our proof is based on a characterization of hyperconvex subspaces of
\ell_{\infty}(X)=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty\}
, due to
[
6
]
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space. If
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space then it is hyperconvex.

Proof.

Fix
h\in T_{d}
and define
T_{d}\!-\!h=\{f-h:f\in T_{d}\}
. Then
(T_{d}\!-\!h,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
\|f-h\|_{\infty}=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h)<\infty
for all
f\in T_{d}
, so
(T_{d}\!-\!h)\subseteq\ell_{\infty}(X)
. By Theorem
2.4
(ii) we have
f\in T_{d}
if and only if for all
i\in X

f(i)=\sup\{d(i,j)-f(j):j\in X\},

if and only if for all
i\in X

f(i)=\max\left[\sup\left\{d(i,j)-f(j):j\in X\setminus\{i\}\right\},0\right].

Hence
g\in T_{d}\!-\!h
if and only if for all
i\in X
,

g(i)=\max\left[\sup\left\{d(i,j)-g(j)-h(j):j\in X\setminus\{i\}\right\},0\right]-h(i).

For each
i\in X
, let
\widehat{\pi}_{i}
denote the restriction map from
\mathbb{R}^{X}
to
\mathbb{R}^{X\setminus\{i\}}
given by

\widehat{\pi}_{i}(f)(j)=f(j)\mbox{ for all $j\in X\setminus\{i\}$ }.

We define
r_{i}:\ell_{\infty}(X\setminus\{i\})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

r_{i}(\pi)=\max\left[\sup\left\{d(i,j)-\pi(j)-h(j):j\in X\setminus\{i\}\right\},0\right]

and define
\underline{r}_{i}=\overline{r}_{i}=r_{i}
for all
i
.
As
h\in P_{d}
we have for each
g\in\ell_{\infty}(X)
that

d(i,j)-g(j)-h(j)\leq(h(i)+h(j))-g(j)-h(j)\leq h(i)-g(j)\leq h(i)+\|g\|_{\infty},

so
r_{i}(\widehat{\pi}_{i}(g))<\infty
.

Let
I=I_{-}=I_{+}=X
. In the notation of Theorem 1.2 of
[
6
]
we then have
Q_{-}=Q_{+}=Q
where

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\{g\in\ell_{\infty}(X):r_{i}(\widehat{\pi}_{i}(g))=g(i)\mbox{ for all }i\in X\}

\displaystyle=\{g\in\ell_{\infty}(X):g(i)=\max\left[\sup\left\{d(i,j)-g(j)-h(j):j\in X\setminus\{i\}\right\},0\right]-h(i)\mbox{ for all }i\in X\}

\displaystyle=T_{d}-h.

To apply Theorem 1.2 of
[
6
]
and show that
(T_{d}-h,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and hence
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
are hyperconvex it remains to show that the maps
r_{i}
are non-expansive.

Let
g,g^{\prime}\in\ell_{\infty}(X\setminus\{i\})
and define, for all
j\in X\setminus\{i\}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=\max\{0,d(i,j)-g(j)-h(j)\}

\displaystyle\beta_{j}

\displaystyle=\max\{0,d(i,j)-g^{\prime}(j)-h(j)\}.

Then, since

(d(i,j)-g(j)-h(j))-(d(i,j)-g^{\prime}(j)-h(j))=g^{\prime}(j)-g(j),

we have
|\alpha_{j}-\beta_{j}|\leq\|g-g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
and, therefore,

\displaystyle|r_{i}(g)-r_{i}(g^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|\sup\{\alpha_{j}:j\in X\setminus\{i\}\}-\sup\{\beta_{j}:j\in X\setminus\{i\}\}|

\displaystyle\leq\|g-g^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

as required.
∎

We next establish several properties of the subsets
\kappa(z)
of
T_{d}
which we will make use of later.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space such that
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space. For each
z\in X
the subspace
(\kappa(z),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is non-empty, hyperconvex, complete and geodesic.

Proof.

If
T_{d}\neq\emptyset
then
M(d)\neq\emptyset
by Proposition
2.6
. For any
\rho\in M(d)
there
exists
h\in T_{d}
such that
h\preceq h_{z}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}
, so that
h\in\kappa(z)\neq\emptyset
.

To show that
(\kappa(z),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex we slightly modify the non-expansive maps
used in the proof of Theorem
2.10
by choosing
h\in\kappa(z)
and
letting, for all
i\in X
and all
\pi\in\ell_{\infty}(X\setminus\{i\})
,

r_{i}(\pi):=\begin{cases}\max\left[\sup\Big\{d(i,j)-\pi(j)-h(j):j\in X\setminus\{i\}\Big\},0\right]&\mbox{ if $i\neq z$;}\\
h(z)&\mbox{ if $i=z$.}\end{cases}

That
(\kappa(z),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is geodesic and complete now follows from Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Define
K:=\bigcup_{x\in X}\kappa(x)
so that, by Proposition
2.6
,
K
equals the set of minimal elements of

\{h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}:x\in X,\,\rho\in M(d)\}

and
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a subspace of the metric space
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. We have shown that if
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space then it is hyperconvex. We now show that, in this case,
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric with the
metric
tight span of the metric space

(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. Hence, the tight span of a distance space is either not a metric space, or it equals a metric tight span.

For the following we let
T_{K}
denote the minimal elements in

P_{K}:=\{F\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:F(k_{1})+F(k_{2})\geq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(k_{1},k_{2})\mbox{ for all $k_{1},k_{2}\in K$}\},

so that
(T_{K},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is the metric tight span of
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
, noting the slight abuse of terminology with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a distance space. If the tight span
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)

of
(X,d)
is a metric space then it is isometric to the tight span

(T_{K},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
of the metric space
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.

Proof.

Define the map
\psi:T_{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
by letting
\psi(f)
be the function
F
such that
F(k)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,k)
for all
k\in K
.
We note that for all
g\in K
we have

\sup\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h)-F(h):h\in K\}=\sup\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h)-d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h):h\in K\}=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=F(g)

so, by Theorem
2.1
(iii),
F\in T_{K}
.

Suppose
f,g\in T_{d}
,
F=\psi(f)
and
G=\psi(g)
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(F,G)

\displaystyle=\sup\{|d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,k)-d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,k)|:k\in K\}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g).

For all
\epsilon>0
there is (without loss of generality)
x\in X
such that

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)-\epsilon/2<f(x)-g(x)\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g).

By Theorem
2.9
there is
h\in\kappa(x)
such that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h)<g(x)+\epsilon/2
so that

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)\leq f(x)-g(x)+\epsilon/2\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h)-d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h)+\epsilon\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(F,G)+\epsilon.

Taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
we have

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(F,G)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)

so that
\psi
is an isometry and
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to a subspace of
(T_{K},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.

As
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a subspace of
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex, Theorem
2.2
(ii) implies that there is an isometric embedding from the tight span
(T_{K},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
of
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
in
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. As
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to
(\psi(T_{d}),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
\psi(T_{d})\subseteq T_{K}
we conclude that
\psi(T_{d})=T_{K}
and
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to
(T_{K},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.
∎

As an immediate corollary we have

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space such that
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space. Then
\psi:T_{d}\rightarrow T_{K}
given by

\psi(f)(k)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,k)

is an isometry, with inverse

\psi^{-1}(F)(x)=\inf_{k\in\kappa(x)}F(k).

The next result is the distance space generalization of Theorem
2.1
(vi). It can almost be proved by taking the proof of
(1.9) in
[
10
]
and replacing ‘metric’ by ‘distance’, as observed by
[
19
]
, Lemma 2.2. The following paraphrases and adds details to the version of the proof in
[
10
]
which avoids Zorn’s Lemma.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space such that
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space. There is
\phi:P_{d}\rightarrow T_{d}
such that for all
f,g\in P_{d}
we have
\phi(f)\preceq f
and
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(\phi(f),\phi(g))\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)
.

Proof.

For all
f\in P_{d}
define the map
f_{\#}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by putting

f_{\#}(x)=\sup\{d(x,y)-f(y):y\in Y\}

(13)

for all
x\in X
. As
f(x)+f(y)\geq d(x,y)
for all
f\in P_{d}
and
x,y\in X
we have
f_{\#}(x)\leq f(x)
for all
x\in X
.

Consider
f\in P_{d}
. Put
f^{(0)}=f
and, for all
k>0
,

f^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}+f^{(k-1)}_{\#})
. Then, assuming that
f^{(k-1)}\in P_{d}
, we have, for all
x,y\in X
,

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(x)+f^{(k)}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(x)+f^{(k-1)}_{\#}(x))+\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(y)+f^{(k-1)}_{\#}(y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(x)+f^{(k-1)}_{\#}(y))+\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(y)+f^{(k-1)}_{\#}(x))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(x)+d(x,y)-f^{(k-1)}(x))+\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k-1)}(y)+d(x,y)-f^{(k-1)}(y))

\displaystyle=d(x,y),

where the inequality in the fourth line holds in view of (
13
). Hence
f^{(k)}\in P_{d}
and

f_{\#}^{(k)}\preceq f_{\#}^{(k+1)}\preceq f^{(k+1)}\preceq f^{(k)}

(14)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For all
x\in X
,

\displaystyle|f^{(k+1)}(x)-f_{\#}^{(k+1)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|f^{(k+1)}(x)-f_{\#}^{(k)}(x)|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k)}(x)+f^{(k)}_{\#}(x))-f_{\#}^{(k)}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|f^{(k)}(x)-f_{\#}^{(k)}(x)|,

implying that the sequence
\{f^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges pointwise to some
f^{*}\in P_{d}
. Moreover, we have
f^{*}_{\#}=f^{*}
and, therefore, by Theorem
2.4
,
f^{*}\in T_{d}
.

Define the map
\phi:P_{d}\rightarrow T_{d}
as
\phi(f)=f^{*}
. Consider
f,g\in P_{d}
. If
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
we clearly have

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(\phi(f),\phi(g))<\infty=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g).

So, assume
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)<\infty
. Then, for all
x\in X
, we have

\displaystyle f_{\#}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{d(x,y)-f(y):y\in X\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{d(x,y)-g(y)+g(y)-f(y):y\in X\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{d(x,y)-g(y):y\in X\}+\sup\{g(y)-f(y):y\in X\}

\displaystyle=g_{\#}(x)+\sup\{g(y)-f(y):y\in X\},

implying
\sup\{|f_{\#}(x)-g_{\#}(x)|:x\in X\}\leq\sup\{|g(x)-f(x)|:x\in X\}
. Hence,

\displaystyle d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f^{(k+1)},g^{(k+1)})

\displaystyle=\sup\{\frac{1}{2}(f^{(k)}(x)+f_{\#}^{(k)}(x))-\frac{1}{2}(g^{(k)}(x)+g_{\#}^{(k)}(x)):x\in X\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f^{(k)},g^{(k)})+\frac{1}{2}d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{\#}^{(k)},g_{\#}^{(k)})\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f^{(k)},g^{(k)}).

Thus,
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(\phi(f),\phi(g))=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f^{*},g^{*})\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)
,
as required.
∎

The following proposition can be viewed as an extension of Proposition 2.3 in
[
19
]
from finite to infinite sets.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space such that
(T_{d},d_{\infty})
is a metric space and let
\rho\in M(d)
be minimal with respect to
\preceq
. Then there exists a map
\psi:X\rightarrow T_{d}
such that
\psi(x)\in\kappa(x)
and
d_{\infty}(\psi(x),\psi(y))=\rho(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in M(d)
be minimal with respect to
\preceq
. For each
x\in X
consider the Kuratowski map
h^{(\rho)}_{x}\in T_{\rho}\subseteq P_{d}
. Using the map
\phi
from Lemma
2.14
, we obtain a map
\psi:X\rightarrow T_{d}

by putting
\psi(x)=\phi(h^{(\rho)}_{x})
. Then we have
\psi(x)\in\kappa(x)
in view of
h^{(\rho)}_{x}(x)=0
and
\phi(h^{(\rho)}_{x})\preceq h^{(\rho)}_{x}
. Thus, we have

d(x,y)\leq d_{\infty}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\leq d_{\infty}(h^{(\rho)}_{x},h^{(\rho)}_{y})=\rho(x,y),

where the first inequality holds in view of Theorem
2.9
.
Hence, since
\rho
is minimal in
M(d)
, we must have
d_{\infty}(\psi(x),\psi(y))=\rho(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
.
∎

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
\rho
be a minimal element of
M(d)
. Then there is an isometric embedding from
(T_{\rho},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
into
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.15
there is an isometric embedding
\psi
from
(X,\rho)
into
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and, since
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex, there is an isometric embedding
\phi
from
(T_{\rho},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
into
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
such that
\phi(h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}})=\psi(h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}})
for all
x\in X
.
∎

Example
2.7
shows that the isometric embedding given by Theorem
2.16
cannot be strengthened to a statement of inclusion such as
T_{\rho}\subseteq T_{d}
.

2.4
When does a distance space have a tight span?

By Proposition
2.6
, the distance tight span
T_{d}
is non-empty if and only if the set
M(d)
of metrics dominating
d
is non-empty.
Thus, we see immediately that
M(d)
is non-empty whenever
X
is finite, and we will see below that
M(d)
is non-empty if
X
is countable. We show here that
M(d)
, and hence the tight span
T_{d}
, can be empty when
X
is uncountable.

Example 2.17
.

Let
X
be the real interval
[0,1]
and define the distance function
d
on
X
by

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|x-y|}&\mbox{ if $x\neq y$, }\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We claim that
M(d)
is empty. Suppose not, and that
\rho\in M(d)
. As
X
is uncountable but can be covered by a countable number of closed balls

X\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(0,n)

there is
m
such that
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(0,m)
is infinite. The set
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(0,m)
contains a limit point
x
with respect to the standard metric on
[0,1]
. Let
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots
be a sequence in
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(0,m)\setminus\{x\}
which converges to
x
with the standard metric. As

|x_{n}-x|\rightarrow 0

we have

d(x_{n},x)=\frac{1}{|x_{n}-x|}\rightarrow\infty,

contradicting the fact that for all
n
,

d(x,x_{n})\leq\rho(x,x_{n})\leq\rho(0,x)+\rho(0,x_{n})\leq 2m.

∎

The argument used in Example
2.17
suggests a general characterization for when this holds. We say that a subset
B
of a distance space
(X,d)
is
bounded
if
\sup\{d(x,y):x,y\in B\}<\infty.

Proposition 2.18
.

A distance space
(X,d)
is dominated by a metric if and only if there is a countable chain
X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\cdots
of bounded subsets which cover
X
.

Proof.

If there is
\rho\in M(d)
then for any
x\in X
the sets
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(x,n)
,
n=1,2,3,\ldots
provide such a chain.

Conversely, let
X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\cdots
be a chain of subsets which cover
X
so that the ‘diameter’

\mathrm{diam}(X_{i}):=\sup\{d(x,y):x,y\in X_{i}\}

is finite for all
i
. For each
x\in X
there is a minimal index
i
such that
x\in X_{i}
; we denote this index by
n(x)
.

We define the symmetric map
\rho:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\rho(x,x)=0
for all
x
and

\rho(x,y)=\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(x)})+\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(y)})+1

for distinct
x,y\in X
. Both
\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(x)})
and
\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(y)})
are non-negative, so
\rho
satisfies the triangle inequality. For all
x,y\in X
we have
x,y\in X_{\max\{n(x),n(y)\}}
so

d(x,y)\leq\mathrm{diam}(X_{\max\{n(x),n(y)\}})\leq\max\{\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(x)}),\mathrm{diam}(X_{n(y)})\}<\rho(x,y)

so that
\rho\in M(d)
.
∎

As a corollary we note that
M(d)
is non-empty whenever
X
is countable or when
(X,d)
is bounded.

The next example illustrates that even when
T_{d}
is non-empty and
X
is countable, the pair
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
can fail to be a metric space as there can be
f,g\in T_{d}
with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
.

Example 2.19
.

Define

X:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\{y_{k},z_{k}\}

and let
d
be the distance function on
X
for which
d(y_{k},z_{k})=k
for all
k=1,2,\ldots
and with all other distances
0
. Let
(\ell_{\infty})
denote the Banach space of infinite sequences with bounded infinity norm. There is a set-valued embedding
\psi
of
(X,d)
into
(\ell_{\infty},d_{\infty})
given by

\displaystyle\psi(y_{k})

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\ell_{\infty}:x_{k}=0\}

\displaystyle\psi(z_{k})

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\ell_{\infty}:x_{k}=k\}

\displaystyle k=1,2,\ldots,.

Define
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
f(y_{k})=0
and
f(z_{k})=k
for all
k=1,2,\ldots
. Let
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the complementary function given by
g(y_{k})=k
and
g(z_{k})=0
for all
k=1,2,\ldots
. Then
f,g\in T_{d}
and

d_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup\{|f(x)-g(x)|\,:\,x\in X\}=\infty.

∎

We have not yet found a satisfying characterization for when a distance space
(X,d)
has a tight span that is a metric space. One consequence of Theorem
2.12
is that if
K
equals the set of minimal elements of

\{h_{x}^{\hskip-2.84526pt{}^{(\rho)}}:x\in X,\,\rho\in M(d)\},

then
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space exactly when
(K,d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is. Hence
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
will be a metric space whenever
X
is finite, but not necessarily when
X
is merely countable, as we have seen in Example
2.19
.

2.5
Hyperconvexity of smetrics

As mentioned in the introduction, Smyth and Tsaur
[
29
]
defined a form of hyperconvexity for distance spaces (which they call
smetrics
). They call a distance space
hyperconvex
if for any indexed collection of closed balls
\{\mathcal{B}_{d}(x_{\gamma},r_{\gamma})\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
satisfying
d(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})\leq r_{\alpha}+r_{\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
it follows that
\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathcal{B}_{d}(x_{\gamma},r_{\gamma})\neq\emptyset
.

We find this definition to be too general for our purposes. Indeed, if
(X,d)
is
any
distance space then appending a point at distance zero to all others produces a hyperconvex distance space, according to this definition.

3
Characterization of subtree distances

A metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
is a
real tree
if, for all
a,b\in Z
,

(T1)

there exists a unique geodesic path from
a
to
b
, and

(T2)

d_{Z}(a,\pi(t))+d_{Z}(\pi(t),b)=d_{Z}(a,b)
for all paths
\pi:[0,d_{Z}(a,b)]\rightarrow Z
from
a
to
b
and all
0\leq t\leq d_{Z}(a,b)
.

This definition of real trees follows
[
10
]
, where it is shown that a metric space
(X,d)
satisfies (
1
) if and only if
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a real tree
[
10
, Thm. 8]
. Note that (T2) can be replaced by various equivalent conditions (see e.g.
[
23
]
). Moreover, in view of the fact that a real tree is hyperconvex if and only if it is complete (see e.g.
[
13
]
), we will restrict in the following to complete real trees.

A
subtree
of a complete real tree
(Z,d_{Z})
is a complete, geodesic,
non-empty subset of
Z
. A
subtree representation

of a distance space
(X,d)
is a complete real tree
(Z,d_{Z})
together with a family
\{Z_{x}\}_{x\in X}
of subtrees of
(Z,d_{Z})
such that

d(x,y)=\inf\{d_{Z}(a,b)\,:\,a\in Z_{x},\ b\in Z_{y}\}

(15)

holds for all
x,y\in X
.

In this section, we prove our main result (Theorem
3.22
), that Hirai’s characterization of distance spaces
(X,d)
that have a subtree representation holds for general
X
. The proof relies on the following lemmas which establish key properties of
T_{d}
when the distance space
(X,d)
satisfies the extended four-point property (
2
).

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space that satisfies the extended four-point condition (
2
). Then
T_{d}
is non-empty and
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space.

Proof.

We first show that
T_{d}\neq\emptyset
. If
d(u,v)=0
for all
u,v\in X
, we have
T_{d}=\{f\}\neq\emptyset
with
f(x)=0
for all
x\in X
. So, assume that there exist
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)>0
. For
n=1,2,\dots
, put
X_{n}=\{z\in X:\max\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\}\leq\frac{n}{2}\}
. Then
X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\dots
is a chain of subsets of
X
such that
X=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
. To apply Proposition
2.18
we need to show that each set
X_{n}
is bounded in
(X,d)
.

Consider
z,w\in X_{n}
. All of
d(x,z),d(w,x),d(w,y),d(y,z)
are bounded above by
n/2
. From (
2
) we have that
d(x,y)+d(w,z)
is bounded above by one of:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}d(x,y),&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq 0\\
d(w,x)+d(y,z),&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq n/2+n/2-d(x,y)\\
d(x,z)+d(w,y),&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq n/2+n/2-d(x,y)\\
(d(x,y)+d(y,z)+d(z,x))/2,&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq n/4+n/4-d(x,y)/2\\
(d(x,y)+d(y,w)+d(w,x))/2&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq n/4+n/4-d(x,y)/2\\
(d(x,z)+d(z,w)+d(w,x))/2&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq 2(n/4+n/4)-2d(x,y)\\
(d(y,z)+d(z,w)+d(w,y))/2&\mbox{ and so }&d(w,z)\leq 2(n/4+n/4)-2d(x,y).\end{array}

We note that we cannot have
d(x,y)+d(w,z)\leq d(w,z)
since
d(x,y)>0
. In all of these cases,
d(z,w)
, and hence
\mathrm{diam}(X_{n})
, is bounded. By Proposition
2.18
,
T_{d}\neq\emptyset
, as required.

Next we show that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)<\infty
for all
f,g\in T_{d}
. Let
f,g\in T_{d}
. Assume for contradiction that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
. Without loss of generality we assume that there exists
u\in X
with
f(u)>0
. By Theorem
2.4
(ii), there exists
v\in X
such that

f(u)+f(v)-\epsilon=d(u,v)

for some constant
\epsilon
with
f(u)>\epsilon\geq 0
. By the assumption that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)=\infty
there exists
w\in X
such that
f(w)=g(w)+C
for some constant
C>\max\{g(u)+3\epsilon,g(v)+3\epsilon\}
. Moreover, again by Theorem
2.4
(ii), there exists some
z\in X
such that

f(w)+f(z)-\epsilon^{\prime}=d(w,z)

for some constant
\epsilon^{\prime}
with
\min\{\epsilon,f(w)\}>\epsilon^{\prime}\geq 0
.

We now consider the cases in (
2
). In view of
f(u)>0
,
f(w)>0
and the choice of
\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
we must have
d(u,v)>0
and
d(w,z)>0
. Hence, we cannot have
d(u,v)+d(w,z)\leq\max\{d(u,v),d(w,z)\}
. Also, we cannot have

d(u,v)+d(w,z)\leq(d(u,v)+d(v,z)+d(u,z))/2

since this would imply

f(u)+f(v)-\epsilon+2f(w)+2f(z)-2\epsilon^{\prime}\leq f(v)+f(u)+2f(z)

which simplifies to
2f(w)-\epsilon-2\epsilon^{\prime}\leq 0
, contradicting the choice of
C
,
\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
. Hence, up to symmetry, there remain only three cases in (
2
) to consider:

•

d(u,v)+d(w,z)\leq d(u,w)+d(v,z)
. Then we have

f(u)+f(v)-\epsilon+f(w)+f(z)-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(u,w)+f(v)+f(z),

implying
f(w)-\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(u,w)
.

•

d(u,v)+d(w,z)\leq(d(u,v)+d(v,w)+d(u,w))/2
. Then we have

f(u)+f(v)-\epsilon+2f(w)+2f(z)-2\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(v,w)+f(u)+f(w),

implying
f(w)-\epsilon-2\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(v,w)
.

•

d(u,v)+d(w,z)\leq(d(u,w)+d(w,z)+d(u,z))/2
. Then we have

2f(u)+2f(v)-2\epsilon+f(w)+f(z)-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(u,w)+f(u)+f(z),

implying
f(w)-2\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq d(u,w)
.

As the result of this case analysis we obtain

f(w)-3\epsilon\leq\max\{d(u,w),d(v,w)\}.

But this implies, in view of the choice of
C
,

\displaystyle f(w)-3\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\max\{g(u)+g(w),g(v)+g(w)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{g(u)+f(w)-C,g(v)+f(w)-C\}<f(w)-3\epsilon,

a contradiction. Hence,
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)<\infty
, as required.
∎

We now show that
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
satisfies the classical four-point condition.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space that satisfies (
2
). Then
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a metric space which satisfies (
1
), the classical four-point condition.

Proof.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in T_{d}
.

\displaystyle d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup\{d(x,y)-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y):x,y\in X\}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\sup\{d(w,z)-g_{1}(w)-g_{2}(z):w,z\in X\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{d(x,y)+d(w,z)-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)-g_{1}(w)-g_{2}(z):w,x,y,z\in X\},

where the equality in the third line holds by Theorem
2.4
(iii). In the following we will make repeated use of this theorem, the fact that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt
satisfies the triangle inequality and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}(a+b)\leq\max\{a,b\}
holds for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Fix
w,x,y,z\in X
and let
\Delta=d(x,y)+d(w,z)-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)-g_{1}(w)-g_{2}(z)
.
Assume that
(X,d)
satisfies (
2
). By symmetry, it suffices to consider the following cases:

•

d(x,y)+d(z,w)\leq d(x,y)
, in which case

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq d(x,y)-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)-g_{1}(w)-g_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}(d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{1})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},f_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{2},f_{2})).

•

d(x,y)+d(z,w)\leq d(w,x)+d(y,z)
, in which case

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq d(w,x)-f_{1}(x)-g_{1}(w)+d(y,z)-f_{2}(y)-g_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{1})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{2}).

•

d(x,y)+d(z,w)\leq(d(x,y)+d(y,z)+d(z,x))/2
, in which case

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq(d(x,y)+d(y,z)+d(z,x))/2-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)-g_{1}(w)-g_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{2}))/2.

Hence, for all
\epsilon>0
, there are
w,x,y,z
such that

\displaystyle d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\max\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{1}),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{1})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{2})\}+\epsilon.

Taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
we obtain

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},g_{2})\leq\max\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{2})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{1}),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},g_{1})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{2},g_{2})\},

as required.
∎

With the help of Lemma
3.21
we now establish our main result in this section.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
(X,d)
be a distance space. The following are equivalent:

(i)

(X,d)
satisfies the extended four-point condition (
2
);

(ii)

(X,d)
has a subtree representation;

(iii)

(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a complete real tree;

(iv)

(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
together with the family
\{\kappa(x)\}_{x\in X}

is a subtree representation of
(X,d)
.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(iii)
: Assume
(X,d)
satisfies (
2
). Then, by Theorem
2.10
and Lemma
3.21
,
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a hyperconvex metric space that satisfies (
1
). Thus, in view
[
10
, Thm. 8]
,
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a real tree that is hyperconvex and, therefore, complete.

(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
: Assume that
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a complete real tree. By Proposition
2.11
, for all
x\in X
, the subset
\kappa(x)
of
T_{d}
is non-empty, complete and geodesic and, thus, a subtree of
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. Further, in view of Theorem
2.9
, we have, for all
x,y\in X
, that

d(x,y)=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g):f(x)\in\kappa(x),\,g(y)\in\kappa(y)\}.

Hence,
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
together with the family
\{\kappa(x)\}_{x\in X}

of subtrees of
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a subtree representation of
(X,d)
.

(iv)\Rightarrow(ii)
: This is clear.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
: Assume that
(X,d)
has a subtree representation.
Then the restriction of
(X,d)
to any finite subspace also has a subtree representation
and so
(X,d)
satisfies (
2
) by
[
17
, Thm. 1.2]
.
∎

Intuitively, Theorem
3.22
states that the tight
span of a distance space that satisfies the extended four-point
condition (
2
) is 1-dimensional. For metric spaces,
this intuition is made more precise in
[
10
, Sec. 5]
by
introducing the concept of the combinatorial dimension of a metric
space and studying metric spaces of bounded combinatorial dimension.
It could be interesting to explore in future work how to extend this
concept to distance spaces.

We close this section establishing that, in the sense made precise in the following theorem,
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
together with the family
\{\kappa(x)\}_{x\in X}
is the unique minimal subtree representation of a distance space
(X,d)
that satisfies (
2
). This can be regarded as a generalization of
the uniqueness of subtree representations in the finite case (cf.
[
27
]
).

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
(X,d)
be a (non-null) distance space that satisfies the extended four-point condition (
2
). Then, for any subtree representation of
(X,d)
consisting of a complete real tree
(Z,d_{Z})
together with the family
\{Z_{x}\}_{x\in X}
, there exists an isometric embedding
\phi:T_{d}\rightarrow Z
of
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
into
(Z,d_{Z})
such that
\phi(\kappa(x))\subseteq Z_{x}
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.22
, since
(X,d)
satisfies (
2
), the tight span
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
together with the family
\{\kappa(x)\}_{x\in X}
make up a subtree representation of
(X,d)
. For all
(x,y)\in X\times X
with
d(x,y)>0
and all
t\in[0,d(x,y)]
, we define
f_{(x,y,t)}
as the unique map
f\in T_{d}
with
f(x)=t
and
f(y)=d(x,y)-t
. Intuitively,
f_{(x,y,t)}
is the unique point on the geodesic path in
T_{d}
between
\kappa(x)
and
\kappa(y)
with distance
t
from
\kappa(x)
and distance
d(x,y)-t
from
\kappa(y)
. Let
F_{d}
denote the closure in
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
of the set

F_{d}^{\circ}=\{f_{(x,y,t)}:(x,y)\in X\times X,\ d(x,y)>0,\ t\in[0,d(x,y)]\}.

Hence
F_{d}\subseteq T_{d}
.

We show that
T_{d}\subseteq F_{d}
.
Let
f\in T_{d}
. Since
(X,d)
is non-null there must exist some
x\in X
with
f(x)>0
. Let
f(x)>\epsilon>0
. By Theorem
2.4
(ii) there is
y\in X
such that
f(x)<d(x,y)-f(y)+\epsilon
. Let
t=f(x)-\epsilon
and consider
g=f_{(x,y,t)}\in F_{d}^{\circ}
.

There is
h_{x}\in\kappa(x)
and
h_{y}\in\kappa(y)
such that
g(x)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{x})=t
,
g(y)=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{y})=d(x,y)-t
and
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(h_{x},h_{y})=d(x,y)
.
Since
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
together with the family
\{\kappa(z)\}_{z\in X}
form a subtree representation of
(X,d)
, we have
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{x})\leq f(x)+\epsilon
and
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{y})\leq f(y)+\epsilon
.

As
(T_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
satisfies the (classical) four point condition (
1
) we have

\displaystyle d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{x})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{y}),d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h_{y})+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{x})\}-d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(h_{x},h_{y})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{f(x)+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{y})+\epsilon,f(y)+d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h_{x})+\epsilon\}-d(x,y)

\displaystyle=\max\{f(x)+d(x,y)-t+\epsilon,f(y)+t+\epsilon\}-d(x,y)

\displaystyle=\max\{2\epsilon,f(y)+f(x)-d(x,y)\}\leq\max\{2\epsilon,\epsilon\}\leq 2\epsilon.

Hence
F_{d}^{\circ}
is dense in
T_{d}
.

In an analogous way we define, for all
(x,y)\in X\times X
with
d(x,y)>0
and all
t\in[0,d(x,y)]
, the unique point
p_{(x,y,t)}
in
Z
that lies on the geodesic path between
Z_{x}
and
Z_{y}
such that

d(x,y)=\inf\{d_{Z}(p_{(x,y,t)},q):q\in Z_{x}\}+\inf\{d_{Z}(p_{(x,y,t)},q):q\in Z_{y}\}.

Let
G_{d}
denote the completion in
(Z,d_{Z})
of the set

G_{d}^{\circ}=\{p_{(x,y,t)}:(x,y)\in X\times X,\ d(x,y)>0,\ t\in[0,d(x,y)]\}.

By construction, we have
G_{d}\subseteq Z
but equality need not hold.
Thus, it suffices to give an isometry
\phi:F_{d}\rightarrow G_{d}
between
(F_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
(G_{d},d_{Z})
. Let
(x,y),(a,b)\in X\times X
with
d(x,y)>0
and
d(a,b)>0
. Consider the restriction of
d
to the finite set
M=\{x,y,a,b\}
. Then, in view of
[
27
, Thm. 2]
, we have

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{(x,y,t)},f_{(a,b,t^{\prime})})=d_{Z}(p_{(x,y,t)},p_{(a,b,t^{\prime})})

for all
t\in[0,d(x,y)]
and all
t^{\prime}\in[0,d(a,b)]
. Hence, putting
\phi(f_{(x,y,t)})=p_{(x,y,t)}
for all
(x,y)\in X\times X
with
d(x,y)>0
and all
t\in[0,d(x,y)]
yields an isometry between
(F_{d}^{\circ},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
(G_{d}^{\circ},d_{Z})
that can be extended to an isometry between
(F_{d},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and
(G_{d},d_{Z})
.

It remains to show that
\phi(\kappa(x))\subseteq Z_{x}
for all
x\in X
. Assume for a contradiction that there exist
(a,b)\in X\times X
with
d(a,b)>0
,
t\in[0,d(a,b)]
and
x\in X
such that
f_{(a,b,t)}(x)=0
but
p_{(a,b,t)}\not\in Z_{x}
. Then, without loss of generality, we have

d(a,x)>d_{Z}(p_{(a,b,0)},p_{(a,b,t)})=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{(a,b,0)},f_{(a,b,t)})\geq d(a,x),

a contradiction. Thus, we have
\phi(\kappa(x)\cap F_{d}^{\circ})\subseteq Z_{x}
for all
x\in X
, implying, in view of the fact that both
\kappa(x)
and
Z_{x}
are complete, that
\phi(\kappa(x))\subseteq Z_{x}
, as required.
∎

The following example illustrates that we cannot remove the condition that there exist
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)>0
from Theorem
3.23
.

Example 3.24
.

Let
X=\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and consider the distance
d
on
X
with
d(a_{i},a_{j})=0
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then we have
T_{d}=\{f\}
with
f(a_{i})=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa(a_{i})=\{f\}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. The real tree with
Z=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\geq 0\}
and
d_{Z}(x,y)=|x-y|
together with the family
\{Z_{a_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
Z_{a_{i}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\geq i\}
is a subtree representation of

. There exists, however, no point
z\in Z
with
z\in Z_{a_{i}}
for all

, implying that there exists no embedding

.
∎

4
Diversities

In this section we study the relationship between the tight span of diversities and distances. We begin by recalling some relevant facts about diversities.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
denote the set of all finite subsets of
X
and
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
the non-empty finite subsets of
X
. A
diversity
on
X
is a pair
(X,\delta)
such that
\delta:\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies

(i)

\delta(A)=0
if and only if
|A|\leq 1
, and

(ii)

\delta(A\cup C)\leq\delta(A\cup B)+\delta(B\cup C)
if
B\neq\emptyset
.

for all
A,B,C\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
.
Note that the term diversity is used
for both the pair
(X,\delta)
and the map
\delta
. One direct consequence of the definition of a diversity is that the restriction of
\delta
to pairs is a metric, called the metric
induced
by
\delta
on
X
.

We recall some examples of diversities from
[
4
, Sec. 1]
that we will return to later on.

Example 4.25
.

(i)

Any metric space
(X,d)
with
d(x,y)>0
if
x\neq y
gives rise to the
diameter diversity

\delta=\delta_{d}
on
X
defined by putting
\delta(\emptyset)=0
and
\delta(A)=\max\{d(x,y):x,y\in A\}
for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. Clearly, the metric induced by the diameter diversity on
X
is just
d
.

(ii)

For
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
the
\ell_{1}
-
diversity

\delta
on
X
is defined by putting
\delta(\emptyset)=0
and

\delta(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\{|x_{i}-y_{i}|:x,y\in A\}

for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
. The diversity equals the sum of the lengths in each direction of the smallest axis-aligned rectangle containing
A
. The induced metric of this diversity is the
\ell_{1}
-metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(iii)

Let
(Z,d_{Z})
be a complete real tree. The intuition behind the
real-tree
diversity
(Z,\delta_{Z})
for
(Z,d_{Z})
is that for each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(Z)
, the diversity
\delta_{Z}(A)
of
A
equals the length of the shortest subtree connecting
A

[
15
]
.
More formally, let
\mu
be the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
(Z,d_{Z})

[
11
]
. We note that
\mu
is defined on all Borel sets, it is monotone, and it is additive on disjoint sets, and that if
[a,b]
denotes the points on the path from
a
to
b
in
Z
then
\mu([a,b])=d(a,b)
. Let
\mathrm{conv}(A)=\bigcup_{a,b\in A}[a,b]
denote the convex hull of
A
, which is closed when
A
is finite. We then have

\delta_{Z}(A)=\mu\left(\mathrm{conv}(A)\right).

A diversity
\delta
on a set
X
is a
phylogenetic diversity
if there exists a complete real tree
(Z,d_{Z})
with
X\subseteq Z
and
\delta
is the restriction of
\delta_{Z}
to
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a diversity. For
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
we write
f\preceq g
if
f(A)\leq g(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. Put

\displaystyle P_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}:f(\emptyset)=0\ \text{and}\ \sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}f(C)\geq\delta(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}})\ \text{for all finite }\ \mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)\},

\displaystyle T_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\{f\in P_{\delta}:f\ \text{is minimal with respect to}\ \preceq\}.

(16)

T_{\delta}
is called the
tight span
of
(X,\delta)
. By
[
4
, Thm. 2.7]
, the map
\delta_{T}:\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(T_{\delta})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by putting
\delta_{T}(\emptyset)=0
and, for
F\neq\emptyset
,

\delta_{T}(F)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\\
\mathcal{C}\ \text{finite}\end{subarray}}\left\{\delta(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}})-\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\inf_{f\in F}f(C)\right\}

(17)

is a diversity on
T_{\delta}
. Defining, for all
x\in X
, the map
g_{x}:\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by putting
g_{x}(A):=\delta(A\cup\{x\})
, we have, by
[
4
, Thm. 2.8]
, that
g_{x}\in T_{\delta}
and

\delta(C)=\delta_{T}(\{g_{c}:c\in C\})

(18)

for all
C\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. That is, the diversity
(X,\delta)
is embedded in the diversity
(T_{\delta},\delta_{T})
. In addition, it follows from
[
4
, Lem. 2.6]
that the metric induced by
\delta_{T}
on
T_{\delta}
coincides with
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt
.

Given a diversity
(X,\delta)
, we define a distance
D_{\delta}
on
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)

by putting

D_{\delta}(A,B):=\max\{\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B),0\}

(cf.
[
16
]
).
As we shall see, this construction provides us with a link between the tight span of diversities and the tight span of distance spaces. In particular,
we show in this section that there is an embedding of the tight span of the diversity
(X,\delta)
into the tight span of the distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D_{\delta})
, and we characterize when this embedding is bijective.

Note that, for any diversity
(X,\delta)
, both
T_{\delta}
and the tight span
T_{D}
of the distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D)=(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D_{\delta})
consist of maps from
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
to
\mathbb{R}
. In addition, also by definition,
\delta
is itself a map from
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
to
\mathbb{R}
. With this in mind, the next theorem describes how
T_{\delta}
and
T_{D}
are related (for finite
X
this relationship is stated in
[
16
, p. 2517]
).

Theorem 4.26
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a diversity,
D=D_{\delta}
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
.
If
f\in T_{\delta}
, then
f-\delta\in T_{D}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f\in T_{\delta}
and let
g=f-\delta
. Then, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
, we have

g(A)+g(B)=f(A)-\delta(A)+f(B)-\delta(B)\geq 0

and

g(A)+g(B)=f(A)+f(B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B)\geq\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B).

Hence,

g(A)+g(B)\geq\max\{\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B),0\}=D(A,B)

and, therefore,
g\in P_{D}
.

To show that
g\in T_{D}
, consider
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. If
f(A)=\delta(A)
we have
g(A)=0=D(A,A)
. Otherwise, by
[
4
, Prop. 2.4 (5)]
there is some
B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
such that

0\leq f(A)=\delta(A\cup B)-f(B).

Thus
\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B)\geq 0
and

g(A)=\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B)-g(B)=D(A,B)-g(B),

implying
g(A)=\sup\{D(A,B)-g(B):B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\}
. Hence, by Theorem
2.4
(ii),
g\in T_{D}
.
∎

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
4.26
we obtain that
T_{\delta}
is isometrically embedded into
T_{D}
.

Corollary 4.27
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a diversity,
D=D_{\delta}
and
f_{1},f_{2}\in T_{\delta}
. Put
g_{1}=f_{1}-\delta
and
g_{2}=f_{2}-\delta
. Then

d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},g_{2})<\infty.

Proof.

We have
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})=\delta_{T}(\{f_{1},f_{2}\})
implying that
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})<\infty
. Moreover, we clearly have
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f_{1},f_{2})=d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g_{1},g_{2})
by construction of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
.
∎

Example 4.28
.

Consider

and the diversity
\delta

\delta(\emptyset)=0

\delta(A)=|A|-1
for all

f=\Big[f(\emptyset),f(\{x\}),f(\{y\}),f(\{z\}),f(\{x,y\}),f(\{x,z\}),f(\{y,z\}),f(\{x,y,z\})\Big]

for a map
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
. The tight span
T_{\delta}
is the triangular pyramid shown in Figure
4
(a). In contrast, the tight span
T_{D}
for the distance
D=D_{\delta}
is the triangular bi-pyramid shown in Figure
4
(b). Thus,
T_{D}
contains a translate of
T_{\delta}
as described in Theorem
4.26
but it is not in bijection with
T_{\delta}
.
∎

As can be seen from Example
4.28
, the embedding of
T_{\delta}
into
T_{D}
via Theorem
4.26
need not be a bijection. We shall see in the remainder of this section that there are diversities for which the embedding is a bijection. Theorem
4.29
below gives a characterization of these diversities. This characterization involves the following relaxation of the conditions that define the sets
P_{\delta}
and
T_{\delta}
in (
16
) for a diversity
(X,\delta)
:

\displaystyle P^{(2)}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}:f(\emptyset)=0\ \text{and}\ f(A)+f(B)\geq\delta(A\cup B)\ \text{for all}\ A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\},

\displaystyle T^{(2)}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\{f\in P^{(2)}_{\delta}:f\ \text{minimal with respect to}\ \preceq\}.

(19)

Recall that for the distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D)
and
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
we have

\kappa(A)=\{g\in T_{D}:g(A)=0\}.

Theorem 4.29
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a diversity and
D=D_{\delta}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

P_{\delta}=P^{(2)}_{\delta}
;

(ii)

T_{\delta}=T^{(2)}_{\delta}
;

(iii)

For all
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
,
f\in T_{\delta}
if and only if
f-\delta\in T_{D}
;

(iv)

The metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is hyperconvex and
\kappa(A)+\delta\subseteq T_{\delta}
for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset,X\}
.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
: Clearly, if
P_{\delta}=P^{(2)}_{\delta}
then
T_{\delta}=T^{(2)}_{\delta}
.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
: Suppose
T_{\delta}=T^{(2)}_{\delta}
and consider
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
. Put
g=f-\delta
. In view of Theorem
4.26
, we have
g\in T_{D}
if
f\in T_{\delta}
. It remains to show that
g\in T_{D}
implies
f\in T_{\delta}
. So, assume
g\in T_{D}
. Then, for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
,

g(A)+g(A)\geq D(A,A)\geq 0

so that
g
is non-negative, and, in view of Theorem
2.4
(ii) and the definition of
D
,

g(\emptyset)=\sup\{D(\emptyset,A)-g(A):A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\}\leq 0

so that
g(\emptyset)=0
and, thus,
f(\emptyset)=0
.
Moreover, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
(not necessarily distinct) we have

f(A)-\delta(A)+f(B)-\delta(B)=g(A)+g(B)\geq D(A,B)\geq\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B)

so that
f(A)+f(B)\geq\delta(A\cup B)
. Hence
f\in P^{(2)}_{\delta}
.

To show that
f\in T_{\delta}
, consider
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. If
g(A)=0
then
f(A)-\delta(A)=0=-f(\emptyset)
, so
f(A)+f(\emptyset)=\delta(A)
. Otherwise, since
g\in T_{D}
, we have, again in view of Theorem
2.4
(ii),

g(A)=\sup\{D(A,B)-g(B):B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\}>0,

implying that it suffices to take the supremum over those
B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
with
0<D(A,B)=\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B)
.
Thus, by definition of
g
, we have

f(A)=\sup\{\delta(A\cup B)-f(B):B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\}.

Hence,
f
is minimal in
P^{(2)}_{\delta}
with respect to pointwise comparison
\preceq
, implying, by definition,
f\in T^{(2)}_{\delta}=T_{\delta}
.

(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
: By definition, we have
P_{\delta}\subseteq P^{(2)}_{\delta}
. To show that also
P^{(2)}_{\delta}\subseteq P_{\delta}
, consider
f\in P^{(2)}_{\delta}
. Then, by definition, we have
f(\emptyset)=0
,
f(A)\geq\delta(A)
,
f(B)\geq\delta(B)
and

f(A)+f(B)\geq\delta(A\cup B)

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
.
Let
g=f-\delta
. Then
g(A)\geq 0
,
g(B)\geq 0
and

g(A)+g(B)\geq\max\{\delta(A\cup B)-\delta(A)-\delta(B),0\}=D(A,B),

implying
g\in P_{D}
. Hence, by Theorem
2.4
(i), there exists

g^{\prime}\in T_{D}
such that
g^{\prime}\preceq g
. In view of (iii), this implies
f^{\prime}=g^{\prime}+\delta\in T_{\delta}
. Hence, also for all finite
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 3
we have

\delta(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}C)\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}f^{\prime}(C)=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}(g^{\prime}(C)+\delta(C))\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}(g(C)+\delta(C))=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}f(C),

implying
f\in P_{\delta}
.

(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
:
Suppose that (iii) holds. Then, by Theorem
4.26
and Corollary
4.27
, the metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to the metric space
(T_{D},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. In particular,
T_{D}\neq\emptyset
and
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,g)<\infty
for all
f,g\in T_{D}
. Thus, by Theorem
2.10
,
(T_{D},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
and, therefore,
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
as well are hyperconvex metric spaces. Moreover, by the assumption that
T_{D}=T_{\delta}-\delta
, we clearly have
\kappa(A)+\delta\subseteq T_{\delta}
for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset,X\}
, as required.

(iv)\Rightarrow(iii)
: assume that
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a hyperconvex metric space and

\kappa(A)+\delta=\{g\in T_{D}:g(A)=0\}+\delta\subseteq T_{\delta}

for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
.
By Theorem
4.26
and Corollary
4.27
there exists an isometric embedding
\pi:T_{\delta}\rightarrow T_{D}
, with
\pi(f)=f-\delta
, and the identity map
\phi:T_{\delta}\rightarrow T_{\delta}
clearly is non-expansive. Hence, by the assumption that
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a hyperconvex and, therefore, injective metric space, there exists a non-expansive map
\psi:T_{D}\rightarrow T_{\delta}
such that
\psi(\pi(f))=f
for all
f\in T_{\delta}
.

Now, to show that (iii) holds, it suffices to show that
\psi
is a bijection. Consider
g\in T_{D}
and put
f=\psi(g)
. Note that
D(\emptyset,B)=0
for all
B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and if
X
is finite we further have
D(X,B)=0
. Hence, by Theorem
2.4
(ii), we have

g(\emptyset)=\sup\{D(\emptyset,B)-D(B):B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\}=0

so that

g(\emptyset)=f(\emptyset)-\delta(\emptyset)

and, when
X
is finite,
g(X)=0
and

g(X)=f(X)-\delta(X).

Moreover, since
\psi
is non-expansive, we have, in view of Theorem
2.9
,

\displaystyle g(A)

\displaystyle=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(g,h):h\in\kappa(A)\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(\psi(g),\psi(h)):h\in\kappa(A)\}

(as
\psi
is non-expansive)

\displaystyle=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f,h+\delta):h\in\kappa(A)\}

(as
\psi(h)=h+\delta
)

\displaystyle=\inf\{d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt(f-\delta,h):h\in\kappa(A)\}

\displaystyle=f(A)-\delta(A)

for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset,X\}
. Since, by the definition of
T_{D}
,
g
is minimal with respect to
\preceq
in
T_{D}
and
f-\delta\in T_{D}
, we must have
g=f-\delta=\pi(f)
. Hence,
\psi
is a bijection.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.29
we determine two important classes of diversities for which
T_{D}=T_{\delta}-\delta
.

Corollary 4.30
.

For the following diversities
(X,\delta)
we have
T_{D}=T_{\delta}-\delta
with
D=D_{\delta}
:

1.

The diameter diversity
\delta=\delta_{d}
on
X
associated to any metric space
(X,d)
with
d(x,y)>0
if
x\neq y
.

2.

The
\ell_{1}
-diversity
\delta
on
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
4.29
and the fact that
P_{\delta}\subseteq P^{(2)}_{\delta}
, it suffices to show that
P_{\delta}^{(2)}\subseteq P_{\delta}
.

1.

Let
f\in P_{\delta}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
finite with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 3
. Then we have

\displaystyle\delta\left(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}C\right)

\displaystyle=\max\{\delta(C\cup C^{\prime}):C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},C\neq C^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{f(C)+f(C^{\prime}):C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},C\neq C^{\prime}\}\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}f(C),

so
f\in P_{\delta}
, as required.

2.

For
X
a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
it was shown in
[
16
, Thm. 7.4]
that
P_{\delta^{\prime}}^{(2)}=P_{\delta^{\prime}}
for the restriction
\delta^{\prime}
of
\delta
to
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. To see that this also holds for
\delta
, we use a similar argument as for diameter diversities above: Let
f\in P_{\delta}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
finite with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 3
. Then by applying Proposition 5 of
[
5
]
to every possible ordering of
\mathcal{C}
we have

\displaystyle\delta(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}C)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|-1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{C,C^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\\
C\neq C^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\delta(C\cup C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|-1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{C,C^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\\
C\neq C^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f(C)+f(C^{\prime})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}f(C),

implying
f\in P_{\delta}
, as required.

∎

5
Arboreal Diversities

One of the main motivations for the development of
diversity theory was the study of phylogenetic diversities.
These have several interesting properties, one of
which being that they have tight spans which induce metric spaces that are real trees
[
4
, Thm. 5.7]
.
More generally, we shall say that a diversity
(X,\delta)
is
arboreal

if its tight span
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
has an induced metric space that is a real tree.
In this section, we shall give a characterization for such diversities. Note that,
as can be seen from the following example, there exist arboreal diversities
(X,\delta)
such that
\delta
is
not
a phylogenetic diversity. These provide a counter-example to Theorem 7.3 of
[
16
]
.

Figure 5:
(a) The tight span of the diversity
(X,\delta)
in Example
5.31
with
X=\{a,b,c\}
, which is a real tree with three leaves. Each leaf corresponds to an element in
X
. Every edge in the tree has length 1. (b) The subtrees representing the subsets
\{c\}
(a single leaf) and
\{a,b\}
(drawn bold) of
X
in the subtree representation of
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D)
with
D=D_{\delta}
.

Example 5.31
.

Consider the diversity
\delta
on
X=\{a,b,c\}
defined as
\delta(\emptyset)=\delta(\{a\})=\delta(\{b\})=\delta(\{c\})=0
,

\delta(\{a,b\})=\delta(\{a,c\})=\delta(\{b,c\})=4
and

\delta(\{a,b,c\})=5
. The tight span
T_{\delta}
with the metric
d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt
is a real tree and shown in Figure
5
(a).
\delta
is, however, not a phylogenetic diversity because then
\delta(X)
would need to equal 6, the total length of the real tree in Figure
5
(a). Even so, the distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D=D_{\delta})
has a subtree representation and, thus, satisfies (
2
). In Figure
5
(b) the subtrees
S_{\{a,b\}}
and
S_{\{c\}}
obtained from the real tree in Figure
5
(a) are shown for the two subsets
\{a,b\}
and
\{c\}
. It can be seen that
D(\{a,b\},\{c\})=\max\{\delta(\{a,b,c\})-\delta(\{a,b\})-\delta(\{c\}),0\}=1
equals the distance between the two subtrees.
∎

As we shall see, a key tool to establish a characterization of arboreal diversities is hyperconvexity. A diversity
(X,\delta)
is
hyperconvex
if, for any collection
\{A_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
of subsets in
\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and non-negative real numbers
\{r_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
such that

\delta\left(\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}A_{\gamma}\right)\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}r_{\gamma}

(20)

for all finite non-empty subsets
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
, there exists
z\in X
with
\delta(A_{\gamma}\cup\{z\})\leq r_{\gamma}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
It is shown in
[
4
, Thm. 3.6]
that, for any diversity
(X,\delta)
, the tight span
(T_{\delta},\delta_{T})
is a hyperconvex diversity. However, as can be seen in Example
4.28
by considering the set of three points
\{g_{x},g_{y},g_{z}\}
and
r_{x}=r_{y}=r_{z}=\frac{1}{2}
, the metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
need not be hyperconvex (see also
[
14
]
).

We first establish some basic properties of the metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.

Lemma 5.32
.

The induced metric space
(X,d)
of a hyperconvex diversity
(X,\delta)
is complete and geodesic.

Proof.

That
(X,d)
is complete was established in Proposition 3.10 of
[
14
]
.

We now show that
(X,d)
is geodesic.
Let
x,y\in X
and suppose
\ell=\delta(\{x,y\})=d(x,y)>0
. Let
Y_{1}=\{0,\ell\}
and let
\delta_{1}
be the unique diversity on
Y_{1}
with
\delta_{1}(\{0,\ell\})=\ell
. Let
Y_{2}
be the real interval
[0,\ell]
and let
\delta_{2}
be the diameter diversity on
Y_{2}
with the standard metric, that is,
\delta_{2}(A)=\max(|a_{1}-a_{2}|:a_{1},a_{2}\in A\}
for all finite
A\subseteq[0,\ell]
. Let
\pi:Y_{1}\rightarrow Y_{2}
be the embedding with
\pi(0)=0
and
\pi(\ell)=\ell
, and let
\phi:Y_{1}\rightarrow X
be the map with
\phi(0)=x
and
\phi(\ell)=y
. By Theorem 3.3 of
[
4
]
there is a non-expansive map
\psi
from
Y_{2}=[0,\ell]
to
X
such that
\psi(0)=x
and
\psi(\ell)=y
. It follows that
\psi
is a geodesic.
∎

The next lemma gives a sufficient condition for a diversity
(X,\delta)
to be arboreal.

Lemma 5.33
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a diversity such that the associated distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D=D_{\delta})
satisfies the extended four-point condition (
2
). Then
(X,\delta)
is arboreal.

Proof.

By
[
4
, Thm. 3.6]
, the diversity
(T_{\delta},\delta_{T})
is hyperconvex and so, by Lemma
5.32
, the metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is geodesic.
As
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D)
satisfies (
2
),
(T_{D},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a real tree, in view of Theorem
3.22
. Thus, by Theorem
4.26
and Corollary
4.27
,
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is isometric to a non-empty, path-connected subset of the real tree
(T_{D},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
. This implies that the induced metric space
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
is a real tree and, thus,
(X,\delta)
is arboreal.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we will show that the converse of Lemma
5.33
holds. By Theorem
4.26
there is an isometric embedding of
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
into
(T_{D},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
however this embedding need not be bijective. Instead we establish several new results about hyperconvex diversities and subsequently
construct such a subtree representation of
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D)
directly from
(T_{\delta},d_{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{$\infty$}}}\hskip-0.85358pt)
.

For a diversity
(X,\delta)
and
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)

we define

\mathcal{B}_{A}:=\{x\in X:\delta(A\cup\{x\})\leq\delta(A)\}.

Note that
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{A}
, so
\mathcal{B}_{A}
is non-empty.

Proposition 5.34
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a hyperconvex diversity and
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
. Then
(\mathcal{B}_{A},\delta)
is also a hyperconvex diversity.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition 3.8 in
[
14
]
, letting
Z=A
and
r(Z^{\prime})=\delta(A)
for all
Z^{\prime}\subseteq A
, so that
Y=\mathcal{B}_{A}
.
∎

In view of Lemma
5.32
, we immediately obtain the following consequence of Proposition
5.34
:

Corollary 5.35
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a hyperconvex diversity with induced metric
(X,d)
and suppose
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
. The restriction of
(X,d)
to
\mathcal{B}_{A}
is complete and geodesic.

The next proposition links, for any hyperconvex diversity
(X,\delta)
, the induced distances between the sets
\mathcal{B}_{A}
for
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
and the distance space
(\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X),D_{\delta})
.

Proposition 5.36
.

Let
(X,\delta)
be a hyperconvex diversity and
d=d_{\delta}
be the metric induced by
\delta
on
X
. Then, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
,

D_{\delta}(A,B)=\inf\{d(x,y):x\in\mathcal{B}_{A},\,y\in\mathcal{B}_{B}\}.

Proof.

Let
\ell=\inf\{d(x,y)\,:\,x\in\mathcal{B}_{A},\,y\in\mathcal{B}_{B}\}
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
and
y\in\mathcal{B}_{B}
we have

\displaystyle\delta(A\cup B)

\displaystyle\leq\delta(A\cup\{x\})+\delta(\{x,y\})+\delta(B\cup\{y\})

\displaystyle=\delta(A)+d(x,y)+\delta(B)

so that
D_{\delta}(A,B)\leq\ell
.

To show that also
\ell\leq D_{\delta}(A,B)
, first consider
r_{A}=\delta(A)
and
r_{B}=D_{\delta}(A,B)+\delta(B)
. Then we have
\delta(A)\leq r_{A}
,
\delta(B)\leq r_{B}
and
\delta(A\cup B)\leq r_{A}+r_{B}
. Thus, since
(X,\delta)
is hyperconvex, there exists
x^{*}\in X
such that
\delta(A\cup\{x^{*}\})\leq r_{A}=\delta(A)
and
\delta(B\cup\{x^{*}\})\leq r_{B}=D_{\delta}(A,B)+\delta(B)
.
In particular,
x^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
.

Next consider
r_{\{x^{*}\}}=D_{\delta}(A,B)
and
r_{B}=\delta(B)
. Then we have
\delta(\{x^{*}\})\leq r_{\{x^{*}\}}
,
\delta(B)\leq r_{B}
and
\delta(B\cup\{x^{*}\})\leq D_{\delta}(A,B)+\delta(B)=r_{\{x^{*}\}}+r_{B}
. Thus, again in view of the fact that
(X,\delta)
is hyperconvex, there exists
y^{*}\in X
such that
d(x^{*},y^{*})=\delta(\{x^{*},y^{*}\})\leq r(\{x^{*}\})=D_{\delta}(A,B)
and
\delta(B\cup\{y^{*}\})\leq r_{B}=\delta(B)
. Hence,
y^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{B}
and
d(x^{*},y^{*})\leq D_{\delta}(A,B)
, as required.
∎

We can now prove our characterization theorem for arboreal diversities. As above, for each
x\in X
, we let
g_{x}
denote the map given by
g_{x}(A)=\delta(A\cup\{x\})
for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. To apply Corollary
5.35
and Proposition
5.36
, we define, for any diversity
(X,\delta)
and any
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\hskip-0.56905pt\mathrm{fin}}^{*}(X)
,
