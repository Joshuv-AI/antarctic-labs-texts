Feasibility study of unsupervised anomaly detection using Wasserstein GAN in SPECT image
Authors: Ryosuke Kasai, Hideki Otsuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn this study, we constructed an anomaly detection system based on the Wasserstein generative model in brain single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) images, and conducted a basic study on the feasibility of the system.MethodsThe proposed method used a Wasserstein generative adversarial network approach based on optimal transport theory. Anomaly detection was performed using only healthy images, and based on the anomaly score calculated from the loss function. Theoretical validation was performed using a numerical phantom which simulated medical image with varying image noise and signal levels. The results were evaluated with receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC). Brain SPECT images from clinical scenarios were used to investigate the feasibility of this system through subtraction images and various quantitative evaluations.ResultsThe numerical phantom showed the relationship between the noise and signal values of the system, and the anomaly detection ability based on the anomaly score value indicated an AUC of 0.9994. The correlation between the anomaly score and the anomaly image was confirmed in the brain SPECT image, and the detection region was clearly observed in the difference image.ConclusionWe proposed and investigated the feasibility of an anomaly detection system based on the Wasserstein generative model. Its effectiveness is expected to reduce the workload of human operators.Keywords: Anomaly detection, Wasserstein generative adversarial networks, Wasserstein distance, Machine learning, Single photon emission computed tomography

Introduction

Anomaly detection plays a vital role in diagnostic imaging and the evaluation of pathological conditions in medical imaging [1]. Rule-based processes are often insufficient for accurately identifying disease or anomalous regions in target images; in contrast, machine learning can assist radiologists with diagnosis [2]. However, the application of machine learning to anomaly detection presents several challenges. In nuclear medicine examinations, accumulation patterns are highly complex and vary across different disease. Therefore, collecting the same number of uniformly defined anomaly images as healthy images (images with no accumulation) is not an easy task [3, 4]. Anomaly detection for differentiating healthy images from anomaly images cannot be achieved successfully if a sufficient number of anomaly healthy images cannot be obtained as training images for machine learning. Moreover, supervised machine learning approaches requires adding labels to all images even if the same number of anomalous and healthy images are obtained. Most of this complex and extremely time-consuming task is performed by human operators [5–7].

In this study, the anomaly class is defined as images presenting radiotracer accumulation in regions where accumulation is not expected (healthy classes). However, patient variations and image noise can make distinguishing between these the two classes challenging [8]. The classification problem in anomaly detection can be approached using two models: discriminative models, which search for linear or nonlinear boundaries between classes, and generative models, which aim to characterize the distribution of each class. Discriminative models struggle to define boundaries effectively when the amount of training data is limited, and they often fail to classify unseen data correctly. Conversely, generative models may fail to capture the full diversity of class distributions, particularly when the generated image quality is insufficient [9, 10]. Constructing a model that can generate high-quality images is expected to overcome the above limitations and enable effective anomaly detection in nuclear medicine images. In addition, only few experiments have focused on anomaly detection using nuclear medicine imaging. Therefore, in this study, we constructed an anomaly detection system based on the Wasserstein generation model for brain single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) images among nuclear medicine images. We aimed at a basic study of the feasibility of the proposed system.

This study employed anomaly detection based on the generative adversarial network AnoGAN [11]. AnoGAN, a widely used method for anomaly image detection, was the first approach to apply generative models for anomaly detection. AnoGAN detects anomalies using a generator trained to produce only healthy images. In addition, AnoGAN introduces a loss function capable of computing anomaly scores by quantifying the differences between the input and generated images. Anomalous regions can also be visualized by highlighting the differences between the generated healthy images and the input anomalous images. Providing a basis for judgment is vital in diagnostic imaging. However, the GAN used in AnoGAN has several limitations, including unstable training, which can hinder its ability to represent small structures in brain SPECT images [12–17]. To address these issues, the present study employed Wasserstein GAN (WGAN), which incorporates the Wasserstein distance based on the optimal transport theory, for anomaly detection. The Wasserstein distance serves as a measure of dissimilarity between images and improves the training stability and performance of the original GAN [18]. We hypothesized that this approach would enable the high-quality nuclear medicine images and facilitate more accurate detection. Several methods have been proposed to improve the computational efficiency and image quality of GAN-based approaches following the introduction of AnoGAN [19–23]. However to our knowledge, no studies have applied these approaches to detect anomalies in nuclear medicine images using both numerical phantoms and clinical data. Moreover, the relationship between noise specific to nuclear medicine images and signal values in abnormal regions remains unclear in the context of anomaly detection, particularly given the various factors introduced by differences in scanning conditions.

Therefore, in this study, we employed WGAN for anomaly detection and conducted a basic feasibility study to evaluate its performance across various noise and signal levels. Numerical phantoms were used to focus solely on noise and signal characteristics, without the confounding effects of body motion or imaging artifacts. The results obtained from the numerical phantoms provide a theoretical foundation for applying anomaly detection in brain SPECT imaging. To assess whether these findings generalize to real-world nuclear medicine images, we further applied the method to clinical SPECT data. The results demonstrated consistency between the numerical phantom experiments and the theoretical predictions, and the approach also performed successfully with clinical data.

By training a GAN exclusively on healthy images, the model acquires the ability to represent healthy image features without explicitly defining them. The trained model can generate realistic healthy images, but fails to reproduce abnormal images due to its limited training scope. This discrepancy enables AnoGAN to discriminate between healthy and anomalous images based on the quality of the generated outputs. As high-quality image generation is critical for improving AnoGAN’s anomaly detection performance, WGAN-GP was adopted as the generative model in this study. Experiments

Computer simulation using numerical phantom

Experiments using a numerical phantom confirmed that anomaly detection in brain SPECT images produced the expected results. In medical imaging, obtaining a true distribution image is challenging due to variations in imaging conditions, equipment, and patient-specific factors. Machine learning models, on the other hand, evaluate their performance based on differences from the training data; however, it is often difficult to exclude the influence of factors unrelated to the target anomalies in patient data. As a result, accurately assessing the performance of anomaly detection methods on clinical images becomes problematic. Anomaly detection performance is affected by noise in medical images and signal intensity in anomalous regions. Therefore, the present study focused on numerical phantom experiments, which allowed us to control and isolate the effects of noise and signal values.

A numerical phantom image comprising with pixels, as shown in Fig. 2, was used in this study. The Shepp–Logan phantom [29], a widely used model for evaluating image reconstruction and image quality [30, 31], was chosen because its shape resembles the human brain.

Image noise in nuclear medicine is known to follow a Poisson distribution [32–34]. Therefore, numerical noise phantoms with Poisson-distributed noise were applied to simulate this characteristic.

For implementation, 1000 images were used for both training and testing. WGAN-GP was trained using RMSprop with a learning rate of 0.00019, as it is a widely used optimization method [36]. The gradient penalty parameter in WGAN-GP was set to in Equation (3) [28]. AnoGAN was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 [37]. The anomaly detection system developed in this study was implemented in Python 3.11.5.

The accuracy of anomaly detection was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, quantified by the corresponding area under the ROC curve (AUC) values.

Clinical SPECT data

Clinical SPECT images were used as validation targets to assess whether the findings from numerical phantoms could be applied to real-world data. In this study, we utilized 201Tl brain SPECT images, which are commonly used to diagnose brain tumors and other neurological conditions. Computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) are widely used to evaluate malignant tumors. CT and MRI yield superior performance for morphological evaluation; however, qualitative evaluation of tumors using these modalities is challenging. The detection rate of brain tumors using 18F–fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET is low owing to the physiological accumulation of 18F–FDG in healthy brain cells [38]. In contrast, 201Tl does not cross the blood-brain barrier and is not taken up by healthy brain tissue; making 201Tl SPECT particularly valuable for brain tumor detection. 201Tl accumulation is primarily observed in the skull with minimal uptake in the brain parenchyma. Regions exhibiting higher accumulation than the background in the brain parenchyma were visually identified as areas of abnormal uptake.

Patient population

Brain SPECT images from 100 patients with a final clinical diagnosis of no brain accumulation who underwent 201Tl brain scans between 2020 and 2022, were used in this study. SPECT images of 10 patients with anomalous accumulation in the brain were acquired for evaluation images. Anomalous images were used to confirm the diagnosis of brain accumulation and validate anomaly detection. All images were acquired during the early phase and images with artifacts and body movements were excluded. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokushima University Hospital (approval number: 4295-1). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Tokushima University Hospital due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Imaging protocol

Following the intravenous infusion of 201TlCl (111 MBq), early-phase SPECT images were acquired after 10 min, and late-phase SPECT images were acquired after 180 min. An e.cam (Canon Medical Systems Co., Tochigi, Japan) with a high-resolution, low-energy collimator (LEHR) was used in this study. Images were acquired for 28 min (circular orbit; angle of rotation, 180°; 45 views). Two energy windows of 166 keV ± 7.5% and 70 keV ± 10% were used, and the image matrix size was set as 128 128 pixels. The reconstruction method is the FBP method using the Butterworth filter without scatter correction and attenuation correction. We performed the reconstruction by summing the projection data from two energy windows, 166 keV and 70 keV. The early and late phases are reconstructed using their respective projection data. This study investigate the feasibility of anomaly detection using WGAN, and the early and late phase images were mixed and used for training data.

An example of an acquired 201Tl brain SPECT image is shown in Fig. 5. Images without accumulation were defined as healthy, whereas images with intracranial accumulation were defined as anomalous. The unit of the image was the counts, and all images were normalized in the range [0, 1] for data preprocessing [39].

Image analysis

For the quantitative evaluation of images generated by WGAN-GP, we calculated the structural similarity index [40] (SSIM) between the generated and patient images. SSIM quantifies the similarity between two images within the range , where values closer to 0 indicate lower similarity and values closer to 1 indicate higher similarity.

We further analyzed the anomaly scores of 10 patients exhibiting abnormal brain accumulation. The anomaly scores, computed using Eq. (4), were obtained for both the early and late phase images of each patient. To examine whether the anomaly score reflects the degree of radiotracer uptake, we calculated the mean counts from a circular ROI manually placed on the abnormal region in accordance with clinical practice.

where each ratio was obtained from the mean counts (counts/pixel) within a pair of circular ROIs. In accordance with clinical practice, a circular ROI was manually placed on the abnormal region, centered on the area showing the highest radiotracer uptake, and a corresponding ROI was positioned in the contralateral normal background. The early and delayed ratios were calculated using the mean counts within these ROIs. Although the size and shape of the ROIs were principally kept identical between the lesion and contralateral side, the mean count was used even in cases where strictly identical ROIs could not be applied because of differences in lesion size or location. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the anomaly score ratio and the RI was then calculated using Python 3.11.5.

Discussion

This study developed a system for detecting anomalous images, focusing on brain SPECT among nuclear medicine images. By incorporating WGAN-GP, which generates high-quality images, into AnoGAN, we conducted a basic feasibility study of anomaly detection in brain SPECT using numerical phantoms with varying noise and signal levels. The proposed system requires only healthy images for training, thereby addressing the challenge of collecting sufficient anomalous images in nuclear medicine. Moreover, as the system does not require manual labeling of healthy and abnormal images, it significantly reduces time and labor costs.

The Lack of basic research on noise and signal levels in nuclear medicine images using numerical phantoms has created uncertainty about their applicability to clinical imaging. In this study, the Shepp–Logan phantom (Fig. 2) was used as a numerical phantom to investigate these factors. Varying the noise and signal levels and combining the generative models WGAN-GP and AnoGAN, we demonstrated that anomaly detection can be achieved with high accuracy. While several previous studies have used additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) as image noise, statistical variations in nuclear medicine imaging are often dominated by Poisson processes. As AWGN does not accurately reflect these variations, numerical phantoms are advantageous because they allow the addition of Poisson noise at any count level while excluding other confounding factors. In this study, we simulated two noise levels: the total counts of the images and . The results showed that anomaly detection accuracy decreased in noisier images with lower counts. In particular, detection was impaired when the anomalous region’s signal intensity approached that of the surrounding tissue, potentially leading to false negatives. The shape of the numerical phantom was also varied randomly to simulate inter-patient anatomical differences. The findings revealed that noise and signal levels are dominant factors influencing anomaly detection performance. As shown in Table 1, the AUC values differed substantially (0.6715 vs. 0.8249) between noise levels, even for the same signal intensity. Notably, a very high AUC was achieved even in noisy images when the signal intensity of the anomalous region was high. These results, validated experimentally using both numerical phantoms and clinical 201Tl brain SPECT images, underscore the critical role of noise and signal levels in reliable anomaly detection.

The results of the anomaly detection experiment were evaluated based on anomaly scores. The parameter in Eq. (4) was set to 0.1, following the study by Schlegl et al. [11]. Although other values were tested, the best performance was achieved with . This setting places greater weight on the residual loss than on the discrimination loss, emphasizing reconstruction quality. Consequently, AnoGAN’s anomaly detection performance is highly dependent on the quality of the generated images. WGAN-GP, incorporating the Wasserstein distance based on optimal transport theory [44], provides improved training stability and enables the generation of high-quality images. This advantage underscores the significance of the results obtained in the present study.

Anomalous images in the clinical 201Tl SPECT data were characterized by abnormal radiotracer accumulation in the brain. As shown in Fig. 10, the proposed system successfully generated high-quality images that were visually indistinguishable from actual patient images. The SSIM between the generated and patient images reached 0.8306, demonstrating excellent reconstruction performance. Quantitative measures such as AUC values, along with visual and qualitative comparisons, are essential in nuclear medicine imaging. In particular, Figs. 11 and 12 show that subtraction images effectively highlight areas of abnormal accumulation, isolating the regions of interest as theoretically expected. Notably, the signal intensity in the anomalous regions varied from faint to strong, closely reflecting real-world patterns of radiotracer uptake and underscoring the method’s utility for diagnostic imaging. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 13, anomaly scores exhibited a strong correlation with radiotracer counts in the anomaly regions. Higher anomaly scores corresponded to higher uptake counts when defining healthy and anomalous images based on the presence of accumulation. This trend was observed consistently across both early and late imaging phases. Table 2 summarizes the Retention Index, a recognized indicator for differentiating intracranial tumors, alongside the anomaly scores. The mean difference between the anomaly score ratios and the RI was 0.069, and the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.941, indicating a strong positive correlation. This finding highlights the potential of anomaly scores as a novel quantitative metric for assessing tumor characteristics, including their benign or malignant nature and tissue type.

This experiment investigated the feasibility of anomaly detection in brain SPECT images using a generative model. The study has several limitations: the use of a simplified numerical phantom for noise and shape variation, basic reconstruction techniques without scattering or attenuation correction, and anomaly detection based solely on the presence of radiotracer accumulation. Additionally, it should be noted that the anomaly score depends on the quality of the training model and represents a relative, not absolute, measure between healthy and anomalous images. Despite these limitations, this study clarified the relationship between noise and signal levels in a simplified model and demonstrated a correlation between anomaly scores and radiotracer counts, supporting the effectiveness of WGAN-based anomaly detection. These findings suggest the potential for extending the approach to more complex phantoms, reconstructed images with greater anatomical detail, and other medical imaging modalities beyond brain SPECT. This work provides a foundational basis for future applications of generative models in anomaly detection.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that noise and signal values within anomalous regions significantly affect the accuracy of anomaly detection in medical imaging, including nuclear medicine. These findings, obtained through numerical experiments with simulated medical images, emphasize key considerations for implementing anomaly detection in nuclear medicine for diagnostic and pathological applications. Furthermore, we found that applying anomaly detection to 201Tl brain SPECT images is effective for supporting diagnostic imaging. The use of a high-quality generative model enables accurate visualization of anomalous regions. The calculated anomaly scores and subtraction images provide a novel quantitative index for SPECT images, facilitating the differentiation of benign and malignant tumors. Additionally, these metrics may help establish relationships with tumor tissue classifications, which will be explored in future studies.
