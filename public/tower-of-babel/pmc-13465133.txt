A smartphone application for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors
Authors: Mohadeseh Sadat Khorashadizadeh, Haleh Ayatollahi, Marjan HajAhmadi Pour Rafsanjani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with millions of survivors facing long-term health complications, particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy can induce cardiotoxicity, resulting in significant cardiovascular morbidity. As cancer survival rates improve, the need for effective management strategies to address the cardiovascular health of cancer survivors has become increasingly critical. This study aimed to develop a smartphone-based application for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors.MethodsThis research was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, users’ requirements were investigated by using a questionnaire. The participants were 31 cardio-oncologists. In the second phase, a prototype of the application was designed and implemented, and its usability was evaluated quantitatively by 27 cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications and eight cardio-oncologists using a QUIS questionnaire (version 5.5). The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software, version 28.ResultsMost of the proposed educational content, data elements, and application features, including necessary steps to prevent cardiovascular complications in cancer survivors, risk factors for cardiovascular complications in cancer survivors, guidance on quitting smoking and exercise training, the ability to track personal and clinical data, fatigue and anxiety assessments, and communication with physicians, were considered essential items for the application. Ultimately, users evaluated the application’s usability, resulting in an average mean score of 8.19 out of 9 from patients’ perspectives and 8.22 out of 9 from cardio-oncologists, indicating a “good” level of user satisfaction.ConclusionThe development of a smartphone-based application for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors represents a significant step toward improving cardiovascular health management in this vulnerable population. However, more research is needed to compare the effects of remote education versus face-to-face rehabilitation on improving health literacy and patient exercise adherence.Keywords: Cardio-oncology, Rehabilitation, Smartphone, Application

Introduction

Cancer is one of the most significant public health challenges of the 21st century and has been recognized as the second leading cause of death globally in 2023, following cardiovascular diseases [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), it is predicted that by 2040, there will be approximately 30 million new cases of cancer and 10 million cancer-related deaths [2]. As the number of cancer survivors continues to rise, understanding and addressing the cardiovascular implications of cancer treatment has become increasingly critical. Cancer can lead to various complications, including pain, fatigue, nervous system problems, issues with endocrine glands, cardiovascular complications, secondary cancers, and digestive issues [3]. Of these, cardiovascular complications are known to be the most common ones in cancer patients [4, 5]. In 2017, many deaths among cancer patients were not directly attributed to the cancer itself; instead, more than 40% of deaths were linked to cardiovascular complications. The age-standardized mortality rate of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) varied from 73.6 per 100,000 in high- income Asia Pacific to 432.3 per 100,000 in Eastern European regions in 2022 [6]. Furthermore, the toxic effects of cancer treatment can also harm the cardiovascular system of patients and survivors, both during the acute phase and after treatment, leading to cardiovascular complications in this group [7]. Cancer survivors are often at higher risk of cardiovascular issues due to exposure to other risk factors, such as obesity and smoking [8, 9], and are more likely to suffer from cardiovascular diseases than others [10].

Although recent advances in chemotherapy and radiotherapy have improved tumor and cancer treatment [11], both methods are linked to impaired cardiac function, which is a leading cause of death among cancer patients [12]. A growing body of evidence indicates that these treatments can induce cardiotoxicity, leading to various cardiovascular complications such as heart failure, arrhythmias, and hypertension. For instance, Anthracyclines, a widely used class of chemotherapy drugs, are known for their dose-dependent cardiotoxic effects, potentially resulting in heart failure in up to 30% of patients treated with these medications within ten years post-therapy [13, 14]. Other treatments, such as Trastuzumab, also pose significant risks for developing cardiac dysfunction, particularly in patients with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions [15].

The presence of shared risk factors between cancer and cardiovascular disease further exacerbates the risk of cardiovascular morbidity. Many cancer patients present with obesity, sedentary lifestyles, and smoking habits, all of which are known to contribute to cardiovascular disease. This overlapping risk profile necessitates a comprehensive approach to patient care, integrating both oncological and cardiovascular health strategies to optimize outcomes. As a result, the American Heart Association (AHA) has recognized the importance of cardio-oncology as a speciality that focuses on managing cardiovascular issues in patients with cancer and survivors. This multidisciplinary approach emphasizes the need for ongoing cardiovascular monitoring and intervention, as many patients may experience cardiovascular issues that manifest long after cancer treatment has concluded [16].

To reduce cardiovascular complications in patients and survivors of various cancers, cardio-oncology rehabilitation has been used [7]. This program typically includes exercise, education, and psychosocial support for patients. In many settings, these structured rehabilitation efforts are implemented within the framework of the Cardio-Oncology Rehabilitation (CORE) program, which provides an organized approach to deliver tailored exercise and supportive care for oncology populations. It is a multi-component exercise-based intervention tailored to the needs of cancer patients and survivors, offering risk factor management, medication therapy, and structured exercise plans to enhance cardiovascular health, alleviate symptoms, improve quality of life, and increase survival rates in cancer patients [17, 18]. One way to enhance the quality of life for cancer patients with cardiovascular issues is to establish a cardio-oncology rehabilitation program that utilizes telehealth technology to expand access to care, even in remote areas [7, 19]. Generally, telerehabilitation programs can help to address issues with patients’ non-participation or limited engagement in exercise programs by enabling them to exercise at home. These interventions are supported by remote monitoring through telephone or text messages [19, 20].

Although telerehabilitation programs have been developed for diseases such as stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic heart failure [21], it seems that literature regarding the development of a smartphone-based application for the rehabilitation of cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications is scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a smartphone-based application for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors.

Methods

This research was conducted in 2024 and consisted of two phases. To determine the content of a smartphone-based application for the rehabilitation of cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications, different databases, such as Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and IEEE were searched to retrive articles and guidelines related to the study aim. Cardio-oncology rehabilitation and exercise (CORE) programme and its components was the main model used in this research to provide necessary educational content, data elements, and application functions for patients. The CORE programme is a comprehensive rehabilitation framework proposed by the American Heart Association to address cardiovascular complications in cancer patients and survivors through a multidisciplinary and preventive approach. Its major components include baseline medical assessment, nutritional counseling, cardiovascular risk-factor management (such as blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, weight, and smoking), psychosocial support, and structured exercise training and physical activity guidance [22]. Moreover, some applications related to the cardio-oncology rehabilitation were searched on the Google Play Store and the iOS App Store, downloaded and their data elements and functions were extracted. Then, a list of the educational content, data elements, and application functions was developed to cover different components of the CORE programme, and a cardiologist was consulted to understand which items should be removed or included in a questionnaire. The cardiologist’s experience helped to modify the list based on the routine medical practices and contextual requirements. Then, to obtain a more realistic understanding of the items necessary to be included in the application and to investigate other cardio-oncologists’ and cardiologists’ opinions, a questionnaire was designed based on the five-point Likert scale which consisted of four main sections and 83 items.

The first section was related to the participants’ demographic questions (5 items), and the second section comprised educational content (13 items), data elements (42 items), and application functions (23 items). The educational content included education on risk factors and measures to prevent cardiovascular complications (2 items), nutrition counseling (2 items), blood pressure management (3 items), blood cholesterol control (1 items), diabetes management (1 items), weight management (1 items), smoking cessation (1 items), and patient exercise and activity management (1 items). The data elements required for the application included individual (15 items) and clinical data (27 items). Participants were asked to determine the importance of each item from unimportant (1) to very important (5). At the end of each part, an open-ended question was posed for participants to add any new items from their perspective. Five cardio-oncologists assessed the face and content validity of the questionnaire. The questionnaire’s reliability was also evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.95).

The questionnaire was distributed among 126 cardio-oncologists and cardiologists working at different teaching hospitals affiliated with three medical universities. Their opinions could help to identify the most important items to be included in the application. To analyze the data, descriptive statistics were used, and frequency distributions, mean values, and standard deviations were calculated in SPSS version 27. To include the most essential educational content, data elements, and functions in the application, a 75% cut off was established. This means that if the mean value for each item was 3.75 or higher, that item would be included in the application; otherwise, it would be removed.

In the second phase, a prototype of the application was designed based on the results derived from the first phase of the study. The questionnaire of user interface satisfaction (QUIS) was then used to evaluate the application’s usability. The participants of this phase were cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications (n = 27) and cardio-oncologists (n = 8) who were selected using convenience sampling methodology. The application was installed on the patients’ smartphones, and they worked with it. They were then asked to complete the QUIS questionnaire. This questionnaire consisted of six sections: personal information (3 items), overall reaction to the software (6 items), screen (4 items), terminology and system information (6 items), learning (6 items), and system capabilities (5 items). This questionnaire was designed based on a 10-point Likert scale, and its validity and reliability were confirmed in previous studies [23]. In the present study, internal consistency was also evaluated, demonstrating excellent reliability (α = 0.95). For the second phase of the research, we analyzed the data using descriptive statistics and calculated the mean values for each section of the questionnaire. We then grouped the results into three levels: “poor” (0–3), “average” (3.1–6), and “good” (6.1–9), and reported the overall level of user satisfaction.

Results

Phase 1: necessary content for the application

In the first phase, 31 out of 126 (24%) cardio-oncologists and cardiologists completed a questionnaire to identify the educational content, data elements, and functions needed in the application. The highest frequency of the participants belonged to the age group of 36–40 years (n = 15, 48.38%), and a majority of the participants (n = 19, 61.29%) were female. According to the participants’ perspectives, most of the items listed in the questionnaire were found necessary and included in the application.

Eductional content

Regarding the important educational needs (Table 1), all items including measures to prevent cardiovascular complications (4.74 ± 0.56), risk factors for cardiovascular complications in patients with cancer (4.71 ± 0.62), guidance on smoking cessation (4.68 ± 0.52), exercise training (4.48 ± 0.66), and controlling hemoglobin A1C (4.03 ± 0.85) were included in the application, as their mean values were higher than 3.75. The entire set of educational materials in multimedia (e.g., texts, images) format was made available to all patients regardless of their specific clinical status.

Individual data elements

The most important individual data elements were height (4.67 ± 0.46), body mass index (BMI) (4.65 ± 0.59), weight (4.65 ± 0.53), and sex (4.64 ± 0.47), respectively. The least important data element was the individual’s home address (1.13 ± 0.71). Since four data elements (waist circumference, place of birth, home address, and contact number) had mean values below 3.75, they were excluded from the application.

Clinical data elements

The most important clinical data elements identified by the physicians were family history of cancer (4.77 ± 0.55), family history of cardiovascular disease (4.68 ± 0.74), type of cancer (4.68 ± 0.64), type of cancer treatment (4.61 ± 0.55), and history of chemotherapy (4.61 ± 0.55). The least important clinical data element was other diabetes- related diseases in patients with cancer (4.09 ± 0.92). Since the mean value of all items was higher than 3.75, all of them were included in the application.

Application functions

The most important functional requirements were the possibility of providing a report for the physician (4.48 ± 0.71), editing data (4.42 ± 0.79), recording height (4.41 ± 0.70), calculating body mass index (4.41 ± 0.66), and sending messages to the physician (4.39 ± 0.55). The least important item was the possibility of recording waist circumference (3.42 ± 0.91). Since this item had a mean below 3.75, it was excluded from the application.

Phase 2: development and evaluation of the application

Based on the results derived from the first phase of the study, a prototype of the application was designed. It was developed in the Kotlin programming language, and SQLite was used to create the database. Both patients and physicians could use the application, as they had two different panels. Initially, the user (patient or physician) could enter the main page of the application by selecting the “Login” option. On this page, users entered the application by providing a username and password; if they did not have a username and password; then, registeration was required.

Patient panel

The patient panel included four main sections: personal health records, contact doctor, educational contents, and information about healthcare centers. In the “personl health records”, the user entered personal and clinical data along with information on stress, anxiety, and fatigue. To evaluate these psychological parameters, the validated Persian version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21) was embedded into the application. Patients completed the questionnaire electronically through their panel, and the system automatically calculated and stored the individual subscale scores [24]. Patients could enter, edit and save their data like a medical record and could extract a report on changes overtime. Physicians could access and view these data from their own panel, and could compare changes for better decision-making.

In the “contact doctor” section, patient could send text messages to their physicians using the application and by selecting their own physician. The patient could ask questions, and the physician could respond. In the “educational contents” section, the contents regarding cancer, its cardiovascular complications, standard treatment approaches, and rehabilitation exercises were categorized under several topics and provided in multimedia format (text, images, and videos). The content was extracted from the well-known textbooks and approved by a cardiologist. It included some general, simple, non-interactive materials for people with different level of literacy and various stages of cancer with cardiovascular complications. It was made available to all patients regardless of their specific clinical status. In “information about healthcare centers”, the patient could view the addresses and phone numbers of the healthcare centers that provided rehabilitation services for cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications (Fig. 1).

Physician panel

The application was developed to provide cancer survivors with the educational content for cardiovascular complications and help them to do rehabilitation exercises. They could be also in touch with their cardio-oncologists/ cardiologists to report their conditions and be monitored overtime. Patients could ask questions and receive guidance via text chat. Furthermore, their physicians could access patient medical records to help and advise them on their health status and medications.

In the physician panel, there were two main sections: “Patient lis” and “Contact patients” (Fig. 2). The “patient list” displayed a list of cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications, and physicians could view patient details and their medical records, if data were entered into the application. In addition, physicians could search the desired patient based on their national identification number or the first and last name, and could communicate with patients through the application by selecting the their names, writing a message, and sending it as a response to the patient’s query.

Discussion

In this study, a smartphone-based application was developed for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors. The application included educational content, including general information about cancer, patient lifestyle, symptom management, and treatment options. Additionally, necessary individual and clinical data, along with the application functions were identified. After developing the application, its usability was examined and the results showed that the users were generally satisfied with the application.

In cancer survivors, structured cardiovascular rehabilitation has become increasingly important due to the persistent cardiotoxic effects of cancer therapies and their impact on long-term functional status. Mobile-based solutions offer a practical way to extend rehabilitation support beyond clinical settings, enabling patients to access education, track symptoms, and manage their condition more effectively. Developing such applicationsl; however, requires careful attention to clinical relevance, usability, and the specific informational needs of this patient population. Therefore, we incorporated targeted feedback from cardiologists and cardio-oncologists during the design process to identify essential content and refine core functionalities. This needs-driven approach strengthened the alignment of the application with current clinical expectations and enhanced its potential value for both patients and healthcare providers.

In different studies, researchers reported various data elements and functions for their applications. For example, Gholi Tabar et al. conducted a review study to identify the key rehabilitation needs of patients with heart failure, aiming to summarize the essential components required for designing comprehensive rehabilitation programs. Using a narrative review approach, their study synthesized findings from previous clinical and rehabilitation research and extracted core domains such as exercise-based rehabilitation, home self-care support, telerehabilitation capabilities, physical rehabilitation, counseling and education, electrical muscle stimulation, and behavior-change techniques. These domains closely align with the functional requirements considered in our system, particularly in terms of patient education, home-based monitoring, remote communication, and behavior support. Their findings provided an evidence-based foundation that guided the selection of data elements and functional modules included in the present cardio-oncology rehabilitation application [25]. However, in the present study, electrical muscle stimulation and behavior change techniqueswere not considered necessary to be included in the application. This issue emphasises the importance of the context and specialists’ perspectives in selecting the educational materials for patients.

In this study, particular attention was paid to functions that could genuinely assist cardio-oncology patients in managing challenges that they experienced during treatment and recovery. Features such as personalized monitoring, exercise and activity guidance, nutrition and sleep support, and stress management tools allow patients to track their health more consistently and adopt healthier daily habits. Additional components, like access to medical advice and medication or comorbidity management, help strengthen treatment adherence and maintain clearer communication with healthcare providers. Prior research has shown that mhealth applications equipped with such integrated and patient-centered functions can meaningfully enhance engagement and continuity of care in cancer populations [26]. Based on this evidence and the needs identified in our assessment phase, these functions were incorporated into the design of the present application.

As mentioned before, the usability of the application was evaluated by patients and their physicians using a standard questionaire. Usability evaluation has become a cornerstone in the development of contemporary mhealth systems, particularly in high risk clinical populations such as cancer and cardio oncology patients. Beyond assessing ease of use, modern frameworks emphasize the role of users’ cognitive load, interaction efficiency, and system learnability as factors that directly influence adherence and long term clinical outcomes [27]. Similar to the current study, several recent mhealth systems developed for oncology and cardiovascular care highlighted the critical role of structured usability evaluation. For example, Dinari et al. designed and evaluated a mobile-based self-care application for patients with gastrointestinal cancer to help them manage chemotherapy-related side effects. They assessed the usability of this application using the QUIS questionnaire, and patients reported good levels of usability [28]. It is notable that in the present study, the opinions of both patients and physicians were investigated to evaluate the usability of the application, as it was provided for both group of users. Investigating users’ opinions could help to improve the application to be integrated into the clinical workflows in future [29]. Overall, the findings of this study demonstrated that a systematically designed mobile-based application can effectively address the educational, clinical, and self-management needs of cancer survivors undergoing cardiovascular rehabilitation. By integrating expert-driven content and ensuring high usability for both patients and physicians, the application provided a practical foundation for supporting symptom monitoring, lifestyle adherence, and long-term risk reduction. These results are aligned with and extend previous evidence emphasizing the value of patient-centered digital tools in rehabilitation care. With further refinement and clinical validation, the application has the potential to be integrated into routine cardio-oncology practice and serve as a scalable model for future digital rehabilitation interventions.

Research implications

The smartphone-based application developed in the current study provided a digital platform to support cardiovascular rehabilitation among cancer survivors, particularly those who are experiencing or are at risk of cardiovascular complications related to cancer treatment. By integrating educational resources, rehabilitation guidance, and patient reported health information within a single mobile environment, the application enables patients to access evidence based recommendations and follow rehabilitation strategies in their daily lives. In practice, patients can use the application to review educational materials, follow recommended rehabilitation activities, and monitor relevant health parameters, thereby promoting greater awareness and adherence to cardiovascular risk-reduction strategies. At the same time, the collected information can support clinicians in better understanding patients’ rehabilitation needs and facilitating more informed clinical decision making. Such digital solutions may complement conventional center-based rehabilitation programs by extending access to rehabilitation guidance beyond clinical settings and supporting continuous patient engagement. From a research perspective, the application provides a foundation for evaluating the effectiveness of mobile health interventions in cardio-oncology care. Future studies should investigate its impact on clinical outcomes such as treatment adherence, cardiovascular risk management, and quality of life. In addition, integrating advanced features such as personalized feedback, remote monitoring, and multimedia-based rehabilitation training may further enhance the scalability, usability, and clinical impact of similar digital health interventions.

Limitations of the study

This study had some limitations. In the first phase of the study, while the number of potential participants was a lot, a small number of physicians (about one fourth) completed the questionnaire. This might be due to their workload or their interest about the research topic. Although the number of the participants was limited, both cardio-oncologists and cardiologists took part in this study and the application was developed based on their responses. In the second phase of the research, the application was used for a limited time period, and then its usability was evaluated. As the results may not be generalized, other evaluation studies are recommended to explore the effectiveness of the application overtime.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop a smartphone-based application for cardiovascular rehabilitation in cancer survivors. Initially, educational content, data elements, and functional requirements were identified based on the physicinas’ perspectives. Then, the application was developed, and evaluated by both patients and physicians who were generally satisfied with the application. Building upon a needs-oriented framework, the application development process was guided by the cardio-oncologists and cardiovascular medicine specialis’ opinions, thereby enhancing the relevance and practical applicability of the application. By integrating structured educational materials with functional rehabilitation components, the application provided a comprehensive platform designed to support informed self-management and continuity of care. The favorable evaluations from both patients and physicians suggested that mobile health technologies may serve as a feasible and acceptable adjunct to conventional rehabilitation services, with the potential to overcome some of the accessibility and participation limitations associated with traditional center-based rehabilitation programs, particularly in facilitating access to evidence-based guidance and promoting patient engagement in long-term cardiovascular care. However, further research is required to investigate the impact of this application on the quality of life of cancer survivors with cardiovascular complications, as well as its cost-effectiveness.
