Psychometric evaluation of the persian version of Existential Concern Questionnaire (ECQ) in Iranian general population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Seyyed Muhammad Mahdi Mahdavinoor, Maede Teimouri, Aghil Mollaei, Seyyed Hatam Mahdavinoor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExistential anxiety is the anxiety that goes beyond objective threats and is related to a person’s existence. Due to the lack of appropriate instrument to measure all five domains of existential anxiety in the Iranian population, this study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the Existential Concern Questionnaire (ECQ) in the Iranian general population.MethodsThis online cross-sectional survey was conducted from October 9 to December 11, 2023 in Tehran, Iran. We used an Internet-based data collection method. A total of 549 participants completed the questionnaires. The research instruments included the Existential Concern Questionnaire, Existential Anxiety Questionnaire, Meaning in Life Questionnaire, Short version of the Beck Depression Inventory, Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale-12, and Death Attitude Profile Revised. Confirmatory factor analysis, convergent validity, and concurrent validity were used to measure the validity of ECQ. Cronbach’s alpha and split-half methods were employed to assess the reliability of the ECQ.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis showed a good fit of the unidimensional model (CMIN/DF: 2.802, RMR: 0.066, GFI: 0.914, NFI: 0.911, CFI: 0.941, PNFI: 0.769, PCFI: 0.794, RMSEA: 0.057). Also, the correlation of ECQ with other variables was as expected except for the search for meaning in life (r: 0.010, P = 0.808), which indicates the validity of this questionnaire. The reliability of the questionnaire was also acceptable (Cronbach’s Alpha: 0.935).ConclusionsThe results supported the validity and reliability of the ECQ. The mentioned questionnaire can be used in Iranian general population. Considering the importance of existential anxiety in etiology of some disorders, it is suggested that the future studies focus on validation of the ECQ in different clinical samples and cultures.Keywords: Existential anxiety, Psychometrics, Iran, ECQ, Concern

Background

So far, many psychologists and psychiatrists have discussed the concept of existential anxiety and mentioned its importance [1–5]. Existential anxiety is anxiety beyond objective threats that is related to existence as a whole [2]. In general, any threat that endangers or questions a person’s existence can cause existential anxiety. It is like an underlying discomfort that can come to the fore and sometimes debilitate a person in times of crisis. In fact, existential anxiety is a vague and distressing awareness of the insoluble problems of existence, which usually involves a non-specific feeling of danger such as the sentiment that we may die, that we are separate from others, that we are responsible for our own lives, and that we may not be able to observe our moral rules [4] .

The concept of existential anxiety can help us to understand the pathology of mental disorders [6]. Indeed, understanding the prevalence of existential anxiety and its relationship with clinical symptoms may provide important insights into disorders such as depression and anxiety disorders by elucidating the main factors in the cognitive consequences of mental problems [7]. There is an inverse relationship between existential anxiety and meaning in life, self-esteem, life satisfaction, and happiness [8, 9]. It also has a significant positive relationship with depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms [10, 11]. By examining 325 teenagers who were exposed to a violent storm, Weems et al. found that people who had higher existential anxiety were more likely to suffer from depression and PTSD [12]. Also, research has shown that there is a positive correlation between existential anxiety and experiencing trauma during life, concluding that existential anxiety is probably an interesting construct to further investigate in the assessment and treatment of PTSD [13]. After examining 210 young Indonesians, Iqbal et al. concluded that existential anxiety indicators are probably related to violent extremism [14]. Bruggen et al. suggested that existential anxiety could be an important transdiagnostic factor in understanding mental health disorders and that paying attention to it would contribute to a person-centered view of health that improves treatment methods [15].

Existential theorists have considered different dimensions of existential anxiety. For example, Yalom considers existential anxiety to have four dimensions, including death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness [5]. Tillich considered existential anxiety in three dimensions, including fate and death, emptiness and meaninglessness, and the third dimension, namely guilt and condemnation [3]. The terror management theory also considered five dimensions, including death, isolation, identity, guilt, and meaninglessness [16].

So far, various instruments have been created to measure existential anxiety. According to the systematic review conducted by Bruggen et al., up to November 2012, five instruments were developed to measure existential anxiety. After evaluating the instruments, they concluded that all but one were outdated or poorly supported. The only scale that obtained good results in validity and reliability was the Existential Anxiety Questionnaire (EAQ) But it also measures only three dimensions of existential anxiety [6].

A few years after developing the EAQ, Bruggen et al. [1] developed a new questionnaire named “Existential Concern Questionnaire (ECQ)” to measure existential anxiety with an integrative view of various theories in this field. They developed their questionnaire based on different concepts that Tillich [3], Glas [2], and Yalom [5] expressed, as well as the concepts of terror management theory [16]. The ECQ includes 22 items and has no subscales. This questionnaire is developed based on the following five theoretical domains:

Death: Death in this questionnaire has two aspects: (1) The fact that a person’s life ends at an unknown moment; (2) The threat of world as an unsafe place where something unfortunate may happen at any moment [1].

Meaninglessness

The sentiment that each person has a unique experience of the meaning in life and that the meaning of one person’s life may not convince other people. In fact, meaning systems are relative and there are many of them [1].

Guilt

In this questionnaire, guilt refers to the experience of not being able to meet one’s expectations about life [1].

Social isolation

The experience of not connecting with other people and the inability to fully share another person’s point of view [1].

Identity

Lack of complete self-awareness with inconsistency in the experience of oneself and the world [1].

Among the advantages of ECQ compared to other questionnaires, we can mention its integrated view of the 4 famous theories of this field as well as the measurement of all five dimensions of existential anxiety [1, 6].

Due to the fact that existential anxiety is somewhat dependent on culture [17–21], the psychometric properties of its instruments should be investigated in different cultures. According to the search we conducted, the psychometric properties of two instruments have been investigated in Iran including EAQ and Existential Anxiety Scale (EAS) (for example, see: [22–24]).

EAS is a 32-item self-report scale [25]. This scale was developed based on the theories of Frankel [26, 27]. The developers renamed the concept of existential vacuum, which is the focus of Frankel’s theory, to existential anxiety. It seems that this scale is more related to meaninglessness than existential anxiety. It is also not clear to what extent this scale can measure different existential concerns because it does not use anxiety or similar terms [6].

EAQ was developed by Weems et al. based on Tillich’s theory. This questionnaire has 13 items and three dimensions as follows: (1) Death and fate; (2) Emptiness and meaninglessness; (3) Guilt and condemnation. EAQ can measure the dimensions of existential anxiety separately [7]. Among the limitations of this questionnaire, it can be pointed out that it is limited to Tillich’s theory, which considers only three dimensions for existential anxiety and ignores other dimensions, such as isolation and identity.

Due to the limitation of EAS and EAQ that measure only three dimensions of existential anxiety, it seems that there is a need for a valid and reliable instrument to measure existential anxiety in Iran. Considering the strengths of the ECQ compared to other questionnaires, we decided to translate the ECQ into Persian and examine its psychometric properties in the Iranian general population.

Methods

Research question

Is the Existential Concern Questionnaire (ECQ) a valid and reliable tool to be used in the general population of Tehran?

Research design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from October 9 to December 11, 2023 in Tehran, Iran. This study was carried out according to the Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Psychological Instruments [28] and the Guideline for Developing and Validating Psychological Scales [29].

Procedure

First, a written permission to validate the ECQ was obtained from its main developer. Then, the process of translation as well as validity and reliability assessment began. The statistical population of this research included the people of Tehran who lived in this city at the time of filling out the questionnaire. We used an Internet-based data collection method. A questionnaire link was distributed in groups and channels in cyberspace, and it was mentioned that people who are not residents of Tehran should not answer. Finally, 549 people answered the questions completely. We limited the number of responses per device. Therefore, each person could answer the questionnaire only once with each device. All the items had to be answered for the final submission of the answer sheet, so we did not have any missing data.

Inclusion criteria

People who are (1) over 17 years old, (2) live in Tehran and, (3) can understand Persian.

Exclusion criteria

Unwillingness to participate in the study. Because of online submission of the questionnaires and the need for final submitting, after completing all the items, each participant could refuse to answer the items and submit them at any time.

Sample size

As a rule of thumb in validating the instruments, if the A statistical population is a general population (i.e. people of a city or country), a sample size between 375 and 500 is sufficient [30]. Also, for conducting factor analysis, a minimum sample size of 500 people is appropriate [31]. In addition, for the item-to-response ratio, the suggestions vary from 1:3 to 1:20 [29, 32]. Considering that our statistical population was the general population of Tehran and the number of ECQ items was 22, it seems that the sample size of 500 people is sufficient [29, 30, 33].

Translation and piloting

According to the Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Psychological Instruments [28], the back-translation method is mainly used for cross-cultural comparative studies, so we decided to use this method [28, 34, 35]. Two experienced translators who were fluent in English and Persian were invited independently. The first translator translated the questionnaire from English to Persian. Then, the second translator was asked to translate the translated questionnaire from Persian to English without knowing about the first translation. Afterwards, the translated text was checked by a three-person team (a philosophy expert, an English language expert, and a psychologist) to see whether it contradicted the original version. At each stage of the translation, if an item was ambiguous or there was a difference of opinion in the translation, the main developer of the questionnaire, Dr. Van Bruggen, was consulted for help.

The translated questionnaire was piloted on 20 people from the target population to modify it in case the items were difficult to understand.

Existential Concern Questionnaire (ECQ): This questionnaire was developed in 2017 by Bruggen et al. They developed their questionnaire based on different concepts that Tillich [3], Glas [2], and Yalom [5] expressed, as well as the concepts of terror management theory [16]. This 22-item questionnaire measures death anxiety, guilt, identity, social isolation, and meaninglessness. Some examples of items are as follows: “The question of whether life has meaning makes me anxious”, “It frightens me when I realize how many choices life offers”, “I worry about not being at home in the world, as if I do not belong here” and “Existence feels threatening to me, as if at any moment something terrible could happen to me”. Bruggen could not extract a subscale for this questionnaire and suggested using the total score of ECQ to measure existential anxiety. The higher the total score, the higher the existential anxiety. The developers found that ECQ has Structural, Construct, and Incremental validity in both clinical and non-clinical samples. Using test-retest method, they also found an intraclass correlation coefficient (single measures) of 0.87, n = 128, p < 0.001, 95% confidence interval (0.82, 0.91). They found Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92 in non-clinical samples [1].

Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ): This questionnaire is the most widely used questionnaire for assessing the meaning in life in various populations and cultures [36]. It is composed of two dimensions, namely the presence of meaning and search for meaning in life. Each dimension has five items. MLQ was developed by Steger et al. [37]. Mesrabadi et al. found that the MLQ has an acceptable construct validity in Iranian students [38]. Similarly, other studies also indicated the validity and reliability of this questionnaire in the Iranian population [36, 39, 40]. The answers to this questionnaire are rated on a seven-point Likert scale from completely disagree to completely agree. The minimum score for each dimension was 5 and the maximum score was 35. In the dimension of presence of meaning, the higher the score, the more valuable meaning a person has in life. In another dimension, higher scores indicate that the person is looking more for meaning to give value to life. We found Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84 for MLQ.

The Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS): It was developed by Freeston et al. in 1994 [41]. The purpose of this scale is to measure people’s tolerance to uncertain situations. It consists of 27 items that respondents answer based on a five-point Likert scale. A shortened version of this scale (IUS-12) was developed in 2007 by Carleton et al. with 12 items [42]. The answer sheet of this scale is in the form of a five-point Likert scale. The higher the overall score, the lower the tolerance of the individual against uncertainty. We used IUS-12 in this study. In the study conducted by Rashtbari et al., they found that the Persian version of IUS-12 has an acceptable convergent validity among Iranian students and estimated its Cronbach’s alpha to be 0.89 [43]. The developers of IUS-12 found Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 [42]. We found Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 for IUS-12.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed data using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v.26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Amos (Analysis of Moment Structures) v.22. Descriptive statistics was employed to describe qualitative variables using frequency and percentage frequency. To describe quantitative data, mean, standard deviation, maximum, and minimum were used. Pearson correlation test investigated the correlation between ECQ and Depression, EAQ, fear of death, meaning in life, and intolerance of uncertainty. Confirmatory factor analysis was performed using the maximum likelihood method. The fit indices CMIN/DF, RMR, GFI, NFI, CFI, PNFI, PCFI and RMSEA were used to evaluate the model fit. Cronbach’s alpha and split-half methods were employed to assess the reliability of the questionnaire. The significance level of the tests was considered to be 0.05.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

When we are going to translate and psychometrically evaluate an instrument, we can do confirmatory factor analysis based on dimensions that are already known [29, 51]. Bruggen et al. [1] in their study proposed a unidimensional model. In this research, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to see if the unidimensional model is confirmed in the Iranian general population. Figure 1 shows the confirmatory factor analysis model for ECQ as the output of Amos software (version 22). To evaluate the model fit, we used the following indices: chi-square (χ²), relative chi-square (CMIN/DF), RMR, GFI, NFI, CFI, PNFI, PCFI, and RMSEA. The chi-square index, which is a common fit index, indicates good model fit if it is not statistically significant at 0.05 level [52]. However, this index is sensitive to sample size [53]. Specifically, in large samples, it may lead to the rejection of the model, while in small samples, it loses its power. As a result, the minimum discrepancy function by degrees of freedom divided (CMIN/DF) was introduced [54]. According to CMIN/DF statistic that is used to evaluate the fit between the proposed model and the observed data, the model fit is good (values > 5 indicate good model fit) [55]. The RMSEA index reflects the control of measurement error in the model. A value < 0.08 indicates a good model fit [53]. In the present model, the RMSEA value was 0.057, which suggests that the model had a good fit. When > 0.90, NFI, CFI, and GFI indices also indicate good model fit [53]. As shown in Table 2, the values for these indices were 0.911, 0.941, and 0.914, respectively. Additionally, an RMR value ≤ 0.08 signifies a good-fitting model [56]. In this study, the RMR value was 0.066, further supporting the model’s adequacy. The rest of indices, including PNFI = 0.769 > 0.50 and PCFI = 0.794 > 0.50, also indicated good model fit [57] (Table 2).

Convergent validity

We expected that ECQ would have a strong positive correlation with other instruments of negative existential experiences. Two instruments were selected to measure fear of death and intolerance of uncertainty. We also expected that ECQ has a positive relationship with the search for meaning but a significant negative relationship with the presence of meaning in life. The results showed that the correlation between ECQ and the tolerance of uncertainty was 0.582 (P < 0.001). Fear of death was also positively correlated with existential anxiety (r: 0.504, P < 0.01). As we expected, the presence of meaning in life was negatively correlated with ECQ (r: -0.540, P < 0.001). However, no significant correlation was observed between ECQ and the search for meaning in life (r: 0.010, P = 0.808) (Table 3).

Concurrent validity

According to the literature review, it was expected that existential anxiety and depression should have a positive relationship with each other. Besides, in Iran, EAQ was used to measure existential anxiety. We expected ECQ and EAQ to have a positive relationship with each other. According to results, a positive and significant correlation of 0.670 was observed between ECQ and depression (P < 0.001). Also as expected, a positive and significant correlation was seen between EAQ and ECQ (r: 0.676, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

According to our knowledge among all the instruments measuring existential anxiety, only the psychometric properties of the EAQ in Iran have been investigated in detail [22]. It does not seem to be a perfect questionnaire because it does not measure the dimensions of isolation and identity. Our purpose in this study was to investigate the validity and reliability of the Existential Concern questionnaire (ECQ) in Iranian general population. ECQ is designed with an integrated view of different theories in this field and measures all five dimensions of existential anxiety. Based on our knowledge, there have been few investigations in this field in Iran. Considering the novelty of this research, it can shed light on the field of existential anxiety.

The results of confirmatory factor analysis showed a good fit of the unidimensional model of Bruggen et al. [1].

As predicted, the results of convergent validity study showed that existential anxiety has a strong relationship with intolerance of uncertainty, fear of death, and the presence of meaning in life. Also, Previous studies support our results [1, 8]. However, contrary to what we expected, existential anxiety and the search for meaning in life did not have a relationship with each other probably because of people’s inclination towards nihilism. Iran is a country with Muslim majority, which means that people should believe in the afterlife as a meaning in life component. Recently, some pieces of evidence have shown that some people are moving towards nihilism. This means that people are adopting the belief that there will be no more meaning in the world to give value to life. The result of this thinking, in addition to meaninglessness, is that other people do not look for meaning [58, 59]. The results of a study on students of Tehran University of Medical Sciences also showed that about 95% of students did not have meaning in life, nor were they even looking for meaning [59]. Considering that ECQ measures five dimensions of existential anxiety, only one of which is the meaning in life, if people move towards nihilism, it might lead to such a result, i.e., no relationship between the search for meaning and ECQ can be found. One of the reasons for such a result could be the age of the participants because most of them were young. However, the relationship between existential anxiety and the search for meaning can be complex and further studies are needed to fully understand the relationship between the two.

The results of concurrent validity also showed that, as we predicted, ECQ has a strong positive relationship with EAQ and depression. The relationship between existential anxiety and depression was also shown in other studies, which were in line with the results of our study [7, 11, 60, 61].

Iranian society is currently in the stage of crisis of identity and meaning of life. The seclusion of religious ceremonies, lack of participation in elections despite the insistence of religious scholars [62, 63], and recent protests [64] known as “Women, Life, Freedom” show that the people of Iran are going beyond their past beliefs. If we look at religion as a source for the meaning in life and identity [65, 66], Iranian people are gradually losing this source. Therefore, existential anxiety becomes more important in countries like Iran that are struggling with these crises.

ECQ is a new questionnaire meant to measure existential anxiety, which was developed by Bruggen et al. Due to the fact that this questionnaire was created based on different theories in this field and measures all five dimensions of existential anxiety, it seems that its use is preferred over other instruments of existential anxiety. In addition, the results of this research showed that the ECQ is a reliable questionnaire that can be used in the Iranian general population.

Limitation

Our statistical population in this study was the general population of Tehran over 17 years old. Due to the fact that the method of collecting information was internet-based, most of the participants in the study were young people. The number of middle-aged and old participants in the study was less than that of young people. In addition, since different people from a variety of cultures live in Tehran, our participants may not adequately represent the society. In this study, we used Parallel-forms method, Split-half method, and Internal consistency method to measure reliability, but due to the limitations we had, we could not use the test-retest reliability method.

Clinical implication

In this research, we examined some psychometric properties of the ECQ and found that this questionnaire is valid and reliable in the Iranian general population.

Existential anxiety is one of the problems of people nowadays. Issues such as identity, isolation, meaning in life, death and freedom have become the challenges of humanity today. These challenges can lead to existential anxiety. In Iran, there is no appropriate tool to measure existential anxiety. This questionnaire can be helpful in diagnosing the level of existential anxiety of people.

The validated ECQ can be utilized in various clinical contexts, including psychotherapy, counseling, and mental health assessments. By employing this questionnaire, clinicians can gain insights into their clients’ levels of existential anxiety, enabling them to tailor interventions more effectively.

Practical considerations for implementation include training clinicians on how to interpret the results accurately and integrate them into treatment plans. Moreover, ensuring that the questionnaire is accessible and culturally relevant to the Iranian population will enhance its utility in clinical practice.

Future direction

Each culture has a different view of human existence due to their different beliefs. Therefore, the reaction of the people of each culture to existential anxiety is likely to be different. Considering that Iran is a multicultural country, it is recommended that future researches examine the psychometric properties of this questionnaire in different Iranian cultures. These researches can give us a clearer picture of existential anxiety and clarify our vision. While the exploration of the ECQ across different Iranian cultures is a valuable avenue, it would be beneficial to extend this inquiry to include a variety of cultural contexts beyond Iran. This cross-cultural examination could reveal how existential anxiety manifests differently across diverse populations, thereby enriching our understanding of its universal and culture-specific aspects.

Moreover, incorporating longitudinal studies into future research is of importance. By assessing the stability and evolution of existential anxiety over time, researchers can gain insights into how these concerns develop and change in response to life events, personal growth, and societal shifts.

Lastly, investigating the utility and applicability of the ECQ in various clinical populations—such as individuals with mental health disorders, chronic illnesses, or those undergoing significant life transitions—would provide critical information about the questionnaire’s robustness and versatility.

In summary, future research should consider a comprehensive approach that includes cross-cultural studies, longitudinal assessments, and evaluations within diverse clinical settings to fully explore the complexities of existential anxiety.
