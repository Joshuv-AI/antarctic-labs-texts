Knowledge graph embedding and alignment of incomplete electronic health records for critical care applications
Authors: Shervin Mehryar, Michel Dumontier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData-driven AI models in clinical care increasingly rely on integrating data from heterogeneous sources to improve predictive performance. Traditional Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems however pose significant challenges for data integration due to the use of diverse standards and inconsistent semantics. Personalized Health Knowledge Graphs (PHKG), which leverage biomedical ontologies to encode and interconnect clinical knowledge, have emerged as a promising solution for unifying heterogeneous health data. Yet the process of PHKG integration can result in incomplete and ambiguous representations.ObjectiveOur primary objective is to apply advanced embedding techniques to mitigate the incompleteness problem in PHKGs. We propose a framework that combines schema-based semantic integration, domain-specific ontology alignment, and patient context representations through embedding techniques to improve incomplete and ambiguous PHKG representations. We further demonstrate the framework’s capabilities for producing enriched representations in a use case concerning cardiovascular outcome prediction by training machine learning models which utilize the learned embeddings.ResultsWe embed EHR from a critical care unit as structured PHKGs mapped with two different schemas for comparison across knowledge completion and alignment tasks following a modular design. Each module is individually optimized using three baseline embedding methods with enhanced loss functions. We evaluate each task for both accuracy and semantic consistency and show the contribution of each module to the overall performance. We find and report settings for each module in which the proposed framework outperforms baseline results. The learned representations are subsequently used to generate patient contexts for the task of Heart Failure diagnosis as a use case. Our experiments demonstrate that semantically enhanced PHKG embeddings achieve better precision and recall scores compared to baseline models.ConclusionOur proposed method addresses the challenges in generating heterogenuous Personalized Health Knowledge Graphs (PHKG) through a modular framework that integrates schema mapping, ontology alignment, and contextual patient embeddings. Results on real-world patient records support the potential for improved performance in clinical decision making. Particularly in scenarios where sparse and fragmented health records prove problematic for data driven applications, our method can provide a robust approach for the disambiguation and completion of coded information. Our implementation is available at: https://github.com/AIDAVA-DEV/kge-framework.Keywords: Personalized health knowledge graph, Knowledge graph embedding, Electronic health records, MIMIC, System interoperability, SPHN, SNOMED CT, Cardiovascular disease diagnosis

Introduction

The amount of data stored as electronic health records (EHR) has grown significantly and now presents new opportunities for data-driven and AI applications. Many new use-cases have emerged as a result ranging from clinical event trajectory forecasting [1] and readmission prediction [2] to explainable medical code prediction [3].

While access to data is an essential part of future AI developments, integrating data across traditional EHR systems remains challenging due to diverse data models and inconsistent standards that are continually created [4]. The existence of numerous data models is inevitable as sources are developed independently and have different requirements in terms of coverage and granularity of data [5]. Post-hoc harmonization of data sources using existing standards, models, and coding schemes is a resource-intensive and time-consuming process [6]. In real-world applications, it requires record aggregation, schema transformation, and the generation of consistent, unambiguous representations for downstream AI tasks [7]. The post-coordination required to make traditional EHR systems interoperable is as such highly demanding. In its absence, data representations will remain fragmented, hindering model development and the ability to perform effective querying and analysis [8].

Personalized Health Knowledge Graphs (PHKGs) on the other hand provide a structured approach for integrating EHR, with the flexibility to incorporate both established and emerging clinical standards and terminologies [9]. Data models based on diverse standards can be transformed into PHKGs, where entities are represented under relevant classes and annotated with attributes that accommodate variations in granularity and semantic richness. Health information related to disparate patients can be integrated under core clinical categories and in a personalized manner at scale. The ability to align with specific ontologies further allows for domain specialization and contextualized representations in PHKGs. The PHKG therefore can capture rich semantics across heterogeneous sources and encode a wealth of existing medical knowledge for AI development.

Transforming EHR data into PHKGs introduces several chalenges, as depicted in Fig. 1. Firstly, original data sources require mapping to the concepts and attributes of the target PHKG structure. Secondly, existing information within a knowledge source must be precisely and unambiguously integrated into the PHKG. Lastly, the context surrounding a patient’s data should be sufficiently captured to enable effective algorithm development. Inaccuracies, including missing contextual details or ambiguous coding, can degrade predictive performance. These challenges are further explored and addressed in the following sections.

Schema-based semantic annotation

In order to integrate heterogeneous sources into PHKGs, established schema and agreed upon data models can be leveraged [10]. Sources can be mapped to a fixed schema specific to clinical data interoperability to prevent incorrect identifications and overcome fragmentation [11]. The Swiss Personal Health Network (SPHN) schema for instance is one such model that enables the semantic and technical interoperability of EHR data by providing a blueprint for mapping heterogeneous local and hospital-specific datasets onto a unified format. Implementation however involves manual inspection, programmatic mapping, view generation and transformation into Resource Description Framework (RDF) triples [12, 13].

To address challenges in schema-based integration, another approach in [9] decouples formalizations of data by dividing the problem into a local versus a global view. At local level, data elements related to disease diagnosis, medical procedures, admission types and intents are codified employing several terminologies. Schema-level mapping through OMOP (a data model for observational databases) around lower-layer concepts (e.g. diagnosis, admission, drug, prescription, etc) is applied for cross-source and global interoperability. The terminologies that are used therein are of relatively flat structure, thus resulting in the lack of important context. For instance,“Type 2 Diabetes” should but may not link to“insulin resistance”, “obesity”, or “risk of neuropathy” in a machine-readable way. Globally aligning these entities across multiple sources would therefore require several bootstrapping steps to disambiguate their semantic representations.

Domain-specific ontology alignment

In order to disambiguate and contextualize knowledge with unified representations, domain specific alignment of data according to clinical and biomedical ontologies can be adopted [14]. Well curated biomedical ontologies exist, such as Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms1 (SNOMED CT), that are best suited for medical applications needing high quality and personalized context. Sources which use different vocabularies, for instance the International Classification of Diseases2 (ICD) for classifying diseases, symptoms, and medical procedures, can benefit from alignment to SNOMED CT. As more sources are aligned with the same domain-specific ontology, it has the added benefit that larger amounts of data are available for model training due to interoperability. Despite such benefits, approaches for ontology alignment remain limited, requiring extensive a prior analysis and manual effort to implement [3].

A number of approaches have attempted to explore the potential of ontology alignment for PHKG integration through representation learning [15, 16]. MEDTO [17] proposes a graph representation learning approach that maps tabular data sources to a domain specific ontology to overcome post-coordination issues. In particular, tables are mapped to SNOMED CT for context enrichment such that the context of “renal failure” for instance appears as a diagnosis condition annotated with “measurements of renal function” and “kidney structure” locally and with types “clinical finding”, “procedure”, and “body structure” globally. Alignment to a domain specific ontology therefore can mitigate the presence of ambiguous information in local sources [3].

Clinical care model development

In order to extract and apply enriched data for model training in downstream tasks, e.g. diagnosis code prediction, the contextual data must be void of ambiguity. When the above operations are done manually or semi-automatically by clinical data stewards, the final PHKG is often incomplete because certain facts are unknown or due to error at multiple possible points in the process. Important context related to a patient’s conditions therefore can be missing in the PHKG that will have a negative effect on decision making. Particularly for the critical care use of AI models, the reliance on data that is well curated plays an important role.

As a case in point, the ICD system is typically used for insurance payments, health reports, or billing as opposed to critical care decisions inside hospitals. While decision support systems for billing or insurance may be able to tolerate inaccuracies in data to a certain degree, in other scenarios incompleteness can lead to more serious and in the worst case life-threatening consequences for a subject under treatment [18]. As previously argued, the inherent limitations of such coding systems necessitate the ontological alignment of raw data for more expressiveness. Knowledge completion strategies can further be applied to uncover latent or missing relationships for specialized clinical model development [19].

The main goal of this work is to address the challenges in transforming EHR records into scalable PHKG representations for AI-ready clinical applications. We investigate the impact of schema mapping and domain-specific ontology alignment on PHKG integration and propose a robust framework for consolidating tabular EHR data from critical care units into semantically consistent health knowledge graphs. Source data are transformed according to different schema specifications. State-of-the-art knowledge graph embedding methods are applied to implicitly complete missing context. We examine the role of ontology alignment in improving the quality of learned representations by linking semantically related entities across sources. Our central hypothesis posits that alignment with a domain-specific ontology refines ambiguous concepts, leading to superior predictive performance.

To showcase its practical utility, we apply the learned representations to a diagnosis task targeting patients with heart failure conditions. Heart Failure (HF) represents a rapidly growing and high-risk category of cardiovascular disease, associated with substantial mortality and significant healthcare costs when not diagnosed in a timely manner [20]. The performance is evaluated using precision, recall, f1, and area-under-the-curve scores, to quantify how much knowledge completion and ontology alignment contribute to data-driven predictive accuracy in critical care settings. Feature-based importance scores are also computed to improve interpretability for model’s output probabilities.

Methodology

In order to predict clinical outcomes we propose a two stage framework for constructing a personalized health knowledge graph using codified data. In stage one, patient-centric knowledge graphs are constructed by embedding entities and relations into a shared vector space. In the second stage, integrated and contextualized representations are leveraged in order to perform high accuracy code prediction for our use case. We provide details for the implementation of each stage in the following.

In stage one as depicted in Fig. 2, we begin by embedding associated patient records into a common vector space and utilize the mechanism of link prediction for completing data with a patient-centric view. The patient views are created by integrating EHR records sampled and grouped by admission identifiers (Step 1). The extracted information are then mapped to core concepts and relations from a fixed RDF schema (Step 2) to form the PHKGs. The constructed PHKGs are embedded using a number of translational embedding methods. The embedding vectors are further enhanced through subsumption and instance checking for reasoning capabilities (Step 3). Using link prediction the missing data are further completed (Step 4) and embeddings are refined through alignment with a reference ontology (Step 5).

Overview of the EHR embedding framework and our contributions: 1) multi-source integration; 2) automated mapping of tables to schemas; 3) PHKG embedding generation and enhancement; 4) link prediction for PHKG completion; 5) alignment with domain specific ontologies

The collection of embedded records across patient admissions allows for effective diagnosis prediction as done through contextualized retrieval in stage two, depicted in Fig. 5. For this purpose, we retrieve the relevant context from a patient knowledge graph by leveraging reification to model relationships such as ‘has procedure code’, ‘has lab code’, and ‘has drug code’. Reification enables the explicit representation of associations between patients and clinical concepts encoded by terminologies such as LOINC3, ICD, and SNOMED CT. By incorporating these relational as well as embedded representations into predictive modeling, essential context is established for facilitating high precision, high recall data-driven decision-making.

Demonstration of use case for diagnosis prediction based on contextualized embeddings related to prescriptions, drugs, and procedures from a patient’s PHKG, retrieved, reified and fed into a binary classifier for outcome prediction

To summarize, relevant patient entities are first extracted from multiple relational data sources and assigned unique identifiers based on a fixed RDF schema. The result PHKG is subsequently transformed and vectorized into translational embeddings, excluding class axiomatic and hierarchical information. In the next module, the embeddings are enhanced using instance checking and subsumption rules. Subsequently, entities are extracted and aligned with a domain specific medical ontology in order to improve embedded representations. At the end of this stage, patient codes and embeddings are retrieved and passed through the last module where clinical diagnosis prediction is performed as a use case. In what follows we provide a detailed description about EHR datasets, RDF schemas, reference ontology, embedding techniques, and predictive modeling that are used to develop the proposed framework.

PHKG generation

In order to generate PHKGs form input relational data, table entries are mapped to core concepts retrieved from the RDF schema with foreign keys establishing links between entities across tables (e.g. linking a diagnosis to a patient). Rows are identified with unique Uniform Resource Identifiers (URI), as instances of the corresponding RDF class using admission numbers corresponding to one or more visits as the primary key. Column attributes (e.g. “Diagnosis Code”) are mapped to retrieved RDF predicates (e.g. “has code”). Biomedical codes (e.g. ICD, NDC, etc) are transformed into unique identifiers to resolve semantically equivalent instances. After resolving and mapping all entities, the final knowledge graph is generated in N-Triple format with semantically consistent resources linked within the scope of each entry (e.g. lab test or prescription correctly associated with relevant admission).

Datasets

We use the MIMIC repository containing tabular data for patients to ultimately generate triples that form a PHKG for training and evaluation purposes. All records for a set of patients are extracted according to predefined criteria. The criteria by which the records are obtained include the intent for hospitalization, table names and concepts, as well as entity types and relations. The intent for hospitalization can be set in order to limit the scope of the task to a particular use case. In this work, records from patients that are hospitalized for Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) are separated based on their ICD codes. Since many tables already exist as data sources, the scope is further narrowed down to main concepts related to any medication prescribed, lab results and measurements taken, hospital procedures performed, diagnoses made, and patient demographic information recorded. The relevant data are stored in separate tables and can be as such processed. Within each table, a subset of columns and relations are selected by examination, according to an ontology of choice, as explained in subsequent sections. The dataset is provided in two versions which we include to account for the effect of infrastructural changes and versioning in the dataset.

MIMIC III - the first version of the repository that we use contains data associated with 53,423 distinct hospital admissions for adult patients (aged 16 years or above) admitted to critical care units between 2001 and 2012. The data covers 38,597 distinct adult patients and 49,785 hospital admissions. The median age of adult patients is 65.8 years, 55.9% patients are male, and in-hospital mortality is 11.5%. In addition, it contains data for 7870 neonates admitted between 2001 and 2008. The median length of an ICU stay is 2.1 days and the median length of a hospital stay is 6.9 days. A mean of 4579 charted observations (‘chartevents’) and 380 laboratory measurements (‘labevents’) are available for each hospital admission [21].

MIMIC IV - the second version of the repository that we use improves upon the previous version by covering a more recent time period (2008–2019) and offering a modular structure, dividing data into core, hospital, and ICU components for easier access. It expands beyond ICU data to include hospital-wide information, increasing the dataset’s scope and diversity. MIMIC-IV also enhances data quality and uses both ICD-9 and ICD-10 coding reflecting disparities in coding versions. These updates make MIMIC-IV a more comprehensive and flexible resource for clinical research compared to MIMIC-III. Among other reasons, we are interested in understanding the effect of version-based variations by including the second repository.

Specifically, we focus on a smaller subset related to CVD diagnosis and separate admissions encoded by ICD-9 code range 410–430, such as 428.22 (Chronic systolic heart failure), 428.23 (Acute on chronic systolic heart failure), 428.32 (Chronic diastolic heart failure), 428.33 (Acute on chronic diastolic heart failure), 428.42 (Chronic combined systolic and diastolic heart failure), and 428.43 (Acute on chronic combined systolic and diastolic heart failure). These codes categorize various forms and severities of heart failure based on the systolic and diastolic dysfunction of the heart. In ICD-10, these codes are largely replaced by categories under I50 (Heart Failure). To generate this subset, we identify and store the admissions for those patients that have at least one of the above ICD codes associated with them and exclude any diagnosis codes outside the above scope for our final set of patients.

Schema-based semantic annotation

In this section we describe the process of data to schema mapping, also referred to as semantic annotation, for knowledge graph generation based on the groundings of concepts and relations from a reference schema. The role of the reference is to provide type annotations for entities given a set of tables. We represent relations among entities and columns similar to nodes and concepts in the reference schema/ontology. This view subsequently allows the construction of an embedding space in which the entities from the flat mapping of table data are aligned with the relevant part in the reference, whereby entity and column type annotations become node and link predictions, respectively. In this work, we consider the following two schemas.

SPHN4- is the first fixed RDF schema that we use in order to generate patient-centric PHKGs. The Swiss Personal Health Network (SPHN) model is a schema that defines core concepts and predicates for modeling clinical patient records (i.e. EHR) for the purpose of seamless and extensible interoperability. SPHN is deliberately adopted to overcome the heterogeneity and fragmentation of electronic health record data. Its flexible core design allows seamless adaptation to diverse use cases, such as critical care or heart failure, while preserving semantic depth through precisely defined clinical concepts and native integration with external standards (e.g. SNOMED CT) [12].

In particular, we focus on 14 core concepts, namely, ‘LabTestEvents’, ‘LabResult’, ‘Code’, ‘DrugPrescription’, ‘Drug’, ‘Substance’, ‘Diagnosis’, ‘BilledProcedure’, ‘AdministrativeCase’, ‘SubjectPseudoIdentifier’, ‘BirthDate’, ‘MedicalProcedure’, ‘BodySite’, and ‘AdministrativeGender’. We also consider an additional concept named ‘Patient’ in order to model the individual patients. As for predicates, we model a total of 9, namely, ‘hasCode’, ‘hasLabTest’, ‘hasAdministrativeCase’, ‘hasSubjectPseudoIdentifier’, ‘hasDrug’, ‘hasActiveIngredient’, ‘hasAdministrationRoute’, ‘hasBirthdate’, ‘hasAdministrativeGender’, to capture the relations between the entities. Additionally, we include ‘is a’ relation to indicate the type assertions, ‘rdfs:subClassOf’ to indicate membership, and ‘owl:sameAs’ to indicate equivalent codes.

MGDB - is the second schema we use in order to generate and compare the quality of PHKGs, which is developed based on a MIMIC schema implemented in GraphDB (MGDB) by [10]. It contains a total of 9 predicates and 11 concepts, selected to represent EHR data of patients related to diagnoses, procedures, lab tests, and medications. The main feature of the ontology is its tree-like structure tailored for modeling MIMIC ICU stays. Particularly, diagnosis and procedures represented with a medical code are represented through specific entities of ICU Stay admission type, which we deem particularly useful for admission based diagnosis prediction. All codes related to prescriptions, diagnosis, and procedures are instances of type Codes, while lab items are encoded separately.

The task of mapping data to the above reference schemas can be performed by optimizing three sets of loss functions, namely ontology-view, instance-view, and anchor loss using our previous method developed in [22]. The instance-view loss function captures the information directly contained in tables, similar to the concept of triples which represent facts in knowledge graphs, by viewing two cell entries in one row of the table as a (flat) triple related by the corresponding column attribute. The instance view loss learns a set of embeddings for cell and column headers, and the relation between them. To support one-to-many, many-to-one, and qualifier relations, an added loss term is introduced which also ensures global and structural properties of tables are preserved.

The set of triples from the reference ontology are embedded separately, by extracting relevant parts of the entire source before embedding. Given a potentially large, reference ontology and a corresponding set of data-grounded concepts (e.g. diagnosis, etc), the relevant information from the source ontology can be extracted by entity type and property type queries using ‘rdf:type’ and ‘sub-class’ predicates down to child nodes. This process is not expected to be perfect at this stage due to noise, variations, missing information, and other sources of error in the data. However it can performed to provide sufficiently large coverage for annotations. Lastly, anchor nodes between the instance view and the ontology view entities are identified and the representations (including schema and table values) are re-optimized via the proposed anchoring technique until alignment between the two representation spaces is achieved, neuro-symbolically. The output of this process is an RDF representation of the tabular data where entity, property, and column types, as well as hierarchical semantics, are derived from the reference SPHN and MGDB schemas.

Domain-specific ontology alignment

In order to generate the PHKG from semantically annotated entities as described above, the RDF data for each patient is grouped across visits and stored with unique URIs as instances of the corresponding RDF class. A patient’s record can specifically include coded entities from one or multiple coding systems - ICD for Diagnosis and Procedures, LOINC for Lab Results, and NDC (National Drug Code) for drugs/prescriptions. All clinical codes are transformed and consolidated with unique identifiers to ensure semantic equivalence across sources. After resolving and mapping all entities, the final patient knowledge graph is generated in N-Triple format with semantically consistent resources that are in scope, i.e. lab test or prescription correctly associated with relevant admission. These coding systems are both comprehensive and extensive, however lack use-case specific ontological structure. It stands therefore to reason that creating and enriching a knowledge-graph-embedding based representation of patient data aligned with a domain specific ontology can prove more suitable for diagnosis prediction.

SNOMED CT - is a comprehensive, multilingual healthcare terminology used to encode clinical information in a consistent and structured way. It enables the representation of clinical concepts such as diagnoses, procedures, and symptoms. SNOMED CT defines clinical terms through structured relations between concepts, enabling detailed descriptions such as ‘Pain in the left arm’ defined with ‘finding site’ connected to anatomical site ‘left arm’. SNOMED CT features allow users to compose new clinical expressions from existing terms as shown in Fig. 3. This mechanism facilitates precise clinical data modeling and improves the extraction of structured information from medical records [23]. Nevertheless, SNOMED CT codification is absent from the relational datasets used in this work. MIMIC-III primarily uses ICD-9 and MIMIC-IV uses the expansion of ICD terminology and its modular structure using ICD-10 system. Mapping diagnosis codes to SNOMED CT, it is our hypothesis, could improve the quality of PHKGs for downstream tasks.

The PHKG-SSN alignment contains a single mapping for the ICD code in either version to SNOMED CT without its corresponding matched subgraph following membership rule ‘is a’ or ‘rdfs:subClassOf’. Each code mapped according to its hierarchical path origin as provided by Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) [24]. Given that UMLS allows the same concept to appear in multiple hierarchies, we selected the most comprehensive hierarchical paths to ensure thorough coverage. We derived comprehensive information for each mapped concept, tracing the lineage from the top-level ancestor to the immediate parent, removing ambiguous paths and selected a single match for generating PHKG-SSN, as shown in Fig. 3.

To help disambiguate ICD coded records and enrich their semantic context, we created the PHKG-MSN variation including all possible matches from SNOMED CT. For any code with multiple ambiguous matches, each match is added using a conjunctive relation ‘and’ along with their respective membership graphs through ‘is a’ or ‘rdfs:subClassOf’ relations. The combination of membership and multi-code extensions using SNOMED CT is believed to improve the semantic richness and hierarchical structure of the PHKG even further, specifically for the task of knowledge graph completion as well as the downstream of task of diagnosis code prediction, explained in subsequent sections.

PHKG completion

Knowledge graph embedding is an approach to represent entities and relationships in a knowledge graph as low dimensional vectors, also known as embeddings. This technique allows practitioners to leverage the structural information present in knowledge graphs for a range of applications ranging from link prediction and node classification to relation extraction, categorized as completion task [25–27]. In this section we provide details related to this task specifically for PHKG completion and use in alignment with a reference ontology.

Knowledge graph completion is the task of discovering facts in the form of non-existing triples in order to expand the knowledge base or recover missing information. Specifically, using the existing knowledge in RDF N-triple format, embedding methods can be applied to learn vector representations for nodes and relations. Formally, embedded representations are learned for entities and relations in the PHKG assuming translation vector algebra hold between entities, so that the embedding of the tail entity is “almost” equal to the summation (or dot product) of the embedding of the head entity and the embedding of the relation. The loss for a single fact in this triple format is expressed as the second norm of the discrepancy between the translated vector and tail embedding. The extension to K-hop reasoning follows the path-based strategy so that the translation vector algebra hold over multiple relations (i.e. hops) from a head entity to the K-th tail entity. Unseen (missing) values can be predicted (imputed) using this setup where the target node is of the relevant type (e.g. diagnosis code). This is applied to complete the missing links in the PHKG with respect to coded entries.

In a parallel task, we align the embeddings from coded entries within the PHKG with embeddings from the selected domain specific ontology. This is motivated by the containment of morphologically rich and structurally precise knowledge from another source [28]. The approach in [29] shows that aligned embeddings can predict missing hierarchical relationships in the source terminology set without relying on lexical features. Their approach however focuses on improving internal representations based on other structured parts of the same ontology (i.e. still within the same ontology). In our approach, we focus on improving information contained within the PHKG and apply embeddings to enrich its hierarchical information with respect to an external reference ontology, specifically between ICD (internal) and SNOMED CT (external).

Given a set of patient-centric triplets describing information related to a patient visit from the relevant part of the PHKG, in our case embeddings are learned to subsequently perform reasoning tasks around patients having certain unseen diagnoses. In particular, for patients with recorded procedures, lab observations, and drug prescriptions, the task of embedding based inference is to predict missing diagnosis codes from a patient’s history. In this vein, it is deemed crucial that the embedding method supports transitive properties as well as multi-hop reasoning. Therefore we choose translational embedding methods, namely TransE, TorusE, and RotatE, and further enhance their performance for inferring subsumption and instance assertions using our algorithm previously developed in [30]. The details on how the embeddings are reused in form of pre-trained representations for context-based diagnosis prediction are given in the next section.

Outcome prediction

The representations trained based on the knowledge completion task described above can be reused for clinical purposes such as diagnosis prediction given the context vector representation of a patient. In a recent study, embeddings generated based on MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV datasets have been applied to the tasks of mortality prediction, readmission prediction, length of stay prediction, and drug recommendation [31]. Follow up work has further focused on applications such as comorbidity [32] and risk factor evaluation [33]. In our work, we are interested in diagnosis code prediction based on patient admission records. In particular, we formulate the problem as a binary classification task where the outcome is positive when a patient has been diagnosed with a diagnosis code and negative otherwise. We propose a pipeline as shown in Fig. 5, for retrieval and reification of patient properties along with their pre-trained embeddings to perform binary outcome prediction.

For the use case of diagnosis prediction, embeddings trained through the process described in the previous section are utilized. For a given patient, firstly the relevant context is retrieved using a SPARQL query along with the vector representations for retrieved nodes using the pre-trained embeddings. A binary classification method is then employed that performs outcome prediction based on retrieved embeddings as input features. The binary outcome determines whether the patient has been diagnosed with a given diagnosis code. We utilize a Logistic Regression (LR) model for outcome prediction in our use case5. The next section provides the details of experimentation along with results that demonstrate the prediction and semantic capabilities of the proposed scheme.

Additionally, we perform ablation experiments to further understand the role of data source versioning, scale, and semantic consistency on different source datasets.

Link prediction for clinical codes

The completion task over patient EHR is performed by embedding the PHKGs ingested from tabular data and transformed through schema mapping. Given the use case, we focus on link prediction for clinical codes. In particular, the patient data are grouped by admission ID, resulting in a PHKG that includes procedures (by ICD codes), prescriptions (by NDC codes) and lab events (by LOINC codes), unified through unique identifiers. We thereby embed and perform code prediction over the PHKG data for completion.

Patient records are extracted from 200 admissions sampled at random and embedded using the PyKeen6 library, through three different translation based methodologies, i.e. TransE, RotatE, and TorusE. Impact of ontology alignment on linked prediction

One of the key contributions of the proposed method is due to the alignment of coded entities onto a reference ontology. In particular, we utilize the SNOMED CT ontology to enrich the ICD codifications. In each version of the data source, the ICD codes are matched with their SNOMED counter parts as one-to-one or one-to-many (if exists) relations using an added predicate. In the MIMIC III structured data that are integrated, the 149 ICD codes as such are extended to 570 SNOMED codes (including 421 one-to-many cases). In the MIMIC IV structured data that are integrated, the 386 ICD codes are extended to 2856 SNOMED codes (including 2470 one-to-many cases). We further extract the subsumption graph associated with “is a” and “subclass of” relations from SNOMED CT ontology. The subsumption graph extensions result in an addition of 1360 and 12,380 nodes capturing the hierarchy of SNOMED codes over ICD 9 and ICD 10 codes, respectively, that add to their semantic richness.

The impact of ontology extension in terms of NDCG@10 results are summarized in Fig. 7 for 200 randomly sampled admissions over MIMIC III (with ICD 9 codes) and MIMIC IV (ICD 10 codes). For all embedding methods, mapping the coded data to the SNOMED ontology concepts (denoted domain-specific ontology alignment or DSOA) consistently exhibits an increase of 0.01 over no extension mapping (denoted None). The former case corresponds to PHKG-MSN shown in Fig. 3 as opposed to PHKG-ICD, which contains patient records ingested into their PHKG with ICD codes only. Combined with other variations, the effect of DSOA at scale is further highlighted due to reduction in ambiguous representations it affects.

Optimal configuration of models

The proposed framework is initially intended to leverage disambiguated representations in order to address the challenge of missing information (i.e. clinical context and codes) in PHKG integration. To this end, schema-based transformations of EHR are performed followed by the alignment between clinical terminologies and biomedical ontologies. The former allows extention to domain specific coding systems while the latter provides important context for semantic enrichment of representations. By embedding the PHKGs into a vector space, knowledge completion through link prediction can be achieved. The embeddings from the completed and contextualized representations can ultimately be used as input feature vectors for predictive model training and development in clinical applications.

Link prediction through learned embeddings can be performed efficiently using addition and scalar product operations. Recent research has shown that transitive properties can explicitly be encoded using translational operations to enhance inference [30]. Among translational embedding methods that could be equipped with reasoning capabilities, TorusE achieved highest results for the task of diagnosis link prediction as depicted in Fig. 11. We observe that by introducing SNOMED CT alignment highlighted as DSOA, the NDCG scores of the learned embeddings consistently rise above levels at which reliable retrieval and ranking can be performed for clinical model development.

Carvedilol as a beta-blocker useful for blood circulation is ranked reasonably high as a indicator for ICD-9 428.32 condition. It is worth mentioning that our work has limited scope in that patient histories are not accounted for. While it is ensured that the EHR data are structured into distinct PHKGs per administrative visit from which context vectors are drawn, the causal relations between visits for a given patient are not tracked. While this would be beneficial in certain clinical and medical environments, we argue that for the scope of ICU patient data defined in this study it is of a lesser factor. Prior work had been dedicated to inter-visit cause and effect investigation as in [47] which as such falls out of the scope in this paper. Furthermore, while we use patient context and encoded data, we don’t incorporate numerical insights in the same sense that clinical staff would analyze lab and other reports. This would require further analysis, for example in the case of lab events where range or average measurements are exceeded. Future work should be dedicated therefore to the processing of prescriptions, lab events, and procedural outcome levels. With the modular design paradigm followed in our framework, such extensions are deemed feasible and thus left as future research direction.

It must further be noted that the contribution values are calculated as conditional probabilities, given the true label, for analysis purposes. In practice, these labels are not provided apriori and therefore the reported feature contributions are not trustworthy for individual decision making. Therefore, the use of the proposed methodology for integrating missing diagnosis codes in knowledge graph completion tasks for instance, must require medical validation. Furthermore, since the treatment-diagnosis relationships are not explicitly stated in our datasets, the aggregation mechanism used for computing patient representations disregards causal relation between features (e.g. lab event resulting in a diagnosis or a medication). More importantly, the diagnosis codes are not medical diagnosis and provided primarily for insurance purposes in the datasets [18]. Utilizing higher-quality visit histories with annotations and result validation by experts towards medical system integration is left as future research direction.

Conclusion

In this work we investigate the capabilities of advanced knowledge graph embedding techniques for EHR data modeling, codification, and integration. In particular, we propose a framework that processes tabular patient records into personalized health knowledge graphs given a fixed schema, followed by an alignment step with respect to a domain specific biomedical ontology. The semantically annotated and mapped data are embedded using translational embedding models equipped with RDF/s reasoning capabilities for link prediction. The refined representations are further used to contextualize patient information and perform diagnosis tasks with machine learning. Our experiments demonstrate that in cardiovascular outcome prediction based on real-world data, improved scores can be achieved over the state-of-the-art results. Particularly on datasets from critical care units, the proposed approach in this work robustly recovers from losses in recall and ultimately F1 scores due to ambiguous and incomplete context for outcome prediction. For the task of Heart Failure prediction, using learned representations via our knowledge graph embedding and alignment framework for classifier training outperform existing methods. The framework further enables feature-based interpretation of the output decisions, thereby enabling the examination of entity contributions for predicted outcome and explainability.
