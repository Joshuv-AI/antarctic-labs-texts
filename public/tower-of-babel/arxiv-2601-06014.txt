On the Effect of Misspecifying the Embedding Dimension in Low-rank Network Models
Roddy Taing, Keith Levin

Abstract
As network data has become ubiquitous in the sciences, there has been growing interest in network models whose structure is driven by latent node-level variables in a (typically low-dimensional) latent geometric space. These “latent positions” are often estimated via embeddings, whereby the nodes of a network are mapped to points in Euclidean space so that “similar” nodes are mapped to nearby points. Under certain model assumptions, these embeddings are consistent estimates of the latent positions, but most such results require that the embedding dimension be chosen correctly, typically equal to the dimension of the latent space. Methods for estimating this correct embedding dimension have been studied extensive in recent years, but there has been little work to date characterizing the behavior of embeddings when this embedding dimension is misspecified. In this work, we provide theoretical descriptions of the effects of misspecifying the embedding dimension of the adjacency spectral embedding under the random dot product graph, a class of latent space network models that includes a number of widely-used network models as special cases, including the stochastic blockmodel. We consider both the case in which the dimension is chosen too small, where we prove estimation error lower-bounds, and the case where the dimension is chosen too large, where we show that consistency still holds, albeit at a slower rate than when the embedding dimension is chosen correctly. A range of synthetic data experiments support our theoretical results. Our main technical result, which may be of independent interest, is a generalization of earlier work in random matrix theory, showing that all non-signal eigenvectors of a low-rank matrix subject to additive noise are delocalized.

2
Setup and Modeling

We are concerned in the present work with network models in which the adjacency matrix given by a low-rank signal matrix subject to additive noise.
Models of this form are often termed “signal-plus-noise” models in the literature

(
15
;
82
, see, e.g.,)
.
We thus consider

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E},

(1)

where
\mathbf{E}
is a mean-zero noise matrix and
\mathbf{P}
is an
n\times n
symmetric matrix of rank
r
whose spectral decomposition is given by

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}_{1:r}\mathbf{S}_{1:r}\mathbf{U}_{1:r}^{\top},

(2)

where
\mathbf{S}_{1:r}
is a diagonal matrix of non-zero eigenvalues sorted in non-increasing order (
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}>0
) and
\mathbf{U}_{1:r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is the matrix of corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors.

In latent variable network models such as the SBM, RDPG and variants thereof discussed in Section
1
,
\mathbf{P}
is expressible as

\mathbf{P}=\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}=\left(\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}\right)\left(\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}\right)^{\top},

where the rows of
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
encode vertex-level
r
-dimensional latent variables and
\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
can be interpreted as a signal strength parameter.
We consider in this work the task of recovering the scaled latent structure
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
from the observed
\mathbf{A}
.
From the spectral decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
in Equation (
2
), we may take

\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}_{1:r}\mathbf{S}^{1/2}_{1:r}.

(3)

An obstacle arises from the observation that for any
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
,

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W})(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W})^{\top}.

(4)

That is, any orthogonal rotation of
\mathbf{X}
gives rise to the same expected adjacency matrix
\mathbf{P}
.
Thus, in estimating the scaled latent positions
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
, we must account for this non-identifiability.
Specifically, we will be content with estimators that recover
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
up to orthogonal rotation as in the above display equation.

The expression in Equation (
3
) suggests a natural approach to estimating
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
, namely using the leading eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the observed
\mathbf{A}
in place of those of
\mathbf{P}
.
This motivates the adjacency spectral embedding, first considered in
72
.

Definition 1
(Adjacency Spectral Embedding).

Given a real, symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
, whose spectral decomposition is given by

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}\mathbf{\hat{S}}\mathbf{\hat{U}}^{\top},

we define the
d
-dimensional adjacency spectral embedding
(
72
, ASE;)
of
\mathbf{A}
to be

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:d}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{1:d}|\mathbf{\hat{S}}|^{1/2}_{1:d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The vast majority of recent work on models like the one just described evaluates estimation error via the maximum Euclidean norm between the true structure
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
and its estimate
(
7
;
67
;
54
;
16
;
56
, see, e.g.,)
, after accounting for non-identifiability.
That is, a good estimator
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
will be one that achieves small values of

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2,\infty},

where
\left\lVert\mathbf{Z}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}
is defined the be the maximum Euclidean norm of the rows of matrix
\mathbf{Z}
.
Convergence in this
(2,\infty)
-norm implies uniform recovery of vertex-level structures encoded in the rows of
\mathbf{X}

(
59
)
.
Such convergence can be guaranteed under a number of latent variable network models, among which we consider the Random Dot Product Graph
(
7
;
84
, RDPG;)
, a model in which network structure is driven by latent vertex-level geometric structure.

Definition 2
(Random Dot Product Graph).

Let
F
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, and let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
be drawn i.i.d. according to
F
and collect these into the rows of
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}

and let
(\rho_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence with
\rho_{n}\in[0,1]
for all
n=1,2,\dots
.
We say that
\mathbf{A}
is distributed according to a weighted RDPG with latent positions
\mathbf{X}
and signal strength
\rho_{n}
if, conditional on
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{A}-\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
has independent entries (up to symmetry).
When
F
is such that
\bf x^{\top}\bf y\in[0,1]
for all
\bf x,\bf y
in the support of
F
, we say that
\mathbf{A}
is distributed according to a binary RDPG with latent positions
\mathbf{X}
and sparsity parameter
\rho_{n}
if

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{X}\right)=\prod_{i<j}(\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{j})^{A_{ij}}(1-\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{j})^{1-A_{ij}}.

(5)

In either case, we write
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X})\sim\operatorname{RDPG}(F,n)
with dimension
r
and parameter
\rho_{n}
clear from context, and refer to the rows of
\mathbf{X}
as the latent positions of the network.

The RDPG extends a number of widely-used network models.
For example, it recovers the SBM as a special case by taking the latent positions to be drawn from a suitably-chosen mixture of point masses.
Similarly, the RDPG recovers the degree-corrected SBM
(
46
)
as a special case by taking
\mathbf{X}_{i}=c_{i}Y_{i}
, where
c_{i}\in(0,1)
and
Y_{i}
is randomly chosen to be one of
r
distinct vectors
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
The RDPG can also be extended and generalized in a number of ways.
For example, one may modify the model so that edge probabilities are given by a kernel function on the latent space
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]

(
76
)
.
In the RDPG as given in Definition
2
,
\kappa
is simply the inner product and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
The positive semidefinite structure of
\mathbf{P}=\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
can also be relaxed
(
67
)
.
See
7
for a more in-depth discussion of the RDPG.

Remark 1
.

The reader may have noticed that the parameter
\rho_{n}
in Definition
1
is playing two distinct but related roles.
Under the binary RDPG,
\rho_{n}
describes sparsity, in the sense familiar to most network researchers: as the number of vertices
n
grows, a vanishing fraction of the possible
n^{2}
edges are actually present in the observed network.
That is, the probability of an edge appearing between two particular nodes shrinks to zero as the number of nodes grows.
As discussed in Definition
2
, we incorporate this into our model through the parameter
\rho_{n}
, which, for binary networks, we will refer to as the sparsity parameter.
In the case of the weighted RDPG,
\rho_{n}
describes a signal strength: if
\rho_{n}=1
, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
would grow linearly with
n
(see Lemma
B.2
in Appendix
B
).
Having
\rho_{n}
shrink to zero as the network size grows allows us to make the estimation problem harder by having the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
grow sublinearly.
The key distinction between these two settings is that in the weighted RDPG, we allow for the variance of the entries of
\mathbf{E}
to be specified separately from the signal strength
\rho_{n}
, while in the case of the binary RDPG, the variance of the edges, being Bernoullis, is inherently tied to the behavior of
\rho_{n}
.

2.1
Model Misspecification

The consistency of the ASE has been studied extensively in the context where the embedding dimension is correctly specified
(
7
;
56
;
72
;
67
;
55
;
75
;
60
, i.e.,
k=0
in our notation above; see)
.
Of course, in practice, the model rank
r
is typically unknown and needs to be estimated from observed data.
Methods to estimate the embedding dimension have been widely explored in the literature, as discussed in Section
1
.
Even with the best embedding dimension estimation techniques, however, it is still possible to misspecify the embedding dimension.
Thus, our goal in the present paper is to characterize the behavior of the ASE when the embedding dimension is incorrectly chosen.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to do so.

Showing consistency of the ASE in the
(2,\infty)
-norm when the true embedding dimension
r
is known require that we control the quantity

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty},

(6)

where we recall that the minimization over
\mathbf{W}
accounts for the non-identifiability of the latent positions.
The quantity described in Equation (
6
), along with related ones, has been controlled in a range of settings
(
60
;
56
;
54
;
67
;
16
)
when the true embedding dimension is known.
These results are contingent on being able to apply the Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
theorem or variants thereof
(
23
;
85
;
25
)
, which requires a non-zero eigengap.
When we embed into
d\neq r
dimensions, these approaches are no longer feasible.
In particular, when the embedding dimension is chosen too large, the ASE includes eigenvectors whose associated population eigenvalues are zero, resulting in a zero eigengap.
Thus, if we are to characterize the behavior of the ASE when the embedding dimension is misspecified, we will need to deploy different tools.

In investigating the effects of dimension misspecification, we will find it easiest to speak of the true embedding dimension
r
and the difference
k=d-r
between the selected dimension and true dimension.
We will then consider the
(r+k)
-dimensional adjacency spectral embedding,

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{1:r+k}|\mathbf{\hat{S}}|^{1/2}_{1:r+k},

where
r
is the rank of
\mathbf{P}
and
k=d-r\in\{-r,-r+1,\dots,n-r\}
describes the difference between the true model rank and the selected embedding dimension.

In adapting the quantity in Equation (
6
) to the setting where the embedding dimension is incorrectly chosen, we will need the following notation to precisely formulate the estimation error for the ASE.
When we choose our embedding dimension too small (i.e.,
k<0
), we will need to pad our estimate
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}
with additional columns of zeros so that it can sensibly be compared with the
n
-by-
r
matrix
\mathbf{X}_{1:r}
.
Similarly, when the embedding dimension is chosen too large (i.e.,
k>0
), we will need to pad
\mathbf{X}_{1:r}
with additional columns of zeros to ensure that our matrix of embeddings and true latent positions are conformable.
Hence, in order to study the setting where
k<0
, we define
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
to be a zero-padded version of
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}
.
That is, when
k<0
, we define

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}&\mathbf{0}_{r+k+1:r}\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

Similarly, when
k>0
, we define
\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}
as the
n\times(r+k)
matrix

\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}&\mathbf{0}_{r+1:r+k}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

In understanding the estimation error of the ASE, in the setting where the embedding dimension is chosen too large (
k>0
), we perform the following decomposition, ignoring the non-identifiability for the time being:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}+\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}}_{\text{Controlled via standard techniques}}\hskip-2.84544pt+\underbrace{\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}}_{\text{Goal}}.

From this decomposition, we see that there are two terms, one corresponding to the rate that would hold if the embedding dimension were correctly chosen, and the other corresponding to the
“trailing”
dimensions.
We formalize this intuition in the following lemma, which gives results both for the case where the embedding dimension is chosen too large and for the case where it is chosen too small.
A proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.1
(Misspecified Model Bounds).

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}=\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\mathbf{E}
, and denote the
(r+k)
-dimensional ASE of
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{1:r+k}|\mathbf{\hat{S}}|^{1/2}_{1:r+k}
.
When

,

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\geq\sqrt{\sum_{j=r+k+1}^{r}\frac{s_{j}}{n}}.

On the other hand, when
k>0
, suppose that there exists a sequence of
\mathbf{W}^{*}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}\mathbf{W}^{*}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\phi_{n},

(9)

for some sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}

Then there exists a sequence of
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\phi_{n}+\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{E}\right\rVert^{1/2}.

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E}=\rho_{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\mathbf{E}
, and suppose that conditional on
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, the entries of
\mathbf{E}
are independent
(\nu,b)
-sub-gamma random variables, with
n(\nu+b^{2})\log^{2}n\ll s_{r}^{2}
.
Then there exists a sequence of
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\frac{r(\log n)\sqrt{\nu+b^{2}}}{s_{r}^{1/2}}+\frac{rn(\log n)^{2}(\nu+b^{2})s_{1}}{s_{r}^{5/2}}

with high probability.

From Lemma
2.1
, we see that showing consistency when the embedding dimension is chosen too large (i.e.,
k>0
) amounts to being able to control
\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}
.
By Definition
1
, the columns of
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}
can be expressed as

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{u}_{r+1}|\hat{s}_{r+1}|^{1/2}&\hat{u}_{r+2}|\hat{s}_{r+2}|^{1/2}&\dots&\hat{u}_{r+k}|\hat{s}_{r+k}|^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r+k)}.

Bounding the eigenvalues as
\hat{s}_{r+k}\leq\|\mathbf{E}\|
, we may deploy a range of tools from the matrix concentration inequalities literature
(
51
;
28
;
79
, see, e.g.,)
, depending on the distribution of the entries of
\mathbf{E}
.
Thus, the challenge lies in controlling the entries of the eigenvectors associated with these
“trailing”
eigenvalues.
Intuitively, the entries of the ASE associated with these eigenvectors must shrink to zero, as these are zero in the true latent positions
\mathbf{X}
.
Since
\left\lVert\mathbf{E}\right\rVert\lesssim\sqrt{n}
(ignoring logarithmic factors), the entries of
\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}
must vanish uniformly.
Many random matrix models produce eigenvectors whose entries are bounded as
C(\log n)^{c}/\sqrt{n}
for some
c>0
.
These eigenvectors are called
delocalized
, and they have been widely explored in the random matrix literature
(
28
;
32
;
30
;
64
;
10
;
69
)
.
To show delocalization for our setting of a real, symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we assume that
\mathbf{E}
is an
n\times n
real, symmetric matrix whose entries are mean-zero, conditionally independent random variables given
\mathbf{P}
(up to symmetry).
Moreover, we will make the following assumptions on the spectral properties of
\mathbf{P}
and the moments of
E_{ij}
, both of which are allowed to depend on
n
:

(
A1
)

Assumption 1 (Delocalization): The eigenvectors associated with non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
are delocalized.
That is, for some
\gamma\geq 0
with no dependence on
n
.,

|u_{ij}|\lesssim\frac{(\log n)^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}

for all
i\in[n]
, and
j\in[r]
where
v_{ij}
refers to the
i
-th entry of the
j
-th eigenvector of
\mathbf{P}
.

(
A2
)

Assumption 2: The signal strength parameter
\rho_{n}=\omega(1/\sqrt{n})

(
A3
)

Assumption 3: The non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
are distinct.

(
A4
)

Assumption 4: The non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
are of order
\rho_{n}n
:
s_{j}\asymp\rho_{n}n
for
j\in[r]
.

(
A5
)

Assumption 5:
r=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P})\ll(\log n)^{\zeta}
for some
\zeta\geq 0
.

(
A6
)

Assumption 6 (Eigengap): The eigengap
\Delta:=\min_{j\in[r]}\min_{i\neq j}|s_{i}-s_{j}|
obeys
\Delta=\Omega\left(\rho_{n}n\right)
.

(
A7
)

Assumption 7: The entries of
\mathbf{E}
satisfy the moment conditions

\mathbb{E}\left[E_{ij}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[|E_{ij}|^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[|E_{ij}|^{p}\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}^{p}}{\tilde{q}_{n}^{(p/2-1)}}\quad(p>2)

where
\tilde{q}_{n}\in\left[n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{6},1\right]
is a parameter the controls the tail behavior of
\mathbf{E}
, corresponding to the sparsity parameter in the context of binary networks.

Remark 2
.

We pause to briefly discuss some of these assumptions.
Assumption
A1
is similar to the incoherence condition often found in spectral methods
(
22
;
82
;
86
)
.
It is crucial for our main delocalization result to hold. In general, without such conditions, we do not necessarily expect eigenvectors of random matrices to delocalize
(see
64
, for further discussion)
.
In the case of the RDPG, it can be shown that
\left\lVert\mathbf{U}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
(see Corollary
B.3.1
below).
Assumption
A3
assumes distinct non-zero eigenvalues in
\mathbf{P}
.
We expect that this assumption can be relaxed at the expense of increased notational complexity in tracking the eigenspaces corresponding to repeated eigenvalues in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, a task we leave to future work.
Assumptions
A2
and
A6
are necessary to use results from spectral methods.
We suspect delocalization of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
still hold even when these assumptions are relaxed, as suggested by results in
82
.
Finally, the moment conditions for
\mathbf{E}
in Assumption
A7
allow for sub-exponential decay.
See Remark 2.5 from
28
for details.

3
Theoretical Results

We now state our main theoretical results for the case of weighted networks.
These results rely basic properties of the
(2,\infty)
-norm, as well as on fundamental results in spectral methods and random matrix theory.
Our first result establishes that the trailing eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
delocalize under the assumptions given above.
A proof is given in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E}
, and suppose Assumptions (
A1
) – (
A7
) hold. Then, we have

\max_{\alpha>r}\left|\hat{u}_{j\alpha}\right|\lesssim\frac{r^{2}(\log n)^{4+6\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}

(11)

with high probability.

The proof of this result is primarily based on the approach given in
28
, where they prove the semicircle law for the case where
\mathbf{P}
in Equation (
1
) has rank one.
Our extension to a more general low-rank signal matrix requires us to deal with a non-trivial interaction between the eigenspaces corresponding to the non-zero signal eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
.
We first obtain a local law for a low-rank expectation matrix whose spectral norm is not bounded by a constant after scaling, which is possible due to our assumption on
\left\lVert\mathbf{U}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}
.
Eigenvector delocalization is then a corollary of the local law.
There are similar works that prove a local law for an expectation matrix whose spectral norm is either bounded by a constant or assumed to be diagonal but with moment conditions different to our setting
(
27
;
50
)
.

17
investigates the distribution of the largest eigenvalue for a low-rank-plus-noise model, but in the setting where the spectral norm of
\mathbf{P}
is bounded by a constant.
Our proof requires the matrix of entry-wise variances of
\mathbf{E}
to be doubly stochastic, which binary networks do not satisfy, in general.
However, we suspect that delocalization still occurs even when this assumption is relaxed.
We offer more discussion of this point in Section
3.1
.
In another direction, we expect that Assumption (
A5
) can be relaxed to allow
r
to grow as fast as
r\ll n^{1/4}
, possibly at the expense of a small polynomial factor in the delocalization bound.

Remark 3
.

Results from random matrix theory concerning the distribution of the eigenvalues have received attention in the networks literature for use in model selection
(
52
;
11
, i.e., embedding dimension selection; see)
.
We emphasize that we are concerned with the behavior of the ASE when it is (potentially) misspecified, which occurs downstream of selecting an embedding dimension and requires understanding the behavior of the eigenvectors associated with the perturbed null space, rather than the behaviors of the eigenvalues alone.

Most important for the purposes of the present work, Theorem
3.1
allows us to characterize the eigenvectors associated with the perturbed null eigenvalues.
In particular, we are able to show that for some
c\geq 4

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{k}\log^{c}n}{\sqrt{n}}

with high probability.
We highlight the use of this bound in our main contribution—quantifying the behavior of the ASE when its embedding dimension is misspecified.
A proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.2
(Estimation Bounds for Weighted Networks).

Suppose that
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X})\sim\operatorname{RDPG}(F,n)
is an
r
-dimensional weighted RDPG with signal strength
\rho_{n}
, and suppose Assumptions (
A1
) – (
A7
) hold.
Denote the
(r+k)
-dimensional adjacency spectral embedding of
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{1:r+k}|\mathbf{\hat{S}}|^{1/2}_{1:r+k}
and assume the existence of a sequence of
\mathbf{W}^{*}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
and
(\phi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
as in Equation (
9
).
Then there exists a sequence of
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}
such that for
k>0
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\phi_{n}+\sqrt{\sigma^{2}k}\frac{r^{2}(\log n)^{5+6\gamma}}{n^{1/4}}

(12)

with high probability.
On the other hand, for
k<0
,

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\gtrsim\sqrt{|k|\rho_{n}}.

3.1
Consistency for Binary Networks

Theorem
3.2
shows consistency in
(2,\infty)
-norm for weighted networks when the embedding dimension is chosen at least as large as
r
.
The proof relies on being able to show that the eigenvectors of the observed matrix
\mathbf{A}
are delocalized, as we have done in Theorem
3.1
. Binary networks do not always satisfy the moment conditions as outlined in Assumption
A7
required by our current work because of the heterogeneous entries (probabilities) in
\mathbf{P}
. These probabilities determine determine the variance of the entries of
\mathbf{E}
, since the entries of
\mathbf{A}
are Bernoulli distributed. However, we strongly suspect the core ideas developed in the previous section can be extended to binary networks. Binary networks, in general, violate the doubly stochastic assumption of the matrix of variances, which is needed for us to show delocalization of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
. Recent developments in random matrix theory
(
5
;
4
)
have allowed for the doubly stochastic assumption to be relaxed to allow for more general variances. Based on these recent theoretical advancements, along with the experimental results for binary networks shown in Section
4.2
, we suspect we can also relax the doubly stochastic assumption, extending the delocalization result to more general binary networks.
In particular, we conjecture that the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
are delocalized, analogous to Theorem
3.1

for general binary networks,

Conjecture 1
(Delocalization).

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E}
, and Assumptions (
A1
) – (
A7
) hold, with Assumption (
A7
) relaxed to the condition that
c\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|E_{ij}|^{2}\right]\leq C
.
Then we have for all
j\in[n]
,

\max_{\alpha>r}\left|\hat{v}_{j\alpha}\right|\lesssim\frac{r^{2}(\log n)^{4+6\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}

An immediate corollary of this conjecture, if it can be proven true, would be consistent estimation of the latent positions under the binary RDPG when the embedding dimension is chosen too large
(k>0)
, provided that the sparsity parameter
\rho_{n}
does not converge to zero too fast.
For example, delocalization would yield the following, using the same arguments outlined for the case of weighted networks and Theorem 5 from
60
(quoted above as Theorem
2.2
).

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{r}\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{\rho_{n}n}}+\sqrt{k}r^{2}(\log n)^{4}\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{n}\right)^{1/4}.

(13)

with high probability. Similarly, for
k<0
,

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\gtrsim\sqrt{|k|\rho_{n}}.

Similarly, another corollary of Conjecture (
1
) would yield the following for the generalized random dot product graph (GRDPG), based on Theorem
10
from above,

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\lesssim\frac{\log^{c}n}{\sqrt{n}}+\sqrt{k}r^{2}(\log n)^{4}\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{n}\right)^{1/4}

with high probability, where
c>1
. Similarly, for
k<0
,

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\gtrsim\sqrt{|k|\rho_{n}}.

4.2
Binary Networks

We now investigate the behavior of the ASE under the setting of binary networks.
As discussed in Section
3.1
, our main results presented in Section
3
are not applicable to binary networks because the matrix of entry-wise variances of the adjacency matrix is not doubly-stochastic.
In general, under models like the RDPG, binary matrices have heterogeneous entry-wise variances.
Nonetheless, we expect the eigenvectors of the observed adjacency matrix to still be delocalized, as described in Conjecture
1
, along with similar behaviors to weighted networks for when the embedding dimension is incorrectly chosen. For our first set of binary network experiments, we consider the stochastic blockmodel.
The SBM models network formation by assigning each vertex to one of
r
communities, and generating edges conditionally independently given these community assignments, in such a way that the probability of two vertices forming an edge is determined by their community memberships.
We store these probabilities in the entries of a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{B}\in[0,1]^{r\times r}
, so that
B_{k,\ell}
encodes the probability that
A_{ij}=1
, given that vertex
i
is in community
k
and vertex
j
is in community
\ell
.
All told,

1.

Draw a random probability vector
\pi\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a vector of non-negative reals.

2.

Assign each node
i\in[n]
to one of
r
communities independently according to
\pi
and record these memberships as one-hot encodings in the rows of
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.

3.

Construct
\mathbf{B}\in[0,1]^{r\times r}
by setting the within-community probabilities (i.e., on-diagonal entries) to
0.9
and the inter-community edge probabilities (i.e., off-diagonal entries) to
0.1
.

4.

Set
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
and obtain
\mathbf{X}
from the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
as
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}_{1:r}\mathbf{S}^{1/2}_{1:r}
.

5.

Generate
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
according to

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{X}\right)=\prod_{i<j}P_{ij}^{A_{ij}}(1-P_{ij})^{1-A_{ij}}.

(17)

We generated
\mathbf{A}
according to the procedure described above, with true number of communities (and hence true latent dimension) again set to
r=5

5
Discussion

In this work, we have considered how different choices of embedding dimension impact estimation of the latent positions under the RDPG, and more generally under signal-plus-noise matrix models
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E}

Our results characterize how different choices of embedding dimension affect the estimation rate of the ASE, as measured in
(2,\infty)
-norm.
Theorem (
3.2
) and our experiments in Section
4.1
show that under a weighted version of the RDPG, when the embedding dimension is chosen too small, consistency in the
(2,\infty)
-norm is not guaranteed for the ASE.
Indeed, we see from Theorem (
3.2
) that when the embedding dimension is chosen too small, the estimation error is lower bounded by the signal strength parameter
\rho_{n}
.
This implies that the ASE is inconsistent when
\rho_{n}
is non-vanishing and the embedding dimension is chosen too small.
We may think of this as the embedding failing to capture all of the information contained in the network.
When the embedding dimension is correctly specified, all of the signal present in the network is captured, with no trailing noise eigenvalues, and we achieve the
n^{-1/2}
convergence rate (ignoring log-factors) seen in the literature and and widely believed to be optimal
(
81
;
82
, see e.g., discussion in)
.
On the other hand, Theorem (
3.2
) and our experiments in Section
4.1
show that when the embedding dimension is chosen too large, consistency may still hold, so long as the embedding dimension is not too much larger than the true dimension.
This result confirms the conjecture in Section 7 of
7
: as long as all of the signal information is captured, the ASE should still be consistent.
This consistency is a direct consequence of the delocalization of the eigenvectors associated with the perturbed null space.
Because of the stark differences in the asymptotic behaviors under these three regimes (embedding dimension either too small, chosen correctly, or too large), our results lend support to the folklore in network embeddings and in model selection more broadly, that it is better to err on the side of choosing model rank too large.

The most immediate avenue for future work is to extend our delocalization proofs to the setting where the matrix of entry-wise variances of the adjacency matrix is no longer doubly stochastic, as described in Section
3.1
.
This would prove our Conjecture
1
, with which we could extend Theorem
3.2
to the binary RDPG.
Our experiments for binary networks described in Section
4.2
show that the behavior of the ASE is similar to that predicted by Theorem
3.2
, lending strong evidence to the validity of our delocalization conjecture.
Other directions for future work include investigating the effects of model misspecification on downstream tasks, such as community detection and hypothesis testing; and extending our results to other node embedding methods such as the Laplacian spectral embedding
(
75
)
, Laplacian eigenmaps
(
25
)
and multiple-network versions of the ASE
(
54
)
.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

We first consider the case where
k>0
.
Define
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}
according to

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}^{*}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\mathbf{I}_{r+1:r+k}\end{bmatrix},

(19)

where
\mathbf{W}^{*}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
is the matrix guaranteed by our assumption in Equation (
9

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}.

(20)

Now, express
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}
as the block matrix

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}&\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\end{bmatrix}.

Decomposing
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}
similarly, recalling from Equation (
8
) that the trailing
k
columns of
\mathbf{X}
are zero by construction, and using the definition of
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
from Equation (
19
), we have

\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r_{k}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}\mathbf{W}^{*}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right)&\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r+k)}.

By the triangle inequality, it follows that

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}\mathbf{W}^{*}-\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}+\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}.

By our choice of
\mathbf{W}^{*}
, Equation (
9
) allows us to bound the first right-hand term and write

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq C\phi_{n}+\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}.

Applying this to Equation (
20
),

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r+k}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\mathbf{W}-\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq C\phi_{n}+\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\mathbf{W}^{*}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}.

(21)

Recall that by construction,
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}=\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}|\mathbf{\hat{S}}_{r+1:r+k}|^{1/2}
.
Thus, using basic properties of the
(2,\infty)
-norm
(
16
, see, e.g.,)
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{r+1:r+k}\mathbf{W}^{*}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{S}}_{r+1:r+k}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}^{*}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{U}}_{r+1:r+k}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{E}\right\rVert^{1/2},

(22)

where the second inequality follows from unitary invariance of the spectral norm and Weyl’s inequality.
Substituting Equation (
22
) into Equation (
21
) completes the claim for the case of
k>0
.

For the lower-bound half of our claim, which holds when
k<0
, notice that by definition of the
(2,\infty)
-norm as a maximum, recalling the definition of
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}
from Equation (
7
), we have

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\rVert_{2,\infty}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n}\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}}\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(23)

Expanding the Frobenius norm, for any
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{r}
,

\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}-2\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W})^{\top}\left(\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right).

Using unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm and noting that
\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\right\rVert_{F}
by construction,

\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}-2\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W})^{\top}\left(\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right).

(24)

Recalling that
\sqrt{\hat{s}_{j}}
and
\sqrt{s_{j}}
are the singular values of
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}
and
\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}

(see, e.g.,

yields

\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W})^{\top}(\rho_{n}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}_{1:r})\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sqrt{s_{j}\hat{s}_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{r+k}\sqrt{s_{j}\hat{s}_{j}},

where we have used the fact that the singular values of
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}
are invariant to right-multiplication by
\mathbf{W}
and the fact that
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r+k}
has at most
r+k

Applying this bound in Equation (
24
),

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{r+k}\hat{s}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r+k}\sqrt{s_{j}\hat{s}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r+k}\left(\sqrt{\hat{s}_{j}}-\sqrt{s_{j}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=r+k+1}^{r}s_{j}.

Trivially lower-bounding the first sum by zero,

\left\|\mathbf{\hat{X}}_{1:r}^{\circ}\mathbf{W}-\rho^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{1:r}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=r+k+1}^{r}s_{j}.

Applying this lower-bound to Equation (
23
) and taking square roots completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Basic Results from Spectral Methods

Here we collect a handful of basic results related to matrix spectra and eigenspaces for use in Appendix
C
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\{s_{i}\}
be the eigenvalues of a rank
r
matrix
\mathbf{P}
, and let
\{\hat{s}_{i}\}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{E}
.
Suppose Assumption (
A3
) holds and define
\Delta=\min_{j\in[r]}\min_{i\neq j}|s_{i}-s_{j}|
.
Then for
j\in[r]
and
i\neq j
,

|\hat{s}_{i}-s_{j}|\geq\Delta-\left\lVert\mathbf{E}\right\rVert.

(27)

Proof.

Fix
j\in[r]
and let
i\neq j
.
By the reverse triangle inequality,

\left|\hat{s}_{i}-s_{j}\right|\geq\left||\hat{s}_{i}-s_{i}|-|s_{j}-s_{i}|\right|\geq\Delta-\left|\hat{s}_{i}-s_{i}\right|,

where the second inequality follows from the definition of
\Delta
.
Applying Weyl’s inequality, it follows that

\left|\hat{s}_{i}-s_{j}\right|\geq\Delta-\|\mathbf{E}\|,

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
are identically distributed
r
-dimensional random vectors with
\left\lVert\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\rVert\leq M<\infty
almost surely and define
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{X}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.
Suppose that
r
obeys Assumption (
A5
).
Then with high probability,
\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}-n\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{1}\right])\right\|\leq Cr\sqrt{n\log n}
.

Proof.

Since
\left\lVert\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\rVert\leq M
almost surely, the coordinates of
\mathbf{X}_{1}
are bounded random variables.
By Hoeffding’s inequality, we see that for any
j,k\in[r]
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right)_{jk}-n[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\right]]_{jk}\right|\geq C\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}.

From a union bound over
j,k\in[r]
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-n\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert^{2}_{F}\geq r^{2}(C\sqrt{n\log n})^{2}\right)\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{n^{2}}.

Using the fact that the Frobenius norm is an upper bound on the spectral norm, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-n\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert\geq Cr\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{n^{2}},

and the result follows after using our assumption in Equation (
A5
) to ensure that this bound converges to zero suitably quickly.
∎

Appendix C
Delocalization Results

In this section, we prove that the eigenvectors of the weighted RDPG are delocalized, in the sense that the entries of the eigenvectors are bounded by
Cn^{-1/2}\log^{c}n
, for some constants
C,c>0
.
Our approach is based on earlier work by
28
proving the local semicircle law for eigenvalues in the bulk under the Erdős–Rényi model, the expectation of which is a rank-one real symmetric matrix.
We extend their approach to encompass the more general setting of a low-rank, real, and symmetric expectation.
For background on the technical tools used in this section, we refer the interested reader to
29
;
9
;
32
;
6
.

The semicircle density is defined as

\mu_{\text{sc}}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}

for
x\in[-2,2]
, with its associated Stieltjes transform for
\operatorname{Im}z>0
,

m_{\operatorname{sc}}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu_{\text{sc}}(x)}{x-z}\text{d}x.

(29)

Our goal in this section is to show that for a suitably recentered and rescaled version of our adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, the Stieltjes transform of its empirical eigenvalue density converges to
m_{\operatorname{sc}}(z)
.
This is done in Theorem
C.13
below.
This result may be of independent interest to researchers working with low-rank signal-plus-noise network models like the RDPG.
Most important for the present work, however, is that we may then use this result to show that the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
are delocalized, which we do in Theorem
C.14
.

Notational Change: Eigenvalue Ordering and Submatrices

For this section and this section only, we make a notational change in order to agree with the convention of the vast majority of random matrix theory results
(and, in particular, the convention followed in
28
, , whose results we extend)
.
In what follows, we number the eigenvalues from smallest to largest, rather than the largest-to-smallest indexing used in the main text.
Thus, in this section, the eigenvalues
d_{i}
of some matrix
\mathbf{D}
will be indexed so that

d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\cdots\leq d_{N}.

That is,
d_{N}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{D}
.
To mark this change of convention in what follows, we use
N
rather than
n
to denote the number of nodes in the network, but stress that
N=n

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
satisfy Equation (
34
). Then for any

\left|\tilde{\Lambda}(z)-\Lambda(z)\right|\leq\frac{r\pi}{N\eta}

Proof.

The proof largely follows that of Lemma 7.1 in
28
.
The key difference is in the relation between
\tilde{n}(x)
and
n(x)
.
In our setting,
|\tilde{n}(x)-n(x)|\leq r/N
, which can be seen from Lemma
C.23
and noticing that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{B}
are interlaced at most every
r
indices, as we now demonstrate.
Recalling the definitions of
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
from Equation (
41
) and applying the reverse triangle inequality,

|\tilde{\Lambda}(z)-\Lambda(z)|\leq|\tilde{m}(z)-m_{\operatorname{sc}}(z)-m(z)+m_{\operatorname{sc}}(z)|=|\tilde{m}(z)-m(z)|.

(45)

Recalling the definitions of
\tilde{m}(z)
and
m(z)
from Equation (
39
),

\tilde{m}(z)-m(z)=\int\frac{\tilde{\mu}(x)-\mu(x)}{x-z}dx=\int\frac{\tilde{n}(x)-n(x)}{(x-z)^{2}}dx,

where the second equality follows from integration by parts.
Substituting this into Equation (
45
) and applying Jensen’s inequality,

|\tilde{\Lambda}(z)-\Lambda(z)|\leq\int\frac{|\tilde{n}(x)-n(x)|}{|x-z|^{2}}dx.

(46)

Then Lemma
C.23
implies the interlacing

\omega_{i}\leq\omega_{i+1}\leq\dots\leq\omega_{i+r-1}\leq\hat{\lambda}_{i}\leq\dots\leq\hat{\lambda}_{i+r-1}\leq\omega_{i+r}\leq\hat{\lambda}_{i+r}.

From these inequalities, we can see that there are at most
r
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
between the

-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
and the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}
, since the
(i+r)
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
must come after the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}
.
Thus, for any

\left|\left|\{\alpha:-\infty<\omega_{\alpha}\leq x\}\right|-\left|\{\alpha:-\infty<\hat{\lambda}_{\alpha}\leq x\}\right|\right|\leq r.

It then follows that
|\tilde{n}(x)-n(x)|\leq r/N
.
Applying this to Equation (
46

|\tilde{\Lambda}(z)-\Lambda(z)|\leq\frac{r}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|x-z|^{2}}dx=\frac{r}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(x-E)^{2}+\eta^{2}}dx=\frac{r}{N\eta}\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left[\tan^{-1}(t)-\tan^{-1}(-t)\right]=\frac{r\pi}{N\eta},

as we set out to show.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

\mathbf{B}
satisfy Equation

Lemma C.3
.

For each
\alpha=1,2,\dots,N
, let
\hat{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
be the orthonormal eigenvectors of
\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]=\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]+\mathbf{H}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]

Then for all

and any
\mathbb{T}\subset[N]
where

(\xi,\nu)
-high probability

\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha\neq j}|\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}|^{2}}\leq\frac{Cr(\log N)^{2\gamma}}{\lambda_{j}},

where
\bm{v}_{j}
is the eigenvector associated with the

-th smallest eigenvalue of

Proof.

Following the proof of the Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
theorem from Chapter 2 in

for a given

via the eigendecomposition

\mathbf{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{j}&\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{j}&0\\

(52)

where
\mathbf{U}_{\perp}
is the
(N-1)\times(N-1)
matrix of eigenvectors that span the orthogonal complement of
\bm{v}_{j}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{\perp}
is the diagonal matrix of associated eigenvalues.
Write the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]
similarly:

\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}&\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}&0\\
0&\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}&\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(53)

where
\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
is the eigenvector associated with
\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
, the
j
-th smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]
,
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
is the matrix of orthonormal eigenvectors that span the orthogonal complement of
\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]}
is the diagonal matrix of associated eigenvalues.

By construction,
\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{H}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{T}]=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})
, and thus we may view
\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})
as the error between
\mathbf{L}
and

\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})=\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\mathbf{L}

We will now employ a centering trick to bound the eigenvalues of

away from

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L}-\lambda_{j}\mathbf{I}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathbf{B}}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]=\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\lambda_{j}\mathbf{I}.

(55)

From these surrogate matrices, we can see that

\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})=\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\lambda_{j}\mathbf{I}+\lambda_{j}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}.

We observe that by Lemma

(-\infty,\lambda_{j}-\Delta^{\circ}+\left\lVert\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})\right\rVert]\cup[\lambda_{j}+\Delta^{\circ}-\left\lVert\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})\right\rVert,\infty)

The eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]

to the spectra of
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{B}[\mathbb{T}^{c}]

\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\perp}^{[\mathbb{T}^{c}]})\geq\Delta^{\circ}-\left\lVert\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{T})\right\rVert.
