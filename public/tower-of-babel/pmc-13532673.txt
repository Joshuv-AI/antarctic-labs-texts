Real-World Effectiveness and Safety of Imrecoxib Across Diverse Pain Conditions: A Multicenter, Retrospective Cohort Study of 30,214 Patients
Authors: Jingyi Wang, Yong Chen, Yiqiu Cao, Xuanwei Hu, Aoyuan Fan, Zhiying Pang, Feng Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImrecoxib, a selective COX-2 inhibitor approved for osteoarthritis pain, has shown therapeutic potential in various inflammatory conditions. However, real-world evidence of application patterns and effectiveness in routine practice remains limited.MethodsA non-interventional, registry-based real-world study was performed from August 2023 to January 2025 at 95 centers in China. Patients with at least one record of imrecoxib treatment were enrolled. Data on demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, pain scores (numeric rating scale, NRS), and adverse events were collected. Descriptive analyses were performed for baseline characteristics, while inferential statistics (including Wilcoxon signed-rank test and chi-square test) were used to compare pre- and post-treatment outcomes. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate treatment effects across different patient characteristics, and linear mixed-effects models were applied to adjust for potential confounders (baseline NRS, age, sex, and pain category). Effective pain control was defined as achieving an NRS score of ≤3 (successful pain relief), or a decrease in pain severity category (pain alleviation).ResultsA total of 30,214 patients were included. Orthopedic settings accounted for 50.8% of prescriptions, with osteoarthritis (17.2%), bone fracture pain relief (17.1%), and perioperative pain control (16.5%) being the primary indications. Additional use was observed in lumbar/cervical spondylosis (12.2%), rheumatoid arthritis (2.6%), and gout (1.6%). Notably, the recorded prevalence of comorbidities (24.0%) appeared lower than that reported in the general population. The majority of patients (96.0%) received the standard dosage (100 mg twice daily), with a median treatment duration of 8 days (interquartile range: 5–12). Monotherapy was predominant (71.83%), while concomitant use with other analgesics was uncommon. Post-treatment NRS pain scores showed a significant reduction compared to baseline. Successful pain relief was achieved in 75.8% of patients, and treatment effectiveness was rated at 76.1%. Adverse events (AEs) were infrequent (95 cases, primarily mild cardiovascular or gastrointestinal events).ConclusionIn this large, multicenter real-world study, imrecoxib was widely prescribed for various pain conditions. The results suggest favorable effectiveness and a generally favorable safety profile in routine clinical practice.Keywords: orthopedic pain, COX-2 inhibitor, imrecoxib, real-world study, effectiveness

Introduction

Pain, particularly musculoskeletal and inflammatory pain, constitutes a major clinical and public health burden.1 The clinical needs of pain control span diverse populations: from acute post-operative or injury-related pain to chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), low back pain, gout, and soft tissue disorders.2

Cyclooxygenase isoforms COX-1 and COX-2 facilitate the transformation of arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and related eicosanoid compounds, prostaglandins mediate inflammation, pain sensitization, and fever.3 NSAIDs (Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs) exert their therapeutic effects (reducing inflammation, pain, fever) primarily by inhibiting COX enzymes, thereby blocking the production of pro-inflammatory prostaglandins (especially PGE2 derived from COX-2).4 NSAIDs have broad clinical applications as primary therapy for various pains, including musculoskeletal pain, acute gout flares, tendonitis, and bursitis, as well as post-operative pain, sports injuries, and minor trauma.5–7 Additionally, NSAIDs manage soft tissue inflammation, including strains, sprains, and related inflammatory conditions.8,9

While COX-1 maintains consistent expression across most cell types and supports fundamental physiological processes, COX-2 typically exhibits minimal or negligible baseline presence in tissues, while becomes markedly elevated in response to activating stimuli.10 Simultaneous inhibition of COX-1 in the gastric mucosa drastically reduces the production of protective prostaglandins (mainly PGE2 and PGI2). The gastric lining becomes vulnerable to attack by gastric acid, pepsin, bile salts, and other irritants. This leads to gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects (AEs).11 Approximately 40% of NSAID users experience upper GI symptoms, while gastric ulcers are found in 10% to 30%.12 A previous study in the United States has shown that NSAIDs cause 103,000 hospitalizations and 16,500 deaths each year.13 Drugs designed to have high affinity and selectivity for inhibiting the COX-2 enzyme while having minimal effect on COX-1 at therapeutic anti-inflammatory doses result in significantly better GI safety compared to traditional NSAIDs.14 Selective COX-2 inhibitors were developed to maintain efficacy while reducing GI toxicity, however, cardiovascular risks remain a concern with both traditional NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors.15 Ongoing researches keep focusing on developing novel NSAID formulations and delivery systems to enhance effectiveness and reduce toxicity.

Imrecoxib, a selective COX-2 inhibitor developed in China, was approved on May 20th, 2011 for the pain relief of OA. Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated its promising therapeutic efficacy across various inflammatory conditions. In OA, imrecoxib reduces pain and cartilage degeneration by modulating synovial macrophage polarization via the COX-2/PGE2 signaling pathway.16 It has also shown favorable clinical efficacy in patients with axial spondyloarthritis.17 In the field of perioperative pain management, imrecoxib provided non-inferior analgesic effects compared to celecoxib in both arthroscopic knee surgery and total hip arthroplasty.18 Furthermore, a multicenter double-blind trial highlighted the potential of combined parecoxib and imrecoxib in reducing organ failure in severe acute pancreatitis19 and previous research has demonstrated that imrecoxib attenuates pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway.20

However, despite these clinical trial data and small-scale cohort studies, evidence from broad, unselected real-world clinical populations remains scarce. Specific aspects requiring further elucidation include: the application patterns and safety profiles of imrecoxib across diverse pain subgroups (eg, internal organ pain vs musculoskeletal pain), the influence of multiple comorbidities on its effectiveness, and the optimal treatment duration with respect to long-term pain control in routine practice.

In real-world Chinese clinical settings, imrecoxib is widely utilized in orthopedic and rheumatology departments due to its favorable gastrointestinal safety profile and cost-effectiveness compared to other selective COX-2 inhibitors. Moreover, its prescription often involves diverse patient populations with varying ages and complex comorbidities, a scenario rarely captured in strictly controlled RCTs. Therefore, we intend to conduct a multicenter, registry-based, real-world study to assess the application patterns, effectiveness, and safety of imrecoxib in a broad population, aiming to generate robust evidence to inform safer and more precise clinical utilization in routine practice.

Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, retrospective, real-world study conducted to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of imrecoxib for pain relief in a broad patient population. Eligible patients received imrecoxib treatment for pain control from August 2023 to January 2025 at 95 centers in China were enrolled, and their clinical data were collected. The characteristics of patients, as well as pain control effectiveness and safety were assessed. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hainan Jialong Internet Hospital, Hainan Jialong Internet Hospital, China (approval/reference number: JLIENOSEC20230417; approval date: May 5, 2023). Given the retrospective, observational, and non-interventional nature of the study, and because the study did not interfere with patients’ diagnosis or treatment, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All patient data were anonymized or de-identified before analysis, and no personally identifiable information was used or reported.

Patients

Eligible patients included adults aged ≥18 years with self-report osteoarticular or soft tissue pain, such as OA, axSpA, RA, gout, or postoperative pain due to elective surgery. Inclusion criteria required at least one recorded use of imrecoxib. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete records of or missing NRS pain scores before or after treatment. In addition, the common contradictions for imrecoxib treatment included: 1) Women who are pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning to become pregnant within 3 months; 2) Patients known to be allergic to this product or other similar drugs and sulfonamides. 3) Patients who experience asthma, hives, or allergic reactions after taking aspirin or other NSAIDs. 4) Contraindicated for the treatment of pain in the perioperative period of coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG). 5) Patients with a history of GI bleeding or perforation after using NSAIDs. 6) Patients with active GI ulcers/bleeding or a history of recurrent ulcers/bleeding. 7) Patients with severe heart failure.

Data Extraction and Processing

The following data were extracted for eligible patients: demographic information including age, sex, and province of living; diagnosis for pain treatment; comorbidities; starting and ending date, as well as dosage of imrecoxib treatment; concomitant medications; NRS pain scores before and after treatment; and adverse events (AEs) records.

Pain diagnosis was categorized according to their primary and secondary clinical diagnoses. Since the original medical records lacked a uniform ICD coding system, a rigorous data cleaning protocol was applied: duplicate and synonymous diagnostic entries were consolidated, followed by manual review to map them into standardized, clinically meaningful categories. Comorbidities were grouped using the same standardized mapping procedure. Patients may be with multiple pain diagnosis or comorbidities when they met more than one category of pain diagnosis or comorbidities. Durations of imrecoxib treatment were calculated based on starting and ending date of treatment. The record of concomitant medications during imrecoxib treatment were used to summarize numbers, as well as the categories of concomitant medications.

Effectiveness and Safety Outcomes

NRS pain scores were collected during the study period as regular clinical process. NRS scores were categorized as follows: 0 = pain-free, 1–3 = mild pain, 4–7 = moderate pain, and 8–10 = severe pain. Treatment response was classified into four categories: (1) successful pain relief, defined as a reduction to pain-free or mild pain (NRS≤3); (2) alleviated, defined as a decrease in severity without achieving pain-free or mild status; (3) stable, defined as no change; and (4) worsened, defined as an increase in severity after treatment.

The primary effective outcome was effective pain control, defined as either successful pain relief or pain alleviation. Persistent or worsened pain was classified as not effective. Secondary effectiveness outcomes included treatment effect category, changes in NRS scores, and post-treatment severity of pain. As safety outcome, AEs were retrospectively identified and collected by reviewing the electronic medical records (EMRs) of all enrolled patients. Special attention was paid to AEs commonly associated with NSAID use, including gastrointestinal (eg, bleeding, ulceration, perforation), cardiovascular (eg, myocardial infarction, heart failure), renal, and hepatic events.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed for baseline data to depict the characteristics of treated patients, including demographic parameters, pain diagnosis categories, comorbidities, treatment regimens and concomitant categories, as well as AEs. Treatment effects were analyzed by comparing pre- and post-treatment NRS, as well as descriptive analysis of treatment effect categories.

Skewed distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared between two paired groups was performed using Kruskal–Wallis test among multiple groups. Paired data were compared among groups using Wilcoxon test. Categorical data were expressed and frequency (percentage) and compared between groups using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

To describe the real-world clinical indications for imrecoxib use, pain was classified according to patients’ primary diagnoses and supplementary clinical information. As summarized in Figure 1, imrecoxib was prescribed for various indications. Orthopedic treatment appeared to be the most common application of imrecoxib, with the most common treatment scenarios being osteoarthritis (17.2%), perioperative pain control (16.5%), and bone fracture pain relief (17.1%, including vertebral and non-vertebral fractures). Substantial percentages of patients were also treated for lumbar and cervical spondylosis (12.1%), rheumatoid arthritis (2.6%) and gout (1.6%). In addition, more than 20% of prescriptions were issued for joint and soft tissue pain, while a smaller but notable proportion (2.6%) prescribed for internal organ–related or neuropathic pain.

Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity, present in more than half of the affected patients (53.5%). Treatment Regimens and Concomitant Medications

The majority of patients (96.0%) received imrecoxib at the dosage of 100 mg twice daily, and more than 98% of prescriptions administered as morning or evening 100 mg doses. Approximately 82.5% of patients were treated for 3–14 days, whereas 4.81% of patients received prolonged treatment exceeding 28 days (Figure 3A and B). Nearly 72% of the patients had no recorded co-medications (Figure 3C). Among those with co-prescriptions, fewer than 15% (accounting for total 4.08% of all patients) were co-prescribed medications conventionally used for pain or inflammation, including disease modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), opioids, local anesthetics, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, corticosteroids, other NSAIDs, or antiepileptic or psychiatric medications. In most cases, concomitant therapy consisted of drugs for unrelated conditions or traditional Chinese medicines with purported anti-inflammatory properties that could not be readily categorized.

Treatment Effects of Pain Relief

Imrecoxib treatment was associated with a significant reduction in NRS pain scores. Post-treatment scores (median 2, IQR 2–3) were significantly lower than pre-treatment scores (median 6, IQR 5–7; P < 0.0001; Table 2). Before treatment, most patients experienced moderate (75.4%) or severe (17.7%) pain. After treatment, 79.5% of patients reported mild pain, and 2.9% achieved complete pain relief (NRS = 0). Only 17.1% remained in moderate pain and 0.45% reported severe pain post-treatment. The rates of successful pain relief and effective pain control were 75.8% and 76.1%, respectively. In contrast, 17.5% of patients did not show significant improvement of pain score after treatment, and 5.9% of patients experienced worsening of pain after treatment, potentially attributable to disease progression (Table 2 and Figure 4).

Subgroup Analysis

To further investigate the effectiveness and safety of imrecoxib across diverse patient populations, changes in NRS scores and effective rates of pain control were compared among subgroups stratified by different patient characteristics. To account for potential confounding, the pre- to post-treatment changes in NRS scores were adjusted for baseline NRS, age, sex, and pain category using a linear mixed-effects model.

Baseline Pain Level

As expected, pre-treatment pain severity was closely associated with the extent of pain reduction. As shown in Figure 5A, NRS score reductions (mean changes and 95% CI) was 5.27 (5.22–5.32) in patients with severe pain, comparing to 3.36 (3.35–3.38) in patients with moderate pain and 1.15 (1.09–1.20) in patients with mild pain. The rate of effective pain relief was also lower in patients with severe pain (70.1%) compared to that in patients with moderate pain (84.8%) (Table 4). However, no significant difference in AEs were observed between patients with different pain severity.

Subgroup Analysis of Treatment Effects and AEs

Age

As shown in Table 4, elderly patients (≥65) did not show a significant difference in effective rate (76.0%) from those under 65 years old (76.8%). In addition, there were no differences in AE rates observed in elderly patients. Further analysis of NRS pain score changes (Figure 5B) showed that patients aged ≥65 achieved a slightly greater reduction than that those <65 years, with the most pronounced reduction of pain score observed in patients aged ≥75.

Pain Indication

Associations between NRS reductions and different primary diagnosis of pain were shown in Figure 5C. Osteoarthritis, joint pain, fascial tendon inflammation appeared to be associated with lower level of pain reduction, while patients with perioperative pain, bone fracture, rheumatoid arthritis, internal organ pain, and gout exhibited more significant pain reductions compared with other patients. Differences in rates of AEs were not detected across subgroups stratified by primary pain diagnosis.

Comorbidities

As shown in Figure 5D, existence of various comorbidities did not appear to result in less effectiveness of pain reduction. In contrast, patients with hypertension, circulation system diseases, metabolic or nutritional disorders, or digestion system diseases showed more significant reduction in pain compared to the patients without these comorbidities.

Treatment Duration and Concomitant Medications

As shown in Figure 6A, the interaction between treatment duration and pain reduction was statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.0008), indicating that the magnitude of NRS improvement varied across different treatment durations. Rather than a simple linear correlation, the forest plot displayed a nonlinear “U-shaped” trend: patients treated for short (≤14 days) or extremely long (>84 days) periods achieved larger NRS reductions, while those treated for intermediate durations (15–56 days) exhibited relatively smaller pain reductions.

To further clarify the specific differences between these subgroups, we analyzed the effective rates in Figure 6C. Consistent with the observations in Figure 6A, the pairwise comparisons revealed that the short-term (≤14 days) and ultra-long-term (>84 days) groups had comparable effective rates (no significant difference, ns). However, both the ≤14 days group and the >84 days group demonstrated significantly higher effective rates compared to the intermediate 15–28 days treatment group (P < 0.0001 and P = 0.030, respectively). This further corroborates that the non-linear, “U-shaped” relationship in pain relief is driven by a decline in therapeutic response during mid-term administration.

Most patients received imrecoxib monotherapy. When patients co-treated with other medications were analyzed (Figure 6B), an overall significant difference in pain reduction was observed between those with and without concomitant medications (P for interaction < 0.0001), probably driven by the poorer pain reduction in patients receiving “Other medications”. However, when focusing on specific conventional pain-relieving co-treatments (including DMARDs, opioid analgesics/local anesthetics, and corticosteroids), their NRS changes showed no significant differences compared to imrecoxib monotherapy (all P for interaction > 0.05). Consistently, Table 4, which compared the specific co-treatment subgroups (None, DMARDs, Opioid analgesics/local anesthetics, and Corticosteroids), confirmed that there were no statistically significant differences in either the effective rates (P = 0.729) or AE rates (P = 0.078) among these groups, further supporting that the addition of conventional analgesics did not significantly alter the clinical effectiveness or safety of imrecoxib.

Discussion

Our large, patient population-based study including 30,214 patients across China demonstrates that imrecoxib is widely prescribed for diverse pain conditions in real-world clinical practice. The common indications included OA, perioperative pain, bone fracture, lumbar and cervical spondylosis, RA, gout, as well as joint and soft tissue pain. The majority of patients were treated with standard regimen of 100 mg twice daily. Among the 28% of the patients received concomitant medications, they were rarely other standard pain control medications, but rather other facilitating treatments. In terms of pain control, more than three-quarters of the patients achieved successful pain control, with AE reported only in 3 per 1000 patients. These results indicated that imrecoxib provides effective pain relief with a favorable safety profile across wide range of pain conditions.

Patients in this study received imrecoxib for a broad range of indications, encompassing multiple diagnostic categories such as: perioperative pain, orthopedic pain (fractures, arthritis, and spondylosis), gout, and various other soft tissue–related pain conditions. This broad application was consistent with the well-established use of NSAIDs as anti-inflammatory agents across these fields. As extensively reported, NSAIDs were considered standard therapy for OA,21 RA,22 lower back pain,23 cervical disorders,24 acute postoperative pain,25 acute gout,26 soft tissue analgesia,27 and tendinopathy.28 Furthermore, although not specifically approved for cancer pain, NSAIDs have been suggested to potentially improve cancer prognosis due to their anti-inflammatory properties.29 While imrecoxib is primarily indicated for osteoarthritis, our results highlight its widespread real-world application as an NSAID across diverse clinical scenarios where NSAIDs are typically utilized.

Potential concerns regarding NSAIDs in clinical practice include their effects on underlying diseases and interactions with concomitant medications.30 Previous studies have reported that NSAIDs may reduce the efficacy of many antihypertensive drugs, potentially increasing cardiovascular risk.31,32 In this study, a considerable proportion of patients had baseline hypertension and cardiovascular diseases, along with other comorbidities such as diabetes and dyslipidemia. However, the presence of comorbidities, as well as concomitant medications, did not appear to be associated with detectable differences in the effectiveness or safety of imrecoxib treatment. These results suggested that in real-world practice, treatment with imrecoxib may be a safe and effective therapeutic option in complex patients. Patients requiring NSAIDs therapy often present with multiple comorbidities, among with hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease are predominant, which often accompanied with multiple medications. It is advisable to prioritize drugs with favorable safety profiles to reduce adverse drug reactions and interaction-related issues.

Treatment duration of NSAIDs in clinical practice varies substantially depending on age and disease chronicity. Median NSAIDs durations were 14.3 days in younger patients and 25.1 days in the elder,33 while osteoarthritis cohort study reported up to 59 days.21 In contrast, imrecoxib was typically prescribed for acute episodes, yielding a median exposure of only 8 days (IQR 5–12). This short-course pattern supports its utility as a rapid, effective analgesic for acute pain with minimal cumulative risk.

Although NSAID-opioid combinations are widely used for acute and chronic pain,34,35 only 30% of patients received combination therapy in our study. Moreover, conventional analgesics, such as opioids, constituted only a minor proportion (4.09%) of these co-treatment regimens and concurrent medications were mainly adjunctive or supportive therapies. As polypharmacy for pain management is generally discouraged in clinical practice due to increased safety risks, the findings demonstrating comparable efficacy between imrecoxib monotherapy and combination regimens are significant. These data suggest that imrecoxib alone represents a sufficient and potentially preferable therapeutic choice for pain control in most clinical scenarios.

Assessment of pain control effectiveness using the NRS demonstrated a median reduction of 4 points from baseline. Using an NRS ≤ 3 as the threshold for successful pain relief.36,37 This level of effectiveness is comparable to, or potentially exceeds, outcomes reported in previous NSAID studies: while typical NRS reductions range from 2 to 5 points.38–42 Success rates vary with definition: stringent thresholds such as VAS < 2 (closely related to NRS) yielded high success rates of 70 to 92.3%,43,44 whereas conventional benchmarks (≥3-point reduction or ≥30% improvement) produce 30 to 65%.38,39,45 Exploration of differences between effective and ineffective treatment indicated that baseline pain level may be the main contributing factor (data not shown).

The observed AE rate was relatively low in this study. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as in real-world data, mild and transient events are often underreported, while serious AEs are more likely to be documented. Moreover, the pharmacological profile of imrecoxib, characterized by selective COX-2 inhibition and minimal COX-1 activity, confers reduced gastrointestinal toxicity and a more favorable cardiovascular profile compared with traditional NSAIDs. Besides, the short median treatment exposure (8 days) may have curtailed cumulative risks. Collectively, the low observed AE rate likely reflects a combination of these methodological aspects and the intrinsic safety of imrecoxib in short-term clinical practice.

Subgroup analyses in this study demonstrated that both primary pain diagnosis and baseline pain severity were associated with treatment effectiveness. Interestingly, initial analysis indicated that patients receiving short-term treatment (≤2 weeks) exhibited higher response rates compared to those undergoing longer-term therapy; however, further analysis revealed that during prolonged treatment, efficacy increased progressively with duration. These findings suggest that for patients requiring long-term pain management, treatment persistence is crucial, as prolonged imrecoxib therapy ultimately achieved efficacy rates comparable to shorter courses. Importantly, in our cohort, prolonged use was not associated with an increase in the observed AE risk, suggesting that imrecoxib may be safely maintained for patients requiring long-term management within the scope of the observed treatment durations.

The principal strength of this study is its exceptionally large sample size of more than 30,000 patients, which substantially exceeds most prior real-world and meta-analyses21,27,46 investigations of NSAIDs and provides strong statistical power for evaluating treatment patterns and outcomes. In addition, data collection followed preset standardized protocols, ensuring completeness and reliability of key variables, including pain scores (NRS), treatment duration, diagnosis information, comorbidities, and concomitant treatments. These features enhance the robustness of the analysis and allow for a more comprehensive and reliable depiction of imrecoxib’s use, effectiveness, and safety in routine practice.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design carries the inherent risk of selection bias and incomplete data capture, particularly for mild adverse events that may not be systematically documented. Furthermore, as the study captured only prescriptions issued during hospital visits, treatment duration was relatively short, limiting the ability to assess long-term safety and effectiveness. Finally, the participating departments did not encompass all possible specialties, the findings may not fully represent the entire spectrum of imrecoxib use across healthcare settings. These considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

In summary, this large real-world study showed that imrecoxib provides effective and durable pain relief across a wide spectrum of clinical scenarios, including osteoarthritis, perioperative pain, fracture-related pain, spinal disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, and soft tissue conditions. Importantly, the observed safety profile was favorable, with low reported rates of cardiovascular and gastrointestinal events even in patients with substantial comorbidity burdens such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. It is important to acknowledge that the relatively short median treatment duration (8 days) warranted caution when interpreting the safety profile. Collectively, our findings position imrecoxib as a reliable NSAID option for routine use, particularly in patients where safety considerations are paramount.

Furthermore, our results suggest that treatment persistence with imrecoxib may yield incremental benefits: while short-term therapy provides rapid pain control, prolonged treatment maintained or even improved effectiveness without a significant increase in reported adverse events within the observed timeframe. This finding is especially relevant for patients with chronic or severe pain who often require long-term NSAID therapy. While these results are promising, prospective studies with longer follow-up and active AE monitoring are warranted to fully establish the long-term safety of imrecoxib. Taken together, these data underscore the clinical utility of imrecoxib as a safe and effective analgesic suitable for broad patient populations and diverse indications. Future prospective and comparative real-world studies will be valuable to further define its role in long-term pain management and to establish evidence-based strategies for optimizing treatment duration in different patient groups.
