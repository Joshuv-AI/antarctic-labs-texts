Preparation and evaluation of [64Cu]Cu-labeled bisphosphonate amide of DOTA using reactor-produced 64Cu as an affordable skeletal PET imaging agent
Authors: Priyalata S S Shetty, K V Vimalnath, Sourav Patra, Sutapa Rakhit, Avik Chakraborty, Sudipta Chakraborty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-invasive imaging of skeletal metastases by Positron Emission Tomography (PET) for cancer prognosis, staging and treatment monitoring is one of the prominent fields of radiopharmaceutical research. Among the radionuclides suitable for PET imaging, 64Cu [T1/2 = 12.7 h., EC = 45%, β− =37.1%, β+ =17.9%, Eβ+(max) = 0.65 MeV] is a versatile candidate due to its attractive decay properties. In the present article, we have investigated the potential of 64Cu-labeled 4-{[(bis (phosphonomethyl)) carbamoyl] methyl}-7,10-bis (carboxymethyl)-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododec-1-yl) acetic acid (BPAMD), prepared using low specific activity 64Cu produced in research reactor toward skeletal imaging by PET. An optimized protocol for the radiolabeling of 64Cu (~ 150 MBq) with the BPAMD in high radiochemical purity (> 95%) and excellent in vitro stability was achieved. Comparative hydroxyapatite binding affinity analysis showed that [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD demonstrated significantly higher affinity compared to the [64Cu]Cu-complex of corresponding polyaza macrocyclic ligand DOTMP (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1′,4′,7′,10′-tetramethylenephosphic acid). In vivo evaluation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD in healthy Wistar rats showed selective bone uptake with good contrast and rapid clearance from non-target organs. Overall, the approach of using low specific activity reactor produced 64Cu presents a cost-effective, cyclotron independent alternative to 68Ga/18F based PET agents for detection of skeletal lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43985-9.Keywords: Skeletal metastases, PET imaging, Bisphosphonates, BPAMD, 64Cu, Research reactor, Low specific activitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Metastatic bone lesions are a distinctive hallmark of advanced stage of malignancies and most commonly associated with cancers of the breast, prostate, and lung1. Their presence presages numerous clinical complications and typically signifies a grim prognosis. Hence, early and accurate detection and monitoring of skeletal metastases is imperative for staging of cancer and assessment of therapeutic efficacy especially in cases where the patient may be unsuitable for invasive procedures such as biopsy.

Radiometal complexes of geminal bisphosphonates have been extensively utilized in clinical settings for more than five decades as imaging probes for efficient detection of skeletal metastases and more recently as palliative agents to alleviate pain arising out of skeletal metastases2–4. The choice of geminal bisphosphonates is attributed to their strong and selective affinity for Ca2+ ions in hydroxyapatite (HA), the principle mineralised component in osseous tissues, leading to their preferential accumulation in regions of high bone turnover such as metastatic lesions5. Preferential localization of bisphosphonates in the sites of metastatic bone lesions combined with suitable radionuclides has led to the development of various ‘bone seeking’ radiopharmaceuticals for diagnostic as well as therapeutic applications. Among the different nuclear medicine approaches, bone scintigraphy or single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) based on 99mTc-labeled bisphosphonates, particularly [99mTc]Tc-MDP (MDP = methylene diphosphonate) is the most widely used imaging modality6. However, introduction of bone seeking positron emission tomography (PET) imaging probes and use of PET/CT hybrid imaging technique not only offered superior image resolution and sensitivity but also allow quantification of the radiotracer accumulation in the skeletal lesions. Consequently, detection and monitoring of skeletal metastases became more efficient and sensitive, which in turn facilitates early and efficient diagnosis of cancer metastases. This has driven increasing efforts toward the development of new PET imaging agents for detection of metastatic skeletal lesions. In this endeavour, flourine-18 (18F, T1/2 = 110 min) and gallium-68 (68Ga, T1/2 = 68 min) were primarily the radioisotopes of choice7–10.

While the cyclotron produced 18F as sodium fluoride [18F]NaF is presently the most commonly used PET radiotracer for bone imaging, its widespread application is restricted by logistical challenges due to its short half-life, thus limiting its practical utility to facilities having proximity to a local cyclotron. On the other hand, generator produced 68Ga although more accessible, its relatively shorter half-life limits its application for delayed imaging acquisition in cases of slow tracer uptake, whereas its higher positron energy [Eβ+(max) = 1.9 MeV, 89%] may affect the spatial resolution. Nevertheless, several 68Ga-labeled macrocyclic bisphosphonates have been successfully evaluated clinically for skeletal imaging11–13. In recent times, there is a renewed interest in the use of 64Cu (T1/2 = 12.7 h., EC 45%, β− 37.1%, β+ 17.9%) owing to its moderate half-life and unique decay properties for the development of radiopharmaceuticals with theranostic potential14. The relatively lower positron energy [Eβ+(max) = 0.65 MeV] of 64Cu when compared to 68Ga offers higher spatial resolution in PET imaging which may help in the detection of small, early-stage metastases. It also shows versatility with respect to available production routes. No carrier added (NCA) 64Cu albeit most commonly produced via 64Ni (p, n) 64Cu route in a cyclotron, can also be obtained in a research reactor employing 64Zn (n, p) 64Cu method. The direct production of 64Cu via 63Cu (n, γ) 64Cu reaction in nuclear reactor yields low specific activity (LSA) 64Cu but offers key advantages such as simplified post-irradiation radiochemical processing with minimal radioactive waste, scalability to multi-curie level production leading to its cost-effectiveness.

The use of LSA 64Cu produced via 63Cu (n, γ) 64Cu route in medium flux research reactor as a PET radiotracer for different malignancies and in renal imaging has been demonstrated by our group in earlier studies15,16. The present study was undertaken to expand the potential application of reactor produced 64Cu LSA in PET imaging of skeletal lesions by radiolabeling with a DOTA (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid)-conjugated bisphosphonate, namely, BPAMD (4-{[bis-(phosphonomethyl)) carbamoyl] methyl}−7,10-bis(carboxymethyl)−1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododec-1-yl) acetic acid). It is worth mentioning at this point that the number of medium-low energy cyclotrons (incident proton energy < 30 MeV) has significantly increased in recent years, and the half-life of 64Cu (12.7 h) does not prevent its distribution from a centralized production facility. Nonetheless, it should be recognized that the availability of an alternative production method of 64Cu can help ensure the distribution of some of 64Cu-radiopahrmaceutical formulations, such as 64Cu-based PET radiotracers imaging skeletal metastases, in the event of a cyclotron production network failure.

Macrocyclic bisphosphonates such as BPAMD when radiolabelled with 68Ga and 177Lu have been reported to exhibit enhanced bone uptake compared to acyclic phosphonates9,17–19. Toward this, we have conducted radiolabelling studies across multiple parameters to establish an optimized protocol to obtain [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation using LSA 64Cu. In vitro stability of the formulation was monitored in physiological saline and human serum whereas its bone specificity determined by in vitro HA binding studies. A comparative study to evaluate the HA binding affinity of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD with the [64Cu]Cu-complex of corresponding polyaza macrocyclic ligand, namely DOTMP [1,4,7,10-Tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetrayl-tetrakis(methylphosphonic acid] was also carried out. The adsorption properties of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD on hydroxyapatite was investigated using different isotherm models. Additionally, an attempt was made to determine the mechanism of binding of non-radioactive Cu-BPAMD complex on HA by radiotracer technique using 45Ca-labeled HA particles. Subsequently, pre-clinical evaluation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD was carried out by in vivo PET imaging and ex vivo biodistribution studies in healthy Wistar rats.

Our results on radiochemical studies and pre-clinical investigations demonstrate the potential utility of the radiotracer prepared using LSA 64Cu produced by cost-effective 63Cu (n, γ) 64Cu route. The scientific significance of the reported work thereby lies in the development of a potent, affordable and cyclotron independent alternative for PET imaging of skeletal metastases. Experimental

Materials

Copper (II) oxide (CuO) powder of natural isotopic composition (69.1% 63Cu, > 99.99% chemical purity, Merck, Germany) was used as the target material for production of 64Cu. Hydrochloric acid 30% (Suprapur®) and sodium hydrogen carbonate (Spectroscopic grade) were purchased from Merck (Germany). 4-{[(bis (phosphonomethyl)) carbamoyl] methyl}−7,10-bis (carboxymethyl)−1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododec-1-yl) acetic acid (BPAMD) was purchased from ABX Advanced Biochemical Compounds, Germany. 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetramethylenephosphonic acid (DOTMP) was sourced from Macrocyclics Inc., USA. Sterile, pyrogen free normal saline was procured from Ives Drugs, India. For the investigation of in vitro stability of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD complex, fresh human serum was procured from Merck, India. Hydroxyapatite (HA) microparticles of 1–10 μm size range used in in vitro binding studies were synthesized and characterized following the procedure reported earlier20. Citric acid, sodium citrate and all other chemicals used in this study were of analytical reagent (AR) grade and procured from Merck (Germany). Demineralised water (Milli-Q®, resistivity ~ 18.2 MΩ.cm) was used in all the radiochemical experiments.

Methods

Radioactivity yield of 64Cu was assayed using a pre-calibrated Curiementor 3 Isotope Calibrator (PTW-Freiburg, Germany) whereas its radionuclidic purity determined by gamma ray spectrometry using High purity germanium (HPGe) detector (Canberra Eurisys, France) coupled to a 4 K multichannel analyser (MCA) system. Energy and efficiency calibration was performed using a 152Eu reference source (Amersham Inc., USA). Radio-thin layer chromatography (radio-TLC) was performed using silica gel 60, F254 coated TLC aluminium sheets (Merck, Germany). The distribution of radioactivity on the TLC strips was analysed using a radio-chromatography scanner (MiniGITA, Raytest, Germany). All other radioactivity measurements were made using a well type NaI(Tl) scintillation counter (Mucha, Raytest, Germany) unless mentioned otherwise.

Sterile filtration of the radiolabeled formulation was conducted using Millex-GP syringe filters (0.22 μm) obtained from Merck-Millipore, Germany. Preclinical biological evaluation, whole body Positron Emission Tomography (PET) images of healthy Wistar rats recorded using Siemens Biograph 6 (Siemens Corporation, Germany) PET scanner. All animal experiments were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations approved by the institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (Reference: BAEC/12/2024). It is also confirmed that all methods related to animal experiments were performed in accordance with ARRIVE relevant guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Production and quality control of [64Cu]CuCl2 radiochemical precursor

Copper-64 was produced by direct neutron activation of natural CuO target (69.1% 63Cu). A measured amount (~ 5 mg) of CuO powder was irradiated in Dhruva reactor for 3–4 days at a thermal neutron flux of ~ 1.6 × 1014 n.cm− 2.s− 1. At the end of irradiation (EOI), the target was dissolved in 10 mL 1 M HCl by gentle warming inside a 100 mm lead shielded glove box equipped with remote handling devices. The resultant clear solution was evaporated to near dryness and reconstituted in 5 mL of Milli-Q® water. The last step was repeated twice and subsequently, the final reconstitution was done in 5 mL of Milli-Q® water to obtain [64Cu]CuCl2 solution.

The total radioactivity content of 64Cu produced was assayed using a pre-calibrated isotope dose calibrator. Radionuclidic purity of [64Cu]CuCl2 was determined by γ-ray spectrometry of appropriately diluted aliquots using pre-calibrated HPGe detector coupled to a 4 K MCA system whereas the radiochemical purity of 64Cu in the form of 64Cu2+was assessed by radio-TLC using 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH ~ 4–5) as the mobile phase.

Formulation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD complex

The optimum protocol for the preparation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation was arrived at by systematically studying the effect of different parameters such as ligand concentration, reaction time and temperature on the radiolabelling yield. The general radiolabelling protocol followed was addition of 50 µL [64Cu]CuCl2 solution (~ 150 MBq, 0.63 ± 0.06 µmol Cu) to a 500 µL solution of 0.5–2.0 mg BPAMD (0.86–3.44 µmol) in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH ~ 5.0). The resultant reaction mixtures (pH ~ 4.5) were incubated at room temperature as well as 90° C for varied time duration up to maximum 60 min. Subsequently, radiochemical purity (% RCP) of each reaction mixture was evaluated by radio-TLC and radio-HPLC techniques. Radio-TLC was performed using Ammonia: Ethanol (EtOH): H2O (1:20:40, v/v) as the developing solvent. As a control, radio-TLC pattern of [64Cu]CuCl2 was also obtained using the same protocol. For radio-HPLC, a dual pump HPLC unit with a C-18 reversed phase HiQ-Sil (5 µM, 25 cm×0.46 cm) column (JASCO PU 1580 Plus, JASCO, Japan), a tunable UV/Vis detector (JASCO 1575 Plus, JASCO, Japan) and a well type NaI(Tl) scintillation detector (Mucha, Raytest, Germany) was used. All the solvents used for HPLC analyses were of HPLC grade and degassed and filtered prior to use. Water (A) and acetonitrile (B) mixtures with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid were used as the mobile phase and the following gradient elution technique was adopted: 0–2 min 100% A, 2–4 min 100% A to 0% A (0% B to 100% B), 4–14 min 100% B, 14–5 min 100% B to 0% B (0% A to 100% A). The elution was monitored both by detecting UV signals at 220 nm as well as by radioactivity signal using NaI(Tl) detector.

The radiopharmaceutical formulation prepared following the optimized protocol was passed through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and collected in a sealed sterile glass and subsequently tested for in vitro stability and biological efficacy.

Preparation of cold Cu-BPAMD complex

Cu-BPAMD complex was prepared by following the optimized synthesis protocol established for [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD resulting in > 95% complexation yield, only using natCuCl2 solution instead of [64Cu]CuCl2 radiochemical.

Characterization of Cu-BPAMD complex by UV-Vis, 1H-NMR and ESI-MS

The formation of Cu-BPAMD complex following the synthesis protocol described in the previous section using [64Cu]CuCl2 was confirmed by recording UV-Vis, 1H-NMR and Electrospray ionization-mass spectroscopy (ESI-MS) using cold Cu-BPAMD complex. For UV-Vis spectroscopy, spectral scans of the samples taken in a 1 cm quartz cuvette were recorded over 200–800 nm range using OceanHDX miniature Spectrometer, Germany. For comparison, the UV-Vis spectrum of CuCl2 solution and BPAMD solution were also recorded under similar conditions. 1H-NMR spectra of BPAMD and Cu-BPAMD complex were recorded on a 60 MHz Nanalysis NMR Spectrometer, Canada. Mass spectra were recorded on ExpressionL Avion Mass Spectrometer USA, using Electrospray ionization (ESI) both in positive and negative modes.

In vitro stability studies

The in vitro stability of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation was tested in physiological saline and human serum. For this study, 0.1 mL aliquot of the formulation was mixed with 0.9 mL of physiological saline as well as freshly procured human serum and the mixtures were incubated at 37° C up to 48 h post preparation. Aliquots were taken from both the mixtures at defined time points during the incubation period and the %RCP of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD ascertained by radio-TLC and radio-HPLC as described previously. To assess serum stability, ethanol was added to the aliquots to precipitate serum proteins and the supernatant was analysed.

Investigation of the mechanism of uptake of Cu-BPAMD on hydroxyapatite

The possible mechanism of uptake of Cu-BPAMD complex on hydroxyapatite (HA), considered to mimic the mineralized component of bone matrix, was investigated by radiotracer technique. For this, radioactive HA particles, intrinsically radiolabeled with 45Ca [T1/2 = 163 d, Eβ-(max) = 0.3 MeV] were synthesized as per the procedure described in ‘Supplementary File Sect. 3’. In order to study the binding mechanism, Cu-BPAMD complex was incubated with a measured amount of [45Ca]Ca-HA (of known radioactivity) suspension in 1 ml Milli-Q water at ambient temperature. Radioactivity (due to 45Ca) released into the supernatant was monitored at various time intervals up to 7 days with reference to blank (prepared without addition of the Cu-complex). As a control, same amount of CuCl2 solution used for preparation of Cu-BPAMD was also incubated with 45Ca-HA under identical conditions.

Pre-clinical evaluation in animal model

Pre-clinical biological evaluation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation was carried out by in vivo PET imaging and ex vivo biodistribution studies in healthy Wistar rats, bred and reared in the institutional animal house facility of Bhabha Atomic Research Centre. For both the studies, [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation was freshly prepared following optimized protocol before commencing the experimental procedure. For PET imaging, male Wistar rats (n = 4), each weighing 200–225 g, were used. Anaesthesia was induced in each animal through controlled CO2 exposure followed by administration of ~ 0.1 mL of appropriately diluted sterile [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation (~ 7.4 MBq) through one of the lateral tail veins. At the end of 1 h and 24 h post-administration, whole body PET images were acquired to evaluate the uptake and clearance of the radiolabelled formulation from the regions of interest (ROIs) including bone, liver and kidneys, etc. For ex vivo biodistribution studies, male Wistar rats (n = 16), each weighing 200–225 g, were randomly divided into different groups each comprising of four animals. Each animal was administered with ~ 0.1 mL of appropriately diluted solution of the formulation (~ 2.0 MBq) through one of the lateral tail veins. One group of four animals were sacrificed at the end of 30 min, 1 h, 3 h and 24 h post-injection (p.i.) of the radiotracer by CO2 asphyxiation. Various organs and tissues were excised following sacrifice, washed with normal saline, dried and the radioactivity associated with each organ and tissue was determined using a flat-type NaI(Tl) counter. The weight of the organs and samples of various tissues were also determined by using an analytical balance. The percent injected activity (dose) in various organs/tissue (%ID/organ) and %ID/g of each organ/tissue were calculated from the above data. Appropriate decay correction on the activity injected into each animal initially was employed at the time of counting the radioactivity at various time points. This gives the biodistribution pattern [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD for various time points post-administration specified above. The total uptake in blood, bone and muscles were calculated by assuming that 7%, 10% and 40% of the body weight are constituted by this organs/tissue, respectively21.

Results and discussion

Production and quality control of [64Cu]CuCl2 radiochemical precursor

Batch yield and specific activity of 64Cu produced by direct neutron activation of natural CuO target were 17.5 ± 0.9 GBq (473 ± 24 mCi) and 4.3 ± 0.2 GBq/mg (117.3 ± 7.2 mCi/mg) at the end of irradiation (EOI), averaged over six batches. The production parameters were optimized as reported previously to minimize the formation of 65Zn impurity22. Gamma ray spectra of the [64Cu]CuCl2 solution obtained after radiochemical processing of irradiated CuO target showed photopeaks at 511 and 1345 keV only, which are characteristics of 64Cu (Fig. 2a). The level of 65Zn present in 64Cu determined by analysis of the γ-ray spectra of decayed samples was found to be < 10− 5% at the end radiochemical processing. Radio-TLC pattern of [64Cu]CuCl2 solution in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH ~ 4–5) (Fig. 2b) showed the movement of the radioactive specie along with the solvent front (Rf ~ 0.9) indicating that 64Cu exists as ionic 64Cu2+ and almost no 64Cu in colloidal form. Radiochemical purity of [64Cu]CuCl2 formulation as desired 64Cu2+ form was > 98% in all the batches. Optimization of protocol for the formulation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD

Percentage radiolabeling yields (or radiochemical purities) of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD complex were determined for various reaction conditions by radio-TLC using ammonia: ethanol: water (1:20:40 v/v) as the developing solvent, where the radiolabeled complex moved along the solvent front (Rf ~ 0.9–1.0.9.0) whereas unlabelled 64Cu remained at the point of spotting (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b shows a typical HPLC radio-chromatogram of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation prepared using the optimized protocol, where the radiolabeled complex was found to be eluted as a single peak having retention time of 10.9 min. The kinetics of complexation of BPAMD with 64Cu2+ were evaluated at room temperature and at 90° C using varying amounts of ligand while maintaining a fixed concentration of the radiometal (0.63 ± 0.06 µmol of Cu containing ~ 150 MBq of 64Cu radioactivity). The time-dependent radiolabeling yields as a function concentration of BPAMD ligand at room temperature and 90° C are shown in Fig. 4a,b, respectively. It was observed that at room temperature, radiolabelling yield > 90% could be obtained after a reaction time of 45 min only when 2 mg BPAMD was used. However, when the reaction was carried out at 100° C, radiolabelling yield > 95% was achieved with even 1 mg ligand and reaction time of 45 min. Based on these experimental findings, an optimized protocol for the formulation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD was established, employing a ligand to radiometal molar ratio of ~ 2.7:1 and a reaction time of 45 min at 90 °C. The typical clinical dose administered for PET imaging using 64Cu is in the range of 216–370 MBq (6–10 mCi)23. Assuming a minimum specific activity of 64Cu at 1.85 GBq/mg (50 mCi/mg), the total copper content in the formulation would not exceed 0.2 mg which is well below the reported cytotoxic threshold for copper24. This estimate includes potential decay losses during radiochemical processing and distribution logistics to the PET imaging facility.

Characterization of Cu-BPAMD complex

The UV-Vis spectrum of Cu-BPAMD complex prepared following the optimized protocol for formulation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD is shown in Fig. 5 along with that of CuCl2 and BPAMD. It was found that when Cu-BPAMD complex was formed, there is a shift in the λmax compared to those of CuCl2 and BPAMD along with a notable increase in absorbance compared to the same concentration of CuCl2 and BPAMD used for formulation of Cu-BPAMD. Additionally, a new shoulder peak between 250 and 280 nm also appears in the formation of the Cu-BPAMD complex which was absent in the UV-Vis spectra of BPAMD and CuCl2 solutions. The shoulder peak possibly signifies new electronic transitions associated with the interactions between the metal centre and the ligand25. Comparison of 1H-NMR (D2O, 60 MHz) spectra of Cu-BPAMD and BPAMD showed disappearance of δ 3.8–3.9 (bs, 16 H, cyclen-CH2-N) peak of BPAMD in the 1H-NMR spectrum of Cu-BPAMD. Since Cu2+ is paramagnetic, it results downfield shift of δ 3.8–3.9 (bs, 16 H, cyclen-CH2-N) peak of BPAMD once it is complexed with Cu2+, which eventually got merged with the solvent peak at δ 4.8. This indirectly proved the formation of Cu-BPAMD complex. Formation of Cu-BPAMD complex was also confirmed from MS (ESI+): mass (calculated) C17H31N5O13P2Cu 640.97; [m + 4 H]4+ (observed) 161.5 [63Cu] & 163.7 [65Cu].

In vitro hydroxyapatite binding study

The percentage binding of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD and [64Cu]Cu-DOTMP complexes with varying amounts of HA are depicted in Fig. 7 whereas Table 2 lists the calculated values for distribution coefficient (Kd) of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD and [64Cu]Cu-DOTMP complexes for HA microparticles. It is seen that when equilibrated with 10 mg of HA, binding of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD with the solid matrix was ~ 100%, whereas that of [64Cu]Cu-DOTMP was significantly low (~ 35%) under identical conditions. Moreover, from the Kd values, it can be seen that adsorption of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD on hydroxyapatite is significantly higher as compared to [64Cu]Cu-DOTMP. The enhanced affinity of the 64Cu-complex macrocyclic bisphosphonate ligand for hydroxyapatite (HA) over that of polyaza macrocyclic phosphonate ligand could be attributed to the coordination of radiometal in [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD exclusively through the nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms of the DOTA moiety, leaving the phosphonate groups unbound and available for stronger interaction with hydroxyapatite (HA). This also established that unlike radiolanthanides such as 153Sm and 177Lu, use of bisphosphonate ligand framework is essential in designing 64Cu-radiopharmaceutical targeting bone lesions. Hence, presence of a macrocyclic chelator framework attached with bisphosphonate moiety, as in the case of BPMAD forms the most preferred ligand framework for 64Cu-radiopharmaceuticals for targeting metastatic skeletal lesions.

Adsorption studies of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD on HA

The adsorption isotherms obtained by linear fitting of the experimentally determined values of qe and Ce are shown in Fig. 8 (Langmuir isotherm) and Fig. S1 and S2 (Freundlich and Langmuir-Freundlich isotherms respectively). It was observed the value of correlation coefficient (R2) in case of Langmuir model was very close to 1 (R2 = 0.99) thus implying monolayer adsorption of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD over hydroxyapatite particles whereas Freundlich and Langmuir-Freundlich isotherms models showed poor fit as the value of R2 was 0.82 for both models and hence were found unsuitable. From the slope of the Langmuir plot, the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity (qmax) was determined to be 54.76 mg.g− 1.

Linear fitting of Langmuir isotherm model for sorption of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD on HA microparticles.

The van’t Hoff plot obtained by plotting ln Kc against 1/T is depicted in Fig. 9. From the values of slope and intercept and using Eqs. (5,6), the calculated values of ΔH° and ΔS° were 71.5 kJ.mol− 1 and 0.25 kJ.mol− 1.K− 1, respectively, confirming the endothermic nature of the adsorption process. The values of ΔG° at 298, 311 and 333 K were − 3.02, −6.27 and − 11.77 kJ.mol− 1 respectively indicating that adsorption of of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD over hydroxyapatite particles is mostly physical in nature at ambient temperature and at 311 K (38⁰ C) and ion exchange may possibly occur only at higher temperatures26,27.

Investigation of the mechanism of uptake of Cu-BPAMD on hydroxyapatite

Radiotracer experiments were carried out using 45Ca-labeled HA to ascertain a probable mechanism of binding of the copper-bisphosphonate complex with the HA matrix, which represents the mineralized tissue of bone surface. Figure 10 shows percentage of 45Ca released from 45Ca-labeled HA after different time of incubation with Cu-BPAMD complex as well as free Cu2+. The results demonstrated that the release of 45Ca from the HA matrix into the supernatant was minimal over the course of the incubation period. These findings suggest that the binding of Cu-BPAMD to HA possibly occurs via surface coordination rather than through ionic exchange or competitive displacement of calcium ions.

In-vivo biological evaluation

Representative whole body PET images of a health male Wistar rat recorded at 1 h and 24 h p.i. of ~ 7.4 MBq of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation are shown in Fig. 11a,b, respectively. Semi-quantitative assessment of the distribution of radiotracer activity was carried out by determining SUVmax values in skeleton, liver and kidneys from the PET/CT images. The results are summarized in Table 3. It was found that at 1 h p.i., SUVmax in pelvis and thoracic vertebra were 8.2 ± 1.3 and 7.3 ± 2.2, respectively. On the other hand, SUVmax in liver and kidneys were 1.2 ± 0.3 and 8.4 ± 1.2 at the same time point. These make it obvious that the formulation demonstrates rapid skeletal accumulation with almost no accumulation in any other major organ/tissue except in the kidneys [Fig. 11a, Table 3]. Uptake in the kidneys is indicative of the clearance of the residual injected radioactivity not accumulated in skeleton through urinary route. The delayed image recorded at 24 h p.i. (~ 2 half-lives of 64Cu) and SUVmax values obtained from it, [Fig. 11b and Table 3] show that the accumulated [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation is retained in skeleton without being leached out to any other non-target organ/tissue. All these are potential attributes towards its future utility in clinical PET imaging of skeletal metastases. These results were further corroborated by carrying out ex vivo biodistribution studies in the same animal model, wherein a quantitative assessment of uptake and clearance pattern of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD could be obtained. The biodistribution pattern is presented in Fig. 12. It was observed that 46.82 ± 2.14% of the injected activity accumulated in the skeleton within 30 min p.i. without any significant accumulation in any other major organ/tissue except kidneys. Accumulation in the kidneys was 3.27 ± 0.52% of injected activity at 30 min p.i., which gradually reduced to 0.08 ± 0.02% at 24 h indicating clearance of residual activity not bound to skeleton via renal route. Clearance from blood was very fast, only 0.83 ± 0.12% of injected activity retained in blood at 30 min p.i. Activity accumulated in skeleton found to retain till 24 p.i. up to which study was carried out (46.21 ± 2.32% of injected activity in skeleton at 24 h p.i.). Biodistribution pattern of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD formulation in healthy Wistar rats also established the kinetic rigidity of the formulation in vivo, as almost no uptake of administered activity could be observed in non-target organs, especially liver, upto 24 h p.i.

Several 68Ga-labeled macrocyclic bisphosphonates have been proposed and successfully evaluated for skeletal imaging, [68Ga]Ga-BPAMD is most prominent among these9,10,13,28. Consequently, a comparison between the developed [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD and [68Ga]Ga-BPAMD would be important. In this case, skeletal uptake of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD was found to be significantly higher compared to [64Cu]Cu-DOTMP (p = 0.0002). These data clearly established the utility of bisphosphonate ligand for designing a potent bone-targeting PET using 64Cu.

Conclusion

In this study, we have explored the feasibility of utilizing low specific activity 64Cu produced via the (n, γ) neutron activation route in the formulation of a potent radiopharmaceutical formulation for skeletal PET imaging. Toward this, we have developed and optimized a robust protocol for the formulation of [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD with high radiochemical yield and excellent in vitro stability. Adsorption studies on hydroxyapatite indicated that physical adsorption by surface coordination could be the possible mechanism of uptake of the [64Cu]Cu-BPAMD on the mineralized surface of bone matrix. The radiolabeled complex demonstrated favorable biodistribution in preclinical settings, suggesting its potential utility for bone imaging applications. The approach of using low specific activity 64Cu in the development of the radiolabeled BPAMD complex offers a cost-effective alternative to currently used PET radiopharmaceuticals with 68Ga or 18F. Moreover, this approach would improve the accessibility of formulations for clinical PET imaging, particularly in settings with limited access to cyclotron.
