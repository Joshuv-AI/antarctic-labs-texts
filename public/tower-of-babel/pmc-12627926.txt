Post-discharge Virtual Reality Rehabilitation for a Stroke Patient with Unilateral Spatial Neglect: Improving Daily Function and Caregiver Perceptions
Authors: Tatsushi Okawa, Yuki Uchiyama, Tomoyo Taketa, Kaito Yamamoto, Nao Ogaki, Yukihisa Hashimoto, Kazuhisa Domen
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Unilateral spatial neglect (USN) is characterized by impaired detection, reporting, response, and orientation to stimuli that are contralateral to a lesion in a cerebral hemisphere.1) Approximately 90% of USN cases are associated with right hemisphere damage. Symptoms persist for greater than 3 months in 17% of patients,2) impairing activities of daily living (ADL) and increasing caregiver burden.3)

Virtual reality (VR)-based rehabilitation has been proposed as an effective approach for USN and other cognitive impairments.4,5) With advances in VR technology, research on immersive VR rehabilitation using head-mounted displays has increased.4) Salatino et al.4) reported that immersive VR enables user-centered environments, making it valuable for tailored rehabilitation protocols. However, most reports have focused on improvements in tabletop assessments, with limited evidence supporting the impact of immersive VR on ADL and instrumental ADL (IADL).4,5,6) Furthermore, most studies on VR rehabilitation for USN have focused on post-stroke patients in the acute or subacute phase,4) with few investigating its effects in patients after discharge. Additionally, the challenges faced by patients and caregivers post-discharge also remain underexplored.

In this case, we performed immersive VR rehabilitation for a post-discharge stroke patient with persistent left-sided neglect and attention deficits following a right putaminal hemorrhage. The intervention aimed to reduce the impact of USN on daily life, enhance participation in meaningful activities, and ultimately reduce the caregiver’s burden.

CASE

A 74-year-old right-handed woman with hypertension was admitted with left hemiplegia. She had been independent in ADL. On admission, she was alert and oriented, but demonstrated dysarthria, left hemiplegia, left-sided USN, and moderate superficial sensory impairment in the left upper and lower limbs. The Stroke Impairment Assessment Set motor scores were 2 for the upper extremity (shoulder flexion), 3 for the hand (finger function), and 3/3/3 for the lower extremity (hip flexion, knee extension, and ankle dorsiflexion).7) A head computed tomography scan revealed a right putaminal hemorrhage (Fig. 1). Anti-hypertensive therapy was initiated. Rehabilitation was started on day 1, including ambulatory exercises, upper-limb functional training, ADL training, and swallowing training. The hemiplegia and sensory impairment gradually improved but USN persisted. On day 13, she was transferred to a convalescent rehabilitation hospital. At transfer, her Functional Independence Measure (FIM) score was 66 (motor, 38; cognitive, 28).8) During rehabilitation, she regained ambulatory ability and was discharged home on day 105. At discharge, she could walk independently up to 500 m but required supervision because of residual USN and attention deficits. Her FIM score had improved to 105 (motor, 76; cognitive, 29).

Computed tomography scan of the patient’s head at admission. A cerebral hemorrhage was observed in the right putamen: maximum diameter, 52.25 mm; estimated volume, ~20 mL.

On day 115, the patient and her daughter, who was also her caregiver, visited our outpatient clinic to participate in a rehabilitation program using immersive VR for USN. Written informed consent was obtained for publication. Tables 1 and 2 summarize the pre- and post-intervention results. Initial assessment revealed a near-normal Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score9) (28/30) and the Trail-Making Test (TMT)10) score indicated mild executive dysfunction. The conventional subtests of the Behavioral Inattention Test (BIT)11) were within the normal range (cutoff, 131), whereas the Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE)12) score (57/66) indicated that upper-extremity motor function was moderately impaired. Although the FMA-UE score improved by 5 points from pre- to post-intervention, no significant changes were noted in the other subscales. In contrast, Catherine Bergego Scale (CBS)13,14) scores were 6 and 13 for self-assessment and caregiver assessment (range, 0–30), respectively, showing a substantial discrepancy. Higher scores on CBS indicate greater severity of neglect symptoms. The difference was particularly evident for item 6 [forgets about the left side of the body] and item 8 [collides with objects on the left], suggesting functional impairment related to USN and anosognosia. According to the caregiver, the patient had difficulty choosing a seat on public transportation, struggled to stay focused on household tasks, and failed to notice people approaching from behind. Although cooking was restricted after discharge for safety, the patient wished to resume preparing meals independently.

Clinical evaluation before and after VR intervention

The immersive VR rehabilitation program consisted of 17 sessions, each lasting 40–60 min. Sessions were held two or three times per week. Details of the VR systems are shown in Fig. 2. Initially, reaction time was assessed using a VR system (Videre; SysNet Technologies) (Fig. 2C, D). Delayed responses were observed across various locations, including on the right side (Fig. 3A).

Virtual reality rehabilitation program. (A, B) Reaching tasks using the mediVR KAGURA system. Within the VR environment, the patient was tasked with reaching movements to touch target objects appearing at various distances and angles. The system categorized reaching distances into short (arm length), medium (intermediate distance), and long (90% of maximum reach), with targets appearing randomly within a range of 0°–180°. (C, D) Assessment of reaction time using the Videre system. Wearing a head-mounted display with free head movement, the patient was tasked with visually searching for and recognizing spherical targets appearing within a virtual space measuring 10 × 20 m. Upon locating a target, the patient pressed a switch to indicate recognition. Targets appeared randomly at a total of 231 locations within the virtual environment, varying in height (low, 0.8 m; middle, 1.2 m; high, 1.6 m), radius (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 m), and angular position (0°–180°). The location and reaction time for each target were recorded. (E) Dual-task training using the Videre system. The task involved sorting objects, such as numbers, animals, or colored items, into correctly labeled boxes placed in the near space, while simultaneously reading numbers displayed in the far space.

Reaction times within the VR space evaluated using the Videre system. (A) Results of the pre-intervention evaluation. (B) Results of the post-intervention assessment. Colors represent the reaction times to targets: blue, <1 s; light blue, 1–2.99 s; green, 3–4.99 s; yellow, 5–6.99 s; red, ≥7 s. The light blue and light green areas were reduced in the post-intervention assessment but remained to some extent, whereas yellow and red areas disappeared completely.

From the fifth session, immersive VR training was initiated using a medical VR system (KAGURA; mediVR) (Fig. 2A, B) to provide structured, stimulus-driven reaching tasks. This training was performed for 15 min per session, twice daily, over five sessions.

From the tenth session, dual-task training was introduced using the Videre system, which was selected for its ability to simulate ecologically valid multi-tasking environments involving both near and far space (Fig. 2E). The task required simultaneous object-sorting and number-reading (Fig. 2E). Dual-task training lasted 15 min per session, twice daily, for six sessions. The training parameters were individualized to the patient’s performance and fatigue, following the manufacturer’s recommendations and previous studies.4) Task difficulty was progressively increased once the patient achieved 70%–80% success, and dual-task training was introduced after stable single-task performance without excessive fatigue. Feedback was provided through visual cues within the system and therapist instructions when necessary. No adverse events were observed during rehabilitation using immersive VR. In addition to VR interventions, the patient underwent multi-task training, such as ring toss or cooking while boiling water. Counseling was provided for daily USN management, such as placing reminders to avoid neglecting food on the left side of the plate.

The patient’s progress was reassessed on the final day of the intervention period (day 180). The BIT score changed only slightly (Table 1). However, post-intervention, the reaction time assessment using the Videre system (Fig. 3B) showed that delayed responses were reduced. The self-rated CBS and caregiver-rated scores decreased to 1 and 7, respectively, indicating improvement in USN symptoms (Table 2). In the caregiver assessment, items 6 and 8 of the CBS were noted as showing the most improvement after the intervention. By the end of the program, the patient had regained partial independence in IADL. She was able to commute alone to our hospital using public transport on familiar routes, and she could prepare simple meals, such as boiling noodles, leading to reduced caregiver burden.

DISCUSSION

This report highlights the potential of immersive VR-based rehabilitation to address daily challenges faced by patients with USN in the post-discharge phase. This stage has received limited attention in conventional rehabilitation research, especially regarding caregiver perspectives and burden. The patient described here experienced considerable difficulties in daily life because of persistent USN and generalized attention deficits. After assessing impairments at home from both patient and caregiver perspectives, immersive VR rehabilitation was implemented as a structured, repetitive approach that promoted patient engagement and real-world adaptability. This approach mitigated the impact of USN on daily life and contributed to patient independence and caregiver well-being.

In this case, although the BIT score was above the cutoff, the CBS-assessed impact of USN on daily life was evident. Previous research reported that the frequency of symptoms of neglect can differ between tabletop assessments and real-life situations.15) Factors such as far-space neglect, dynamic targets, and dual-task conditions might contribute to this discrepancy.16,17) Moreover, neglect often manifests as an inability to respond to stimuli in the neglected space,15,18) resulting in frequent omissions and errors in response to passive stimuli.

We used the CBS score as the primary outcome because it directly reflected ADL difficulties not detected by the BIT. The CBS standardizes the evaluation of neglect behavior in daily life and assesses patients’ awareness of their symptoms by comparing observational and interview data.19,20) Although the CBS was designed as a therapist-rated and self-rated scale, we incorporated caregiver ratings to better capture the patient’s neglect-related difficulties at home. Combining CBS evaluations with interviews that identified home-specific issues and goals provided deeper insights into practical USN-related problems, underscoring the importance of incorporating multiple perspectives in rehabilitation planning.

Neglect-related difficulties in ADL are difficult to address with conventional rehabilitation. In this case, Videre evaluation focused on stimulus-driven (bottom-up) attention and showed delayed responses mainly on the left side but also on the right side, indicating impaired spatial attention and generalized deficits in automatic attention. Although the Trail-Making Test score suggested only mild impairment, interviews suggested broader deficits in divided, sustained, and selective attention, reflecting impairments in goal-directed (top-down) control. Immersive VR rehabilitation provides structured, repetitive training with immediate feedback while targeting both bottom-up and top-down processes, potentially enhancing neuroplasticity and real-world functioning.21,22)

Another key component was dual-task training, which enhances cognitive flexibility and attention control.23,24) Patients with USN frequently struggle with dual-task situations, exacerbating attention overload and impacting functional independence.17) The VR program simulated such real-world demands and might have reinforced the patient’s ability to shift and divide their attention, contributing to improvements in IADL. Nevertheless, these improvements were anecdotal and were not confirmed by objective data, so cautious interpretation is warranted.

The discrepancy between self-rated and caregiver-rated CBS scores showed little reduction, suggesting that anosognosia persisted despite functional gains. This might be attributable to the chronic nature of anosognosia and the heightened sensitivity of caregivers to subtle daily-life difficulties, indicating that self-awareness of deficits is less amenable to change than attentional performance. Nevertheless, immersive VR may promote self-monitoring through immediate sensory feedback, and its potential to enhance self-awareness warrants further study.

This report has several limitations. First, it is based on a single case, limiting generalizability. Second, concurrent non-VR therapies and possible spontaneous recovery in the chronic phase might have influenced the outcomes. Most recovery from USN is considered to occur within 12–14 weeks after onset,25) and the improvement in reaction times on the Videre system suggests a task-specific contribution of VR. Third, caregiver-rated CBS scores might have been influenced by expectancy bias or the Hawthorne effect. Fourth, longitudinal CBS data were not collected, leaving the improvement trajectory unclear. Weekly assessments were omitted because frequent testing could have introduced bias and burden. Despite these limitations, this case highlights the novelty of applying immersive VR rehabilitation in the post-discharge phase, when caregiver perspectives and burden are especially important. Structured, feedback-based training with immersive VR may support daily life adaptation and help reduce the burden of chronic USN for both patients and caregivers.
