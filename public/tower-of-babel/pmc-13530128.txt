Ultrasonographic fractal dimension analysis for quantitative assessment of smoking-related parenchymal pattern complexity of the parotid gland
Authors: Şuheda Erdem, Mehmet Egemen Aydemir, Şule Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCigarette smoking may induce oxidative, inflammatory, and microvascular alterations in salivary gland tissue. However, subtle parenchymal changes may not be readily appreciable on conventional ultrasonography. This study aimed to evaluate smoking-related differences in image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity using fractal analysis of B-mode ultrasonographic images. This prospective cross-sectional study included 95 adults: 47 smokers and 48 non-smokers. All participants underwent standardized B-mode ultrasonographic evaluation of both parotid glands. Three predefined regions of interest (ROIs) were analyzed in each gland, and fractal dimension (FD) values were calculated using the two-dimensional box-counting method. Between-group comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Correlation, ROC, multivariable regression, effect-size estimation, and intraobserver reproducibility analyses were also performed. FD values differed between smokers and non-smokers in a region-specific pattern. Significant between-group differences were observed in right ROI 2, left ROI 1, and the mean FD value of the left parotid gland. The mean FD value of the left parotid gland showed the largest effect size and the highest area under the curve. Within the smoker group, left ROI 2 was negatively correlated with age and smoking duration. After adjustment for age and gender, smoking status remained independently associated with right ROI 2, left ROI 1, and the mean FD value of the left parotid gland. Intraobserver reproducibility was high, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.87 to 0.92. Quantitative ultrasonographic fractal analysis demonstrated region-specific differences in image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity between smokers and non-smokers. This reproducible, non-invasive approach may complement conventional ultrasonography by providing an objective assessment of subtle smoking-related parotid gland alterations.Keywords: Parotid gland, Salivary glands, Smoking, Ultrasonography, Fractals, Image processingSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The parotid gland, the largest of the major salivary glands, plays a fundamental role in maintaining oral and systemic homeostasis through the production of saliva. It is primarily composed of serous acini responsible for secreting an α-amylase–rich, serous saliva that facilitates digestion and protects oral tissues1. Saliva contributes to multiple essential functions, including lubrication, pH buffering, antimicrobial defense, remineralization of enamel, and preservation of mucosal integrity2. Its secretion is tightly regulated by autonomic innervation, which coordinates acinar and ductal cell activity to maintain a stable internal environment under varying physiological conditions3,4. Disruption of salivary gland structure and function may therefore have important local and systemic consequences, and has been associated with alterations in systemic homeostasis5. A variety of systemic conditions and environmental exposures can influence salivary gland physiology, among which cigarette smoking represents a major modifiable risk factor.

Cigarette smoke contains numerous toxic and carcinogenic compounds that induce oxidative stress, inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and fibrotic changes in biological tissues. In salivary glands, these mechanisms have been associated with acinar degeneration, ductal alterations, impaired microcirculation, and changes in glandular elasticity and volume6–8. The parotid gland is composed predominantly of serous acini, and its secretory function requires coordinated autonomic regulation and adequate glandular perfusion1,3,4. These characteristics provide a biological rationale for investigating the parotid gland as a potential target of smoking-related oxidative and microvascular injury. Consistent with these mechanisms, ultrasonographic studies in smokers have demonstrated parenchymal heterogeneity, altered vascular patterns, increased tissue stiffness, and changes in glandular volume8. Similar ultrasonographic alterations have been reported in systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, further illustrating the responsiveness of salivary gland parenchyma to systemic and environmental stressors9. However, subtle smoking-related differences in parenchymal image patterns may remain difficult to identify through conventional visual assessment.

Ultrasonography is widely used in the evaluation of salivary glands due to its non-invasive nature, accessibility, and absence of ionizing radiation. It enables assessment of glandular morphology, echogenicity, vascularity, and structural integrity, and has demonstrated high diagnostic performance in various conditions, including autoimmune disorders such as Sjögren’s syndrome10. Nevertheless, conventional ultrasonographic evaluation is largely qualitative and operator-dependent, which limits its sensitivity for detecting subtle parenchymal alterations and reduces reproducibility across observers11. These limitations have led to increasing interest in objective and reproducible quantitative imaging approaches capable of characterizing tissue architecture beyond visual interpretation. Among these approaches, texture analysis and fractal analysis have emerged as promising tools for the quantitative characterization of biological tissues. Fractal geometry provides a mathematical framework for describing complex, self-similar structures, and the fractal dimension (FD) serves as an image-derived quantitative descriptor of spatial pattern complexity and irregularity12. In biological systems, alterations in fractal properties have been associated with pathological processes such as degeneration, inflammation, and fibrosis, reflecting underlying changes in tissue organization13. Fractal and related texture-based analyses have shown potential for characterizing salivary gland tumors and treatment-related parenchymal alterations, including those following radiotherapy14,15.

Despite these advances, the application of fractal analysis to ultrasonographic evaluation of salivary gland parenchyma remains limited. Recent studies have demonstrated that fractal analysis can characterize salivary gland structure and distinguish between gland types; however, its sensitivity to functional or physiological changes appears limited16. More importantly, although smoking is known to induce microstructural and vascular alterations in salivary gland tissue, there is a lack of quantitative imaging studies investigating whether these changes can be detected using fractal analysis of ultrasonographic images. In addition, structural alterations within the parotid gland are unlikely to occur uniformly throughout the entire parenchyma. Instead, such changes may exhibit spatial heterogeneity, reflecting localized variations in vascular supply, tissue composition, and exposure-related effects. Therefore, region-specific evaluation of glandular parenchyma may provide increased sensitivity for detecting subtle structural differences compared to global or whole-gland assessments.

Given the need for objective, reproducible, and non-invasive methods capable of capturing subtle parenchymal alterations, fractal analysis of ultrasonographic images may offer a valuable quantitative approach for evaluating smoking-related differences in image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity. Quantitative characterization of smoking-related parotid alterations may improve the interpretation of ultrasonographic findings by providing an objective reference for exposure-related parenchymal variation. Such information may also help reduce potential confusion between smoking-associated physiological changes and pathological abnormalities. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate differences in image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity between smokers and non-smokers using region-based ultrasonographic fractal analysis. By integrating standardized imaging acquisition with quantitative fractal analysis, this study seeks to explore the potential of fractal dimension as a marker of subtle parenchymal alterations associated with cigarette smoking.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a prospective cross-sectional investigation. Ethical approval was obtained prior to study initiation from the Burdur Mehmet Akif Ersoy University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/2253, Date: 10.12.2025), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study procedures. Participants were categorized into two groups based on smoking status: smokers and non-smokers. The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a two-tailed independent-samples t-test comparing FD values between smokers and non-smokers. Assuming a standardized effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.68, a significance level of 0.05, 90% statistical power, and an allocation ratio of 1:1, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 94 participants. The final study sample consisted of 95 participants, including 47 smokers and 48 non-smokers.

The inclusion criteria for the smoker group were: age ≥ 18 years, absence of current medication use, and a history of active cigarette smoking for at least one year. For the non-smoker group, inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, absence of current medication use, and no history of cigarette smoking, defined as never having smoked in their lifetime. Exclusion criteria for both groups included: history of head and neck radiotherapy, active malignancy, prior parotid gland pathology, previous surgery involving the salivary glands or adjacent structures, rheumatologic diseases including rheumatoid arthritis and Sjögren’s syndrome, diabetes mellitus, other endocrine disorders, and chronic infectious diseases such as HIV and hepatitis B or C.

Ultrasonographic imaging protocol

Bilateral parotid glands were evaluated on the same day using B-mode ultrasonography with a high-frequency 12L-RS wideband linear array transducer on a GE Versana Premier system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). All examinations were performed by a single experienced operator trained in head and neck ultrasonography to minimize inter-operator variability. Imaging parameters, including transducer frequency, imaging depth, focal zone, gain, and dynamic range, were standardized and kept constant across all examinations using a predefined preset. Participants were examined in the supine position with slight contralateral head rotation to optimize gland visualization. After application of acoustic coupling gel, the transducer was placed gently on the skin surface, avoiding excessive pressure to prevent deformation of the glandular parenchyma.

Multiple transverse images were acquired at successive craniocaudal levels of each parotid gland using a standardized anatomical orientation. Because of interindividual anatomical variation, no single fixed absolute craniocaudal level was imposed. The transverse imaging plane was standardized using the mandibular ramus as the anterior landmark and the masseter muscle as the deep reference structure. For each gland, one image was selected for analysis using predefined criteria: simultaneous visualization of the mandibular ramus and masseter muscle, clear delineation of a sufficiently broad and continuous area of superficial parotid parenchyma, and absence of motion artifacts, acoustic shadowing, or other image distortions. These criteria were applied consistently in all participants to standardize the selected transverse plane. All images were archived in the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and subsequently exported for fractal analysis.

Fractal analysis

All ultrasonographic images were converted to TIFF format and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined within the parotid parenchyma on the selected transverse images.

For each parotid gland, the visible superficial parenchyma was visually divided according to its relative anteroposterior extent into approximate anterior, central, and posterior regions. No fixed geometric boundaries or predefined proportional coordinates were used. Three identical square ROIs, each measuring 30 × 30 pixels, were placed approximately at the center of these regions and recorded as ROI-1, ROI-2, and ROI-3, respectively (Fig. 1). The ROIs were positioned within the superficial parenchymal portion of the gland, at a comparable relative depth beneath the skin surface and approximately parallel to the skin contour. A fixed millimetric skin-to-ROI distance was not used because of interindividual anatomical variation. Deeper parenchymal portions were not selected because their visualization was less consistent and they more frequently contained vascular structures or acoustic artifacts. All ROIs were located entirely within homogeneous glandular parenchyma, avoiding gland boundaries, visible ducts, vascular structures, intraparotid lymph nodes, acoustic shadows, and focal artifacts. Participants in whom any of these structures or artifacts prevented standardized placement of all three ROIs were excluded from the study. The same sequence of operations, with identical parameter values, was applied to every ROI for every participant, without any image-specific adjustments at any stage. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. For comparisons between smokers and non-smokers, the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for parametric tests and r = Z/√N for Mann–Whitney U tests. Associations between quantitative variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed data. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to eight prespecified primary between-group FD comparisons: Right ROI 1, Right ROI 2, Right ROI 3, Right mean FD, Left ROI 1, Left ROI 2, Left ROI 3, and Left mean FD. Correlation, ROC, and multivariable regression analyses were considered secondary exploratory analyses and were not subjected to FDR correction.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of FD parameters for smoking status. For each parameter, the area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut-off value were calculated. The optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index. To assess whether associations between smoking status and FD values were independent of potential confounders, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed for each ROI and for mean gland FD values. In these models, FD values were entered as dependent variables, while smoking status, age, and gender were included as independent variables. Regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were reported.

Intraobserver reproducibility was evaluated in a randomly selected subset of 19 participants. FD measurements were repeated after a two-week interval by the same blinded observer. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. Statistical significance for the eight primary between-group comparisons was determined using FDR-adjusted p-values, whereas a raw p-value < 0.05 was used for the secondary exploratory analyses.

Results

A total of 95 adults were included in the study, comprising 47 smokers and 48 non-smokers. The overall cohort consisted of 46 males (48.4%) and 49 females (51.6%). The mean age of the participants was 28.89 ± 8.62 years, with a mean age of 27.72 ± 8.08 years in the smoker group and 30.04 ± 8.05 years in the non-smoker group. The median duration of smoking among smokers was 7.0 years (IQR: 2.5–11.0 years).

FD values differed between smokers and non-smokers in a region-specific pattern (Table 1). After Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, between-group differences remained statistically significant for right ROI 2 (adjusted p = 0.030), left ROI 1 (adjusted p = 0.004), and the mean FD value of the left parotid gland (adjusted p < 0.001) (Table 1). The difference in left ROI 3 was significant before correction (raw p = 0.038) but did not remain significant after FDR adjustment (adjusted p = 0.076). No significant differences were observed for the remaining parameters. The mean FD value of the left parotid gland had the largest effect size among all evaluated parameters.

Discussion

This study investigated image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity in smokers using quantitative ultrasonographic fractal analysis. The findings demonstrated that FD differences were not uniformly distributed across all regions but followed a region-specific pattern. The most consistent differences were observed in the left parotid gland. This pattern suggests that smoking-related differences in image-derived parenchymal complexity may occur in a spatially heterogeneous rather than uniform manner. The observed region-dependent variation in FD values indicates spatial heterogeneity in image-derived parenchymal pattern complexity among smokers. Whether these differences correspond to specific underlying biological changes remains uncertain.

The more pronounced findings in the left parotid gland may reflect subtle bilateral differences in glandular morphology, perfusion, or regional susceptibility to smoking-related oxidative and microvascular injury. Although no established mechanism currently explains preferential left-sided involvement, anatomical asymmetry and regional variation in tissue organization may contribute to this pattern. Technical factors related to the acoustic window and single-slice sampling should also be considered. The apparent left-sided predominance was not an a priori hypothesis of the study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. It may represent a chance finding related to multiple regional comparisons, single-slice sampling, or technical factors rather than a biologically established unilateral effect. Accordingly, this laterality should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating observation requiring confirmation in larger bilateral and volumetric studies.

The region-specific nature of these findings is consistent with the biological complexity of salivary gland architecture. Chronic exposure to cigarette smoke is known to induce oxidative stress through the generation of reactive oxygen species. This process may lead to acinar degeneration, vascular constriction, and interstitial fibrosis within salivary tissues18. In addition, nicotine-mediated vasoconstriction and inflammatory cytokine release may impair microvascular perfusion, resulting in localized hypoxia and subsequent tissue remodeling19. Previous experimental and imaging studies have further demonstrated that long-term smoking exposure can lead to acinar atrophy, fatty infiltration, and fibrosis in salivary glands8,20. These previously reported biological alterations provide plausible hypotheses for the region-specific imaging differences observed in the present study. However, the current data do not establish a direct relationship between the observed FD differences and any specific histological or microvascular alteration.

Fractal dimension is an image-derived quantitative descriptor of spatial pattern complexity and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of a specific histopathological process. Accordingly, the higher FD values observed in selected regions in the present study indicate differences in the complexity of ultrasonographic image patterns between smokers and non-smokers. Previous studies have associated alterations in fractal properties with changes in tissue organization, including inflammatory and fibrotic processes13; however, such mechanisms remain hypothetical explanations for the present findings. The current study did not directly assess fibrosis, inflammation, acinar degeneration, or microvascular injury.

The present findings are in agreement with and extend previous research on quantitative imaging of salivary glands. Chikui et al.14 demonstrated that fractal analysis of ultrasonographic images could differentiate between normal and neoplastic salivary tissues based on texture complexity. Similarly, texture-based approaches have been shown to detect structural changes in parotid glands following radiotherapy. In these studies, variations in fractal dimension were associated with tissue remodeling and functional impairment15. Other studies have highlighted the ability of ultrasound-based texture parameters to distinguish pathological from normal glandular tissue and to improve diagnostic objectivity11. In contrast, fractal analysis applied to physiological conditions, such as gland stimulation, has shown limited sensitivity to transient functional changes, suggesting that FD primarily reflects structural rather than short-term functional alterations16. Within this context, the present study provides preliminary evidence that ultrasonographic FD measurements may reflect subtle exposure-related structural differences in otherwise healthy individuals.

Another important finding of this study is the independent association between smoking status and selected FD parameters after adjustment for age and gender. This finding indicates that the observed associations were not explained solely by age and gender. However, it does not establish a specific biological mechanism, and the observed FD differences may be compatible with smoking-related alterations in glandular tissue. The negative correlations of FD values in left ROI 2 with both age and smoking duration may reflect progressive age- and exposure-related alterations. However, these exploratory correlations should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional design and the absence of detailed cumulative smoking-exposure data. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that chronic exposure to systemic or environmental stressors can influence salivary gland structure over time9. The moderate discriminatory performance observed in the ROC analysis indicates that FD parameters should not be interpreted as standalone diagnostic markers. Furthermore, the optimal cut-off values were derived and evaluated in the same sample without internal or external validation and should therefore be considered exploratory rather than clinically applicable thresholds. From a clinical perspective, quantitative fractal analysis may complement conventional ultrasonography by providing an objective measure of parenchymal complexity. Characterizing smoking-related parotid alterations may help clinicians contextualize subtle ultrasound findings and reduce the risk of interpreting exposure-related variation directly as glandular pathology. The relatively higher performance of the mean FD value of the left parotid gland also suggests that region-specific and combined measurements may be more informative than isolated ROI values. Nevertheless, because the present study did not include patients with established parotid disease, the ability of this approach to differentiate smoking-related alterations from pathological changes remains unproven and should be evaluated in future comparative studies.

The high intraobserver reproducibility observed in this study represents an additional strength. The ICC values obtained indicate good-to-excellent agreement, supporting the reliability of the fractal analysis protocol and its potential applicability in clinical and research settings. Given that conventional ultrasonography is inherently operator-dependent, the use of standardized quantitative approaches such as fractal analysis may help improve measurement consistency and reduce subjective variability.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the relatively limited sample size may restrict generalizability. Daily cigarette consumption and pack-year data were unavailable, preventing a detailed assessment of cumulative exposure. The study was conducted at a single center using one ultrasound system and a single operator. The preprocessing workflow was adapted from a dental radiographic fractal-analysis protocol and has not been specifically validated for ultrasonographic parotid images. In addition, the Gaussian blur parameter was large relative to the ROI dimensions, and the resulting FD values may therefore be sensitive to image-boundary handling, sigma selection, and ROI size. Future studies should evaluate the robustness of this preprocessing approach across different sigma values and ROI dimensions. Fractal analysis was based on manually selected ROIs within the superficial parenchyma of a single two-dimensional image from each gland. In addition, the subdivision of the visible parenchyma into anterior, central, and posterior regions and the relative depth of ROI placement were based on visual assessment rather than fixed geometric boundaries or predefined coordinates. Therefore, some degree of investigator-dependent variability in ROI placement cannot be excluded. Deeper and three-dimensional glandular architecture may not have been fully represented. Although intraobserver reproducibility was high, interobserver reliability was not assessed. The apparent left-sided predominance may also have been influenced by anatomical, technical, or sampling-related factors. In addition, the absence of biochemical salivary markers limited functional correlation, and the lack of a pathological comparison group and direct histological validation prevented the observed FD differences from being linked to specific underlying tissue alterations. The differential diagnostic value of the method therefore remains to be established. Future multicenter studies incorporating detailed smoking-exposure data, volumetric imaging, functional assessment, and pathological comparison groups are needed.

Conclusion

Quantitative ultrasonographic fractal analysis provides a reproducible and non-invasive approach for assessing image-derived parotid parenchymal pattern complexity. Smoking was associated with region-specific differences in FD values, indicating a spatially heterogeneous pattern of image-derived parenchymal complexity rather than a uniform bilateral pattern. Although the observed differences support the potential of fractal analysis as a complementary quantitative approach for characterizing subtle parenchymal variation, its clinical diagnostic utility remains to be established.
