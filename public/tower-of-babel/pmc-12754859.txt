IMProving older people’s health through physical ACTivity: the implementation of a community-based framework to promote park-based active ageing
Authors: Pazit Levinger, Marcia Fearn, Bronwyn Dreher, Adrian Bauman, Sze-Ee Soh, Natasha K Brusco, Andrew Gilbert, Rachel Muoio, Elissa Burton, Keith Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutdoor environments such as recreational spaces with age-friendly exercise equipment offer promising and sustainable approaches to promote physical activity among older people. Strategies are needed to effectively engage older adults to use such spaces. The ENJOY IMP-ACT project aimed to increase physical activity participation among older people through a structured implementation framework across six communities in Victoria, Australia.MethodThis hybrid type II implementation study used a pre-post mixed methods design across six public parks. Following a 3-month control phase, local councils implemented a 9-month TERM framework intervention (Training, Engagement, Resource development, and Marketing), followed by 3-months maintenance. Direct observations assessed park usage, user demographics, use of Seniors Exercise Park equipment and participation in physical activity. Intercept surveys with older park users collected physical activity levels and health-related characteristics. Generalised linear models were used to evaluate the number of older park users engaging in physical activity, while regression models adjusted for key sociodemographic characteristics were used to examine their physical activity levels.ResultsA total of 12,444 visitors attended the six parks during all data collection periods, 15% (n = 1,884) were older people. The number of older park users was four times higher following the intervention (at 9-months intervention, IRR 4.03; 95% CI 2.55; 6.36) relative to the baseline period. The number of older people who were physically active in the parks was also higher at the 9-months intervention period (IRR 2.03; 95% CI 1.50, 2.75). Across study periods, 301 intercept surveys were collected. In the intervention and maintenance periods, over 70% of respondents met recommended physical activity levels although no change was observed following the intervention amongst park users.DiscussionThe TERM intervention incorporating various community engagement and capacity building activities led to increased usage of active park equipment by older adults, demonstrating the value of a collaborative framework involving councils and community members.Trial registrationThis trial is prospectively registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry. Trial registration number ACTRN12622001256763. Date registered 20/09/2022. https://www.anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12622001256763.aspx.Keywords: Seniors Exercise Park, Physical activity, Exercise, Built environment, Older people

Introduction

Global populations are ageing, including in Australia, where the number of older adults is projected to double to 8.8 million by 2057, comprising 22% of the total population [1]. Ageing is associated with complex health challenges, but regular physical activity is recognised as important for reducing chronic disease risk and maintaining functional capacity [2, 3]. Despite strong evidence, most older adults do not meet the recommended physical activity guidelines [4] and participation in exercise programs remains generally low [5]. Similarly, despite the many proven physical activity interventions, few have been implemented at scale [6]. It has been acknowledged that scaling up evidence-based physical activity interventions into practice requires substantial implementation effort [7]. As the older population continues to grow, supporting sustainable physical activity programs is important for promoting their health and well-being.

Over the past decade, we have developed and rigorously evaluated an innovative outdoor physical activity program featuring specialised outdoor exercise equipment designed for older adults, known as the ENJOY Seniors Exercise Park. The Seniors Exercise Park outdoor equipment features multi work-stations design to improve multiple domains including, balance, strength, mobility and function. Our research has demonstrated its cost-effectiveness, scalability, and long-term sustainability in enhancing older people’s health and well-being and increasing their participation in physical activity in the community [8–14]. Through extensive studies of active community engagement, we have shown that creating age-friendly parks—by installing the Seniors Exercise Park and offering supportive programs—significantly increased park visits and encouraged older adults to engage in park-based physical activity [15, 16]. Notably, partnerships with local governments and meaningful community involvement have proven essential for co-creating impactful changes in both the built environment and the community [8, 17].

Building on these findings, we have developed an implementation framework aimed at capacity-building activities, fostering knowledge and sustainable practices within communities to further increase park visits and park-based physical activity participation among older adults [8]. The ENJOY IMP-ACT (IMProving older people’s health through physical ACTivity) study aimed to scale up this innovative approach across several local governments (councils/municipalities) in Victoria, Australia, including in regional areas. The project focused on both the process of integrating this approach within communities and its broader impact. Specifically, the ENJOY IMP-ACT project examined how the implementation framework facilitated the adoption and usage of the Seniors Exercise Park in six local areas and older people’s physical activity participation. In doing so, the study provides both an evaluation of the framework as an implementation strategy and evidence of its potential as a scalable, community-based public health promotion model.

Methodology

Study design

The ENJOY IMP-ACT study was a large implementation research project employing a hybrid II implementation-effectiveness pre-post mixed method design [18]. The aim of the present paper is to report on program implementation process (how the framework was applied and adopted by the councils and communities), as well as its effectiveness outcomes, specifically - the change in the number of older people using the Seniors Exercise Parks and the number being physically active at the Seniors Exercise Parks. Older people’s types of usage and uptake (e.g., organised programs, independent usage), characteristics of equipment usage, physical activity levels and perceptions of using the Seniors Exercise Parks were assessed.

Six sites in Victoria, including two regional sites, participated in the study with a pragmatic staggered commencement. Each site underwent a 3-month control period (baseline) followed by a 9-month implementation intervention period (TERM framework intervention: Training, Engagement, Resources development, Marketing and promotion), and a maintenance phase (3 months). Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures involved in this trial were conducted in compliance with the National Statement on Ethical Human Research and the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research. The study was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee, Melbourne Australia (Project ID: 35502). The study was designed according to the Strengthening The Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [20]. Verbal or written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Study setting and partners

Five local governments (six parks/sites) participated in the project (four in metropolitan Melbourne and one in regional Victoria, Australia), including: City of Whittlesea (metropolitan), Nillumbik Shire Council (metropolitan), Hobsons Bay City Council (metropolitan), Wyndham City Council (metropolitan), and Mitchell Shire Council (two sites in regional Victoria). Each site had the Seniors Exercise Park outdoor equipment (Lappset Group Ltd) installed within these parks prior to participation in the study.

Study population

Park visitors

All park visitors were included in the observational data collection at the six sites. Consent from park visitors was not required as participants remained anonymous and the behaviours occurred in a public setting with no collection of personal or identifiable data (as approved by Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee, Melbourne Australia).

Intercept surveys

Older people who visited the participating Seniors Exercise Park areas (who appeared more mature in age) were approached to take part in the intercept surveys. Information about the study was provided to residents/visitors via verbal communication as well as via hard copies of the information sheet. A verbal agreement/consent by the participant was received prior to commencing the survey.

To take part in the face-to-face intercept survey at the park, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) adults who identified themselves as being age 60 and over, (2) adults who were able to understand basic English and have conversational English.

Data collection/procedures

On-site direct park observation

Observation of park visitors and their activities was collected using the System for Observing Play and Recreation in Communities (SOPARC), which is a reliable and feasible instrument for assessing physical activity and associated contextual data in community settings based on momentary time sampling techniques in pre-determined target areas in parks [21]. A single target area that comprised the Seniors Exercise Park equipment and the immediate surrounding space (adjacent facilities/amenities/areas where visitors might interact) was included in the observation data collection. Further details about each site can be found elsewhere [22].

A modified version of the SOPARC was used to record the number of visitors, gender, estimated age group (child -infant up to 12 years, teen − 13–20 years, adult – 21–59 years, older people – 60 years of age and over) and visitors activity types in that space [17]. The type of usage of the Seniors Exercise Park equipment was coded (i.e., using equipment as intended, playing, looking/sitting or passing by) and their physical activity level (inactive, light, moderate, vigorous) [17, 23]. Physical activity level was categorised as follows: inactive – lying down, sitting or standing in place; light – slow pace walking, moving from sitting to standing, stretching, standing on an exercise station; moderate – using the exercise equipment at moderate pace (e.g., stepping up and down, moving from one station to another), brisk walking; vigorous – using the equipment at fast pace, running, jumping, climbing on the equipment. Systematic scans were conducted over a 10-day period (including weekends) with a total of 14 scans per day as follows: 10 min for every 30 min period of all visitors in the study area during early morning (07:30–10:30), mid-day (12:00–13:30) and late afternoon (15:00–17:30) [19].

Evaluation took place monthly during the 3-month control period (baseline), every 3 months during the 9-month implementation intervention phase (I1, I2, I3) and once during the 3-month maintenance phase (M1). In instances where scans were missed (e.g. due to days with extreme weather conditions), scans were rescheduled to an equivalent weekday. Research staff underwent extensive training on how to use the SOPARC method, including attending several practical on-site sessions at the parks with the availability of resources and a project manual/protocol to ensure consistent data collection.

Face to face intercept surveys

Face-to-face intercept surveys were conducted during the park observation periods at the following timepoints: baseline, during the implementation intervention and at the end of the maintenance period. The intercept surveys took approximately 15–20 min to complete and collected demographic information (e.g., age group, gender, country of birth, marital status), their motivation to use the Seniors Exercise Park equipment, frequency of visits, their usual physical activity level, their leisure/recreation activity at the park, and general health and well-being. The socio-economic status of Seniors Exercise Park users was estimated using postcodes to obtain the Australian Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) 2021 Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage. The first SEIFA decile represents geographical areas with the greatest socioeconomic disadvantage, while the tenth decile represents areas with the least disadvantage (or greatest advantage) [24].

The following validated questionnaires were also included within the intercept survey:

Self-reported physical activity was measured using the Active Australia Survey [25] and included six questions that assess walking, moderate and vigorous activity, in addition to an indicator of total activity. We dichotomised total physical activity to determine whether park users met the Australian physical activity guidelines: sufficiently active (≥ 150 min/week) vs. not sufficiently active (< 150 min/week) [26].

Health-related quality of life using the EQ-5D-5 L [27] which comprises five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression), as well as an overall self-rated health status (Visual Analog Scale (VAS) 0–100) where a higher score represents better health. A single utility score ranging from < 0 (quality of life worse than death) and 1 (full quality of life), was derived from the EQ-5D-5 L responses using recently established Australian-specific weights [28].

General well-being was assessed using the five-item World Health Organisation (WHO-5) Well-being questionnaire which provided measures of psychological well-being and depressive symptoms [29, 30]. A percentage score was calculated using the raw score, which ranges from 0 (representing worst imaginable well-being) to 100 (representing best imaginable well-being).

Intervention - Implementation framework ‘TERM’

The implementation strategies were based on our previous piloted implementation framework [8] which included several elements (‘TERM’): Training, Engagement, Resources development, Marketing and promotion. The ‘TERM’ components were considered as important mechanisms in building capacity, knowledge, upskilling and engagement, and may be related to increased usage and uptake of physical activity using the Seniors Exercise Park. Full details of the intervention are provided in the protocol paper [19]. Brief details about the activities that took place under each component as part of the TERM framework delivery and implementation process are described below.

‘TERM’ - Training – knowledge transfer

Training of allied health professionals and community volunteers to support engagement and ongoing usage of the Seniors Exercise Parks was based on our previous work [15, 17] and included the following:

Allied health professionals workshops: a half-day workshop training program was delivered by research staff from the National Ageing Research Institute (NARI) at each site covering safe use, exercise prescription and program design, incorporating risk management, theoretical and practical sessions. Five allied health training modules were delivered in total, across the five participating council locations with a total of 54 attendees, comprising 24 physiotherapists/exercise physiologists/occupational therapists, 4 fitness instructors, 6 allied health assistants, 4 other health related occupations (nurses/dietician/psychologist/chiropractors), 7 council staff, 5 volunteers, and 4 others.

Train the Trainer module (ENJOY volunteers/champions training): a 5-week training module run by NARI, incorporated practical and educational components for people residing within the participating municipalities. The ENJOY champions were trained to welcome and induct community members on how to use the Seniors Exercise Park safely with councils committing to run weekly ‘come and try’ sessions following the volunteers training completion. Volunteers were recruited from the local community by the council through a formal recruitment process with suitable individuals chosen as formal council volunteers. A total of six Train the Trainer modules were delivered – one per project site. Thirty-seven community volunteers completed the training (28 females, 75.7%; 9 males, 24.3%), with 32 volunteers aged over 60. In two sites an additional three volunteers were trained, and by project completion 32 volunteers remained active.

Induction sessions by qualified research staff (e.g., accredited exercise physiologist or physiotherapist): induction sessions were also offered at each site for extra support of park users who may require additional supervision. Approximately 11–12 induction sessions (a total of 69 sessions) were provided to each site during the intervention phase.

‘TERM’ - Engagement

Engagement with the community, older people, and stakeholders (local governments/health and leisure providers) was through: (1) a signed formal agreement with participating councils; (2) facilitation of local community events (e.g., ‘come and try’ volunteers-led sessions, open days, Seniors’ Weeks, Community Health Expos); (3) the establishment of local advisory groups at each site; and (4) the establishment of an overarching project advisory committee (Community of Practice committee), which met approximately every 3–4 months during the delivery of the project, to share successes and problem solve challenges faced at individual parks. Regular meetings between the research and council staff also took place throughout the project, as required.

Community sessions and events - Following the Train the Trainer programs, all councils organised regular ‘come and try’/exercise sessions for community members, which were led by the trained volunteers/champions. Throughout the intervention period, sites ran 1–3 sessions per week (total number of sessions per site range between 13 and 57, with 34–220 participants per site). In the maintenance period, most sites ran weekly sessions. At the completion of the project, all sites were running at least one session per week. A total of eight community events (6 during the intervention and 2 during the maintenance phases) were coordinated and delivered by the councils to promote and support use of the park to the community.

‘TERM’ - Resource development

Relevant resources were developed which comprised on-site information (instructional signage with graphic illustrations of the exercises), traditional hard copy flyers, and an online platform (website, video resources and an online mobile app, My ENJOY Health ® (https://www.nari.net.au/enjoy-me-app). Workshop and training resources were also developed including on-line resources (video materials) and written educational materials (e.g., manuals).

The previously developed web application [31] was further developed to be accessible as a native mobile application (My ENJOY Health ®), suitable for iOS and Android platforms. On-site Quick Response (QR) codes were also fitted on the various exercise stations that allowed direct access to simple instructions, safety tips, and exercise videos [17], as well as access to download the native mobile MY ENJOY Health ® app. Usage of the native app allowed access to additional features, such as workouts and programs, as well as advanced exercises. Extraction of usage data (e.g., number of downloads) and QR codes scans occurred through an online data platform (Metabase) with 165 registered users across the intervention and maintenance periods and 3,702 QR codes scanned during the project period.

‘TERM’ - Marketing and promotion

The council marketing and promotion teams were involved in the promotion of all park activities and events, such as mail out flyers, on-site signage, newsletter stories, local radio stations, social media, and video promotions/photo shoots. The research team worked closely with the relevant council staff and provided a marketing resource kit that each council was able to use and adjust based on their requirements. Councils promoted the Seniors Exercise Park program between 8 and 82 times per site over the intervention period, for between 2 and 9 months, and mainly for the purpose of advertising ‘come & try’ days. Between 4 and 9 mediums for promotion were used, most commonly across Facebook (6 sites), print news (6 sites), and e-news (6 sites), followed by Instagram (4 sites), webpages (4 sites), emails (4 sites), and flyers (4 sites). During the maintenance period, councils promoted the Seniors Exercise Park program between 0 and 15 times per site, for between 1 and 3 months. Similarly to the intervention period, the promotions were mainly for the purpose of advertising ‘come & try’ days, and between 1 and 6 mediums were used.

Statistical analysis

A target outcome for successful Seniors Exercise Park usage

In our previous study, we observed over a twofold increase in the number of older people using the Seniors Exercise Park in a typical week following park upgrade and site activation (e.g., installation of the Seniors Exercise Park equipment complemented by capacity building activities and promotion) [15]. Assuming a baseline count of 10 older adults, we hypothesised that following the implementation intervention, there would be a 100% increase in the number of users (a combination of using the equipment as part of organised classes or independently) in a typical week while accounting for weather impact [32]. After adjusting for site and weather conditions [33], a power of over 0.95 can be achieved to detect a 100% increase in park visitors using a Poisson or negative binomial distribution with this sample size.

Descriptive statistics (counts and relative proportions) were used to report the overall number of park visitors and the various age groups, the number of older people using the Seniors Exercise Parks, as well as the type of usage and uptake. Generalised linear models [33] were used to examine the impact of the implementation strategies on the number of older people using the Seniors Exercise Park as well as those who engaged in physical activity given that both outcomes (i.e. dependent variable) were count data. All models included main effects for the intervention timepoint (i.e. independent variable) and site and season as control variables. Overdispersion was handled using a negative binomial distribution.

Data from the intercept surveys were used to summarise the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics and equipment usage during the intervention and maintenance periods. The physical activity level and health-related characteristics of Seniors Exercise Park area users collected in the intercept survey were reported for each time period: baseline, intervention (combined survey data from Intervention 1, 2 and 3 (I1, I2, I3 respectively) and maintenance. Logistic regression models were used to examine the impact of the implementation intervention on physical activity levels by determining the odds of participants meeting the Australian physical activity guidelines (i.e. dependent variable) in a sample of Senior Exercise Park users. Age, sex, season, site and socio-economic status were included as control variables in these models, with the intervention timepoint as the independent variable. The fit of all models was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with smaller values indicating a better model fit. All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel and Stata/SE18.0 (StataCorp College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

The project was executed sequentially (timing of installation, control periods and activation activities were staggered across the six parks), with data collection between January 2023 and March 2025. At one site, a detection of asbestos led to the park’s closure for three months. All research activities at this location were temporarily paused and resumed when the park reopened to the public. The three-month delay was added to the original schedule, extending the project timeline for this site accordingly.

Impact of implementation framework on usage of the Seniors Exercise Park

A total of 12,444 visitors attended the six parks during all data collection periods, 15% (n = 1,884) were older people. During the baseline period, a total of 179 older people (average across the three baseline timepoints, 10% of all visitors) visited the space, 22% (n = 39) were observed as using the Seniors Exercise Park for exercise, Table 1. During the intervention and maintenance periods the number of older people who attended the space ranged between 266 and 414, with 36–56% observed exercising using the Seniors Exercise Park. Overall, most older people (83–92%) across all periods were observed to be physically active within these spaces, engaging in a range of activities including play, exercise and walking, Table 1. The proportion of older people engaged in exercise using the Seniors Exercise Park increased by 13.7–24.9% during the intervention period time point at the six parks, and by 33.8% during the maintenance period when compared to the baseline.

Park visitors’ demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, physical activity and equipment usage

A total of 301 intercept surveys were collected across the study periods, with the baseline data previously reported [22]. Only three respondents had data missing for a single item across all survey items, resulting in a loss of < 1% of data. Over the intervention and maintenance periods there were 253 intercept surveys collected (190 during the intervention and 63 during maintenance) across the six locations with a 24.7% decline rate (83 refused to be surveyed). Demographic details and park usage during the intervention and maintenance periods are presented in Tables 3, 4 and 5. Most respondents were aged 65–79, female, lived near the park (~ 65%), and were born in Australia, with English as their primary language. Most respondents had visited the park before (84% during intervention, 96% during maintenance), with 49% and 68% using it 1–3 times per week respectively, typically between 10 and 60 min (> 50%), Table 4. Across both periods, most users exercised with company, primarily in group sessions (40% during intervention, 54% during maintenance). The most common activity levels were light (25% and 16%) to moderate (46% and 65%). Regardless of the period, most participants rated their health as good or very good (73–76%), with over 70% meeting recommended physical activity levels, Table 5. Self-reported quality of life (EQ-5D VAS and utility index) was also reported to be good across all time periods.

After adjustment for age, sex, site, seasonal effects and socio-economic status, we did not observe a change in total physical activity levels of respondents across all three timepoints (Table 5). The odds of meeting physical activity guidelines appeared to be higher in the intervention and maintenance periods relative to baseline, however, this was not statistically significant.

Discussion

The growing ageing population highlights the importance of providing supporting environments that permit scaling of effective strategies to improve older people’s physical activity. The ENJOY IMP-ACT project builds on recent evidence showing that upgrading parks with the installation of age-friendly outdoor equipment, the Seniors Exercise Park, significantly increases older adults’ access and use [15]. This evidence-informed approach underpins the present implementation research, focused on embedding and scaling the model across six communities in Victoria. In the present study the number of older people who accessed and used the Seniors Exercise Park increased significantly following implementation across all intervention timepoints and was four times higher during the last intervention phase compared to baseline. Importantly, the number of older people who engaged in physical activity using the outdoor equipment had doubled. The significant increase in access and park usage by older people is consistent with our previous study [15], suggesting that the framework utilised, which was underpinned by a collaborative approach between councils and local community members, is effective in reaching and engaging older people. Both councils and residents were actively involved in supporting the delivery of the intervention, which was essential for its success. This also highlights the framework’s potential as a scalable, community-based and council-supported public health promotion model.

The TERM framework used strategies such as supervised-induction sessions, structured volunteer training, and promotional activities to engage the local community. These aspects are important to better reach and support the community [8]. The framework incorporated multiple, interconnected components that may have collectively contributed to the overall success of the program. The induction sessions, community events and weekly ‘come and try’ sessions, if promoted well by the councils, seemed popular, with large numbers of attendees. Training and upskilling older people as champions can support and empower their peers to maintain participation in physical activity [34]. Using this approach maximises the engagement of the wider community with Seniors Exercise Parks as a sustainable health promotion approach [35]. At the completion of the study all sites maintained the regular weekly ‘come and try’ sessions, highlighting the commitment of the participating councils to support the older demographic longer term.

People who access local parks are more likely to meet the recommended physical activity guidelines by engaging in recreational and incidental movements [36]. The present study demonstrated a similar outcome, where the majority of park users, irrespective of the time period (e.g., baseline, intervention, maintenance), reported high levels of physical activity and met the recommended physical activity guidelines. It was therefore not surprising that we did not observe any changes in the physical activity levels of park equipment users following the intervention. Given that the number of older people who visit local parks and engage in park-based physical activity is relatively low compared to other age groups [36–38], efforts and strategies to increase their visits and access to parks are important, as evidenced in greater visits over time in this study. It is also important to work closely with community groups to identify strategies that encourage participation among those who do not meet the recommended physical activity guidelines or who have lower levels of general health. While the TERM framework incorporated multiple components to engage communities, future adaptations could include targeted outreach and tailored programming for culturally diverse older adults and those who are less physically active, supported by multilingual promotional materials and proactive visits to local seniors’ clubs or community groups.

The availability of age-friendly outdoor exercise equipment enables engagement in forms of exercise other than walking, which can improve strength and balance [31]. The social aspect of exercising with company in group activities also contributed to older people’s engagement in physical activity [16, 35]. In the present study, most respondents from the intercept surveys during the intervention (73%) and maintenance (82%) periods reported using the Seniors Exercise Park during their visits. A large number of older visitors reported using it for group exercise and to engage in light to moderate physical activity. The concept of peer led sessions for community members, as implemented by the present study through the champions training, may further encourage older people to attend their local parks and exercise with their peers. This offers many health benefits, including for physical and social health and well-being.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study employed a pre-post design, with a baseline period serving as the comparator, and did not include control sites. While this limits the ability to draw causal inferences, the selection of comparable control parks and securing participation from local governments in a non-intervention condition would have been impractical. Councils are less inclined to participate in studies where their communities receive no benefit, making randomised site allocation infeasible in real-world settings. Secondly, site-specific factors—including park location, internal council operations, and seasonal weather impact—also influenced implementation timelines. In some instances, the 9-month intervention period overlapped with public holidays or winter, affecting the scheduling of training and community events. Extending the intervention period by an additional three months could assist in mitigating these challenges in future research. Similarly, extending the follow up period beyond 3-months could further provide a valuable insight into how well the intervention became embedded in the community’s structures and culture longer term. Finally, the data collection was based on direct observations, which relied on researcher estimations of individual’s age and visual appearance. While this approach may not always accurately capture age groupings or the full range of visitor activity, it is the most acceptable and widely used method in evaluations of community park equipment over time. Moreover, observations were limited to targeted areas surrounding the Seniors Exercise Park and may not fully represent activity in other parts of the parks. Despite these limitations, the study’s pragmatic and collaborative design is a significant strength. The active involvement of local councils and communities enabled real-world implementation and generated sustainable increases in park usage and activity among older people. This partnership-based approach offers a practical scalable model for future public health interventions targeting age-friendly environments.

Conclusion

A significant increase in both the number of older people using the Seniors Exercise Parks and their participation in physical activity was observed during and following the implementation of the intervention. This suggests that the framework employed was effective in reaching and engaging older adults. The success of the intervention was strongly supported by its collaborative design, which actively involved local councils and community members in its delivery.
