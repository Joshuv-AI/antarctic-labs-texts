Next-generation digital polymerase chain reaction assay for BCR::ABL1 in chronic myeloid leukemia: comprehensive validation for standardized measurable residual disease detection
Authors: Yong-Hwi Kim, Min-Sik Jang, Shin-Hyun Kim, In-Young Roh, Bong-Suk Kim, Kyung-Mi Kee, Soo-Hyun Kim, Dong-Wook Kim, Jae-Hoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe sensitivity of real-time quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) for detecting measurable residual disease (MRD) in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) who have achieved a deep molecular response is limited. We validated a digital PCR (dPCR) assay (LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit) for MRD quantification.MethodsValidation was performed according to CLSI guidelines EP17-A2, EP06, EP05-A3, and EP07 using BCR::ABL1-positive and -negative materials and the World Health Organization (WHO) 1st International Standard. A nonparametric analysis of the limit of blank was used to establish a positivity cutoff of ≥ 2 droplets. The limit of detection (LoD) was verified using a detection criterion of ≥ 95%, and the limit of quantitation (LoQ) was defined as the lowest level meeting ≥ 95% detection and a study-defined %CV of ≤ 76%. Clinical validation included 72 CML specimens for molecular response (MR) category comparisons and 67 specimens for paired quantitative comparisons.ResultsThe LoD and LoQ were both 0.0025% on the International Scale (IS) with a detection rate of 95.8%. The assay showed linearity over 5 logs (R2 = 0.9916–0.9972), within-run %CV ≤ 1.52%, cross-condition %CV ≤ 2.76%, and WHO-traceable accuracy. In 67 paired specimens, the correlation coefficient was r = 0.9740, with a Passing–Bablok slope of 1.0189, intercept of −0.2552, and Bland–Altman mean bias of 0.2266. Among the 72 specimens, dPCR classified 20 as having a deeper MR than RT-qPCR. Reproducibility was characterized by r = 0.99 and a mean bias of 0.0967. Whole blood was preferably processed within 48 h, with processing at 72 h considered acceptable only when BCR QC criteria were met.ConclusionThe LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit enabled sensitive, precise, and reproducible BCR::ABL1 quantification at MR4.6. The clinical significance of a deeper MR classification using dPCR, particularly for treatment-free remission, requires longitudinal validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44313-026-00188-5.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm driven by the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene arising from the reciprocal translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11) [1, 2]. This genetic rearrangement is present in > 90% of patients and produces three principal fusion transcript classes based on breakpoint location—Major (M‑BCR), Minor (m‑BCR), and Micro (μ‑BCR) [3]. The advent of tyrosine kinase inhibitors has dramatically improved prognosis, and achieving a deep molecular response (DMR) has enabled treatment-free remission (TFR) in selected patients [4]. Accordingly, sensitive and accurate quantification of BCR::ABL1 transcripts, which serve as key molecular markers of measurable residual disease (MRD), is central to clinical decision-making, including decisions regarding treatment modification and potential discontinuation [4, 5].

Molecular response (MR) is reported on the International Scale (%IS), with standard thresholds of MR1 (10%IS), MR2 (1%IS), MR3 (0.1%IS), MR4 (0.01%IS), and MR4.5 (0.0032%IS) [6, 7]. In real-world practice, reliable MRD measurements in the DMR range are essential for safe TFR attempts [8]. However, the current standard, real-time quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‑qPCR), relies on standard curves and is subject to inter-laboratory and inter-operator variability [9]. Variability is particularly high at or beyond MR4.5, potentially compromising measurement reliability and increasing the risk of false-negative results [10, 11].

Droplet digital PCR (dPCR) has emerged as a promising alternative, enabling absolute quantification by partitioning reactions into numerous microdroplets and estimating target copy numbers using Poisson statistics. It also demonstrates robustness to variations in PCR efficiency [12, 13]. DPCR can detect low levels of BCR::ABL1 transcripts in the MR4.5–MR5 range [12, 14]. Moreover, dPCR results can be converted to %IS through calibration against World Health Organization (WHO) international standards [11].

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the analytical and clinical validity of a newly designed dPCR assay that simultaneously quantifies the major CML transcript variants, e13a2 and e14a2, and the BCR reference gene, in a single reaction. Specifically, we sought to validate key analytical performance characteristics—sensitivity, accuracy, precision, and specificity—according to international guidelines and compare the assay’s performance with that of conventional RT‑qPCR using clinical specimens to assess its clinical applicability.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Overall, 104 patients with CML were included in this study. Clinical performance and reproducibility were assessed using anonymized total RNA from 79 and 10 patients, respectively, obtained retrospectively from GC Labs (IRB No. GCL-2024–2024-06), and Uijeongbu Eulji Medical Center (IRB No. UEMC 2022–01–019) biobanks. Among the 79 clinical performance specimens, seven failed the initial RNA quality control because the OD260/OD230 ratio was < 1.2, leaving 72 specimens for categorical clinical comparison. Of these, 5 yielded negative dPCR results according to the predefined reporting criterion (≤ 1 positive droplet, below the limit of blank [LoB]) and were included in categorical comparison but not in paired log-transformed quantitative analyses, leaving 67 specimens for Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis. Peripheral blood samples from 15 adult patients with CML were collected at the Uijeongbu Eulji Medical Center, anonymized, and used for analysis (IRB No. UEMC 2025–04–011).

Cell line samples

Five cell lines (K-562, KCL-22, HL-60, AR230-r, and SUP-B15) and BCR-ABL1 WHO standard panels (08/192, 08/194, 08/196, and 08/198) were used. K-562 (ATCC CCL-243™), AR230-r (e1a2-transcript CML, ATCC CRL-3346), SUP-B15 (ALL, ATCC CRL-1929), and HL-60 (APL, ATCC CCL-240™) were purchased from ATCC; KCL-22 (JCRB1317) was purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank; and WHO standard panels were purchased from NIBSC. Cells were cultured under supplier-recommended conditions [15] and maintained at > 90% viability.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA from blood specimens and cell lines was extracted using the High Pure RNA Isolation Kit (F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified and quality-checked (OD260/OD280 and OD260/OD230 ratios) using NanoDrop One/OneC (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Mannheim, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Whole-blood specimen stability

Whole blood from 15 adult patients with CML was collected in 10-mL EDTA tubes and stored at 2–8 °C. Cell lysates were prepared at approximately 24, 48, 72, and 96 h and assessed for BCR reference gene expression using LQ Suite dPCR. At each time point, 1,000 ng of total RNA per specimen was tested in 10 replicates. Specimens were considered stable when BCR was ≥ 2,000 copies/μL at all time points; lysates were stored at ≤ − 70 °C until analysis.

LoB

LoB was determined using total RNA from BCR::ABL1-negative HL-60 cells, and two lots of the LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit (BioTNS Co., Ltd., Daejeon, Republic of Korea) were tested in 60 replicates. According to CLSI EP17-A2 [16], a nonparametric approach designated the 95th percentile (mean of the 57th and 58th ordered measurements) as the LoB.

Limit of Detection (LoD)

LoD was established per CLSI EP17-A2 [16] using e13a2/e14a2 panels at six levels (0.0010%IS–0.0032%IS, MR4.5–MR5.0; 24 replicates/level across 2 lots; 576 data points). Probit analysis via the GLM function in IBM SPSS Statistics v25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and a nonparametric criterion (≥ 95% hit rate) were applied [6].

Limit of Quantitation (LoQ)

The LoQ was derived from the LoD dataset by calculating %IS-based coefficient of variation (%CV) for e13a2 and e14a2 at concentrations ≥ LoD. The LoQ was defined as the lowest level meeting %CV ≤ 76% [17]. This threshold was adopted as a study-specific acceptance criterion based on the FDA-cleared QXDx assay precedent [17] rather than as a universal CLSI-recommended cutoff and was applied specifically to the assay evaluated in this study.

Linearity

Linearity was evaluated using CLSI EP06 [18]. Positive RNA from KCL-22 (e13a2) and K-562 (e14a2) was quantified in %IS using the Real-Q BCR-ABL Quantification Kit (BioSewoom Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and serially diluted with HL-60 RNA to six levels (10.4301%IS [MR0.97] to 0.0006%IS [MR5.26]). Each level was measured in triplicate across two lots of the LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit. Linear regression evaluated log10-transformed expected versus observed values, with acceptance criteria of slope 0.83–1.20 and R2 0.97 [19].

Analytical precision

Precision was evaluated per CLSI EP05-A3 [20] and Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety guidelines [21] using K-562 (BCR::ABL1-positive) spiked into HL-60 (BCR::ABL1-negative) at 1%IS (MR2), 0.1%IS (MR3), and 0.01%IS (MR4). Repeatability was assessed under single conditions (1 lot, 1 instrument, and 1 operator; 2 runs/day, 3 replicates/run for 20 days). Reproducibility was tested across three lots, sites, instruments, and operators (2 runs/day, 3 replicates/run for 5 days).

Analytical specificity

Interference was assessed per CLSI EP07 [22] by spiking potential interferents into low-positive e14a2 RNA (0.1%IS, MR3) and a negative whole-blood matrix. Cholesterol (400 mg/dL), bilirubin (40 mg/dL), hemoglobin (1000 mg/dL), triglyceride (1500 mg/dL), albumin (10 g/dL), heparin (330 units/dL), guanidinium-containing lysis buffer (1%), EDTA (3.4 μmol/L), EtOH (0.1%), and genomic DNA (50 ng/RT) were tested in triplicate.

Analytical accuracy

Accuracy was evaluated per CLSI EP06 [18] using the WHO 1 st International Standard panel (NIBSC code: 09/138; 4 levels, 0.0195%IS–16.3129%IS) tested in triplicate across three lots. Acceptance criteria were slope 0.83–1.20 and R2 ≥ 0.97 [19].

Clinical validation

The clinical performance was assessed using 79 retrospectively collected specimens. Seven samples failed the predefined RNA quality control criterion (OD260/OD230 ratio < 1.2), leaving 72 samples for categorical comparison between LQ Suite dPCR and real-time quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Five specimens showed low-level positivity by RT-qPCR (MR3.92–4.47) but yielded negative dPCR results according to the predefined reporting criterion (≤ 1 positive droplet, below the LoB). These results were treated as valid categorical negative results and were included in the MR-category agreement analyses. However, because negative dPCR results could not be log10-transformed, these five specimens were excluded from the paired quantitative analyses. Therefore, Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analyses were performed using the 67 specimens with quantitative results from both methods. Categorical comparisons used Cohen’s kappa coefficient for MR-category agreement. For the 67 quantifiable specimens, %IS comparison used Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis with the following criteria: r ≥ 0.95 [23], evaluation of proportional and systematic biases via 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for slope and intercept (including 1 and 0, respectively) [24], and overall clinical agreement within a mean bias of ±0.5 log10 [14]. Reproducibility assessed 10 specimens (MR, 0.04–3.93) across three sites, instruments, and operators versus RT-qPCR.

RT-qPCR assays

RT-qPCR was performed as the comparator using the Real-Q BCR-ABL Quantification Kit (BioSewoom Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). cDNA from 1 µg total RNA was analyzed on a QuantStudio™ 5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) per the kit protocol. The BCR::ABL1/ABL1 ratio was converted to %IS and MR using a validated laboratory conversion factor.

LQ suite dPCR assays

BCR::ABL1 was quantified using the LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit in the LQ Suite dPCR system (BioTNS Co., Ltd., Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Reaction mixtures were partitioned using the LQ Suite Droplet Generator, transferred to 96-well plates, and amplified on a SimpliAmp™ Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s and 58 °C for 60 s (2.5 °C/s), and then 98 °C for 10 min. Droplet fluorescence was read using an LQ Suite Droplet Analyzer, and %IS/MR was calculated using NanoFlex software version 1.2.1 (BioTNS Co., Ltd., Daejeon, Republic of Korea).

Statistical analyses

BCR::ABL1 stability was assessed at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test with Greenhouse–Geisser correction for violated sphericity and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. For analytical performance, linearity was evaluated using polynomial and linear regression analyses, and analytical precision (repeatability and reproducibility) was expressed as %CV. Clinical comparisons used Cohen’s kappa coefficient for MR-category agreement, Passing–Bablok regression for quantitative correlation, and Bland–Altman analysis for mean bias and 95% limits of agreement.

Analyses used MedCalc v23.3.5 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium), IBM SPSS Statistics v25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and Microsoft Excel® (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Whole-blood specimen stability

Whole-blood samples from 15 adult patients with CML were stored at 2–8 °C, and BCR control-gene copy numbers (copies/μL) were measured at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using the LQ Suite dPCR system, with 10 replicates per patient (n = 150 per time point, N = 600 overall). At each time point, the mean copy numbers and standard deviations were as follows: 24 h, 2929.7 ± 1508.9; 48 h, 2449.6 ± 1375.8; 72 h, 2201.4 ± 1168.7; and 96 h, 1346.6 ± 831.5 copies/μL. The median values were as follows: 24 h, 2589.05; 48 h, 2190.88; 72 h, 1876.66; and 96 h, 1186.13, with corresponding interquartile ranges of 2120.5, 2284.9, 1461.0, and 847.1, respectively. Repeated-measures ANOVA showed significant group differences (P < 0.001, Greenhouse–Geisser correction). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc analysis confirmed that copy numbers at 96 h were significantly lower than those at earlier time points (P < 0.001); the decrease was also significant between 24 and 72 h (P = 0.001), but the differences between adjacent early time points were not significant. The proportion of samples meeting the assay validity threshold (≥ 2,000 copies/μL) declined over time, and at 96 h, only a minority remained valid (Fig. 1). In summary, BCR reference gene copy number declined significantly between 24 and 72 h (P = 0.001). LoB

The LoB for the LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit was determined using total RNA extracted from the HL-60 cell line. As the measurement values were not normally distributed, a CLSI EP17‑A2-based nonparametric approach was applied to 60 replicates ordered in ascending rank; the 95th percentile (the 57.5th position) corresponded to the 57–58th observations, yielding a BCR::ABL1 LoB of one positive droplet. To ensure specificity based on this nonparametric LoB analysis, the positivity cut‑off was set at ≥ 2 positive droplets (≈0.14 copies/μL) (Supplemental Table 1). Setting a stringent cutoff is paramount in the context of CML management, as it minimizes the risk of false-positive results that can incorrectly influence crucial decisions.

LoD

Both genotypes achieved a 95.8% hit rate at 0.0025%IS (MR4.6), with transcript-specific LoD values of MR4.5990 (e13a2) and MR4.5770 (e14a2). Additional model‑estimated probabilities were 85% at MR4.633 (0.0023%IS), 75% at MR4.659 (0.0022%IS), 50% at MR4.71 (0.0019%IS), and 25% at MR4.76 (0.0017%IS). For reproducibility, %CVs across lots, sites, instruments, and operators ranged from 0.20% to 2.76%, with particularly low between‑instrument and between‑operator %CVs of 0.20% and 0.31%, respectively, confirming minimal variation attributable to manufacturing, testing environment, instruments, or operators (Table 4). Analytical specificity

Analytical specificity was evaluated using interference and cross-reactivity testing. Endogenous interferents (cholesterol, bilirubin, hemoglobin, and triglycerides), exogenous substances (heparin, guanidinium-containing lysis buffer, EDTA, and EtOH), medications, and genomic DNA showed no significant effects on %IS or MR in positive or negative samples (P > 0.05). Even when the interferents were combined, the mean, SD, bias, and %CV met the acceptance criteria (Table 5). Cross-reactivity testing of hematological cancer cell lines, normal human blood, and related gene RNA showed no nonspecific BCR::ABL1 detection; internal controls and the BCR reference gene were consistently detected, confirming the assay validity (Table 6). Clinical validation

Clinical performance was evaluated in 72 adult CML specimens from the Republic of Korea for MR-category comparisons between LQ Suite dPCR and RT-qPCR. A quantitative %IS comparison using Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis was performed on 67 specimens with quantitative results from both methods (Figs. 5 and 6). In the 67 quantifiable specimens, Passing–Bablok regression yielded a slope of 1.0189 (95% CI: 0.9643 to 1.0679), which included one, confirming the absence of proportional bias (Fig. 5). The intercept was −0.2552 (95% CI, − 0.2964 to −0.2332); although it did not include zero, this constant systematic difference remained within the predefined acceptable clinical limit of ±0.5 log10 (Fig. 5). For the log10-transformed %IS, the methods were strongly correlated (r = 0.9740, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5). Bland–Altman analysis indicated a mean bias of 0.2266 (RT-qPCR minus LQ Suite dPCR), with most observations falling within the 95% limit of agreement (Fig. 6). MR-category agreement in the 72-specimen categorical comparison was moderate (Cohen’s kappa = 0.54), with 63.9% (46/72) concordance. Among the 26 discordant cases, 20 were classified as having deeper MR by dPCR, one was classified as having deeper MR by RT-qPCR, and 5 showed low-level positivity by RT-qPCR but negative dPCR results, according to the predefined LoB-based reporting criterion (Table 7). Reproducibility across three sites, instruments, and operators using 10 specimens spanning MR 0.04–3.93 met the criteria at each site, with slope 95% CIs including 1, constant offsets within the predefined ±0.5 log10 clinical limit, and r = 0.99 correlation (Fig. 7). Bland–Altman analysis of log10-transformed %IS demonstrated a low mean bias of 0.0967, with all observations within the 95% limit of agreement and no outliers (Fig. 8). Discussion

This study validated the analytical and clinical performance of the LQ Check BCR::ABL1 Major %IS Kit, demonstrating high sensitivity, precision, specificity, accuracy, and reproducibility compared with conventional RT‑qPCR. The LoD and LoQ were established at 0.0025%IS (MR4.6), exceeding the sensitivity required to detect the MR4.5 (0.0032%IS) threshold recommended by major guidelines for considering TFR [28]. This finding contrasts with previous reports that dPCR sensitivity may be limited to MR4.0 (0.01%IS) owing to false positives [29]. The LoQ of MR4.6 was established using a study-specific criterion of CV ≤ 76%, adopted from a precedent involving an FDA-cleared dPCR assay [17]. Because this is not a universally recommended CLSI cutoff, the resulting LoQ should not be generalized to other BCR::ABL1 dPCR assays.

A quantitative comparison involving 67 Korean CML specimens showed a high correlation (r = 0.9740) and a Bland–Altman mean difference of 0.2266 (RT-qPCR minus LQ Suite dPCR). In the categorical comparison involving 72 specimens, 20 were assigned to deeper MR categories by dPCR, whereas five specimens with low-level positive RT-qPCR results were negative by dPCR according to the predefined LoB-based reporting criterion. Although differences in analytical sensitivity may contribute to these discordances, stochastic variation in target molecule sampling at very low concentrations, followed by Poisson-based partitioning, may also lead to changes between the assays [30]. Furthermore, differences in template integrity or freeze–thaw effects associated with retrospectively archived RNA specimens may have influenced agreement between the assays. Without longitudinal follow-up after TKI discontinuation, whether these deeper MR classifications are associated with improved TFR outcomes remains unknown.

Reproducibility was high across the three institutions, instruments, and operators, supporting the potential for multicenter standardization (Figs. 7 and 8). Analytical specificity testing demonstrated no interference from endogenous or exogenous substances and no cross-reactivity with related genes (Tables 5 and 6). Accuracy testing also confirmed WHO traceability, enabling laboratories to report results directly on the %IS using lot-specific conversion factors (Fig. 4). The clinical relevance of material‑dependent bias also warrants consideration. Passing–Bablok regression of results from 67 clinical specimens yielded a slope of 1.0189 (95% CI, 0.9643 to 1.0679), indicating that the proportional bias observed in cell‑line dilutions (slope, 0.87–0.90) was largely absent in clinical specimens, with the slope approaching the ideal value of 1. This finding supports the assay’s robustness in patient samples (Fig. 5). Although the intercept of −0.2552 (95% CI, − 0.2964 to −0.2332) did not include 0, indicating a small constant systematic difference, this offset remained well within the predefined clinically acceptable equivalence margin of ±0.5 Log10, supporting analytical and clinical comparability between the assays.

Compared with existing droplet-based platforms, the LQ Suite dPCR system offers a streamlined, cost-effective workflow that may reduce hands-on time in clinical laboratories. Regarding specimen handling, total RNA extraction within 48 h is preferred; 72 h represents an exceptional upper limit, with reporting contingent on BCR ≥ 2,000 copies/μL.

Although the study was limited by its pilot scale (n = 72) and the retrospective nature of the archived specimen cohort, strict adherence to multiple CLSI guidelines supports the analytical reliability of the assay for clinical implementation. Future multicenter international studies incorporating longitudinal clinical outcomes are necessary to establish the generalizability of these findings and determine clinical implications for TFR, particularly for the major transcripts that account for > 90% of patients with CML.

In conclusion, the LQ Suite dPCR system provides reliable, reproducible BCR::ABL1 quantification at MR4.6 within the clinically critical DMR range, supporting standardized MRD reporting. However, the observed tendency toward deeper MR classification requires confirmation in prospective studies evaluating TFR outcomes to establish its clinical significance.
