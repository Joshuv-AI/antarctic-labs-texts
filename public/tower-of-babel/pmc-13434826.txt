Bayesian inference of haematopoietic stem/progenitor cell differentiation phenotypic manifolds and their bifurcation points using Gaussian processes and Gibbs sampling
Authors: R D Dowling, J Mellet, E Wolmarans, C Durandt, F Joubert, J P de Villiers, M S Pepper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell differentiation is a fundamental biological process, where cells progress through different stages of maturation to become specialised cell types. Understanding this process is important, and has led to the development of various mathematical models to represent cell behaviour during maturation. Advancements in these models are owed to researchers’ ability to obtain high-throughput genome-scale single-cell gene expression data, which has revolutionised our understanding of complex processes like haematopoiesis. Here we introduce BAGEL: Bayesian Analysis of Gene Expression Lineages, a novel statistical model. BAGEL offers new insights into cell differentiation through (i) a robust Bayesian inference approach that models cell differentiation as a continuous process; and (ii) a powerful projection method that enables visualisation and investigation of similarities and differences between intra- and inter-species single-cell gene expression datasets. The ability of BAGEL’s projection method to harness the collective power of multiple datasets is enormous as it can potentially accelerate and enhance our understanding of cell differentiation. Although this manuscript focuses on haematopoiesis, BAGEL should apply to various single-cell gene expression datasets, providing a deeper understanding of cellular complexity.Keywords: Bayesian inference, Bifurcation points, Cell differentiation, Continuous modelling, Frenet frame, Gaussian process, Gene expression, Gibbs sampler, Haematopoiesis, Single-cell, Tangent vectorSubject terms: Computational models, Machine learning, Statistical methods, Gene expression

Introduction

Cell differentiation is a fundamental biological process which is complex and not yet fully understood1–8. This process is essential for cellular development, where a single cell progresses through a series of orchestrated and continuous stages to form a terminally differentiated cell. Differentiation usually starts with an immature cell that evolves into a diverse set of mature cell types6,9 through the up- and down-regulation of many genes. Haematopoiesis is one example of cell differentiation, as haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) progress through different stages to produce and maintain a continuous supply of mature blood cells10.

According to the traditional model of haematopoiesis, HSPCs are categorised into long-term (LT)-HSPCs and short-term (ST)-HSPCs, with LT-HSPCs being at the apex of the haematopoietic hierarchy. LT-HSPCs have self-renewal capabilities and give rise to ST-HSPCs with limited self-renewal capabilities. ST-HSPCs differentiate into multipotent progenitors (MPP), which are precursors to common lymphoid progenitors (CLP) and common myeloid progenitors (CMP). While MPPs lack self-renewal abilities, they can differentiate into all blood lineages. CLPs further differentiate into lymphoid and natural killer (NK) cells, whereas CMPs differentiate into granulocyte–macrophage progenitors (GMP) and megakaryocyte–erythrocyte progenitors (MEP). GMPs give rise to granulocytes and macrophages, and MEPs develop into erythrocytes and megakaryocytes11.

Even though haematopoiesis has been studied for many years, the HSPC hierarchy is more complex than the traditional model suggests. A deeper understanding of haematopoiesis, leading to adaptations in the traditional hierarchical model, is the consequence of mammalian single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), first performed in 200912. Single-cell RNA-seq enables researchers to uncover the uniqueness of each cell to investigate cell differentiation and cell fate1,3–5,7,8,13–16. This approach provides a novel view of cellular states, enabling an in-depth understanding of the complex biological systems and processes that individual cells are involved in, which is not possible with bulk RNA-seq13,15,17.

To better understand and interpret the data provided by these advancements, many mathematical models have been developed to predict lineage differentiation based on unique gene expression patterns uncovered during single-cell sampling1–9,18–21. For example, in Lönnberg et al.22, maximum likelihood, a frequentist estimation approach, is used to estimate a single bifurcation point within a cell’s developmental path. In this paper, we explore the use of Bayesian inference to model cell differentiation. Bayesian inference can provide a quantitative measure of multiple bifurcation points (e.g. no bifurcation point, one bifurcation point or multiple bifurcation points) within a cell’s developmental path and validate the evidence for each point using Bayes factor23. Bayesian model selection naturally chooses the simplest model that explains the data. The value of a Bayesian approach in trajectory modelling is its ability to quantify uncertainty and compare competing lineage hypotheses using model evidence, as reflected by recent single-cell frameworks such as VITAE24, which infer trajectories with a variational autoencoder and mixture prior. The framework proposed here uses a methodologically distinct Bayesian formulation based on Palantir-derived manifold construction, window-based Frenet framing, and Gibbs-sampler-based Bayesian model selection. A key property of Bayesian inference is that it provides a full posterior predictive distribution, allowing uncertainty in regression predictions to be quantified explicitly. This property extends naturally to Gaussian-process regression, which models continuous cell differentiation trajectories from noisy measurements while retaining uncertainty estimates. The ability of Gaussian processes to provide quantitative insights into cell differentiation is evident from their recent application in the literature22,25–32.

We have developed BAGEL: Bayesian Analysis of Gene Expression Lineages, a novel statistical model which will allow researchers to gain new insights into the process of cell differentiation based on (i) a robust Bayesian inference approach that models cell differentiation as a continuous process; and (ii) an effective projection method that enables visualisation and investigation of similarities and differences between intra- and inter-species single-cell gene expression datasets. As BAGEL’s projection method integrates datasets from different studies, platforms, and processing pipelines, batch effects remain a central challenge that can confound biological comparisons15,33. Hence, methods such as Harmony have been developed to address this cross-dataset integration problem34. BAGEL’s projection framework is however intended for comparative trajectory interpretation and does not replace dedicated batch-correction workflows. Despite this batching challenge, the potential impact of BAGEL’s projection method in harnessing the collective power of multiple datasets is considerable and can accelerate and enhance our understanding of cell differentiation. Some examples of how BAGEL’s projection method can be utilised include that (i) it can enable cross-study data comparisons; (ii) it can reduce “data waste”, as sparse and/or incomplete datasets can be used to provide robust conclusions; (iii) it can help studies with limited resources as obtaining well-sampled data can be costly, time-consuming, or resource-intensive; and (iv) it can enable longitudinal studies, as subsequent studies can be easily appended to the original study. The potential of BAGEL’s projection method can move the field of developmental biology forward as it can be challenging to provide sufficiently sampled single-cell gene expression data that are competitive in the single-cell field. This might be due to various factors such as (i) the high cost associated with consumables for generating single-cell gene expression data; and (ii) various quality control measures during the sampling process that reduce the number of samples with reliable data.

A flow diagram illustrating how BAGEL achieves its modelling goals is shown in Fig. 1. The cell differentiation modelling process starts (S.1) by defining initial model parameters (P.1.1) and inputting single-cell gene expression data (P.1.2). A key feature of BAGEL is its ability to use two different datasets as input, referred to as the primary and secondary datasets, respectively. This capability enables the combination of datasets by projecting the secondary dataset onto the phenotypic manifold of the primary dataset. This allows users to visualise small numbers of sub-sampled cells (Cells are usually sub-sampled by influencing factors as discussed in Methods.) from a secondary dataset within the differentiation path of an optimally sequenced primary dataset, consisting of many cells. One limitation of this functionality is that the primary dataset should contain an order of magnitude more cell data points than the secondary dataset.

Flow diagram of the process by which BAGEL models cell differentiation to estimate PC-lineages using gene expression data.

The increased number of primary cell data points ensures that the secondary dataset does not distort the visualisation of the primary dataset’s phenotypic manifold. Hence, during BAGEL implementation, the total number of cell data points in the secondary dataset should typically be no more than 10% of the total in the primary dataset. In addition, the datasets should express similar differentiation precursors. Despite these restrictions, BAGEL has produced credible results even though primary and secondary datasets were sequenced separately.

The first decision block (D.1) allows input of either one (P.2.1) or two (P.2.2) datasets into BAGEL, depending on whether cell differentiation modelling is performed using a single dataset, or involves projecting a secondary dataset onto a primary dataset, such as in the case of an inter-species comparison. If a single dataset is used (P.2.1), the visualisation step of the Palantir algorithm1, which is normally based on t-SNE for dimensionality reduction, is changed to PCA. This change in dimensionality reduction technique is necessary because t-SNE, as a stochastic (non-deterministic) transformation (“black box” model), can produce different outputs with each run. In contrast, PCA is a non-stochastic (deterministic) transformation, which ensures consistent results with each simulation36 (see Supplementary material - A.6.1 - Dataset projection). However, when two datasets are used (P.2.2), PCA is used to minimise the influence of the secondary dataset’s projection on the Palantir algorithm’s low dimensional phenotypic manifold development of the primary dataset (see Supplementary material - A.6.1 - Dataset projection, for an in-depth description). After this, the visualisation step is changed from t-SNE to PCA. Both P.2.1 and P.2.2 result in: (i) the dataset represented as coordinates in the form of p; (ii) the Palantir algorithm’s well-defined phenotypic manifold of the cell developmental trajectory; (iii) pseudo temporal ordering of cells; and (iv) terminal states (cell fates).

The output of the Palantir algorithm serves as input to a continuous BAGEL-loop (see Fig. 1), which forms the core of BAGEL. Single-cell gene expression data points in the space are ordered according to the pseudo-time dimension, and fed into the BAGEL-loop. The primary purpose of the BAGEL-loop is to determine if a bifurcation point exists in the input dataset. If the BAGEL-loop fails to detect a bifurcation point, the dataset is defined as a single, distinct PC-lineage. However, if a bifurcation point is identified, it is used to define two distinct PC-lineages in the dataset.

The BAGEL-loop consists of four main components: lineage check, all lineages detected? (D.2), window method (P.3.1), bifurcation estimation (P.3.2), and data association (P.3.3). After receiving the input dataset in coordinate format () at D.2, the BAGEL-loop starts with the window method (P.3.1 - also refer to Supplementary material - Figure A.5). This method defines an interval (window) along the cell developmental trajectory in space . Sequential windows are then used to create a set of tangent vectors through window-based Frenet framing along the trajectory. The data points in these intervals are also used to infer whether the cell trajectory is bifurcating (resulting in two clusters) or continuing as a single trajectory (resulting in one cluster). The first step of the window method (P.3.1) involves defining a pseudo-time interval (), in which a user-defined number of cell data points are selected from the input dataset. Cell data points inside a pseudo-time-interval should follow the cell developmental trajectory sequentially based on pseudo-time. Next, these cell data points in the pseudo-time-interval are used to define a tangent vector PC parallel to the cell developmental trajectory in space (this tangent vector is an output of window-based Frenet framing). Finally, a new interval containing unique cell data points (cell data points that are represented in previous windows can by definition be included in subsequent windows see Supplementary material - A.7.2 - Window method) is defined tangent to the cell developmental trajectory in space by selecting a user defined number of cell data points from the pseudo-time-interval in the direction of the vector tangent to the cell developmental trajectory. The output of the window method (P.3.1) is (i) the tangent vector of the window-based Frenet frame; and (ii) a cluster of cell data points of the cell developmental trajectory partitioned according to the window. These unique cell data points are used to evaluate each sequential window for evidence of bifurcation (P.3.2) via Bayesian model selection.

Bayesian model selection determines whether the cell data points within a window are optimally represented by one (Model 1) or a mixture of two (Model 2) multivariate Gaussian distributions. This is achieved by comparing their model evidence (marginal likelihoods), which are usually difficult to calculate as the complexity of their marginalisation integrals is directly proportional to the number of model parameters. In the case of BAGEL the marginalisation integral can consist of up to 5 model parameters. To overcome the difficulty of computing these integrals, BAGEL utilises a “gold standard” known as the basic marginal likelihood identity (BMI)37 to calculate the complex marginalisation integrals. The BMI rearranges Baye’s theorem to determine the model evidence instead of the posterior distribution that holds for any number of model parameters (θ), which means all that is required to calculate the marginal likelihood of a given model is (i) the log-likelihood; (ii) the log prior; and (iii) an expected value of the posterior density. The logs of the likelihood and the prior are usually easily computed in closed form, whereas the expected value of the posterior density can be calculated by a Monte Carlo simulation that is based on draws from a Gibbs sampler. For each window, BAGEL therefore compares the model evidences either on the linear scale or, equivalently, on the log scale. On the linear scale, a Bayes factor greater than one indicates that the numerator model is favoured. Suppose Bayesian model selection selects Model 1 (Model 2 selection will be discussed in the next paragraph) as the best fit for the cell data points of the current window. In that case, all of the cell data points from the window are associated with one distinct PC-lineage (P.3.4). After associating the cell data points to the PC-lineage, BAGEL-loop is repeated by creating a new window (P.3.1) based on the next pseudo-time-interval from the input dataset of D.2 and the process of model selection (P.3.2) and data association (P.3.3) is repeated. When no bifurcation point is detected in all of the input data (the series of sequential windows of the input dataset is best represented with Model 1) BAGEL will define the input data of D.2 as a distinct detected PC-lineage.

However, if Bayesian model selection selects Model 2 as the best fit for the data points of the current window, BAGEL first applies validation steps to this window-level detection before defining it as an actual bifurcation point. By applying these steps BAGEL ensures the credibility of a detected bifurcation point by decreasing the chance of falsely assigning a bifurcation point to a window. The two validation steps utilised by BAGEL are known as local and global validation (see Supplementary material - A.7.4 - Bifurcation validation). Local validation validates the fit of Model 2 to the current window data points, to ensure that the detected clusters are not sequential to each other with respect to pseudo-time. Thereafter, global validation compares the subsequent sequential windows that have passed local validation by a majority voting rule on three windows, to determine whether the majority of these windows favour Model 1 or Model 2 (see Supplementary material - A.7.4 - Global validation for possible exceptions). A window that favours Model 2 is therefore not defined as a bifurcation point; instead, the sequential windows considered in global validation are used together to determine whether Model 2 is confirmed, where the first of these windows in pseudo-time is treated as the bifurcation window. The bifurcation point is then defined at the maximum pseudo-time value of the Model 1 window immediately preceding that bifurcation window, together with the mean of that window in the PC1-PC2 plane. If global validation confirms Model 2 and a bifurcation point is detected, BAGEL defines the input dataset of D.2 as containing two distinct PC-lineages by: (i) duplicating the distinct PC-lineage before the detected bifurcation point which consists of a series of sequential windows of the input dataset where Model 1 is their best fit; and (ii) associating all of the cell data points after the bifurcation point to the PC-lineage they most likely represent (P.3.4) (see Supplementary material - A.7.5 Data association). After defining the two PC-lineages, each PC-lineage is used as an input to the BAGEL-loop. This step refines the defined PC-lineages to ensure detection of any additional bifurcation points in the remainder of the identified lineages. This process continues until no remaining bifurcation points are detected in any defined PC-lineages (all of the individual PC-lineages are best represented with a series of sequential windows where Model 1 is their best fit) implying that all of the possible PC-lineages have been detected (D.2). Finally, BAGEL stops (S.2) after modelling each detected PC-lineage as a continuous process using a Gaussian process (P.4).

Results

Three different single-cell gene expression datasets (Table 2) were used to illustrate the practical capabilities of BAGEL namely: (i) mouse bone marrow dataset (Fig. 2); (ii) human bone marrow dataset (Fig. 3); and (iii) human umbilical cord blood (UCB) dataset (secondary) projected onto the phenotypic manifold of a human bone marrow dataset (primary) (Fig. 4). For each dataset, BAGEL was used to construct the phenotypic manifold, visualise lineage-specific gene expression, infer bifurcation points, construct PC-lineages and model these lineages as continuous processes using Gaussian processes. In the multi-panel result figures below, the phenotypic manifold and gene expression panels are followed by the PC-lineage and Gaussian process panels, and then by the window-based Frenet-frame and bifurcation panels. The gene expression panels plot transformed relative expression values for visualisation, so negative plotted values can occur on this display scale and do not indicate negative raw counts. The methodology used in obtaining these results is presented in the Methods section. As BAGEL only provides mathematical knowledge about the single-cell gene expression data, prior biological knowledge is used to interpret the detected PC-lineages. The cluster of differentiation 34 (CD34) gene is abundantly expressed in HSPCs, whereas myeloperoxidase (MPO) and GATA-binding factor 1 (GATA1) genes are expressed in cells of myeloid and early megakaryocyte-erythroid origin, respectively. Hence, the expression of these molecular markers assisted in identifying HSPCs, myeloid and megakaryocyte-erythroid PC-lineages. As the projection of the human UCB dataset onto the human bone marrow dataset (intra-species projection) was successful, the question of single-cell gene expression data projection between different species was inevitable. The projection of the human UCB dataset onto the mouse bone marrow dataset is shown in Supplementary material - B. Although this projection showed promise, it is outside the scope of this paper as interspecies modelling requires more investigation and will be included in future work.

The inferred PC-lineages were interpreted using the expression of lineage-specific genes, CD34, MPO and GATA1. These lineage-specific genes assisted in identifying PClineages detected by BAGEL of the mouse bone marrow dataset (Fig. 2b), the human bone marrow dataset (Fig. 3b), and the projection of the human UCB dataset onto the human bone marrow dataset (Fig. 4b). Mouse bone marrow showed an increased number of cells expressing CD34 closer to the starting cell, i.e. start of cell differentiation. Cells with low CD34 expression were also observed close to the starting cell. This is in line with other studies indicating that Lin–c-Kit+Sca-1+ mouse HSPCs consist of both C34+ and CD34- cells38,39. Expression of MPO and GATA1 indicates the presence of myeloid and erythroid precursor cells, respectively (Fig. 2b). This was confirmed through the continuous modelling of PC-lineages (Fig. 2c-g). Overall, BAGEL detected one bifurcation point and two PC-lineages in the mouse bone marrow dataset.

Early HSPCs closer to the starting cell (blue hexagon) showed early differentiation into the megakaryocyte-erythroid PC-lineage through the expression of GATA1 (Fig. 3b). Multipotent progenitors (MPP1–4) with lineage bias have recently been identified, including a megakaryocyte-biased MPP2 population that differentiates directly into the megakaryocyte-erythroid lineage, bypassing CMP/MEP progenitors40. Increasing evidence suggests that self-renewing HSPCs in the bone marrow have bias in differentiating into the megakaryocyte-erythroid lineage based on the expression of GATA140–43,43,44. This phenomenon has only recently become known and cannot be explained by the traditional model of haematopoiesis which has resulted in a revised model. Overall, BAGEL detected two bifurcation points and three PC-lineages in the human bone marrow dataset.

The projected UCB CD34+ HSPCs (black dots) on the contrary showed minimal direct differentiation from early HSPCs towards the megakaryocyte-erythroid PC-lineage. An increased number of UCB-derived CD34+ HSPCs was observed in the myeloid PC-lineage capable of later differentiating into the megakaryocyte-erythroid PC-lineage (Fig. 4a and b). This is clear in Fig. 4a, where human UCB CD34+ HSPCs are spread out in a triangle-like arrangement with cells closer to the starting cell expressing higher levels of CD34, whereas CD34+ progenitor cells located between start and terminal states are likely already committed to either myeloid or erythroid PC-lineages. This suggests that UCB CD34+ HSPCs consist of heterogeneous MPPs and/or CMPs with the potential to differentiate into a megakaryocyte-erythroid PC-lineage. The lineages detected based on expression of the above-mentioned genes are accurate and in line with results from similar studies. Overall, BAGEL detected one bifurcation point and two PC-lineages in the human UCB dataset projected on the human bone marrow phenotypic manifold.

Discussion

We have developed a novel statistical model, which will allow researchers to gain new insight into the process of cell differentiation based on (i) a sound Bayesian inference approach to model cell differentiation as a continuous process; and (ii) an effective projection method which opens the door to visualise and investigate the similarities and differences between intra- and inter-species single-cell gene expression datasets. Two primary datasets were used to test the operational capabilities of BAGEL namely (i) mouse bone marrow gene expression data (Fig. 2) and (ii) human bone marrow gene expression data (Fig. 3). The high cost associated with single-cell RNA-seq and the quality control measures in place to ensure that the data generated is reliable often result in a limited number of cells for analysis. BAGEL provides a novel manner in which to project a secondary single-cell gene expression dataset (human UCB) onto the phenotypic manifold of a primary single-cell gene expression dataset (human bone marrow) (Fig. 4). BAGEL produced consistent results across the three analysed single-cell gene expression datasets with the same modelling parameters (see Methods section), suggesting that BAGEL requires minimal model tuning across these datasets. Direct comparative benchmarking against broader established trajectory-inference methods (including VITAE, Slingshot, Monocle, and standalone Palantir) was not included in the present study. BAGEL conditions on a Palantir-derived phenotypic manifold and pseudo-time assignment, whereas these methods rely on different upstream modelling assumptions and produce different trajectory representations and downstream outputs. In BAGEL, these downstream outputs include window-based bifurcation calls and Gaussian-process PC-lineages, so a fair direct comparison would require harmonised preprocessing, root-state selection, manifold/latent representation, and aligned definitions of lineages and bifurcation calls. Benchmarking on simulated ground-truth trajectories or standard benchmark atlases therefore remains future work. Likewise, a dedicated runtime and memory scaling analysis was not included, and practical implementation details for torch-based acceleration and AnnData/Scanpy-native interoperability are not yet fully described; these are also future work. Although the main focus of this manuscript is haematopoiesis, BAGEL should be suitable for other datasets.

BAGEL is dependent on the output of the Palantir algorithm. The Palantir algorithm produces (i) a well-defined phenotypic manifold of the cell developmental trajectory; (ii) pseudo temporal ordering of the cells; and (iii) terminal states of a given dataset. These outputs are used to construct a combined pseudo-time-principal-component space , which is used to obtain well-defined bifurcation points of the cell development trajectory. Because pseudo-time and PCA coordinates are both derived from the same upstream Palantir manifold context, coordinates in are coupled. Pseudo-time in should therefore be interpreted as an ordering and stabilising coordinate for trajectory-aligned window construction and local tangent estimation, rather than as an axis orthogonal to the PCA coordinates. Tangent-vector orientation and bifurcation interpretation in should thus be read within this trajectory-aligned working representation rather than as independent Euclidean geometry. As seen in Figs. 2g, 3h and 4g, the bifurcation points defined by a Gibbs sampler and Bayesian model selection in space are at the exact instances where PC-lineages branch from each other. It should be noted that the capability of BAGEL to identify bifurcation points is dependent on well-defined terminal states within the single-cell gene expression data. As a consequence of these well-defined bifurcation points in space , BAGEL was able to construct PC-lineages (Figs. 2e, 3f and 4e). The significance of these constructed PC-lineages is that all of them can be modelled with individual Gaussian processes to obtain an overall continuous model of cell differentiation in space (Figs. 2c, d, 3c, e and 4c, d). An important output of the Gaussian process model is its ability to characterise the uncertainty of the inferred PC-lineages. It should be noted that the continuous modelling of the cell developmental trajectory performs better when many cell data points are included, as this decreases the uncertainty of the Gaussian process model.

Discussion continue

The tangent vector of the window-based Frenet frame of the cell developmental trajectory used for bifurcation point estimation is obtained: (i) by dividing the data into windows, which are intervals aligned with the cell developmental trajectory; and (ii) by determining if a window is best defined with one or a mixture of two multivariate Gaussian distributions. During the window defining step, BAGEL defines a tangent vector parallel to the cell developmental trajectory of each window (see Supplementary material - A.7.2 - Window method). When these tangent vectors are concatenated, a tangent vector window-based Frenet frame of the entire cell developmental trajectory in space is obtained (Fig. 2f, 3g and 4f). These tangent vectors of the window-based Frenet frames are important as they provide an overview of the most likely future fate of cell differentiation in space along its developmental path.

The projection of a secondary single-cell gene expression dataset onto the phenotypic manifold of a primary single-cell gene expression dataset is accomplished by manipulating the first step of the Palantir algorithm’s dimensionality reduction (PCA). The innovative manipulation of the PCA allows one to project the secondary dataset onto the manifold of the primary dataset, one cell at a time. This technique is effective because the influence of the secondary dataset is directly proportional to the ratio of the number of cell data points in the secondary dataset to the number of cell data points in the primary dataset. A visual validation of the projection method, and the limiting visual distortion it has on the developed phenotypic manifold of a primary dataset, can be seen when comparing the original phenotypic manifold in Fig. 3a to the projected phenotypic manifold in Fig. 4a. The terminal states observed on the original phenotypic manifold of the primary dataset are not the same as in the projected phenotypic manifold – one terminal cell data point is missing in the projected phenotypic manifold (Fig. 4a). The reason for the missing terminal states is because the Palantir algorithm approaches terminal state detection statistically. The statistical approach leads to the developed Markov chain of the projected data not converging to all of the same extrema of the diffusion components as the non-projected data. Hence, the projected data does not converge to all the same extremes as the non-projected data and therefore does not have all the same statistical terminal cell data points.

Conclusion

BAGEL is capable of (i) transforming cell differentiation pseudo-time to a representation of sequential windows that are translated and rotated within a combined pseudo-time-principal-component space using the tangent vectors of window-based Frenet frames; (ii) inferring bifurcation points via a Gibbs sampler and Bayesian model selection; (iii) constructing cell lineage representations of single-cell gene expression data in the combined pseudo-time-principal-component space, that describe the cell developmental trajectory from the start of cell differentiation to a terminal state within a given dataset; (iv) modelling cell differentiation as a continuous process using a Gaussian process; and (v) projecting a related dataset onto the cell developmental trajectory of a primary dataset’s phenotypic manifold. This projection process allows intraspecies and interspecies single-cell gene expression data to be compared.

Methods

Datasets

Single-cell gene expression datasets utilised by BAGEL

Three different single-cell gene expression datasets were used to illustrate the functional capabilities of BAGEL. The first dataset is Lin–c-Kit+Sca-1+ mouse bone marrow (publicly available)45 consisting of 4423 cells expressing 2312 genes in total. The genes expressed in this dataset correspond to genes within the erythroid and myeloid lineages6. The second dataset (publicly available)1 is CD34+ human bone marrow single-cell RNA-seq data consisting of 4142 cells expressing 16106 genes in total. This human dataset will hence forth be referred to as human dataset 1. The third dataset is a self-generated single-cell RNA-seq dataset derived from human UCB.

UCB was obtained from mothers undergoing caesarians at a private hospital in Pretoria, South Africa. CD34+ HSPCs were purified by fluorescent activated cell sorting (FACS) using a FACSAria cell sorter (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA) and were directly sorted into C1 Single-Cell Auto Prep Array Integrated Fluidics Circuit (IFC) plates (Fluidigm, San Francisco, California, USA). Capture, lysis, reverse transcription, and amplification of single cells were performed using the Fluidigm C1 Single-Cell Auto Prep System (Fluidigm, San Francisco, California, USA) according to the user manual. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by the Agricultural Research Council (ARC) at Onderstepoort Veterinary Research Campus, Onderstepoort, Gauteng, South Africa, using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) and Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing system (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The average sequencing depth was reads per cell.

Trimmomatic was used to remove sequencing adapters and poor-quality reads46. The bcbio-nextgen workflow47 was used to assess the quality of the data using FASTQC; align reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the alignment tool HISAT248; and perform gene quantification using featureCounts49.

Sub-sampled UCB dataset

The Fluidigm C1 system was used to capture single CD34+ HSPCs from UCB of six individual donors with a mean capture efficiency of 46% (±12.1%). The Fluidigm C1 system allows capture of a maximum of 96 cells at a time and with a capture efficiency of 46% on average for the HSPCs, it has been challenging to increase the cell numbers in order to be competitive in the single-cell field. The high cost associated with the consumables for this system is another limitation, especially if experiments need to be repeated in order to reach the cell numbers required for single-cell publications. The various quality control measures along the process further resulted in a decreased total number of cells analysed.

Gibbs samples and burn-in period

In practice there is no recommended total number of Gibbs samples or burn-in period when using a Gibbs sampler, as the optimal number of samples is problem-specific23. In BAGEL, a Gibbs sampler is used to calculate model evidence whose accuracy is directly proportional to the total number of samples as seen in an article published by Chib37. This proportional relationship will intuitively lead to selecting a large number of samples for the Gibbs sampler. However, when a Gibbs sampler was utilised during model selection (see Supplementary material - A.7.3 - Detecting bifurcation points with Bayesian model selection) it was noted that an increase in the total number of Gibbs samples had neglectable small changes in the outcome of model selection. It was found that when the data points within a window is best represented with Model 1 or Model 2 respectively, an increased number of Gibbs samples will not change the overall outcome. Therefore, the Gibbs samples was set to 2000 samples with a burn-in period of 500 samples to allow the Markov chain to converge.
