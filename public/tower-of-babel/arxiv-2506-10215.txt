Isoholonomic inequalities and speed limits for cyclic quantum systems
Ole Sönnerborn

Abstract
Quantum speed limits set fundamental lower bounds on the time required for a quantum system to evolve between states. Traditional bounds—such as those by Mandelstam-Tamm and Margolus-Levitin—rely on state distinguishability and become trivial for cyclic evolutions where the initial and final states coincide. In this work, we explore an alternative approach based on isoholonomic inequalities, which bound the length of closed state space trajectories in terms of their holonomy. Building on a gauge-theoretic framework for mixed state geometric phases, we extend the concept of isoholonomic inequalities to closed curves of isospectral and isodegenerate density operators. This allows us to derive new quantum speed limits that remain nontrivial for cyclic evolutions. Our results reveal deep connections between the temporal behavior of cyclic quantum systems and holonomy.



I  Introduction

A quantum speed limit is a fundamental lower bound on the minimum time required for a quantum system to evolve from one state to another.

Most quantum speed limits, including the classical ones attributed to Mandelstam and Tamm
MaTa1991
;
AnAh1990
;
AnHe2014

Isoholonomic inequalities offer a powerful tool for estimating the return time of cyclic processes. These inequalities provide lower bounds on the length of closed curves—such as the trajectory of the state of a periodic quantum system—in terms of the curve’s holonomy.

For example, the Fubini-Study length of a closed curve of pure states is bounded below by a quantity known as the isoholonomic bound
HoSo2023c
. This bound is determined solely by the phase of the Aharonov-Anandan holonomy associated with the curve. From this isoholonomic inequality, we can conclude that the time required for a unitary evolving system to return to its initial state is at least the ratio of the isoholonomic bound to the system’s time-averaged energy uncertainty, as demonstrated in Ref.
HoSo2023c
.

Isoholonomic inequalities have also been applied in the context of holonomic quantum computation

;
So2025b

II  Isoholonomic inequality and speed limit of cyclic systems in pure states

Consider a quantum system evolving through a piecewise smooth family of pure states
\rho_{t}
that returns to the initial state at a time
\tau>0
. The isoholonomic inequality
HoSo2023c
(see also the Supplemental Material in Ref.
So2024a
) provides a lower bound—the isoholonomic bound—on the Fubini-Study length of this closed trajectory of the state:

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{t}]\geq\operatorname{IB}[\rho_{t}].

(1)

The Fubini-Study length quantifies the total geometric distance traversed by the state and is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{t}]=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\rho}_{t}^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2)

The isoholonomic bound depends only on the representative
\theta
of the Aharonov-Anandan geometric phase of
\rho_{t}
in the interval
[0,2\pi)
and is given by

\operatorname{IB}[\rho_{t}]=\sqrt{\theta(2\pi-\theta)}.

(3)

Recall that the geometric phase is the argument of the Aharonov-Anandan holonomy of
\rho_{t}
, defined as

\Gamma[\rho_{t}]=\langle\psi_{0}|\psi_{\tau}\rangle\exp\Big(\!-\!\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\psi_{t}|\dot{\psi}_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\Big),

(4)

where
|\psi_{t}\rangle
is any smooth curve of unit vectors satisfying
\rho_{t}=|\psi_{t}\rangle\langle\psi_{t}|
; see Ref.
AhAn1987
. The holonomy is a phase factor accumulated during the evolution with a purely geometric origin, and
\theta
appearing in the isoholonomic bound is the argument of the holonomy in the interval
[0,2\pi)
.

If the system evolves unitarily, so that
\rho_{t}=U_{t}\rho U_{t}^{\dagger}
, with
U_{t}
being the propagator generated by a Hamiltonian
H_{t}
, the Fubini-Study speed coincides with the instantaneous energy uncertainty
AnAh1990
:

\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\rho}_{t}^{2})}=\Delta H_{t},\quad\Delta^{2}H_{t}=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{t}H_{t}^{2})-\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{t}H_{t})^{2}.

(5)

Letting
\Delta E
denote the average energy uncertainty over the evolution time interval, the Fubini-Study length of the path
\rho_{t}
can be expressed as
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{t}]=\tau\Delta E
. Inserting this relation into the isoholonomic inequality (
1
), we arrive at a quantum speed limit of Mandelstam-Tamm type that applies to cyclic evolutions
HoSo2023c
:

\tau\geq\frac{\sqrt{\theta(2\pi-\theta)}}{\Delta E}.

(6)

Unlike the Mandelstam-Tamm bound
MaTa1991
;
AnAh1990
;
AnHe2014
;
HoAlSo2022
, which relates the time it takes for a system to evolve between two states to the Fubini-Study distance between them, this bound provides a nontrivial lower limit on the time
\tau
at which the system returns to its initial state.

III  Geometric phase for systems evolving through isodegenerate states

Consider a curve of density operators
\rho_{t}
that share a spectrum of nondegenerate positive eigenvalues
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{l}
.
2
2

2

The density operators have the same rank. If the rank is not full, zero is also a common eigenvalue and may be degenerate. For technical reasons, we exclude such zero eigenvalues from the spectrum.

Each density operator then admits a unique spectral decomposition of the form

\rho_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}|\psi_{j;t}\rangle\langle\psi_{j;t}|,

(7)

where
|\psi_{j;t}\rangle\langle\psi_{j;t}|
is the orthogonal projection onto the one-dimensional eigenspace of
\rho_{t}
associated with eigenvalue
p_{j}
. While these projectors are uniquely determined by
\rho_{t}
, each eigenvector
|\psi_{j;t}\rangle
is defined only up to a phase factor.

Given an initial choice of eigenvectors
|\psi_{j;0}\rangle
, there exist unique smooth curves of instantaneous eigenvectors satisfying the Aharonov-Anandan horizontality condition

\langle\psi_{j;t}|\dot{\psi}_{j;t}\rangle=0.

(8)

Following Sjöqvist
et al.

SjPaEkAnErOiVe2000
, we refer to a spectral decomposition of
\rho_{t}
in which each eigenvector evolves horizontally as a parallel-transported decomposition, and we define the geometric phase of
\rho_{t}
as

\theta_{\textrm{geo}}=\arg\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}\langle\psi_{j;0}|\psi_{j;\tau}\rangle.

(9)

When the common eigenvalues of the
\rho_{t}
s are degenerate, one must generalize the horizontality condition (
8
) to account for the higher-dimensional eigenspaces
ToSjKwOh2004
: Suppose that
p_{j}
has degeneracy
m_{j}
. Then
\rho_{t}
admits a spectral decomposition of the form

\rho_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle\langle\psi_{ja;t}|,

(10)

where for each
j
and each
t
, the
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
s are normalized and pairwise orthogonal eigenvectors of
\rho_{t}
spanning the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
p_{j}
.

The natural generalization of the parallel transport condition in this setting requires that for
j=1,2,\dots,l
, the velocity vectors
{|\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}\rangle}
lie orthogonal to the corresponding eigenspace. That is, the spectral decomposition is parallel transported if

\langle\psi_{ja;t}|\dot{\psi}_{jb;t}\rangle=0,\quad a,b=1,2,\dots,m_{j}.

(11)

For a curve of density operators with a parallel-transported spectral decomposition, we define the geometric phase as

\theta_{\textrm{geo}}=\arg\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}\langle\psi_{ja;0}|\psi_{ja;\tau}\rangle.

(12)

The parallel transport condition (
11
) remains applicable even when the eigenvalues of the density operator change over time, provided that their multiplicities remain constant throughout the evolution
ToSjKwOh2004
. Let
p_{1;t},p_{2;t},\dots,p_{l;t}

be the time-dependent eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
, and let
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{l}

be the corresponding fixed multiplicities. In this setting,
\rho_{t}
admits a spectral decomposition

\rho_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j;t}|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle\langle\psi_{ja;t}|

(13)

with orthonormal eigenvectors
{|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle}
that evolve according to the parallel transport condition (
11
). The geometric phase associated with such a parallel-transported decomposition is defined as

\theta_{\textrm{geo}}=\arg\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\sqrt{p_{j;0}p_{j;\tau}}\langle\psi_{ja;0}|\psi_{ja;\tau}\rangle.

(14)

This expression generalizes the earlier definitions, reducing to Eq. (
12
) when the spectrum is time independent and further simplifying to Eq. (
9
) in the case of a nondegenerate spectrum.

IV  Gauge theories for isospectral and isodegenerate states

A density operator
is fully characterized by its set of distinct positive eigenvalues
\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{l})
,
which we assume are arranged in descending order, and the corresponding family of orthogonal projection operators
\bm{\Pi}=(\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\dots,\Pi_{l})
,
where each
\Pi_{j}
projects onto the eigenspace associated with the eigenvalue
p_{j}
. We refer to
\bm{p}
as the eigenvalue spectrum and to
\bm{\Pi}
as the eigenprojector spectrum of the density operator. If the density operator is not of full rank,
0
is also an eigenvalue. However, for technical reasons, we exclude
0
from
\bm{p}
and omit the corresponding projection operator from
\bm{\Pi}
.

When an eigenprojector spectrum is specified without an accompanying eigenvalue spectrum, it is implicitly understood that any associated density operator possesses a set of positive eigenvalues ordered in descending magnitude, and that the
j
th projector corresponds to the eigenspace of the
j
th largest eigenvalue.

The rank of each projection operator
\Pi_{j}
equals the multiplicity
m_{j}
of the corresponding eigenvalue
p_{j}
. We refer to the sequence
\bm{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{l})

as the degeneracy spectrum of the density operator. As before, no entry of
\bm{m}
accounts for the multiplicity of a possible zero eigenvalue. Consequently, if a density operator has degeneracy spectrum
\bm{m}
, its rank is
m_{1}+m_{2}+\dots+m_{l}
.

By prescribing specific spectral data, we can define various spaces of density operators. Let
\bm{p}
be an eigenvalue spectrum with associated degeneracy spectrum
\bm{m}
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
the space of all density operators on
\mathcal{H}
that have eigenvalues
\bm{p}
with corresponding multiplicities given by
\bm{m}
. The space
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
is the unitary orbit of each of its elements: for any
\rho
in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
,

\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})=\{U\rho U^{\dagger}:U\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})\}.

(15)

We call the density operators in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
isospectral, since they share the same eigenvalue spectrum and degeneracy structure.

The space
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})
consists of all density operators on
\mathcal{H}
that share a common eigenprojector spectrum
\bm{\Pi}
. While the eigenvalues of these operators may vary, they all exhibit the same multiplicity structure given by
\bm{m}
. Moreover, they all commute. We assume that the degeneracy spectrum
\bm{m}
is compatible with
\bm{\Pi}
, meaning that the rank of the
j
th projector in
\bm{\Pi}
equals the
j
th multiplicity
m_{j}
in
\bm{m}
. If
l
is the common length of the sequences
\bm{\Pi}
and
\bm{m}
, then
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})
forms a smooth manifold canonically diffeomorphic to the open simplex

\big\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{l}:x_{j}>x_{j+1}>0,\;\sum_{j=1}^{l}x_{j}m_{j}=1\big\}.

(16)

Given a degeneracy spectrum
\bm{m}
, we let
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
denote the space of all density operators whose (ordered) nonzero eigenvalues have multiplicities given by
\bm{m}
. Two density operators in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
need neither be isospectral nor have the same eigenprojector spectrum. However, they have the same rank. The space
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
is a manifold, with
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
and
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})
forming submanifolds of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
, provided the three spectra have the same length. If
l
is the common length, and
r
is the sum of all multiplicities, then the dimensions of these manifolds are given by

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})

\displaystyle=2nr-r^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}m_{j}^{2},

(17a)

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})

\displaystyle=l-1,

(17b)

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})

\displaystyle=2nr-r^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}m_{j}^{2}+l-1.

(17c)

IV.B  Holonomy

Let
\rho_{t}
be a curve in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
starting at
\rho
. A standard result from the theory of principal fiber bundles (see, e.g,
KoNo1996
) ensures that for any amplitude
W
in the fiber over
\rho
, there exists a unique curve of amplitudes
W_{t}
that starts at
W
, projects onto
\rho_{t}
, and has a horizontal velocity field:

\mathcal{A}(\dot{W}_{t})=0.

(26)

We refer to
W_{t}
as the horizontal lift of
\rho_{t}
starting at
W
.

Since the horizontal lift of
\rho_{t}
is unique once the initial amplitude is specified, we can define a map from the fiber over the initial density operator
\rho
to the fiber over the final density operator
\rho_{\tau}
,

\Gamma[\rho_{t}]:\eta^{-1}(\rho)\to\eta^{-1}(\rho_{\tau}),\quad W\mapsto\Gamma[\rho_{t}](W),

(27)

by defining
\Gamma[\rho_{t}](W)
to be the final amplitude of the unique horizontal lift of
\rho_{t}
that starts at
W
.
This map is the parallel transport operator associated with
\rho_{t}
.

If the curve
\rho_{t}
is closed, so that
\rho_{\tau}=\rho
, the parallel transport operator maps the fiber over
\rho
back to itself. In this case, the parallel transport operator is referred to as the holonomy of
\rho_{t}
.

The collection of all holonomies that arise from closed curves based at
\rho
forms a group under composition, called the holonomy group at
\rho
. The group operation is defined via concatenation of curves: if
\rho_{1;t}
and
\rho_{2;t}
are closed curves at
\rho
, defined on intervals
[0,\tau_{1}]
and
[0,\tau_{2}]
, respectively, their concatenation is

\rho_{1;t}\ast\rho_{2;t}=\begin{cases}\rho_{1;t}&\text{ for }\quad 0\leq t\leq\tau_{1},\\
\rho_{2;t-\tau_{1}}&\text{ for }\quad\tau_{1}\leq t\leq\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}.\end{cases}

(28)

which is again a closed curve at
\rho
. The corresponding parallel transport operators satisfy

\Gamma[\rho_{2;t}]\Gamma[\rho_{1;t}]=\Gamma[\rho_{1;t}\ast\rho_{2;t}].

(29)

The identity element of the group is the holonomy of a constant curve at
\rho
, and the holonomy of the time-reversal of
\rho_{t}
gives the inverse of the holonomy associated with
\rho_{t}
.

The holonomy group at
\rho
can be identified (though not canonically) with a subgroup of the gauge group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
. For each amplitude
W
located in the fiber over
\rho
, we define the holonomy of
\rho_{t}
at
W
to be the unique unitary operator
\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]
in
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
satisfying

\Gamma[\rho_{t}](W)=W\Gamma[\rho_{t};W].

(30)

The assignment
\Gamma[\rho_{t}]\mapsto\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]
is a group monomorphism that identifies the holonomy group at
\rho
with a subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
. By multiplying both sides of Eq. (
30
) on the left by the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
W
, we obtain

\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]=W^{+}\Gamma[\rho_{t}](W).

(31)

IV.B.1  Holonomy invariants

Two closed curves based at the same density operator are called holonomy equivalent if their holonomies coincide. Any property shared by all holonomy equivalent curves is called a holonomy invariant.

The amplitude-based holonomies transform covariantly with respect to changes in the amplitude:

\Gamma[\rho_{t};WU]=U\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]U^{\dagger},\quad U\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda}).

(32)

Consequently, any unitarily invariant property of
\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]
is a holonomy invariant of the curve
\rho_{t}
.

Let
f
be any function that depends only on the eigenvalues of unitary operators in
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
, such as the trace or determinant. Since such functions are unitarily invariant, the map
\rho_{t}\mapsto f(\Gamma[\rho_{t};W])
yields a holonomy invariant.
4
4

4

When
f
is the trace, the resulting holonomy invariant is often referred to as the Wilson loop.
An important example of a holonomy invariant is the geometric phase; which is discussed in the next section. In Sec.
V
, we will encounter another holonomy invariant which we call the isoholonomic bound.

Remark 3
.

The holonomy of a closed curve is invariant under orientation-preserving reparameterizations. Therefore, when calculating the holonomy or a holonomy invariant of a curve, one may freely apply orientation-preserving reparameterizations to the curve.

\Lambda_{j}
, a
\bm{\Lambda}
-adapted basis.
In Appendix
C
, we show that any
\bm{\Lambda}
-adapted basis establishes a one-to-one correspondence between parallel-transported spectral decompositions and horizontal lifts of
\rho_{t}
. This correspondence is given by the relation

W_{t}|ja\rangle=\sqrt{p_{j;t}}|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle,

(36)

where the
p_{j;t}
s are the positive eigenvalues of
\rho_{t}
.
Thus, given a parallel-transported spectral decomposition as in Eq. (
13
), one can define a horizontal lift
W_{t}
by specifying that Eq. (
36
) describes the action of
W_{t}
on the
\bm{\Lambda}
-adapted basis vectors:

W_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\sqrt{p_{j;t}}|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle\langle ja|.

(37)

Conversely, if
W_{t}
is a horizontal lift of
\rho_{t}
, the vectors

|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle=p_{j;t}^{-1/2}W_{t}|ja\rangle

(38)

form a parallel-transported spectral decomposition of
\rho_{t}
, as described in Eq. (
13
).

For a horizontal lift
W_{t}
of
\rho_{t}
, with a parallel-transported spectral decomposition established through relation (
36
), the geometric phase of
\rho_{t}
is given by

\begin{split}&\theta_{\textrm{geo}}[\rho_{t}]=\arg\operatorname{tr}(W_{0}^{\dagger}W_{\tau})\\
&=\arg\operatorname{tr}\sum_{j,k=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\sum_{b=1}^{m_{k}}\sqrt{p_{j;0}p_{k;\tau}}|ja\rangle\langle\psi_{ja;0}|\psi_{kb;\tau}\rangle\langle kb|\\
&=\arg\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\sqrt{p_{j;0}p_{j;\tau}}\langle\psi_{ja;0}|\psi_{ja;\tau}\rangle.\end{split}

(39)

This expression coincides with the expression for the geometric phase in Eq. (
14
).

IV.C  Gauge theory for isospectral and spectrally bounded states

For any amplitude
W
in
\mathcal{W}(\bm{\Lambda})
, the density operators
W^{\dagger}W
and
WW^{\dagger}
share the same set of positive eigenvalues. Consequently, if
\bm{p}
is an eigenvalue spectrum compatible with
\bm{m}
, the amplitudes
W
that project into the space of isospectral states
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
must satisfy the condition

W^{\dagger}W=\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}\Lambda_{j}.

(40)

We denote the space of such amplitudes by
\mathcal{W}(\bm{p},\bm{\Lambda})
. It is shown in Ref.
AnHe2015
(see also
An2018
) that
\eta
restricts to a principal bundle
\eta_{\bm{p}}
over
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
with total space
\mathcal{W}(\bm{p},\bm{\Lambda})
, gauge group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
, and connection
1
-form
\mathcal{A}
. In this case, one can express the connection form as

\mathcal{A}(\dot{W})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}W^{\dagger}\dot{W}\Lambda_{j},\quad\dot{W}\in\mathrm{T}_{W}\mathcal{W}(\bm{\Lambda}).

(41)

See Appendix
A
for details.

More generally, let
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{l})
be a decreasing sequence of positive real numbers. Consider the subset
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
consisting of those density operators whose eigenvalue spectra satisfy the bounds

p_{j}\geq\alpha_{j},\quad j=1,2,\dots,l.

(42)

The amplitudes
W
that project into
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
are characterized by the condition that
W^{\dagger}W
is a density operator on
\mathcal{K}
whose eigenvalue spectrum satisfies (
42
). We denote the space of such amplitudes by
\mathcal{W}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\Lambda})
. As before,
\eta
restricts to a principal bundle
\eta_{\bm{\alpha}}
over
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
with total space
\mathcal{W}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\Lambda})
, gauge group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{K};\bm{\Lambda})
, and connection
1
-form
\mathcal{A}
given by Eq. (

).

Remark 4
.

The bundle
\eta
also restricts to a principal bundle over
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})
. Since the eigenvectors remain constant along any curve in this space, every horizontal lift of a closed curve is itself closed. Consequently, all closed curves in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Pi},\bm{m})
have trivial holonomy.

V  Isoholonomic inequalities

The metric
G
induces a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
, which makes
\eta
a Riemannian submersion. The metric
g
is defined as follows: Suppose
\dot{\rho}_{1}
and
\dot{\rho}_{2}
are tangent vectors to
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
at
\rho
. Choose any amplitude
W
of
\rho
, and let
\dot{W}_{1}
and
\dot{W}_{2}
be the horizontal lifts of
\dot{\rho}_{1}
and
\dot{\rho}_{2}
to
W
. Then,

g(\dot{\rho}_{1},\dot{\rho}_{2})=G(\dot{W}_{1},\dot{W}_{2}).

(43)

The horizontal lifts
\dot{W}_{1}
and
\dot{W}_{2}
exist and are unique because
d\eta
restricts to an isomorphism from the horizontal space at
W
to the tangent space at
\rho
. Moreover, the value of the inner product on the right-hand side is independent of the choice of amplitude
W
, as the gauge group acts through isometries.

Remark 5
.

In the case of pure states (cf. Remark
2
), the induced metric coincides with the Fubini-Study metric.

In Appendix
B
, we prove that the metric
g
does not depend on the choice of eigenprojector spectrum
\bm{\Lambda}
.

V.A  Length and kinetic energy

An important property of Riemannian submersions is that they preserve the lengths and kinetic energies of horizontal curves. As a result, for any curve
\rho_{t}
in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
and any horizontal lift
W_{t}
of
\rho_{t}
, the lengths and kinetic energies of
\rho_{t}
and
W_{t}
are equal:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}[W_{t}],

(44a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}[W_{t}].

(44b)

These functionals are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{g(\dot{\rho}_{t},\dot{\rho}_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(45a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}g(\dot{\rho}_{t},\dot{\rho}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

(45b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[W_{t}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{G(\dot{W}_{t},\dot{W}_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(45c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[W_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}G(\dot{W}_{t},\dot{W}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

(45d)

From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]^{2}\leq 2\tau\mathcal{E}[\rho_{t}].

(46)

Equality holds if and only if
\rho_{t}
is parameterized proportionally to arc length.

Remark 6
.

The length of a curve is invariant under reparameterization. Therefore, when calculating or estimating the length, we can assume the curve is parameterized in proportion to arc length, meaning it has constant speed.

V.A.1  Fisher-Rao length and kinetic energy

Let
\bm{p}_{t}=(p_{1;t},p_{2;t},\dots,p_{l;t})
be the time-dependent eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
. The time-varying vector

P_{t}=(m_{1}p_{1;t},m_{2}p_{2;t},\dots,m_{l}p_{l;t})

(47)

defines a curve within the open simplex of positive probability distributions of length
l
. The Fisher-Rao metric
Fi1925
;
Ra1992
induces a natural Riemannian structure on this simplex, and the instantaneous squared Fisher-Rao speed of
P_{t}
is

g_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}(\dot{P}_{t},\dot{P}_{t})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{l}m_{j}\frac{\dot{p}_{j;t}^{2}}{p_{j;t}}.

(48)

We write
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]
and
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]
for its Fisher-Rao length and kinetic energy, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{l}m_{j}\frac{\dot{p}_{j;t}^{2}}{p_{j;t}}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(49a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{l}m_{j}\frac{\dot{p}_{j;t}^{2}}{p_{j;t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(49b)

These quantities capture the statistical length and kinetic energy of the evolution of the eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
.

Remark 7
.

When a curve of isodegenerate states is reparameterized, its curve of eigenvalues is also reparameterized. However, this does not affect the Fisher-Rao length of the corresponding curve of probability distributions.

V.B  The isoholonomic inequality for isospectral dynamics

Consider the fiber bundle
\eta_{\bm{p}}

over the space of density operators
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
that share an eigenvalue spectrum
\bm{p}
.

Since the unitary operators in the gauge group commute with the operators in
\bm{\Lambda}
,
any such unitary operator
U
can be decomposed as

U=U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{l},

(50)

where
U_{j}
is a unitary operator on the support of
\Lambda_{j}
. We define a unitarily invariant function on the gauge group as follows: For a unitary
U
with a decomposition as in Eq. (
50
), let
\theta_{j1},\theta_{j2},\dots,\theta_{jm_{j}}
be the phases of the eigenvalues of
U_{j}
within the interval
[0,2\pi)
. Then, define

\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}(U)=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}\theta_{ja}(2\pi-\theta_{ja})}.

(51)

The function
\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}
is unitarily invariant because
\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}(U)
depends only on the spectrum of
U
.

Fix a density operator
\rho
in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
, and let
W
be any amplitude of
\rho
. Since the holonomies at
W
of closed curves in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
at
\rho
are elements in the gauge group, and since
\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}
is a unitarily invariant function on the gauge group,

\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}[\rho_{t}]=\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}(\Gamma[\rho_{t};W])

(52)

is a holonomy invariant. We call this invariant the isoholonomic bound of
\rho_{t}
. The isoholonomic inequality says that
the length of
\rho_{t}
is lower bounded by its isoholonomic bound:

\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]\geq\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}[\rho_{t}]

(53)

Consequently, the lengths of all closed curves in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
at
\rho
with a given holonomy are bounded from below by the isoholonomic bound associated with that holonomy.

In Sec.
VII
, we will show that if the dimension of the Hilbert space is at least twice the common rank of the density operators in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{m})
, the isoholonomic inequality (
53
) is tight. This means that for every element of the holonomy group at
\rho
, there exists a closed curve at
\rho
whose length matches the isoholonomic bound associated with that holonomy.

Remark 8
.

The concept of an isoholonomic inequality was introduced by Montgomery as a partial response to the isoholonomic problem, which he formulated in Ref.
Mo1990
:

“Among all loops with a fixed holonomy,

find the one with shortest length.”

V.C  The isoholonomic inequality for spectrally constrained isodegenerate dynamics

If we allow for the eigenvalue spectrum to vary with time, we need to adjust the definition of the isoholonomic bound accordingly. In this case, we restrict our focus to density operators whose eigenvalue spectrum is constrained by a sequence
\bm{\alpha}
, as described in Eq. (
42
).
For a unitary
U
with a decomposition as described in Eq. (
50
), we define

\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}(U)=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j}\theta_{ja}(2\pi-\theta_{ja})},

(54)

where, as before,
\theta_{j1},\theta_{j2},\dots,\theta_{jm_{j}}
are the phases of the eigenvalues of
U_{j}
within the interval
[0,2\pi)
. Now let
W
be any amplitude of
\rho
in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
. Then

\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}[\rho_{t}]=\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Gamma[\rho_{t};W])

(55)

is a holonomy invariant, which we call the
\bm{\alpha}
-constrained isoholonomic bound of
\rho_{t}
.

The length of any closed curve
\rho_{t}
in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
is lower bounded by its
\bm{\alpha}
-constrained isoholonomic bound:

\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]\geq\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}[\rho_{t}].

(56)

However, in this case, a stronger inequality can be established: Let
\bm{p}_{t}
be the time-evolving eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
. Then we have that

\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]^{2}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]^{2}+\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}[\rho_{t}]^{2}.

(57)

We can conclude that the isoholonomic inequality (
56
)
is always strict, unless the holonomy is the identity operator: If the eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
changes with time,
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]
is positive, and if the eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
remains constant,
\bm{p}_{t}=\bm{p}
, the length of
\rho_{t}
is bounded from below by
\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}[\rho_{t}]
, which, according to the bounds (
42
), is strictly greater than
\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}[\rho_{t}]
.
6
6

6

The reason the inequality in Eq. (
42
) is strict is to ensure that
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
is a smooth manifold. Otherwise
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{m})
would be a “manifold-with-corners.”

V.D  Derivations of the isoholonomic inequalities

Let
\rho_{t}
be a curve in
\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})
that starts and ends at
\rho
.
Reparameterize it proportionally to arc length while maintaining its orientation.

Let
W
be any amplitude of
\rho
.
The holonomy of
\rho_{t}
at
W
can be decomposed as

\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]=\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{1}\oplus\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{l},

(58)

where
\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{j}
is a unitary operator on the support of
\Lambda_{j}
. Let
\theta_{j1},\theta_{j2},\dots,\theta_{jm_{j}}
be the phases of the eigenvalues of
\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{j}
within
[0,2\pi)
. Furthermore, fix a
\bm{\Lambda}
-adapted basis as specified in Eq. (
35
), with each
|ja\rangle
being an eigenvector of
\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{j}
:

\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]_{j}|ja\rangle=e^{i\theta_{ja}}|ja\rangle.

(59)

Let
W_{t}
be the horizontal lift of
\rho_{t}
starting at
W
. We define
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
as shown in Eq. (
38
) and set
\rho_{ja;t}=|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle\langle\psi_{ja;t}|
. Since we choose
|ja\rangle
as an eigenvector of the holonomy,
the final vector
|\psi_{ja;\tau}\rangle
is a phase factor multiple of the initial vector of the curve
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
:

\begin{split}|\psi_{ja;\tau}\rangle&=p_{j;\tau}^{-1/2}W_{\tau}|ja\rangle\\
&=p_{j;\tau}^{-1/2}W\Gamma[\rho_{t};W]|ja\rangle\\
&=e^{i\theta_{ja}}p_{j;0}^{-1/2}W|ja\rangle\\
&=e^{i\theta_{ja}}|\psi_{ja;0}\rangle.\end{split}

(60)

This means that
\rho_{ja;t}
is a closed curve. Moreover, since the
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
s satisfies the parallel transport condition (
11
), each
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
is Aharonov-Anandan horizontal, and
\theta_{ja}
is the Aharonov-Anandan geometric phase of
\rho_{ja;t}
in
[0,2\pi)
.
The isoholonomic inequality for pure states (
1
) implies that

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}]^{2}\geq\theta_{ja}(2\pi-\theta_{ja}).

(61)

Since
\rho_{t}
is parameterized proportionally to arc length, and the kinetic energies of
\rho_{t}
and
W_{t}
agree, we have that

\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]^{2}=2\tau\mathcal{E}[\rho_{t}]=2\tau\mathcal{E}[W_{t}].

(62)

The kinetic energy of
W_{t}
can be divided into two components, one of which is the Fisher-Rao kinetic energy of the probability distribution associated with the eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
:

\mathcal{E}[W_{t}]=\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\int_{0}^{\tau}p_{j;t}\langle\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}|\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

(63)

Now, suppose that
\rho_{t}

is an evolution through isospectral density operators, so that
\bm{p}_{t}=\bm{p}
. Then the kinetic energy of the probability distribution is zero. Moreover, the second term in Eq. (
63
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\int_{0}^{\tau}p_{j;t}\langle\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}|\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}].

(64)

By combining the results from Eqs. (
61
)–(
64
), we obtain:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]^{2}&=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}2\tau p_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}]\\
&\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}]^{2}\\
&\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}p_{j}\theta_{ja}(2\pi-\theta_{ja})\\
&=\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}[\rho_{t}]^{2}\end{split}

(65)

This proves the isoholonomic inequality (
53
).

If the eigenvalue spectrum of
\rho_{t}
is changing with time but is constrained by the sequence
\bm{\alpha}
, as indicated in Eq. (
42
),
we can estimate the second term in (
63
) as follows:

\hskip-3.0pt\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\int_{0}^{\tau}p_{j;t}\langle\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}|\dot{\psi}_{ja;t}\rangle\mathrm{d}t\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}].

(66)

It follows that

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]^{2}&\geq 2\tau\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}2\tau\alpha_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}]\\
&\geq\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FS}}[\rho_{ja;t}]^{2}\\
&\geq\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j}\theta_{ja}(2\pi-\theta_{ja})\\
&=\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]^{2}+\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{\alpha}}[\rho_{t}]^{2}.\end{split}

(67)

This establishes Eq. (
57
) and, consequently, proves the isoholonomic inequality (
56
).

Remark 9
.

Reparameterizing
\rho
also affects the parameterization of the eigenvalue spectrum. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]
does not necessarily correspond to the kinetic energy of the probability distribution associated with the original time-varying spectrum of
\rho_{t}
. However, the value of the Fisher-Rao length functional remains unchanged under reparameterization. This means that
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{\tiny FR}}[\bm{p}_{t},\bm{m}]
is the Fisher-Rao length of the probability distribution associated with the original time-varying spectrum of
\rho_{t}
.

VI  Speed limit for the return time of unitarily evolving cyclic systems

Assume that the state evolves unitarily along a closed curve
\rho_{t}=U_{t}\rho U_{t}^{\dagger}
, where
U_{t}
is the time propagator generated by a Hamiltonian
H_{t}
. Let
\bm{p}
be the constant eigenvalue spectrum of the state, and write
\Delta E
for the average energy uncertainty along the evolution:

\Delta E=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Delta H_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\Delta^{2}H_{t}=\operatorname{tr}(H_{t}^{2}\rho_{t})-\operatorname{tr}(H_{t}\rho_{t})^{2}.

(68)

In this section, we show that the total evolution time
\tau
required for the system to return to its initial state is bounded from below by the isoholonomic bound associated with
\rho_{t}
, divided by the average energy uncertainty:

\tau\geq\frac{\operatorname{IB}_{\bm{p}}[\rho_{t}]}{\Delta E}.

(69)

VI.A  Derivation of the quantum speed limit

Choose any amplitude
W
of
\rho
, and define a lift of the curve
\rho_{t}
by as
W_{t}=U_{t}W
. This lift is not necessarily horizontal, since the velocity vector
\dot{W}_{t}=-iH_{t}W_{t}
need not be orthogonal to the vertical space at
W_{t}
for all
t
.

To derive the horizontal component of the velocity vector, let
\Pi_{j;t}
be the orthogonal projection operator onto the eigenspace of
\rho_{t}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
p_{j}
. Furthermore, if
\rho_{t}
does not have full rank, let
\Pi_{0;t}
be the orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal complement of the support of
\rho_{t}
; otherwise, let
\Pi_{0;t}
be the zero operator. We then define the state-incoherent component of
H_{t}
as

H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}\Pi_{j;t}H_{t}\Pi_{j;t},\\

(70)

and the state-coherent component of
H_{t}
as

H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}=H_{t}-H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}}.

(71)

Since the state-incoherent component commutes with
\rho_{t}
, the velocity vector field of
\rho_{t}
reduces as

\dot{\rho}_{t}=-i[H_{t},\rho_{t}]=-i[H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}},\rho_{t}].

(72)

Moreover, since
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{t}\{H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}},H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}\})=0
and
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{t}H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}})=0
, the energy variance splits into two contributions: the variances of the state-coherent and state-incoherent components of the Hamiltonian:

\Delta^{2}H_{t}=\Delta^{2}H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}+\Delta^{2}H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}}.

(73)

The first term on the right-hand side equals the square of the speed of
\rho_{t}
. To see this, decompose the velocity vector field of
W_{t}
as

\dot{W}_{t}=-iH_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}}W_{t}-iH_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}W_{t}.

(74)

The first term on the right-hand side is vertical, as it lies in the kernel of the differential of the bundle projection:

d\eta(-iH_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}}W_{t})=-i[H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny in}},\rho_{t}]=0.

(75)

To show that the second term is horizontal, expand
W_{t}
as in Eq. (
37
), where the vectors
|\psi_{ja;t}\rangle
form a parallel-transported spectral decomposition of
\rho_{t}
. Then,

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(-iH_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}W_{t})&=-i\sum_{j=1}^{l}p_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}W_{t}^{\dagger}H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}W_{t}\Lambda_{j}\\
&=-i\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{a,b=1}^{m_{j}}|ja\rangle\langle\psi_{ja;t}|H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}|\psi_{jb;t}\rangle\langle jb|\\
&=0.\end{split}

(76)

The last equality follows from the fact that
\Pi_{j;t}H_{t}^{\textrm{\tiny co}}\Pi_{j;t}=0
.
Thus, the decomposition in Eq. (
74
) is the decomposition of
\dot{W}_{t}
into its vertical and horizontal components.

It follows that the speed of
\rho_{t}
equals the uncertainty of the state-coherent component of
H_{t}
:

VIII  Summary

Quantum speed limits set fundamental bounds on how fast quantum systems can evolve. While speed limits based on fidelity measures often become trivial for cyclic evolutions, isoholonomic inequalities offer a robust alternative for bounding the minimum duration of such processes. These inequalities relate the length of a closed trajectory in state space to the holonomy it generates, providing meaningful constraints even when the system returns to its initial state.

In this paper, we derived isoholonomic inequalities and corresponding quantum speed limits for systems undergoing cyclic evolution through isodegenerate mixed states. Building on the gauge-theoretic framework underlying the geometric phase for mixed states, we defined natural notions of length and holonomy for closed curves in the space of isodegenerate density operators. At the core of this development is the isoholonomic bound—a holonomy-dependent invariant that provides a lower bound on the length of the curve. From this inequality, we established a Mandelstam-Tamm type quantum speed limit on the return time of unitarily evolving cyclic systems. In the case where the Hilbert space dimension is at least twice the rank of the state, the isoholonomic inequality becomes tight, and the associated speed limit can be saturated.
