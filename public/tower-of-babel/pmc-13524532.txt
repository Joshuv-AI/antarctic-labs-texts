Highlights recently published in the International Journal of Cardiology Heart & Vasculature on sex differences in cardiovascular disease diagnosis and management
Authors: Anke C. Fender, Dobromir Dobrev
Published: 2026

Women and men often develop cardiovascular diseases (CVD) over different time-courses and with different symptoms. We here highlight a collection of studies recently published in the International Journal of Cardiology Heart & Vasculature that showcase how biological sex can impact on CVD diagnosis and management, by either explicitly exploring sex differences, or by providing insights that become apparent when the data is viewed through the “sex differences lens”. In the accompanying Highlights article, we present studies that focused on sex differences in how CVD develops, manifests and determines short- and long-term outcomes.

Sex differences in CVD diagnosis

The comprehensive review by Hendriks and Lee [1] discussed how sex-specific risk factors and symptoms can be overlooked in routine clinical practice, in many cases delaying proper diagnosis and appropriate treatment in women. Healthcare aspects specifically related to women and their individual risk, family planning and hormonal dynamics show large variability in terms of how they are perceived and discussed by patients and practitioners. Christenson et al. [2] highlighted the qualitative differences in guidance-based care and patient counselling between specialized and non-specialized centers, in this instance pertaining to management of spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD). The authors applied a system-wide electronic medical record search to individuals with SCAD diagnosis, and compared guidance-based care for individuals with index visits to either a special SCAD outpatient clinic versus non-SCAD clinic providers. It became apparent that female patients first presenting to a specialized SCAD clinic received detailed counselling regarding contraception and pregnancy, menopausal hormone replacement therapy, migraine-triggering medication and statin use for primary prevention, while this was the case in none or nearly none of the non-specialized clinic outpatients. Screening for fibromuscular dysplasia was also provided to all patients at the SCAD clinics, but to only 1/3 of patients first presenting at non-SCAD clinics. The authors called for future quality improvement initiatives to integrate these aspects of targeted healthcare into cardiology training.

Sex differences in diagnostic read-outs and prediction modalities

Modified or extended risk scoring can aid cost-effective decision making and fine-tune patient stratification. But do men and women benefit in the same manner or to the same extent from these adapted scoring modalities? Several recently published studies in this journal demonstrate that male versus female sex can impact on a number of CVD diagnostic read-outs.

Liu et al. [3] reported that biological sex is an independent determinant of two key functional parameters of CVD, namely carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity, a measure of arterial stiffness, and the ankle-brachial index (ABI), which is used to diagnose peripheral artery disease. The coronary artery calcium score (CACS) is another important tool for the evaluation of atherosclerotic burden and fine-tuning of risk assessment in coronary artery disease (CAD). In some patients presenting for a first-time cardiology consultation for stable angina, non-gated chest computer tomography (CT) may have previously been performed for non-cardiac indications, and may be implemented as a gate-keeper for additional cost-intensive radiation-based imaging [4]. Yet such surrogate measures are only as good as the reference values on which they are based. Quite often, sex-specific shifts in age-dependent norm ranges are not considered or available. Duelli et al. [5] provided comprehensive age- and sex-specific reference values for CACS derived by multi-slice CT. Over 18,000 patients referred for non-invasive cardiac imaging were enrolled in this single-center, retrospective study, excluding those with CAD, cardiomyopathy, cardiac surgery or intervention, renal or hepatic failure. Age and sex were found to be the most influential predictors of CACS, with men consistently displaying significantly higher median scores than women across all age groups. The authors commendably provide a detailed reference value map of sex-specific CACS distribution across 9 age groups, and comprehensively discuss the relative contributions of these plus additional driving factors in this large-scale, real-world cohort.

A higher CACS in male cohorts was similarly found by Aker et al. [6], who examined the long-term value of CACS to predict CV outcome in asymptomatic patients with type 2 diabetes. Increasing CACS values independently predicted major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), with each additional affected artery providing a cumulative risk. The authors focused on age- and sex-adjusted read-outs and did not specifically look for sex differences. They did, however, point out that the cohorts with increasing CACS were to a greater proportion male, as well as being older, with greater adiposity and duration of diabetes and associated complications. The group with CACS = 0 consisted of approximately 30% males; with increasing CASC the proportion of male individuals also rose progressively, to approximately 70% in the highest CASC quartile. Median age of course increased throughout the groups as well, from around 62 to 65 years. Bjerking et al. [7] also did not explicitly examine sex differences in their study of risk assessment of obstructive CAD, but a look at the clinical data uncovers some interesting sex-dependent differences. The authors assessed the additive value of the non-invasive CADScor®System on top of the pre-test probability (PTP) model. Patients with symptoms suggestive of CAD underwent coronary CT angiography and same-day PTP scoring based on sex, age, and symptoms. A PTP >15% is generally seen as at-risk, warranting further testing; a CAD-score ≤ 20 was chosen as the cut-off for a low likelihood of CAD. In quite a number of patients (around 20% in total, up to 37% in specific subgroups) designated as PTP >15%, the additional CAD-scoring led to a reclassification as having a low probability of CAD. Overall, the authors showed that this tandem scoring could help to fine-tune risk stratification and defer invasive and resource-intense testing. By and large, those with PTP >15% were older (62 versus 53 years) and showed an overall higher comorbidity burden. Striking, they were also predominantly male: 72.5% of those with PTP >15% compared to just 2.5% in the group with PTP <15%. The overall male/female proportion in the study was 53 versus 47%, indicating a certain skew at least in the conventional PTP scoring, that may be related to the overall lower obstructive-type CAD burden in females. Regardless of sex, however, the tandem scoring can reduce risk estimates and invasive diagnostics, at least for cohorts aged under 70 years showing CAD-symptoms, but no overt hypertension.

These two studies align with the general concept that women with CAD more frequently develop non-calcified plaques, which may be missed by conventional modalities based on calcium burden detection. This is not a universal finding though. The retrospective cohort study by D'Costa et al. [8] examined coronary plaque composition of 100 individuals with low total atheroma volume. The data revealed that women had significantly lower total and non-calcified plaque burden compared to men, but the degrees of calcified and low-density non-calcified plaque volumes did not differ significantly between both sexes. The distinct feature of this study was the application of an AI-enabled coronary computed tomography angiography approach, which the authors suggest might be a useful approach for comprehensive plaque profiling to detect subclinical CAD, particularly in low-risk cohorts.

A more biochemical assessment of calcification was utilized by Chao et al. [9] in a high-risk cohort of patients with end-stage kidney disease. The authors examined the individual and combined prognostic value of high versus low alkaline phosphatase levels and absence or presence of manifest valvular calcification, and concluded that the combined measures could identify high CV risk in subgroups of patients with end-stage kidney disease. Although some co-drivers like body weight, CAD and duration of dialysis showed some differences between the four assessed groups, there were no consistent patterns. Interestingly, women appeared to be somewhat more frequently represented in the cohorts with elevated AP levels, and with the highest CV risk. High alkaline phosphatase may indicate increased bone turnover, in keeping with the osteoporosis risk attached with peri-and post-menopausal women. Further study is warranted to delineate how alkaline phosphatase dynamics define sex-dependent risks and net outcome, and to what extent its detection may aid individual risk stratification.

Caullery et al. [10] examined the prognostic value of a new non-invasive angiography-derived index of microvascular resistance in patients with myocardial infarction. The AngioIMR index was calculated from computational flow and pressure simulation immediately after percutaneous coronary intervention. A value >40 was set to define the presence of significant coronary microvascular dysfunction; this was verified to predict a significantly higher risk of all-cause death or hospitalization for heart failure. Of the 178 (74% being male) enrolled patients, 133 (74%) were male. AngioIMR >40 was detected in 72 individuals (40%), of whom 52 (72%) were male; the group with AngioIMR ≤40 consisted of 106 individuals in total, 81 of these (76%) being male. MACE occurred in 56 patients, including 33 (59%) males. In this cohort therefore, women made up less than one-third of those with a high microvascular risk score, but represented 41% of those with adverse outcome during follow-up. While sex-specific differences in the score-outcome axis was not the objective of this study, the data support the concept that some disease scoring systems may inadequately capture net CV risk in females.

In line with this, the study by Khoudry et al. [11] questioned the validity of standard diagnostic cut-off values that do not consider possible sex differences. The authors examined septal hypertrophy indices as a predictor of mortality in over 70,000 consecutive patients undergoing echocardiography. Around half of the included patients died during the 5-year median follow-up, 9631 (25%) males and 8429 (26%) females. In the female cohort, mortality was predicted by much lower threshold values for septal hypertrophy than in males, suggesting that septal hypertrophy indices should be adjusted to consider sex in order to facilitate timely diagnosis and improved outcome. Similar findings were reported by Nilsen et al. [12], who showed that inflammatory and thrombotic risk markers do not provide the same prognostic utility in men and women with acute myocardial infarction. The acute-phase proteins α1-antichymotrypsin, high sensitivity C-reactive protein, and pentraxin 3 robustly predicted all-cause mortality in men, but not in women. Conversely, the thrombo-fibrinolytic markers D-dimer and fibrin monomer were significant predictors of all-cause mortality in females, but not males.

Sex differences in patient care

Flege et al. [13] examined gaps in patient care after myocardial infarction based on one-year follow-up data of nearly 25,000 patients discharged from Danish hospitals. Those patients more likely to receive guideline-conform lipid-lowering therapy, post-myocardial infarction cholesterol monitoring and specialist consultation during follow-up were younger, less co-morbid, more highly educated, and predominantly male. Although not specifically addressing the sex-specific aspect of healthcare discrepancies, the authors stress that optimal outcome requires monitoring of treatment adherence and efficacy, and critically discuss issues within the healthcare system as well as physician- and patient-associated factors that likely contribute and provide a platform for improvement.

As regards management of atrial fibrillation data presented by Giskes et al. [14] indicate that sex-specific gaps in guideline-conform stroke risk management appear to be closing. Sex-based inequality, however, remains in terms of invasive rhythm control, as showcased by Lappalainen et al. [15]. The Finnish nationwide cohort study revealed that patients undergoing atrial fibrillation ablation are still predominantly male, while women more frequently undergo rate control by atrioventricular node ablation. The authors critically reflect on possible reasons for the sex-specific management of atrial fibrillation, but conclude that the descriptive analyses do not permit causative interpretation.

Healthcare utilization by men and women also features quantitative differences. Middledorp et al. [16] report on healthcare visits by patients with atrial fibrillation, based on retrospective analysis of electronic health records from three hospitals in the Netherlands. The authors show no differences in overall emergency, in-patient or out-patient hospital visits, but upon stratification by age, identify more outpatient visits by females versus males aged <60 years, while in patients aged >75, more males than females required outpatient visits. Notably, in the older group, females predominate in terms of inpatient stays and emergency visits. One possible explanation is that in women, atrial fibrillation develop later in life, possibly coinciding with a higher net morbidity burden and symptom severity.

Sex differences in clinical trial representation

Finally, Gupta et al. [17] reported on the continued issue of underrepresentation of women and racial minorities in clinical trials, in this particular instance those related to SGLT2 inhibitors and heart failure. Across 43 randomized controlled trials performed between 2018 and 2024, the more recent studies and those performed in the US were more likely to have enrolled at least 30% female participants. Despite this ongoing improvement, barriers to equality in trial enrollment warrant attention. Considering that SGLT2 inhibitors are now first-line treatments for heart failure, it is worth considering possible sex-specific discrepancies in the net benefit and risk assessment. For example, women are more prone to the urinary tract infections that constitute a major and common adverse effect of the SGLT2 inhibitors.

Overall, these studies highlight that CVD is often diagnosed and managed differently between male and female patients, and that these need to be considered more routinely in risk prediction modalities and patient counselling. Clearly, more future studies performed in different patient cohorts prospectively comparing female and male patients are required to further improve the early diagnosis and to guide the management of CVD in women.
