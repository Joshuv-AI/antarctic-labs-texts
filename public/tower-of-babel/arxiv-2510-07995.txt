Quantum Max-Cut is NP-hard to approximate
Stephen Piddock

Abstract
We unconditionally prove that it is NP-hard to compute a constant multiplicative approximation to the Quantum Max-Cut problem on an unweighted graph of constant bounded degree. The proof works in two stages: first a generic reduction to computing the optimal value of a quantum problem, from the optimal value over product states. Then an approximation preserving reduction from Max-Cut to Product-QMC , the product state version of Quantum Max-Cut . More precisely, in the second part, we construct a PTAS reduction from Max-Cut k (the rank- k k constrained version of Max-Cut ) to Max-Cut k+1 , where Max-Cut and Product-QMC coincide with Max-Cut 1 and Max-Cut 3 respectively. We thus prove that Max-Cut k is APX-complete for all constant k.

Introduction

Quantum Max-Cut
is a quantum generalisation of the classical
Max-Cut
problem . In
Max-Cut
, the aim is to assign values
s_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
for each vertex in a graph
G
in order to maximise the number of edges
ij
with different signs
s_{i}\neq s_{j}
.
More precisely, given a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
, with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m

\textsc{Max-Cut}(G)=\max_{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\sum_{ij\in E}w_{ij}\frac{1}{2}(1-s_{i}s_{j}).

Quantum Max-Cut
is similar except that there is qubit at each vertex of the graph and the problem is to compute
\OPT(H)
, the maximum energy
\Tr(H\rho)
over
n
-qubit quantum states
\rho
with respect to the Hamiltonian
H
:

H=\sum_{ij\in E}w_{ij}\frac{1}{4}(I-X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j}-Z_{i}Z_{j}).

where
X,Y,Z
are the Pauli matrices and the subscript
i
denotes the action of an operator on the
i
th qubit.
Here the weight
w_{ij}
is interpreted as the strength of the interaction
I-X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j}-Z_{i}Z_{j}
.

Computing
\OPT(H)
exactly is equivalent to calculating the maximum eigenvalue of
H
, or the ground state energy of
-H
, an antiferromagnetic Heisenberg Hamiltonian.
The ground state energy is the physically relevant quantity, and the “Local Hamiltonian problem” is the task of computing the ground state energy to inverse polynomial accuracy. Here we focus on the maximisation formulation as it mirrors the classical
Max-Cut
problem, and is better suited to approximation algorithms.

As the only two local qubit interaction with
SU(2)
symmetry (i.e. it is invariant under conjugation by
U\otimes U
for any
U\in SU(2)
), the Heisenberg interaction is in a sense one of the simplest and most natural two-qubit interactions.
It is closely related to the Fermi-Hubbard model at half-filling, a simple but important model of quantum magnetism.

Due to its symmetry, the Heisenberg interaction plays a central part in the proof of the complexity classification of local Hamiltonian problems
[
1
]
, and in the construction of universal qubit Hamiltonians
[
2
]
.
The decision variant of
Quantum Max-Cut
is QMA-complete
[
3
]
, where the aim is to decide if
\OPT(H)
is above
a
or below
b
for thresholds
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a-b\geq 1/\poly(n)
.
A major drawback of this result is that the weights
w_{ij}
vary throughout the graph, becoming as large as
\poly(n)
. This distances the model from the most physically relevant setting where the weights are all equal to 1.

Since it is hard to compute
Quantum Max-Cut
to additive error, attention has turned to approximation algorithms
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
These algorithms do not find the true optimal solution, but find states

which are guaranteed to achieve an energy
\Tr(H\rho)
that comes within a constant multiplicative factor

\textsc{Quantum Max-Cut}(G)
.

The approximation algorithm of
[
4
]
is based on the famous Goemans-Williamson approximation algorithm
[

[

.
For

Even without the unique games conjecture,

[

]

For

, the situation is very different. A number of extensions and improvements have been developed since the algorithm of

4

, with the best achievable approximation ratio increasing over time
[

,
6
,

,
8
]
, and the simple SDP based method of
[

On the hardness side, we currently have no way to extend the QMA-hardness result of
[
3
]
to the regime of constant approximation ratio algorithms, since this would require proving a quantum version of the PCP theorem, which appears to be beyond the reach of current tools.
Although
Quantum Max-Cut
is introduced as a generalisation of
Max-Cut
, we do not have any way to directly embed
Max-Cut
instances into
Quantum Max-Cut

Recently, there has been an attempt

Is

hard when the interaction strengths are
O(1)
, or even uniform? Is it hard to approximate up to a constant approximation ratio? Can we prove a more direct reduction from
Max-Cut
to
Quantum Max-Cut
?
We remark that the first and third question here are the essence of open questions 3.2 and 3.3 from Gharibian’s recent survey paper on quantum versions of NP
[
15

We resolve these questions in our main result:

Theorem 1
.

There is a constant
\alpha<1
such that it is NP-hard to compute a value that approximates the optimal
Quantum Max-Cut
value within approximation ratio
\alpha
.
This holds even when the graph
G
is unweighted (
w_{ij}=1

) and each vertex has bounded (constant) degree.

We believe that this result is the first hardness of approximation result for a family of quantum local Hamiltonian problems that does not require any conjectures or include a known hard-to-approximate classical CSP in that family.

Theorem
1
is proven by reducing from
Max-Cut
to
Quantum Max-Cut
, although the reduction is not completely direct. As an intermediate problem, we consider the product state variant of
Quantum Max-Cut
.

First we show that approximating
\textsc{Quantum Max-Cut}(G)=\OPT(H)
is at least as hard as approximating
\textsc{Product-QMC}(G)=\OPTprod(H)
, the maximal value over product states:

\textsc{Product-QMC}(G)=\OPTprod(H)=\max_{\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{n}}\Tr(H\rho)

We do this by taking a graph
G
, and replacing each vertex
i
with a cloud of vertices

where
|T_{i}|=T
, with edges between all vertices in
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
iff
ij\in E
.
The new graph
G^{\prime}
has the same rough structure as

, but now each vertex has high degree, and since each qubit cannot be highly entangled with its neighbours, product states are now close to optimal.

In fact the same idea applies generically to any

Theorem 2

Let
H=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}h_{\mathbf{i}}

T^{k}\OPTprod(H)\leq\OPT(H^{\prime})\leq(T+2)^{k}\OPTprod(H)

We remark that Theorem

[
16

. Indeed, their Corollary 4 shows that for a Hamiltonian with an interaction graph of high degree, there is a product state which is nearly optimal.
With an additional argument relating the optimal product state for

and the optimal product state for

2
using their result.

However we instead provide a novel proof of Theorem
2
based on the representation theory of
SU(2)
and an inequality due to Lieb
[
17
]
.
This has the advantage of being simple, easily extends the results to
k>2
and achieves better constant factors.
The disadvantage of this method is that it apparently cannot be generalised to systems of qu
d
its

Qudits are quantum subsystems with a complex state space of dimension
d
. For example, qubits are qudits of dimension

.
.

The recent work of
[
18

classifies the complexity of the product state problem for
\mathcal{S}
-Hamiltonians, the family of Hamiltonians consisting of interactions from a fixed set

(with positive and negative weights allowed). Therefore as a corollary of Theorem

Let

\mathcal{S}

NP-hard otherwise. Furthermore, this holds even when the interaction strengths are

.

Note that this corollary is weaker than the classification of

Armed with Theorem
2
, our focus in the second part of the paper is to prove hardness of approximation for finding the optimal product state
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{n}
for
\textsc{Product-QMC}(G)
.
By decomposing a single qubit density matrix in the Pauli basis as
\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+x_{1}X+x_{2}Y+x_{3}Z)
, we can identify
\rho
with the 3-dimensional unit vector of Pauli coefficients
\mathbf{x}
, and the product state
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{n}
with the list of unit vectors
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\dots\mathbf{x_{n}})
.

The rank-
k
constrained
Max-Cut
problem, which we denote
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is a generalisation of
Max-Cut
, where each vertex is assigned a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, i.e. an element of the sphere
S_{k-1}
.

\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)=\max_{\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{n}})\in(S_{k-1})^{n}}\sum_{ij\in E}\frac{1}{2}(1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{j}})

Therefore, as has been observed previously (for example in
[
4
]
),
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{3}(G)=2\times\textsc{Product-QMC}(G)
; and note that
\textsc{Max-Cut}=\textsc{Max-Cut}_{1}
.
Briët, de Oliveira Filho and Vallentin showed how the Goeman-Williams approximation algorithm for
Max-Cut
can be generalised to
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
for every
k

[
10
]
.
Strong evidence was provided in
[
14
]
that the approximation algorithms of
[
10
]
are optimal: they proved that, assuming the unique games conjecture, and a newly conjectured inequality referred to as the vector-valued Borel inequality, it is NP-hard to approximate
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
to any better approximation ratio.

However, without these assumptions, the hardness of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is less well understood. While
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{1}
was on Karp’s original list of NP-complete problems
[
19
]
, and Lovász and others have conjectured that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is hard for other values of
k
(see e.g. p236 of
[
20
]
), it is only very recently that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{3}
was proven to be NP-complete for additive inverse polynomial accuracy
[
18
]
, and no other hardness results are known for
k\neq 1,3
.

Furthermore no hardness of approximation results are known without additional assumptions, except for
k=1
, where it is known that
Max-Cut
is APX-complete
[
12
]
. We extend these results to all
k>1
in Theorem
3
.

Theorem 3
.

There is a PTAS reduction from
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
to
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
.
Therefore
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is APX-complete for all (constant)
k\geq 1
.

We remark that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{2}=2\times\textsc{Product-QMC}_{XY}(G)
, the problem of finding the optimal product state for the Hamiltonian
H_{XY}
:

H_{XY}=\sum_{ij\in E}w_{ij}\frac{1}{4}(I-X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j})

and thus we also get a hardness of approximation result for
\textsc{Product-QMC}_{XY}(G)
.

To reduce from
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
to
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
, it is necessary to find a way to effectively reduce the available dimensions at each vertex in a
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
instance. One natural way to do this is to look for a gadget which forces two vertices to be orthogonal (or approximately orthogonal). Then by selecting a reference vertex
0
, and applying this gadget to all pairs
i,0
, one can ensure that
\mathbf{x_{i}}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{x_{0}}
for all
i
, and thus the
\mathbf{x_{i}}
are effectively in a lower dimensional space. Wright
[
21
]
had suggested a gadget based on a long odd length cycle for this task.

Our insight is to recognise that it is not necessary to make
\mathbf{x_{i}}
and
\mathbf{x_{0}}
orthogonal, but it is enough to make sure that
\mathbf{x_{i}}
and
\mathbf{x_{0}}
are at a fixed angle from each other for all
i
. For this task, almost any gadget will do, as long as it is not a bipartite graph, and we focus on the simplest example of a triangle.
In a triangle graph, the optimal assignment for
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
has an angle of
2\pi/3
between each of the vertices on the triangle.

We sketch a simplified version of our construction based on this idea. Take an instance
G
of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
and add a vertex
c_{i}
for each
i
and an extra vertex
0
. Include the original edges of
G
, and add heavily weighted edges between the vertices of the triangle
0,i,c_{i}
for all
i
to create a new graph
G^{\prime}
.
The heavily weighted triangle gadgets mean that any assignment
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
close to optimal must have
\mathbf{x_{i}}=\cos(\theta_i)\mathbf{x_{0}}+\sin(\theta_i)\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}
for some
\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{x_{0}}
, and
\theta_{i}\approx 2\pi/3
.
Since the
\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}
are orthogonal to
\mathbf{x_{0}}
, we can interpret them living in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, rather than
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
.

Therefore the optimal value
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})
will be approximately equal to a simple function of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)
. Furthermore by projecting the vector
\mathbf{x_{i}}
onto the space orthogonal to
\mathbf{x_{0}}
and renormalising for all
i
, we have a map
g:(S_{k})^{n+1}\rightarrow(S_{k-1})^{n}
which maps good solutions for
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})
to good solutions for
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)
.
This is essential for showing that the construction is a PTAS reduction.

While this sketch can be formalised into a PTAS reduction and hence prove
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is APX-hard, there are a couple of drawbacks of this simple approach. Firstly, the heavily weighted terms are undesirable: although they only need to be large constant values (not scaling with
n
, the number of vertices), we want a construction where all weights are equal to 1. Second, the reference vertex
0
has very high degree as it is connected to all other vertices.

We overcome these difficulties by replacing the single reference vertex
0
with a large bipartite expander graph of additional vertices. The optimal assignment for a bipartite graph is to have all vertices on one side of the bipartition set to the same vector
\mathbf{z}
and all vertices on the other side of the partition set to
-\mathbf{z}
. Rather than have a heavily weighted triangle between
i
and
0
, we instead have multiple unweighted triangles between
i
and different vertices from one side of the bipartite graph. This then reproduces the behaviour of the simple reduction but without any weighted edges or high degree vertices.

We briefly contrast our proof method to that of
[
18
]
, where in their Theorem 1.5, they prove that it is NP-hard to determine the optimal value of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{3}
up to additive inverse polynomial accuracy. Rather than reduce from
Max-Cut
, their method instead reduces from
3
-
COLORING
. The construction involves taking an instance
G
of
3
-
COLORING
, and replacing each edge
ij
with a clique of four vertices, then taking that new graph and replacing each edge with a clique of three vertices. Finally an additional vertex is added and connected to some of the vertices. This final vertex plays a similar role to the vertex
0
described in the sketch of our construction above, in that it provides a reference state to fix a basis for the other vertices.

Our proof method is arguably simpler, provides a direct connection to
Max-Cut
, and obtains the stronger result of APX-completeness for
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{3}
.

Preliminaries

2.1
SU(2) representation theory

The Lie group
SU(2)
is the group of unitary
2\times 2
matrices with determinant
1
. The associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is the space of traceless anti-hermitian
2\times 2
matrices.
Equivalently, any element
A\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
can be decomposed as
A=a_{1}\sigma^{(1)}+a_{2}\sigma^{(2)}+a_{3}\sigma^{(3)}
for real numbers
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
and where the
\sigma^{(j)}
are the Pauli matrices:

\sigma^{(1)}=X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma^{(2)}=Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma^{(3)}=Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

For convenience, we will also write
\sigma^{(0)}
for the
2\times 2
identity matrix
\sigma^{(0)}=I=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, so that
\{\sigma^{(j)}\}_{j=0}^{4}
is a basis for all hermitian
2\times 2
matrices.

Note that
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is closed under the action of the matrix commutator (sometimes referred to as the Lie bracket)
[A,B]=AB-BA
.

A
representation

R
of dimension
d
is a map
R:\mathfrak{su}(2)\rightarrow M(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, where
M(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is the space of complex square matrices of dimension
d
, such that

[R(A),R(B)]=[A,B]\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }A,B\in\mathfrak{su}(2).

The representation
R
is
irreducible
if there is no non-trivial subspace
W\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
for which
R(A)
maps
W
to itself for all
A\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

For each positive integer
d
, there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
of dimension
d
. We denote this
d
-dimensional irreducible representation
R_{J}
where
J=\frac{d-1}{2}
, and will refer to it as the
spin-
J
representation
, matching the terminology common in physics.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is
semi-simple
which means that any representation
R
is isomorphic to a direct sum of irreducible representations.
Concretely, for a representation
R
there is a unitary basis change
U
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that:

R(A)=U\left(\bigoplus_{J}R_{J}(A)\right)U^{\dagger}\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }A\in\mathfrak{su}(2).

(1)

Given two representations
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
one can define the
tensor product representation

R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}
:

R_{1}\otimes R_{2}(A)=R_{1}(A)\otimes I_{d_{2}}+I_{d_{1}}\otimes R_{2}(A)

We are particularly interested in the representation
\hat{R}
produced by taking the tensor product of
T
copies of the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
representation.
This acts on the space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes T}
, which we can identify with the space of
T
qubits:

\hat{R}(A)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}

where
A_{t}
denotes the operator
A
acting on qubit
t
, and identity elsewhere.

The decomposition of
\hat{R}
into irreducible representations as in equation (
1
) is very well understood. In fact, it is best described via Schur-Weyl duality, which connects to the representation theory of the symmetric group.
However all we need is that there exists a unitary
U
such that

\hat{R}(A)=U\left(\bigoplus_{J\in\mathcal{T}}R_{J}(A)\otimes I_{g_{J}}\right)U^{\dagger}\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }A\in\mathfrak{su}(2).

(2)

where
I_{g_{J}}
is the identity matrix on a space of dimension

and
\mathcal{T}=\{T/2,T/2-1,T/2-2,\dots\}
(continuing down to

if

if

2.3
The APX class and PTAS reductions

An NP optimisation (NPO) problem is a triple
(I,S,m)
where:

1.

I
is a set of instances.

2.

Given an instance
x\in I
, the set
S(x)
is the set of valid solutions to
x
.

3.

Given an instance
x\in I
and a valid solution
y\in S(x)
, the value achieved by
y
is
m(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
.

The sets
I
and
S(x)
are subsets of
\{0,1\}^{*}
which can be recognised in polynomial time, and the function
m
is computable in polynomial time.

An NP optimisation problem can be a either a maximisation problem or a minimisation problem. Given an instance
x
of a maximisation NPO problem, the aim is to find a valid solution
y
which maximises the value
m(x,y)
.
An algorithm that, for any instance
x
, finds a
y
such that
m(x,y)
is at least
\alpha
times the optimal value, is said to have approximation ratio
\alpha
.

An NP optimisation problem is in the class APX if there exists a constant
\alpha
and a polynomial time algorithm
T
that achieves an approximation ratio
\alpha
. The class PTAS is a subset of APX, and consists of all NPO problems for which there exists a polynomial time approximation scheme. That is, a problem is in PTAS if there exists an algorithm
T
such that for any instance
x
and approximation ratio
\alpha
,
T(x,\alpha)
returns a valid solution
y
that achieves the approximation ratio
\alpha
, and
T
runs in time
q_{r}(|x|)
for some polynomial
q_{r}
.

When proving that a problem is hard for the class APX, it is necessary to use PTAS reductions, which are a type of approximation-preserving reduction which preserves containment in the class PTAS. One reason for this is that we want APX-hardness to imply that a problem does not have a polynomial time approximation scheme (assuming that APX
\neq
PTAS).

Formally a PTAS reduction from problem A to problem B is a tuple of functions
(f,g,c)
such that, for any instance
x
of A and
\alpha<1
:

1.

f(x,\alpha)
is an instance of
B
computable in polynomial time in
|x|
.

2.

for any valid solution
y
to
f(x,\alpha)
, then
g(x,y,\alpha)
is a valid solution of
x
, computable in polynomial time in
|x|
and
|y|
.

3.

for any valid solution
y
to
f(x,\alpha)
, if
y
achieves an approximation ratio at least
c(\alpha)
for the instance
f(x,\alpha)
, then
g(x,y,\alpha)
achieves an approximation ratio at least
\alpha
for the instance
x
.

Therefore a PTAS reduction provides a map between instances of A and B with related optimal values, but also map back such that ‘good’ solutions of the B instance are mapped back to ‘good’ solutions of the A instance.
Note that a PTAS reduction from A to B provides an algorithm for A with approximation ratio
\alpha
, if there is an algorithm for B which achieves an approximation ratio
c(\alpha)
.
If
c
is not the identity function, then it may be that the approximation ratio
c(\alpha)
required for B is a lot higher than the desired approximation ratio
\alpha
for A.

Other (simpler) kinds of approximation-preserving reductions are often used when proving APX-completeness results, such as L-reductions or AP-reductions, see
[
22
]
for more.
However the reduction we use in Lemma
1
constructs a different instance
G^{\prime}
depending on a parameter
\eta
which is chosen to achieve particular approximation ratios
\alpha
. That is, the function
f(x,\alpha)
in the PTAS reduction really does depend on
\alpha
, which is not allowed in the other types of approximation-preserving reductions.

Reduction from product state problem

In this section we prove Theorem
2
, which relates the optimal product state value of a local Hamiltonian
H
to the optimal value of another Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
, constructed from the same interactions as
H
. First we describe how to construct
H^{\prime}
from

.

Using the notation introduced in Section
2.2
, we write
H=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}h_{\mathbf{i}}
for interactions
h_{\mathbf{i}}
which act non trivially only on the qubits in
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{k})
, and can be expressed in the Pauli basis as in equation (
3
), so that

H=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}}M(\mathbf{i})_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots a_{k}}\sigma_{i_{1}}^{(a_{1})}\sigma_{i_{2}}^{(a_{2})}\dots\sigma_{i_{n}}^{(a_{k})}

(7)

We construct
H^{\prime}
by replacing each qubit of

with a cluster of
T
qubits for some parameter
T
which is used to control the quality of approximation. We label the
nT
qubits by
(i,t)
for some
i\in[n]
and
t\in[T]
. Each term
h_{\mathbf{i}}
in
H
is replaced with the same interaction applied to qubits
(i_{1},t_{1}),(i_{2},t_{2}),\dots(i_{k},t_{k})
for all combinations of

. The overall Hamiltonian

is therefore:

H^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in[T]^{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}}M(\mathbf{i})_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots a_{k}}\sigma_{(i_{1},t_{1})}^{(a_{1})}\sigma_{(i_{2},t_{2})}^{(a_{2})}\dots\sigma_{(i_{k},t_{k})}^{(a_{k})}.

(8)

Example interaction graph of
H^{\prime}
. At the top of the figure is a simple interaction graph of a 2-local Hamiltonian

on

Theorem
2
.

Let
H=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}h_{\mathbf{i}}
be a
k
-local qubit Hamiltonian with
h_{\mathbf{i}}\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{i}\in I
. Let
H^{\prime}
be constructed from
H
as above. Then

T^{k}\OPTprod(H)\leq\OPT(H^{\prime})\leq(T+2)^{k}\OPTprod(H)

Proof.

First we prove the lower bound: let
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{n}
be the product state that achieves
\Tr(H\rho)=\OPTprod(H)
.
Extend this to a product state
\rho^{\prime}
on
nT
qubits where qubit
(i,t)
is in state
\rho_{i}
for all
t
.
Then

\OPT(H^{\prime})\geq\Tr(H'\rho')=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in[T]^{k}}\Tr(H\rho)=T^{k}\OPTprod(H).

It remains to prove the upper bound.
Let
\hat{S}^{(a)}_{i}=\sum_{t\in T}\sigma^{(a)}_{(i,t)}
and observe that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}}M(\mathbf{i})_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots a_{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in[T]^{k}}\sigma_{(i_{1},t_{1})}^{(a_{1})}\sigma_{(i_{2},t_{2})}^{(a_{2})}\dots\sigma_{(i_{k},t_{k})}^{(a_{k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}}M(\mathbf{i})_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots a_{k}}\left(\sum_{t_{1}\in[T]}\sigma_{(i_{1},t_{1})}^{(a_{1})}\right)\left(\sum_{t_{2}\in[T]}\sigma_{(i_{2},t_{2})}^{(a_{1})}\right)\dots\left(\sum_{t_{k}\in[T]}\sigma_{(i_{k},t_{k})}^{(a_{1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in I}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}}M(\mathbf{i})_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots a_{k}}\hat{S}_{i_{1}}^{(a_{1})}\hat{S}_{i_{2}}^{(a_{2})}\dots\hat{S}_{i_{k}}^{(a_{k})}.

The operator
\hat{S}^{(a)}_{i}
is the tensor product representation
\hat{R}
(of
T
copies of the spin
\frac{1}{2}
representation) applied to
\sigma^{(a)}
, and it acts on the set of
T
qubits
\{(i,t)\}_{t\in[T]}
which replaced the original qubit
i
in
H
.
Therefore, as in equation (
2
) the tensor product representation
\hat{R}
decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible representations:

\hat{S}^{(a)}=U\left(\bigoplus_{J\in\mathcal{T}}R_{J}(\sigma^{(a)})\otimes I_{g_{J}}\right)U^{\dagger}

for some unitary
U
, and where the sum is over all
J\in\mathcal{T}=\{T/2,T/2-1,T/2-2,\dots\}
(continuing down to
0
if
T
is even, or
1/2
if
T
is odd).

Therefore

H^{\prime}=U^{\otimes n}\left(\bigoplus_{\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}}H(\mathbf{J})\otimes I_{g_{\mathbf{J}}}\right)(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}

where for each
\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},J_{2},\dots,J_{n})\in\mathcal{T}^{n}
,
I_{g_{\mathbf{J}}}
is the identity matrix on a space of dimension
g_{\mathbf{J}}=g_{J_{1}}\times g_{J_{2}}\dots\times g_{J_{n}}
, and
H(\mathbf{J})
is the variant of
H
where each qubit
i
is replaced by a spin
J_{i}
particle, as defined in equation (
4
).

So the optimal energy for
H^{\prime}
is the maximum out of the optimal energies
\OPT(H(\mathbf{J}))
out of all possible values of
\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}

\OPT(H^{\prime})=\max_{\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}}\OPT(H(\mathbf{J})).

(9)

Applying Lieb’s inequality (equation (
6
) above) to this gives:

\OPT(H^{\prime})\leq\max_{\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}}\OPTprod(\tilde{H}(\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}))

(10)

where
\mathbf{1}
is the all ones vector and
\tilde{H}(\mathbf{J})
is as defined in equation (
5
).

Since
h_{\mathbf{i}}\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{i}
, increasing the value of
J_{i}
for some
i
can only increase the value of
\OPTprod(\tilde{H}(\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}))
.
Therefore the maximum in equation (
10
) is obtained when each
J_{i}
is maximised, i.e. at
\mathbf{J^{*}}=(T/2,T/2,\dots,T/2)\in\mathcal{T}^{n}
. For this

, we have
\tilde{H}(\mathbf{J^{*}}+\mathbf{1})=(T+2)^{k}H
and so

\OPT(H^{\prime})\leq\OPTprod(\tilde{H}(\mathbf{J^{*}}+\mathbf{1}))=(T+2)^{k}\OPTprod(H).

∎

4
Rank constrained Max-Cut

In this section we prove Theorem
3
, that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is APX-complete, by demonstrating a PTAS reduction from
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
to
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
.

We first introduce some notation for the cost function which is maximised in
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
.
Let
F_{k}^{G}:(S_{k-1})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined by:

F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{ij\in E}w_{ij}\frac{1}{2}(1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{j}})

so that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)=\max_{\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}}F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
.

In analogy to
Quantum Max-Cut
, we sometimes refer to
F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
as the energy of
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
. For the remainder we now take
w_{ij}=1
.

The main technical lemma which is used to prove Theorem
3
is Lemma
1
:

Lemma 1
.

Given an instance
G
of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
and an error parameter
\eta
, we construct an instance
G^{\prime}
of
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
which is only polynomilally larger than
G
and prove that there exist constants
c,C
such that:

1.

\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})\geq C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)

2.

there is a map
g:(S_{k})^{n+6\eta m}\rightarrow(S_{k-1})^{n}
such that for any
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}\in(S_{k})^{n+6\eta m}
:

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\leq C\eta m+\frac{cm}{\eta}+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(g(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})).

We first prove how Theorem
3
follows from Lemma
1
, delaying the proof of Lemma
1
until Section
4.4
.

Theorem
3
.

There is a PTAS reduction from
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
to
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}
.
Therefore
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}
is APX-complete for all (constant)
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

To prove that the construction of Lemma
1
is a PTAS reduction, we need to show that for any desired
\beta<1
, we can choose
\eta
and
\alpha
such that:

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\geq\alpha\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})\quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{k}^{G}(g(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}))\geq\beta\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)

So suppose that
F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\geq\alpha\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})
for some
\alpha
to be chosen later. Using both parts of Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle C\eta m+\frac{cm}{\eta}+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(g(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}))

\displaystyle\geq F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\alpha(C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G))

Rearranging this gives

\displaystyle F_{k}^{G}(g(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}))

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)+[(\alpha-1)C\eta-c/\eta]4m/3

\displaystyle\geq\left[\alpha+\frac{8}{3}(\alpha-1)C\eta-\frac{8c}{3\eta}\right]\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)

where we’ve used the fact that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)\geq m/2
and
[(\alpha-1)C\eta-c/\eta]<0
.
Therefore it suffices to choose

\eta\geq\frac{16c}{3(1-\beta)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha=\frac{\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\frac{8C}{3}}{1+\frac{8C}{3}}

so that
\alpha+\frac{8}{3}(\alpha-1)C\eta=\frac{1+\beta}{2}
and
\frac{8c}{3\eta}\leq(1-\beta)/2
.
∎

Before we prove Lemma

4.2
Triangle gadget

First we consider a triangle gadget, consisting of three vertices

Lemma 2
.

Let
G_{\Delta}=(V,E)
where
V=\{0,1,2\}
and
E=\{01,12,02\}
.
Let
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x_{0}},\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}})

Then

\max_{\mathbf{x_{2}}}F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{9}{4}-\frac{1}{4}(2\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}-1)^{2}\leq\frac{9}{4}-\frac{9}{16\pi^{2}}(\theta-\tfrac{2\pi}{3})^{2}

Proof.

\displaystyle F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{uv\in E}1-\mathbf{x_{u}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(3-\mathbf{x_{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}})-\mathbf{x_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{1}})

This expression is maximised by choosing
\mathbf{x_{2}}
in the
-(\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}})
direction. i.e. by setting
\mathbf{x_{2}}=-\frac{\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}}}{\norm{\vec{x_0}+\vec{x_1}}}
. Substituting this in above gives

\max_{\mathbf{x_{2}}}F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}(3+\norm{\vec{x_0}+\vec{x_1}}-\mathbf{x_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{1}})

Note that the above substitution is only possible if
\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}}\neq 0
. If
\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}}=0
, then

is independent of
\mathbf{x_{2}}
, and the above expression for
\max_{\mathbf{x_{2}}}F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
still holds.

Now use the fact that
\norm{\vec{x_0}+\vec{x_1}}^{2}=(\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}})\cdot(\mathbf{x_{0}}+\mathbf{x_{1}})=2+2\mathbf{x_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{1}}
to get

\max_{\mathbf{x_{2}}}F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{2+2\mathbf{x_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{1}}}-\mathbf{x_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{1}}).

Substitute in

and rearrange to show

\max_{\mathbf{x_{2}}}F^{G_{\Delta}}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{2+2\cos\theta}-\cos\theta)=\frac{9}{4}-\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{2+2\cos\theta}-1)^{2}

Finally, substitute in the half-angle formula

To prove the inequality, it remains to show that

Note that

.

Observe that
\sum_{l=1}^{k}\norm{\vec{\hat{e}_l}}^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{il}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}
, so we have

F^{G}_{k}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\lambda_{\max}n-\Delta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}\right).

Noting that
\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}\geq 0
for all
i\in B
, all that remains is to show that
\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}\geq\epsilon_{i}^{2}/\pi^{2}
for all
i\in A
.
We actually prove the slightly stronger bound of

\norm{\vec{e_i}}\geq\begin{cases}\sin(\epsilon_i)&\text{ if }\epsilon_{i}\in[0,\pi/2]\\
1&\text{ if }\epsilon_{i}\in[\pi/2,\pi].\end{cases}

Since
\mathbf{e^{*}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{e}_{l}}
are orthogonal, we can take the inner product of equation (
11
) with
\mathbf{e^{*}}
to get:

z_{l}\norm{\vec{e^*}}^{2}=\mathbf{\hat{x}_{l}}\cdot\mathbf{e^{*}}=\sum_{i\in A}x_{il}-\sum_{i\in B}x_{il}.

So
\mathbf{z}=(\sum_{i\in A}\mathbf{x_{i}}-\sum_{i\in B}\mathbf{x_{i}})/n
is the
k
-dimensional vector with coordinates
z_{l}
.
Looking at the
i
th coordinate of
\mathbf{\hat{x}_{l}}
in equation (
11
) for
i\in A
, we have:

x_{il}=z_{l}+e_{il}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{x_{i}}=\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{e_{i}}.

The angle
\epsilon_{i}
between
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x_{i}}
satisfies
\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x_{i}}=\norm{\vec{z}}\norm{\vec{x_i}}\cos(\epsilon_i)=\norm{\vec{z}}\cos(\epsilon_i)
, and so

\displaystyle\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x_{i}}-\mathbf{z})\cdot(\mathbf{x_{i}}-\mathbf{z})

(12)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{i}}-2\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{z}

(13)

\displaystyle=1-2\norm{\vec{z}}\cos(\epsilon_i)+\norm{\vec{z}}^{2}.

(14)

If
\epsilon_{i}\in[\pi/2,\pi]
, then
\cos(\epsilon_i)\geq 0
, and so
\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}\geq 1
.
Otherwise, if
\epsilon_{i}\in[0,\pi/2]
, we can complete the square in equation (
14
) to get

\norm{\vec{e_i}}^{2}=1-\cos^{2}(\epsilon_{i})+\left(\norm{\vec{z}}-\cos(\epsilon_i)\right)^{2}\geq\sin^{2}(\epsilon_{i}).

∎

4.4
Proof of Lemma
1

We are now ready to prove Lemma
1
.

Proof.

For part 1. of Lemma
1
, let
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
be the assignment that achieves
F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)
.
Embed each vector
\mathbf{x_{i}}
into
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
by padding with an extra
0
and call the resulting vector
\mathbf{x^{\prime}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
. Let
\mathbf{z}=(0,0,\dots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
be the new direction.

Define
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}\in(S_{k+1})^{V^{\prime}}
by:

\mathbf{y_{i}}=\cos(2\pi/3)\mathbf{z}+\sin(2\pi/3)\mathbf{x^{\prime}_{i}}

\mathbf{y_{c_{i,\alpha}}}=\cos(4\pi/3)\mathbf{z}+\sin(4\pi/3)\mathbf{x^{\prime}_{i}}

\mathbf{y_{a_{i,\alpha}}}=\mathbf{z}\quad\text{ and }\mathbf{y_{b_{i,\alpha}}}=-\mathbf{z}.

This assignment achieves the maximum possible
9/4
for each triangle
i,a_{i,\alpha},c_{i,\alpha}
. The expander graph includes
4\eta m
vertices in total, and so
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}
achieves the maximum of
\lambda_{\max}\eta m
for the expander graph.
For the edges in
G^{\prime}
corresponding to the original edges of
G
:

\frac{1}{2}[1-\mathbf{y_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{y_{j}}]=\frac{1}{2}[1-\cos^{2}(2\pi/3)-\sin^{2}(2\pi/3)\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{j}}]=\frac{3}{4}\times\frac{1}{2}[1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{j}}].

So in total:

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{9}{4}2\eta m+\lambda_{\max}\eta m+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}).

Noting that
\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})\geq F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})
and
F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)
, we have

\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k+1}(G^{\prime})\geq C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}\textsc{Max-Cut}_{k}(G)

where
C=9/2+\lambda_{\max}
.

The remainder of the proof focuses on part 2. of Lemma
1
. Note that we reuse the variable names
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{z}
, although they are not the same as in part 1.
We start by describing the map
g:(S_{k})^{n+6\eta m}\rightarrow(S_{k-1})^{n}
which maps inputs for
F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}
to inputs for
F_{k}^{G}
.

Let
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}}\in(S_{k})^{n+6\eta m}
and set
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{a\in A}\mathbf{y_{a}}-\sum_{b\in B}\mathbf{y_{b}}/\norm{\sum_{a \in A} \vec{y_{a}}-\sum_{b\in B} \vec{y_{b}}}
.
Now change basis so that
\mathbf{z}=(0,0,\dots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
. For each
i\in V
, let
\mathbf{x_{i}}
be the unit vector you get by taking the first
k
entries of
\mathbf{y_{i}}
in this basis and renormalising.
Let
g(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\dots\mathbf{x_{n}})
.

We write
\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
for the embedding of
\mathbf{x}
into
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
by padding with an extra zero; or equivalently
\mathbf{x_{i}^{\prime}}
is the (renormalised) projection of
\mathbf{y_{i}}
onto the space orthogonal to
\mathbf{z}
.
Since
\mathbf{y_{i}}
,
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x_{i}^{\prime}}
are all unit vectors for all
i\in V
, we have:

\mathbf{y_{i}}=\cos(\theta_i)\mathbf{z}+\sin(\theta_i)\mathbf{x^{\prime}_{i}}

for some angle
\theta_{i}
between
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{y_{i}}
.

We can now bound the contribution from different parts of
G^{\prime}
. First consider the expander graph across
A
and
B
.
Let
\epsilon_{i,\alpha}
be the angle between
\mathbf{y_{a_{i,\alpha}}}
and
\mathbf{z}
.
By Lemma
3
, the contribution to
F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})
from the bipartite graph is at most

\lambda_{\max}\eta m-\frac{\Delta}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{i,\alpha}\epsilon_{i,\alpha}^{2}.

Second, consider the triangle gadgets. The angle between
\mathbf{y_{i}}
and
\mathbf{y_{a_{i,\alpha}}}
is in
[\theta_{i}-\epsilon_{i,\alpha},\theta_{i}+\epsilon_{i,\alpha}]
. Let
\phi_{i}=\frac{2\pi}{3}-\theta_{i}
so that by Lemma
2
, the contribution from the triangle
i,a_{i,\alpha},c_{i,\alpha}
is at most

\frac{9}{4}-\frac{9}{16\pi^{2}}(\phi_{i}+\epsilon_{i,\alpha})^{2}.

Third, consider the original edges from
G
between the vertices in
V
. For an edge
ij\in E
, if
\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}=2\pi/3
(i.e.
\phi_{i}=0=\phi_{j}
), then the contribution of that edge to
F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})
is

\frac{3}{8}(1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{i}}).

If
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{j}
are non-zero, this can change the angle
\xi_{i}
between
\mathbf{y_{i}}
and
\mathbf{y_{j}}
by at most
|\phi_{i}|+|\phi_{j}|
.
Since
\mathbf{y_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{y_{j}}=\cos(\xi_i)
, the rate of change with respect to
\xi_{i}
is at most
|\frac{d}{d{\xi_{i}}}\cos(\xi_i)|=|\sin(\xi_i)|\leq 1
.
Therefore the edge
ij
can contribute at most

\frac{3}{8}(1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{i}})+|\phi_{i}|+|\phi_{j}|.

Adding all these contributions together gives:

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\leq\lambda_{\max}\eta m-\frac{\Delta}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{i,\alpha}\epsilon_{i,\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{i,\alpha}\left(\frac{9}{4}-\frac{9}{16\pi^{2}}(\phi_{i}+\epsilon_{i,\alpha})^{2}\right)

\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{ij\in E}\left(\frac{3}{8}(1-\mathbf{x_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{i}})+|\phi_{i}|+|\phi_{j}|\right)

\qquad\qquad=C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})-\sum_{i,\alpha}\left(c_{1}\epsilon_{i,\alpha}^{2}+c_{2}(\phi_{i}+\epsilon_{i,\alpha})^{2}\right)+\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}|\phi_{i}|

where
C=9/2+\lambda_{\max}
,
c_{1}=\frac{\Delta}{4\pi^{2}}
and
c_{2}=\frac{9}{16\pi^{2}}
.
If the bipartite expander graph is chosen to be low degree and with good spectral expansion such that
\lambda_{\max}
and
\Delta
are constant, then
C,c_{1},c_{2}
are all constant.

Looking at the terms with
\epsilon_{i,\alpha_{i}}
and rearranging we have

c_{1}\epsilon_{i,\alpha}^{2}+c_{2}(\phi_{i}+\epsilon_{i,\alpha})^{2}=(c_{1}+c_{2})(\epsilon_{i,\alpha}+\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}+c_{2}}\phi_{i})^{2}+\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{c_{1}+c_{2}}\phi_{i}^{2}\geq c^{\prime}\phi_{i}^{2}

where
c^{\prime}=\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{c_{1}+c_{2}}
. Substituting this back in and summing over
\alpha
, we get:

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\leq C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}(-\eta c^{\prime}\phi_{i}^{2}+|\phi_{i}|).

To upper bound
-\eta c^{\prime}\phi_{i}^{2}+|\phi_{i}|
, note that it is symmetric about zero, and for
\phi_{i}\geq 0
, one can complete the square
-\eta c^{\prime}\phi_{i}^{2}+\phi_{i}=-\eta c^{\prime}(\phi_{i}-\frac{1}{2\eta c^{\prime}})^{2}+\frac{1}{4\eta c^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{4\eta c^{\prime}}
.
Putting this back in leads to

F_{k+1}^{G^{\prime}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{y}})\leq C\eta m+\frac{3}{4}F_{k}^{G}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2c^{\prime}\eta}m.

∎
