Bridging the diagnostic gap: diagnostic capacity and pediatric quality of care in LMICs
Authors: Dan Wang, Zhaoyu Li, Mengyao Li, Zhongliang Zhou, Wenhua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChild mortality remains a major health burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where adherence to pediatric clinical guidelines is often suboptimal. Diagnostic capacity, the availability of essential laboratory and imaging tests, is a key structural input for delivering high-quality pediatric care, yet its relationship with quality of care has received limited empirical attention. This study examines the association between diagnostic capacity and adherence to evidence-based pediatric care guidelines across eight LMICs.MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative Service Provision Assessment (SPA) data from 5311 health facilities across eight LMICs (Afghanistan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, Haiti, Malawi, Nepal, Senegal, and Tanzania) collected between 2014 and 2022. Quality of care was assessed using a 10-item adherence index derived from direct observations of outpatient consultations, aligned with the WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) protocol. Diagnostic capacity was measured using a 16-item index reflecting the availability of essential laboratory and imaging tests recommended in the WHO Essential Diagnostics List. Associations between diagnostic capacity and adherence to clinical guidelines were examined using multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear models, adjusting for facility-, provider-, and patient-level covariates.ResultsDiagnostic capacity varied substantially across countries and facility levels. Among primary care facilities, median diagnostic availability ranged from 20.1% in Malawi to 72.4% in Senegal; among hospitals, from 64.7% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo to 88.5% in Ethiopia. On a 10-point scale, pediatric care quality scores were lowest in Afghanistan (3.45) and highest in DR Congo (4.89). Higher diagnostic capacity was significantly associated with greater adherence to pediatric clinical guidelines (adjusted log-odds β = 0.138; 95% CI: 0.061–0.216; p < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses using alternative outcome definitions, exposure measures, subgroup classifications, and model specifications yielded consistent results.ConclusionsThis multi-country observational study identifies a positive association between diagnostic readiness and the quality of pediatric care in LMICs. These findings suggest that diagnostic capacity may serve as an enabling structural condition for delivering higher-quality pediatric care in LMICs, reinforcing the role of diagnostics within health system strengthening strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04517-w.

Background

Preventable conditions such as pneumonia, diarrhea, and malaria remain the leading causes of pediatric mortality globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Reducing child mortality requires not only expanded access to healthcare services but also improvement in the quality of care, especially adherence to evidence-based pediatric clinical guidelines [2].

Diagnostic capacity, defined as the availability and functionality of essential diagnostic tools, is a key component of high-quality pediatric care [3]. Previous evidence has shown that adequate diagnostics are associated with more timely and accurate disease identification, which may be associated with reduced reliance on syndromic management and greater adherence to guidelines [4]. However, diagnostic capacity remains limited in many LMICs, particularly at the primary care level. Facilities frequently lack tools such as malaria microscopy, hemoglobin testing, and basic imaging, and many operate without the minimal infrastructure required for effective case management [5, 6]. Data from the Service Provision Assessment (SPA) surveys have described these systemic limitations across countries and health system levels [7].

To address this challenge, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Essential Diagnostics List (EDL), aimed at improving diagnostic access and guiding national and global investment priorities [8]. Despite this initiative, notable disparities in diagnostic availability persist across facility levels. Tertiary hospitals tend to be better equipped, while primary care providers often rely on syndromic approaches or empirical treatments [9]. Limited diagnostic infrastructure has been identified as a potential constraint on the delivery of care that adheres to established clinical protocols in LMIC settings [10].

The Donabedian model offers a widely used framework for evaluating quality of care by distinguishing between structure, process, and outcome domains [11]. Within this framework, diagnostic capacity constitutes a critical structural input that directly influences adherence to clinical protocols [12]. Facilities with greater diagnostic availability are expected to demonstrate better adherence to pediatric care standards, such as those defined in the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) guidelines, which emphasize history-taking, physical examination, and caregiver counseling [13]. In contrast, limited access to diagnostics may contribute to incomplete assessments and deviations from evidence-based practices [14, 15].

While global policy attention to diagnostics has increased following the introduction of the WHO EDL and advocacy for improved pediatric care, empirical evidence linking diagnostic capacity to clinical quality remains scarce [9]. One multicounty study using SPA data from ten LMICs assessed the availability of 16 EDL-aligned diagnostic tests and documented substantial variation, ranging from 14.9% in Bangladesh to 89.6% in Namibia [7]. However, this study focused on country-level characteristics such as income and population density, without evaluating associations with service delivery quality.

Although structural readiness has been examined in relation to healthcare quality in previous multicountry studies [2, 16–18], diagnostic capacity is rarely analyzed as a distinct dimension. Prior research, including work by Leslie et al. and Kruk et al., typically includes diagnostics within broader indices of facility readiness or equipment availability [18], making it difficult to isolate their specific contributions. Additionally, readiness frameworks such as SARA and SPA often rely on narrow tracer indicators, such as malaria rapid diagnostic tests or hemoglobin tests [17]. While useful for benchmarking, these indicators do not capture the broader range of diagnostics required for managing prevalent childhood illnesses like pneumonia, anemia, and urinary tract infections, which collectively account for a significant share of under-five mortality [19].

Several country-specific studies from settings such as Malawi and Bangladesh have explored associations between diagnostic availability and adherence to IMCI protocols [20–25]. However, findings have been mixed, and the literature is limited by variations in study design, inconsistent definitions of diagnostic capacity, and lack of standardized measurement tools. As a result, there is limited comparative evidence assessing whether facilities with broader diagnostic availability demonstrate higher quality of pediatric clinical care across different LMIC settings.

To address this gap, we conducted a multicountry analysis using nationally representative SPA data from eight LMICs. A harmonized diagnostic capacity index was developed based on WHO EDL criteria and applied across facility levels. Pediatric quality of care was measured using a validated ten-item IMCI-aligned process index derived from observed clinical encounters. By linking diagnostic capacity to directly observed provider performance, this study aims to generate robust cross-national evidence on the relationship between diagnostic readiness and pediatric care quality in LMIC contexts.

Methods

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study analyzed nationally representative SPA surveys conducted between 2014 and 2022 in eight LMICs [26]: Afghanistan (2019), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2018), Ethiopia (2022), Haiti (2018), Malawi (2014), Nepal (2021), Senegal (2019), and Tanzania (2015). Developed by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), SPA surveys collect standardized data on facility readiness, service delivery, and quality of care using facility inventories, provider interviews, and direct clinical observations [27].

Countries were selected based on predefined criteria to ensure internal validity and measurement comparability. Eligible surveys were required to (i) be nationally representative with standard SPA sampling; and (ii) include all three core modules (facility inventory, provider interviews, and observed sick-child consultations) within the same wave. To ensure contemporary relevance, only the most recent survey from the past decade was included for each country. Bangladesh (2017) was excluded due to the absence of direct observation data.

The final analytic sample consisted of 5311 healthcare facilities that provided pediatric care. Survey sampling weights were applied to maintain national representativeness and enable robust cross-country comparisons of diagnostic capacity and quality of care.

Measures and variables

The primary outcome, quality of care, was measured using a composite adherence score reflecting compliance with evidence-based pediatric care guidelines. The score was based on ten binary items aligned with the WHO IMCI protocol and the pediatric clinical quality literature [28, 29]. These items captured three clinical domains: history-taking, physical examination, and caregiver counseling (see Additional File 1: Table S1). Components included assessment of feeding and respiratory symptoms, hydration status, weight measurement, and caregiver education on danger signs and follow-up care. Each completed task was scored 1 point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 10. All items were equally weighted, and facility-level mean scores were computed to represent average performance.

The primary exposure, diagnostic capacity, was defined as the availability and functionality of 16 essential diagnostic tests. These were selected based on the WHO Essential Diagnostics List and the Interagency List of Priority Medical Devices for Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health [8, 30], prioritizing diagnostics for conditions with high pediatric mortality (e.g., malaria, anemia) and those recommended for use at primary and referral levels.

To reflect health system stratification, diagnostic availability was assessed separately for primary and hospital-level facilities. For primary care, the index included nine items: HIV testing, urine glucose, urine protein, glucometer, hemoglobin, malaria testing, syphilis testing, microscopy, and ultrasound. For hospitals, seven additional items were included: X-ray, CT scan, chlamydia testing, tuberculosis testing, gram stain, chemical analysis, and heme analyzer. Hemoglobin and glucometer tests were excluded from the hospital index, as these functions are typically integrated into broader analyzers.

A diagnostic was considered “available” if equipment was both present and functional. For off-site services (e.g., tuberculosis testing), the existence of a specimen referral system was considered sufficient. Missing values were coded as unavailability, in line with established practices in SPA-based research [7]. The index was constructed uniformly across countries using harmonized definitions. Full item mapping is provided in Additional File 1: Tables S2 [13, 31–44] and S3.

Covariates

Potential confounders and effect modifiers were selected based on the Donabedian framework and prior literature on quality of care in LMICs [27]. Facility-level covariates included ownership (public vs. private) and geographical location (urban vs. rural), reflecting structural and resource-related differences. Provider-level characteristics included professional cadre (physician, nurse, or other), as well as years of experience, capturing variations in clinical expertise. Client-level factors included caregiver age, caregiver-child relationship (mother vs. non-mother), and child characteristics (age in months, gender), recognizing the potential influence of demographic and caregiving dynamics on care quality.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using survey weights. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as weighted proportions with 95% confidence intervals. Diagnostic availability at the country level was estimated as the median of all test-specific availabilities within each facility tier. Within-country variability was evaluated using interquartile ranges (IQRs) and quartile coefficients of dispersion (QCD).

Multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear models with a binomial distribution and logit link were used to examine the association between diagnostic capacity and pediatric care quality. Random intercepts at the facility level were included to account for clustering. Three models were estimated: (i) an unadjusted model; (ii) a model adjusted for country fixed effects to control for unobserved country-level heterogeneity; and (iii) a fully adjusted model including client-, provider-, and facility-level covariates.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the analytical findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses across four domains: outcome definition, exposure construction, facility classification, and model specification. First, to evaluate the influence of outcome measurement, we replaced the primary 10-item pediatric care index with (i) an expanded 20-item index that included additional diagnostic and management actions (e.g., auscultation, referral; Additional file 1: Table S1); (ii) domain-weighted and principal component analysis (PCA)-weighted versions of the original index (Additional file 1: Fig. S1); and (iii) a binary indicator of provider-reported adherence to under-five clinical guidelines.

Second, to examine alternative constructions of diagnostic capacity, we tested a 3-item WHO SARA-based index (malaria testing, hemoglobin testing, and microscopy) and a 12-item version that excluded advanced technologies such as CT and chemical analyzers.

Third, to explore heterogeneity by facility type, we applied two classification schemes: (i) a three-tier typology (basic primary, advanced primary, and hospital) aligned with WHO EDL, and (ii) a binary categorization based on cesarean section capability.

Fourth, all models were re-estimated using generalized linear models with survey weights and weighted binomial regressions including country fixed effects (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Additional model variants tested for non-linearity using quadratic and piecewise specifications (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

Lastly, to examine consistency across settings and clinical tasks, we conducted supplementary analyses: country-stratified models to assess setting-specific patterns, leave-one-country-out analyses to examine the influence of individual countries, and task-level models disaggregating consultations into binary clinical actions.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18, and data visualizations were created using Python version 3.13 to ensure reproducibility and transparency.

Child demographic characteristics were similar across countries, with mean age ranging from 17.23 months (SD: 15.49) in Afghanistan to 22.58 months (SD: 15.58) in Nepal. The overall sex distribution was balanced, with boys representing 52.91% of the sample. Most children were accompanied by their mothers, although this proportion ranged from 66.36% in Afghanistan to 89.20% in Senegal.

Provider profiles varied substantially. In Afghanistan, all observed visits were conducted by physicians (100%), whereas in Malawi, only 2.85% were. Nurse- or non-physician-led visits predominated in Congo (67.11%) and Malawi (80.66%). The average years of provider experience ranged from 4.82 (SD: 5.30) in Nepal to 6.99 (SD: 7.80) in Haiti. Weekly working hours were highest in Senegal (64.42 h, SD: 20.53) and Malawi (60.97 h, SD: 21.62), and lowest in Haiti (35.82 h, SD: 15.35).

Facility-level characteristics also revealed notable differences. Government ownership was predominant in Ethiopia (71.57%) and Nepal (94.12%), whereas private ownership was more common in Afghanistan (87.95%) and Haiti (65.42%). Urban facilities dominated in Senegal (100.00%) and Haiti (69.23%), while rural settings were more common in Congo (81.14%) and Malawi (75.81%). Across all countries, the majority of visits took place in primary care settings, ranging from 51.81% in Afghanistan to 96.26% in Senegal.

Diagnostic availability was consistently higher in hospital settings, ranging from 64.68% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo to 88.47% in Ethiopia. Ethiopia and Tanzania reported the highest availability (88.47% and 85.33%, respectively), while Malawi (69.37%) and Senegal (68.89%) were among the lowest. Within-country variability was again most pronounced in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (IQR: 30.54%) and least in Ethiopia (IQR: 10.76%).

Across both facility types, point-of-care diagnostics were more commonly available than laboratory-based or imaging services. Malaria testing coverage was consistently high (e.g., 93.0% in Senegal, 96.9% in Afghanistan for primary care; 97.6% in Ethiopia, 93.2% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo for hospitals). HIV testing availability exceeded 60% in several countries, including Afghanistan and Haiti.

In contrast, availability of laboratory-based diagnostics such as tuberculosis testing (ranging from 55.2% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo to 97.6% in Ethiopia) and chemical analysis (ranging from 34.6% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo to 87.1% in Nepal) was more variable. Imaging modalities, particularly X-ray and ultrasound, were predominantly concentrated in hospital settings, with the highest availability in Ethiopia (X-ray: 96.9%) and Tanzania (ultrasound: 97.1%).

Multilevel regression analysis of diagnostic capacity and quality of care

In the unadjusted model, higher diagnostic capacity was significantly associated with improved quality of care (β = 0.240; 95% CI: 0.176–0.303; p < 0.001). This association remained robust after adjusting for country-level fixed effects (β = 0.183; 95% CI: 0.118–0.248; p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, which controlled for patient-, provider-, and facility-level covariates, the association attenuated but remained statistically significant (β = 0.138; 95% CI: 0.061–0.216; p < 0.001).

Several covariates were also associated with care quality. Compared to physicians, consultations conducted by nurses were associated with significantly lower quality (β = − 0.072; 95% CI: − 0.130 to − 0.014; p < 0.05). Similarly, children accompanied by non-mother caregivers received lower-quality care (β = − 0.042; 95% CI: − 0.071 to − 0.013; p < 0.01). Provider-level support was positively associated with quality (β = 0.112; 95% CI: 0.074–0.149; p < 0.001). Facilities that were private (vs. public) or located in rural areas (vs. urban) also exhibited higher quality scores (β = 0.084 and β = 0.124, respectively; both p < 0.001). Facility type (hospital vs. primary care) was not significantly associated with care quality (β = 0.006; 95% CI: − 0.041 to 0.053; p = 0.798).

Sensitivity analyses

Findings were generally consistent across all sensitivity specifications (Fig. 3). Expanding the outcome to a 20-item process quality index yielded a substantially larger average marginal effect (AME = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.40–1.51), suggesting that broader measurement may strengthen the observed association. Substituting the outcome with a binary provider-reported indicator of guideline adherence produced a smaller yet statistically significant effect (AME = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.10–0.23). Discussion

This study examined the association between diagnostic capacity and the quality of pediatric care across 5311 health facilities in eight LMICs. Descriptive results indicated considerable variability in diagnostic availability across countries and facility types. Despite persistently low adherence to evidence-based pediatric protocols, higher diagnostic readiness was consistently associated with better clinical quality across multiple model specifications and sensitivity checks.

Comparison with existing evidence

Previous research has documented widespread deficiencies in pediatric care quality in LMICs, even in contexts where standardized protocols such as the IMCI have been introduced [45]. Structural readiness, often defined by the availability of infrastructure, equipment, and human resources, has been explored in relation to service delivery performance [46]. However, few studies have focused specifically on diagnostic capacity as a distinct construct within structural readiness. Prior multi-country analyses, including those by Leslie et al. and Carter et al., have highlighted the importance of structural-process linkages, but have rarely operationalized diagnostics as a standalone domain [17, 18].

Existing service readiness indices, such as those embedded within the Service Provision Assessment (SPA), include only a limited number of diagnostic items relevant to pediatric care. These typically cover malaria testing, hemoglobin, and stool examination [17]. Although some single‑country studies have examined associations between diagnostic availability and IMCI implementation, these analyses often relied on narrow tracer indicators and lacked standardized measurement frameworks [21, 47].

To address this gap, the present study developed a harmonized 16-item diagnostic index based on the WHO Essential Diagnostics List and aligned with pediatric clinical priorities. This approach introduces a standardized and policy-relevant method for measuring diagnostic capacity across LMICs. By relating these indicators to directly observed clinical quality outcomes, the analysis illustrates how structural inputs are associated with clinical processes in real-world settings.

Cross-country and facility-level variation

While the overall association between diagnostic capacity and clinical quality was positive, patterns varied across countries and facility types. For example, countries such as Senegal and Afghanistan exhibited relatively high diagnostic readiness but did not consistently show strong associations with clinical quality. In contrast, countries with lower diagnostic scores sometimes demonstrated stronger associations. These variations may reflect differences in how diagnostics are integrated into care pathways, provider familiarity with diagnostic procedures, or broader facility-level accountability mechanisms [9, 48].

These findings are in line with prior research from Uganda, where investments in diagnostic infrastructure were associated with service quality only in contexts characterized by additional systems such as provider training, supportive supervision, and workflow integration [45]. Together, these findings suggest that diagnostic availability alone may not be consistently associated with improved care quality, and that such associations may be influenced by broader contextual or organizational factors [49, 50].

Contextual correlates of quality of care

In addition to diagnostic capacity, several contextual factors were associated with quality-of-care outcomes. Consultations involving caregivers other than mothers were associated with lower adherence to clinical guidelines. This pattern may be influenced by differences in communication dynamics, symptom recall, or caregiver familiarity with the child’s condition [51–54].

Provider cadre also emerged as a relevant factor. Nurses and non-physician providers generally exhibited lower adherence to clinical protocols than physicians, consistent with prior research suggesting variation in clinical performance by provider type [55]. These differences may reflect disparities in clinical training, access to continuing education, or decision-making authority. Furthermore, facilities characterized by stronger internal support systems, such as routine supervision or professional development opportunities, tended to report higher quality scores. These findings are aligned with broader evidence on the role of workplace environment in shaping service delivery quality [56].

Geographic and institutional characteristics also played a role. Some models suggested that private and rural facilities had relatively better performance compared to public and urban counterparts. However, such patterns varied by country and may be influenced by sector-specific governance, incentive structures, or resource allocation practices [57–59]. These observations underscore the need to account for facility-level heterogeneity when designing interventions to improve quality [2].

Interpreting diagnostic capacity–quality relationships

Diagnostic capacity is a fundamental component of high-quality service delivery, as it is closely linked to providers’ capacity to classify illness severity, determine appropriate management, and avoid misdiagnosis-related harms [9]. While this study does not establish causality, the analysis identified a consistent positive relationship between diagnostic readiness and adherence to pediatric care guidelines across multiple model specifications [60]. Syndromic protocols such as IMCI require accurate classification of childhood illnesses, which may be facilitated by the availability of simple point-of-care tests. Diagnostic tools used in the classification of anemia or febrile illness may be linked to providers’ ability to select appropriate management strategies [15, 61, 62].

Nevertheless, prior studies indicate that diagnostic tools are not always used as intended. Evidence from Malawi and Uganda suggests that diagnostics may be bypassed due to low provider confidence, perceived test inaccuracy, or time constraints [21, 45, 63]. Interventions aimed at improving diagnostic use have emphasized the importance of supportive supervision, algorithm-based decision aids, and digital health integration [64]. Future research should further explore how diagnostics function as part of a broader quality ecosystem involving health workforce capacity, health information systems, and accountability structures.

Contributions and policy relevance

This study adds to the existing literature by introducing a harmonized diagnostic index that can be applied across countries and used to benchmark structural readiness in pediatric care. By focusing specifically on diagnostics, it provides more granular insight into structural components of service delivery and their associations with observed quality. These findings may inform national and global initiatives seeking to improve child health outcomes through more targeted investments in health facility capacity.

From a policy perspective, these findings may inform more deliberate integration of diagnostic capacity within broader quality improvement frameworks. Ensuring the availability and appropriate use of essential diagnostics may be associated with fewer observed gaps in care quality. Policymakers may also consider strategies that promote the integration of diagnostics into service delivery platforms, with attention to context-specific implementation challenges.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the SPA data precludes any inference regarding causal relationships. Although the models adjusted for a comprehensive set of facility-, provider-, and client-level characteristics, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. Second, while the diagnostic index was developed using internationally relevant tools aligned with the WHO Essential Diagnostics List, several items are applicable to both adult and pediatric populations. Their inclusion reflects the integrated nature of diagnostic services in primary care settings but may reduce the specificity of the index for pediatric care. Third, the SPA inventory records the presence of diagnostic equipment but does not verify functionality, availability at the time of care, or actual utilization, which may introduce non-differential measurement error and potentially bias estimates toward the null. Finally, the models could not account for country-specific governance, financing structures, or supervision systems that may shape structure–process relationships and potentially confound observed associations.

Conclusions

This multi-country observational study identifies a statistically significant and policy-relevant association between diagnostic readiness and the quality of pediatric care in LMICs. By introducing a standardized diagnostic capacity index aligned with the WHO Essential Diagnostics List and linking it to directly observed IMCI-based care practices, this study contributes empirical insight into structure–process relationships in pediatric service delivery. While the findings advance understanding of diagnostic capacity as a structural input for high-quality care, further longitudinal and interventional research is needed to elucidate causal pathways and inform implementation strategies.
