Contribution of point of care ultrasound for ruling out small bowel obstruction in emergency departments: study protocol of the diagnostic, observational, prospective and multicentre OCCLUS-POCUS study
Authors: Mathilde Papin, Quentin Le Bastard, Maëva Saignes, Aurélie Le Thuaut, Philippe Le Conte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbdominal pain is a common presentation in emergency departments. Among these cases, small bowel obstruction (SBO) is a common diagnosis. Diagnostic procedure is currently based on CT, an imaging modality associated with radiation exposure. Studies suggest good accuracy of point of care ultrasound (POCUS) for SBO. Through the OCCLUS-POCUS study, we aim to evaluate the POCUS ability to rule out SBO in patients with low to moderate clinical suspicion, thereby avoiding unnecessary CT scans.Methods and analysisThis prospective, observational and multicentre study will involve 15 French emergency departments. Eligible patients will be adults presenting with low or moderate suspicion of SBO (based on Gestalt clinical probability). Exclusion criteria will include prior imaging confirmed SBO, pregnancy or breastfeeding. POCUS will be performed by emergency physicians prior to CT scans, which serve as the diagnostic gold standard. The primary endpoint will be the negative predictive value (NPV) of POCUS for SBO. Secondary endpoints will include POCUS diagnostic accuracy, time intervals between POCUS and CT and factors associated with false-positive or false-negative results. Statistical analyses will estimate the NPV and other diagnostic parameters with 95% CIs. While CT remains the gold standard for diagnosing SBO, this study seeks to validate POCUS as a reliable method for ruling out SBO in low-risk cases, potentially reducing radiation exposure, costs and emergency department length of stay.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was obtained from the relevant committee (V1.1 approved on 6 February 2025 by “Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord Ouest 2” – SI24.05744.000388 – 2024-A01942-45). Introduction

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is a frequent diagnosis in emergency medicine. The current diagnostic strategy relies on abdominal CT, which is also necessary to guide therapeutic decisions, including medical and surgical interventions. However, CT use is associated with drawbacks, such as radiation exposure, increased costs and prolonged emergency department length of stay.1

A recent meta-analysis showed that point of care ultrasound (POCUS) had a good diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 83% (95%CI 71.7% to 90.4%)), specificity 93% (95%CI 55.3% to 99.3%)).2 Another meta-analysis found rather similar results (sensitivity 92.4% (95% CI 89.0% to 94.7%), specificity 96.6% (95% CI 88.4% to 99.1%)).3 A recent prospective observational study found that CT ruled out SBO in 45% of patients (95% CI 37 to 53%). This study integrated Gestalt pretest probability assessments by emergency physicians, classifying patients into low, moderate or high risk for SBO. In patients with low or moderate Gestalt probability, the authors found that this combined strategy had a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI 88 to 100) and negative predictive value (NPV) 100% (92–100%). However, the lower margin of the sensitivity was only 88%.4

By focusing on patients with a low or moderate Gestalt clinical probability and increasing the number of patients included, we intend to demonstrate that POCUS could reliably exclude SBO in this population. This would avoid unnecessary CT and thus lower costs, ED length of stay and hospital radiologists’ workload.5

In this study, POCUS will be followed by a CT (gold standard). This CT will be performed and interpreted blind to the POCUS results. The main objective will be the ability of POCUS to rule-out SBO in patients with low or moderate Gestalt clinical probability.

Methods

Study design

This will be a prospective, observational, multicentre study conducted across 15 French hospitals, including university and non-university settings, to extend the external validity of the results.

Objectives

Evaluate the ability of POCUS to rule out SBO in low-to-moderate risk patients.

Participants

Potentially eligible patients are individuals presenting to the emergency department with abdominal symptoms compatible with small bowel obstruction. Given that the performance of ultrasound examinations depends on the availability of trained physicians at the time of patient presentation, the sample will be a convenience sample. Patients will be screened according to the clinical data present in the electronic medical file. The patient’s treating physician can therefore verify these clinical data. For eligible patients, information on the research protocol will be provided. The patient’s non-opposition will then be collected after an opportunity to ask any questions and, if desired, to take the time needed for reflection.

Intervention

Following collection of non-opposition, patients will undergo a POCUS exam conducted by trained emergency physicians using curvilinear probes in B-mode. Validated training in clinical ultrasound includes either emergency physicians certified since 2021—who receive comprehensive training in clinical ultrasound as part of the emergency medicine residency curriculum or physicians who have completed a recognised supplementary POCUS training programme. As an extension of the clinical examination, the practitioner performing POCUS will have access to the patient’s clinical history and findings.

The practitioner will perform an ultrasound examination aimed at systematically assessing each abdominal region. The following standard views are typically described, depending on patient accessibility, echogenicity and haemodynamic and respiratory stability: a sagittal epigastric, an oblique right and left hypochondrial view, a transverse and sagittal hypogastric view, a transverse view of the right and left iliac fossa, a transverse view of the anterior abdominal wall.

Findings suggestive of SBO include dilated, incompressible, fluid-filled intestinal loops (≥25 mm) with back-and-forth fluid movements. These features suggestive of SBO will be assessed across all regions of the abdomen. The absence of these findings across all abdominal regions will classify the ultrasound as negative. Practitioners will have the option to classify ultrasound findings as ‘indeterminate’, thereby enabling a more refined analysis of both positive and negative POCUS results. Beyond the assessment of SBO-specific features, practitioners will also report any incidental findings suggestive of alternative diagnoses encountered during the examination, reflecting the broader diagnostic scope of point-of-care ultrasound in the context of undifferentiated abdominal pain. Such alternative diagnoses may include but are not limited to biliary tract pathology, urological conditions, gynaecological pathology or findings suggestive of hollow organ perforation or haemoperitoneum.

A CT scan will follow and will be the diagnostic gold standard. The CT scan will be performed after the POCUS and interpreted blind to its results. The CT scan acquisition protocol will be at the discretion of the radiologist; the use of contrast enhancement and its timing will depend on the diagnostic hypotheses and on the findings from the non-contrast acquisition. In addition to confirming or ruling out SBO, the radiologist will provide a comprehensive interpretation of all imaging findings, systematically identifying any alternative or concomitant diagnoses.

Sample size calculation

Based on prior studies, achieving an NPV of 98% with ±2% precision (ie, being sure to exclude the diagnosis of SBO in 98% of cases) requires 189 negative POCUS cases. To account for a 30% SBO prevalence and 5% attrition, the study aims to enrol 667 patients. Nevertheless, we would like to see a similarly high sensitivity (98%±2%). So, 189 patients with a positive CT would be required. With the addition of a 5% attrition rate, enrolment would be stopped after the inclusion of 200 positive scans, ie, around 667 patients based on an assumption of 30% SBO diagnosis by CT.

Statistical analysis

Primary and secondary outcomes will be analysed using descriptive statistics and logistic regression models. CIs (95%) will accompany all diagnostic accuracy metrics. No imputation will be performed on missing data.

Monitoring

Monitoring will be carried out by the Promotion Department of the Research Division. A Clinical Research Associate will regularly visit each site to check the quality of the data reported in the case report forms.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval was granted by the relevant committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes 02/06/2025). Results will be published in an international Emergency Medicine journal, and raw data will be available on reasonable request.

Discussion

This study has several methodological strengths, including its multicentre, nationwide design, which supports good external validity within healthcare settings operating under systems similar to the French model. It also relies on a validated reference standard and evaluates an intervention that is routinely performed at the bedside, thereby reflecting real-world clinical practice. The prospective observational design minimises the risk of recall bias and ensures systematic data collection across all participating sites. The inclusion of both university and community hospitals further enhances the representativeness of the sample, capturing a broad spectrum of clinical settings and environments. Additionally, the blinded interpretation of CT scans from POCUS findings reduces the risk of bias and strengthens the validity of the diagnostic accuracy. Finally, the collection of data on CT prescription forms, including reasons for requesting CT despite a negative POCUS result, will provide valuable insights on the real-life barriers against POCUS-guided clinical decision processes.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged

Convenience sampling: Like a majority of diagnostic studies conducted in emergency settings, patients will be enrolled on a convenience basis. This reflects the inherent constraints of real-world clinical practice. While this approach may introduce some degree of selection bias—particularly related to the availability of POCUS-trained physicians—it also ensures that the study closely mirrors routine emergency care. The multicentre design, spanning 15 hospitals with varying levels of resources and patient volumes, intends to mitigate the potential impact of this limitation on the generalisability of the results.

Operator experience: The definition of operator training adopted in this study was deliberately designed to reflect the current landscape of POCUS practice in French emergency departments. Two types of ultrasound training are represented: recently certified emergency physicians with included ultrasound training and experienced physicians with supplementary ultrasound training. Although this may introduce some variability in individual performance, it allows the results to be interpreted in a pragmatic context representative of real-life implementation. Furthermore, the planned analysis of characteristics associated with false-negative and false-positive findings will provide valuable insights into the potential influence of operator-related factors on diagnostic accuracy.

Generalisability beyond the French context: The present study will be conducted across a broad range of French emergency departments, including both university and community hospitals, with the explicit goal of maximising the external validity of the findings. While differences in training curricula, certification standards and institutional protocols may limit direct extrapolation to other healthcare systems, the results are expected to provide a robust methodological framework

The implications of this study for patients are significant: if the diagnostic performance of POCUS proves sufficient to safely rule out small bowel obstruction, patients could benefit from a less irradiating procedure and a reduced length of stay. At the healthcare system level, favourable results could translate into decreased costs and shorter hospital stays.
