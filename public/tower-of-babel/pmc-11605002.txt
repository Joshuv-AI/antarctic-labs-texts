Efficient removal of crystal violet and acid red 88 dyes from aqueous environments using easily synthesized copper ferrite nanoparticles
Authors: Asma S. Al-Wasidi, Ehab A. Abdelrahman, Khalil ur Rehman, Fawaz A. Saad, Alaa M. Munshi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study details the synthesis and application of magnetic copper ferrite (CuFe2O4) nanoparticles for the efficient removal of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes from aqueous solutions. Utilizing a combustion method, nanoparticles were synthesized with succinic and malic acids as fuels, yielding samples with crystallite sizes of 28.54 ± 0.90 nm for the sample synthesized with malic acid and 19.79 ± 0.75 nm for the sample synthesized with succinic acid. Optimum removal conditions were found at a pH of 2 for acid red 88 and pH 10 for crystal violet, with a contact time of 80 min and an adsorbent dosage of 0.05 g in a 100 mL solution. Under these conditions, the sample synthesized using succinic acid achieved sorptive capacities of 452.49 mg/g for acid red 88 and 446.43 mg/g for crystal violet, while the sample synthesized using malic acid reached 408.16 mg/g and 374.53 mg/g, respectively. Both adsorption processes followed the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and aligned with the Langmuir isotherm. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed the process as exothermic and spontaneous. Practical trials demonstrated removal efficiencies above 95% for both dyes in real wastewater samples, underscoring the method’s practical potential in water purification applications.

Introduction

Industrial processes, particularly those in the textile, paper, and leather industries, are primary contributors to water contamination by organic dyes1–3. These industries often use large volumes of synthetic dyes, which are not fully retained in the final products and are discharged into water bodies through wastewater. Many existing wastewater treatment facilities are not equipped to adequately remove these dyes, leading to significant contamination. Factors contributing to this issue include outdated treatment technologies, a lack of enforcement of environmental regulations, and economic constraints that prevent industries from adopting cleaner technologies4–6. Organic dyes are persistent environmental pollutants due to their complex chemical structures that resist biodegradation. They can adversely impede aquatic organisms by diminishing light availability and depleting oxygen concentrations in water environments, thereby disrupting environmental systems. For humans, being subjected to water polluted with certain dyes can contribute to serious health complications such as skin diseases, breathing difficulties, and even carcinogenic risks7–9. These substances can accumulate in the body over time, posing long-term health risks. Specifically, acid red 88 and crystal violet are synthetic dyes known for their use in various industries and associated health risks. Crystal violet has been classified as a potential carcinogen, while acid red 88 is suspected of causing adverse effects on the skin and eyes upon exposure10,11. Their presence in water can lead to direct exposure through skin contact or indirectly through the consumption of contaminated water and food products. Water purification is crucial for maintaining a sustainable environment due to the adverse effects of pollutants on ecosystems and human health. Efficient water treatment methods are essential to address the contamination caused by industrial processes. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of water purification in mitigating the environmental impact of organic pollutants and heavy metals. For instance, the recycling of valuable metals from electroplating sludge through innovative methods has shown promising results in reducing environmental pollution12. Additionally, effective management of electroplating sludge can prevent the release of heavy metals into water bodies, thereby protecting aquatic life and human health. Advanced separation techniques, such as stepwise extraction, have been developed to efficiently remove impurities and recycle valuable resources from waste streams13,14. Besides, a range of strategies is available for the elimination of organic dyes from effluents, including adsorption15, electrodialysis16, advanced oxidation processes17–23, and biological treatment24. Electrodialysis, while effective, is often costly and energy-intensive, limiting its practical application on a larger scale. Advanced oxidation processes, although highly efficient in degrading dyes, involve the use of strong oxidizing agents, which can generate secondary pollution and require careful handling. Biological treatments are environmentally friendly but are often slow and may not be effective for all types of dyes due to the complex chemical structures that resist biodegradation. The adsorption technique offers several advantages for dye removal in water treatment, including its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to operate at ambient temperatures without requiring complex equipment. Adsorption is also highly adaptable, as it can be applied to a wide range of contaminants and adjusted for different types of adsorbents. Importantly, adsorbents can often be regenerated and reused, reducing waste and overall operational costs, which is particularly beneficial in large-scale wastewater treatments25–28 However, current adsorption technologies face several limitations, such as low adsorption capacities, difficulty of separation from aqueous solutions, poor regeneration abilities, and the requirement for high energy input during adsorbent preparation. Nano-metal oxides, such as ZnO/MgO and ZrO2/CdMn2O4/CdO, have been recognized for their high efficiency in dye adsorption29,30. Their nanoscale size provides a large surface area, enhancing their interaction with dye molecules and improving their adsorption capacity. Also, nanoparticles are of significant interest due to their unique properties that differ from their bulk counterparts. These properties include high surface area, tunable pore size, and the ability to modify their surface chemistry. Such characteristics make nanoparticles highly effective in applications like catalysis, sensing, and environmental remediation31–33. Ferrite nanoparticles have gained significant attention due to their unique magnetic and dielectric properties, making them suitable for various applications such as magnetic fluids, recording media, water treatment, and medical imaging34–39. Recent investigations have revealed the efficacy of copper ferrite nanoparticles in the elimination of various dyes from aqueous solutions and wastewater40–42. For instance, copper ferrite nanoparticles have been successfully employed to remove dyes such as methyl orange, malachite green, acid blue 113, and indigo carmine, showcasing high adsorption capacities and reusability. The magnetic properties of copper ferrite nanoparticles facilitate easy separation of the nanoparticles from water using an external magnetic field, suggesting it as a favorable material for wastewater treatment. Besides, the combustion synthesis of nano-metal oxides is a cost-effective and effective method that allows for the rapid production of nanoparticles with controlled size and morphology. This method is particularly beneficial for scaling up production in industrial applications43,44. Hence, the primary objective of this study is to develop a novel, efficient, and environmentally friendly magnetic adsorbent (CuFe2O4 nanoparticles) for the efficient removal of hazardous dyes such as acid red 88 and crystal violet from wastewater. The choice of CuFe2O4 nanoparticles, synthesized via a green combustion method using simple organic fuels such as succinic acid and malic acid, is driven by their potential high adsorption capacities compared to several adsorbents in the literature, ease of synthesis, cost-effectiveness, ease of regeneration and reusability, high surface area, low crystallite size, and ease of separation from aqueous media by a magnet. By addressing the pressing issue of dye pollution with a focus on practical applications, this research contributes significantly to the field of water treatment technologies. The combustion method used in this study is favored for its simplicity and rapid synthesis. However, other techniques such as hydrothermal, sol–gel, and co-precipitation have also been utilized to synthesize copper ferrite nanoparticles40–42,45. These methods differ in complexity, cost, and their ability to control particle size and morphology. For example, the sol–gel method, despite offering high purity and uniformity, involves multiple steps and has higher costs compared to the combustion method. In contrast, the co-precipitation method is simpler and more cost-effective but may provide less control over particle size and distribution. Patil and Behera compared these methods and found that while the co-precipitation method is cost-effective, it results in a lower Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and adsorption capacity compared to the sol–gel method46. Many current dye removal methods face challenges, including limited adsorption capacities, difficulty in adsorbent separation post-treatment, and complex or costly synthesis processes47–49. Traditional adsorbents, such as activated carbon, zeolites, and certain metal oxides, often lack the efficiency and reusability required for practical wastewater applications. Although magnetic nanoparticles offer promise, there remains a need for simpler, cost-effective synthesis methods that provide high adsorption capacities and allow for efficient magnetic separation. This study addresses this gap by introducing copper ferrite nanoparticles synthesized via a green combustion method using succinic and malic acids as fuels. This innovative approach not only enhances adsorption capacity but also facilitates simple recovery and economic feasibility. Hence, this study introduces a novel approach by synthesizing copper ferrite nanoparticles via a green combustion method using succinic and malic acids as fuels. Unlike traditional adsorbents, these nanoparticles exhibit enhanced adsorption capacities due to their small crystallite size, large surface area, and magnetic properties that facilitate easy separation post-treatment. By employing a cost-effective synthesis method and achieving superior removal efficiencies for acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes, this research provides a significant advancement in sustainable water purification technologies, especially for wastewater applications where rapid and efficient dye removal is critical.

Experimental

Chemicals

Copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2.3H2O, 99.99% purity), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37% purity), succinic acid (C4H6O4, 99.99% purity), iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3.9H2O, 99.99% purity), malic acid (C4H6O5, 99.99% purity), potassium chloride (KCl, 99.99% purity), crystal violet dye (C25H30ClN3, 99% purity), acid red 88 dye (C20H11N2Na3O10S3, 98% purity), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98% purity) were of analytical grade and obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, USA, and utilized without additional purification.

Fabrication of copper ferrite nanoparticles

Figure 1 outlines the methodology for fabricating CuFe2O4 nanoparticles through the combustion technique, utilizing succinic acid and malic acid as fuels. Initially, 4.85 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.0120 mol) was solubilized in 40 mL of deionized water to produce a ferric solution. Following this, 1.45 g of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (0.0060 mol) was solubilized in 40 mL of deionized water to produce a cupric solution. Concurrently, a succinic acid solution was freshly prepared through dissolving 2.03 g of the acid (0.0172 mol) in 40 mL of deionized water. These solutions containing Fe(III), Cu(II), and the succinic acid-based fuel were subsequently mixed and agitated magnetically at 120 °C until they reached a dry state. The dry sample was then calcined at 650 °C for 3 h, producing CuFe2O4 nanoparticles, which were abbreviated as FS. Similarly, for the preparation involving malic acid, 2.68 g of the acid (0.0200 mol) was solubilized in 40 mL of deionized water to form a malic acid-based fuel aqueous solution. This solution, along with the Cu(II) and Fe(III) solutions, were mixed and subjected to magnetic stirring at 120 °C until they reached a dry state. The dry sample was then calcined at 650 °C for 3 h, producing CuFe2O4 nanoparticles, which were abbreviated as FM. To ensure reproducibility and consistent properties of the synthesized copper ferrite nanoparticles, the synthesis was performed in triplicate. The synthesis yielded 73% (mass/mass) for the succinic acid-based sample (FS) and 70% (mass/mass) for the malic acid-based sample (FM), indicating efficient conversion during the combustion process.Fig. 1Procedural steps for producing CuFe2O4 nanoparticles using the combustion method.

Procedural steps for producing CuFe2O4 nanoparticles using the combustion method.

Instrumentation

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Thermo Fisher Nicolet IS10 spectrometer. The morphology and elemental composition were analyzed using a JEOL JSM-6510LV field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) coupled with an energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analyzer. The specific surface area and porosity were measured using a Quantachrome NOVA2000 series N₂ gas analyzer. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images were captured with a JEOL JEM-2100Plus microscope.

Adsorption of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes from aqueous solutions

Before beginning the adsorption experiments, the effect of pH on the color stability of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes was assessed. It was observed that, within the pH range used in this study (2–12), both dyes maintained their characteristic colors, indicating that the pH adjustments did not significantly alter their visible properties. All the adsorption experiments were carried out in a dark place. The investigation on how pH affects the adsorption mechanism was conducted by adjusting the pH values from 2 to 12. This was done while keeping the dye’s concentration at 250 mg/L, its volume at 100 mL, and using 50 mg of adsorbent at a constant temperature (298 K) for a duration of 3 h. Additionally, the role of adsorption time on adsorption was explored from 10 to 120 min, maintaining the same dye concentration, volume, and amount of adsorbent. The pH value was adjusted to 2 for acid red 88 dye or 10 for crystal violet dye. A 250 mg/L dye concentration was selected for determining the effects of pH and contact time because this concentration allows for consistent and comparable results across different conditions. By using a single concentration, the impacts of pH and contact time on the adsorption process can be effectively isolated and analyzed without the variability introduced by changing dye concentrations. The exploration of temperature’s effect on adsorption covered a range from 298 to 328 K, with all other conditions, such as dye concentration, volume, and adsorbent quantity, remaining unchanged, and the adsorption time set at 80 min; pH was preserved at 2 for acid red 88 and at 10 for crystal violet dye. Moreover, an examination was carried out to investigate how a range of dye initial concentrations (50–400 mg/L) influences adsorption while keeping the dye volume in addition to adsorbent quantity constant. The experiments were conducted at 298 K, with an adsorption period of 80 min and the pH values as previously established for both types of dyes. Besides, the chosen concentration range for this study was based on typical dye concentrations found in wastewater from textile industries. This range was selected to simulate realistic conditions encountered in industrial effluents, thereby ensuring the practical applicability of our findings. Each test was performed three times, and the mean values were calculated. After each experimental condition was studied, the adsorbent was separated using a magnet.

The concentration of dye solutions during the adsorption experiments was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Acid red 88 and crystal violet dye concentrations were monitored at their maximum absorbance wavelengths, 507 nm and 590 nm, respectively. These measurements were taken to determine the initial and equilibrium concentrations of the dyes in the solution.

After adsorption, the dye-laden CuFe2O4 nanoparticles were separated from the solution using an external magnetic field, enabling straightforward collection and minimizing secondary contamination. For regeneration, the dye-loaded particles can undergo thermal treatment, where calcination at 550 °C for 2 h effectively removes adsorbed dyes, restoring the adsorbent’s surface for reuse. The performance of this regenerated adsorbent was then assessed over five cycles under the best adsorption conditions to determine its reusability. For each adsorption cycle, the conditions were consistent: a starting dye concentration of 250 mg/L, a 100 mL volume of dye solution, and 0.05 g of adsorbent were used. The pH values were adjusted to 2 for adsorbing acid red 88 dye and to 10 for crystal violet dye. Every cycle encompassed an 80-min duration at a uniform temperature of 298 K. Moreover, these conditions were meticulously maintained to accurately assess the adsorbent’s effectiveness and its viability for continued use in extracting dyes from water-based solutions.

Measurement of the point of zero charge (pHPZC) for the FS and FM Products

Solutions of 50 mL each were prepared with initial pH values varying between 2.50 and 11.50, using a 0.1 mol/L solution of KCl. To adjust the initial pH (pHi), either 0.1 mol/L HCl or NaOH was used. Subsequently, each solution received 0.25 g of the adsorbent, followed by continuous agitation for a duration of 12 h. Upon completion of agitation, the solutions were filtered to measure the final pH (pHf) of the clear liquid. The difference in pH (∆pH) was assessed by evaluating the discrepancy between the initial and final pH measurements. Utilizing the obtained data, a chart was generated to display the relationship between △pH and the initial pH. The intersection of the △pH and initial pH curves with the X-axis was pinpointed to ascertain the adsorbent’s point of zero charge29.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of CuFe2O4 nanoparticles

In Fig. 2, the synthesis of copper ferrite nanoparticles via the combustion method is depicted, utilizing two different carboxylic acids as fuel: succinic acid (Fig. 2A) and malic acid (Fig. 2B). The formation of copper ferrite nanoparticles via combustion synthesis can be attributed to the redox reactions between metal nitrates (as oxidizers) and organic acids (as fuels). Initially, iron(III) nitrate and copper(II) nitrate decompose upon heating, releasing metal ions. The organic acids (succinic and malic acids) then react with these metal ions, promoting the generation of gases such as CO2, H2O, and N2, which act as dispersing agents, preventing particle agglomeration. As the temperature rises, the combustion process culminates in the formation of a crystalline CuFe2O4 spinel structure. The gases released during the reaction help form porous structures, leading to nanoparticles with high surface area and uniform morphology. The amounts of succinic acid (2.03 g) and malic acid (2.68 g) were chosen based on stoichiometric calculations, as shown in Fig. 2, to achieve an optimal fuel-to-oxidizer ratio. This ratio ensures complete combustion and efficient formation of CuFe2O4 nanoparticles.Fig. 2Combustion-based production of copper ferrite nanoparticles.

Combustion-based production of copper ferrite nanoparticles.

XRD was employed to study the crystallinity of the CuFe2O4 products, designated as FS and FM, with results presented in Fig. 3A-B, respectively. Referring to the standard JCPDS reference No. 77–0010, two products displayed sharp diffraction XRD peaks consistent with the cubic spinel-type architecture of copper ferrite42. The peaks, which were observed at 2Ɵ angles of 24.28°, 33.19°, 35.66°, 38.79°, 40.89°, 43.37°, 53.93°, 57.33°, and 62.38°, are associated with the (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (311), (511), (422), and (440) miller indices of CuFe2O4, respectively. By utilizing the Scherrer formula50, the mean crystallite size for the FS and FM samples was calculated to be 19.79 ± 0.75 nm and 28.54 ± 0.90 nm, respectively. The XRD analysis revealed slight differences in lattice parameters between the FS and FM samples. The FS sample, synthesized with succinic acid, exhibited a lattice parameter of 8.392 Å, while the FM sample, synthesized with malic acid, had a slightly smaller lattice parameter of 8.375 Å. Removal of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes from aqueous media

Effect of pH

Figure 9A-B illustrates the variation in the adsorption percentage of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes as well as the adsorption capacity of the FM and FS adsorbents over different pH levels, respectively. For the anionic acid red 88 dye, the adsorption percentage or adsorption capacity significantly decreases as the solution pH increases from a minimum of 2 to a maximum of 12. At a pH 2, the removal percentages for acid red 88 dye on the FM and FS adsorbents are 79.09% and 87.94%, respectively. Also, the adsorption capacities of the FM and FS adsorbents are 395.48 and 439.72 mg/g, respectively, at the same pH. Conversely, for the cationic crystal violet dye, the adsorption percentage or adsorption capacity significantly increases as the pH rises from 2 to 10, then slightly increases as the pH rises from 10 to 12. The removal of crystal violet dye using the FM and FS products at pH 10 reaches 73.87% and 85.83%, respectively. Also, the adsorption capacities of the FM and FS adsorbents are 369.36 and 429.16 mg/g, respectively, at the same pH. The adsorption capacity of the FS sample is larger than that of the FM sample because the FS sample has an elevated BET surface area (69.28 m2/g in comparison with 40.63 m2/g), a greater total pore volume (0.1132 cc/g compared to 0.0493 cc/g), and a larger average pore size (3.27 nm compared to 2.65 nm), providing more active sites and better accessibility for dye molecules. These factors enhance the adsorption efficiency and capacity of the FS sample compared to the FM sample.Fig. 9Effect of solution pH on the removal percentage of the studied dyes (A) and the adsorption capacity of the FM and FS products (B).

Effect of solution pH on the removal percentage of the studied dyes (A) and the adsorption capacity of the FM and FS products (B).

Adsorption mechanism

The pH significantly impacts adsorption, which can be attributed to the pHPZC of adsorbent. As depicted in Fig. 10, the pHPZC for the FM and FS adsorbents is determined to be 5.22 and 5.67, respectively. For the anionic acid red 88 dye, there’s a marked decrease in adsorption percentage or adsorption capacity with an increase in pH from 2 to 10. This implies that at pH values below the pHPZC, the adsorbent surfaces exhibit a positive charge, enhancing the adsorption of the negatively charged acid red 88 dye via electrostatic attraction, as indicated in Fig. 11ref29,42. Conversely, when the pH surpasses the pHPZC, the adsorbent surface acquires a negative charge, diminishing the adsorption percentage for the anionic acid red 88 dye due to electrostatic repulsion29. In contrast, for the cationic crystal violet dye, an opposite pH-adsorption relationship is observed in comparison to the acid red 88 dye. This is likely because of the cationic nature of the crystal violet dye, which leads to reduced adsorption below the pHPZC due to repulsion between the dye’s positive charge and the positive charge of the adsorbent surfaces. Above the pHPZC, the increased negative charge on the adsorbents enhances the adsorption of the crystal violet dye due to stronger electrostatic attraction, as detailed in Fig. 11 ref42.Fig. 10The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the FM and FS samples.Fig. 11Schematic representation of the adsorption mechanism of crystal violet and acid red 88 dyes onto CuFe2O4 nanoparticles.

Effect of contact time

Figure 12A-B clarifies the impact of interaction duration on the removal percentage of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes as well as the adsorption capacity of the FM and FS samples, respectively. The data reveal a rapid increase in adsorption percentage, or adsorption capacity, from 10 to 80 min for both dye varieties, indicating a robust initial interaction with the nanoparticles. After this quick phase of adsorption, a state of equilibrium is reached around the 80-min mark, a consequence of the saturation of available active sites on the nanoparticles. For acid red 88 dye, the adsorption percentages achieved by the FM and FS samples at 80 min are 78.63 and 87.35%, respectively. Also, the adsorption capacities of the FM and FS samples are 393.16 and 436.74 mg/g, respectively, at the same time. Similarly, the removal percentages for crystal violet dye on the FM and FS products at the same time interval are 73.31 and 85.43%, respectively. Besides, the adsorption capacities of the FM and FS samples are 366.54 and 427.14 mg/g, respectively, at the same time. The trends observed in Fig. 12, showing similar adsorption behavior with slight variations between samples, are consistent with previous studies on dye adsorption using magnetic nanoparticles. Comparable adsorption kinetics and saturation trends have been reported for similar adsorbent-dye systems, where rapid initial adsorption is followed by equilibrium within a short contact time52.Fig. 12Influence of adsorption time on dye removal efficiency (A) and adsorptive capacities of FM and FS products (B).

The differences in the adsorption capacities of copper ferrite nanoparticles for acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes are influenced by several factors, including the molecular structure of the dyes and the interaction mechanisms between the dyes and the adsorbent. Acid red 88 is an anionic dye, while crystal violet is a cationic dye. This difference in electrostatic interactions is a primary reason for the varying adsorption capacities. Moreover, the size and shape of the dye molecules influence their adsorption behavior. Figure 22 examines the effect of five sequential adsorption/desorption cycles on the elimination of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes by the FM and FS products. The findings indicate a slight reduction in the percentage of dyes adsorbed as the number of adsorption/desorption cycles grows. Consequently, it is obvious that the FM and FS samples maintain remarkable reusability for capturing acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes. After completing the regeneration study, structural and morphological analyses were conducted to verify the stability of CuFe2O4 nanoparticles. HR-TEM and XRD analyses confirmed that both FS and FM samples retained their crystalline structure and morphology after multiple adsorption cycles, with no significant changes observed. Removal of the mixture of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes

To evaluate the adsorption efficiency of CuFe₂O₄ nanoparticles for a mixture of dyes, experiments were conducted using a combined solution of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes. The initial concentrations of both dyes were set at 250 mg/L, with an overall volume of 100 mL, and the adsorption was performed using 0.05 g of the adsorbent at 298 K for 80 min. The data indicate a significant removal efficiency for both dyes when present together in the solution. The FM sample showed adsorption capacities of 310.25 mg/g for acid red 88 dye and 260.47 mg/g for crystal violet dye, while the FS sample demonstrated adsorption capacities of 343.17 mg/g for acid red 88 dye and 290.12 mg/g for crystal violet dye.

Practical applications

The practical applications of this work are demonstrated through experiments involving the removal of dyes from real wastewater samples. For instance, 0.05 g of the FM or FS sample was added to 100 ml of a sample from students’ laboratory wastewater containing acid red 88 dye (60 mg/L) with the pH adjusted to 2 and a contact time of 80 min. The removal percentages of acid red 88 dye using the FM and FS samples were found to be 95.12% and 97.23%, respectively. Similarly, 0.05 g of the FM or FS sample was added to 100 ml of a sample from the students’ laboratory containing crystal violet dye (55 mg/L) with the pH adjusted to 10 and a contact time of 80 min. The removal percentages of crystal violet dye using the FM and FS samples were found to be 94.26% and 97.56%, respectively. These results indicate the high efficiency and practical applicability of the synthesized CuFe2O4 nanoparticles in real-world scenarios, making them suitable for use in wastewater treatment facilities to effectively remove hazardous dyes and mitigate environmental pollution.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrates the synthesis and application of magnetic copper ferrite nanoparticles, fabricated using a combustion method with succinic acid and malic acid as fuels, for the efficient removal of acid red 88 and crystal violet dyes from aqueous media. The nanoparticles, characterized by XRD, FTIR, SEM, EDX, and BET analyses, exhibited crystallite sizes of 28.54 nm for the malic acid sample (FM) and 19.79 nm for the succinic acid sample (FS). Under optimal conditions, the FS sample achieved adsorption capacities of 452.49 mg/g for acid red 88 and 446.43 mg/g for crystal violet, while the FM sample reached 408.16 mg/g and 374.53 mg/g, respectively. Adsorption followed the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Langmuir isotherm, indicating a monolayer adsorption process. Thermodynamic studies confirmed the adsorption process to be physical, exothermic, and spontaneous. In practical applications, the CuFe2O4 nanoparticles demonstrated high removal efficiencies of over 95% for both dyes in real wastewater samples, underscoring their potential for sustainable water purification technologies. Future studies could focus on enhancing the stability and reusability of these nanoparticles in diverse wastewater conditions. Additionally, exploring functional modifications or composite formulations may further improve adsorption efficiency and selectivity for a wider range of pollutants.
