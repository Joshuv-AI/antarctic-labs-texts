Gut Trouble: A Rare Case of Amoxicillin-Clavulanate-Induced Ischemic Colitis in a Young Adult
Authors: Logan Oliver, Melanie Wiseman, Brett W Sadowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic colitis is a potentially life-threatening complication resulting from diminished blood flow to the colon. While the classic presentation is acute, crampy abdominal pain preceding hematochezia in older patients with cardiovascular comorbidities, it can present atypically in different demographics. The diagnosis can be established through appropriate clinical suspicion, along with endoscopic and histological confirmation. We report a case of amoxicillin-clavulanate-induced ischemic colitis in a young male without comorbidities. Following cessation of the medication, he demonstrated full clinical resolution of his symptoms. With this case report, we hope to highlight the importance of recognizing drug-induced ischemic colitis, specifically with the use of amoxicillin-clavulanate, an antibiotic commonly prescribed in outpatient clinical settings.

Introduction

Ischemic colitis is the most common form of intestinal ischemia, with patients typically presenting with acute, crampy abdominal pain preceding hematochezia due to reduced blood flow to the large intestine. Risk factors include atherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [1]. Though ischemic colitis more commonly occurs in older patients, it can affect young individuals without risk factors, such as our patient, a young male without comorbidities who developed lower gastrointestinal bleeding following outpatient administration of amoxicillin-clavulanate for a routine dental procedure. Through this case, we hope to highlight the importance of recognizing drug-induced ischemic colitis, especially in young adults who may present atypically and without known risk factors.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old male without a past medical history presented to the emergency room with a three-day history of diarrhea and abdominal pain. He reported painful hematochezia over the preceding day, with approximately 20 bowel movements per day leading up to his emergency room visit. Of note, he underwent a dental extraction and implant four days prior and had been taking twice-daily amoxicillin-clavulanate since the procedure. He reported using as-needed acetaminophen for tooth pain but denied use of any other medications (including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS)) or supplements. He was afebrile, with a normal heart rate and normal to hypertensive blood pressure. On evaluation, he had pertinent laboratory values as listed in Table 1.

Initial considerations in the differential diagnosis following the above workup included ischemic colitis, infectious colitis, acute mesenteric ischemia, new-onset inflammatory bowel disease, and diverticulitis. The patient ultimately had a negative infectious evaluation, including for Clostridioides difficile. Contrast-enhanced CT imaging demonstrated bowel thickening and mucosal hyperemia of the splenic flexure (Figure 1).

Given his clinical presentation and concern for active lower gastrointestinal bleeding, he was admitted for endoscopic evaluation. A colonoscopy showed erythema in the sigmoid colon and approximately 30 cm of circumferential edema and erythema without ulceration in the transverse and descending colon (Figure 2), with clearly demarcated edges straddled by normal mucosa (Figure 3). Colonic biopsies (Figure 4) showed decreased mucin vacuoles, attenuated crypts, and a hemorrhagic and fibrotic lamina propria, consistent with ischemic colitis.

Two days following cessation of his previously prescribed amoxicillin-clavulanate, along with continued supportive care throughout his inpatient admission, the patient’s symptoms completely resolved. He was subsequently discharged home without recurrence of his symptoms.

Discussion

Ischemic colitis makes up approximately 10% to 19% of cases of lower gastrointestinal bleeding [1]. Patients often present with acute, crampy abdominal pain preceding hematochezia. However, other symptoms can include diarrhea, nausea, fever, and bloating [2]. Ischemic colitis typically affects older adults with a median age of 70 years, with individuals aged 70 to 79 having a 3.7-fold higher risk compared to those aged 50-59. The left colon is more frequently involved in ischemic colitis than the right, with sparing of the rectum due to its dual blood supply [3].

As ischemic colitis is typically triggered by either small vessel occlusion or hypoperfusion, the possible etiologies of ischemic colitis are broad. Common causes include atherosclerosis, hemorrhagic shock, pancreatitis, and vasculitis [4]. It is important to consider drug-induced ischemic colitis in the differential, as there is a rare association of ischemic colitis with administration of medications such as digoxin, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and pseudoephedrine [5]. Amoxicillin-clavulanate-induced ischemic colitis is exceedingly rare, with only two prior case reports found in the medical literature. However, both reported cases, in addition to our case, involved young adults [6,7].

Per the American College of Gastroenterology, early colonoscopy (within 48 hours of presentation) with biopsy remains the gold standard in diagnosing ischemic colitis, which allowed us to make a prompt diagnosis [8]. With early clinical suspicion of ischemic colitis and cessation of the presumed offending agent in cases of drug-induced ischemic colitis, patients often demonstrate full clinical resolution [9]. Conservative management with intravenous hydration may further aid in treatment. However, in extreme cases of ischemic colitis, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics and surgical consultation should be obtained if bowel resection is warranted [10]. Drug-induced ischemic colitis is an important differential to consider in the appropriate clinical context and is easy to overlook given it can occur in younger patients without comorbidities.

Conclusions

Though the etiologies of ischemic colitis are quite broad, drug-induced ischemic colitis caused by the antibiotic amoxicillin-clavulanate is exceedingly rare, with only a few documented in the medical literature. We hope to highlight the importance of recognizing drug-induced ischemic colitis in patients presenting with lower gastrointestinal bleeding, especially in the setting of outpatient antibiotic administration.
