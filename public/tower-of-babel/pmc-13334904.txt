Prevention of erosive, abrasive and erosive/abrasive tooth wear by a high-concentration fluoride dentifrice: an in vitro study
Authors: Silvia Mayer, Florian J Wegehaupt, Blend Hamza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis in-vitro study compared the protective effects of a 5000-ppm fluoride dentifrice with an anti-erosive 1450-ppm formulation containing stannous chloride and chitosan under erosive, abrasive, and erosive/abrasive conditions.Materials and methodsA total of 240 bovine enamel specimens were randomly assigned to three models: erosion followed by abrasion (EA), erosion only (E), and abrasion only (A) (n = 80 per model; n = 20 per treatment). Four treatments were tested: artificial saliva (AS), a conventional 1450-ppm NaF and SnF2 dentifrice (CT; Colgate Total), an anti-erosive 1450-ppm AmF dentifrice containing stannous chloride and chitosan (EPOS; Elmex professional Opti-Schmelz), and a 5000-ppm NaF dentifrice (CD; Colgate Duraphat). Erosive challenge consisted of 1% citric acid (pH 2.6) for 2 min. In EA, erosion was followed by brushing (1 min); in E, specimens were exposed to AS or slurry without brushing; in A, specimens were brushed without prior erosion. Brushing was performed with a manual toothbrush at 60 strokes/min under 2.5 N. Dentifrices were applied as slurries (1:6.5, artificial saliva). Ten cycles were performed with 1 h storage in artificial saliva between cycles. Enamel surface loss (µm) was measured by contact profilometry at baseline and after 10 cycles. Data were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis and Conover post-hoc tests.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in EA and E (p < 0.05), but not in A (p = 0.40). In E, only EPOS reduced enamel surface loss compared with AS (p = 0.01), while CT and CD showed no significant differences. In EA, a significant difference was found only between AS and EPOS. CD did not show superior protective effects in any model.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this in-vitro study, increasing fluoride concentration to 5000 ppm did not enhance protection against erosive or erosive/abrasive enamel surface loss. These findings suggest that formulation-related factors, including stannous-based interactions, may be more relevant than fluoride concentration alone for preventing erosive tooth wear.Keywords: erosive tooth wear, abrasive tooth wear, high-fluoride dentifrice, tooth wear prevention, stannous chloride

Introduction

In recent years erosive tooth wear has increasingly been recognized as a relevant clinical issue, with both its prevalence and incidence rising markedly [1–3]. Fluoride has demonstrated a beneficial role in dental erosion by reducing mineral loss. In addition, it could promote the formation of a protective CaF₂-like layer, which may act as a temporary reservoir and enhance acid resistance of enamel. However, under highly erosive conditions, this layer is relatively unstable and may be rapidly dissolved [4, 5]. High-fluoride dentifrices containing 5000 ppm fluoride were introduced into clinical practice in the early 2000s primarily to enhance the prevention of dental caries [6–8]. Numerous studies demonstrated its superior efficacy in managing caries, compared to the traditional 1450-ppm dentifrice, with further research exploring its potential role in erosion protection [8–10].

These studies prompted the investigation of a possible dose-dependent effect of fluoride on erosive tooth wear. Rios et al. [11] reported in their in situ study that treatments with dentifrices containing 5000 ppm compared to 1100 ppm fluoride, were not more effective in preventing enamel erosion [11]. In contrast, subsequent experiments demonstrated that dentifrices with 5000 ppm fluoride can provide superior protection compared to conventional formulations with 1450 ppm, particularly under in situ conditions or when enamel is subjected to severe acidic challenges [12, 13].

Beyond the effects of fluoride alone, adjunctive components such as chitosan and stannous chloride have also been widely investigated. Studies revealed that dentifrices containing these agents with approximately 1450 ppm fluoride confer measurable anti-erosive and anti-abrasive benefits and surpass conventional NaF-based formulations in effectiveness. Stannous ions (Sn²⁺) have been shown to interact directly with the enamel surface, forming acid-resistant, tin-rich deposits that can reduce enamel solubility. Chitosan, a biopolymer, may further contribute by forming a protective organic layer on the enamel surface and enhancing the retention of active components. The combination of these agents has been associated with improved resistance to erosive and erosive–abrasive challenges [14–18].

Considering that erosive and abrasive tooth wear constitutes an increasing clinical concern and acknowledging that the loss of dental hard tissues is irreversible and may ultimately involve the entire dentition, the prevention of enamel surface softening and subsequent tissue loss represents a primary objective in the management of dental erosion. Despite the known benefits of high-fluoride dentifrices in caries management, their efficacy in preventing dental erosion, particularly in comparison to formulations containing additional anti-erosive agents, remains inadequately explored.

Therefore, this in vitro study aimed to assess the protective potential of a 5000-ppm fluoride dentifrice against erosive and erosive/abrasive tooth wear in comparison with an anti-erosive 1450 ppm formulation containing stannous chloride and chitosan. Specifically, to determine whether high-fluoride concentrations would offer superior protection against enamel loss compared with the anti-erosive formulation. Based on the identified research gap, this study tested the null hypothesis that there would be no difference in enamel loss between the investigated dentifrices within each challenge category (erosion/abrasion, only erosion and only abrasion).

Materials and methods

Experimental design

An illustration of the experimental design is provided in Fig. 1. The study followed a two-factor experimental design with the factors “experimental model” (three levels: EA, E, A) and “dentifrice type” (four levels: artificial saliva, 1450-ppm NaF, 1450-ppm AmF/Sn/chitosan, 5000-ppm NaF), with enamel surface loss (µm) as the primary response variable. All procedures were performed by a single, non-blinded operator. The erosive protocol using citric acid was selected as a clinically relevant model for dietary erosion and is consistent with previously published in vitro and in situ studies investigating erosive tooth wear [19, 20]. The combined erosion–abrasion approach reflects established experimental models used to simulate clinical conditions. Brushing parameters, including force and frequency, were selected based on previous toothbrushing abrasion studies using clinically relevant conditions [14, 21].

Experimental design

Specimens in group EA were immersed in 5 ml of a 1% citric acid solution (pH 2.6) for two minutes and rinsed with tap water. Subsequently, abrasion was afterwards performed with either artificial saliva according to Klimek [22] alone or slurries of artificial saliva and the respective dentifrices at a ratio of 1:6.5 [23] according to the above mentioned subgroups. Brushing was conducted with a medium-bristle stiffness toothbrush (ParoM43, Esro AG, Thalwil, Switzerland) at 60 strokes per minute for one minute applying a constant force of 2.5 N.

Samples of group E underwent the erosive procedure under identical conditions as described above. As opposed to group EA no abrasion was carried out, and the samples were then exposed to either artificial saliva or the designated slurries for one minute.

Specimens in group A were not subjected to the erosive challenge. Instead of citric acid, specimens were stored in tap water for two minutes and brushing was performed according to the same protocols described above with the agents assigned to each subgroup. At the end of one experimental cycle, the samples were stored in artificial saliva for one hour before re-treatment. Based on the preliminary testing, each group underwent ten repetitions of the described protocol.

Sample preparation and allocation

For the study 240 enamel specimens gained from extracted bovine incisors were prepared. The bovine teeth used in this in vitro study were obtained as by-products of cattle slaughter for human consumption. The animals were sacrificed exclusively for food production purposes, and the present study was not associated with, nor did it influence, the decision to slaughter the animals. As the specimens were collected post mortem from routine food-processing procedures, no additional ethical approval was required. Cylindrical enamel samples with a diameter of 3 mm were prepared using a diamond-coated trephine mill (Proxxon, Brütsch/Rüegger Tools AG, Urdorf, Switzerland) under constant water cooling. Each specimen was subsequently embedded in a polymethylmethacrylate-based resin (Paladur Rosa; Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany), to facilitate its handling. An automatic grinding machine was used to grind and polish the enamel surface using sequential water-cooled 1,000-, 2,000- and 4,000-grit carborundum discs (silicon carbide papers, Struers, Tegramin-30, Germany). Final trimming was performed to standardize the specimen height across all the samples (3 mm). Two shallow parallel reference grooves were inscribed on the embedding acrylic resin of each specimen for precise repositioning during the later profilometric evaluation. A thin strip of adhesive tape (Scotch Crystal 600, 3 M Switzerland GmbH, Rüschlikon, Switzerland) was positioned parallel to the reference grooves to protect a defined enamel area, which functioned as an internal reference surface for the quantification of profilometric enamel loss. Throughout all preparation steps specimens were stored in tap water at room temperature.

All processed specimens were randomly allocated to the experimental groups after preparation using a simple randomization approach. Allocation was performed using an Excel-based random number assignment. Group sizes were predefined, resulting in approximately balanced distribution across the experimental conditions. specimens were randomly allocated to three main groups according to the respective experimental protocol: Erosion followed by abrasion with a dentifrice slurry (EA), only erosion (E) and only abrasion (A). Each main group was further divided into four subgroups, resulting in a total of twelve subgroups as follows: Subgroup one treated only with artificial saliva according to Klimek [22], subgroup two with a regular dentifrice (Colgate Total), subgroup three with an anti-erosive dentifrice (Elmex professional Opti-Schmelz) and subgroup four with a high-fluoride dentifrice (Colgate Duraphat). A detailed composition of these products is listed in Table 1. Care was taken, that not more than one sample gained from a single tooth was allocated to the same specific subgroup.

Measurement of erosive and erosive/abrasive enamel loss

Prior to the experimental procedure five baseline surface profiles (distance = 250 μm, recording accuracy = 40 nm) of each specimen were recorded using a stylus profilometer (Perthometer S2, Mahr, Göttingen, Germany). Both the profilometer and specimens were fitted with a tailor-made alignment jig to ensure precise repositioning throughout repeated measurements. After the completion of the ten experimental cycles, the final profiles were obtained. The baseline and final profiles were superimposed by a custom-made software tool. The discrepancy between the two profiles within the treated area yielded enamel loss caused by erosion, abrasion or erosion/abrasion. When the profile values fell below the detection threshold of the profilometer (0.105 μm), they were assigned a value of 0.000 μm [24]. A mean value was calculated from the five measured differences of the five profiles per sample.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and organized using Microsoft Excel software. Statistical computations were performed with R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed visually using standard diagnostic plots from the ANOVA model, including Q–Q plots and residual plots. Given the clear deviations from these assumptions, particularly in the Abrasion group, a non-parametric approach was deemed more appropriate.

Initially, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to identify the differences in enamel loss among the different product groups. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were assessed using the Conover test. For the adjustment of the p-values, the Holm method was used. p ≤ 0.05 results were regarded as statistically significant.

Discussion

The findings of the present study are intended to expand the evidence base for preventive approaches to support decision-making in the management of erosive and erosive/abrasive tooth wear. As statistical significance was detected in the groups EA and E, the null hypothesis for these groups could be rejected. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed within the abrasion-only group (A), indicating that the null hypothesis for this challenge could not be rejected.

In the EA group, the superior performance of AS compared with EPOS can likely be attributed to the absence of abrasive components, thereby avoiding additional mechanically induced surface loss. In contrast, EPOS contains abrasive particles, reflected by its reported RDA values (40–100), which may have contributed to increased enamel wear under combined erosive and abrasive conditions.

It should be noted, however, that RDA values were originally developed to quantify dentin abrasivity and are therefore not directly indicative of abrasiveness on enamel surfaces. In this context, RDA values should be interpreted with caution, as they do not reliably predict enamel wear [25, 26]. Consequently, the observed reduction in protective efficacy of the anti-erosive dentifrice in the present study is more likely attributable to the interaction between mechanical abrasion and erosive softening rather than to its nominal RDA value alone.

Conversely, EPOS demonstrated the highest protective efficacy in the erosion-only (E) group. Under purely erosive conditions, abrasion did not influence the outcome, rendering the chemical composition of the dentifrices the decisive factor. Notably, only EPOS resulted in a statistically significant reduction in enamel loss compared with AS, whereas neither the conventional 1450-ppm fluoride dentifrice (CT) nor the high-fluoride dentifrice containing 5000 ppm fluoride (CD) differed significantly from the saliva control. CT contains sodium and stannous fluoride (1450 ppm) as active fluoride compound, whereas EPOS combines amine fluoride (1450 ppm) with additional functional ingredients, namely chitosan and stannous chloride. In contrast, CD is formulated exclusively with sodium fluoride at a substantially higher concentration (5000 ppm). The inclusion of chitosan and stannous chloride has been shown to confer measurable anti-erosive and anti-abrasive effects [14–16]. This is consistent with the formulation of EPOS, which has repeatedly demonstrated enhanced protective efficacy against erosive tooth wear and is therefore frequently used as a reference formulation in preventive dentistry research, which was also the case in the present study [14, 27].

Importantly, the present findings indicate that increasing fluoride concentration alone did not enhance protection against erosion, as the 5000-ppm fluoride dentifrice did not outperform the 1450-ppm anti-erosive formulation under erosion-only conditions. The present findings are in line with those reported by Amaral et al. [28], who also observed that increasing the fluoride concentration from 1450 to 5000 ppm did not result in superior protection against enamel erosion in a simulated oral environment. In their study, neither a high-fluoride NaF dentifrice nor a CPP-ACPF formulation was able to prevent enamel softening or surface alterations when compared with conventional fluoride toothpaste or placebo. Although both studies addressed erosion-related outcomes, important methodological differences exist between the studies. Amaral et al. assessed enamel changes primarily by microhardness, surface roughness, and qualitative SEM analysis, whereas the present study quantified actual enamel surface loss using contact profilometry, thereby focusing on irreversible tissue loss rather than surface softening alone. Furthermore, their experimental protocol involved short, repeated exposures to a cola drink combined with continuous artificial saliva flow over a prolonged period, while the present model employed defined citric acid challenges and distinctly separated erosion, abrasion, and erosion–abrasion conditions. However, and despite these differences in the methodology, both studies consistently indicated that increasing fluoride concentration alone does not necessarily enhance protection against erosive enamel damage, underscoring the importance of formulation-specific factors beyond nominal fluoride content. Moreover, and as stated above, enamel protection is not governed by fluoride concentration alone. Rather, formulation-dependent factors such as the presence of stannous ions, calcium and phosphate availability, particle characteristics, and wettability play a critical role [29]. This point is further supported by the present results, in which the 1450-ppm anti-erosive dentifrice demonstrated superior efficacy compared with the 5000-ppm NaF formulation, suggesting that multi-component systems may provide enhanced protection beyond increasing fluoride concentration alone.

It should be noted that, in comparison to enamel, dentin is significantly more prone to erosion and abrasion, whether isolated or combined [30]. Clinically, erosion results in dentin exposure, which is correlated with heightened hypersensitivity [31, 32]. Consequently, the findings of this study cannot be extrapolated to dentin.

While the present findings provide important insights, several methodological aspects of the present study should be considered when interpreting the results. For specimen preparation bovine incisor enamel was selected, as these teeth are widely regarded as a reliable proxy for human enamel in studies on erosive demineralization [33–36]. Additionally, these substrates were devoid of prior carious lesions or exposure to fluoridation, which are conditions known to potentially alter their mechanical and chemical characteristics. Although bovine enamel is more prone to erosive and erosive/abrasive challenges than human enamel [33], its use is justified in this study since only relative intra-group comparisons were evaluated. Furthermore, citric acid was used as the erosive agent, as it represents one of the most clinically relevant dietary acids, commonly present in fruit juices and soft drinks. This should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings.

From a clinical perspective, it should be considered that erosive tooth wear is a frequency-dependent process, in which repeated acid exposure plays a critical role in lesion progression. Consequently, preventive strategies should primarily focus on controlling etiological factors such as dietary acid intake and behavioural habits. In this context, the use of dentifrices represents an adjunctive measure rather than a primary preventive approach. Based on the present findings, formulation-specific effects may be more relevant than fluoride concentration alone. However, clinical management should prioritize patient education, modification of erosive challenges, and individualized preventive strategies.

Further aspects of the experimental design require critical consideration. The 60-second application time for dentifrice slurries and abrasion cycles were selected to approximate realistic oral contact times during tooth brushing, yet this duration may not fully replicate the dynamic and variable exposure in vivo. The choice of the Paro M43 medium-bristle toothbrush and the applied force of 2.5 N were aligned with common clinical brushing parameters [37], aiming to standardize abrasion while reflecting typical patient behaviour. Nonetheless, variations in brushing technique, force, and frequency among individuals could affect the generalizability of these findings.

Rios et al. [11] reported that the dilution of the dentifrice and brief fluoride application period might have prevented the formation of a calcium fluoride-like layer capable of protection against erosive or abrasive challenges. In their study, the toothpaste slurry was diluted with water rather than artificial saliva, which could have reduced the bioavailability of fluoride and impaired the development of a protective layer. Conversely, Magalhães et al. [38] used undiluted dentifrices instead of slurries, which may account for their more favourable results regarding the efficacy of fluoride-containing toothpastes. In our study, dentifrices were diluted with artificial saliva at realistic ratio designed to closely replicate in vivo conditions while toothbrushing [23], thereby providing a more physiologically relevant environment for assessing fluoride protective efficacy.

Several limitations inherent to the in vitro design of the present study should be acknowledged. Although the experimental protocol allowed for a certain degree of standardization and reproducibility, a controlled laboratory environment cannot fully reproduce the complex and dynamic conditions of the oral cavity. Factors such as the formation and maturation of the acquired pellicle, the presence of saliva with individual compositional variability, and patient-specific behavioural patterns (i.e., dietary habits and toothbrushing techniques) were not accounted for in this study. Moreover, the erosive and abrasive challenges applied in vitro represent rather simplified and time-compressed models that do not fully reflect the cumulative and adaptive processes occurring in vivo. Consequently, while the present findings provide valuable – mechanistic – insights into the protective effects of different dentifrice formulations under defined erosive and erosive–abrasive conditions, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results directly to clinical situations. Further in situ and clinical studies are required to confirm whether the observed effects translate into meaningful long-term protection against erosive tooth wear in patients.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study it can be concluded that a 5000 ppm fluoride dentifrice (Colgate Duraphat) did not demonstrate superior protective effects against erosive and erosive/abrasive enamel wear compared to an anti-erosive 1450 ppm formulation containing stannous chloride and chitosan (Elmex professional Opti-Schmelz). Clinically, the observed differences between the tested dentifrices were not considered to be relevant across the different enamel conditions evaluated. Hence, its clinical use may not confer further benefits for the prevention of erosion and abrasion under in vivo conditions. Future clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings and to guide evidence-based recommendations for managing erosive tooth wear.
