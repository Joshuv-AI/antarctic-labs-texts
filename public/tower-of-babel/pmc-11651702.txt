Single-Cell Analysis of Subcutaneous Fat Reveals Profibrotic Cells That Correlate With Visceral Adiposity in HIV
Authors: Samuel S Bailin, Curtis L Gabriel, Rama D Gangula, LaToya Hannah, Sangeeta Nair, John Jeffrey Carr, James G Terry, Heidi J Silver, Joshua D Simmons, Mona Mashayekhi, Spyros A Kalams, Simon Mallal, Jonathan A Kropski, Celestine N Wanjalla, John R Koethe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextCardiometabolic diseases are common in persons with HIV (PWH) on antiretroviral therapy (ART), which has been attributed to preferential lipid storage in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) compared with subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT). However, the relationship of SAT-specific cellular and molecular programs with VAT volume is poorly understood in PWH.ObjectiveWe characterized SAT cell-type specific composition and transcriptional programs that are associated with greater VAT volume in PWH on contemporary ART.MethodsWe enrolled PWH on long-term ART with a spectrum of metabolic health. Ninety-two participants underwent SAT biopsy for bulk RNA sequencing and 43 had single-cell RNA sequencing. Computed tomography quantified VAT volume and insulin resistance was calculated using the Homeostasis Model Assessment 2 Insulin Resistance (HOMA2-IR).ResultsVAT volume was associated with HOMA2-IR (P < .001). Higher proportions of SAT intermediate macrophages (IMs), myofibroblasts, and MYOC+ fibroblasts were associated with greater VAT volume using partial Spearman's correlation adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index (r = 0.34-0.49, P < .05 for all). Whole SAT transcriptomics showed PWH with greater VAT volume have increased expression of extracellular matrix (ECM)– and inflammation-associated genes, and reduced expression of lipolysis- and fatty acid metabolism–associated genes.ConclusionIn PWH, greater VAT volume is associated with a higher proportion of SAT IMs and fibroblasts, and a SAT ECM and inflammatory transcriptome, which is similar to findings in HIV-negative persons with obesity. These data identify SAT cell-type specific changes associated with VAT volume in PWH that could underlie the high rates of cardiometabolic diseases in PWH, though additional longitudinal studies are needed to define directionality and mechanisms.

Persons with HIV (PWH) on contemporary antiretroviral therapy (ART) have higher rates of cardiometabolic diseases than members of the general population of similar age and demographics, which is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality (1-3). Adipose tissue is important in regulating systemic metabolic health (4). Broadly, adipose tissue is categorized into 2 related depots, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), and changes in composition and distribution have been linked to the development of metabolic diseases (4). Greater VAT volume is associated with insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease in the general population and in PWH (5-8). PWH are particularly vulnerable to ectopic lipid deposition, which occurs progressively on ART and is greater in PWH than in HIV-negative persons with a similar body mass index (BMI) (7, 9-11). SAT in PWH is characterized by increased fibrosis (12, 13), greater macrophage-derived inflammatory cytokines (14), and infiltration of CD8+ T cells (15, 16) compared with persons without HIV. These SAT changes persist even with virologic suppression on contemporary ART (17-19). Detrimental changes in SAT function in PWH have been linked to systemic immune dysfunction, a persistent viral reservoir, and intestinal microbiota dysbiosis and translocation (20). Thus, understanding the SAT cellular and molecular transcriptional programs and its relationship with ectopic lipid deposition remains critically relevant research in PWH to reduce cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality.

SAT transcriptional and cellular changes are associated with obesity (as measured by BMI) and insulin resistance, though these data are primarily from HIV-negative populations and murine models (21-27). Obesity is characterized by increased extracellular matrix (ECM), increased inflammatory mediators, and increased infiltration of SAT by inflammatory macrophages and T cells that improve with weight loss in HIV-negative persons (22, 23, 28). The advent of single-cell (sc) and single-nuclei (sn) RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) has greatly improved the resolution of cell populations associated with obesity, including the identification of a lipid-associated macrophage (LAM) that is principally involved in metabolizing lipids (29-31). However, BMI is a poor measure of adiposity and metabolic risk (6, 32), and few studies have explored the relationship of SAT transcriptional profile with VAT volume. We have previously performed scRNA-seq on SAT to define the compositional and transcriptional programs associated with glucose intolerance in PWH (33). We found that a higher proportion of LAMs and intermediate macrophages (IMs) were present in the SAT of individuals with prediabetes and diabetes. Based on our previous work and studies in persons without HIV, we hypothesized that greater SAT macrophage and fibroblast composition, as well as global SAT inflammation and increased ECM expression, will be associated with higher VAT volume in PWH on contemporary ART in this descriptive study.

Materials and Methods

Cohort

Participants were members of the HIV, Adipose Tissue Immunology, and Metabolism (HATIM) study recruited to evaluate metabolic complications related to chronic immune activation in PWH from the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Comprehensive Care Clinic between August 2017 and June 2018 (ClinicalTrials.gov registration NCT04451980) (16, 34). Participants were on ART for ≥18 months, were virologically suppressed for ≥12 months, had a CD4+ T cell count ≥350 cells/mm3, had no known inflammatory or rheumatological conditions, and no reported excessive alcohol use or recreational drug use disorder. Participants were recruited in 3 groups according to metabolic health: no diabetes (hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] < 5.7% and/or fasting blood glucose [FBG] < 100 mg/dL), prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7-6.4% and/or FBG 100-125 mg/dL), or diabetes (HbA1c > 6.4%, FBG > 125 mg/dL, and/or on antidiabetic medications). All participants fasted for a minimum of 8 hours before collection of plasma FBG, HbA1c, and insulin. The Homeostasis Model Assessment 2 Insulin Resistance (HOMA2-IR) score was calculated for each participant using FBG and fasting plasma insulin. Anthropometric measurements were obtained at the same visit. The Vanderbilt Institutional Review Board approved the research, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Computed Tomography

Abdominal (diaphragm to lumbosacral junction) computed tomography (CT) was performed on a Siemens Somatom Force multidetector scanner using an imaging protocol and interpretation approach that has been published previously (35). Abdominal SAT and VAT volumes were measured within a 10-mm block composed of 8 images 1.25 mm thick at the L4 to L5 vertebrae using Osirix software (35, 36).

Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue Biopsy

Detailed methods have been published (16, 34). Briefly, SAT biopsies were collected 3 cm to the right of the umbilicus with a 2.1-mm blunt, side-ported liposuction catheter (Tulip CellFriendly GEMS system Miller Harvester, Tulip Medical Products). Tissue was placed in cold saline, visible blood clots were removed, and the sample was transferred to a 70-μm filter before dissociation using a gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) followed by incubation with collagenase D and cryopreservation as a cellular suspension.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Library Preparation

We used single-cell data and methods previously described (33). The single-cell raw and processed data are available on GEO using the accession number GSE198809 (37). Briefly, libraries were generated from single-cell SAT stromal vascular fraction suspensions without index cell sorting from a subset of individuals representative of the 3 metabolic groups. A Total Seq C mix containing 45 common markers was added to each sample and each sample was labeled with a unique hashtag antibody. Samples were multiplexed in groups of 4 to target 20 000 cells (5000 for each sample) and loaded onto a Chromium Single Cell 5′ assay (10× Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 s2 platform (Illumina), raw reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using STAR v. 2.7.2a, and cells were called using Cellranger count (v.6.0.0) with default settings. Souporcell was used to genetically demultiplex the samples and SoupX was used with default parameters to remove ambient RNA contamination (38, 39).

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Analysis

Seurat V4 was used for further analysis (40). Cells with >25% mitochondrial gene transcripts, <800 transcript reads, and <200 genes were filtered out. DoubletFinder was used to identify heterotypic doublets (41). Cells that were identified by DoubletFinder as doublets, cells expressing genes from 2 or more major cell lineages, clusters with >60% of cells identified as doublets, or homotypic doublets identified by Souporcell were removed. Downstream processing, including feature selection, principal component analysis, dimension reduction, nearest neighbor embedding, and cluster identification, was performed using Seurat. We used the harmony algorithm to integrate across lanes (42). We performed manual annotations based on canonical markers and markers previously identified in scRNA-seq datasets. Cluster gene markers were obtained using Wilcoxon Rank Sum in FindMarkers as implemented in Seurat including only genes with a minimum log2 fold change of 0.25 difference between cell types and expressed in a minimum of 10% of cells.

Bulk RNA Sequencing

The raw FASTQ files and processed count file is available on GEO using the accession number GSE243957. Total RNA was extracted from cryopreserved SAT samples. First, adipose tissue was homogenized using the Qiagen TissueRuptor in Qiazol Lysis Reagent. RNA was purified using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini kit. The manufacturer’s instructions were followed except after tissue homogenization: chloroform extraction was performed with 1/5 the volume of chloroform, the sample was shaken vigorously for 20 seconds, and then spun for 15 minutes at 10 000g. The gDNA eliminator column was not used. Messenger RNA (mRNA) enrichment was performed using NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB), and the sequencing library was constructed by using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB) following the manufacturer's instructions (NEB #E7765). Samples were sequenced in 2 separate runs on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) using paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp). Around 50 to 80 million pairs of reads were generated from each library. Read quality was assessed and adapter read-through sequences were trimmed using fastp v0.20.1 (43). The reads were mapped to Human GENCODE release 34 CRCh38 using STAR v2.7.3a (44). The parameters for STAR run were “STAR --runThreadN 8 --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate --quantMode GeneCounts.” FeatureCounts was used to assign reads to features using GENCODE v34 annotation as the reference with the following parameters (default unless specified): -p -s 2 -T 5 (45). We performed quality control by looking at alignment parameters. Additionally, we used sample distance matrices and principal component visualization to identify outlier samples that were removed before downstream analyses.

Compositional Analyses

To determine the predictors of VAT volume, we used an ordinal regression model with VAT volume as the dependent variable and age, BMI, sex, self-identified race (White/other), and thymidine analog exposure (yes/no) as predictor variables using the R package RMS. Race was self-identified and used in the model as it has previously been shown to be a predictor of VAT volume and may capture important unknown socioeconomic factors (46). We adjusted for BMI since this is a commonly used measure but is not as closely linked to metabolic disease risk as VAT volume (47). The results were converted to an odds ratio from the 25th to 75th percentile of the outcome. Optimism was calculated by resampling for 500 repetitions. Calibration plots were generated through bootstrapping applied to an ordinary least squares model to get bias-corrected estimates vs observed values. The relationship of VAT volume with FBG, HOMA2-IR, HbA1c, and triglyceride: high-density lipoprotein ratio was assessed using an ordinal regression model including only individuals without diabetes with glucose measurement as the dependent variable and VAT volume as the independent variable adjusted for age, BMI, sex, self-identified race, and older thymidine analog exposure.

To determine the relationship of SAT cell proportion and VAT volume, we used partial Spearman's correlations as implemented in the R package PResiduals (48), adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. We also performed sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with thymidine analog exposure or diabetes, or additionally adjusting for self-identified race, or integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) use. Samples with less than 30 total cells were excluded from analysis due to variability with low cell numbers. For secondary analyses, we used a linear modeling-based algorithm as implemented in Propeller to evaluate the relationship of all cell types in SAT to scaled and log2-transformed VAT volume in both an unadjusted model and a model adjusted for age, sex, and scaled log2-transformed BMI (49). The P values are derived from the logit-transformed model and cell proportions were derived from the untransformed model as recommended by the authors.

Transcriptomic Analyses

We used DIALOGUE to evaluate whether VAT volume is associated with a coordinated transcriptional program among cell types in SAT (50). DIALOGUE was run with default settings. The multilevel models were fit by VAT volume (high or low) and adjusted for technical variability, sex, age, and BMI. Averaged scaled expression of top genes from multicellular program 1 were sorted by expression and cell type (columns), and samples were plotted with ward D2 hierarchical clustering (rows) and labeled with clinical data including VAT volume using ComplexHeatmap (51). For intercellular ligand–ligand receptor analysis, we used CellChat as implemented in R (52). We compared the highest VAT tertile with the lowest VAT tertile by performing cell communication analysis separately on each dataset. Communication probabilities were calculated with population.size set to “true” and only included genes with expression in 10% or greater of cells in the dataset. Predicted communications were retained in only those with a minimum of 10 cells. We then compared the signaling patterns between the groups, with a specific focus on signaling to and from IMs with stromal cells.

To evaluate whole tissue differential gene expression, we used DESeq2 with the output of FeatureCounts as the input count matrix (53). We split VAT volume into tertiles and adjusted the model for sex, age, BMI, self-identified race, and batch. Gene counts were normalized and genes with ≥10 counts in ≥2 samples were retained. We compared the highest VAT volume tertile with the lowest VAT volume tertile to generate log2 fold change. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjimani–Hochberg. Wiki gene set enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler implemented in R with log2 fold change as the rank (54). We performed pathway activity inference using decoupleR (55). We used the get_progeny function to retrieve Pathway Responsive Genes for activity inference (PROGENy) from OmniPath and used run_wmean to run the weighted mean method with a list of differentially expressed genes between high VAT volume vs low VAT volume ranked by the Wald statistic. We also ran a sensitivity model excluding participants with diabetes and evaluated VAT volume as a continuous variable adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and self-identified race. VAT volume and BMI were scaled and log2-transformed.

We mapped significantly (false discovery rate at 5%) differentially expressed genes from the bulk transcriptomic analysis onto the SAT scRNA-seq dataset. The average normalized expression of differentially expressed genes for each cell type was calculated using the AverageExpression function in Seurat. The scaled expression (row) was plotted by cell type (column) in a heatmap using Ward D2 hierarchical clustering in ComplexHeatmaps. The genes positively associated with VAT volume were used to calculate an average expression score using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. We repeated this for genes (false discovery rate at 10%) that were part of the ECM organization Reactome pathway. Finally, we performed Spearman's correlations between the proportion of IMs and myofibroblasts and gene expression for those in select KEGG (Adipocytokine signaling, has04920; Fatty acid degradation, has00071; Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, has04923; JAK-STAT signaling pathway, has04630) and Reactome (ECM organization, R-HAS-1474244) pathways. Read counts were normalized to counts per million (cpm), and genes with cpm greater or equal to 1 in more than 50% of samples were retained using edgeR. cpm were log2-transformed with a prior count of 2.

Results

Cohort Description

A total of 112 participants had CT measurements of VAT. The median age was 49 years, median BMI was 31.5 kg/m2, 76% were male, and 48% self-identified as non-White non-Hispanic (Table 1). Most individuals were on INSTI-based regimens (63%), the median CD4 count was 836 cell/mm3, and 18% had exposure to older thymidine analogs. Compared with people without diabetes, individuals with diabetes were older (P < .001), had a higher BMI (P = .005), had greater VAT volume (P < .001), and higher measures of glucose and insulin resistance (P < .001).

Visceral Adipose Tissue Volume Is Associated With Insulin Resistance

Older age, male sex, higher BMI, self-identified White race, and thymidine analog exposure were all significantly associated with greater VAT volume in an ordinal regression model (overall r = 0.70, R2 = 0.51, P < .001) (Table 2; Table S1 (56)). Greater SAT volume was associated with younger age, higher BMI, and female sex, but not race or thymidine analog exposure (overall r = 0.71, R2 = 0.52, P < .001). In an analysis limited to PWH without diabetes, VAT volume was significantly associated with FBG (bcoef = 0.012, OR 2.8, P = .003) and HOMA2-IR (bcoef = 0.025, OR 8.8, P < .001) but not HbA1c (bcoef = 0.0035, P = .39) (Fig. 1A-1C; Tables S2 and S3) (56). Finally, we found that the ratio of triglyceride to HDL, which is higher in individuals with diabetes (57), was significantly associated with VAT volume (bcoef = 0.017, OR 4.4, P < .001) (Fig. 1D; Table S4 (56)). SAT volume was not associated with FBG, HOMA2-IR, or HbA1c. Taken together, our findings show VAT volume is significantly associated with HOMA2-IR, FBG, and higher TG/HDL ratio but not HbA1c in PWH even after adjustment for BMI.

Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue MYOC+ Fibroblast, Myofibroblast, and Intermediate Macrophage Proportions are Independently Associated With Visceral Adipose Tissue Volume

Among our study participants, a subgroup of 43 individuals with CT measurements of VAT volume had SAT biopsies and scRNA-seq of SVF (no index cell sorting) as part of a SAT single-cell atlas (33). Based on our previous work (58), we hypothesized that that SAT macrophages and fibroblast composition are associated with greater VAT volume. The SAT cell types were identified using variable feature selection, principal component analysis, dimension reduction, shared nearest-neighbor graph construction, clustering, and annotation based on literature defined gene markers as described previously (33). These included SPP1+ LAMs (TREM2 and SPP1), IMs (APOE and APOC1), perivascular M2-like macrophages (PVMs) (LYVE1 and MRC1), and monocyte-derived macrophages (VCAN and HLA-DRA) (Fig. 2A and 2B). Similarly, we identified PCOLCE+ fibroblasts (CD55, PCOLCE2, and DPP4), MYOC+ fibroblasts (MYOC and IGFB7), and myofibroblasts (POSTN and TIMP1) (Fig. 2C and 2D).

We first used partial Spearman's correlation to assess the relationship between cell proportion and VAT volume adjusted for age, BMI, and sex. Higher proportions of MYOC+ fibroblasts (r = 0.49, P = .003), myofibroblasts (r = 0.48, P = .003), and IMs (r = 0.34, P = .03) were significantly associated with VAT volume. The proportions of PVMs and PCOLCE+ fibroblasts, however, were not associated with VAT volume (Table 3 and Fig. 3A-3D). As hypothesis generation, we also assessed the relationship of VAT volume with all other SAT cell types using linear-based modeling as implemented in the R package Propeller, with VAT volume treated as a continuous variable in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, which yielded similar results to Spearman's analysis (Supplementary Data (56)). In sensitivity analyses, the addition of self-identified race, INSTI use, as well as the exclusion of individuals with older thymidine analog exposure or diabetes did not substantively change the primary results (Tables S5-S8 (56)). Female sex was associated with lower proportion of SAT IMs and myofibroblasts, but this was considerably attenuated once the models were adjusted for VAT volume (Tables S9 and S10 (56)). SAT volume was not associated with the proportion of IMs, LAMS, myofibroblasts, or MYOC+ fibroblasts but was inversely associated with PCOLCE+ fibroblasts (r = −0.36, P = .05).

Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue Regulatory Transcriptional Profile is Shifted Towards Lipid Metabolism, Inflammation, and Fibrosis With Greater VAT Volume

We next assessed whether there is a coordinated gene expression program among cell types that identifies individuals with greater VAT volume. We used DIALOGUE, which identifies highly correlated gene expression across cell types, and then applied a generalized linear model to assess whether this pattern is associated with VAT volume (50). One advantage of assessing for expression programs across cell types is increased sensitivity to detect subtle shifts in cell populations that may be missed with compositional analyses. We included macrophages, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and fibroblasts. We also included progenitor and preadipocyte cells since these are closely related to the fibroblast population. VAT volume was significantly associated with Multicellular Program 1 after adjusting for BMI, age, and sex where individuals with high VAT volume had a negative score and individuals with low VAT volume had a positive score (Fig. 4A). Pathway analysis of the downregulated compartment (associated with higher VAT volume) showed enrichment of genes related to neutrophil degranulation, leukocyte migration and proliferation, and response to interferon-γ, whereas pathway analysis of the upregulated compartment (lower VAT volume) showed enrichment of genes related to the response to oxidative stress, response to unfolded proteins, and T cell activation (Fig. 4B). Individuals with higher VAT volume had enrichment of inflammatory and cytotoxic genes in CD4 T cells (GZMK, IFITM3, LGALS3, IFNG, GBP5, IL32, CCL5, KLRG1), macrophage metabolic reprogramming (AREG, TREM2), and ECM genes in preadipocytes, fibroblasts, and progenitors (TIMP1, BGN, TMEM176A, TMSB4X, A2M) (Fig. 4C). Individuals with lower VAT volume had enrichment for cell migration (CCR7, SELL), chemokines and alternative macrophage polarization (CCL2, CCL8, FOSB, CEBPB, MAFB), adipogenesis (EGR1, EGFR, FOSB, KLF4, PDGFRA, ZFP36L2), and stem cell marker CD34.

Intercellular signaling has an important role in establishing cell function in both health and disease. For hypothesis generation, we performed intercellular signaling communication analyses and compared persons in the highest VAT tertile volume with persons in the lowest VAT tertile volume. Overall, persons with greater VAT volume had a greater number of interactions and strength of interactions than those with lower VAT volume (Fig. 5A). Prioritizing IMs and fibroblasts, persons with higher VAT had enhanced signaling through several well-known pathways including transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), chemerin, and canonical WNT pathways from and to stromal cells as compared to persons with lower VAT volumes (Fig. 5B-5F). Identified cells types involved in TGF-β (sender: IM, target: PCOLCE+ fibroblast, PVM, LAM), Wnt (sender: PCOLCE+ fibroblast, target: preadipocyte), and chemerin (sender: preadipocyte, target: IM) signaling have biological plausibility and have been previously implicated in regulation of adipogenesis, fibrosis, and inflammation (59-61). Thus, changes in cellular composition and signaling of macrophage and fibroblast-like cell populations may mediate whole tissue transcriptional changes that are associated with VAT volume.

Whole Tissue Transcriptomics Reveals Proinflammatory, Profibrotic, and Altered Adipocyte Expression Programs

To gain a better understanding of the global regulatory programs in SAT, including mature adipocyte transcripts, accompanying greater VAT volume, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on SAT. A total of 92 PWH with CT imaging also had bulk RNA-seq data that passed quality control. As VAT volume is a continuous variable, we created tertiles and compared the highest VAT tertile with the lowest VAT tertile adjusting for age, sex, self-identified race, sequencing batch, and BMI. We found 358 genes and 509 genes with higher and lower expression, respectively, and adjusted P < .1 (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Data (56)). We first performed gene set enrichment analysis using the WikiPathway. The top enriched pathways associated with greater VAT volume include proinflammatory and profibrotic mediators, TYROBP causal network in microglia, and toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 6B). In contrast, fatty acid beta-oxidation, amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathways had a negative enrichment score (Fig. 6B). Important genes related to adipocyte maturation had reduced expression including ADIPOQ, PPARG, and PLIN1, as well as genes related to lipolysis (LPL, NPR1, PNPLA2, IRS1, PDE3B, GNAI1) (Padj < .1) (Fig. 6C). Genes related to ECM regulation had higher expression, including TNC, FN1, MMP9, SFRP2, BGN, MFAP5, ADAMTSL2, TIMP1, COL14A1, PDGFA. Additionally, several chemokines and interleukins had higher expression including IL10, IL34, CCL13, and CCL3. Pathway inference showed activation of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), NFKB, EGFR, TGF-β, and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) in persons with greater VAT volume (Fig. 6D). These results did not change substantially after adjusting for INSTI use or thymidine analogue exposure. Since diabetes may be associated with a treatment effect, we reran the model excluding individuals with diabetes and observed a significantly greater number of differentially expressed genes (n = 2168), but similar pathway enrichment (Supplementary Data (56)). Finally, additionally adjusting for HOMA2-IR attenuated the effect size of many genes related to VAT volume, but several genes were still significant, including GLUT4, CD163, MRC1, COL6A6, and CD40. In contrast, SAT volume was associated with very few differentially expressed genes. Taken together with the single-cell data, the global transcriptomic data suggest greater macrophage related inflammation, reduced lipolysis and maturation of adipocytes, and increasing fibrotic signaling in SAT is associated with greater VAT volume in PWH.

Differentially Expressed Genes Map to Cell Types Identified in Single-Cell Analyses Including Intermediate Macrophages and Fibroblasts

We next mapped the differentially expressed genes from bulk RNA-seq data onto cell types identified by scRNA-seq to determine which cell types are potentially responsible for transcriptional changes. Preadipocytes and adipose progenitor cells generally mapped to genes that are reduced with greater VAT volumes, while MYOC+ fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and cycling myofibroblasts express genes that are higher with greater VAT volumes (Fig. 7A). Additionally, myeloid cells generally expressed genes that are higher with greater VAT volumes, including IMs and LAMs. The average scaled expression program for high VAT volume identified these same cell types (Fig. 7B). Finally, differentially expressed genes (Padj < .1) that were in the ECM reactome pathway showed high expression in myofibroblasts and MYOC+ fibroblasts (Fig. 7C). We also found that the proportion of myofibroblast and IM were associated with several genes related to lipolysis, adipocyte signaling, free fatty acid metabolism, and ECM pathways (Supplementary Data (56)).

Discussion

In a contemporary cohort of PWH on stable, long-term ART, we used scRNA-seq to show that a greater VAT volume is associated with a greater proportion of SAT IMs, myofibroblasts, and MYOC+ fibroblasts. This compositional difference is accompanied by a coordinated SAT cell expression program enriched for inflammatory- and ECM-associated genes. Finally, the global SAT transcriptional profile suggests altered adipogenesis and lipolysis, as well as enhanced ECM production and inflammation, are associated with greater VAT volume, with several differentially expressed genes mapping to cell populations identified in the scRNA-seq. Together, these results imply coordinated changes in SAT transcriptome and ectopic lipid deposition, which may in part underlie the risk for cardiometabolic disease in persons with HIV. While our findings share some similarities with prior analyses of HIV-negative cohorts, future studies that concurrently recruit individuals with and without HIV are needed to define differences more fully.

VAT volume has been closely linked with metabolic disease in studies of both PWH and HIV-negative persons (5, 7). In individuals with obesity, VAT volume is more closely associated with insulin resistance than BMI alone (62). Yet in many contemporary studies of SAT transcriptome and single-cell composition, obesity defined by BMI is often used as the outcome. In our study, VAT volume was a major predictor of HOMA2-IR in persons without diabetes and explained nearly 43% of the variance, while BMI was not significantly predictive of HOMA2-IR after adjustment for VAT volume. The current cohort included persons who were overweight or had obesity, so the relationship between VAT volume and insulin resistance may differ at lower BMIs and a larger cohort including individuals with lower BMI would be necessary to address this question. BMI is recognized as an imperfect measure of adiposity as it does not discriminate between lean and fat mass or quantify regional differences in fat distribution (47). PWH are highly susceptible to ectopic lipid deposition and have greater VAT volume than persons without HIV with similar BMI (7). Thus, our results adjusted for BMI are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that measures of body fat distribution, as opposed to body weight, are necessary to accurately categorize a person's risk for metabolic disease.

The proportions of SAT IMs, MYOC+ fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts were associated with VAT but not abdominal SAT volume in the current study. IMs have low expression of SPP1 and are likely analogous to a recently described pre-LAM population in mice that expand with high-fat diet and early obesity, though further phenotypic characterizations in humans are necessary (63). Furthermore, TREM2 (LAM marker) and AREG (metabolic reprogramming) were associated with a high VAT volume expression program (29, 64). These findings are consistent with murine and HIV-negative human studies that have identified LAMs as a dominant macrophage subtype in obesity as defined by BMI (29). LAMs may have a protective role in SAT. TREM2 deficient mice had substantial adipocyte hypertrophy, elevated insulin levels, and elevated circulating low-density lipoprotein and cholesterol when metabolically challenged, suggesting a role for metabolic homeostasis (29). Thus, one possibility is that alterations in SAT cellular composition and transcriptional profile may represent a compensatory response to metabolic stress and are not casually related to VAT volume expansion. Another possibility is that reduced ability of SAT to store lipids may directly lead to ectopic fat deposition and VAT expansion. Future mechanistic and longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the relationship between SAT and VAT in PWH.

In this study, a significant proportion of MKI67+ proliferating macrophages express TREM2+, suggesting that these cells may accumulate at least in part by self-renewal. The preadipocyte and fibroblast single-cell transcriptional profiles were strongly associated with VAT volume and express several genes previously associated with obesity, adipose tissue inflammation, and fibrosis, including MIF (65), IL34 (66), PDGFD (67), and TIMP1 (68). Transcriptional analysis from single-cell data is limited by nonbiological gene drop out and inability to capture mature adipocytes. The bulk RNA sequencing confirmed that persons with higher VAT volume had reduced expression of genes important for fatty acid metabolism and lipolysis and increased expression of genes related to ECM and chemokines. Pathway inference shows activity of TNF- α, which is known to promote insulin resistance and impair fatty acid metabolism of adipocytes (69). Our findings of increased SAT ECM and inflammation-related gene expression and reduced insulin-responsive genes (GLUT4, IRS1) with greater VAT volume are similar to the findings with obesity and weight loss surgery studies in HIV-negative individuals (22, 23, 70, 71). In contrast to prior studies, we were further able to map differentially expressed genes onto the scRNA-seq populations, which highlighted the same cell types with compositional differences by VAT volume.

Overall, we found similar global transcriptional differences with VAT volume that has been observed with obesity (defined by BMI) in HIV-negative persons. These results do not rule out the possibility of unique cellular and molecular pathways operating in PWH. Alternatively, unique metabolic challenges associated with treatment of HIV and HIV itself could underlie the increased risk for ectopic lipid deposition and metabolic disease that act through similar pathways as metabolic disease in persons without HIV. Untreated HIV is associated with increased resting basal metabolism and increased protein catabolism, which promote weight loss (72, 73), and the overall reduction in body fat may mask any concomitant shift towards metabolic dysregulation. Once ART is initiated, the excess energy expenditure of HIV is rapidly reversed, and in an obesogenic environment, excess weight gain and VAT expansion occurs. This is supported by a large study incorporating data from thousands of individuals across the United States, which found body weight among PWH after 3 years of ART treatment was equivalent to similar members of the general population (74).

Several studies suggest that HIV-specific changes to adipose tissue are a major contributor to metabolic disease. HIV viral proteins including Vpr can disrupt PPARG, a critical regulator of adipogenesis, and promote metabolic complications which persist despite suppression of plasma viremia (17). Additionally, in rhesus macaques infected with SIV, SAT and VAT have decreased adipogenesis and advanced fibrosis similar to PWH on ART, mediated partly through Tat or Nef-induced fibrosis (12). In ACTG study A5317, ART treatment reduced SAT fibrosis over a 48-week period, suggesting some reversibility of fibrosis, though the stability of this change and whether it influences fat partitioning is not clear (75). Finally, some evidence suggests that INSTI-based regimens may be profibrotic in adipose tissue (76). Further studies evaluating persons before and after ART initiation and animal models are needed to untangle these complex relationships and determine whether the development of metabolic disease is unique or shared with persons without HIV.

One interesting finding is that self-identified non-White race was associated with lower VAT volume, which has been shown in other studies (77). Most self-identified non-White individuals in our cohort were Black, with very few self-identified Asians. Interestingly, in the bulk RNA sequencing data, the inclusion of self-identified race attenuated the relationship of many genes that were associated with VAT volume. The etiology of this finding is unclear and whether this represents an unmeasured socioeconomic or environmental factor needs further investigation.

Intercellular communication analysis similarly supported enhanced inflammatory and fibrotic pathway signaling in persons with greater VAT volume. In particular, TGF-β signaling from IMs to PCOLCE+ fibroblasts was enhanced with greater VAT volume. Prior studies have shown CD206+ M2-like macrophages in mice attenuate progenitor cell proliferation and differentiation and promote glucose intolerance through TGF-β signaling (78). PVMs are CD206+ and are a source of TGF-β in our study. However, IMs also express MRC1 (CD206+) and greater proportion is associated with VAT volume, suggesting that these cell types might contribute to greater signaling to progenitor cells in individuals with higher VAT volume. Finally, we also found enhanced signaling between IMs and preadipocytes through chemerin, which has been implicated in the formation of desmoplastic structures in cancer (61). This signaling involved only IMs and did not involve SSP1+ LAMs. These results are predictions and will need to be experimentally validated, but provide cell-specific and pathway-specific information to inform future studies.

This study has several strengths. This is a large scRNA-seq cohort with well-phenotype metabolic outcomes that also has morphometric analyses to accurately determine VAT volume. Additionally, we performed bulk RNA sequencing to understand the whole tissue transcriptomic regulation, account for technical gene drop out with scRNA-seq platforms, and capture mature adipocyte transcripts. We performed abdominal CT imaging to quantify the volume of VAT, which is a much stronger correlate of metabolic health compared to BMI (the metric used in most prior SAT single-cell studies). Additionally, our cohort was representative of populations most at risk for HIV and included a substantial number of women. However, there are also some limitations. The cross-sectional design identified associations but cannot assign cause or directionality and therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed. Second, scRNA-seq does not capture mature adipocytes, which are very important for AT function. We included mature adipocytes using bulk RNA-sequencing but future studies isolating single-nuclei are needed to define the mature adipocyte population transcriptional profile. Third, intercellular communication analysis does not control for possible confounders so we cannot rule out that interactions we identified might be influenced by other factors and these findings will need to be experimentally validated. Fourth, as discussed above, we do not have HIV-negative controls to understand whether these findings are unique to PWH. While this is an important question, we were principally interested in understanding the pathophysiology of cardiometabolic diseases in PWH, a group at exceedingly high risk of poor health outcomes. Future studies incorporating HIV-negative controls, difficult to recruit for studies of PWH, are necessary given the few similar studies like this that have been performed in HIV-negative persons. Fifth, we did not observe any effect of INSTI use or thymidine analog exposure on SAT transcriptional profile. The use of newer generation INSTI and few individuals on thymidine analog in this study might explain this observation (79). Lastly, diabetes treatment may affect SAT gene expression. We performed sensitivity analyses by excluding individuals with diabetes and found a similar transcriptional program.

Leveraging bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq, and CT morphometric data in a cross-sectional descriptive cohort of PWH on contemporary ART, we found greater expression of genes involved in ECM and inflammation and reduced expression of genes related to fatty acid metabolism and lipolysis with greater VAT volume, implicating SAT IMs and fibroblast populations as major contributors for these observed changes. Investigation of these cell types in SAT may uncover mechanisms contributing to ectopic fat deposition and identify novel therapeutic or preventative strategies for cardiometabolic disease in PWH.
