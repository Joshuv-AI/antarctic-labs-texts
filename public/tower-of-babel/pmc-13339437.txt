Anifrolumab Dose Regimen Selection for a Phase 3 Trial in Lupus Nephritis
Authors: Joachim Almquist, Yen Lin Chia, Raj Tummala, Lynn Bertagnolli, David Jayne, Weifeng Tang, Catharina Lindholm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), increased type I interferon signaling is associated with increased disease activity across organs, including in the kidney, which can cause lupus nephritis (LN). Anifrolumab, which abrogates type I interferon signaling, is an approved treatment for moderate to severe SLE. In a phase 2 trial in patients with LN (TULIP‐LN; NCT02547922), promising clinical benefit was observed with an intensified regimen (IR) of intravenous anifrolumab (3× 900 mg Q4W, 300 mg thereafter) compared with the approved SLE dose (basic regimen [BR], 300 mg Q4W). Anifrolumab clearance was greater with higher levels of proteinuria; however, the quantitative relationship between proteinuria and anifrolumab exposure was not fully characterized. Here, we describe a mathematical model of time‐varying anifrolumab pharmacokinetics, 24‐hour urine protein–creatinine ratio (UPCR24), and investigational product discontinuation in patients with LN. The model evaluated both TULIP‐LN anifrolumab dosing regimens (IR and BR) and revealed a temporal association between clearance and proteinuria in patients with LN, with each mg/mg UPCR reduction resulting in a 21% decrease in linear anifrolumab clearance. Our model indicated that an intensified regimen including 6 initial anifrolumab 900 mg doses provided adequate exposure and rapid UPCR24 reduction. Higher anifrolumab exposure and reduced UPCR24 were each associated with decreased risk of treatment discontinuation. These model results guided the selection of an optimized, longer intensified anifrolumab dosing regimen (6× 900 mg Q4W, 300 mg thereafter) for the ongoing phase 3 IRIS trial (NCT05138133).

Anifrolumab is a human IgG1κ monoclonal antibody (mAb) approved for the treatment of adult patients with moderate to severe systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [1, 2]. Anifrolumab abrogates type I interferon (IFN) signaling by blocking the type I interferon alpha receptor subunit 1 (IFNAR1) and reducing the type I IFN gene signature (IFNGS), which is a marker of SLE disease activity [1, 2, 3]. Anifrolumab has been demonstrated to reduce disease activity and glucocorticoid burden in patients with non‐renal SLE [1, 4, 5]. Elevated IFNGS is observed in approximately 80% of patients with lupus nephritis (LN), one of the most common severe clinical manifestations of SLE [6, 7]. Multiple lines of evidence support the role of dysregulated type I IFN signaling in LN driving kidney inflammation, proteinuria, and renal damage, [8, 9] justifying the investigation of anifrolumab as an LN treatment.

The TULIP‐LN trial was a phase 2 study of anifrolumab or placebo alongside standard therapy in patients with LN (NCT02547922) [10]. Based on findings with other biologics in patients with kidney diseases where higher clearance was associated with higher proteinuria, [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17] the TULIP‐LN study design accommodated for the possibility that elevated proteinuria in patients with LN may result in suboptimal anifrolumab exposure due to increased clearance. As such, TULIP‐LN included an intensified regimen (IR) in addition to the basic regimen (BR) of anifrolumab dosing that is approved for SLE (300 mg IV Q4W) [18]. In the IR, 3 initial higher doses of anifrolumab preceded the approved SLE anifrolumab dosing—that is, patients in the IR group received 3× 900 mg IV Q4W, then 300 mg thereafter [10]—with the aim of increasing anifrolumab exposure while proteinuria is highest (i.e., before it can be reduced by treatment). There were no differences in reported safety events assessed as possibly related to investigational product (IP) by investigators between patients with LN who received IR and BR anifrolumab dosing in TULIP‐LN [10].

Despite inclusion of the IR, the TULIP‐LN study failed to reach the primary endpoint of relative difference in the mean change in 24‐hour urine protein–creatinine ratio (UPCR24) from baseline to Week 52 for the combined anifrolumab IR and BR groups compared with placebo, as mean UPCR24 improved similarly from baseline in these groups (by 69% and 70%, respectively) [10]. However, the anifrolumab IR was associated with numerically greater proportions of patients attaining a complete renal response at Week 52 compared with placebo (46% vs. 31%); no such effect was seen with the BR [10]. Pharmacokinetic (PK) results revealed inadequate anifrolumab exposure in both the BR and IR groups in the first 24–36 weeks of treatment [10]. Specifically, BR dosing led to serum concentrations in patients with LN that were ~ 50% lower than with equivalent dosing in patients with SLE [10]. This suboptimal PK exposure with anifrolumab BR in patients with LN was reflected by incomplete pharmacodynamic (PD) IFNGS neutralization, which could be expected to reflect reduced efficacy [10, 19]. An additional challenge in interpreting results for the TULIP‐LN trial was the large proportion of patients who discontinued the IP early (with greater discontinuation rates with placebo [43%] than in the anifrolumab groups [BR, 29%; IR, 20%]), partly driven by the study design requiring that early glucocorticoid tapering targets were met to allow continued IP dosing, which may also have confounded results [10].

These findings suggested that extending the duration of initial 900 mg dosing was a promising step toward an optimized LN dose. However, there remained an unmet need for a deeper quantitative understanding of the relationship between proteinuria, anifrolumab exposure, and the drivers of patient dropout, in order to optimize the anifrolumab dose regimen and drug exposure for a phase 3 LN trial. It was important to determine whether extending the duration of the anifrolumab IR could achieve adequate anifrolumab exposure and/or a greater UPCR24 response. It was also of interest to quantify the impact of the anifrolumab dose regimen on patient dropout. A pharmacometric analysis was pursued to address these outstanding questions.

Here, we describe mathematical modeling of time‐varying anifrolumab PK, UPCR24, and investigational product discontinuation in patients with LN. Specifically, we investigated whether prolonging the anifrolumab IR can provide adequate exposure and allow more patients to attain a UPCR24 response and quantified the impact of a prolonged anifrolumab IR on patient dropout. Our results allowed for model‐informed dose selection for the IRIS phase 3 study of anifrolumab treatment for patients with LN receiving standard therapy [20].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trial design

TULIP‐LN was a phase 2, multicenter, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of anifrolumab in addition to standard therapy, including mycophenolate mofetil and glucocorticoids (GCs), in adults with LN [10]. Eligible patients had biopsy‐proven diagnosis of active proliferating Class III or IV (± coexistent Class V) LN, according to the 2003 International Society of Nephrology (ISN)/Renal Pathology Society (RPS) criteria within 3 months of screening (UPCR24 > 1 mg/mg [113.17 mg/mmol]; eGFR ≥ 35 mL/min/1.73m2) and fulfilled the updated 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) SLE classification criteria. The modified intention‐to‐treat population in TULIP‐LN included 145 patients who were randomized 1:1:1 to receive either anifrolumab BR (300 mg IV Q4W; n = 45), IR (3× 900 mg IV Q4W, then 300 mg thereafter; n = 51), or placebo (IV Q4W; n = 49) for 52 weeks alongside standard therapy; eligible patients could continue receiving treatment into Year 2, with a final efficacy assessment at Week 104 [21]. Key patient demographics (Table S1 ) and baseline characteristics in the TULIP‐LN study have been previously published [10].

Software

Estimation and simulation were performed using NONMEM 7.3.0 (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, Maryland); additional details, including the NONMEM model, are provided in the Supplementary Methods .

PK‐UPCR model

The PK part of the PK‐UPCR model was based on a previously established population PK (popPK) model in patients with SLE. Due to the limited and sparse PK data in patients with LN compared with patients with non‐renal SLE, intercompartmental clearance, Q, and steady‐state constant, KSS, were fixed to previously estimated values. Inter‐individual variability (IIV) of the parameter VP was omitted, and we redefined anifrolumab linear clearance to omit the empirical time‐varying factor and included a dependence on the time‐dependent model state variable describing UPCR24, Ut, according to: (1) CL=CLTV×FIFN×FWT×FUPCR×eη. Here, CLTV is the typical value of the clearance and η is a random effect parameter. The factor FIFN captures the effect of the IFN test on clearance, with FIFN=1 for patients with high baseline IFN gene signature test results (IFNGS‐high) and FIFN=CLIFNLOW for IFNGS‐low patients, where CLIFNLOW is an estimated parameter. The factor FWT captures the effect of body weight on clearance according to: (2) FWT=BW63.1CLWT, where CLWT is an estimated parameter, and the reference value 63.1 is the observed typical body weight in patients with LN. The factor FUPCR captures the time‐dependent change in clearance due to UPCR dynamics, according to: (3) FUPCR=1+CLUPCR×Ut−2.53, where CLUPCR, describing the relative change in anifrolumab clearance per mg/mg change in UPCR24, is an estimated parameter, Ut is the time‐dependent UPCR24, and the reference value 2.53 is the observed typical baseline UPCR24 in patients with LN in phase 2. Based on the objective function value (OFV), a linear model was selected from among a linear model, a power model, and an exponential model.

UPCR24 dynamics were described by the differential equation: (4) dUtdt=Eat×log2k×1−Es×Ubx−Utx, where Eat is the time‐dependent effect of anifrolumab concentrations on the UPCR24 dynamics, k is a characteristic time parameter, Es is the steady state effect of standard therapy on the UPCR24 dynamics, Ub is the baseline UPCR24, and x is a shape parameter. The shape parameter x introduces flexibility that allows UPCR24 dynamics to deviate from perfect exponential behavior. This is a phenomenological, parsimonious model chosen to capture non‐exponential behavior observed in the data. The time‐dependent effect of anifrolumab on UPCR24 dynamics was further defined as: (5) Eat=1+a×ct, where a is a parameter determining the strength of the time‐dependent effect of anifrolumab on UPCR24 dynamics and ct is the anifrolumab serum concentration measured in μM. An Emax‐type model was also evaluated. The OFV remained essentially identical to the linear model, and the Emax model “collapsed” to a scenario where it only operated in the linear range.

When x=1, and in the absence of anifrolumab so that Eat=1, the parameter k becomes the half‐life of the equilibration process of Ut. Thus, the effect of anifrolumab can be interpreted as a decrease of the UPCR equilibration half‐life, or, when x≠1, the decrease of a more general characteristic time for onset of effect. An additional effect of anifrolumab on the UPCR24 steady state, that is, modulating the term 1−Es×Ubx, was explored as an alternative, but did not improve the model significantly.

Lognormal IIV was defined for the parameters k, Ub, and the standard deviation of the residual error. IIV for Es was defined according to Es=1−1−EsTVeη, where EsTV is the typical value of Es and η is a random effect parameter. This maneuver effectively makes 1−Es lognormal but has the purpose of having parameter Es in the model with the interpretation of UPCR reduction due to standard therapy.

To examine the hypothesis that anifrolumab does not affect proteinuria, and all UPCR improvement comes from standard therapy rather than anifrolumab, we set up an alternative model with a fixed to zero. When fitting that model to the data, the OFV increased by 10.4 units, suggesting substantial evidence for an effect of anifrolumab on UPCR (complementing the finding that the confidence intervals for a excluded 0 in our model).

IP discontinuation and dropout model

A subset of dropout events was manually curated from all dropout events in the TULIP‐LN study such that only events that would require dropout in the phase 3 IRIS study were included.

The following proportional hazards model was developed: (6) β0×eβPK×ct+βUPCR×Ut, where β0 is the constant base hazard, and βPK and βUPCR are parameters describing the association of risk with the time‐dependent concentrations of anifrolumab and UPCR24 according to the PK‐UPCR model.

Details on PK data collection, proteinuria assessment, model evaluation, simulations, and analysis software are provided in the Supplementary Methods .

RESULTS

For PK, there were a total of 612 observations in 95 individuals; 21 (3.4%) were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) and were excluded from the analysis. For UPCR, 931 observations in 145 individuals were included.

Our combined PK‐UPCR model was built upon a similar PK structure to previously described models for patients with SLE and healthy volunteers [22], with the empirical time‐dependence of anifrolumab clearance removed and dependence of clearance on modeled time‐dependent UPCR24 introduced. In our model, UPCR dynamics were influenced by standard therapy and time‐dependent anifrolumab exposure, with time‐dependent levels of UPCR continuously modulating the systemic linear clearance of anifrolumab, effectively creating a feedback loop between PK and UPCR (Figure 1 ).

PK results

Each mg/mg reduction in UPCR, relative to the reference value, resulted in a 21.0% (95% CI, 18.9 to 23.0) decrease in linear anifrolumab clearance (Table 1 ). The full estimated relationship between anifrolumab systemic linear clearance and UPCR24 in a typical patient with LN is shown in Figure S2 . With decreasing UPCR24 in the BR and IR regimens (Figure 2 b ), anifrolumab clearance decreased over time, leading to gradually increasing anifrolumab exposures (Figure 2 a ). The increase in exposure over time is seen most clearly in BR, where the increase is monotonic; with IR, the trend is partly concealed by the higher exposure at Week 12 due to the three recent 900 mg doses.

Body weight and high IFNGS at baseline were previously identified as PK model covariates in patients with non‐renal SLE [22]. Here, body weight was a covariate on both clearance (point estimate 0.408 [95% CI, 0.133 to 0.702]) and central volume (point estimate 0.585 [95% CI, 0.325 to 0.796]) in patients with LN (Table 1 ). After controlling for body weight, the systemic linear anifrolumab clearance in a typical patient with LN at baseline was 39% higher than in patients with SLE in the previously developed model. Anifrolumab clearance was 21.9% lower in IFNGS‐low patients with LN (CLINFLOW) than IFNGS‐high patients with LN (point estimate 0.781 [95% CI, 0.530 to 1.03]). Despite the 95% CI including 1 (where 1 means no effect of IFNGS), IFNGS was kept as a covariate on clearance in the PK‐UPCR model because of the small number of IFNGS‐low patients in the study (n = 5) and the similarity of the point estimate to that of the PK model developed in SLE (0.793) [22]. UPCR was the only modeled covariate with time‐dependent effects on clearance in this model. The previous SLE popPK model included an empirical time‐dependent decrease in clearance over time. In the present model, no time‐dependency was identified in addition to that mediated by changes in UPCR.

UPCR results

To illustrate the principles and behavior of the model, UPCR was simulated over 2 years for a typical patient with LN receiving standard therapy in addition to placebo or anifrolumab according to 3 dosing regimens: BR, IR, or the proposed phase 3 (Ph3) (Figure S3 ). The Ph3 regimen consisted of 6× 900 mg anifrolumab IV Q4W, then 300 mg thereafter. Regardless of treatment group or dosing regimen, a similar UPCR24 steady state was reached by Week 104 (i.e., a 73% reduction in UPCR24). However, at Week 52, UPCR24 reductions from baseline (the primary endpoint of TULIP‐LN [10]) were greater and happened sooner with anifrolumab than with placebo (reductions of 67%, 69%, and 71% with BR, IR, or Ph3 anifrolumab, respectively, vs. 61% with placebo; Figure S3 ). IIV for UPCR‐related parameters and residual variability for UPCR was large; therefore, additional simulations were required to understand population level UPCR variability.

Dropout model

A dropout model was developed to determine if anifrolumab exposure, or its impact on UPCR, was associated with the risk of IP discontinuation. VPCs indicated a satisfactory model fit (Figure 2 c ); parameter estimates are shown in Table 1 . Patients with high UPCR were more likely to discontinue treatment, with twice the momentary dropout risk at a UPCR level of 2.8 mg/mg versus 0.5 mg/mg. Patients with greater anifrolumab exposure were less likely to discontinue from treatment; the momentary dropout risk was reduced by 50% with an anifrolumab concentration of 21 μg/mL compared with no exposure. As the lowest median exposure of the simulated Ph3 regimen was 18.3 μg/mL at Week 44 (Figure 3 c ), the median exposure from the Ph3 regimen would reduce the momentary dropout risk to at least this degree at most timepoints.

Simulations of the PK‐UPCR‐dropout model

PK simulation results

To assess if anifrolumab exposure was adequate, the trough concentrations of anifrolumab in patients with LN across BR, IR, and Ph3 regimens were compared to a previously established model from non‐renal SLE (Figure 3 ) [22]. With the BR regimen, both the median and the 10th percentile of anifrolumab exposure were predicted to result in lower trough concentrations in patients with LN compared with those with SLE for all timepoints up to Week 44. While the IR provided higher exposure compared to the BR, especially during the first half‐year, IR trough concentrations were still lower than those predicted for SLE between Weeks 20 and 44. With the proposed Ph3 regimen, trough concentrations were not predicted to be lower in patients with LN vs. SLE at any timepoint, except just slightly lower in the 10th percentile around Week 36. Based on these simulations, only the Ph3 regimen demonstrated adequate anifrolumab exposure in patients with LN as it was non‐inferior to patients with non‐renal SLE.

UPCR simulation results

The simulated proportions of UPCR responders (< 0.5 mg/mg) at Weeks 12, 24, 36, and 52 were 6.1%, 13%, 17%, and 21% with placebo; 8.5%, 18%, 26%, and 31% with anifrolumab BR; 15%, 24%, 30%, and 35% with anifrolumab IR; and 15%, 29%, 34%, and 37% with the Ph3 anifrolumab regimen (Figure 4 ). Using a UPCR response threshold of 0.7 mg/mg yielded higher proportions of UPCR responders in all groups, but the differences between the proportions in the anifrolumab groups and placebo were similar (Figure S4 ).

Impact of IR duration on UPCR responders

To better understand how prolonging IR treatment in patients with LN impacts UPCR response and dropout, the fraction of UPCR responders at Week 52 was simulated across increasing numbers of initial 900 mg doses before the switch to 300 mg, ranging from zero 900 mg doses (equivalent to anifrolumab BR) to 900 mg Q4W throughout a full year of treatment (Figure 6 ). We looked at UPCR response as UPCR24 < 0.5 mg/mg with no IP discontinuation (i.e., the model definition of UPCR response), and UPCR24 < 0.5 mg/mg irrespective of dropout status. With more anifrolumab 900 mg doses, there was a convergence of UPCR responders and the proportion of patients with LN achieving UPCR24 < 0.5 mg/mg regardless of dropout status (40% simulated response rate at Week 52, Figure 6 ), as increased anifrolumab exposure resulted in an overall decrease in dropout, particularly in patients with low UPCR.

DISCUSSION

Findings in the TULIP‐LN trial revealed an unmet need for a quantitative understanding of the relationship between proteinuria, anifrolumab exposure, and the drivers of patient dropout, in order to optimize the anifrolumab dose regimen and drug exposure in patients with LN. Here, we present a mathematical model describing the interactions between PK, UPCR24, and dropout for patients with LN treated with anifrolumab in addition to standard therapy. Our model incorporates both PK and UPCR and is more complex than what would ordinarily be required for a confirmatory analysis of a phase 3 study; in that instance, modeling the impact of UPCR changes on anifrolumab clearance through the inclusion of a time‐dependent covariate (UPCR) on clearance would have been sufficient. Here, however, we performed a pharmacometric analysis utilizing data from a phase 2 study to optimize dose selection for a new and untested regimen for the phase 3 IRIS study. Therefore, our model was required to not only account for the effect of UPCR on anifrolumab clearance, but also for the effect of treatment on UPCR; we also required a model to assess the impact of treatment on patient dropout.

Our model indicates that anifrolumab treatment drives improvement of UPCR response in three ways: direct concentration‐dependent improvement of UPCR, direct dropout reduction, and indirect dropout reduction through UPCR improvement. These improvements are amplified by a feedback loop, whereby UPCR improvement leads to reduced proteinuria‐mediated clearance, which then increases anifrolumab exposure.

The PK component of our LN model was based on a model developed to investigate anifrolumab PK in patients with SLE; thus, we compared model parameter estimates to those estimated for SLE [22]. Given previous observations that clearance of biologics is higher with higher proteinuria in renal diseases [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17], we specifically interrogated the relationships between these variables. Compared with non‐renal SLE, our PK analysis indicated greater anifrolumab clearance in patients with LN and identified a temporal association between anifrolumab clearance and proteinuria levels, in which proteinuria resolution was followed by a reduction in clearance to similar levels observed in patients with non‐renal SLE at baseline [22]. Anifrolumab PK in patients with LN was otherwise similar to patients with non‐renal SLE.

The differences in anifrolumab PK in patients with LN versus those with non‐renal SLE led to a need for an alternative dosing regimen in future LN trials of anifrolumab. This need was exemplified by the fact that the phase 2 TULIP‐LN trial failed to meet the primary endpoint, likely owing to inadequate anifrolumab exposures seen in both BR and IR treatment groups [10]. Our model predictions revealed that prolonging anifrolumab IR from three to six doses of 900 mg before switching to 300 mg facilitates increased anifrolumab exposures through Week 52, larger and more rapid reductions of UPCR, and—with increased exposure in turn facilitating this rapid improvement—reduced patient dropout. Initial doses greater than 300 mg but lower than 900 mg were not considered for the IRIS trial because the IR was previously shown to be safe and tolerated in TULIP‐LN, and rapid improvement and control of renal inflammation is important to prevent the accumulation of irreversible renal damage [23, 24]. It is hoped that a rapid reduction in UPCR with the Ph3 IR would enable patients with LN to then receive the 300 mg IV Q4W regimen approved for patients with non‐renal SLE [18].

In our model, UPCR reduction was greater with anifrolumab than placebo at Week 52, indicating that UPCR improvements were more rapid with anifrolumab. While patients achieved the same UPCR steady state by Week 104 with anifrolumab IR and placebo alongside standard therapy, the placebo group had a much higher dropout rate than any of the anifrolumab dose regimens tested. This increased dropout with standard therapy alone means fewer patients would experience a clinical benefit compared with anifrolumab treatment. Our estimated long‐term reduction of UPCR in patients on standard therapy plus placebo (73.4%) is consistent with previously reported proteinuria reductions [25, 26].

Low UPCR and high anifrolumab exposure were independently associated with a decreased risk of patient dropout in our model. The UPCR association is easily understood, because patients with improved UPCR experience clinical benefit from anifrolumab treatment and so are less likely to discontinue treatment. The association between anifrolumab exposure and dropout is less obvious, given that the downstream impact on UPCR is accounted for. However, it is possible that higher anifrolumab exposures may be associated with other clinically‐relevant outcomes independent of UPCR that may reduce dropout rates, such as the improvements in lupus disease activity measured using SLEDAI‐2 K and Physician and Patient Global Assessments with anifrolumab IR versus placebo seen in TULIP‐LN [10]. Similarly, a lower dropout rate was also observed in patients receiving active drug in the phase 3 study of belimumab in patients with LN.

The safety of prolonged anifrolumab IR has not been fully established; however, during the phase 2 MUSE study of anifrolumab in patients with non‐renal SLE, there were no major safety concerns during the first year of treatment with 1000 mg Q4W [1]. Anifrolumab had an acceptable long‐term safety and tolerability profile in the 3‐year open‐label extension of the MUSE study, where patients received 1000 mg Q4W and then 300 mg Q4W with few adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation [1, 27]. Herpes zoster incidence was associated with anifrolumab dose in MUSE (300 mg vs. 1000 mg), and there was a numerically higher incidence of herpes zoster with anifrolumab 300 mg versus placebo in the first year in exposure‐safety analyses of phase 3 anifrolumab trials in non‐renal SLE [28]. However, the incidence was similar in the 150 mg and 300 mg groups, and there was no association between the incidence of herpes zoster and anifrolumab serum concentrations [28]. In the phase 2 TULIP‐LN study and its extension, treatment with anifrolumab for up to 2 years demonstrated an acceptable safety profile that was generally consistent with the established safety profile in patients with non‐renal SLE [10, 21].

One limitation of this study is the use of UPCR24 response (< 0.5 mg/mg) as a marker of clinical efficacy rather than CRR, the endpoint used in TULIP‐LN [18] and applied in IRIS [10, 20]. However, UPCR24 is a key component of CRR criteria, and although proteinuria is currently considered the best marker of renal inflammation for clinical trials, it cannot distinguish active inflammation from renal damage. The use of UPCR24 as an indicator of response may be limited by the large variability in measurements both between and within patients, because patients close to the responder cut‐off may be classified on either side due to chance. Extrapolation of the model to untested regimens may also be considered a limitation; however, the observed PK concentration range of the IR dosing (3× 900 mg) in TULIP‐LN does not differ substantially from the predicted PK concentration range of IRIS (6× 900 mg), so it is only the duration of the exposure that was extrapolated with the IRIS predictions.

Overall, our analysis indicated greater anifrolumab clearance in patients with LN than in those with non‐renal SLE, highlighting a temporal association between anifrolumab clearance and proteinuria. It also revealed that greater anifrolumab exposure and reduced UPCR were associated with decreased risk of treatment discontinuation. Thus, prolonged anifrolumab IR can provide adequate anifrolumab exposure for patients with LN and has the potential to provide clinical benefit.
