1.
Introduction and main results

Nevanlinna theory
[
13
,
27
]
, developed by Rolf Nevanlinna in the 1920s, is a fundamental framework in complex analysis that examines the value distribution of meromorphic functions. Nevanlinna theory provides a quantitative description of how often a meromorphic function takes specific values, extending the Picard theorem on omitted values. The theory focuses on two main components: the
characteristic function

T(r,f)
, which measures the growth of
f
, and the
counting function

N(r,a,f)
, which counts the
a
-points of
f
. The First Main Theorem establishes a fundamental relation between these quantities, while the deeper Second Main Theorem provides inequalities governing the distribution of values. Its higher-dimensional generalizations investigate holomorphic maps
f\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to X
into complex manifolds, with profound applications in complex geometry and Diophantine approximation (See, for example,
[
8
,
28
,
29
]
).

Tropical geometry
[
14
,
22
,
23
]
, also referred to as max-plus algebra, has emerged as a powerful framework connecting algebraic geometry with combinatorial mathematics. The tropical semiring
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is equipped with tropical operations:

\displaystyle x\oplus y:=\max(x,y)\,\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\,x\otimes y:=x+y,

We also use the notations
x\oslash y:=\frac{x}{y}\oslash=x-y
and
x^{\otimes a}:=ax
for
a\in\mathbb{R}.
The set
\mathbb{T}
endowed with tropical arithmetic operators is called the set of tropical numbers. These operations lead to a completely different algebraic structure compared to classical complex analysis, yet remarkably parallel results can be established.
In 2009 Halburd and Southall
[
10
]
first established the tropical Nevanlinna theory for continuous piecewise integer linear real functions in the one-dimensional tropical affine space
\mathbb{R}
(called tropical meromorphic functions). They first proposed the tropical versions of the Poisson-Jensen theorem, the first main theorem, the logarithmic derivative lemma, and the Clunie theorem for tropical meromorphic functions with finite order. In 2011, Laine and Tohge
[
19
]
considered extended the definition of tropical meromrophic functions with arbitrary real slopes and established the tropical Nevanlinna’s second main theorem for tropical meromorphic functions with hyperorder strictly less than one. In this case the multiplicities of poles (respectively, roots) may be arbitrary real numbers instead of being integers (respectively, rational numbers) which is in certain respects fundamentally different from the counterparts in the classical meromorphic functions of one complex variable. In 2016, Korhonen and Tohge
[
17
]
extended the tropical Nevanlinna theory to a higher-dimensional tropical projective space
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}^{m}
for tropical holomorphic curves and obtained a tropical analogue of Cartan’s second main theorem. Recently, the present first author and Zheng
[
3
]
extended the tropical Nevanlinna theory to the case of tropical hypersurfaces in tropical projective spaces in which the growth of order is improved to the subnormal growth (or called minimal hypertype),
i.e.,\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log T_{f}(r)}{r}=0,
due to an improvement of the tropical logarithmic derivative lemma. This is an emerging field that combines classical value distribution theory with tropical geometry. However, up to now, there are no any results on tropical Nevanlinna theory in several variables. The key point is how to describe the properties of Nevanlinna’s characterstic functions (including proximity function and counting function) for tropical meromorphic functions in several variables.

The main purpose of this paper is to develop a comprehensive theory of tropical meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and then establish some fundamental results in the tropical Nevanlinna theory for higher dimensions, by combining the classical value distribution theory with tropical geometry.

1.1.
Tropical meromorphic functions in several variables and Nevanlinna theory

In Section
2
, we will introduce tropical meromorphic functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
defined through tropical rational operations:

f(x)=\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{+\infty}a_{i}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}\right)\oslash\left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{+\infty}b_{j}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{n}_{j}}\right),

where
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{m}_{i},\mathbf{n}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are exponent vectors. One can see that these functions are piecewise linear with polyhedral complexes as their natural domains. There are some properties of tropical meromorphic functions as follows:

•

Global continuity and piecewise linearity in all variables

•

Polyhedral complex structure (finite or infinite unions of concave/convex polyhedral cells)

•

Real-valued gradients corresponding to exponent vectors
\mathbf{m}_{i}
and
\mathbf{n}_{j}

We will classify all points of a tropical meromorphic function as (Definition
2.7
):

•

Tropical smooth points:
J_{f}(x;\varphi)=0
for all directions
\varphi

•

Tropical poles:
J_{f}(x;\varphi_{0})<0
for some
\varphi_{0}

•

Tropical roots: all other non-smooth points

where
J_{f}(x;\varphi)
captures the so-called
\varphi
-positive directional derivative operator (Definition
2.5
).

We will develop the basic definitions and notations of the tropical Nevanlinna theory in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in Section
3
, which closely generalizes the one-dimensional case studied by Halburd-Southall
[
10
]
and Laine and Tohge
[

]
(see also
[
16
]
). The fundamental components are:

•

the
proximity function
(Definition
3.7

m(r,f):=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}f^{+}(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta);

•

the
counting function
for poles (Definition
3.9
):

N(r,f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r}n(t,f)dt,

where

\displaystyle n(t,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}n(t,f_{\theta})\,d\sigma(\theta);

•

the

T(r,f):=m(r,f)+N(r,f).

There are identical concepts of the proximity function and the counting function, see Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
3.11

3.15

T(r,f)-T(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash f)=f(0),

leads to the First Main Theorem for tropical meromorphic functions (Theorem

T(r,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash(f\oplus a))=T(r,f)+O(1),

where
a\in\mathbb{T}
satisfying
a<L_{f}:=\inf\{f(b):b\text{ is a pole of }f\}.

In Section
4
, we make use of the Dirac function to obtain a useful lemma (Lemma

[

(see also
[

•

For difference operators (Theorem
4.3
):

m(r,f(x+c)\oslash f(x))=o(T(r,f))

holds for a tropical meromorphic function with submormal growth, where
r
runs to infinity outside of a set

•

For

m(r,f(qx)\oslash f(x))=o(T(r,f))

1.2.
Tropical holomorphic maps and Nevanlinna theory

Define the tropical projective space as

\mathbb{TP}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{m+1}/\mathbb{R}(1,1,\ldots,1).
In Section
5
, we introduce the tropical holomorphic maps
f:=[f_{0}:f_{1}:\cdots:f_{m}]:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{TP}^{m},
where
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
are tropical entire functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and do not have any roots which are common to all of them. Denote
\mathbf{f}=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{m+1}.
Then the map
\mathbf{f}
is called a reduced representation of the tropical holomorphic map
f
in
\mathbb{TP}^{m}.
The tropical Cartan’s characteristic function of
f
is defined by

T_{\mathbf{f}}(r):=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\|f(r\theta)\|d\sigma(\theta)-\|f(0)\|,

where
\|f(r\theta)\|=\max\bigl\{f_{0}(r\theta),\ldots,f_{m}(r\theta)\bigr\},

r\theta=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\theta\in S^{n-1}(1).
The first main theorem for a tropical holomorphic map
f
intersecting tropical hypersurface
V_{p}
(Theorem
5.10
) states that
if
f(\mathbb{R}^{n})\not\subset V_{P},
then we have

\displaystyle m_{f}(r,V_{P})+N(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash P(f))=dT_{f}(r)+O(1).

The above tropical logarithmic derivative lemmas enable us to obtain the Second Main Theorems for tropical hypersurfaces (Theorem
5.11
and Theorem
5.16
), which for algebraically nondegenerate maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{TP}^{m}
with subnormal growth intersecting tropical hypersurfaces
\{V_{P_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{q}
with degree
d_{j}
states:

\|\,\,\,(q-M-1-\lambda)T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{j=M+2}^{q}\tfrac{1}{d_{j}}N(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash P_{j}\circ f)+o(T_{f}(r)),

where
d=lcd(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}),

M=\binom{m+d}{d}-1
and
\lambda:=ddg(\{P_{M+2}\circ f,\ldots,P_{q}\circ f\})
measures degeneracy (see in Subsection
5.2
).

Finally, in Section
6
we present a growth-free Second Main Theorem (Theorem
6.1
) for a tropical holomorphic map intersecting a complete tropical polynomials (i.e., all coefficients are finite real values):

T_{f}(r)=\frac{1}{d}N\left(r,\frac{1_{\mathbb{T}}}{P\circ f}\oslash\right)+O(1),

which generalizes Halonen-Korhonen-Filipuk’s results in
[
11
]
, and in particular whenever
\mathbb{TP}^{1}
this recovers the relationship:

T(r,f)=N\left(r,\frac{1_{\mathbb{T}}}{f\oplus a}\oslash\right)-N(r,f\oplus a)+N(r,f)+O(1)

for any
a\in\mathbb{R}.

In a word, our work in this paper strongly extends previous results by Laine and Tohge
[
19
]
, Korhonen-Tohge
[
17
]
and Cao-Zheng
[
3
]
to the case of several variables, while many new techniques are introduced.

Tropical meromorphic functions in

2.1.
Tropical algebra

Tropical operations (max-plus) are defined for two real variables
x,y\in\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},

\displaystyle x\oplus y

\displaystyle:=\max(x,y),

\displaystyle x\otimes y

\displaystyle:=x+y,

\displaystyle x\oslash y

\displaystyle:=x-y.

Obviously, the tropical additional unit
0_{\mathbb{T}}:=-\infty
and the tropical multiplication unit
1_{\mathbb{T}}:=0.

2.2.
Tropical meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Here, we introduce the concept of tropical meromorphic functions in higher dimensions as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Tropical meromorphic function)
.

A tropical meromorphic function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
is defined in the tropical algebra (max-plus) as follows:

(1)

\displaystyle f(x)=\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{+\infty}a_{i}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}\right)\oslash\left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{+\infty}b_{j}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{n}_{j}}\right),

where:

•

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

•

The exponential operation is given by:

x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}:=m_{i1}x_{1}+m_{i2}x_{2}+\cdots+m_{in}x_{n},

where
\mathbf{m}_{i}=(m_{i1},m_{i2},\ldots,m_{in})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

•

All coefficients
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathbb{T}.

By translating the max-plus operations into standard arithmetic operations, (
1
) can be expressed as:

\displaystyle f(x)=\max_{0\leq i\leq\infty}\left\{a_{i}+m_{i1}x_{1}+\cdots+m_{in}x_{n}\right\}-\max_{0\leq j\leq\infty}\left\{b_{j}+n_{i1}x_{1}+\cdots+n_{in}x_{n}\right\}.

One can see that a tropical meromorphic function
f
satisfies the following properties:

•

f
is a globally continuous piecewise linear function in several variables
x
.
In particular, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the univariate slice

x_{i}\mapsto f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n})
is also a continuous piecewise linear function.

•

The non-smooth locus
\Sigma_{f}
of
f
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional polyhedral complex,
consisting of convex polyhedral cells glued together along common faces.

•

Each linear region (cell) of
f
has a constant real-valued gradient vector, corresponding to the exponent vector

\mathbf{m}_{i}
or
\mathbf{n}_{j}
of the dominating tropical monomial in that region.

Example 2.2

(Tropical polynomials)
.

A tropical polynomial
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
is defined as:

\displaystyle P(x)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}.

The roots of
P(x)
consist by all points where the maximum is achieved by at least two terms, i. e.,

\Sigma_{P}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\max_{i=0}^{k}\{a_{i}+m_{i}x\}\,\,\mbox{is attained by at least two terms}\}.

Example 2.3

(Tropical rational functions)
.

A tropical rational function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
is defined in the tropical algebra (max-plus) as:

(2)

\displaystyle f(x)=\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{p}a_{i}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}\right)\oslash\left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{q}b_{j}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{n}_{j}}\right),

Example 2.4

(Tropical entire functions)
.

A tropical entire function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
is defined in the tropical algebra (max-plus) as:

\displaystyle f(x)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{+\infty}a_{i}\otimes x^{\otimes\mathbf{m}_{i}}

which is a polyhedral complex: a finite or infinite union of convex polyhedral cells glued together along faces, i.e., has no poles.

2.3.
Tropical roots and poles for meromorphic functions

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we express it as
x=t\theta
, where:

•

if
x\neq 0,
then
t=\|x\|>0
and
\theta=\frac{x}{\|x\|}\in S^{n-1}(1):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|y\|=1\};

•

if

then
t=0
and there are infinitely many directions.

For each fixed
\theta\in S^{n-1}(1)
, define
f_{\theta}(t):=f(t\theta)
for
t\in[0,\infty)
. Note that
f_{\theta}(0)=f(0)
is independent of
\theta

Then for each fixed

can be regarded as a one-dimensional tropical meromorphic function for the variable

It is known that for the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n},
the surface area
\omega_{n}
of the unit sphere

is given by
[

where
\Gamma

\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!

\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}d\sigma(\varphi)=\omega_{n},

where
d\sigma(\varphi)
is the standard surface measure.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
be a tropical meromorphic function. For any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a direction
\varphi\in S^{n-1}(1),
we define the
\varphi

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi}^{+}f(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\to 0^{+}\\
h>0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(x+h\varphi)-f(x)}{h}.

Set

J_{f}(x;\varphi):=\partial_{\varphi}^{+}f(x)+\partial_{-\varphi}^{+}f(x)

and

\overline{J_{f}}(x):=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}J_{f}(x;\varphi)d\sigma(\varphi),

where
d\sigma(\varphi)
is the standard surface measure.

According to the definition, direct verification shows the following result:

(3)

J_{f}(x;\varphi)=J_{f}(x;-\varphi).

Example 2.6
.

3.1.
Basis on the Dirac functions

Firstly, we recall a basic result on the Dirac functions as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
5
, Ch.1, Sec.1]

The fundamental property of the Dirac delta distribution in one dimension:

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta(x-a)\phi(x)dx=\phi(a);

By Lemma
3.1
, we get a result which will play a key role in describing the basics of the tropical Nevanlinna theory in higher dimensions.

Lemma 3.2
.

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta(\|x\|-r)F(x)dx=r^{n-1}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}F(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta),

where
x=r\theta
and
d\sigma(\theta)
is surface element.

Proof.

We perform a change of variables to spherical coordinates, setting
x=\rho\theta
, where
\rho\in[0,\infty)
and
\theta\in S^{n-1}(1)
. The volume element in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
becomes
dx=\rho^{n-1}d\rho\,d\sigma(\theta)
. Substituting into the integral gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta(\|x\|-r)\,F(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\delta(\rho-r)\,F(\rho\theta)\,\rho^{n-1}\,d\sigma(\theta)\,d\rho.

Using the standard property of the Dirac delta function (
4
),
we evaluate the
\rho
-integral:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(\rho-r)\,F(\rho\theta)\,\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho=r^{n-1}F(r\theta).

Substituting this into the expression yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta(\|x\|-r)\,F(x)\,dx=r^{n-1}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}F(r\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta),

as required.
∎

.

[
21
, Ch.2]
If
g

3.2.
Tropical Nevanlinna theory in one variable

Now let’s recall the Nevanlinna theory of tropical meromorphic functions in one dimension (see
[
10
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
).

For any real number
x\in\mathbb{T},
we define
x^{+}:=\max\{x,0\}.
For a tropical meromorphic function
f
we set
f^{+}(x):=\max(f(x),0).
Define the proximity function of
f
by taking the average of the positive parts of
f
at the endpoints in
[-r,+r]
as follows:

m(r,f):=\frac{f^{+}(r)+f^{+}(-r)}{2}.

Denote by
n(r,f)
the number of poles of
f
in the interval
(-r,r)
, counted with multiplicity. The counting function of
f
is defined by

N(r,f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r}n(t,f)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}(r-|b_{\nu}|),

where
b_{\nu}
are all poles of
f
(counting multiplicities) in
(-r,r).
The characteristic function of
f
is defined by:

T(r,f):=m(r,f)+N(r,f).

The tropical Poisson-Jensen formula is given as follows.

Lemma 3.4

(Poisson-Jensen formula)
.

[
10
]

Suppose that
f
is a tropical meromorphic function on
[-r,r]
, for some
r>0
and denote the roots of
f
in this interval by
a_{\mu},\,\mu=1,\ldots,M
, and the poles by
b_{\nu},\,\nu=1,\ldots,N
, where roots and poles are listed according to their multiplicities. Then for any
x\in(-r,r)
we have

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{f(r)+f(-r)\right\}+\frac{x}{2r}\left\{f(r)-f(-r)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\left\{r^{2}-|a_{\mu}-x|r-a_{\mu}x\right\}+\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}\left\{r^{2}-|b_{\nu}-x|r-b_{\nu}x\right\}.

In particular,

f(0)=\frac{f(r)+f(-r)}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}(r-|a_{\mu}|)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}(r-|b_{\nu}|).

which satisfies

T(r,f)=T(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash f)+f(0).

This relation serves as a weak analogue version of Nevanlinna’s first main theorem. Furthermore, a general form of the first main theorem is shown as follows.

Lemma 3.5

(First main theorem)
.

[

f
be a tropical meromorphic function of one variable. Then for any

satisfying
a<L_{f}:=\inf\{f(b):b\text{ is a pole of }f\},
we have

T(r,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash(f\oplus a))=T(r,f)+O(1).

The estimation on tropical logarithmic derivative in one variable is given by Laine and Tohge

]
.

Notations for tropical Nevanlinna theory in higher dimension

Now we establish some basic notations for the higher-dimensional tropical value distribution theory.

Firstly, we introduce the high-dimensional tropical proximity function

Definition 3.7

(Proximity function)
.

For a tropical meromorphic function
f

\mathbb{R}^{n},

Lemma 3.8
.

It follows from the definition (

(7)

\displaystyle m(r,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f^{+}(x)\,\delta(\|x\|-r)\,dx,

It follows directly from (

\displaystyle m(r,f)

\displaystyle=

Similarly, we define the high-dimensional tropical counting function as follows.

Example 3.10
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}

Then non-smooth locus

(n-1)

For a fixed

by the local finiteness of

is bounded on the product space

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Sigma_{f}\cap[-t,t]\varphi\\
J_{f}(x;\varphi)<0\end{subarray}}|J_{f}(x;\varphi)|d\sigma(\varphi)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{f}\cap B_{t}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{\{\varphi\in S^{n-1}(1):\,J_{f}(x;\varphi)<0\}}|J_{f}(x;\varphi)|\,d\sigma(\varphi)\,d\mathcal{H}^{\,n-1}(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{f}\cap B_{t}}\nu_{f}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{\,n-1}(x),

Lemma 3.11
.

It follows from the definition (

\displaystyle N(r,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}N(r,f(x))\,\delta(\|x\|-r)\,\,dx,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}N(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta).

Proof.

By Definition (
10

\displaystyle N(r,f(x))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\left(\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\sum_{\|x\|<t,J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)<0}|J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)|d\sigma(\theta)\right)dt.

For any fixed
\theta,
we consider the one variable function
f_{\theta}
and have

\displaystyle N(r,f_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r}n(t,f_{\theta})\ dt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\|x\|<r,J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)<0}|J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)|(r-\|x\|).

Then combing the above two equalities, we obtain

\displaystyle N(r,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{\|x\|<t,J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)<0}|J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)|\ dtd\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\sum_{\|x\|<r,J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)<0}|J_{f_{\theta}}(x;1)|(r-\|x\|)d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}N(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta).

Together with (

\displaystyle N(r,f)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}N(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}N(r,f(x))\delta(\|x\|-r)dx.

∎

As usually, we call also

T(r,f):=m(r,f)+N(r,f)

to be the characteristic function of a tropical meromorphic function
f.
By the above two lemmas, we have the following property for
T(r,f).

Lemma 3.12
.

\displaystyle T(r,f)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}[m(r,f_{\theta})+N(r,f_{\theta})]d\sigma(\theta)=:\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}T(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta).

Lemma 3.13
.

T(r,f)
is a convex function about
r
.

Proof.

By the above lemma, we have

T(r,f)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}T(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta),

where
f_{\theta}(t)=f(t\theta)
and
\omega_{n}
is the surface area of
S^{n-1}(1)
.

For each fixed
\theta
, it is known that
T(r,f_{\theta})
is convex in
r
(see
[
16
, Corollay 3.10]
). Thus for any
r_{1},r_{2}>0
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
:

T(\lambda r_{1}+(1-\lambda)r_{2},f_{\theta})\leq\lambda T(r_{1},f_{\theta})+(1-\lambda)T(r_{2},f_{\theta}).

By integrating the above inequalities over
S^{n-1}(1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}T(\lambda r_{1}+(1-\lambda)r_{2},f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\left[\lambda T(r_{1},f_{\theta})+(1-\lambda)T(r_{2},f_{\theta})\right]d\sigma(\theta).

This implies that

T(\lambda r_{1}+(1-\lambda)r_{2},f)\leq\lambda T(r_{1},f)+(1-\lambda)T(r_{2},f).

and thus
T(r,f)
is convex in
r
.

∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Given a positive number
\alpha
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
then

\displaystyle m(r,f^{\otimes\alpha})=m(r,\alpha f)=\alpha m(r,f),

\displaystyle N(r,f^{\otimes\alpha})=N(r,\alpha f)=\alpha N(r,f),

\displaystyle T(r,f^{\otimes\alpha})=T(r,\alpha f)=\alpha T(r,f),

\displaystyle m(r,f\oplus g)\leq m(r,f)+m(r,g),

\displaystyle m(r,f\otimes g)\leq m(r,f)+m(r,g),

\displaystyle N(r,f\otimes g)\leq N(r,f)+N(r,g),

\displaystyle T(r,f\otimes g)\leq T(r,f)+T(r,g),

\displaystyle T(r,f\oplus g)\leq T(r,f)+T(r,g).

Proof.

One can directly deduce them according to the definitions of
m(r,f),

N(r,f)
and
T(r,f).
It is known that the one dimensional case has been proved by Laine and Tohge (see
[
16
]
). Thus for any fixed
\theta,
we have all the properties for the function
f_{\theta}(t)
which is regarded as a tropical meromorphic function in one variable. Then it is easy to get the lemma by the above lemmas. We omit the details.∎

3.4.
The first main theorem in higher dimension

We get the softed form of the tropical first main theorem in higher dimension.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a tropical meromorphic function. Then the following identity holds:

T(r,f)-T(r,-f)=f(0).

Proof.

For each fixed direction
\theta\in S^{n-1}(1)
, consider the one-dimensional slice
f_{\theta}(t):=f(t\theta)
, which is a tropical meromorphic function on
\mathbb{R}
. By the one-dimensional tropical Jensen formula (
3.2
), we have

m(r,f_{\theta})+N(r,f_{\theta})-m(r,-f_{\theta})-N(r,-f_{\theta})=f_{\theta}(0)=f(0).

Averaging both sides of the identity over all directions
\theta\in S^{n-1}(1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}\left[m(r,f_{\theta})+N(r,f_{\theta})-m(r,-f_{\theta})-N(r,-f_{\theta})\right]d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(r,f)+N(r,f)-m(r,-f)+N(r,-f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}f(0)d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(0).

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.12
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}m(r,f_{\theta})+N(r,f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=T(r,f)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}m(r,-f_{\theta})+N(r,-f_{\theta})d\sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=T(r,-f).

Therefore,

T(r,f)-T(r,-f)=f(0).

∎

From the proof of Lemma
3.15
, we derive the high-dimensional tropical Jensen formula as follows:

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}f(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta)=N(r,-f)-N(r,f)+O(1)

which is frequently used in the subsequent text.
We can also establish the general form of the tropical first main theorem in higher dimension.

Theorem 3.16

(First main theorem in higher dimension)
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{T}
satisfying
a<L_{f}:=\inf\{f(b):b\text{ is a pole of }f\},
we have

T(r,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash(f\oplus a))=T(r,f)+O(1).

Proof.

Making use of Lemma
3.15
, we immediately conclude that

\displaystyle T(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash(f\oplus a))

\displaystyle=T(r,f\oplus a)-(f(0,\cdots,0)\oplus a)

\displaystyle\leq T(r,f)+T(r,a)-(f(0,\cdots,0)\oplus a)

\displaystyle\leq T(r,f)+\max\{a,0\}-(f(0,\cdots,0)\oplus a)

for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
and for any
r>0
. Here we also used the inequality
T(r,f\oplus g)\leq T(r,f)+T(r,g).
It follows from Lemma
3.14
that
T(r,a)=a^{+}=\max\{a,0\}
. In addition,

\displaystyle\max\{a,0\}-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0),a\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a-f(0,\cdots,0)\leq 0;&a>0,f(0,\cdots,0)\geq a\\
a-a=0;&a>0,f(0,\cdots,0)<a\\
0-f(0,\cdots,0)\leq|a|;&a\leq 0,f(0,\cdots,0)\geq a\\
0-a=|a|;&a\leq 0,f(0,\cdots,0)<a\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|a|.

To obtain the asserted asymptotic equality, suppose first that
f
has at least one pole and that
-\infty<a<L_{f}
. In this case, we have
N(r,\max\{f,a\})=N(r,f)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle T(r,1_{\circ}\oslash(f\oplus a))

\displaystyle=T(r,f\oplus a)-(f(0,\cdots,0)\oplus a)

\displaystyle=m(r,\max\{f,a\})+N(r,\max\{f,a\})-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0),a\}

\displaystyle\geq m(r,f)+N(r,f)-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0),a\}

\displaystyle\geq T(r,f)-\max\{f(0),a\},

according to the monotonicity of
m(r,f)
with respect to
f
.

Finally, if
L_{f}=+\infty
, that is, if
f
has no poles, the asymptotic equality holds as well. In fact, because of
T(r,f)=m(r,f)
then and
f\oplus a\geq f
for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle T(r,1_{\circ}\oslash(f\oplus a))

\displaystyle=T(r,f\oplus a)-(f(0,\cdots,0)\oplus a)

\displaystyle=T(r,\max\{f,a\})-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0)),a\}

\displaystyle\geq m(r,\max\{f,a\})-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0),a\}

\displaystyle\geq m(r,f)-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0)),a\}

\displaystyle=T(r,f)-\max\{f(0,\cdots,0),a\}.

∎

4.
The tropical version of the logarithmic derivative lemma

For a high-dimensional tropical meromorphic function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, the order is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\rho(f):=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log T(r,f)}{\log r},

where
T(r,f)
is the characteristic function that quantifies the growth rate of the function. The hyper-order
\rho_{2}(f)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(f):=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log T(r,f)}{\log r}.

Subnormal growth (also called minimal hypertype) for
f
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log T(r,f)}{r}.

We now present a key lemma that will be useful in proving the tropical version of the logarithmic derivative lemma in higher dimension.

Lemma 4.2

[

Let
T(r)
be a nondecreasing positive, convex, continuous function on

5.1.
Tropical projective space and holomorphic maps

The tropical projective space is defined as

\mathbb{TP}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{m+1}/\mathbb{R}(1,1,\ldots,1),
that

\mathbb{TP}^{m}=\mathbb{T}^{m+1}\setminus\{0_{\mathbb{T}}\}/\sim

where

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=\lambda\otimes(b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=(\lambda\otimes b_{0},\lambda\otimes b_{1},\ldots,\lambda\otimes b_{m})

for some

Denote by
[a_{0}:a_{1}:\cdots:a_{m}]

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}).
For instance, the one dimensional tropical projective
\mathbb{TP}:=\mathbb{TP}^{1}

The space

is a compact tropical variety
[

]

[

Definition 5.1
.

Let

are tropical entire functions in

Then the map

\mathbb{TP}^{m}.

Definition 5.2
.

Let

be a tropical holomorphic map with a reduced representation

. We define the tropical Cartan’s characteristic function of

by

The order and hyperorder of

\displaystyle\rho(f)=\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log T_{f}(r)}{\log r},

and

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(f)=\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log T_{f}(r)}{\log r},

respectively.

A\oplus B=(a_{ij}\oplus b_{ij})

and

A\otimes B:=\left(\bigoplus_{k=0}^{m}a_{ik}\otimes b_{kj}\right),
respectively. If an
(m+1)\times(m+1)

matrix
A
contains at least one element different from
0_{\mathbb{T}}
in each row, then
A
is called regular.
The tropical determinant
|A|_{o}
of
A
is defined by

|A|_{o}=\bigoplus a_{0\pi(0)}\otimes a_{1\pi(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n\pi(n)},

where the sum is taken over all permutations
\{\pi(0),\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(m)\}
of the set

\{0,1,\ldots,n\}.
Note that an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
A
is regular if and only if
|A|_{0}\neq 0_{\mathbb{T}}.

There are two ways to define linear dependent or independent over tropical sem-field. Tropical meromorphic functions
g_{0},\ldots,g_{m}
are tropical linearly dependent (respectively independent) if the max term

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}\otimes g_{i}

is attained at least twice. They are called to be linearly dependent (respectively independent) in the Gondran-Minoux sense
[
6
]
[
7
]
if there exist (respectively there
do not exist) two disjoint subsets
I
and
J
of
K:=\{0,\ldots,m\}
such that
I\cup J=K
and

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i\in I}a_{i}\otimes g_{i}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}a_{j}\otimes g_{j},

that is,

\displaystyle\max_{i\in I}\{a_{i}+g_{i}\}=\max_{j\in J}\{a_{j}+g_{j}\},

where the constants
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{T}
are not all equal to
0_{\mathbb{T}}.
Usually, linearly dependent in the Gondran-Minoux sense should be tropical linear dependent, however, the inverse may be not true.

If
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{T}
and
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}
are tropical entire functions, then

\displaystyle F=\bigoplus_{\nu=0}^{m}a_{\nu}\otimes f_{\nu}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{j}a_{k_{i}}\otimes f_{k_{i}}

is called a tropical linear combination of
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
over
\mathbb{T},

where the index set
\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{j}\}\subset\{0,\ldots,m\}
is such that
a_{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,j\},
while
a_{\nu}=0_{\mathbb{T}}
if
\nu\not\in\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{j}\}.

Note that if
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}
are linearly independent in the
sense of Gondran and Minous, then the express of
F
cannot be
rewritten by means of any other index set which is different from
the set
\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{j}\}.

Let
G=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}(\neq\{0_{\mathbb{T}}\})
be a set of tropical entire functions, linearly independent in
the Gondran-Minoux sense, and denote

\mathcal{L}_{G}=span<f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}>=\left\{\bigoplus_{k=0}^{m}(a_{k}\otimes f_{k}):(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}_{\max}\right\}

to be their linear span. The collection
G
is called the spanning basis of
\mathcal{L}_{G}.

The dimension of
\mathcal{L}_{G}
is defined by

\dim(\mathcal{L}_{G})=\max\{\ell(F):F\in\mathcal{L}_{G}\setminus\{0_{\mathbb{T}}\}\},

where
\ell(F)
is the
shortest length of the representation of
F\in\mathcal{L}_{G}\setminus\{0_{\mathbb{T}}\}
defined by

\ell(F)=\min\{j\in\{1,\ldots,m+1\}:F=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{j}(a_{k_{i}}\otimes f_{k_{i}})\}

where

a_{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
with integers
0\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{j}\leq m.
Note that usually the dimension of the tropical linear span
space of
G
may not be m+1, which is different from the classical
linear algebraic. If
\ell(F)=m+1
for a tropical linear combination

F
of
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m},
then
F
is said to be complete, that
is, the coefficients
a_{k}
in any expression of
F
of the form

F=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{m}(a_{k}\otimes f_{k})
must satisfy
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
and in this case,

\mathcal{L}_{G}=m+1.

Let
G=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}
be a set of tropical entire functions, linearly independent in the Gondran-Minoux
sense, and let
Q\subset\mathcal{L}_{G}
be a collection of tropical linear combinations of
G
over

\mathbb{T}.
The degree of degeneracy of
Q
is defined to be

ddg(Q):=card(\{F\in Q:\ell(F)<m+1\}).

If
ddg(Q)=0,
then we say
Q
is non-degenerate. This means that the degree of degeneracy of a set of tropical
linear combinations is the number of its non-complete elements. In this way the number of complete elements of

Q
is the ‘actual dimension’ of the subspace spanned by
Q,
and thus the
ddg(Q)
is the ‘codimension’ of the
subspace spanned by
Q
(see
[
16
, Page 120-121]
).

There are general definition of tropical hypersurfaces associated to a tropical Laurent polynomial

Definition 5.4

\displaystyle P(f)

\displaystyle P\circ f=\bigoplus_{i_{0}+i_{1}+\ldots+i_{m}=d}c_{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}\otimes f_{0}^{\otimes i_{0}}\otimes f_{1}^{\otimes i_{1}}\cdots f_{m}^{\otimes i_{m}}

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i=0}^{M}c_{I_{i}}\otimes f^{I_{i}}

Definition 5.5
.

Tropical meromorphic functions

are tropical algebraically dependent (respectively independent) if and only if

are tropical linearly dependent (respectively independent). They are called to be algebraically dependent (respectively independent) in the Gondran-Minoux sense if and only if

are linearly dependent (respectively independent) in the Gondran-Minoux sense.

Definition 5.6
.

Let

Definition 5.7

(Tropical algebraically nondegenerated)

Proposition 5.8

If a tropical holomorphic map

and

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i\in I}a_{i}\otimes f_{0}^{\otimes i_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{m}^{\otimes i_{m}}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}a_{j}\otimes f_{0}^{\otimes j_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{m}^{\otimes j_{m}}

where

Definition 5.9

(Weil function and proximity function)

\displaystyle m_{f}(r,V_{P})+N(r,1_{\mathbb{T}}\oslash P(f))=dT_{f}(r)+O(1).

Theorem 6.1

Let a tropical holomorphic curve

be tropical algebraically nondegenerated (i.e., its image not in any tropical hypersurface). If a tropical homogeneous polynomial

\displaystyle P(x)=\bigoplus_{i_{0}+i_{1}+\ldots+i_{m}=d}a_{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}\otimes x_{0}^{\otimes i_{0}}\otimes x_{1}^{\otimes i_{1}}\cdots\otimes x_{m}^{\otimes i_{m}},

T_{f}(r)=\frac{1}{d}N\left(r,\frac{1_{\mathbb{T}}}{P\circ f}\oslash\right)+O(1)

and thus
\delta_{f}(V_{P})=0.

Proof.

Since
P(f)
is an entire function, by Jensen formula (
11
) we have

(64)

\displaystyle N\left(r,\frac{1_{\mathbb{T}}}{P\circ f}\oslash\right)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}(1)}P(f)(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta)-P(f)(0).

Furthermore,

(65)

\displaystyle\min\{a_{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}\}+d\|f(x)\|\leq P(f(x))\leq\max\{a_{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}\}+d\|f(x)\|,

This gives that

P(f)(x)=d\|f(x)\|+O(1).

Then combining (
64
) and (

T_{f}(r)=\frac{1}{d}N\left(r,\frac{1_{\mathbb{T}}}{P\circ f}\oslash\right)+O(1).
