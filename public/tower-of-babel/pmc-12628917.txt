Gamma radiation-induced mutation breeding for enhanced yield and stress tolerance in grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.)
Authors: Naeem Ud-Din, Syed Riaz Ahmed, Saima Jameel, Atif Bilal Asad, Zafran Khan, Nimra Gul, Anisa Umer, Shajia Ali, Mohammad Aslam, Ahmed Aziz Kurd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change poses a significant threat to grapevine survival and productivity worldwide. Mutation breeding is an effective technique for inducing genetic diversity in grapevines, enabling the modulation of phytochemical and biochemical activities as well as genes associated with morphological traits. This study evaluated the effects of Cobalt-60 gamma irradiation on four grapevine cultivars (maintained at Horticulture Research Institute, Khuzdar, Pakistan) to identify the optimal dose for beneficial changes. Hardwood cuttings were exposed to five radiation levels in a randomized complete block design, followed by assessments of morphological, phytochemical and biochemical traits. 10 Gy irradiation accelerated sprouting, increase plant height, leaf size, and node number and enhanced phenolic, flavonoid, and antioxidant activities across cultivars. In contrast, higher doses delayed development and reduced growth and biochemical performance. Cluster and principal component analyses confirmed the dose dependent responses. Overall, 10 Gy was identified as the optimal dose for promoting growth and metabolic resilience in grapevines. This study provides the first systematic evidence from Pakistan on radiation-induced mutagenesis in grapevines, filling a major knowledge gap and demonstrating the potential of mutation breeding as a rapid and effective strategy for developing climate-resilient cultivars to support sustainable viticulture and regional food security.Keywords: Grapevine, Gamma rays, Cobalt-60, Phytochemical, Antioxidants, Mutation

Introduction

Grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is a woody perennial horticulture fruit crop cultivated worldwide, covering around 260 million hectares in 2022 [1]. It has been reported that every year around 80 million tons of grapes are produced and a significant portion of this production is used to make wine, raisins and juice [2]. Besides, grape seeds also composed of saturated fatty acides (10%), oleic acid (10%) and unsaturated fatty acids (90%). Moreover, these are also rich in tannins, catechins, procyanidins, flavonoids and phenolic compounds [2]. Five countries including Turkey (5.96%), Italy (9.77%), France (10.84%), China (11.76%) and Spain (13.1%) cover nearly 50% of the global vineyard area [3]. International Organisation of Vine and Wine has reported that around 33 grape varieties cover almost 50% of the vineyard area and 13 varieties more than one-third [4]. In addition to the earlier mentioned countries, grapes are also cultivated extensively in Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan. In Pakistan, grapes are grown in various districts of Balochistan (the largest province by area), including Mastung, Quetta, Killa Abdullah, Chaman, Kalat, Khuzdar and Pishin. The area under grapes cultivation in Balochistan is around 8,262 hectares and the total production is about 41,775 tonnes [4]. From 2001 to 2018, the vineyard area in Balochistan increased from 12.3% to 46.7%, respectively. However, due to severe climate change, the area covered by vineyards and grapes production in Balochistan has declined significantly in recent years [5].

Climate change has become a serious issue recently, affecting agriculture and horticulture productivity, as well as economy worldwide. Unpredicted rainfall patterns, high temperatures, and prolonged dry spells caused by climate change severely affect semi-arid and arid regions like Balochistan [6]. Over the past five years, the groundwater level in Balochistan has dropped alarmingly from an average of 300 feet to 1,200 feet across various districts, including Pishin, Kalat, and Mastung [7]. This drastic decline has intensified drought conditions, exacerbating food security concerns in the region. Grapevine, a vital fruit crop in Balochistan, is highly susceptible to climate change, particularly drought and temperature fluctuations. It has been reported that drought stress during critical growth stages leads to reductions in berry size, number of nodes, length of leaf petiole, leaf area, leaf ratio and plant height, total soluble solids, titratable acidity, and anthocyanin content, thereby compromising both yield and fruit quality [8]. Additionally, excessive heat stress disrupts key physiological processes, such as photosynthesis and stomatal conductance, leading to oxidative stress, reduced carbohydrate accumulation, and alterations in phenolic composition [9]. Studies have shown that high temperatures (>35 °C) accelerate berry ripening, causing imbalances in sugar-acid ratios, while prolonged drought limits canopy development, decreasing overall productivity [10, 11]. Furthermore, climate-induced abiotic stress weakens plant defense mechanisms, making grapevines more susceptible to fungal pathogens and insect infestations [12].

To mitigate these adverse effects, the adoption of drought and heat resilient grape cultivars, improved irrigation strategies (such as deficit irrigation and mulching), and the application of biostimulants have been proposed as effective strategies [13]. Additionally, radiation-induced mutagenesis has shown potential in improving several agronomic and few horticulture crops such as wheat, rice, mung bean and pomegranate to environmental stresses [13–16]. It has been reported that low to moderate doses of gamma radiation can enhance tolerance to abiotic stresses by inducing genetic variations that improve root architecture, water-use efficiency, and antioxidant enzyme activity, thereby strengthening the plant’s defense mechanisms against oxidative stress [16, 17]. When gamma radiation interacts with cellular atoms or molecules, particularly water, it generates free radicals [13]. These highly reactive molecules can cause significant alterations in DNA or chromosomal structures, leading to an increased mutation rate [18, 19]. Exposure to radiation induces various structural and metabolic changes in plant cells, including modifications in thylakoid membrane organization, alterations in photosynthetic activity, activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, and increased accumulation of phenolic compounds [20]. Both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems, such as phenolics and proline, play a crucial role in neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can otherwise cause oxidative stress and cellular damage [21]. One of the primary byproducts of this process, malondialdehyde (MDA), reacts with membrane proteins and nucleic acids, compromising membrane stability and increasing permeability [22]. Consequently, these disruptions impair cellular integrity, hinder physiological functions, accelerate aging, and may ultimately result in cell death due to pathogenic changes. Phenolic compounds help mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals, facilitated by their conjugated ring structures and hydroxyl groups, which enable them to function as reductants, hydrogen donors, and singlet oxygen quenchers. Additionally, flavonoids enhance a plant’s resilience to radiation stress [23].

Genetic diversity plays a crucial role in plant breeding, providing the foundation for crop improvement and adaptation to environmental challenges. Mutation breeding is a powerful tool for generating novel genetic variations within a short time, but its success largely depends on identifying the optimal radiation dose for a given species [24, 25]. Despite its potential, research on radiation-induced mutagenesis in grapevines remains extremely limited, with only few published studies available on this subject. To address this knowledge gap, the present study aims to evaluate the impact of different radiation doses on the morphological, phytochemical, biochemical, and quality traits of grapevine cuttings. Additionally, this research seeks to identify the most effective radiation dose for enhancing grapevine resilience to climate change, ultimately contributing to sustainable viticulture and regional food security. This study is the first of its kind in Pakistan, providing novel insights into the potential of radiation mutagenesis for grapevine improvement.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and plant material

The present investigation was conducted under the local climatic conditions of Khuzdar (27.9733° N, 66.5211° E), at PARC-Horticulture Research Institute (HRI), Balochistan, Pakistan. Hardwood cuttings of four different grapes cultivars including Black Grapes, Haitha Black, Haitha White, and Sundarkhani were prepared using pruning shears. These above-mentioned cultivars have been maintained and propagated at HRI-Khuzdar for several years and formal identification has been carried out by researchers who are expert in grape cultivation and breeding. All cuttings were of uniform size (7-inches) having pencil thickness. The cuttings were immediately buried in soil for callusing to promote earlier vegetative plant growth for 15 days. After callusing, the cuttings were exposed to different gamma radiation (source: 60Co) doses i.e., 0 (non-treated), 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 Gy at the Nuclear Institute of Agriculture and Biology (NIAB), Faisalabad, with corresponding exposure times ranging from 7 min (10 Gy) to 35 min (50 Gy). After 24 h of radiation exposure, the treated cuttings were planted in the field with an 8-inch plant-to-plant distance, using the factorial arrangement within a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications, each containing 6 cuttings per treatment. After 45 days of plantation, data for different quantitative and qualitative traits was measured. Quantitative traits included days to sprouting (DTS), total cuttings (TC), number of sprouted cuttings (NSC), sprouting percentage (SP), plant height (PH cm), length of tendrils (LT cm), leaf length (LL cm), leaf width (LW cm), leaf ration (LR), leaf area (LA), length of leaf petiole (LLP cm), number of nodes (NN) and internodal distance (ID cm). Qualitative traits included shoot form of tip (YSFT), anthocyanin color of tip (ACT), shoot attitude habit (SAH), number of consecutive tendrils (NCT), young leaf color of upper surface (YLCUS), mature leaf number of lobes (MLNL), shape of blade (SOB), shape of teeth (SOT), length of teeth (LOT), general shape of petiole sinus (GSPS), tooth at petiole sinus (TAPS) and shape of upper lateral sinus (SULS). All measurable morphological traits such as PH and leaf related traits were recorded using a measuring scale, and data for the aforementioned qualitative and quantitative traits were collected following the IPGRI (IPGRI, 1997) grapevine descriptors.

Soil analysis

The soil of the experimental site was analyzed before planting the cuttings to determine the composition of essential nutrients and organic matter necessary for the growth and development of grapevine cuttings. The soil was collected from the surface layer to a depth of 0 to 30 cm and contained 162 ppm potassium, 7.23 ppm phosphorus, 0.074% nitrogen (equivalent to 740 mg/kg), 1.77% organic matter, an EC of 1.2 dS m⁻¹, and a pH of 7.22. Total nitrogen was determined by the Kjeldahl method, available phosphorus by Olsen method, and exchangeable potassium by flame photometry. Organic matter content was estimated using the Walkley Black method, while pH and EC were measured with digital pH and conductivity meter, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data, including quantitative, and biochemical attributes, were subjected to two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to determine the statistical significance of differences among mean values, followed by Tukey’s HSD test (p < 0.05) for multiple comparisons. To determine the combined radiation doses effect and correlation among quantitative traits and biochemical attributes, cluster plot and Pearson’s correlation coefficient analyses were performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to identify the patterns and relationship among quantitative and biochemical attributes and reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. All statistical analyses were performed using R-Studio (Version 4.2.2.) software with specific packages including factoextra with ggplot2 (for cluster plot), corrplot (for correlation), FactoMineR and factoextra (for PCA) and agricolae (for ANOVA).

Results

Effect of mutagen on quantitative traits

In the present study different doses of mutagen showed significant effect on the quantitative traits. Radiation had a significant effect on DTS, with a clear dose and cultivar dependent responses (Fig. 1A). Black Grapes exhibited the longest DTS (26 days) under zero radiation treatment and Sundarkhani had the shortest DTS (15 days) under the same conditions. As radiation doses increased, a consistent trend of prolonged DTS was observed across all cultivars, reflecting the inhibitory effect of radiation on sprouting. Black Grapes under 30 Gy exhibited the longest DTS (29 days), demonstrating a moderate but noticeable delay in sprouting time. Sundarkhani experienced a more pronounced delay, with DTS increasing to 20 days at 30 Gy. This suggests that Sundarkhani, while quicker to sprout initially, becomes more affected by higher radiation levels. Haitha Black and Haitha White also showed significant delays in sprouting as radiation doses increased. Haitha Black DTS increased from 17 days under control conditions to 24 days at 30 Gy, and Haitha White DTS extended from 17 days under zero radiation to 22 days at 30 Gy, showing a slower but consistent increase in sprouting time. Overall, these results indicate that higher radiation doses significantly prolong the days to sprouting in grape varieties, with each variety displaying a unique level of sensitivity.

For TC significant differences were observed in the Haitha Black and Haitha White when compared to control conditions under varying radiation doses (Fig. 1B). Initially, under zero radiation, the TC count for all cultivars was 6. Notably, Black Grapes showed no significant changes in TC across all radiation doses, indicating a relative resistance to radiation exposure. Sundarkhani showed 33.3% reduction in TC at 30 Gy. In contrast, the Haitha Black exhibited a marked sensitivity to radiation. At a dose of 20 and 30 Gy, there was a significant decrease in TC, with the count dropping by approximately 66% compared to the control group. This suggests that Haitha Black may be particularly susceptible to high doses of radiation. Moreover, Haitha White demonstrated 34%, 50% and 66% TC reduction at 10, 20 and 30 Gy, respectively.

Significant variations in the NSC were observed across all grape cultivar s in response to different radiation doses (Fig. 1C). Under zero radiation exposure, all cultivars consistently exhibited 6 sprouted cuttings. However, radiation exposure had a notable impact on the sprouting of cuttings across the different cultivars. NSC significantly decreased at 20 Gy and 30 Gy in Black Grapes (50%) and Sundarkhani (83%). This dramatic reduction suggests that these cultivars are highly sensitive to higher levels of radiation, which severely affects their ability to sprout. However, at the lower radiation dose (10 Gy), no significant change in NSC was detected in either cultivar (except Haitha Black), indicating a threshold below which radiation does not markedly affect sprouting. Haitha Black showed even more pronounced effects at certain radiation levels. At both 10 Gy and 30 Gy, only one cutting managed to sprout, highlighting the extreme sensitivity of this cultivar to radiation exposure, particularly at higher doses. In Haitha White at 30 Gy, only 50% of the cuttings sprouted, demonstrating a considerable reduction in sprouting capacity. However, at lower radiation doses, no significant variation in NSC was observed, suggesting that Haitha White is less sensitive to moderate radiation levels but is adversely affected when exposure reaches higher thresholds.

SP varied significantly among cultivars and radiation doses (Fig. 1D). Black Grapes at 10, 20 and 30 Gy resulted in 67%, 50% and 50% SP, respectively. The similar trend was also observed in Sundarkhani. At doses of 20 and 30 Gy the SP was 50%. Conversely, at 10 Gy SP significantly increased (83%). Black Grapes showed a steady decline with increasing radiation, whereas Haitha Black was highly sensitive, exhibiting poor sprouting at most doses. These findings indicate that cultivars differ in their resilience to radiation stress, with Sundarkhani and Haitha White showing relatively greater tolerance.

Effect of mutagen on growth related traits

PH responses varied significantly among cultivars (Fig. 2A). Black Grapes showed a strong decline, with PH reduced by ~ 35% at 20 and 30 Gy compared with controls, indicating high sensitivity to radiation stress. Sundarkhani exhibited growth stimulation at low doses, with an ~ 18% increased at 10 Gy and a smaller gain at 20 Gy, but no change at 30 Gy, suggesting a threshold effect. Haitha Black remained stable at 10 Gy and 20 Gy but showed a ~ 22% reduction at 30 Gy, reflecting tolerance to moderate doses but vulnerability to higher ones. In contrast, Haitha White displayed an early sensitivity, with a ~ 17% decrease at 10 Gy, yet maintained control like growth at higher dose. These findings highlight cultivar specific differences, with Sundarkhani benefiting from low dose exposure, while Black Grapes and Haitha White were more sensitive, and Haitha Black showed conditional tolerance.

Comparison of response of grape cultivars for Plant Height and Length of Tendril under different radiation doses (mean value ± SD). Means with different alphabets are significantly different (Tukey’s HSD, p˂0.05)

Tendril length of each cultivar exhibited a significant distinct pattern of change as the radiation dose increased. Black Grapes at 10 Gy exhibited a strong decline in LT by 23% compared to control. This reduction became more pronounced at higher doses, with a decrease of about 50% at 20 Gy and 51% at 30 Gy (Fig. 2B). In Sundarkhani at 10 Gy, there was a significant decrease of about 28% in LT, indicating early sensitivity to radiation. The reduction in LT continued at higher doses, with decreases of 52% at 20 Gy and 43% at 30 Gy. Haitha Black also exhibited significant decreases in LT as radiation doses increased. At the lowest dose of 10 Gy, a modest reduction of about 9% in LT was observed, indicating some initial resilience. However, at 20 Gy, LT decreased by approximately 42%, and at 30 Gy, the reduction reached 60%, highlighting a sharp decline in tendril growth as radiation exposure increased. Haitha White at 10 Gy, 20 Gy and 30 Gy demonstrated 51%, 59% and 49% reduction in LT, respectively. These findings reveal that tendril length in grape cultivars is highly susceptible to radiation, with all varieties experiencing significant reductions in LT as radiation doses increase. Black Grapes and Sundarkhani showed consistent decreases in tendril length with increasing radiation, while Haitha Black and Haitha White exhibited even more pronounced reductions at higher doses.

Effect of mutagen on leaf traits

Leaf length varied significantly (p < 0.05) in all cultivars when exposed to radiation doses (Fig. 3A). Each cultivar displayed distinct responses, reflecting their differing sensitivities to radiation. In Black Grapes at 10 Gy LL decreased by 22% compared to control. This reduction became more pronounced at higher doses, with LL decreasing by approximately 36% at 20 Gy and 42% at 30 Gy. Sundarkhani and Haitha White, however, exhibited remarkable resilience to radiation across all tested doses. This stability suggests that both Sundarkhani and Haitha White have a robust tolerance to radiation, maintaining normal leaf growth even under exposure. In Haitha Black at 10 Gy, no significant change in LL was observed, indicating initial resistance to radiation. However, at 20 Gy, LL decreased by 30.7% in LL, signaling the cultivar sensitivity at moderate radiation levels. Interestingly, at 30 Gy, LL decreased only by 18%, suggesting a less severe impact compared to the moderate dose, possibly indicating a non-linear response to radiation in this cultivar.

Comparison of response of grape cultivars for Leaf Length, Leaf Width, Leaf Ratio and Leaf Area under different radiation doses (mean value ± SD). Means with different alphabets are significantly different (Tukey’s HSD, p˂0.05)

The impact of radiation doses on leaf width across various grape cultivars was generally non-significant, with the exception of the Black grape variety (Fig. 3B). In Black grapes, exposure to 10 Gy of radiation did not result in a significant change in LW when compared to non-irradiated plants. However, at higher radiation doses, a notable reduction in leaf width was observed. Specifically, a 20 Gy dose led to a 35% decrease in LW, while a 30 Gy dose resulted in a 34% reduction. Both Sundarkhani and Haitha Black displayed 12% and 1.7% increase in LW at 10 Gy as compare to control, respectively. However, at 20 Gy, Sundarkhani, Haitha Black and Haitha White showed a decrease in LW by 3.1%, 2.4% and 15.7%, respectively, compare to control. These findings suggest that lower doses of radiation may have minimal impact on leaf morphology in all grape cultivars and higher doses can substantially reduce leaf width, potentially affecting overall plant vigor and productivity.

The effects of varying radiation doses on the leaf ratio across different grape cultivars were significant (Fig. 3C). In Black Grapes, LR decreased by 12.5%, 5.1% and 14.7% at 10 Gy, 20 Gy and 30 Gy, respectively. Sundarkhani exhibited a 21.5% decrease at 10 Gy and 3.2% by 20 Gy, whereas at 30 Gy, LR increased by 3.2%. Similarly, Haitha Black showed a reduction of 28.3% and 22.6% at 20 Gy and 30 Gy, respectively, while exhibiting a slight increase of 1.9% at 10 Gy. On the other hand, Haitha White displayed a distinct response compared to other cultivars, showing a consistent increase in LR with the increase in a radiation doses. Specially, LR increased by 1.3%, 6.3% and 8.9% at 10 Gy, 20 Gy and 30 Gy, respectively.

The effects of radiation on leaf area across various grape cultivars were observed. Black grape demonstrated a pronounced sensitivity to radiation (Fig. 3D). In Black grapes, exposure to 10 Gy of radiation caused a significant reduction of about 34% in LA compared to non-irradiated plants. As the radiation dose increased, this reduction in LA became even more pronounced. Dose of 20 Gy led to a substantial decrease of 59% in LA, while a 30 Gy dose resulted in an even greater reduction of 62%. These findings underscore the susceptibility of Black grapes to radiation, with even moderate doses leading to considerable declines in leaf area. However, the decline was most pronounced at 20 Gy, where LA was significantly lower in Sundarkhani (9.1%), Haitha Black (31.2%), and Haitha White (23.7%) compared to the control and other radiation doses.

All grape cultivars exhibited a significant variation in the length of leaf petiole when subjected to different radiation doses compared to the control (Fig. 4). Length of leaf petiole decreased with the increase in radiation dose in Black Grapes and Haitha Black. For Black grapes, a notable decrease in LLP was observed only at the highest radiation dose of 30 Gy, where a reduction of 21% was recorded compared to the control, while lower doses did not produce any significant changes. In contrast, the Sundarkhani responded differently, showing a significant increase in LLP by 14% at the lowest dose of 10 Gy, with no further changes at higher radiation levels. Haitha Black demonstrated no change in LLP at the 10 Gy dose, but higher doses of 20 Gy and 30 Gy resulted in significant reductions of 17% and 19%, respectively. Similarly, Haitha White displayed no significant variation in LLP at 10 Gy and 20 Gy, but at the highest dose of 30 Gy, there was a significant reduction of 18% compared to the non-radiated control. These results highlight the differential sensitivity of grape cultivars to radiation, with some exhibiting increased LLP at lower doses and others showing substantial decreases only at higher doses, reflecting the complex interplay between radiation levels and varietal response in grape plants for this trait.

Effect of mutagen on number of nodes and internodal distance

The effects of radiation doses on the number of nodes across various grape cultivars exhibited distinct patterns of response. At 10 Gy, NN increased in all grape cultivars, with Black Grapes, Sundarkhani, Haitha Black and Haitha White showing increases of 20.3%, 22.5%, 6.8% and 3.4%, respectively, compared to control (Fig. 5A). However, at higher radiation, NN decreased by 10.2% and 9.3% at 20 Gy and 30 Gy, respectively. Sundarkhani exhibited a 6.2% reduction at 30 Gy, Haitha Black and Haitha White showed significant decreased of 17% and 18.6% at 20 Gy, respectively, compared to control. Notably, Haitha White demonstrated the highest increase in NN, with a 23.4% rise at 30 Gy, suggesting its potential resistance to high radiation exposure.

Significant variations in internodal distance (ID) were observed across different grape cultivars when exposed to varying radiation doses (Fig. 5B). In Black grapes, a 10 Gy dose led to a substantial decrease in ID, with a reduction of approximately 21% as compared to control plants, followed by a 24% decrease at 20 Gy and a 31% decrease at 30 Gy, indicating a clear dose-dependent sensitivity. Sundarkhani grapes displayed a contrasting response, with a significant increase of 22% in ID at 10 Gy, suggesting a stimulatory effect at lower radiation levels. However, this trend reversed at higher doses, with a 21% decrease in ID at 20 Gy and a 25% decrease at 30 Gy. Haitha Black showed a marked reduction of 32% in ID at 30 Gy, while no changes were observed at lower doses, highlighting its sensitivity to high radiation exposure. In contrast, Haitha White remained largely unaffected across all radiation levels, except for a significant increase of 24% in ID at 20 Gy, suggesting a unique response to moderate radiation.

Effect of mutagen on phytochemical analysis

Total phenolic content

Significant changes were observed in the total phenolic content of studied grape cultivars under different radiation doses. At the lowest dose of 10 Gy, black grapes exhibited the highest increase in TPC, with a value of 901 ± 0.27 mg GAE/g FW. At 20 Gy, a notable increase of 752 ± 27.49 mg GAE/g FW was recorded compared to the control. However, at 30 Gy, there was a significant decrease, with TPC dropping to 448 ± 13.92 mg GAE/g FW. Overall as compared to control, black grapes showed a marked increase in TPC of approximately 48% and 24% at 10 Gy and 20 Gy, respectively (Fig. 6A). However, at 30 Gy, there was a significant decrease of around 26%. A similar trend was observed in the Sundarkhani variety. At the lowest dose of 10 Gy, the highest increase in TPC was recorded at 788 ± 22.30 mg GAE/g FW. At 20 Gy, TPC increased to 716 ± 41.55 mg GAE/g FW. However, at the highest dose of 30 Gy, there was a significant decrease in TPC to 406 ± 27.97 mg GAE/g FW. Overall, compared to the control, the increase in TPC was 40% at 10 Gy and 27% at 20 Gy, while at 30 Gy, a decrease of 27% was observed. Both Haitha Black and Haitha White showed a similar pattern. For Haitha Black, TPC increased to 955 ± 0.0 mg GAE/g FW at 10 Gy and 705 ± 26.95 mg GAE/g FW at 20 Gy, but decreased significantly to 416 ± 0.00 mg GAE/g FW at 30 Gy. Compared to the control, this corresponds to a 64% and 21% increase at 10 Gy and 20 Gy, respectively, and a 29% decrease at 30 Gy. In the case of Haitha White, TPC increased to 934 ± 12.34 mg GAE/g FW at 10 Gy and 723 ± 28.21 mg GAE/g FW at 20 Gy. However, at 30 Gy, TPC dropped to 416 ± 0.00 mg GAE/g FW. Compared to the untreated control plants, TPC in Haitha White increased by 67% at 10 Gy and 29% at 20 Gy, while it decreased by 27% at 30 Gy. Among all the grape cultivars, the highest increase in TPC at the lowest dose of 10 Gy was observed in Haitha White, with a 67% increase, followed closely by Haitha Black, which showed a 64% increase.

Total flavonoid content

Significant changes were also observed in the total flavonoid content of all grape cultivars under different radiation doses. In black grapes, a decreasing trend in TFC was evident as the radiation dose increased (Fig. 6B). At the lowest dose of 10 Gy, black grapes exhibited the highest increase in TFC, with a value of 506 ± 7.49 mg CE/g FW. At 20 Gy, there was still a notable increase to 407 ± 14.70 mg CE/g FW compared to the control. However, at 30 Gy, TFC significantly decreased to 197 ± 18.66 mg CE/g FW. Overall, compared to the control, black grapes showed a marked increase of approximately 54% at 10 Gy and 24% at 20 Gy, while at 30 Gy, there was a sharp decrease of about 60%. A similar trend was observed in the Sundarkhani, where at 10 Gy, the highest increase in TFC was recorded at 419 ± 12.65 mg CE/g FW, while at 20 Gy, TFC increased to 337 ± 27.36 mg CE/g FW. Discussion

Grapes, being one of the most valuable and important horticulture fruit crops, are being badly affected by the current climate change patterns worldwide. The rapid development and introduction of climate-smart resilient grape varieties are urgently needed to ensure global food security, particularly underdevelopment regions such as Balochistan. Mutation breeding is one of the best options available to employees and improves grapevines’ quantitative, phytochemical, qualitative and biochemical attributes. So far, gamma radiation has been successfully applied in different tree species [16, 35–37]. However, the application of gamma radiation in grape improvement remains limited. Due to their high penetration ability and availability, gamma rays are more effective and cost-efficient as compared to other forms of ionizing radiation. Abiotic stresses, such as heat and drought, are known to adversely affect grape growth, development and yield. Generally, the production of free radicals through ionization can improve plant morphology, phytochemical and biochemical processes. For example, exposure to radiation significantly reduced DTS, while PH, LL, LW, LR, LA, LLP, NN and ID were significantly increased (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Similarly, TPC, TFC, POD, SOD, CAT, total antioxidant capacity, PC, TS, RS and NRS significantly increased compared to the control (Figs. 6 and 7). However, lower radiation dose is known to have a more beneficial and favorable influence on overall plant development, a phenomenon called hormesis. Notably, in the present investigation, the lowest DTS was recorded at 10 Gy across all grape cultivars (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the smallest reduction in TC was recorded at 10 Gy in Haitha Black and Haitha White, while no reduction was observed in Black Grapes and Sundarkhani at the same radiation dose (Fig. 1B). A similar trend was also observed for NSC and SP. These finding are consistent with those of [16, 37, 38]. Moreover, maximum values for PH, LT, LL, LA, LLP, NN and ID, were observed at 10 Gy across different grape cultivars, as compared to 20 and 30 Gy. These results are in agreement with the results reported by [39–41]. Under abiotic stress conditions, ROS production increases, leading to oxidative stress that damages cellular components such as DNA, proteins, and lipids. To counteract this damage, plants must produce sufficient quantities of antioxidants (free radical scavengers) to mitigate stress effects. Gamma irradiation has been shown to be an effective means of enhancing antioxidant production in plant cells. Our results demonstrate that total antioxidant capacity increased significantly in all grape cultivars at 10 and 20 Gy doses. However, a slight decrease in total antioxidant capacity was observed in Black Grapes and Sundarkhani at 30 Gy compared to the control (Fig. 7A). Similarly, the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT were significantly elevated across all cultivars at 10 and 20 Gy (Fig. 7B, C and D). In contrast, at 30 Gy, all tested cultivars (Black Grapes, Sundarkhani, Haitha White, and Haitha Black) exhibited reduced SOD, POD, and CAT activities. These results align with the results reported by [42–44] while studying the effect of gamma rays on different crops physiology and biochemistry.

Plants adopt several strategies, including secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds and flavonoids, as their defense components to combat all kinds of stresses. Phenolic and flavonoids neutralize ROS and donate electrons to stabilize free radicals, preventing damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA [45]. Phenolics integrate into cell membranes to maintain membrane integrity and reduce lipid peroxidation under osmotic stress, such as salinity and drought [21]. Flavonoids influence hormone regulation, enhancing plant tolerance to stresses and improving plant growth and development [46]. In this study, we observed a significant increase in the concentrations of both phenolic and flavonoid contents when exposed to radiation doses (Fig. 6A and B). At 10 and 20 Gy, the concentration of TPC and TFC significantly increased in all grape cultivars, indicating a protective role of phenolics and flavonoids against ROS and membrane integrity and stability. The increase in phenolic content may be attributed to the breakdown of larger compounds into smaller ones, as well as the release of additional phenolic compounds from their glycosidic forms under gamma irradiation [47]. Moreover, protein content, total sugar, reducing sugar and non-reducing sugar also play crucial roles in adjusting metabolic activities to develop tolerance in plants to abiotic stresses. PC helps to maintain cell structure and function during stress, while TS act as osmolytes to maintain cell turgor during salinity and drought conditions [48]. RS are rapidly metabolized to provide energy and act as signaling molecules to regulative stress responsive genes during stress [49]. NRS are primarily involved in sugar transport that are associated with the stabilization of cellular membrane and proteins during stress [50]. Therefore, increased concentrations of PC, TS, RS and NRS under stresses are important indicators of enhanced stress tolerance in plants. In our study, PC levels increased significantly in all the grape cultivars when exposed to radiation dose of 10 Gy (Fig. 8A). The highest increase was observed in Haitha White (88%), followed by Sundarkhani (86%), Black Grapes (79%), and Haitha Black (66%) compared to the control. However, further increases in radiation dose (20–30 Gy) led to a decline in PC across all cultivars, indicating the detrimental effects of medium to high levels of gamma radiation. A similar trend was observed for TS. At 10 Gy, the highest increase in TS was recorded in Black Grapes (41%), followed by Sundarkhani, Haitha Black and Haitha Wihite. Likewise, RS levels peaked at 10 Gy, with Haitha Black showing the greatest increase (56%), followed by Sundarkhani, Black Grapes and Haith White. The highest NRS accumulation at 10 Gy was observed in Black Grapes, with a 79% increase compared to the control. In contrast, at 20 and 30 Gy, TS, RS and NRS contents decreased, highlighting the sensitivity of these biochemical components to higher dose of gamma radiation. These finding are consistent with those of [51–55].

Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive and negative correlations among the studied traits. For example, NSC, LA, LL, PC, RS, TFC, TPC and SOD exhibited strong positive correlation with SP, LW, LA, RS, TFC, POD, RS and POD, respectively (Fig. 9A). These results suggest that, an increase in LA is associated with a corresponding increase in LW, higher PC levels correlate with elevated RS content. However, we also observed strong negative correlation among the traits such as PH with DTS. Cluster plot analysis of the studied traits revealed a total explained variation of 59.6% (Fig. 10). Cluster analysis segregated the grape cultivars into four distinct groups based on their differential responses to radiation doses. Under control conditions (0 Gy), all cultivars including Black Grapes, Sundarkhani, Haitha Black, and Haitha White clustered together, indicating similar baseline phytochemical and biochemical profiles [56]. At 10 Gy radiation, Black Grapes, Haitha Black, and Haitha White formed a separate cluster, while Sundarkhani showed differential grouping. Further dose escalation (20 and 30 Gy) resulted in additional cluster separation, with cultivars regrouping based on radiation sensitivity. These results demonstrate a clear dose-dependent response pattern across cultivars and distinct quantitative, qualitative phytochemical and biochemical responses at 10 Gy compared to higher doses (20–30 Gy). Differential radiation sensitivity among cultivars, particularly evident in Sundarkhani’s unique clustering. PCA further validated the dose-dependent cultivar grouping observed in the cluster analysis. Multiple quantitative and biochemical traits exhibited strong loading toward the 10 Gy radiation treatment, indicating a consistent phytochemical response across cultivars at this specific dose (Fig. 11A and B). Positive associations among traits that clustered together along similar principal component axes. Negative associations among traits that showed diametrically opposed loading patterns. These PCA results provide strong evidence that grape cultivar responses to gamma radiation are trait-specific and dose-dependent. The analysis not only helped in simplifying complex multivariate relationships but also guided the identification of cultivar-treatment combinations with promising phytochemical and biochemical profiles. These findings provide a strong baseline for accelerating grapevine breeding programs through the use of radiation sources to induce useful mutations, which can subsequently be characterized using advanced molecular breeding techniques such as GWAS and related approaches [57].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that gamma radiation can effectively enhance morphological, phytochemical, and biochemical traits in grapevine cultivars, with responses strongly dose and cultivar-dependent. An optimal dose of 10 Gy consistently improved growth, sprouting, antioxidant activity, and secondary metabolite accumulation, while higher doses (20 and 30 Gy) reduced performance. Cultivar-specific responses were evident. Sundarkhani emerged as the most resilient, showing accelerated sprouting, increased height, larger leaves, and high phenolic accumulation, making it a strong candidate for breeding climate-resilient varieties. In contrast, Black Grapes was the most sensitive, exhibiting reduced growth but heightened biochemical stress responses. Haitha Black maintained stability at moderate doses but declined at higher radiation, while Haitha White showed early sensitivity yet maintained tolerance at elevated doses. These findings not only establish 10 Gy as an effective mutagenic dose for grapevine improvement but also provide the first systematic evidence from Pakistan on radiation-induced variation in this crop. By revealing cultivar-specific radiation responses, this work highlights mutation breeding as a rapid and cost-effective strategy for generating genetic diversity, improving stress resilience, and accelerating the development of climate-smart grape cultivars to support sustainable viticulture and regional food security.
