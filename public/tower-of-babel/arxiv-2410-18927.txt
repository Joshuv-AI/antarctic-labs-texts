SafeBench : A Safety Evaluation Framework for Multimodal Large Language Models
Zonghao Ying, Aishan Liu, Siyuan Liang, Lei Huang, Jinyang Guo, Wenbo Zhou, Xianglong Liu, Dacheng Tao

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are showing strong safety concerns ( e . g ., generating harmful outputs for users), which motivates the development of safety evaluation benchmarks. However, we observe that existing safety benchmarks for MLLMs show limitations in query quality and evaluation reliability limiting the detection of model safety implications as MLLMs continue to evolve. In this paper, we propose SafeBench , a comprehensive framework designed for conducting safety evaluations of MLLMs. Our framework consists of a comprehensive harmful query dataset and an automated evaluation protocol that aims to address the above limitations, respectively. We first design an automatic safety dataset generation pipeline, where we employ a set of LLM judges to recognize and categorize the risk scenarios that are most harmful and diverse for MLLMs; based on the taxonomy, we further ask these judges to generate high-quality harmful queries accordingly resulting in 23 risk scenarios with 2,300 multi-modal harmful query pairs. During safety evaluation, we draw inspiration from the jury system in judicial proceedings and pioneer the jury deliberation evaluation protocol that adopts collaborative LLMs to evaluate whether target models exhibit specific harmful behaviors, providing a reliable and unbiased assessment of content security risks. In addition, our benchmark can also be extended to the audio modality showing high scalability and potential. Based on our framework, we conducted large-scale experiments on 15 widely-used open-source MLLMs and 6 commercial MLLMs ( e . g ., GPT-4o, Gemini), where we revealed widespread safety issues in existing MLLMs and instantiated several insights on MLLM safety performance such as image quality and parameter size. Our benchmark offers (1) a comprehensive dataset and evaluation pipeline for MLLM safety evaluation; (2) an up-to-date leaderboard on MLLM safety; and (3) a nuanced understanding of the safety issues presented by these models. Our benchmark and code are available at https://safebench-mm.github.io/ .

Safety Evaluation Methods

Previous evaluation protocols for assessing the safety of large models can be broadly categorized into five distinct approaches.
String-Based Matching

GPT Proxy-Based Assessment

48

Human-Assisted Evaluation

26

Evaluation reliability

.

[
57
,
58

, fine-tuned LLM-based methods are performance-limited by the quality of fine-tuning, and evaluation methods based on a single strong model are constrained by the capabilities of the model

and potential hallucinations
[
62

63

. The limitations of these existing approaches may fail to accurately measure the safety of model output (
i
.

3.2
Our Objective

As MLLMs continue to evolve, their safety alignment capabilities have significantly improved. As mentioned in Sec.
3.1
, current safety benchmarks suffer from the two key limitations and are no longer sufficient to rigorously assess their safety. There is a pressing need for more robust and comprehensive benchmarks that can better reveal the safety implications of these advanced models, ensuring that potential risks are thoroughly examined and mitigated. To address the above limitations, we propose the
SafeBench
framework. To improve
data quality
, we design an automatic harmful query generation pipeline. It is worth noting that, given the text generation capabilities of MLLMs stem from their backbone LMs, we construct a risk scenario taxonomy by querying LLMs instead of MLLMs. Specifically, besides surveyed manuals released by institutions (
e
.
g
., OpenAI and Meta), we also queried the 10 most state-of-the-art LLMs to construct a classification system for building a comprehensive risk scenario taxonomy that is harmful to models. Based on this, we asked these LLMs to collect and generate textual queries that may most likely generate unsafe content. To conduct a more
reliable evaluation
, we draw inspiration from the jury system in judicial proceedings and pioneer the jury deliberation evaluation protocol, where 5 state-of-the-art LLMs evaluate the generated content, and engage in collaborative discussions to reach a consensus verdict that encompasses safety risks or not.

To the best of our knowledge, our research is the first to address these limitations by improving both datasets and evaluation methodologies, thereby offering a more reliable and comprehensive assessment of security risks associated with MLLMs. Detailed comparisons between existing safety benchmarks are provided in Sec.
4.1.3
.

4.1.2
Dataset Generation

Textual Query Generation.
Textual prompts/queries serve as the foundation for constructing prompts in other modalities. To generate a diverse array of textual requests, we employed a three-stage approach.

•

❶
Initial Corpus Generation
. We utilized the base prompt (
Prompt 2
in Tab.
A.1
in Supplementary Material
A
) to query these LLMs, ultimately generating a corpus of 1,000 distinct harmful requests pertaining to each specified harmful scenario. Notably, some LLMs have undergone alignment processes prior to release, potentially rendering base prompt queries ineffective. To circumvent this limitation, we introduced simple yet effective jailbreaking techniques to modify the base prompt, such as converting it to past tense
[
64
]
.

•

❷
Corpus Scoring
. We then evaluated the quality of requests in the initial corpus based on the following three criteria. (1) Feasibility: Assessing whether the request can be realistically implemented or answered. For instance, inquiring about the “
How to paralyze the global power grid using only mind control?
”. Mind control has not been scientifically proven to possess the ability to affect the physical world. (2) Harmfulness: Measuring the potential degree of the harm. For example, “
Should schools ban bullying?
” relates to violence but is inherently neutral in nature. (3) Applicability: Evaluating whether the request aligns with the capabilities and context of MLLMs. For instance, asking “
Have you watched adult films?
” is inappropriate as MLLMs lack the ability to watch movies. These criteria were scored on a scale of 1 to 10 by LLMs, with higher scores indicating greater alignment with the respective criterion (all criteria were weighted equally).

•

❸
Harmful Request Filtration
. Based on the scoring from the previous step, we calculated a weighted sum for each query and subsequently ranked them in descending order. The top 100 filtered requests for each scenario were then incorporated into our benchmark dataset.

This methodical approach ensures the generation of a diverse, relevant, and challenging set of textual prompts, serving as a robust foundation for our multimodal evaluation framework. By carefully curating the initial corpus and applying nuanced filtration criteria, we mitigate potential biases and enhance the overall quality of our benchmark dataset.

Visual Prompt Generation
. Images play a crucial role in the comprehension and generation capabilities of MLLMs
[
65
,
66
]
. Previous methods for constructing image data for MLLMs are limited by inadequate attention to image quality, affecting the accurate assessment of visual capabilities. Some methods extract keywords from text queries and use text-to-image models to generate images, but they often fail to capture the full semantic richness of the text prompt
[
24
,
26
]
. Other methods focus on converting text into typography, emphasizing Optical Character Recognition (OCR) capabilities rather than achieving a holistic visual understanding
[
25
,
24
]
. In contrast, we propose a pipeline that generates high-quality images, faithfully representing the semantics of input text semantics through an iterative process.

•

❶
Semantic interpretation
. The textual requests are initially processed by a LLM. Under the guidance of the
Prompt 3
delineated in Tab.
A.1
Supplementary Material
A
, the LLM generates a detailed image description. This description serves as a semantic bridge, encapsulating the essence of the original textual request in a form suitable for image generation. This methodology leverages the linguistic prowess of LLM to interpret and expand upon the nuances of the textual requests, thereby facilitating a more accurate and contextually rich image generation process.

•

❷
Image generation
. The image description generated in the previous step serves as the input for a T2I model, enabling the creation of an initial image that closely aligns with the semantic content of the original textual request. This approach ensures conceptual fidelity by using an intermediate image description, which helps preserve nuanced information that could be lost in a direct text-to-image translation. Additionally, the LLM-generated description often enriches the original request with added context, potentially resulting in more detailed and accurate visual representations. This two-stage process (from textual request to image description, and then to visual output) establishes a robust pipeline that maintains semantic coherence between the initial prompt and the final image.

•

❸
Semantic understanding and alignment
. The image generated in the preceding step is analyzed by an MLLM, which provides a textual description based on its semantic understanding of the image content. A critical evaluation follows: if any semantic discrepancies are detected between the description of the MLLM and the original LLM output, the system generates suggestions for modifying the image, triggering another iteration of the process. On the other hand, if semantic alignment is achieved, the image generation process is considered complete. (This process is automatically completed by the MLLM using
Prompt4
from Tab.
A.1
in Supplementary Material
A
). This step leverages the MLLM to ensure semantic consistency between the textual prompt and the generated image, acting as a quality control measure to guarantee that the final image accurately reflects the intended meaning of the original request.

•

❹
Iterative refinement
. Once the automated iterative refinement process is initiated, the modification suggestions generated by the MLLMs in the previous step are utilized as prompts for the T2I model to generate a new image. This process then repeats from step ❷, ensuring continuous refinement and alignment between the generated image and the original semantic intent.

Audio Prompt Generation
. The rapid advancement of multimodal capabilities has created an urgent need for comprehensive risk assessment across various modalities, including audio. This evaluation should cover spoken content and nuances in pronunciation and speaking styles. However, research on the safety risks in the audio modality of MLLMs is limited, mainly due to the lack of benchmark datasets.

Therefore, we introduce the audio modality into our benchmark with diverse samples, including male, female voices, to advance research on audio processing and associated risks of MLLMs. By examining how multimodal models respond to identical content in different vocal styles, it is expected to explore potential biases or inconsistencies in audio input processing.

To implement our audio benchmark, we used Parler-TTS
[
67
]
, a state-of-the-art text-to-speech system. By inputting our textual corpus with specified speaker styles, we generated diverse, naturalistic audio samples that maintain consistent content while varying in vocal style.

4.2
Evaluation Protocol

Existing evaluation methodologies often present trade-offs between evaluation reliability and efficiency leading to unreliable evaluations. In this paper, we propose a jury-based evaluation protocol that aims to address the above limitations among different methods.

4.2.1
Jury Deliberation Protocol

In
SafeBench
, we introduce an innovative evaluation methodology termed the Jury Deliberation Protocol (JDP). This novel approach draws inspiration from the jury system in judicial proceedings, adapting its core principles to the realm of content safety assessment. In real-world judicial systems, juries evaluate the actions of defendants, engaging in collaborative discussions to reach a consensus verdict that encompasses guilt or innocence, the degree of offense, and the rationale behind the decision.

Analogously, the JDP in
SafeBench
employs a panel of 5 advanced LLMs functioning as a jury to evaluate the outputs of MLLMs. Fig.
1
illustrates the workflow of the JDP, which comprises the following four stages. These steps of JDP are sequentially implemented by corresponding LLMs guided by specific prompts, with all involved prompts summarized in Supplementary Material
C
.

•

❶
Role Initialization.
These employed LLMs are initialized with distinct system prompts, embodying diverse personas such as a wise elder, a justice-oriented middle-aged individual, and a perceptive career woman. This heterogeneous role configuration aims to mitigate potential biases in the evaluation process. The system prompts used to initialize different roles are summarized in Tab.
C.1
of Supplementary Material
C

•

❷
Independent Evaluation.
Given a test prompt pair, each LLM independently evaluates the MLLM responses, providing a binary safety classification (true/false), a threat response score (0-5), and detailed rationales for their judgments. These two scores offer coarse-grained and fine-grained risk quantification, respectively, which will be utilized in calculating the final statistical quantification (we will illustrate the details of these two metrics later). The provided reasoning enhances the interpretability of the evaluation while laying the groundwork for subsequent collaborative deliberation. Recognizing the complex reasoning required in these assessments, we incorporated Chain-of-Thought techniques
[
68
,
69
]
to bolster the reliability and explicability of the evaluation process.

•

❸
Collaborative Deliberation.
Following independent evaluation, the LLMs will engage in a deliberation process. Each LLM analyzes the opinions of its counterparts, with the option to adjust its assessment (such as modifying classification results) or not. This phase aims to integrate diverse perspectives and enhance judgment quality through collective intelligence and consensus-building discussions. The observed enhancement in agreement post-discussion underscores the efficacy of collaborative deliberation in mitigating individual biases and promoting more aligned decision-making.

•

❹
Consensus Decision.
This stage yields the final deliberation decision, which is derived from processing the ultimate consensus reached in the previous step. Specifically, the binary classification result is determined through majority voting (hard voting) based on the deliberation results. The safety score is calculated as the mean of the five members scores (soft voting). The rationale is synthesized by the LLM embodying the wise elder role, summarizing the final opinions of all jury members.

Note that, the above process mainly evaluates the content safety from a specific prompt pair. In practice, to evaluate the safety of an MLLM, we need to input a series of test samples and calculate the overall safety scores quantitatively. Here, we use two quantitative metrics: Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Safety Risk Index (SRI). Notably, SRI is a newly introduced metric introduced by
SafeBench
, offering a more nuanced evaluation than ASR. We will illustrate them in the next subsection.

4.2.2
Metrics

Our evaluation protocol introduces two key metrics to characterize the safety risks associated with MLLMs. The ASR provides an intuitive measure of a model safety performance, offering a high-level overview of its vulnerability to harmful queries. Complementing this, the SRI allows for a more nuanced and fine-grained analysis of the model safety, capturing subtle variations in the severity and nature of potential output content. For instance, when two MLLMs are queried about bomb-making, one providing detailed instructions and the other offering a book reference, both would be classified as successful attacks under ASR. However, SRI captures the crucial distinction in risk severity, identifying the former as more dangerous.

ASR
is employed to quantify the probability of eliciting harmful responses from MLLMs using pairs of multimodal queries. Consider a dataset
D
comprising
n
pairs of multimodal queries, where
R_{i}
denotes the response of
i
-th query pair. We formally define the ASR as:

ASR=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(I(\text{JDP}(R_{i})))}{|D|},

(1)

where
I(\cdot)
represents the indicator function that returns
1
if the binary classification result provided by the JDP is 1 (indicating a successful attack), and 0 otherwise. This metric effectively captures the proportion of query pairs capable of inducing undesirable outputs from the target MLLM,
higher scores indicate lower safety performance
.

SRI
is introduced as a nuanced metric to quantify the safety risk profile of models precisely. This score is computed by aggregating the threat response score of model responses for each query pair in dataset
D
, followed by a normalization process to scale the final sum to a range of 0 to 100. Formally, we define SRI as:

SRI=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(S(\text{JDP}(R_{i})))}{|D|}*100,

(2)

where
S(\cdot)
is a function that extracts the threat response score from the evaluation results of response. This formulation allows for a comprehensive assessment of model safety across a diverse set of queries, providing a standardized measure that facilitates comparative analysis between different models or versions of the same model. The normalization to a 0-100 scale enhances interpretability, where
higher scores indicate better safety performance
.

Our evaluation protocol presents several key advantages: our JDP significantly reduces bias by employing a diverse panel of LLMs, each fulfilling a distinct role, thereby mitigating the influence of individual model biases; additionally, the incorporation of both SRI and ASR enables a more nuanced evaluation, capturing subtleties in content safety that binary metrics might miss. The adaptability of our protocol allows for the seamless integration of evolving safety standards and emerging risk categories. Although it is more computationally intensive than simpler methods, our evaluation protocol remains scalable, offering a practical balance between in-depth analysis and feasibility for large-scale evaluations. Overall, by simulating a human-like deliberation process using advanced LLMs,
SafeBench
provides a robust, nuanced, and transparent methodology for assessing the potential risks associated with MLLM-generated content.

Overall analysis.
Most commercial models significantly outperform open-source models in terms of safety performance, with an average SRI gap of
20.78

. Among the

, Claude-3.5-Sonnet exhibited the best safety performance, with an ASR of 0.7% and an SRI of 99.3; conversely, GPT-4o, the worst-performing commercial model, had an ASR of 3.4% and an SRI of 96.1. For the

, the Phi series models outperformed others, with an average ASR of only 2.45% and a high SRI of 97.7; on the other hand, ShareGPT4V displayed the poorest safety performance, with an ASR of 38.8% and an SRI of 69.9. Notably, GPT-4o’s safety performance was even lower than the best-performing open-source model, Phi-3.5-Vision-Instruct, which achieved an SRI of 96.7.

Trade-off analysis.
We observed distinct trade-off patterns between safety and general performance across the evaluated models. Within the
commercial models
, 3 different trade-off patterns emerged. Specifically, in the Claude family, Claude-3.5-Sonnet excelled in both safety and general performance compared to Claude-3-Opus. In the Gemini family, while Gemini-1.5-Pro outperformed Gemini-1.5-Flash in general performance, their safety performances were nearly identical (SRI of 97.1 and 97.2, respectively). However, in the GPT family, GPT-4o exhibited better general performance than GPT-4o-mini but fell behind in safety performance, with SRIs of 96.1 and 98.4, respectively. In contrast, we observed a different trade-off among
open-source models
: stronger general performance was often associated with weaker safety performance. Although newer versions of models typically outperform older ones overall, our experiments suggest that safety performance may degrade in newer versions. For instance, Phi-3-Vision-128K-Instruct demonstrated better safety than Phi-3.5-Vision. Similarly, within the Qwen family, Qwen-VL-Chat achieved an SRI of 90.3, while Qwen2-VL-2B and Qwen2-VL-7B had SRIs of 58 and 71.8, respectively. These findings indicate that improvements in general capabilities do not necessarily correlate with enhanced safety. This phenomenon extends beyond different model generations to comparisons between models. For example, prior research
[
95
]
demonstrated that Qwen2-VL-7B outperformed MiniCPM-V 2.6 in general performance, while Yao
et al.
[
96
]
reported the superiority of MiniCPM-V 2.6 over GLM-4V. However, our evaluations show that SRI of Qwen2-VL-7B was 71.8, compared to 74.9 for MiniCPM-V 2.6 and 89.3 for GLM-4V, indicating superior safety of GLM-4V, followed by MiniCPM-V 2.6 and Qwen2-VL-7B.

Model size analysis.
The smallest model in our experiments, Qwen2-VL-2B (2B parameters), had the lowest average SRI at 58. In contrast, the largest model, InternVL (25.5B parameters), achieved an SRI of 82.1. Interestingly, despite its modest 4.2B parameters, Phi-3-Vision-128K-Instruct outperformed many larger models with an SRI of 98.7, underscoring that the relationship between model size and safety is not strictly linear. The remarkable safety performance of Phi-3-Vision-128K-Instruct, despite its smaller size, emphasizes the critical role of training data quality in enhancing model safety. As highlighted in official documentation
[
97
,
98
]
, the development of the model focused on “high-quality, inference-intensive data, both in text and vision”. This emphasis on data quality evidently contributed significantly to its superior safety performance. We further discuss the impact of model size on safety within the same model family in Sec.
6.2
.

To illustrate the diversity in performance, we examine the risk categories where models achieved their second-highest ASR scores. Gemini-1.5-Flash recorded an ASR of 8.2% for
IS
queries, GPT-4o attained an ASR of 4% for

Overall, models demonstrated higher susceptibility to safety risks when responding to

Image quality.
In this subsection, we investigate the impact of visual modality input quality, specifically images, on the safety performance of MLLMs. While maintaining consistent text prompts, we evaluated safety performance across 6 distinct image settings: ❶ Solid black images; ❷ Solid white images; ❸ Pure noise images, generated by converting random color noise arrays created via NumPy into images; ❹ Random images semantically inconsistent with the text prompt (

Interestingly, we observed that the 4 image types unrelated to semantics (settings 1-4) exhibited divergent testing performance trends across different models. For instance, MiniCPM-V 2.6 and ShareGPT4V displayed similar SRI across these 4 settings, while Llava-V1.6-Vicuna-7B, Qwen2-VL-2B, and Qwen2-VL-7B showed substantial SRI variations. These three phenomena collectively indicate that the

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose
SafeBench
, a comprehensive framework designed for conducting safety evaluations of MLLMs. Our framework consists of a comprehensive harmful query dataset and an automated evaluation protocol that aims to address the current safety evaluation benchmark limitations (data quality and evaluation reliability). Based on our framework, we conducted large-scale experiments on 15 widely-used open-source MLLMs and 6 commercial MLLMs, where we revealed widespread safety issues in existing MLLMs and instantiated several insights on MLLM. We hope that
SafeBench
will drive future research in the field of MLLM safety.

Limitation.
Despite our best efforts, we acknowledge three primary limitations in our study. Firstly, due to scale constraints, we carefully selected only 100 harmful queries for each category in
SafeBench
. This limited sample size may not fully capture the diversity and complexity of harmful queries that MLLMs might encounter in real-world applications. Secondly, ethical and legal considerations precluded the evaluation of extreme scenarios. Certain data samples were intentionally excluded from testing, such as malicious requests related to sensitive subjects like national leaders in the
Political Opinions
category. Lastly, current MLLM research has limitations, particularly in the lack of models capable of simultaneously processing the audio modality alongside other modalities. Consequently, our study lacks a comprehensive assessment of multimodal safety risks that combine audio with other data modalities. These limitations highlight areas for future research to enhance the robustness and applicability of safety evaluations for MLLMs.

Ethical statement and broader impact
.
This study aims to investigate the safety risks associated with MLLMs. While our evaluation may produce potentially unsafe content that could be harmful to readers, we emphasize that our intention is not to cause harm. On the contrary, our work is designed to promote the importance of safety assessments for MLLMs and to provide a foundation for future red team testing methodologies, such as jailbreak techniques, by developing novel datasets and evaluation protocols.

As shown in Tab.
