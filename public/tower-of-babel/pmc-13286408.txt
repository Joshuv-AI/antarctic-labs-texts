Fracture–isthmus diameter ratio predicts delayed union in tibial nailing
Authors: Li-Kai Kuo, Chun-Yu Chen, Kai-Cheng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Intramedullary nailing is widely acknowledged as the preferred treatment for tibial shaft fractures. However, delayed union remains common in non-isthmal patterns. We aimed to assess the factors and imaging parameters associated with nonunion or delayed union following intramedullary nailing in these patients.Methods:This retrospective study included patients with non-isthmal tibial shaft fractures who underwent intramedullary nailing from 2015 to 2020. The fracture site diameter to the isthmus canal diameter ratio (FI ratio) was measured on pre- and postoperative anteroposterior (AP) radiographs. Delayed union was defined as no union at 6 months, with union by 12 months. The variables were tested with univariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression; receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis determined a cutoff.Results:A total of 66 patients were included, and delayed union occurred in 34 (51.5%). Forty-seven patients sustained infra-isthmal fractures, while 19 patients experienced supra-isthmal fractures. The postoperative FI ratio was higher in the delayed union group (median 1.67 vs 1.36; p = 0.001) and was the only independent predictor on multivariable analysis (p = 0.007). A postoperative FI ratio ≥1.43 predicted delayed union with sensitivity 0.79 and specificity 0.66 (area under the curve [AUC] 0.74); delayed union was more frequent when FI ≥1.43 than <1.43 (27/39 [69.2%] vs 7/27 [25.9%], odds ratio [OR] = 6.43). Intraoperative blood loss was associated with univariate testing (p = 0.013) but not after adjustment; nail–canal ratio, fracture length, and screw–fracture distance were not significant.Conclusion:The postoperative FI ratio is a feasible indicator of delayed union in patients who received intramedullary nailing for supra-isthmal or infra-isthmal tibial shaft fractures. A postoperative FI ratio ≥1.43 indicates a significantly higher risk of delayed union. In such cases, it may be recommended that patients undergo prolonged protected weight-bearing and shortened follow-up intervals.Keywords: Antegrade intramedullary nailing, Delayed union, FI ratio, Non-isthmal tibial shaft fracture, Nonunion

1. INTRODUCTION

The tibia is the most frequently fractured long bone and is responsible for approximately 2% of all adult fractures.1 The mechanism of tibial fracture exhibits a bimodal distribution; that is, low-energy trauma is more prevalent in patients over 50 years old and more often presents a spiral pattern, and high-energy trauma is more frequently observed in patients under 30 years old and likely to exhibit a transverse or comminuted pattern.2 The management of tibial fractures is primarily influenced by the mechanism, fracture pattern, and soft tissue condition. External fixators, plates, and intramedullary nails are the surgical interventions that are implemented most frequently.3 Intramedullary nailing is widely recognized as a viable treatment for tibial shaft fractures.4,5 This fixation technique offers the advantages of load sharing, extraosseous blood supply sparing, and limited soft tissue dissection, which enhance mechanical stability and the biological environment for fracture healing, thereby minimizing the risk of complications.6,7 However, there is a need for improvements in the management of complications following intramedullary nailing for tibial shaft fractures.8 Complications that may occur following intramedullary nailing of the tibia include delayed union, nonunion, malalignment, shortening, anterior knee pain, limited knee or ankle range of motion (ROM), implant failure, neurovascular injury, superficial wound infection, and osteomyelitis.9,10 Delayed union and nonunion are the most prominent among all the reported complications.9

Previous studies have reported multiple risk factors for delayed union and nonunion, such as age, smoking, closed or open fractures, fracture morphology according to the Müller AO/Orthopedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification, cortical continuity, fracture gap, infection, and osteomyelitis.11–14

Concerning imaging parameters in long bone fractures of the lower extremities, Donegan et al6 reported that nail–canal ratio is linked to tibial shaft fracture healing in patients undergoing intramedullary nailing for extra-articular tibial shaft fractures. However, the femur was the primary focus of most investigations. The risk of nonunion and delayed union in femur nailing can be determined by the FI ratio and the distance from the proximal screw to the fracture site.15,16 In a retrospective study on complications in femoral antegrade intramedullary nailing, Yang et al17 reported that the FI ratio is correlated with a higher complication rate. This is because the intramedullary nail is unable to achieve rigid cortical contact due to the short working length and large metaphyseal volume.

The FI ratio has been demonstrated to be associated with poor prognosis in femur fracture patients. Nevertheless, there is a lack of a comprehensive examination of FI in tibial fractures that are treated with intramedullary nail fixation. It is essential to explore the role of this indicator in the prognosis of tibial fractures, especially supra- or infra-isthmal fractures, which are regarded as having a propensity for unstable fixation.

In the current study, risk factors and preoperative/postoperative imaging parameters associated with nonunion and delayed union following intramedullary nailing for infra-isthmal and supra-isthmal tibial shaft fractures were identified and analyzed. Because the current literature on the FI ratio only measures preoperative anteroposterior (AP) views, bias may be induced by various angulations and rotations of the deformed long bone. Therefore, we additionally included the postoperative AP views to eliminate such bias.

We postulated that a higher rate of nonunion or delayed union is associated with a larger intramedullary diameter at the extension end of a fracture site in the intramedullary fixation of the non-isthmal tibial shaft.

2. METHODS

2.1. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This retrospective study was conducted at a single medical center. The study design and protocol were approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (approval number: KSVGH21-CT7-06). The study included patients with non-isthmal tibial shaft fractures (AO/OTA: 42, excluding fractures confined within the narrowest part of the middle-third tibia) who underwent intramedullary nailing with the Expert Tibial Nail (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA) from January 2015 to April 2020 and had a minimum of 1 year of follow-up. The exclusion criteria were: (1) previous trauma to the ipsilateral lower limb; (2) multiple traumas that necessitated additional surgery besides tibial fixation; (3) pathological fractures; (4) patients who have not undergone the minimum 1 year of follow-up; and (5) proximal and distal tibial fractures (AO/OTA: 41, 43 types) patients.

2.2. Operative method/rehabilitation protocol/follow-up timeframe

A total of six orthopedic surgeons, all experts in fracture and trauma care, performed the surgeries. All surgical procedures are standardized and adhered to the surgical technique guide pertaining to the Synthes Expert Tibial Nail. To achieve a flexion position of the knee, we used a radiolucent triangle beneath the knee in conjunction with a supine position on the radiolucent table. Next, using the trans-patellar tendon approach to start the awl, reaming 1 to 1.5 mm larger than the nail diameter, and after placing the nail. The proximal and distal screw fixation always consists of a dynamic screw and at least one static screw. All screws were oriented perpendicular to the nail.18

The rehabilitation protocol and follow-up plan adhered to a standardized protocol that has been in place at our institution for several years. The patients underwent passive ROM exercises immediately after surgery. Partial weight-bearing with quadricane was recommended and complete weight-bearing was initiated after 6 weeks as per the patient’s tolerance.

The medical records of all patients included in the study were reviewed, and data related to cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus, body mass index (BMI), and American Society of Anesthesiologists Classification (ASA Class) were documented.19

The type of open fracture was defined clinically based on the Gustilo–Anderson classification system. The intraoperative data, which included the operative duration and blood loss during the operation, and the presence of combined fibular fractures, were documented.

The follow-up timeframe was monthly for the first 3 months, and then every 3 months during the first year post-surgery. Two orthopedic surgeons with a subspecialization in trauma reviewed the radiographic images. A third orthopedic surgeon was consulted if the two were unable to concur.

2.3. Radiographic evaluation

Plain radiographs (AP and lateral views) were obtained before and immediately after surgery and at each follow-up visit at the outpatient department. The Radiographic Union Scale for Tibial fractures was used to determine the extent of bone regeneration.20–22 Delayed union was defined as the absence of bony union at 6 months, which was completed by 12 months following the initial injury. In the AP view, the tibial intramedullary diameter of the isthmus was measured at the narrowest part of the tibial shaft. Depending on whether the fracture was supra-isthmal or infra-isthmal, the intramedullary diameter was measured at the most proximal or distal part from the fracture site, perpendicular to the anatomical axis of the tibia. The most proximal part was used for supra-isthmal fractures, while the most distal part was used for infra-isthmal fractures. Fracture length was determined as the distance from the most proximal to the most distal fracture line along the tibial axis. The distance from the nearest screw to the fracture site was also measured along the tibial axis.

The FI ratio was defined as the ratio of the fracture site diameter to the tibial intramedullary diameter of the isthmus.17 Both pre- and postoperative FI ratios were evaluated (Fig. 1). The nail–canal ratio was defined as the diameter of the intramedullary nail divided by the intramedullary diameter of the isthmus.6

2.4. Statistics methods

We compared the differences between the delayed union and non-delayed union groups based on various parameters. This study represented continuous data as mean ± SD, while categorical data were reported as percentages. To assess inter-rater reliability, two independent observers measured five radiographic parameters: Preoperative FI ratio, postoperative FI ratio, nail–canal ratio, fracture length, and distance from the nearest screw. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model (ICC [3,1]) to assess absolute agreement. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric methods were applied to normally distributed data, while nonparametric methods were used for non-normally distributed data. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using independent t tests for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For categorical variables, χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests were used. Multivariate regression analysis was performed to ascertain the risk factors that contributed to delayed union, including variables with a p value <0.05 in the comparison analysis. Clinically significant variables were also considered for adjustment. All data obtained in the study were recorded and analyzed using the SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). A p value <0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

This study included 66 patients with a mean age of 47.6 years (SD = 19.2 years) and a mean BMI of 24.7 kg/m2 (SD = 4.52 kg/m2). Forty-four (66.7%) patients were male, while 22 (33.3%) were female. Nineteen patients were smokers (28.8%), and nine provided a medical history of diabetes mellitus (13.6%). Twenty-five (37.9%) patients had experienced open fractures, of which five were Gustilo type I (20.0%), 11 were Gustilo type II (44.0%), and nine were Gustilo type III (36.0%). Fifteen of 25 patients underwent two-stage surgery (60%). Three patients in this patient cohort experienced infection and required debridement and antibiotic treatment (4.5%).

Delayed union was observed in 34 patients (51.5%). The average time to union for the entire patient cohort was 26.33 weeks; 16.7 weeks for the non-delay group and 36.1 weeks for the delayed group. Only two patients underwent a second operation for bone grafting and were identified to have experienced nonunion (3.0%). The first patient did not achieve union until 7 months after the operation and underwent autologous bone grafting (harvested from the proximal tibia) and structural augmentation with a side plate. The other patient was diagnosed with a Gustilo IIIB open fracture and did not achieve union until 6 months after the operation. The patient received autologous bone grafting from the left iliac crest because of a more significant defect at the nonunion site. Both patients finally attained bone union.

In terms of trauma mechanics, 28 patients (82.4%) in the delayed union group were injured in traffic accidents, while the remaining six patients (17.6%) were injured in falls. In the non-delayed union group, 26 patients (81.3%) were injured in traffic accidents, and six patients (18.7%) sustained a fracture as a consequence of a fall, which showed no significant difference concerning injury mechanism in both groups (p = 0.705).

Thirty (45.5%) patients experienced a simple fracture, whereas 36 (54.5%) experienced a comminuted tibial shaft fracture (AO/OTA type 42-B or 32-C). Fifty-five (83.3%) patients suffered from concomitant ipsilateral fibular fractures: 29 patients in the delayed union group (52.7%) and 26 patients in the non-delayed union group (47.3%). Of these, 29 patients were at the same level as tibial fracture (18 delayed, 11 non-delayed). In the delayed group, 18 of 29 patients (18/29, 62.1%) were at the same level, while in the non-delayed group, 11 of 26 patients (11/26, 42.3%) were at the same level. These findings demonstrated no significant difference (p = 0.143). A total of 17 patients’ fibula were fixed, with only one patient requiring fixation with a K-pin. The remaining 16 were treated with 1/3 tubular plates (17/55 = 30.1%).

Forty-seven (71.2%) patients experienced infra-isthmal fractures, whereas 19 (28.8%) experienced supra-isthmal fractures. The incidence of delayed union in patients with proximal third tibial fractures treated with nailing was 70% (14/20), while in those with fractures in the distal third, it was 43.48% (20/46). The average recorded time from ER admission to definite fixation was 41.5 hours.

The median intraoperative blood loss was 175 and 100 mL, respectively, in the delayed union and non-delayed union groups (p = 0.013). The median postoperative FI ratios were 1.67 and 1.36 (p = 0.001), respectively. The mean nail–canal ratios were 0.90 and 0.86, respectively (p = 0.193). The median fracture lengths were 5.97 and 4.06, respectively (p = 0.202), and the median distances from the nearest screw to the fracture site were 2.46 and 2.98, respectively (p = 0.649). The ICC results demonstrated good to excellent reliability across all measurements, with values of 0.800 for preoperative FI ratio, 0.884 for postoperative FI ratio, 0.851 for nail–canal ratio, 0.916 for fracture length, and 0.882 for distance from the nearest screw. These findings indicate a high degree of consistency between the two independent observers, ensuring the reliability of the radiographic assessments in this study. Univariate analysis revealed that intraoperative blood loss and postoperative FI ratio were significantly associated with delayed union (Table 1). The multivariate logistic regression analysis included variables such as blood loss during the operation, categories of open fracture, and postoperative FI ratio. The results suggested that intraoperative blood loss and open fractures did not exhibit any statistical significance (Table 2). However, the postoperative FI ratio was observed to be an independent risk factor for delayed union (p = 0.007).

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to determine the cutoff value of the FI ratio for predicting delayed union. The ROC curve showed that in predicting delayed union, a postoperative FI ratio ≥1.43 had a sensitivity of 0.79 and a specificity of 0.66 (Fig. 2). Thus, the delayed union rate was significantly higher in patients with an FI ratio ≥1.43 (27/39, 69.2%) compared with that in patients with an FI ratio <1.43 (7/27, 25.9%; area under the curve [AUC] = 0.74).

4. DISCUSSION

In our study, a higher postoperative FI ratio (≥1.43, 27/39, 69.2%; AUC = 0.74) was observed to be an independent risk factor for nonunion or delayed union in patients who underwent intramedullary nailing for supra-isthmal or infra-isthmal tibial shaft fractures. This outcome aligns with our hypothesis that a larger intramedullary diameter at the extension end of a fracture site is associated with a higher rate of nonunion or delayed union.

Tibial shaft fractures constitute approximately 17% of all lower limb fractures.23 Intramedullary nailing fixation is regarded a reliable option, despite the ongoing controversy surrounding the use of a plate or intramedullary nail for the fixation of extra-articular tibial fractures.24–27 Xue et al28 conducted a systemic review and meta-analysis to compare the efficacy of intramedullary nails and plates in the treatment of extra-articular tibial fractures. They reported better functional recovery and a lower risk of infection but a higher malunion rate with intramedullary nailing-based fixation. Besides the extensive application of intramedullary nailing in mid-shaft isthmal tibial fractures, it can also be used in the supra-isthmal and infra-isthmal tibial fracture treatment.29,30 In the absence of a precise reduction, the intramedullary nail may be used to fix supra-isthmal and infra-isthmal fractures with an axial/sagittal angulation or rotation malalignment.31

Dailey et al12 observed a higher risk of nonunion in patients in their middle age and with an open fracture, infection, and high-energy injury patterns. Smoking, age (as a continuous variable), and sex did not elevate the risk of nonunion in their cohort. The number of nonunion cases was insignificant in our study however, the delayed union cases constituted more than half (34/66, 51.52%) of all cases. The increased prevalence of delayed union in our cohort may be attributable to the relatively broad boundaries of our definition. We also did not exclude patients with open fractures or severely comminuted fracture patterns. Nevertheless, no significant difference was observed in such characteristics between the two groups. We categorized the patients into two groups to identify factors related to delayed union and found no difference between the two groups in terms of age, sex, weight, diabetes mellitus, and smoking. Considering that the biological rationale of the harmful effects of smoking is well recognized, previous studies hypothesized that the paradoxical results were caused by the relatively young distribution of the trauma patients, which compromised the harmful effects of smoking.32,33 Based on flaps, compartment syndrome, chronic conditions, open fractures, male sex, ASA score, and cortical contact, O’Halloran et al14 developed a clinical nonunion prediction score for tibial shaft fractures treated with intramedullary nails. However, due to a lack of robust external validation, the clinical validity of the score was undetermined.34,35 An open-type fracture was not detected to be a predictor of delayed union in our study, likely due to our institution’s aggressive debridement policy within 12 hours after injury. A more comminuted fracture pattern is considered a significant risk factor for nonunion in many studies.13,14,36 However, our study did not identify any correlation between fracture morphology and delayed union. Two possible reasons for this seemingly paradoxical phenomenon were identified. First, this may be attributed to a higher incidence of delayed union in a less comminuted fracture pattern, which is a result of increased tissue stripping rather than bone injury that is typically observed in good-quality bone. Second, it could be due to the altered interfragmentary mechanical strains during weight-bearing, which is induced by the fracture morphology.12 In more comminuted fractures resolved by indirect reduction and intramedullary nail fixation, a slight increase in the strain between each fracture fragment positively impacts the secondary healing process, which is frequently linked to an increase in callus.

The influence of an intact fibula on intramedullary nailing for tibial fracture remains controversial. Teitz et al37 discovered that an intact fibula could result in delayed union and the sequelae of joint disturbances observed in the conservative treatment of tibial shaft fractures. Alternatively, Strauss et al38 proposed that the fixation of the fibular fracture at the same level as the tibial fracture enhanced stability, while Kabukçuoğlu et al39 suggested that an intact fibula in tibial diaphysis fractures treated with intramedullary nailing did not influence the union rate or reduction quality. The rate of delayed union did not exhibit a statistically significant difference between intact and fractured fibulas in our data. The complexity of surgical intervention is reflected in the prolonged operating time and operative blood loss, which in turn affect the development of postoperative complications in orthopedic surgeries.40,41 Compared with open reduction and plate osteosynthesis, intramedullary nailing for distal extra-articular tibial fractures results in a higher intraoperative blood loss, radiation time, and rotational malalignment.42 Greater operative blood loss leads to higher blood transfusion rates, which is related to significantly greater surgical site and overall infection rates in orthopedic surgery.43 Blood loss during intramedullary reaming impacts the degree of morbidity resulting from acute anemia and transfusion postoperatively.26 However, in our study, multivariate analysis suggested that blood loss is not an independent predictor for delayed union in patients who underwent intramedullary nailing for non-isthmal tibial fractures.

The incidence of malalignment in the intramedullary nailing of isthmal fractures is reduced by the tight endosteal fitting of the nail in the medullary canal. In contrast, the intramedullary nailing for fractures in the proximal or distal third of the tibial shaft was regarded as controversial due to difficulties in fracture reduction and susceptibility to malalignment due to marginal widening of the medullary canal.44 Therefore, intramedullary fixation does not seem to be as effective for supra-isthmal and infra-isthmal fractures as it is for isthmal tibial fractures. Lang et al45 noted a high complication rate in patients receiving intramedullary nailing for fractures of the proximal third of the tibial shaft, with 28% of the patients requiring reoperation (exchange nailing), 13% requiring a bone graft, and 25% experiencing a loss of reduction. Nevertheless, with appropriate skills, intramedullary nailing can still be advantageous in supra-isthmal and infra-isthmal tibial fractures.7 By identifying specific risk factors for delayed union, we can optimize the postoperative care for patients with supra-isthmal and infra-isthmal tibial fractures.

Yang et al17 reported that the FI ratio is related to an increased incidence of complications, including nonunion, malalignment, fixation failure, and progressive loss of reduction in the intramedullary nailing of the femur. The FI ratio considers both the influence of the distance of the distal fragment and the width of the femoral canal, which are both related to the stability of the nail. We believe that the same concept may be utilized in intramedullary nailing of the tibia, especially in non-isthmal fractures. In the present study, we evaluated the preoperative and postoperative FI ratios in patients who underwent tibial intramedullary nailing. We detected no statistical difference in the preoperative FI ratio between the two groups. Nevertheless, the postoperative FI ratio exhibited a substantial degree of variation, with the delayed union group exhibiting a substantially higher postoperative FI ratio. The postoperative ratio may eliminate the bias induced by various angulations and rotations of the deformed tibia observed in preoperative AP X-ray views. Furthermore, the postoperative FI ratio demonstrated a higher ICC of 0.884, compared with 0.800 for the preoperative FI ratio, indicating superior measurement reliability. This suggests that the postoperative ratio is less susceptible to variability and is more precisely quantifiable. As a result, it provides a more accurate representation of fracture characteristics and serves as a better predictor of delayed union risk. By performing multivariate logistic regression analysis, we determined the postoperative FI ratio for tibial fractures to be an independent risk factor for delayed union (p = 0.007). The delayed union rate was significantly higher with a postoperative FI ratio ≥1.43 (27/39, 69.2%; AUC = 0.74). Patients with such conditions may be recommended to undergo guarded weight-bearing for a longer duration and shorter follow-up intervals. In the present study, only two patients underwent a second operation for bone grafting and were diagnosed with bone nonunion. At 2.11 and 2.53, their FI ratios were both significantly higher than the threshold value. However, both of them achieved bone union after bone grafting (Fig. 3). Donegan et al6 reported that a nail–canal ratio between 0.8 and 0.99 can maximize tibial shaft fracture healing after intramedullary nailing for extra-articular tibial shaft fractures. However, in our study, the nail–canal ratio exhibited no significant difference between the delayed union and non-delayed union groups. Considering that our hospital’s surgical protocol endorses reaming and tight endosteal fitting of the nail in the medullary canal, the mean nail–canal ratio in both groups was between 0.8 and 0.99 (delayed union: 0.90; non-delayed union group: 0.86). Therefore, no significant difference was identified in the nail–canal ratios between the delayed union and non-delayed union groups. The distance from the proximal screw to the fracture site in intramedullary nailing of the tibia was not examined in previous studies in relation to nonunion or delayed union. However, a biomechanical study on antegrade intramedullary nailing of the femur revealed that, in distal femoral shaft fractures, optimal results (surviving 1 million compression/bending cycles) could be achieved when the fracture site was located more than 3 cm away from the most proximal distal screw.46 We did not observe a statistically significant correlation between the distance from the nearest screw to the fracture site and nonunion or delayed union in the intramedullary nailing of the tibia (p = 0.649), due to the relatively short distance in both delayed union and non-delayed union groups (2.46 and 2.98, respectively).

We limited the implant choice solely to one type of intramedullary nail to reduce bias concerning different implants. In addition, this implant is also the most frequently employed at our institution, and all surgeons possess extensive experience with it. This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective nature of our study design may influence the precision of patient data. Second, we focused on patients who were treated with a single implant type. While this may mitigate the bias associated with various implants, it may also restrict the generalizability of our findings. Third, the number of cases was small. Further studies with a larger dataset may provide stronger evidence for interpretation.

In conclusion, the postoperative FI ratio is a valuable indicator of delayed union in patients who underwent intramedullary nailing for supra-isthmal or infra-isthmal tibial shaft fractures. A postoperative FI ratio ≥1.43 indicates a significantly higher delayed union rate. Prolonged guarded weight-bearing and shorter follow-up intervals may be recommended in such patients. Future studies with a larger dataset or focusing on other kinds of intramedullary nails will further clarify the efficacy of the FI ratio in predicting delayed union in patients undergoing intramedullary nailing for tibial fractures.
