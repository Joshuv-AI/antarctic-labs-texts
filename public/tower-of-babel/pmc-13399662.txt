Angiocatheter‐Assisted All‐Inside Repair of Meniscal Ramp Lesions: A Simple and Cost‐Effective Technique
Authors: Mohammad Ayati Firoozabadi, Mohammad Rastegar, Pouya Tabatabaei Irani, Rahmatullah Azimi, Armin Akbarzadeh, Seyed Mohammad Javad Mortazavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRamp lesions of the medial meniscus are frequently associated with anterior cruciate ligament injuries and contribute to persistent instability if untreated. Diagnosis is challenging, and repair techniques often require posteromedial portals or specialized devices. We describe a simplified angiocatheter‐assisted all‐inside repair performed entirely through anterior portals. Using an 18‐gauge angiocatheter loaded with FiberWire sutures (Arthrex, Naples, FL), sutures are passed across the meniscocapsular junction and secured with a sliding Duncan knot. This method avoids posteromedial dissection, reduces operative time, and eliminates dependence on costly implants. It is safe, reproducible, and cost‐effective, offering a practical alternative when conventional repair systems are unavailable.

Arthroscopy is performed through standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals. The right knee is completely evaluated. Then, the arthroscope is advanced between the posterior cruciate ligament and the medial femoral condyle, to assess the meniscocapsular region. Once the ramp lesion is identified, initial shaving of the tear site is performed via the anteromedial portal. The angiocatheter is passed through the capsule and meniscus at the tear site, and the suture is retrieved intra‐articularly. The catheter is withdrawn from the meniscocapsular junction while maintaining approximately 5 mm of distance from the skin, allowing the suture to remain within the lumen. The suture is then advanced again through the meniscocapsular region, resulting in 2 passes approximately 5 mm apart. A sliding knot is tied using a knot pusher, ensuring secure fixation. Ramp lesions are frequently associated with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears, with a reported prevalence ranging from 9% to 40% in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction. 1 , 2 Their clinical importance lies in the medial meniscus's stabilizing role, which limits anterior tibial translation, particularly in ACL‐deficient knees. 3

Diagnosis remains challenging, as magnetic resonance imaging shows variable sensitivity ranging from 53.9% to 84.6%. 4 Therefore, systematic arthroscopic evaluation—using the trans‐notch view (modified Gillquist maneuver) or an accessory posteromedial portal—is essential to avoid underdiagnosis, which may occur in up to 40% of cases if these views are not employed. 5 , 6

Treatment strategies depend on lesion size and stability. Small, stable lesions may be managed with biological techniques such as debridement or trephination, whereas larger or unstable lesions typically require surgical repair. 7 , 8 , 9 Recent meta‐analyses suggest that repair yields superior outcomes in unstable lesions, whereas biologic augmentation may suffice for stable lesions. 10

Several surgical approaches have been described. The posteromedial portal with an outside‐in technique is widely used, 11 whereas all‐inside implants have gained popularity due to their ability to avoid accessory portals, reduce operative time, and achieve high healing rates with low complication rates. 12 , 13 Some authors, however, advocate posteromedial portals for improved access and potentially reduced reoperation rates. 14

We describe a simple all‐inside technique using only anterior portals and a standard angiocatheter. This approach eliminates posteromedial portal complications and provides a reliable alternative when meniscal repair systems are unavailable.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The patient is positioned supine under spinal anesthesia, with a tourniquet applied to the proximal thigh and inflated to 100 mmHg above systolic pressure after sterile preparation and draping. The operative leg is allowed to hang off the side of the table (Figure 1), and arthroscopy is performed through standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals.

The operated right knee is hanging off the table. Anteromedial and anterolateral portals are marked (right side).

The knee is systematically evaluated. To improve visualization, the medial collateral ligament is released using the pie‐crust technique with a needle (Table 1). The arthroscope is advanced between the femoral condyles, specifically between the posterior cruciate ligament and the medial femoral condyle, to assess the meniscocapsular region. When a ramp lesion is identified (Figure 2), initial shaving of the tear site is performed via the anteromedial portal.

An 18‐gauge angiocatheter loaded with a FiberWire suture (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is then introduced (Figure 3). The angiocatheter is passed through the capsule and meniscus at the tear site, and the suture is retrieved intra‐articularly. The catheter is withdrawn from the meniscocapsular junction while maintaining approximately 5 mm of distance from the skin, allowing the suture to remain within the lumen. The suture is then advanced through the meniscocapsular region, resulting in 2 passes approximately 5 mm apart (Figure 4). A sliding Duncan knot is tied using a knot pusher, ensuring secure fixation entirely within the joint for an all‐inside repair. Excess suture is trimmed after confirming knot stability.

This technique allows repair to be performed exclusively through the 2 standard anterior portals. In the posteromedial region, the angiocatheter is advanced without creating an accessory portal, thereby avoiding potential complications associated with posteromedial access. Postoperative Care

Patients are discharged the day after surgery, begin immediate partial weight‐bearing, and commence physiotherapy with full range‐of‐motion exercises.

DISCUSSION

Multiple surgical techniques have been described for the management of medial meniscal ramp lesions, yet no universally accepted gold standard exists. Ramp lesions are recognized as a major contributor to recurrent instability following ACL reconstruction. 15 , 16 Biomechanical studies confirm that these lesions increase tibial rotation and anterior tibial translation when associated with ACL injury. 17 Although isolated ACL reconstruction restores anterior tibial stability, it does not eliminate the pivot shift in the presence of a ramp lesion. 3

Traditional repair methods often rely on posteromedial portals with suture hook devices. 18 Fast‐fix and Scorpion‐assisted all‐inside techniques have shown high healing rates with low complication profiles. 19 , 20 However, these approaches require specialized instruments that may not be universally available. Our angiocatheter‐assisted technique offers a simple, cost‐effective alternative using equipment readily accessible in any operating theater (Table 2).

Advantages and Disadvantages

Gómez Cáceres et al. described a similar approach employing the pie‐crust technique and standard anterior portals, but their method still required specific devices. 13 Brito de Alencar Neto et al. used the Knee Scorpion suture passer, which provides precise fixation but is costly and dependent on specialized availability. 20 Perelli et al. reported a needle‐assisted repair through a posteromedial portal, which simplified the procedure but retained the risk of neurovascular injury inherent to posteromedial dissection. 21 In contrast, our angiocatheter‐assisted all‐inside technique achieves comparable fixation entirely through anterior portals, reducing operative time, avoiding posteromedial complications, and minimizing costs. Systematic reviews consistently emphasize that unstable lesions require repair, with favorable outcomes regardless of technique. 10 Our method builds on this evidence by eliminating the need for accessory portals and specialized implants, aligning with simplified all‐inside and no‐implant strategies. The angiocatheter is inexpensive, widely available, and safe, making this technique reproducible across diverse surgical settings. It also serves as a practical alternative when conventional meniscal repair devices are unavailable or malfunction intraoperatively. Ultimately, the choice of technique should be guided by the surgeon's expertise, lesion characteristics, and available resources. Future randomized controlled trials comparing simplified, cost‐effective methods with established techniques are warranted to validate long‐term outcomes.
