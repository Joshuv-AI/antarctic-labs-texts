Three methods for measuring the Earth’s magnetic field intensity in Apis mellifera (Hymenoptera: Apidae) drone congregation areas
Authors: Harold Pulido-Guarín, Ana Carolina Monmany-Garzia, Alejandra Carla Scannapieco, Andrés Felipe Ramírez-Mejía, Martín Geria, Alberto Galindo-Cardona, Juliana Rangel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presence of magnetite in the abdomen of Apis mellifera suggests a role in magnetic signal perception, aiding orientation and navigation. Reproduction occurs in drone congregation areas (DCA), which are spatially and temporally stable, appearing in spring–summer during regionally specific mating flight times (midday to evening), when Earth’s magnetic field intensity (EMFI) often increases. This coincidence points to a possible link between magnetism and DCA location. We evaluated 3 EMFI measurement methods—2 mobile apps (Crowdmag, Phyphox) and a low-cost magnetometer—at 3 known DCA in Argentina. At each site, 80 georeferenced sampling points were arranged in a 6 m grid. EMFI was recorded daily during the morning and evening at 10 and 150 cm above the ground. Results showed moderate positive correlations between EMFI and geographic coordinates and height. A weak positive correlation was also detected between EMFI and time of the day. All methods detected EMFI variation, but the low-cost magnetometer gave the most consistent readings, supporting its value as an affordable, reliable tool for EMFI assessment in apicultural studies. Present findings provide a baseline for investigating methodologies to assess the potential role of EMFI affecting DCA formation and adult male honey bee orientation during mating flights, with potential applications in improving reproductive management of A. mellifera colonies.Keywords: mating behavior, Earth’s magnetism, honey bee drone, spatial pattern

Introduction

Bees—particularly honey bees, Apis mellifera (Hymenoptera: Apidae)—have evolved the ability to detect and utilize the Earth’s magnetic field as a navigation aid, a crucial adaptation that supports foraging efficiency, reproductive behavior, and overall colony fitness (Gould et al. 1978, Lambinet et al. 2017b, Chicas-Mosier et al. 2020). Although honey bee behavior is strongly influenced by visual cues and prior experience with the landscape (Klotz and Jander 2009), magnetoreception may serve as a complementary mechanism, particularly when visual information is limited to guide navigation. Magnetoreception in the honey bee is mediated by specialized sensory structures housed within trophocytes of the fat body tissue (Hsu and Li 1993, Hsu et al. 2007). These large cells (∼75 µm diameter) encircle the abdomen beneath the cuticle, predominantly in the ventral region, and contain these iron granules (0.32 ± 0.07 µm diameter) that are randomly distributed within the cytoplasm (Hsu and Li 1994, Hsu et al. 2007). The granules reside in iron deposition vesicles attached to cytoskeletal elements, and the trophocytes are innervated and electrically coupled, enabling signal transduction to the brain via the ventral nerve cord (Kirschvink et al. 1997, Hsu et al. 2007, Lambinet et al. 2017a). Behavioral experiments have shown that severing this neural connection abolishes magnetic conditioning while preserving olfactory responses, thereby localizing magnetoreception to the abdomen (Lambinet et al. 2017a, 2017b). These structures enable bees to detect geomagnetic variations as subtle as 26 nanoTesla (nT, Desoil et al. 2005, Lambinet et al. 2017a), guiding spatial orientation during foraging and homing (Gould et al. 1980, Wajnberg et al. 2010, Lambinet et al. 2017a). Although most research has focused on adult worker bees (Hsu and Li 1994, Kirschvink et al. 1997, Hsu et al. 2007), similar magnetite-bearing structures have also been identified in adult male honey bees (drones), likely contributing to their navigation to drone congregation areas (DCA), where they mate with virgin queens from different colonies (Loper 1992). However, the role of Earth’s magnetism in DCA formation remains poorly understood.

Earth’s magnetic field intensity (EMFI), which measures local magnetic anomalies across time and space may influence honey bees in several ways. Although the waggle dance of worker bees appears to be unaffected by fluctuations in magnetic field intensity (Kirs Kirschvink 1981, Lambinet et al. 2014), other studies have documented disorientation (Ferrari 2014) and altered flight patterns under varying magnetic conditions (Lambinet et al. 2017b). Anecdotal evidence further suggests that honey bee drones may respond to the vertical component of the magnetic field, potentially leading to congregation preferences for sites with stronger electromagnetic fluctuations (Loper 1992). These unique observations, made with a proton precision type magnetometer that was elevated some meters above the ground, and the evidence on magnetite in queens and drones bodies suggest that magnetoreception in honey bee reproductive castes is critical for colony reproduction. However, testing these hypotheses requires the development of robust protocols for quantifying EMFI during honey bee reproductive activity.

The DCA are spatially and temporally stable (Ruttner 1985), and colonies of A. mellifera recently introduced into an area are readily represented by drones at local DCAs (Tribe 1982). Globally, drones begin their flights at the median time of 1:30 PM, showing a peak in the afternoon (Ayup et al. 2021, Steed et al. 2025). A few environmental variables have been correlated to this flight activity; for instance, mating flights occur when a threshold of 18 to 20 °C minimum temperature is reached (Scheiner et al. 2013) and they are positively correlated to the intensity of ambient light, which is determined by the sun’s elevation (Streinzer et al. 2013). Notably, this timing also coincides with a daily increase in EMFI, which rises from lower morning values to maximum values in the early afternoon, then decreasing toward the night (Yamazaki and Maute 2017). Although small compared to the much stronger background field of 25,000 to 65,000 nT at the Earth’s surface (Finlay et al. 2010), such variations may nevertheless be biologically meaningful. In addition, EMFI varies with height above the ground. While such variation is well documented at larger spatial scales (eg kilometers; Hammer et al. 2021), differences can also occur at centimeters-to meter-scale distances due to local magnetic anomalies (Cook and Carts 1962). Studying these subtle changes in magnetism could help us understand its relative importance in comparison to other environmental factors in determining the spatial fidelity of DCA, an aspect of honey bee reproductive ecology that remains largely unexplored (Loper 1992).

In this study, we aimed to compare the performance of three methods for measuring spatial and temporal variation in EMFI at A. mellifera DCA. To this end, EMFI was measured using 2 mobile applications (Crowdmag and Phyphox) and a low-cost experimental gradiometer (SCL007-FGM-Magnetic Field Sensors) at 3 previously identified DCA located in Tucumán, northern Argentina. To compare their performance, we used the 3 methods to examine: the spatial structure of EMFI, EMFI values in the morning and in the afternoon, and values at 2 elevations above ground (10 and 150 cm). Our expectation was that the best method would confirm previous studies: EMFI is spatially structured, it increases toward early afternoon, and values at 10 cm are not significantly different from values at 150 cm.

Methods

Study Area

This study was conducted between February and March 2024 in the province of Tucumán, northwestern Argentina (Fig. 1). The study area is located in the Yungas biome, a seasonal subtropical Andean forest that ranges from 22° to 29°S (Brown and Malizia 2004). This region has a marked seasonality with low rainfall between June and October and high rainfall between November and March.

Right: map of the province of Tucumán showing elevation gradient (inset showing Argentina). Left: Google satellite image showing the location of the 3 Drone Congregation Areas (Escuela, Rio Muerto, and Modulo) in Horco Molle, Yerba Buena.

Magnetic Data Collection

We collected Earth’s magnetic field intensity at 3 previously identified DCA located near the Horco Molle Experimental Reserve in the municipality of Yerba Buena, Tucumán (Galindo-Cardona et al. 2020). The 3 DCA were: “Rio Muerto” (−26.784944, −65.330317) characterized by dense Yungas vegetation and the presence of zingiberales plants; “Escuela” (−26.783024, −65.318761) located within a sugarcane crop, and “Modulo” (−26.791732, −65.324321) surrounded by a Eucalyptus plantation (Fig. 1).

Before data collection, each DCA was mapped and its nucleus and boundaries were determined. The DCA nucleus was delimited by direct observation of drone density, while a helium balloon with an attached queen pheromone was elevated above ground, registering maximum concentration of aerial pursuits (flying kites) within the DCA (Galindo-Cardona et al. 2012, Galindo-Cardona et al. 2020). The DCA boundaries corresponded to abrupt drops (>80%) in drone density. At each DCA, a square grid including 80 georeferenced points (8 × 10) was established, with points uniformly separated by 6 m, covering an area of ∼0.23 hectares. Grids were centered on the DCA nucleus with all points within the validated boundaries. Measurements of EMFI were taken at each point twice (in the morning from 08:00 to 12:00 h and in the afternoon, from 14:30 to 18:00 h) and at 2 elevations (10 and 150 cm above ground). The afternoon sampling corresponds to the local drone flight time and the elevations were selected according to our logistic possibilities. Each DCA was visited twice: once in February and once in March 2024.

Three EMFI measurement methods were evaluated: Crowdmag (CRW), an application developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration that enables the collection of geomagnetic data (nanoTesla, nT) by any person, contributing to citizen science (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/crowdmag-magnetic-data); Phyphox (PH), an application created by RWTH Aachen University, that facilitates physical experiments using smartphone sensors (https://phyphox.org/) and also records data in nanoTeslas (nT); and a “SCL007-FGM-Magnetic Field Sensor” (MC), a low-cost (around US$500) 2 sensor fluxgate gradiometer (https://magnetometer-kit.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/DIY-fluxgate-gradiometer-magnetometer-kit-documentation.pdf) (Fig. 2). The MC operates by subtracting the signals of 2 aligned FGM 3-PRO fluxgate sensors. MC readings are expressed in dimensionless digital units (d.u.) reflecting relative magnetic gradient values and are not directly comparable in magnitude to the absolute EMFI values (nT) recorded by CRW and PH (smartphone applications). Briefly, the advantages of the applications over the MC are that they are free to download and update, they automatically and systematically record EMFI while walking and no additional equipment is needed. In contrast, the MC needs some economic resources for its assemblage and maintenance, and requires a field notebook and a platform for measurements at 150 cm. A measurement protocol was established for each of the 3 methods (see Supplementary Material S1).

Data Visualization and Analysis

All magnetic field measurements were first organized by method and sampling condition (time of day and elevation). For CRW and PH the units were nT and MC worked with digital units. The data from 2 sampling days were averaged for the following steps. Secondly, values were interpolated using the inverse distance weighting (IDW) method in QGIS 3.32 (QGIS.org 2023) to generate 1 map for each DCA considering time of the day, sampling height, and measurement method. Finally, from each interpolated map, a minimum of 100 data points were extracted using a virtual grid in QGIS 3.32 to be used in subsequent statistical analyses.

Using the measurement values at each point within each site, we built geographic distance matrices to conduct Mantel tests. Mantel tests are commonly used in landscape ecology to detect spatial patterns and account for spatial correlation between datasets (Legendre et al. 2015). For this aim, in Mantel tests a geographic distance matrix is analyzed in correlation with a matrix of the variable of interest (eg MC, CRW, or PH readings during the morning) to examine if both values (distance and field measurements) vary across the space in the same manner. In addition, a third matrix may be added (partial Mantel test) to test if any 2 matrices are correlated (eg readings in the morning and readings in the afternoon), while controlling for the geographic distance. Thus, the test evaluates if the spatial variation of both matrices is similar. Here, we used the tests to examine the ability of each of the 3 methods to capture the spatial variability of their respective measurements, specifically in relation to geographic distance, time of the day and height. This approach allowed us to evaluate the relative performance of each method based on its sensitivity to detect spatial and temporal variation. To run the tests, we used the values obtained by each method on the interpolated images generated in the previous step. For the analysis we used the “vegan” (Oksanen et al. 2025) and “geosphere” (Hijmans 2024) packages in R v.4.4.2 (R Core Team 2023), employing Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices (ie geographic distance among sampling points and field measurements) and Pearson correlation. In addition, considering that the 80-point grid that we first used was time consuming, we conducted a power test to determine the minimum number of data points needed for a clear visualization of spatial magnetic variation at each site (see Supplementary Material S2).

Results

The power test run to determine the minimum number of data points needed for a clear visualization of spatial magnetic variation at each site yielded 35 samples (Supplementary Material S2). Given that 80 samples had been taken at each site, the following results are based on a minimum of 100 virtual points extracted from the interpolated maps generated in QGIS 3.32.

The 2 smartphone methods CRW and PH recorded absolute EMFI values ranging from 21,297 to 24,094 nT across all sites, times of the day, and elevations (Table 1). MC recorded relative magnetic gradient values ranging from 0.678 to 1.792 d.u. across the same conditions (Table 1) (see Supplementary Material S3). Across all 3 measurement methods, EMFI showed the strongest positive correlation with geographic distance, with Rio Muerto exhibiting the strongest association (MC: r = 0.463, CRW and PH: r ranging from 0.291 to 0.463; all P = 0.0001; Table 2).

Regarding temporal variation, CRW and PH also varied throughout the day, ranging mean values from 21,736 to 24,094 nT in the morning and mean values between 21,297 and 22,523 nT in the afternoon across sites (Table 1). MC morning values ranged from 0.970 to 1.792 d.u. and afternoon values from 0.679 to 1.598 d.u. (Table 1). This daily variation was consistently observed across all the analyzed DCAs (P < 0.05). The MC method detected an association between field measurements and time of the day at all 3 DCAs, whereas CRW and PH failed to detect a significant association at Modulo (Table 2).

In addition, CRW and PH recorded mean values of elevation between 21,531 and 22,676 nT at 10 cm above ground and 21,858 and 23,480 nT at 150 cm above ground (Table 1). MC recorded mean values of 0.850 to 1.477 d.u. at 10 cm and 0.799 to 1.719 d.u. at 150 cm (Table 1). A positive correlation between field measurements and elevation was observed across all DCAs; however, this pattern varied among measurement methods. The MC method consistently detected a significant correlation (P < 0.005), with the strongest association at Rio Muerto (r = 0.637, P = 0.0001), whereas CRW and PH detected significant correlations only at this site.

The MC method produced consistent results across the 3 DCAs, capturing temporal variation throughout the day and revealing associations between gradient values measured at 10 and 150 cm above ground (Table 2). Given this consistency, gradient maps were generated for each DCA, time of the day, and elevation to facilitate visualization (Figs 3–5). At the Escuela DCA, moderate but significant spatial variation in EMFI values was observed across both times of the day and elevations (Fig. 3). At the Escuela DCA, mean MC values were higher in the afternoon than in the morning (1.598 ± 0.270 vs. 1.337 ± 0.320 d.u.), and higher at 150 cm than at 10 cm above ground (1.719 ± 0.188 vs. 1.216 ± 0.219 d.u.), despite relatively large standard deviations (Table 1).

At the Modulo DCA, the MC method also detected spatial structure in gradient values (Fig. 4). Differences between morning and afternoon readings, as well as between 10 and 150-cm measurements, were detected, although these patterns were weaker compared to those observed at the Escuela DCA. Mean MC values were weaker in the afternoon than in the morning (0.679 ± 0.292 vs. 0.970 ± 0.336 d.u.), in contrast to the pattern observed at the Escuela DCA. At the Rio Muerto DCA, gradient values were spatially structured (Fig. 5). Temporal differences between morning and afternoon values were more pronounced than differences between elevations, with a general trend toward higher gradient values in the morning than in the afternoon (1.792 ± 0.268 vs. 1.285 ± 0.458 d.u.) and higher values at 150 cm than at 10 cm above ground (1.600 ± 0.444 vs. 1.477 ± 0.453 d.u.; Table 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of 3 methods (ie 2 mobile applications and a low-cost fluxgate gradiometer) used to measure spatial and temporal variation in EMFI at different times of the day and at 2 elevations above the ground in DCAs of A. mellifera. The spatial structure of EMFI (ie correlation with geographic coordinates) was detectable with the 3 methods, indicating their general ability to register geographic patterns of magnetic field. However, the MC sensor outperformed the 2 mobile applications (CWR and PH) particularly in detecting subtle differences in EMFI (represented by digital units) across times of the day (morning vs. afternoon) and between elevations (10 vs. 150 cm) above the ground. Both smartphone applications were useful to capture EMFI variations but were not consistent across the analyzed DCAs. This suggests that sensor- and site-specific environmental factors should be considered when selecting a measuring method for EMFI.

Our findings revealed that only the MC method consistently captured the spatial variation in magnetism within DCAs during the mornings and afternoons. In contrast, the mobile applications failed to capture these differences at one of the sites (Modulo). A possible explanation for this discrepancy is the applications’ dependence on internet connectivity, which is typically poor with irregular signals at these locations. Even when measurements were registered twice at each DCA, errors may have been sufficiently large to compromise the accuracy and reliability of the mobile applications. If this is the case, one advantage of the MC method in areas with limited internet connectivity is its capacity to register magnetic gradient values in a reliable manner. Still, using the mobile applications was faster and easier than using MC, due to their small size, portability, and screen accessibility. If the exact magnetism value is needed, an additional advantage is that both applications report nanoTeslas as the measurement unit. If limited connectivity was the cause of the differences observed, we would expect improved performance of the applications in sites with stable, high-quality signal. Regarding temporal variation, the MC method showed lower gradient values in the afternoon than in the morning at 2 of the 3 DCAs, contrary to our expectations. This pattern may be explained by the timing of our sampling, which in some cases occurred after the daily EMFI peak at these 2 DCAs. Because sampling hours were randomized across DCAs, some measurements were recorded relatively late in the afternoon. In this sense, we recommend focusing EMFI measurements during the peak period of the regional drone flight times rather than fixed clock hours, as flight windows vary geographically. At our sites, this peak occurs in the early afternoon (13:30 to 17:00 LAT) that corresponds to the period of highest drone activity (Supplementary Table S1; Ayup et al. 2021).

In relation to elevation, our results showed even more consistency among the 3 DCAs when using the MC method compared to smartphone applications. The MC recorded higher gradient values at 150 cm than at 10 cm above ground at all DCAs. In line with Loper (1992), who observed that DCAs often occur in locations characterized by vertical magnetic gradients, maps based on the MC data revealed that this gradient (lower values at 10 cm and higher values at 150 cm) was spatially structured within DCAs. If confirmed, this gradient likely continues vertically beyond 150 cm above the ground within DCAs but would likely not be detectable in sites with no DCA. It is worth noting that the feasibility of stratifying measurements above 150 cm differs between the MC and smartphone applications, as smartphones are more easily mounted on platforms such as unmanned aerial vehicles to obtain measurements at higher altitudes.

As observed for temporal variation, the mobile applications did not capture EMFI differences between elevations at one of the DCAs (Modulo). As discussed above, this may be partly due to poor internet connectivity; however, additional factors affecting the sensitivity of the applications should also be considered. It has been shown that nearby electrical currents generate magnetic fields that can mask EMFI and may interfere with sensor performance (Lee et al. 2020). In fact, the Modulo DCA, where both applications failed to detect clear patterns, was surrounded by houses, cars, cables, and one large antenna, which are acknowledged as sources of magnetic interference and may have affected the mobile applications’ performance. Another external factor that may have interfered with their performance, but not the MC, is environmental temperature (Greene et al. 2023), which was high during field sampling. Given that these applications depend on phone-specific characteristics, such as temperature tolerance, they may be less reliable under harsh field conditions. Other interactions between sensor- and site-specific environmental factors may have also occurred in our DCAs. In previous studies, differences in DCA slope orientation, trail or road density, and land cover have been considered (Galindo-Cardona et al. 2012). In the DCAs analyzed in this study, some of these factors—such as trail density—were more variable than others (eg slope orientation was similar across the 3 DCAs). In this sense, the performance of the 3 methods should be compared under contrasting trail densities and land covers, with the expectation that higher trail densities and a greater proportion of urban land cover would favor MC over smartphone applications, given the potentially different influence of external factors on sensor performance. It is worth noting that our EMFI measurements were restricted to known DCAs, and no comparisons were made with surrounding non-DCA areas. As a result, it is not currently possible to determine whether the EMFI patterns observed here are specific to DCAs or reflect broader background variation in the landscape. Future studies incorporating measurements outside DCAs would be necessary to establish baseline EMFI conditions and evaluate potential differences between DCA and non-DCA environments. In this context, although smartphone-based applications showed limited sensitivity for detecting fine-scale variation within DCAs (eg differences across time of day or sampling elevations), they may still represent useful tools for detecting larger scale contrasts in EMFI.

Understanding the influence of EMFI on DCA formation and honey bee mating holds both biological and practical significance. By comparing EMFI values between DCA and non-DCA sites, we can gain insight into the role of this variable as a potential descriptor of DCA locations (Galindo-Cardona et al. 2012). Investigating the mechanisms underlying magnetic field detection in bees not only deepens our understanding of their behavioral ecology but also has practical implications for improving reproductive success and enhancing the positive impacts of bees on ecosystems. Further research is needed to clarify the role of magnetoreception in drones and queens, including how variations in magnetic fields may interact with environmental and climatic factors to influence bee behavior and DCA dynamics.

This study describes, for the first time, the spatial distributions of EMFI within DCAs using 3 different methods and provides valuable insights into the measurement of this variable and its potential influence on drone behavior. The robust performance of the MC method highlights its value as a reliable and cost-effective tool for future studies on the interplay between Earth’s magnetism and honey bee reproduction, including how magnetic field variations interact with environmental and climatic factors to shape DCA dynamics. Technological advances also offer promising tools for exploring bee behavior. For example, precision pollination technologies that leverage bees’ magnetic field memory enable real-time tracking of pollinator activity, providing valuable applications for pollinator management in both agriculture and conservation contexts (Santos et al. 2021).
