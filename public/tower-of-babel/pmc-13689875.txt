Unexplained Massive Pericardial Effusion Revealing Intrapericardial Lymphangioma

Lymphangiomas are rare benign malformations of the lymphatic system that most commonly occur in the head, neck, and axillary regions during childhood.1 Thoracic involvement is uncommon, and intrapericardial lymphangioma is exceptionally rare.2 Clinical presentation varies depending on lesion size and location, ranging from incidental findings to symptoms related to compression of adjacent structures or pericardial effusion. Pericardial masses include a variety of benign and malignant lesions, such as pericardial cysts, lipomas, metastatic tumors, and primary pericardial neoplasms. Because intrapericardial lymphangioma may mimic these disorders, diagnosis based solely on clinical and imaging findings can be difficult.3 Histopathologic confirmation is often required to establish a definitive diagnosis.

History of Presentation

A 33-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea. On arrival at the emergency department, her blood pressure was 141/87 mm Hg, heart rate was 73 beats/min, oxygen saturation was 98% on room air, and body temperature was normal. Physical examination revealed no jugular venous distension, cardiac murmurs, or pericardial friction rub, and she denied pleuritic chest pain. Baseline laboratory tests, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were unremarkable. Electrocardiography demonstrated normal sinus rhythm. Initial evaluation revealed a large pericardial effusion without overt signs of cardiac tamponade.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical history.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for a large pericardial effusion accompanied by pericardial and mediastinal lesions included a broad spectrum of conditions. Pericardial cyst was considered given the cystic appearance on imaging; however, the presence of multiloculated and infiltrative features raised concern for alternative etiologies. Pericardial neoplasms, both benign and malignant, were also considered, particularly in the setting of associated soft-tissue lesions. Infectious causes such as tuberculous pericarditis were included in the differential diagnosis, especially given the high prevalence in endemic regions, although initial laboratory findings were not strongly supportive. In addition, lymphoproliferative disorders and other mediastinal tumors were considered, as these can present with both mass-like lesions and secondary pericardial effusion. Overall, the combination of imaging findings and clinical presentation necessitated further diagnostic evaluation to establish a definitive etiology.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large pericardial effusion without definite signs of cardiac tamponade (Figure 1). Chest computed tomography demonstrated a large pericardial effusion accompanied by an enhancing soft-tissue lesion along the visceral pericardium, as well as an additional low-attenuation mass-like lesion in the superior mediastinum (Figure 2). Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed an approximately 7-cm mass in the left anterior mediastinum and left para-aortic area (Figure 3). Given the technical difficulty of pericardiocentesis, the patient underwent pericardiostomy with biopsy via left-sided video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. The pericardial fluid appeared markedly turbid with a pinkish, milky appearance (Figure 4A). Pericardial fluid analysis showed an exudative profile (protein level, 3.0 g/dL) with a leukocyte count of 4,000/μL and minimal red blood cells. Glucose (84 mg/dL) and lactate dehydrogenase (109 IU/L) levels were not suggestive of a highly inflammatory process. The adenosine deaminase level was 10.3 IU/L, and acid-fast bacilli staining was negative, making tuberculous pericarditis unlikely. Overall, the findings indicate a noninfectious, nonpurulent effusion, which is compatible with the clinical context of a pericardial mass. On gross examination, the biopsy specimen revealed translucent cystic masses, both intact (white arrow) and ruptured (black arrow) (Figures 4B and 4C). Microscopically, multiloculated cystic spaces located between the fibrous pericardium and mediastinal serosa were lined by a single layer of attenuated endothelial cells without significant cytologic atypia (Figures 5A to 5C). The irregular luminal architecture and the presence of stromal lymphoid aggregates favored a diagnosis of lymphangioma over hemangioma. Management

The patient was ultimately diagnosed with cystic lymphangioma and underwent definitive surgical treatment via median sternotomy. Pericardiectomy and partial mediastinal pleurectomy were performed to remove the involved tissue, and the pericardium was subsequently reconstructed using a Prolene mesh (Ethicon). A low-fat diet was initiated immediately after surgery. Postoperatively, chest tube drainage of lymphatic fluid was approximately 100 mL/d for several days and gradually decreased thereafter. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 6, and the patient was discharged without complications on postoperative day 11. The long-term postoperative course has been uneventful, and the patient remains under follow-up without evidence of recurrence.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and remains under follow-up without evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Intrapericardial lymphangioma is an exceedingly rare benign tumor of lymphatic origin that only rarely involves the mediastinum or pericardium.1,2 When present in the thoracic cavity, it may manifest with nonspecific symptoms or be associated with pericardial effusion, often leading to diagnostic uncertainty.4 In the present case, the predominant clinical manifestation was massive pericardial effusion rather than compressive symptoms, highlighting an atypical presentation.

The mechanism of effusion in this patient is most plausibly explained by continuous lymphatic leakage from dilated cystic spaces into the pericardial cavity. Histopathologic findings demonstrated multiloculated cystic structures consistent with lymphatic channels, and the turbid, milky appearance of the pericardial fluid further supports lymphatic involvement. This presentation differs from the more typical manifestation of lymphangioma as a space-occupying mass and suggests that lymphatic tumors can primarily present through fluid accumulation, potentially resulting in significant pericardial effusion and hemodynamic consequences.5,6

This case also underscores the limitations of noninvasive diagnostic modalities. Although transthoracic echocardiography, computed tomography, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging identified multiloculated pericardial and mediastinal lesions, these findings were insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis because imaging characteristics may overlap with other pericardial or mediastinal pathologies.7 Similarly, pericardial fluid analysis was not diagnostic, as biochemical parameters lacked specificity despite the suggestive gross appearance.

Definitive diagnosis was achieved only through histopathologic examination combined with immunohistochemical confirmation of lymphatic endothelial markers (ETS-related gene, CD31, and D2-40), establishing lymphatic origin.1,6 This highlights that, despite advances in multimodality imaging, tissue diagnosis remains essential when evaluating unexplained pericardial effusion associated with structural abnormalities.

From a clinical perspective, this case suggests a pragmatic diagnostic approach for unexplained pericardial effusion. Initial evaluation should include multimodality imaging to assess the presence of associated pericardial or mediastinal lesions. When imaging reveals structural abnormalities but remains nondiagnostic, and when pericardial fluid analysis is inconclusive, early consideration of tissue acquisition—particularly via surgical or thoracoscopic biopsy—should be pursued. This stepwise approach may help avoid diagnostic delay and facilitate timely, etiology-directed management in patients with atypical presentations.

Conclusions

Intrapericardial lymphangioma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained pericardial effusion.
