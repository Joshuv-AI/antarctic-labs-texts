A systems biology approach to identify key targets in KRAS/BRAF-mutated colorectal cancer
Authors: Shiva Shiravi, Negar Mottaghi-Dastjerdi, Behzad Shahbazi, Nahid Ahmadi, Mohammad Soltany-Rezaee-Rad, Abozar Ghorbani, Hamed Montazeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKRAS and BRAF mutations are critical oncogenic drivers in colorectal cancer (CRC), with distinct molecular and clinical implications.MethodsTo uncover genes differentially expressed between these mutations, we compared KRAS G12D- and BRAF V600E-mutated CRC cell lines. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction, pathway enrichment, survival analysis, and drug target screening were performed to identify therapeutic opportunities.ResultsTen hub genes—TNF, IL1B, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9—were identified as critical in KRAS-driven CRC. Enrichment analysis highlighted immune and inflammatory pathways, including “SARS-CoV-2 Signaling” and “Macrophage Stimulating Protein Signaling,” as well as cellular processes like “Positive Regulation of PI3K Signaling”. IL1B was the only hub gene significantly associated with overall survival, suggesting its role as a favorable prognostic marker. Drug screening identified selective inhibitors such as Canakinumab and Rilonacept targeting IL1B, with docking studies revealing the strongest interaction for Omeprazole with AHR, followed by Tapinarof with AHR and Donepezil with IL1B.ConclusionsThis study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms of KRAS-mutated CRC, emphasizing IL1B as a prognostic marker. Among the identified therapeutic agents, Omeprazole demonstrated the strongest interaction with AHR, suggesting a potential for repurposing in CRC treatment, while IL1B-targeting inhibitors such as Canakinumab emerged as selective candidates for modulating tumor-promoting inflammation. Novel genes such as EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9 warrant further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03966-2.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a primary global health concern, ranking third in incidence and second in mortality among all cancers worldwide [1]. As per the latest statistics from GLOBOCAN 2024, CRC was responsible for around 1.9 million newly diagnosed cases worldwide and caused nearly 935,000 deaths in the same year [2]. These statistics emphasize the significant burden of CRC and underscore the necessity for developed diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Mutations in KRAS and BRAF genes are considered crucial genetic factors contributing to the pathogenesis of CRC. In CRC, the KRAS G12D and BRAF V600E mutations are widespread and have a significant role in driving the malignant transformation of colonic epithelial cells [3–5]. The KRAS gene is a critical component of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling pathway, playing a crucial role in cell proliferation and survival [6]. The G12D mutation causes the KRAS protein to remain continuously activated, leading to uncontrolled cellular growth [7]. Similarly, the BRAF gene is a vital component of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, and its V600E mutation results in an active kinase that stimulates oncogenic processes independent of upstream signaling [8].

Considering the crucial roles played by these mutations in CRC development and progression, they are promising targets for therapeutic intervention and diagnostic applications. Gene expression profiling has been widely utilized in gastrointestinal cancers to uncover critical molecular mechanisms, identify biomarkers, and explore therapeutic targets. Studies have demonstrated the significance of analyzing differentially expressed genes to understand tumor progression, chemoresistance, and autophagy-related pathways, particularly in gastric, esophageal, and colorectal cancers [9–12]. Key findings have highlighted the role of overexpressed genes associated with poor prognosis and actionable therapeutic targets within pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and inflammatory signaling [13]. Additionally, regulatory molecules, including miRNAs, have been shown to influence oncogenic pathways like Wnt/β-catenin, underscoring their relevance in tumorigenesis and drug resistance [14–16]. Systems biology approaches further emphasize the importance of pathway-level analyses for elucidating the pathogenesis of gastrointestinal cancers and advancing personalized medicine [17]. Comparative analyses of gene expression between oncogenic mutations, such as those in MAPK and JAK-STAT pathways, have revealed distinct molecular signatures and highlighted potential for translational applications in cancer therapeutics. Building on this foundation, the current study focuses on the KRAS G12D and BRAF V600E mutations in colorectal cancer to identify unique molecular pathways and overexpressed genes driving KRAS-mutated CRC, offering insights into diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The entire GEO database, including 19 series of human colorectal cancers, was screened for relevant datasets based on the following criteria: (1) data based on expression profiling by high-throughput sequencing (RNA-seq), (2) human CRC-related projects, (3) analysis of colorectal cancerous KRAS G12D vs. BRAF V600E mutated cell lines, (4) at least two samples analyzed, and (5) supplementary filter: CEL. After precise screening, the GSE123416 dataset was selected as the database of choice. A total of six specimens, including three CRC KRAS-mutated and three CRC BRAF-mutated cell lines, were available based on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 (Homo sapiens) platform.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The GEO2R analysis tool was utilized to compare the expression of genes between CRC KRAS-mutated and BRAF-mutated cell lines in the GSE123416 dataset. GEO2R was designed to offer immediate, web-based, and user-driven GEO data analysis. It is a web-based tool that facilitates the analysis of the GEO Series [18], using the Bioconductor packages [19], GEOQuery [20] and limma in R [21], and the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate method for [22] multiple-testing correction as its default method. Genes with significant differences in expression levels between KRAS- and BRAF-mutated cell lines at an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a log fold-change (LogFC) more or less than 0 were considered as differentially expressed genes. Moreover, genes with a LogFC greater than 0 were further analyzed for overexpression.

Protein-protein interaction network construction and selection of hub genes

STRING is an online database providing information on predicted and confirmed protein interactions. The analysis focused on significantly overexpressed genes using the STRING platform, version 12 (http://string-db.org) [23], with an interaction score threshold of 0.4 (indicating medium confidence). This resulted in the compilation of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) list. The list was then imported into Cytoscape (version 3.10.0), a freely available software for visualizing complex molecular interaction networks and integrating diverse data types. Within Cytoscape, the CytoHubba plugin (version 0.1) [24] was utilized to identify key hub proteins across all network nodes. Topological analysis methods such as MCC, Degree, DMNC, and MNC were employed to determine the importance of these nodes within the biological network. Each method identified four prominent proteins designated as central hub nodes. Subsequently, a subnetwork illustrating the interactions among these hub nodes was created using the CytoHubba plugin.

Pathway and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

To elucidate the functional roles and biological processes associated with the identified hub genes, pathway, and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed using the ENRICHR tool [25–27]. ENRICHR is a comprehensive gene set enrichment analysis tool that facilitates the identification of biological pathways, molecular functions, cellular components, and biological processes enriched in a given gene set. The hub genes identified in the PPI network analysis were input into ENRICHR to determine their associated pathways (WikiPathway2023) and GO terms. The results from ENRICHR provided insights into the signaling pathways and functional categories significantly enriched in the hub genes.

Cluster analysis of the network

The network nodes were clustered using CytoCluster (version 2.1.0) and the IPCA (Identifying Protein Complex Algorithm). This algorithm is designed to identify densely connected subgraphs in protein interaction networks by calculating edge weights based on common neighbors between connected nodes [28]The weight of each node is assessed by summing the weights of its incident edges using a threshold of 0.9. After clustering, the list of the genes in each cluster was imported into ENRICHR for further analysis to better understand these genes’ involvement in pathways using WikiPathway2023.

Expression analysis of the hub genes

The University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer (UALCAN) data analysis portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu) was used to retrieve the expression levels of the identified 10 hub genes in both tumor and normal colorectal tissues.

Survival analysis of the hub genes

To assess the prognostic relevance of the identified hub genes in colorectal cancer (CRC), a survival analysis was performed using the GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) tool. GEPIA2 facilitates the generation of Kaplan-Meier plots and calculates hazard ratios (HR) and log-rank p-values to determine the significance of gene expression in relation to overall survival (OS). The analysis included ten hub genes (TNF, IL1B, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9). Patients were stratified into high- and low- expression groups based on the median expression levels of each gene across both colon and rectum cancer datasets. The log-rank test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of survival differences between these groups, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Drug target analysis

To explore potential therapeutic applications of the identified hub genes, we conducted a drug target analysis using the DrugBank database. This analysis aimed to identify approved drugs directly targeting the hub proteins (AHR, FN1, IL1B, EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, SOX9). Each hub protein was queried in DrugBank to retrieve information on associated drugs, including the type of action (agonist, inhibitor, modulator, etc.) and the number of protein targets for each drug. The analysis focused on highlighting medicines already approved for clinical use and targeting the identified hub proteins, providing insights into potential therapeutic interventions.

Structure preparation of proteins and drugs

In the search for understanding molecular interactions, getting the correct structures of proteins and drugs is crucial. The protein structures of AHR (PDB ID: 7zub) and IL1B (PDB ID: 8c3u) were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [29]. After that, the receptor structures were optimized using Dock prep software in Chimera version 1.17.3. The protein structures have been optimized by adding hydrogen and charge, while water molecules and ligands have been deleted [30]. The conversion from PDB protein files to PDBQT formats was performed using Autodock Tools. The 3D structures of approved drugs were downloaded from PubChem in “sdf” format and then converted to “PDB” format using Discovery Studio ver.4.5. The compound geometry was optimized using HyperChem 8.0 [31]. Using AutoDock Tools software, all PDB ligand files were converted into the PDBQT format.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking plays a key role in understanding how proteins and drugs interact. This method reveals how drugs attach to their target proteins and how strong these connections are. Learning this is very important for discovering new medicines. It helps create treatments that are specific and effective. This information is of paramount importance in the scope of drug discovery approaches, as it allows for the design of specific and effective therapeutics. Among the suite of computational tools used to this end, AutoDock Vina shines for its extraordinary efficiency and accuracy. In addition, AutoDock Vina has been shown to perform well on large dataset handling capabilities, providing the rationale for being integrated into virtual screening procedures [32]. The interactions between the drug candidates and the HAR and IL1B proteins were analyzed using AutoDock Vina [33]. we selected the drug compounds displaying the most favorable binding affinities based on the binding affinity values. PyMol version 1.1.7 and Discovery Studio version 4.5 enabled the production of respective visual illustrations of these results [34].

Results

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

There were two groups of colorectal cancer cell lines in this study, one with KRASG12D and the other with BRAFV600E mutations. The GEO2R online analysis tool with default parameters was used to screen the DEGs, employing an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a LogFC more or less than 0. Figure 1 shows the results of the GEO2R analysis on the selected dataset, indicating the Volcano plot, Venn diagram, UMAP plot, and Boxplot of DEGs between CRC KRAS-mutated and BRAF-mutated cell lines. A total of 4792 up-regulated genes were identified in this study. Further analysis was conducted on these genes to identify potential diagnostic/therapeutic targets.

Pathway and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

The pathway and GO enrichment analyses using ENRICHR revealed several significant pathways and GO terms associated with the hub genes. Notable pathways identified by pathway enrichment analysis include the SARS-CoV-2 signaling pathway (WP5115), macrophage stimulating protein signaling (WP5353), and prostaglandin signaling (WP5088), indicating the involvement of these genes in critical signaling mechanisms (Fig. 3a). Gene ontology analysis highlighted various biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Figure 3b presents the top biological process GO terms enriched in the hub genes. Key processes include positive regulation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling (GO:0014068), regulation of nucleic acid-templated transcription (GO:1903508), and MAPK cascade (GO:0000165), reflecting the regulatory roles of these genes in cellular signaling and transcription. Figure 3c illustrates the cellular component GO terms significantly associated with the hub genes. Important components include platelet alpha granule lumen (GO:0031093), flotillin complex (GO:0016600), and endoplasmic reticulum lumen (GO:0005788), highlighting the localization of these genes in specific cellular structures. Figure 3d displays the molecular function GO terms enriched in the hub genes. Significant functions include receptor ligand activity (GO:0048018), growth factor receptor binding (GO:0070851), and transcription cis-regulatory region binding (GO:0000976), emphasizing the functional diversity of the hub genes in receptor binding and transcription regulation. These results underscore the multifaceted roles of the hub genes in colorectal cancer pathogenesis, providing potential targets for therapeutic intervention and diagnostic development.

Cluster analysis of the network

The network clustering analysis identified four distinct clusters of genes (Fig. 4), each representing densely connected subgraphs within the PPI network. These clusters were determined using the CytoCluster tool and the IPCA. Subsequently, the clusters were analyzed using the ENRICHR tool to identify enriched pathways associated with each group of genes.

As shown in Table 2, cluster 1 consists of 14 nodes and 89 edges, with prominent genes such as TNF, IL1B, and IL18. This cluster is significantly involved in inflammation and immune response pathways, including the Prostaglandin Signaling and Cytokines and Inflammatory Response pathways. Cluster 2 contains 14 nodes and 90 edges, including key genes such as IFIT1, OAS1, and RSAD2. This cluster is enriched in pathways related to SARS-CoV-2 signaling and innate immunity, highlighting its potential role in viral response mechanisms. Cluster 3 also comprises 14 nodes with 91 edges, similar to Cluster 2, sharing many of the same genes and pathways, underscoring a robust association with SARS-CoV-2 innate immunity and interferon signaling pathways. Cluster 4 includes 13 nodes and 76 edges, featuring genes such as COL1A2, COL6A1, and BGN. The primary pathways associated with this cluster are related to extracellular matrix organization and cellular adhesion, including the PI3K-Akt Signaling Pathway and Focal Adhesion.

Expression analysis of the hub genes

The expression levels of the ten hub genes identified in this study were analyzed using the UALCAN data analysis portal, comparing their expression in normal colorectal tissues versus primary tumor tissues from colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) samples (Fig. 5). The analysis revealed that AHR was significantly upregulated in primary tumor tissues, suggesting its involvement in colorectal cancer progression. CDH1, a key gene in cell adhesion, displayed a slight decrease in expression in tumor tissues, indicating a potential loss of cell adhesion in cancerous cells. EGF expression was notably lower in tumor tissues, which may reflect its diminished role in tumor development or progression. EPSTI1 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues, highlighting its possible involvement in tumor-stroma interactions within the microenvironment. FN1 showed a marked increase in expression, indicating its role in extracellular matrix remodeling and potentially in enhancing tumor invasiveness. Similarly, IFI44L expression was elevated in tumor tissues, suggesting a role in immune response modulation within the tumor microenvironment. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL1B exhibited increased expression in tumor tissues, possibly contributing to the inflammatory milieu associated with colorectal cancer. SOX9 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues, consistent with its known role in stem cell maintenance and tumorigenesis. Finally, TNF expression was slightly decreased in primary tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, suggesting a potential reduction in its pro-inflammatory activity within the tumor microenvironment. This decrease could indicate a complex role of TNF in colorectal cancer, where its expression might be modulated during tumor progression. The differential expression of these hub genes between normal and tumor tissues underscores their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in colorectal cancer.

Survival analysis of the hub genes

Among the ten hub genes analyzed in patients with both colon and rectum cancers, IL1B was the only gene found to have a statistically significant association with overall survival. The Kaplan-Meier survival curve for IL1B indicated that patients with high IL1B expression had better survival outcomes than those with low expression (HR = 0.63, log-rank p = 0.038). This result suggests higher IL1B expression may be a favorable prognostic marker in colorectal cancer. In contrast, the remaining hub genes—TNF, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9—did not show statistically meaningful differences in survival between high and low expression groups, with p-values above the 0.05 threshold. Therefore, IL1B stands out as the only hub gene with significant prognostic value in colorectal cancer based on this analysis (Fig. 6).

Drug target analysis

The drug target analysis identified several approved drugs targeting the hub proteins AHR, FN1, and IL1B, while no approved drugs were found for EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, or SOX9 (Table 3). For AHR gene, drugs such as atorvastatin, leflunomide, and nimodipine were identified as agonists, indicating their potential role in modulating AHR-related pathways. FN1 (Fibronectin 1) was found to be modulated by Ocriplasmin, which acts by cleaving protein matrices, and zinc, which plays a role in cellular processes. IL1B (Interleukin 1 Beta) is targeted by several inhibitors, including Canakinumab, an IL-1β blocker used to treat inflammatory conditions, and Diacerein, an IL-1 inhibitor used for osteoarthritis treatment. These findings suggest potential repurposing opportunities for these drugs to target the pathways influenced by AHR, FN1, and IL1B in colorectal cancer.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparative gene expression analysis between KRAS G12D-mutated and BRAF V600E-mutated CRC samples, identifying genes overexpressed specifically in KRAS-mutated CRC. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed through PPI network construction and pathway enrichment analysis, resulting in the identification of ten hub genes—TNF, IL1B, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9—that play critical roles in KRAS-driven CRC. These hub genes were ranked based on their topological importance within the network, underscoring their potential as diagnostic or therapeutic targets.

The pathway enrichment analysis revealed that overexpressed genes in KRAS-mutated CRC were significantly associated with immune and inflammatory pathways, including the “SARS-CoV-2 Signaling Pathway” and the “Macrophage Stimulating Protein Signaling Pathway.” Enrichment of cellular processes such as “Positive Regulation of Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase Signaling” and “Regulation of Nucleic Acid-Templated Transcription” underscores the importance of these genes in cell proliferation and transcription regulation, critical for KRAS-driven oncogenesis. Additionally, GO analysis identified key roles in extracellular matrix interactions and membrane signaling, as evidenced by enrichment in components like the “Platelet Alpha Granule Lumen” and “Flotillin Complex.” Enriched molecular functions, including “Receptor Ligand Activity” and “Growth Factor Receptor Binding,” further support the involvement of these genes in tumor-promoting signaling pathways in KRAS-mutated CRC.

The survival analysis indicated that, among the hub genes, only IL1B demonstrated a statistically significant association with overall survival in CRC patients. Higher expression of IL1B was linked to improved survival outcomes (HR = 0.63, log-rank p = 0.038), suggesting its potential as a favorable prognostic marker in colorectal cancer. However, it is important to note that this analysis did not account for the specific types of mutations (e.g., KRAS or BRAF mutations) in the CRC samples, meaning the results cannot directly establish the relevance of IL1B expression to these specific mutations. IL1B’s role as a pro-inflammatory cytokine typically supports tumor progression, but its overexpression in specific contexts may result in an immune response that leads to better patient outcomes [35–37]. This complexity highlights the need for further studies on IL1B’s function across different CRC scenarios. Meanwhile, the other hub genes—TNF, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9—did not show significant survival associations, although they remain crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms of KRAS-mutated CRC.

The drug screening analysis revealed several inhibitory drugs targeting hub proteins with limited targets, indicating their potential for lower off-target effects and reduced side effects. Currently, three IL-1 blockers—anakinra, canakinumab, and rilonacept—are approved for clinical use, with two additional blockers, gevokizumab and bermekimab, expected to receive approval [38]. Canakinumab, an IL1B-blocking agent, stands out with only one target, offering a highly specific therapeutic option. By inhibiting IL1B, canakinumab could play a crucial role in controlling the inflammatory processes associated with colorectal cancer, where IL1B is notably overexpressed [39]. Similarly, Rilonacept, Gallium nitrate, and Glucosamine, each with only three targets, provide additional therapeutic possibilities. Their high specificity suggests more precise effects and reduced toxicity, making them promising candidates for targeting IL1B in colorectal cancer. Rilonacept, in particular, functions as an IL-1 inhibitor, capable of modulating immune responses within the tumor microenvironment and potentially reducing tumor-promoting inflammation [38]. These inhibitory drugs, with focused targeting and minimal off-target effects, present a compelling avenue for therapeutic intervention in colorectal cancer by inhibiting key overexpressed genes like IL1B. This strategy highlights the value of using selective inhibitors to treat colorectal cancer, mainly when targeting inflammatory and tumor-promoting pathways.

In contrast, no approved drugs were identified for EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, or SOX9, underscoring the need to further explore these genes as potential therapeutic targets in colorectal cancer. Additional research is required to elucidate their roles in tumor biology and to investigate new drug development strategies targeting these pathways.

Additionally, docking studies revealed strong interactions between specific drugs and the receptors of hub genes. Notably, Tapinarof and Omeprazole demonstrated significant binding to the AHR receptor, while Donepezil showed strong interaction with the IL1B receptor. Tapinarof, an aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) modulator, has shown anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in cancer, including non-small cell lung cancer [40]. Omeprazole, an AhR agonist, has been reported to inhibit CXCR4 transcription and reduce tumor growth and invasion in breast cancer and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [41, 42]. Notably, the combination of omeprazole and tepotinib has been shown to reduce tumor growth and downregulate key metabolic enzymes in KRAS-mutant cell lines [43]. These findings suggest that these drugs could modulate specific molecular pathways in KRAS-mutated CRC and offer potential therapeutic benefits.

In conclusion, this study highlights the distinct molecular mechanisms underlying KRAS-mutated CRC and underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting hub genes such as IL1B. Identifying specific inhibitory drugs with minimal off-target effects provides a promising avenue for developing precise treatments for KRAS-driven CRC. Furthermore, the unexplored therapeutic potential of genes such as EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9 offers opportunities for future research and drug development in CRC.

Limitations

This study is based solely on the in-silico analysis of publicly available databases and lacks experimental validation. While bioinformatics approaches provide valuable insights, the findings should be interpreted with caution until validated through wet-lab experiments. Additionally, the sample size and heterogeneity of the dataset used may introduce biases, and the identified hub genes and pathways require further investigation in clinical settings to confirm their relevance in KRAS/BRAF-mutated colorectal cancer. Future studies should focus on experimental validation of the identified targets and their therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of KRAS G12D-mutated versus BRAF V600E-mutated colorectal cancer (CRC), identifying ten hub genes—TNF, IL1B, FN1, EGF, IFI44L, EPSTI1, AHR, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9—overexpressed in KRAS-mutated CRC and associated with key immune, inflammatory, and oncogenic pathways. Among these, IL1B emerged as a significant prognostic marker, with higher expression linked to improved survival outcomes, highlighting its dual role in inflammation and tumor progression. Drug screening identified selective inhibitors like Canakinumab, Rilonacept, and Gallium nitrate, targeting IL1B with high specificity and minimal off-target effects, offering promising therapeutic avenues. Additionally, strong drug-receptor interactions, such as Tapinarof with AHR and Donepezil with IL1B, further support their potential efficacy. Unexplored genes such as EPSTI1, COL20A1, CDH1, and SOX9 present opportunities for future drug development, emphasizing the need for continued research into KRAS-driven CRC.
