Assessing China’s COVID-19 control measures: a quantitative analysis based on a SIRD model
Authors: Yan Wu, Ming Yi, Daojun Bi, Hongzhong Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper established a Susceptible-Infected-Recovered-Dead (SIRD) model to fit and predict the COVID-19 epidemic in China. On the premise of passing the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) robustness check, we counterfactually assessed the effectiveness of partial unlocking and vaccination policies. Our key findings are as follows: (1) The SIRD model predicted that China could fully contain COVID-19 in July, 2020, which is consistent with reality; (2) If the strict social distancing policies are relaxed before the epidemic ends, it will lead to a second rebound. Even worse, the rebound increases with the advance of relaxation time and relaxation degree; (3) Timely vaccination is the best strategy if it is available. However, delayed vaccination is much less effective. (4) When further taking natural population growth rates into account, we find that the principal conclusions remain robust. The inclusion of demographic dynamics helps mitigate potential fitting bias resulting from changes in population size over extended epidemic periods like the COVID-19 pandemic. Empirically, the model shows a certain degree of applicability in regions with similar transmission trends. This paper theoretically and quantitatively supports the view that the effectiveness of China’s comprehensive prevention and control measures, and empirically verifies that the model has potential reference significance for some regions with similar transmission trends.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-64483-y.

Introduction

COVID-19 has rapidly swept the world since the first case in late December, 2019. As of November 2021, more than 200 million people around the world had been infected and more than 6 million people had died. As the Delta, Omicron and other variants strike, the second even the third wave continues to ravage the world. In the beginning, there were insufficient vaccines and effective treatments, governments struggled to adopt a variety of active interventions to avoid greater losses of lives, as well as the happening of economic and social crises. Some countries, such as China, have taken strict social distancing measures such as complete stopping of production, blockading of traffic and travel. Some countries, e.g., Germany and U.S., have taken partial lockdown measures, such as closing only large public places and schools. Other countries, such as Sweden, has adopted” non-mandatory precaution” measures, the government hopes its people achieve herd immunity together and then wait for the epidemic to vanish automatically. Countries with different systems and cultural backgrounds lead to different prevention levels and results. Among the major economies(USA, Brazil, India, Russia, China), China had the least infections (Fig. 1) and deaths (Fig. 2), while other countries are several times greater than China’s. China fully contained the epidemic as early as July 2020 and gradually ushering in economic recovery. Within our modeling framework, the findings support the view that strict social distancing measures are effective in the absence of vaccines, likely contributing to lives saved.Fig. 1Total cases trend from January 5, 2020 to June 6, 2021.Fig. 2Total deaths trend from January 5, 2020 to June 6, 2021.

Quantifying the impact of government prevention and control measures on the transmission of COVID-19 is an important issue worth studying, which has a significant reference value for mankind to overcome + COVID-19 and other public health crises may occur in the future. All governments’ interventions are designed to minimize the losses of lives, minimize economic losses and maximize social welfare. Among strict social distancing, partial containment and vaccination, which intervention measure is the most effective is worth further discussing. Furthermore, it’s worth investigating whether the model can still generate similar trend results when natural population dynamics are incorporated, and how the size of the natural population would evolve under such conditions. What’s more, is the model applicable only to a single specific economy? What are the analytical frameworks and conclusions of the model that may be relevant to other economies when it comes to the characteristics of epidemic transmission trends? These are all questions worth exploring.

There is a large body of research literature on the epidemic, which falls into the following categories: the first is research on measures related to outbreak prevention and control1. points out that Brazil’s healthcare system is under pressure due to a lag in high-level decision-making2. analyze spatio-temporal data and find that untimely and uncoordinated responses exacerbated virus transmission, and that vaccine import dependence and inefficient distribution pushed up the burden of infection and death in Brazil3. compare multinational measures and argue that the US federal system has led to a disjointed response and late vaccine interventions4. modeling suggests that without intervention, the US outbreak would have been delayed until 2022, and that a combination of containment, detection, and treatment would have been effective in slowing the spread5. notes that infection rates above 1% and 10% would trigger healthcare congestion and infrastructure shortages, respectively. China, Singapore, and South Korea have managed to control the epidemic well with centralized leadership, early and rapid response, and robust healthcare systems, with Singapore keeping the number of deaths low; Russia, despite the early development of vaccines and the high level of cooperation among the population, has seen an increase in infections due to economic pressures to liberalize controls midway through the year and election rallies6, but the scale is still smaller than that of Brazil and the United States. At the level of specific measures7, suggest that surveillance is more cost-effective than blockade, while8 introduces voluntary isolation selection behavior by individuals. There are also studies focusing on spillover effects9–11. Overall, the effectiveness of prevention and control depends on the intensity of social isolation and medical control, and is more influenced by the timeliness of decision-making, cultural context, and leadership consensus, which is the key to China’s leadership in controlling the outbreak.

The second category of literature closely related to this paper is based on outbreak data-driven measurement studies4. predicted the trend of the US outbreak and explored prevention and control approaches, but its parameters relied on direct corrections and failed to reflect the dynamic spread of the outbreak. In contrast, we captured the dynamic spread process of different intervention policies and stages by parameter-fitting the data, and the results were more relevant to reality12. pointed out that applying early Chinese parameters to the Italian epidemic can fit the trend better at the beginning of the outbreak, which is consistent with some of the findings in this paper. What’s more, we used the model to analyze the Italian and Brazilian outbreaks: Italy had a “wave-shaped” outbreak with multiple large rebounds due to the opening of the European Union to tourism and strain mutations, which exceeded the fitting ability of the single rebound hypothesis in this paper; whereas Brazil, despite the high burden of infections and deaths2, but the overall infection data are “inverted U-shaped”, drawing on the experience of China, which roughly matches the model in this paper, suggesting that the model is more suitable for simulating the “unimodal inverted U-shaped” epidemic curve formed under strong and sustained intervention, like the cases of China and Brazil; While for “multimodal” epidemics caused by policy fluctuations, significant strain variations, the multi-peak and multi-valley trend of the spread, such as the case in Italy (The results of the empirical fitting are detailed in APPENDIX2), more complex models are really needed. For example, the model can be further iteratively fitted by the segmentation function, i.e., the whole epidemic period is divided into phases according to the dominant factors for more segmentation, and the model is iteratively corrected by continuously comparing the simulation results with the deviation of the real data until the multi-peak trend can be reproduced. Through this dynamic, refined and segmented iterative modeling approach, the basic model can be expanded into a tool for complex epidemic analysis, which will be tried in our future’s work13. monitored and compared infections in Spain, Germany, Sydney and South Korea in real time14. tracked outbreaks in India, Italy, and the United States by age group, noting that extensive testing and social distancing can be effective in controlling at-risk areas15 and16 examined secondary rebound in India after the unsealing of a blockade and Mexican outbreaks respectively processes, but neither considered natural birth and death rates, whereas this paper further quantifies the impact of natural demographic changes outside the baseline model, an important difference. Overall, the experience of most countries with prolonged epidemics, with the exception of China, where the epidemic rapidly recovered, is worth quantifying in depth for lessons learned. Methodologically17, and18 proposed fractional-order modeling19. pointed out the effect of reinfection rate on transmission through closed-loop control and bifurcation analysis; and20 used novel two-stage modeling tracking infected individuals before analyzing changes in transmission rates, and quantitatively analyzing and discussing real data.

A third strand of literature focuses on the trade-off between epidemic prevention and control embargo measures and economic development21. embed epidemics as exogenous shocks in a Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) model and find that quarantine and control significantly exacerbate recessions22. draws similar conclusions in a neoclassical growth framework, noting that premature relaxation of controls in an epidemic will result in long-term human capital23. further clarifies in SIR-macro model that a blockade, although it comes at the expense of GDP and pushes up indebtedness, is optimal for large-scale firms with high labor force participation24. simulate aggregation controls from the perspective of consumption taxation and analyze the economic consequences of delayed or early unblocking. Other studies echo this trade-off dilemma25: explore the “life versus livelihood” trade-off26, assess the dual health and economic impacts of multiple types of infectious diseases, and27 present a study on the economic consequences of Keynesian supply shocks from epidemics28, focuses on output and price volatility29, track the pace of economic damage and recovery30, carve out healthcare-economic feedback loops, and there are also studies that directly correlate viral impacts directly to the statistical value of life31,32. Although stringent control measures may be accompanied by some short-term economic costs, the quantitative analysis in this study highlights the significant and lasting benefits in the public health dimension. These benefits are directly reflected in the significant reduction in the number of infections and deaths, and the large number of lives saved, thus preserving the vital human capital on which society’s long-term development depends, which in itself is a profound social value.

Meanwhile, with advancements in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for relational data modeling, existing research has increasingly explored their application in epidemiological scenarios to leverage network structural information for enhanced individual-level prediction and intervention capabilities. Distinct from the macroscopic SIRD model that assumes homogeneous mixing, GNNs construct connections at the microscopic level, which is more effective for analyzing heterogeneity. DeepTrace33 exemplifies this: it uses GNNs for bidirectional contact tracing to identify super-spreaders without needing a global network view. Our SIRD model, though effective for aggregate forecasts, does not explicitly model contact networks due to data constraints, thus limiting its ability to capture regional or demographic heterogeneity. Incorporating GNNs to improve fine-grained predictions remains a key future priority34. demonstrated via a stochastic branching model that backward tracing (to the source and its “sibling” cases) exponentially amplifies epidemic control, even under low ascertainment, low testing sensitivity, and high asymptomatic rates, with the greatest effect for the reproduction number R between 1.25 and 3.25. Since our study calculates R from SIRD compartment proportions, integrating bidirectional tracing in the future could further enhance our analysis35. utilized epidemic centrality and message-passing algorithms to rigorously solve the mathematical challenge of accurately identifying the “epidemic source” in real-world contact tracing networks characterized by “cycles” and “irregular nodes.” In the present study, both the initial epidemic transmission phase and the counterfactual analysis phase operate under the assumption that the epidemic source is known. Future research could draw upon their methodology, applying source detection techniques to identify the origins of potential secondary resurgences. This is crucial because it is impossible to pinpoint the exact location of the outbreak for every secondary rebound in real-time, and different locations may spawn distinct resurgences. Similarly36, comprehensively covered source detection for both epidemics and infodemics, combining mathematical analysis with network algorithms to trace origins of new COVID‑19 variants and vaccine‑related misinformation. Our SIRD model focuses on biological transmission, yet the pandemic is accompanied by an infodemic. In Section "Model extension analysis", we preliminarily analysed potential misclassification (e.g., attributing natural deaths to COVID‑19), which involves informational inaccuracies. Thus, our counterfactual framework could be extended to model the interplay between epidemic spread and misinformation—for instance, vaccine rumours lowering uptake and altering the epidemic trajectory—which remains an important direction for future work. The review paper by37 covers privacy-preserving technologies in contact tracing, which can enable more fine-grained and timely model updates while safeguarding privacy. In contrast, the SIRD model proposed in this paper relies on aggregated data. Therefore, in future research, privacy-enhancing technologies could be adopted to adapt the SIRD model to distributed data while avoiding the centralization of sensitive health information38. proposed Proactive Contact Tracing (PCT) over Binary Contact Tracing (BCT) based on binary test results, treating infectiousness as continuous and predicted from mobile-phone features via privacy-preserving distributed ensembles, with anonymized risk feedback39. proposed a formalized framework based on relative centrality, generalizing traditional centrality into a conditional probability format—that is, the importance of one set of nodes relative to another. Drawing upon this concept of “relative importance” could potentially offer valuable insights for identifying super-spreaders40. preliminarily proposed a vaccination strategy that combines a Graph Neural Network framework (GNNA) with the SIRD model, utilizing the Microscopic Markov Chain Approach (MMCA). In our future research, with sufficient data support, we will attempt to further draw on the perspectives from these literatures and try to establish relevant coupled models, so as to predict and analyse the spread of public health emergencies such as COVID-19 in a more granular manner.

This paper departs from the literature in three primary ways: (1) Much related literature explained the epidemic by using constant parameters such as fixed transmission rate, recovery rate, etc. However, it is obviously not advisable to assume constant parameters of the virus in different stages because people’s reaction (such as reducing social contacts) and governments’ intervention will affect the spread of the virus. In this paper, based on real data, the dynamic changing process of parameters in different stages is considered, and the robustness test is carried out based on MCMC chain, which makes it closer to reality and more convincing. (2) To the best of our knowledge, one of the biggest commonalities in the literature, tend to treat the total population as a static constant, which essentially assumes a closed and non-endogenously varying population and ignores the dynamic benchmark of natural turnover in the composition of births and deaths. By incorporating population as a continuously changing dynamic process into the core infection dynamics, the present model not only better fits the real demographic structure of economy, but also fundamentally corrects the theoretical calibration bias on key indicators such as transmission rate and infection size that may result from ignoring the natural variation of population. Thus, this study moves forward relevant theoretical modeling, providing new analytical perspectives for assessing long-term trends in epidemics and the effects of intervention policies in a more realistic dynamic demographic context. (3) The model in this paper is not only applicable to the analysis of the epidemic in China, but also applied to the empirical analysis of Brazil and Italy, and found that the model can roughly fit the “inverted U-shape” epidemic trend in Brazil, but it is difficult to capture the large rebound in Italy due to the opening up of tourism and the mutation of virulent strains, which suggests that the model still has some explanatory power for the countries with similar transmission models, detailed empirical results can be found in Appendix2. However, much of the existing literature examines the spread of epidemics within a particular economy and does not further consider the applicability of the model to other economies. Overall, both in terms of methodological rigor and relevance, the analysis presented in this paper offers potential implications for the practical modeling of public health events such as the Covid-19 pandemic.

We first constructed a modified SIRD model, assuming that the population is homogeneously mixed. The model includes four groups of people: susceptible(S), infected(I), recovered(R) and dead(D). By matching world epidemic data from World Health Organization (WHO) with daily new domestic recovery data provided by National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (NHCPRC), we generated the integrated data. Then, we used our model to fit the data and tried to describe the development trend of COVID-19 epidemic in China. What’s more, we counterfactually assessed the spreading of COVID-19 under the situation of vaccination intervention and partial relaxation. Next, we compared the effectiveness of different intervention measures. Finally, we took the dynamic change process of the natural population growth rate into consideration, while empirically analyzing the applicability of the model to fit the Brazilian and Italian epidemic data.

From the perspective of the daily new infections, the SIRD model predicted that China would fully contain COVID-19 in late July, 2020, which is consistent with reality. As long as the epidemic is not over, relaxation would certainly cause the second outbreak. What’s more, different timings and degrees of relaxation would lead to different rebound results. Timely vaccination is the best strategy, followed by complete prevention and control, and partial release was the worst. In the absence of a sufficiently effective vaccine, strict social distancing is the optimal. After taking into account the changes in the natural population growth rate, the basic conclusion still holds. Of course, the overall conclusion is even more comprehensive: As the number of deaths increases and the number of births decreases, the net population growth quantity undergoes a process of first increasing and then decreasing. In other words, the total population also experienced a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, which is in accordance with China’s actual situation. Furthermore, by fitting the model separately to the COVID-19 data of Italy and Brazil, we found that the epidemic could roughly fit the data of the Brazilian epidemic but not that of the Italian epidemic. This indicates that our model is applicable to regions with similar transmission trends, but it has limitations for multiple significant rebounds in the epidemic situation.

To make sure our analysis is consistent with the real situation in China, we made use of data from January 5, 2020 to July 11, 2020, totally 190 days, and we argue that is enough to predict our results. First, from data itself, up to August 2021, such 190 period data accounted for at least 66.14% of the cumulative confirmed cases, which covered the vast majority information on the development of epidemic in China. Second, epidemic data began to be available since January 4, 2020. On January 23, 2020, China took strict lock- down measures and the epidemic reached the peak in mid-February, 2020. Since then, the number of the infected, the deaths decreased every day, and the recovered people increased rapidly. On April 8, 2020, traffic was gradually resumed. On May 14, 2020, Wuhan carried out nucleic acid tests for all citizens. These series of measures prevented a second wave rebound, and gradually realized the resumption of work and production, indicating that the epidemic had been fully controlled. Therefore, the data to July, 2020 were sufficient to characterize the main development process of Covid-19. Third, although sporadic cases occurred in July, 2020 and thereafter. For instance, due to the impact of the international epidemic, Shijiazhuang and Nanjing experienced small epidemic fluctuations for their international airport. However, such an incident did not stop the steady decline of the epidemic, which is still under control. The above three points show that our data is sufficient to predict the trend and progress of the epidemic development in China. On April 17, 2020, there were 1,290 new deaths, an anomaly point in the data set, much higher than the daily increase at other days, which was mainly because of changes in Wuhan data. NHCPRC offered the following explanations, in the early stage of the epidemic: (1) The number of patients continued to increase, the medical staff were insufficient and the treatment pressure was huge, and there were delayed reporting, omitted reporting and false reporting; (2) Nucleic acid testing and treatment capacity were seriously inadequate. As time went by, medical workers started to track the infected and construct hospital beds. By February 20, 2020, the number of designated hospitals had increased from 2 to 48, and 16 makeshift hospitals had been built, which initially realized all patients’ willing to went to hospitals. Before February 20, 2020, some confirmed patients hadn’t been treated in time, and the deaths hadn’t been reported; (3) Finally, due to the imperfect public health statistic system, a few medical institutions failed to connect with the disease prevention and control information system.

Reproduction number

In epidemic models, the basic reproduction number is an important parameter to depict the early onset of an infectious disease. It represents the expectation of the number of people infected by an infected person in the average infection cycle of the susceptible population8,43. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma_{t} + \delta_{t}$$\end{document} is the average removal rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{{\gamma_{t} + \delta_{t} }}$$\end{document} represents an average cycle of infected individuals in the infected state, so the basic reproduction number is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{\beta_{t} }}{{\gamma_{t} + \delta_{t} }}$$\end{document}. Because we need to take into account the effect of changes in the proportion of susceptible persons over time in a time-varying situation, which provides a more accurate picture of the actual spreading capacity of the outbreak. Therefore, in this paper, the effective reproduction number Z(t) is used to describe it:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z(t) = \frac{{\beta_{t} }}{{\gamma_{t} + \delta_{t} }}\frac{{S_{t} }}{{N_{t} }}$$\end{document}

As discussed in the literature review, if we can use GNNs to estimate the effective reproduction number from contact network data, then use that estimate to calibrate the SIRD model, it would enable us to further enhance the accuracy of our predictions and analyses, which we will try in our future’s work.

MCMC iteration

We then adopt Metropolis Hastings(M-H) algorithm to perform MCMC iteration, we sampling from uniform distribution, get the rand number, i.e., rand N(0,1), we denoted as rand.

We limit our acceptance matrix is:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha_{ij} = \alpha (x_{t} ,x^{ * } ) = \min \{ e^{{\pi_{i} - \pi_{j} }} ,1\} ,i \ne j$$\end{document}

Which is the core mathematical design that ensures the smooth distribution of the chain is our target distribution, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i = 1,2, \cdots ,8000;j = 1,2, \cdots ,8000$$\end{document}. If rand < α, accept probability transfer, i.e., for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\forall i{\kern 1pt} {\kern 1pt}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i} = x_{i + 1}$$\end{document}. Otherwise, the transfer will not be accepted, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i + 1} = x_{i}$$\end{document}.

After completing 8000 MCMC iterations, we obtain a sample set containing 8000 sets of parameter estimates, whose data structure is an 8000 × 8 matrix, where each row represents the values of all 8 parameters in one iteration. In order to assess the convergence of the Markov chain and obtain robust posterior statistics, we divide the iteration process into two stages: the first 6000 iterations are considered as the “annealing period”, which allows the chain to gradually get rid of the influence of the initial values and explore and converge to the target posterior distribution. Analysis of reproduction number

It has been emphasized in 3.3 that effective reproduction number represents the expected number of susceptible people that the infected person can infect in the average period of infection. We here use the SIRD model directly for analysis and correction. In the future, if we could consider using GNNs for analysis and then applying the results to calibrate the SIRD model, this might further improve the granular predictions of the evaluation, while also allowing for some analysis and comparison of the two methods.

When effective reproduction number is less than 1, the virus will not continue to spread (Fig. 8). Therefore, according to Eq. (11), we calculated the trend of the dynamic change of reproduction number over time (Fig. 8(a)). Figure. 8(a) showed that (Red solid line with diamond shapes) when there was no intervention at the beginning, effective reproduction number was large, with the implementation of the intervention policies, effective reproduction number declined rapidly until less than 1, no longer spreading, which also reflected the effectiveness of the intervention policies from another aspect. Counterfactual assessment

The number of infections, recoveries and deaths associated with interventions has been analyzed previously. This section we are interested in the development of the COVID-19 epidemic under the following hypothetical scenarios: (1) Relax to a certain degree after a period of strict social distancing, including different degree of relaxation at the same time and the same degree of relaxation at different time. (2) Consider getting a vaccination after a period of prevention and control: one scenario is immediately getting a vaccination after the epidemic reach an inflection point. In other case, we assume vaccination is delayed because it takes time to research and develop. From above circumstances, we try to provide a quantitative description for the trend of epidemic development from the side at least before the virus mutates. What’s more, we assume the epidemic source is known, that is, the initial outbreak, then we can easily analyze the second rebond.

The effect of partially relaxation policies

The counterfactual analysis in this study assumes that the epidemic source is known (i.e., the initial outbreak location), which is a reasonable simplification when explaining macroscopic rebound mechanisms. However, in real-world epidemic prevention and control scenarios, once a resurgence occurs, questions such as “who the exact source of the rebound is” or “from which specific node/location the transmission restarts” are often highly uncertain, which has benn proposed by35. From the perspective of targeted interventions, if a rebound does indeed occur, applying the source detection algorithm by35 enables the rapid localization of the initial spreader of the rebound based on partially observed data from contact tracing networks. This empowers public health authorities to implement targeted interventions (such as precision isolation and localized lockdowns) during the early stages of a rebound, rather than resorting to large-scale, “one-size-fits-all” measures. Consequently, a more optimal balance between epidemic control and the maintenance of socio-economic activities could potentially be achieved.

Furthermore, once the source is detected and interventions are implemented, comparing the structural changes in the contact network before and after the intervention (e.g., the distribution of node degrees and the evolution of network density)—specifically, the magnitude of the reduction in the transmission rate within the infection source’s contact network—can reflect and evaluate the actual impact of social distancing policies on the effective transmission rate. We will explore these methodological improvements in our future research.

In this part, we adopt a simplified “control variable” setting, focusing the effect of deregulation on the change in contact rate β, based on the following considerations: (1) The aim is to isolate and quantify the effect of the dominant mechanism of “change in frequency of contact”. Fixing the γ and δ helps to establish a clear baseline for causal interpretation; (2) From the perspective of model interpretability, fitting multiple time-varying parameters at the same time significantly increases uncertainty and makes attribution of results difficult. The model serves as a basic assessment tool with a transparent structure and intuitive conclusions, which can provide a comparative benchmark for subsequent multifactor coupled models. In addition, the relaxation set is relatively small (≤ 10%) and has limited possible impact on other parameters. Of course, it is true that this setting may lead to an underestimation of the overall risk, and we emphasize the importance of providing estimates of the lower bounds of the impact in the case of “increased exposure only”, which is an important reference for decision-making.

For vaccination, we used the idealized assumption of “immediate full protection” to assess the theoretical upper limit of protection under optimal conditions of vaccine coverage. This setting provides a reference benchmark for the effect of protection discounts caused by factors such as insufficient vaccine efficacy, delayed vaccination and uneven distribution in reality. Therefore, the “effect discount factor” should be considered on the basis of the results of this paper in the actual prevention and control planning process.

In reality, the transmission of COVID-19 is accompanied by an “infodemic” (i.e., the spread of misinformation). False news tends to propagate much faster on networks and social media than factual information, exerting a further impact on the spread of the epidemic. Our current SIRD model focuses primarily on epidemic transmission (biological contagion) and does not account for the infodemic (misinformation propagation). In contrast, the source detection algorithm proposed by33 can be applied to both domains: it can be used not only to track the origins of new SARS-CoV-2 variants (epidemic sources) but also to monitor and trace the origins of misinformation campaigns regarding vaccines (infodemic sources).Therefore, to evaluate the potential impact of this confounding factor, we will endeavor in our future work to extend our current vaccine counterfactual framework to model the interplay between epidemic and infodemic transmission. Specifically, misinformation about vaccines may reduce vaccination rates (or increase vaccine hesitancy), thereby altering the trajectory of the epidemic. This would require the development of a coupled SIRD-infodemic model. A preliminary conceptual pathway for this is that the infodemic indirectly alters the course of the epidemic by influencing the population’s willingness to be vaccinated.

Conclusion

In this paper, we established a modified SIRD model to fit and predict the development process of COVID-19 in China. The MCMC robustness checks support the view that the model’s fitting and forecasting performance remains strong under dynamic parameters, including the infection, mortality, and recovery rates. In terms of the number of daily new infections, China managed to control the epidemic in July, 2020, which is consist with reality. The main results of the model can still remain robust after including the analysis of the impact of the dynamic natural population growth rate parameter. Of course, adding this consideration does help to reduce the bias due to the change in the number of natural population growth, and it further enriches the results of the model so that we can not only see the results of the number of each type of population due to the epidemic, but also the trend of the total population size, natural deaths, and natural births under the model parameters, expanding the analysis and research related to this public epidemic.

In the counterfactual assessment, there are two scenarios: partial unlocking and vaccination policies. Partial unlocking shows that if strict social distancing policies are relaxed before the epidemic completely ends, it will lead to a second rebound of the epidemic. Further analysis shows that if relaxation time is the same, as relaxation degree increases, the second rebound will be more serious, the peak rebound will be higher, and the end time of the epidemic will be later. If the degree of relaxation is the same, the earlier the relaxation time, the more serious the second rebound. Although there are no significant differences with the end of the time of the epidemic within similar relaxation time points, it still increases the difficulty of epidemic prevention and control. Even worse, it will delay the end time of the epidemic. This may provide an indirect insight into the recurrent COVID-19 resurgences in some developed countries, and further are consistent with the view that the effectiveness of strict social distancing policies in China during that time.

Analysis of vaccination shows that timely vaccination is the best strategy, which minimized the total number of confirmed cases, deaths and hastened the end of outbreak. For vaccinated people who have antibodies that would protect them from infection, at least for a period time. In addition, given the time it takes to develop a vaccine, we assume that taking a vaccination in a period later, and find that it’s much less effective, as the total number of cases and deaths will increase due to the accumulated infection. In order to verify the generalizability of the model in our paper, we also used the model for the empirical validation of the epidemic data in Brazil and Italy, and found that the model can fit the epidemic trend in Brazil more roughly but is far from the trend of the epidemic in Italy, which proves that our model has some applicability to the countries with similar trend of the epidemic data, and that there are indeed some limitations in fitting and predicting the countries with multiple serious rebound of epidemics. It provides a quantitative analysis path and potential explanation for some countries with similar epidemic trends, and also support the view that the effectiveness and necessity of implementing complete prevention and control in China when no vaccine is available.

In conclusion, although strict control may come with economic costs, this study quantifies its enormous benefits in public health. We construct a time-varying parametric model to better fit the dynamic reality of epidemic development, correcting the theoretical bias caused by static demographic assumptions by incorporating endogenous demographic processes into the core framework. Furthermore, through cross-country empirical tests—examining the model’s explanatory power for economies with similar transmission patterns like Brazil and its limitations under complex external factors like Italy—we provide a more realistic methodological benchmark and clarify its analytical boundaries for modeling related public health events.

Discussions and implications

However, there are still some limitations. First, in terms of modeling assumptions, the SIRD model adopts the “perfectly homogeneous mixing” assumption, which posits that any infected individual in the population has an equal probability of coming into contact with any susceptible individual. Although this assumption mathematically ensures the model’s solvability and computational efficiency—and provides a good fit for the overall evolution of the epidemic at the macro level—it overlooks significant heterogeneity among individuals in terms of age, social behavior, geographic distribution, and contact patterns. For non-pharmaceutical interventions such as targeted lockdowns and localized quarantines, the homogeneous mixing assumption struggles to accurately capture the actual impact of these differentiated policies on the epidemic. In contrast, graph neural network (GNN) methods such as DeepTrace capture individual heterogeneity and transmission patterns at the micro level by utilizing contact network structures, thereby complementing the macro-level modeling of the SIRD model. Future research could introduce age stratification into the SIRD model or draw on GNN methods to estimate contact network structures from mobility data, thereby constructing networked SEIR-type models that combine macro-level trend forecasting with micro-level network analysis.

Second, at the model framework level, the current SIRD model focuses solely on the biological transmission process of the epidemic (i.e., the spread of the virus among the population), while another important characteristic of the COVID-19 pandemic—the “information epidemic” (such as the rapid spread of misinformation about vaccines)—has not been incorporated into the model framework. The disease outbreak and the information epidemic do not follow two independent, parallel trajectories; rather, there is significant bidirectional feedback: misinformation regarding vaccine safety and efficacy reduces public willingness to get vaccinated, thereby increasing the proportion of susceptible individuals and leading to a higher risk of resurgence and a larger scale of the outbreak. Future research will attempt to construct a coupled SIRD-information epidemic model, linking the transmission dynamics of the information epidemic with the SIRD model, so that vaccination rates become an endogenous variable dynamically regulated by the state of the information epidemic, thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of the “dual nature” of the pandemic.

Third, at the level of source detection and intervention, the counterfactual analysis in this paper assumes that the initial outbreak site is known (i.e., the initial outbreak scenario hypothesized in findings 2). However, in real-world epidemic control scenarios, once a resurgence occurs, its source is often uncertain—new cases may result from external importation, hidden community transmission, or reactivation of previous infections. The source detection method proposed by 35, which is based on epidemiological centrality and a message-passing algorithm, can infer the most likely initial transmission node from a partially observed contact tracing network. Future research should incorporate such source detection techniques into the counterfactual analysis framework, enabling models not only to predict “whether a resurgence will occur,” but also to answer “where the resurgence originated” after it occurs, thereby providing more direct and actionable guidance for targeted interventions.

Fourth, in terms of data and privacy, the current SIRD model uses macro-level aggregated data (such as daily new cases, recoveries, and deaths at the national or provincial level), which is relatively secure from a privacy protection perspective but also limits the model’s ability to capture regional heterogeneity and the granularity of model updates. The federated learning and differential privacy methods systematically reviewed by37 systematically reviewed federated learning and differential privacy methods, providing a viable path for adapting the SIRD model to distributed data. Future research could develop a federated SIRD framework—in which each region fits the SIRD model based on its local data, uploads only the updated model parameters to a central server for aggregation, and then distributes the updated global model—thereby enabling cross-regional joint modeling without centralizing sensitive health data. Differential privacy allows for the addition of calibration noise during the parameter aggregation stage, ensuring that even if model parameters are shared, no individual’s health information can be inferred. This framework also supports counterfactual analysis of policy effectiveness using privacy-protected data from multiple regions, enabling cross-regional comparisons without compromising privacy.

In conclusion, future research will focus on four areas—hierarchical structured modeling, the coupling of information and epidemics, the integration of source detection, and federated differential privacy protection—to gradually refine and expand the analytical framework of the current SIRD model, with the aim of providing more precise and comprehensive theoretical support for epidemic modeling and policy evaluation.
