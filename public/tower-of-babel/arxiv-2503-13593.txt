Gravitational Wave Scattering via the Born Series: Scalar Tidal Matching to 𝒪 ⁡ ( G 7 ) {\cal O}(G^{7}) and Beyond
Simon Caron-Huot, Miguel Correia, Giulia Isabella, Mikhail Solon

Abstract
We introduce a novel method to compute gravitational wave amplitudes within the framework of effective field theory. By reinterpreting the Feynman diagram expansion as a Born series, our method offers several key advantages. It directly yields partial wave amplitudes, streamlining the matching with black hole perturbation theory. Long-distance gravitational interactions are unambiguously factorized from short-distance tidal effects, including dissipation, which are systematically incorporated via an in-in worldline effective action. Crucially, at every order in perturbation theory, integrals are expressed in terms of harmonic polylogarithms, enabling an end-to-end computation scalable to arbitrary orders. We illustrate the method with new predictions for scalar black hole Love numbers and their Renormalization Group equations to 𝒪 ⁡ ( G 7 ) \mathcal{O}(G^{7}) .

Setup – The Effective Wave Equation.
Due to the connection with the Born series, we can interpret the scattering amplitude calculated in the EFT as arising from an underlying wave equation, which we refer to as the
effective wave equation
. This equation can be derived in the standard manner from an effective action, where the parameters are determined by matching the physical scattering phases to those of the complete underlying theory. In our scalar model, given that
\omega\ll M

as indicated by (
1
), the scalar field
\phi
does not produce its own gravitational field. As a result, its long-distance dynamics are governed solely by the action

\,S_{\text{Grav}}=-{1\over 2}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi\,,

(2)

where
g^{\mu\nu}
is the Schwarzschild metric in
d
-spacetime dimensions,

ds^{2}=-fdt^{2}+f^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}_{d-2}

(3)

with

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!f(r)=1-{2GM\,n_{d}\,\mu^{4-d}\over r^{d-3}}\,,\quad\!\!\!\!n_{d}\equiv{4\pi^{3-d\over 2}\Gamma\big({d-1\over 2}\big)\over d-2}\,.

(4)

The metric
g^{\mu\nu}
is understood to be perturbatively expanded in Newton’s constant
G
, whose units are kept as in four-dimensions via the introduction of the dimensionful scale
\mu
. This allows us to accurately replicate the Feynman diagram computation in dimensional regularization where as usual
d=4-2\epsilon
.
As we will see, the perturbative diagrams are dominated by the single scale
r\sim\omega^{-1}\gg GM
and sensitivity to the near-horizon region will appear in the form of ultraviolet (UV) divergences regulated by
\epsilon

We supplement (
2

\displaystyle\!\!\!S_{\text{Love}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,n}\frac{\mu^{4-d}}{\ell!}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\Big[C_{\ell,n}(\nabla_{\!\ell}\,\phi_{+})\partial_{\tau}^{n}(\nabla_{\!\ell}\,\phi_{-})\Big]

(5)

where the spatial derivative is defined as
\nabla^{\mu}=(g^{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}u^{\nu})\nabla_{\nu}
, and
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
is the 4-velocity of the compact object. The symbol
\nabla_{\ell}
denotes the symmetrized and trace-subtracted product of
\ell
spatial derivatives. The coefficients
C_{\ell,n}
are the Love numbers,
and we again inserted a power of the scale
\mu
to ensure that their units are as in four-dimensions:
[C_{\ell,n}]=-2\ell-n-1

Being supported on
\mathbf{r}=0
, where
x^{\mu}=(t,\mathbf{r})
, the worldline action (
5
) adds a potential proportional to
\delta^{d-1}(\mathbf{r})
to the wave equation.

An essential aspect of tidal physics is dissipation.
As for other open systems, an action can be written using the in-in field doubling. We use the Keldysh basis
[

\phi_{+}\!=\!\frac{1}{2}(\phi_{1}{+}\phi_{2})

\phi_{-}\!=\!\phi_{1}{-}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle\!\!S[\phi]\!\supset\!\!\!\int\!\!dt\frac{\phi\partial_{t}^{n}\phi}{2}\!\Rightarrow S_{\text{in-in}}\!\supset\!\frac{1{+}({-}1)^{n}}{2}\!\!\!\int\!\!dt\,\phi_{+}\partial_{t}^{n}\phi_{-}

(6)

while the non-conservative Love numbers,

We will focus on the retarded two-point amplitude

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{Grav}}(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left({2GMn_{d}\,\mu^{2\epsilon}\over r^{1-2\epsilon}}\right)^{\!n}\Big[{2\epsilon-1\over r}{d\over dr}

Born Series,
S
-matrix and Boundary Conditions.
The radial wave equation (
7
) can be solved perturbatively via the Born series as in quantum mechanics,

\displaystyle\psi(r)=\Psi_{0}(r)+\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathrm{G}(r,r_{1})V(r_{1})\Psi_{0}(r_{1})

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathrm{G}(r,r_{1})V(r_{1})\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}\,\mathrm{G}(r_{1},r_{2})V(r_{2})\Psi_{0}(r_{2})\dots

where the Green’s function
\mathrm{G}(r,r^{\prime})
and the free solution
\Psi_{0}(r)
satisfy the free radial wave equation given by (
7
) when
V(r)=0
. The free solution
\Psi_{0}(r)
is a linear combination of Riccati-Hankel functions
[
54
]
,

\Psi_{0}(r)=A_{\text{in}}\,h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{-}(\omega r)+A_{\text{out}}\,h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{+}(\omega r)\,,

(12)

which behave as free radial waves at infinity,

h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{\pm}(r\to\infty)=e^{\pm i\left(\omega r-\frac{\pi}{2}(\ell-\epsilon)\right)}\,

(13)

As is customary in scattering theory
[
54
,
55
]
, we assume that interactions decay sufficiently rapidly at infinity,
V(r\to\infty)\lesssim 1/r
, ensuring that
\psi(r\to\infty)\approx\Psi_{0}(r)
. The
S
-matrix, or more precisely, the partial wave amplitude, is defined as the ratio of the coefficients of the outgoing and ingoing free waves,

S_{\ell}(\omega)\equiv-\frac{A_{\mathrm{out}}}{A_{\mathrm{in}}}\,.

(14)

Moreover, it is also assumed that the centrifugal potential
{(\ell-\epsilon)(\ell-\epsilon+1)\over r^{2}}
dominates at small distances over the interaction potential
V(r\to 0)
, so that
\psi(r\to 0)\approx\Psi_{0}(r)
. The Riccati-Hankel functions in this limit behave as linear combinations of “regular” and “irregular” terms

\displaystyle h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{+}(r\to 0)\;-\;h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{-}(r\to 0)

\displaystyle\propto\;r^{1+\ell-\epsilon}\,,

(15)

\displaystyle h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{+}(r\to 0)\;+\;h_{\ell-\epsilon}^{-}(r\to 0)

\displaystyle\propto\;r^{\epsilon-\ell}\,.

At
r\to 0
the solution then assumes the form

\psi(r\to 0)=B_{\mathrm{reg}}\,r^{1+\ell-\epsilon}+\frac{B_{\mathrm{irr}}\,\mu^{2\epsilon}}{2\ell+1-2\epsilon}r^{\epsilon-\ell}\,.

(16)

When
V\!=\!0
the
\phi_{+}
equation of motion at the origin
imposes the boundary condition
B_{\text{irr}}=0
.
This can be seen from the fact that the irregular solution produces a
\delta
-function term (recall that
\phi_{+}\propto\Psi/r^{\frac{d-2}{2}}
):

\vec{\nabla}^{2}\frac{r^{\epsilon}}{r^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}=-\frac{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d-3}{2})}\delta^{d-1}(\mathbf{r})\,.

(17)

Using this identity and its derivatives, it is easy to see
that canceling the
\delta
-function in the equation of motion including the Love number potential
V_{\text{Love}}
now imposes a modified boundary condition relating the two coefficients, where using (
9
)
we find specifically:

\displaystyle\frac{B_{\text{irr}}}{B_{\text{reg}}}=c_{\ell}\,F_{\ell}(\omega),\,\,\,\,c_{\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell}}{2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{3}{2}+\ell-\epsilon\Big).

(18)

This closely resembles the definition of Love numbers found in the GR literature
[
17
,
16
,
56
]
.
The advantage here is that
F_{\ell}(\omega)
has a gauge-invariant definition based on the worldline action (
5
). Equation (
18
) thus connects the GR and EFT definitions of Love numbers, which is one of our main conceptual results. Because it allows to trade
V_{\text{Love}}(r)
for a re-definition of the boundary condition at
r\to 0
, it unambigously factorizes short-distance and long-distance effects.

It may seem surprising that the gravitational potential
V_{\text{grav}}
does not modify this boundary condition.
An intuitive explanation is that in dimensional regularization,
the
r\to 0
behavior is in effect regulated by treating
r^{-1+2\epsilon}
as small when
r\to 0
.

In the free case (
V(r)=0
), the boundary condition
B_{\text{irr}}=0
imposes that
A_{\text{in}}=-A_{\text{out}}
via the relation (
15
). Consequently, this implies
S_{\ell}(\omega)=1
, as expected. In the general case, however, the relationship between
A_{\text{out}}
and
A_{\text{in}}
is more intricate and can be determined perturbatively in the potential
V(r)
using the Born series (
11
).

The assumptions that the interacting solution
\psi(r)
reduces to the free solution
\Psi_{0}(r)
both at infinity (
r\to\infty
) and at the origin (
r\to 0
), while satisfied within dimensional regularization, are in fact
not
satisfied when
\epsilon=0
.
This is the fundamental reason why the Born series and the partial wave amplitude

exhibit IR and UV divergences which we need to regularize by keeping
\epsilon\neq 0
, in close parallel with the Feynman diagram computation
[
32
]
. The IR divergences are entirely due to the Coulomb term

in
V_{\text{Grav}}(r)
and obey a very simple exponentiation
[
54
]
.
The UV divergences, on the other hand, which must cancel
between
V_{\rm Love}
and
V_{\rm Grav}
contributions, originate from structure at the Schwarzschild radius which is not resolved by the perturbative expansion in the region
r\sim\omega^{-1}\gg GM

Concretely, the relationship between partial waves
S_{\ell}(\omega)
and momentum-space Feynman diagrams involves an integral over Legendre polynomials

[
32
]
. Notably, the EFT matching with BHPT is performed at the level of the partial waves
[
32
]
and, crucially, our method yields directly the partial wave
S_{\ell}(\omega)
within the EFT framework, eliminating the need for this intermediate step.

General Strategy and Near-Far Factorization.
With the boundary conditions and the connection to the amplitude in (

Our strategy is to separate the problem in two different regions which we refer to as

and

where
we can make distinct approximations.
The idea is similar to the method of “matched asymptotic expansions”
[
57
]
and the “method of regions” in the context of Feynman integrals
[
58

In the far-zone region we choose the Green’s function
\mathrm{G}^{\text{far}}(r,r^{\prime})
to only have support for ordered radii
0{<}r{<}r^{\prime}
. This eliminates any possible UV divergence from

and allows for a regular expansion in
\epsilon
where the starting point are Riccati-Hankel functions

that reduce to elementary functions for integer
\ell

\!r^{\prime}=\!0

V_{\text{Grav}}(r)
which contribute to

can then be conveniently expressed in terms of harmonic polylogarithms (HPLs), as we detail in the next section.

In the near-zone region, on the other hand, we choose a Green’s function
\mathrm{G}^{\text{near}}(r,r^{\prime})
that only has support for
r^{\prime}{<}r

\epsilon

The near-zone Born series then proceeds by starting with the boundary condition (

]
. This also suggests an alternative way of regulating UV divergences by shifting
\ell
away from integer values, as noticed in
[
42
,
61

.

The UV divergences are of course tightly related to a Renormalization Group (RG) equation which predicts logarithmic running of Love numbers.
All UV divergences in our approach originate from the boundary condition (
16
) becoming degenerate when
\epsilon=0
.
By a change of basis

(B_{\rm reg},B_{\rm irr})^{T}=Z(\bar{B}_{\rm reg},\bar{B}_{\rm irr})^{T}

we can thus ensure that the solution expressed in terms of the
renormalized coefficients

\bar{B}
remains finite as
\epsilon\to 0

According to standard renormalization theory, the price to pay is that coefficients become scale-dependent and satisfy a renormalization group equation:
(\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}+\gamma^{(\ell)})(\bar{B}_{\rm reg},\bar{B}_{\rm irr})^{T}=0
.
The matrix
Z
and anomalous dimension in a minimal subtraction scheme are recorded in Supplementary Material
C

c_{\ell}\bar{F}_{\ell}=\bar{B}_{\mathrm{irr}}/\bar{B}_{\mathrm{reg}}
can then be interpreted as a boundary condition in the region
GM\,{\ll}\,r\,{\ll}\,\omega^{-1}
, providing a precise dictionary between the renormalization scheme used in the worldline EFT and the GR definition of Love numbers (see (

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\bar{F}_{\ell}(\omega)=c_{\ell}\gamma^{(\ell)}_{1,2}\bar{F}_{\ell}^{2}+\big(\gamma^{(\ell)}_{1,1}-\gamma^{(\ell)}_{2,2}\big)\bar{F}_{\ell}-\frac{\gamma^{(\ell)}_{2,1}}{c_{\ell}}\,.

(26)

We note the presence of a quadratic term, which starts contributing at
\mathcal{O}(G^{5})
in the black hole case. To our knowledge it had not been identified before.

We anticipate that our method will enable the determination of the observationally relevant gravitational black hole Love numbers, which start at
\mathcal{O}(G^{5})

Potential challenges include handling the graviton’s spin-2 structure within dimensional regularization and accounting for gravitational backreaction on the worldline, which introduces additional contact terms. We also expect that the far-zone integrals, being expressible in terms of harmonic polylogarithms, can enhance BHPT computations

Appendix A
In-in and time-ordered amplitudes

Dissipative effects on the compact object are naturally captured by the in-in (Schwinger-Keldysh) formalism. We highlight here similarities and distinctions with in-out or time-ordered amplitudes that have not been widely appreciated in the literature.
In the Keldysh basis the propagator coming from the free bulk action (
2
) has two nonvanishing components, the retarded and symmetrized propagators

\begin{split}&\langle\phi_{-}\phi_{+}\rangle=\,\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{\includegraphics[width=48.36958pt]{ret.pdf}}\,=\frac{i}{(p_{0}+i\epsilon)^{2}-{\bf p}^{2}},\\
&\langle\phi_{+}\phi_{+}\rangle=\,\raisebox{-3.99994pt}{\includegraphics[width=48.36958pt]{sym.pdf}}\,=\pi\delta(p^{2})\,.\end{split}

(27)

The largest time equation implies that the
propagator between two
\phi_{-}
fields vanishes
and that the full
\langle\phi_{-}\phi_{+}\rangle
satisfies retarded boundary conditions. We use a graphical notation from
[
63
]
which emphasizes the forward propagation of time toward
+
vertices.

According to response theory
[
64
,
65
]
, the linear effect of an incoming wave on the expectation value of the far field is expressed by a commutator, here of asymptotic creation/annihilation operators
[
13
,
48
]
in the presence of the black hole:

\!\!\!\!\!\!\langle{\rm BH}|\,[a^{\rm out}_{k^{\prime}},a^{\rm in}_{k}]\,|{\rm BH}\rangle\!=\\
2\pi\delta(k^{\prime 0}{-}k^{0})2k^{0}S^{\rm ret}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})

(28)

where
a^{\rm out}=S^{\dagger}a^{\rm in}S
.
By a generalization of the LSZ reduction formula
[
48
]
, the (connected part of this)
retarded amplitude
is equal to the on-shell limit of the amputated retarded two-point function
\langle\phi_{-}\phi_{+}\rangle
.

It is easy to see that all diagrams contributing to (
28
) consist of an uninterrupted sequence of retarded propagators and vertices, for example:

S^{\text{ret}}\;\supset\;\raisebox{-10.00002pt}{\includegraphics[width=68.28644pt]{Sret2.pdf}}\,\,\,.

(29)

This is due to the vanishing of the
\langle\phi_{-}\phi_{-}\rangle
propagator and the absence of a
\phi_{+}\phi_{+}
term in the worldline action, both due to the largest time equation. This is a chief advantages of the Keldysh basis and ensures monotonic flow of time from source to observation. Thus
S^{\text{ret}}
is given by a standard Born series, albeit with retarded (causal) boundary conditions.
Fourier transforming the propagator to position space and projecting onto spherical harmonics, as described above (
7
), yields the following radial Green’s function
[
54
]

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{-}(r)\,\psi_{+}(r^{\prime})\rangle={j_{\ell}(\omega r_{<})\,h_{\ell}^{+}(\omega r_{>})\over\omega}

(30)

where
r_{<}=\min(r,r^{\prime}),\;\;r_{>}=\max(r,r^{\prime})
and
j_{\ell}(z)=[h^{+}_{\ell}(z)-h_{\ell}^{-}(z)]/2i
is the regular solution near the origin (see (
15
)).
This confirms diagrammatically that in the absence of
V_{\text{Love}}
the (dimensionally-regulated) Feynman diagram expansion constructs the purely regular solution near the origin, while the
V_{\rm Love}
terms localize to
r^{\prime}=0
and source the irregular solution (see (
16
)).

The Green’s function obtained from the Feynman rules, (
30
), differs from the ones used in the text by homogeneous solutions, for example:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\text{G}^{\rm far}(r,r^{\prime})=\langle\psi_{-}(r)\psi_{+}(r^{\prime})\rangle\!-\!j_{\ell}(\omega r^{\prime})h_{\ell}^{+}(\omega r)/\omega.

(31)

The choice made in the text simplifies the iterated integrals but ultimately leads to a multiple of the same solution once the boundary condition at the origin is accounted for.

For positive frequencies, the retarded Green’s function (
27
) coincides with the time-ordered one. In particular, the purely gravitational contribution to the retarded and time-ordered phase-shifts are identical (
\delta_{\rm Grav}
below). Could we not simply replace (
28
) with the standard time-ordered product?

The answer is no, due to fluctuation effects on the worldline.
Dissipation in time-ordered perturbation theory could be captured by adding additional degrees of freedom (see e.g.
[
6
]
).
However, in analogy with e.g. Brownian motion
[
66
]
, one should expect the time-ordered amplitude to probe additional fluctuations. The full worldline action can contain other vertices besides (
5
):

S_{\rm Love}^{\prime}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\ell}\frac{1}{\ell!}\int d\tau\nabla_{\ell}\phi_{-}H_{\ell}(i\partial_{\tau})\nabla_{\ell}\phi_{-}

(32)

where we expect
H_{\ell}(\omega)\approx\coth\frac{\omega}{2T}{\rm Im}\,F_{\ell}(\omega)
by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
Note that since
{\rm Im}\,F_{\ell}(\omega)\propto\omega
,
H_{\ell}(\omega)
is analytic at low frequencies and thus local in time in agreement with EFT logic. (Fluctuation-dissipation may not apply exactly to a black hole formed by gravitational collapse, which is not in equilibrium with its environment and is typically modeled by the so-called Unruh vacuum. We expect a small difference in the near zone at low frequencies, as can be seen using eq. (5) of
[
67
]
, where infalling modes are small:,
\overleftarrow{R}_{\ell}(\omega)\propto(GM\omega)^{2\ell+1}
.)

The action (
32
) does not contribute to the retarded amplitude (
28
) since it cannot connect to external
\phi_{-}\phi_{+}
using the propagators (
27
).
However, it contributes to the
time-ordered amplitude
\langle\mathcal{T}\,\phi\phi\rangle_{\text{BH}}=\langle\phi_{1}\phi_{1}\rangle_{\text{BH}}
since

\phi_{1}=\phi_{+}+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{-}
.
This leads to additional diagrams

S^{\mathcal{T}}-S^{\text{ret}}\,\supset\raisebox{-16.99998pt}{\includegraphics[width=46.94687pt]{HLove.pdf}}\,\,.

(33)

These contributions
are enhanced at low frequencies by the Bose-Einstein factor
\sim\frac{2T}{\omega}
below (
32
):

\displaystyle\!\!\!H_{\ell=0}(\omega)\!\approx\!\frac{2T}{\omega}{\rm Im}\,F_{\ell=0}(\omega)=4GM+\mathcal{O}(G^{3}\omega^{2})

(34)

where we used the Hawking temperature
T=\frac{1}{8\pi GM}
and the first nonvanishing Love number from Table
1
. We stress that while the Hawking temperature represents a small, quantum, energy (
T\propto\hbar
), it is large compared with the energy of the considered wave:
T/\omega\gg 1
.
This is why the worldline action (
32
) starts at order
G
, even though (
5
) starts at order
G^{2}
.
It predicts field fluctuations in the near-zone region
GM\ll r\ll\omega^{-1}
which are approximately frequency-independent and thus appear like white noise on time scales
t\gg r
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}\phi(r,t)\phi(r^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{BH}}\approx\frac{H_{\ell=0}(0)}{(4\pi)^{2}rr^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(35)

The “fluctuation Love numbers”
H_{\ell}
could be measured in principle from decoherence effects which they quantitatively control
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
]
.

In summary, the linear response of the black hole is captured by a retarded amplitude which admits a simple Born series, a smooth classical limit, and is completely insensitive to Hawking radiation. In contrast, the low frequency limit of the time-ordered amplitude is dominated by Bose-enhanced fluctuations that could be detected in interference experiments.

Appendix B
Iterated exponential integrals

Here we exemplify our method to evaluate the interated integrals (
22
) in the limit

in terms of multi-zeta values. A distinct method was discussed in
[
62
]
.
Since the limit in general contains logarithmic divergences the expansion is best organized by introducing the function

\displaystyle\equiv\eta\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz_{n+1}}{z_{n_{1}}}(z_{n+1})^{\eta}\,{\cal I}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(z_{n+1})

(36)

whose Laurent series up to order

is in one-to-one correspondence with powers of
\log z_{n+1}
in the limit.
This is precisely the function given in (
24
).
For the one-index case the integrals (
22
) give incomplete
\Gamma
integrals

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{j}(z_{2})

\displaystyle=-\int_{z_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}}e^{2iz_{1}}\frac{\log(\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}})^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}

(37)

while the complete integral (
36
) evaluates to

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(\eta)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz_{2}}{z_{2}}(z_{2})^{\eta}{\cal I}_{j}(z_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(1{+}\eta)}{(-\eta)^{j}(-2i)^{\eta}}\,,

(38)

in agreement with (
24
). Here the integral can be performed directly by swapping the order and integrating over
z_{2}
before
z_{1}
.
By multiplying (
37
) by
z^{-\eta}
and keeping terms of order
\eta^{0}
as

, one immediately deduces the limit of
{\cal I}_{j}(z)
, for example:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\to\log\left(-2ize^{\gamma_{\text{E}}}\right)+O(z)\equiv L(z)+{\cal O}(z),

(39)

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\to\frac{L(z)^{2}}{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+{\cal O}(z),

(40)

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{3}(z)

\displaystyle\to\frac{L(z)^{3}}{3!}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}L(z)+\frac{\zeta_{3}}{3}+{\cal O}(z)\,.

(41)

After these leading terms are obtained, subleading powers as
z\to 0
can be obtained more easily by expanding the integrand in (
22
) and using the simple identity
\int_{z}^{\infty}=\int_{\delta}^{\infty}-\int_{\delta}^{z}
with
\delta{\to}0
. All subleading terms here have simple rational coefficients.

In the two-index case the integral (
22
) reads

(42)

Notice the alternating sign in the exponent compared with (
37
), which is natural from the perspective of how the integrals appear from the Born series.
The regulated complete integral (

) then reads

which now does not admit a simple closed form.
Nonetheless, the
\eta\to 0
limit can be computed using HPL technology and we find for example

\displaystyle\!\!\!{\cal I}_{1,1}^{(\eta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2ie^{\gamma_{\text{E}}})^{\eta}}\left[\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}+{\cal O}(\eta)\right]\!,

(43)

\displaystyle\!\!\!{\cal I}_{1,2}^{(\eta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2ie^{\gamma_{\text{E}}})^{\eta}}\left[\frac{-1}{\eta^{3}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\eta}+\frac{\zeta_{3}}{3}+{\cal O}(\eta)\right]\!,

(44)

\displaystyle\!\!\!{\cal I}_{2,1}^{(\eta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2ie^{\gamma_{\text{E}}})^{\eta}}\left[\frac{-1}{\eta^{3}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12\eta}-\frac{2\zeta_{3}}{3}+{\cal O}(\eta)\right]\!.

(45)

These immediately imply corresponding limits for the incomplete integrals, for example

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2,1}(z)

\displaystyle\to\frac{L(z)^{3}}{3!}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}L(z)-\frac{2\zeta_{3}}{3}+{\cal O}(z)

(46)

with
L(z)
as in (
39
).
Again, subleading powers of
z
are easier to calculate and can be obtained by expanding the integrand in (
42
),
or alternatively, using the general differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}{\cal I}_{\ldots,j_{n}}(z)=\frac{1{-}\delta_{j_{n},1}}{z}{\cal I}_{\ldots,j_{n}-1}(z)+\delta_{j_{n},1}\frac{e^{\sigma_{n}z}}{z}{\cal I}_{\ldots}(z)\,.

(47)

We conclude this appendix by recording the remaining complete integral of transcendental weight three:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{1,1,1}^{(\eta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2ie^{\gamma_{\text{E}}})^{\eta}}\left[\frac{-1}{\eta^{3}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\eta}+\frac{7\zeta_{3}}{3}+{\cal O}(\eta)\right].

(48)
