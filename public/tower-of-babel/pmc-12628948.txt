Dietary patterns, polygenic risk, and kidney cancer incidence: a UK Biobank cohort study
Authors: Mingrui Zou, Ruiyi Deng, Jianhui Qiu, Kaiyuan Mao, Jiaheng Shang, Sainan Zhu, Lin Cai, Yizhou Wang, Kan Gong, Jingcheng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiet and genetic risk are established risk factors for kidney cancer, but their interaction remains unclear. This study aims to systematically investigate the associations between various dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk, as well as to evaluate the combined effects of diet and genetic risk on kidney cancer risk.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study involving 113,594 participants from the UK Biobank who completed at least two 24-hour dietary recalls, and adherence to four dietary patterns (Alternate Mediterranean Index (aMED), Alternative Healthy Eating Index 2010 (AHEI), dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH), and dietary inflammatory index (DII)) was assessed. Incident kidney cancer cases were ascertained via records. Genetic risk was quantified using polygenic risk score (PRS). Associations were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, adjusting for confounders. Furthermore, mediation analyses were conducted to identify potential mediators.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 11.44 years, 425 participants developed kidney cancer. Higher aMED (HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.54–0.94) and DASH (HR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.49–0.90) were associated with reduced risk of kidney cancer, while higher DII was associated with increased risk (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.02–1.79). Moreover, participants with high genetic risk combined with low aMED, AHEI and DASH scores or high DII score presented the highest kidney cancer risk. Additive interaction was observed between genetic risk and AHEI as well as DII. Moreover, a series of biochemical indicators were identified as potential mediators, with the mediation proportions ranging from 0.76% to 8.40%.ConclusionAdherence to healthy dietary patterns, such as aMED, AHEI, and DASH is associated with a reduced risk of kidney cancer, whereas a pro-inflammatory diet confers a greater risk, particularly in high-genetic-risk populations. These findings provide strong evidence that promoting these healthy dietary patterns represents a promising public health strategy for the primary prevention of kidney cancer.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07373-7.

Introduction

Kidney cancer is one of the most common urological malignancies, with its incidence rising steadily in recent years [1]. According to the latest projections from American Cancer Society, over 400,000 new cases were diagnosed globally annually [2]. Due to the paucity of early symptoms, about 30% of patients present with metastatic disease at their initial diagnosis, which is typically associated with poor prognosis and high recurrence rates [3, 4]. Although targeted therapies such as sunitinib and sorafenib have been developed and introduced into clinical practice, their therapeutic efficacy remains limited, with a substantial proportion of patients experiencing drug resistance or disease progression within 6 to 15 months of treatment [5, 6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to investigate modifiable factors, particularly lifestyle determinants, that may contribute to the primary prevention of kidney cancer.

It is well-established that smoking, obesity, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and hypertension are well-established risk factors for kidney cancer [7–9]. Moreover, a limited number of studies have suggested that the consumption of fruits, vegetables, moderate coffee and tea, and light-to-moderate amounts of alcohol may be associated with a reduced risk of this disease. However, the collective evidence on the role of overall dietary patterns in kidney cancer risk remains inconclusive. Furthermore, previous investigations into the association between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk have predominantly focused on the intake of individual food items or specific nutrients, rather than examining comprehensive dietary patterns or habits [10]. For instance, Bock et al. reported that micronutrients such as α-carotene, β-carotene may reduce the risk of kidney cancer [11]. In addition, a study conducted by the Korean National Center in 2024 identified 7 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) with diagnostic potential for kidney cancer [12]. However, the clinical significance of these findings warrants cautious interpretation due to limited sample size and the case-control design, which inherently restricts its evidential strength. To the best of our knowledge, no study to date has systematically investigated the association between various dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk, nor explored the underlying biological mechanisms involved. Consequently, large-scale, population-based prospective studies are urgently needed to provide robust evidence.

In recent years, the concept of “gene × lifestyle interaction” has emerged as a pivotal focus in precision medicine, highlighting that modifiable lifestyle factors may differentially modulate disease susceptibility depending on an individual’s genetic architecture [13]. With the advancements in genome-wide association study (GWAS) and the development of polygenic risk score (PRS), assessing an individual’s genetic susceptibility to specific diseases has become feasible [14–16]. Furthermore, an expanding body of evidence suggests that the relationship between dietary patterns and cancer incidence may be modulated by genetic factors [17–19]. However, the potential interaction between specific dietary patterns and genetic predisposition in determining kidney cancer risk remains largely unexplored and poorly understood. Systematically investigating this gene-diet interaction is not only essential for resolving the current inconsistencies in the literature, but also holds profound clinical significance. Specifically, such research could pave the way for developing individualized dietary prevention strategies tailored to an individual’s genetic risk profile, thereby advancing the field of precision preventive medicine.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to conduct a large population-based prospective study to investigate the associations between 4 diet quality indices and kidney cancer incidence while concurrently evaluating their joint effect with genetic risk of kidney cancer. Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a population-based prospective cohort that enrolled over 500,000 individuals aged 40–70 years at recruitment between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centers in the United Kingdom [20]. At their initial visit, participants underwent comprehensive baseline assessments, including physical examinations, computer-assisted interviews, touchscreen questionnaires, and the collection of biological samples. Detailed information on socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors and medical history was systematically recorded. Ethical approval was granted by the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (reference 11/NW/0382 and 21/NW/0157), with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

For this study, we utilized dietary data from the “Oxford WebQ” platform, which collected web-based 24-hour dietary assessments from 210,965 participants between 2009 and 2012. To enhance the reliability of dietary pattern analysis, we included individuals who completed ≥ 2 valid 24-hour assessments. After exclusions, the analytical cohort comprised 126,825 participants. For the analysis of incident kidney cancer, we excluded subjects with kidney cancer, other prevalent cancer, or missing diet information at baseline. Overall, 113,594 participants were analyzed in the primary analysis. In the second-stage analysis, we investigated the joint effects of dietary patterns and PRS on kidney cancer risk. After excluding participants with missing genetic data, the final analytical cohort comprised 111,362 participants (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Study variables

Diet quality assessment

Dietary intake was evaluated at baseline (2009–2012) using the “Oxford WebQ”, a web-based 24-hour dietary recall instrument [21]. This open-ended assessment tool presented participants with a comprehensive list of 238 commonly consumed items (206 food products and 32 beverage categories), prompting them to report their consumption within the preceding 24-hour period. During 2009–2012, 5 assessments were conducted. A total of 210,965 participants completed ≥ 1 reliable assessment during this period, with the following completion frequency: 84,147 (39.9%) completed a single assessment; 48,107 (22.8%) completed 2 assessments; 42,480 (20.1%) completed 3 assessments; 30,466 (14.4%) completed 4 assessments, and 5765 (2.8%) completed 5 assessments. In the present study, we included only participants who completed ≥ 2 valid 24-hour assessments to enhance data reliability.

The alternate Mediterranean diet (aMED) score is a refined quantitative assessment tool based on the traditional Mediterranean diet described by Trichopoulou. It is designed to objectively evaluate the degree of adherence to this dietary pattern [22]. This scoring system comprises 9 key components: vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts and seeds, legumes, fish, the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fatty acids, alcohol consumption, and red/processed meat. Participants received one point for each of the first seven beneficial components if their intake exceeded the cohort median; otherwise, they received zero points. For alcohol, moderate alcohol consumption (5–25 g/day) was assigned one point, while intakes outside this range received zero. For red/processed meat, consumption below the median received one point, while intake above yielded zero. The total score ranges from 0 (minimal adherence) to 9 (maximal adherence), providing a comprehensive measure of dietary alignment with Mediterranean principles (Supplementary Table S1).

The alternative healthy eating index (AHEI) is a dietary quality assessment tool designed to evaluate the association between dietary patterns and chronic disease risk [23]. In this study, we employed the improved 2010-version of AHEI, which comprises 11 dietary components including vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts and legumes, etc [24]. However, due to a lack of available data in the UK Biobank, energy of trans-fat was not included. Each food component was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 10 points, with proportional scoring applied between the minimum and maximum intake thresholds. Values beyond these thresholds received either 0 or 10 points. The total score ranges from 0 (minimal adherence) to 100 (maximal adherence) (Supplementary Table S2).

The Dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH) score is a dietary assessment tool originally designed to guide dietary interventions aimed at reducing hypertension risk, which has also been demonstrated to provide significant benefits for cardiovascular health, weight management, and diabetes prevention [25, 26]. This scoring system evaluates food categories based on intake levels, assigning scores in a positive direction (ranging from 1 to 5 for fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, whole grains, and low-fat dairy) or a reverse direction (ranging from 5 to 1 for sodium, red and processed meats, and sugar-sweetened beverages). Each participant’s total score is calculated as the sum of these component scores, with a possible range from 8 (minimal adherence) to 40 (maximal adherence) (Supplementary Table S3).

The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) is a literature-derived scoring system that reflects the overall inflammatory potential of an individual’s diet, providing a valuable tool for investigating the relationship between dietary patterns and risk of inflammation-related diseases, as inflammation serves as a central mechanism underlying various chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer [27]. Of the 45 food items used to calculate the DII, data for 28 were available in the UK Biobank. To construct the index, the Z-score for each dietary component is calculated by subtracting the mean intake from the individual intake and dividing the result by the standard deviation (SD); this Z-score is then converted into a percentile, multiplied by 2, and subtracted by 1. The resulting value is further multiplied by the corresponding inflammatory effect score, with positive values assigned to pro-inflammatory items and negative values to anti-inflammatory items. Finally, the scores for all dietary components are summed to yield the overall DII score, where higher scores indicate a greater inflammatory potential of the diet (Supplementary Table S4).

Kidney cancer assessment

Cancer diagnoses in the UK Biobank were ascertained through linkage to National Health Service Information Centre for participants from England and Wales and the National Health Service Central Register for Scotland. The primary outcome for this study was the incident kidney cancer (ICD-10 code: C64). Participants were followed from baseline until the first diagnosis of kidney cancer, death, or the end of follow-up on 31 October, 2022, whichever occurred first.

Covariates

Based on a review of established literature, a comprehensive set of potential confounders was selected to be included in our statistical models. Baseline data on demographic characteristics (including age, sex, education level and ethnicity) and lifestyle behaviors (including smoking and drinking status) were collected via a touchscreen questionnaire. Specifically, age at baseline was treated as a continuous variable. Sex was categorized as male or female. Education level was grouped into three categories: college degree, other qualifications and unknown. Ethnicity was divided into white and non-white. Smoking status and drinking status were categorized as never, previous, and current. Health-related factors included body mass index (BMI), physical activity and sleep duration. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms (kg) by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). In addition to being a continuous variable, BMI was grouped into 4 categories according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria: Underweight (BMI < 18.5), Normal (18.5 ≤ BMI < 25), Overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30), and Obesity (BMI ≥ 30) [28]. For physical activity, participants were considered to have regular physical activity if they met any of the following criteria: 1) More than 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week; 2) More than 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity per week; 3) Moderate activity on at least five days per week; 4) One vigorous activity per week [29]. Self-reported sleep duration was ascertained from the question, “About how many hours of sleep do you get in every 24 hours? (Please include naps)”. Socioeconomic characteristics including average total before-tax household income and Townsend deprivation index (TDI) were also included. Household income was classified into 5 categories: < £18,000, £18,000−£30,999, £31,000−£51,999, £52,000−£100,000, and > £100,000. TDI, calculated based on participants’ postcode, is a composite index reflecting area-level socioeconomic deprivation derived from census data on unemployment, non-car and non-home ownership, and household overcrowding, with higher values indicating greater deprivation. Furthermore, through self-reported diagnosis and face-to-face interviews, information on personal history of disease (hypertension and diabetes) was collected. To handle missing covariate data, we applied multiple imputation aiming to enhance statistical efficiency and mitigate bias.

Genotyping and weighted polygenic risk score

The details of genotyping processes, imputation, and quality control of genetic data in the UK Biobank have been described in a previous study in detail [30]. To calculate the PRS of kidney cancer, we selected the latest genome-wide association study (GWAS) (GSCT90320057), which included 25,890 cases and 743,585 controls [31]. The PRS construction followed a standardized protocol. First, we selected single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with kidney cancer at a genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) from the GWAS. To ensure the independence of the selected SNPs, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (kb = 10,000, r2 < 0.001) using the European-ancestry samples from the 1000 Genomes Project as the reference panel. Finally, a total of 49 SNPs were selected for PRS construction (Supplemental Table S5). The PRS was calculated in PLINK 2.0 as a weighted sum of risk alleles for each SNP. The weights were the corresponding effect sizes (log odds ratios) for each SNP as reported in the source GWAS, and PRS was calculated using the formula: PRS = (β1 × SNP1+β2 × SNP2 + … … +βn × SNPn), where SNPn represents risk allele number of the SNP, and βn is the weight for SNPn. Moreover, the PRS was categorized into 3 levels of genetic risk: low (lowest tertile), intermediate (second tertile), and high (highest tertile), consistent with previous polygenic risk stratification approaches.

Statistical analyses

Four dietary pattern scores (aMED, AHEI, DASH, and DII) were arranged in ascending order and categorized into quartiles. For baseline characteristics, continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using t-test. While continuous variables with abnormal distribution were expressed by median (interquartile range (IQR)), and group comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage), and comparisons between groups were conducted using Chi-square test.

We used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate the associations between each of the dietary patterns and the risk of incident kidney cancer. Results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual method and was satisfied (p > 0.05). In this study, 3 models were constructed for different sets of covariates: model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, education, ethnicity, income and TDI, and model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, BMI, physical activity and sleep duration. The cumulative incidence of kidney cancer throughout the follow-up was estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Moreover, Gray’s test was employed to evaluate differences in cumulative incidence among the different dietary quartile groups. To investigate the dose-response relationships, we conducted two complementary analyses. Firstly, to test for a linear trend across quartiles of each dietary pattern, we created an ordinal variable by assigning the integer values 1, 2, 3, and 4 to participants in the first, second, third, and fourth quartiles, respectively [32]. This variable was then modeled as a continuous term in the multivariable Cox model (Model 3). Secondly, to flexibly model potential non-linear associations between the continuous dietary pattern scores and kidney cancer risk, we used restricted cubic splines (RCS) within a Cox proportional hazards regression model adjusted for the covariates in model 3. This method allows the shape of the association to be determined by the data without imposing a strict linear assumption. The HRs and 95% CIs were estimated across the range of each score, using the median value of the score as the reference point (HR = 1.0). A likelihood ratio test was used to assess for non-linearity by comparing the model with both linear and non-linear spline terms to a model with only the linear term. These analyses were conducted using the “rcssci” package (V.0.4.0) in R [33].

To evaluate the association between PRS and kidney cancer risk, we employed a Cox proportional hazards regression model. Subsequently, to investigate whether the association between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk was modified by genetic susceptibility, we tested for both additive and multiplicative interactions [34]. This was a pre-specified secondary objective of our study. We assessed interaction on the multiplicative scale using the likelihood ratio test, comparing the goodness-of-fit of a model with the main effects and the interaction term to a model with only the main effects. Furthermore, we assessed interaction on the additive scale, which is often considered more relevant for public health as it evaluates the extent to which two exposures acting together result in an excess risk beyond the sum of their individual effects. We calculated two standard measures of additive interaction: the Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI) and the Attributable Proportion due to Interaction (AP), with 95% CIs derived using the delta method. A positive RERI ( > 0) indicates that the combined effect is greater than the sum of the individual effects (synergism), while a negative RERI ( < 0) suggests the combined effect is less than the sum (antagonism). The AP quantifies the proportion of the risk in doubly exposed individuals that is attributable to the interaction itself, providing a direct measure of its public health impact [35, 36].

To explore potential biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk, we conducted a formal mediation analysis. 28 biochemical indicators were selected as potential mediators based on an extensive review of literature implicating several core biological processes in the pathogenesis of kidney cancer. These processes include inflammation and immune function (e.g., C-reactive protein), oxidative stress, metabolic health (e.g., lipids, glucose, insulin-like growth factor-1), kidney function (e.g., creatinine, urea, cystatin C), and hormonal regulation (e.g., sex hormone-binding globulin) [37, 38]. This approach, consistent with a systems biology perspective, allowed us to systematically investigate how diet might influence kidney cancer risk through multiple interconnected physiological processes. Importantly, these selected biomarkers are routinely measured in clinical practice, making them highly relevant for translation. If confirmed as significant mediators, they could serve as valuable targets for monitoring and intervention in kidney cancer prevention strategies. Notably, our study design benefits from a clear temporal sequence that supports the hypothesized causal pathway, as the dietary data (exposure) were collected at baseline (2009–2012), while the biochemical indicators (potential mediators) were measured at a subsequent follow-up visit (2016–2017), preceding the diagnosis of kidney cancer. The analysis followed a two-step process [39]. First, we assessed the associations of dietary patterns with each potential mediator using multivariable linear regression. Second, we evaluated the associations of the mediators with kidney cancer risk using Cox proportional hazards models. Finally, formal mediation analyses were conducted using the “mediation” package (V.4.5.0) in R to estimate the proportion of the total effect mediated by each indicator.

To enhance the robustness of our findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) Excluding diagnoses of kidney cancer within the first 2 years of follow-up to mitigate potential reverse causality; (2) Further adjusting for chronic diseases associated with kidney cancer risk (such as diabetes and hypertension); (3) Further adjusting for regular non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) use (such as ibuprofen and aspirin), given their nephrotoxic effects with prolonged use, and several studies have reported the association between NSAIDs use and higher risk of kidney cancer [40, 41]; (4) Employing sub-distribution hazard regression models to address potential bias arising from competing events, where kidney cancer incidence was considered as the primary event of interest, while death or loss to follow-up was treated as a competing event [42]; (5) Further adjusting for the days of the week the dietary information was collected to address the potential influence of day-to-day dietary variability; (6) Including only participants with 3 or more diet assessments (N = 66,720); (7) Performing leave-one-out analyses for each of the four dietary patterns. In this analysis, we systematically removed one food or nutrient group at a time from each index to evaluate whether our results were driven by any single component of the dietary scores; (8) Constructing subgroup analyses stratified by all covariates in model 3, and multiplicative interactions between dietary pattern scores and covariates were tested; (9) To formally assess the potential for selection bias arising from our inclusion criterion (completion of ≥ 2 dietary assessments), we compared a comprehensive set of baseline demographic and lifestyle characteristics between the included participants and those who completed only one dietary assessment. To avoid misleading conclusions from p-values in this large-scale cohort, we used standardized mean differences (SMDs) to quantify the magnitude of any differences. A conventional threshold of SMD < 0.1 was used to indicate a negligible difference between groups.

All statistical analyses were performed by R software version 4.4.0. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In multivariable-adjusted models (Fig. 2), higher scores on the AHEI score (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99) and DASH score (HR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99) were significantly associated with a lower risk of kidney cancer. Genetic risk and its joint effect with dietary patterns on kidney cancer risk

In the fully adjusted model, per SD increase in PRS was associated with a 111% higher risk of incident kidney cancer (HR = 2.11, 1.69–2.63, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S11). Furthermore, compared to low genetic risk group, participants with high genetic risk exhibited a significantly higher risk of kidney cancer (HR = 2.28, 1.77–2.94, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S11). Moreover, RCS analysis suggested a linear relationship between PRS and kidney cancer risk, with no significant deviation from linearity (p for nonlinearity = 0.069; Supplementary Fig. S4).

In the joint analysis, we observed significant additive interactions between genetic risk and both AHEI and DII (Supplementary Table S12). The RERI and AP values for additive interaction between AHEI and genetic risk on kidney cancer risk were −0.92 (95% CI: −1.80, −0.04) and −0.53 (95% CI: −1.04, −0.02). This indicates a significant antagonistic interaction, suggesting that in the absence of low AHEI and high PRS scenario, the incidence of kidney cancer would decrease by 53%. Similarly, the RERI and AP values for additive interaction between DII and genetic risk on kidney cancer risk were 0.86 (95% CI: 0.11, 1.62) and 0.30 (95% CI: 0.05, 0.54). This signifies a synergistic interaction, which meant a 30% reduction in kidney cancer incidence if the scenario of high DII and high PRS did not exist. However, no significant multiplicative interaction was observed between 4 dietary patterns and genetic risk (p = 0.821, p = 0.128, p = 0.425, and p = 0.245). Moreover, stratified analyses by genetic risk revealed graded joint associations between dietary patterns and genetic risk on kidney cancer risk. Mediation analyses and sensitivity analyses

As demonstrated in Fig. S5, mediation analysis identified several biomarkers as potential mediators of the associations between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk. For the AHEI score, urate, urea, cystatin C, creatinine as well as sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) were identified as potential mediators, with estimated mediation proportions ranging from 1.45% to 7.56%. Similarly, urate, urea, cystatin C, and creatinine appeared to mediate the association between DASH score and kidney cancer risk, with mediation proportions ranging from 2.77% to 8.40%. For DII score, urate, cystatin C, and creatinine were identified as potential mediators, with mediation proportions ranging from 0.76% to 4.56%. In contrast, no significant mediator was identified for aMED score. After FDR correction, only urate, cystatin C, and creatinine retained significant mediating effects in the association between DII score and kidney cancer risk. Detailed results of mediation analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S13. Furthermore, results of linear regression analyses between 4 dietary pattern scores and 28 biochemical indicators as well as the Cox regression analyses examining the associations between biochemical indicators and kidney cancer risk are presented in Supplementary Tables S14-S15, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses provided strong support for the robustness of the associations observed between 4 dietary pattern scores and kidney cancer risk. To be specific, after excluding outcome events occurred within 2 years of follow-up, the associations remained unchanged (Supplementary Table S16). These findings also remained stable after additional adjustment for chronic diseases associated with kidney cancer (including hypertension and diabetes) (Supplementary Table S17) and NSAIDs use (Supplementary Table S18). In addition, to address the potential bias introduced by competing events (such as death), Fine-Gray models were further applied to perform competing risk analyses. The results were consistent with those obtained from initial analyses using Cox proportional hazards models (Supplementary Table S19). Furthermore, the associations between the four dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk were materially unchanged when we restricted the analysis to participants with three or more dietary assessments (Supplementary Table S21) or further adjusted for the days of dietary data collection (Supplementary Table S20). When excluding each main component of each dietary pattern score individually, we detected no statistically significant changes in the associations, except nuts, vegetables and whole grains for aMED; sugar-sweetened beverages and vegetables for AHEI; vegetables for DASH, indicating that these food groups had greater impacts on kidney cancer risk, and they are indispensable in assessing corresponding dietary patterns (Supplementary Tables S22–S25). In subgroup analyses, the associations between 4 dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk were consistently observed across subgroups stratified by age, TDI, sleep duration, gender, ethnicity, education, household income, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, and drinking status (Supplementary Fig. S6–S9). The associations between 4 dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk were seldom modified by these factors (p for interaction > 0.05). We only observed significant interactions between aMED and sleep duration as well as AHEI and sleep duration on kidney cancer risk (P for interaction < 0.05).

Discussion

Discussion of principal findings

In this prospective analysis of participants from the UK Biobank, we observed significant inverse associations between adherence to aMED, AHEI and DASH dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk. Conversely, a pro-inflammatory diet, as indicated by a higher DII score, was positively associated with an elevated risk of kidney cancer. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our findings. Furthermore, we demonstrated that a high genetic predisposition was a strong, independent risk factor, conferring a 2.28-fold risk of kidney cancer (HR = 2.28, 1.77–2.94). Among individuals with high genetic risk, we observed significant additive interactions between AHEI and genetic risk, as well as DII and genetic risk, with kidney cancer risk progressively decreasing as AHEI score increased (or DII score decreased) and genetic risk diminished. Our mediation analysis also identified urate, urea, cystatin C, creatinine and SHBG as potential mediators in the association between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk. This suggested that these biomarkers could serve as valuable indicators for assessing the efficacy of dietary interventions. Consistent with previous studies, our findings indicate that a healthy dietary pattern, characterized by the consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, and legumes, etc. is associated with decreased risk of kidney cancer [11]. These protective effects may be attributed to the capacity of such diets to mitigate systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, reduce blood pressure, and improve renal function [43, 44].

The compelling associations observed between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk are biologically plausible and grounded in the well-established metabolic etiology of RCC [45–47]. RCC pathogenesis is intrinsically linked to profound metabolic dysregulation [48]. Hallmark alterations include the dysregulation of the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway, often initiated by von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene mutations, driving aberrant angiogenesis and a shift towards aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) [49, 50]. Crucially, systemic metabolic disturbances significantly fuel RCC development [51, 52]. Furthermore, obesity, a major risk factor, fosters a chronic inflammatory state, insulin resistance, and the dysregulation of adipokines and growth factors, collectively promoting tumorigenesis [53]. Additionally, hypertension and dyslipidemia further contribute to renal damage and the creation of an oncogenic microenvironment [54]. Within this context of metabolic dysfunction, diet serves as a primary modifiable environmental factor capable of profoundly influencing these interconnected metabolic axes [55]. It directly modulates energy balance, nutrient-sensing pathways, gut microbiota composition and metabolites, systemic inflammation, and redox balance [56]. Therefore, the observed protective effects of aMED, AHEI, and DASH, and the detrimental effect of DII may be attributable to their collective ability to modulate metabolic derangements, reduce oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, and favorably regulate the renal microenvironment.

In our study, a higher aMED score was associated with a significant protective effect against kidney cancer (HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.54–0.94). The aMED diet, which emphasizes abundant fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and healthy fats, while limiting red and processed meats, has been robustly linked to reduced risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and CKD, along with improved prognosis [22, 57, 58]. Based on existing evidence, we postulate that aMED dietary pattern may reduce kidney cancer risk through several interrelated mechanisms. Firstly, the abundance of antioxidants (such as vitamins C, vitamins E, and β-carotene) in aMED components may mitigate oxidative stress. This, in turn, can help regulate metabolic abnormalities, attenuate inflammatory responses, and improve the renal microenvironments, potentially slowing chronic kidney disease progression and subsequent carcinogenesis [57, 59]. Secondly, this dietary pattern is known to ameliorate established risk factors of kidney cancer including obesity, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome [60, 61]. Notably, a Singapore Chinese cohort study revealed a significant interaction between BMI and aMED in influencing kidney cancer risk, with more pronounced protective effect among individuals with BMI ≥ 27.5 [62]. Furthermore, aMED adherence correlates with improved blood pressure control and metabolic parameters, indirectly reducing kidney cancer risk [63, 64]. Collectively, these pathways suggest that aMED dietary pattern may lower the kidney cancer incidence by alleviating oxidative stress and favorably modulating key metabolic risk factors. Future studies should aim to validate these effects across diverse populations and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms to inform novel prevention strategies.

AHEI dietary pattern serves as a quantifiable dietary standard designed to promote healthy eating habits and mitigate chronic disease risk [62]. Similar to aMED, AHEI emphasizes the consumption of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts, and legumes while restricting intake of red meat, processed meat products, sugars, and trans-fats [65]. A key strength of AHEI lies in its adaptability—it can be tailored to regional and cultural dietary practices. For instance, the AHEI-Taiwan is a localized version developed specifically for Taiwanese dietary patterns, enhancing its regional applicability and practical utility [66]. This flexibility facilitates the global adoption of AHEI, enabling personalized dietary guidance across diverse populations. A previous study demonstrated that kidney transplant recipients often had a higher risk of kidney cancer development, and a high AHEI score was found to exert a protective effect against kidney cancer, which is consistent with our findings [67]. Mechanistically, the high-fiber components of AHEI promote the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites (e.g., acetate and butyrate) by gut microbiota, which may modulate renal inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby preserving kidney function and reducing malignant transformation [68, 69]. Additionally, the restriction of red meat intake reduces the risk of kidney cancer by eliminating a potential risk factor [70, 71]. In this study, besides protective effect on kidney cancer risk (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99), we also identified a significant antagonistic interaction between AHEI and high genetic risk (RERI = −0.92, 95% CI: −1.80～-0.04; AP = −0.53, 95% CI: −1.04~−0.02). As is well established, the development of kidney cancer is a result of the interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors [72, 73]. Our findings powerfully illustrate this principle, indicating that high adherence to AHEI-compliant diet can substantially mitigate the kidney cancer risk conferred by high genetic susceptibility. This provides crucial insights for targeted prevention, suggesting that dietary interventions could be particularly effective for individuals identified as being at high genetic risk.

The DASH diet, a dietary pattern aimed at reducing hypertension, is characterized by a high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products, while restricting the consumption of sodium, red meat, sugars, and high-fat foods [74]. Although it shares a similar food structure and health-promoting goals with both aMED and AHEI diets, it differs in its specific food choices and scoring criteria. Unlike aMED’s focus on Mediterranean-specific components like olive oil and seafood, or AHEI’s broader health-oriented approach prioritizing fiber and whole grains, the DASH diet specifically targets blood pressure control through sodium restriction, potassium enhancement, and reduced fat intake [75, 76]. Mechanistically, DASH dietary pattern is thought to lower kidney cancer risk primarily by reducing blood pressure, thereby alleviating the burden on kidney and protecting renal function. This, in turn, slows the progression of CKD and reduces the risk of kidney cancer [62, 77]. Moreover, the rich content of fruits, vegetables, grains, and nuts in the DASH diet also exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which reduce kidney cancer risk [78]. Notably, our study helps to fill a significant research gap. While the blood pressure-lowering effects of DASH are well-established, few studies have explored its specific association with reduced kidney cancer risk. Our findings provide important evidence in a large prospective cohort, underscoring the need for further research in multiethnic populations to validate these observations and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

DII dietary pattern serves as a standardized tool designed to quantify the inflammatory potential of an individual’s diet, with accumulating studies demonstrating its significant associations with various chronic conditions including metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and malignancies [79, 80]. Specifically for kidney cancer, multiple epidemiological studies have consistently shown a positive correlation between DII and elevated kidney cancer risk. A Chinese prospective cohort study revealed that compared with the lowest DII group, the highest DII group had a 38% increased risk of kidney cancer (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.03–1.84) [81]. Similarly, a Swedish cohort study suggested that anti-inflammatory dietary patterns (indicative of a lower DII score) was protective against kidney cancer (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.52–0.89), particularly among female participants (HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.30–0.75) [82]. These findings are further supported by a meta-analysis demonstrating a significant dose-response relationship between DII and kidney cancer risk, with each one-unit increase in DII corresponding to a 45% increase in kidney cancer risk (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.14–1.83) [83]. In our study, DII was found to be associated with elevated risk of kidney cancer (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03–1.15), which is consistent with previous studies. A more detailed examination of this association revealed a statistically significant positive linear trend (p for trend = 0.020), suggesting a dose-response relationship where a more pro-inflammatory diet is associated with a progressively higher risk. While the direct comparison between the highest and lowest quartiles did not reach statistical significance, this is not necessarily contradictory. The test for trend is often more statistically powerful as it utilizes data from all participants across all four quartiles to assess a consistent, monotonic relationship [84]. The lack of significance in the extreme quartile comparison may simply reflect insufficient power for that specific contrast, whereas the overall dose-response trend across the entire cohort remains a robust and biologically plausible finding. Furthermore, a significant additive interaction between DII score and high genetic risk was observed (RERI = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.11–1.62; AP = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.05, 0.54). Mechanistically, chronic inflammation is recognized as a critical driver in the initiation and progression of kidney cancer and other malignancies [85]. Prolonged inflammatory responses alter the renal tissue microenvironment, disrupting normal cellular function while fostering conditions conducive to tumor initiation and progression [85]. Chronic inflammation promotes cell proliferation and angiogenesis in kidney through multiple pathways: (1) inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) activate key oncogenic signaling cascades, enhancing tumor cell survival and proliferation [86, 87]; (2) sustained inflammation induces neovascularization, further supporting tumor growth [86]; and (3) dynamic crosstalk between tumor cells and immune cells, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). For instance, tumor-secreted chemokines recruit macrophages into the tumor niche, where they often exhibit M2 polarization phenotype, thereby accelerating tumor progression and metastatic potential [88]. Our findings thereby provide strong evidence that individuals with high genetic risk should prioritize anti-inflammatory dietary patterns over pro-inflammatory (high-DII) diets to mitigate kidney cancer risk.

This study provides valuable insights into the current limited evidence linking dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk, particularly in European populations. We demonstrate that adherence to aMED, AHEI, and DASH dietary patterns is associated with a reduced risk of kidney cancer, whereas DII is linked to an increased risk. Thus, tailoring dietary practices according to these patterns may aid in the primary prevention and early detection of kidney cancer. In addition, urate, urea, cystatin C, creatinine and SHBG as potential mediators suggest these biomarkers could be used to monitor the effectiveness of the dietary interventions. Moreover, from the public health perspective, our findings advocate for policies that integrate dietary guidelines with culturally sensitive education and community-based interventions. Rather than narrowly focusing on isolated food categories or nutrients, these policies should emphasize the promotion of holistic dietary patterns that align with individual preferences, cultural traditions, and socioeconomic contexts. This could include public awareness campaigns, school-based nutrition programs, and collaborations with local food providers to make healthy choices more accessible. By shifting the discourse from restrictive dietary rules to sustainable and inclusive eating habits, such strategies are more likely to improve long-term health outcomes and foster greater public adherence.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. Firstly, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic investigation into the associations among various dietary patterns, genetic risk, and kidney cancer risk. Secondly, prospective design, long follow-up, use of evidence-based dietary indices, and data with high diagnostic reliability enhance the rigor and reliability of our study, positioning it as a high-level evidence source. Thirdly, we integrated GWAS data with individual-level data from the UK Biobank, and constructed PRS to elucidate the interplay between dietary patterns and genetic risk in kidney cancer onset. Finally, the inclusion of mediation analyses provided initial insights into potential biological pathways, and the robustness of our findings was strengthened by a comprehensive set of sensitivity analyses.

Nevertheless, some limitations cannot be ignored. Firstly, while our prospective cohort design minimizes the potential for reverse causality and we have adjusted for a comprehensive set of known risk factors, it is crucial to emphasize that our findings should not be interpreted as establishing a definitive causal link between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk [89]. The possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors persists [90, 91]. For instance, variables such as the use of specific over-the-counter medications, detailed environmental or occupational exposures, or more granular socioeconomic indicators not fully captured by our covariates could potentially influence the observed associations [92–94]. Therefore, confirming the causality of these associations will require further investigation using different strategies. Future research, including randomized controlled trials and mendelian randomization studies, will be essential to corroborate our findings and establish causality [95, 96].

Secondly, although significant associations between AHEI (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99) as well as DASH (HR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99) and kidney cancer were observed, the hazard ratios were close to 1.0, indicating that the effects were relatively small. However, it is crucial to interpret these findings within a public health context. Even a small reduction in relative risk, when applied to a ubiquitous and modifiable exposure like diet, can translate into a substantial reduction in the absolute number of cancer cases at the population level [97, 98]. Furthermore, while the associations for the continuous AHEI and DASH scores were modest, the comparisons between the highest and lowest quartiles for the aMED (HR = 0.71) and DASH (HR = 0.67) patterns revealed more substantial risk reductions of approximately 30%. Most importantly, the public health relevance of these dietary patterns is significantly amplified in genetically susceptible individuals. Our interaction analysis demonstrates that the protective effects of a healthy diet are pronounced in those at the highest genetic risk for kidney cancer. This highlights that dietary intervention is a particularly powerful and practical prevention strategy for this high-risk subgroup, representing a key finding for personalized public health and precision nutrition.

Moreover, we have to acknowledge that the use of self-reported 24-hour dietary recalls is subject to inherent limitations, including potential recall bias and significant day-to-day variability in food intake [99]. While we sought to mitigate this by averaging intake from at least two recalls to better represent habitual diet, this approach improves reliability but cannot entirely eliminate measurement error. However, to rigorously test the stability of our findings against this variability, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with three or more dietary assessments and further adjusted for days of dietary data collection. The results were consistent with our primary analysis, which strongly bolsters the robustness of our conclusions (Supplementary Table S20 and S21).

Furthermore, we acknowledge that restricting our analytical cohort to participants who completed at least two dietary assessments could introduce selection bias [100]. To rigorously investigate this possibility, we compared a wide range of baseline characteristics between the included and excluded groups. Recognizing that traditional significance testing is often uninformative in large cohorts, we instead quantified the magnitude of differences using standardized mean differences (SMDs) [101]. Our analysis provides strong evidence that any selection bias is likely minimal; the baseline profiles of the two groups were highly comparable, with SMDs for nearly all key covariates falling well below the negligible threshold of 0.1 (Supplementary Table S10). While a small difference was observed for education level (SMD = 0.151), a common finding in studies requiring longitudinal participation, this minor imbalance was statistically controlled for in our main multivariable-adjusted models. Therefore, it is unlikely that selection bias arising from our inclusion criteria materially affected our findings.

Another limitation is the potential for under- or over-estimation of certain food groups or nutrients, which is common in self-reported dietary data [102, 103]. Besides, the UK Biobank’s food composition database may not capture every component needed to calculate each dietary index with perfect precision (e.g., specific spices for the DII score). This type of non-differential misclassification would most likely bias our results toward the null, suggesting that the true associations may be even stronger than what we observed [104]. To address the concern that our findings might be disproportionately influenced by a single component, we performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each dietary pattern. While the overall associations generally remained robust, it is noteworthy that the exclusion of certain key components—such as vegetables, nuts, whole grains, and sugar-sweetened beverages—did attenuate the associations for the aMED, AHEI, and DASH (Supplementary Tables S22–S25). These findings do not invalidate the dietary pattern approach; on the contrary, they reinforce the biological importance of these specific food groups. It suggests that while the overall dietary pattern is crucial, these components are indispensable and likely the most potent drivers of the observed protective effects. Therefore, our results indicate that the associations are attributable to the holistic dietary patterns, with these specific food groups playing a particularly critical role in the risk reduction for kidney cancer.

A further consideration is the statistical power required to detect gene-diet interactions. We acknowledge that tests for interaction are often underpowered compared to tests for main effects, and null findings should be interpreted with caution [105, 106]. A common rule of thumb suggests that detecting an interaction requires a sample size approximately four times larger than that needed to detect a main effect of a similar magnitude [107]. A major strength of our study is the large sample size of the UK Biobank cohort, with 113,594 participants. This provided us with substantial statistical power, lending greater confidence to both our significant findings for additive interaction and our null findings for multiplicative interaction.

For our mediation analysis, it is important to interpret the findings with caution, as the results should be considered suggestive and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory of causal pathways [108, 109]. Causal mediation analysis in an observational setting relies on a set of strong, and largely untestable, assumptions [110]. The most critical of these is the assumption of no unmeasured confounding for the exposure-outcome, exposure-mediator, and particularly the mediator-outcome relationships [111]. Although we adjusted for a wide range of covariates, we cannot rule out the possibility that unmeasured factors (e.g., specific genetic predispositions or environmental exposures) could still confound the association between the biochemical indicators and kidney cancer risk [112]. However, a key strength of our analysis is the established temporal sequence, with dietary data collected years before the biochemical mediators were measured. This design feature provides strong support for the hypothesized direction of causality from exposure to mediator, strengthening the plausibility of our findings. Nevertheless, due to the critical assumption regarding unmeasured confounding, these results primarily serve to highlight plausible biological pathways that warrant further investigation in future mechanistic studies.

Finally, a key limitation of our study pertains to the generalizability of its findings. The UK Biobank cohort, while large, is not fully representative of the general population. It consists predominantly of individuals of European ancestry and is known to exhibit a “healthy volunteer bias,” meaning participants are, on average, healthier, of higher socioeconomic status, and less likely to engage in high-risk behaviors compared to the broader UK population [113]. This lack of ethnic diversity and the selection bias may limit the external validity of our results. The magnitude of the associations we observed between dietary patterns and kidney cancer risk might differ in other ethnic groups due to variations in genetic backgrounds, unmeasured lifestyle factors, and culturally specific dietary habits [114, 115]. Moreover, the polygenic risk score (PRS) used in this study was derived from a GWAS conducted predominantly in individuals of European ancestry, and there is substantial evidence that its predictive performance is significantly attenuated when applied to non-European populations, such as those of African or Asian descent [116, 117]. This well-documented issue arises from differences in allele frequencies, LD patterns, and potential gene-environment interactions across diverse ancestral groups [118]. However, it is important to note that the ancestry of the PRS discovery sample is well-matched to our study cohort. This alignment strengthens the internal validity of our risk estimation and gene-diet interaction analyses within this specific context, as it minimizes potential biases from population stratification. Nevertheless, this underscores that our findings, particularly the gene-diet interactions, should not be extrapolated to other populations. The development and validation of ancestry-specific PRS are urgently needed to ensure equitable application in precision medicine and to avoid exacerbating health disparities. Therefore, while our findings provide robust evidence within this specific cohort, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to more diverse or general populations. Future studies in multi-ethnic and more representative cohorts are crucial to validate and extend our findings.

Future directions

Our findings open several important avenues for future research. Firstly, there is a critical need for replication and validation of our findings in other large, prospective cohorts, particularly those with greater ethnic diversity, to confirm the generalizability of these diet-cancer and gene-diet associations. Secondly, to move from association to causation, future studies should employ designs that can provide stronger causal evidence. These include interventional trials, such as randomized controlled trials of specific dietary patterns in high-risk populations, and advanced observational methods like mendelian randomization to investigate the causal role of specific dietary components. Finally, the suggestive findings from our mediation analysis highlight the need for dedicated mechanistic experiments to elucidate the precise biological pathways—such as those involving urate and cystatin C—through which diet modulates kidney cancer risk.

Conclusion

In conclusion, high adherence to aMED, AHEI, and DASH dietary patterns is associated with a lower risk of kidney cancer, whereas high adherence to DII dietary pattern is associated with greater risk, particularly in high-genetic-risk populations. Biochemical indicators such as urea, cystatin C, and creatinine may serve as potential mediators of the aforementioned associations. Overall, these findings highlight the critical importance of healthy dietary patterns for kidney cancer prevention, especially in genetically susceptible populations, suggesting that prioritizing these diets is a promising public health strategy for reducing kidney cancer risk.
