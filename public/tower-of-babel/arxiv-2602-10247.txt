Discretization-free Bayesian inverse problems in distribution spaces
D Calvetti, E Somersalo

Abstract
The Bayesian approach to inverse problems provides a practical way to solve ill-posed problems by augmenting the observation model with prior information. Due to the measure-theoretic underpinnings, the approach has raised theoretical interest, leading to a rather comprehensive description in infinite-dimensional function spaces. The goal of this article is to bridge the infinite-dimensional theory for linear inverse problems in distribution spaces and associated computational inverse problems without resorting to a discrete approximation of the forward model. We will shown that under certain assumptions, discretization of the unknown of interest is not necessary for the numerical treatment of the problem, the only approximations required being numerical quadratures that are independent of any discrete representation of the unknown. An analysis of the connection between the proposed approach and discretization-based ones is also provided.



1
Introduction

In most inverse problems the goal is to find a meaningful estimate of an unknown and unobservable quantity of interest based on noisy measurements of a related quantity that allows a direct observation. The bridge between the unknown of interest and the observed quantity is provided by the mathematical forward model, often referred to as a causal or predictive model. Thus the challenges of inverse problems arise from the ill-posed nature of inferring hidden causes from observed consequences. In the Bayesian interpretation, unknown quantities are modeled as random variables, the randomness representing epistemic uncertainty about their values as well as model uncertainties. A priori beliefs about the unknown of interest are encoded in the prior probability distribution, while the forward model with observation errors and model uncertainties constitute the likelihood model: the posterior distribution obtained by Bayes’ formula represent the solution to the inverse problem.

Since the seminal work of Tarantola and Valette
[
19
]
in early 80s, Bayesian methods have become one of the mainstream approaches to inverse problems
[
20
,
11
,
1
,
3
]
. The approach is particularly appealing because it naturally bridges classical inversion theory and uncertainty quantification
[
2
]
. While being fundamentally a practical approach to explicitly compute solutions in finite dimensional spaces, the measure theoretical underpinnings of the Bayesian approach have raised also significant interest in its formalism in the infinite dimensional function spaces where most physics-based models of inverse problems are originally formulated
[
7
,
14
,
13
,
18
,
6
]
. A key question that has motivated much of the work in the infinite dimensional framework is whether and how the discretization, seen as an inevitable step for practical computations, affects the inverse problems theory. As stated by Stuart in
[
18
]
, the aim of the Hilbert space theory of inverse problems and its extensions to Banach spaces was to
avoid discretization until the last possible moment.
A similar motivation inspired
[
13
]
, where the Bayesian theory for Gaussian measures was developed in distribution spaces. The cited works, and numerous follow-up articles contribute to our understanding of inverse problems in the infinite dimensional setting. In particular, one of the major motivations to develop the infinite dimensional theory of the Bayesian inversion is to understand the limit behavior of the analysis as the dimensionality of the problem grows, with the infinite dimensional model representing an ideal target of the limiting process.
In particular, the analysis has led, e.g., to better converging sampling algorithms through the insight in the curse of dimensionality
[
5
]
.
However, a gap between the practical Bayesian computing and the theoretically exact infinite-dimensional models continues to exist even in the most thoroughly understood case of linear inverse problems with Gaussian prior and likelihood model. In fact, the infinite dimensional theory provides closed form solutions for the posterior mean and covariance operators in the function spaces, however, these formulas typically cannot be used in practical calculations without restating the problem in
discretized and truncated form.

In this article, we revisit Gaussian linear inverse problems in infinite dimensional setting, modeling unknown quantities of interest as generalized random variables, i.e., random variables taking on values in the space of distributions equipped with the weak topology and
\sigma
-algebra spanned by cylinder sets. Measurements are defined through dualities with test functions. We highlight that in this context
there is no need to discretize random variables
, e.g., by represent them in any basis spanning a function space. We postulate that while the observable is defines as a distribution, the data of inverse problems are a finite dimensional measurement of the observed random variable. The solution of the inverse problem is a probability distribution of a generalized random variable. To probe the random variable of interest, any finite set of test functions can be selected, and the probability distribution of the corresponding finite measurement can be computed. It will be shown that, under the Gaussian linear assumptions, the computational inverse problem can be completely solved without any discretization of the unknown of interest. Moreover, unlike in the basis-based Hilbert space theory, if the set of test functions probing the unknown of interest changes, the formulas for the mean and covariance of the posterior distribution make it possible to solve the modified problems without the need of restating the problem and carrying out the analysis from scratch. The passage of information from a finite measurement, i.e., the data, to any choice of finite measurement of the unknown of interest occurs through a finite dimensional correlation matrix that can be approximated numerically by quadratures, with no reference to any preselected basis of functions. It is in this sense that the theory developed here is discretization free, and devoid of any truncation errors due to basis selections.

and finally in subsection

2
Measurements as dual evaluations

We start by outlining the general setting of this work.
We denote by
U
the topological vector space of test functions, e.g., the set of rapidly decreasing
C^{\infty}
test functions
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
in the Euclidean space, or
C^{\infty}
-functions vanishing outside a compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, or periodic
C^{\infty}
-functions over the unit circle.
We denote by
H
the dual space of continuous linear functionals on
U
, consisting of the corresponding
distributions or generalized functions.
The test function space is equipped by the Fréchet topology, and it dual by the induced weak
∗
topology.
The duality between
U
and
H
is denoted by
\langle\varphi,x\rangle
, where
\varphi\in U
,
x\in H
.
Although topological considerations are not of central importance here, for the sake of making the article self-contained, some technical details are collected in the Appendix. For further details, see, e.g.,

We start with the definition of a measurement of a quantity in
H
.

Definition 2.1

Let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}
be any finite collection of test functions in
U
.
A measurement of a quantity
x\in H
through the measurement set
\Phi
is a mapping,

H\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\quad x\mapsto\Phi(x)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\langle\varphi_{1},x\rangle\\
\langle\varphi_{2},x\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle\varphi_{n},x\rangle\end{array}\right].

The general theory of inverse problems developed in the framework of Bayesian computing
[
11
,
3
]
, requires a stochastic extension of the deterministic model. To this end, let
(\Omega,{\mathfrak{S}},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space, and define
generalized random variables

[
8
]
as measurable mappings

X:\Omega\to H,

where
H
is equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra induced by the cylinder sets,

C=\big\{x\in H\mid\big(\langle u_{1},x\rangle,\ldots,\langle u_{k},x\rangle\big)\in B,\;B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\mbox{ open, }u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in U\big\},

that form the basis of the weak
∗
topology of
H
.

Of particular interest in this work are inverse problems arising from linear observation models. Let
U
and
V
be two test function spaces, and let
H=U^{*}
and
K=V^{*}
their topological duals. Consider a linear operator
A:V\to U
, and let
A^{*}:H\to K
denote its extension to the duals: For a given
x\in H
,
A^{*}x\in K
is defined through the identity

\langle v,A^{*}x\rangle=\langle Av,x\rangle,\quad v\in V\mbox{ arbitrary.}

For simplicity, we assume here that
A
maps smooth functions to smooth functions. This assumption covers a large class of operators, such as pseudodifferential operators with smooth symbols that are pseudolocal, including, e.g., convolution operators with an integrable kernel. For a generalization to cases of non-smooth operators, we refer to the Appendix.
In the classical deterministic setting, a linear inverse problem seeks to estimate a variable
x\in H
based on the noisy observation of
b\in K
,

b=A^{*}x+e,

where
e\in K
is a distribution representing the observation noise. We extend the linear model to generalized random variables, following the notational convention to use uppercase letters to refer to random variables, and lowercase letters to their realizations. Thus the stochastic extension of the above equation can be written as

B=A^{*}X+E,

(1)

where
B
and
E
are
K
-valued generalized random variables, and
X
is an
H
-valued random variable. We formalize the concept of a finite-dimensional measurement and the corresponding inverse problem formulation in the following definition.

Definition 2.2

(a)

A finite-dimensional direct measurement through a measurement set
\Phi=\big\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\big\}
of a generalized random variable
X
is defined as the random variable

M:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\quad M=\Phi(X)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\langle\varphi_{1},X\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle\varphi_{n},X\rangle\end{array}\right].

(b)

Given the forward model (
1
), the indirect noisy measurement of
X
is any direct measurement
of the generalized random variable
B
,

Z:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\quad Z=\Psi(B)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\langle\psi_{1},y\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle\psi_{m},y\rangle\end{array}\right],

where
\Psi=\big\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}\big\}\subset V
is a given measurement set.

(c)

The generalized linear inverse problem is to estimate any direct measurement
M=\Phi(X)
of
X
based on the given indirect measurement
Z=\Psi(B)
, where
X
and
B
are related to each other through the equation (
1
).

The above definition aims at keeping the approach practical in the sense that the data are finite, although defined in terms of infinite dimensional model, and the solution
X
is interrogated through arbitrary measurement sets, allowing the passage to an infinite dimensional limit, if necessary or desired.

In the following section, we analyze the problem in the Bayesian framework, summarizing some central ideas, and briefly revisit the infinite-dimensional theory. Furthermore, we examine in more detail the case of Gaussian random variables, as the Gaussian setting is better suited for a detailed analysis of the differences between models based on discretization and the proposed discretization-free framework.

3
Inverse problems in the Bayesian framework

In this section we present a brief overview of the finite dimensional theory of linear inverse problems with Gaussian densities and a review of its extension to Hilbert spaces. We then develop the formalism for the current approach.

3.1
Gaussian models in the traditional setting: a review

The Bayesian solution to the inverse problem (
1
) is the posterior probability distribution of
X
conditioned on
B=b
, where
b
is the observed datum. Assuming that the random variables are finite dimensional with Euclidean state spaces,
X:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
B,E:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and that their probability distributions are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, the distributions can be expressed in terms of probability densities. According to Bayes’ formula for probability densities, the posterior density is proportional to the product of the prior and the likelihood densities,

\pi_{X\mid B}(x\mid b)\propto\pi_{X}(x)\pi_{B\mid X}(b\mid x).

(2)

Assuming a finite dimensional version of the linear relation (
1
), the operator
A
being defined as a matrix
{\mathsf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
with the adjoint operator
A^{*}
defined by the matrix transpose
{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
and independence of
X
and
E
, the likelihood is obtained by a shift of the noise density,

\pi_{B\mid X}(b\mid x)=\pi_{E}(b-{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}x).

(3)

Furthermore, if
X
and
E
are mutually independent and normally distributed,

X\sim{\mathcal{N}}(x_{0},{\mathsf{\Gamma}}),\quad E\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\mathsf{\Sigma}}),

where for simplicity we assumed vanishing mean of the noise, and the covariance matrices
{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
are symmetric and positive definite (SPD), it is well-known that the posterior density is Gaussian,

X\mid(B=b)\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\overline{x},{\mathsf{C}}),

(4)

where the posterior mean and covariance matrix are given in two equivalent ways by

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{T}}*+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left({\mathsf{\Gamma}}^{-1}x_{0}+{\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}^{-1}b\right)=x_{0}+{\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}}\big({\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}}+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\big)^{-1}b,

(5)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}={\mathsf{\Gamma}}-{\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}}\big({\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}}+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\big)^{-1}{\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}},

(6)

the equivalence of the two alternative formulas being a direct consequence of the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula. Observe that the expressions for the posterior mean and covariance look slightly non-standard, as the forward map in (
1
) is defined in terms of
{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the transpose of
{\mathsf{A}}
.

The above formulas are a direct consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Let
Z:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
B:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be two multivariate zero mean Gaussian random variables, and denote by
Y
the combined random variable,

Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z\\
B\end{array}\right]:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m},\quad Y\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\mathsf{C}}),

and partition joint covariance matrix as

{\mathsf{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathsf{C}}^{11}&{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\\
{\mathsf{C}}^{21}&{\mathsf{C}}^{22}\end{array}\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},

where

{\mathsf{C}}^{11}={\mathbb{E}}\big(ZZ^{\mathsf{T}}),\quad{\mathsf{C}}^{12}={\mathbb{E}}\big(ZB^{\mathsf{T}})=\big({\mathsf{C}}^{21}\big)^{\mathsf{T}},\quad{\mathsf{C}}^{22}={\mathbb{E}}\big(BB^{\mathsf{T}}).

Then the posterior distribution of
Z
conditioned on
B=b
is Gaussian with mean and covariance

\displaystyle\overline{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\big({\mathsf{C}}^{22}\big)^{-1}b,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{11}-{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\big({\mathsf{C}}^{22}\big)^{-1}{\mathsf{C}}^{21}={\mathsf{C}}/{\mathsf{C}}^{22},

i.e., the conditional covariance is the Schur complement of
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
.

The formulas (
5
) and (
6
) follow by defining
Z=X-x_{0}
and observing that

{\mathsf{C}}^{11}={\mathsf{\Gamma}},\quad{\mathsf{C}}^{12}={\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}},\quad{\mathsf{C}}^{22}={\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}{\mathsf{A}}+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}.

The above linear Gaussian theory in finite dimensional spaces can be extended to the infinite-dimensional case. While the restriction to linear problems and Gaussian distributions is not needed and the theory covers more generally separable Banach spaces

[
18
,
6
]
, we limit our discussion here to linear models in Hilbert spaces. For a general reference to Gaussian random variables in Hilbert spaces, see
[
12
,
16
]

Assume that
A^{*}
is a continuous linear map between two separable Hilbert spaces,
A^{*}:H_{0}\to H_{1}
, adjoint to the continuous linear map
A:H_{1}\to H_{0}
. Assume further that
X:\Omega\to H_{0}
and
E:\Omega\to H_{1}
are independent and Gaussian as in (
4
). In order for these Gaussian random variables to be well-defined,
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
need to be positive definite
nuclear
, or
trace class
operators: Given an orthonormal basis
\{v_{k}\}
of
H_{0}
, we require that

{\rm Trace}(\Gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle v_{j},\Gamma v_{j}\rangle<\infty,

and similarly for
\Sigma
. As in the infinite-dimensional case, a reference to Lebesgue measure is meaningless, and the theory cannot be formulated in terms of densities as in (
2
). Denote the prior Gaussian distribution by
\mu_{X}
, and the noise distribution by
\mu_{E}
. Furthermore, denote the posterior density of
X
conditional on
B=b
by
\mu_{X\mid B}^{b}
. The posterior distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the prior, hence the likelihood density, denoted by
\pi_{X\mid B}(b\mid x)=\pi_{X\mid B}^{b}(x)
, is defined through the Radon-Nikodym derivative,

\pi_{B\mid X}(b\mid x)=\frac{d\mu^{b}_{X\mid B}}{d\mu_{0}}(x),\quad x\in H_{0}.

This formula is the infinite dimensional equivalent of the Bayes’ formula (
2
), as it implicitly defines the posterior density in terms of the prior and the likelihood.
Observe that in the finite-dimensional case, the likelihood density is given explicitly as

\pi_{B\mid X}(b\mid x)\propto{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|b-{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}x\|^{2}_{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\right),

(7)

where
\|z\|_{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{2}=z^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{-1}z
is used. In the infinite-dimensional case, formula (
7
) does not make sense, as
{\mathsf{\Sigma}}
is non-invertible. The likelihood measure
\mu_{B\mid X}
needs to be defined through the Gaussian measure
\mu_{E}
by shifting it by
A^{*}x
,

\mu_{B\mid X}(Q)=\mu_{E}(Q-A^{*}x),\quad\mbox{$Q\subset H_{1}$ measurable},

assuming that
\mu_{B\mid X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{E}
.
However, by the Cameron-Martin theorem
[
4
]
, a shifted Gaussian measure is equivalent with the original one only if the shift
A^{*}x
is in the Cameron-Martin space that has measure zero, limiting the generality of the above formula. One can show that formulas (
5
) and (
6
) have corresponding equivalents in the infinite dimensional setting, however, the infinite-dimensional equivalent of the inverse of the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
in the finite-dimensional model is no longer a bounded linear operator, but needs to be interpreted as a measurable linear transformation, see, e.g.,
[
14
,
13
]
. For further technical details of the Hilbert-space theory, we refer to
[
18
,
6
]
, and for the extension to distribution spaces, see
[
13
]
.

While it is reassuring to know that the finite dimensional theory has a consistent extension in the infinite-dimensional setting, the formulas for the infinite-dimensional random variables are not particularly useful in practice, as there is no immediate way of projecting the Gaussian posterior density to finite dimensional spaces, and in practice, the calculations always reduce to the matrix version (
5
)-(
6
) of some finite dimensional version of the problem.

One motivation to develop the present theory is to use the theory of distribution spaces while avoiding the complications in the infinite-dimensional theory, and to give a consistent finite dimensional description of the posterior density that bypasses the standard “discretize and truncate”- process.

3.2
Posterior density through dual evaluations

Consider the linear model (
1
), and a measurement of the quantity
B
by a measurement set
\Psi=\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
. We have

\Psi(B)_{j}=\langle\psi_{j},B\rangle=\langle\psi_{j},A^{*}X\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},E\rangle=\langle A\psi_{j},X\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},E\rangle,

(8)

or

\Psi(B)=(A\Psi)(X)+\Psi(E),

(9)

where
A\Psi
stands for the measurement set
A\Psi=\{A\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
. In other words, an indirect noisy measurement with the measurement set
\Psi
is equivalent to a direct noisy measurement with the measurement set
A\Psi
. Typically, for inverse problems, the measurement set
A\Psi
is not the preferred set unless we consider a simple denoising problem where
A
is the identity. The practical formulation of the linear inverse problem in the Bayesian setting can be stated as follows.

Definition 3.2

Given the measurement sets
\Psi=\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}\subset V
and
\Phi=\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset U
, find the posterior probability distribution of the
n
-variate random variable
\Phi(X)
based on the observation
\Psi(B)
given in (
9
).

In a Hilbert space setting, the problem can be formulated in terms of projections:
Estimate the projection of
X
on a finite dimensional subspace
{\rm span}(\Phi)
based on the noisy observation of its projection on the subspace
{\rm span}({\mathsf{A}}\Psi)
.
We point out that the problem is not requiring any discretization of the variables, but only a numerical approximation for computing the projections.

We shall consider now the problem in the case of Gaussian distributions, further details being given in the Appendix. Let
X
be an
H
-valued random variable. Recall that
X
is a Gaussian generalized random variable if for every
n
and every measurement set
\Phi=\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, the
n
-variate random variables
\Phi(X)
are Gaussian. The mean
\mu_{X}\in H
and covariance operator
C_{X}:U\to U
are defined by the identities

{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\varphi,X\rangle)=\langle\varphi,\mu_{X}\rangle,

{\mathbb{E}}\big((\langle\varphi,X\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu_{X}\rangle)(\langle\phi,X\rangle-\langle\phi,\mu_{X}\rangle)\big)=\langle\varphi,C_{X}\phi\rangle,

where
\varphi,\phi\in U
are arbitrary test functions. We use the standard notation
X\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mu_{X},C_{X})
. Similarly, we assume that the noise
E
is a Gaussian
K
-valued generalized random variable,
E\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,C_{E})
, where
C_{E}:V\to V
. For simplicity, assume that
X
and
E
are mutually independent. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
X
has zero mean,
\mu_{X}=0
. Adding the mean to the formulas afterwards is a straightforward matter.
The joint covariance matrix
{\mathsf{C}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
of the pair
(\Phi(X),\Psi(B))
is

{\mathsf{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathsf{C}}^{11}&{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\\
{\mathsf{C}}^{21}&{\mathsf{C}}^{22}\end{array}\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},

where

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{11}_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\varphi_{j},X\rangle\langle\varphi_{k},X\rangle)=\langle\varphi_{j},C_{X}\varphi_{k}\rangle,\quad 1\leq j,k\leq n,

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{12}_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\varphi_{j},X\rangle\langle\psi_{k},A^{*}X+E\rangle)=\langle\varphi_{j},C_{X}A\psi_{k}\rangle,\quad 1\leq j\leq n,\;1\leq,k\leq m,

(11)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{21}_{kj}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{12}_{jk},

(12)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{22}_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\psi_{j},A^{*}X+E\rangle\langle\psi_{k},A^{*}X+E\rangle)=\langle A\psi_{j},C_{X}A\psi_{k}\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},C_{E}\psi_{k}\rangle,\quad 1\leq j,k\leq m.

(13)

The posterior probability density is Gaussian,

\pi_{\Phi(X)\mid\Psi(B)}(\Phi(x)\mid\Psi(b))\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\overline{x},{\mathsf{D}}),

where

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\big({\mathsf{C}}^{22}\big)^{-1}\Psi(b),

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{11}-{\mathsf{C}}^{12}\big({\mathsf{C}}^{22}\big)^{-1}{\mathsf{C}}^{21}={\mathsf{C}}/{\mathsf{C}}^{22},

(15)

i.e., the posterior covariance is the Schur complement of
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
.

3.3
Discretization-free matrix evaluations

The numerical evaluation of the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
, that plays an important role in the characterization of the posterior, does not depend on measurement set
\Phi
. More specifically, if the forward operator
A
and the covariance operator
C_{X}
are given as integral operators, the numerical approximation of the entries
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}_{jk}
can be done by means of quadrature rules.

In particular, to estimate the unknown by the posterior mean (
14
), we may compute the vector

\widetilde{b}=\big({\mathsf{C}}^{22}\big)^{-1}\Psi(b)

(16)

before deciding which measurement set
\Phi
will be chosen to extract information about
X
.

Similarly, after the measurement set
\Phi=\big\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\big\}
has been selected, the numerical estimation of the matrices
{\mathsf{C}}^{11}
,
{\mathsf{C}}^{12}
and
{\mathsf{C}}^{21}
can be also done through quadrature rules. We summarize the results in the following observations that are clarified in practice in the next section.

1.

The numerical evaluation of the covariance matrix
{\mathsf{C}}
that defines the posterior density through equations (
14
)-(
15
) does not require the selection of a representation basis for the unknown
X
. Formulas (
14
)-(
15
) are not affected in any manner by truncation error, although their accuracy will depend on the exactness of the quadrature rules used to evaluate the integrals.

2.

If the forward model
A
and the prior covariance
C_{X}
are defined as integral operators, the numerical evaluation of the matrix can be done via a quadrature rule, and its accuracy depends on the degree of exactness of the quadrature.

3.

The dependency between the data
b
and the unknown
x
is not based on a discretized forward model, but on the cross-correlation of the stochastic extension of the model. In particular, changing the measurement set
\Phi
does not affect (
16
), but affects only the computation and application of the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{12}
.

The above analysis suggests that any linear inverse problem can be viewed as a denoising problem. Indeed, if we choose the measurement set as

\Phi=A\Psi=\big\{A\psi_{1},\ldots,A\psi_{m}\},

i.e,.
\varphi_{j}=A\psi_{j}
and
n=m
, we have

\Psi(B)_{j}=\langle A\psi_{j},X\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},E\rangle=\langle\varphi_{j},X\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},E\rangle

In this case, the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}
is given by

{\mathsf{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathsf{\Gamma}}&{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\\
{\mathsf{\Gamma}}&{\mathsf{\Gamma}}+{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\end{array}\right],

(17)

where the matrices
{\mathsf{\Gamma}},{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
are given by

{\mathsf{\Gamma}}_{jk}=\langle A\psi_{j},C_{X}A\psi_{k}\rangle,\quad{\mathsf{\Sigma}}_{jk}=\langle\psi,C_{E}\psi_{k}\rangle.

Typically, however, the test functions

A\psi_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{A}}\psi_{m}
are not particularly informative: If
A
is a compact operator as is often the case in inverse problems, these measurement functions are strongly collinear and may not carry much local information about the distribution
x
.

In the following section, we elaborate further the above result, illustrating the process step by step and providing an interpretation in the context of a realistic example modeling X-ray fanbeam tomography. In the light of this example we discuss how the proposed approach differs from the standard one based on discretization and truncation of the unknown. We also derive an explicit formula for the modeling error arising from this procedure.

4
Example: Fan beam tomography

Consider the following measurement setting: Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
denote the X-ray tomography image window containing an object of unknown density, and assume that
F
represents the unknown density distribution in
\Omega
. We assume that
F
is supported on a disc
\overline{D}\subset\Omega
, that is,
\langle\varphi,F\rangle=0
for every test function with
{\rm supp}(\varphi)\cup D=\emptyset
. The object is illuminated by X-rays emanating from a single point
x_{0}
outside the imaging window, and the attenuated radiation is measured along a screen at the opposite side of the object. For notational convenience, we assume that the screen
S
can be modeled by a curve segment on a circle of radius
R
centered at the source
x_{0}
, as shown schematically in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The geometric arrangement of a single projection in the fanbeam tomography measurement (left). The imaging window
\Omega
is a square, and the unknown density is supported on the disc in
\Omega
. The red dot indicates the X-ray source and
S
is the detector screen. On the right, the screen
S
is represented as a linear array of detectors
s_{k}
collecting photons and integrating the result in a single pixel value, the sensitivity of the detector being represented by some device function
\psi_{k}
. The full data consists of several projections obtained by rotating the object
\Omega
over a discrete set of projection angles.

To derive the forward model for distributions, assume first that the unknown
F
is given as a classical continuous function supported on
\overline{D}
.
The idealized measurement with a single source-receiver position consists of integral of the function
F
along rays from
x_{0}
to
s\in S
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{0}
and
S
are positioned symmetrically with respect to the
x_{1}
-axis as in the figure, so the data along
S
can be modeled as

B(\theta)=\int_{0}^{R}F(x_{0}+t\omega(\theta))dt,\quad\omega(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\quad-\alpha<\theta<\alpha.

We assume that the screen
S
consists of
m
pixels, e.g., CCD detectors, modeled as disjoint intervals
s_{k}\subset S
,
1\leq k\leq m
. Each detector integrates all photons arriving in
s_{k}
into the single value. We may model the measurement by pixel values

b_{k}=\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}B(\theta)\psi_{k}(\theta)d\theta,\quad{\rm supp}(\psi_{k})\subset s_{k},

where the measurement function
\psi_{k}
is parametrized by the opening angle of the fanbeam and models the sensitivity distribution of the
k
th detector. We write this integral as

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\psi_{k}(\theta)\left(\int_{0}^{R}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}F(x_{0}+t\omega(\theta^{\prime}))\delta(\theta-\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\psi_{k}(\theta^{\prime})}{t}F(x_{0}+t\omega(\theta^{\prime}))td\theta^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(x)F(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A\psi_{k},F\rangle,

where we parametrized the domain
\Omega
using polar coordinates with origin at
x_{0}
, noticing that
{\rm supp}(F)\subset\Omega
, and defined

\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(x)=(A\psi_{k})(x)=\frac{1}{|x-x_{0}|}\psi_{k}(\theta),\quad\theta={\arcsin}\frac{(x-x_{0})_{2}}{|x-x_{0}|}.

(18)

If we use smooth measurement functions and define the test function spaces
V
and
U
as
V=C^{\infty}_{0}(S)
,
U=C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, equipped with the Fréchet topology, then (
18
)
defines a linear mapping

A:V\to U
. This mapping is visualized in Figure
2
. Having defined the forward map, we may then extend the unknown
F
to represent generalized random variable in
X=U^{*}
, the space of distributions supported in
\Omega
.

Figure 2:
The linear mapping
{\mathsf{A}}:V\to U
corresponding to a single fanbeam projection. The test function
\psi
is a one–dimensional profile defined over the screen
S
, and
{\mathsf{A}}\psi
is a test function defined over the imaging area
\Omega
. Observe that in this visualization of the mapping
{\mathsf{A}}
, we do not assume that the test function
\psi
is necessarily associated to any physical measurement device.

To compute the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
, assume for simplicity that the covariance operator
C_{X}
can be represented as a convolution integral operator with a kernel
c_{X}
,

C_{X}\phi(x)=\int c_{X}(x-y)\phi(y)dy.

(19)

Introducing the notation
\Gamma_{k}={\rm supp}\widetilde{\psi}_{k}\cap\Omega
, the entries of
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
are given by

{\mathsf{C}}^{22}_{jk}=\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\int_{\Gamma_{k}}\widetilde{\psi}_{j}(x)c_{X}(x-y)\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(y)dxdy.

As remarked in the previous section, a numerical approximation of these integrals can be done using quadrature rules over the Cartesian product of the conical sets
\Gamma_{j}
and
\Gamma_{k}
, without requiring any discretization of the unknown.

The X-ray reconstruction is represented typically as a pixel image. Let
\overline{\Omega}=[0,1]\times[0,1]
denote the square image domain divided in regular
n=N\times N
pixels
p_{j}
of edge length
1/N
. We define the measurement set for the unknown density
\Phi=\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
,
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
{\rm supp}(\varphi_{j})\subset p_{j}
. The full tomographic inverse problem consist of collecting the noisy evaluations
\langle A\psi_{k},F\rangle
with different projection directions by rotating the object and
\Omega
, and subsequently estimating the evaluations
\langle\varphi_{j},F\rangle
by the pixel-based measurement set. Under the assumption (
19
), the entries of the cross correlation matrix are the double integrals

{\mathsf{C}}^{12}_{jk}=\int_{p_{j}}\int_{\Gamma_{k}}\varphi_{j}(x)c_{X}(x-y)\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(y)dxdy,

which can be approximated by numerical quadrature rules. We point out that in practice, the conical domains
\Gamma_{k}
are typically extremely narrow concentrated around a line
\ell_{k}
crossing the computational domain connecting the source point
x_{0}
and the midpoint of the
k
th detector, justifying the approximation

{\mathsf{C}}^{22}_{jk}\approx\int_{\ell_{j}}\int_{\ell_{k}}\widetilde{\psi}_{j}(x)c_{X}(x-y)\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(y)ds(x)ds(y),

where the integrations are along the lines with respect to the arclengths. Similarly, if the pixels are small, and
x_{j}
is the center of the
j
th pixel, the cross correlations can be approximated by

{\mathsf{C}}^{12}_{jk}\approx\int_{\ell_{k}}c_{X}(x_{j}-y)\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(y)ds(y),

of much lower computational cost.

We remark that unlike the traditional discretization-based description of computed tomography problem, this formulation does not define a “tomography matrix”. However, the formulas for the covariance matrices are closely related to the analysis of tomography matrices in
[
10
,
9
]
.

5
Comparison with discretized model

Since the proposed formalism differs from the standard computational approach based on discretization and truncation of the primary unknown, we consider here the differences between these two approaches in the framework of Gaussian linear theory.

Let
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
denote a complete set of test functions, and assume for simplicity that they are
L^{2}
-orthogonal. For the sake of definiteness, one may think the test functions
\varphi_{\ell}
to represent the trigonometric basis. As in the traditional framework based on discretization and truncation, we write a discrete approximation of the distribution
X
as

X=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}\varphi_{j}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\varphi_{j}=X^{n},

where the coefficients
X_{j}
are defined through the duality

X_{j}=\langle\varphi_{j},X\rangle.

This approximation yields the discretized model

B\approx B^{n}=A^{*}X^{n}+E.

We write the approximation of the measurement of
B^{n}
through the measurement set
\Psi
,

\Psi(B^{n})_{j}=\langle\psi_{j},B^{n}\rangle=\langle A\psi_{j},X^{n}\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},E\rangle.

To compute the covariance matrices for the discretized model, we calculate

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle A\psi_{j},X^{n}\rangle\langle A\psi_{k},X^{n}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\big(X_{\ell}X_{\ell^{\prime}}\big)\langle A\psi_{j},\varphi_{\ell}\rangle\langle A\psi_{k},\varphi_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{n}\langle\varphi_{\ell},C_{X}\varphi_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle\langle A\psi_{j},\varphi_{\ell}\rangle\langle A\psi_{k},\varphi_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A\psi_{j},(P^{n})^{*}C_{X}P^{n}A\psi_{k}\rangle,

where
P^{n}
is the operator

P^{n}:U\to U,\quad P^{n}u=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\langle u,\varphi_{l}\rangle\varphi_{\ell},

and
(P^{n})^{*}
is the adjoint operator,

(P^{n})^{*}:H\to U\hookrightarrow H,\quad\big(P^{n}\big)^{*}\xi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\langle\varphi_{\ell},\xi\rangle\varphi_{\ell}.

Therefore the discretization process leads to the approximation of the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{22}
by the matrix
({\mathsf{C}}^{n})^{22}
with entries

({\mathsf{C}}^{n})^{22}_{jk}=\langle{\mathsf{A}}\psi_{j},C_{X}^{n}{\mathsf{A}}\psi_{k}\rangle+\langle\psi_{j},C_{E}\psi_{k}\rangle,\quad 1\leq j,k\leq m,

where the approximate prior covariance operator is

C_{X}^{n}=(P^{n})^{*}C_{X}P^{n}.

Similarly, by the independence of
X
and
E
the approximation of the cross correlation matrix is

\displaystyle({\mathsf{C}}^{n})^{12}_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\varphi_{j},X^{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{k},A^{*}X^{n}\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}(\langle\varphi_{j},X\rangle\langle\varphi_{\ell},X\rangle\langle A\psi_{k},\varphi_{\ell}\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\langle\varphi_{j},C_{X}\varphi_{\ell}\rangle\langle A\psi_{k},\varphi_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{j},C_{X}P^{n}A\psi_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{j},C^{n}_{X}A\psi_{k}\rangle.

Finally, since
P^{n}\varphi_{j}=\varphi_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq n
, the matrix
{\mathsf{C}}^{11}
remains unaltered.

The dependency of the approximation
({\mathsf{C}}^{n})^{22}
on the discretization, or the truncation level
n
when the set
\{\varphi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
forms a basis that allows the representation of the unknown, is a downside for the discretization-based infinite dimensional theory, requiring that every time the discretization is changed the posterior density is derived again from scratch. The distribution-theoretic theory that we have outlined avoids this problem altogether.

6
Discussion

The present article aims at bridging the computational Bayesian inverse problems methodology and the theoretical approaches in infinite dimensional Hilbert and Banach spaces in the framework of distribution spaces. The distributional environment provides an infinite-dimensional context for the theory, however, the evaluations of distributions are typically finite-dimensional, and the finite dimensional evaluations are the basis for both the topology and the
\sigma
-algebras of these spaces.
Since the goal of this paper is more conceptual than computational, we do not discuss in detail how the numerical implementations should be carried out, e.g., to allow matrix-free implementations of solving the linear systems for a numerical evaluation of the posterior mean and density. Furthermore, to underline the conceptual structure, the forward model was assumed here to be generated by an operator that maps smooth functions to smooth functions. As pointed out in the Appendix, this simplification is not necessary, and the functional analytic details depend on the particular problem at hand. The extension of the proposed discretization-free approach to nonlinear problems, and a discussion of how the design algorithms that interface well with matrix-free iterative linear solvers will be topics of future investigations.

Appendix

For the convenience of the reader, in this appendix we summarize some central results concerning the functional analytic framework on which the analysis is based. For further details, we refer to
[
13
]
and references therein.

Let
U
be a separable complete vector space equipped with a system of inner products
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{n}
,
n=1,2,3.\ldots
such that the corresponding induced norms
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{n}
are non-decreasing, i.e.,

\|u\|_{n}\leq\|u\|_{n+1}
for all
u\in U
and
n=1,2,\ldots
. We denote by
U_{n}
the completion of
U
with respect to the norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{n}
, defining a separable Hilbert space. It follows from the monotonicity of the norms that
U_{1}\supseteq U_{2}\supseteq\ldots
, and

U=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_{n}.

(20)

The space
U
defined as an intersection of nested Hilbert spaces (
20
) is referred to as a
countably-Hilbert space
. Its topology is spanned by neighborhoods of zero of the form
\{u\in U\mid\|u\|_{n}<\varepsilon\,\;1\leq n\leq N\}
,
N\geq 1
,
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
H
denote the space of continuous linear functionals of
U
, equipped with the weak
∗
topology, and let
H_{n}
denote the dual space of
U_{n}
.
We have
H_{1}\subseteq H_{2}\subseteq\ldots
, and we may identify
H
as

H=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}H_{n},

(21)

We denote the duality
U\times H\to{\mathbb{R}}
(or
{\mathbb{C}}
) by
(u,\xi)\mapsto\langle u,\xi\rangle
.

A bounded linear operator
B:U_{n}\to U_{n}
is said to be
nuclear
, or
trace-class
, if

{\rm Trace}(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(v_{j},Bv_{j})_{n},

where
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is any orthonormal basis of
U_{n}
.
The countably-Hilbert space is called
nuclear
, if for every
k
, the bilinear functional
B_{k}
representing the
k
th inner product,
B_{k}(u,v)=(u,v)_{k}
,
u,v\in U
, is represented in some
U_{n}
,
n>k
, as

B_{k}(u,v)=(u,B_{k}^{n}v)_{n},\quad u,v\in U,

where the operator
B_{k}^{n}:U_{n}\to U_{n}
is nuclear.

To make these concepts more concrete, consider periodic
C^{\infty}
-functions on the unit circle,

U=C^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}^{1}),

and the inner products

(u,v)_{n}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle j\rangle^{2n}\widehat{u}_{j}\overline{\widehat{v}_{j}},

where
\langle j\rangle=(1+j^{2})^{1/2}
, and
\{\widehat{u}_{j}\}_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}
is the Fourier transform of the function
u
.
By Parseval’s theorem, the completion of
U
with respect to the norm
\|u\|_{n}=\big\|(\langle j\rangle^{n}\widehat{u}_{j})_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\big\|_{\ell^{2}}
defines the Sobolev space with smoothness index
n
,
W^{n}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})=U_{n}
.
The norms are non-increasing, and for every
k
and
n>k
, we have

(u,v)_{k}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle j\rangle^{2k}\widehat{u}_{j}\overline{\widehat{v}_{j}}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle j\rangle^{2n}\big(\langle j\rangle^{2(k-n)}\widehat{u}_{j}\big)\overline{\widehat{v}_{j}}=(B_{k}^{n}u,v)_{n},

where
B_{k}^{n}
is the Fourier multiplier

B_{k}^{n}:u\mapsto\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ij\theta}\langle j\rangle^{2(k-n)}\widehat{u}_{j},

which is trace class operator for
n\geq k+1
, showing that
U
is a nuclear countably-Hilbert space.
We define the dual pairing through extension of the
L^{2}
-inner product.
In this example, the space
H
consists of the distributions on the unit circle,
H={\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})
, and the spaces
H_{n}
can be identified with Sobolev spaces with negative smoothness index,
H_{n}=W^{-n}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})
.

Another example relevant for the present discussion is the Schwartz class of rapidly decreasing
C^{\infty}
-functions,

U={\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\big\{u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\mid|x^{\alpha}D^{\beta}u(x)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\mbox{ for all $\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}$}\big\},

the space
H={\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
being the class of tempered distributions,
and

U={\mathscr{D}}(\Omega)=\big\{u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\mid u(x)=0\mbox{ for $x\notin\overline{\Omega}$}\big\}

for some bounded smooth
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
H={\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}(\Omega)
are distributions over the set
\Omega
. The construction of the countably-Hilbert space structure for this example is along the lines of the discussion for the previous example, requiring more technical operator-theoretic details that can be found, e.g., in
[
15
]
for the former, and
[
16
]
for the latter.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathfrak{S}},P)
, consider a measurable mapping
\xi:U\to L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathfrak{S}},P)
. If
\xi
is continuous and linear, we call
\xi
a
linear random functional
(LRF). We define the mean
\mu
and correlation functional
C
of
\xi
by the formulas

\mu(u)={\mathbb{E}}\big(\xi(u)\big),\quad C(u,v)={\mathbb{E}}\big((\xi(u)-\mu(u))(\xi(v)-\mu(v)\big).

The continuity of the mapping
\xi
implies that the mean and correlation functional admit representations of the form

\mu(u)=(u,A_{n})_{n},\quad C(u,v)=(B_{n}u,v)_{n}

for some
n\geq 1
, where
A_{n}\in U_{n}
and
B_{n}\in L(U_{n})
, i.e.,
B_{n}
is a linear bounded operator in
U_{n}
. Consequently, the LRF can be extended to the Hilbert space
U_{n}
, thus defining an LRF in the Hilbert space

U_{n}
.

A LRF on
U
is Gaussian if, for every
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in U
, the multivariate random variables
(\xi(u_{1}),\ldots,\xi(u_{k}))
are Gaussian. In the following, we restrict the discussion to Gaussian random variables.

To define random variables in the dual space
H
, we equip it with a
\sigma
-algebra spanned by the cylinder sets of the form

{\mathcal{C}}=\big\{\xi\in H\mid(\langle u_{1},\xi\rangle,\ldots,\langle u_{k},\xi\rangle)\in B,\;u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in U,\;B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\mbox{ open}\big\}.

It turns out that this
\sigma
-algebra coincides with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra relative to the weak
∗
topology, see
[
16
]
.
A natural question is whether a LRF defines an
H
-valued
generalized random variable

\Xi:\Omega\to H\mbox{ such that $\xi(u)=\langle u,\Xi\rangle$ for all $u\in U$.}

(22)

In the affirmative case, we refer to the random variable
\Xi
as a
Gaussian linear functional
.
As demonstrated in
[
16
]
, for a Gaussian LRF, the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the representation as a Gaussian linear functional is that the covariance operator
B_{n}
is a trace-class operator with respect to some orthonormal basis of
U_{n}
. Since we have assumed that the countably-Hilbert space
U
is nuclear, this condition is automatically satisfied, as
B_{n}
can always be interpreted as a trace-class operator in
U_{n+k}
for some
k>0
. We therefore conclude that
in a nuclear countably-Hilbert space
U
, every Gaussian LRF can be represented as a Gaussian linear functional (
22
)

Finally, we discuss briefly some extension of the formalism to cases where the forward model

that maps the test functions in

J_{n}:H_{n}\to U_{n}
denote the canonical representation of the dual of the Hilbert space

in

,
\langle\xi,u\rangle=(J_{n}\xi,u)_{n}
,
and define
A_{n}=J_{n}A:V\to U_{n}
. We can then define the adjoint
A_{n}^{*}:H_{n}\to K
, and the inverse problem is defined for a random variable
X
assuming that

with probability one. For more detailed continuity considerations of the adjoint

, we refer to
[

]
.
