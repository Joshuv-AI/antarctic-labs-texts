Elucidating the vasoprotective mechanism of Tetrahydrocurcumin: from unbiased transcriptomic discovery to targeted validation of the PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Yi-Lin Chiu, Tsai-Jung Lin, Hsing-Fan Lai, Yu-Sin Chang, Tzu-Chiao Lin, Yen-Lien Chou, Cheng-Chung Cheng, Sharon Fong, Tsung-Neng Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endothelial dysfunction is a critical initiating event in atherosclerosis. Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) plays a key role in maintaining vascular health by producing nitric oxide (NO) to regulate vascular tone. Tetrahydrocurcumin (THCu), a stable metabolite of curcumin, exhibits promising bioactivity; however, its precise vasoprotective mechanism remains unclear. This study investigated the hypothesis that THCu enhances eNOS function in human umbilical vein endothelial cells by activating the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway. Our results demonstrate that THCu treatment robustly and dose-dependently increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation. This upstream activation directly led to eNOS phosphorylation at Ser1177, significantly boosting NO bioavailability. Pharmacological inhibition of PI3K or AKT completely abolished THCu-induced eNOS phosphorylation and NO release. In conclusion, this research delineates a clear mechanistic framework establishing the PI3K/AKT cascade as the essential pathway for THCu-mediated eNOS activation, providing a strong scientific rationale for its further investigation as a candidate for mitigating endothelial dysfunction in atherosclerosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05326-1.

Background

Atherosclerosis is a complex and progressive disease characterized by the accumulation of lipids and inflammatory cells within the arterial wall, representing a major cause of cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of this condition is critically linked to endothelial dysfunction, a state defined by reduced bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO), increased oxidative stress, and heightened inflammation [3, 4]. The endothelium, a monolayer of cells lining the blood vessels, is an essential regulator of vascular homeostasis [5, 6]. Therefore, the impairment of its function is considered a key initiating event in the development of atherosclerosis. A primary factor in maintaining endothelial health is the enzyme endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which is responsible for the production of NO [7, 8]. NO is a potent vasodilator and plays a crucial role in preventing inflammation and platelet aggregation [9, 10]. Consequently, impaired eNOS activity and the resulting decrease in NO production are hallmarks of endothelial dysfunction and contribute directly to the progression of atherosclerosis [11, 12]. The activity of eNOS is tightly regulated and can be disrupted by factors such as oxidative stress, which is prevalent in the atherosclerotic microenvironment [13, 14].

Tetrahydrocurcumin (THCu), a major metabolite of curcumin, has emerged as a promising therapeutic agent due to its enhanced stability and biological activity compared to its parent compound [15, 16]. THCu is a potent antioxidant, recognized for its superior ability to scavenge free radicals and mitigate the oxidative stress associated with cardiovascular disorders [17–19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that THCu can ameliorate endothelial dysfunction by modulating key inflammatory signaling pathways and enhancing eNOS activity [16, 20]. While the vasoprotective effects of THCu are increasingly acknowledged, the precise molecular mechanism by which it activates eNOS in human endothelial cells remains to be fully elucidated [20]. The phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway is a well-established upstream regulator of eNOS function [21, 22]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the hypothesis that THCu enhances eNOS expression and subsequent NO production in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) through the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Methods

Reagents and cell culture

HUVECs were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD, USA). The cells were cultured in Endothelial Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2, Lonza) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For all experiments, HUVECs were used upon reaching 80–90% confluence. Tetrahydrocurcumin (Catalog No. CFN90583; CAS No. 36062-04-1) was purchased from ChemFaces (Wuhan, China). The batch number was CFS202201, with a purity of 99.0% as determined by HPLC. The molecular formula is C₂₁H₂₄O₆, and the compound was provided as a powder. The solvent compatibility included chloroform, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, DMSO, and acetone. The product was characterized by 1H-NMR and HPLC, with results consistent with the expected structure. It was stored protected from air and light at 2–8 °C. Quality control data were provided by the supplier in the Certificate of Analysis. For treatments, cells were incubated for 24 h with either a vehicle control (dimethyl sulfoxide; DMSO; final concentration 0.1%; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or varying concentrations of THCu (2.5, 5, and 10 µM).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from THCu-treated HUVECs using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The concentration and quality of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA integrity was further verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, mRNA was isolated from total RNA using Poly(A) mRNA magnetic beads and an rRNA removal kit. The purified mRNA was fragmented, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using ProtoScript II reverse transcriptase with random hexamer primers. This was followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. The resulting DNA fragments were subjected to end-repair and dA-tailing, after which sequencing adaptors were ligated. Fragments of approximately 420 bp were selected using magnetic beads, and the library was amplified via a 13-cycle PCR using P5 and P7 primers. The final library was quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform by GENEWIZ®. The resulting raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed for bioinformatic analysis. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, GENCODE v41) using the HISAT2 aligner. Following alignment, the number of reads mapping to each gene was quantified using featureCounts from the Subread package [23]. This process generated a raw gene count matrix, which served as the input for downstream analysis. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v1.46.0) in the R statistical environment. The criteria for identifying significant DEGs were an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and a Benjamini-Hochberg (B–H) adjusted p-value < 0.05 [24]. For subsequent Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), a gene list was prepared by ranking all expressed genes in descending order according to their log2 fold change (log2FC) values. To further explore the cellular context of the transcriptional changes, xCell signature analysis was performed. GSEA was conducted using the clusterProfiler package [25], leveraging the comprehensive cell type-related gene sets provided by Aran et al. [26]. The enrichment results for individual cell types were subsequently visualized as radial plots using the ggplot2 package. For other details, please refer to the article by Chan et al. [27].

Gene functional activity score analysis

To infer the functional activity of upstream genes and pathways based on our differential gene expression profile, we implemented a custom analysis pipeline using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). A ranked gene list was first generated from the differential expression results, ordered by the log2FoldChange values. GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. The analysis utilized the LINCS L1000 CRISPR Knock-Out Consensus Signatures gene set library available in EnrichR (LINCS_L1000_CRISPR_KO_Consensus_Sigs.txt) [28, 29]. This library contains curated pairs of gene sets for each specific gene perturbation performed via CRISPR-mediated knockout. For each knocked-out gene, the library provides a set of genes consistently upregulated upon its knockout (the “Up” set) and a corresponding set of genes consistently downregulated (the “Down” set). A custom “Gene Functional Activity Score” was calculated to represent the overall functional status of each knocked-out gene in the context of our experimental data. This score was defined by subtracting the Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) of the “Up” gene set from the NES of the “Down” gene set (Activity Score = NES_Down - NES_Up). Consequently, a high negative score indicated that our experimental gene profile resembled the signature of the gene being knocked out (defined as “Inhibited” activity), while a high positive score indicated an opposing profile (“Activated” activity). To generate a single significance value for each gene perturbation, the individual p-values from the “Up” and “Down” GSEA results were statistically combined using Fisher’s method. This method calculates a test statistic, X2, based on the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^2=-2\sum\limits_{i=1}^k\mathrm{In}(p_i)$$\end{document}

where pi​ is the individual p-value and k is the number of p-values being combined (in this case, k = 2). This X2 statistic follows a chi-squared (χ2) distribution with 2k degrees of freedom, which is used to derive the final combined p-value. The results were then visualized as an enrichment volcano plot, with the Gene Functional Activity Score on the x-axis and the -log10 of the Fisher’s combined p-value on the y-axis. Gene perturbations were deemed significantly “Activated” or “Inhibited” if their activity score exceeded a threshold of |3| and the combined p-value was less than 0.05. For clarity, the top 30 most statistically significant gene perturbations were labeled on the plot for identification. To further investigate the biological roles of these genes, the lists of significantly “Activated” and “Inhibited” genes were subsequently imported into the Webgestalt (WEB-based Gene SeT AnaLysis Toolkit) online platform [30]. An Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) was performed against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database [31]. Enriched pathways were visualized using the integrated Pathview tool, which renders the input gene data onto the corresponding KEGG pathway maps.

Tube formation assay

The angiogenic capacity of HUVECs was evaluated using an in vitro tube formation assay. Briefly, each well of a chilled 96-well plate was coated with 50 µL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel matrix (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for one hour to allow the matrix to solidify. Following solidification, HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel surface at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 100 µL of medium containing the indicated treatments. After a 6-hour incubation, the formation of capillary-like networks was assessed and imaged using a phase-contrast microscope. The total tube length in the resulting images was then quantified using ImageJ software.

Reverse-transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) and quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HUVECs grown in 3.5-cm culture dishes using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration of the isolated RNA was determined by spectrophotometry at an absorbance of 260 nm. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA in a 20 µL reaction volume. The reverse transcription reaction contained random hexamers (Protech Technology, Taipei, Taiwan), 1 mM dNTPs, an RNase inhibitor (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and 5 U/µL Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus (M-MLV) Reverse Transcriptase (Protech Technology). The synthesis was performed at 42 °C for 90 min. The resulting cDNA was used as a template for both quantitative and end-point PCR to assess the mRNA levels of eNOS, PIK3CA, AKT, and the housekeeping gene GAPDH. Quantitative PCR was performed on a CFX Opus Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and a combined annealing/extension step at 60 °C for 1 min. Western blot analysis

For protein analysis, cell lysates were prepared by washing HUVECs with PBS and scraping the cells on ice into RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Total protein concentrations were determined using a standard protein assay, and equal amounts of protein (20 µg per sample) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS; #9572), phospho-eNOS (Ser1177; #9571), PI3K p85 (#4257), phospho-PI3K p85 (Tyr458; #4228), AKT (#4691), and phospho-AKT (Ser473; #9271) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), as well as β-actin (sc-47778; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), all used at a dilution of 1:1000. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at a dilution of 1:5000. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Merck KGaA). For quantitative analysis, phosphorylated proteins were normalized to their respective total protein levels (p-PI3K/Total PI3K and p-AKT/Total AKT), rather than to the loading control, to ensure accurate assessment of pathway activation.

Nitric Oxide (NO) production assay

The bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO) was assessed by quantifying the accumulation of its stable metabolite, nitrite (NO2−), in the cell culture supernatant was quantified using a Nitric Oxide Colorimetric Assay Kit (BioVision Inc., CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Statistical comparisons between treatment groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test in SPSS software. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Immunofluorescent assays

For immunofluorescence analysis, HUVECs grown on coverslips were fixed with chilled methanol for 20 min. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) before being incubated for 1 h with a primary antibody against eNOS (1:200 dilution; A1548, ABclonal, Inc., MA, USA). After washing, the cells were incubated with an appropriate species-specific, fluorescently-conjugated secondary antibody. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using a Leica DMI3000B fluorescence microscope (Leica Biosystems, Mannheim, Germany).

Analysis of public gene expression datasets

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the PI3K/AKT/eNOS signaling axis, two publicly available microarray datasets were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. First, normalized gene expression data from dataset GSE100927 were utilized to compare the mRNA levels of NOS3, PIK3CA, AKT1, AKT2, and AKT3 between atherosclerotic and control arterial samples [32]. This comparison was performed separately for three anatomical locations: the carotid, femoral, and infra-popliteal arteries.For further validation and to examine expression changes during disease progression, dataset GSE28829 was analyzed [33]. In this cohort, expression levels of the same genes were compared between samples from early-stage (n = 13) and advanced (n = 16) atherosclerotic plaques within the carotid artery. For both datasets, the relevant expression values were extracted, and the data were visualized using violin and box plots created with the ggplot2 package in the R statistical environment.

Machine learning analysis

To classify the state of atherosclerosis and predict the effect of THCu treatment, a supervised machine learning pipeline was implemented using Orange data mining software (v3.38) [34]. The models were trained on the public dataset GSE28829, and GSVA scores calculated from 50 Hallmark gene-sets served as the input features for the models [35]. Several supervised learning algorithms were trained and evaluated using a 5-fold stratified cross-validation approach to ensure model robustness and prevent overfitting. All algorithms were implemented using their default parameters in Orange. The configurations for each model were as follows: Support Vector Machine (SVM): Configured with a cost (C) of 1, a regression loss epsilon of 0.1, a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, and an iteration limit of 100. Random Forest: Trained with 10 trees and constrained by not splitting subsets smaller than 5 samples. Neural Network: Designed with a single hidden layer of 100 neurons, using the ReLU activation function and the Adam solver. Regularization (α) was set to 0.0001 with a maximum of 200 iterations. k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN): Utilized 5 neighbors, the Euclidean distance metric, and uniform weights. Logistic Regression: Employed Ridge (L2) regularization with a strength (C) of 1. Naive Bayes: Included with its default parameters. The performance of these models was assessed using standard metrics, including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The trained models were then used to generate predictions on our experimental HUVEC samples (Control, THCu low, and THCu high). The resulting prediction scores, indicating the proportion of models classifying a sample as having an “advanced atherosclerotic plaque” profile, were exported for further analysis. These scores were visualized using boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package in R. For other details, please refer to the article by Fan et al. [24].

Statistical analysis

For in vitro biochemical experiments, statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test using SPSS statistics version 17 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For the analysis of public transcriptomic datasets (GSE100927 and GSE28829), differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the limma package in R. Statistical significance in these datasets was assessed using linear models with empirical Bayes moderation to stabilize variance estimates. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comprehensive transcriptional effects of THCu on HUVECs

In a previous study, curcumin demonstrated vascular protective effects in the context of NO production [36]. THCu, a metabolite derived from curcumin, has also exhibited anti-inflammatory properties in vivo [37]. To assess whether THCu treatment maintains vascular identity and enhances endothelial-associated gene programs in our HUVEC model, we utilized GSEA with the xCell database (Fig. 1A). This analysis served to confirm that THCu treatment reinforces the endothelial phenotype and does not induce aberrant dedifferentiation. The analysis revealed that at both low (2.5 µM) and high (5 µM) concentrations, THCu induced a strong and consistent enrichment for vasculature-related gene signatures (Fig. 1B). Notably, the pronounced enrichment for endothelial subtypes (including microvascular endothelial signatures) suggests a robust maintenance of endothelial-specific transcriptional programs, distinguishing the treated cells from non-vascular lineages (Fig. 1C). Conversely, signatures related to non-vascular lineages, particularly various immune and stem cells, were generally downregulated.

Unbiased functional transcriptomic analysis identifies the phosphatidylinositol signaling system as a key pathway modulated by THCu

Having confirmed the maintenance of endothelial identity, we next sought to identify the specific signaling pathways driving THCu’s effects in an unbiased manner. As suggested by standard transcriptomic workflows, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 2A). This approach identified key genes whose functional activities were significantly modulated by THCu. For instance, the activities of genes such as IRAK2 and FUK were consistently activated, while those of GUCY2D and COQ8B were inhibited under both low and high-dose THCu conditions (Fig. 2B). Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis using Webgestalt revealed that the “Phosphatidylinositol signaling system” was the most significantly enriched KEGG pathway in both treatment groups (Fig. 2C) [30]. The visualization of this pathway using Pathview highlighted several key components that were affected by THCu treatment (Fig. 2D) [38]. The strong enrichment of this particular pathway is highly significant, as it is known to contain crucial upstream regulators, such as PI3K, which directly influence the AKT signaling axis.

THCu upregulates PI3K and AKT expression and phosphorylation

To validate the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway, we investigated the direct effects of THCu on the expression and activation of its core components in HUVECs. Treatment with THCu resulted in a significant, dose-dependent upregulation of both PI3KCA and AKT mRNA levels, as confirmed by RT-PCR and quantitative real-time PCR (Fig. 3A-C). At the protein level, Western blot analysis revealed that THCu modestly increased total PI3K expression, which became significant at 10 µM, but did not alter the total expression of AKT (Fig. 3D, F and H). Crucially, THCu induced a strong, dose-dependent increase in the phosphorylation of both PI3K and AKT, with statistical significance observed at 5 and 10 µM (Fig. 3D, E and G). These results demonstrate that THCu not only increases the expression of PI3K and AKT but also robustly activates the pathway via phosphorylation.

THCu increased phosphorylated ENOS activity and NO production but did not affect tube formation

Given that the PI3K/AKT pathway is a critical upstream regulator of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [39], our investigation proceeded to determine if the THCu-induced activation of this pathway would translate to downstream effects on eNOS expression and function. At the transcriptional level, we first observed that THCu treatment of HUVECs led to a significant upregulation of NOS3 mRNA, with a peak effect at a concentration of 5 µM, as demonstrated by both RT-PCR and qPCR analyses (Fig. 4A and B). Moving to the protein level, Western blot analysis revealed a key mechanistic insight. While the total protein abundance of eNOS remained unchanged following THCu treatment, the level of phosphorylated eNOS at the Ser1177 activation site increased dramatically and significantly in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4C and D). This distinction underscores that THCu promotes the activation of the eNOS enzyme primarily by enhancing its phosphorylation—a finding consistent with the activation of the upstream PI3K/AKT pathway—rather than by increasing the total amount of eNOS protein.

The functional consequence of this enzymatic activation was immediately apparent. Enhanced eNOS phosphorylation resulted in a significant and dose-dependent increase in NO bioavailability (measured as nitrite accumulation), which was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining and a colorimetric assay (Fig. 5A and B). However, we sought to determine if this increase in NO, a known signaling molecule in angiogenesis, would alter cellular behavior. Despite the substantially elevated NO levels, THCu did not modulate the angiogenic potential of these cells. An in vitro tube formation assay showed no significant difference in the total length of capillary-like structures formed by HUVECs when compared to the untreated control (Fig. 5C and D).

PI3K and AKT expression Is critical for THCu-induced ENOS phosphorylation and NO production

NO production is pivotal for endothelial cell functionality and atherosclerosis progression [39]. To elucidate the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway in THCu-mediated eNOS expression and NO production, HUVECs were treated with THCu along with distinct inhibitors. The findings indicate that the inhibition of PI3K (LY294002) and AKT (A6730) significantly attenuates eNOS expression at the RNA level (Figs. 6A and B and 7A and B). Subsequently, protein expression was assessed via Western blot analysis, revealing the involvement of PI3K and AKT in THCu-induced eNOS phosphorylation (Figs. 6C and D and 7C and D). To further explore the effect of PI3K and AKT on NO production, immunofluorescence and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were performed. Our results demonstrate that the fluorescence intensity (Supplemental Fig. 1) of eNOS expression and NO production increase following THCu treatment and diminish upon treatment with PI3K and AKT inhibitors (Figs. 6E and 7E).

The PI3K/AKT pathway is essential for THCu-mediated eNOS activation and NO production

To rigorously establish a causal link and confirm that the PI3K/AKT signaling axis is indispensable for the observed effects of THCu, we conducted a series of experiments using specific pharmacological inhibitors. First, to elucidate the role of PI3K, we pre-treated HUVECs with LY294002, a well-established and specific PI3K inhibitor. At the transcriptional level, this inhibition produced a profound effect, completely preventing the previously observed THCu-induced upregulation of NOS3, PI3KCA, and AKT mRNA levels and returning them to baseline conditions (Fig. 6A and B). More critically, we assessed the impact on protein phosphorylation, the direct marker of pathway activation. Inhibition of PI3K with LY294002 completely abolished the THCu-stimulated phosphorylation of PI3K itself, demonstrating effective target engagement. Consequently, the phosphorylation of its immediate downstream target, AKT, was also completely blocked. This upstream blockade then propagated down the cascade, resulting in the complete prevention of THCu-induced eNOS phosphorylation (Fig. 6C and D).

To further dissect the pathway and confirm the linear nature of this signaling cascade, we employed a similar strategy using the specific AKT inhibitor, A6730. At the transcriptional level, pre-treatment with A6730 significantly suppressed the THCu-induced upregulation of both NOS3 and AKT mRNA (Fig. 7A and B). As expected, at the protein level, the inhibitor effectively prevented the phosphorylation of AKT itself. Crucially, this direct inhibition of AKT was sufficient to completely abolish the downstream phosphorylation of eNOS (Fig. 7C and D). The functional ramifications of disrupting this pathway were unequivocal. The blockade of signaling, whether initiated at the PI3K level with LY294002 or at the AKT level with A6730, resulted in the complete and significant abrogation of the THCu-stimulated increase in NO production (Figs. 6E and 7E). In both inhibitor experiments, NO levels were reduced to those of the untreated control group.

Collectively, the results from these pharmacological inhibition studies provide compelling evidence for our proposed mechanism. They establish not only that PI3K and AKT are individually necessary for the effects of THCu, but also that THCu stimulates eNOS activation and subsequent NO production through a sequential and linear PI3K/AKT-dependent signaling cascade. The complete reversal of THCu’s effects by these inhibitors validates this pathway as the primary mechanism of action in human endothelial cells.

Alteration of the PI3K/AKT signaling machinery in human atherosclerotic plaques

To assess the clinical relevance of our in vitro findings, we analyzed the expression of the PI3K/AKT/eNOS signaling axis in publicly available gene expression datasets from patients with atherosclerosis. First, in the GSE100927 dataset, comparing atherosclerotic plaques to control tissues, we observed a distinct transcriptional suppression of key upstream kinases. Specifically, AKT3 expression was consistently and significantly downregulated across all three examined vascular beds (carotid, femoral, and infra-popliteal arteries) (p < 0.05, Fig. 8A). Furthermore, significant tissue-specific downregulation was observed for PIK3CA in carotid arteries and AKT2 in femoral arteries. While NOS3 mRNA levels showed no statistically significant difference, the broad suppression of its upstream activators suggests a functional impairment of the signaling axis in the disease state.

To further investigate the pathway’s dynamics during plaque progression, we analyzed the GSE28829 dataset (early vs. advanced carotid plaques). In this cohort, we observed a trend toward reduced expression of the upstream kinases PIK3CA and AKT1 in advanced plaques compared to early-stage lesions (Fig. 8B). Although these differences did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), likely due to sample size limitations or inter-patient heterogeneity, the pattern of downregulation aligns with our hypothesis that the protective PI3K/AKT signaling axis is progressively compromised as atherosclerosis advances. Taken together, these clinical data highlight the loss of upstream survival signaling as a critical feature of established atherosclerosis, supporting the therapeutic potential of agents like THCu that can robustly reactivate this pathway.

Machine learning models predict that THCu reverses the transcriptional signature of advanced atherosclerosis

To computationally evaluate the potential of THCu to reverse transcriptional signatures associated with atherosclerosis, we developed a machine learning framework. This framework was trained on the GSE28829 clinical dataset, which includes arterial samples from early and advanced atherosclerotic plaques, using GSVA scores from 50 Hallmark gene-sets as predictive features (Fig. 9A). All six trained models demonstrated high predictive accuracy in distinguishing between early and advanced plaque states, with the neural network and SVM models achieving the highest Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.978 (Fig. 9B). Feature importance analysis identified that gene sets such as HALLMARK OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION, GLYCOLYSIS, and ADIPOGENESIS were key predictors of an advanced plaque state, whereas HALLMARK ESTROGEN RESPONSE EARLY was associated with an early plaque signature (Fig. 9C).

We then applied this validated predictive framework to our RNA-seq data from HUVECs treated with THCu. The models predicted that the transcriptional profile of untreated control cells closely resembled that of advanced atherosclerotic plaques. In contrast, treatment with both low (2.5 µM) and high (5 µM) concentrations of THCu significantly shifted the predicted cellular state toward a signature characteristic of early-stage plaques (**p < 0.01), suggesting a reversal of the pro-atherosclerotic phenotype (Fig. 9D). No significant difference was observed between the low and high THCu concentrations. These in silico findings strongly suggest that THCu can ameliorate the endothelial cell phenotype associated with advanced atherosclerosis by modulating key transcriptional pathways.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive, multi-faceted investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying the vasoprotective effects of THCu on human endothelial cells. Beginning with an unbiased transcriptomic analysis, we first identified the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a primary target of THCu using functional genomics. We then present detailed biochemical evidence demonstrating that THCu not only upregulates the expression of key pathway components but, more importantly, induces the sequential phosphorylation and activation of PI3K, AKT, and their downstream target, eNOS. Crucially, through the use of specific pharmacological inhibitors, we establish that this PI3K/AKT signaling axis is indispensable for THCu-mediated nitric oxide production. Furthermore, we bridge our in vitro findings to clinical relevance by demonstrating that this pathway is progressively downregulated in advanced human atherosclerotic plaques and show, via predictive machine learning models, that THCu treatment can revert the cellular transcriptional signature associated with advanced disease toward an early-stage profile.

While our data clearly establishes the PI3K/AKT/eNOS axis as the core pathway, the upstream molecular events initiating this cascade and the full spectrum of downstream functional consequences warrant deeper consideration. THCu is a lipophilic polyphenol with markedly greater metabolic stability and bioavailability compared to its parent compound, curcumin, allowing it to more effectively reach target tissues [40]. Given its lipophilicity, a plausible upstream mechanism involves THCu’s interaction with the endothelial cell plasma membrane. Many signaling cascades, including those initiated by receptor tyrosine kinases that activate PI3K, are spatially organized within specialized membrane microdomains known as lipid rafts [41]. These cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich platforms act as signaling hubs, and their biophysical properties can be modulated by exogenous lipophilic compounds [42]. We hypothesize that THCu may intercalate into the lipid bilayer, altering the fluidity, curvature, or protein composition of these rafts. Such a perturbation of the membrane microenvironment could facilitate the clustering or allosteric activation of upstream receptors, thereby enhancing the recruitment and activation of PI3K at the membrane [43]. This biophysical model provides a testable hypothesis for the initial molecular action of THCu that precedes PI3K phosphorylation.

The downstream functional consequences of THCu-induced NO production are profoundly anti-atherosclerotic. Endothelial dysfunction, characterized by reduced NO bioavailability, is a critical initiating event in atherosclerosis [44]. The increased NO resulting from the pathway we have identified would counteract this pathology through at least two well-established mechanisms. First, NO is a potent anti-inflammatory mediator. It is known to suppress endothelial activation by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, a master regulator of inflammation [45]. This inhibition prevents the transcriptional upregulation of key adhesion molecules, such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, which are essential for the recruitment and transmigration of leukocytes into the subendothelial space—a foundational step in plaque formation [46]. Second, NO is a powerful endogenous anti-thrombotic agent. It acts in a paracrine fashion to inhibit platelet activation, adhesion, and aggregation, primarily by activating soluble guanylyl cyclase within platelets [47]. By enhancing NO production, THCu would be expected to create a less thrombogenic endothelial surface, reducing the risk of acute thrombotic events that complicate advanced atherosclerotic plaques [48].

One of the most intriguing findings of this study is the observed decoupling of NO-mediated vasodilation from angiogenesis. Despite a robust and significant increase in NO production, THCu treatment did not promote angiogenesis in the tube formation assay. This result is initially counterintuitive, as NO is classically regarded as a pro-angiogenic signaling molecule [20]. However, machine learning analysis of clinical and experimental transcriptomes provides a critical clue to resolving this apparent paradox. Our models identified “HALLMARK OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION” and “HALLMARK GLYCOLYSIS” as key signatures distinguishing advanced, pathological plaques from early-stage lesions. Importantly, THCu treatment significantly reversed this transcriptional signature. Pathological processes such as sustained inflammation and angiogenesis are highly energy-demanding and are fueled by a metabolic switch towards high-rate aerobic glycolysis [49]. By suppressing this hyper-metabolic program, we propose that THCu induces a state of “protective quiescence” in endothelial cells. While it activates the PI3K/AKT/eNOS pathway to generate vasoprotective NO, it simultaneously restricts the glycolytic fuel required for pathological neovascularization, thereby promoting a stable, healthy endothelial phenotype rather than a proliferative, unstable one.

The capacity of THCu to induce profound shifts in cellular signaling also sheds light on the seemingly paradoxical transcriptomic changes observed in our array data (Fig. 2B), specifically the upregulation of the pro-inflammatory marker IRAK2 and the downregulation of the CoQ10 biosynthetic gene COQ8B. We propose that these changes reflect compensatory cellular feedback loops in response to the potent pharmacological actions of THCu rather than a direct pro-inflammatory or pro-oxidant effect. First, regarding IRAK2, THCu exerts its anti-inflammatory effects primarily by inhibiting the post-translational activation (phosphorylation) and nuclear translocation of NF-κB, as confirmed by our protein assays. The observed upregulation of IRAK2 mRNA likely represents a compensatory transcriptional attempt by the cell to restore signaling flux in response to this potent blockade of the downstream NF-κB machinery. Second, the downregulation of COQ8B likely results from a metabolic adjustment to redox homeostasis. Since THCu itself acts as a potent exogenous antioxidant and free radical scavenger, its presence rapidly reduces intracellular oxidative stress. Consequently, the cell may downregulate its endogenous antioxidant synthesis machinery, including COQ8B, to conserve metabolic energy. Thus, these gene expression patterns are consistent with the cell’s adaptive response to the robust anti-inflammatory and antioxidant environment established by THCu.

The clinical imperative for reactivating this pathway is underscored by our limma-based differential analysis of human atherosclerotic lesions. Data from the GSE100927 cohort revealed a robust and consistent downregulation of AKT3 across all vascular beds, along with tissue-specific reductions in PIK3CA and AKT2 in atherosclerotic arteries compared to controls. Similarly, analysis of the GSE28829 cohort indicates that this loss of upstream survival signaling is a feature established early in the disease process. Although NOS3 mRNA levels themselves remained relatively stable in some comparisons, the broad suppression of its obligate upstream activators (PIK3CA and AKT isoforms) indicates a functional collapse of the signaling axis in the disease state [44].

A central feature of this dysfunction is eNOS “uncoupling,” where the enzyme produces damaging superoxide instead of NO due to substrate or cofactor depletion [50]. The PI3K/AKT pathway is a crucial regulator of eNOS coupling, notably through Ser1177 phosphorylation, which maintains the enzyme’s functional dimeric state [51]. The observed clinical downregulation of upstream PI3K/AKT signaling likely drives eNOS uncoupling and the pro-oxidant state of the atherosclerotic endothelium. Therefore, the ability of THCu to potently reactivate this upstream pathway suggests it functions not merely as an NO donor, but as an “eNOS recoupling agent.” By restoring PI3K/AKT signaling, THCu may shift the enzymatic output of eNOS back from superoxide to NO, thereby simultaneously enhancing vasoprotection and reducing oxidative stress [52].

Finally, the novel application of machine learning in this study provides a powerful in silico validation of THCu’s therapeutic potential. By training a suite of models on transcriptomic data from human plaques, we created a robust classifier capable of distinguishing early versus advanced disease states with high accuracy (AUC up to 0.978). The application of this framework to our experimental data predicted that THCu treatment significantly reverses the transcriptional signature associated with advanced atherosclerosis, pushing the endothelial cell profile towards that of an early-stage, healthier state. This approach aligns with the growing paradigm of using artificial intelligence and systems biology to analyze complex transcriptomic datasets, predict drug efficacy, and prioritize therapeutic candidates for further development [53].

While our machine learning analysis provides compelling predictive insights, we acknowledge certain limitations in bridging in silico models with in vitro experiments. The models were trained on bulk carotid plaque tissues, which represent a heterogeneous mixture of cell types, whereas our validation utilized isolated HUVECs. Consequently, the predicted reversal of the atherosclerotic signature likely reflects a global shift in inflammatory and metabolic status rather than a tissue-wide restoration. Furthermore, HUVECs cultured under static conditions inherently exhibit a pro-inflammatory phenotype resembling disturbed flow regions in vivo. The ability of THCu to revert this signature suggests a potent anti-inflammatory effect, consistent with the known role of NO in suppressing NF-κB and adhesion molecule expression. However, whether this protection is solely dependent on the PI3K/AKT/eNOS axis or involves auxiliary pathways requires confirmation in multicellular or in vivo animal models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study delineates a clear molecular pathway through which THCu enhances endothelial function. As illustrated in Fig. 10, THCu initiates a signaling cascade by promoting the phosphorylation and activation of PI3K and its downstream target, AKT. Activated AKT then directly phosphorylates eNOS, boosting its enzymatic activity and leading to increased NO production. This rise in NO exerts multiple vasoprotective effects, including promoting endothelial-dependent vascular dilation while simultaneously inhibiting key pathological events such as platelet adhesion, smooth muscle proliferation, and platelet aggregation. The study also provides a compelling hypothesis for THCu’s nuanced effects on cell metabolism and angiogenesis and establishes the clinical relevance of this pathway in human atherosclerosis. These comprehensive findings provide a strong rationale for the continued investigation of THCu as a promising bioactive compound aimed at preventing or reversing endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis.
