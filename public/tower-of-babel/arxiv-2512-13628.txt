Certified-Everlasting Quantum NIZK Proofs
Nikhil Pappu nikpappu@pdx.edu

Abstract
We study non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs (NIZKs) for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} satisfying: 1) statistical soundness, 2) computational zero-knowledge and 3) certified-everlasting zero-knowledge (CE-ZK). The CE-ZK property allows a verifier of a quantum proof to revoke the proof in a way that can be checked (certified) by the prover. Conditioned on successful certification, the verifier’s state can be efficiently simulated with only the statement, in a statistically indistinguishable way. Our contributions regarding these certified-everlasting NIZKs (CE-NIZKs) are as follows: • We identify a barrier to obtaining CE-NIZKs in the CRS model via generalizations of known interactive proofs that satisfy CE-ZK. • We circumvent this by constructing CE-NIZK from black-box use of NIZK for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} satisfying certain properties, along with OWFs. As a result, we obtain CE-NIZKs for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} in the CRS model, based on polynomial hardness of the learning with errors (LWE) assumption. • In addition, we observe that the aforementioned barrier does not apply to the shared EPR model. Consequently, we present a CE-NIZK for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} in this model based on any statistical binding hidden-bits generator, which can be based on LWE. The only quantum computation in this protocol involves single-qubit measurements of the shared EPR pairs.

Introduction

1.1
Background

A non-interactive zero-knowledge proof (NIZK)
[
25
]
allows a

prover
to demonstrate the validity of a statement to a

verifier
with a
single
message, without revealing
any other information. As an example, if Alice knows a solution to a
Sudoku puzzle, she can convince Bob that the puzzle is indeed
solvable, without leaking the solution itself. Crucially, if the
puzzle does not actually have a solution, then a cheating Alice should not be
able to fool Bob, a guarantee formally known as
soundness
. On
the other hand, the inability of a cheating Bob to learn any additional
information is referred to as
zero-knowledge
. This latter
property is formalized by the existence of an efficient algorithm
called a simulator, that can recreate Bob’s view using the statement
alone. Intuitively, it captures the fact that whatever Bob learns
from a proof, he can compute efficiently without the
proof.

NIZKs have been a cornerstone of cryptography with widespread
theoretical and practical impact. For example,
they play an important role in constructions of CCA-secure PKE

[
67
]
, digital signatures
[
21
]
,
ZAPs
[
37
]
,
round-efficient MPC
[
12
]
,
and functional encryption
[
41
]
to name a few. On the
practical front, they have been instrumental in designing anonymous credentials

[
20
]
, group signatures
[
26
]
,
cryptocurrencies
[
76
]
and verifiable computation

[
42
]
.

Setup Models.

It is well-known that NIZKs suffer a
drawback compared to their multi-round (interactive) counterparts.
Specifically, they are impossible to obtain in the plain model

[
43
]
. Fortunately, this can be circumvented in a fairly
realistic
setup model called the common reference string (CRS) model. Here, a
trusted party samples a random (possibly non-uniform) public string
before the protocol, and security is required to hold with high
probability over the choice of CRS.
Other commonly studied setup models include the designated verifier

[
73
]
and random oracle models
[
22
]
.

In the quantum world, recent works
[
65
,
3
]
have
considered two-party protocols where the parties share different
registers of an entangled quantum state. These are sometimes known to
provide stronger guarantees than the CRS model
[
17
]
.
Recently, more complex yet localized quantum setups have also
garnered attention in two-party protocols
[
6
,
63
]
.

Unbounded Adversaries.

Due to their widespread use cases,
NIZKs satisfying various security properties have been explored. Of
these, perhaps the most important characterization is in regards to
whether security holds against an unbounded adversary, or only a
computationally-bounded one. For instance,
soundness
typically
only requires that an
efficient
malicious prover cannot
convince the verifier of a
false
statement. Likewise, the
standard notion of
zero-knowledge
requires that an

efficient

verifier learns nothing
from the transcript. However, one can consider upgrades of both of
these in the form of
statistical soundness
and

statistical zero-knowledge
which provide security against an
unbounded prover and an unbounded distinguisher (equivalently, the
verifier in the NIZK case) respectively. Hence, many NIZKs in the
literature satisfy these stronger properties

[
24
,
38
,
43
,
45
,
75
,
23
,
32
,
71
,
81
]
.

Statistical Soundness or Zero-Knowledge?.

Although statistical soundness
and statistical zero-knowledge are both achievable from standard assumptions, it is
known to be impossible to obtain both simultaneously
1
1

1

One can
consider strong setups where this is false, such as the secret-parameter model
[
69
]
.
, except for a
small subclass of
\mathsf{NP}
languages
[
69
]
. In-fact, this is the case
even for the case of interactive-protocols
[
40
]
, with evidence
that quantum protocols do not help either
[
62
]
. Consequently, one
has to choose which of these two properties to give up in favor of the
other, depending on the application. Typically, statistical zero-knowledge
is preferred as soundness only needs to hold during the protocol
execution, while zero-knowledge can be compromised anytime in the
future to leak sensitive information. Still, this is not always
ideal. Consider a large corporation (prover) that might have various means to
cut down the time to break certain computational assumptions, with the
verifier being an average user. In this case, a computational bounded
assumption on the verifier could be more reasonable. A statistically
sound NIZK can also remove the need to refresh the CRS
periodically in such a setting. However, we then
have other problems, such as the verifier being able to sell the data
to a rival corporation, who might have the means to eventually exploit it.

A Quantum Compromise.

Such a
tradeoff seems inevitable in the classical setting, but previous works
have proposed a novel compromise using quantum resources, in the
context of interactive zero-knowledge
[
49
,
18
]
. In
particular, the prover and verifier engage in a statistically-sound
quantum protocol, after which the verifier is asked to return a

certificate of deletion
. Then, an everlasting
guarantee akin to statistical zero-knowledge holds, given that the
certificate is verified to be valid. Note that this only makes sense
in a quantum world due to the no-cloning theorem. If the proof were
classical, the verifier can simply make a copy of it that can be
retained indefinitely.

Observe also that the requirement to
delete the proof does not interfere with verification. This is because
verification can be performed first without disturbing the state (by
gentle measurement), and the proof can be deleted having served its
purpose. Another interesting aspect is that the verifier can
immediately verify and revoke the proof, which can then be checked.
As a result, the parties need not maintain the
state for long unlike other primitives with
certified-deletion. This can be quite useful due to the difficulty of
maintaining quantum states over long periods.

The aforementioned works then showed
that such proofs can be obtained for all languages in QMA (the quantum
generalization of
\mathsf{NP}
) assuming only OWFs, or even weaker
quantum-assumptions such as the existence of pseudo-random states

[
51
]
.
Naturally, one can
consider the non-interactive analogue where the prover sends a single
message to the verifier, and the verifier sends back a deletion
certificate. While one might argue this is no better than a two-round
protocol, the deployed applications wouldn’t be slowed down by the
second message, which could be batched and sent at a later point.
Hence, at the expense of using quantum resources and relying on the
ability to penalize a non-confirming verifier, there exists the
possibility of obtaining statistically-sound NIZKs with an everlasting
zero-knowledge guarantee. Despite this hope, and
the construction of several advanced certified-everlasting primitives
in recent years (See
Section
1.3
), CE-NIZKs are yet to be realized.
In this work, we study this possibility in detail.

1.2
Contributions

1.

Definitions and Impossibility.
We first present
definitions of certified-everlasting NIZK proofs (CE-NIZKs) in the CRS
and shared-EPR models. Then, we examine natural
approaches to constructing CE-NIZKs in the CRS model. Specifically, we
identify a class of protocols we call
deletion-resistant

CE-NIZKs. At a high level, these admit a deletion algorithm that
splits the proof state into two registers, one that passes
verification of deletion, and another that passes proof verification.
We then rule out the existence of
such protocols by showing that they imply NIZKs that are both
statistically-sound and statistically zero-knowledge. We also argue
that non-interactive counterparts of known interactive
protocols fall into this category.

2.

Feasibility in the CRS Model.
As a result of this barrier, we
provide a unique approach to constructing CE-NIZKs in the CRS model,
where we employ two-levels of NIZK proofs that are generated in
quantum-superposition. In order to prove security, we employ the
two-slot technique
[
67
]
and the OR proof strategy

[
38
]
, and reduce to a certified-deletion
theorem of BB84 states
[
18
]
. This allows us to construct a
CE-NIZK for
\mathsf{NP}
based on black-box use of a quantum-secure NIZK
proof for
\mathsf{NP}
2
2

2

We
require two special notions from the NIZK: quantum-secure adaptive
zero-knowledge and post-quantum non-adaptive zero-knowledge with statistical CRS
indistinguishability which are satisfied by the
LWE-based NIZKs of
[
82
,
81
]
.

(Definition
3.5
), along with just post-quantum OWFs.
We observe that
quantum-secure NIZKs (different from post-quantum ones) are implied by
LWE due to the construction of
[
81
]
. Hence, we
obtain our result based on LWE, matching
the state-of-the-art assumption for post-quantum NIZK.

3.

Bypassing the Impossibility with Shared Entanglement.
We
then explore a natural quantum setup model where the prover and
verifier share entanglement in the form of halves of EPR pairs.
Surprisingly, we find that the aforementioned impossibility no longer
applies to this setting. As a consequence, we show that a
generalization of the hidden-bits compiler of
[
73
]

suffices to obtain CE-NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
in this setting. In particular, we obtain
CE-NIZKs based on any statistically binding hidden-bits generator

[
73
]
, which can be based on LWE

[
81
]
. Although the assumption is the same as our CRS
protocol, the protocol is highly efficient in regards to the quantum
operations involved. For comparison, our CRS protocol generates and
verifies proofs in superposition of a highly entangled state. On the
other hand, this protocol only requires single-qubit measurements of
the EPR halves in one of two basis, and uses classical computation and
communication otherwise.

1.3
Related Work

Certified-Everlasting Cryptography.

The pioneering work of Broadbent and Islam
[
28
]
constructed
encryption with certified-deletion, where ciphertexts can be deleted
in a way that the plaintext becomes unrecoverable (by an unbounded
adversary) even if the secret-key is leaked later. This was followed
by a series of works on certified-everlasting security, which refers
to a bounded adversary that cannot break some
guarantee in the future using unbounded computation, assuming it provides a valid
certificate during the computationally bounded stage. We
discuss these works briefly as follows.

The work of Hiroka, Morimae, Nishimaki and Yamakawa
[
49
]

constructed a certified-everlasting statistical-binding commitment
scheme and used it to obtain a certified-everlasting zero-knowledge proof
system for QMA. Later, the work of Bartusek and Khurana

[
18
]
introduced an
elegant compiler for obtaining primitives with certified-deletion.
Through it, they obtained PKE, ABE, FHE, commitments (and ZK via

[
49
]
), witness encryption, timed-release encryption, MPC
protocols and secret-sharing schemes with certified-everlasting
security. The work of Hiroka et al.
[
48
]
constructed public-key
functional encryption (and other variants), compute-and-compare
obfuscation and garbled circuits with such security guarantees.
Advanced secret-sharing schemes with certified-everlasting security
were studied in the work of Bartusek and Raizes
[
19
]
.
The work of Champion,
Kitagawa, Nishimaki and Yamakawa
[
33
]
constructed a certified-everlasting
variant of untelegraphable encryption, a relaxation of unclonable
encryption. A notion of differing inputs obfuscation (dIO) with
certified-everlasting security was introduced and constructed in the
work of Bartusek et al.
[
14
]
. Their construction allows
for publicly-verifiable deletion, which is an important property
explored by several works
[
59
,
16
,
15
,
53
]
.

Quantum-Enabled NIZKs.

In recent years, several works have
studied NIZKs with interesting properties that are only feasible in a
quantum world. However, these are quite different from our notion of
certified-everlasting NIZK. In particular, these works construct
schemes that prevent copying attacks, i.e., they ensure that the
recipient of a proof cannot convince several other verifiers, without
itself knowing a witness. These “unclonable” NIZKs and variants were
studied in the concurrent works of Jawale and Khurana

[
50
]
and
Goyal, Malavolta and Raizes
[
44
]
. A related notion was
explored by the work of Abbaszadeh and Katz
[
1
]
where the
recipient must generate a valid proof of deletion, and cannot convince
another verifier thereafter. While the unclonable variants were shown
to imply quantum money, this certified-deletion variant was obtained
from standard assumptions. The work of Çakan, Goyal and Raizes

[
31
]

studied NIZK with a stronger form of certified-deletion called
certified-deniability. Intuitively, this is a simulation-based notion
that guarantees that the state after deletion of the proof could be
computed without the proof itself. The work presents a feasibility for
this in the random oracle model, along with evidence against its
existence in the plain model. Finally, the work of
[
47
]

studies a notion of one-time security for NIZK and presents a
construction from iO and LWE. This notions allows an authority to
distribute quantum proving tokens to a prover. Then, the prover can
only convince a verifier of as many statements as the number of tokens
received. This notion is shown to imply public-key quantum money which
justifies the use of the strong assumption of iO. We emphasize that
all of these quantum NIZK notions are orthogonal to that of certified-everlasting
zero-knowledge which we study. For a detailed comparison of our
work with some of these works, see Section
2.6
.

Revocable Cryptography.

Revocable cryptography is closely
related to the notions discussed above in that it involves a
deletion (revocation) phase, after which certain
information/capability is lost. This was pioneered by the work of
Unruh
[
80
]

which introduced revocable time-released encryption. Revocable notions
for encryption/signatures were also studied in
[
9
,
64
]
, and are
similar to the notions of certified-deletion in the sense that
secret data is being deleted. On the other hand, a long line of works
on secure software leasing
[
8
,
58
,
29
,
36
]
and secure key leasing
[
60
,
10
,
4
,
72
,
7
,
34
,
55
,
57
,
56
,
54
]

consider the following high-level premise. A user is provided a
quantum state that can be used to evaluate some software or
cryptographic functionality. Later, the user can be asked to provide a
deletion-certificate, after which it is guaranteed that they can no
longer evaluate the functionality. We remark that
almost all these schemes only consider a computationally-bounded
second-stage (post-deletion) adversary. This restriction is necessary in
several contexts like the leasing of decryption keys of a PKE scheme.

Technical Overview

2.1
Defining Certified-Everlasting NIZKs

We will begin by defining the syntax of a certified-everlasting
non-interactive zero-knowledge proof (CE-NIZK) (Definition

4.1
). A CE-NIZK for a
language
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
is a tuple of four algorithms
(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Cert})
described as follows. The classical algorithm

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
outputs a common reference string
\mathsf{crs}
as usual.
The quantum prover’s algorithm
\mathsf{P}
on input the CRS
\mathsf{crs}
, a statement
x\in\mathcal{L}
and corresponding witness
\omega\in R_{\mathcal{L}}(x)
, outputs a quantum
proof state
\sigma
, along with a (possibly classical) quantum
state
\rho_{\mathsf{P}}
, which will later be used to validate returned
proofs. The quantum verifier’s algorithm
\mathsf{V}
on input
\mathsf{crs}
,
x
and proof

\sigma
outputs a decision bit
b
along with a quantum state
\rho_{\mathsf{V}}
.
The quantum certification algorithm
\mathsf{Cert}

takes as input quantum states
\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{\mathsf{V}}
and outputs
\top
(accept)
or
\bot
(reject). The context is that the verifier would verify the
validity of the proof
\sigma
to obtain
\rho_{\mathsf{V}}
(which would be close in trace-distance
due to completeness and gentle-measurement) and send it to the prover who checks
that the proof is revoked by running
\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{\mathsf{V}})
.
Note that when we refer to a CE-NIZK for
\mathsf{NP}
, we mean one that is a CE-NIZK
for an NP-complete language.

We require a CE-NIZK for
\mathsf{NP}
to satisfy the notions of completeness,
statistical soundness and computational zero-knowledge which must also hold
for a standard NIZK proof for
\mathsf{NP}
. In addition, it must also
satisfy the notion of certified-everlasting zero-knowledge (CE-ZK).
Intuitively, the notion guarantees that the view of any QPT malicious
verifier
\mathsf{V}^{*}
can be simulated in a statistically close manner
with just the statement
x
, conditioned on the fact that
certification is successful. More formally, for every QPT malicious verifier

\mathsf{V}^{*}
, there must exist a QPT simulator
\mathsf{Sim}_{\mathsf{V}^{*}}
such that the
following guarantee holds for every true statement
x
and
corresponding witness
\omega
:

\left[\widetilde{\rho}\approx_{s}\rho^{\prime}\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)\\
&\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}\leftarrow\mathsf{V}^{*}(\mathsf{crs},x,\sigma)\\
&\textrm{If}\;\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}})=\top\;\textrm{then}\;\widetilde{\rho}\coloneq\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}.\\
&\textrm{Else}\;\widetilde{\rho}\coloneq\bot.\\
&\rho^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{\mathsf{V}^{*}}(x)\end{array}\right]

Let us now explain this notion. First, a
\mathsf{crs}
is sampled using

\mathsf{Setup}
and a real proof
\sigma
and prover state
\rho_{\mathsf{P}}
are
computed. Then, the proof
\sigma
is provided to the malicious
verifier, who outputs a bi-partite state
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
. Then, the notion
sets a state
\widetilde{\rho}
to be
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
(the residual state on register

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}
) only if the state
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}}
on register
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}
passes
certification by
\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}})
. If not,
\widetilde{\rho}
is simply set to

\bot
, thereby giving a distinguisher no additional information when
the certification is unsuccessful. Then, we require that the
simulator
\mathsf{Sim}_{\mathsf{V}^{*}}
on input
x
produces a simulated state

\rho^{\prime}
that is close in trace distance to
\widetilde{\rho}
from the real
execution. Note that our protocols will come with a simulator that makes
black-box use of
\mathsf{V}^{*}
, but we define a non-black-box one here for
generality.

2.2
Non-Existence of Deletion-Resistant Protocols

Before we introduce our approach to realizing CE-NIZKs, we will
discuss some challenges in achieving them.
Specifically, we identify a natural class of CE-NIZKs that
are impossible to construct. The intuition is that these come with a
deletion algorithm that splits the state into two, the first which passes
prover certification, and a second which passes
\mathsf{NP}
verification.
Consequently, this operation can be performed at
the prover’s end before sending the second part to the verifier.
We observe
that these imply NIZKs which are both statistically sound and statistically
zero-knowledge which are unlikely to exist. We define this subclass of CE-NIZKs
formally as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Deletion-Resistant CE-NIZKs)
.

A non-interactive
protocol with the syntax of CE-NIZK (Definition
4.1
) is
said to be a deletion-resistant CE-NIZK if it satisfies
the CE-ZK property, along with the following two properties. There exist
QPT algorithms
\mathsf{V}^{*},\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}
such that the
following conditions hold for every
x\in\mathcal{L}
,
\omega\in R_{\mathcal{L}}(x)
and

\widetilde{x}\notin\mathcal{L}
:

\Pr\left[\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\big(\mathsf{crs},x,\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}})[1]\to 1\land\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}})\to\top\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)\\
&\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}\leftarrow\mathsf{V}^{*}(\sigma)\end{array}\right]\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

AND

\Pr_{\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})}\left[\exists\widetilde{\sigma}:\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}(\mathsf{crs},\widetilde{x},\widetilde{\sigma})[1]\to 1\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

Theorem 2.2
.

If there exists a deletion-resistant CE-NIZK for a language
\mathcal{L}
,
then there exists a quantum NIZK protocol for
\mathcal{L}
satisfying
statistical soundness and statistical zero-knowledge.

Proof.

Let
\Pi
be a deletion-resistant CE-NIZK with the syntax of a CE-NIZK
for a language
\mathcal{L}
. This means it has algorithms
(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Cert})
. Furthermore, there exist QPT algorithms

\mathsf{V}^{*},\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}
satisfying the conditions of the theorem by assumption.
We now define the following prover
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
:

\underline{\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)}
:

•

Compute
(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)
.

•

Compute
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}\leftarrow\mathsf{V}^{*}(\sigma)
.

•

Output
(\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}},\rho_{\mathsf{P}})
.

Observe now that the NIZK proof
\widetilde{\Pi}\coloneq(\mathsf{Setup},\widetilde{\mathsf{P}},\widetilde{\mathsf{V}})
is
statistically-sound due to the second condition of the theorem, and
complete from the first condition. Next, we utilize the assumed CE-ZK
property to argue statistical zero-knowledge. Consider the
aforementioned malicious QPT verifier
\mathsf{V}^{*}
attacking the CE-ZK
property of
\Pi
. By the first condition of the theorem, we have that

\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}})\to\top
with overwhelming probability. Let us post-select on
this outcome and let
\widetilde{\rho}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
be the resulting state on register

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}
. By the CE-ZK
property,
\widetilde{\rho}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
can be statistically simulated. Also, from
the gentle-measurement principle,
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
can be statistically
simulated as well. This means that
\widetilde{\Pi}
satisfies statistical
zero-knowledge, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

We remark that an analogous implication follows for protocols with
weaker certified-everlasting witness-privacy notions, such as
a certified-everlasting variant of non-interactive witness-indistinguishable (NIWI) proofs.

2.3
On Generalizations of Prior Work

We now recall previous works on CE-ZK proofs in the interactive
setting by Hiroka et al.
[
49
]
and
Bartusek and Khurana
[
18
]
. The work of Hiroka et al. constructed
CE-ZK proofs for languages in QMA based on a new primitive they
introduced called commitments with
statistical binding and certified-everlasting hiding. The
certified-everlasting hiding property refers to the fact that after
deletion, even an unbounded receiver is unable to learn anything about
the contents of the commitment. While such commitments were
constructed in the random oracle model in their work, Bartusek and
Khurana constructed them from any statistical binding commitment
scheme in the plain model. Note that such statistically binding
commitments can be based on OWFs
[
68
]
and from potentially
weaker quantum-cryptographic assumptions
[
66
,
11
]
.

We now argue that these protocols do not generalize easily for
constructing CE-NIZKs. At a high level, the CE-ZK proofs established
by these works rely on the following template:

Template for CE-ZK Proofs:

•

First, the prover sends some
n=\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,|x|,|w|)
commitments
\ket{\mathsf{com}^{1}},\ldots,\ket{\mathsf{com}^{n}}
to the
verifier, where
\{\ket{\mathsf{com}^{i}}\}_{i\in[n]}
correspond to the
certified-everlasting quantum commitment scheme.

•

Then, the verifier sends a randomly chosen opening set
S\subset[n]
to the prover, along with deletion certificates

\{\mathsf{cert}^{i}\}_{i\in[n]\setminus S}
corresponding to the commitments
that are not to be opened.

•

Finally, the prover sends the openings of the
commitments
\{\mathsf{open}^{i}\}_{i\in S}
for the set
S
. These openings are
then sufficient for the verifier to determine the validity of the
statement.

Note that while these works use classical certificates

\{\mathsf{cert}^{i}\}_{i\in[n]\setminus S}
, the same ideas also apply if
these certificates were quantum. The main idea here is that since
the unopened commitments are deleted by the verifier, even an
unbounded distinguisher cannot tell apart the real and simulated
views. Naturally, one might expect that a
Fiat-Shamir
[
39
,
32
,
71
]
based approach in the CRS model could be employed to turn these interactive
protocols into non-interactive ones.
However, we argue that such protocols would be deletion-resistant, and
hence run into Theorem
2.2
. To see this, consider a CE-NIZK
\Pi=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Cert})
with a similar template, where the
prover
\mathsf{P}
determines the opening set
S
by itself. Since
\mathsf{V}

would only need to check the commitments in
S
as part of the
protocol, we can consider
\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\coloneq\mathsf{V}
and a malicious QPT
verifier
\mathsf{V}^{*}
that splits the commitments in
S
and the
commitments in
[n]\setminus S
into two different registers.
Consequently, the state
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}
produced by

\mathsf{V}^{*}
is such that
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}}}
would pass certification while
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}

would be accepted by
\mathsf{V}
. Note that designing
\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Cert}
to
artificially deviate from the template doesn’t help. For instance,

\mathsf{V}
clearly cannot check all the commitments, since the proof
system should satisfy computational zero-knowledge. Moreover, even if

\mathsf{Cert}
requires all the commitments to be revoked,
\mathsf{V}^{*}
can
easily clone the contents of the opened commitments.

We note that natural attempts at generalizing the hidden-bits
paradigm
[
38
]
to the CE-NIZK setting do not work, due to a
similar argument. Despite this barrier, we show that CE-NIZKs are
indeed possible to construct in the CRS model from standard
assumptions. We now discuss this feasibility result.

2.4
Feasibility in the CRS Model

The high-level idea of our construction is to utilize an
unclonable quantum state that is entangled with NIZK proofs. The hope
is that this prevents the splitting attack discussed in the previous
section. We require several concrete ideas to realize this, which
we present as follows.
The main ingredient we require is a classical NIZK proof
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}=(\mathsf{Setup}_{\mathsf{nizk}},\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{nizk}},\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}})
for
\mathsf{NP}
. Our
construction utilizes this NIZK scheme at two-levels, with an “inner”
proof that is provided in classically encrypted form, and “outer”
proofs that are entangled with a BB84 (Wiesner) state. The inner proof

\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
is the actual NIZK proof, generated as
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}\leftarrow\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\omega)
for a statement-witness pair
(x,\omega)
and a CRS sampled as
\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(1^{\lambda})
. The
problem is that if
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
were given out in the clear, then the
protocol cannot satisfy CE-ZK as
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
is
statistically-sound. To remedy this, we sample
\ell
-many BB84 quantum
states of the form
\ket{y^{i}}^{\theta^{i}}\coloneq H^{\theta^{i}[1]}\ket{y^{i}[1]}\otimes\ldots\otimes H^{\theta^{i}[\lambda]}\ket{y^{i}[\lambda]}
where
y^{i},\theta^{i}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
,
y^{i}[j]
denotes the
j
-th bit of

y^{i}
,
H
is the Hadamard transform, and
\ell
is the size of

\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
. Consider now the
\ell\lambda
-qubit BB84 state

\ket{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}}^{\theta}=\sum_{\mathsf{z}}\alpha^{\mathsf{z}}\ket{\mathsf{z}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}}
where
y\coloneq y^{1}\|\ldots\|y^{\ell}
and
\theta^{1}\coloneq\theta^{1}\|\ldots\|\theta^{\ell}
. Let us now define a function
f
and a value
\mathsf{pad}
such
that
\mathsf{pad}\coloneq f(\theta,y)\coloneq\bigoplus_{j:\theta^{1}[j]=0}y^{1}[j]\|\ldots\|\bigoplus_{j:\theta^{\ell}[j]=0}y^{\ell}[j]
. In other words, each of the

\ell
-bits of
\mathsf{pad}
are obtained by taking the XOR of the
computational basis positions of the corresponding
\lambda
-size “block” of the
BB84 state
\ket{y}^{\theta}
. Then, instead of
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
being
provided to the adversary directly, it is one-time pad encrypted as

\pi_{\mathsf{in}}\oplus\mathsf{pad}
. The purpose of this one-time
pad will be clear later, when we invoke a certified-deletion theorem
of BB84 states by
[
18
]
to ensure that
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
is
information-theoretically removed from the verifier’s view.
Note also that in actuality, we need to provide an OTP encryption

\mathsf{ct}\coloneq\pi_{\mathsf{in}}\oplus\mathsf{pad}\oplus\mathsf{k}
for
\mathsf{k}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
instead of just

\pi_{\mathsf{in}}\oplus\mathsf{pad}
due to some technicalities in invoking the
certified-deletion theorem of
[
18
]
.

The important aspect here is that every term
\mathsf{z}
in the
superposition of the quantum state
\ket{y}^{\theta}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}}
can be used to compute the common
value
\mathsf{pad}
using
\theta
as
\mathsf{pad}=f(\theta,\mathsf{z})
. We leverage
this fact to produce the “outer” proofs
\pi_{\mathsf{out}}^{\mathsf{z}}
in
superposition as
\ket{\psi}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}\coloneq\sum\limits_{\mathsf{z}}\ket{\mathsf{z}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}}\ket{\pi^{\mathsf{z}}_{\mathsf{out}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}
.
In particular, each
\pi^{\mathsf{z}}_{\mathsf{out}}
proves the following
\mathsf{NP}
statement
x^{\mathsf{z}}
:

\underline{\text{Statement}\;x^{\mathsf{z}}}:\;\;\exists\big(\theta,\mathsf{k}\big)\;:\;\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}\Big(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\mathsf{ct}\oplus f(\theta,\mathsf{z})\oplus\mathsf{k}\Big)=1

We observe that by proving the statement
x^{\mathsf{z}}
, the proof
\pi^{\mathsf{z}}

also proves the validity of the statement
x
. We argue this using the

statistical adaptive
soundness property of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
. First,
observe that completeness follows from the fact that
\mathsf{ct}\oplus f(\theta,\mathsf{z})\oplus\mathsf{k}=\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
for each
\mathsf{z}
in superposition
of
\ket{\psi}
, and
\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\pi_{\mathsf{in}})=1
follows
from the completeness of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
. Now, consider the case when
x

is false, which means there is no accepting proof
\widetilde{\pi}_{\mathsf{in}}
that
makes
\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\cdot)
accept (whp over choice of

\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}}
). As a result, all of
the derived statements
x^{\mathsf{z}}
would also be false and hence would be
rejected whp by
\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{out}},x^{\mathsf{z}},\cdot)
where
\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{out}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
is another part of the full CRS
\mathsf{crs}\coloneq\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}}\|\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{out}}
. Observe that if
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
were only
computationally sound (which we don’t consider), then such an
argument doesn’t make sense as there would exist accepting proofs for
false statements as well. Notice also that adaptive soundness
ensures that even if the statements
x^{\mathsf{z}}
are based on
\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{out}}
,
soundness still holds. Furthermore, computational zero-knowledge
follows due to the fact that
(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},\pi_{\mathsf{in}})
can be replaced
with
(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{Sim}},\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}})
which are guaranteed by the computational
zero-knowledge of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
. Let us now focus on the
certified-everlasting zero-knowledge (CE-ZK) property.

The rough idea is to reduce to the aforementioned deletion theorem for
BB84 states by Bartusek and Khurana. Intuitively, the theorem allows
us to replace each bit of the plaintext
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
inside the OTP encrypted
ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
with a bit of our choice in a statistically
indistinguishable way, conditioned on the fact that the BB84 state

\ket{y}^{\theta}
is verifiably returned. In order to enforce such a
revocation, we require the verifier send back the original state

\ket{\psi}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}
and have the prover uncompute
the proofs on the
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}
register, followed by verifying if the
resulting state is equivalent to
\ket{y}^{\theta}
or not. However,
there is an important detail involved in invoking their deletion
theorem. It only guarantees security if the state provided to the
adversary computationally hides the basis information
\theta
.
However, the problem with the real proofs
\pi^{\mathsf{z}}_{\mathsf{out}}
is that they
require
(\theta,\mathsf{k})
as the witness. Fortunately, this is exactly
what the zero-knowledge guarantee of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
for the outer proofs
provides us, allowing to simulate the proof state without access to

(\theta,\mathsf{k})
. However, the traditional notion of computational
zero-knowledge is insufficient, as the outer proofs are generated for
a superposition of statements wrt the same CRS. We observe that such a
quantum-secure zero-knowledge notion (first explored in

[
2
]
) is satisfied by the LWE-based NIZKs of

[
82
,
81
]
. Apart from this quantum-secure ZK
property, we need one more special property from the underlying NIZK.
In particular, we require that the marginal distribution of the CRS
output by the non-adaptive ZK simulator of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}

as
(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{Sim}},\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}})\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(x)
, is statistically close to the real
CRS distribution. Once again, we find that NIZKs obtained via the
hidden-bits paradigm such as the LWE ones mentioned above satisfy this
property. On the other hand, we find that LWE-based NIZKs obtained via
the Fiat-Shamir approach
[
32
,
71
]
do not
satisfy this property as their simulator samples a “fake” key for a
keyed correlation-intractable hash function, that is only
computationally indistinguishable from a real key used in the
construction. It is also unclear if these Fiat-Shamir based NIZKs satisfy quantum-secure
ZK. Equipped with these two special properties of
\Pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}
, we now
explain how CE-ZK is proved.

The main idea is to swap out the plaintext
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
bit-by-bit
into a simulated proof
\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}}
generated by
(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{Sim}},\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}})\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(x)
, in a statistically indistinguishably way using the deletion
theorem. Note that we would first switch
\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}}
into
\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{Sim}}

due to the aforementioned statistical CRS indistinguishability. The
problem with this argument is that the moment a bit of
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
is
flipped to get
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}^{\prime}
, there is no guarantee that the
computational zero-knowledge of the proofs
\pi^{\mathsf{z}}_{\mathsf{out}}
holds as

\pi_{\mathsf{in}}^{\prime}
need not be accepted by
\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\cdot)
.
Consequently, we employ the two-slot technique
[
67
]

also used in
[
14
]
. Note however, that our setting is
quite different
from the iO centric context of
[
14
]
.
Hence, we actually utilize a
2\ell\lambda
qubit BB84 state

\ket{y}^{\theta}
which
encodes two pads
\mathsf{pad}^{0},\mathsf{pad}^{1}
and provide two ciphertexts

\mathsf{ct}^{0}\coloneq\pi_{\mathsf{in}}\oplus\mathsf{pad}^{0}\oplus\mathsf{k}^{0}
and
\mathsf{ct}^{1}\coloneq 0^{\ell}\oplus\mathsf{pad}^{1}\oplus\mathsf{k}^{1}
. Moreover, the statements
x^{\mathsf{z}}

corresponding to the superposition terms of the BB84 state are
modified as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{\text{Statement}\;x^{\mathsf{z}}}:\;\;\exists\big(\theta,\mathsf{k}^{0},\mathsf{k}^{1}\big)\;:\;\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}\Big(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\mathsf{ct}^{0}

\displaystyle\oplus f^{0}(\theta,\mathsf{z})\oplus\mathsf{k}^{0}\Big)=1

OR

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}\Big(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{in}},x,\mathsf{ct}^{1}

\displaystyle\oplus f^{1}(\theta,\mathsf{z})\oplus\mathsf{k}^{1}\Big)=1

where
f^{0}
is defined similar to
f
for the first
\ell
-qubits
(which encode
\mathsf{pad}^{0}
)
while
f^{1}
corresponds to the next
\ell
-qubits (which encode

\mathsf{pad}^{1}
). Due to the nature
of this OR proof, we are able to swap the
0^{\ell}
plaintext
in
\mathsf{ct}^{1}
into a simulated proof
\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}}
one bit at a time. We no
longer have the aforementioned issue as
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
inside
\mathsf{ct}^{0}
is
sufficient to satisfy the OR proof, and hence invoke quantum-secure
zero-knowledge of the outer proofs. Then, once
\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}}
is placed in

\mathsf{ct}^{1}
, we can replace
\pi_{\mathsf{in}}
bit-by-bit into
0^{\ell}
since

\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}}
also satisfies
\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{nizk}}(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{Sim}},x,\pi_{\mathsf{Sim}})=1
by
zero-knowledge. Finally, observe that the resulting distribution can be
produced with the statement
x
alone, ensuring that the CE-ZK
property is satisfied. Note that apart from the NIZK for
\mathsf{NP}
we also
rely on a quantum-secure PRF (implied by OWFs) to simulate uniform randomness in
superposition, used to compute and uncompute the NIZK proofs. We also
face the following subtle issue in the reduction to the deletion
theorem of BB84 states. The quantum state returned by the adversary
may also have superposition terms
\widetilde{\mathsf{z}}
such that

(\theta,\mathsf{k}^{0},\mathsf{k}^{1})
is not a valid witness for statement

x^{\widetilde{\mathsf{z}}}
. Consequently, we cannot argue that the state
after uncomputation of real proofs is computationally close to the
state after uncomputation of simulated proofs, based on the
quantum-secure adaptive zero-knowledge property. To circumvent this
issue, we employ the key-testability technique of
[
57
]

based on OWFs. At a high level, this involves producing a kind of
Lamport signature for each term of the BB84 state sent to the
adversary, which can be verified after revocation. Intuitively, this
ensures that the adversary cannot produce a state with different
superposition terms (that have non-negligible amplitude) without
breaking unforgeability of the signature. In summary, we obtain the
following theorem:

Theorem 2.4

(CE-NIZK in the CRS Model)
.

There exist CE-NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
in the CRS model
satisfying 1) statistical soundness, 2)
computational zero-knowledge, and 3) certified-everlasting zero-knowledge,
assuming the existence of OWFs and NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
in the CRS model satisfying 1)
statistical (adaptive) soundness, 2) computational (non-adaptive)
zero-knowledge with statistical CRS indistinguishability, and 3)
quantum-secure (adaptive) zero-knowledge.

Since such NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
are known from LWE due to

[
81
]
, we have the following:

Corollary 2.5

(CE-NIZK from LWE)
.

There exist CE-NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
in the CRS model based on the
polynomial hardness of the learning with errors (LWE) problem.

2.5
Quantum-Efficient Protocol in the Shared EPR Model

Previously, we showed that generalizations of the Fiat-Shamir and
hidden-bits paradigms to the CE-NIZK setting do not work, as they run
into Theorem
2.2
. Surprisingly,
we observe that this impossibility does not apply to the shared EPR
model where the prover and verifier are initialized with halves of
polynomially-many EPR pairs. Such a model has recently garnered
attention in the context of non-interactive MPC
[
3
,
17
]
and NIZK for
QMA
[
17
,
65
]
. Recall that Theorem

2.2
relies on the following idea: a prover could
split the quantum proof the verifier is supposed to receive into two
parts, one which passes certification while the other suffices for
verification. Hence, the prover need only send the part which is
required for verification. This then implies a statistically sound and
statistically ZK proof. In the EPR model, the prover cannot perform
this “split” as it does not control the entire quantum proof state, since
the verifier’s EPR halves do not originate from the prover.

Our observation has some similarities to the work of

[
17
]
, which separated the CRS and shared EPR models in
the context of non-interactive randomized oblivious transfer. In our
case, the separation between the models only applies to the
aforementioned deletion-resistant protocols, as evident from our CRS
feasibility result. However, we demonstrate that this difference is
useful too, by constructing a protocol in the shared EPR model that is
much more efficient in its use of quantum resources. Specifically, our
protocol only involves performing computational or Hadamard basis
single-qubit measurements of the EPR pairs, and classical computation
otherwise. We now present a brief overview of our approach.

We make use of the well-known hidden-bits paradigm
[
38
]
that we adapt
to the shared EPR model for the context of obtaining
certified-everlasting ZK. We note that Fiat-Shamir variants of
previous interactive protocols would also bypass the impossibility in
the EPR model. However, we find the hidden-bits approach to be cleaner
and also helps to avoid the statistically far simulated CRS
issue with
[
32
,
71
]
mentioned earlier. We also
emphasize that we assume a common reference string in addition to the
shared EPRs. This is because it is not clear how to simulate a
structured reference string using EPRs unlike a common random string.
Moreover, statistically sound NIZKs from LWE

[
32
,
71
,
82
,
81
]
are all in the
common reference string model. We believe that the NIZK of Bitansky
and Paneth
[
23
]
based on iO and OWFs would also work for
our purpose, allowing to use EPRs alone as it only requires a common
random string.

The hidden bits paradigm for constructing NIZK utilizes two
components: (1) a hidden bits generator (HBG) and (2) a statistical
sound and statistically zero-knowledge NIZK in the hidden-bits model.
In the (idealized) hidden-bits model, there is a uniformly random string that is
sampled and made available to the prover, but is hidden from the
verifier. Then, the prover has the ability to specify some indices
of its choice to a trusted third party, which then reveals the corresponding
hidden bits to the verifier (and nothing about the other bits). Since
NIZKs in the hidden-bits model are known unconditionally
[
38
]
,
most works focus on designing an HBG. Intuitively, the purpose of the
HBG is to realize the idealized hidden bits in the CRS model. In
particular, it allows the prover
\mathsf{P}
to sample a succinct
commitment
\mathsf{com}
, which along with the CRS, fixes a hidden bit
string. The HBG then allows the prover to provably open certain
positions of this fixed hidden bit string to the verifier.
Crucially, the
statistical-binding
security of the HBG ensures
that the prover is bound to this string, and cannot reveal anything
else to the verifier. On the other hand, the computational hiding
property ensures that any unopened hidden bits are pseudorandom in the
verifier’s view.

Our idea is that instead of determining the actual hidden bits string via
the commitment
\mathsf{com}
and the CRS
\mathsf{crs}
, these are utilized
to fix a common basis string
\theta
for both parties. Then, the
prover would reveal parts of
\theta
to the verifier, and both
parties would obtain the actual hidden bits by measuring their halves
of the EPR pairs in the
\theta
basis. Specifically, the hidden bits
are set to be the XOR of outcomes of computational
basis positions corresponding to different “blocks” of the EPR
pairs. Note that the nature of EPR
pairs ensures that both parties would derive the same measurement
outcomes on measuring in the same basis. Statistical soundness is
ensured due to the observation that we can consider an equivalent
verifier that measures all its EPRs in the computational basis before the
prover is even initialized. Consequently, the prover can only
manipulate the hidden bits via the basis string
\theta
which is
restricted by the HBG commitment
\mathsf{com}
. Note that the inability of
this hypothetical verifier to pass certification is not a problem, as
it is only used as a proof tool to argue soundness. While
computational zero-knowledge follows easily by arguments similar to
prior work
[
73
]
, certified-everlasting zero-knowledge is argued
based on the following fact. The verifier is asked to delete all the
unopened hidden bits by measuring the corresponding EPRs in the Hadamard basis.
Consequently, even if computational hiding of the HBG eventually leaks

\theta
to an unbounded verifier/distinguisher, the
certified-deletion theorem of
[
18
]
can be applied to
ensure that these hidden bits are information-theoretically hidden.
Consequently, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 2.6
.

There exist CE-NIZKs for
\mathsf{NP}
in the Shared EPR Model (Definition

4.2
), assuming the existence of any statistical binding hidden bits
generator (Definition
6.1
).

From the fact that statistical binding HBGs are known from LWE

[
81
]
, and due to the structure of our protocol, we have the
following corollary:

Corollary 2.7
.

Assuming the polynomial hardness of LWE, there exist CE-NIZKs for

\mathsf{NP}
in the shared EPR Model, where the prover and verifier only
perform single-qubit measurements apart from arbitrary classical
computation.

2.6
Relation with Other Quantum-Enabled NIZKs

The works of
[
50
]
and
[
44
]
introduced
unclonable NIZK which is primitive that at a high level, guarantees
the following property. Consider an adversary that is provided with a
quantum proof of some
\mathsf{NP}
statement
x
. Then, if it can produce two
separate quantum proofs that both pass verification, then one can
efficiently extract out a witness
w
for the statement
x
from the
adversary. Intuitively, this ensures that if an adversary only has
the proof and not the witness, it cannot produce more than one valid
proof. Clearly, this is only feasible in the quantum setting due to
the no-cloning principle.
In
[
50
]
, it was also informally mentioned that such
proofs are a
prerequisite for obtaining NIZKs with some form of certified deletion,
while also mentioning certified everlasting security. The intuition is
that if the adversary could clone proof states, then it could delete one of
them successfully while retaining the other. Moreover, these works show that
unclonable NIZK implies public-key quantum money (PKQM). On the other hand,
we establish CE-NIZK in this paper from LWE, which is not known to
imply PKQM. The reasoning is that CE-NIZKs need not be unclonable in
the sense of
[
50
]
, which actually doesn’t require the adversary
to generate two copies of the state. To see this, observe the
following attack on our CE-NIZK in the CRS model that clearly breaks
unclonable security. The adversary on obtaining the proof state

\ket{\psi}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}\otimes{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}=\sum\limits_{\mathsf{t}}\ket{\mathsf{t}}
,
simply applies CNOTs to get the state

\sum\limits_{\mathsf{t}}\ket{\mathsf{t}}\ket{\mathsf{t}}
and gives the two entangled
registers to two verifiers. Since verification is done entirely in the
computational basis, both verifiers would accept the proof. We also
mention that by utilizing coset states along with iO (which is
sufficient for PKQM) instead of BB84
states, one might be able to obtain both unclonability and certified-everlasting
security.

In the related work of
[
1
]
, a NIZK with certified deletion
(NIZK-CD) was obtained from standard assumptions, specifically from any
NIZK (with certain properties) and a statistically binding commitment
scheme. The security notion intuitively ensures that if an adversary
is able to pass deletion and simultaneously produce a valid proof,
then one can efficiently extract a witness from the adversary.
Our construction also seems to at-least intuitively
ensure that an adversary that deletes cannot produce a valid proof.
Consequently, it
might be desirable to formally explore the possibility of a NIZK
satisfying both everlasting security and the inability to prove, with a
single deletion certificate. We also emphasize that it is unclear
whether CE-NIZK implies NIZK-CD. This is because it might be possible
for an adversary to delete the proof of a CE-NIZK scheme, while
somehow retaining information in the form of a different proof that
satisfies statistical zero-knowledge. Consequently, this doesn’t
contradict certified-everlasting security but still allows the
adversary to prove.

2.7
Possible Extensions

1.

Publicly-Verifiable Deletion:
While our construction only
satisfies privately-verifiable deletion, obtaining the publicly-verifiable
variant is more desirable. We believe this should be possible by
employing coset states
[
35
]
instead of BB84 states, along
with iO (by employing techniques from
[
14
]
).
Note that coset states and iO are sufficient to obtain
public-key quantum money (PKQM). However, it is not clear that CE-NIZK with
publicly-verifiable deletion implies PKQM because an efficient
verifier may not be able to exploit the fact that an adversarial state contains
some information about the witness. Still, it is not obvious how to
obtain publicly-verifiable deletion without iO, and we leave it as an
interesting open question.

2.

Classical Certificates
: In our construction in the CRS
model, the verifier needs to send back the quantum proof state after
verification. However, it is ideal to have the verifier send back only
a classical certificate. This also has the advantage that no party has
to maintain a quantum state for long (the prover’s verification key is
classical in our construction). We believe that our scheme can be
upgraded to satisfy this property by utilizing a hybrid (leveled)
quantum fully homomorphic encryption scheme (QFHE) that is known from
LWE
[
61
,
27
]
as used in the works of

[
78
,
79
]
. In particular, the verifier is also
provided with a ciphertext that encrypts
(\theta,\mathsf{k}_{0},\mathsf{k}_{1})

required to perform uncomputation under the encryption. This way, the
verifier can obtain an encryption of the Hadamard basis measurements
of the underlying BB84 state. This latter part also relies on the fact
that hybrid QFHE encryption of a BB84 state also produces a BB84
state. One subtlety is that we cannot directly reduce to the deletion
theorem of
[
18
]
because the reduction cannot be revealed
the QFHE secret key as the ciphertext encrypts
\theta
. However, we believe that
the theorem of
[
18
]
can be extended to handle the case
where the adversary produces an encryption of the certificate, instead
of providing the certificate in the clear.

3.

Certified-Everlasting NIWIs in the Plain Model
: It is
also interesting to consider other non-interactive proof systems such
as NIWIs, which are possible to obtain even in the plain model

[
37
,
13
]
. In the post-quantum setting,
these can be based on post-quantum iO
[
23
]
. In
particular, one can try to obtain a NIWI with statistical soundness along with
computational witness indistinguishability (WI) and
certified-everlasting WI in the plain model.
Our construction seems to be general enough to
work for this case, except that the outer proofs
generated in superposition can no longer be simulated by the computational
zero-knowledge property. Recall that this is an important part of our proof, as it
is needed to invoke the certified deletion theorem. However, it might
be possible just to rely on the witness hiding property to ensure not
all of
(\theta,\mathsf{k}_{0},\mathsf{k}_{1})
is leaked. Then, one might be able
to extend the certified deletion theorem appropriately, in conjunction
with techniques from leakage-resilient cryptography
[
52
,
30
]
.

4.

Fiat-Shamir Approaches and CE-NIZKs for QMA
: In this
work, we focus on obtaining CE-NIZK for
\mathsf{NP}
, due to the challenges
in obtaining NIZK for QMA in the CRS model (See
[
77
]
). On the other
hand, interactive CE-ZK is known for all of QMA
[
49
]
. It would
still be interesting to explore NIZK for QMA with
certified-everlasting properties in other setup models. For
inspiration, the works of
[
77
]
and
[
65
]
show NIZKs for QMA in
the designated-verifier and shared-EPR models respectively, by
utilizing variants of the Fiat-Shamir paradigm.

3.1
Notation and Quantum Information

A pure state
\ket{\psi}
is a vector in a complex Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
with unit norm
\left\lVert\ket{\psi}\right\rVert=1
. An
operator
P
which satisfies
P^{\dagger}=P
is said to be a Hermitian
operator. Let
S(\mathcal{H})
be the set of Hermitian operators on

\mathcal{H}
. A density matrix
\rho\in S(\mathcal{H})
is a
positive semi-definite operator with unit trace
\mathsf{Tr}(\rho)=1
. A
density matrix represents a mixture over pure states, and the density
matrix of a pure state
\ket{\psi}
is
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\,\langle{\psi}|}
. A
Hilbert space can be split into registers
\mathcal{H}\coloneq\mathcal{H}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R_{1}}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R_{2}}}}
. A unitary matrix
U
is one that satisfies

UU^{\dagger}=U^{\dagger}U=\mathbb{I}
. Applying
U
to a state

\ket{\psi}
transforms it into the state
U\ket{\psi}
. For a mixed
state
\rho
, applying
U
transforms it into
U\rho U^{\dagger}
. A
projector
\mathsf{\Pi}
is a Hermitian operator additionally
satisfying
\mathsf{\Pi}^{2}=\mathsf{\Pi}
. The trace distance between
two mixed states
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
is given by

\mathsf{TD}\big(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Tr}(\sqrt{(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})}\big)
. It provides an
upper bound on the distinguishing advantage of
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
by any
quantum algorithm. A quantum polynomial-time (QPT) algorithm refers to
a polynomial-size quantum circuit composed of some universal gate set,
along with some non-uniform advice state
\ket{\psi}
.
We denote the
Hadamard transform/operator by
H
, which is defined as

H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}
.
For
y,\theta\in\{0,1\}^{n}
for some polynomial
n
in
\lambda
, we consider BB84
(Wiesner) states of the following form, where
y_{i},\theta_{i}
denote
the
i

y,\theta
respectively:

\ket{y}^{\theta}\coloneq H^{\theta_{1}}\ket{y_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes H^{\theta_{n}}\ket{y_{n}}

We denote a (possibly quantum) algorithm
\mathsf{P}
with input
1^{\lambda}

and outputs
\mathsf{m},\mathsf{ct}
as
\mathsf{P}(1^{\lambda})\to(\mathsf{m},\mathsf{ct})
or
(\mathsf{m},\mathsf{ct})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(1^{\lambda})
. Here,
\lambda
denotes a security parameter
and
1^{\lambda}\coloneq 1\|\ldots(\lambda\text{ times})\|1
, where

\|
denotes concatenation and
\coloneq
denotes defining (also used
for assigning/substituting). We use
\mathsf{P}(1^{\lambda})[1]
and

\mathsf{P}(1^{\lambda})[2]
to denote the first and second indices of the
output tuple respectively. For a set
S
, by
s\leftarrow S
, we mean
s
to
be sampled uniformly from
S
. For a distribution
D
, by
s\leftarrow D
,
we mean sampling

according to
D
.
The functions
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
and
\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

denote a polynomial and a negligible function in
\lambda

respectively. We use the notation
\mathcal{A}^{\ket{\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},\cdot,\cdot)}}
to denote that the quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
gets
quantum access to a quantum algorithm
\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},\cdot,\cdot)
on
the second and third inputs. For a language
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
, we consider
the relation
R_{\mathcal{L}}
consisting of statement-witness pairs. Hence,

denotes the set of witnesses of a statement

. We denote
quantum registers as
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{A}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}
etc.
We use the notation
y_{i}
to denote the
i
-th bit of a string
y

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{R}}_{i}

i

i

y_{I}

for some set

I

y^{i}
doesn’t mean the
i

y

notation
y^{I}
denotes
\{y^{i}\}_{i\in I}

of
\mathsf{Sim}_{1}
and
\pi_{\mathsf{nizk}}

serif font. Sometimes, we use
\overline{I}

s_{I}\coloneq t_{I}\oplus r_{I}

For a classical algorithm
\mathsf{C}

s

.

.

For the sake of simplicity, we often use
\rho_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{B}}}

An EPR pair is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\ket{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}\ket{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{V}}}+\ket{1}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}}}\ket{1}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{V}}}\big)

For two distributions

D_{0}\equiv D_{1}

Cryptography Basics

Definition 3.2

(Learning with Errors (LWE)
[
74
]
)
.

Consider integers functions
n,m,q\in\mathbb{Z}
of the security
parameter
\lambda
. Let
\chi(\lambda)
be an error
distribution over
\mathbb{Z}
. The LWE assumption is said to hold if the
following holds for every QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
:

\Bigg\lvert\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}\big(A,s^{\intercal}A+e\big)\to 1\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&A\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}_{q}\\
&s\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}\\
&e\leftarrow\chi^{m}\end{array}\right]-\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}\big(A,u\big)\to 1\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&A\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}_{q}\\
&u\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{q}\\
\end{array}\right]\Bigg\rvert\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

Definition 3.3

(Quantum-Secure Pseudo-Random Function)
.

Let
\{\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{prfk}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell_{\mathsf{in}}}\to\{0,1\}^{\ell_{\mathsf{out}}}\}_{\mathsf{prfk}\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}}

be an efficiently computable family of functions, where
\ell_{\mathsf{in}},\ell_{\mathsf{out}}
are some polynomials in
\lambda
. Then,

\{\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{prfk}}\}_{\mathsf{prfk}}
is said to be a quantum-secure PRF if the
following holds for every QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is
the set of all functions with input
\ell_{\mathsf{in}}
and output

\ell_{\mathsf{out}}
:

\bigg\lvert\Pr\left[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\ket{f(\cdot)}}(1^{\lambda})\;\;\big\lvert\;\;f\leftarrow\mathcal{R}\right]-\Pr\left[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\ket{\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{prfk}}(\cdot)}}(1^{\lambda})\;\;\big\lvert\;\;\mathsf{prfk}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\right]\bigg\rvert\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

]

For every

\end{array}\right]

Certified-Everlasting NIZK Proofs

4.1
Common Reference String Model

Definition 4.1

(CE-NIZK)
.

A certified-everlasting non-interactive zero-knowledge
proof (CE-NIZK) in the CRS model for a language
\mathcal{L}
is
a tuple of four algorithms
(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\allowbreak\mathsf{Cert})
with
the following syntax:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\to\mathsf{crs}:
The classical setup algorithm takes
as input a security parameter, and outputs a common reference string (CRS)
\mathsf{crs}
.

•

\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)\to(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}}):
The quantum prover’s
algorithm takes as input a CRS
\mathsf{crs}
, a statement
x\in\mathcal{L}
, and
a witness
\omega\in R_{\mathcal{L}}(x)
. It outputs a quantum proof state
\sigma
and a
residual state
\rho_{\mathsf{P}}
.

•

\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{crs},x,\sigma)\to(b,\rho_{\mathsf{V}}):
The quantum verifier’s
algorithm takes as input the CRS
\mathsf{crs}
, a statement
x\in\mathcal{L}
, and
a quantum proof
\sigma
. It outputs a bit
b
along with a residual state
\rho_{\mathsf{V}}
.

•

\mathsf{Cert}(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{\mathsf{V}})\to\top/\bot:
The
certification algorithm takes as input a prover’s state

\rho_{\mathsf{P}}
and a verifier’s state
\rho_{\mathsf{V}}
. It outputs
\top
(accept) or
\bot
(reject).

A CE-NIZK must satisfy the following properties:

•

Completeness:
The following holds for every
x\in\mathcal{L}
and

\omega\in R_{\mathcal{L}}(x)
:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{V}\big(\mathsf{crs},x,\sigma\big)[1]\to 0\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)\\
\end{array}\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

•

Deletion Correctness:
The following holds for every
x\in\mathcal{L}

and
\omega\in R_{\mathcal{L}}(x)
:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Cert}\big(\rho_{\mathsf{P}},\rho_{\mathsf{V}}\big)\to\bot\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(\sigma,\rho_{\mathsf{P}})\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{crs},x,\omega)\\
&(b,\rho_{\mathsf{V}})\leftarrow\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{crs},x,\sigma)\end{array}\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

•

Statistical Soundness:
For every
\widetilde{x}\notin\mathcal{L}
, the following holds:

\Pr_{\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})}\left[\exists\widetilde{\sigma}:\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{crs},\widetilde{x},\widetilde{\sigma})[1]\to 1\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

•

Computational Zero-Knowledge:
There exists a simulator

\mathsf{Sim}
such that for all

4.2
Shared EPR Model

In this subsection, we will define the notion of certified-everlasting
NIZK in a model where the prover and verifier share entanglement.
Specifically, we consider that they share polynomially many halves of
EPR pairs along with a classical common reference string. This is
because EPR pairs can only simulate a common random string. Note that
one might also consider more general quantum state setups which can
simulate arbitrary common reference strings but we stick to the EPR
case due to its simplicity.

Definition 6.1

(Hidden Bits Generator
[
73
]
)
.

A hidden bits generator (HBG) is a triple of algorithms
(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{GenBits},\mathsf{Verify})
with the following syntax:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},1^{k})\to\mathsf{crs}:
The setup algorithm takes as
input a security parameter and the number of hidden-bits
k
, and
outputs a common reference string
\mathsf{crs}
.

•

\mathsf{GenBits}(\mathsf{crs})\to\big(\mathsf{com},r,\{\pi\}_{i\in[k]}\big):
The algorithm
outputs a commitment
\mathsf{com}
, hidden bits
r\in\{0,1\}^{k}
and openings

\{\pi\}_{i\in[k]}
for each of the bits.

•

\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},i,r_{i},\pi_{i})\to\top/\bot:
The
verification algorithm takes an index
i
, hidden bit
r_{i}
and proof

\pi_{i}
along with a CRS
\mathsf{crs}
and commitment
\mathsf{com}
. It outputs

\top
(accept) or
\bot
(reject).

An HBG must satisfy the following properties:

•

Correctness:
For all
i\in[k]
, the following holds:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},i,r_{i},\pi_{i})=\bot\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},1^{k})\\
&(\mathsf{com},r,\pi_{[k]})\leftarrow\mathsf{GenBits}(\mathsf{crs})\end{array}\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

•

Succinct Commitment:
There exists a set of commitments

\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)
such that for all
\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},1^{k})
and every
\mathsf{com}

output by
\mathsf{GenBits}(\mathsf{crs})
,
\mathsf{com}\in\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)
. Moreover, there exists
a constant
\delta<1
such that
|\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)|\leq 2^{k^{\delta}\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)}
. Furthermore, for all
\mathsf{com}\notin\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)
,

\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},\cdot,\cdot)
always outputs
\bot
.

•

Statistical Binding:
There exists an (inefficient)
deterministic algorithm
\mathsf{Open}(1^{k},\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com})
such that for every

k=\mathsf{poly}(\lambda),\mathsf{crs}
and
\mathsf{com}
, it outputs
r
such that the
following holds for every unbounded malicious prover
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
:

\Pr\left[r_{i}^{*}\neq r_{i}\land\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},i,r_{i}^{*},\pi_{i})=\top\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},1^{k})\\
&(\mathsf{com},i,r_{i}^{*},\pi_{i})\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}(\mathsf{crs})\\
&r\leftarrow\mathsf{Open}(1^{k},\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com})\end{array}\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

•

Computational Hiding:
For all
k=\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
and
I\subseteq[k]
, the following holds:

\left[(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},I,r_{I},\pi_{I},r_{\overline{I}})\approx_{c}(\mathsf{crs},\mathsf{com},I,r_{I},\pi_{I},r^{\prime}_{\overline{I}})\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},1^{k})\\
&(\mathsf{com},r,\pi_{[k]})\leftarrow\mathsf{GenBits}(\mathsf{crs})\\
&r^{\prime}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}\end{array}\right]

t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}
are fixed as they are
computed as the parity of the computational basis values. Observe now
that by statistical binding of
\mathsf{HBG}
, except with negligible
probability, we have
r^{*}_{I}=t_{I}\oplus s_{I}
where
t_{i}=\bigoplus\limits_{j:\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{j}=0}y_{j}^{i}
for each
i\in I
and

\widetilde{\theta}^{1}\|\ldots\|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ell}\coloneq\mathsf{Open}(1^{k\ell},\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}^{*})
. Moreover,
succinctness of
\mathsf{HBG}
guarantees that
\mathsf{com}^{*}\in\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)
. Hence, we have
the following:

v(\lambda)\coloneq\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{hb}}(I,r_{I},x,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}})=\top\\
\land\;\;x\notin\mathcal{L}\\
\land\;\;\mathsf{com}^{*}\in\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)\end{array}\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}=(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},s)\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(x,\pi^{*}\coloneq(I,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}},\mathsf{com}^{*},\theta^{I},\mathsf{op}^{I}))\leftarrow\mathsf{P}^{*}(\mathsf{crs},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}})\\
&\widetilde{\theta}^{1}\|\ldots\|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ell}\coloneq\mathsf{Open}(1^{k\ell},\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}^{*})\\
&\forall i\in[\ell]:t_{i}\coloneq\bigoplus_{j:\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{j}=0}y_{j}^{i}\\
&r=t\oplus s\end{array}\right]\geq\mu(\lambda)-\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

Next, we define a similar probability for any fixed
\mathsf{com}\in\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)
:

\displaystyle v_{\mathsf{com}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{hb}}(I,r_{I},x,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}})=\top\\
\land\;\;x\notin\mathcal{L}\\
\land\;\;\mathsf{com}^{*}=\mathsf{com}\end{array}\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}=(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},s)\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&(x,\pi^{*}\coloneq(I,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}},\mathsf{com}^{*},\theta^{I},\mathsf{op}^{I}))\leftarrow\mathsf{P}^{*}(\mathsf{crs},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\mathrm{P}})\\
&\widetilde{\theta}^{1}\|\ldots\|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ell}\coloneq\mathsf{Open}(1^{k\ell},\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}^{*})\\
&\forall i\in[\ell]:t_{i}\coloneq\bigoplus_{j:\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{j}=0}y_{j}^{i}\\
&r=t\oplus s\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{hb}}(I,r_{I},x,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}})=\top\\
\land\\
x\notin\mathcal{L}\end{array}\ :\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathsf{crs}=(\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},s)\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
&\widetilde{\theta}^{1}\|\ldots\|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ell}\coloneq\mathsf{Open}(1^{k\ell},\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}^{*})\\
&\forall i\in[\ell]:t_{i}\coloneq\bigoplus_{j:\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{j}=0}y_{j}^{i}\\
&r=t\oplus s\\
&(x,I,\pi_{\mathsf{hb}})\leftarrow\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}}(r)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-q(\lambda,n)}\cdot\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

The first inequality follows because
r
is already fixed by
\mathsf{com}

and the second follows from the soundness of the hidden bits NIZK by
viewing
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathsf{crs}_{\mathsf{bg}},\mathsf{com}}(r)
as a malicious hidden
bits model prover and from the fact that
r
is uniformly random
(because
s
is uniform).

Next, we set
q(\lambda,n)^{1-\delta}=k\cdot k^{\prime}\cdot p(\lambda)
so that:

(k\ell)^{\delta}\cdot p(\lambda)=(k\cdot k^{\prime}\cdot q(\lambda,n))^{\delta}\cdot p(\lambda)\leq q^{\delta}(\lambda,n)\cdot q^{1-\delta}(\lambda,n)

This ensures that
|\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)|\cdot v_{\mathsf{com}}(\lambda)\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
. Therefore,
a union bound gives us the following:

\mu(\lambda)-\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)\leq v(\lambda)\leq\sum_{\mathsf{com}\in\mathcal{COM}(\lambda)}v_{\mathsf{com}}(\lambda)\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

This proves that
\mu(\lambda)\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
, ensuring soundness.

∎
