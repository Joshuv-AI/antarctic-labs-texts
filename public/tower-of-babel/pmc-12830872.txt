PLX3397 attenuated tumor growth and remodeled tumor microenvironment of recurrent glioblastoma
Authors: Chen Yang, Linzhi Cai, Xi Xiang, Xiwei Zhu, Chengli Liu, Jianming Liao, Zhan Zhang, Mingchang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor microenvironment (TME) is critically implicated in glioblastoma (GBM) recurrence, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion. By analyzing both primary and recurrent GBM from Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) and clinical samples, we identified significant differences in TME. To overcome the limitations of traditional murine recurrent GBM model, we successfully established a new murine model and characterized the TME differences between primary and recurrent GBM in vivo. Based on immune chemokine profiling within the CGGA dataset, we selected PLX3397, a Colony stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor, to target recurrent GBM in our murine model. PLX3397 treatment significantly attenuated tumor growth and remodeled TME. Collectively, our study firstly analyzed TME combine a large number of database samples and clinical samples, and made the first application of PLX3397 in a murine recurrent GBM model, thereby providing a novel therapeutic strategy and experimental foundation for recurrent GBM research.Keywords: Recurrent glioblastoma, Tumor microenvironment, Murine glioblastoma model, CSF1R inhibit, PLX3397Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary intracranial malignant tumor, characterized by high recurrence rates, aggressive invasiveness, and the poorest prognosis among brain tumors1,2. At present, the treatment of GBM is mainly surgical resection, combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy3. Despite this multimodal approach, recurrent GBM invariably develops, for which no standard effective therapies exist, resulting in markedly abbreviated patient survival4,5.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is an important component of GBM, contributing to therapeutic resistance, blood-brain barrier function, and immune evasion6. Within the GBM TME, tumor cells coexist with diverse infiltrating immune populations, such as tumor-associated macrophages/microglia, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and dendritic cells7,8. Research shows that TME of primary glioblastoma and recurrent glioblastoma is significantly different in cells, blood vessels, and so on9,10. Therefore, exploring differences in TME of primary and recurrent GBM may be critical to overcoming therapeutic resistance and reducing the recurrence, holding substantial implications for both in clinical and preclinical studies.

Currently, the traditional preclinical animal model of recurrent GBM face significant limitations. Surgical resection in animal models is technically challenging, yields high mortality rates, and suffers from poor reproducibility11–14. Radiation treatment requires a large dose of radiation, resulting in damage to the surrounding tissue and leading to complications15,16. As a result, these constraints have hindered investigation into the difference in TME between primary and recurrent GBM.

To address the critical need for a reliable model of recurrent GBM, we developed a novel murine recurrent GBM model. In this model, tumor cells were engineered to express a suicide gene Caspase9. Following establishment of primary tumors, AP20187 specifically activated the suicide gene in tumor cells, inducing their death to mimic surgical tumor resection. The surviving tumor cells enabled the outgrowth of recurrent tumor. Our murine recurrent GBM model successfully simulated the recurrence of tumors in clinical patients after surgery.

Therefore, we combined Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database17, verified glioblastoma patient specimens and animal model specimens to analyze the difference in TME between primary and recurrent GBM. Furthermore, we employed the murine model to conduct the first preclinical investigation of the Colony stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor PLX3397 in recurrent GBM. Collectively, our study provides a new platform for the exploration of the mechanisms related to the tumor microenvironment and the in-depth clinical transformation.

Then, we established a recurrent glioblastoma mouse model (Fig. 3b). Orthotopically implanted G9-Luc cells formed primary tumors in C57BL/6 mice. At day 14 post-implantation, intraperitoneal AP20187 administration (0.5 mg/kg) activated iCaspase9, inducing apoptosis in tumor cells to simulate surgical resection. Residual cells subsequently generated recurrent tumors. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed significantly smaller tumor volumes in recurrent mice versus primary controls at matched timepoints (Fig. 3c). Critically, mice in recurrent group could survive until 35 days after modeling, while all the primary mice died before 35 days.

Meanwhile, the tumor size of the mice in recurrent group was smaller than that in primary group at the same period.

Discussion

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain malignancy, exhibits more aggressive, more likely to recur, and worse prognosis, compared with others. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is increasingly recognized as the key to curing glioma. However, due to the lack of high-quality animal models, most of the research on recurrent glioma can only be conducted through clinical experiments, resulting in minimal and slow progress5,18–20. The existing animal models of recurrent gliomas, such as those constructed by radiotherapy, medicine, or surgery, have certain limitations. Through our experiments, we compared the differences in the tumor microenvironment in primary and recurrent glioma. Crucially, we established an inducible recurrent GBM model using the iCaspase9 suicide gene system, which can partly reflect the tumor conditions of clinical patients. Leveraging this model, we pioneer the evaluation of CSF-1R inhibition in murine recurrent GBM, revealing time-dependent therapeutic efficacy.

This study leveraged the CGGA database—one of the first large-scale repositories enabling bioinformatic interrogation of the recurrent glioma TME17. Using the TIMER algorithm21, we quantified immune cell infiltration and identified significant TME differences between primary and recurrent gliomas. These findings align with a 2024 report of enhanced immune infiltration in recurrent gliomas22. However, discrepancies exist across studies. Fu et al. observed reduced tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) proportions in recurrent gliomas via CyTOF21, and a single-cell analysis of 86 pairs of primary-recurrent glioma samples found similar TAM/lymphocyte infiltration before and after recurrence23. Inconsistent results may be related to differences in research methods, the heterogeneity of gliomas, sample sources, and quantities. Studies reporting stable immune infiltration typically employed single-cell sequencing technology24–26—a technique limited by high costs, stringent specimen requirements, and small sample sizes that may obscure inter-individual variation. In contrast, our TIMER-based approach leverages validated signature genes and large-scale RNA sequencing data, effectively minimizing technical noise21. Furthermore, previous investigations primarily utilized The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) or Western cohorts with a limited number of recurrent GBM samples27, whereas CGGA provides substantial recurrent glioma samples. Critically, immunofluorescence analysis of surgical specimens from our institution confirmed TME patterns consistent with CGGA-derived data, reinforcing the robustness of our findings.

Further analysis reveals that elevated infiltration of most immune cell populations significantly correlates with reduced patient survival. We observed increased numbers of total macrophages, pro-tumor (M2-like), and anti-tumor (M1-like) phenotype macrophages following tumor recurrence. This suggests complex dynamic reprogramming of macrophage composition and function in recurrent GBM28,29. The rise in CD86 + macrophages may reflect an attempted anti-tumor response through M1-like polarization during recurrence30. However, the GBM microenvironment harbors abundant immunosuppressive factors—including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10)—which not only inhibit M1-type macrophage activity but also promote their conversion toward the M2 phenotype31. Consequently, despite numerical increases, CD86⁺ macrophages may exhibit substantially diminished anti-tumor functionality in recurrent GBM. Furthermore, M2-type macrophages support tumor survival and invasion via growth factor secretion and extracellular matrix remodeling while exacerbating immunosuppression32. This paradoxical coexistence of opposing phenotypes and their functional impairment likely constitutes a key mechanism underlying treatment resistance and poor survival in recurrent GBM33,34.

T cells constitute core components of the adaptive immune system and play pivotal roles in anti-tumor immunity35. In our study, CD8 + T cell infiltration was significantly higher in rGBM compared with primary GBM (pGBM), consistent with previous reports36–38. Several factors may contribute to this enhanced infiltration. CD8 + T cells in rGBM often co-express PD-1 and CD3939, indicating that these cells are tumor-reactive and exist in a precursor exhausted (TEX) state, retaining proliferative potential and effector function, which may drive their accumulation in response to persistent antigen stimulation. Moreover, CD8 + T cells are more frequently localized around blood vessels rather than within the tumor parenchyma37,39, suggesting that the perivascular niche provides a supportive environment for immune cell survival and proliferation, while the immunosuppressive tumor parenchyma may restrict TIL migration and persistence. In addition, most blood vessels in rGBM are enveloped by extracellular matrix (ECM) and densely populated with immune cells, the majority of which are CD163 + TAMs38. These immunosuppressive TAMs and associated proteins may impede the egress of tumor-reactive CD8 + T cells from the perivascular space, resulting in local accumulation. Taken together, the increased CD8 + T cell infiltration in rGBM likely reflects the combined effects of persistent tumor antigen stimulation, the presence of precursor exhausted T cells, and the physical and functional restrictions imposed by ECM and TAMs on T cell trafficking. Although CD8⁺ T cells typically suppress tumor growth through direct cytolytic activity and correlate with improved prognosis35, within GBM’s profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment, these cells exhibit functional exhaustion. This is characterized by elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules including PD-1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4)40.

Pathological angiogenesis represents a hallmark feature of GBM, driving rapid tumor progression and invasion41. CD31⁺ staining demonstrated substantially augmented vascular density with aberrant morphological complexity in recurrent tumor. This maladaptive vasculature not only provides essential nutrients for post-therapeutic tumor survival but also exacerbates immunosuppression through hypoxia-driven pathways. Resultant acidic microenvironments induce overexpression of immunosuppressive mediators including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). These factors, on the one hand, further stimulate angiogenesis, and on the other hand, inhibit the functions of immune cells such as T cells and M1-type macrophages.

Current preclinical research on recurrent glioma faces significant challenges, primarily due to limitations in existing animal models. Conventional modeling approaches—chemotherapy induction, surgical resection, and radiotherapy—exhibit critical shortcomings13,14,21,42. Chemotherapy models frequently induce non-physiological resistance due to interspecies dose tolerance disparities. Although surgical resection has developed many detection methods, it is difficult to standardize the postoperative lesions, and tumors often show diffuse recurrence. Radiotherapy models cause off-target tissue damage through imprecise tumor targeting and clinically irrelevant supratherapeutic radiation doses. To address these constraints, we established a murine recurrent GBM model via iCasp9 suicide gene. The results showed that within 7 days after activating the suicide gene by AP20187, the tumor volume of mice showed a downward trend, while after 7 days, the tumor volume increased rapidly. This pattern of shrinking first and then recurring well simulates the physiological process in clinical patients, where the tumor volume decreases and then recurs after surgical resection. This suppression-rebound trajectory accurately recapitulates clinical postsurgical recurrence patterns while establishing a defined therapeutic window for investigating recurrence biology. Meanwhile, our model has the characteristics of strong repeatability and simple operation.

In this study, we selected 14 days post-model establishment as the time point for inducing glioma recurrence. According to previous literature, GL261 primary glioma mice begin to die around 20 days after tumor implantation43,44, with approximately 40% to 60% mortality between days 20 and 3045, which is consistent with our experimental observations. Considering that AP20187 induces recurrent glioma through continuous injection over 5 days, we chose day 14 to start AP20187 administration in order to avoid mortality due to primary glioma and to better observe the effects of the recurrent model. We acknowledge that tumors at day 14 were relatively small and may not fully reflect the situation of late-stage glioma patients undergoing surgery. In future experiments, we will explore different time points for initiating recurrence induction to improve the clinical relevance of our model.

In clinical practice, patients typically receive surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy simultaneously before glioma recurrence. However, in constructing our mouse model, we only simulated surgical resection of glioma. This approach was chosen for two reasons: first, to avoid non-physiological drug resistance arising from interspecies differences in chemotherapy dosing and to prevent normal tissue damage from radiotherapy14,46; second, to better observe the direct effects of the targeted drug PLX3397 on the immune microenvironment and its related mechanisms in recurrent glioma, excluding the potential influence of Temozolomide (TMZ). Nevertheless, while temozolomide primarily acts on tumor cells, previous studies have shown that TMZ treatment can increase the proportion of myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment, enhance the expression of genes related to myeloid cell inflammatory responses, and increase the number of Iba1 + myeloid cells within the tumor core47,48. These effects may partly explain the differences in TAM dynamics between human and mouse tumor microenvironments.

Through the analysis of the tumor microenvironment in mice, we found that except for CD8 + T cells, the changes in the tumor microenvironment showed the same trend as those in clinical specimens, but there were still certain differences. These divergences likely stem from fundamental differences in immunoregulatory mechanisms between mouse GL261 cells and human gliomas, temporal sampling constraints, and model idiosyncrasies. Mouse microglia may respond more rapidly to injury signals, but their behaviors in terms of phenotypic polarization may not be completely consistent with those of human macrophages. This experiment was conducted at a fixed time point after recurrence, which might have missed the dynamic change window of immune cells. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the recurrent GBM immune landscape. By establishing an inducible and dynamically visualized recurrent glioma model, we not only successfully simulated the recurrence process of GBM but also revealed the dynamic changes of immune cells and angiogenesis during the recurrence stage. These findings have laid a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted immunotherapy strategies.

To address the predicament in the clinical treatment of recurrent glioma, we focused on macrophages and analyzed the cytokines closely related to the physiological activities of macrophages. Analysis revealed significant correlations between specific cytokine expression shifts and macrophage infiltration during recurrence. These cytokines thus had an impact on the immune microenvironment and affected the survival time of patients. Through analysis, we found that both CSF1 and CSF1R were highly correlated with macrophage infiltration in both primary and recurrent gliomas, which provided ideas for the selection of our treatment strategies.

Administration of PLX3397 to murine recurrent GBM models significantly inhibited the growth of tumor, despite conferring no survival advantage. The dissociation between tumor suppression and survival benefit may suggested the compensatory resistance mechanisms post-CSF1R inhibition and the necessity for combinatorial strategies targeting alternative pathways.

PLX3397 exerted significant remodeling effects on the tumor microenvironment, characterized by marked reduction of pro-tumor macrophages (CD206⁺) and an upward trend in anti-tumor macrophage markers (CD86⁺). Crucially, this therapeutic efficacy demonstrated strict time-dependence, likely attributable to dynamic TME evolution during recurrence progression21,49. In the early recurrence stages, the relatively plastic TME permits effective intervention. PLX3397 administration blocks myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) recruitment and inhibits tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization toward M2-like phenotypes before immunosuppressive networks consolidate. Conversely, delayed treatment initiation (day 21 post-recurrence) encounters a matured TME featuring stabilized vascular networks and collagen-rich desmoplastic stroma50. This architecture provides physical support for tumor cells while impeding drug penetration—dense extracellular matrix components form diffusion barriers that limit PLX3397 bioavailability in deep tumor regions, reducing effective drug concentrations51. Furthermore, established late-stage immunosuppressive networks exhibit enhanced complexity, where regulatory T cells (Tregs) and MDSCs synergistically counteract therapeutic effects through multi-factorial suppression (e.g., PD-L1/CTLA-4 overexpression, adenosine secretion). Consequently, although late intervention achieved measurable tumor control, its efficacy significantly trailed early treatment. This difference in treatment timing is somewhat consistent with clinical observations: when GBM patients receive treatment in the late stage of recurrence, they often face stronger treatment resistance and poorer prognosis, while early intervention usually significantly improves survival52,53. The results provide experimental support for this clinical phenomenon and further suggest that when designing treatment plans targeting macrophages, choosing the appropriate intervention timing is critical for improving the therapeutic effect54.

PLX3397 significantly increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration while minimally affecting CD4⁺ T cells, and early administration substantially reduced vessel density. CSF-1R inhibition likely alleviates T cell suppression by depleting immunosuppressive TAMs, particularly for CD8⁺ T cell55. As the main immunosuppressive cells in the GBM microenvironment, TAMs inhibit the anti-tumor activity of T cells through multiple mechanisms. For example, IL-10 and TGF-β secreted by TAMs can directly inhibit the proliferation and effector function of T cells. Furthermore, TAMs may also induce T cell exhaustion by expressing PD-L1 and binding to PD-1 on the surface of T cells56,57. PLX3397 reduces the number of TAMs and the tumor-promoting phenotype, thereby creating favorable conditions for the activation and expansion of T cells. CD8 + T cells, as the core effector cells of anti-tumor immunity, a significant increase in their quantity may directly enhance the killing effect on tumor cells, thereby further promoting the therapeutic effect of PLX339758,59. This coordinated remodeling of macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and vasculature provides mechanistic rationale for combinatorial therapies. In the future, we will combine PLX3397 with other drugs for treatment, approach from multiple perspectives, and jointly affect the tumor microenvironment to achieve better therapeutic effects51.

Conclusion

This study integrates CGGA database analysis with clinical specimens to delineate immune microenvironment remodeling during glioma recurrence, and established a novel iCaspase9 suicide gene-mediated murine recurrent GBM model. Through systematic profiling of macrophage-associated pathways, CSF1R was identified as a therapeutic target, and preclinical efficacy was validated using this model, revealing time-dependent TME modulation. While limitations exist—including reliance on a single algorithm (TIMER) for bioinformatic analysis and GL261 cell line constraints partially recapitulating human TME—future work will incorporate multi-omic validation and diverse cell line models. Notwithstanding these constraints, this research provides the first large-scale characterization of recurrent glioma TME dynamics, pioneers a reproducible inducible model, and mechanistically validates CSF1R targeting in recurrent GBM, thereby establishing a preclinical platform that addresses fundamental translational challenges and informs combinatorial clinical strategies.

Materials and methods

Human glioblastoma samples

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (WDRY2021-KS008). All human glioma specimens were taken from patients in the Department of Neurosurgery of Wuhan University People’s Hospital from January 2016 to June 2021.

Bioinformation analysis

Clinical information and tumor mRNA sequencing data of 271 patients with recurrent glioma and 422 patients with primary glioma were downloaded from the Chinese Brain Glioma Gene Atlas Project Database (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/). Transcriptome sequencing was performed on 10 cases of recurrent glioblastoma and 46 cases of primary glioblastoma from patients in the Department of Neurosurgery of Wuhan University People’s Hospital from January 2016 to June 2021. The algorithm of TIMER, a web server for tumor immunity estimation (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/), was applied to systemic analyze glioma-infiltrating immune cells.

Cell culture

We procured all cell lines from Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., LTD. GL261 cells were cultured in a complete medium that contained DMEM (Absin, abs9483) with high glucose, 10% FBS (Bioexplorer, BS1618-109), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Absin, abs962) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were cultured every 3–4 days until 80–90% confluence.

Cell transduction

GL261 cells were transfected with Polybrene using the iCaspase9 and luciferase gene. Firstly, we constructed a lentivirus (pcSLenti-EF1-Luc-F2A-Puro-CMV-iCasp9-WPRE, OBIO) to transfect GL261 cells. After puromycin screening, we got the stable GL261-iCasp9-Luc cell line, and designated it G9-Luc.

Ethical approval for mice

Mice were euthanized following ethical approval from the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University and following institutional guidelines. The criteria for euthanasia included: (1) body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline weight, (2) signs of distress or pain (e.g., labored breathing, hunched posture, or inactivity), (3) evidence of significant tumor burden, including visibly enlarged tumors or signs of compromised mobility, and (4) failure to thrive or complete immobility. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by confirmation of death by lack of a heartbeat.

Our study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Construction of recurrent glioblastoma model in mice

The 8-week-old male C57/BL6J were purchased from SANXIA Laboratory Animal Center (Hubei, China). Mice were group-housed with a 12 h light/dark cycle20,21. All procedures followed the National Institutes of Health guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University.

After digestion, G9-Luc cells were prepared into a 100,000/µl cell solution and stored on ice. We used isoflurane as the inhalation anesthetic(induction concentration: 4%; maintain concentration: 2%). After the mice were anesthetized, the eyes of the mice were protected with an eye cream, the head was fixed horizontally on a stereotactic apparatus, and the skin of the head was disinfected and cut along the median line. The injection point was located at 1.5 mm anterior and 2 mm right of the bregma. A hole was made at skull by drilling with a 23-gauge needle tip. A microinjector was inserted 4 mm into the mouse brain vertically. A small space was made in the mouse brain after removing the syringe 1 mm up. Then, 1 µl cell suspension with 105 G9-Luc cells was injected into the mouse brain at 0.5 µl per minute. Then, the wound was sewn, and the animal was returned to the animal facility.

After 14 days from injecting cells, mice were i.p. injected with AP20187(1 mg/kg) every day for 5 days. AP20187 was dissolved in a solution of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% saline.

PLX3397 treatment

Firstly, mice with recurrent glioma were randomly divided into 3 groups: control group, PLX3397 treatment group 1, and PLX3397 treatment group 2. PLX3397 treatment group 1 began to receive PLX3397 (100 mg/kg) daily in administration on the 21 st day of modeling; PLX3397 treatment group 2 received daily intragastric administration of PLX3397 (100 mg/kg) since the 35th day of modeling. PLX3397 was dissolved in a solution of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% saline.

Histopathology staining

On different days (7,14,21,28,35) after injecting cells, the mice were euthanized and perfused intracardially with 0.1 mmol PBS (pH 7.4) and 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4). A fixed brain was immersed in 4% formaldehyde at 4 °C for 6–8 h and then immersed in 30% sucrose solution until the tissue sank (2 days). The formalin-fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned with a microtome. Brain specimens on glass microscope slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined using light microscopy.

Immunofluorescence

We euthanized mice and infused them with phosphate-buffered saline PBS before immersing their brain in paraformaldehyde (PFA). As part of the study, brain samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into four mm coronal sections, which were placed on slides. After washing the slices with PBS, the slides were blocked for 1 h at room temperature. We washed the cells with PBS, then added the primary antibody and incubated them for 12 h at 4 °C overnight. Incubation at 25 °C was completed with the corresponding secondary antibody. Finally, the slices were sealed and observed.

In vivo imaging system (IVIS)

We prepared the D-Luciferin potassium (Life-iLab, AC19L012) at a concentration of 150 mg/kg in sterile PBS and administered it via intraperitoneal injection 5–10 min before imaging to ensure adequate distribution and reaction time. The mice were then positioned in the imaging chamber of the IVIS Lumina III (Perkin Elmer).We acquired images at specified time points. Data analysis was performed using the Living Image software, which measured the total flux (photons per second per square centimeter per steradian) within regions of interest (ROI).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 was used for all statistical analyses. According to the 50% cut-off point of gene expression, patients were divided into a high-expression group or a low-expression group. Spearman correlation analysis was used to test the correlation. The difference in survival rate between groups was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank significance test. The number of individual replicates, and or repetitions of independent experiments, is indicated in the text of the figures. When comparing two groups, values are reported as the mean ± SD, and an unpaired, non-parametric Student’s t-test was used to determine statistical significance. Statistical significance was assumed if p < 0.05; P-values are indicated in figures as *** p < 0.001; ** p < 0.01; * p < 0.05; and N.S.: no significance.
