Determination of Fluctuation correlation time in solid-state Nuclear Magnetic Resonance
Saptarshi Saha, Rangeet Bhattacharyya

Abstract
Solid-state NMR provides a wide variety of experimental techniques to detect and analyze a material’s chemical and physical environment. Here, we offer a theoretical demonstration of a new approach that could be a promising candidate for characterizing the local environments and shifts. Prethermalization by applying a spin-locking pulse brings a new research paradigm in solid-state NMR and has not been hitherto explored for such purposes. We show that a prethermal state can also be effective in this case. A prethermal state is described using its lifetime and the value of transverse magnetization. Using these two variables, we successfully detect the changes in the environmental parameter and chemical and dynamical shifts (such as Lamb shifts). Our results exhibit that the lifetime increases with increasing environmental correlation time. On the other hand, the transverse magnetization decreases with the increase in the strength of the shift parameter. Based on these observations, we propose that the prethermalization dynamics can yield important information on local environment.

II
Description of the system

To mimic the system consisting of
13
C atoms in the presence of an on-resonance periodic drive, we consider a rudimentary model of dipolar-coupled spin-half particles externally coupled to the environment. For simplicity, we only consider a two-spin ensemble in our analysis; hence, it represents a dilute sample. A spin-locking pulse is also applied in the ‘
X
’ direction. For modeling the effect of the local electronic environment, we assume a Jaynes-Cummins type Hamiltonian, where the spins are coupled with environmental degrees of freedom

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}^{\circ}+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\circ}+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm DR}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm CS}+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm DD}+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm SL}+\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}(t).

(1)

We give the details on the individual terms of the Hamiltonian below.

\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}^{\circ}

represents the Zeeman interaction and the analytical form is given by
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2}\omega_{\circ}I_{z}^{i}
. Here,
\omega_{\circ}

I_{\alpha}^{i}=\sigma_{\alpha}^{i}/2,\,\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}
,

\vec{\sigma}

i

2.

The free Hamiltonian of the environment is
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\circ}=\omega_{L}L_{+}L_{-}
, where
\omega_{L}
is the frequency and
L_{\pm}
are the corresponding creation and
annihilation operators of the local environment of each spin. The environment is always in equilibrium with a constant temperature (the inverse temperature is defined as
\beta

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eq}=e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\circ}}/\mathcal{Z_{L}}

\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm DR}(t)

The motion of the electron cloud around the nuclear spins results in a localized field, which causes a shift to the energy levels of the non-interacting spins

\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm CS}

5.

The dipolar coupling between spins in terms of spherical tensors is given
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm DD}=\sum\limits_{m=-2}^{2}(-1)^{m}\omega_{d_{m}}\mathcal{T}^{m}_{2}

, where,
\omega_{d_{m}}

6.

Following the Jaynes-Cummings model,

\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm SL}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm SL}(I_{+}^{i}L_{-}^{i}+h.c.)

Here,

is the system-local environment coupling amplitude

.

7.

\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}(t)

III
Dynamical equation of the system

It is already known that if such a system is initially oriented in the ‘
X
’ direction, on-resonance periodic excitation leads to a long-lived prethermal plateau
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2023
. To explain such dynamics, we need to go beyond the unitary quantum evolution. A clear understanding of the relevant timescales and the relaxation processes is also required in this case. There exists a timescale separation in this system, i.e., bath correlation time is much shorter than the system relaxation time,
\tau_{c}<T_{1},T_{2}
. Here,
T_{1},T_{2}
are the system relaxation time scales. The system also evolves through the following relaxation processes: drive-induced dissipation (DID)
Chakrabarti and Bhattacharyya 2018
, dipolar relaxation
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2022a
;
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2022b
, and system-bath relaxation
Heinz-Peter Breuer 2002
. A typical Born-Markov master equation could not explain the DID. On the other hand, the Redfield equation does not apply to the solid-state NMR
Redfield 1957
. It is noted that a recently formulated fluctuation-regulated quantum master equation (FRQME) performs better in this case to describe the underlying physics of the spin-locking process
Chakrabarti and Bhattacharyya 2018
. Such a formalism also gets lots of success in explaining other phenomena in driven dissipative quantum systems
Chanda and Bhattacharyya 2020
;
Chanda and Bhattacharyya 2021
;
Chatterjee and Bhattacharyya 2020
;
Das and Bhattacharyya 2024
;
Chatterjee and Bhattacharyya 2024
;
Chanda et al. 2023
;
Fency et al. 2025
;
Saha et al. 2025
. Hence, we use FRQME to derive the dynamical equation of the system.

III.1
Fluctuation-regulated quantum master equation (FRQME)

The analytical form of FRQME in the interaction

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\text{Tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}\Big[H_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eff}(t),\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\otimes\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eq}\Big]^{\rm sec}

(2)

\displaystyle-\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\text{Tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}\Big[H_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eff}(t),\Big[H_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eff}(t-\tau),\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\otimes\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm eq}\Big]\Big]^{\rm sec}e^{-\frac{|\tau|}{\tau_{c}}},

In the interaction frame,

We will follow a Liouville space prescription to solve the dynamical equation numerically.
In Liouville space, the dynamical equation can be written as
\frac{d\hat{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)}{dt}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)

1.

\mathcal{L}_{sec}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)=-i\left[\mathcal{H}_{tot}^{sec},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)\right]-\tau_{c}\left[\mathcal{H}_{tot}^{sec},\left[\mathcal{H}_{tot}^{sec},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)\right]\right]

Here,

2.

\mathcal{L}_{n}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\sum\limits_{m=-2}^{2}|\omega_{d_{m}}|^{2}\tau_{c}\mathcal{Z}(m)\left[2\mathcal{T}_{2}^{-m}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\mathcal{T}_{2}^{m}-\left\{\mathcal{T}_{2}^{m}\mathcal{T}_{2}^{-m},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right\}\right]\quad[\forall m\neq 0]

Here,
\mathcal{Z}(m)=1/(1+(m\omega_{\circ}\tau_{c})^{2})

3.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny{SL}}}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2}P_{\mp}\left[2I_{\mp}^{i}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}I_{\pm}^{i}-\{I_{\pm}^{i}I_{\mp}^{i},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\}\right]

are defined as the downward and upward transition probabilities.
We define,
P_{+}+P_{-}=1/T_{1}

In this prescription, we neglect all the other shift terms (e.g., Lamb shift, Bloch-Siegert shift, etc) as they have no significant contribution to the dynamics.
The two spin density matrices can also be written as a sum of relevant observables, which are given by,

\displaystyle\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}A_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}I_{\alpha^{\prime}}\otimes I_{\beta^{\prime}},

III.2
Different relaxation times of the system

Depending on the strength of the three terms in the Liouvillian, different time scales exist in the system. Following the experimental parameters, we are working in the regime,
|\mathcal{L}_{Sec}|>|\mathcal{L}_{n}|>|\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny{SL}}}|
. We note that such a regime naturally arises in solid-state NMR. The corresponding relaxation times are given by,

1.

The terms corresponding to
|\mathcal{L}_{sec}|
decay at a much faster time scale, the expression for the relaxation rate is given by,
\omega_{\rm sec}^{2}\tau_{c}
. Here,
\omega_{\rm sec}
is the average strength of the local interaction.

2.

The terms corresponding to
|\mathcal{L}_{n}|
decays at relatively longer time scale. The relaxation rate is given by,
\omega_{\rm sec}^{2}\tau_{c}/(1+\omega_{\circ}^{2}\tau_{c}^{2})
.

3.

Finally
|\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny{SL}}}|
decays with the longest timescale and the corresponding rate is given by,
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm SL}^{2}\tau_{c}/(1+\omega_{\circ}^{2}\tau_{c}^{2})
.

V
Discussion

For a good characterization of the chemical components, the process or pulse technique must be able to detect small changes in those elements. We show that the spin-locking fulfills the criteria. Previous works are concentrated on the measurement of
T_{1\rho}

Almeida et al. 2024
. Here, we choose a different path. Recently, prethermalization using spin-locking has been explored in several contexts
Beatrez et al. 2021
;
Beatrez et al. 2023b
;
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2023
;
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2024a
. Hence, we decide to provide a characterization protocol using the prethermal state. We find that the value of the traverse magnetization at the prethermal state and the prethermal state lifetime is a good identifier for that.

We focus on the two cases, (i) the chemical components of the external environment and
(ii) the presence of any chemical or dynamical shifts. For characterizing the local
environments, we find that the lifetime of the prethermal state is efficient. We define
the lifetime of the prethermal state in Eq. (
5
) and plot it as a function of

\tau_{c}
. We show that the environment has a very short fluctuation timescale
(\tau_{c}<1/\omega_{\circ})
(e.g., amorphous solids), then there is no existence of a prethermal
state. On the other limit
(\tau_{c}>1/\omega_{\circ})
(e.g., crystalline solids), the
lifetime of the prethermal state grows with increasing fluctuation-time scale (shown in
Fig.
2
). As such, for a crystalline environment, the lifetime could be
improved by freezing or reducing the degrees of freedom. Hence, from the value of

\tau_{c}
, the properties of the local environments can easily be identified.

Similarly, our theoretical results will also be helpful in depicting chemical shifts. We note that for a stronger shift (i.e., stronger than the dipolar interaction), our methods will not work as the prethermal state will thermalize quickly. For a weaker shift, we show that the value of
M_{x}
changes when the shift strength is changed. Therefore, measuring the value of
M_{x}
or
\mathcal{F}(M_{x})
will be helpful in the characterization of the chemical shift.

Figure 4:
depicts the contour plot of
T_{\rm pre}
(defined in Eq. (
6
)) as a function of the relative strength of chemical shift
\alpha/\omega_{d}
and environmental correlation time
\tau_{c}
. the lifetime increases for increasing
\tau_{c}
and decreasing
\alpha/\omega_{d}

We also show the contour plot of the lifetime of the prethermal state by varying
\alpha
and
\tau_{c}
. An alternative theoretical definition of such a lifetime is provided, which can be used for the numerical verification of those experimental outcomes. The dynamical equation has the form,
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho
. We note that
\mathcal{L}
always has an eigenvector with a zero eigenvalue, which ensures the trace preservation and the existence of a steady state (i.e., thermal state). In the case of prethermalization, there exist other eigenvectors whose eigenvalues are close to zero compared to the remaining ones
Saha and Bhattacharyya 2024c
. The real part of the corresponding eigenvalues represents the decay rate. Hence, the lifetime of the prethermal state can be defined as,

\displaystyle T_{\rm pre}=1/|\mathcal{L}_{NNN}-\mathcal{L}_{NN}|.

(6)

Here,
NN
and
NNN
represent the nearest neighbor and next nearest neighbor, respectively.
1/|\mathcal{L}_{NNN}|
is the timescale for the creation of the prethermal state, and
1/|\mathcal{L}_{NN}|
gives the notion of decay time of the prethermal state.

Using this definition, we plot
T_{\rm pre}
as a function of
\alpha
and
\tau_{c}
in
Fig.
4
. We show that higher values of
\tau_{c}
and lower values of
\alpha

provide a long-lived prethermal state. Hence, the lifetime of the prethermal state will be
a good quantity for characterizing the environment and the shift. We note that the recent
developments in solid-state NMR to explore the prethermal state and its properties can
also be helpful to verify our theoretical prediction
Beatrez et al. 2021
;
Beatrez et al. 2023b
;
Beatrez et al. 2023a
.

In the manuscript, we only study a homonuclear system consisting of two dipolar-coupled
spins to exhibit several uses of a prethermal state. An extension of our analysis can
also be done for the heteronuclear case. We hope that the extra detuning term will be
effective in identifying the chemical components. On the other hand, a rigorous study
considering the anisotropic part of the chemical shift is required for the detailed
analysis of the prethermal state in the presence of chemical shift. Similarly, we like to
verify our claims for a real experimental setup of a dissipative quantum many-body system,
where the dipolar-coupled spins are randomly oriented. The orientation of the spins and
the geometry of the system may play a vital role. Another open question would be to
predict the lifetime for a non-Markovian bath, where the chemical environment has some
finite memory, i.e., instead of a delta correlation, which we assume in this case, there
would be a Gaussian or Lorentzian correlation.

VI
Conclusion

We revisit the theory of prethermalization using a spin-locking pulse in solid-state NMR.
We have used the recently formulated FRQME for the analysis. We predicted that such a
prethermal state would be helpful in characterizing the local environment. The prethermal
state can be specified by its lifetime and the value of transverse magnetization. We show
that the lifetime could be a good identifier for the characterization of the chemical
environment. Similarly, transverse magnetization can also be used to identify the presence
of weak chemical shifts. We envisage that this method will be a valuable addition to the existing arsenal of
solid state NMR methods.
