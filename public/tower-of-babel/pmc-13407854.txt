Prevalence of color vision deficiency among first-year health science students at a medical university in Vietnam
Authors: Linh Chi Giang, Yen Vi Bui, Hoang Lam Nguyen, Dinh Thi Nguyen, Duc Dat Nguyen, Thi Quynh Anh Bui, Van Thuan Hoang, Nang Trong Hoang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColor vision deficiency (CVD) affects clinical performance in color-dependent tasks. While studies have reported a high prevalence of CVD among health science students in several Asian countries, no data are available from Vietnam. To evaluate the prevalence and identify factors correlated with CVD among first-year health science students in Vietnam. A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted among all first-year students in the 2024–2025 academic year. CVD was first screened using the online Ishihara test questionnaire. Students who made five or more errors on this questionnaire were subsequently referred for confirmatory ophthalmic examination. 1,491 students were included. Overall, 1.9% (28/1,491) were clinically confirmed to have CVD. Male students had a 16-fold higher odds of color vision deficiency than female students (OR = 16.60; 95% CI: 5.32–82.8). Students with less than 7 h of daily sleep were correlated with twice the risk level compared to those with a longer sleep duration (OR = 2.33; 95% CI: 1.10–5.00). Our findings underscore the importance of early screening of CVD, particularly among medical and pharmacy students. Identifying correlated factors, including sex and sleep duration, underscores the necessity of prioritizing screening for first-year students, thereby facilitating informed academic planning and future career guidance.Keywords: Color vision deficiency, prevalence; First-year health science students, medical education; Health science; Student; VietnamSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Color vision deficiency (CVD), also known as color blindness, is the inability or reduced ability to perceive color differences under normal lighting conditions. Color confusions are mainly between red, yellow, and green colors or between blue-green and grey. Red–green CVD is the most common form1, 2, 3. CVD can be congenital or acquired4, 5. Congenital CVD is a genetic disorder in which red–green color deficiency is usually transmitted as an X chromosome-linked recessive trait6. There are also connections between abnormal color vision and ocular disease such as glaucoma, cataract, retinal diseases, optic nerve defects and other chronic conditions7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15. The prevalence of color vision defects is also impacted by environmental and occupational factors, toxic substances and some drugs16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23.

For health professions students, accurate color recognition is a core clinical competence. Clinicians and nurses use color changes of the skin, mucosa, nails, and body fluids to inform clinical assessment. These observations support the identification of conditions such as hypoxia, anemia, jaundice, cyanosis, dehydration, and shock10. In medical imaging, color interpretation is required to read color-based modalities. This includes color Doppler ultrasound for vascular flow, perfusion, and anatomical mapping, as well as color-coded post-processing in CT, MRI, and digital radiology. Errors in color perception can affect the identification of abnormal structures and functional signals. In laboratory settings, technicians interpret biochemical and immunological assays through colorimetric reactions. Many tests generate results based on color intensity or color shift, including enzyme assays, ELISA, rapid diagnostic kits, and urinalysis strips. Small color differences can determine test categories and clinical decisions. Pharmacists use color recognition to verify medicines, distinguish formulations, and reduce dispensing errors, especially when drugs have similar shapes, packaging, or labeling but differ in color coding. Across clinical, imaging, laboratory, and pharmaceutical practice, color recognition contributes important information for patient assessment, diagnostic reasoning, and treatment planning.

Previous studies have shown that visual impairment is common in the general population as well as in health science students11, 12. The global prevalence of red–green CVD is estimated at 8% in males and 0.4% in females of European ancestry, and 4–6.5% in East Asian males3. Current evidence does not support the exclusion of people with CVD from practicing medicine13. However, studies have highlighted the need for screening to raise awareness and provide people with CVD with better advice on choosing the right career path14, 15, 16.

In Vietnam, students receive annual health check-ups. In ophthalmologic screening, only visual acuity is routinely assessed, while color vision testing is not included. To our knowledge, no published studies have evaluated CVD among medical students in Vietnam, while the studies in Asian countries have reported that prevalence estimates ranging from 2.0% to 6.5%3, 17. Therefore, we conducted this study to assess the prevalence and correlated factors of CVD among health science students at a medical university in Vietnam. We conceptualized that demographic factors (sex, age) represent underlying biological susceptibility, while lifestyle factors (sleep duration, outdoor activity, physical exercise, study load) may influence functional visual performance and the likelihood of CVD during screening.

Because the number of CVD cases was small, we first applied LASSO logistic regression to identify the most relevant variables. The optimal penalty parameter (λ.min = 0.0036) yielded a model with strong discrimination (AUC = 0.833). LASSO selected four variables with non-zero coefficients: sex, sleep duration, outdoor activity time, and age. Physical exercise time, self-study time, refractive error, and smoking were shrunk to zero and were therefore excluded from further modeling (Table 3).

Male gender was strongly correlated with a higher rate of CVD (adjusted OR = 16.6; 95% CI: 5.32–82.9; p < 0.001). Shorter sleep duration < 7 h per day was also correlated with higher proportion of CVD (adjusted OR = 2.33; 95% CI: 1.10–5.00; p = 0.03). Outdoor activity time showed no significant correlation (adjusted OR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.30–1.48; p = 0.29). Age was also not correlated with the presence of CVD (adjusted OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.23–2.45; p = 0.81).

The final Firth model demonstrated good overall fit (likelihood ratio test: χ² = 43.21, df = 4, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study found that 1.9% of health science students were affected by CVD. The prevalence of CVD in this study was slightly lower than that reported among students from other countries worldwide, including Nigeria (2.5%, 58/2,326)18, Egypt (6.9%, 99/1,426)19, Ethiopia (2.9% (114/4,000)20, and Saudi Arabia (3.9%, 8/203)21. Another Malaysian study22 conducted among medical students showed that 3.2% (45/1,427) of participants reported CVD. These differences may be explained by the broader age ranges, ethnic variations, or genetic factors examined in previous studies3. Indeed, males are known to have a higher risk of CVD than females. Fareed et al. reported a prevalence of 7.5% in males and 0.83% in females (sex ratio = 9:1)5. This gender disparity is attributed to the X-linked genetic nature of CVD, which makes it more common in males. However, in our study, the majority of participants were female, which might have contributed to a lower overall prevalence of CVD.

Our findings showed a strong correlation between outdoor activity levels and CVD in univariable analysis, as students who participated occasionally or rarely were less likely to have CVD. We believe this does not represent a causal relationship in which lower participation provides protection. Instead, this observation is likely to reflect an effect of the condition itself; students with CVD may experience difficulties that limit their participation in outdoor activities. Therefore, reduced participation is a consequence of CVD, not a factor that lowers its risk. However, this factor was no longer statistically significant in the multivariable analysis. This may be because the number of detected CVD cases was small, reducing the statistical power needed to confirm a clear correlation. Another explanation could be the correlation between gender and outdoor activity. Based on our observations, male students are generally more likely than females to participate in outdoor activities, and males also have a higher risk of CVD. As a result, outdoor activity no longer shows a significant correlation in the multivariable model.

Interestingly, our study identified sleep duration as an important factor correlated with CVD in both analytical models. Students who slept fewer than 7 h per day had a higher proportion of CVD than those who slept longer. Because congenital CVD is genetically determined, sleep duration cannot be considered a causal risk factor for the condition. Instead, our findings suggest that short sleep duration may influence functional color discrimination or increase the likelihood of detecting impairment during screening. Visual fatigue, reduced perceptual accuracy, or attentional effects correlated with insufficient sleep may exacerbate difficulties in color-based tasks. Reverse causality is also possible, as individuals with impaired color perception may experience greater visual strain that disrupts sleep patterns. Indeed, a study on vision-related functional burden and sleep disorders reported relevant results23. The authors concluded that difficulties related to visual functioning were the main factor contributing to sleep disturbances, while simple visual impairment did not play the same role23. Conversely, it is also possible that poor color discrimination may adversely affect sleep quality among individuals with CVD.

Regarding disease severity, a substantial proportion of these cases presented with severe red–green CVD, indicating that although the overall prevalence of CVD is low, the degree of impairment among those affected can be considerable. Students with CVD reported significantly lower mean scores for general health, role difficulties, and color vision functioning compared with students who had normal color perception19.

In Vietnam, routine student health screening currently includes only visual acuity and does not assess color vision. Our findings support adding a simple Ishihara-based screening test at entry to medical and health science programs. Early identification would allow universities to: (1) counsel affected students on study strategies for color-dependent subjects including histology, pathology, and microbiology, (2) provide targeted academic support, and (3) offer career guidance for specialties where color discrimination is critical. At the institutional level, results from screening could inform teaching adaptations, such as adding labels, patterns, or numeric scales to color-based materials rather than relying on color alone. At the policy level, the incorporation of color vision testing into first-year health checks would be a low-cost intervention that improves student safety, learning outcomes, and patient care readiness, particularly in resource-limited medical training settings.

Our study has some limitations. First, the initial screening test was administered online using the Ishihara test. Ishihara testing is the most widely used screening tool for red–green color vision deficiency and is recommended for large-scale population screening. However, it does not detect all forms of CVD, particularly blue–yellow defects, and therefore may underestimate the overall prevalence. Future studies should consider incorporating complementary tests for a more comprehensive assessment. Although our screening approach allowed rapid and large-scale assessment, the accuracy of color perception may have been affected by differences in device screen resolution, brightness, color calibration, and ambient lighting conditions. Moreover, device type (phone, tablet, or desktop) and display settings were not recorded during the online screening. These factors could lead to both false-positive and false-negative results. This partially explains the higher rate of suspected cases compared with the actual prevalence of CVD. However, to minimize misclassification, all students who failed the screening were invited for confirmatory examination by an ophthalmologist using standardized printed Ishihara plates. Confirmatory ophthalmologic examination was performed only for participants who screened positive; screen-negative students were not re-tested. As a result, false-negative cases may have been missed, which could underestimate CVD prevalence and dilute observed correlations. In addition, this was a single-center study conducted at one medical university. Although the sample included students from multiple health-related programs and represented nearly the entire first-year cohort, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all medical schools or health science institutions in Vietnam. Multi-center studies are therefore needed to confirm the national prevalence and factors correlated with CVD. Moreover, the set of independent variables was limited to those available in the questionnaire and those most frequently reported in the literature. Other potentially important factors, such as family history of color vision deficiency, genetic background, or detailed ophthalmologic history, were not assessed. Although LASSO was used to select the most relevant predictors among the available variables, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified that male gender and sleep duration of less than seven hours were correlated with the presence of CVD. Because of the cross-sectional design and the congenital nature of CVD, these variables should be interpreted as correlates rather than causal determinants. Routine color vision testing and early identification of CVD among first-year health science students, especially in groups with higher observed prevalence, may help inform academic support, training pathways, and career orientation. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the relationship between sleep patterns and CVD-related outcomes.

Methods

Study design, location, and studied population

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted among first-year heath-science students at Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Hung Yen Province, Vietnam.

Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy is recognized as one of the leading training institutions in the medical field in Vietnam. The university admits over 1,000 new students annually and offers full-time as well as inter-level undergraduate programs in various majors, including general medicine, traditional medicine, preventive medicine, public health, nursing, and medical laboratory science.

At the time of study (the 2024–2025 academic year), 1503 students were recorded as first-year students.

The minimum sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula. Assuming a 95% confidence level, an expected CVD prevalence of 3.0% based on previous study17, and a margin of error of 1%, the minimum required sample size was 1,118 students.

All first-year health science students enrolled in the 2024–2025 academic year were invited to participate, using a census (total population) sampling approach. A total of 1,491 students agreed and were ultimately included in the study, exceeding the required sample size.

CVD screening and data collection

CVD screening was performed with two steps (Fig. 1). In the first step, an online survey questionnaire was designed via Google Form with 36 questions, divided into three domains: (1) Ishihara screening plates24, (2) sociodemographic characteristics, and (3) lifestyle and visual health factors. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published student CVD surveys19, 25, but no formal psychometric validation was performed. The estimated time to complete the questionnaire was approximately 10 min.

All first-year students, regardless of major, were invited to complete the questionnaire. The study objectives, number of questions, and estimated completion time were clearly described in the introduction. Only students who agreed to participate were able to proceed to the questionnaire. Participation was entirely voluntary; students could decline or withdraw at any time without any consequences. Participation or non-participation did not affect their academic results or personal interests.

All questions were set as mandatory fields and marked with an asterisk. Students were required to complete all items before submitting the questionnaire.

To prevent duplicate responses and to support subsequent examination procedures, students’ names and class information were collected. Identifying information (names and class codes) was stored in a password-protected file separate from the analytical dataset. Only the research team had access to this file for the purpose of contacting students for confirmatory examinations. Before data analysis, all identifiers were removed and replaced with anonymous study codes to ensure confidentiality.

The first 21 plates were used for CVD screening, and the remaining 4 plates were used to determine the type of CVD. Students were instructed to view each plate for 3 s, hold the device screen at a distance of 40–50 cm from their eyes, and perform the test under natural light. They were allowed to wear their usual corrective glasses.

In the second step, students with suspected CVD screening results (defined as answering 5 or more plates incorrectly) were invited for an in-person examination by an ophthalmologist at the Department of Ophthalmology, Thai Binh Medical University Hospital. A printed Ishihara test was used to confirm the presence of color vision deficiency.

Statistical analysis

Data were downloaded from Google Form into a Microsoft Excel file and cleaned before analysis. Statistical analysis was done using RStudio v4.5.0. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Our main outcome was the presence of CVD. The Chi-square (χ2) test was used to compare differences in proportions.

To explore factors correlated with CVD, each variable was first analyzed individually using univariable logistic regression to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs). Second, a multivariable analysis was used, including variables that have been previously identified in the literature13, 26, 27, 28 as factors related to CVD (male gender, sleep duration, physical exercise duration, outdoor activity time, self-study time, refractive error, and smoking history). This model was also adjusted for age as confounding factor. Prior to model fitting, we assessed multicollinearity among candidate predictors using pairwise correlation matrices and variance inflation factors (VIF) from a standard logistic model; no problematic multicollinearity was detected (all |r| < 0.7 and all VIF < 5). Because only 28 confirmed CVD cases were observed, the number of outcome events was insufficient for a conventional multivariable logistic regression model including all candidate predictors. With eight planned predictors, the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio would have been approximately 28/8 = 3.5, which is far below the commonly recommended minimum of 10 EPV to ensure stable and unbiased estimates. Under such conditions, standard logistic regression is prone to overfitting, inflated odds ratios, and wide confidence intervals. To address this issue, we applied LASSO logistic regression using 10-fold cross-validation to select factors correlated with CVD. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal penalty parameter were retained. These selected predictors were subsequently refitted using a Firth penalized logistic regression model to obtain unbiased odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05 and a 95% CI not including the value of 1.
