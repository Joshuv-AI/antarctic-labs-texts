ABSTRACT
Vaccines are widely used in both research and clinical settings. To facilitate FAIR data practices, we urgently need to standardize vaccine representation, integrate information across diverse vaccine types, and support computer-assisted reasoning. Accordingly, we have since 2007 developed the community-based Vaccine Ontology (VO), which aligns with the Basic Formal Ontology and adheres to OBO Foundry principles. VO ontologically models vaccines, vaccine components, vaccine immune responses, vaccine investigation studies and other vaccine-related topics. VO represents more than 10,000 vaccines targeting 289 infectious pathogens and cancers in humans and over 30 nonhuman animal species. VO provides mappings to external resources such as RxNorm, CVX, FDA, and USDA. VO facilitates vaccine standardization in resources such as the VIOLIN vaccine database, ImmPort, and the Vaccine Adjuvant Compendium (VAC). VO enables semantic queries on vaccine data. It has been shown to enhance the analysis of experimental and clinical vaccine datasets, as well as vaccine-related literature mining. Overall, VO standardizes vaccine modeling and representation and greatly supports vaccine AI research in the Semantic Web era.

Introduction

The invention and use of vaccines have dramatically improved human and other animal health over the past two centuries. Vaccines have achieved significant successes in public health by preventing many diseases, such as smallpox, polio, measles, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, influenza, varicella zoster (chickenpox and shingles), and hepatitis B1. The impact of vaccines on the prevalence of formerly common childhood diseases is unparalleled by any other medical intervention in human history2. Recently, vaccines have played a crucial role in combating the COVID-19 pandemic, reducing global deaths by over 60% during the first year of COVID-19 vaccination3. The widespread administration of these vaccines has significantly mitigated the impact of the pandemic and set a precedent for rapid vaccine development in response to future global health threats.

Many vaccines have been approved and licensed for human or other animal use and have been recorded in various resources. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) developed and maintains the Codes for Vaccine Administered (CVX), a system in which each vaccine is given a specific code (https://www2.cdc.gov/vaccines/iis/iisstandards/vaccines.asp?rpt=cvxv). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved over 100 vaccines for human use, each linked to a unique submission tracking number (https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/vaccines/vaccines-licensed-use-united-states). The National Library of Medicine has developed the RxNorm vocabulary, which standardizes the naming of prescription medications approved for human use in the United States (https://www.nlm.nih.gov/research/umls/rxnorm/index.html). RxNorm includes thousands of human vaccines used in the U.S. For the standardization of vaccines not approved in the U.S., the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) initiative developed the RxNorm Extension (RxE)4,5, which includes drug and vaccine terms used outside the U.S. and not covered by RxNorm. OHDSI harmonizes health data using the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) and its standardized vocabularies4. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) has approved about 1000 veterinary vaccines for use within the country, assigning each a unique code (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/veterinary-biologics/licensed-products). Many organizations have assigned codes or identifiers for easy reference and data exchange within a specific domain or context. However, these standards are inherently not interoperable, hindering seamless data integration and analysis. This is the problem that the VO is designed to address.

Beyond already approved vaccines, thousands more, especially cancer vaccines, are in various stages of clinical trials or pre-trial laboratory research. Data about these trials can be found on resources like clinicaltrials.gov6 and the NCI Thesaurus7. Furthermore, extensive research has been conducted on vaccines and vaccination. As of March 18, 2026, over 580,000 vaccine-related articles are available in PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term = vaccine), reflecting the depth and breadth of ongoing inquiry in this critical area. Given the large amount of vaccine data distributed across these resources, efficient retrieval, standardization, and integration remain challenging.

Biomedical ontologies are sets of terms and relations that represent entities in the biomedical world and how they relate to each other. Ontology terms form a tree, representing classes of entities at different levels of generality. Each term is associated with documentation, including alphanumeric identifiers, labels, and definitions, which are expressed both in human-readable form and in a logical language to enable support for automated reasoning. In the past two decades, hundreds of biomedical ontologies, such as the Gene Ontology (GO)8,9 and the Ontology for Biomedical Investigations (OBI)10,11, have been developed to provide controlled and standardized terms for use in different biological and biomedical domains. These terms are used to annotate hundreds of different types of data in a consistent fashion, and the resultant annotation-based data repositories serve as valuable resource for querying and analyzing high-throughput data. Biomedical ontologies have been widely used in multiple applications, including data harmonization, integration, and computational data analysis and reasoning. They have also facilitated artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) research on large-scale biological and biomedical data. For example, a deep neural network model predicts protein function from sequence by leveraging both GO annotations and the hierarchical structure of the GO12. The Human Disease Ontology (DOID)13,14 has been applied to improve the accuracy of Named Entity Recognition (NER) systems based on deep learning15.

To promote vaccine data standardization, integration, analysis, and computer-assisted reasoning, we have developed the Vaccine Ontology (VO) in a collaborative, community-based fashion following the Open Biological and Biomedical Ontology (OBO) Foundry principles16,17. The development of the ontology began in 2007, led by a team of domain and ontology experts representing various groups whose work required standardized representations of vaccine information. The VO was first presented at the 1st International Conference on Biomedical Ontology in 2009 in Buffalo, NY, USA18. Over the past 18 years, more than 400 papers have cited VO. However, no previous article has provided a comprehensive, systematic description of the complete VO framework. As the first comprehensive journal article describing the Vaccine Ontology (VO), this paper describes the VO design principles, high-level structure, and hierarchy, and cross-references to external resources, and demonstrates through concrete use cases how VO supports data standardization and addresses research questions in the vaccine domain.

Results

High-level VO classes and their alignment with reference ontologies

Figure 1 illustrates the high-level hierarchical structure of the VO. Overall, all VO terms are aligned with the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO)19, a top-level ontology conformant to the ISO/IEC 21,838 standard (https://www.iso.org/standard/74572.html). As a domain-neutral framework, BFO includes two main branches: continuant and occurrent (Fig. 1). A continuant is an entity that persists, endures, or continues to exist through time while maintaining its identity. Independent continuants, such as vaccines and organisms, can exist by themselves, while dependent continuants, such as quality and function, depend for their existence on the independent continuants. Occurrents include time intervals and any entity that unfolds in a time-dependent manner, such as a process of vaccination or of response to a vaccine. More than 600 ontologies have adopted BFO (https://basic-formal-ontology.org/users.html), and the adoption of BFO by VO thus supports its broad interoperability with ontologies in other domains.Fig. 1High-level structure of VO. This diagram illustrates the top-level class hierarchy of the Vaccine Ontology, with some intermediate non-VO terms omitted for simplicity.

High-level structure of VO. This diagram illustrates the top-level class hierarchy of the Vaccine Ontology, with some intermediate non-VO terms omitted for simplicity.

VO reused many reference ontologies in the OBO library, including the OBI10,11, the Phenotype and Trait Ontology (PATO)20, and the Ontology for General Medical Science (OGMS)21 (Fig. 1). OBI provides a set of terms commonly used in biomedical investigations, for example, materials used in processes, organisms, antigen and pathogen roles, assay and analysis types10,11,22. PATO describes various phenotypes and qualities20, and OGMS defines general terms used in clinical encounters such as disease, sign, and symptom21.

New VO terms can be created in a semantically uniform way under the guidance of the VO high-level structure (Fig. 1) and by the BFO and intermediate layers of OBO reference ontologies (e.g., OBI, PATO, and OGMS) incorporated therein. Under the BFO:continuant branch are: vaccines, vaccine components (e.g., vaccine antigen and adjuvants), sites (e.g., route of administration), and vaccine-associated functions and roles. Under the BFO:occurrent branch are: vaccine preparation, vaccination, vaccine immunization, vaccine-induced immune responses, and vaccine immune profiles, etc. The definitions and associated classifications of these terms are detailed below.

Ontological modeling of vaccine hierarchies and design patterns in VO

VO defines ‘vaccine’ (VO:0000001) as “material entity that is manufactured to realize the vaccine function.” The definition emphasizes the ‘vaccine function’ to distinguish ‘vaccine’ from other manufactured materials such as ‘devices’ that have functions such as containing, measuring, and so forth. In addition, ‘vaccine’ itself is a processed material that excludes those natural pathogen organisms that can induce immune responses.

Figure 2 illustrates the classification of various vaccine types and their hierarchies in the VO. The central branch of the hierarchy is labelled ‘vaccine against disease.’ Terms in this branch categorize vaccines based on the targeted disease under headings such as infectious disease, cancer, and allergy. Traditionally, vaccines against infectious diseases are classified based on the type of causative agent: bacteria, virus, or eukaryotic microorganism such as fungi and protozoans. To classify the associated vaccines, VO deploys two parallel definition strategies: 1. based on the infectious disease type (for example: tuberculosis, AIDS) and 2. based on the underlying causal pathogens (for example Mycobacterium tuberculosis, human immunodeficiency virus).Fig. 2Representative vaccine types and their associated hierarchies as defined in the VO. Vaccines are classified by targeted disease, causative agent type, and vaccine preparation method.

Representative vaccine types and their associated hierarchies as defined in the VO. Vaccines are classified by targeted disease, causative agent type, and vaccine preparation method.

Different vaccine types are often classified based on their roles. Overall, the ‘vaccine role’ is a role that inheres in a prepared material entity that is designed to induce protection or treatment for a disease or infection. VO includes first vaccine roles such as the ‘licensed vaccine role’ and ‘authorized vaccine role,’ which are assigned to vaccines by specific organizations. Additionally, VO uses roles such as ‘vaccine in clinical trial role,’ and ‘vaccine in research role,’ alongside the licensed and authorized vaccine roles, to signify the life cycle phase of a vaccine (Fig. 2).

Vaccines are often classified on the basis of the mode of vaccine production (Fig. 2). This reflects the fact that vaccines can be prepared using different methods or technologies, which reflect how the vaccine delivers antigens to the recipient’s immune system. Different vaccine preparation methods produce vaccines with specific roles, which are acquired during the process of preparation and realized in the process called ‘vaccine immunization.’ These diverse vaccine roles facilitate logical inference by the ontology reasoner. Further examples include: ‘live attenuated vaccine role,’ ‘inactivated vaccine role,’ ‘subunit vaccine role,’ ‘RNA vaccine role,’ ‘DNA vaccine role,’ ‘conjugate vaccine role,’ ‘toxoid vaccine role,’ and ‘virus-like particle vaccine role.’ Each of these terms indicates a corresponding type of vaccine, such as ‘live attenuated vaccine,’ ‘inactivated vaccine,’ and so forth. VO defines the latter in terms of the former as a strategy to avoid the forking of data which arises when a single ontology term has two direct parents within the ontology hierarchy.

Vaccines can also be defined using other characteristics. For example, ‘therapeutic vaccine’ and ‘preventive vaccine’ are defined based on two types of vaccine functions, where the function of an entity is defined in terms of the entity’s reason for existence (the purpose it is designed to serve). While most existing vaccines are preventive, many therapeutic vaccines are being developed and tested in areas such as control against various cancer types23,24. Examples of other vaccine types are: contraceptive vaccines25,26, and editable vaccines, which are produced in genetically modified plants or other foods, which can be eaten to deliver immunization against specific diseases27,28. Heterologous prime-boost vaccines deploy a vaccination strategy that uses different vaccine types for the initial (prime) and subsequent (boost) doses to enhance the immune response by combining the strengths of multiple vaccine platforms29,30.

An ontology design pattern (ODP) is a reusable template used to define a specific type of entity31,32. Figure 3a illustrates the vaccine design pattern that covers features shared by most vaccines. Specifically, a vaccine can be defined or categorized33 along a number of dimensions, including: 1) vaccine immunized recipient, 2) vaccine targeted disease or pathogen, 3) vaccine function, 4) vaccine components, 5) vaccine development status, 6) vaccine manufacturer, and 7) vaccination routes. As the output of the ‘vaccine preparation’ process, a ‘vaccine’ contains multiple components, such as a protein component with ‘vaccine antigen role’ and a ‘vaccine adjuvant.’ Roles, functions, and process profiles are often used for vaccine knowledge representation. For example, a ‘vaccine’ is administered to the organism that has the ‘vaccine recipient role’ at a ‘route of administration’ site. After the ‘vaccination’ process there follows a ‘vaccine immunization’ that is induced by the vaccination with the ‘vaccine,’ and this in turn has outcome ‘vaccine-induced host immune response’ with a process profile ‘correlated profile of immune protection.’ The ‘vaccine function’ is realized through ‘vaccine immunization’ immunization against ‘pathogen’ infection and disease. In VO, the vaccine manufacturers and vaccination route are generally indicated in connection with a licensed vaccine product (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3Vaccine ontology design pattern (ODP) in VO. Our vaccine ODP effectively addresses the complexity of multiple inheritance by asserting a vaccine under a single parent, namely that defined by the vaccine-targeted diseases or pathogens, and utilizing an OWL reasoner to infer additional parents. A specific vaccine can be classified simultaneously via multiple of the above-listed categories. For example, a vaccine can be simultaneously an ‘influenza virus vaccine,’ ‘inactivated vaccine,’ and ‘licensed human vaccine.’ Multiple inheritance occurs when a single vaccine is associated with multiple parent categories. To enhance clarity, modularity, and maintainability, it is greatly recommended to avoid asserting multiple parents in ontology development34. In VO, each vaccine is asserted under only one parent, with the vaccine-targeted diseases or pathogens being the major classification category. Remaining parents are automatically inferred based on an OWL reasoner such as ELK35.

Figure 3b,c illustrate how the vaccine ODP represents the ‘Afluria vaccine.’ First, the term ‘Afluria vaccine’ is asserted under ‘trivalent influenza vaccine,’ which is a subtype of influenza vaccine. The Afluria vaccine antigen is the whole viral organism that has the quality ‘inactivated’. The vaccine itself is asserted under a single parent in VO. We rely on OWL reasoners to infer additional parents. Based on the logical axioms governing ‘Afluria vaccine,’ a reasoner will classify it as an ‘inactivated influenza vaccine,’ ‘licensed human influenza virus vaccine,’ ‘prophylactic vaccine,’ and ‘USA licensed human vaccine.’

Ontological modeling of vaccine adjuvants in VO

A vaccine adjuvant is a critical component added to vaccines to enhance and direct the immune response to the protective antigen. VO includes a wide variety of vaccine adjuvants and potential adjuvant candidates. Vaccine adjuvants have been classified primarily by their sources and their chemical and physical characteristics. VO also captures additional key information, such as the specific molecular receptors targeted by adjuvants and the immune profiles they induce. These attributes are associated with vaccine adjuvants using logical axioms. The logical representation of terms within an ontology enables semantic Description Logic (DL) queries36. For example, ‘LTA1 vaccine adjuvant’ induces immune profile ‘Th1/Th2/Th17 mixed immune profile’37 (Fig. 4a). DL queries based on the ‘Th1/Th2/Th17 mixed immune profile’ retrieved the ‘LTA1 vaccine adjuvant’ and five other adjuvants that induce the same immune profile (Fig. 4b), demonstrating the use of DL queries for automated knowledge discovery.Fig. 4VO representation of the LTA1 vaccine adjuvant. In addition to vaccine adjuvants, the VO ontological model also covers other vaccine components and formulations, which have been described in detail in our previous publication38. Additionally, vaccination and vaccine-induced immune responses are represented in VO, with further details provided in a manuscript currently under review39.

VO statistics

The latest release of the Vaccine Ontology (VO), version 2026-03-06, contains a total of 15,661 terms. These include 14,787 classes, 122 object properties, 162 annotation properties, and 590 instances. Of these, 14,269 terms (91%) are specific to VO, including 13,629 classes, 45 object properties, 25 annotation properties, and 570 instances/individuals. The remaining terms are imported from external ontologies. Currently, VO has imported 1,158 classes from 22 external ontologies, such as the OBI11, NCBI organismal classification (NCBITaxon)40, DOID13,14, and the Ontology for Parasite Lifecycle (OPL)41. Detailed statistics regarding VO terms based on their respective prefixes are available at the Zenodo repository42 (VO_statistics-v2026-03-06.xlsx).

Most VO terms designate vaccines. As of 2026-03-06, VO represents 10,929 vaccines or vaccine candidates targeting either humans or one or more of 33 different genera or species of other animals. While most of these vaccines (9,984) are directed against 289 infectious pathogens, some target non-infectious diseases, including 677 cancer vaccines, 4 arthritis vaccines, 4 diabetes vaccines, and 2 autoimmune disease vaccines.

Table 1 summarizes the distribution of vaccines according to their term resources and their development phases. 7,723 VO vaccines are licensed or authorized for use, including 6,848 for human use and 875 for veterinary use; 805 are vaccine candidates in clinical trials, and 1,273 are vaccine candidates in the research phase. For licensed human vaccines, we have collected 122 FDA-approved vaccines (https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/vaccines/vaccines-licensed-use-united-states) (retrieved on February 18, 2025), 272 CVX codes (https://www2.cdc.gov/vaccines/iis/iisstandards/vaccines.asp?rpt = cvx) (extracted on May 22, 2024), 2,051 vaccine-related terms from RxNorm (version 2023-07-03)43, and 5,389 vaccine-related terms from the RxE (version 2023-08-24)43. Currently, VO contains all FDA-approved vaccines, the complete CVX code set44, the full list of RxNorm terms, including 2,029 vaccines and 22 vaccine ingredients43, and 4,091 terms from the RxE, including 4,074 unique vaccines43. In addition to licensed human vaccines, VO also includes 788 USDA vaccines licensed for veterinary use. There are approximately 1,000 vaccines in the USDA catalog (released on January 30, 2025), and efforts are ongoing to incorporate into VO all licensed veterinary vaccines from the catalog. Another major category of VO terms is vaccine adjuvants. VO includes 480 vaccine adjuvant terms, including 32 adjuvant types used to categorize 448 distinct adjuvants. These adjuvants are sourced from the Vaccine Adjuvant Compendium (VAC; https://vac.niaid.nih.gov) and from direct literature annotations. VAC, an extramural program of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), contributes 88 of the 448 distinct vaccine adjuvants represented in VO.

To automate VO development, we created 20 ROBOT templates based on the VO ODPs. ROBOT45 is a command-line tool used to automate ontology development workflows, especially for OBO Foundry ontologies. A ROBOT template can be used for standardized data annotation, and the standardized data can be processed by ROBOT for automatic ontology term generation, ontology integration, and format conversion. These VO-specific ROBOT templates were converted to the corresponding modules using the ROBOT tool for integration into the VO. These modules encompass vaccine, vaccine adjuvant, non-adjuvant vaccine components, VO individuals, VO object properties, and VO annotation properties. Seven templates/modules were designed to support the mapping of annotations between VO and external vaccine data resources. Detailed descriptions of the templates are available at https://github.com/vaccineontology/VO/wiki/Editing-VO#vo-templates.

VO records cross-referencing and mapping to external data resources

VO references terms collected from external terminologies or resources using different annotation properties. For example, VO uses the annotation properties ‘CVX code’ and ‘RxNorm ID’ to cross-reference CVX and RxNorm identifiers for specific vaccines. The related information from original sources of these terms, including their original unique identifiers and names or labels, has been collected in the ROBOT templates and incorporated into the VO. These mappings, which represent relationships between terms in different ontologies, vocabularies, or classification systems, are a significant contribution to data interoperability and integration across diverse vaccine domains and resources.

To further enhance vaccine data exchange and support text mining, we generated term mappings between VO and external resources using the Simple Standard for Sharing Ontological Mappings (SSSOM) standardized format46. The SSSOM format mapping files were developed from the ROBOT templates include: VO to RxNorm vaccines, VO to CVX codes, VO to concept IDs in OHDSI Standardized Vocabularies or OHDSI vocabularies for brevity (including RxNorm, RxE vaccines, and CVX codes), VO to FDA vaccines, VO to USDA veterinary vaccines, and VO to VAC vaccine adjuvants. These files are available on the GitHub site: https://github.com/vaccineontology/VO/tree/master/mappings.

Standardization of vaccine data in VIOLIN

Vaccine Investigation and Online Information Network (VIOLIN, https://violinet.org) is the first and most comprehensive vaccine knowledge database and analysis system covering the complete vaccine life cycle57,58. VIOLIN provides detailed information on more than 6,100 vaccines targeting 217 pathogens and non-infectious diseases, such as cancer and allergies. The database includes licensed vaccines, vaccines in clinical trials, and those experimentally shown to be effective in at least one animal model. VIOLIN is an invaluable resource for exploring various aspects of vaccines, including antigens, host responses, adverse events, and formulation strategies. VIOLIN also houses several specialized sub-databases, such as Vaxjo, with 448 vaccine adjuvants. Each vaccine record in VIOLIN contains vaccine name, vaccine components (such as vectors and adjuvants), preparation methods, targeted pathogens and diseases, species for which they are licensed for use, and manufacturers, etc. VIOLIN is the initial source of VO development. VO has been used to standardize the representation of vaccines, vaccine adjuvants, vaccine vectors, vaccine types based on production methods, and routes of vaccine administration drawing on the integrated data in VIOLIN covering the complex mechanisms underlying vaccine function59–62. Advancing Vaccine Classification and Data Analysis in Clinical Data

The OHDSI initiative is a multi-stakeholder, interdisciplinary, open-science collaborative whose goal is to bring out the value of health data through large-scale analytics. Since January 2023, two key VO developers, Drs. He and Lin, have served as co-chairs of the OHDSI Vaccine Vocabulary Workgroup. The workgroup’s overall goal is to utilize VO to harmonize vaccine hierarchy structures, enhance vaccine classification, and facilitate vaccine-related OHDSI data analysis. Multiple terminologies, including RxNorm, RxE, and CVX codes, are used as standards for vaccine concepts within the OHDSI Standardized Vocabularies system. These vocabularies have very different structures, which makes it challenging to integrate them into a unified hierarchy. The hierarchical structure of RxNorm and RxE is primarily based on ingredients with incremental addition of other attributes such as dose form and drug strength. On the other hand, CVX offers more generic codes organized as a flat list of terms without any hierarchical structure. Moreover, differences in active component representation complicate alignment of RxNorm/RxE and CVX: while the latter maintains broad description of the component, RxNorm/RxE vaccine ingredients may embed additional details such as strain.

VO integrates vaccine terms from RxNorm, RxE, and CVX codes, aligning them under the VO hierarchical structure. Additionally, VO specifies vaccine targeted disease, targeted pathogen, mode of production, and salient seasons of vaccination for influenza vaccines43. As a result, VO offers a unified hierarchy for vaccine terms in the OHDSI Standardized Vocabularies. Discussion

This report provides the first comprehensive, systematic description of the Vaccine Ontology (VO), which has become the leading community-based vaccine standardization resource. VO integrates into its semantic framework vaccine related terms and associated definitions from multiple resources, including vocabularies, terminologies, literature, and websites. Many biological and biomedical resources and databases, such as VIOLIN57,58, ImmPort47, IEDB66,67, VAC, and HIPC68, have used VO to harmonize vaccine related data. As a result, VO has made significant contributions to enhancing data FAIRness.

As a collaborative community-based effort, VO development has involved close collaboration with many other biomedical ontology initiatives. For example, the earlier stage of VO development involved close collaboration with the development of the Ontology for Biomedical Investigations (OBI)10,11. Several key terms such as ‘vaccine’ and ‘vaccination’ were defined in VO on the basis of close communication with the OBI team, and the early stage of OBI ‘assay’ modeling also utilized a vaccine investigation use case10. As another example, the VO team has worked with the Ontology for Parasite Lifecycle (OPL) development team by incorporating parasite lifecycle classes in VO’s account of vaccine development59. The combined application of VO and OPL provides a superior means for modeling and analyzing novel strategies for developing vaccines targeted to different stages of the life cycle of parasitic pathogens59.

Beyond the five areas of VO applications reported in this manuscript, VO has the potential to significantly advance our systematic understanding of vaccine mechanisms and to facilitate vaccine development. For example, we have started to categorize and define a number of different types of vaccine-induced immune responses and immune profiles by referencing newly identified scientific findings and wide discussions in the vaccine community39. In addition, our VIOLIN and VO projects have collected data on thousands of vaccine antigens69 and vaccine-induced immune factors70. Further ontology-based AI analysis of these data presents new opportunities to uncover how vaccines work and what types of immune pathways vaccines induce, which may suggest new directions for future research.

With the wealth of vaccine data accessible from the literature and other public resources, integrating all available vaccine data into the VO presents a significant challenge. To address this, we have developed templates and modules and generated a Makefile to automate importing terms from external resources as vehicles for creating new ontology terms in VO. Nevertheless, vaccine data collected from various sources, particularly from literature, still requires manual curation. This process is time-consuming and slows VO development, though it fosters high-quality standards in its results. Efforts to automate the integration of new vaccines into the VO are ongoing. Recently, we developed VaxLLMs, a tool that automates the annotation of Brucella vaccine data from literature71. This tool efficiently retrieves structured Brucella vaccine data from the literature with high performance. In the future, we plan to enhance the functionality of VaxLLMs and incorporate it into the broader VO development pipeline.

While large language models (LLMs) are being used extensively in research, they are only the beginning of the myriad possibilities that ML can contribute to advancement in modern scientific discovery. The drug R&D pipeline is a flow of research activities that range from machine-assisted hypothesis generation with computational biology and laboratory experiments to compound validation and large-scale manufacturing and quality assurance and control. This process is also monitored by a robust regulatory framework. This complex environment of biomedical research activities calls for a hybrid approach requiring human expertise in diverse domains (including algorithm development, advanced statistical analysis, semantic technology, laboratory and in vivo experimentation) alongside computational infrastructure that can scale appropriately. To construct this hybrid solution, one must map the various ML/AI methods to the different steps along this drug R&D pipeline. It will then be apparent that this hybrid solution must be established on the solid foundation of well-curated data, primarily organized through ontological representation alongside machine-readable graph representations. VO applications have already begun leveraging generative AI in this context72,73.

Many directions exist for future VO development. Currently, VO primarily focuses on the representation of vaccines in English-speaking countries. A future effort is to extend VO’s inclusion of licensed vaccines from non-English speaking countries and regions. This inclusion will allow advanced integration and intelligent analysis of the large amount of vaccine data produced around the world. We are also developing LLMs to improve our literature mining performance in order to capture more vaccine-data from the literature. We plan to standardize every vaccine record in clinical trial databases such as clinicaltrials.gov, but achieving such an ambitious goal will require significant effort from a broader range of stakeholders. Meanwhile, new applications can be developed to demonstrate further the power of VO in supporting advanced vaccine research and development in the current era of AI-assisted research.

Methods

Collaborative development to reach consensus in VO

In the first three years, domain and ontology experts met regularly in virtual meetings and annual face-to-face meetings were also held. During this period, key terms in vaccinology were identified and incorporated into the VO, and ontology design patterns were discussed and developed. Once the high-level hierarchy and design patterns were established, meetings were held to address emerging issues.

Beginning in January 2024, we started holding weekly meetings, focusing on reviewing the VO’s high-level classes and design patterns. These sessions improved class definitions and design patterns, ensuring greater accuracy and consistent representation within VO.

Since January 2023, we have also organized regular bi-weekly meetings as part of the OHDSI Vaccine Vocabulary Workgroup. These meetings emphasize the hierarchical classification of licensed human vaccines using VO and their application in EHR data analysis43,64,74. We are closely collaborating with the OHDSI vocabulary workgroup, aiming to provide a unified hierarchical structure and enhance the classification of vaccines in the OHDSI vocabulary through the use of VO.

VO developers and external users were encouraged to submit a term request or report issues/bugs through the VO issue tracker on GitHub (https://github.com/vaccineontology/VO/issues); this mechanism to request changes or additions has actively been maintained to this day. This approach supports the accuracy of VO terms and helps ensure VO meets the needs of the communities it serves.

VO development strategies

VO is a community-driven ontology in vaccinology that adheres to the OBO Foundry principles (e.g., openness and collaboration)17 and eXtensible Ontology Development (XOD) strategy75. The latter includes four basic methods: ontology term reuse, semantic alignment, use of ontology design patterns for new term generation, and community effort75.

If terms already exist in VO, no further action is needed. If terms are found in other OBO Foundry ontologies, they are imported into the VO using the OntoFox tool65. A combination of top-down and bottom-up methods was used in VO development. The top-down approach was initiated using the BFO as an upper-level ontology, a framework widely adopted by the OBO Foundry and other ontologies to facilitate interoperability16,19. Domain experts identified the key terms in the field of vaccinology to ensure that VO covers all aspects of the field. Serving as high-level concepts, these terms were defined and aligned with BFO and other OBO Foundry ontologies.

The bottom-up approach concerns vaccine data gathered from a wide range of sources to ensure comprehensive coverage and meet community needs, encompassing both human and nonhuman animal vaccines at various stages of development and approval. For human-licensed vaccines, data were sourced from established drug terminologies, including RxNorm and RxE5, as well as from public websites. Both RxNorm and RxE terminologies were provided by the OHDSI vocabulary workgroup. CVX codes, which are part of the Health Level Seven International (HL7) standards and used to standardize vaccine data across various healthcare systems, were downloaded from the CDC website (https://www2.cdc.gov/vaccines/iis/iisstandards/vaccines.asp?rpt = cvx).

Information on vaccines licensed for use in the United States was downloaded from the FDA vaccine site (https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/vaccines/vaccines-licensed-use-united-states). Licensed veterinary vaccines were retrieved from the USDA product catalog downloaded from the USDA website (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/veterinary-biologics/licensed-products). Vaccines under development were primarily identified through scientific publications accessed via PubMed, providing insights into experimental and emerging vaccine technologies. Vaccines in clinical trials were tracked using data from clinicaltrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov), which provides data in HL7® and FHIR® format to support scientific data sharing and reuse, and NCI Thesaurus (https://ncithesaurus.nci.nih.gov/), which is free of copyright and openly available for reuse (https://www.cancer.gov/policies/copyright-reuse). Both clinicaltrials.gov and NCI Thesaurus, along with associated references, offer detailed information on vaccine candidates tested in various phases of clinical evaluation. Developed by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the NCI Thesaurus7 is a comprehensive reference terminology for cancer-related research, and the vaccines identified within it are cancer vaccines.

Besides vaccines, vaccine adjuvants were also extracted from the literature via PubMed. Additionally, vaccine adjuvants were added to VO based on requests from the Vaccine Adjuvant Compendium (VAC) (https://www.niaid.nih.gov/research/vaccine-adjuvant-compendium-vac). The VAC team sought support from the VO development group upon its initiation in 2021. The VAC database catalogs novel, accessible vaccine adjuvants, including those developed with NIAID support. The database collects and displays the characteristics and metadata pertaining to vaccine adjuvant types in order to help vaccine developers identify adjuvants suitable for use in vaccines against given immune-mediated and infectious diseases and against cancer. Data entered into VAC details properties of an adjuvant, such as its chemical nature, receptor, induced immune profile, and route of immunization, as well as associated publications and contact information for establishing agreements. VO’s developers have collaborated closely with the VAC team to systematically represent each VAC adjuvant record in VO. Each VAC record is represented in VO with a unique identifier, and the vaccine adjuvant-induced immune profile (e.g., Th1-biased immune profile) is also represented, supporting standardized annotation and query based on various adjuvant types (e.g., microbial derivative vaccine adjuvant, TLR agonist vaccine adjuvant, emulsion vaccine adjuvant) and vaccine-induced immune profiles.

All sources used by the VO are openly accessible and can be located by other researchers using the provided URLs. OHDSI Standardized Vocabularies are publicly available at athena.ohdsi.org. Licensing and terms of use for each dataset are referenced above.

This multi-faceted approach to vaccine data collection ensures that researchers and healthcare professionals have access to reliable and up-to-date information on vaccines across both human and veterinary domains. It encompasses vaccines at various stages of development and targets not only infectious diseases but also other conditions, such as cancer.

Reuse of existing OBO ontology resources

To establish an ontology interoperable with OBO Foundry ontologies, VO was developed using BFO. In addition, VO utilized the OBO Metadata Ontology (OMO) (https://github.com/information-artifact-ontology/ontology-metadata), an ontology of specified annotation properties that are used to annotate ontology terms for all OBO Foundry ontologies. The relation terms defined in the Relation Ontology (RO) were employed to represent commonly used relations76. RO Core is a small subset of RO containing the widely used relations in all OBO Foundry ontologies. The full versions of BFO, OMO, and RO core relations were imported into VO using OWL ontology importing. Other reused terms from existing ontologies were extracted and then imported on a term-by-term basis using OntoFox (http://ontofox.hegroup.org/)65.

OBO Foundry ontologies are released under a Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Unported (CC BY 3.0, https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/) license or later, or dedicated to the public domain under Creative Commons CC0 1.0 (CC0 1.0, https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/); both options permit free reuse.

Adding to and editing of VO

VO is developed using the W3C standard OWL2 Web Ontology Language (http://www.w3.org/TR/owl-guide/). Initially, VO terms were manually added to VO and edited using Protégé OWL editor (http://protege.stanford.edu/)77. However, adding many terms manually is time-consuming, error-prone, and requires OWL knowledge. To facilitate the efficient addition of terms to VO, ODPs were developed to create patterns for various VO terms38,61. The terms and their associated data that follow the same design pattern were formalized in a structured template in tabular format. The ROBOT tool template feature was used to convert the template into an OWL format file45. The terms were added or edited by making changes in the template and rerunning the ROBOT tool45. The Protégé editor 5.6.4 was primarily used to visualize and check the consistency of the ontology using ELK 0.6.0 reasoner35.

Naming conventions for vaccines and vaccine adjuvants

VO collects data on vaccines and vaccine adjuvants from various sources. As a result, the original names are often not descriptive or unambiguous, especially for those still at the research stage. To improve clarity, names are standardized when added to VO. Specifically, the suffixes ‘vaccine’ and ‘vaccine adjuvant’ are appended if they are missing from the original names. For vaccines, the vaccine type is included in the name when available but not indicated in the original label. To facilitate reference to the original sources and support NLP applications, original names are added as synonyms in VO.

Ontology release and quality control

The VO is released regularly, every 1-2 months. The latest VO OWL file is always available at http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/vo.owl. The release pipeline was established and automated using a Makefile, which streamlines the workflow. The process begins by merging multiple OWL files into a single file. Next, a file with the fully inferred hierarchy is generated using the ELK 0.6.0 reasoner. The final released VO OWL file includes a specific URI, with the release date as the version IRI. This means specific releases remain available through this permanent, dated URL, and provides stability to users who can try newer releases and revert to a previously dated one if needed. As part of the release process, the ELK reasoner is utilized to verify ontology consistency and generate the inferred hierarchy, while the ROBOT tool’s report feature is employed to check the quality of the release file16,45, ensuring compliance with OBO Foundry principles17. For each release, VO must pass ROBOT quality checks with no errors and show no inconsistencies when evaluated with the ELK reasoner. Together, these automated checks, combined with the lexical-based identification of missing relations and the community-driven issue tracking via GitHub, provide a multi-layered approach to maintaining the completeness, consistency, and accuracy of VO classes.

Beyond the standard release quality checks conducted with ROBOT, an automated lexical-based tool78, is employed to identify missing “is-a” relations and duplicate terms, supporting further quality assurance. This tool operates by constructing “linked” and “unlinked” concept pairs based on shared lexical features, and generating Acquired Term Pairs (ATPs) to capture their unique word differences. When an ATP from a linked pair matches one from an unlinked pair, it suggests a potentially missing “is-a” relation. Such candidate relations undergo expert review and validation before incorporation into VO. This ATP-based tool is applied to the inferred hierarchy of the ontology, complementing the ELK reasoner.

SPARQL and DL queries

The ontology was queried using both the Simple Protocol and Resource Description Framework (RDF) Query Language (SPARQL) and DL languages. SPARQL queries were executed via Ontobee’s SPARQL endpoint (https://sparql.hegroup.org/sparql/), while DL queries were conducted using the DL Query tab in the Protégé OWL editor36.

VO applications

A systematical literature survey was performed to identify all the papers citing the VO. Representative VO applications were reported to demonstrate the usage of VO in data annotation, data modeling, data analysis, and text mining.
