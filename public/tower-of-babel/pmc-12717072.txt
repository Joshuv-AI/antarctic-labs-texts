Impact of novel probiotic strains isolated from Algerian fermented butter and green tea waste on broilers’ production quality
Authors: Mohamed Cherif Bentahar, Djilali Benabdelmoumene, Said Dahmouni, Wasim S. M. Qadi, Zineb Bengharbi, Bouasria Benbouziane, Ahmed Mediani, Faidruz Azura Jam, Mohd Asraf Mohd Zainudin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Probiotics have emerged as a promising alternative to antibiotic growth promoters, with increasing interest in strains isolated from traditional and agro-industrial sources due to their natural adaptation and functional potential in animal feeds. This study investigated the effect of four probiotic strains on the growth performance and meat quality of broiler chicken. The selected strains included Levilactobacillus brevis DC01-A and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum DC04 isolated from traditional Algerian fermented foods (Dhan), as well as Levilactobacillus brevis TF03 and Levilactobacillus brevis TF13 isolated from fermented green tea waste. A total of 150 Arbor Acres chicks were randomly allocated into five groups, including a non-supplemented control, and raised until 42 days of age. Probiotic administration significantly enhanced final body weight, with TF03 and TF13 groups reaching 2787.5 g and 2750.0 g, respectively, compared to 2356.8 g in the control (p < 0.05). Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was significantly improved for the TF groups while no significant effect exert for the DC and the control groups. Meat from probiotic-treated broilers showed increased dry matter (TF03: 28.50%), protein (TF03: 22.50%), and lipid content (TF03: 4.15%) compared to the control (DM: 26.53%, protein: 21.36%, lipids: 3.45%). Mineral content also improved significantly in TF03 (1.20%) and TF13 (1.19%) groups. Lipid oxidation, assessed by TBARS, was lowest in TF03 (0.59 mg MDA/kg) and TF13 (0.60 mg MDA/kg) versus 0.82 mg MDA/kg in the control. The dietary supplementation of broilers with selected Lactobacillus strains resulted in notable modifications in the fatty acid composition. The control group exhibited a typical fatty acid profile dominated by palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), and oleic acid (C18:1). Among the treated groups, the TF03 and TF13 strains induced significant shifts toward healthier lipid profiles, characterized by a reduction in saturated fatty acids (SFA) and an enrichment in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA). These results confirm that probiotics derived from traditional and sustainable sources can significantly improve broiler performance, carcass quality, and meat oxidative stability, supporting their application as natural feed additives.

Introduction

The incorporation of probiotics in poultry nutrition has gained considerable attention as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters, aiming to enhance gut health, immune function, and overall production performance1,2. Probiotics, primarily consisting of beneficial bacteria strains such as Lactobacillus spp., Bacillus spp., and Lactococcus spp., play a crucial role in promoting digestion, enhancing nutrient absorption, and modulating gut microbiota composition. These effects contribute to improved feed efficiency and meat quality, thereby supporting sustainable poultry production practices3,4.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in isolating probiotics from traditional fermented foods, as these naturally occurring microorganisms exhibit strong adaptability to the gastrointestinal environment and possess unique bioactive properties that contribute to poultry health5,6. Fermented feeds have been shown to enhance broiler performance by proliferation of beneficial microbial populations, suppressing pathogenic bacteria, and strengthening immune responses3,7,8. Among the various sources of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), traditional Algerian fermented foods, such as Dhan (fermented goat milk butter), represent an underexplored reservoir of probiotic strains with potential applications in poultry nutrition5. Additionally, agricultural by-products, such as fermented green tea waste, have gained attention as sustainable sources of functional probiotics capable of improving feed efficiency and meat quality in poultry production9,10.

The impact of probiotics on broiler meat quality has been extensively documented, with notable improvements observed in protein content, fatty acid profiles, and oxidative stability of meat11,12. Especially probiotics strains such as Bacillus subtilis and LAB strains, have been associated with increased carcass yield, improved amino acid composition, and favorable lipid profiles4,13. Beyond their role in growth performance, probiotics have also been shown to enhance bone mass, immune response, and organ development in broilers8,12. While the benefits of probiotics in poultry production are well established, research on probiotic strains isolated from traditional fermented foods and agro-industrial by-products remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the effects of probiotics isolated from Dhan and fermented green tea waste on broiler growth performance, organ weight, and meat quality. By assessing the functional properties of these novel probiotic strains, this research seeks to contribute to the development of sustainable and effective probiotic-based feed additives for poultry production.

Materials and methods

Lactic acid bacteria strains

The four Lactobacillus strains utilized in this study were obtained from the strain collection of the Laboratory of Applied Animal Physiology, University of Mostaganem, Algeria, ensuring access to well-characterized and preserved bacterial isolates. Two strains, Levilactobacillus brevis DC01-A and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum DC04 were previously isolated from traditional Algerian fermented foods (Dhan a goat milk butter) and characterized utilizing molecular methods using 16 S rRNA as stated by Bentahar et al.5. The remaining two strains Lactobacillus were newly isolated from fermented green tea waste in this study. Selection criteria for all strains were based on their potential probiotic properties, including their tolerance to various stress conditions and their ability to thrive in diverse environments. The availability of both previously identified and newly isolated strains provided robust foundation for assessing their functional and probiotic potential in poultry nutrition.

Isolation of Lactobacillus strains from fermented green tea waste and culture conditions

A total of 10 g of green tea (Camellia sinensis) waste was combined with 90 mL of peptone water supplemented with 20% glucose in a sterilized 250 mL Schott flask, which served as the fermentation vessel. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized to achieve a uniform suspension and subsequently incubated under anaerobic condition at 30 °C for 7 days. The fermentation medium was meticulously formulated to promote optimal microbial growth and metabolic activity. Lactobacillus were isolated from fermented green tea waste by suspending 1 mL of sample in 9 mL of sterile normal saline solution (0.85% NaCl). The suspension was vortexed, serially diluted in NaCl-peptone solution (0.85% w/v NaCl, 0.1% w/v peptone), and plated onto modified De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Conda Lab, Madrid, Spain) (pH 5.4) supplemented with 0.5% calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) using the spread plate method. The plates were then incubated aerobically at 35 °C for 24–72 h14. Colonies exhibiting a creamy-white appearance with clear zones around colonies were identified as potential Lactobacillus candidates15. All bacterial isolates, including the reference probiotic strain, were cultivated aerobically in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h prior to further analysis. For long term preservation, the strains were stored at −80 °C in MRS broth supplemented with 30% (v/v) glycerol to maintain viability and genetic stability16.

Identification of Lactobacillus isolates

Molecular identification

The selected Lactobacillus TF03 and TF13 isolates were identified at the species level through sequencing of the 16 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene. Genomic DNA extraction was performed following the protocol described by Boucard and Thomas17, with minor modifications. Specifically, cells from a single fresh colony were lysed in a suspension of 500 µL containing approximately 10^10 cell and 0.1 g of 0.1 mm glass beads (Retsch, Haan, Germany). Cell lysis was carried out using the Precellys Evolution homogenizer apparatus (Bertin Instruments, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) through two cycles of 15 s at 5,800 × g. Following cell disruption, the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 20 °C to remove cellular debris, and genomic DNA was subsequently isolated from the supernatant.

The 16S ribosomal RNA amplification

PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was conducted using primers J4 (5′-ACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) and J7 (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) (Eurofins, Luxembourg), as described by Sukrama, Pinatih18. The reaction mixture (25 µL total volume) consisted of 1 µL of bacterial DNA template (50 ng), 0.1 µL of DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5 µL of dNTP Mix 10 mM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA), 0.5 µL of Green Buffer (10X; Jena Bioscience, Germany), 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 20.9 µL of nuclease-free water. Amplifications were performed in a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The process concluded with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min.

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic evaluation

The 16S rRNA gene sequences of all DNA samples were verified through sequencing (Eurofins MWG Operon, Ebersberg, Germany) utilizing universal primers J4 and J719. The obtained sequences underwent analysis using the Unipro UGENE software20,21, and were subjected to BLAST analysis via the NCBI database22. To assess the sequences’ similarity to known strains, they were compared with entries in the EzTaxon genome database23,24. Subsequently, the 16S rRNA gene sequences of the identified Lactobacillus strains were deposited in NCBI GenBank. Sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW within the MEGA 11 program, and a phylogenetic tree was generated using the neighbour-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Species assignment for isolates was based on high sequence similarity and distinct phylogenetic grouping25,26.

Biosafety testing of Lactobacillus isolates

A comprehensive assessment of the safety profile of the isolates was conducted by evaluating their respective haemolytic activity, DNase production, and gelatinase enzymatic activity.

Haemolytic assay

The hemolytic activity of the Lactobacillus isolates was determined following the method described by Yasmin et al.27, with minor modifications. Briefly, the isolates (log 8 Colony-Forming Unit (CFU)/mL) were sub-cultured on Colombia agar plates (BioMérieux®, France) supplemented with 5% sheep blood and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Haemolytic activity was evaluated based on the presence and characteristics of zones surrounding the bacterial colonies. The observed haemolytic patterns were classified into three groups: γ-hemolysis, characterized by the absence of zone formation around colonies indicating nonpathogenic; α-hemolysis, marked by greenish zones surrounding the colonies; and β-hemolysis, evidenced by the presence of transparent zones around colonies, indicating complete lysis of red blood cells28.

Deoxyribonuclease (DNase) activity

The DNase activity of the Lactobacillus isolates was evaluated using a modified protocol reported by Colombo et al.29. The isolate was inoculated onto DNase methyl green agar plates (HiMedia Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) using the streak plate method followed by incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the plates were examined for the presence of clear halos around the colonies, indicating DNase activity through degradation of DNA in the medium. This evaluation is crucial for determining the potential application of Lactobacillus strains in probiotic formulations.

Production of gelatinase

The assessment of gelatinase activity was conducted using nutrient gelatin tubes containing a medium composed of meat extract (0.3%), peptone (0.5%) and gelatin (12%) (Himedia, Mumbai, India). The tubes were incubated for 7–14 days at 30 °C, with daily observation for signs of liquefaction. Persistent liquefaction, even following a cooling period of 1 h at 4 °C, was interpreted as a positive sign for gelatinase activity and the enzymatic hydrolysis of gelatin30.

Preparation of Lactobacillus isolates and microencapsulation

Preparation of Lactobacillus isolates

Lactobacillus isolates were cultivated overnight in MRS broth at 37 °C. Following incubation, the bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and subsequently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 6.5) to remove residual media components. The pellet was then resuspended in 0.9% saline solution and stored at 4 °C until further analysis31.

Preparation of encapsulation solutions

Microcapsules were prepared using the methods described by Bambace, Alvarez32. The coating solution consisted of a sterile aqueous solution of 2% (w/v) food-grade sodium alginate (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). For the probiotic formulation, alginate solutions were loaded with Lactobacillus isolates in equal proportions (1:1) and aseptically mixed using a homogenizer for 1 min at 9000 rpm to obtain probiotic suspensions of approximately 5 × 1011 CFU/mL. The alginate isolate mixture was then loaded into a sterile 10 mL syringe with a blunt 27-gauge needle and dripped into a sterile aqueous solution of 2% (w/v) calcium chloride (CaCl₂) with stirring at 5000 rpm for 30 min. The formation of microcapsules occurred immediately through crosslinking upon contact with the CaCl₂ solution. The resulting microcapsules were washed three times with saline by centrifugation at 6000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min and then resuspended in physiological saline. Additionally, microcapsules without bacteria were used as the negative controls.

Ethical considerations

All treatments employed adhered to authorized standards for the ethical handling of animals in accordance with Algerian laws and regulations, and were approved by Abdelhamid Ibn Badis University in Mostaganem, Algeria (Ref No 15/25; February 2025). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Animal study

The animal experiment was conducted at the poultry research facility of the Laboratory of Applied Animal Physiology, University of Mostaganem, Algeria. A total of 150 one-day-old Arbor Acres broiler chicks were procured from a certified commercial hatchery and raised under controlled management and environmental conditions in accordance with standard poultry production guidelines. The chicks were randomly assigned into five experimental groups (n = 30 chicks per group), ensuring balanced and homogeneous initial body weights across treatments. Each group received a specific probiotic treatment as follows: Control group without probiotic supplementation; Lactobacillus strains DC01-A, DC04, TF03, and TF13 received their respective probiotic treatments via drinking water. Throughout the experimental period, all groups were fed a nutritionally balanced commercial diet formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements for broiler chickens. Feed and drinking water were provided ad libitum to ensure unrestricted access, thereby facilitating optimal growth conditions. Each group was housed in floor pens measuring 1.5 m × 1.2 m (1.8 m²), with 30 chicks per pen (16.7 birds/m²). Fresh wood shavings were used as bedding. The facility was maintained at a temperature of 32 ± 1 °C during the first week, gradually reduced by 2–3 °C per week to 24 °C by day 35, with relative humidity maintained at 60–70%. Lighting was provided for 23 h/day during the first week, then adjusted to 18 h light and 6 h dark for the remainder of the trial. Environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, light) were continuously monitored and regulated using automated digital sensors33.

Probiotic administration commenced on day 10 post-hatch and continued until day 42, covering both the critical growth and finishing phases of broiler production. The rationale behind selecting this particular period was based on established evidence highlighting the rapid development and functional maturation of the gastrointestinal tract during this period. Administering probiotics during this physiological window offers the greatest potential to positively influence gut microbiota composition, improve nutrient absorption efficiency, modulate immune responses, and consequently enhance growth performance and overall animal health. The probiotic strains were freshly prepared daily and administered via drinking water at a target concentration of approximately 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL. To ensure accurate and consistent probiotic intake, water consumption was recorded daily for each pen, with the mean daily water intake per bird calculated by dividing the total pen water intake by the number of birds per pen (30 birds). Across the supplementation period, the average daily water intake per bird was 130 ± 15 mL (range: 110–150 mL/bird/day), resulting in an estimated probiotic dose of 1.3 × 10¹¹ CFU per bird per day. No significant differences in water intake were observed among groups, ensuring consistent dosing across replicates. To maximize probiotic viability and stability, probiotic solutions were prepared fresh each morning and administered immediately. All procedures conformed to institutional and international animal care and welfare guidelines34.

Growth performance parameters

Growth performance parameters, including body weight, feed intake, average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR), were systematically measured in vivo throughout the experimental period. Each bird was individually weighed on days 10, 17, 24, 31, 38, and 42 using an electronic precision balance (± 0.1 g accuracy) according to established poultry research standards35. Feed intake was recorded weekly by accurately measuring the provided quantity and subtracting the remaining amount at the end of each week, facilitating precise calculation of weekly feed consumption per pen36. Subsequently, average daily gain (ADG, g/day) was calculated by dividing total body weight gain within each period by the number of days elapsed between measurements35. Additionally, feed conversion ratio (FCR) was determined by dividing cumulative feed intake by cumulative body weight gain over the corresponding intervals35,37. At the conclusion of the trial (day 42), final body weights and cumulative growth performance metrics were summarized for each experimental group to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of probiotic supplementation on broiler growth performance.

Evaluation of meat quality attributes

At the end of the experimental period (day 42), meat samples were collected from the breast muscles (Pectoralis major) of randomly selected broilers (n = 10 per treatment group). Samples were stored immediately at − 20 °C until further analysis. The samples were subsequently evaluated for dry matter, minerals, proteins, lipids, lipid oxidation (TBARS), and fatty acid composition.

Dry matter determination

Dry matter (DM) content was determined following the standard Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) method38. Briefly, approximately 5 g of homogenized muscle samples were weighed accurately and placed in pre-weighed aluminum dishes. The samples were then dried in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h. following the drying, the samples were cooled in a desiccator and weighed until constant weight was achieved. The DM percentage was calculated by weight difference before and after drying.

Mineral analysis

Total mineral (ash) content was measured according to AOAC36. Briefly, approximately 2 g of meat samples were placed in pre-weighed porcelain crucibles and incinerated in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 8 h until a white-grey ash obtained. The crucibles were then cooled in a desiccator to prevent moisture absorption before being reweighed. The total mineral content was calculated by the weigh difference and expressed as a percentage of wet sample weight.

Lipid content

Total lipid content was extracted using Folch method39. Briefly, approximately 5 g of homogenized meat was subjected to extraction using a chloroform-methanol solution (2:1, v/v). The resulting mixture was then filtered through a Whatman No. 1 filter to remove particulate matter. The filtrate was washed with 0.88% potassium chloride (KCl) solution to remove non-lipid contaminants and ensure the separation of the organic (chloroform) phase. The collected organic phase was collected and subsequently evaporated under nitrogen stream to prevent oxidative degradation. The total lipids content was then quantified by gravimetric analysis and expressed as a percentage of wet meat weight.

Protein determination

Protein content was quantified using the Kjeldahl method according to AOAC procedures36. Approximately 0.5 g of meat sample was digested in concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) in the presence of catalyst tablets containing copper sulfate (CuSO₄) and potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄) until a clear solution was obtained. The nitrogen content was then determined using an automatic distillation-titration system (Büchi Labortechnik AG, Switzerland). The protein content was calculated using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25 and expressed as percentage of the sample weight.

Lipid oxidation (TBARS Assay)

The extent of lipid oxidation in meat samples was evaluated by measuring thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS), following the method described by Botsoglou et al.40. Briefly, approximately 2 g of homogenized meat sample was mixed with 10 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), and the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C using a refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf 5804 R, Hamburg, Germany). Subsequently, 2 mL of the resulting supernatant was combined with 2 mL of 0.02 M thiobarbituric acid solution (TBA, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and incubated in a water bath (Memmert WNB14, Memmert GmbH, Schwabach, Germany) at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 532 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Lipid oxidation levels were expressed as milligrams (mg) of malondialdehyde (MDA) equivalents per kilogram (kg) of meat.

Fatty acid composition analysis

The fatty acid profile of meat samples was assessed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Lipids extraction was performed following the protocol established by Folch et al.39. The obtained lipid extracts were then trans esterified into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) using methanolic potassium hydroxide, as outlined by Christie and Han41. The FAMEs were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a mass spectrometer and a capillary column (DB-23, 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness). The injector temperature was maintained at 250 °C, operating in split mode with a ratio of 20:1. The oven temperature was programmed from 50 °C, increasing at 10 °C per min until reaching 230 °C. Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Identification of individual fatty acids was carried out by comparing retention times and mass spectra with those of a standard FAME mixture (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Fatty acid composition was expressed as relative percentages of the total identified fatty acids.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 26.0. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences among treatment groups, followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test when significant differences were detected. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Identification of Lactobacillus strains by 16S rRNA sequencing

Molecular techniques play a crucial role in strain identification, providing superior accuracy in characterizing LAB compared to traditional phenotypic methods42,43. Recent taxonomic revisions of the Lactobacillus genus have resulted in the establishment of 23 new genera, such as Levilactobacillus, Lentilactobacillus, and Lactiplantibacillus, as reclassified by Zheng et al.44 through a polyphasic approach integrating phenotypic and genotypic analyses.

In this study, the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced from the total genomic DNA of selected Lactobacillus isolates to determine their taxonomic identities. The sequences obtained exhibited a high similarity of over 99% to 100% when compared with GenBank reference entries (Table 1), surpassing the 98.5–98.7% threshold for species delineation established by Yarza et al.45 The 16S rRNA gene analysis confirmed that three bacterial strains belonged to the genus Levilactobacillus, while one strain was classified as Lactiplantibacillus. These taxonomic assignments were further validated using EzBioCloud (Table 2).

Biosafety evaluation of Lactobacillus strains

The safety assessment of probiotic Lactobacillus strains is crucial, as they can potentially exhibit pathogenic traits in rare cases46, while Lactobacillus species are generally recognized as safe (GRAS), some strains have been associated with opportunistic infections such as bacteremia, endocarditis, and peritonitis, particularly in individuals with pre-existing health conditions47. Safety assessments should be conducted at the strain level, as health claims and potential risks are strain-specific48. Many pathogens have evolved mechanisms to evade neutrophil extracellular traps, primarily through the secretion of nucleases that degrade the DNA backbone49. This strategy is employed by various microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and parasites49.

Hemolysis assays are commonly used to evaluate the safety of bacterial strains, including those considered GRAS50,51. Strains lacking hemolytic activity and other virulence factors are considered safer candidates for probiotic use29,51,52. Gelatinase assays also are crucial for evaluating the safety of LAB strains used in food applications. Colombo et al.29 found that none of the 15 LAB strains tested produced gelatinase, indicating their safety for potential use in the food industry. Similarly, Todorov et al.53 also highlighted low frequencies of virulence genes in LAB strains, including gelatinase, suggesting their suitability for fermented food products. The safety of LAB strains as potential probiotics for human use or in the food and pharmaceutical industry was evaluated by assessing the production of DNase enzyme. DNase is an enzyme that degrades DNA and its production by LAB strains could potentially have negative consequences on human health or on the quality and safety of food and pharmaceutical products. If DNase activity was detected, this could indicate that the LAB strains have the potential to cause harm or have a negative impact on the quality and safety of products54.

In present study, the Lactobacillus isolates exhibited no hemolytic, DNase and gelatinase activities, further confirming their safety for potential application as probiotic microbial cultures (Table 3). These findings were consistent with previous research on Lactobacillus strains isolated from fermented tea and goat milk, which also reported negative results for hemolytic, DNase and gelatinase activities52,54–58. These findings highlight their suitability for incorporation into food products and probiotic formulations, reinforcing their potential for functional and health-promoting practices.

Assessment of growth performance in broiler chickens

Bodyweight evolution

The results presented in Fig. 2 indicate that dietary supplementation with selected Lactobacillus strains (DC01-A, DC04, TF03, and TF13) significantly improved body weight compared to the control group. Specifically, the TF03 and TF13 groups exhibited the highest final body weights, reaching 2787.5 g and 2750.0 g respectively by day 42, significantly higher (p < 0.05) than the control group (2356.83 g). Intermediate improvements were recorded with the DC01-A strain (2536.66 g), whereas the DC04 strain (2431.66 g) presented significant yet relatively lower improvements. These results align with previous studies reporting enhanced broiler growth following probiotic administration59,60.

The superior body weight gains observed in TF03 and TF13 groups could be explained by beneficial effects on gut morphology, microbial balance, and intestinal health, consistent with previous observations in probiotic-supplemented broilers60. TF03, exhibiting the most pronounced growth-enhancing effect, and TF13, with comparable performance, likely share key probiotic functionalities such as adhesion capability, acid and bile salt resistance, and antimicrobial activity, warranting further molecular characterization. The DC01-A strain, closely related to Lactobacillus rhamnosus, induced moderate body weight improvements, consistent with findings reported by Shah et al.61, demonstrating physiological and economic benefits attributed to novel probiotic strains in poultry. The comparatively lower efficacy observed with DC04 could reflect variations in strain viability, gut colonization efficiency, or metabolite production, as previously highlighted by Poberezhets and Kupchuk62. The observed growth trajectory from day 10 to day 42 followed the typical sigmoid growth pattern documented in broilers, characterized by rapid weight gain during the growth phase (days 17–38), followed by stabilization toward the end of the finishing period60,63. This supports the timing chosen for probiotic supplementation, emphasizing its impact during critical stages of growth and intestinal maturation.

Feed intake, average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR)

The findings of the current study (Fig. 3) reveal that probiotic supplementation had a substantial influence on the productive performance of broiler chickens, particularly in terms of feed intake, average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Notably, birds receiving Lactobacillus strains TF03 and TF13 demonstrated the highest average daily gain although there was not significantly different with both DC strains value in term of the feed intake. Meanwhile, there was significantly lower FCR values compared to that of DC strains group and control group, suggesting enhanced feed utilization efficiency. The observed increase in feed intake among probiotic-supplemented groups is consistent with the results of Abdel-Hafeez et al.64, who reported that probiotics improve feed palatability and stimulate digestive enzyme activity, thereby promoting higher voluntary feed consumption. Similarly, Bitterncourt et al.65 found that broilers supplemented with probiotics consumed significantly more feed while achieving higher weight gain, particularly during the grower and finisher phases. In our study, the TF03 and TF13 strains outperformed the control group in both ADG and FCR, indicating enhanced nutrient absorption and metabolic efficiency. These findings align with previous research conducted by He et al.66, who demonstrated that Enterococcus faecium PNC01 significantly reduced FCR in broilers by modulating gut microbiota composition and enhancing gut morphology. Likewise, Suvorov et al.67 confirmed that supplementation with E. faecium L3 as a feed additive improved feed efficiency and growth metrics through immunomodulatory and antimicrobial mechanisms.

The difference between the FCR values observed for the DC strains and TF strains further support the probiotic efficacy in enhancing growth performance relative to feed intake. Skvortsova et al.68 emphasized that probiotics could enhance the biological potential of broiler chickens by promoting gut health and nutrient retention, which translates to improve FCR and economic returns. In this context, Akbarimehr et al.69 reported that a multi-strain probiotic supplement improved FCR and health biomarkers in quail, qualifying the general applicability of probiotic blends in poultry species. Our results also reflect those of Patel et al.70, who observed a significant decrease in FCR and increased body weight gain in broilers fed diets supplemented with either single or mixed probiotic cultures. The improvements in feed efficiency observed in our trial can be attributed to a combination of factors including enhanced villus height, competitive exclusion of pathogens, and modulation of gut microbial ecology—mechanisms consistently reported in probiotic studies across various avian models65,67. Additionally, the superior performance of TF03 and TF13 may be attributed to their strain-specific probiotic properties, such as bile salt hydrolase activity, lactic acid production, and tolerance to acidic pH, all of which favor colonization in the gastrointestinal tract. These characteristics support sustained enzymatic activity, improved digestion, and more efficient feed conversion68,70. The Lactobacillus population in the broiler’s gut during growing and harvesting was not significantly affected by the conditioning temperature at time on the animal feed71.

Among the tested groups, strains TF03 and TF13 consistently exhibited superior meat quality, with higher dry matter (28.50% and 28.40%, respectively), elevated protein levels (22.50% and 22.40%), and increased lipid content (4.15% and 4.08%), compared to the control group (Table 4).

The observed increase in dry matter and crude protein content following probiotic supplementation (Table 4) aligns with the findings of Swamy and Upendra72, who reported enhanced dry matter retention and protein accumulation in the pectoral muscle of broilers supplemented with Lactobacillus-based probiotics. Similarly, Podolian73, demonstrated that probiotic administration improved the chemical composition of meat, including protein and ash content, likely due to enhanced nutrient absorption and amino acid assimilation. Our results corroborate those of Poberezhets et al.62, who noted that probiotics positively affect nutrient digestibility and retention, thereby supporting the synthesis of structural proteins and contributing to higher meat protein content. This is further supported by Khaksefidi and Rahimi74, who observed significant improvements in carcass quality and meat protein levels in broilers receiving probiotic-enriched diets.

The enhanced mineral content recorded in the probiotic groups, particularly with TF03 and TF13 (Table 4), suggests improved mineral bioavailability. This effect is likely linked to probiotic-induced improvements in gut health and increased expression of nutrient transporters in the intestinal epithelium, as previously reported by Swiatkiewicz et al.75. The higher ash content reflects better retention of calcium, phosphorus, and trace elements in muscle tissue, contributing to improved meat mineral density. The higher minerals absorptions in the animals could probably contribute to the form of chelated transition mineral which proved to increase the minerals in chicken bones and egg quality76. Interestingly, while probiotics are often associated with leaner carcasses, our study found a moderate yet significant increase in intramuscular fat (IMF) in probiotic-treated groups. This aligns with the findings of Nduku et al.77, who reported that probiotics can modulate lipid metabolism by altering gut microbiota and short-chain fatty acid production, thereby influencing fat deposition pathways. The higher IMF content may contribute positively to meat juiciness and flavor, key quality attributes in consumer preference. Numerous sensory studies have demonstrated that increased intramuscular fat is associated with enhanced juiciness, tenderness, and overall palatability in poultry and red meat, which are highly valued by consumers33,34.

Evaluation of lipid oxidation in meat samples

The TBARS results from this study indicate that probiotic supplementation significantly reduced lipid oxidation in broiler breast meat compared to the control group (Fig. 4). The control group exhibited the highest TBARS value (0.82 mg MDA/kg), indicating increased malondialdehyde formation and thus greater oxidative degradation of muscle lipids. In contrast, the probiotic-supplemented groups DC01-A, DC04, TF03, and TF13 showed significantly lower TBARS values (ranging from 0.59 to 0.73 mg MDA/kg) compared to control group. Interestingly, TF03, and TF13 exhibited close results and significantly lower TBARS values (p < 0.05) in comparison with DC01-A, DC04. These findings suggest that probiotic strains confer protective effects against lipid peroxidation, thereby improving the oxidative stability of broiler meat. Wahyono et al.78 emphasized in their meta-analysis that TBARS serve as a reliable biomarker for assessing meat quality degradation cause by lipid oxidation, particularly under storage or oxidative stress conditions. The reduced MDA levels observed here align with the mechanism whereby probiotics enhance gut-derived antioxidant defenses and may influence systemic oxidative balance through the production of metabolites such as glutathione and short-chain fatty acids79.

Furthermore, the stability of TBARS values among different probiotic strains indicates that while all strains exhibited antioxidant activity, none demonstrated superior antioxidant protection under the tested conditions. This outcome is consistent with findings that natural antioxidants such as aqueous coriander and saffron extract effectively reduced TBARS level, however, differences between treatments may become less pronounced when basal oxidative stress is already low80,81. Additionally, the strong correlation between lipid oxidation and the generation of biogenic amines and volatile compounds associated with off-odors, both of which influence meat quality and shelf life82,83. Lower TBARS values may therefore reflect not only indicate improved nutritional retention but also contribute to enhanced sensory and microbiological quality in probiotic-fed broilers. Furthermore, Mancinelli et al.84 also linked reduced TBARS levels to a more favorable fatty acid profiles and improved oxidative balance in different chicken strains, further supporting the potential benefit of probiotic supplementation in poultry production. Interestingly, diet with antioxidant supplements also showed to increase in poultry bone strength even under heat stress condition85.

Fatty acid composition

The dietary supplementation of broilers with selected Lactobacillus strains resulted in notable modifications in the fatty acid composition of breast meat, reflecting both the metabolic impact of probiotics and their potential to enhance meat quality (Table 5). The control group exhibited a typical fatty acid profile dominated by palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), and oleic acid (C18:1), which are commonly reported as major contributors to the lipid fraction in poultry9,86. Among the treated groups, the TF03 and TF13 strains induced significant shifts toward healthier lipid profiles, characterized by a reduction in saturated fatty acids (SFA) and an enrichment in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), particularly those of the n-3 series. In terms of SFA, TF13 showed the lowest total content (36.06 ± 0.02%), whereas TF03 had the highest (37.16 ± 0.14%), both still within acceptable nutritional limits. These changes may be attributed to the strain-specific modulation of host lipid metabolism or to enhance microbial enzymatic activities that influence fatty acid absorption and biosynthesis. This aligns with the work of Wang et al.87, who reported that Lactobacillus johnsonii modulated lipid deposits in chickens by altering host-microbial interactions. The minor SFA, such as arachidic (C20:0) and behenic acid (C22:0), remained stable across treatments, suggesting that probiotics predominantly influence major lipid fractions.

The MUFA profile, dominated by oleic acid (C18:1), showed a significant increase in DC01-A (35.99 ± 0.11%) compared to the other treatments, confirming previous studies that link probiotic supplementation with elevated MUFA levels in meat9,11. However, TF03 and TF04 exhibited slightly lower MUFA levels, which might be explained by competitive shifts in fatty acid metabolism favoring PUFA biosynthesis over MUFA storage. Notably, the most desirable effect was observed in the PUFA fraction. TF03 and TF13 significantly enhanced total PUFA levels (21.70 ± 0.06% and 21.55 ± 0.04%, respectively), with higher concentrations of linoleic acid (C18:2n-6) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3). These findings are consistent with those of Gheorghe et al.88 and Liu et al.89, who reported increased incorporation of PUFA in broiler meat following probiotic administration. The observed increase in essential n-3 fatty acids, such as EPA and DHA, particularly in TF13, may also be linked to the improved gut microbiota balance and enhanced intestinal absorption induced by probiotic activity90. Moreover, the modulation of desaturase and elongase enzymes by probiotic metabolites might explain the improved n-3 fatty acid synthesis, as suggested by Krysiak et al.1 and Yang et al.91.The high unsaturated fatty acid can improve the profile of fatty acids in thigh and enhance immune responses in broiler chickens92.

Overall, these results confirm that Lactobacillus strains, especially TF03 and TF13, can enhance the nutritional quality of poultry meat by promoting a favorable fatty acid profile, increasing PUFA (particularly n-3 series), and maintaining an optimal SFA/MUFA/PUFA balance. These improvements not only contribute to a better meat health index but also respond to consumer demand for functional animal-derived foods. The outcomes of this study reinforce the growing body of evidence supporting probiotic supplementation as a sustainable strategy for improving meat lipid composition and overall poultry product quality.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrates that Lactobacillus strains isolated from traditional Algerian fermented Dhan and green tea waste significantly enhance growth performance, feed efficiency, and meat quality in broilers. Strains TF03 and TF13 notably outperformed other treatments, showing the highest final body weights, improved FCR, and superior meat composition, including higher dry matter, protein, and intramuscular fat levels. Furthermore, all probiotic-supplemented groups exhibited reduced lipid oxidation, confirming their role in preserving oxidative stability and extending meat shelf life. These benefits, combined with the proven biosafety of the strains, underscore their potential as functional and sustainable alternatives to synthetic growth promoters in poultry production. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, including gut microbiota modulation, metabolite production, and host immune interactions, as well as evaluating the shelf life and sensory attributes of probiotic-treated meat under storage and retail conditions.
