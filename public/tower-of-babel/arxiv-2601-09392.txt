The wanted extension of Fujii and Tsurumaru’s formula for the spectral radius of the Bell-CHSH operator
Albrecht Böttcher, Ilya M. Spitkovsky

Abstract
. This paper is motivated by a recent paper of Yuki Fujii and Toyohiro Tsurumaru in which they established a beautiful formula for the spectral radius of the Bell-CHSH operator on finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. To tackle the operator on infinite-dimensional spaces, they elaborated a method based on appropriate approximation of commutators of infinite-dimensional orthogonal projections by commutators of orthogonal projections on finite-dimensional spaces. We here give a proof of Fujii and Tsurumaru’s original formula that works in all dimensions. We also present an alternative approximation procedure, uncover the connection of the problem with block Toeplitz operators, and derive good estimates and explicit expressions for the spectral radius in concrete cases.

1.
The Bell-CHSH operator

Let
P_{1},Q_{1}
and
P_{2},Q_{2}
be two pairs of orthogonal projections on separable complex Hilbert spaces

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, respectively,
and put
A_{i}=2P_{i}-I,B_{i}=2Q_{i}-I
. The Bell-CHSH operator is the operator

{\mathcal{B}}=(A_{1}+B_{1})\otimes A_{2}+(A_{1}-B_{1})\otimes B_{2}

on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
. The spectral radius
\varrho({\mathcal{B}})
is of interest in quantum mecanics.
See, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
12
]
.
Taking into account that
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are involutions,
A_{i}^{2}=B_{i}^{2}=I
,
a simple straightforward
computation gives

{\mathcal{B}}^{2}=4(I\otimes I)+[A_{1},B_{1}]\otimes[B_{2},A_{2}],

where
[A,B]=AB-BA
. Consequently, since
\varrho(S)=\varrho(-S)
for every operator
S
, we get the
well-known formula

\varrho({\mathcal{B}})=\sqrt{4+\varrho([A_{1},B_{1}])\varrho([A_{2},B_{2}])},

(1)

As
[A_{i},B_{i}]=4[P_{i},Q_{i}]
, we are so led to the study of
the spectral radius of the commutator of two selfadjoint involutions or
equivalently, of two orthogonal projections.

Thus, let
P,Q
be two orthogonal projections on a separable complex Hilbert space
V

and put
A=2P-I,B=2Q-I
. We have
\|A\|^{2}=\|AA^{*}\|=\|A^{2}\|=1
and, analogously,

\|B\|=1
. It follows that

\varrho([A,B])\leq\|[A,B]\|\leq\|A\|\,\|B\|+\|B\|\,\|A\|=2,

and hence (1) implies that
\varrho({\mathcal{B}})\leq\sqrt{4+2\cdot 2}=2\sqrt{2}
, which is
Tsirelson’s famous inequality
[
12
]
. We here are interested in expressions for
\varrho([P,Q])

and thus for
\varrho([A,B])=4\varrho([P,Q])
and
\varrho({\mathcal{B}})
that allow us the exact computation of these
quantities for concrete operators
P,Q
.

2.
An exact formula for the spectral radius

For a bounded linear operator
T
, we denote by
\sigma(T)
the spectrum of
T
.

Theorem 1
.

We have

\varrho([A,B])=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A+B)}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}(4-\lambda^{2})}=\sqrt{\lambda_{0}^{2}(4-\lambda_{0}^{2})},

where
\lambda_{0}
in
\sigma(A+B)
is a point
for which
\lambda^{2}
is closest to
2
.

Proof.

We use Halmos’ theorem
[
8
]
as quoted in Theorem 1.2 of
[
2
]
.
Let us first assume that
P
and
Q
are in general position,
which means that the terms
(1,1,0,0)
and
(1,0,1,0)
in Theorem 1.2 of
[
2
]

are absent.
Then
A+B-\lambda I
is unitarily similar to
\begin{pmatrix}2(I-H)-\lambda I&2W\\
2W&2(H-I)-\lambda I\end{pmatrix}
. By computing the determinant of this matrix we see that
\lambda
is in the spectrum of
A+B
if and only if

1-\lambda^{2}/4
is in the spectrum of
H
. The spectrum of
W^{2}=H(I-H)
is therefore
the set of

\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\right)\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\right)\right)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\right)

where
\lambda
ranges over
the spectrum of
A+B
. The spectrum of
[P,Q]
is the set of
\nu
for which

\det\begin{pmatrix}-\nu I&W\\
-W&-\nu I\end{pmatrix}=\nu^{2}I+W^{2}

is not invertible. These values
\nu
are purely imaginary and we may write
\nu=i\mu
with real
\mu
.
Consequently, the spectrum of
[P,Q]
is the set of
\nu
such that

-\nu^{2}=\mu^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\right)
with
\lambda
in the spectrum of
A+B
.
The maximum of
|\mu|
is attained at a
\lambda_{0}
for which
\lambda^{2}/4
is closest to
1/2
or equivalently,
at a
\lambda_{0}
for which
\lambda^{2}
is closest to
2
.
For this
\lambda_{0}
, we have

\varrho([A,B])=4\varrho([P,Q])=4|\mu|=4\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{4}\right)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{0}^{2}(4-\lambda_{0}^{2})},

as asserted.

If
P,Q
are not in general position, then
[P,Q]
is unitarily similar
to

(0,0,0,0)\oplus\begin{pmatrix}0&W\\
-W&0\end{pmatrix}

(2)

and hence
the spectrum of
[P,Q]

is the union of
\{0\}
and the above set of
\nu
for which

-\nu^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\right)
with
\lambda
in the spectrum of

\begin{pmatrix}2(I-H)&2W\\
2W&2(H-I)\end{pmatrix}
, and we therefore get the same expression for
\varrho([A,B])
.
\square

For finite-dimensional spaces
V
, this theorem was established by Fujii and Tsurumaru in
[
6
]
.
They wrote
“when
V
is infinite-dimensional, it is not known
whether or how the equality
\varrho([A,B])=\sqrt{\lambda_{0}^{2}(4-\lambda_{0}^{2})}
can be extended, either in the form of an
equality or an inequality.” Theorem 1 is the extension they were asking about.

The meta theorem of our paper
[
3
]
is that Halmos’ two projections theorem
is something like Robert Sheckley’s Answerer: no question
about two orthogonal projections
will go unanswered, provided the question is not foolish, and questions that are not foolish
are those where one already knows most of the answer.
Fujii and Tsurumaru also employed Halmos’ theorem, but they did this only for
the irreducible
2\times 2
blocks of the orthogonal decomposition of
[A,B]
resulting from
the unitary representation of the infinite dihedral group
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}\ast{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
on
V
. We here
apply Halmos’ theorem in its full generality directly to
[A,B]
, without using an orthogonal
decomposition as an intermediate step. Since we knew the result of
[
6
]
in advance
and thus most of the answer, the present study is a nice illustration of the message we tried to
convey in
[
3
]
.

Incidentally, and this is again in the vein of
[
3
]
,
one may also employ Halmos’ theorem in its full generality to prove Tsirelson’s inequality.
Indeed, we know that
[P,Q]
is unitarily similar to the operator (2), it is easily seen that the spectral radius of this operator is equal to the spectral radius of
W
, and the latter
is
\sqrt{x(1-x)}
where
x
is the point in the spectrum of
H
that is closest
to
1/2
. Consequently, we always have
\varrho([P,Q])\leq 1/2
, whence

\varrho([A,B])\leq 2
and
\varrho({\mathcal{B}})\leq 2\sqrt{2}
.

In
[
6
]
it is claimed that the
\lambda_{0}

in the formula
\varrho([A,B])=\sqrt{\lambda_{0}^{2}(4-\lambda_{0}^{2})}

is the
\lambda_{0}
closest to
\sqrt{2}
. However, there is
a subtlety in the matter: a
\lambda_{0}
closest to
\sqrt{2}
need not
be a
\lambda_{0}
for which
\lambda^{2}
is closest to
2
. Indeed, if, for example,
A
and
B
are given
on
{\mathbf{C}}^{3}
by the selfadjoint and involutive matrices

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&2x-1&\sqrt{x(1-x)}\\
0&\sqrt{x(1-x)}&1-2x\end{pmatrix}

with
x=0.01
, then the spectrum
\sigma(A+B)
equals
\{-2\sqrt{x},2\sqrt{x},2\}=\{-0.2,0.2,2\}
,
the point
\lambda=2
is closest to
\sqrt{2}
, but
\lambda=\pm 0.2
are the points for which
\lambda^{2}
is closest
to
2
.

3.
Pairs in convergent-angle one-shifted form

Let
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and put
s=\sin\theta
and
c=\cos\theta
.
Note that
c^{2}+s^{2}=1
and thus
\frac{1-c}{s}=\frac{s}{1+c}
and
\frac{1+c}{s}=\frac{s}{1-c}
.
Furthermore, if
\theta<\pi/2
then
c+s>1
, and if
\theta>\pi/2
then
-1<c+s<1
.
Fujii and Tsurumaru consider pairs of involutions in so-called
constant-angle one-shifted form
. Such a pair is
given by

\displaystyle A(\theta)={\rm diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\ldots\right),

\displaystyle B(\theta)={\rm diag}\left(1,\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\
s&-c\end{pmatrix},\ldots\right).

In
[
6
]
, it is claimed that
\sigma(A(\theta)+B(\theta))=[-2s,2s]
for all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
In fact, the following is true.

Theorem 2
.

If
\theta\leq\pi/2
then
\sigma(A(\theta)+B(\theta))=[-2s,2s]\cup\{2\}
, and if

\theta\geq\pi/2
then
\sigma(A(\theta)+B(\theta))=[-2s,2s]
.

We will give two proofs. They are both based on the observation that

A(\theta)+B(\theta)=T(a)+K
where
T(a)
is the infinite
tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with the symbol
a(e^{i\varphi})=s(e^{-i\varphi}+e^{i\varphi})=2s\cos\varphi
and

K
is the matrix whose
1,1
entry is
1+c
and the other entries of which are zero.
We here encounter an instance of a situation in which work with infinite Toeplitz matrices
is simpler than working with finite Toeplitz matrices. We note that the appearance of the outlier

2
should not come as too much a surprise. If
\theta=0
, then

A(0)={\rm diag}(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots),\quad B(0)={\rm diag}(1,1,-1,1,-1,\ldots),

whence
A(0)+B(0)={\rm diag}(2,0,0,0,0,\ldots)
with spectrum
\{0,2\}
. On the other
hand, if
\theta=\pi
, we get

A(\pi)={\rm diag}(-1,1,-1,1,\ldots),\quad B(\pi)={\rm diag}(1,-1,1,-1,1,\ldots)

and thus
A(\pi)+B(\pi)={\rm diag}(0,0,0,0,0,\ldots)
with spectrum
\{0\}
.

The essential
spectrum
\sigma_{\rm ess}(T)
is the set of
\lambda\in{\mathbf{C}}
for which
T-\lambda I
is not Fredholm,
that is, not invertible modulo compact operators.

First proof.

The essential spectrum of
T(a)+K
is
[-2s,2s]
, and hence the spectrum of
T(a)+K

is a subset of
{\mathbf{R}}
containing
[-2s,2s]
. Take
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}\setminus[-2s,2s]
. Since

T(a)+K-\lambda I
is Fredholm of index zero,
\lambda
is in the spectrum of
T(a)+K
if and only
if the kernel of
T(a)+K-\lambda I
is not trivial.

Put
\mu=\lambda/s
. The equation
(T(a)+K-\lambda I)x=(T(a)+K-s\mu I)x=0
is the infinite system

\displaystyle(1+c-s\mu)x_{0}+sx_{1}=0

\displaystyle x_{0}-\mu x_{1}+x_{2}=0

\displaystyle x_{1}-\mu x_{2}+x_{3}=0

\displaystyle\ldots.

Suppose this system has a non-trivial solution.
Ignoring the first equation, we get
x_{n}=\alpha q_{1}^{n}+\beta q_{2}^{n}
where
q_{1},q_{2}
satisfy
the equation

q^{2}-\mu q+1=0
. As
q_{1}q_{2}=1
and
|q_{1}+q_{2}|=|\mu|>2
, we have
|q_{1}|<1
and
|q_{2}|>1
.
Thus, we get
x_{n}=\alpha q_{1}^{n}
. Since
q_{1}-\mu=-1/q_{1}
, the first equation of the infinite system
becomes

0=(1+c-s\mu)\alpha+s\alpha q_{1}=\alpha[1+c+s(q_{1}-\mu)]=\alpha[1+c-s/q_{1}],

and since
\alpha\neq 0
, it follows that
q_{1}=s/(1+c)
.

We have
s/(1+c)=(1-c)/s\geq 1
for

\theta\geq\pi/2
, contradicting the requirement that
|q_{1}|<1
. Consequently, for
\theta\geq\pi/2

no point outside
[-2s,2s]
is in
\sigma(A(\theta)+B(\theta))
.

So let
\theta<\pi/2
. In that case
q_{1}=s/(1+c)=(1-c)/s
lies in
(0,1)
, we get
q_{2}=1/q_{1}=(1+c)/s
,
and hence

\frac{\lambda}{s}=\mu=q_{1}+q_{2}=\frac{1+c}{s}+\frac{s}{1+c}=\frac{2(1+c)}{s(1+c)}=\frac{2}{s}.

It results that
\lambda=2
. For
\lambda=2
and
x_{n}=\alpha q_{1}^{n}
, the first equation of the system reads

(c-1)\alpha+s\alpha q_{1}=\alpha[c-1+s(1-c)/s]=0,

the remaining equations amount to

1-\frac{2}{s}q_{1}+q_{1}^{2}=1-\frac{2}{s}\frac{s}{1+c}+\frac{1-c}{s}\frac{s}{1+c}=0,

and as this is true,
\lambda=2
belongs indeed to the spectrum of
T(a)+K
.
\square

Second proof.
We know that
[-2s,2s]
is contained in the spectrum of
T(a)+K
.
Take again
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}\setminus[-2s,2s]
. The spectrum of
T(a)
is
[-2s,2s]
and hence

T(a)-\lambda I=T(a-\lambda)
is invertible. So we may write

T(a)+K-\lambda I=T(a-\lambda)[I+T^{-1}(a-\lambda)K],

and it follows that
T(a)+K-\lambda I
is invertible if and only if so is
I+T^{-1}(a-\lambda)K
.
The matrix of the latter operator is the identity matrix plus
1+c
times the first column of

T^{-1}(a-\lambda)
, and therefore it is invertible if and only if its
1,1
entry is nonzero.

To get the inverse of
T(a-\lambda)
, we employ Wiener-Hopf factorization. With
t=e^{i\varphi}
, we
have

\displaystyle a(t)-\lambda=s(t^{-1}+t)-\lambda=st^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{s}t+t^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=st^{-1}(t-q_{1})(t-q_{2})=-sq_{2}\left(1-\frac{q_{1}}{t}\right)\left(1-\frac{t}{q_{2}}\right),

where
q_{1},q_{2}
are as in the first proof. It follows that

\displaystyle T^{-1}(a-\lambda)=-\frac{1}{sq_{2}}T\left((1-t/q_{2})^{-1}\right)T\left((1-q_{1}/t)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{sq_{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&&&\\
1/q_{2}&1&&\\
1/q_{2}^{2}&1/q_{2}&1&\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&q_{1}&q_{1}^{2}&\ldots\\
&1&q_{1}&\ldots\\
&&1&\ldots\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\end{array}\right),

which shows that the
1,1
entry of
I+T^{-1}(a-\lambda)K
is
1-\frac{1+c}{sq_{2}}
. This is zero
if and only if
q_{2}=(1+c)/s=s/(1-c)
. As
|q_{2}|>1
and
s/(1-c)\leq 1
for
\theta\geq\pi/2
,
we conclude that
\lambda
is not in the spectrum of
T(a)+K
if
\theta\geq\pi/2
. So let
\theta<\pi/2
.
Then
q_{2}>1
and since

1-\frac{\lambda}{s}q_{2}+q_{2}^{2}=0
, we obtain

\frac{\lambda}{s}=\frac{1}{q_{2}}(1+q_{2}^{2})=\frac{1}{q_{2}}+q_{2}=\frac{1+c}{s}+\frac{s}{1+c}=\frac{2(1+c)}{s(1+c)}=\frac{2}{s}.

Thus, necessarily
\lambda=2
. Conversely, if
\lambda=2
, then
q_{2}=(1+c)/s=s/(1-c)
satisfies the equation

1-\frac{\lambda}{s}q_{2}+q_{2}^{2}=0
and is greater than
1
. As
1-\frac{1+c}{sq_{2}}=1-\frac{1+c}{s}\frac{s}{1+c}=0
,
we see that the
1,1
entry of
I+T^{-1}(a-\lambda)K
is zero, and consequently,
\lambda=2
is in the spectrum
of
T(a)+K
.
\square

The following theorem is already in
[
6
]
, but the proof given there is based
on the incorrect assumption that
\sigma(A(\theta)+B(\theta))=[-2s,2s]
for
\theta\in(0,\pi/4)
,
which mistake was fortunately neutralized by the fact that the outlier
\lambda=2
, though
being closest to
\sqrt{2}
, is not the
\lambda
for which
\lambda^{2}
is closest to
2
.

Theorem 3
.

In the case of a constant-angle one-shifted form we have

\varrho([A(\theta),B(\theta)])=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2\sin(2\theta)&\mbox{if}&0<\theta\leq\pi/4,\\
2&\mbox{if}&\pi/4\leq\theta\leq 3\pi/4,\\
2|\sin(2\theta)|&\mbox{if}&3\pi/4\leq\theta<\pi.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We use Theorems 1 and 2.
If
\theta\leq\pi/4
, then
\lambda^{2}=2^{2}=4
has distance
2
to
2
whereas
\lambda^{2}=(2s)^{2}=4s^{2}

is at the distance
2-4s^{2}
to
2
. Consequently,
\lambda_{0}=2s
is a point in the spectrum for which

\lambda^{2}
is closest to
2
. This implies that

\varrho([A(\theta),B(\theta)])=\sqrt{4s^{2}(4-4s^{2})}=4s\sqrt{1-s^{2}}=4sc=2\sin(2\theta).

If
\pi/4\leq\theta\leq 3\pi/4
,
then
\sqrt{2}\in[-2s,2s]
and hence
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{2}
minimizes the distance between
\lambda^{2}
and
2
.
Thus,
\varrho([A(\theta),B(\theta)])=\sqrt{2(4-2)}=2
. Finally, if
3\pi/4\leq\theta<\pi
,
then again
\lambda_{0}^{2}=(2s)^{2}=4s^{2}
is closest to
2
, and since
c<0
in this case, we obtain

\varrho([A(\theta),B(\theta)])=2|\sin(2\theta)|
.
\square

The general case of two involutions in one-shifted form is given by

A={\rm diag}\left(r(\omega_{1}),r(\omega_{2}),r(\omega_{3}),\ldots\right),\quad B={\rm diag}\left(1,r(\theta_{1}),r(\theta_{2}),r(\theta_{3}),\ldots\right).

where
r(\eta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\eta&\sin\eta\\
\sin\eta&-\cos\eta\end{pmatrix}
and
\{\omega_{j}\}_{=1}^{\infty},\{\theta_{j}\}_{=1}^{\infty}

are sequences of points in
(0,\pi)
.
We say that two such involutions are in
convergent-angle one-shifted form

if there is a
\theta\in(0,\pi)
such that
\omega_{j}\to\theta
and
\theta_{j}\to\theta
as
j\to\infty
. Let
P_{n}
be the projection that sends a sequence

\{x_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
to the sequence
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1},0,0,\ldots\}
. If
T
is an operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbf{Z}}_{+})
, then
P_{n}TP_{n}

may be identified with an
n\times n
matrix in the natural fashion. The spectrum
\sigma(P_{n}TP_{n})
is nothing but the set of the
eigenvalues of the matrix
P_{n}TP_{n}
.

Theorem 4
.

If
A
and
B
are in convergent-angle one-shifted form, then the spectrum of
P_{n}(A+B)P_{n}

converges in the Hausdorff metric to the spectrum of
A+B
.

Proof.
In the case at hand, we have
A+B=T(a)+K
where, as above,
T(a)
is the infinite tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
whose symbol is
a(e^{i\varphi})=2s\cos\varphi
with
s=\sin\theta
and
K
is a compact operator (given by a tridiagonal matrix
whose entries go to zero along each diagonal).
By Proposition 2.33 and Theorem 4.16 of
[
1
]
, the spectrum of

P_{n}(T(a)+K)P_{n}
converges in the Hausdorff metric to
\sigma(T(a)+K)\cup\sigma(T(a))
. (Note that
T(a)
is symmetric,
which implies that the
T(\widetilde{a})
occurring in Theorem 4.16 coincides with
T(a)
.) Since
\sigma(T(a))=[-2s,2s]
and

\sigma(T(a)+K)
contains the essential spectrum
\sigma_{\rm ess}(T(a))=[-2s,2s]
, we arrive at the assertion.
\square

Examples.
Let
\omega,\delta\in(0,\pi)
and

A={\rm diag}\left(r(\omega),r(\theta),r(\theta),\ldots\right),\quad B={\rm diag}\left(1,r(\theta),r(\theta),r(\theta),\ldots\right).

(4)

Put
c=\cos\theta,s=\sin\theta,\gamma=\cos\omega,\delta=\sin\omega
. The matrix of
A+B=T(a)+K
is the tridiagonal
Toeplitz matrix generated by
a(e^{i\varphi})=2s\cos\varphi
with the upper left
2\times 2
corner matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&s\\
s&0\end{pmatrix}
replaced by
\begin{pmatrix}1+\gamma&\delta\\
\delta&c-\gamma\end{pmatrix}
.
We know that
[-2s,2s]\subset\sigma(A+B)
and that
A+B-\lambda I
is Fredholm of index zero for
\lambda\notin[-2s,2s]
.
Hence, a point
\lambda
outside
[-2s,2s]
is in
\sigma(A+B)
if and only if
A+B
has a nontrivial kernel in
\ell^{2}({\mathbf{Z}}_{+})
.
Since
\|A\|=\|B\|=1
, we also have always
\sigma(A+B)\subset[-2,2]
. Furthermore, if
\pi/4\leq\theta\leq 3\pi/4
,
then
2s\geq\sqrt{2}
and hence
\sqrt{2}\in\sigma(A+B)
, which implies that
\varrho([A,B])=\sqrt{2(4-2)}=2
in this case.

The equation
(A+B-\lambda I)x=0
is the system

\displaystyle(1+\gamma-\lambda)x_{0}+\delta x_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\delta x_{0}+(c-\gamma-\lambda)x_{1}+sx_{2}=0,

\displaystyle sx_{n}-\lambda x_{n+1}+sx_{n+2}=0\;\>(n\geq 1).

This is satisfied if and only if
x_{n}=\alpha q^{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
, where
\alpha\neq 0
and
q
is the solution of the equation

q^{2}-(\lambda/s)q+1=0
whose modulus is less than
1
, and if
x_{0}
can be chosen so that the first two equations
of the system hold. Since
x_{1}=1

4.
Pairs in one-shifted form with two converging angles

A natural generalization of pairs in convergent-angle one-shifted form is pairs
of the form

A={\rm diag}\left(r(\omega_{1}),r(\omega_{2}),r(\omega_{3}),\ldots\right),\quad B={\rm diag}\left(1,r(\theta_{1}),r(\theta_{2}),r(\theta_{3}),\ldots\right)

with numbers
\omega,\theta\in(0,\pi)
such that
\omega_{j}\to\omega
and
\theta_{j}\to\theta
as
j\to\infty
.
Then
A+B=T(a)+K
with a selfadjoint block Toeplitz matrix
T(a)
and a compact operator
K
.
The diagonal block of
T(a)
is

\begin{pmatrix}\gamma-c&\delta\\
\delta&c-\gamma\end{pmatrix}
,
the block in the subdiagonal equals
\begin{pmatrix}0&s\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
,
the block in the superdiagonal is equal to

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
s&0\end{pmatrix}
, and the other blocks are zero.
Consequently,

a(e^{i\varphi})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma-c&\delta+se^{i\varphi}\\
\delta+se^{-i\varphi}&c-\gamma\end{pmatrix},

(7)

where
\gamma=\cos\omega,\delta=\sin\omega,c=\cos\theta,s=\sin\theta
. Put

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\sqrt{(c-\gamma)^{2}+(s-\delta)^{2}}=2\left|\sin\frac{\theta-\omega}{2}\right|,

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\sqrt{(c-\gamma)^{2}+(s+\delta)^{2}}=2\left|\sin\frac{\theta+\omega}{2}\right|.

Note that
s
and
\delta
are positive.
Unless
s=\delta
, which case was disposed of in the previous section, we have

-2\leq-\lambda_{2}<-\lambda_{1}<c-\gamma<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq 2.

(8)

In cases in which the spectrum of
A+B=T(a)+K
is available, Theorem 1 gives the spectral
radius. To determine this spectrum, it would be desirable to have a result like Theorem 4.
Since the blocks of
T(a)
are of size
2
, we consider
only
2n\times 2n
truncations.
It is known that

\sigma(P_{2n}(T(a)+K)P_{2n})\to\sigma(T(a)+K)\cup\sigma(T({\tilde{a}}))=:\Lambda

(9)

in the Hausdorff metric; see page 197 of
[
2
]
. Here
{\tilde{a}}
is given by

{\tilde{a}}(e^{i\varphi})=a(e^{-i\varphi})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma-c&\delta+se^{-i\varphi}\\
\delta+se^{i\varphi}&c-\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

Thus, the convergence of
\sigma(P_{2n}(T(a)+K)P_{2n})
to some limit set
\Lambda
is guaranteed.
However, the appearance of the spectrum
\sigma(T({\tilde{a}}))
in (8) causes some trouble
since it forces us to find out which parts of the limit set
\Lambda
constitute
\sigma(T(a)+K)=\sigma(A+B)

and which parts result only from the contribution of
\sigma(T({\tilde{a}}))
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
a
be given by
(6)
. The essential spectra of
T(a)
and
T({\tilde{a}})
are

\sigma_{\rm ess}(T(a))=\sigma_{\rm ess}(T({\tilde{a}}))=[-\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{1}]\cup[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]

and the spectrum of
T({\tilde{a}})
is
\sigma_{\rm ess}(T({\tilde{a}}))
if
s\leq\delta
and

\sigma_{\rm ess}(T({\tilde{a}}))\cup\{c-\gamma\}
if
s>\delta
.

Proof.
The essential spectrum of
T(a)
is the set of
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}
for which the determinant
of
a(e^{i\varphi})-\lambda I
vanishes for some
e^{i\varphi}\in{\mathbf{T}}
; see, e.g., Theorem 6.5 of
[
2
]
.
We have

\det(a(e^{i\varphi})-\lambda I)=\lambda^{2}-(c-\gamma)^{2}-\delta^{2}-2\delta s\cos\varphi-s^{2}=0

if and only if

\lambda^{2}=(c-\gamma)^{2}+\delta^{2}+2\delta s\cos\varphi+s^{2}
, and since
2\cos\varphi
fills the segment
[-2,2]
,
it follows that the essential spectrum of

is equal to
[-\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{1}]\cup[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
.
As
\cos\varphi=\cos(-\varphi)
, we get the same result for
\sigma_{\rm ess}(T({\tilde{a}}))
.

That the spectrum
\sigma(T({\tilde{a}}))
is a subset of the segment
[-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2}]

can be deduced from general results on
positively definite Hermitian matrix function; see Section 7.3
of
[
11
]
. However, we have also to look whether or not the spectrum contains points
in the gap
(-\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. We therefore proceed as follows.

Take
\lambda\notin\sigma_{\rm ess}(T({\tilde{a}}))
. Then the selfadjoint operator
T({\tilde{a}})-\lambda I
is Fredholm
of index zero, and hence
\lambda
belongs to
\sigma(T({\tilde{a}}))
if and only if the null space of this

is nontrivial. The equation

(T({\tilde{a}})-\lambda I)\begin{pmatrix}f_{+}\\
g_{+}\end{pmatrix}=0

with
f_{+},g_{+}
in
the Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbf{T}})
is equivalent to the existence of functions
h_{-},k_{-}\in\overline{H^{2}}({\mathbf{T}})

such that
h_{-}(0)=k_{-}(0)=0
and

\displaystyle(\gamma-c-\lambda)f_{+}(z)+(\delta+s\overline{z})g_{+}(z)=h_{-}(z),

\displaystyle(\delta+s{z})f_{+}(z)+(c-\gamma-\lambda)g_{+}(z)=k_{-}(z)

for

. A moment’s thought reveals that this can only be true for
k_{-}(z)=0
and
h_{-}(z)=\alpha\overline{z}

with some constant

. Thus, we arrive at the system

\displaystyle(\gamma-c-\lambda)zf_{+}(z)+(\delta z+s)g_{+}(z)=\alpha,

\displaystyle(\delta+sz)f_{+}(z)+(c-\gamma-\lambda)g_{+}(z)=0.

Let
\lambda\neq c-\gamma
. Solving the second of these equation for

and inserting the result
in the first equation, we get after some straightforward computation the equation

p_{+}(z)f_{+}(z)/(c-\gamma+\lambda)=\alpha
with

p_{+}(z)=s\delta\left[1+\frac{2-2c\gamma-\lambda^{2}}{s\delta}z+z^{2}\right].

This equation has a nontrivial solution

if and only if

is in
H^{2}({\mathbf{T}})
, which in turn happens
if and only if both roots of

lie outside the closed unit disk. As, by Vieta, the product of the two roots
is
1
, this is impossible. Consequently, a point
\lambda\neq c-\gamma
never belongs to the spectrum of
T({\tilde{a}})
.

So consider
\lambda=c-\gamma
. Then the system is reduced to

\displaystyle 2(\gamma-c-\lambda)zf_{+}(z)+(\delta z+s)g_{+}(z)=\alpha,

\displaystyle(\delta+sz)f_{+}(z)=0,

and this holds with a
nontrivial function

if and only if
\delta z+s
has no zero in the closed unit disk, that is, if and
only if
s>\delta
.
\square
