Exploring the impact of nutrition and autophagy on infectious diseases to inform the development of homeostatic-nanomedicines for HIV-TB coinfections
Authors: Retsepile Ephraim Maphasa, Sanjeev Rambharose, Nyaradzo Chigorimbo-Murefu, Burtram C Fielding, Admire Dube
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) and the human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immune deficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) remain among the most difficult infectious diseases to manage globally, especially in developing countries. The two diseases have also been shown to have a synergistic relationship that worsens the negative effects of each disease on patient health, disease management, and overall socioeconomic outcomes. Both diseases have been shown to accelerate each other’s progression and are associated with multiple micronutrient deficiencies (MNDs), which further compromise immune competence and perpetuate a cycle of increased vulnerability to infectious diseases and progressive nutritional deterioration. Autophagy, an intracellular catabolic process partially induced by nutrient deprivation, has also been recognized as a vital host defence mechanism that reduces intracellular pathogen load and mitigates disease progression in both HIV and TB infections. Given the observed overlap between nutrition and autophagy, this review explores the roles of autophagy and nutrition to identify agents that can be used as homeostatic medicines. The use of nanoparticles (NPs) to deliver homeostatic nanomedicine-based host-directed therapies (HDTs) is also explored to examine how this strategy could help restore immune homeostasis in infected individuals, while offering an alternative or complementary approach to conventional treatments for HIV, TB, and HIV-TB coinfection.Keywords: Infectious Diseases, HIV-TB Coinfection, Nutrition, Micronutrient deficiencies, Autophagy, Homeostatic Nanomedicines

Introduction

HIV/AIDS and TB, primarily caused by HIV and mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb), are among the most challenging infectious diseases worldwide. Developing countries often face the highest rates of HIV, TB, or co-infection of both [1, 2]. HIV and M. tb interact synergistically, worsening each other’s progression and increasing the risk of premature death if left untreated [1]. This interaction speeds up TB severity, making HIV the leading cause of death among individuals infected with M. tb [3].

TB has also been demonstrated to be the prime cause of death in people living with HIV (PLWH), being responsible for roughly 26% of all AIDS-related deaths in developing countries [1]. Latent TB (LTB) is the most common outcome for most M. tb-infections, where the mycobacterium remains in a dormant state without exhibiting any clinical signs in the host. However, the disease can progress to active TB when the infected individuals become immunocompromised either through immunosuppression from pathogenic infections, immunosuppressive therapy from steroid use, or through malnutrition-related acquired immunodeficiency [2, 3].

There is therefore a clear interconnection between infections, immunity, and micronutrients. Undernourished people are more susceptible to infections due to reduced nutrition, which has been confirmed to cause immune dysfunction [4]. For example, micronutrient deficiencies, also known as “hidden hunger,” increase host susceptibility to infectious diseases by weakening immune defences and by disrupting the normal function of the immune system [5–7]. Consequently, infectious diseases have a profoundly negative impact on the nutritional status of infected individuals; as they cause: (1) appetite loss, resulting in decreased nutrient intake, (2) intestinal damage, resulting in decreased nutrient absorption and malabsorption, along with (3) diarrhea and increased urinary excretion, which result in nutrient losses [1]. People infected with HIV and TB have been demonstrated as having multiple micronutrient deficiencies, especially in developing countries, where poverty reduces access to food, along with a prevalence of diets commonly consisting of staple foods with low nutrient density [8, 9].

The connection between autophagy and the cellular nutritional state has long been a point of interest among many researchers, dating back to the discovery of autophagy in the 1970s. Consequently, starvation became the first known stimulus known to trigger macroautophagy [10]. Earlier studies, performed mainly in rodents, showed that the liver’s autophagic activity is regulated by nutrient availability and hormonal changes induced by food consumption [11]. According to an analysis of lysosome-mediated protein breakdown rates in vivo, glucagon stimulates macroautophagy, whereas insulin is a strong inhibitor of this process [12].

Numerous studies have been conducted to demonstrate that intracellular nutrient sensors mediate the communication between autophagy and nutritional status [13]. For example, malnutrition hampers autophagy, weakening the immune system and making individuals more vulnerable to infections. The cycle of infection and starvation can also be exacerbated by infections that interfere with autophagy and cause nutrient shortages [14]. The regulation of macroautophagy is significantly influenced by the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). Under typical nutritional circumstances, this kinase actively inhibits macroautophagy while integrating nutritional cues (amino acids, insulin, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels) [14].

In this review, we aim to be the first authors to officially establish the concept of homeostatic nanomedicine by exploring the individual roles and the impact of the relationship between nutrition and autophagy on the progression of infectious diseases, especially as it relates to HIV, TB, and the HIV-TB coinfection. We also examine how knowledge gained from understanding these two processes can be leveraged to identify candidate molecules that can be used to develop potential homeostatic nanomedicine-based host-directed therapies (HDTs) for the treatment of either HIV, TB, or HIV-TB coinfections by restoring homeostasis in the infected individuals instead of developing drugs that target the two respective pathogens directly. The benefits of using NPs as delivery vehicles for carrying the HDTs is also discussed, along with exploring how the design of homeostatic nanomedicines can offer promise in overcoming the current limitations associated with the current treatment options available for HIV-TB co-infections. However, the current limitations facing the successful translations of the homeostatic nanomedicines for clinical applications; along with exploring future perspectives.

Nutrition and the immune system

The immune system plays an essential role in maintaining overall health by critically distinguishing between benign dietary compounds, such as nutrients, and potential threats, such as pathogens [15]. The gut-associated lymphoid tissue plays a vital role in this process, actively detecting and responding to substances within the digestive system [16]. Malnutrition, encompassing both under- and over-nutrition (such as overweight or obesity), is frequently associated with chronic inflammation, which impairs immune system function and increases the risk of infection [17].

Malnutrition occurs when the body lacks critical nutrients, which affects growth and physical functions [18]. This could be due to an inadequate diet, poor nutrient absorption, economic factors, or disease. The risk factors include poverty, restricted food access, poor dietary habits, health issues, and a lack of knowledge [19]. Being overweight or obese, often stemming from poor dietary habits and inactivity, similarly leads to persistent inflammation, reduced immunological function, and potentially diminished vaccine efficacy [19]. Severe malnutrition is a leading cause of immunodeficiency, particularly impacting children’s innate and adaptive immune functions, often linked to deficiencies in critical nutrients like zinc and vitamin A [20, 21].

A significant global health challenge arises from multiple micronutrient deficiencies, which are especially prevalent in developing countries due to monotonous, nutritionally poor diets [4]. These deficiencies contribute to undernutrition and infectious illnesses, which are major concerns in low-income nations and are exacerbated by poverty. Infections and nutritional deficiencies also interact reciprocally; infections worsen nutritional status, which in turn increases disease-related morbidity and mortality [22]. Proper nutrition is thus paramount for a robust immune system. Maintaining immune function necessitates a balanced diet rich in essential nutrients, including vitamins C, D, and E, minerals like zinc and selenium, and various bioactive substances [23].

Deficiencies in these elements can significantly weaken immune responses and heighten susceptibility to infections. Conversely, beneficial dietary components such as antioxidants and anti-inflammatory compounds from fruits and vegetables, along with probiotics from fermented foods, can enhance immune function by balancing the gut microbiome [24]. The extensive synergistic relationship between malnutrition and infectious diseases was first noted 50 years ago and has been observed for the following years since [25]. A substantial amount of research has also since been published in this field, and there is concurrence that malnourished children die at a higher rate when compared to their healthy counterparts [4, 26].

However, various crucial factors about the influence of malnutrition on host defence mechanisms have not been thoroughly researched, and there is a scarcity of studies that have investigated the outcomes of nutritional interventions on overcoming the synergy between malnutrition and infection [26]. However, current research outputs present enough data showing a correlation between micronutrients, immunity, and infections as shown in Fig. 1. Undernourished individuals also exhibit immunological malfunction, making them susceptible to infections [5]. Micronutrient deficiencies, sometimes referred to as “hidden hunger,” interfere with the normal functions of the immune system, lowering immunological defences and increasing vulnerability to different infectious diseases [4, 6]. Consequently, Infections have severe impacts on the host’s nutritional status, leading to decreased intake of nutrients due to lack of appetite, decreased nutrient absorption due to intestinal damage and malabsorption, and nutrient losses caused by diarrhoea and increased urine excretion [4].

The role of nutrition in HIV-TB coinfection

Most relevant studies have mainly focused on the link between widespread malnutrition or micronutrient deficiencies and childhood infections. Controlling infectious illnesses is becoming increasingly important in the prevention of malnutrition, and evidence-based nutritional interventions have been devised to reduce childhood morbidity and death [22]. Conversely, little research has been conducted to create efficient nutritional interventions for two of the most prevalent infectious diseases in adults, namely HIV and TB infection. Despite their clear distinctions, HIV and TB are both associated with wasting and frequently affect people from disadvantaged backgrounds [22].

Notably, the effector memory cluster of differentiation 4 positive (CD4+) thymus (T) lymphocytes in the gut mucosa are selectively reduced during the first HIV infection. These immunopathogenic characteristics, along with the systemic and persistent state of immunological activation, such as increased T cell turnover, are believed to contribute to the pathogenesis of HIV [27]. As a result, a dysfunctional T cell population devoid of functional potential, such as cytotoxic activity and cytokine generation, as well as a loss of proliferative ability in response to antigen stimulation, has been found as a feature of chronic antigenic stimulation. Furthermore, the resulting immune imbalance between regulatory T cells and T-helper 17 (Th17) cells during HIV disease progression has recently been associated with gut permeability and HIV pathogenesis [28].

This permeabilization of gut integrity can therefore affect patients’ ability to absorb necessary micronutrients, known as “hidden hunger”, that help the body fight the disease; as shown by the various micronutrient deficiencies associated with HIV, TB, and HIV-TB co-infections [4]. Because infections are strongly linked to nutritional status due to decreased nutrient intake, absorption, and nutrient losses, micronutrient deficiencies affect the immune system and the progression of illnesses. Immunity, infection, and micronutrients are thus connected [4]. An increased susceptibility to both HIV and M. tb has been linked to a dominant type-2 T helper polarized immune response, while coinfection amplifies the progression of each respective disease [29].

A cross-sectional study conducted in Ghana discovered that children with HIV/AIDS had increased rates of undernutrition, with low caloric and micronutrient intakes, especially folate, vitamin C, and iron deficits [30]. Low Body Mass Index (BMI) and inadequate early weight gain among TB patients starting treatment have both been linked to increased mortality and other undesirable treatment outcomes [31, 32]. Likewise, low BMI and low weight gain during the early stage of Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) are a predictor of mortality among HIV patients [22].

Additionally, HIV/AIDS is an underlying factor of malnutrition in children. There is a malnutrition prevalence rate of 40–64% in children with HIV/AIDS [33]. Similarly, M. tb is the leading cause of death from an infectious disease and is believed to have infected one-third of the world [34]. TB is particularly affected by protein-energy malnutrition, which is also common in developing countries where this infection is a primary cause of morbidity and mortality [35, 36]. Undernutrition can also have an impact on the clinical outcomes of TB. A recent meta-analysis identified a relationship between an increased risk of active TB and low vitamin D levels in serum [37].

It is worth noting that TB is a common disease whose progression is characterized by continuing inflammatory processes that worsen malnutrition and cause classic cachexia. Immunoglobulin 1 (IgG1) antibodies have been linked to increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines (Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and Interleukin 6 (IL-6)) but not anti-inflammatory cytokines (Interleukin 10 (IL-10)) [38]. Individuals with TB are more likely to have been undernourished at the time of exposure, because poverty is associated with both undernutrition and risk factors for TB exposure, such as smoking, drinking, and crowding [39]. Similarly, HIV is associated with food insecurity and unsafe sex. Although HIV often thrives in the environment of socioeconomic inequality rather than being driven exclusively by poverty, impoverished people are far more exposed to the repercussions of HIV [40].

Both HIV and TB lower an individual’s capacity to work, which leads to food insecurity in households even in the early stages of the disease. There is also a clear correlation between nutritional balance and low energy levels. Additionally, undernutrition is undoubtedly a risk factor for the development of TB disease including a reactivation of latent TB and can increase vulnerability to HIV and TB infection [22]. The occurrence of extremely drug-resistant strains of Mycobacterium has mainly been attributed to HIV coinfection and noncompliance with treatment protocols. It has also been suggested that the rise in drug-resistant M. tb strains is partly due to malnutrition [41]. Overall, proper nutrition is critical in HIV-TB coinfection, for boosting the immune system, enhancing treatment results, and decreasing comorbidities. Malnutrition impairs the immune system, rendering people more vulnerable to both HIV and TB, whereas proper nutrition can enhance treatment adherence and drug absorption [22].

In addition, nutritional deficiencies can affect various disease-specific outcomes among individuals with latent or symptomatic infections (such as TB and HIV), or who are currently on treatment for these diseases, as shown in Table 1 [22]. Dietary deficits or a lack of food to accompany the medication may have a negative impact on drug adherence, absorption, and metabolism, during TB or HIV therapy. These conditions can cause medication concentrations to be too high or too low, resulting in toxicities and efficacy impairment, ultimately leading to treatment failure, resistance, or both. Nutritional deficits can have a direct impact on pathogens and host immune functions. For example, reduced zinc consumption causes the loss of the thymus, a critical immune organ in both young children and animals [42]. Furthermore, a laboratory animal model has also been used to demonstrate that host selenium deficiency can turn avirulent coxsackie virus virulent [43].

Nutritional interventions used to treat deficiencies can be dangerous, even if the deficiency in question is deemed dangerous. This has largely been a concern for micronutrient supplementation, as iron supplementation has been demonstrated to increase the morbidity of infectious diseases in children residing in areas with high malaria incidences [44]. Nutrients delivered in a food matrix may have fewer negative effects unless given in large quantities, although the potential cannot be ruled out. Likewise, giving energy to starving people may also be dangerous, as it can lead to the potentially lethal refeeding syndrome [45, 46]. A dietary intervention, therefore, cannot be simply presumed to be beneficial or even safe, but must be tested in rigorous studies before being applied.

Autophagy

Autophagy is a broad term for mechanisms responsible for the transportation of cytoplasmic material, such as soluble macromolecules and organelles, by autophagosomes to lysosomes for degradation [47]. At least three forms of autophagy exist: chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), macroautophagy, and microautophagy, although macroautophagy is often referred to as autophagy [48]. As shown on Fig. 2, autophagosomes are formed when an isolating membrane called a ‘phagophore’ engulfs a section of the cytoplasm (0.5–1 μm in diameter). The autophagosome’s outer membrane combines with the lysosome to form an autolysosome, which uses lysosomal enzymes to degrade autophagosomal contents. Autophagosomes can also fuse with multivesicular structures called endosomes and with compartments that load the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule, MHC class II [48].

Schematic overview summarising the different forms autophagy. Adapted from [13]. The authors generated this figure with BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/)

During CMA, Heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein (Hsc70) is the cytosolic chaperone responsible for recognizing substrate proteins that contain a specific Lysine (K), Phenylalanine (F), Glutamic acid (E), Arginine (R), and Glutamine (Q) - pentapeptide sequence known as the KFERQ-like motif. Hsc70 guides these proteins to the lysosome, where they bind to the lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP-2 A) isoform receptor, unfold, and are translocated into the lysosome lumen for degradation [49]. Autolysosomes expand upon merging with additional autophagosomes and lysosomes. However, lysosomes are tubulated and fragmented for renewal at the termination phase [50]. Autophagy is critical in the relationship between nutrition and infectious diseases by eliminating cellular waste and pathogens. Autophagy is activated by nutritional deprivation and infection, which influences immune responses and pathogen elimination. Malnutrition can negatively affect autophagy, rendering people more vulnerable to infections; infections can therefore disrupt autophagy and cause malnutrition [13].

The autophagy system is believed to have evolved as a stress response to aid unicellular eukaryotic animals in surviving severe conditions, most likely by controlling protein and organelle quality and/or energy homeostasis. In complex metazoan organisms, a vast majority of cellular stress response pathways, such as those involved in regulating inflammation and immune responses, have been shown to interact with the autophagy mechanism [51]. This relationship occurs not just throughout the autophagy process, but also through direct interactions between autophagy proteins and immunological signalling molecules [52]. The autophagy pathway/proteins have a complex reciprocal relationship with immunity and inflammation processes; autophagy proteins are involved in both the induction and suppression of immune and inflammatory responses, whereas immune and inflammatory signals are involved in both autophagy initiation and inhibition [53].

Furthermore, as with cancer, neurological ailments, and aging, autophagy anomalies produced by gene mutation and/or microbial antagonism may contribute to the pathogenesis of a variety of inflammatory syndromes and infectious diseases [13]. Nutrient deficiency induces autophagy by inhibiting mTOR, which results in the translocation of the mTOR substrate complex (Unc-51-like kinase 1 or 2 (ULK1/2), Autophagy-related protein 13 (ATG13), FAK family-interacting protein of 200 kDa (FIP200) also known as Retinoblastoma 1-inducible coiled-coil 1 (RB1CC1), and Autophagy-related protein 101 (ATG101)) from the cytosol to domains of the endothelium reticulum (ER) or closely adjacent structures (Mizushima, 2010; Itakura and Mizushima, 2010). This results in the recruitment of the class III phosphatidylinositol-3-OH kinase (PI (3)K) complex, consisting of Autophagy-related protein 14 (ATG14), beclin 1, Vacuolar Protein Sorting (VPS) also known as Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase catalytic subunit type 3 (PIK3C3), and VPS15 (Phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 4 (PIK3R4) and the protein150 (p150) PI 3-Kinase Adaptor Protein) to the ER [54].

Ptdylinositol-3-phosphate (PtdIns (3)P), which is produced by the PI (3)K complex, attracts effectors such WD repeat domain phosphoinositide-interacting (WIPI) family proteins and double FYVE-containing protein 1 (DFCP1) [13]. Omegasomes are ER-associated Ω-like structures that are produced when DFCP1, which is diffusely distributed on the ER or Golgi, translocates to the autophagosome formation site in a PtdIns (3)P-dependent manner [55]. The most prevalent of the four WD-repeat protein Interacting with PhosphoInositides (WIPI) isoforms in most cell types is WIPI2, which operates downstream of DFCP1 and may encourage the growth of omegasomes into autophagosomes or isolation membranes [56]. Vacuole membrane protein 1 (VMP1), an ER-associated multi-spanning membrane protein, also plays a key role in autophagosome development. VMP1 interacts with beclin-1 and is present at the autophagosome formation site at an early stage, though it is believed to function at a late stage in autophagy [54, 57, 58].

Two ubiquitin-like conjugates are required for the final step in autophagosome formation: elongation of the separation membrane and/or enclosure completion. The first is the Autophagy-related (ATG) protein conjugate: ATG12-ATG5, which is generated by the ATG7 and ATG10 enzymes. It operates as a dimeric complex with Autophagy Related 16 Like 1 (ATG16L1) [59]. The second category includes the ATG7 and ATG3 enzyme-dependent; Gamma-aminobutyric acid receptor-associated protein (GABARAP), Golgi-Associated ATPase Enhancer of 16 kDa (GATE16), and the phosphatidylethanolamine (PE)-conjugated ATG8 homologues, Microtubule-Associated Protein 1 A/1B-Light Chain 3 (LC3) [60, 61]. Autophagy and trafficking across the trans-Golgi network, endosomes, and autophagosome precursors depend on another multi-spanning membrane protein called ATG9 [62]. According to research, the nuclear membrane, plasma membrane, and mitochondria may all serve as membrane sources to produce autophagosomes [63, 64].

Autophagy and infectious diseases

Several studies have shown that autophagy plays an important role in human cell defence against mycobacterial infection, and a genome-wide analysis of host genes that control M. tb replication indicated that autophagy regulators predominated [65]. Immune cells detect the presence of pathogens by activating pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that interact with pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as tall-like receptors (TLR) and NOD-like receptors (NLR). Signalling via these receptors also appears to underlie the induction of macroautophagy in immune cells that capture or are infected with pathogens [65]. Other signals, such as cytokines and membrane co-receptors, have been demonstrated to influence pathogen-induced autophagy activation in addition to PRR-mediated signals. Autophagy in macrophages is regulated by the Immunity-Related GTPase M (IRGM) and Interferon-γ receptor (IFNγ-R) signalling [66], whereas the opposite effect is attributed to Th2-like cytokines, including Interleukins (IL-4 and IL-13) [67].

Autophagy is triggered by a variety of factors, including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (e.g. ATP, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and misfolded proteins), pathogen receptors (e.g. CD46), PRRs (e.g. TLRs, NLRs, and protein kinase R (PKR)), IFN-γ and downstream immunity related GTPases, Death-associated protein (DAP) kinase (DAPK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), CD40, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), as well as inhibitors of Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) - IκB kinase (IKK) complex and NF-κB. High mobility group box (HMGB) proteins have also been shown to act both as inducers of autophagy as well as universal sensors of nucleic acids in innate immune signalling [68].

Intracellular bacteria and viruses have developed various adaptation mechanisms to host autophagy. They can inhibit autophagy initiation or autophagosomal maturation, avoid autophagic recognition, or employ autophagy pathway components to aid in their own replication or intracellular survival. A widely accepted concept is that microbial blockage of autophagy inhibits both the activities of autophagy in innate and adaptive immunity as well as the xenophagic clearance of intracellular pathogens. Viral strategies to inhibit autophagy consist of blocking positive upstream regulators (e.g., IFN-inducible ribonucleic acid (RNA)-activated eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit alpha (eIF2α) protein kinase receptor (PKR) signalling pathway), activating negative upstream regulators (e.g., nutrient-sensing TOR kinase signalling pathway), or directly targeting the autophagy machinery [51, 69].

It is also hypothesized that HIV evades both innate and adaptive immune responses in dendritic cells by activating mTOR signalling through the HIV envelope protein. HIV Gag antigen presentation to CD4 + T cells, inhibition of HIV transfer to CD4 + T cells, activation of TLR4 and TLR8 ligand responses, and lysosome destruction of incoming virions are all examples of this strategy [70]. The fundamental autophagy protein beclin 1 is targeted by several viral proteins. Interactions between beclin 1 and the oncogenic γ-herpesvirus-encoded viral B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2)-like proteins or the herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) neurovirulence factor infected cell protein 34.5 (ICP34.5) prevent autophagosome initiation, while interactions between beclin 1 and the HIV accessory protein Negative Regulatory Factor (Nef) or the influenza virus matrix protein 2 inhibit autophagosome maturation [13]. Various bacteria have developed mechanisms that enable them to inhabit phagosomes or other vacuole compartments by inhibiting lysosomal fusion or maturation. However, this mechanism can be partially deactivated in mycobacteria using treatments that promote autophagy [69, 71].

Bioinformatics analysis indicates that when human macrophage-like cells are infected with M. tb, biological mechanisms that prevent autophagy are activated. Shigella flexneri and Listeria monocytogenes are examples of intracellular bacteria that adopt camouflage techniques to evade autophagic identification after escaping into the cytoplasm. In addition to opposing autophagy (a cellular activity that endangers their existence), microbes have developed the ability to take advantage of its elements and roles in membrane trafficking for their own selfish ends [68]. Autophagosomes, which are membranes that are LC3-positive, can be used as a scaffold by some cytoplasmic RNA viruses to replicate intracellularly. Autophagy potentially contributes to HIV biogenesis because its mechanism improves the processing of the HIV envelope precursor protein Gag and extracellular viral release. BCL2, T helper 2 (TH2) cytokines, NF-κB, and the canonical nutrient-sensing insulin–Protein Kinase B (PKB)/AKT –TOR pathway has all been also proven to inhibit the onset of autophagy [68].

Potential homeostatic medicines

The living body’s operations entail several chemical and physical reactions, all of which must occur under the appropriate reaction conditions. Homeostasis is the relatively consistent internal condition that an organism maintains to guarantee the efficiency and orderliness of these processes as illustrated in Fig. 3. For regular physiological functions to occur, organisms must maintain internal stability despite their constantly shifting environment; this process is known as homeostasis regulation [72].

The principle of homeostatic medicine. Homeostatic medicine is a field of science that studies the internal balance (homeostasis) of molecules, cells, organs, and the whole body in healthy and disease state. The main objective of homeostatic medicine is to preserve human health and to prevent or treat diseases by regulating the homeostatic balance. Adapted from [73]. The authors generated this figure with BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/)

In a nutshell, homeostasis is a self-regulating mechanism that helps organisms stay steady and continuously adjust to their changing surroundings to improve their chances of survival. The maintenance of homeostasis plays a crucial role in the reciprocal transition of health and sickness, which are opposites that are connected [73]. Health has two components. One is that every bodily system keeps a healthy physiological balance, and the other is that organisms can adjust to changes in their surroundings, outside stimuli, and harmful elements. Disease results from any disturbance of the normal control of homeostasis, which is followed by several structural, functional, and metabolic alterations that show up as aberrant symptoms, indicators, and behaviours [74].

Stated differently, homeostasis is the junction of health and disease. Hence, maintaining homeostasis well is necessary for health, while causing it to be disrupted will unavoidably result in disease. To manage the homeostasis of molecules, cells, organs, and the entire organism, the idea of homeostatic medicine has been proposed. Homeostatic medicine is a broad field that aims to prevent, diagnose and treat diseases to preserve human health by maintaining the balance of the internal environment required by human beings to survive. The primary focus of homeostatic medicine is controlling homeostasis at the molecular, cellular, organ, system, and external environmental levels. This will ultimately result in the elucidation of the relationship between homeostasis and health, as well as how to regulate it to maintain health and treat disorders [73].

Given the limitations previously described for both diseases, it is critical to discover targets in M. tb and HIV-infected host cells that can be used specifically during coinfection. Targeting M. tb factors that enhance HIV titers or HIV-1 factors that decrease host immune responses is a promising approach for developing M. tb-HIV coinfection-specific therapies. Furthermore, the host-specific innate immune pathways that can be activated to eliminate or suppress the spread of both M. tb and HIV should be investigated to develop therapeutics that aim to maintain immunocompetence or prevent the disruption of immune homeostasis as part of the strategy of homeostatic medicines. Homeostatic medicine refers to therapy strategies that restore the body’s ability to maintain equilibrium, or homeostasis, while eradicating infections. Current research focuses on identifying novel compounds that can act as supplements, together with established pharmacological therapy, to enhance the host immune system against a variety of infections [68].

Metabolism

Metabolites

HIV infection changes the metabolism of host cells to facilitate viral reproduction by blocking mitochondrial fusion, along with lowering lipid accumulation and ATP generation. Mitochondrial activity and Immune activation suppression via biosynthetic pathway alterations can both be achieved through the distortion of the urea cycle [75]. Free fatty acids including palmitic acid, myristic acid, and dodecanoic acid were revealed in a metabolome study to be less abundant in monoinfected mice as opposed to coinfected humanized mice [76, 77].

Micronutrients

Stunted newborns were shown to exhibit considerably lower measles Ig concentrations than non-stunted infants, according to a clinical research study on HIV-exposed (but uninfected) infants taking multivitamin supplements consisting of vitamins B complex, C, and E that was conducted in Tanzania [78]. A cross-sectional study conducted in Egypt found that children aged 3 to 17 with sufficient vitamin A levels had increased CD4 and Thymosin β4 (Tβ4) concentrations compared to those who were vitamin A deficient [79]. Another study conducted in Nigerian found a significant association between pneumonia with undernourishment and zinc (Zn) [80]. Low serum zinc levels and hypovitaminosis A have both been associated with severe pneumonia. While Zn deficiency was also discovered in children aged 2 months to 14 years, to be an independent predictor of pneumonia. Furthermore, other studies found a relationship between Zn deficiency and reduced cell-mediated immunity in children with protein-energy deficiencies [81].

In a separate study conducted in Brazil, vitamin A administration was observed to improve CD4 and CD8 T-cell counts in children with hypovitaminosis A and anemia [20]. It also raised CD4 T cells in low-birthweight Bangladeshi newborns and amplified the expression of CCR9, a retinoic acid-sensitive gene product that regulates the intestinal mucosa, reducing intestinal inflammation [82]. These findings imply that vitamin A treatment at birth has long-term benefits for low-birth-weight infants, possibly by increasing intestinal immune activity, which leads to greater nutritional absorption. As previously stated, children who received vitamin A supplements exhibited a reduction of up to 30% in their chances of dying young and a lower severity of sickness [83], while supplementations with Zn or vitamin A were shown to promote reduced clinical malaria outbreaks.

Fresh food consumption was also indicated as crucial for ensuring enough calcium, iron, and Zn intake. In low-income communities, we should incorporate nutrition instruction and locally available food options, making these meals and supplements inexpensive and accessible [84]. Another study investigated the effects of glucan and vitamin C on influenza-induced immunosuppression, and the combination was found to significantly improve overall survival as well as selected immune mechanisms such as phagocytosis, Natural Killer (NK) cell activity, cytokine production, and antibody response. Vitamin D3, on the other hand, has been proven to promote autophagy to aid the destruction of M. tb during an HIV co-infection [85–96].

Leptin

Malnourished individuals frequently have lower levels of the adipokine, leptin, and this shortage in leptin is connected to the compromised immunological function commonly found in malnourished people [97]. Leptin has also been found to change the immune response from an anti-inflammatory to a more proinflammatory one (Fig. 4), supporting the immunosuppressive phenotype observed in such leptin-deficient individuals with malnutrition [21].

Leptin influences the immune response by interacting with both effector T (Teff) and regulatory T (Treg) cells. Under homeostatic conditions, adipocytes release basal amounts of leptin, which favourably regulates Th1 immunity by promoting Teff cell proliferation while negatively regulating Treg cell expansion. This results in an optimum immunological profile consisting of a balance of Teff and Treg cells, with Tregs mediating immune tolerance through anti-inflammatory cytokine production and Th1 cells conferring protection from infections via proinflammatory cytokine production. Reduced leptin levels (hypoleptinaemia) caused by weight loss or malnutrition (starvation) inhibit Teff cell proliferation while increasing Treg cell expansion. This suppresses Th1 immunity and results in an immune response that is skewed toward an anti-inflammatory profile, increasing a person’s susceptibility to infections by lowering Th1-mediated inflammation. As a result, malnourished people are more vulnerable to infections, yet this anti-inflammatory profile can protect them against hyperinflammatory disorders. Conversely, excessive leptin secretion (hyperleptinaemia), which is caused by increased adipocyte mass in obesity, promotes Teff expansion and inhibits Treg cell proliferation. This shifts the immune response toward a proinflammatory profile, increasing the likelihood of hyperinflammatory diseases by inhibiting the Treg-mediated anti-inflammatory response. On a troubling note, the data from these trials imply that hunger may also result in a poor vaccine response. The diminished vaccine response may be the consequence of insufficient IL-2 levels to prime effector memory T cells, impaired dendritic cell (DC) function, and an impaired response from effector memory T cells that have formed, as both DCs and IL-2 are crucial for the development of a functional immunological memory [98, 99].

Nutrition and autophagy

Autophagy and its function in immunological responses can be impacted by deficiencies in certain nutrients, such as vitamin D [10]. Research on autophagy regulation during mycobacterial infection has revealed the potential connection between innate immunity and nutrition by studying the role of vitamin D3. A higher risk of developing tuberculosis is linked to low vitamin D3 levels, as vitamin D3 produces the antimycobacterial peptide cathelicidin, which causes autophagy and mycobacterial death in human monocytes [100]. It is unclear how cathelicidin stimulates autophagy, it is however necessary for the transcriptional upregulation of autophagy genes like Beclin-1 and ATG5 in a vitamin D3 dependent process, cathelicidin is also more readily attracted to autophagosomes when vitamin D3 is present [13].

By functioning in central immunological tolerance and the negative regulation of innate and inflammatory signalling, the autophagy machinery does much more than just form autophagosomes to engulf microbes; it somehow allows microbes in phagosomes and vacuoles to be targeted to the lysosome; it allows important immune system cells to develop normally and carry out some of their “normal” functions (like producing IFN, secreting antimicrobial peptides, or presenting antigens to stimulate adaptive immunity); and it makes sure that these responses remain regulated [13, 51]. Trehalose has also been reported to induce bacterial killing by activating autophagy and xenophagy in M. tb infected macrophages. In addition, trehalose has also been shown to be effective against an HIV and M. tb coinfection and regulates the survival of M. tb by overriding the HIV mediated blockade of autophagy [70].

Nanomedicines and host directed therapy

HDT has gained popularity as a novel treatment strategy that works by improving the host’s defence mechanism, primarily by strengthening the immune system’s ability to eradicate pathogens and by targeting specific host cellular factors used by the pathogen [69, 101]. Therefore, HDT medications have distinct advantages over standard TB treatment by reducing the potential development of drug resistance, treatment duration, and unpleasant side effects [102]. This decade has also seen NPs garnering substantial interest for application disease therapy [103]. NPs are engineered particles with a typical size range of 1–100 nm, but can also be up to 1000 nm [104]. The NPs can be categorized as either organic (e.g., polymeric NPs, liposomes, lipid NPs, or dendrimers), ceramic (calcium carbonate NPs, silica NPs), or metallic (gold NPs (GNPs), iron oxide NPs, silver NPs, quantum dots). Nanomedicine therefore refers to the use of these NPs for therapeutic and diagnostics applications. As drug delivery systems, NPs can aid in HDT by enabling drugs to be covalently bonded to the surface of the NP or encapsulated inside the NPs [69].

This approach improves various properties of the drugs, such as their water solubility, chemical stability, and translocation across biological barriers. Drug release can also be managed to occur over a specified time course or at target areas upon an external trigger (e.g., enzymatic action or pH), thus altering intracellular and plasma pharmacokinetics of the drugs [105]. Cells are also more effective at absorbing NPs, making NPs a more advantageous delivery and transport system [106]. NPs have distinct features that provide various advantages as drug delivery systems, including enhanced bioavailability, enhanced hydrophobic solubility, targeted delivery, and controlled drug release [104]. By including specific targeting ligands on the surface of the NP formulations/nanomedicine, the nanomedicine can be specifically targeting the diseased site without causing unnecessary harm to non-disease sites and consequently, reducing possible side effects associated with the nanomedicine. In addition, each type of NP has unique physicochemical properties with specific functionalities. The various NP applications depend on the type of NP but can range from drug delivery to imaging and biosensing [69, 107].

Homeostatic nanomedicines

NPs can also be used to deliver homeostatic medicines or functionalised to act as homeostatic medicines, where these NPs are thus termed homeostatic nanomedicines. For example, traditional drug delivery techniques often limit the efficiency of vitamin D3 and β-glucans, despite their inherent immunotherapeutic characteristics. β-glucans are complex polysaccharides with low aqueous solubility due to their tight triple-helix structure. Furthermore, their large molecular weight can hamper their ability to cross cell membrane barriers [108]. Curdlan is a β-glucan with high molecular weight, low water solubility, and gel-forming characteristics. vitamin D3 on the other hand is a lipophilic vitamin characterized by low stability in the presence of oxygen and heat, poor solubility, and limited oral bioavailability, resulting in functionality loss and reduced therapeutic efficiency [109].

As such, encapsulating these molecules in a nano-delivery system can help improve the bioavailability, controlled release, solubility, targeted distribution, and therapeutic efficacy of the nutraceuticals in question. For example, Dlozi et al. found that macrophages treated with calcitriol-loaded polycaprolactone (PCL) NPs expressed more cathelicidin than those treated with free calcitriol, implying that encapsulation improved calcitriol intracellular absorption and bioavailability. Thus, proving the benefits of encapsulation in a medication delivery system [110]. Though the study was limited to an in vitro setting, it nonetheless demonstrated that vitamin D3 -loaded PCL NPs can potentially be explored further as a potential HDT [110].

An HDT strategy based on biomimetic curdlan-conjugated poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (C-PLGA) NPs was recently investigated and demonstrated promise in terms of autophagy induction in infected macrophages, immune modulation in a mouse model, and eradication of M. tb in both an in vitro and in vivo models [71]. The NPs were demonstrated to induce autophagy and reduced bacterial burden in H37Rv M. tb-infected RAW 264.7 macrophages and in infected C57BL/6 mice. Treatment of the H37Rv infected C57BL/6 mice with these NPs triggered cytokine and chemokine production, and lowered M. tb load in the lungs of the mice. This study therefore suggests that these immunomodulatory NPs are a potential treatment option for TB [71]. NP based therapeutics have also been explored against extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) with limited success [111]. As this field is relatively new, not many publications exist on the topic; especially those outlining the use of these materials in infectious diseases. In addition to serving as delivery systems, NPs have also been demonstrated to possess therapeutic properties of their own. Several NPs have been shown to suppress viral replication in vitro, and it is believed that these effects are due to structural interference caused during viral assembly. In vitro studies have revealed that several fullerenes (C60)-based structures, dendrimers, and inorganic NPs, including gold and silver, exhibit anti-HIV action. While these efforts have not yet gone beyond in vitro investigations, they do demonstrate the potential for therapeutic nanomaterials to limit HIV replication [107].

Comparing standard nanomedicine to homeostatic nanomedicine

It is perhaps important to clarify that homeostatic nanomedicine is not a completely different field from traditional nanomedicine. Nanomedicine is the broad application of nanotechnology to healthcare, using nanoscale tools (1 to 100 nm) to diagnose, prevent, and treat diseases. Homeostatic nanomedicine on the other hand is a highly specific, cutting-edge subfield that focuses on restoring the body’s natural physiological balance (homeostasis) rather than just aggressively killing diseased cells [120–122]. Homeostatic nanomedicine is therefore a medical approach that uses functionalised NPs to intentionally break the internal balance (homeostasis) of diseased cells, or to restore balance in sick tissues depending on what’s necessary to for the disease being addressed. Instead of just killing cells directly, these smart NPs target specific internal networks such as protein modifications or chemical stress limits [73, 122, 123].

As shown on Table 3, the key differences between traditional nanomedicine and homeostatic nanomedicine lies in their core therapeutic approaches: In infectious diseases, standard nanomedicine focuses on targeted drug delivery, direct pathogen destruction, and immune stimulation. In other words, Nanomedicine uses engineered nanoscale materials to directly attack pathogens, deliver antimicrobials, or enhance vaccines. Whereas homeostatic nanomedicine specifically target and disrupt internal cellular or molecular balance mechanisms (homeostasis) within disease environments to trigger death or clearance [122, 124, 125]. To be specific, homeostatic nanomedicine focuses on restoring the body’s homeostasis, using NPs to modulate immune responses, reduce inflammatory damage, and support tissue repair during an infection [126–128].

In other words, while standard nanodiagnostics quickly identify specific pathogens, homeostatic treatments aim to stabilize the host’s internal biological systems, regardless of the exact microbial strain [127, 129]. Standard nanomedicine acts as a direct weapon against the invader. For example, silver nanoparticles damage bacterial DNA and proteins directly, whereas homeostatic nanomedicine acts as a regulator; in this approach nanocarriers are used to control overactive immune responses (like cytokine storms) to allow the body to clear the infection naturally [124]. Homeostatic nanomedicine is often linked to integrative or holistic public health, it utilizes nanoscale natural products or secondary metabolites to support the host’s complex networks and reduce toxicity [127, 130]. Therefore, unlike standard nanomedicine, homeostatic nanomedicine goes beyond treatment and focuses on the complete wellness of the patient.

Challenges of using autophagy inducing NPs for HIV-TB coinfection therapy

Despite the increased use of nanotechnology in clinical trial settings, its clinical translational impact remains small in comparison to its success in other domains [131]. As of October 2024, Gultepe et al. reported that around 4114 nanotechnology-related clinical studies were discovered among the 520,889 trials registered in the Aggregate Content of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database, accounting for approximately 0.8% of all documented trials [131]. Notably, recent years have shown a decline in the distribution of clinical trial phases within nanotechnology-related investigations. For example, between 1991 and 2000, nanomedical trials accounted for over 70% of Phase I/II studies; however, this proportion fell to 40% between 2021 and 2024. Furthermore, the fraction of Phase III/IV remained steady, indicating a general downward trend in trial phase distribution. Combined, these findings highlight important translational hurdles in nanotechnology [131, 132].

When investigating the causes of the observed translational challenges associated with nanomedicines, data reveals that the use of NPs as a HDT for M. tb and HIV treatment is met with various significant obstacles. These hurdles can be described as follows: potential NP toxicity and/or immune clearance, difficulty penetrating dense granulomas and the blood brain barrier (BBB), navigation of immune dysregulation caused by HIV, managing drug-drug interactions with combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), and scaling for clinical manufacturing [133–137].

Drug-drug interactions between cARTs and anti-TB nanomedicines

Clinical studies of such novel nano-formulations for the development pharmaceutical regimens would require a clear safety profile for nanomedicine monotherapy as opposed to combination therapy with existing standard formulations [136]. However, treating both infections requires co-administering anti-TB medications and antiretrovirals (ART), which complicates the design of a single nanocarrier system due to potential drug-drug interactions and stability issues [134, 137–140]. Standard anti-TB drugs are potent modulators of liver enzymes, while nanomedicines aim to alter drug distribution and reduce toxicity; systemic interactions remain a major clinical challenge [141, 142].

Standard anti-TB regimens often feature Rifampicin or Rifabutin, which are potent inducers of hepatic Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes (particularly CYP3A4) and UGTs. This accelerates the metabolism of many cART drugs (e.g., Protease Inhibitors and NNRTIs), severely dropping their blood levels and risking HIV treatment failure [142–146]. Anti-TB nanomedicines (such as liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles) are designed to bypass standard liver metabolism, targeting drugs directly to infected macrophages and reducing systemic side effects. However, when the nanoparticle’s payload eventually releases, the drugs themselves still encounter the same metabolic pathways [136, 143, 147, 148]. Also, encapsulating drugs in NPs significantly changes their absorption, distribution, and half-life. This can potentially create unpredictable overlaps between the absorption and clearance timelines of the cART and anti-TB drugs [149].

Nanotoxicity and immune clearance

Though nanomedicines have shown tremendous potential. To create an effective nanomedicine regimen, it is necessary to determine the NPs’ toxicological and pharmacokinetic characteristics, as well as their distribution in the human body [136]. For example, long-term accumulation of synthetic and metallic NPs in non-target organs can also lead to systemic toxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses [136, 150–153]. The toxicity of the NPs relies on several physicochemical parameters of the NPs in question, including particle size, shape, surface chemistry, and composition. Clinical translations of these nano-formulations is also difficult because some of the in-vitro toxicity responses may not always correspond with clinical data obtained from human trials [154, 155].

Additionally, quantifying NPs can be a challenge because the biodistribution of drugs at the tissue level is more complex in human beings when compared to small animals. Therefore, although some NPs, including various polymers, have been found to be safe in animal toxicity tests, nano-formulations containing these NPs must still be rigorously tested in clinical trials [136]. The physicochemical properties of the NPs also determine if the nano-formulations are not cleared by the immune system before they arrive at the target site, through processes such as opsonisation which allows for the opsonized NPs to be recognised and consumed by immune cells such as macrophages. The reticuloendothelial system (RES) organs (like the liver and spleen) also often recognize and clear circulating NPs before they reach the lungs or lymph nodes where M.tb and HIV hide [136, 156].

Reservoirs granuloma and blood-brain barrier penetration

Many nanotherapeutics perform well in in-vitro cultures or small animal models but fail to replicate in complex human granulomas [133, 157–159]. M. tb establishes protective granulomas that are avascular and fibrotic, limiting the ability of therapeutic NPs to penetrate the core. Additionally, for HIV reservoirs in the central nervous system (CNS), crossing the BBB remains a major barrier [102, 134, 135]. However, targeting strategies in nanomedicine-based tuberculosis therapy are designed to enhance site-specific drug delivery and maximize intracellular drug concentrations at M. tb infection sites. These approaches primarily exploit passive targeting through enhanced macrophage uptake and active targeting via ligand functionalization, such as mannose, folate, peptides, or antibodies, to facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis. Such strategies improve granuloma penetration [157].

HIV-induced immune dysfunction

Because HIV preferentially depletes CD4 + T cells and dysregulates macrophages, it physically impairs the host cells that the NPs are trying to target, making host-directed immune boosting less predictable [137].

Dual action of autophagy during HIV and M. tb infections

It should be noted that autophagy shows a dual action during HIV and M. tb infections by acting as both a host defence mechanism that restricts pathogen survival and a hijacked pathway that promotes viral replication or exacerbates inflammation [160–162]. In its protective role of host defence and pathogen clearance; autophagy delivers intracellular M. tb and HIV components to lysosomes for destruction, overcoming the blockade of phagosome maturation [163]. Autophagy helps limit excessive, tissue-damaging pulmonary inflammation and regulates harmful immune responses during mycobacterial infection. Active metabolites like vitamin D induce autophagic flux and antimicrobial peptides (such as cathelicidin) that suppress both M. tb growth and HIV replication [164, 165].

On a more detrimental note; HIV has been shown to use early, non-degradative stages of autophagy to assist in viral protein processing and early replication [161]. HIV proteins like the negative regulatory factor (Nef) bind to Beclin-1 to shut down late-stage autophagic maturation, protecting the virus from degradation [161]. HIV-induced cellular stress triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and cytoplasmic DNA release, causing autophagy to amplify cyclic GMP-AMP synthase- stimulator of interferon Genes (cGAS-STING) inflammatory signaling and immune dysregulation [160]. It is therefore important to distinguish between the type of induced autophagy to ensure that it will contribute positively to the treatment instead of worsening the disease.

Manufacturing and cost

One of the most fundamental constraints is the scalability and reproducibility of production. Scaling up the production of surface-functionalized, stimuli-responsive NPs with uniform size and consistent drug-loading efficiency is expensive and technically challenging [133, 166–169]. Saxena and team comprehensively documented that cell membrane-coated NPs require technically demanding multi-step fabrication - including cell lysis, membrane isolation [157].

Conclusion

Infectious diseases continue to be a serious global issue and are responsible for the deaths of millions of people annually. HIV, TB remain, along with their coinfection, are among the most challenging infectious diseases. These diseases have a negative impact on society, strain public health systems, disrupt economies, and jeopardize social and economic growth. Especially in developing countries where various pre-existing challenges were already a burden. Patients in these countries who are infected with advanced HIV and/or TB also frequently live in food-insecure environments, and their regular diets do not provide enough energy and nutrients to meet their needs.

Maintenance of a healthy nutrient status has thus proven to be vital in the fight against infectious diseases, as both single and multiple micronutrient deficiencies have been proven to negatively contribute to host resistance mechanisms, by increasing their susceptibility to infectious diseases. Understanding the immune-modulating effects of micronutrients and their connections to HIV and TB is thus pivotal in developing therapeutic strategies against the HIV, TB and their coinfection. As a result, appropriate dietary interventions for HIV and TB patients are necessary to complete medical and social care packages. In addition to improving food security and nutrition, such interventions may have a positive impact on disease and treatment outcomes.

However, there is still a need for tailored research to identify differences in fundamental immunological pathways in patients with HIV, TB, or HIV-TB co-infection to produce innovative non-toxic HDTs for long-term immunocompetency and adequate immunosurveillance in these patients. This can be based on the concept of homeostatic nanomedicine, which promote holistic health to aid in pathogen clearance and illness prevention. Homeostatic nanomedicines as HDTs are a promising approach for enhancing HIV and TB treatment by employing NPs as delivery vehicles to facilitate improved drug delivery, targeting diseased cells or tissues, and stimulating localized immune responses; thus, reducing potential negative side effects by sparing non-affected sites (e.g., cells or organs).

As this approach is complex with many moving parts, it is essential to foster multidisciplinary collaborations between different faculties, including life sciences, community health sciences, social sciences, and computational sciences, among others, to produce enough evidence to convince governments and healthcare policy makers. Overall, this review provides a summary of existing scientific evidence supporting the importance of a more holistic approach for managing infectious diseases, especially TB, HIV or TB-HIV coinfection. We also position homeostatic-nanomedicines-based HDTs as a potentially promising aspect of this proposed strategy, which is underpinned by understanding the interplay between autophagy, nutrition, and infection diseases. However, despite the vision of the field, its translation is plagued by various limitations that necessitate further research and collaborations across different scientific disciplines to improve their clinical applications.

Future perspectives

Future priorities of homeostatic nanomedicine for infectious diseases therefore focus on multi-action systems that simultaneously target pathogens, rebalance immune responses, and restore tissue equilibrium. Key areas include stimuli-responsive drug delivery, bio-mimetic cellular coatings, and inflammation regulation [124, 170, 171]. Therefore, future homeostatic nanomedicine for HIV and M. tb co-infections should focuses on targeted cellular delivery, immune system balancing, and sustained-release formulations. Key future perspectives include macrophage-targeted co-delivery systems, host-directed autophagy regulation, and long-acting inhalable or injectable nanosystems [137, 172, 173]. However, a more detailed breakdown of these future perspectives is available below, grouped under ‘targeted delivery and tissue reservoirs’ or under ‘host-directed immunomodulation’:

Targeted delivery and tissue reservoirs

Targeted delivery of homeostatic nanomedicines to tissue reservoirs is critical, as this approach can target hard to reach pathogens while reducing unintended side effects caused by the damage caused by NPs ending up in the wrong target sites. This strategy can be executed through the following approaches; i). inhalable nanomedicines: utilizing these nanocarriers ensures direct respiratory tract deposition, maximizing anti-tubercular drug concentrations straight at lung alveolar infection sites while minimizing systemic toxicity [134], ii) macrophage precision: NP surfaces (such as polymeric nanoparticles or liposomes) can be functionalized with specific ligands to directly target CD4 + T cells and infected macrophages where both HIV latency and M. tb hide [107, 173, 174], iii) sanctuary penetration: drugs can be loaded to NPs to improve drug passage across tight biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier to clear neurological HIV/TB reservoirs [107, 175–177], iv) stimuli-responsive release: NPs can be designed to react to the local acidic or redox environment of the infection site to release drugs at the species site of infection [135].

Host-directed immunomodulation

The host directed NPs can also be used to induce host-directed immunomodulation through various means, These approaches include (i) Immune Polarization: using empty or drug loaded NPs to shift macrophage activity toward an M1 phenotype to naturally help the body clear out the bacteria and viruses, (ii) Inflammation Control: regulating overactive immune activation and cytokine storms common in HIV-M. tb coinfections without compromising the patient’s underlying immunity [137], (iii) Long-Acting Formulations: the design of the nanocarriers can be optimised to enable slow drug-release during their applications as injectables or implants, to drastically cut pill burdens, lift patient compliance, and prevent drug resistance [173] and lastly (iv) Autophagy Induction: modulate cellular self-cleaning pathways within host cells to starve or destroy intracellular M. tb and HIV safely [133, 136, 172].

Nanomaterials can be properly tailored to target the autophagy pathway, promoting autophagosome synthesis or accelerating the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes in several types of cells, demonstrating nanomaterials’ ability to eliminate intracellular M. tb in macrophages. Furthermore, autophagy-inducing nanomedicines can be modified with functional groups or loaded with anti-TB drugs to kill M. tb synergistically and boost therapeutic efficiency. In addition, nanomedicines can treat tuberculosis by partially increasing the formation of reactive oxygen species and triggering autophagy, or they can directly interact with the bacterial envelope, causing membrane damage and disrupting bacterial function and growth. Combining these anti-TB properties, activating autophagy of M. tb-infected macrophages can be used to eliminate intracellular M. tb for better TB treatment, which is presently serving as a hot research topic that warrants further examination [171].

Safety

Despite their potential, careful investigation is required to guarantee the safety of the NPs in drug delivery and treatment. Concerns about the compatibility and possible toxicity of nanoparticles still exist. It is noteworthy that numerous preclinical and clinical studies have used nanoparticle formulations to test certain anti-inflammatory drugs [178]. The effectiveness of nano curcumin (NC) in lowering oxidative stress markers, boosting endothelial function, lowering inflammation biomarkers, optimizing metabolic variables, improving nutritional status, and improving clinical outcomes in sepsis patients was investigated in a clinical trial [171]. Improved safety profiles of homeostatic nanomedicines against TB-HIV co-infections will also help bring more confidence to this research field allowing more of these formulations to reach and pass clinical trials.
