A Note on Maximal Operators for Moment Curves
Chenjian Wang

Abstract
. We consider a type of maximal operators associated to moment curves in ℝ d , d ≥ 3 . \mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 3. We derive L p L^{p} mapping properties for these operators. In a special case, the estimate is sharp.

1.
Introduction

Geometric measure theory is the study of the
geometric properties of sets, typically in Euclidean space, through measure theory. An interesting genre of problems in this area is determining the size, usually quantified via Lebesgue measure or dimensions, of sets that are quantitatively known to be large. A prototypical example of such a problem is the notorious Kakeya conjecture which aims to quantify the size of a set that contains a unit line segment in every direction. Such sets are called Kakeya sets. Besicovitch
[
2
]
showed that, contrary to intuition, Kakeya sets can have Lebesgue measure zero. This led to the conjecture which states that Kakeya sets must have full Hausdorff dimension.
The problem has been solved for
d=2

[
7
,
6
]
. Recently, Wang and Zahl claimed the resolution of three-dimensional case
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
and the problem is still open in all higher dimensions
[
5
,
23
,
12
]
.

The intense interest in the Kakeya conjecture has led to the study of related problems in the “curved” setting.
For
d=2
, Besicovitch and Rado
[
3
]
and Kinney
[
13
]
constructed a Lebesgue null set that contains all circles with radius
r\in[1,2]
. Inspired by this, such a set in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2
(replace circles by
r\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for
d\geq 3
) is called Besicovitch-Rado-Kinney set, or a BRK set for short. One can ask a similar question to the Kakeya conjecture: what is the Hausdorff dimension of a BRK set? The problem has been solved for all
d\geq 2
: for
d\geq 3
in
[
16
]
and
d=2
in
[
24
]
. It is now known that all BRK sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d},\ d\geq 2
have full Hausdorff dimension.

In this note, we consider problems of a similar flavor in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 3
. Let us denote by

\gamma(t):=(t,t^{2},...,t^{d})

the standard moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We want to study sets that contain many affine copies of
\gamma
. More precisely, we introduce the following BRK-type sets. Throughout the note, we denote

(1)

s^{\prime}=d+1-s.

Definition 1

(
s^{\prime}
-parameter moment BRK type sets)
.

Assume
d\geq 3
. For
s^{\prime}\in\{1,2,...,d\}
, a compact set
S_{s^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
s^{\prime}
-parameter moment BRK type sets
if for all
(x_{s+1},...,x_{d-1},x_{d},r)\in[-1/4,1/4]^{s^{\prime}-1}\times[1/2,2]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}}
, there is
(x_{1},...,x_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
such that the (truncated) moment curve

(2)

H(\mathbf{x},r):=\{\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t):t\in[-1,1]\}\subseteq S_{s^{\prime}},

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{d})
.

In the following parts of the note,

(i)

we will denote the range of each coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
and
r
by

(3)

I_{1}:=[-1/4,1/4],\ I_{2}:=[1/2,2].

and

\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{s}=(x_{1},...,x_{s}),\quad\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{s^{\prime}-1}=(x_{s+1},...,x_{d}).

When
s^{\prime}
is fixed, we will abbreviate these as
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{x}}
.

(ii)

Denote the isotropic
\delta
-neighborhood of the moment curve
H(\mathbf{x},r)
as
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
, More precisely,

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{y}\in H(\mathbf{x},r)}B(\mathbf{y},\delta),

where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},...,y_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B(\mathbf{y},\delta)
is the ball with center
\mathbf{y}
and radius
\delta
. It is elementary to see that when
r\in[1/2,2]
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\approx\delta^{d-1}.

(iii)

All dimensions or
\dim(\cdot)
refer to Hausdorff dimension.

(iv)

As conventions,
A\lesssim B
or
B\gtrsim A
represents “
\exists C>0
(independent of
\delta
but may depend on other irrelevant parameter such as

), such that
A\leq CB

C

as
A\lesssim_{\tau}B.

To state the dimension estimates for the
s^{\prime}
-parameter moment BRK type sets, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2

(
s
-parameter maximal function associated to moment curves)
.

Fix
\delta>0
. For a Schwartz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
,
d\geq 3,
define the
s
-parameter maximal function associated to moment curves

\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}f:I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
as

(4)

\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}f(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{s^{\prime}-1},r):=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))}\int_{H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}|f(\mathbf{y})|d\mathbf{y},s=1,2,...,d

where
\mathcal{L}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and
\ell
is the arc length measure.

Recall our notation in (
1
). In the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}=\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}}
,
s
is the dimension or number of parameters we take supremum over.
s^{\prime}
is the dimension or number of parameters left, which is also the number of variables of the maximal functions in (

By applying Wolff’s circular maximal estimate in
[
24
]
and sharp (exponent) local smoothing estimate by Ko, Lee, and Oh
[

Theorem 1

(maximal estimates)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
and
\varepsilon>0
, then for all
p\geq p_{d},
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
depending on all the aforementioned quantities and

, such that

(5)

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}f\right\|_{L^{p}(I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2})}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\alpha(s)}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}.

Here,

p_{d}=\begin{cases}3,&\text{if }s=d,\\
4d-2,&\text{if }s=1,...,d-1,\end{cases}

and

(6)

\alpha(s)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon+\frac{s-2}{p},&s=2,...,d,\\
0,&s=1.\end{cases}

When
s=1,

is an absolute number independent of
\varepsilon
.

Note that when
s=d
, the result

is better than the general results obtained from local smoothing
p_{d}\geq 4d-2
.

Remark 1
.

When
s=1
, since the operator norm is independent of
\delta,
by a standard limiting argument, one can deduce

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{1}f\right\|_{L}^{p}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{p},

and replace
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{\delta}
by
\mathcal{M}^{1}
, where

\mathcal{M}^{1}f:=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{H_{(}\mathbf{x},r)}|f(\mathbf{y})|d\ell(\mathbf{y}).

To unify notations, we still use
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{\delta}.

When
s=d
, the estimate (
5
) is sharp in the following sense:

(1)

(sharpness of the exponent of
\delta
) If
p\geq 3
,
\not\exists\beta<\frac{d-2}{p}
such that the following holds

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{d}_{\delta}f\right\|_{L^{p}([1/2,2])}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-(\varepsilon+\beta)}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}.

(2)

Corollary 2

We first prove the following standard result passing from maximal estimates, Theorem

to dimension estimates of BRK type sets, Corollary

Proposition 1
.

(i)

s\in\{2,...,d\}
. Assume for some finite
p
, there is
\alpha>0
such that

(8)

\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s}f\right\|_{L^{p}(I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2})}\lesssim\delta^{-\alpha}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

then

\dim(S_{s^{\prime}})\geq d-p\alpha.

(ii)

Assume for some finite
p
, there is
\alpha<0

(9)

\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{1}f\right\|_{L^{p}(I_{1}^{d-1}\times I_{2})}\lesssim\delta^{-\alpha}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(S_{d})>0\Rightarrow\dim(S_{d})=d.

To deduce Corollary
2
, for
s=2,...,d,
by item
(i)
,

\dim(S_{s^{\prime}})\geq d-p\left(\varepsilon+\frac{s-2}{p}\right)=d-s+2-p\varepsilon\to s^{\prime}+1,\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

When
s=1
, the conclusion is derived directly from item
(ii)
.

Before proving Proposition
1
, we first introduce the prerequisites of Hausdorff dimension.

Definition 3

(Hausdorff measure)
.

For a nonnegative real number
s\in(0,d]
, the
s
-dimensional
Hausdorff measure
of a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

is defined as:

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\text{diam}(U_{i}))^{s}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}\text{ and }\text{diam}(U_{i})<\delta\right\}

where
U_{i}
is an open ball and
\text{diam}(U_{i})
denotes its diameter.

For some fixed
\delta_{0},
we also use
\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{0}}^{s}
to denote

(10)

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta_{0}}(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\text{diam}(U_{i}))^{s}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}\text{ and }\text{diam}(U_{i})<\delta_{0}\right\}.

It is elementary to see that if we treat
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta_{0}}(E)
as a function of
\delta_{0}
, then it is a monotonically decreasing function. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)\geq\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta_{0}}(E),\forall\delta_{0}>0.

Then we can introduce the Hausdorff dimension:

Definition 4

(Hausdorff dimension)
.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of a set
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as:

\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)=\inf\{s\geq 0\mid\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0\}=\sup\{s\geq 0\mid\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\}

where
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
denotes the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
E
.

Remark 5
.

When
\delta_{0}=\infty
in (
10
),
H^{s}_{\infty}
is called
s
-dimensional Hausdorff content of
E
.
We will use the following fact

H^{s}_{\infty}(E)<\infty\Rightarrow\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)\leq s.

Unless otherwise, we will always abbreviate
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)
as
\dim(E)
as before. Now we can start by proving Proposition
1
.

Proof.

We start with item
(i)
. Fix

and
\alpha>0
. Suppose

is a

\mathcal{H}^{d-p\alpha-\varepsilon}(S_{s^{\prime}})\geq\mathcal{H}^{d-p\alpha-\varepsilon}_{\frac{1}{100}}(S_{s^{\prime}})\geq C_{\varepsilon},

and this implies

by Definition

. Then by a similar limiting argument, we can obtain

to be non-negative.

Take an arbitrary countable covering of

Denote it as
\bigcup_{j}B(\mathbf{x}_{j},r_{j})
, where

. It suffices to prove

\sum_{j}r_{j}^{d-p\alpha-\varepsilon}\geq C_{\varepsilon}.

We first arrange the balls

with respect to their sizes. By dyadic decomposition, define

J_{k}:=\left\{B_{j}=B(\mathbf{x}_{j},r_{j}):2^{-k-1}<r_{j}\leq 2^{-k}\right\},~k=6,7,...

are disjoint. The sizes of the balls contained in each block are comparable and the radii of the balls in different blocks decreases as

\sum_{j}r_{j}^{d-p\alpha-\varepsilon}=\sum_{k}\sum_{B\in J_{k}}r(B)^{d-p\alpha-\varepsilon}\approx\sum_{k}2^{-k(d-p\alpha-\varepsilon)}\#J_{k}.

For each

3.
Tangency of moment curves

In this section, we discuss the conditions and properties for the exact and almost tangency of two moment curves of the form (
2
). We first provide some basic observations about the moment curves.

Lemma 1

(comparable
\delta
-neighborhoods)
.

Assume
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are two
\delta
-neighborhoods of moment curves. If
|(\mathbf{x},r)-(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})|\leq\delta
, then there is
c
and
C
such that

H_{c\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\subseteq H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})\subseteq CH_{C\delta}(\mathbf{x},r).

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove the right inclusion relation. For any
\mathbf{y}\in H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
there is some
t_{0}\in[-1,1]
such that

|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-r^{\prime}\gamma(t_{0})|\leq\delta.

By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-r^{\prime}\gamma(t_{0})|+|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\gamma(t_{0})-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq\delta+2\delta

\displaystyle\leq 3\delta.

This lemma tells us that
\delta
-perturbation to a moment curve is acceptable and the
\delta
-neighborhood of a moment curve is roughly the union of such perturbations.

3.1.
Exact tangency

We first discuss the exact tangency of two moment curves. In what follows, “two moment curves” always means “two distinct moment curves”.

Definition 5

(tangency of two moment curves)
.

Consider two moment curves
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
with
(\mathbf{x},r)\neq(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
. We will say
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are
tangent at a point
\mathbf{y}
if there are
t,t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
such that

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\gamma(t^{\prime}),

and the tangency vectors to
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
at
\mathbf{y}
are parallel.

Proposition 2

(characterization for tangency of two moment curves)
.

Assume
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are two moment curves, where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{d})
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},...,x_{d}^{\prime})
.

(1)

H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
can be tangent only if
r\neq r^{\prime}.

(2)

When
r\neq r^{\prime}
, define

\Delta_{i}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=|(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})(r-r^{\prime})^{i-1}-(x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime})^{i}|,\ i=2,...,d.

Then
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are tangent at a point
\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\gamma(t^{\prime})

if and only if

(14)

\Delta_{i}=0\text{, for all }i=2,...,d\text{ and }t=t^{\prime}=\frac{x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}-r}\in[-1,1].

Proof.

We will prove
(2)
and observe along the way that
(1)
holds.

Necessity.
If the two moment curves intersect then for some point
t,t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\gamma(t^{\prime})=\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)
, more precisely,

(15)

\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{1}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}t^{\prime}&=x_{1}+rt\\
x_{2}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}t^{\prime 2}&=x_{2}+rt^{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{d}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}t^{\prime d}&=x_{d}+rt^{d}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Moreover, if they are also tangent here, then

(16)

r^{\prime}(1,2t^{\prime},...,dt^{\prime d-1})\parallel r(1,2t,...,dt^{d-1}).

From the limitations on the first two entries we obtain
\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}=\frac{2r^{\prime}t^{\prime}}{2rt}
. This implies that
t^{\prime}=t.

If we also have
r=r^{\prime}
, then take it back to equation (
15
), we deduce that
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
which contradicts with the assumption that the two moment curves are distinct. This concludes the proof of part
(1)
.

Since we have
r\neq r^{\prime},
plug it back to equation (
15
), we obtain the conditions (
14
).

Sufficiency.
Conversely, if the conditions (
14
) hold then the system of equation (
15
) is satisfied with
t=t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
. Thus the two moment curves must intersect at some point where their parameters are the same. The tangent vectors to
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
at the intersection point are
r\gamma^{\prime}(t)
and
r^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
respectively. If
t=t^{\prime}
as assumed in (
14
), these two tangent vectors are parallel.
∎

From Proposition
2
, we can also see that two moment curves can be tangent at no more than one point. This follows from the expression for
t,t^{\prime}
in (
14
). Furthermore, from the first two equations of system (
15
), we also have the following

Lemma 2

(number of intersections)
.

The number of points of intersection of two moment curves is at most
2
.

3.2.
Almost tangency

For the almost tangency case, We have the following conclusions similar to those of Kolasa and Wolff
[
16
, Lemma 3.3]
, Wolff
[
25
, Lemma 11.20]
and Pramanik, Yang and Zahl
[
17
, Lemma 3.8]
.
We first give the following definitions.

Definition 6
.

For two moment curves
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, define

(17)

\overline{d}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=|x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}|+|x_{2}-x_{2}^{\prime}|+|r-r^{\prime}|.

We also define

(18)

\overline{\Delta}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=\frac{|(x_{2}-x_{2}^{\prime})(r-r^{\prime})-(x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime})^{2}|}{|x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}|+|x_{2}-x_{2}^{\prime}|+|r-r^{\prime}|}=\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\overline{d}}.

These
\overline{d}
and
\overline{\Delta}
are used to describe the tangency in the plane. Similar to the computations in Proposition
2
, it is easy to check that when
\overline{\Delta}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=0
, the projections of
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
to the plane which are the two parabolas, are tangent.

Remark 6
.

The reason why we need to divide
\Delta_{2}
by
\overline{d}
is homogeneity. Heuristically, the degree of
\Delta_{2}
is
2
while
\overline{d}
is
1
. We will see from the proof of the following Proposition
3
that it is necessary to maintain the degree of
\overline{\Delta}
and
\overline{d}
the same.

Proposition 3

(intersection volume)
.

Assume
H(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are two moment curves where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d}),\ \mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{d}^{\prime})
.
Then

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
is contained in a union of
\delta
-neighborhood(s) of at most two arc(s) of
H(\mathbf{x},r)
with total length
\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\delta+\overline{\Delta})(\delta+\overline{d})}}
hence with measure
\lesssim\frac{\delta^{d}}{\sqrt{(\delta+\overline{\Delta})(\delta+\overline{d})}}
. The implicit constants are independent of the choice of
H(\mathbf{x},r)
,
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
and
\delta.

Proof.

We first prove the conclusion in the plane from which we can deduce the higher dimensional cases.

We can assume that one of the parabolas is
H(\mathbf{0},1)
and the other one is
H(\mathbf{x},r)
. Since a non-degenerate affine transformation will not affect the intersection relation of two moment curves and furthermore, since our centers and radii are limited in a compact set, the intersection area is changed at most some constant multiples. In addition, we can also assume that the two parabolas intersect. This is because, if
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},1)\cap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\neq\emptyset
(which is the case we care about, otherwise the estimate is trivial), then through a translation, we can find a new parabola
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r)
such that

H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r)\bigcap H(\mathbf{0},1)\neq\emptyset,

and
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\leq\delta
. Therefore, via triangle inequality, we have

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},1)\subseteq H_{(100d)^{d}\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r)\bigcap H_{(100d)^{d}\delta}(\mathbf{0},1).

So it suffices to analyze the intersection case.

The proof is a direct computation. The parabolas are graphs of the following two functions over
[-1,1]
,

f_{1}(t)=t^{2},\quad f_{2}(t)=x_{2}+\frac{(t-x_{1})^{2}}{r}.

The difference between the two quadratic functions is

h(t)=t^{2}-x_{2}-\frac{(t-x_{1})^{2}}{r}=(1-\frac{1}{r})t^{2}+\frac{2x_{1}}{r}t-(x_{2}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{r}).

Since we assume the two parabolas intersect, this means the discriminant is nonnegative. By Lemma
2
, it suffices to consider one of the solutions, denoted as
t_{0},
the other one is similar. Assume
s
satisfies

|h(t_{0}+s)|=(100d)^{d}\delta.

Our goal is to give an upper bound for the smaller
|s|
. Note that
|h(t_{0}+s)|
is also a quadratic function. It is easy to check by direct computations that

|s|\lesssim_{d}\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{r}\sqrt{(r-1)x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}}}\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\overline{\Delta}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{0},1))+\delta)(\overline{d}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{0},1))+\delta)}}

which is desired. In the second
\lesssim
, we used the definition (
18
) of
\Delta_{2}
and Remark
6
below that definition. There are at most two
t_{0}
’s, so the number of such intervals is at most two.

For the higher dimensional case, define the orthogonal projection
\pi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

\pi_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d}):=(x_{1},x_{2}).

When
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})=\emptyset,
the estimate holds trivially, so we assume the intersection is nonempty.
Clearly, the projections of the moment curves

\pi_{2}(H(\mathbf{x},r))=\{(x_{1},x_{2})+r(t,t^{2}):t\in[-1,1]\}=:P(x_{1},x_{2},r)=P

and

\pi_{2}(H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=\{(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})+r^{\prime}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime 2}):t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]\}=:P(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},r^{\prime})=P^{\prime}

are two parabolas in the plane. We are going to prove
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))
and
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))
are
\delta
-neighborhoods in the plane of the the parabolas
P_{\delta}(x_{1},x_{2},r)=P_{\delta}
and
P_{\delta}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},r^{\prime})=P^{\prime}_{\delta}
respectively. That is, we prove the following

(19)

P_{\delta}=\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)).

We first prove
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\subseteq P_{\delta}
. For each
\mathbf{y}\in H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
there is a
\mathbf{p}\in H(\mathbf{x},r)
such that

|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y}|\leq\delta.

Since
\pi_{2}
is Lipschitz with constant
1
, this implies that for the point
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{p})\in\pi_{2}(H(\mathbf{x},r))
,

|\pi_{2}(\mathbf{y})-\pi_{2}(\mathbf{p})|\leq|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y}|\leq\delta.

Since
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{p})\in P
,
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\subseteq P_{\delta}
. For the other inclusion
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\supseteq P_{\delta}
, assume
(y_{1},y_{2})\in P_{\delta}
, then there is a point on the moment curve
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{d})\in H(\mathbf{x},r)
such that

(20)

|(y_{1},y_{2})-\pi_{2}(\mathbf{p})|=|(y_{1},y_{2})-(p_{1},p_{2})|\leq\delta.

By (
20
),
|(y_{1},y_{2},p_{3},...,p_{d})-\mathbf{p}|\leq\delta.
Hence
(y_{1},y_{2},p_{3},...,p_{d})\in H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
whose projection under
\pi_{2}
is exactly
(y_{1},y_{2}).
Therefore,
\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\supseteq P_{\delta}
.

With this fact combined with a similar argument, we can deduce that

(21)

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})=\pi_{2}^{-1}\big(P_{\delta}\cap P^{\prime}_{\delta}\big)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}).

Indeed, since

\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\subseteq\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))\bigcap\pi_{2}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))=P_{\delta}\bigcap P^{\prime}_{\delta}.

This implies that

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\subseteq\pi_{2}^{-1}\left(P_{\delta}\bigcap P^{\prime}_{\delta}\right)

which means the right inclusion of (
21
) holds. Whereas the left inclusion is trivial.

From the first part of the proof, we know that
P_{\delta}\bigcap P^{\prime}_{\delta}
is contained in a union of
\delta
-neighborhood(s) of arc(s) in the plane of total length
\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\delta+\overline{\Delta})(\delta+\overline{d})}}.
Denote this union of the
\delta
-neighborhood(s) as
Q
. From (
21
) and
P_{\delta}\bigcap P^{\prime}_{\delta}\subseteq Q
, we have

H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})\subseteq\pi_{2}^{-1}(Q)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r).

The right-hand side is a union of at most two
\delta
-neighborhoods of
H(\mathbf{x},r)
with dimensions
\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\delta+\overline{\Delta})(\delta+\overline{d})}}\times\delta\times...\times\delta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\delta+\overline{\Delta})(\delta+\overline{d})}}\times\delta^{d-1}
.
∎

For two “very different” moment curves that are exactly tangent, the intersection of their
\delta
-neighborhoods behaves locally like the intersection of two
\delta
-tubes. That is,

Proposition 4
.

Assume that
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
and
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
as before.

(1)

If
\overline{d}((\mathbf{x},r),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\gtrsim 1
and the two moment curves are exactly tangent, then the intersection is comparable to a rectangle with dimensions
\sqrt{\delta}\times\delta\times...\times\delta=\sqrt{\delta}\times\delta^{d-1}
hence,

(22)

\mathcal{L}^{d}\Bigl(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})\Bigr)\sim\delta^{d-\frac{1}{2}}.

(2)

If
\mathbf{z}\in B(\mathbf{x},\frac{\delta}{1000d}),
then a similar estimate also holds, i.e.

(23)

\mathcal{L}^{d}\Bigl(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})\bigcap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{z},r)\Bigr)\sim\delta^{d-\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Since the point of tangency is unique, the intersection of
\delta
-neighborhoods is contained in a
\delta
-neighborhood of one piece of an arc. Since the two moment curves are exactly tangent, (
14
) holds which implies that
\overline{\Delta}=0
. Applying the upper bound given by Proposition
3
,
we obtain the
\lesssim
part.

For the other side, we will prove there is a

\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\delta\times...\times\delta
tube that is contained in both
\delta
-neighborhoods of the two moment curves. Indeed, assume the two moment curves are exactly tangent at

(24)

\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\gamma(t),

Define line segment

L=\{\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)+sr\gamma^{\prime}(t):s\in(0,\frac{1}{(100d)^{d}}\sqrt{\delta})\}.

Consider its
\frac{1}{(100d)^{d}}\delta
-neighborhood
L_{\delta}
. This is a
\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\delta\times...\times\delta
tube. We will prove
L_{\delta}\subseteq H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
. For each
\mathbf{y}\in L_{\delta}
, there is a
s_{o}\in(0,\frac{1}{(100d)^{d}}\sqrt{\delta})
such that

(25)

|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t)-s_{o}r\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{1}{(100d)^{d}}\delta,

then by triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t)-s_{o}r\gamma^{\prime}(t)|+|\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)+s_{o}r\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|

\displaystyle=:I+II.

The first term on the right hand side
I
is under control by (
25
). For the second term
II
, by Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t)+s_{o}r\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{x}-r(\gamma(t)+\gamma^{\prime}(t)s_{o}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)}{2}s_{o}^{2}+o(|s_{o}|^{2}))|

\displaystyle=|r\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)}{2}s_{o}^{2}+o(|s_{o}|^{2})|

\displaystyle\leq 50d^{2}s_{o}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{1000}.

Therefore, we have

(26)

|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|\leq\frac{\delta}{50}.

Combine this estimate with for
I
and
II
, we deduce that
L_{\theta}\subseteq H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r).
By symmetry and the fact that
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
is tangent to
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)
at the same point, we obtain
L_{\theta}\subseteq H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r^{\prime}).
This concludes the proof of the first part .

For the second item, it suffices to prove that
L_{\delta}\subseteq H_{\delta}(\mathbf{z},r)
. By triangle inequality, we have for each
\mathbf{y}\in L_{\delta}
,

|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|\leq|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|+|\mathbf{x}+r\gamma(t+s_{o})-\mathbf{z}-r\gamma(t+s_{o})|.

For the first term, apply (
26
), for the second term, apply
\mathbf{z}\in B(\mathbf{x},\frac{\delta}{1000d})
.
∎

4.
Sharpness of the results

In this section, we test several types of examples that will unravel various types of sharpness for the conclusions.

4.1.
Optimal exponent for
\delta

Example 1
.

In this example, we will show that

Lemma 3
.

For and
p\geq 1
and
s\in\{1,...,d\}
, if the maximal estimate

\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s}f\right\|_{L^{p}(I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2})}\lesssim\delta^{-\alpha}\left\|f\right\|_{p}

holds, then
\alpha\geq\max\{\frac{s-2}{p},0\}
.

Proof.

Assume
p\geq 1
. Define

(27)

E^{s^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\in I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2}}H(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)=\bigcup_{(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\in I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2}}\{(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}})+r\gamma(t):t\in[-1,1]\}

and
E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}
is its
\delta
-neighborhood

Choose

to be the characteristic function of
E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}
. Our goal is to estimate the both sides of (
5
). We are going to prove

(1)

\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}f(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\geq 1
for all
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\in I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2}.

(2)

\left\|f\right\|_{p}\lesssim\min\{\delta^{(s-2)/p}
,1}

By the definition of
\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}
, note that for each
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\in I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2},

H(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\subseteq E,
so
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\subseteq E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}
and
H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\cap E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}=H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{s}_{\delta}f(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))}\int_{H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}|f|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r))}\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\cap E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}})=1,

this concludes the proof of the first item.

For the second item, take a
\delta
-net
A=\{(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)_{i}\}_{i=1}^{O(N)}
of

. Then
N\sim\delta^{-s^{\prime}}
. We claim

(28)

E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}H_{10d\delta}(\mathbf{0},(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)_{i}).

Indeed, for each
\mathbf{y}\in E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}}

Since

, there is a

such that

,
then

\displaystyle|\mathbf{y}-(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}})-r\gamma(t_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbf{y}-(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}}^{0})-r^{0}\gamma(t_{0})|+|(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}}^{0})+r^{0}\gamma(t_{0})-(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}})-r_{0}\gamma(t_{0})|

Therefore, by (

4.2.
Sharpness for the dimension estimate (

Our definition for

\dim(E^{s^{\prime}})=\min\{s^{\prime}+1,d\}.

When

\dim(E_{\delta}^{s^{\prime}})=d

\dim(E^{s^{\prime}})\leq s^{\prime}+1,\quad s^{\prime}=1,...,d-2.

(29)

\dim(E^{s^{\prime}})\leq s^{\prime}+1+\eta.

E^{s^{\prime}}

\dim(I_{1}^{s^{\prime}-1}\times I_{2})=s^{\prime}

(30)

\sum_{i}r(B_{i})^{s^{\prime}+\eta}<\delta.

B_{i}
is
(\underline{\mathbf{x}}^{i},r^{i})

\bigcup_{(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\in B_{i}}H(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\subseteq H(\mathbf{0},\underline{\mathbf{x}}^{i},r^{i})+B(\mathbf{0},Cr(B_{i}))\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

We first introduce Wolff’s seminal result. In

, he proved the following sharp estimate:

Theorem A

(Wolff’s circular maximal theorem)

Denote by

the
\delta
- neighborhood of a circle in the plane centered at

with radius

. Recall Wolff’s circular maximal operator is defined as

Then for all

such that

\left\|W_{\delta}f\right\|_{L^{p}([1/2,2])}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

In our case, the two dimensional moment curve is parabola which means this result for circles cannot be applied directly.

However, combining with Schlag’s result
[
18
]
, Zahl
[
26
]
generalizes this result to the family of curves satisfying the so-called Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition. Additionally, in
[

and
[

, the authors prove the same sharp estimate under a condition that may be slightly rigorously weaker than Sogge’s original definition. In particular, in the appendix of
[

]
, the author verifies that parabolas parametrized by vertex
(x_{1},x_{2})
and dilation
r
, are indeed a family of curves that satisfies the cinematic curvature condition. So we will assume the above Theorem A holds with circle
C
replaced by parabola
P
. Similar to the circle, We also denote as
P_{\delta}(x_{1},_{2},r)
the
\delta
-neighborhood of parabola
P(x_{1},x_{2},r)
with vertex

and dilate factor
r

By applying this planar sharp result, we can prove Theorem
1
for
s=d

Proof of the boundedness of
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{d}
.

Assume
f\geq 0
and denote
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...x_{d})=(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{d-2})=(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}}).
If we write

\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{1}f(r)=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))}\int_{H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}f(\mathbf{s})d\mathbf{s}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r),

where
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},...,s_{d})=(\overline{\mathbf{s}},\underline{\mathbf{s}})
and

\mathcal{A}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r))}\int_{H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}f

is the averaging operator associated to moment curves. Using Fubini’s theorem, we express

as an iterated integral involving the projection of

in the first two coordinates

. Recall that in (

where

In the following computations,
C
is a constant that depends only on dimension and may change from line to line. For any fixed
\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\delta^{d-1}}\int_{H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}f(\mathbf{s})d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{P_{\delta}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},r)}\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{d-2}}\int_{\pi_{2}^{-1}(\overline{\mathbf{s}})\cap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}f(\mathbf{s})d\underline{\mathbf{s}}\right)d\overline{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{P_{\delta}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},r)}\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{d-2}}\int_{\pi_{2}^{-1}(\overline{\mathbf{s}})\cap H_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},r)}F_{\overline{\mathbf{s}}}(\underline{\mathbf{s}})d\underline{\mathbf{s}}\right)d\overline{\mathbf{s}},

\mathbb{R}^{d-2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{C_{\delta}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},r)}\left(\left\|F_{\overline{\mathbf{s}}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d-2})}\delta^{-\frac{d-2}{p}}\right)d\overline{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{C_{\delta}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},r)}G(\overline{\mathbf{s}})\delta^{-\frac{d-2}{p}}d\overline{\mathbf{s}},

where

Take supremum over
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
in the right hand side,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\leq C\delta^{-\frac{d-2}{p}}W_{\delta}G(r),

Since the right hand side is a uniform upper bound independent of

\mathbf{x}

Then applying the parabola version estimate (

\mathfrak{A}_{\delta}f=\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f+\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}f+\mathfrak{A}^{high1}_{\delta}f,

and a corresponding decomposition of maximal operator,

\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s}f=\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,low}f+\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high0}f+\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high1}f.

Before we move on, we briefly explain the motivation of this decomposition. We first decompose the frequency space into low frequency part, i.e.
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\leq 1\}
and high frequency part
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\boldsymbol{\xi}|>1\}
. This is because, by the uncertainty principle, the low frequency part is easier to handle. Heuristically, the uncertainty principle says that if
\widehat{f}
is supported in some ball with radius
R
, then
|f|
is essentially constant in scale
\frac{1}{R}
. Due the the compactness of
\mathfrak{a}_{d},
it is easy to verify that if we fix
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)=(x_{3},...,x_{d},r)
, the Fourier transform of
\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)
with respect to
(x_{1},x_{2})
is contained in a compact set. This mean that
\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)
(as a function of
(x_{1},x_{2})
) is essentially constant in a compact set. So intuitively there is no difference between
\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,low}f(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)
, while the former averaging operator can be nicely controlled. We make this intuition precise in Subsection
6.3
.

The high frequency part is more complicated. Since

m_{\delta,r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\chi_{1}(r)\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)du

is also an oscillatory integral, its behavior is dictated by the location and nature of the critical points of its phase function
\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u).
We note that

\frac{\partial(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u))}{\partial u}=\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(u)=\xi_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}du^{d-1}\xi_{i}.

Since
u\in[-1.5,1.5],
as long as
|\xi_{1}|\geq(100d)^{d}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|
, then

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u))}{\partial u}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\xi_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}du^{d-1}\xi_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\geq|\xi_{1}|-\left(\sum_{i=2}^{d}du^{d-1}|\xi_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim|\xi_{1}|

\displaystyle\gtrsim 1,

when
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\geq 1
.
Thus, the phase function of the oscillatory integral
m_{\delta,r}^{high0}
has no critical point, allowing one to integrate by parts for arbitrarily many times, gaining a super-polynomial decay in
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|
.
Hence its contribution is negligible. This argument has be made rigorous in Subsection
6.4
.

Treatment of
m_{\delta,r}^{high1}
and the resulting maximal operator
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high1}
is the crux of the problem. This is where we need to appeal to
[
15
]
. This part of the proof is presented in Subsection
6.5
.

6.3.
Estimating
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,low}f=\sup\mathfrak{A}_{\delta}^{low}f

We first tackle the low frequency part. We claim

Proposition 5
.

For any
p\geq 1
, there is some constant
C
depending only on dimension and
p
(not depending on
\delta
) such that

(39)

\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

This is definitely stronger than

\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,low}f\right\|_{p}=\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in I_{1}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}})}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\left\|f\right\|_{p},

in the sense that the range of
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
over which we are taking supremum is the entire
\mathbb{R}^{s}
and the finite boundedness instead of
\varepsilon
-loss estimate holds.

Proof.

Let us recall the definition of
\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f
from (
36
)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)=\chi_{1}(r)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\chi_{0}(u)\mathfrak{a}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})du\right)e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Due to the cut-off function
\mathfrak{a}_{d}
, it is easy to check that for any fixed
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)=(x_{s+1},...,x_{d},r),

(40)

\mathrm{supp}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\cdot,\underline{\mathbf{x}},r))\right)\subseteq B^{s}(\mathbf{0},1),

where
B^{s}(\mathbf{0},1)
is the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
centered at zero with radius
1
and
\mathcal{F}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot)
is the Fourier transform with respect to
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
. Indeed,

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle{}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\cdot,\underline{\mathbf{x}},r))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(r)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}e^{-2\pi i\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{x}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)du\right)\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))\mathfrak{a}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\boldsymbol{\xi}d\overline{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(r)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir(\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)du\right)\widehat{\psi}(\delta\eta_{2},...,\delta\eta_{s},\delta\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathfrak{a}_{d}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}})e^{2\pi i\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\underline{\mathbf{x}}}d\underline{\mathbf{x}}.

Note that
\mathfrak{a}_{d}
vanishes for
\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
out side of
B^{s}(\mathbf{0},1)
. Now we are going to apply the uncertainty principle. One specific form of the principle is the following Bernstein’s inequality or reverse Hölder’s inequality.

Proposition 6
.

[
25
, item (2), Proposition 5.3]
Suppose
F\in L^{1}+L^{2}
and supp(
\widehat{F}
)
\subseteq B(\mathbf{0},R)
. Then for any
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
,

(42)

\left\|F\right\|_{q}\leq CR^{d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}\left\|F\right\|_{p}.

Remark 11
.

The reason why Bernstein’s inequality is also called reverse Hölder’s inequality is, for any function
f
supported in a ball
B_{R}
with radius
R
and its averaging
L^{p}
norm

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{\sharp}(B_{R})}:=\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{R})}\int_{B_{R}}|f|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

It is elementary to see by Hölder’s inequality that if
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty,

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{\sharp}(B_{R})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}_{\sharp}(B_{R})}\text{ which is }\left\|f\right\|_{p}\lesssim R^{d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}\left\|f\right\|_{q}.

I.e. we can use the (averaging) norm of
f
with higher exponent
q
to bound the norm of
f
with lower exponent
p
, while Bernstein’s inequality tells us if Fourier support information is known, we can use lower exponent norm to control higher exponent norm, that is equation (
42
). Of course, the right hand side should also be compensated a factor of
R^{d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})}
.

This is exactly what we will repeatedly apply in the rest of the section. We will use an ordinary
L^{p}
norm,
p<\infty
to bound the
L^{\infty}
norm, i.e. the supremum, at an affordable cost.

We apply inequality (
42
) with
d=s
and

F_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})=\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r),

for any
p\geq 1,

(43)

\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\lesssim\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}.

Raising both sides by
p
-th power and integrating over
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)
, in order to prove (
39
), it suffices to prove that

(44)

\left\|\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}\right\|_{L_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}})}=\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{{\mathbf{x}},r}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

According to
[
25
, Proposition 1.3]
, it is easy to see that

m_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\chi_{0}(u)\mathfrak{a}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})du\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

then (
44
) is deduced directly from Young’s convolution inequality and the fact
\int\psi_{\delta}\sim 1
. Indeed, since
\chi_{1}(r)
is a compactly supported function,

(45)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{low}_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{{\mathbf{x}},r}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left\|\chi_{1}\widecheck{m_{\delta}}*f\right\|_{L^{p}_{r}(\mathbb{R})}\right\|_{L_{\mathbf{x}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\widecheck{m_{\delta}}*f\right\|_{L_{\mathbf{x}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\left\|\widecheck{m_{\delta}}\right\|_{L^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|f\right\|_{p}.

It suffices to prove
\left\|\widecheck{m_{\delta}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
is bounded. To simplify the computation, we write
m_{\delta}
into the following form. Recall that
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=1
on
[-1,1],
so we can multiply
m_{\delta}
by
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
without affecting its value, that is

\displaystyle m_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathfrak{a}_{1}(\frac{\xi_{1}}{2})\right)\times\left(\mathfrak{a}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)du\right)

\displaystyle=m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\times m_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\widecheck{m_{\delta}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(m_{1})*\mathcal{F}^{-1}(m_{2})(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(2\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2\cdot)\psi_{\delta}*\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{d}}(\cdot-r\gamma(u))\chi_{0}(u)du)(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int 2\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2\eta_{1})\psi_{\delta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{d}}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\eta}-r\gamma(u))\chi_{0}(u)dud\boldsymbol{\eta}.

So it suffices to prove

(46)

\left\|\widecheck{m_{1}}*\widecheck{m_{2}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\left\|\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2\cdot)\psi_{\delta}*\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}}(\cdot-r\gamma(u))\chi_{0}(u)du\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim 1.

Since we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widecheck{m_{1}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\left\|\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2\cdot)\psi_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2x_{1})\psi_{\delta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})dx_{1}d\tilde{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(2x_{1})dx_{1}\int\psi_{\delta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})d\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\lesssim 1

due to
\int\psi_{\delta}\sim 1
and
\mathfrak{a}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and

\left\|\int\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}}(\cdot-r\gamma(u))\chi_{0}(u)du\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\|\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|\chi_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim 1

due to the Fubini-Tonelli and
\widecheck{\mathfrak{a}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Applying Young’s inequality to (
46
) and plugging these two estimates, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Remark 12
.

For (
43
), while in the case where the
\sup
or
L^{\infty}
norm is considered over a compact set or a half plane, say
(0,\infty)
, Bernstein’s inequality will be replaced by a standard application of fundamental theorem of calculus, as in the proof of Sobolev embedding inequality.

6.4.
Estimating
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high0}f=\sup\mathfrak{A}_{\delta}^{high0}f

Now we start to tackle the high frequency part where there is no critical point of the phase function. We claim

Proposition 7
.

For any
p\geq 1
, there is some constant
C
depending only on dimension and
p
(not depending
\delta
) such that

\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high0}f(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r))\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}})}\leq\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{{high0}}_{\delta}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

Suppressing the dependence of
\delta
, we denote by

(47)

m(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)\mathfrak{c}(\boldsymbol{\xi})du,

where
\mathfrak{c}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\widehat{\psi}(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})(1-\mathfrak{a}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))(1-\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi}))
is a smooth bump function supported in

\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\boldsymbol{\xi}|>1,|\xi_{1}|>(100d)^{d}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{d}|\xi_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\}.

Then by (
37
) and (
47
),

(48)

\mathfrak{A}_{\delta}^{high0}f(\mathbf{x},r)=\chi_{1}(r)\widecheck{m}*f(\mathbf{x}).

Note that we always assume
f\geq 0
. Apply Littlewood-Paley decomposition to the whole frequency space, for
k\geq 0

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P}_{k}f)(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\varphi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})f(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

where
\varphi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is a smooth cut-off function supported in the annulus
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}:2^{k-1}<|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\leq 2^{k+1}\}
. Then by triangle inequality, we have the pointwise control

\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\leq\sum_{k}\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)

for any
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)
, which implies
L^{p}
bound,

\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}}\leq\sum_{k}\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}}.

It suffices to prove the the right hand side is summable. In fact, we have the following classical result.

Lemma 5

(integrate by parts)
.

With above notations, for any
N>0
, there is
C_{N}
such that for any
k\geq 0,

(49)

\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}}\leq C_{N}2^{-k(N-\frac{s}{p})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

If this is the case, by taking
N=10s
, note
p\geq 1
, we obtain

\sum_{k}\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}}\lesssim_{N}\sum_{k}2^{-k}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now turn to the proof of the Lemma
5
, which is a classical argument in oscillatory integral.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

For any fixed
k\geq 0,
due to Littlewood-Paley decomposition, similar to (
41
), it is easy to verify that the Fourier transform of
\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f
(with respect to
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
) is supported in a ball with radius
O(2^{k})
. By Bernstein’s inequality (
42
),

\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\lesssim 2^{\frac{sk}{p}}\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}.

Raising both sides by
p
-th power and integrating over
(\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)
, we obtain

\left\|\sup_{\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}_{\underline{\mathbf{x}},r}}\leq 2^{\frac{sk}{p}}\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

In order to prove (
49
), it suffices to prove for any
N
, the right hand side

\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C_{N}2^{-kN}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Recall (
48
), combining with the cut-off function
\varphi_{k}
, we find the new multiplier for
\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f
is

\chi_{1}(r)m_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\chi_{1}(r)m(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\chi_{1}(r)\mathfrak{c}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ir\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)}\chi_{0}(u)du.

Similar to (
45
), after integrating over
r
first, it suffices to prove

\left\|\mathfrak{A}^{high0}_{\delta}\mathcal{P}_{k}f(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\underline{\mathbf{x}},r)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\lesssim\left\|\widecheck{m}_{k}*f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{N}2^{-kN}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

By Young’s convolution inequality, it suffices to prove

(50)

\left\|\widecheck{m_{k}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{N}2^{-kN}.

In fact, this is just integration by parts, which is repeatedly used in Chapter VII, VIII of Stein’s book
[
19
]
when discussing the decay of oscillatory integrals.

We first fix
\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|\geq 2^{-k(N+1)}
, define differential operator
D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}}
as

D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}}(f)(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{x}.

It can be checked that

D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}}(e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}})=e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}.

As a result, for any
M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

\widecheck{m_{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\int m_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\boldsymbol{\xi}=\int m_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}})d\boldsymbol{\xi}.

It can be verified by integration by parts, that if either
f
or
g
is a compactly supported function, then

\langle f,D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}}g\rangle=-\langle D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}}f,g\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the integral of the product of two functions. Due to the compactness of
m_{k}
, we can transfer
(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}
to
m_{k}
and limit the domain of integration in a ball with radius
O(2^{k})
. So

(51)

\displaystyle\widecheck{m_{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{M}\int(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(m_{k})(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{M}\int_{B(\mathbf{0},O(2^{k}))}(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(m_{k})(\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\boldsymbol{\xi}.

We claim

Lemma 6

(fast decay for
(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(m_{k})(\boldsymbol{\xi})
)
.

For any
J\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, there is a constant
C_{M,J}
depending on
M
,
J
and dimension, such that

(52)

|(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(m_{k})(\boldsymbol{\xi})|\leq C_{M,J}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}|^{M}}2^{-k(J-M)}.

We will put the proof of this lemma in Appendix
6.7
.
Take the lemma for granted and plug the estimate (
52
) back to (
51
), we obtain

|\widecheck{m_{k}}|\leq C_{M,J}2^{kd}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}|^{M}}2^{-k(J-M)}

Note that
|\mathbf{x}|\geq 2^{-k(N+1)}
, as long as
M,J
are chosen to be sufficiently large and appropriately (depending on
d
and
N
),

(53)

\displaystyle\int_{\{\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|\geq 2^{-k(N+1)}\}}|\widecheck{m_{k}}(\mathbf{x})|d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N,M,J}2^{kd}2^{-k(J-M)}\int_{\{\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|\geq 2^{-k(N+1)}\}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}|^{M}}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq C_{N}2^{-kN}.

For
\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|<2^{-k(N+1)}
, by a basic property of Fourier transform and supp
(\varphi_{k})\subseteq B(\mathbf{0},O(2^{k}))
, we obtain

\left\|\widecheck{m_{k}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|m_{k}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\left\|\varphi_{k}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim 2^{kd}.

So

(54)

\int_{\{\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}|<2^{-k(N+1)}\}}|\widecheck{m_{k}}(\mathbf{x})|d\mathbf{x}\leq C_{N}2^{-kdN}\leq C_{N}2^{-kN}.

Combining (
53
) and (
54
), we get the desired (
50
).
∎

6.5.
Estimating
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{s,high1}f=\sup\mathfrak{A}^{{high1}}_{\delta}f

6.5.1.
A local smoothing estimate

In this part, we introduce the setting and the result of Ko, Lee and Oh
[
15
]
.

Let
\Gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth curve and
B\geq 1
be a large number. Assume
\Gamma
satisfies the following two conditions

(55)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq 3d+1}|\Gamma^{(j)}

\displaystyle(u)|\leq B,\quad\forall u\in I,

(56)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\Gamma^{(1)}(u),...,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(d)}(u))\geq 1/B,\quad\forall u\in I,

where
\mathrm{Vol}(v_{1},...,v_{d})
denotes the
d
-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped generated by
v_{1},...v_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 7
.

For
k\geq 0
, let
\mathbb{A}_{k}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:c2^{k-1}\leq|\xi|\leq C2^{k+1}\}
. We say
a\in C^{2d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+2})
is a
symbol of type
(k,d,B)
relative to
\Gamma
if
\operatorname{supp}a\subset I\times[2^{-1},4]\times\mathbb{A}_{k}
, and

(57)

|\partial_{s}^{j}\partial_{t}^{l}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a(s,t,\xi)|\leq B|\xi|^{-|\alpha|}

for
(j,l,\alpha)\in\mathcal{I}_{d}:=\{(j,l,\alpha):0\leq j\leq 1,0\leq l\leq 2d,|\alpha|\leq 2d+2\}
.

We define an integral operator by

6.5.2.
Maximal estimate via local smoothing

Now we adapt our problem to their setting. Recall the definition of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{high1}_{\delta}

By the dyadic decomposition of

,

By the previous computations, we have
2^{k}\lesssim\delta^{-1}
. This implies

so to prove (

6.6.1.
Existence of the cut-off functions

The existence of
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{0}
is trivial. We omit the proof. So it suffices to prove the existence of

Proposition 8
.

There is a function
\psi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a nonnegative smooth function that satisfies that

(1)

\hat{\psi}
is compactly supported and nonnegative.

(2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi>0
.

(3)

\psi(\mathbf{x})\geq 1
for some fixed
\mathbf{x}\in B(\mathbf{0},C_{d})
for some
C_{d}>0
to be determined later.

Proof.

Take a non-zero nonnegative smooth function
\hat{\phi}
with compact support contained in
B(\mathbf{0},1/2).
Then
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}})=\phi^{2}
is nonnegative. Furthermore, supp
(\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}})\subseteq B(\mathbf{0},1)
and by definitions of convolution and conjugate,
\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}}=\hat{\phi}*\hat{\phi}
is also non-negative, non-zero.
By multiplying a factor of
\frac{100}{\int\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}}}
if necessary, we can assume
\int\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}}=c
for some
c>10.
This implies that

\phi^{2}(0)=\int\hat{\phi}*\bar{\hat{\phi}}=c>10.

By continuity, there is a
\varepsilon_{o}>0
depending on
\hat{\phi}
such that for all
|\mathbf{x}|\leq\varepsilon_{o}
,

\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x})\geq 5.

Rescaling the function, we have

(62)

\phi^{2}(\frac{\varepsilon_{o}\mathbf{x}}{C_{d}})\geq 5

for
\mathbf{x}\in B(\mathbf{0},C_{d})
. Define

\psi(\mathbf{x}):=\phi^{2}(\frac{\varepsilon_{o}\mathbf{x}}{C_{d}}).

Apparently its integral is positive so item
(2)
is satisfied and item
(3)
is guaranteed by (
62
). For item
(1)
, it suffices to notice that the dilation factor
\frac{\varepsilon_{o}\mathbf{x}}{C_{d}}
will not affect the compactness in frequency space and the non-negativity of the function.
∎

Smooth version domination

In this part we prove (

For each

\mathbf{y}-r\gamma(t)=\mathbf{s},

where

So trivially we have,

\phi(u,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-2\pi\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u)=-2\pi(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}u^{d}),

whose derivative (as a function of
u
) is

\phi^{\prime}(u,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-2\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\gamma(u))^{\prime}=-2\pi(\xi_{1}+2u\xi_{2}+...+du^{d-1}\xi_{d}).

Due to the existence of
\mathfrak{c}
and
\varphi_{0}
, it is easy to verify that there is no critical point in the supported of this two functions and for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in
supp
(\mathfrak{a})\cap
supp
(\varphi_{0})
and
u\in[-1.5,1.5]
,

|\phi^{\prime}(u,\boldsymbol{\xi})|\geq c_{d}>0.

Similar to the argument in the proof of Lemma
5
, after applying an integration by parts argument for
J
times, we obtain (details can be found in Stein’s book)

(73)

\int_{-1.5}^{1.5}\psi(u,\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{x})e^{i2^{k}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},u)}du=\int_{-1.5}^{1.5}({}^{t}D)^{J}(\psi)\cdot e^{i2^{k}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},u)}du,

for a differential operator with the following form,

{}^{t}D(f)(u):=-\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{f}{2\pi i2^{k}\phi^{\prime}(u,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\right).

We will use induction to prove that

Lemma 9
.

For any
J
, there is a constant
C_{j,J}
such that

(74)

|({}^{t}D)^{J}(\psi)|\leq C_{j,J}2^{-kJ}|\mathbf{x}|^{j}.

Take this for granted and plug (
74
) to (
73
), we get

(75)

\left|\int_{-1.5}^{1.5}\psi(u,\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{x})e^{i2^{k}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},u)}du\right|=\left|\int_{-1.5}^{1.5}({}^{t}D)^{J}(\psi)\cdot e^{i2^{k}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},u)}du\right|\leq C_{j,J}2^{-kJ}|\mathbf{x}|^{j}.

Plugging (
75
) and (
70
) back to (
69
), we get

|\text{LHS of \eqref{intbyparts2}}|\leq C_{j,M}2^{-k(J-M)}|\mathbf{x}|^{M}.

Back to the estimate (
68
), by choosing the the maximal coefficient, the dependence over
j
disappears. So we have

|(D_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathbf{x}})^{M}(m_{k})(\boldsymbol{\xi})|\leq\frac{C_{M,J}}{|\mathbf{x}|^{M}}2^{-k(J-M)}.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
6
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
9
.

When
J=1,
we have

(76)

\displaystyle|\psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle|=\left|\frac{d}{du}({}^{t}D^{0})\psi\right|

\displaystyle=|(j(-2\pi i)^{j}(\mathbf{x}\cdot\gamma(u))^{j-1}\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\chi(u)+(-2\pi i\mathbf{x}\cdot\gamma(u))^{j}\chi^{\prime}(u))\mathfrak{c}(2^{k}\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{j}|\mathbf{x}|^{j}=C_{j}2^{-k0}|\mathbf{x}|^{j}

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle|{}^{t}D(\psi)(u)|

\displaystyle=|\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{\psi}{2\pi i2^{k}\phi^{\prime}(u,\boldsymbol{\xi})}\right)|

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{j}}{2^{k}}|\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{\psi}{\phi^{\prime}(u)}\right)|

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{j}}{2^{k}}|\frac{\psi^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}(u)-\phi^{\prime\prime}(u)\psi}{|\phi^{\prime}(u)|^{2}}|.

Note that
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\sim 1,
so all derivatives of
\phi
will be bounded (the bounds depend on order of the derivative.) So combining this with (

) and (

), we obtain

|{}^{t}D(\psi)(u)|\leq\frac{C|\mathbf{x}|^{j}}{2^{k}}.

Assume the inequality (

) and

\left|\frac{d}{du}({}^{t}D^{J-1})\psi\right|\leq C_{j}2^{-k(J-1)}|\mathbf{x}|^{j},

hold for all

.
When

,

\displaystyle|({}^{t}D)^{n}(\psi)(u)|

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{j}}{2^{k}}\left|\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{({}^{t}D)^{n}(\psi)(u)}{\phi^{\prime}(u)}\right)\right|

6.8.
Verification of (
57
)

Recall

a(u,r,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\chi_{0}(u)\chi_{1}(r)\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

and our goal is to verify

(80)

|\partial^{j}_{u}\partial^{l}_{r}\partial^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}a(u,r,\boldsymbol{\xi})|\leq B|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{-|\alpha|},

for
(j,l,\alpha)\in\{(j,l,\alpha):0\leq j\leq 1,0\leq l\leq 2d,|\alpha|\leq 2d+2\}
.

Proof.

Since
\chi_{i},i=0,1
are smoothing functions, as long as
B
is large enough, all of their derivatives with finite order can be bounded by
B
. So it suffices to consider the decay of partial derivative with
\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Note the support of
\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is
B(\mathbf{0},O(2^{k}))
It suffices to prove that

|\partial^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\leq B2^{-k|\alpha|}.

We take
\alpha=(0,1,...,0)
for example. For other cases, the computations are similar. For the general case, it is also another Leibniz’s rule plus induction argument similar to the proof of Lemma
9
combined with Lemma
8
. So we omit the proof again.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\mathfrak{a}_{1}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{(100d)^{d}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|}\right)\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{(100d)^{d}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|}\right)\cdot\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\frac{\partial\varphi_{k}}{\partial\xi_{2}}\mathfrak{a}.

It is elementary to verify that the absolute value of the second term is
\lesssim 2^{-k}

\left|\frac{\partial\varphi_{k}}{\partial\xi_{2}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|=\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\varphi(2^{-k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|=|2^{-k}\varphi^{\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(2^{-k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\lesssim 2^{-k}.
