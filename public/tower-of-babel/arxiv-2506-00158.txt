Privacy Amplification in Differentially Private Zeroth-Order Optimization with Hidden States
Eli Chien, Wei-Ning Chen, Pan Li

Abstract
Zeroth-order optimization has emerged as a promising approach for fine-tuning large language models on domain-specific data, particularly under differential privacy (DP) and memory constraints. While first-order methods have been extensively studied from a privacy perspective, the privacy analysis and algorithmic design for zeroth-order methods remain significantly underexplored. A critical open question concerns hidden-state DP analysis: although convergent privacy bounds are known for first-order methods, it has remained unclear whether similar guarantees can be established for zeroth-order methods. In this work, we provide an affirmative answer by proving a convergent DP bound for zeroth-order optimization. Our analysis generalizes the celebrated privacy amplification-by-iteration framework to the setting of smooth loss functions in zeroth-order optimization. Furthermore, it induces better DP zeroth-order algorithmic designs that are previously unknown to the literature.

Introduction

The scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
suggest that increasing the amount of training data and model size leads to corresponding increases in model capability. It has demonstrated success in many large-scale vision and language foundational models, which typically consist of billions to hundreds of billions of parameters. However, as model size grows, preserving a model’s privacy (in particular, differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
)
) during the training phase becomes increasingly challenging. This challenge primarily stems from the significant increase in memory usage when optimizing the model via differentially private optimizers such as DP-SGD
(
Abadi et al. 2016
)
. To DP-fy these first-order methods, the standard approach is to clip the gradients of each sample and then inject calibrated Gaussian noise in each training round. The per-sample gradient clipping step, however, incurs huge memory costs since the optimizer has to cache all the intermediate gradients of each sample during back-propagation
1
1

1

While some recent works have proposed methods to alleviate such costs (e.g., via ghost clipping
(
Li et al. 2022
)
), the increase in memory usage remains significant compared to non-DP training
.

To address this issue, recent works by
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
consider differentially private zeroth-order optimization, which relies only on the evaluated loss (i.e., forward pass of the model) and does not require the computation of gradients. While there are several variants of zeroth-order optimization, they generally involve the following steps: in each training round, a random perturbation is generated on the model weights (e.g., sampling from an isotropic Gaussian distribution), and the step size is computed based on the loss values evaluated on the perturbed weights. To ensure DP, one could clip and add noise to the loss values associated with each sample. Since these zeroth-order optimization methods do not require the computation of gradient, they significantly improve both memory and computational costs. Indeed,
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
successfully fine-tuned models with over 60B parameters with DP and the performance is comparable with the state-of-the-art DP first-order methods such as DP-LoRA
(
Yu et al. 2022
)
.

Despite successfully scaling up DP training to models with tens of billions of parameters, the current privacy analysis of the zeroth-order optimization method in
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
relies on the composition theorem, which accounts for the privacy budget spent during each training round. As a result, the privacy cost increases
linearly
with the number of steps, and hence a carefully chosen stopping point is required to balance privacy and utility. Note that such framework assumes that intermediate optimization steps are public, potentially leading to an overestimation of the true privacy cost when only the
last iterate
(i.e., the final model weights) is released.

In this work, we demonstrate that by concealing the intermediate optimization steps and slightly tweaking the perturbation and model update steps, one can “amplify” the privacy guarantees provided by the composition theorem. This type of privacy analysis, known as “privacy amplification by iteration” (PABI), leverages the hidden optimization trajectory to achieve stronger privacy. While PABI has been extensively studied for first-order methods
(
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Altschuler and Talwar 2023
;
Chien et al. 2024a
;
Chien and Li 2025
)
, existing analyses do not extend directly to zeroth-order optimization.

Applying PABI to zeroth-order methods introduces unique challenges, primarily due to the structure of the DP noise. In first-order methods like DP-SGD, gradients are perturbed with isotropic Gaussian noise each round, and privacy analysis relies on a key shifted-reduction lemma
(
Feldman et al. 2018
)
to control Rényi divergence iteratively. However, in zeroth-order optimization, noise is applied to the step size along a random direction
u
, resulting in “anisotropic” noise, i.e., a scalar Gaussian perturbation along
u
rather than in all directions. As a result, the standard shifted-reduction lemmas are no longer applicable.

One could add isotropic Gaussian noise on top of zeroth-order updates and apply existing analysis tools, but prior work has shown that doing so severely worsens the privacy-utility trade-off
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. In zeroth-order methods, discrepancies between adjacent datasets only appear along the update direction
u
, making noise in the orthogonal subspace
u^{\perp}
wasteful. To address this, we design a hybrid noise mechanism that combines the advantages of directional and isotropic noise, achieving tighter privacy bounds. Specifically, our method employs (1) a random
K
-dimensional update each step to reduce variance, (2) Gaussian perturbation on the step size, and (3) a small isotropic Gaussian noise component to enable the shifted-reduction analysis.

Our analysis reveals several privacy advantages in zeroth-order method overlooked by prior work. First, we highlight the role of
K
, the dimension of the update at each iteration. While larger
K
is known to improve utility by reducing the variance of gradient estimation, previous DP zeroth-order studies
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
avoid large
K
due to the
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{K})
increase in privacy cost under standard composition. However, under hidden-state analysis, we show that privacy loss actually
decays
with
K
under a fixed utility constraint. Standard composition fails to capture this benefit, treating the privacy loss as independent of
K

(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, our analysis shows that when
K>1
, choosing orthonormal update directions
\{u_{t,k}\}_{k=1,..,K}
significantly improves the privacy bound compared to using i.i.d. random directions – a benefit not recognized in the existing literature. These findings not only provide tighter privacy guarantees but also offer practical guidelines for designing better differentially private zeroth-order optimization algorithms.

Our contributions.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

PABI for Zeroth-Order Methods
: We develop the first privacy amplification by iteration (PABI) analysis tailored for DP zeroth-order optimization, demonstrating a constant privacy cost that saturates after a fixed number of training steps.

•

Hybrid Noise Mechanism
: We propose a new noise mechanism that combines directional and isotropic noise to enable tighter privacy bounds.

•

Role of Update Dimension
K
: We show that, contrary to prior beliefs, the privacy loss decays with the update dimension
K
under hidden-state analysis, and that using orthonormal directions further improves privacy.

Missing proofs, discussions on limitations, and broader impacts are deferred to the appendices.

Preliminaries and Problem formulation

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
be a training dataset of
n
data points from some domain
\mathcal{X}
. We say a dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is adjacent to
\mathcal{D}
with replacement (denoted by
\mathcal{D}\simeq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
) if
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
can be obtained by replacing one data point
x_{i}
from
\mathcal{D}
with an arbitrary point
x_{i}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{X}
. One could also adopt a different adjacency definition as the one in
Kairouz et al. 2021
. We denote
[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
and
f\sharp\mu
the pushforward of a distribution
\mu
under a function
f
. We use
X_{i:j}
as shorthand for the vector concatenating
X_{i},\cdots,X_{j}
and
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
to denote that random variables
X,Y
are equal in distribution.

2.1
Differential Privacy

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
satisfies differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
;
Mironov 2017
)
, if the following holds:

Definition 2.1

((Rényi) Differential Privacy)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private (DP), if for any
\mathcal{D}\simeq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and any measurable
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathsf{range}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right\}+\delta.

\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\left(\varepsilon,\alpha\right)
-Rényi differential privacy (RDP) if

D_{\alpha}\left({\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})}\middle\|{\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)}\right)\leq\varepsilon,

where
D_{\alpha}
denotes the Rényi divergence:
D_{\alpha}\left(\mu\middle\|\nu\right)\triangleq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\int(\mu(x)/\nu(x))^{\alpha}\nu(x)dx\right)
if
\mu\ll\nu
, and
\infty
otherwise
2
2

2

With a slight abuse of notation, for random variables
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
we define
D_{\alpha}(X||Y)=D_{\alpha}\left(\mu\middle\|\nu\right)
.
.

RDP enjoys several useful properties that will be utilized in our analysis.

Lemma 2.2

(Post-processing property
(
Mironov 2017
)
)
.

For any
\alpha>1
, any function
f
, and any probability distribution
\mu,\nu
,
D_{\alpha}(h\sharp\mu||h\sharp\nu)\leq D_{\alpha}\left(\mu\middle\|\nu\right).

Lemma 2.3

(Strong composition for Rényi divergence
(
Mironov 2017
)
)
.

For any
\alpha>1
and any two sequences of random variables
X_{1},\cdots,X_{k}
and
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{k}
, it holds that

D_{\alpha}(X_{1:k}||Y_{1:k})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sup_{x_{1:i-1}}D_{\alpha}(X_{i}|_{X_{1:i-1}=x_{1:i-1}}||Y_{i}|_{Y_{1:i-1}=x_{1:i-1}}).

Shifted Rényi divergence analysis.
Our hidden-state DP analysis generalize the shifted Rényi divergence approach developed by
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
, which originally only applies to first-order optimization and requires the definitions below. Generalizing shifted Rényi divergence analysis to the zeroth-order optimization method correctly is non-trivial (see Section
3
for details).

Definition 2.4

(
W_{\infty}
distance)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The infinite Wasserstein distance between
\mu,\nu
is defined as

W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\esssup_{(X,Y)\sim\gamma}\|X-Y\|,

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all possible coupling of
\mu,\nu
.

Definition 2.5

(Shifted Rényi divergence)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
z\geq 0
and
\alpha>1
, the shifted Rényi divergence is defined as

D_{\alpha}^{(z)}(\mu||\nu)=\inf_{\mu^{\prime}:W_{\infty}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq z}D_{\alpha}(\mu^{\prime}||\nu).

Note that when
z=0
, we recover the standard Rényi divergence
D_{\alpha}^{(0)}(\mu||\nu)=D_{\alpha}(\mu||\nu)
.

2.2
Zeroth-Order Optimization

We consider the empirical risk minimization (ERM) problem of minimizing the loss function for a parameter vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
over a given dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
:

L(w;\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w;x_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(w).

To this end, we study a zeroth-order gradient descent (ZOGD) algorithm:

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left[w_{t}-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t,k})\right],\text{where}

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t,k}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi};\Delta\right)u_{t,k},

(1)

Here,
\xi\geq 0
is the perturbation scale,
\eta>0
is the step size,
\mathsf{clip}(y;\Delta)
denotes the clipping function (defined as(
\mathsf{clip}(y;\Delta)\triangleq\frac{y}{\max(1,|y|/\Delta)}
), and the operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
denotes projection onto a bounded convex set
\mathcal{K}
with diameter
D
. Note that the estimator
\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t,k})
is a two-point approximation of the directional derivative along
u_{t,k}
and serves as a zeroth-order surrogate for the true gradient
\nabla L(w)
.
We refer to this estimator as the
zeroth-order gradient
throughout the paper. The direction vectors
u_{t,k}
are randomly sampled from an isotropic distribution and will be specified in the algorithm. In the special case
K=1
, it is common to drawn
u
from the Euclidean sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
uniformly
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
or multivariate Gaussian distribution
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
. Our results can be generalized to other zeroth-order optimization methods, such as one-point estimators
(
Flaxman et al. 2005
)
and the directional derivative
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
.

3
Hidden-state DP Analysis for Zeroth-order Optimization

Our goal in this section is to show that the final iterate
w_{T}
of the privatized ZOGD algorithm (
2.2
) satisfies
(\alpha,\varepsilon(\alpha))
-RDP, with
\varepsilon(\alpha)
independent of
T
. This can be translated into
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP via the standard RDP-to-DP conversion
(
Mironov 2017
)
. Equivalently, we aim to upper bound the Rényi divergence between two adjacent output distributions
w_{T}
and
w_{T}^{\prime}
corresponding to neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}\simeq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

To privatize ZOGD (
2.2
),
Zhang et al. 2023
has considered two types of noise injection mechanisms in the case of
K=1
:

\displaystyle\text{(a)}:\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left[w_{t}-\eta\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t})+\eta G_{t}^{(1)}u_{t}\right],\;\text{(b)}:\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left[w_{t}-\eta\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t})+\eta G_{t}^{(2)}\right],

(2)

where
G_{t}^{(1)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
is scalar Gaussian noise applied in the update direction, and
G_{t}^{(2)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}I_{d})
is isotropic Gaussian noise over all coordinates. Under the same privacy budget (i.e.,
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
),
Zhang et al. 2023
showed that mechanism (a) achieves better utility than mechanism (b).

This advantage can be intuitively explained using the composition-based privacy framework: conditioned on the previous iterate
w_{t-1}
, the sensitive information at step
t
is only leaked through the scalar step size
\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t})
along the (random) direction
u_{t}
. Thus, in mechanism (b), noise injected orthogonally to
u_{t}
(i.e., in the remaining
d-1
dimensions) does not contribute to privacy but still degrades utility. Conversely, mechanism (a) is more efficient but presents technical difficulties: due to the directional nature of the noise, standard shifted-divergence analysis (PABI) techniques are no longer directly applicable. This leads to our central question:

Can we design a noise mechanism that (1) concentrates noise along the update directions to improve utility, while (2) still enabling shifted-divergence analysis for final iterate privacy?

3.1
A Unified Noisy ZOGD mechanism

To address the above, we propose a generalized noise injection scheme that subsumes both (a) and (b) as special cases, while enabling tighter hidden-state privacy analysis:

\displaystyle\text{Noisy-ZOGD: }w_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{R}}\left[w_{t}-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t,k})+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}G_{t,k}^{(1)}u_{t,k}+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{d}}G_{t}^{(2)}\right],

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}_{R}
is the
\ell_{2}
ball of radius
R
centered at the origin,
G_{t,k}^{(1)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(1-\beta_{t})\sigma^{2})
, and
G_{t}^{(2)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{t}\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. The vectors
\{u_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are orthonormal and drawn uniformly from the Stiefel manifold
V_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; when
K=1
, this reduces to the uniform distribution on the sphere, i.e.,
u_{t,k}\sim\textsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

A couple of remarks are given in order. First, in zeroth-order optimization, using
K>1
directions improves gradient estimation
(
Duchi et al. 2015
)
, though at higher computational cost per iteration. Prior work typically assumes the directions
\{u_{t,k}\}
are drawn independently from
\textsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. In contrast, our orthonormal construction facilitates tighter privacy analysis under the hidden-state model. Second, the parameterization in Eq. (
3
) ensures equivalent total noise variance (and thus utility guarantees) across all
\beta_{t}\in[0,1]
and
K\geq 1
, following the utility analysis of
Zhang et al. 2023
(see Appendix
A.10
for details). In particular, setting
K=1
with
\beta_{t}=0
and
\beta_{t}=1
recovers mechanisms (a) and (b), respectively. Next, we provide baseline privacy bounds for the above two cases (assuming access to the entire trajectory
\{w_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
) using standard composition.

Theorem 3.1

(Public-State Privacy Baseline)
.

The Noisy-ZOGD update rule (
3
) with
T
iterations satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}\log(1/\delta)T}{n^{2}\sigma^{2}}}\right)\;\text{when }\beta_{t}=0,\quad\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d\Delta^{2}\log(1/\delta)T}{Kn^{2}\sigma^{2}}}\right)\;\text{when }\beta_{t}=1.

(4)

First of all, since
K\leq d
, setting
\beta_{t}=1
always leads to a worse privacy bound under fixed utility constraints. This motivates the design of scalar-directional noise (
\beta_{t}=0
), as explored in
Zhang et al. 2023
. Second, the above privacy bounds assume that all intermediate iterates
w_{1},\dots,w_{T}
are publicly released. As such, the total privacy cost accumulates linearly over iterations, growing unboundedly as
T
increases. Finally, another way to account the final privacy budget is to treat the mechanism as output perturbation, that is, by ignoring all intermediate noise but only focusing on the last step. Since
w_{T}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, the Gaussian noise in the last iterate always ensures an
\left(O\left(\sqrt{{R^{2}\log(1/\delta)}/{\sigma^{2}}}\right),\delta\right)
-DP guarantee, independent of
T
. However, this bound is generally weaker than the composition-based bound in Theorem
3.1
for most practical
T
.

Our main contribution is a
non-trivial convergent
privacy analysis and guarantee for the final iterate
w_{T}
. Specifically, for smooth (strongly) convex problems, we show that it satisfies
\left(O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{n^{2}\sigma^{2}}\min(T,\frac{Rn\sqrt{d}}{K\Delta})}\right),\delta\right)
-DP; see Figure
1
for numerical results and comparisons to the standard composition and output perturbation. While our analysis extends to non-convex settings, the final privacy bound in those cases does not admit a closed-form expression; hence, we primarily focus on convex problems. Importantly, our analysis reveals that by carefully tuning
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
, one can achieve improved privacy-utility trade-offs using hidden-state analysis. Our results also extend to mini-batch settings and incorporate privacy amplification via subsampling
(
Balle et al. 2018
)
.

3.2
Hidden-state Privacy Analysis for Noisy-ZOGD

Next, we present our main theorem, the first
convergent
privacy bound for the zeroth-order ERM.

Theorem 3.2

(Hidden-State RDP Bound)
.

Assume
\ell(\cdot,x)
are
M
-smooth and
\Delta
-Lipschitz for the first argument and all possible data
x
. The Noisy-ZOGD update (
3
) is
(\alpha,\varepsilon(\alpha))
-RDP for any
\alpha>1,d\geq 2K,\epsilon\geq 0
on some high probability event
\mathcal{G}
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\alpha)\leq\min_{\tau,\beta_{t},a_{t}}\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\beta_{t}\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{2\Delta}{n}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha a_{t}^{2}d}{2\eta^{2}\left(1-\beta_{t}\right)\sigma^{2}}\right),\;s.t.\;\tau\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\},\;\beta_{t}\in[0,1],\;

\displaystyle a_{t},z_{t}\geq 0\;\forall t\geq\tau,\;z_{t-1}=c_{1}^{-1}(z_{t}+a_{t}-c_{2}),\;z_{T}=0,\;z_{\tau}\geq\min\left(2R,\frac{2\eta\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{K}}\right).

Here we have
c=1+\frac{\eta M}{K}
,
c_{1}=\sqrt{1-(1-c^{2})\frac{K}{d}+\epsilon(1+c^{2})\frac{K}{d}},\;c_{2}=\eta M\xi,\;\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}\right)\geq 1-2(T-\tau)\exp\left(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}dK}{12(d-K)+8\epsilon(d-2K)}\right).
If
\ell
is also convex and choose
\eta\leq 2K/M
, we have
c=1
. If
\ell
is also
m
-strongly convex and choose
\eta\leq K/M
, we have
c=1-\frac{\eta m}{K}
.

While the tightest privacy bound in Theorem
3.2
requires solving a constrained optimization problem, we emphasize that any suboptimal feasible solution still yields a valid privacy guarantee. Although the optimal bound generally lacks a closed-form expression, we can derive a closed-form result in the case of
m
-strongly convex objectives. By applying the RDP-to-DP conversion
(
Mironov 2017
)
and incorporating the failure probability
1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G})
into the failure term
\delta
, we obtain the following differential privacy result.

Corollary 3.3

(Closed-from DP guarantee of Noisy-ZOGD)
.

Assume
\ell(\cdot,x)
are
M
-smooth,
m
-strongly convex and
\Delta
-Lipschitz for the first argument and all possible data
x
. The Noisy-ZOGD update (
3
) is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with the choice
\eta=K/M
,
\max(\frac{20(1+c^{2})^{2}}{3(1-c^{2})^{2}}\log(\frac{4}{\delta}\lceil\frac{MRn\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta}\rceil),1)\leq K\leq d/2
and
\xi\leq\frac{2\Delta}{n\eta M\sqrt{2d}}
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{n^{2}\sigma^{2}}\min(T,\frac{MRn\sqrt{d}}{K\Delta})}\right),\;c=1-\frac{m}{M}.

The bound on
\varepsilon
in Corollary
3.3
is convergent as
T\to\infty
and becomes inversely proportional to
K
in this regime. This behavior contrasts with the standard composition-based privacy bound in Theorem
3.1
, where using multiple directions (
K>1
) offers no privacy gain when
\beta_{t}=0
. This distinction highlights a core contribution of our work: our hidden-state analysis reveals that incorporating multiple zeroth-order estimates improves not only utility but also privacy—a phenomenon not observed in analogous first-order methods. Compared to prior results in first-order settings
(
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Chien and Li 2025
)
, our results demonstrate that using multiple zeroth-order directions provides its own privacy benefits in the zeroth-order regime.

Before presenting the proof, we introduce several technical lemmas that will be utilized in our hidden-state DP analysis. The first two are related to the generalized shifted divergence framework, adapted to our setting to account for the Lipschitz behavior of the zeroth-order update map.

Lemma 3.4

(Shift reduction lemma
(
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Feldman et al. 2018
)
)
.

For any probability distribution
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha>1
and
a,z\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{(z)}\left(\mu\ast N(0,\sigma^{2}I)||\nu\ast N(0,\sigma^{2}I)\right)\leq D_{\alpha}^{(z+a)}(\mu||\nu)+\frac{\alpha a^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

Definition 3.5

(Generalized Lipschitz)
.

For
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
, the mapping
\phi
is said to be
(c_{1},c_{2})
-generalized Lipschitz if for any
x,y
in its domain,

\|\phi(x)-\phi(y)\|\leq c_{1}\|x-y\|+c_{2}.

Lemma 3.6

(Generalized Lipschitz reduction lemma)
.

Assume for
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
the mapping
\phi
is
(c_{1},c_{2})
-generalized Lipschitz. Then for any probability distribution
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha>1
and
z\geq 0
,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{(c_{1}z+c_{2})}(\phi\sharp\mu||\phi\sharp\nu)\leq D_{\alpha}^{(z)}(\mu||\nu).

We next show that the noiseless ZOGD update
\hat{\psi}_{t}(w)=w-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w;u_{t,k})
is indeed generalized Lipschitz with high probability when its first-order counterpart
\phi_{t}(w)=w-\frac{\eta}{nK}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell_{i}(w)
is Lipschitz, which allows us to leverage Lemma
3.6
. Note that when the loss is at least smooth, one can show that the GD update is indeed Lipschitz with a proper choice of step size
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)

Further Remarks.

There are several important observations regarding our results and analysis. First, although our main analysis focuses on the full-batch setting, Theorem
3.2
naturally extends to the mini-batch regime. In particular, we present results for the case of sampling without replacement, following the shifted Rényi divergence framework developed in prior works on first-order methods
(
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Altschuler et al. 2024
;
Chien and Li 2025
)
. The corresponding privacy bounds and analysis are provided in Appendix
A.9
. While it is also possible to extend our results to other mini-batch schemes, such as shuffled cyclic mini-batches
(
Ye and Shokri 2022
;
Chien and Li 2025
)
, we leave such generalizations for future work.

Second, a key analysis-driven design choice in our method is the selection of update directions
\{u_{t,k}\}
. While standard zeroth-order optimization methods
(
Duchi et al. 2015
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
often sample directions independently (i.i.d.), such a choice would significantly complicate the analysis, particularly the concentration bounds involving the term
c_{1}
in Lemma
3.8
, and result in weaker privacy guarantees. We discuss the implications of the i.i.d. sampling scheme in Appendix
B
. Our use of orthonormal directions enables the application of tight quantile bounds from the beta distribution, which are critical to our privacy analysis. Notably, the privacy benefit of using orthonormal
u_{t,k}
directions appears to be novel in the literature; under the standard composition-based analysis, orthogonality does not influence the privacy guarantee and is therefore overlooked.

Finally, some of the assumptions in our analysis can likely be relaxed. For instance, the requirement that each loss function
\ell_{i}
is both Lipschitz and
M
-smooth simplifies the analysis but is not strictly necessary. Relaxing the Lipschitz assumption, for example, mainly affects the proof that the (noiseless) gradient update map
\phi
is Lipschitz, a property that is crucial for the shifted divergence analysis. Recent work by
Kong and Ribero 2024
has shown that the clipped first-order gradient map remains Lipschitz even without assuming Lipschitz continuity of the loss itself. Additionally,
Chien and Li 2025
demonstrated that the smoothness requirement can be weakened to Hölder continuity of the gradient in the context of shifted divergence for first-order methods. We leave a rigorous extension of our analysis to these broader settings as an important direction for future work.

4
Related Works

Hidden-state Noisy-SGD.
The hidden-state privacy analysis for the first-order optimization method, specifically SGD, has raised great attention recently.
Chourasia et al. 2021
;
Ye and Shokri 2022
leverage the analysis of Langevin dynamics for DP guarantees but require strong convexity on the loss.
Chien et al. 2024b
adopts a similar analysis but for the machine unlearning problem.
Asoodeh and Diaz 2023
establishes the convergent DP bound by establishing the contraction of the hockey stick divergence of the Noisy-SGD process.
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Altschuler et al. 2024
derive convergent DP bound for Noisy-SGD based on formalizing shifted Rényi divergence analysis, albeit the loss is required to be convex.
Chien et al. 2024a
adopted their analysis for establishing privacy guarantees for the machine unlearning problem and
Chien and Li 2025
relaxed the convex and smoothness assumption in these works. Recently, many works have also studied the hidden-state privacy for Noisy-SGD by either establishing DP guarantees or performing privacy auditing
(
Kong and Ribero 2024
;
Annamalai 2024
;
Cebere et al. 2024
;
Steinke et al. 2024
)
. All of these works focus on the first-order optimization method, while we study the hidden-state DP guarantee for the zeroth-order optimization.

Differentially Private Zeroth-order Optimization.
Zeroth-order optimization under DP constraint has only been studied very recently but gain increasing attention.
Zhang et al. 2024
is the first to study DP zeroth-order optimization.
Zhang et al. 2023
improves their results by observing the benefit of scalar Gaussian noise (see our discussion around Theorem
3.1
) and proposed the state-of-the-art algorithm DPZero, albeit they focus on the case
K=1
. Concurrently,
Tang et al. 2024
proposed a method similar to DPZero but with the update direction
u
being drawn from standard normal. They demonstrate the benefit of DP zeroth-order optimization in fine-tuning the Large Languague Model (LLM) but without providing utility analysis.
Liu et al. 2024a
propose a stage-wise zeroth-order optimization DP-ZOSO building upon DPZero and demonstrates empirical gain over DPZero. All of these works do not establish the convergent privacy loss under hidden-state assumption as in our work. Our work provides a non-trivial convergent privacy loss for zeroth-order optimization under DP constraint over the bounded domain. We also elucidate the privacy benefit of leveraging
K\geq 1
zeroth-order gradient and choosing orthonormal update direction
u
, which was not studied in the prior literature as well to the best of our knowledge.

5
Conclusions

We provide the first convergent DP bound under the hidden-state assumption for smooth losses. Our analysis generalizes the celebrated privacy amplification by iteration (PABI) analysis to zeroth-order methods. It also elucidates several privacy-beneficial designs in the zeroth-order method that cannot be demonstrated via the standard DP composition theorem. Our work paves the way to advanced DP zeroth-order approaches, which we believe to achieve a better privacy-utility trade-off in large language model fine-tuning under DP and memory constraints in the future.

A.1
Limitations

While we have established the first convergent DP guarantee for the ZOGD method, it is still far from ready to be directly applied to LLM (or any other general neural networks) fine-tuning. This theory-practice gap is shared by prior theoretical works on hidden-state analysis on first-order methods
(
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
;
Altschuler et al. 2024
;
Ye and Shokri 2022
;
Chien and Li 2025
)
, as we all need several structural assumptions (such as smoothness) to establish such results. Furthermore, as discussed in the main text, we left several generalizations for future work. This includes using shuffled cyclic minibatch
(
Ye and Shokri 2022
)
, relaxing the Lipschitz loss assumption using the analysis of
Kong and Ribero 2024
, and relaxing the smoothness assumption using the analysis of
Chien and Li 2025
.

A.2
Broader Impacts

Our work studies the theoretical convergent DP guarantees of the noisy zeroth-order gradient descent algorithm for smooth problems. We believe our work is a foundational research and does not have a direct path to any negative applications.

where the last inequality is by our constraint
\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}a_{t}^{\prime}\geq\min(2R,\frac{2C\tau}{\sqrt{K}})
. Apparently, both of the equalities are attainable for some
a_{t}^{\prime}\geq 0
. We can choose such a solution of
a_{t}^{\prime}
and plug them in our bound. Additionally, for simplicity we choose
\beta_{t}=1/2
. Together we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(W_{T}|_{\bigcap_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\mathcal{G}_{t}}||W_{T}^{\prime}|_{\bigcap_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\mathcal{G}_{t}})\leq(T-\tau)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}+\frac{2\alpha c_{2}^{2}d}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\alpha\left(\min(2R,\frac{2C\tau}{\sqrt{K}})\right)^{2}d}{(T-\tau)\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(25)

Recall that
c_{2}=\eta M\xi
. When we choose
\xi\leq\frac{2\Delta}{n\eta M\sqrt{2d}}
, we have
\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}+\frac{2\alpha c_{2}^{2}d}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{2\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}
. Thus the bound can be further simplified as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(W_{T}|_{\bigcap_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\mathcal{G}_{t}}||W_{T}^{\prime}|_{\bigcap_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\mathcal{G}_{t}})\leq(T-\tau)\frac{2\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}+\frac{4\alpha\left(\min(2R,\frac{2C\tau}{\sqrt{K}})\right)^{2}d}{(T-\tau)\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq(T-\tau)\frac{2\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}+\frac{4\alpha\left(2R\right)^{2}d}{(T-\tau)\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(27)

Now we can try to choose the best
\tau
to optimize the bound. The optimal integer
\tau
should satisfy

\displaystyle T-\tau=\lceil\frac{nR\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta\eta}\rceil

(28)

Apparently, this choice is possible for sufficiently large
T\geq\lceil\frac{nR\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta\eta}\rceil
. We will discuss the bound for
T<\lceil\frac{nR\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta\eta}\rceil
later. By plugging in this choice and assuming
\lceil\frac{nR\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta\eta}\rceil=\frac{nR\sqrt{2d}}{\Delta\eta}
, our bound becomes

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha\Delta R\sqrt{2d}}{\eta n\sigma^{2}}

(29)

By RDP-DP conversion
(
Mironov 2017
)
, we can optimize
\alpha
for the
(\varepsilon,\frac{\delta}{2})
-DP guarantee, where we reserve the other
\delta/2
for the error probability
1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G})
. The resulting
\varepsilon
will be

\displaystyle\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{M\Delta R\sqrt{2d}\log(1/\delta)}{K\eta n\sigma^{2}}}\right),

(30)

where we recall that
\eta=K/M
. On the other hand, note that we still have to deal with the error probability regarding
c_{1}
. With the choice of
\eta=\frac{K}{M}
, thus we have
c=1-\frac{m}{M}
. This implies that we should choose
\epsilon=\frac{1-c^{2}}{1+c^{2}}
to ensure
c_{1}=1
while keeping
\epsilon
the smallest possible. The corresponding error probability bound can be simplified as

\displaystyle 2(T-\tau)\exp\left(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}K}{20}\right)=2\lceil\frac{Rn\sqrt{2d}}{\eta\Delta}\rceil\exp\left(-(\frac{1-c^{2}}{1+c^{2}})^{2}\frac{3K}{20}\right),

(31)

We need this error probability to be upper bounded by
\delta/2
. This is possible when we choose
K
satisfying

\displaystyle K\geq(\frac{1-c^{2}}{1+c^{2}})^{-2}\frac{20}{3}\log(\frac{4}{\delta}\lceil\frac{Rn\sqrt{2d}}{\eta\Delta}\rceil),\;\eta=\frac{K}{M}.

(32)

This completes the proof for the convergent part. For the case
T<\lceil\frac{Rn\sqrt{2d}}{\eta\Delta}\rceil
, we can simply choose
\tau=0
and then
\beta_{t}=1
, where the bound in stated in the Theorem
3.2
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha T}{2\sigma^{2}}(\frac{2\Delta}{n})^{2}.

(33)

This is exactly the RDP bound via the standard composition theorem. Together we complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.7

Lemma
.

Consider the setting
\hat{\psi}_{t},u_{t,k}
defined in this section. Assume the corresponding first order counterpart
\phi
is
c
-Lipschitz for
\ell_{i},\ell_{i}^{\prime}
being
\Delta
-Lipschitz and
M
-smooth. Then
\hat{\psi}_{t}
is
(c_{1},c_{2})
-generalized Lipschitz, where

\displaystyle c_{1}=\sqrt{1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}+c^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}},\;c_{2}=\eta M\xi,

(34)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}\sim Beta(\frac{K}{2},\frac{d-K}{2}),\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\sim Beta(\frac{K}{2},\frac{d-K}{2}).

(35)

Proof.

Note if
\ell
is
M
-smooth, then for any
w,u
we have

\displaystyle\ell(w+\xi u)

\displaystyle=\ell(w)+\xi\cdot\langle\nabla\ell\left(w\right),u\rangle+\underbrace{\left(\ell(w+\xi u)-\left(\ell(w)+\xi\cdot\langle\nabla\ell\left(w\right),u\rangle\right)\right)}_{\triangleq\Delta\left(w,u,\xi\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}M\xi^{2}}.

By the assumption that
\ell_{i},\ell_{i}^{\prime}
are
\Delta
-Lipschitz, we can safely remove the clipping. These imply that

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{t}(w)=\frac{1}{nK}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\ell_{i}(w+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}(w-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}u_{t,k}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nK}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{2\xi\left\langle\nabla\ell_{i}(w),u_{t,k}\right\rangle+\Delta\left(w,u_{t,k},\xi\right)-\Delta\left(w,-u_{t,k},\xi\right)}{2\xi}u_{t,k}

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nK}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\nabla\ell_{i}(w),u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}+\frac{\Delta\left(w,u_{t,k},\xi\right)-\Delta\left(w,-u_{t,k},\xi\right)}{2\xi}u_{t,k}

\displaystyle\|\hat{\psi}_{t}(x)-\hat{\psi}_{t}(y)\|=\|(x-y)-\eta(\hat{g}_{t}(x)-\hat{g}_{t}(y))\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\|(x-y)-\eta\left(\frac{1}{nK}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\nabla\ell_{i}(x)-\nabla\ell_{i}(y),u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\right)\|+\eta M\xi.

(40)

where (a) is due to
|\Delta\left(w,u,\xi\right)|\leq\frac{1}{2}M\xi^{2}
by

-smoothness of
\ell_{i}
,
u_{t,k}
being unit norm and triangle inequality. Next we denote
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(w)=\bar{\ell}(w)
and denote
\tilde{\eta}=\frac{\eta}{K}
. Then we can proceed as follows

\displaystyle\|(x-y)-\tilde{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)-\nabla\bar{\ell}(y),u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\|^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\|(x-y)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle x-y,u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\|^{2}+\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle x-y,u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}-\tilde{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)-\nabla\bar{\ell}(y),u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(x-y)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle x-y,u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\|^{2}+\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\phi(x)-\phi(y),u_{t,k}\right\rangle u_{t,k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k})\|x-y\|^{2}+(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k})\|\phi(x)-\phi(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}+c^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k})\|x-y\|^{2},

where
\upsilon_{k}=(\left\langle\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|},u_{t,k}\right\rangle)^{2}
,
\gamma_{k}=(\left\langle\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{\|\phi(x)-\phi(y)\|},u_{t,k}\right\rangle)^{2}
. (a) is due to the subspace of the first term being orthogonal to the subspace of the second term. (b) is due to the fact that
u_{t,k}
are orthonormal. (c) is due to the condition that
\phi
is
c
-Lipschitz. Finally, by the argument in Lemma
3.8
, we know that the sum of
\upsilon_{k},\gamma_{k}
follows beta distribution
Beta(\frac{K}{2},\frac{d-K}{2})
. Together we complete the proof.
∎

A.7
Proof of Lemma
3.8

Lemma
.

Let
A,B
be any unit vector and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}u_{k}^{T}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(V_{k}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. Denoting
\upsilon_{k}=(\left\langle A,u_{k}\right\rangle)^{2}
and
\gamma_{k}=(\left\langle B,u_{k}\right\rangle)^{2}
. Then for any
\epsilon\geq 0
and
d\geq 2K
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}+c^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}}\leq\sqrt{1-(1-c^{2})\frac{K}{d}+\epsilon(1+c^{2})\frac{K}{d}}\right)

(42)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\exp(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}dK}{12(d-K)+8\epsilon(d-2K)}).

(43)

Proof.

First observe that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}u_{k}^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{O}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_{k}e_{k}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{O}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(V_{d}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
follows the uniform distribution over all possible rotation matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for any unit vector
A
, we have

\displaystyle[\left\langle A,u_{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle A,u_{1}\right\rangle]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}[\left\langle A,Oe_{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle A,Oe_{K}\right\rangle]=[\left\langle O^{T}A,e_{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle O^{T}A,e_{K}\right\rangle].

(44)

Note that
O^{T}A
follows the distribution of
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, which implies that we have the following characterization.

\displaystyle O^{T}A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{Z}{\|Z\|},

(45)

where
Z\sim N(0,I_{d})
is a standard normal vector. As a result, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\left\langle A,u_{k}\right\rangle)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\left\langle O^{T}A,e_{k}\right\rangle)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}Z_{k}^{2}}{\|Z\|^{2}}\sim Beta(\frac{k}{2},\frac{d-k}{2}),

(46)

where
Z_{k}=\left\langle Z,e_{k}\right\rangle
. The same arguement hold for
\gamma_{k}
, where we also have
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\sim Beta(\frac{k}{2},\frac{d-k}{2})
. Next we introduce the following tail bound for the beta distribution.

Theorem A.1

(Tail bound for beta distribution, simplification of Theorem 1 in
Skorski 2023
)
.

Let
X\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta)
and
\beta\geq\alpha
. Define
v=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}
and
c=\frac{2(\beta-\alpha)}{(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha+\beta+2)}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X>\mathbb{E}X+\epsilon\right)\wedge\mathbb{P}\left(X<\mathbb{E}X-\epsilon\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(v+\frac{c}{3}\epsilon)}\right).

(47)

Note that
\mathbb{E}X=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
for
X\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta)
. As a result,
\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}=\frac{K}{d}
. First we have

\displaystyle v=\frac{\frac{K(d-K)}{4}}{(\frac{d}{2})^{2}(\frac{d}{2}+1)}=\frac{2K(d-K)}{d^{2}(d+2)}\leq\frac{2K(d-K)}{d^{3}},\;c=\frac{d-2K}{\frac{d}{2}(\frac{d}{2}+2)}=\frac{4(d-2K)}{d(d+4)}\leq\frac{4(d-2K)}{d^{2}}.

(48)

By reparametrize
\epsilon\leftarrow\epsilon\frac{K}{d}
in Theorem
A.1
, we can simplify the bound as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{K}{d}\right)\wedge\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}<(1-\epsilon)\frac{K}{d}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\frac{K^{2}}{d^{2}}}{2(v+\frac{cK}{3d}\epsilon)}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\frac{K^{2}}{d^{2}}}{2(\frac{2K(d-K)}{d^{3}}+\frac{K}{3d}\frac{4(d-2K)}{d^{2}}\epsilon)}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}Kd}{12(d-K)+8(d-2K)\epsilon)}\right).

(50)

Note that the same bound hold for
\gamma
part. Finally, by leveraging the lower tail bound for
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upsilon_{k}
, upper tail bound for
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}
and applying Boole’s inequality we complete the proof.
∎

A.8
Proof of Lemma
3.9

Lemma

(Forward Wasserstain distance tracking)
.

Let
w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime}
be the process defined in (
3
) and (
5
).

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\leq\min(2R,\frac{2\eta\Delta t}{\sqrt{K}})

(51)

Proof.

We will start with the definition of Wasserstain distance. Let
\nu_{t},\nu_{t}^{\prime}
be the distribution of
w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime}
respectively.

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu_{t},\nu_{t}^{\prime})}\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma}\|w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}\|.

(52)

Note that the infimum is taking over all possible coupling between two stochastic processes
w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime}
. Hence, choosing any specific coupling will lead to an upper bound. We choose a specific coupling such that all the noise
G_{t}
and
G_{t,k}
are identical to
G_{t}^{\prime}
and
G_{t,k}^{\prime}
, as well as the
u_{t,k}=u_{t,k}^{\prime}
. Let us denote such a coupling as
\gamma^{\star}
, then we have

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}\|

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{t-1}-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1};u_{t,k})-w_{t-1}^{\prime}+\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1}^{\prime};u_{t,k})\|

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{t-1}-w_{t-1}^{\prime}\|+\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1};u_{t,k})-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1}^{\prime};u_{t,k})\|.

(55)

The first term will contribute to a recursive argument. The second term can be further upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\|\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1};u_{t,k})-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t-1}^{\prime};u_{t,k})\|^{2}

(56)

\displaystyle=(\frac{\eta}{K})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}(w_{t-1}+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}(w_{t-1}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi};\Delta\right)

(57)

\displaystyle-\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}(w_{t-1}^{\prime}+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}(w_{t-1}^{\prime}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi};\Delta\right)u_{t,k}\|^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{\eta}{K})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(2\Delta)^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}(2\Delta)^{2}}{K}.

(59)

Note that the second equality and last inequality are due to our choice that
u_{t,k}
are orthonormal. As a result, we have

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}\|

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{t-1}-w_{t-1}^{\prime}\|+\frac{2\eta\Delta}{\sqrt{K}}

(61)

\displaystyle\cdots\leq\esssup_{(w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime})\sim\gamma^{\star}}\|w_{0}-w_{0}^{\prime}\|+\frac{2\eta\Delta t}{\sqrt{K}}=\frac{2\eta\Delta t}{\sqrt{K}},

(62)

where the last step is due to the fact that both processes
w_{t},w_{t}^{\prime}
have the same initialization
w_{0}=w_{0}^{\prime}
. Together we complete the proof.
∎

A.9
Minibatch extensions

Now, we discuss the minibatch generalizations of Noisy-ZOGD (Theorem
3.2
), which we called the corresponding algorithm Noisy-ZOSGD (under subsampling without replacement).

Subsampling without replacement.
For this strategy, the Noisy-ZOSGD update is defined as follows.

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{R}}\left[w_{t}-\frac{\eta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t};u_{t,k},B_{t})+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}G_{t,k}^{(1)}u_{t,k}+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{d}}G_{t}^{(2)}\right],

(63)

\displaystyle\text{where }\hat{g}_{t}(w;u,B)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in B}\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}(w+\xi u)-\ell_{i}(w-\xi u)}{2\xi};\Delta\right)u.

(64)

Here, the minibatch
B
is of size
b
and is sampled from the full index set
[n]
without replacement independently for each time step. The rest setting is the same as the full batch cases, which can be viewed as the special case of
B=[n]
. Before we introduce the corresponding privacy guarantee, we first need to introduce the Sampled Gaussian Mechanism
(
Mironov et al. 2019
)
.

Definition A.2

(Rényi divergence of Sampled Gaussian Mechanism)
.

For any
\alpha>1
, mixing probability
q\in(0,1)
and noise parameter
\sigma>0
, define

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(q,\sigma)=D_{\alpha}(N(0,\sigma^{2})||(1-q)N(0,\sigma^{2})+qN(1,\sigma^{2})).

(65)

Note that
S_{\alpha}
can be computed in practice with a numerically stable procedure for precise computation
Mironov 2017
. Now we are ready to state the result for subsampling without replacement.

Theorem A.3

(RDP guarantee of Noisy-ZOSGD)
.

Assume
\ell(\cdot,x)
are
M
-smooth and
\Delta
-Lipschitz for the first argument and all possible data
x
. The Noisy-ZOSGD under subsampling without replacement update (
63
) is
(\alpha,\varepsilon(\alpha))
-RDP for any
\alpha>1,d\geq 2K,\epsilon\geq 0
on some high probability event
\mathcal{G}
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\alpha)\leq\min_{\tau,\beta_{t},a_{t}}\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\left(KS_{\alpha}(\frac{b}{n},\frac{\sqrt{K\beta_{t}}\sigma b}{2\Delta})+\frac{\alpha a_{t}^{2}d}{2\eta^{2}(1-\beta_{t})\sigma^{2}}\right),\;s.t.\;\tau\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\},\;\beta_{t}\in[0,1],\;

\displaystyle a_{t},z_{t}\geq 0\;\forall t\geq\tau,\;z_{t-1}=c_{1}^{-1}(z_{t}+a_{t}-c_{2}),\;z_{T}=0,\;z_{\tau}\geq\min(2R,\frac{2\eta\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{K}}),\;c=1+\frac{\eta M}{K},

\displaystyle c_{1}=\sqrt{1-(1-c^{2})\frac{K}{d}+\epsilon(1+c^{2})\frac{K}{d}},\;c_{2}=\eta M\xi,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}\right)\geq 1-2(T-\tau^{\star})\exp\left(-\frac{3\epsilon^{2}dK}{12(d-K)+8\epsilon(d-2K)}\right),

where
\tau^{\star}
is the resulting
\tau
of the optimization above. If
\ell
is also convex and choose
\eta\leq 2K/M
, we have
c=1
. If
\ell
is also
m
-strongly convex and choose
\eta\leq K/M
, we have

.

The proof mainly combines the proof of shifted Rényi divergence analysis of the first-order counterpart
Chien and Li 2025
;
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
and the proof of our Theorem
3.2
. Before we state our proof, let us first introduce a technical lemma before we introduce our proof. Note that the original Sampled Gaussian Mechanism is defined in one dimension.
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
extends this notion to a higher dimension by identifying the worst-case scenario therein. Alternatively, one can directly work with high-dimensional Sampled Gaussian Mechanism as in
Altschuler et al. 2024
.

Lemma A.4

(Extrema of Sampled Gaussian Mechanism, Lemma 2.11 in
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
)
.

For any
\alpha>1
,
q\in(0,1)
and the noise parameter
\sigma>0
, dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
and radius

,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(B_{R})}D_{\alpha}(N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})||(1-q)N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})+q(N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\ast\mu))=S_{\alpha}(q,\sigma/R),

(66)

where
\mathcal{P}(B_{R})
denotes set of all Borel probability distributions over the
\ell_{2}
ball of radius

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In practice,

is computed via numerical integral for the tightest possible privacy accounting. Nevertheless, to have a better understanding of the quantity, Lemma 2.12 in
Altschuler and Talwar 2022
shows that it is upper bounded by
2\alpha q^{2}/\sigma^{2}
for some regime of
(\alpha,\sigma,q)
. In what follows, we keep the notation of
S_{\alpha}(q,\sigma)
but is useful to keep this simplified upper bound in mind.

Now we are ready to state our proof.

Proof.

We start with the same proof as in those of Theorem
3.2
until equation (

3.2
, and the only different part is the way of upper bounding the first term (

For the first term, we first observe that the sensitivity
m_{t,k}
of each direction

\displaystyle m_{t,k}:=

(67)

\displaystyle\|\frac{\eta}{bK}\left(\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}^{\prime}(w+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(w-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi};\Delta\right)-\mathsf{clip}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}^{\prime}(w+\xi u_{t,k})-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(w-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi};\Delta\right)\right)u_{t,k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\eta\Delta}{bK}.

(68)

As a result, we can bound the first term (

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(G_{\tau:T-1,1:K}||G_{\tau:T-1,1:K}^{\prime})

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\sup_{g_{\tau:t-1,1:K}}D_{\alpha}(G_{t,1:K}|_{G_{\tau:t-1,1:K}=g_{\tau:t-1,1:K}}||G_{t,1:K}^{\prime}|_{G^{\prime}_{\tau:t-1,1:K}=g_{\tau:t-1,1:K}}),

(70)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}K\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\sup_{g_{\tau:t-1,1}}D_{\alpha}(G_{t,1:K}|_{G_{\tau:t-1,1:K}=g_{\tau:t-1,1:K}}||G_{t,1:K}^{\prime}|_{G^{\prime}_{\tau:t-1,1}=g_{\tau:t-1,1}}),

(71)

\displaystyle=K\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}D_{\alpha}(N(0,\frac{\eta^{2}}{K}\beta_{t}\sigma^{2}I_{K})||(1-\frac{b}{n})N(0,\frac{\eta^{2}}{K}\beta_{t}\sigma^{2}I_{K})+\frac{b}{n}N(m_{t,1},\frac{\eta^{2}}{K}\beta_{t}\sigma^{2}I_{K}))

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}KS_{\alpha}(\frac{b}{n},\frac{\sqrt{K\beta_{t}}\sigma b}{2\Delta}),

(73)

where (a) is again by composition theorem and by the i.i.d. property of the Gaussian noise
G_{t,k}
; the rest analysis we use the fact that
\|m_{t,1}\|\leq\frac{2\eta\Delta}{bK}
almost surely. Hence, one can apply Lemma
A.4
for the last inequality. Together we complete the proof.
∎

Remark.
The analysis above can be slightly simplified to eliminate the effect of
K
. Thanks to our choice of orthonormal
u_{t,k}
, we can instead leverage the standard Gaussian mechanism result in the subspace spanned by
u_{t,k}
. For each time step
t
, the corresponding sensitivity is
\frac{2\eta\Delta}{b\sqrt{K}}
(converting an
\ell_{\infty}
norm to
\ell_{2}
norm will loose a
\sqrt{K}
factor for
K
-dimensional subspace), and thus, following the similar analysis as above leads to a bound

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(G_{\tau:T-1,1:K}||G_{\tau:T-1,1:K}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}S_{\alpha}(\frac{b}{n},\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma b}{2\Delta}).

(74)

In practice, one can choose the smaller bound for computing the final privacy loss. Note that when
b=n
,
S_{\alpha}(1,\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma b}{2\Delta})=\frac{\alpha}{2}(\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma b})^{2}=K\frac{\alpha}{2}(\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{K\beta_{t}}\sigma b})^{2}=KS_{\alpha}(1,\frac{\sqrt{K\beta_{t}}\sigma b}{2\Delta})
, where the two approach coincide. For simplicity, we just choose the first approach in the theorems.

A.10
Utility analysis

The analysis will mainly follow the proof of DPZero
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. The purpose of this section is to identify the fair tradeoff scaling between scalar and vector Gaussian noise in Noisy-ZOGD (
3
). We repeat the proof here merely for self-containedness. As a direct consequence, we also report the resulting privacy-utility tradeoff at the end of the section.

We start by introducing the necessary assumptions for the analysis of DPZero. 1) The loss function is
L
-Lipschitz and
M
-smooth. The averaged loss function is twice differentiable with
-H\preceq\nabla^{2}L(w;\mathcal{D})\preceq H
for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its minimum is finite. Here,
0\preceq H
is a real-valued
d
by
d
matrix such that
\|H\|_{2}\leq M
and
Tr(H)\leq r\|H\|_{2}
for for some
r
as the effective rank or the intrinsic dimension of the problem. These assumptions are exactly the Assumption 3.5 in
Zhang et al. 2023
.

For simplicity, let us denote
\bar{\ell}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(x)
be the total loss function that we study. By
M
-smoothness of
\bar{\ell}(x)
, we know that for any unit vector
u
,

\displaystyle\frac{|\bar{\ell}(x+\xi u)-\bar{\ell}(x-\xi u)|}{2\xi}\leq|\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(x),u\right\rangle|+\frac{1}{2}M\xi.

(75)

Next, the Lemma C.1 in
Zhang et al. 2023
provides a high probability bound for
|\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(x),u\right\rangle|
, where
u
is a unit vector uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Note that one can also use bound for beta distribution as in Lemma
3.8
, but we choose to align as much as possible to DPZero for simplicity here. The results show that for any
C_{0}\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\left\langle u,\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)\right\rangle|\geq C_{0}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{2\pi}\exp(-\frac{dC_{0}^{2}}{8\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)\|^{2}}).

(76)

As a result, by choosing the clipping
\Delta=C_{0}+\frac{1}{2}M\xi
we can safely remove the clipping operator with high probability after applying Boole’s inequality over
nTK
terms. Let us denote
Q_{t}
the event that clipping does not happen at iteration
t
and
Q
the event that clipping does not happen at all
T
iteration. This will be dealt in the end.

Now let us continue with the analysis. By Taylor’s theorem and the low-rank assumption that
\forall x,-H\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}\bar{\ell}(x)\preccurlyeq H
for some positive semi-definite matrix with
Tr(H)\leq rM
and
\|H\|_{2}\leq M
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})\leq\bar{\ell}(w_{t})+\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(x_{t}),w_{t+1}-w_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(w_{t+1}-w_{t})^{T}H(w_{t+1}-w_{t}).

(77)

By taking expectation across all randomness before iteration
t
(including the random direction
u
and the gaussian noise
G
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q_{t}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\leq(t-1)}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]-\eta\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})^{T}H\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]

(78)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma_{1,t}^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}|Q_{t}]+\frac{\sigma_{2,t}^{2}}{2}Tr(H).

(79)

Note that all the cross terms are
0
as
G_{t,k},G_{t}
are zero mean Gaussian independent of all the other randomness. Now, we analyze each term separately. Let us analyze the quadratic terms first.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})^{T}H\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}\right)^{2}u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}|Q_{t}]

(80)

This is due to the fact that
u_{t,k}\perp u_{t,k^{\prime}}
by definition. Then since
0\preccurlyeq H
, by the law of total probability we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}\right)^{2}u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}]

(81)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}\right)^{2}u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}|Q_{t}]\mathbb{P}(Q_{t}).

(82)

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})^{T}H\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}\right)^{2}u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}]

(83)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(2(\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t}),u_{t,k}\right\rangle)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\xi^{2}\right)u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}]

(84)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\left(2(\left\langle\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t}),u_{t,k}\right\rangle)^{2}\right)u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}]+\frac{1}{2d}M^{2}\xi^{2}Tr(H)\right)

(85)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{2d}{d^{2}(d+2)}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}(2\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}H\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})+\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}Tr(H))+\frac{1}{2d}M^{2}\xi^{2}Tr(H)\right)

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{2(2+r)M}{d(d+2)}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{2d}M^{3}\xi^{2}r\right)

(87)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(2+r)M}{d(d+2)\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]+\frac{M^{3}\xi^{2}r}{2d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}.

(88)

where (a) is due to
M
-smoothness and the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
. (b) and (c) are due to Lemma C.1 in
Zhang et al. 2023
.

Similarly we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[u_{t,k}^{T}Hu_{t,k}|Q_{t}]\leq\frac{rMK}{d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}.

(89)

For the inner-product term, let us denote
u^{\prime}=\sqrt{d}u
and
\xi^{\prime}=\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{d}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\hat{g}_{t}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]=\frac{1}{K\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi u_{t,k})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi u_{t,k})}{2\xi}u_{t,k}^{\prime}|Q_{t}]

(90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Kd}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})}{2\xi^{\prime}}u_{t,k}^{\prime}|Q_{t}].

(91)

Following the similar analysis as in
Zhang et al. 2023
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})}{2\xi^{\prime}}u_{t,k}^{\prime}|Q_{t}]

(92)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]}{2\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}}{8\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})}{2\xi^{\prime}}u_{t,k}^{\prime}|\bar{Q}_{t}]\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}

(93)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]}{2\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}}{8\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{L^{2}d\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})},

(94)

where the last inequality is due to the following analysis. Note that by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the fact that
\bar{\ell}
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})^{T}\frac{\bar{\ell}(w_{t}+\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t}-\xi^{\prime}u_{t,k}^{\prime})}{2\xi^{\prime}}u_{t,k}^{\prime}\leq L^{2}\|u_{t,k}^{\prime}\|^{2}=L^{2}d.

(95)

Combining all we have so far leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q_{t}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\leq(t-1)}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]-\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]}{2\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}}{8\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}-\frac{L^{2}d\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}\right)

(96)

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2(2+r)M}{d(d+2)\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]+\frac{M^{3}\xi^{2}r}{2d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{1,t}^{2}rMK}{2d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}+\frac{\sigma_{2,t}^{2}rM}{2}

(97)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\leq(t-1)}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})|Q_{t}]-\frac{\eta}{2d}(1-\frac{\eta 2(2+r)M}{d})\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}+\frac{\eta M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{2}}{8\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}+\frac{\eta L^{2}\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}

(98)

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta^{2}M^{3}\xi^{2}r}{4d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}+\frac{\sigma_{1,t}^{2}rMK}{2d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}+\frac{\sigma_{2,t}^{2}rM}{2}

(99)

Now we choose
\eta=\frac{K}{M}\leq\frac{d}{4(2+r)M}
, where the inequality hold if
K\leq\frac{d}{4(2+r)}
. This results in
(1-\frac{\eta 2(2+r)M}{d})\geq 1/2
and
\frac{2\eta Mr}{d^{2}}<1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})}{\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q_{t}]\right)+M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}+4L^{2}d\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}_{t})

(100)

\displaystyle+\frac{2drM}{\eta}\left(\sigma_{1,t}^{2}\frac{K}{d}+\sigma_{2,t}^{2}\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})\right).

(101)

If we further assume that
|\bar{\ell}(w)|\leq B
for any
w
. Then following the same analysis of
Zhang et al. 2023
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q_{t}]\mathbb{P}(Q_{t})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q]\mathbb{P}(Q)+2B\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q}).

(102)

As a result we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{t})-\bar{\ell}(w_{t+1})|Q]\right)+M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})

(103)

\displaystyle+\frac{2drM}{\eta}\left(\sigma_{1,t}^{2}\frac{K}{d}+\sigma_{2,t}^{2}\right).

(104)

By averaging over
T
iteration, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{\tau})\|^{2}]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]

(105)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)+M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})+\frac{2drM}{\eta}\left(\sigma_{1,t}^{2}\frac{K}{d}+\sigma_{2,t}^{2}\right),

(106)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})\leq 2\sqrt{2\pi}nKT\exp(-\frac{dC_{0}^{2}}{8\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)\|^{2}}).

(107)

If we plug in our parametrization of
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{K}(1-\beta)\sigma^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{d}\beta\sigma^{2}
for any
\beta\in[0,1]
as in the privacy analysis, we further have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{\tau})\|^{2}]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(w_{t})\|^{2}]

(108)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)+M^{2}\xi^{2}d^{3}+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})+2\eta rM\sigma^{2},

(109)

Apparently, this bound remains unchanged for any
\beta\in[0,1]
and
K\geq 1
, and this bound exactly matches the bound derived in
Zhang et al. 2023
for DPZero with the scalar noise case. Thus our parametrization
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{K}(1-\beta)\sigma^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{d}\beta\sigma^{2}
indeed provides the same utility bound.

A.10.1
The privacy-utility trade-off in close-form

We may plug in the value of
\sigma^{2}
according to our privacy bound to achieve
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantee, which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})+2\eta rM\sigma^{2}

(110)

\displaystyle=\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})+\frac{64rMR\sqrt{d}\Delta\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}.

(111)

Next we plug in the expression of
\mathbb{P}(\bar{Q})
, which gives the following upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)+(4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})2\sqrt{2\pi}nKT\exp(-\frac{d\Delta^{2}}{8\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)\|^{2}})

(112)

\displaystyle+\frac{64rMR\sqrt{d}\Delta\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}.

(113)

Recall that under the case
\xi\rightarrow 0
, we have
C_{0}=\Delta
, the zeroth order clipping value. Now we can optimize the bound with respect to
\Delta
, where the tightest analysis is related to W Lambert function and does not have close-form. We instead choose a sub-optimal
\Delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\log((4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})2\sqrt{2\pi}nKT)}
, where we recall that
\|\nabla\bar{\ell}(x)\|\leq L
due to the
L
-Lipschitz assumption made by
Zhang et al. 2023
. This choice makes the second term above at scale
O(1)
. So, the dominant term will be the last one, which is

\displaystyle\frac{64rMR\sqrt{d}\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\log((4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})2\sqrt{2\pi}nKT)}=O(\frac{rR\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\log(\frac{dnKT}{\eta})}).

(114)

Finally, note that we can choose
\eta=\frac{K}{M}
whenever
K\leq\frac{d}{8(2+r)}
. On the other hand, we also need
K=\Omega(\log(T/\delta))
. This implies that choosing
T=O(d)
is valid. Plug-in the choice
K=\Theta(\log(T/\delta))=\Theta(\log(d/\delta))
and
T=\Theta(d)
, we now have

\displaystyle\frac{64rMR\sqrt{d}\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\log((4L^{2}d+\frac{8Bd}{\eta})2\sqrt{2\pi}nKT)}=O(\frac{rR\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\log(d^{2}n)}).

(115)

Recall that now the first term will be

\displaystyle\frac{4d\mathbb{P}(Q)}{T\eta}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\leq t}[\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w_{T})|Q]\right)=O(\frac{1}{\log(d/\delta)}\left(\bar{\ell}(w_{0})-\bar{\ell}(w^{\star})\right)).

(116)

We can see that the third term is the dominant one, where it is of scale

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{rR\log(2/\delta)}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\log(nd)}\right)=O(r\sqrt{\log(d)}),

(117)

where the RHS only cares about the asymptotic of
r
and
d
. This bound is better than DPZero’s bound
O\left(\log(d)\sqrt{r}\right)
whenever
r=o(\sqrt{\log(d)})
. Also note that we will use roughly
K=O(\log(d/\delta))
zeroth-order gradients per iteration. It is possible to derive a better dependency with respect to
r,d
if one jointly optimizes the choice of
K,T
for all three terms simultaneously. We leave such an effort as an interesting future work.

Appendix B
Discussion on i.i.d. updates

In this section, we discuss and analyze the scenario that the update directions
\left\{u_{t,k}\right\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}
in (
3
) are chosen i.i.d. from
\mathsf{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d}\right)
. We prove concentration lemmas similar but looser to Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.8
, which can then be plugged in the proof of Theorem
3.2
and obtain a convergent Rényi DP bound. We begin with a few concentration bounds that would be useful in the proof.

Definition B.1

(
Jin et al. 2019
)
.

A random vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\sigma
-
norm-SubGaussian
, if

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert u-\mathbb{E}\left[u\right]\right\rVert\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

Lemma B.2
.

[Corollary 7 of
Jin et al. 2019
]
Let
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{K}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\sigma
-norm-subGaussian. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{K\sigma^{2}\log\left(\frac{2d}{\delta}\right)}.

(118)

Note that Lemma
B.2
is slightly modified from Corollary 7 of
Jin et al. 2019
, where we specify the absolute constant (which is
1
) by following the analysis of Lemma 5.5 of
Vershynin 2010
in order to facilitate our non-asymptotic bounds.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
B_{k}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathsf{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
. Then it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{k}\geq\left(0.15-\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{K})\right)_{+}\cdot\frac{K}{20\left(d+1+\sqrt{d-2}\right)}\right\}\geq 1-\delta,

where
(\cdot)_{+}
denotes
\max(\cdot,0)
.

Proof.

Let
B\sim\mathsf{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
. Then
B\overset{\text{(d)}}{=}\frac{W_{1}}{W_{1}+W_{2}}
where
W_{1}\sim\chi^{2}\left(1\right)
and
W_{2}\sim\chi^{2}\left(d-1\right)
are independent. Since
W_{1}\overset{\text{(d)}}{=}Z^{2}
for
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, by the lower bound of the Q-function, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left\{W_{1}\geq\kappa_{1}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert Z\right\rvert\geq\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\right\}=2\cdot Q\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\right)\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}}{1+\kappa_{1}}\right)\frac{e^{-\kappa_{1}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

On the other hand, according to the quantile upper bound on the
\chi^{2}
random variables (see, for instance,
(
Inglot 2010
, Theorem A)
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{W_{2}\leq(d-2)+2\log\left(1/\beta\right)+2\sqrt{(d-2)\log\left(1/\beta\right)}\right\}\geq 1-\beta.

These together imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{B\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}+(d-2)+2\log\left(1/\beta\right)+2\sqrt{(d-2)\log\left(1/\beta\right)}}\right\}

(119)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{W_{1}}{W_{1}+W_{2}}\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}+(d-2)+2\log\left(1/\beta\right)+2\sqrt{(d-2)\log\left(1/\beta\right)}}\right\}

(120)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\beta)\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}}{1+\kappa_{1}}\right)\frac{e^{-\kappa_{1}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\triangleq P_{e}.

(121)

Next, write
G_{k}\triangleq\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{B_{k}\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}+(d-2)+2\log\left(1/\beta\right)+2\sqrt{(d-2)\log\left(1/\beta\right)}}\right\}}

so that
\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}\right]\geq P_{e}
and
G_{k}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
(i.e.,
G_{k}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathsf{Ber}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}\right]\right)
). Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{k}\geq\tau\cdot\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}+(d-2)+2\log\left(1/\beta\right)+2\sqrt{(d-2)\log\left(1/\beta\right)}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k}G_{k}<\tau\right\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-e^{-2K\left(P_{e}-\tau/K\right)^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to the following Hoeffding’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k}G_{k}\leq\tau\right\}\leq e^{-2K\left(\mathbb{E}[G_{1}]-\tau/K\right)^{2}}.

Setting
\tau=K\cdot\left(P_{e}-\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2K}\right)_{+}
,
\beta=1/e
, and
\kappa_{1}=1
yields

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{k}\geq\left(0.15-\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{K})\right)_{+}\cdot\frac{K}{20\left(d+1+\sqrt{d-2}\right)}\right\}\geq 1-\delta.

∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{K}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathsf{\mathsf{Unif}}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)
. Then, it holds that, with probability at least
1-\beta
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert v-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle v,u_{k}\rangle u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

(122)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left(\frac{K}{2d+6}\log\left(\frac{4d}{\delta}\right)+\left(1-\left(0.15-\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{K})\right)_{+}\cdot\frac{K}{20\left(d+2+\sqrt{d-1}\right)}\right)^{2}\right).

(123)

Proof.

By the rotational invariance of
u_{k}
, without loss of generality, we assume
v=R\cdot e_{1}
for some
R>0
and let

u_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(z^{k}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}z^{(k)}_{1}\\
z^{(k)}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
z^{(k)}_{d}\end{bmatrix},

for
k=1,...,K
.

Then

\langle v,u_{k}\rangle u_{k}=\frac{R}{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}\\
z^{(k)}_{1}\cdot z^{(k)}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
z^{(k)}_{1}\cdot z^{(k)}_{d}\end{bmatrix}\triangleq w_{k}+w^{\perp}_{k},

where

w_{k}\triangleq\frac{R}{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}

and

w^{\perp}_{k}\triangleq\frac{R}{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
z^{(k)}_{1}\cdot z^{(k)}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
z^{(k)}_{1}\cdot z^{(k)}_{d}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\langle w_{k},w^{\perp}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle=0
and
\langle v,w^{\perp}_{k}\rangle=0
for any
k,k^{\prime}\in[K]
, we can write

\displaystyle\left\lVert v-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle v,u_{k}\rangle u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\lVert v-\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{\text{(a)}}+\underbrace{\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}w^{\perp}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{\text{(b)}}.

(124)

Next, we bound each term separately.

Bounding (a).

Observe that
(a)
can be written as

(a)

\displaystyle=R^{2}\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}}{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}

(125)

\displaystyle\triangleq R^{2}\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{k}\right)^{2},

(126)

where

B_{k}\triangleq\frac{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}}{\left(z^{(k)}_{1}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(z^{(k)}_{d}\right)^{2}}

follows a
\mathsf{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
distribution. Applying Lemma
B.3
, we conclude that, with probability at least
1-\delta_{a}

(a)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert v-\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\left(1-\sum_{k}B_{k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\cdot\left(1-\left(0.15-\frac{\log(1/\delta_{a})}{K})\right)_{+}\frac{K}{20\left(d+2+\sqrt{d-1}\right)}\right)^{2},

where
(\cdot)_{+}
denotes
\max(\cdot,0)
.

Bounding (b).

To bound (b), we first show that
w^{\perp}_{k}
is norm-sub-Gaussian and then apply Hoeffding-type inequalities.

Let

w^{\perp}\triangleq\frac{R}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
z_{1}z_{2}\\
\vdots\\
z_{1}z_{d}\end{bmatrix},

where
z_{j}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for
j\in[d]
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert w^{\perp}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=R^{2}\frac{z_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{d}z_{j}^{2}\right)}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{2}\right)^{2}}\leq R^{2}\frac{z_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{2}\right)}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{2}\right)^{2}}=r^{2}\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{2}}\triangleq B,

where
B\sim\mathsf{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
. As a result, by
Marchal and Arbel 2017
,
w^{\perp}
is
\frac{1}{4(1/2+d/2+1)}=\frac{1}{2d+6}
norm-sub-Gaussian, so applying Lemma
B.2
yields

\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}w^{\perp}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\frac{K}{2d+6}\log\left(\frac{2d}{\delta_{b}}\right),

with probability at least
1-\delta_{b}
.

Combining the upper bound on (a) and (b), we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert v-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle v,u_{k}\rangle u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

(127)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left(\frac{K}{2d+6}\log\left(\frac{4d}{\delta}\right)+\left(1-\left(0.15-\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{K})\right)_{+}\cdot\frac{K}{20\left(d+2+\sqrt{d-1}\right)}\right)^{2}\right).

(128)

∎

3.7

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\Bigg(\left(\frac{K}{2d+6}\log\left(\frac{4d}{\delta}\right)+\left(1-\left(0.15-\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{K})\right)_{+}\cdot\frac{K}{20\left(d+2+\sqrt{d-1}\right)}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}+

\displaystyle c^{2}\left(\frac{K}{2d+6}\log\left(\frac{4d}{\delta}\right)\right)^{1/2}\Bigg)

where (a) follows from the triangular inequality, (b) holds due to Lemma
B.4
the norm-sub-Gaussian property of

, and (c) follows from the Lipschitzness of

. This yields a concentration bound on the generalized Lipschitzness of

Discussion.

Compared to orthonormal update directions (as in Theorem
3.2
), using i.i.d. update rules not only complicates the analysis significantly but also results in a substantially larger generalized Lipschitz constant
c_{1}
. In fact, even when the function
\phi
has a small Lipschitz constant, the corresponding
c_{1}
cannot be reduced below 1. This increase in
c_{1}
stems from two main factors. First, the cross terms in the decomposition of (
129
) are no longer orthogonal and thus do not vanish, requiring the use of the triangle inequality to bound them. Second, the term
\sum_{k}\langle x-y,u_{t,k}\rangle u_{t,k}
no longer admits a closed-form density, forcing the analysis to rely on general concentration inequalities, which can potentially be loose. While the bound we present may not yield the tightest possible constant, we believe that for i.i.d. updates, the Lipschitz constant of
\Psi_{t}
(i.e.,
c_{1}
in Theorem
3.2
) is fundamentally lower-bounded by 1
