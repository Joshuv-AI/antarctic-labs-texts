Integrated expression profiling reveals molecular crosstalk between CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR), and BANK1 in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Seyyedha Abbas, Fizzah Abid, Tahir Mehmood, Sadia Fatima, Janeen H Trembley, Rubina Nazli, Hafsa Aziz, Ali Abusharha, Tayyaba Afsar, Suhail Razak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common malignancy characterised by molecular heterogeneity and poor prognosis. The purpose of this study was to determine differences in the expression of Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR), Fatty Acid Synthase (FASN), FKBP12-rapamycin-associated protein (FRAP; mTOR), and B-cell scaffold protein with ankyrin repeats 1 (BANK1) between HCC patients and controls. A total of 200 blood samples (100 from HCC patients and 100 from controls) were analysed using Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR), with Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) used as an internal control. The results demonstrated significant upregulation of FASN and FRAP (mTOR) (p < 0.0001) and downregulation of CFTR and BANK1 (p < 0.0001), suggesting potential oncogenic and tumour-suppressive roles, respectively. Gene expression levels also differed significantly across Stages I, II, III, and IV. The Ct values for BANK1 were higher than those for CFTR, FASN, and FRAP (mTOR). Because the expression measurements for the four genes were obtained from blood rather than tumour parenchyma, these findings were not interpreted as direct evidence of gene expression within tumour tissue. Survival analysis using a public dataset revealed that dysregulation of these genes was associated with lower survival rates. In silico pathway analysis demonstrated complex interactions among the four genes, linking them to metabolic regulation, immune signalling, and cellular proliferation pathways. Overall, the integrated network involving CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR), and BANK1 may have potential as a novel prognostic marker by reflecting combined metabolic and immune pathway activity. A combined four-gene logistic regression model demonstrated promising discriminative performance in the present cohort (AUC = 0.89). Introduction

HCC is a primary liver malignancy. In 2022, HCC accounted for approximately 75–85% of newly emerging cancer cases, making it the sixth most diagnosed cancer that year1. Its incidence is on the rise globally and is expected to further increase, mainly in the low- and middle-income countries1,2. Despite advances in various surveillance and therapeutic strategies, the majority of patients with HCC are found at an advanced stage, which limits curative options and results in poor overall survival3–5.

Numerous established risk factors contribute to the development of HCC, including obesity, smoking, high body mass index (BMI), viral hepatitis, MALFD, alcohol consumption, aflatoxin exposure, advancing age, family history of liver disease, and sedentary lifestyles6,7. The challenge of late diagnosis further exacerbates mortality rates, as HCC often remains asymptomatic until it reaches advanced stages. This characteristic highlights the highly heterogeneous nature of HCC at both molecular and clinical levels8.

In exploring these molecular mechanisms, the CFTR gene—located on chromosome 7q31.2—encodes a chloride and bicarbonate channel that is a member of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family9. While the CFTR gene has been extensively studied in the context of cystic fibrosis, emerging evidence suggests that it also plays a broader role in inflammation and bile secretion10. Dysregulation of CFTR may also influence epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and activate oncogenic signalling pathways, including NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin, suggesting a broader role in hepatocarcinogenesis beyond ion transport10,11. In-silco analysis revealed that CFTR expression was downregulated in HCC12. Moreover, identifying key molecular drivers and regulatory networks involved in HCC pathogenesis is essential for the development of novel biomarkers and targeted therapies. In this context, investigating the interplay between CFTR and metabolic regulators such as FASN, BANK1 and FRAP, along with normalization against the housekeeping gene GAPDH, may offer critical insights into how CFTR dysregulation contributes to HCC through metabolic and signaling pathways.

One of the key enzymes involved in cancer metabolism is FASN that lead to de novo lipogenesis. FASN expression is upregulated in liver tumors. The transcription factor involved in denovo lipogenesis in HCC binds to and transactivates FASN, which leads to HCC progression13. Moreover, FASN stimulates the accumulation of lipids and provides the necessary building blocks to the rapidly dividing cancer cells14,15. Studies suggest that FASN is an oncogene that facilitates tumor growth and survival and therefore has a therapeutic potential in cancer. Since these lipids are built up by fatty acids, FASN has become a promising oncological therapeutic target16. On the other hand, FRAP (mTOR) is a conserved serine/threonine kinase encoding gene that is a central regulator of cell growth, proliferation, metabolism and survival. FRAP (mTOR) exists in two distinct multiprotein complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC217. Operating through mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes, FRAP (mTOR) coordinates protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and cytoskeletal organization18. FRAP (mTORC1 and mTORC2) expression has been deregulated in HCC19. FRAP (mTOR) is involved in HCC progression20,21. Moreover, FRAP (mTOR) signaling is a critical step in liver tumor development and progression20,22. Its activation is largely driven by upstream growth factor signalling rather than genetic mutations, and it further promotes tumour progression by enhancing lipogenesis through interacting with metabolic enzymes such as FASN23.

On the other hand, studies related to BANK1 have not been reported regarding HCC. The BANK1, primarily expressed in B cells, has recently emerged as a potential modulator of tumour-related immune responses. In BHn subpopulation, the BANK1 helps in recognizing tumor associated antigens to mount an immune response against Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). Reduced BANK1 expression could weaken B-cell activation, leading to tumor progression24. To ensure accurate assessment of gene expression levels, GAPDH was used as an internal control due to its consistent and ubiquitous expression across various tissues, including liver samples from healthy and diseased individuals25.

In previous studies, the expression pattern of CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1 have been linked up with different liver cancer types. However, factors including genetic background play a pivotal role in regulation of gene expression26,27. To verify dysregulations of the expression of CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1 in our population, we carried out blood-based expression analysis, analysed the clinicopathological factors, and biological validation including co-relation with serum Alpha-fetoproteins (AFP) levels. We therefore hypothesised that coordinated dysregulation of these four-gene set reflects a combined metabolic-proliferative and immune-signalling axis in HCC, and that its joint diagnostic value would exceed that of any single gene considered alone. Moreover, there exists a significant gap in knowledge regarding CFTR gene expression at different stages, and their associations of CFTR with clinicopathological characteristics in HCC, which is also the focus of the current study.

Methods

Sample selection

Blood samples of 100 patients and 100 controls were collected. Under the inclusion criteria, samples from HCC patients and controls were taken. The sample size for the study was confirmed through G-Power tool analysis (downloaded from https://gpower.hhu.de/). Before obtaining the samples, ethical clearance was obtained from the hospital’s ethics committee review board. Most of the samples were collected from Nori Hospital, Islamabad. Research was initiated after taking approval from Khyber Medical University (KMU). Research was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki principles28. Informed consent from patients was obtained before blood samples were collected. The questionnaire was designed and translated into two languages, i.e. English and Urdu i.e. (native language), for the ease of patients to comprehend the questions asked.

RNA extraction

The blood of volume 500 µl was poured into an Eppendorf tube to which 700 µl of TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added for RNA extraction. Shaking of the mixture was done for 1 min. The mixture was kept on ice for 15 min. Chloroform (Thermo Fisher Scientific) of volume 200 µl was poured in the mixture, which was then placed back on ice for 5 more minutes. Afterwards, the sample was spun in a centrifuge at 12,000 RCF (Relative Centrifugal Field) for 15 min at 4 degrees centigrade. The top layer, which contained the RNA, was collected. To further purify the RNA, 500 µl of isopropanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added, and the tube was inverted several times for thorough mixing and then placed on ice for 10 min, followed by 10 min’ centrifugation at the same temperature and speed. This step gathered all the RNA in a small pellet, and the supernatant above it was discarded. Next, 1000 µl of 75% chilled ethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the pellet to clean the RNA and remove any impurities that were remaining. Vortexing was done for a few seconds that was followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 7500 RCF. The ethanol was carefully discarded and left to evaporate for 5 min, so that only the RNA pellet remains behind. Finally, the pellet was dissolved in nuclease-free water of volume 20 µl. RNA purity was assessed through Nanodrop (Colibri Microvolume Spectrometer). Absorbance was measured at 260 and 280 nm. RNA samples with an A260/A280 ratio of approximately 2.0 were considered to have high purity.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

Solis Biodyne’s RT cDNA Synthesis KIT (FIREScript®) was used for cDNA synthesis. The RNA template was mixed with a master mix containing oligo (dT) primers, nuclease-free water, reverse transcriptase, RNase inhibitor, dNTP mix, and reaction buffer. The reaction mixture was then incubated sequentially: 5 min at 65 °C, 60 min at 42 °C, followed by 10 min at 70 °C. Finally, the samples were stored at 4 °C.

Real-time PCR amplification

For real-time PCR analysis, each cDNA sample was placed in a separate tube. A master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing SYBR Green, Taq polymerase, dNTPs (as DNA building blocks), and an optimised buffer with magnesium ions was added along with specific primers. The blood-based expression levels of four mRNA targets, CFTR, FASN, FRAP, and BANK1—were analyzed. Primer sequences were obtained from the literature; the CFTR forward primer was: 5′-CACGCTTCAGGCACGAAG-3′ and the reverse primer was 5′-CAAGGTCAGAACATTCACC-3′29. For BANK1, the forward primer was 5′-GAGTATCATATTCAAAGATTCTGAAGAC-3′ and reverse primer5′-CACATGGAATTTCAGTGGGAAGCAC-3′30. For the FASN, the forward primer was 5′-TGCACCTCACAGGCATCAAT-3′ and the reverse primer was 5′-GTCCCACTTGATGTGAGGGG-3′31. The forward primer for FRAP was 5′-TCTTTCATTGGAGACGGTTTGG-3′ and the reverse primer was 5′-AGGTTTTCATGGGATGTCGCT-3′32. For GAPDH Forward Primer: GAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGT, and the reverse primer was TTGATTTTGGAGGGATCTCG33. Primer-BLAST34 was used to verify melting temperatures. The PCR thermocycler was programmed with specific temperature conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by annealing at the primers’ specific temperatures for 60 s, and then extension at 72 °C for 10 min. This cycle was repeated approximately 30 times to amplify the target genes. The annealing temperatures of CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1 were found to be 60 °C, 57 °C, 62 °C and 60 °C. The experiment perform in a triplicate and standard deviation was measured. The relative analysis was done through fold change formula (2−ΔΔCt formula ΔΔCt = ΔCt of targeted gene-ΔCt control gene)35. The validity of GAPDH was determined between HCC patients and controls. To address concerns regarding the validity of GAPDH as a sole internal control, raw GAPDH Ct values were compared between patients and controls, stratified by target-gene batch, using unpaired t-tests.

Multivariable modelling and diagnostic performance

To move beyond univariate comparisons, GAPDH-normalized ΔCt values for the four genes were entered into a LASSO-regularized logistic regression (glmnet package, R) predicting patient versus control status, with the regularisation parameter selected by 10-fold cross-validation on binomial deviance.

Cancer stage: specific gene expression profiling

Blood-based expression profiling across different pathological stages of HCC was conducted. Comparative statistical studies were done to analyze the correlation of genes (CFTR, FRAP, BANK1 and FASN) with cancer development. The 100 patients were categorized according to different stages (I, II, III, and IV). Moreover, the CT values of genes (CFTR, FASN, FRAP and BANK1) were determined at different stages. Validation of differential expression was done through TNMplot36.

Multiple testing correction

The four genes were compared between patients and controls and across four BCLC stages, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to all resulting p-values. Corrected (q) values are reported alongside nominal p-values; all comparisons significant at the nominal p < 0.0001 level remained significant after correction. Statistical analysis was done through R37.

Correlation and co-expression analysis

Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among the four genes, and between each gene and patient age and estimated serum AFP, to assess co-expression and clinical/biological correlation. Hierarchical clustering (heatmap) was performed on patient-level ΔΔCt values to determine whether expression profiles cluster according to BCLC stage.

In-silico

Pathway and enrichment analysis

The interconnections between CFTR, FASN, BANK1, and FRAP (mTOR) were determined after compiling data from the literature. KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), STRING (https://string-db.org/) and GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org) were also accessed to construct the pathway. In addition to this, Enrichment analyses were conducted with Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). KEGG provide frameworks to annotate genes based on their roles, cellular locations, and functions. KEGG analyzes complex pathways and investigates gene–disease associations38. STRING analyses the complexity within the cells; it gives the regulatory interactions among proteins. The most recent developments in STRING involve identifying the complement of encoded proteins through experimentally driven interactions39. Moreover, Enrichr is a popular gene enrichment web server that integrates and visualizes enrichment results across gene set libraries40. GeneMANIA find functionally similar genes using genomics and proteomics data41. ShinyGO is an easy-to-use web application that visualizes gene enrichment results and integrates KEGG and STRING databases to identify biological pathways and protein–protein interaction networks42. From an external public dataset, OncoDB43, Kaplan–Meier plots were downloaded that showed the survival rate during follow-up studies. Gene expression levels were determined to measure their association with overall patient prognosis.

Results

Gene expression analysis

The results of the blood-based mRNA expression analysis indicated that FASN expression was significantly higher in patients than in controls (p < 0.0001). In contrast, CFTR expression was significantly downregulated in patients compared with controls (p < 0.0001). Moreover, FRAP (mTOR) expression was significantly upregulated in patients compared with controls (p < 0.0001). Similarly, BANK1 gene expression was significantly downregulated in patients compared with controls (p < 0.0001).

Within the patient group, Spearman co-expression analysis suggested that the apparent correlation between FASN and FRAP in the combined dataset was driven by case–control separation rather than by true within-tumor co-regulation. BANK1 showed a modest negative correlation with FRAP (rho = − 0.24) and a weak negative correlation with CFTR (rho = − 0.13) within the patient group (Fig. 1; Supplementary File 1).

Analysis using TNMplot across 22 cancer types showed that CFTR had low expression in liver cancer, whereas BANK1 showed negligible differences. FASN demonstrated a modest increase in tumor tissue, while FRAP (mTOR) showed a moderate increase. Collectively, these findings support the functional relevance of FASN, mTOR, and CFTR in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly in relation to lipogenesis and growth signaling (Fig. 2).

Cancer stage-wise expression analysis

Different patients were identified at different stages. At stages I, II, III and IV, the number of patients were 22, 36, 22 and 18, respectively. The Ct value of BANK1 was significantly higher in HCC patients as compared to other genes (FRAP (mTOR), CFTR, and FASN). The median Ct value of FASN, CFTR, FRAP and BANK1 ranged from 12 to 21, 13 to 33, 26 to 32 and 32 to 35, respectively, at different stages (Fig. 3). Across BCLC stages, GAPDH-normalized ΔΔCt values (relative to the control-group mean) showed consistent directionality for all four genes at every stage (I-IV), but no statistically significant monotonic trend with increasing stage was observed for any gene (Fig. 3), indicating that expression changes in this cohort are primarily associated with tumour presence rather than with stage progression.

GAPDH stability and sensitivity analysis

GAPDH Ct values were broadly comparable between groups for the BANK1, CFTR and FRAP batches; however, a wider dispersion and a modest upward shift were observed in the FASN batch, indicating some batch-to-batch instability (Supplementary file 2, Fig. S2). Through sensitivity analysis (Tables 1, 2), GAPDH Ct outliers were flagged per gene-batch and per group (Patient/Control) using a Tukey 1.5 × IQR rule, and all downstream analyses (group comparison, combined logistic regression, LASSO) were re-run after excluding these samples. GAPDH outliers were concentrated in the FASN batch (9 patient-side and 30 control-side outliers of 100, i.e. 37 rows excluded), consistent with the batch instability reported above; the CFTR batch had 10 control-side outliers, while the FRAP and BANK1 batches had none. The direction and statistical significance of all four genes' group differences were unchanged after excluding GAPDH outliers, and fold-change estimates for FASN, FRAP and BANK1 were essentially identical; CFTR's fold-change shifted from 0.044 × to 0.077 × , indicating some sensitivity to the excluded batch but no change in direction or conclusion. For the combined diagnostic model, any subject with a GAPDH outlier in any of the four batches (46 of 100 pairs) were excluded, leaving 54 patients and 54 controls with clean GAPDH values.

Pathway analysis

CFTR interconnected with other genes, including scaffold proteins (SLC9A3R1, SLC9A3R2), molecular chaperones (HSP90AA1, HSPA8), signaling kinases (PRKACA, PRKACB, PRKACG), and ubiquitination-related proteins (RNF5, STUB1) were identified. FASN interconnected with other genes including ACLY, ACACA and ACACB (which produce malonyl-CoA), and AASDHPPT (which activates the acyl carrier domain of FASN) (Fig. 3a–d). Moreover, the AMPK signaling pathway was the “master convergence node” with connectivity to all four gene clusters, including a strong connectivity to FASN/mTOR, moderate to CFTR, and weak to BANK1 (Fig. 4f).

Literature review aided in determining the interactions between CFTR, FASN, FRAP and BANK1. Wnt/-catenin signaling pathway positively regulates FASN, which is important in lipid synthesis and lipid metabolic reprogramming. CFTR links with the mTOR/FRAP pathway, which is important for nutrient and energy signals. AMPK modulates this hub, which indicates the balance between anabolic and catabolic states. Downstream of mTOR, the adaptor protein TRADD is linked to the PI3K signaling pathway, and it activates AKT, a mediator in cell survival and proliferation. BANK1 is shown to interact with BCR, BLK, and STAT1. BANK1 also connect to PI3K, which links with the AKT survival axis. Also, IP3R signalling indicates its involvement in intracellular signaling that relies on calcium (Fig. 5).

The enrichment analysis was done for CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1. The enrichment analysis indicated biologically relevant pathways related to CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1. In the case of CFTR, the enrichment pathway with the highest p-value was seen in defective GALNT12 that causes CRCS1. Conversely, FASN's role was significant in SREBF-mediated gene expression, cholesterol biosynthesis and steroid metabolism. For FRAP (mTOR) the pathway that modify chromatin enzymes was found to have the highest p value. Survival analysis

In Fig. 7, CFTR expression was not significantly associated with overall survival. Although patients with high CFTR expression had a shorter median survival (1624 days) than those with low expression (1852 days), the difference was not statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.39; HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.82–1.65). These findings suggest that CFTR expression does not have a reliable prognostic effect on survival in this cohort. Among the four genes, FASN was the only one significantly associated with survival. Patients with high FASN expression showed a shorter median survival (1622 vs. 1852 days) and a 42% higher risk of death compared with the low-expression group (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.00–2.01; log-rank p = 0.047). Although the result was borderline significant, it suggests that FASN may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker in HCC.

Survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier graph) through OncoDB. (a) CFTR, (b) FASN, (c) FRAP (mTOR) and (d) BANK1.

FRAP (mTOR) showed a similar trend, with high expression associated with shorter survival (1624 vs. 1694 days). However, this association did not reach statistical significance (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.91–1.82; log-rank p = 0.15), indicating only a possible trend toward poorer outcomes. In contrast, BANK1 demonstrated the opposite pattern. Patients with high BANK1 expression had fewer deaths and slightly longer median survival (1685 vs. 1622 days) than those with low expression. However, the association was not statistically significant (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.59–1.17; log-rank p = 0.29), suggesting no clear protective effect in this cohort. Integrated modeling analysis

LASSO cross-validation curve (binomial deviance vs − Log (lambda)) used to select the regularization parameter for the multivariable logistic regression model; the selected model retained FASN, CFTR and BANK1 (Supplementary file 9, Fig. S9). The LASSO procedure retained FASN, CFTR and BANK1 as informative predictors and reduced the FRAP (mTOR) coefficient toward zero, reflecting its collinearity with FASN. A multivariable logistic regression including all four genes simultaneously was then fitted to obtain adjusted odds ratios (Fig. 8). Diagnostic performance of each gene individually, of a composite risk score, and of the combined four-gene model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC) (Fig. 9).

Adjusted odds ratios (95% CI) for HCC (patient vs control) per one-unit increase in GAPDH-normalized ΔCt, from a multivariable logistic regression model including all four genes simultaneously.

ROC curves for individual genes, a composite risk score, and the combined four-gene logistic regression model.

ROC analysis was performed to formally evaluate diagnostic performance (Fig. 9). FASN alone achieved an AUC of 0.87, followed by BANK1 (AUC = 0.73), FRAP (AUC = 0.68) and CFTR (AUC = 0.62). A composite risk score combining all four genes achieved an AUC of 0.85, and a combined logistic-regression model incorporating all four genes achieved the best overall performance (AUC = 0.89), supporting the added diagnostic value of considering these genes jointly rather than individually (Fig. 9). All AUC values above were computed using this single 100-patient/100-control dataset; internal 10-fold cross-validation (used for the LASSO model) reduces but does not eliminate overfitting risk, since cross-validation folds are still drawn from the same cohort.

Biological and clinical validation

To provide biological/clinical validation of the expression signature, ΔΔCt values were correlated with estimated serum AFP concentration (Fig. 10). CFTR ΔΔCt showed a significant negative correlation with AFP (Spearman rho = − 0.23, p = 0.023), whereas BANK1 (rho = − 0.06, p = 0.525), FASN (rho = 0.03, p = 0.789) and FRAP (rho = 0.04, p = 0.689) did not correlate significantly with AFP. This suggests that CFTR dysregulation may track more closely with tumour burden than the other three genes in this cohort. Pairwise correlation analysis of the four genes, together with patient age and serum AFP, is shown in Fig. 10. FASN and FRAP were highly correlated when patients and controls were analyzed jointly (r = 0.97), reflecting the fact that both genes are shifted in the same direction between the two groups; BANK1 was negatively correlated with the other three genes. Neither age nor AFP showed a meaningful correlation with any of the four genes.

Discussion

HCC is one of the most common malignancies worldwide46. HCC development involves a number of successive genetic and epigenetic events47. Genes’ specificity and expression levels play a vital role in the diagnosis of HCC. Thus, there is a need to develop reliable diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets to improve early detection in case of HCC48.

The present study investigated the differential mRNA expression levels of four genes (FASN, CFTR, FRAP (mTOR), BANK1) in HCC patients compared to controls. Notably, FASN and FRAP were significantly upregulated, whereas CFTR and BANK1 were markedly downregulated in HCC patients, suggesting their possible involvement in HCC. In this study, different patients were identified at different cancer stages. The Ct value of BANK1 was significantly higher at different stages in HCC patients as compared to other genes (FRAP (mTOR), CFTR, and FASN). In different cancer stages, the high expression of oncogenes, FRAP (mTOR) and FASN while lower expression of tumor suppressor genes, CFTR and BANK1 contributed to HCC progression. Study indicate differential expression of genes (DEGs) at different cancer stages (I, II, III, and IV)49.

The current study results correlate with the past studies. FASN level was upregulated in CRC tissues and high expression of FASN contributed to TNM stages. Moreover, poor prognosis was reported in patients with CRC that had high expression of FASN50. The FASN role is also significant as a prognostic marker in case of CESC51. Moreover, a study shows that the β-catenin/mTOR complex in the liver promotes the upregulation of lipid synthesis–related genes (p-S6K, FASN, and PPAR-γ), thereby enhancing lipid production and ultimately contributing to the development of hepatic steatosis52. The other study findings indicate that mTOR (FRAP) along with PI3K and AKT, plays a critical role in the progression of breast cancer53. In addition to this aberrant expression of mTOR (FRAP) is reported in breast cancer54. Our study indicates upregulated mTOR (FRAP) expression in HCC.

Conversely, in our study, the significant downregulation of CFTR and BANK1 in HCC patients suggested that these genes may act as tumor suppressors. The results showed higher CFTR gene expression in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. CFTR was down-regulated in LIHC but up-regulated in CHOL. Likewise, in kidney cancer, CFTR had a lower expression in KIRC and KIRP but had high expression in KICH, this suggest that CFTR could have varied role in different cancers. CFTR act as a tumor suppressor gene in most cancers12. In other study, BANK1 was highly expressed in case of gastric cancer55. Whereas, BANK1 expression was reported to be down-regulated in Colorectal cancer (CRC) exosomes in comparison to healthy exosomes56. On the other hand, our result showed downregulated expression of BANK1 in HCC.

In this study, GAPDH Ct values showed batch-to-batch instability, particularly in the FASN assay batch. A sensitivity analysis excluding GAPDH-outlier samples left the core conclusions unchanged and did not reduce diagnostic performance, which is reassuring but not conclusive. Iindependently, validated reference gene (i.e. B2M or ACTB) was not run in parallel due to resource constraints, so residual normalization bias from GAPDH batch fluctuation could not be fully ruled out. This is a major limitation of the present dataset; any follow-up study should incorporate a validated multi-reference-gene normalization strategy from the outset.

Moreover, the combined four-gene logistic model (AUC = 0.89) and the LASSO-selected model (AUC = 0.89) were both developed and evaluated entirely within this single, 100-patient/100-control internal dataset. Although the LASSO model used 10-fold internal cross-validation and a GAPDH-outlier sensitivity check did not weaken discrimination. None of this constitutes external validation, and the risk of overfitting to this specific cohort cannot be fully excluded. These AUC values should be read as showing promising discriminative performance within the present cohort; they do not establish that this four-gene panel can be used clinically for HCC diagnosis. Validation in one or more independent cohorts is required before any clinical or diagnostic application is considered.

Discrimination did not deteriorate after removing GAPDH-unstable samples—if anything, AUC was higher in the GAPDH-clean subset, consistent with batch noise diluting rather than inflating the original signal. Moreover, the clean-subset AUC is based on a smaller sample (n = 108 vs 200) drawn from the same internal cohort, so this is a robustness/sensitivity check, not external validation, and both AUC estimates remain subject to the internal-cohort limitation discussed below. One further nuance worth rep orting transparently: LASSO retained FASN, CFTR and BANK1 in the original (full) sample but dropped CFTR (coefficient shrunk to zero) in the GAPDH-clean subset, retaining only FASN and BANK1—suggesting CFTR's independent contribution to the multivariable model is the least robust of the three retained genes and should be interpreted cautiously pending external validation.

Moreover, total RNA for the expression analysis was extracted from the peripheral blood and not from the tumour parenchymal tissue. The findings were derived from gene expression patterns of peripheral blood rather than from the tumour tissue. Peripheral blood contain transcripts derived predominantly from circulating leukocytes rather from tumour cells. Moreover, variation found between HCC patients and controls may be due to systemic responses to the presence of malignancy, including immune activation, inflammatory responses, and changes in circulating cell populations, rather than to intrinsic transcriptional changes within tumour. Previous studies have demonstrated that differences in the cellular composition of peripheral blood can significantly affect blood gene-expression profiles57,58. This is especially applicable to BANK1, which is expressed in B lymphocytes and has been demonstrated to play a role in BCR and other pathways involved in B-cell signalling. Variations in BANK1 expression in the whole blood could be due to inter-individual differences of circulating B cell proportions or intracellular levels of BANK1 in HCC cells.

Moreover, only borderline statistical significance was identified for FASN overall survival, which need to be validated in independent external cohorts. The associations of CFTR, FRAP (mTOR), and BANK1 with survival were not statistically significant in the current data set. Thus, the four-gene panel must be considered as a potential candidate diagnostic signature that needs to be validated through independent functional studies before clinical application.

Furthermore, BCLC stage should not be taken as a direct indicator of tumour progression. Patients at the same stage may show significant biological and clinical variability. In particular, the HCC patients in intermediate stage also have different tumour sizes, number of tumours and overall tumour burden. The revised BCLC classification recognizes this heterogeneity59,60. To add to this, there is a lack of a clear trend in gene expression across BCLC stages. Although circulating transcripts may help distinguish HCC patients from controls, they may not accurately reflect the molecular changes as the tumour progresses. This can be due to the dilution of signal because of the presence of RNAs from other blood cells. Also, the changes which occur in the tumour may not be clearly evident in the blood61.

HCC is also a molecularly and clinically heterogeneous disease, and slight differences in the proportion of immune cells, disease etiology, as well as in the features of treatment make it difficult to distinguish stage-dependent differences of RNAs in the whole blood62. Also, the sample size in the present cohort was relatively small. The larger longitudinal cohorts of patients could help in reducing sampling bias in the patient cohort and distinguishing progressive changes in the gene expression, whether these changes are associated with tumour progression, tumour burden, or systemic immune responses.

In our study, the pathway analysis for CFTR, FASN, FRAP and BANK1 were done. Also, the intermediate pathways that interconnect these genes were identified. Our results highlight several high-confidence functional partners of CFTR, including scaffold proteins (SLC9A3R1, SLC9A3R2), GOPC, molecular chaperones (HSP90AA1, HSPA8), signaling kinases (PRKACA, PRKACB, PRKACG), and ubiquitination-related proteins (RNF5, STUB1). These interactions emphasize that CFTR function is not isolated but tightly controlled through protein folding, trafficking, degradation, and phosphorylation-dependent signaling pathways63.

Chaperones such as HSP90AA1 and HSPA8 ensure correct folding and stability, while ubiquitin ligases like RNF5 and STUB1 regulate degradation of misfolded CFTR, a process particularly relevant in cystic fibrosis pathology64. Scaffold proteins GOPC, SLC9A3R1, SLC9A3R2 and (NHERF family) link CFTR to the cytoskeleton and membrane complexes, stabilizing its localization and function65–67. Meanwhile, cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA) subunits (PRKACA/B/G) regulate CFTR activation through phosphorylation, integrating extracellular signals into channel activity. Together, this network reflects a balance between synthesis, trafficking, activation, and degradation that determines CFTR functional expression68,69.

In our results, FASN showed connection with other closely associated proteins that support substrate supply and metabolic regulation, including ACLY, ACACA and ACACB (which produce malonyl-CoA)70, and AASDHPPT71 (which activates the acyl carrier domain of FASN). Additionally, transcriptional regulation is mediated by SREBF1, a master regulator of lipid homeostasis72. These interactions demonstrate that FASN operates within a coordinated biosynthetic system that integrates enzymatic activity with transcriptional control and post-translational modification.

Beyond synthesis, the FASN network extends into fatty acid activation and metabolic utilization pathways. Enzymes such as ACSL1 and ACSL4 convert fatty acids into acyl-CoA derivatives, linking synthesis to downstream processes like β-oxidation and lipid signaling73. Mitochondrial-associated proteins (e.g., MCAT, ACSF3) indicate cross-talk between cytosolic and mitochondrial lipid metabolism74. This network reflects a tightly regulated metabolic circuit where carbon flux is directed toward lipid production, storage, or energy utilization. Dysregulation of this system is strongly associated with metabolic disorders and cancer, where elevated FASN activity supports rapid cell growth and membrane biosynthesis75.

In our study, the mTOR protein key interacting partners included RPTOR (scaffold of mTORC1), RICTOR and MAPKAP1 (components of mTORC2)76–78. Activation of mTORC1 via RHEB and Rag GTPases promotes anabolic processes such as protein and lipid synthesis79,80. Downstream effectors like RPS6KB1 and EIF4EBP1 mediate translational control, while ULK1 connects mTOR signaling to autophagy81–83. Conversely, mTORC2 regulates cell survival and cytoskeletal dynamics through AKT signaling and Rho GTPases84. Additionally, DEPTOR acts as an inhibitory regulator85, and TELO2 contributes to mTOR complex stability, reflecting the intricate control of this pathway86.

BANK1 facilitates Lyn-mediated phosphorylation of ITPR1/ITPR287, thereby linking antigen receptor activation to calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum. Our study results identify several high-confidence interacting partners, including kinases such as LYN and BLK initiate and propagate BCR signaling88, while phosphatases like PTPN22 provide negative feedback to prevent overactivation as immune regulator89. Calcium release channels (ITPR1/3) and membrane proteins such as MS4A1/CD20 further amplify intracellular signaling cascades90–92. Transcription factors like IRF5 connect upstream signaling to gene expression changes, particularly in inflammatory and antiviral responses93. This integrated system highlights how BANK1 contributes to both activation and regulation of immune signaling, with dysregulation often linked B cells markedly higher BANK1 expression compared with lymphoma cells, which showed relatively weak BANK1 expression94.

Multi-gene blood-based signatures are particularly relevant to clinical-translational context. Recent literature on HCC biomarkers highlight the importance of the use of combinations of molecular signatures to enhance diagnostic and prognostic value, while acknowledging the complexity of the tumour microenvironment in HCC. In the recent review of emerging diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker strategies in HCC, Xiang et al.95 pointed out that molecular signatures representing multiple biological processes, such as tumour microenvironment related mechanisms are important. In the same way, Chen et al.96 summarized the role of secreted and immune-associated biomarkers in tumour progression and modulation of immune microenvironment, which can give insights into BANK1 as an immune-related component of a circulating transcript signature. In this context, the present four-gene panel should be viewed primarily as candidate blood-based diagnostic signatures rather than validated tumour-tissue based prognostic signatures.

We constructed a pathway with the help of literature review that aided us in identifying the interconnection of CFTR with FASN, BANK1 and FRAP. CFTR reconstructs the signalling network with mTOR/PI3K pathways97,98. FASN is linked downstream of mTOR, and AMPK signaling pathways and contributes to cell proliferation50. Moreover, mTOR physically interact with β-catenin to regulate FASN and lipid metabolism. Also, Wnt/β-catenin and mTOR-FASN interaction lead to hepatosteatosis52. BANK1 interacts with PI3K/AKT pathways that can feed into mTOR signaling99. BANK1 and BLK form relevant gene interaction involved B cell receptor signaling100,101. BANK1’s interactions with STAT1,important for immune-inflammation subnetworks102.

The enrichment analysis pathway was indicated biologically relevant pathways related to CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK138. Moreover, FASN showed relevant linkage with SREBF-mediated gene expression. For FRAP (mTOR) was linked up with modify chromatin enzymes pathway, while in case of BANK1, related with the immune system regulatory pathways103. In our study neither age nor AFP levels demonstrated a notable correlation with the expression of any of the four genes. The serum AFP levels and the expression of the investigated genes was evaluated to determine whether these molecular alterations were associated with established clinical features of HCC104.

To add to this, this study captured a single, cross-sectional blood sample per participant; no prospective follow-up of this cohort was performed, so the survival analysis presented here was obtained from a public database rather than from study’s patients, and should not be conflated with a survival analysis of the present cohort. Also, detailed clinical/pathological baseline characteristics (e.g., etiology of HCC, Child–Pugh score, prior treatment) beyond stage, AFP and age were not available for the full cohort that limited how precisely case-mix differences between patients and controls can be characterized.

Furthermore, only mRNA-level analysis were conducted in the present study; no protein measurement or cellular functional assays were part of the current study, leading to weak evidence for mechanistic inferences. This study only evaluated mRNA transcripts, without protein-level validation such as Western blot or immunohistochemistry, and no cellular gain-/loss-of-function experiments. The regulatory crosstalk network among the four studied genes was developed through in silico analysis. Further more, cellular and animal experiments are required to verify true regulatory crosstalk among CFTR-FASN-FRAP-BANK1 genes.

Conclusion

In our study, CFTR, FASN, FRAP (mTOR) and BANK1 exhibited a significant difference in their expression levels in case of HCC. The molecular crosstalk between these genes demonstrated the complicated network that leads to HCC development. These genes correlation with disease progression is further supported by stage-wise analysis. Moreover, these genes can act as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. However, their clinical relevance and applicability require validation through functional studies.
