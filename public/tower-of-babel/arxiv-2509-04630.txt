Quasi-continuum approximations for nonlinear dispersive waves in general discrete conservation laws
Su Yang

Abstract
In this paper, we study a non-integrable discrete lattice model which is a variant of an integrable discretization of the standard Hopf equation. Interestingly, a direct numerical simulation of the Riemann problem associated with such a discrete lattice shows the emergence of both the dispersive shock wave (DSW) and rarefaction wave (RW). We propose two quasi-continuum models which are represented by partial differential equations (PDEs) in order to both analytically and numerically capture the features of the DSW and RW of the lattice. Accordingly, we apply the DSW fitting method to gain important insights and provide theoretical predictions on various edge features of the DSW including the edge speed and wavenumber. Meanwhile, we analytically compute the self-similar solutions of the quasi-continuum models, which serve as the approximation of the RW of the lattice. We then conduct comparisons between these numerical and analytical results to examine the performance of the approximation of the quasi-continuum models to the discrete lattice.

Introduction

Dispersive shock wave
[
1
]
, which is a unstationary and dispersive wave structure, is numerically observed in a variety of mathematical physics models. Some typical examples include the famous Toda lattice
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, the granular crystal lattice
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,

]
, the nonlinear Schrödinger model
[

,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. Remarkably, besides those numerical emergences of the dispersive shock waves, they are also experimentally observed
[
14
,
15
,
9
]
. Importantly, the core of the DSW is the so-called periodic wave associated with the model, as it can be regarded as the slow spatial and temporal modulation on distinct parameters (e.g. wavenumber, wave amplitude) of the periodic solutions. The analysis of this particular dispersive wave structure is important. The so-called
Whitham modulation theory

[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
provides a formal theoretical analysis, via a system of partial differential equations which are known as Whitham modulation equations, on the space-time evolution of these slowly varying and modulated parameters of the periodic waves. Moreover, the reduction of the Whitham modulation equations near both the linear and solitonic limits gives important insights on the edge features (e.g. solitonic-edge speed, linear-edge wavenumber and speed) of the dispersive shock waves. Such a reduction of the modulation system is entitled with the
DSW fitting
method
[
21
]
. The rarefaction wave, on the other hand, is classified as a simple wave structure
[
16
,
22
]
. The dispersive model also serves as a prototypical medium for the emergence of this particular wave structure. Analytically, the rarefaction wave is described by a self-similar solution of the corresponding model. As one shall see, this work investigates both the dispersive shock and rarefaction wave of a discrete lattice model stemmed from a scalar conservation law.

The continuum scalar conservation law
[
23
]
is a dispersionless system in the sense that its linear dispersion relation has zero second derivative with respect to the wavenumber. For example, both the transport and Hopf equations in
1+1
time-space dimension are dispersionless. Therefore, one should not expect any dispersive wave structure (e.g. DSW) to emerge in these systems. Recently, the work
[
24
]
proposed a discrete lattice model which serves as a discrete and dispersive regularization of the standard continuum Hopf equation:

\frac{du_{n}}{dt}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{n+1}^{2}-u_{n-1}^{2}\right)=0.

(1.1)

Surprisingly, in addition to the regular rarefaction and dispersive shock, many other interesting dispersive wave structures such as the traveling dispersive shock wave have been numerically observed in (
1.1
). Moreover, an alternative discretization scheme of the Hopf equation has also been proposed and studied in
[
25
]
,

\frac{du_{n}}{dt}+u_{n}\left(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1}\right)=0.

(1.2)

The lattice in Eq. (
1.2
) has shown to be integrable by performing a variable transformation
[
26
]
to a model which is directly relevant with the Toda lattice. Based on the numerical studies
[
24
]
of the Riemann problem associated with this alternative discretization (
1.2
), it shows the emergence of both the rarefaction and dispersive shock. However, this paper shall focus on a non-integrable variant of the lattice (
1.2
).

This paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we discuss, in detail, the discrete lattice model which will be the focus of this paper, and meanwhile propose two quasi-continuum models in section
3
, which will be utilized to approximate the DSW and RW of the discrete lattice. We then attempt to analytically derive, in section
4
-
5
, the traveling and periodic waves of both quasi-continuum models. Next, in section
6
, we list some important conservation laws of the two quasi-continuum models, which serve as the prerequisites to derive the system of Whitham modulation equations. In section
7
, we perform the detailed analysis and derivation of the modulation equations which describe the slow spatial-temporal evolution of all the relevant parameters of the periodic waves. The DSW fitting is done in section
8
so that we shall obtain some analytical predictions on multiple edge features of the DSW, and they are to be compared with those numerically estimated DSW edge features that we shall perform in the later section
10
. We next conduct some necessary numerical comparisons in the rest of the sections. Namely, in section
9
, we first compute the analytical self-similar solutions of the quasi-continuum models, and then compare such self-similar solutions with the rarefaction waves of the discrete lattice model and also the two quasi-continuum models. On the other hand, in section
10
, we discuss some important methods of performing numerical estimation of different edge features of the dispersive shock wave, and then we compare these numerically estimated DSW edge features between the two quasi-continuum models and the discrete lattice. Finally, we end this paper in section
11
with some comments about this work and also propose some potential future directions.

Model description

(a) The linear dispersion relation curves of the lattice (

In this paper, we focus on a non-integrable variant of the integrable lattice in Eq. (
1.2
),
which is a discretization of the following continuum Hopf equation,

u_{t}+\left(u^{2}\right)_{x}=0.

(2.1)

Before we display our lattice model, it is worthwhile to note that one can extend the scaler conservation law in Eq. (
2.1
) as follows,

u_{t}+\left[\Phi(u)\right]_{x}=0,

(2.2)

where
\Phi
denotes a continuously differentiable potential function. With the chain rule, the discrete and dispersive regularization of the model Eq. (
2.2
) reads,

\frac{du_{n}}{dt}+\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{\prime}(u_{n})\left(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1}\right)=0.

(2.3)

To further demonstrate that the lattice in Eq. (
1.2
) is dispersive, we look for a plane-wave solution in the form of
u_{n}(t)=\overline{u}+ae^{i\left(kn-\omega t\right)}
, where
0<a\ll 1
is a small parameter, a direct substitution of this ansatz into (
1.2
) yields the following linear dispersion relation,

\omega\left(k,\overline{u}\right)=2\overline{u}\sin(k),

(2.4)

so clearly the linear dispersion relation in Eq. (
2.4
) does not always have zero second derivative with respect to
k
, and hence this shows that the lattice (
1.2
) is dispersion.

One can readily extend the integrable system Eq. (
1.2
) as follows,

\frac{du_{n}}{dt}+\left(u_{n}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}\left(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1}\right)=0.

(2.5)

where
\widetilde{p}
is an arbitrary positive constant.

We notice first that Eq. (
2.5
) is a discretization of the following continuum conservation law,

u_{t}+\frac{2}{\widetilde{p}+1}\left(u^{\widetilde{p}+1}\right)_{x}=0.

(2.6)

Similarly, looking for a plane-wave ansatz yields the following linear dispersion relation for the lattice (
2.5
),

\omega_{0}\left(\overline{u},k\right)=2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}\sin\left(k\right).

(2.7)

We note that integrability may no longer persist in system (
2.5
) when
\widetilde{p}\neq 1
. However, relevant numerical studies have confirmed the emergence of both DSW and RW. As a consequence, it is necessary to find some quasi-continuum models in order to quasi-analytically capture and describe the characteristics of both the DSW and RW observed in system (
2.5
). In this paper, we are interested in the so-called Riemann problem associated with the discrete lattice (
2.5
). That is, we solve the initial value problem of Eq. (
2.5
) with the following step-like initial data,

u_{n}(0)=\begin{cases}u^{-},\quad n\leq 0,\\
u^{+},\quad n>0.\end{cases}

(2.8)

As we shall see, when we have the upward step (
u^{-}>u^{+}
), a DSW shall form, while a RW emerges if we instead have the downward step (
u^{-}<u^{+}
).

) at
t=500
for
\widetilde{p}=1/2
, while the right panel (b) showcases the space-time plot of the associated DSW. Notice that the two dashed black lines in the panel (b) represent the estimation of the leading and the trailing edges of the DSW based on the DSW fitting theoretical predictions from section

.

3
Quasi-continuum approximations

To find some relevant quasi-continuum approximations for the system (
2.5
), we take the following two slow variables on space and time,

X=\epsilon n,\quad T=\epsilon t,

(3.1)

where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a formal smallness parameter.
Because of the definitions of
X,T
in Eq. (
3.1
), we then know

K=\epsilon^{-1}k,\quad\Omega=\epsilon^{-1}\omega.

(3.2)

Then, we can rewrite Eq. (
2.5
) as follows,

\epsilon u_{T}+u^{\widetilde{p}}\left(u\left(X+\epsilon\right)-u\left(X-\epsilon\right)\right)=0.

(3.3)

We Taylor expand Eq. (
3.3
) and collect terms up to order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
to obtain the following
non-regularized
continuum model,

u_{T}+u^{\widetilde{p}}\left(2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX}\right)=0.

(3.4)

Eq. (
3.4
) is the first quasi-continuum model that we shall focus throughout this work. We then derive the linear dispersion relation of Eq. (
3.4
) by taking the plane-wave ansatz
u(X,T)=A+Be^{i\left(KX-\Omega T\right)}
, where
0<B\ll 1
is a small parameter. Substitution of the plane-wave ansatz into Eq. (
3.4
) yields the following linear dispersion relation,

\Omega\left(A,K\right)=2A^{\widetilde{p}}K\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{6}\right).

(3.5)

Moreover, we can regularize the model (
3.4
) via reducing the order of its spatial derivatives. To this end, we rewrite Eq. (
3.4
) as follows,

\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{T}=-2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\partial^{2}_{X}\right)u_{X}.

(3.6)

Inverting the operator
1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\partial_{X}^{2}
on the right hand side of Eq. (
3.6
) yields the following
regularized
quasi-continuum model,

\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{T}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{XXT}=-2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)u_{X}.

(3.7)

Then, we compute the corresponding linear dispersion relation of the regularized by substituting the plane-wave ansatz, which then gives,

\Omega(A,K)=\frac{2A^{\widetilde{p}}K}{1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{6}}.

(3.8)

The figure
depicts the linear dispersion relations of the three models Eqs. (
2.5
), (
3.4
) and (
3.7
). We can clearly see that the dispersion curve (blue) of the non-regularized model (
3.4
) asymptotes to negative infinity drastically as the wavenumber
k
increases, while that of the regularized model (
3.7
) always remains finite with a much smaller magnitude. Hence, we confirm the importance of performing regularization on the non-regularized model (
3.4
).

4
Traveling-wave solutions

For both quasi-continuum models of Eqs. (
3.4
) and (
3.7
), we first look for traveling solitary waves in the form of

u\left(X,T\right)=U\left(Z\right),\quad Z=X-cT,

(4.1)

where
c
denotes the speed of propagation of the traveling wave.

Non-regularized model.

For the non-regularized model (
3.4
), substituting the traveling-wave ansatz (
4.1
) into Eq. (
3.4
) yields,

-cU^{-\widetilde{p}}U_{Z}+2U_{Z}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}U_{ZZZ}=0.

(4.2)

A direct integration of Eq. (
4.2
), with the requests for all constants of integration being zero, yields the following periodic solutions,

U(Z)=\bigg(\frac{c}{(1-\widetilde{p})(2-\widetilde{p})}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}}\cos^{\frac{2}{\widetilde{p}}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}\widetilde{p}}{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}Z\right).

(4.3)

Clearly, we notice that the solution in Eq. (
4.3
) do not hold for
\widetilde{p}=1,2
as it becomes singular. Moreover, the periodic solution in Eq. (
4.3
) can be used to describe the traveling solitary waves by only limiting the domain of the solution to one specific period.

Regularized model.

For the regularized model (
3.7
), we first apply the change of variable
v=u^{1-\widetilde{p}}
, and then substitute the ansatz in terms of
V(Z)=U(Z)^{1-\widetilde{p}}
into Eq. (
3.7
) to obtain that, upon integration with respect
Z
twice,

\frac{\epsilon^{2}c}{6}\left(V_{Z}\right)^{2}=cV^{2}-\frac{4\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)^{2}}{\left(2-\widetilde{p}\right)}V^{\frac{2-\widetilde{p}}{1-\widetilde{p}}}+2AV+B,

(4.4)

where
A,B
are two constants of integration.

With the relation that
U=V^{\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{p}}}
, we set
A=B=0
, and then a direct integration of Eq. (
4.4
) yields,

U(Z)=\left(\frac{c\left(2-\widetilde{p}\right)}{4\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}}\text{sech}^{\frac{2}{\widetilde{p}}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{6}\widetilde{p}}{2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)\epsilon}Z\right).

(4.5)

Clearly, we notice that Eq. (
4.5
) does not hold when
\widetilde{p}=1
, but we are not going to consider the case of
\widetilde{p}=1
as it associates with the integrable lattice (
1.2
).

Finally, it is important and worthwhile to notice that the traveling-wave solutions in Eqs. (
4.3
) and (
4.5
) for the two respective quasi-continuum models can be complex for some cases of
\widetilde{p}
, but in this paper we are only interested in the scenarios where we have real wave field of
U
and hence ignore those cases of
\widetilde{p}
which lead to a complex wave. Meanwhile, since the goal of this paper is to study the nonlinear dispersive waves of the discrete lattice (
2.5
) through the two quasi-continuum models, we will not perform any numerical studies of the traveling solitary waves in (
2.5
) nor its comparison to the two solitary waves we found in Eqs. (
4.3
) and (
4.5
).

6
Conservation laws

In this section, we display some conservation laws of the two quasi-continuum models. Conservation laws are crucially important in the theoretical analysis of the DSW as the so-called Whitham modulation equations shall be derived from these necessary conservation laws of the model. We will illustrate the important role that Whitham modulation equations play in the analysis of the DSW in section
7
.

Non-regularized model.

Firstly, for the non-regularized model (
3.4
), it possesses the following two conservation laws,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{p}}\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{T}+2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2-\widetilde{p}}\left(u^{2-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{T}+\left(u^{2}\right)_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}\left(uu_{XX}\right)_{X}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left[\left(u_{X}\right)^{2}\right]_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

We note that the first equation in system (
6.1
) refers to the conservation of
mass
, while the second equation is the conservation of
momentum
.

However, it is important to notice that the system of conservation laws in (
6.1
) do not hold when
\widetilde{p}=1,2
. Instead, for these two particular cases, the two associated conservation laws read: For
\widetilde{p}=1
,

\displaystyle\left(\log(u)\right)_{T}+2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.2)

\displaystyle u_{T}+\left(u^{2}\right)_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}\left(uu_{XX}\right)_{X}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left[\left(u_{X}\right)^{2}\right]_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

For
\widetilde{p}=2
,

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)_{T}+2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\left(\log(u)\right)_{T}+\left(u^{2}\right)_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}\left(uu_{XX}\right)_{X}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left[\left(u_{X}\right)^{2}\right]_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

Regularized model.

On the other hand, for the regularized model (
3.7
), we have the following conservation laws,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left[u^{1-\widetilde{p}}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{XX}\right]_{T}+2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)u_{X}&=0,\\
\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12}\left[\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{X}\right]^{2}\right]_{T}+\left[\frac{2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)}{\left(2-\widetilde{p}\right)}u^{2-\widetilde{p}}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\left(u^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{XT}\right]_{X}&=0.\end{aligned}

(6.4)

Notice that the above system does not hold for
\widetilde{p}=2
due to the singularity in the second equation of (
6.4
). Instead, the two conservation laws for the regularized model (
3.7
) for
\widetilde{p}=2
read,

\displaystyle\left[u^{-1}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left(u^{-1}\right)_{XX}\right]_{T}-2u_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}u^{-2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12}\left[\left(u^{-1}\right)_{X}\right]^{2}\right]_{T}+\left[-2\log(u)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}u^{-1}\left(u^{-1}\right)_{XT}\right]_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

Similarly, the first and the second equation in system (
6.4
) refer to the conservation of mass and momentum, respectively.

7
Whitham modulation equations

In this section, we perform Whitham analysis on the periodic wave solutions of the quasi-continuum model Eqs. (
3.4
) and (
3.7
) as this is an indispensable process before we can analytically capture the behaviors of the DSWs. We first notice that the periodic wave of the model Eq. (
3.7
) is a three-parameters family solution as the co-traveling frame ODE Eq. (
4.4
) contains three parameters of
c,A,B
where we recall that
c
denotes the speed of propagation of the wave and
A,B
are two constants of integration. This suggests that to capture the complete dynamics of these parameters of the periodic wave, we need three modulation equations to form a complete closed system.

To derive the Whitham modulation equations, we notice that the two spatial and temporal variables
X,T
are already slow variables, and then we introduce the following fast phase variable
\theta
,

\theta=\frac{KX-\Omega T}{\epsilon},

(7.1)

and we seek for periodic wave solution in the following multiple-scale form of

u(X,T)=\phi\left(\theta;X,T\right)+\epsilon u_{1}\left(\theta;X,T\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right),

(7.2)

where
\phi\left(\theta;X,T\right)
refers to a periodic traveling wave with a fixed
2\pi
period and we recall that
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is the same smallness parameter that we applied in the previous sections.

Next, the fast phase variable
\theta
is further defined by
\theta_{X}=K/\epsilon
and
\theta_{T}=-\Omega/\epsilon
, where both now the wavenumber
K
and the frequency
\Omega
are functions of both slowly varying variables of
X,T
. We then observe that the compatible condition of
\theta_{XT}=\theta_{TX}
yields the following equation,

K_{T}+\Omega_{X}=0,

(7.3)

which is the so-called
conservation of waves
. It is the first modulation equation in our closed modulation system.

To derive the remaining modulation equations, we apply the method of averaging the conservation laws
[
21
]
. To this end, we define the following averaging operation: For any function
F
,

\overline{F\left(\phi\right)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F\left(\phi(\theta)\right)d\theta.

(7.4)

We then apply this averaging operation to the conservation laws we derived in section
6
for both quasi-continuum models and then collect at the leading order to obtain the remanining modulation equations.

Modulation system of the non-regularized model.

On the one hand, when
\widetilde{p}\neq 1,2
, the modulation system for the non-regularized model (
3.4
) reads, by dropping all the higher order terms in
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{p}}\left(\overline{\phi^{1-\widetilde{p}}}\right)_{T}+2\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2-\widetilde{p}}\left(\overline{\phi^{2-\widetilde{p}}}\right)_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}-\frac{K^{2}}{2}\overline{\phi_{\theta}^{2}}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

In addition, when
\widetilde{p}=1,2
, the two corresponding modulation systems read,

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.6)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\log(\phi)}\right)_{T}+2\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}-\frac{K^{2}}{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

and

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.7)

\displaystyle-\left(\overline{\phi^{-1}}\right)_{T}+2\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\log(\phi)}\right)_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}-\frac{K^{2}}{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

respectively.

Modulation system of the regularized model.

On the other hand, the modulation system for the regularized model (
3.7
) reads, by ignoring all the higher order terms in
\epsilon
, when
\widetilde{p}\neq 2
,

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.8)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{1-\widetilde{p}}}\right)_{T}+2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\overline{\phi^{2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)}}+\frac{K^{2}}{12}\left[\left(\overline{\phi^{1-\widetilde{p}}}\right)_{\theta}\right]^{2}\right]_{T}+\left[\frac{2\left(1-\widetilde{p}\right)}{\left(2-\widetilde{p}\right)}\overline{\phi^{2-\widetilde{p}}}-\frac{K\Omega}{6}\overline{\left[\left(\phi^{1-\widetilde{p}}\right)_{\theta}\right]^{2}}\right]_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

When
\widetilde{p}=2
, the modulation system reads,

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.9)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{-1}}\right)_{T}-2\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{X}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\overline{\phi^{-2}}+\frac{K^{2}}{12}\overline{\left[\left(\phi^{-1}\right)_{\theta}\right]^{2}}\bigg)_{T}+\bigg(-2\overline{\log\left(\phi\right)}-\frac{K\Omega}{6}\overline{\left[\left(\phi^{-1}\right)_{\theta}\right]^{2}}\bigg)_{X}

\displaystyle=0.

8
DSW fitting

In this section, we apply the so-called DSW fitting method
[
21
]
to obtain some useful analytical insights on some specific properties of the dispersive shock waves. The properties include the edge speeds and the edge wavenumber of the DSW. We later shall compare these analytical foundings with the numerical results to verify the performance of this theoretical method.

8.1
Discrete lattice model

We first perform the DSW fitting
[
21
]
on the discrete lattice (
2.5
).
We notice that the edge information of the DSW of the discrete lattice model can be obtained upon solving the following two initial value problems which are the so-called
simple wave
ODEs
[
21
,
27
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{dk}{d\overline{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\overline{u}}\omega_{0}}{2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}},\hskip 14.22636ptk(u^{+})=0,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widetilde{k}}{d\overline{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\overline{u}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}-\partial_{\widetilde{k}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}},\hskip 14.22636pt\widetilde{k}(u^{-})=0,

where
\omega_{0}
refers to the linear dispersion relation in Eq. (
2.7
) of the lattice and
\widetilde{\omega}_{s}
denotes the following conjugate dispersion relation of the lattice (
2.5
),

\widetilde{\omega}_{s}\left(\overline{u},\widetilde{k}\right)=-i\omega_{0}\left(\overline{u},i\widetilde{k}\right)=2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}\text{sinh}(\widetilde{k}).

(8.2)

A direct integration of the two boundary value problems in Eqs. (
8.1
) yields,

\displaystyle k\left(\overline{u}\right)

\displaystyle=2\cos^{-1}\left(\left(\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{+}}\right)^{-\frac{\widetilde{p}}{2}}\right),

(8.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}\left(\overline{u}\right)

\displaystyle=2\text{cosh}^{-1}\left(\left(\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{-}}\right)^{-\frac{\widetilde{p}}{2}}\right).

Moreover, the edge-speed predictions of the lattice DSW are given as follows,

\displaystyle s^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\omega_{0}}{\partial k}\left(k^{-},u^{-}\right)=4\left(u^{+}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}-2\left(u^{-}\right)^{\widetilde{p}},

(8.4)

\displaystyle s^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{\widetilde{k}}\left(\widetilde{k}^{+},u^{+}\right)=\frac{4\left(u^{+}u^{-}\right)^{\frac{\widetilde{p}}{2}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{u^{-}}{u^{+}}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}-1}}{\widetilde{k}^{+}}.

where
\widetilde{k}^{+}=\widetilde{k}\left(u^{+}\right)
.

8.2
Non-regularized model

Similarly, to perform DSW fitting for the non-regularized model (
3.4
), we solve initial-value problems in system (
8.1
), with the lattice linear dispersion relation
\omega_{0}\left(\overline{u},k\right)
replaced with
\Omega_{0}\left(\overline{u},K\right)
in Eq. (
3.5
).
We notice further that the conjugate dispersion relation for the non-regularized model (
3.4
) reads,

\Omega_{s}(\overline{u},\widetilde{K})=-i\Omega_{0}(\overline{u},i\widetilde{K})=2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}\widetilde{K}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{6}\right).

(8.5)

It then follows that,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{6}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2\widetilde{p}}}=\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{+}},

(8.6)

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{6}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2\widetilde{p}}}=\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{-}}.

Since
K^{-}=K(u^{-})
and
\widetilde{K}^{+}=\widetilde{K}(u^{+})
, we know

\displaystyle\left(K^{-}\right)^{2}=\frac{6}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(1-\left(\frac{u^{+}}{u^{-}}\right)^{\frac{2\widetilde{p}}{3}}\right),

(8.7)

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{K}^{+}\right)^{2}=\frac{6}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\left(\frac{u^{-}}{u^{+}}\right)^{\frac{2\widetilde{p}}{3}}-1\right),

and the edge speeds are given as follows,

\displaystyle s^{-}=\partial_{K}\Omega_{0}\left(u^{-},K^{-}\right)=2\left(u^{-}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}\left(3\left(\frac{u^{+}}{u^{-}}\right)^{\frac{2\widetilde{p}}{3}}-2\right),

(8.8)

\displaystyle s^{+}=\frac{\Omega_{s}}{\widetilde{K}}\left(u^{+},\widetilde{K}^{+}\right)=2\left(u^{+}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}\left(\frac{u^{-}}{u^{+}}\right)^{\frac{2\widetilde{p}}{3}}.

8.3
Regularized model Eq. (
3.7
)

Finally, for the DSW fitting of the continuum regularized model (
3.7
), we again solve the two initial-value problems in system (
8.1
) with the linear dispersion relation given by
\Omega_{0}(\overline{u},K)
in Eq. (
3.8
). Meanwhile, notice that the associated conjugate dispersion of the regularized model (
3.7
) is given as follows,

\widetilde{\Omega}_{s}\left(\overline{u},\widetilde{K}\right)=-i\Omega_{0}\left(\overline{u},i\widetilde{K}\right)=\frac{2\overline{u}^{\widetilde{p}}\widetilde{K}}{1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{6}}.

(8.9)

Similarly, this yields,

\displaystyle\log\left|\overline{u}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{12}+\log\left(6+\epsilon^{2}K^{2}\right)\right)+C,

(8.10)

\displaystyle\log\left|\overline{u}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{12}+\log\left(6-\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}\right)\right)+\widetilde{C},

where
C,\widetilde{C}
are two constants of integration.

Applying the initial conditions, we end up with,

\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{+}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{12}+\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{6}\right)\right),

(8.11)

\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{\overline{u}}{u^{-}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{12}+\log\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{6}\right)\right).

Then, we know at the two edges of the DSW, the wavenumbers are given respectively as,
K^{-}=K\left(u^{-}\right)
, and
\widetilde{K}^{+}=\widetilde{K}\left(u^{+}\right)
, and the associated trailing and leading edge speeds, denoted by
s^{-},s^{+}
respectively, are given as follows,

\displaystyle s^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Omega_{0}}{\partial K}\left(u^{-},K^{-}\right)=\frac{2\left(u^{-}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\left(K^{-}\right)^{2}}{6}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\left(K^{-}\right)^{2}}{6}\right)^{2}},

(8.12)

\displaystyle s^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\Omega}_{s}\left(u^{+},\widetilde{K}^{+}\right)}{\widetilde{K}^{+}}=\frac{2\left(u^{+}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}}{1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\left(\widetilde{K}^{+}\right)^{2}}{6}}.

Finally, we recall that for the regularized model (
3.7
), when
\widetilde{p}=1,2
, we also have the theoretical predictions on the DSW solitonic amplitude based on the speed-amplitude relations derived in Eqs. (
5.8
) and (
5.22
). Namely, when
\widetilde{p}=1/2,2/3
, the theoretical predictions on the DSW amplitude read,

\displaystyle a^{+}_{\widetilde{p}=1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{3s^{+}}{2}-2\sqrt{u^{+}}\right)^{2}-u^{+}\right],

(8.13)

\displaystyle a^{+}_{\widetilde{p}=2/3}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\sqrt{3s^{+}-2\left(u^{+}\right)^{2/3}}-\left(u^{+}\right)^{1/3}\right)^{3}-u^{+}\right].

In this section, we discuss in detail, both analytically and numerically, the rarefaction waves emerging from the numerical simulation. We notice that the rarefaction wave can be described by the self-similar solutions of the dispersionless system of the PDE which, for both Eqs. (
3.4
) and (
3.7
), reads

u_{T}+2u^{\tilde{p}}u_{X}=0,

(9.1)

with the upward step-like initial condition given by

u(X,0)=\begin{cases}u^{-},\hskip 14.22636ptX\leq 0,\\
u^{+},\hskip 14.22636ptX>0,\end{cases}

(9.2)

where now
u^{-}<u^{+}
.

To compute the self-similar solutions to Eq. (
9.1
), we assume the following self-similar ansatz,

u(X,T)=S\left(\kappa\right),\quad\kappa=\frac{X}{T}.

(9.3)

Substitution of the ansatz (
9.3
) into the Eq. (
9.1
) yields

-\kappa S_{\kappa}+2S^{\tilde{p}}S_{\kappa}=0.

(9.4)

We then solve for
S
in Eq. (
9.4
) to obtain the following analytical self-similar solution,

u(X,T)=\begin{cases}u^{-},\quad X\leq 2T\left(u^{-}\right)^{\widetilde{p}},\\
\left(\frac{X}{2T}\right)^{\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}},\quad 2T\left(u^{-}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}<X\leq 2T\left(u^{+}\right)^{\widetilde{p}},\\
u^{+},\quad X>2T\left(u^{+}\right)^{\widetilde{p}}.\end{cases}

(9.5)

Figure
4
shows the comparison of the numerical rarefaction waves with the analytical self-similar solution in Eq. (
9.5
) for the case of
\widetilde{p}=1/2
. From the figure
4
, we can clearly see the close alignment of the analytical solution with the its numerical correspondences. This suggests that the two quasi-continuum models provide a reasonable approximation to the rarefaction waves observed numerically in the discrete lattice (
2.5
).

u\left(X,0\right)=u^{+}-\left(\frac{u^{+}-u^{-}}{2}\right)\bigg[\tanh\bigg(\delta\left(X-a\right)\bigg)-\tanh\bigg(\delta\left(X-b\right)\bigg)\bigg],

10.1
Numerical preliminaries

Before we present all the numerical results, we briefly mention the methods which we applied to numerically compute the three edge features of the DSW including: The leading and trailing edge speeds, denoted as
s^{+}
and
s^{-}
, respectively, and the trailing-edge wavenumber
K^{-}
.

Leading-edge speed.

For
s^{+}
, we first pinpoint the leading-edge location. We treat the associated
x
coordinate of the highest peak of the DSW as the location of the leading edge, and we denote this specific point as
X^{+}
. Then the speed
s^{+}
is simply calculated as follows,

s^{+}=\frac{X^{+}-b}{t_{f}},

(10.2)

where
t_{f}
refers to the total simulation time. Meanwhile, it is important to notice that when computing
s^{+}
of the DSW of the lattice model (
2.5
), we have to further divide the
s^{+}
in Eq. (
10.2
) by
\epsilon
, as
T=\epsilon t
, and according to the chain rule,
\frac{dX}{dT}=\epsilon^{-1}\frac{dX}{dt}
.

Trailing-edge speed.

For
s^{-}
, we simply need to replace the
X^{+}
by
X^{-}
, which is the trailing-edge location of the DSW. However, it is more complex to measure the trailing-edge location
X^{-}
and its measurement cannot be exact. Alternative there shall be various elaborate methods to estimate
X^{-}
, we provide a relatively easy way to estimate
X^{-}
. Firstly, we define the following two quantities,

\displaystyle I^{u}=u^{-}+\frac{|u^{-}-u^{+}|}{N},

(10.3)

\displaystyle I^{l}=u^{-}-\frac{|u^{-}-u^{+}|}{N},

where
N
is a positive integer. Typically, we utilize
N=8
in our numerical simulations. Then, we extrapolate the local maxima and minima of the DSW within the windows of
\left(I^{u}+\delta,I^{u}-\delta\right)
,
\left(I^{l}-\delta,I^{l}+\delta\right)
, where
\delta
is some small numbers which can be choosen to be
\delta=\frac{|u^{-}-u^{+}|}{10}
. Next, we apply a least-square based method to fit two straight lines through these local maxima and the local minima so that these two lines shall intersect at a point in the vicinity of the trailing edge of the DSW. We treat such the point of intersection as the trailing-edge location of the DSW and we denote it by
X^{-}
. To compute the trailing-edge speed
s^{-}
of the DSW, we use the same formula as in Eq. (
10.2
) with
X^{+}
replaced with
X^{-}
to measure the trailing-edge speed
s^{-}
.

T=150(t=1500)
. Notice that the first column depicts the comparison of the DSW of the non-regularized model (

. Finally, note that the red dots refer to the lattice DSW data, while the solid blue curve denote that of the quasi-continuum models.

Trailing-edge wavenumber.

For the trailing-edge wavenumber, we shall not apply the same way to measure such the quantity in both quasi-continuum models Eqs. (
3.4
), (
3.7
) and the discrete lattice (
2.5
). Firstly, for the quasi-continuum models, to estimate the trailing-edge wavenumber
K^{-}
, we first find the nearest local peak to the trailing-edge location
N^{-}
, and compute the associated
x
coordinate of such the peak, denoted as
X_{1}^{-}
. Then, we find another local peak of the DSW which is just right next to
X_{1}^{-}
, and we denote its location as
X_{2}^{-}
. With these two locations of the adjacent peak, we can compute the wavelength
\lambda^{-}=X_{2}^{-}-X_{1}^{-}
at the trailing edge, and then use the following formula to estimate
K^{-}
,

K^{-}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda^{-}}.

(10.4)

On the other hand, for the DSW of the discrete lattice (
2.5
), we use an alternative, but equivalent method. First we recall that our field variable is dependent on the co-traveling frame. Namely,
u_{n}(t)=u(kn-\omega t)
. Then, clearly,

u(kn-\omega t)=u\left(\omega\left(\frac{k}{\omega}n-t\right)\right).

(10.5)

The key thought here is to measure the wave frequency
\omega
. This can be done in a similar fashion as we performed for the computation of the wavenumber of the qausi-continuum models in Eq. (
10.4
). That is to say, we fix the spatial variable
n=n^{-}
, where
n^{-}
refers to the trailing-edge location of the lattice DSW. Then, we observe that the field
u
in Eq. (
10.5
) is essentially a time-series data that only depend on
t
. Then we seek for the wavelength of the adjacent peaks of this time-series data, denoted as
\lambda^{-}_{t}=t^{-}_{2}-t^{-}_{1}
. Then the wave frequency simply is estimated as
\omega=2\pi/\lambda^{-}_{t}
. Once the wave frequency is known, the remaining task is to compute the quantity of
k/\omega
, since the multiplication of
k/\omega
and the known
\omega
will certainly yield the wavenumber
k
. To this end, we pick the grid
n_{2}^{-}
which is just right next to the trailing-edge location
n^{-}
so that
n_{2}^{-}=n^{-}+1
. Then, we observe that the time-series data of
u_{n_{2}^{-}}(t)
differs from that of
u_{n^{-}}(t)
via a phase shift by
k/\omega
amount. This phase shift can be easily measured (e.g. by looking at the
x
vertical distance between the same local max of both
u_{n^{-}}(t)
and
u_{n_{2}^{-}}(t)
), and we finally obtain our estimation for the trailing-edge wavenumber
k^{-}
for the lattice DSW. However, a nature question can be why we do not simply try to apply the formula in Eq. (
10.4
) to calculate
k^{-}
? The reason for this is that when we perform the simulation of the discrete lattice (
2.5
), since
X=\epsilon n
, we know the lattice spacing is
\epsilon
. As mentioned before,
\epsilon=0.1
in all the relevant simulations, so this indicates that the spatial resolution may not be well enough for us to apply the method in Eq. (
10.4
) for wavenumber estimation of the lattice DSW. However, on the other hand, the temporal grid surely has much better resolution than the spatial grid does as the temporal grid spacing, which is the time step, is much smaller than the spatial lattice spacing in the numerical simulation of the lattice (
2.5
), and the wavenumber estimated with this temporal frequency approach will yield much accurate results.

Conclusions and future directions

In this paper, we revisit an integrable discrete lattice model which serves as a dispersive regulation of the dispersionless Hopf equation, and we have proposed and studied a non-integrable variant of such an integrable model. We conduct a systematic numerical studies on the evolution of the associated Riemann problems of the discrete lattice and discover only two dispersive wave structures shown in the phase diagram
1
. Then, we derive two quasi-continuum models to approximate these two wave structures in the lattice. Based on the two quasi-continuum models, we compute their respective traveling and periodic wave solutions for some particular cases of
\widetilde{p}
, provide some necessary and important conservation laws serving as the preliminaries of Whitham analysis. With the methodology of averaging the conservation laws
[
16
]
, we derive the full closed modulation system for both quasi-continuum models and also perform reduction of the Whitham modulation equations near both the linear and solitonic limits to obtain the simple-wave ODEs which encode important information on the edge features of the DSW. We solve these simple-wave ODEs and gain theoretical predictions on multiple DSW edge features including the edge speeds and wavenumber. We conduct comparisons on not only the spatial profile of the DSW and RW between the lattice (
2.5
) and both the quasi-continuum models Eqs. (
3.4
), (
3.7
), but numerous different DSW edge features as well. We find that, through these numerical comparisons, both quasi-continuum models provide a reasonably well approximation to the DSW for situation where the values of parameter
\widetilde{p}
are small, but only good approximation for
\widetilde{p}>1
if the jump
\Delta
of the Riemann initial data is small.

There are still a variety of interesting open questions remaining, and we only list a few of them. Firstly, we have mentioned in the comparison section
10
that, for the case when
\widetilde{p}>1
, both quasi-continuum models failed to yield a good approximation to the DSW of the lattice (
2.5
) if the jump
\Delta
is large. This particular failure of the two quasi-continuum models will naturally lead one to think of other potential dispersive quasi-continuum models which can be possibly derived by performing a different set of change of spatial and temporal variables rather than Eq. (
3.1
) and also a possible scaling of the amplitude of the wave field of
u
. In addition, we notice that one can also investigate the lattice in Eq. (
2.2
) where the potential
\Phi
is not a power function. For instance, an interesting alternative potential can be the exponential function. The reason to change the potential
\Phi
is as follows. We recall that we have only discovered the DSW and RW in the lattice (
2.2
) when a power-type potential is applied. Hence, a different type of potential may excite wave structures other than the DSW and RW so that the lattice becomes more versatile as a media to model various nonlinear dispersive waves. Lastly, it may also be relevant to consider some other discretization scheme for the scaler conservation law in Eq. (
2.2
). We notice that our lattice (
2.5
) uses the standard central difference formula to discretize the spatial derivative of
u_{x}
, so with a different numerical scheme, one shall obtain a distinct lattice, and it may have more abundant structures of wave. All these proposed and open directions are currently under consideration and will be reported in future publications.
