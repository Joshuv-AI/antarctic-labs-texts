Changes in urine dipstick proteinuria and risk of dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury and subsequent ESRD
Authors: Sung Keun Park, Min-Ho Kim, Chang-Mo Oh, Eunhee Ha, Soonsu Shin, Sungjin Kim, Min Hyung Jung, Dosang Cho, Jae-Hong Ryoo, Ah Rah Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is associated with poor prognosis. Dialysis-requiring AKI and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) represents the severe form of AKI. Proteinuria is a reliable prognostic factor for kidney diseases. However, no study has investigated the association between changes in proteinuria and the risk of severe AKI. We included 2,021,548 Koreans who underwent health check-ups in 2005–2006 and 2009. Changes in proteinuria were categorized into four groups (negative: negative → negative; resolved: proteinuria ≥ 1+ → negative; incident: negative → proteinuria ≥ 1+; persistent: proteinuria ≥ 1+ → proteinuria ≥ 1+). based on urine dipstick test. Over a median follow-up of 11.84 years, we assessed adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) (adjusted HR [95% CI]) for incident dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI. The risk for dialysis-requiring AKI was highest in persistent proteinuria (3.643 [2.881–4.607]), followed by incident (1.922 [1.694–2.181]), and resolved proteinuria (1.453 [1.194–1.767]), compared with negative proteinuria (reference). The risk for ESRD also increased in order of persistent (22.883 [15.067–34.755]), incident (7.508 [5.548–10.163]), resolved (4.813 [2.970–7.800]), and negative proteinuria (reference) with the magnified HR. This pattern of relationship was identically observed in participants without chronic kidney disease. All patterns of change in proteinuria were significantly associated with the increased risk of dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD, even in resolved proteinuria. Change in proteinuria may be potential predictor for severe AKI. Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as a sudden decline in renal function, characterized by an increase in serum creatinine and/or a decrease in urine output1. AKI encompasses a broad spectrum of renal dysfunction, ranging from mild impairment to severe kidney failure requiring renal replacement therapy2. Although advances in critical care have improved survival, patients who experience AKI remain at substantial risk for adverse long-term outcomes3–5.

In particular, severe AKI requiring dialysis and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are major clinical issues associated with poor prognosis in AKI patients5–8. Therefore, it is clinically important to investigate prognostic factors for predicting severe AKI requiring dialysis and progression to ESRD in terms of setting therapeutic strategy for AKI.

Proteinuria is a reliable indicator for injury in glomerulus and renal tubular endothelium. Proteinuria predicts future decline in renal function and kidney-related adverse outcome, reflecting kidney damage9. In critically ill patients like burn10, cirrhosis11, and COVID-1912, proteinuria at admission was significantly associated with the increased risk for dialysis-requiring AKI and mortality. These findings suggest that proteinuria may have a role in predicting severe AKI and progression to ESRD in AKI patients. However, evidence supporting this role remains limited. Moreover, proteinuria is not static but can vary over time through the effect of physical and clinical conditions13,14. Nonetheless, no study has examined association between changes in proteinuria and the risk of severe AKI requiring dialysis and subsequent ESRD.

Therefore, in the nationwide cohort study, we evaluated the risk of dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI in relation to baseline proteinuria and changes in proteinuria among 2,020,880 Korean adults.

Methods

Data sources

In Korea, National Health Insurance Corporation (NHIC) provides the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) to the Korean population. NHIS covers most of Korean population (over 97%), and the medical information of NHIS is stored in the National Health Information Database (NHID). Most medical institutions have a contract with the NHIC and provide the medical information of their healthcare users and patients to the NHID. Therefore, NHID represents the medical service usage of the entire Korean population. Korean adults over 40 years of age should receive a national health check-up served by the NHIS annually or biennially, and medical information for their health check-up is stored in the NHID.

In recent years, the NHIS has provided a sampled database for research purposes after deleting information about personal identification. The sampled data includes information of health check-up, which is linked to the medical information about development of dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI in Statistics Korea. Ethics approvals for the study protocol and data analysis were obtained from the institutional review board of Kyung Hee University Hospital. The institutional review board waived the requirement for informed consent because researchers only retrospectively accessed a de-identified database for the study.

Study participants

The study participants were Korean adults who underwent a national health check-up between 2005 and 2006 and 2009. We initially enrolled 2,589,163 participants whose records of urine dipstick proteinuria could be identified during a health check-up between 2005 and 2006 and 2009. Among 2,589,163 participants, 568,283 participants were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria, which might influence adverse renal outcomes as follows: 107,618 had previously diagnosed with cancer (C00-C97), 3,343 had a history of chronic kidney disease (CKD) (N18), 3,054 had a history of unknown kidney disease (N19), 3,839 had a history of AKI (N17), 2,356 had a history of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (M32), 21,346 had a history of diabetic nephropathy (E10.2, E11.2, E12.2, E13.2 and E14.2), 5,968 had a history of obstructive and reflux uropathy (N13), 124 had a history of dialysis, 160,682 were prescribed for non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs longer than 30 days, 809 had no information of serum creatinine, and 264,955 were at estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) under 60.

Because some participants met more than one of the exclusion criteria, 2,020,880 participants were included in the final analysis and monitored for incident dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), registered in the NHID. The total follow-up period was 23,924,913.25 person-years, and average follow-up period was 11.84 (standard deviation: 2.02) person-years.

Definition of change in proteinuria

Information on proteinuria was obtained from a urine dipstick test performed during a biennial health checkup. According to the urine dipstick test at each health check-up between 2005 and 2006 and 2009, study participants were categorized into two groups (negative and proteinuria ≥ 1+) at each health checkup. The study participants were further categorized into four groups based on the changes in proteinuria between the first checkup (2005–2006) and second checkup (2009); negative (negative → negative), resolved (proteinuria ≥ 1+ → negative), incident (negative → proteinuria ≥ 1+) and persistent (proteinuria ≥ 1+ → proteinuria ≥ 1+).

Health survey examinations and laboratory measurements

The items of the medical health checkup included a questionnaire on lifestyle and past medical histories, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory measurements. The smoking amount was expressed in pack-year, which was calculated using a smoking-related equation. (l pack-year = 1 year x 1 pack per day). Alcohol intake was defined as intake more than 3 times per week at least. Physical activity was defined as performing moderate-intensity physical activity at least 30 min per day for more than 4 days each week or vigorous-intensity physical activity at least 20 min per day for more than 4 days each week15. Anti-hypertensive medication was investigated for taking medications including angiotensin II converting enzyme inhibitor, angiotensin II receptor blocker, calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, or diuretics. Study participants with history of taking anti-hypertensive medication were determined in participants who took anti-hypertensive medications longer than 30 days.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (meters). Systolic blood pressure (BP) and diastolic BP were measured by trained examiners. The following laboratory data were measured simultaneously when the participants underwent health examinations: fasting blood glucose, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and serum creatinine (SCr). Kidney function was assessed by eGFR calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration Eq. (16).

Outcome definitions

The NHID was linked to the data on diagnosed diseases from Statistics Korea. In this study, the date of study entry was the date of the last health check-up in 2009 (baseline). The follow-up period for the study participants was defined as the time from the baseline study date (date of health check-up at 2009) to the date of renal outcome diagnosis, loss to follow-up, death, or the final follow-up date (December 31, 2021). Dialysis-requiring AKI was defined as undergoing hemodialysis within 3 days after onset of AKI. ESRD subsequent to AKI was determined in patients whose AKI progressed to ESRD requiring long-term hemodialysis without recover.

Dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI were identified using ICD codes recorded in the NHID. In addition to ICD codes, the NHID also contains information on procedure codes performed for each patient. The procedure code for hemodialysis is O7020, and that for continuous renal replacement therapy is O7031. Specifically, among individuals with the ICD code N17 (AKI), those who were confirmed to have the procedure code O7020 were classified as having dialysis-requiring AKI. Furthermore, among individuals with a history of ICD code N17 (AKI), those who were subsequently identified as having the ICD code for ESRD (N18.6) were classified as having ESRD subsequent to AKI.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and as percentages of the number for categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the X2-test were used to analyze the statistical differences among the characteristics of the study participants at the time of enrollment in relation to the four groups of changes in proteinuria. The person-years were calculated as the sum of follow-up times from the date of baseline (date of health check-up at 2009) up to the date of incident dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI. In case of participants without incident dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI, the person-years were calculated from the date of baseline to December 31, 2021. For participants who were lost to follow-up or death during follow-up, person-years were calculated from the date of baseline to the date of last health check-up right before lost to follow-up or death.

To evaluate the risk of dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI in relation to changes in proteinuria, we used Cox proportional hazards models in calculating adjusted hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for incident dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI according to changes in proteinuria (negative, resolved, incident, and persistent). Cox proportional hazard models were adjusted for multiple confounding factors including age, sex, BMI, systolic BP, fasting blood glucose, eGFR, total cholesterol, GGT, smoking amount(pack-year), alcohol intake, physical activity, hypertensive medication 1 (angiotensin II converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker), and hypertensive medication 2 (calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, or diuretics).

To test the validity of the Cox proportional hazard model, we checked the proportional hazard assumption. The proportional hazard assumption was assessed using the log-minus-log survival function and found to be graphically unviolated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Discussion

In the present study, we showed that that the risks for dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI increased in order of persistent, incident, resolved, and negative proteinuria. In particular, persistent proteinuria was associated with the considerably increased risk for dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI, compared with other groups. This finding indicates that long-standing proteinuria may be most detrimental to AKI patients. In addition, it is presumed that pre-admission proteinuria is a useful indicator for the severity and progression of AKI. Although no study has investigated the association between changes in proteinuria and AKI, our study findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that proteinuria is not only a predictor of AKI but also a reliable indicator of AKI severity and prognosis. Results from a meta-analysis and a case-control study have demonstrated that urinary albumin excretion manifested by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio appears to be a promising, low-cost biomarker for predicting AKI17,18. A population-based study indicated that urine dipstick proteinuria was dose-dependently associated with postoperative AKI and 30-day unplanned readmission independent of preoperative eGFR19. Pre-admission proteinuria before AKI and post-AKI proteinuria were important prognostic factors for recovery from dialysis-requiring AKI and for CKD progression to ESRD, respectively20,21. By incorporating the trajectory of proteinuria prior to AKI, our study provides additional insight into prognostic value of proteinuria for AKI. Even among patients with the same proteinuria status (negative or positive), the risk of severe AKI may vary depending on prior proteinuria status. These findings suggest that serial assessment of proteinuria may be useful for risk stratification and prognostication in patients at high risk for AKI.

Remission of proteinuria is regarded as good sign in medical condition. Evidence has indicated that decreased or resolved proteinuria is associated with improved prognosis in kidney diseases22–24. Interestingly, our analysis shows that the risk for dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI continues to increase even in resolved proteinuria. This result indicates that the risk for severe AKI and ESRD remains high in people with once-manifested proteinuria even after remission of proteinuria. Plausible explanation for this finding may be that once manifested proteinuria may reflect adverse renal condition with long-lasting effect on kidney. Proteinuria is a marker for both epithelial damage and endothelial dysfunction on glomerulus and renal tubule25,26. Filtered protein has toxic effects on proximal tubular epithelium, which enhances tubulointerstitial damage like tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis27. These pathophysiologic states may persist even after remission of proteinuria, and consequently, predispose AKI patients to critical conditions including requiring-dialysis and permanent loss of kidney function. Another possible explanation is that serious cardiovascular conditions, such as heart failure or acute coronary syndrome, may lead to transient proteinuria through cardiorenal syndrome, mediated by hemodynamic alterations and systemic vascular endothelial dysfunction28. These adverse cardiovascular diseases may have exerted sustained effects even after the resolution of transient proteinuria, thereby increasing the risk of dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI. Finally, positive proteinuria may have been misclassified as negative proteinuria due to the inherent limitations of the urine dipstick test. In such cases, resolved proteinuria may in fact be persistent proteinuria. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing our findings regarding the association between resolved proteinuria and severe AKI to otherwise healthy individuals without underlying comorbidities.

There has been growing awareness for the close linking between AKI and CKD. While AKI is a risk factor of the development of CKD, CKD patients are more predisposed to AKI29. In particular, it has been reported that AKI superimposed to CKD is more likely to have poor prognosis than AKI in patients without CKD30. Therefore, to minimize the effect of CKD on AKI, we conducted subgroup analysis among participants without preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 80 ml/min/1.73 m2). Even in subgroup with preserved renal function, the risk for dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD was highest in persistent proteinuria, followed by incident, resolved, and negative proteinuria. This finding suggests that long-standing proteinuria, new-onset proteinuria and once manifested proteinuria with remission are associated with severe AKI and poor prognosis independent of renal function.

Several limitations should be considered. First, residual confounding could not be completely addressed. Although we adjusted for multiple covariates, we were unable to account for the long-term cumulative effects of hypertension and hyperglycemia, and cardiovascular conditions (e.g., heart failure and acute coronary syndrome). These factors, along with unmeasured contributors such as acute clinical conditions (e.g., sepsis and surgery), healthcare-related factors (e.g., hospitalization and ICU admission), and exposure to nephrotoxic medications, may have influenced both proteinuria and AKI outcomes, potentially leading to residual confounding. Second, dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD were defined using ICD and procedure codes from NHID, which precluded precise assessment of severity and may have limited our ability to capture the full clinical spectrum of AKI. Future studies using detailed patient-level clinical data are warranted. Third, proteinuria was assessed using urine dipstick testing, a semi-quantitative method with inherent variability influenced by hydration status and other physiological conditions. This may have resulted in misclassification, particularly in the resolved proteinuria group, which could have included individuals with true negative or persistent proteinuria, thereby affecting the observed associations. Fourth, our study participants are composed of relatively healthy individuals undergoing health screening, with excluding those with CKD and major comorbidities. Therefore, we acknowledge that our findings may not be generalizable to hospitalized or critically ill populations, in whom AKI is most clinically relevant.

In conclusion, changes in proteinuria were significantly associated with the severe AKI and ESRD. The risk for dialysis-requiring AKI and ESRD subsequent to AKI increased in order of persistent, incident, and resolved proteinuria, compared with negative proteinuria. This pattern of relationship was identically found in participants without CKD. These findings suggest that assessment of proteinuria trajectory may be useful for prognostication in patients with AKI.
