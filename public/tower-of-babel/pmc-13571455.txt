Response to: ‘Re-assessing the clinical significance of long-term neuromuscular alterations following onabotulinumtoxinA in chronic migraine’
Authors: Tina Plensäll, Maarika Liik, Artor Pogosean, Annika Eriksson, Malin Garrett, Jan Olszowy, Sven Jackmann, Anna Rostedt Punga
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We thank Argyriou and colleagues for their thoughtful comments on our study. We agree that onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNTA) is an effective and generally well-tolerated treatment for chronic migraine and that extensive trial and real-world evidence supports its continued clinical use. Indeed, our own cohort demonstrated a significant and sustained reduction in migraine days (Plensäll et al., 2026). Our study was not intended to challenge the established benefit-risk profile of BoNTA or to discourage its use, but to characterize and highlight neuromuscular effects that may not be captured by conventional efficacy and general safety outcomes.

We respectfully disagree, however, that our findings should be regarded exclusively as subclinical. This term may appropriately describe electrophysiological abnormalities detected in otherwise asymptomatic individuals and transient differences in serum neurofilament light chain levels, whose clinical significance remains uncertain. It does not describe symptoms reported by patients. In our study, adverse-event assessment specifically focused on predefined potential BoNTA-related neuromuscular symptoms, including ptosis, dysphagia, diplopia, blurred vision, and shoulder or neck weakness. Among the 33 participants with available side-effect data, ptosis was reported by 13 (39%), shoulder weakness by 10 (30%), swallowing or chewing difficulties by 4 (12%), and diplopia by 4 (12%) (Plensäll et al., 2026). These symptoms may have been mild or transient and did not necessarily outweigh the therapeutic benefit, but they were clinical rather than subclinical observations.

The apparent discrepancy between our findings and the lower adverse-event frequencies reported in the studies cited in the Letter is likely explained, at least in part, by treatment duration and differences in ascertainment and study populations. Argyriou et al. primarily evaluated long-term adherence in a selected extension cohort (Argyriou et al., 2022). Eligibility required an initial response of at least 50%, completion of the preceding 3-year extension study, and consistent completion of headache diaries. The preceding 3-year extension study by Vikelis et al. was similarly responder-enriched, requiring a ≥ 50% reduction in average monthly headache days after three treatment sessions; its findings therefore provide limited information on tolerability among nonresponders or patients who discontinued earlier (Vikelis et al., 2018). Safety was assessed from adverse effects reported and recorded in the patients' files; the publication does not describe systematic, symptom-specific questioning for ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, blurred vision, or shoulder and neck weakness. Treatment adherence, satisfaction, and the absence of discontinuation because of a severe adverse event are important outcomes, but they are not sensitive measures of the frequency of mild moderate or intermittent neuromuscular symptoms.

Similarly, Navarro-Pérez et al. assessed safety through patient self-report, general non-directed questioning, physical examination, and review of medical records (Navarro-Pérez et al., 2024). Patients who had discontinued BoNTA because of poor tolerability were excluded. This design is well suited to identify clinically prominent events documented during routine care, such as ptosis or visible temporal and trapezius atrophy, but may under-detect intermittent or subjective symptoms, as well as muscle weakness, unless they are specifically sought. The exclusion of patients with poor tolerability also introduces tolerability-related survivor bias. Consequently, raw adverse-event proportions from these studies cannot be directly compared with those obtained through targeted ascertainment of predefined neuromuscular symptoms. Conversely, our estimates from a modest, all-female cohort should not be interpreted as population-level incidence rates and should be confirmed in larger prospective cohorts.

Santoro et al. provide valuable complementary real-world evidence from a large retrospective cohort (Santoro et al., 2025). However, their methods state that treatment-emergent adverse events were recorded, and that database entries were cross-checked against original records, but do not describe standardized, symptom-specific questioning or a dedicated neuromuscular examination. Although the observation period extended to 11 years for some patients, the median follow-up was 15 months (interquartile range, 6–36 months), only 66 of 579 patients reached 60 months, and 39 patients who discontinued after the first injection cycle were excluded. The recorded events comprised neck pain (n = 28) and ptosis (n = 4). Accordingly, the absence of newly recorded adverse events after 36 months cannot exclude milder subjective ocular, bulbar, or proximal weakness symptoms that were not specifically sought, particularly in the context of diminishing numbers at risk. These data support long-term effectiveness and routine-care tolerability, but do not directly answer the symptom-ascertainment question addressed by our study.

The same distinction is relevant to the neurophysiological findings. Local chemodenervation is an expected pharmacological effect of BoNTA, but the persistence and cumulative patterns of reduced CMAP amplitudes, denervation activity, and myopathic motor-unit changes, together with neuromuscular transmission abnormalities in a non-injected muscle, warrant further investigation. These findings typically correlate with muscle weakness or fatigability. However, we did not interpret these observations as evidence of progressive systemic neurodegeneration. All NfL values remained within published reference ranges, and we explicitly acknowledged the uncertainty of their clinical significance and the possible influence of age.

Finally, we did not propose routine electrophysiological or NfL monitoring for every asymptomatic patient. Rather, our findings support greater clinical awareness of neuromuscular symptoms during long-term treatment and consideration of targeted neurophysiological evaluation in selected patients, for example those with persistent muscle weakness, ocular or bulbar symptoms, high cumulative exposure, or repeated injections into vulnerable muscle groups. Prospective studies using standardized, symptom-specific safety assessments, including symptom severity, duration, functional impact, and temporal relationship to injection cycles, are needed to determine the true frequency and clinical relevance of these effects in larger coh.

In conclusion, the established efficacy and overall favorable safety profile of BoNTA in chronic migraine are not incompatible with the possibility that mild neuromuscular adverse effects are under-recognized when they are not specifically sought. Our findings therefore support informed clinical vigilance and further research, not avoidance of an effective treatment.
