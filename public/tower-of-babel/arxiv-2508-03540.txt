Abstract
We study the performance of different methods for processing information, incorporating narrative selection within an evolutionary model. All agents update their beliefs according to Bayes’ Rule, but some strategically choose the narrative they use in updating according to heterogeneous criteria. We simulate the endogenous composition of the population, considering different laws of motion for the underlying state of the world. We find that conformists—that is, agents that choose the narrative to conform to the average belief in the population—have an evolutionary advantage over other agents across all specifications. The survival chances of the remaining types depend on the uncertainty regarding the state of the world. Agents who tend to develop mild beliefs perform better when the uncertainty is high, whereas agents who tend to develop extreme beliefs perform better when the uncertainty is low. Keywords: beliefs updating, narrative selection, conformism, evolutionary stability. JEL Codes: C73, D80, D83

Introduction

How do people process new information? This question lies at the heart of numerous socio-economic studies
(
Enke 2024
)
. A substantial body of research agrees that people exhibit confirmation bias when updating their beliefs—giving greater weight to information that aligns with their preexisting views. Recent works in this area assume that people actively select narratives that best fit their current beliefs
(
Schwartzstein and Sunderam 2021
)
. However, is this truly the most effective way to interpret new evidence?

In this paper, we argue that this is not the case. We study an evolutionary model encompassing several types of agents using different criteria to choose a narrative—i.e., a particular way to interpret new information. We show that the best-performing type incorporates other agents’ beliefs in the narrative choice (what we will refer to as conformism). Furthermore, we find that other types survive natural selection, and their survival chances depend on the underlying uncertainty regarding the state of the world. Specifically, types that tend to develop extreme beliefs have an evolutionary advantage when uncertainty is low, while those developing mild beliefs have an advantage when the uncertainty is high.

In our model, agents learn about an uncertain binary state of the world. The state evolves over time following a given law of motion. In each period, each agent receives a piece of information about the state. The information process is exogenous. Agents can receive heterogeneous information and have heterogeneous preferences. In particular, we consider five types of agents: anti-conformist, auto-referential, conformist, naive, and skeptical. All agents update their beliefs according to Bayes’ rule. Naive agents know the correct information-generating process and use it when updating beliefs. The other types suffer from information misspecification: they do not know the actual information-generating process. They apply different criteria to choose a narrative to interpret new information.

Auto-referential agents choose the narrative that best fits the information received among all competing interpretations
(
Schwartzstein and Sunderam 2021
)
. The fit of a particular narrative to the information received depends on agents’ prior beliefs, which depend on past information. Conformist agents care about sharing the same beliefs as their peers. We assume that conformist agents wish to match---myopically---their peers’ average prior beliefs.
1
1

1

This allows us to rule out strategic considerations about peers’ choice of narratives.
Anti-conformist agents behave in an opposite way to conformists; skeptical agents behave oppositely to auto-referential agents. Since agents receive different information and have heterogeneous preferences, they may choose different narratives.

We adopt the indirect evolutionary approach
(
Güth and Yaari 1992
;
Güth 1995
)
to study which narrative selection criterion is best in improving agents’ ability to predict the state (i.e., which one brings agents the closest to the “truth”). With the help of numerical simulations, we investigate the evolution of the population over time. In particular, we study four different laws of motion concerning the state of the world. Firstly, we consider an independent state: in each period, the state of the world is the realization of a random variable. Secondly, an independent state with persistence: the state of the world is the same realization of a random variable for a given number of periods. Thirdly, an auto-correlated state: in each period, the realization of the state depends on the realization of the state in the previous period. Lastly, a self-fulfilling state: in each period, the probability of each realization of the state is a combination of an exogenous probability and the agents’ average belief about the state.

We assume a random initial distribution of types in the population. All agents start with the same prior belief, assigning equal probability to both states. We define each agent’s evolutionary fitness as their error in predicting the state of the world, namely, the squared difference between their belief and the actual probability of the state. In our benchmark analysis, every ten periods, each agent with an error larger than the average error dies and is reborn with a random type. We then calculate the average long-run distribution of types in the population across simulations for different probabilities of the state of the world.

We find that conformists perform better than all the other types across all the different laws of motion. Indeed, conformists constitute the majority of the population across all specifications and consistently have an error lower than the other types. This result is a consequence of conformists’ ability to adapt and aggregate information. Specifically, since conformists never go too far from the average belief, they take the best out of the other types. They tend to behave skeptically when state uncertainty is high, whereas they update faster when uncertainty is low. Not surprisingly, since anti-conformists behave oppositely to conformists, they are consistently the worst-performing type across all specifications.

Interestingly, the survival chances of the other types depend on the probability of the state of the world (i.e., on the uncertainty). On the one hand, skeptical agents perform well when the uncertainty is high (i.e., the state’s probability is close to
0.5
) because the other agents develop extreme beliefs (thus, far from
0.5
), while skeptical agents develop mild beliefs (hence, closer to
0.5
). On the other hand, the survival chances of naive and auto-referential agents increase when the uncertainty is low (i.e., the probability of the state of the world is far from
0.5
). Indeed, in these situations, it pays to have extreme beliefs, and thus, both naive and auto-referential agents perform relatively better than skeptical agents who are more cautious. This result is evident for three laws of motion: independent state, auto-correlated state, and self-fulfilling state. Instead, when the state persists over time, auto-referential and naive agents have a further advantage even for high uncertainty, since it pays to have firm beliefs given the stickiness of the state.

We perform some robustness checks by varying variables fixed in our benchmark analysis. Our main findings hold across all specifications with some quantitative differences. Notably, as the precision of the information increases, conformists perform relatively worse across all specifications—yet they remain the largest group in nearly all cases. In contrast, the performance of skeptical agents improves, even when there is less uncertainty about the state. Indeed, higher precision implies that agents tend to
receive the same information, worsening conformists’ ability to aggregate information. Similarly, reducing information misspecification worsens conformists’ performance, favoring skeptical agents when uncertainty is high. In other words, conformists’ evolutionary advantage ultimately depends on the set of narratives from which agents can choose.

Our results are also not sensitive to a specification where agents update their types more quickly than in the benchmark. However, we observe a weaker predominance of conformists under this specification. Indeed, with a quicker update, agents develop less extreme beliefs, and thus, the disadvantage of naive and auto-referential agents is lower than under the benchmark. Our results also hold when considering the opposite specification, i.e., when agents update their types more slowly than in the benchmark. Again, in this specification, we observe a weaker predominance of conformists. Interestingly, the reverse logic of the previous scenario may explain this result. Since agents update their type more slowly than in the benchmark, all agents have more time to develop extreme beliefs, and again, the disadvantage of naive and auto-referential agents is lower.

Finally, we study how our results depend on the number of agents interacting. We find that conformists remain the dominant type even when the number of agents is small. Thus, although information aggregation plays a role, the primary source of conformists’ evolutionary advantage is their ability to strategically adapt to the context when selecting a narrative.

Our results have relevant implications. First, we show the importance of considering conformism in future research on strategic narrative selection. Second, we show the coexistence in the long run of agents having different narrative selection criteria. Under this perspective, our results align with experimental evidence from cognitive psychology, which finds that individuals tend to update their beliefs based on the aggregated beliefs
(
Asch 1951
;
Bond and Smith 1996
)
. Coherently with our findings, most of these studies find that people who follow more rigid updating rules coexist with those following the majority
(
Friend et al. 1990
;
Oktar and Lombrozo 2025
)
.

Importantly, in much of the experiments a là Asch, conformists arrive at incorrect beliefs due to deliberate deception by the experimenters. This result does not invalidate our theory for two main reasons. First, the tendency to conform likely develops outside the lab, where it is generally an effective heuristic, even if it fails in an artificial setting. Second, in the experiment, belief aggregation was inherently flawed because experimenters deliberately manipulated beliefs to be false, making conformity suboptimal.
Third, the evolutionary advantage of conformists calls for policies such as financial literacy programs that encourage investors to interpret financial data in line with mainstream economic consensus rather than chasing contrarian, speculative narratives.

Literature review

We contribute to a recent strand of literature - pioneered by
Schwartzstein and Sunderam 2021
- which studies strategic narrative selection: an agent receiving a piece of information can choose alternative interpretations of such information. The agent chooses a narrative---i.e., a particular information-generating process that the agent believes has generated such information---to maximize his utility.
4
4

4

Differently from
Schwartzstein and Sunderam 2021
, we do not consider a strategic Sender providing alternative narratives, and we consider a specific class of information processes.
We contribute to this literature by studying the role of strategic narrative selection in determining evolutionary outcomes, comparing strategic and non-strategic types and, for the former, different purposes they may try to fulfill when choosing a narrative. We find that strategic narrative selection can help agents to survive in a competitive environment. Whereas the literature has focused on strategic narrative selection motivated by reconciling evidence with private beliefs, our analysis shows that it has greater potential (at least from an evolutionary perspective) when motivated by conformism.

Buechel et al. 2023
show that heterogeneous signals paired with heterogeneous information transmission capacities can lead to inefficient information aggregation.
Jadbabaie et al. 2012
combine Bayesian updating with social learning from peers, identifying the conditions leading to successful information aggregation.

We also contribute to the literature on opinion dynamics. Starting from the influential paper by
DeGroot 1974
, many papers have considered economic agents updating their beliefs based on their peers’ opinions. This literature studies the speed and the efficiency of the convergence of beliefs in a network based on the properties of such a structure
(
Golub and Jackson 2012
, e.g., homophily as in)
. For instance,
Förster et al. 2013
,
Buechel et al. 2015
, and
Grabisch et al. 2019
study the properties of convergence to a common belief in the presence of conformist and anti-conformist agents.
Our model incorporates social learning, assuming that individuals may aim to reach a consensus. In contrast with the standard approach, we do not assume that individuals adjust their beliefs to others’ beliefs directly. Instead, we assume that their interpretation of information may depend on a desire to match the average belief.

6

6

Several studies show that imitation is often a successful behavioral rule. See, for instance,
Goerg and Selten 2009
;
Schipper 2009
;
Duersch et al. 2012
;
LiCalzi and Mühlenbernd 2019
;
Alós-Ferrer and Ritschel 2021
. We show that conformists have an evolutionary advantage over the other types. Unlike imitators, conformists in our model do not imitate other—more successful—types but imitate the average belief.
In other words, we merge two strands of literature: agents choose which narrative to use to interpret information among competing narratives. Such a choice may be oriented to conform with others’ beliefs.

Our paper shows that, from an evolutionary perspective, different criteria for narrative selection (and, hence, the interpretation of information) can coexist in the long run. By doing so, we contribute to the vast literature highlighting the possibility of the coexistence of different learning rules. For instance,
Heller 2015
shows that agents with different foresight abilities might coexist in a Prisoner’s Dilemma with an uncertain horizon. The finance literature also shows the possibility that different heuristics coexist
(

;

Anufriev and Hommes 2012b
, see)
.

Finally, we contribute to the debate on confirmation bias. The literature shows that confirmation bias might emerge from the Bayesian updating process
(
Wilson 2014
;
Fryer Jr et al. 2019
)
or in other forms
(
Rabin and Schrag 1999
)
.

7

Rabin and Schrag 1999
show that individuals who interpret new information as supporting their previous hypothesis tend to become over-optimistic about the realization of an event.
Wilson 2014
incorporates perfect recall into Bayesian updating, showing how it can lead to confirmation bias.
Fryer Jr et al. 2019
show that confirmation bias might emerge when individuals interpret uncertain signals in favor of their priors. This behavior can lead to polarization even when individuals receive the same evidence.
We show that an individual basing narrative selection on confirming their prior beliefs will usually not be very successful from an evolutionary perspective. Although auto-referential agents exist in the long run, being skeptical (the opposite behavior) is consistently a better option. At the same time, having a bias towards matching the average belief constitutes a significant evolutionary advantage.

Model

We study a dynamic model with
n
agents. We denote by
I\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
the set of agents. Time is discrete, and we index it with
t\in\mathbbm{N}
.

State of the World.

The state of the world is the realization of a binary random variable:
\omega\in\Omega\coloneqq\{A,B\}
. The state is stochastic:
\omega_{t}
is its realization at time
t
, and we denote with
\hat{\omega}_{t}\coloneqq\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{t}\}
the history of the state realizations up to time
t
. A parameter
q\in(0,1)
governs the evolution of the state. In particular, we define a law of motion
f(\omega_{t};\hat{\omega}_{t-1})
as a function of
q
: for any time
t
,
f(\omega_{t};\hat{\omega}_{t-1})
is the probability that
\omega_{t}
is the realization of the state at time
t
given the realizations of the state up to time
t-1
.
We consider four laws of motion: see Section
3.2
for the details.

Beliefs and Information.

At time
t=0
, agents hold a common prior belief
\mu_{0}
that the state is
\omega=A
. At each time
t
, each agent
i\in I
receives a signal
s_{i,t}\in S=\{a,b\}
, where
a
indicates
\omega=A
and
b
indicates
\omega=B
. The signal has an exogenous precision
p\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
, that is the probability that the signal matches the state, i.e.,
\pi(a|A)=\pi(b|B)=p
.
We assume that some agents are subject to information misspecification. In other words, an agent may not know
p
and think that the precision is either
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
, such that
\frac{1}{2}<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}<1
. At each time
t
, all agents change their beliefs using Bayesian updating after receiving information. However, each agent may update their belief differently. Agent
i
belief’s updating depends on the signal
s_{i,t}
they received and its perceived precision
\rho_{i,t}
. In particular, we denote by
x_{i,t}=(s_{i,t},\rho_{i,t})
the information received by agent
i
and their interpretation of the latter at time
t
, and by
h_{i,t}=\left\{x_{i,1},x_{i,2},\dots,x_{i,t-1}\right\}
the history of agent
i
at time
t
.
Therefore, at each time
t
, the posterior belief of agent
i
that
\omega=A
is:

\mu_{i,t}(s_{i,t},h_{i,t},\rho_{i,t})=\frac{\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|A)\mu_{i,t-1}(h_{i,t})}{\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|A)\mu_{i,t-1}(h_{i,t})+\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|B)(1-\mu_{i,t-1}(h_{i,t}))}

We denote by
\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}
the information generating process associated with precision
\rho_{i,t}
. It holds that
\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(a|A)=\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(b|B)=\rho_{i,t}
and
\mu_{i,-1}(h_{i,0})=\mu_{0}
for any
i\in I
.

Agents’ Types.

Agents are heterogeneous depending on how they process information. In particular, we distinguish five types:

1.

Naive agents
. They know the correct model, which has precision

2.

The fit of a model (either

\rho_{2}
) at time

P(\rho_{i,t})=\mu_{i,t-1}\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|A)+(1-\mu_{i,t-1})\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|B)

(1)

where

is the prior belief that

that maximizes (

3.

Skeptical agents
. At each time

, each skeptical agent
i
chooses their perceived precision
\rho_{i,t}\in\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}

s_{i,t}

chooses
\rho_{i,t}
that minimizes (
1
).

4.

Conformist agents
. At each time

i

\bar{\mu}_{t}(h_{t})

h_{t}=\left\{h_{1,t},h_{2,t},\dots,h_{n,t}\right\}
the vector of all agents’ histories up to

. The utility of agent

at time

is:

C_{i,t}(\rho_{i,t},s_{i,t},h_{t})=-(\mu_{i,t}(s_{i,t},h_{i,t},\rho_{i,t})-\bar{\mu}_{t}(h_{t}))^{2}

5.

Anti-conformist agents
. At each time
t
, each anti-conformist agent
i
chooses strategically their perceived precision
\rho_{i,t}\in\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}
, after observing the signal’s realization
s_{i,t}
, to maximize the distance between their posterior belief and

at time

We assume that the initial population splits into five groups, one for each type of agents (
n_{1}

We also assume that the composition of the population changes over time, particularly every

\psi_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i,t}

\epsilon_{i,t}\coloneqq(q-\mu_{i,t})^{2}

\epsilon_{i,t}\coloneqq(\hat{q}_{t}-\mu_{i,t})^{2}

(3)

where

\hat{q}_{t}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mbox{if }\omega_{t}=A\\
0&\mbox{if }\omega_{t}=B\end{array}\right.

An agent

.

We study how the composition of the population evolves, assuming that the precision in predicting the likelihood of each state determines the survival of each type. However, agents update their beliefs about the state
\omega
, not about the probability
q
. The underlying assumption is that agents are unaware of
q
but instead receive evidence about the current state realization of
\omega

We also assume that the total population is constant over time. The evolutionary rule we adopt determines discrete changes in the shares of narrative selection criteria over the population. We adopt such rule to avoid expanding or shrinking populations in the simulations. Our results are unlikely driven by such an assumption. As for the survival selection criterion, considering

3.1
Optimal precision

For those agents that are strategic, we study their problem of identifying the optimal interpretation of information, that is, the optimal
\rho_{i,t}
.

Auto-referential and Skeptical agents.

The fit of a model (
1
) is a linear function of an agent’s prior belief
\mu
at any time
t
. Therefore, an auto-referential agent
i
maximizes the fit for a given signal
s_{i,t}
if they choose
\rho_{i,t}
such that they maximize the weight
\pi_{\rho_{i,t}}(s_{i,t}|\omega)
on the state realization they believe to be more likely. Formally,

\rho_{i,t}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{1}&\mbox{if }(\mu_{i,t-1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\land s_{i,t}=b)\lor(\mu_{i,t-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\land s_{i,t}=a)\\
\rho_{2}&\mbox{if }(\mu_{i,t-1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\land s_{i,t}=a)\lor(\mu_{i,t-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\land s_{i,t}=b)\end{array}\right.

In other words, agent
i
rationalizes pro-attitudinal evidence with a higher precision model (
\rho_{2}
) and counter-attitudinal evidence with a lower precision model (
\rho_{1}
). A skeptical agent chooses the model that minimizes the fit. Therefore, opposite considerations hold for such agents.

Conformist and Anti-conformist agents.

Each conformist agent
i
chooses
\rho_{i,t}
that minimizes the distance between the posterior belief
\mu_{i,t}(s_{i,t},h_{i,t},\rho_{i,t})
and the average prior belief in the population
\bar{\mu}_{t}(h_{t})
. We need to distinguish two scenarios, depending on whether the agent receives either pro-attitudinal evidence or counter-attitudinal evidence:

1.

A pro-attitudinal signal makes the agent more extreme. Therefore, the agent chooses
\rho_{1}
if they are already more extreme than the average, whereas they choose
\rho_{2}
if this makes their belief more in line with the average. In other words, an extremist should interpret pro-attitudinal information as less reliable to counterbalance their extremism. Instead, a moderate should interpret pro-attitudinal information as more reliable to cope with the average belief.

2.

A counter-attitudinal signal makes the agent less extreme. Therefore, the agent chooses
\rho_{1}
if they are already less extreme than the average, whereas they choose
\rho_{2}
if this makes their belief more in line with the average.

An anti-conformist agent chooses the model to maximize the distance between their posterior belief and the average prior belief in the population. Thus, opposite considerations hold for such agents.

3.2
Law of Motion

We examine how the population evolves under four different laws of motion for the state of the world.

Independent State:

f(A,\hat{\omega}_{t-1})=f(A)=q
and
\epsilon_{i,t}
defined by equation (
2

The simplest scenario an agent may face is that the state
\omega
has a different realization each period, and realizations are independent over time. For instance, consider a firm choosing whether to buy an advertisement slot online. The return from this investment depends on the number of potential customers who will click on the advertisement on a particular day. The number of clicks on a particular day is the independent realization of a random variable. The firm’s profit depends on its ability to predict whether buying an advertisement slot on a particular day will be profitable or not.

Independent state with persistence:

f(\omega_{t},\hat{\omega}_{t-1})=1
if and only if
\omega_{t}=\omega_{t-1}
for any
t\neq\tau,2\tau,\dots,T-\tau

f(A,\hat{\omega}_{t-1})=f(A)=q
for any
t=\tau,2\tau,\dots,T-\tau
and
\epsilon_{i,t}
defined by equation (
3

In this specification, we consider a setting where agents receive more than one signal about the state of the world before this changes, and their utility depends on their ability to guess the realized state of the world in a given period. Consider, for instance, a farmer who must decide each season how to allocate land between two crops: one is more productive in dry conditions, whereas the other is more productive in periods of heavy rainfall. The farmer’s utility depends on their ability to predict the current weather conditions and continuously adapt to them. Unlike online consumers in the previous example, weather conditions are persistent; similarly to online consumers, weather conditions are independent of past realizations.

f(A,\hat{\omega}_{t-1})=f(A,\omega_{t-1})=(\phi_{1},1-\phi_{2})

P=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{1}&1-\phi_{1}\\

In this case,

The previous laws of motion assume that the realization of the state at each period is independent of past realizations. However, most real-world events display some degree of path dependence. Thus, we study the possibility that the state is auto-correlated. For instance, consider an investment decision. A firm must decide whether to open a shop in a new location. The decision depends on the current and future profitability of the shop. The economic conditions behind the profitability of such an investment (e.g., the demand structure) display some degree of persistence. Therefore, if opening the shop is profitable today, it will likely be profitable tomorrow. However, there is also a chance that some shocks in the future may change the economic environment and, thus, the profitability of the investment. The firm’s profit depends on its ability to predict the long-run profitability of an investment. This law of motion allows us to account for the effect of auto-correlation on the evolutionary performance of different types.

Self-fulfilling State:

f(A,\hat{\omega}_{t-1})=\delta\overline{\mu}_{t-1}+(1-\delta)q
, where
\delta\in[0,1]
is the importance of the average belief in the population
\overline{\mu}_{t-1}
in determining the state’s realization, and
\epsilon_{i,t}
defined by equation (

).

There are scenarios where the probability of an event depends on the beliefs about such an event. For instance, this frequently happens in the financial markets where the evolution in the price of a stock, and thus the profitability of buying or selling it, depends on the inherent profitability of the firm but also on the investors’ beliefs about its profitability. If enough people believe the price of a stock will increase and thus buy it, the chance that the price will indeed increase is higher. This law of motion captures all scenarios where the utility of an agent (and, thus, its likelihood to survive) depends on an underlying state (e.g., quality of a firm), but the realization of the state (e.g., the price of the firm’s stock) also depends on the agents’ beliefs.

Procedures.

An alternative interpretation of this rule is that all agents die after
\tau

4.1
Comparative statics on state uncertainty

In this section, we focus on the role of state uncertainty, varying

Independent state.

Let us start with the case of an independent state. Our results show that conformists are the best-performing agents for any value of
q
, with a share included between 45% and 75%. For each other type, a small minority survives (ranging between 5% and 20% for each type). Therefore, the first result of our analysis is that, although conformists dominate, the population is steadily heterogeneous in the long run. Notably, the relative performance of the minority types depends on
q
: skeptical agents perform well when there is high uncertainty, whereas auto-referential, anti-conformists, and naive agents significantly increase their performance when there is low uncertainty. Indeed, these types tend to have extreme beliefs. Thus, their performance (i.e., the mean squared error) improves when the probability of the state is closer to 1. Instead, for
q
close to
\frac{1}{2}
, skeptical agents perform better: their updating towards extreme beliefs is slower because they discount evidence confirming their prior beliefs. Conformists are the best-performing agents because they aggregate information and they adapt their behavior, particularly their model choice, to the environment, namely, state uncertainty. By doing that, they tend to behave like skeptical agents when
q
is relatively low, thus choosing as model
\rho_{1}
, slowing down the convergence of their beliefs to 1. Instead, when
q
is high, they tend to behave like auto-referential agents, choosing as model
\rho_{2}
. Thus, conformists are a mix of skeptical and auto-referential agents, taking the best out of these two types.

4.2.1
Comparative statics on the precision of information

We repeat the analysis, changing the precision of information
p
compared to the benchmark. In particular, we now assume
p=0.9
. Figure
A2
shows the results. As
p
increases, given the state, the information received by each agent is more precise. Naive agents are endowed with the correct model, which is now
p=0.9
, and thus converge more quickly to extreme beliefs, slightly worsening their performance when there is high uncertainty. Instead, all other agents still choose the narrative strategically. However, higher precision implies that agents tend to receive the same information, thus reducing belief dispersion. The latter effect reduces the evolutionary advantage of conformists while mainly benefiting skeptical agents. Indeed, in each period, the agents’ beliefs when
p=0.9
tend to move all in the same direction, and, as a consequence, the average belief is highly volatile and erratic. Therefore, conformists’ ability to aggregate information worsens.

4.2.2
Comparative statics on the model choice

We perform the same numerical simulations as before, changing the models among which agents can choose compared to the benchmark. In particular, we now assume
\rho_{2}=0.7
. Figure
A3
shows the results. When agents can choose between two imprecise models, i.e.,
\rho_{1}=0.6
and
\rho_{2}=0.7
, the results are reasonably consistent with the benchmark. The only significant difference is an improvement in the performance of skeptical agents when there is high uncertainty. Indeed, the performance of skeptical agents depends on their ability not to converge (contrary to other types) to extreme beliefs, and this ability follows
\rho_{1}=0.6
. At the same time, when
\rho_{2}=0.7
, the conformists need more time to converge toward the average belief, thus reducing their evolutionary advantage.

4.2.3
Comparative statics on the speed of adjustment

We consider different speeds of adjustment
\tau
compared to the benchmark. In particular, we now assume
\tau=5
in Figure
A4
and
\tau=20

\tau=5

\frac{1}{2}

Otherwise, we observe that skeptical agents perform slightly worse when the speed of adjustment is higher because the other types do not have enough time to form extreme beliefs. Instead, when the adjustment is slower (i.e.,
\tau=20

Conformists are still the majoritarian type, but their share is slightly smaller, and there are more skeptical agents when uncertainty is high and more naive or auto-referential agents when uncertainty is low.

Comparative statics on the number of agents

We repeat the analysis for smaller population sizes. In particular, we now assume
n=50
in Figure
A6
and
n=10
in Figure
A7
. The evolutionary advantage of conformists derives either from their superior adaptability or their ability to aggregate information. The latter depends on the number of agents receiving signals, from whom conformists learn. When the number of agents is lower—even significantly lower,
\frac{1}{10}
or
\frac{1}{50}

Thus, even though the aggregation of information plays a role, the main source of conformists’ evolutionary advantage is their ability to strategically adapt to the context when selecting a narrative.

5
Conclusion

This paper investigates how agents using different criteria for deciding how to interpret information perform from an evolutionary perspective. We use the precision of an agent in predicting the probability distribution of the state of the world as the evolutionary fitness, assuming that more precise types are more likely to survive. We explore the long-run composition of the population in terms of types. Conformists are more likely to survive and be the dominant type. Nevertheless, all other types (naive, auto-referential, skeptical, and anti-conformist agents) also have a minority share in the long-run population. Thus, our findings point to the coexistence of agents using different criteria for narrative selection and, hence, for interpreting new information. Nevertheless, we show that, while the literature has focused on auto-referential agents—who choose the narrative based on its fit with the data and their prior beliefs—conformism should receive more attention as a narrative selection criterion. Finally, our paper explains what conditions favor one type or the other. In particular, conformists proliferate in environments with moderate uncertainty and less precise information.

Our research has important implications, for instance, for information design. Imagine a politician wishing to persuade voters. From a static perspective, the politician must account for the existence of agents that process information differently. Moreover, in the long run, information design could impact the evolutionary path of different types. In other words, the designer can shape information today to favor types that are easier to persuade, thus creating a more favorable environment tomorrow. This interaction between information design and population evolution is worth studying in future research.
