Effective sequential therapy of linezolid and contezolid against HA-MRSA-induced necrotizing pneumonia in a 95-year-old patient with multimorbidity
Authors: Ran Wang, Shuping Shen, Tianping Zheng, Yuxing Zhang, Wei Xu, Jianqing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite progress in new antibiotic development, active surveillance, and infection prevention, health care-associated methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (HA-MRSA) remains a major pathogen; it continues to pose a high-risk threat, especially in extremely elderly patients with necrotizing pneumonia and multiorgan dysfunction on polypharmacy. Multimorbidity, immunosenescence, and frailty in the elderly increase the risk of adverse outcomes, severely restricting therapeutic options.Case presentationWe present the case of a long-term hospitalized 95-year-old woman with multimorbidity and multiorgan dysfunction who developed hospital-acquired necrotizing pneumonia due to MRSA. The diagnosis was established through metagenomic next-generation sequencing, bacterial culture of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and imaging. Linezolid was initially incorporated into the antimicrobial treatment regimen. After three weeks, owing to the bone marrow suppression caused by linezolid, contezolid (400 mg PO every 12 h) was adopted as an alternative therapy; this led to a significant reduction in the size of the lung cavity. Considering the cost-effectiveness and persistent risk of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Elisabethia pacificus infection, as evidenced by the BALF culture results obtained at that time, the treatment was subsequently adjusted to oral linezolid combined with intravenous levofloxacin to achieve better infection control.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the effectiveness of a sequential approach with a linezolid‒contezolid strategy for treating HA-MRSA-induced necrotizing pneumonia, leading to the regression of cavitary lesions and significant clinical improvement in elderly patients with multimorbidity, highlighting the importance of individualized treatment strategies in managing complex infections in vulnerable patient populations and offering valuable insights for future clinical practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12161-1.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a gram-positive, coagulase-positive coccus in the phylum Firmicutes that predominantly colonizes warm and moist mucous membranes, with a particular predilection for the nasal vestibule. Health care-associated methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus Staphylococcus aureus (HA-MRSA) has emerged as a major concern. This pathogen has evolved resistance to an extensive array of drugs, including penicillin derivatives, macrolides, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones [1]. A defining characteristic of HA-MRSA is its ability to form biofilms. These biofilms act as protective shields, safeguarding bacteria from both the host’s immune defenses and the action of antibiotics [2].

In long-term hospitalized patients, especially those with complex medical histories, chronic colonization by HA-MRSA is alarmingly common. This colonization significantly elevates the risk of developing severe infections, such as necrotizing pneumonia. The vulnerability is particularly pronounced in elderly patients burdened with multimorbidity, as exemplified by the case we are about to present. Despite remarkable progress in the development of novel antibiotics, the implementation of comprehensive active surveillance programs, and substantial improvements in infection prevention strategies, HA-MRSA persists as a formidable pathogen; its associated infections continue to pose a critical challenge in the treatment of elderly patients, frequently culminating in unacceptably high mortality rates [1].

Numerous factors contribute to an increased susceptibility to HA-MRSA. Patients admitted to an intensive care unit (ICU) are at increased risk, often due to their compromised health status and exposure to a wide range of medical interventions. Prolonged exposure to antibiotics, particularly cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, exerts selective pressure on the bacterial population, promoting the emergence of resistant strains. Surgical procedures, hemodialysis, the presence of chronic wounds, and the use of indwelling invasive devices further disrupt the body’s natural defenses, creating fertile ground for HA-MRSA colonization and infection [3]. Additionally, asymptomatic MRSA colonization in humans serves as a major reservoir for human-to-human transmission and is a significant risk factor for recurrent infections [4]. In elderly individuals, underlying diseases and functional impairments not only predispose them to carrying staphylococcal bacteria but also increase their vulnerability to developing severe infections, such as bloodstream infections and pneumonia. The complex interplay of patient-related factors, including multimorbidity, organ dysfunction, potential drug‒antibiotic interactions, impaired immunity, and metabolic disorders, severely restricts the availability of therapeutic options [3].

Currently, in adult patients with confirmed MRSA pneumonia, monotherapy with either linezolid (LZD) or vancomycin is often regarded as the first-line treatment option [5], notwithstanding the elevated age-related mortality rates. However, challenges such as drug-related adverse effects and individual patient tolerances persist. Herein, we present a case of nosocomial necrotizing pneumonia caused by MRSA in a 95-year-old patient with multiple organ dysfunction. This patient was successfully treated with sequential therapy consisting of parenteral LZD followed by oral contezolid (CZD), a novel oxazolidinone antibiotic. Our case provides valuable insights into an alternative therapeutic approach for patients who are intolerant of LZD [6].

Case report

A 95-year-old female patient was admitted to the emergency department after presenting with a chief complaint of recurrent fever, copious sputum production, and confusion that had persisted for over 2 months. Her comprehensive personal medical history included multiple chronic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, cerebral tumor, cerebral infarction, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, chronic cardiac insufficiency, and chronic kidney disease. Two months prior to the current admission, the patient had been admitted to the ICU for acute respiratory failure. During that hospitalization, she underwent endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation on three separate occasions. Through comprehensive medical management, her condition gradually improved, and she was successfully extubated three times, ultimately transitioning to noninvasive ventilation.

At the time of admission, the patient exhibited a temperature of 37.8 °C, lethargy, and a tidal respiratory rhythm. Given her advanced age, multimorbidity, recurrent episodes of aspiration pneumonia, and the irreversibility of the central causative factors of her disease, after thorough communication with the patient’s family, a tracheotomy was performed on April 12, 2024, and ventilator-assisted respiration was initiated. Initial laboratory investigations revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 9.47 × 109/L, with neutrophils accounting for 88.1% and lymphocytes accounting for 7.5%. The C-reactive protein (CRP) level was elevated at 142.25 mg/L, and the procalcitonin (PCT) level was 0.292 ng/ml. Sputum culture results revealed the presence of P. aeruginosa and multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (MDRAB). Chest computed tomography (CT) scans revealed scattered patchy opacities in multiple lung lobes (Fig. 1A). As a result, a treatment regimen of cefoperazone sulbactam in combination with tigecycline was instituted to manage the infection. After half a month of treatment, the patient’s condition stabilized, and antimicrobial therapy was discontinued.

On May 3, the patient’s temperature rose to 38.2 °C, and her Glasgow coma scale score was 4 points. The laboratory findings at this time were as follows: WBC count, 15.91 × 109/L; CRP, 91.49 mg/L; and PCT, 0.189 ng/ml. IgM was positive for both influenza A and influenza B. Sputum smear examination revealed a significant number of gram-negative bacilli, along with leukocytes engaged in phagocytosis. Moreover, urine culture detected Candida tropicalis at a concentration > 104 cfu/ml. In response, the patient was administered baloxavir marboxil (40 mg PO as a single dose for antiviral treatment against influenza A/B), meropenem (1.0 g IV q12h, carbapenem antibiotic) adjusted for chronic kidney disease with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 40 ml/min/1.73 m² and fluconazole (200 mg IV qd) for antifungal treatment due to positive urine culture for C. tropicalis.

On May 6, bronchoscopy was conducted, revealing that the mucosa of the right main bronchus was congested and edematous, with a large volume of yellow purulent secretions. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) obtained from the right middle lobe tested positive for MRSA DNA via RT‒PCR. The BALF culture results indicated that the concentration of MDRAB was > 105 cfu/ml and that of MRSA was > 104 cfu/ml. Bedside radiography revealed a large patchy hyperdense shadow in the right lung (Fig. 2A). LZD was subsequently added to the existing treatment regimen at a dose of 600 mg intravenously every 12 h. The LZD trough concentration was initially measured at 17.26 µg/ml, and the dose was later adjusted to 600 mg intravenously once daily, resulting in an LZD trough concentration of 3.15 µg/ml (Fig. 2B).

After 3 weeks of LZD treatment, the patient’s body temperature normalized, and inflammatory marker levels decreased. CT scans revealed a rounded shadow in the right middle lobe (Fig. 1B). Despite multiple transfusions of erythrocyte suspension, the patient’s hemoglobin level progressively declined (Fig. 3A) while her reticulocyte count did not increase (Fig. 3B). Considering these findings, bone marrow suppression secondary to LZD use was suspected. Given the patient’s frailty, multimorbidity, and the imperative to minimize adverse effects, CZD, a new generation oxazolidinone antibiotic with a distinct structure from LZD, was selected as an alternative treatment option. On May 28, the treatment regimen was adjusted from LZD to CZD (400 mg orally every 12 h) to mitigate potential side effects.

Following the switch to CZD, the patient’s airway secretions gradually diminished. Although her body temperature initially continued to fluctuate, the overall clinical condition showed signs of improvement. During this illness, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of BALF detected E. pacificus (sequence number: 129, 778), S. aureus (sequence number: 24, 965), and P. aeruginosa (sequence number: 17, 658). The drug resistance genes identified in S. aureus included mecA, blaZ, and ErmA (Supplementary sequencing data are presented in Tables S1 and S2). The results of BALF culture further corroborated the pathogen identification. A subsequent chest CT scan on June 26 clearly demonstrated that the size of the cavities had decreased compared with previous scans, providing evidence of the efficacy of CZD in treating MRSA-induced necrotizing pneumonia (Fig. 1C).

Considering the cost-effectiveness and persistent risk of P. aeruginosa and E. pacificus infection, as evidenced by the BALF culture results obtained at that time, the treatment was subsequently adjusted to oral LZD combined with intravenous levofloxacin for anti-infection purposes (Fig. 2B), and the LZD drug concentration was closely monitored. Consequentially, the inflammation-related indicators eventually reached a stable state, with the white blood cell count (WBC) ranging from 6.92 to 9.54 × 10⁹/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) from 11.37 to 52.61 mg/L, and procalcitonin (PCT) from 0.08 to 0.198 ng/ml (Figure S1). A chest CT review on July 25 revealed a patchy shadow in the middle lobe of the right lung (Fig. 1D). Despite treatment, the patient remained intubated and sedated and underwent mechanical ventilation as a result of irreversible respiratory failure and multiorgan dysfunction. Once the infection was under control, on July 30, 2024, she was transferred to a long-term care facility for continued supportive care. Given her advanced age and poor functional prognosis, attempts at extubation were not pursued.

Discussion

In the past two decades, there has been a remarkable increase in the number of advanced-aged patients (aged ≥ 80 years) admitted to ICUs worldwide [7]. HA-MRSA is one of the primary pathogens causing severe infections in elderly individuals. In this patient demographic, the coexistence of multimorbidity, polypharmacy, immunosenescence, and frailty substantially elevates the risk of adverse outcomes. When HA-MRSA infections occur in these vulnerable patients, they consistently lead to significant morbidity, high mortality rates, extended hospital stays, and substantial financial burdens [8].

Our 95-year-old patient presented with numerous risk factors for HA-MRSA infection. These included recent hospitalization, the use of intravenous antimicrobials, invasive procedures, mechanical ventilation, influenza-like symptoms, and cavitary pneumonia. Through rapid RT‒PCR testing for the MRSA mecA gene, we were able to promptly initiate targeted anti-MRSA therapy while concurrently minimizing the use of unnecessary antibiotics. The S. aureus isolate from the patient harbored drug resistance genes such as mecA, blaZ, and ErmA, which are characteristic of HA-MRSA and play pivotal roles in its multidrug resistance phenotype [9]. Although direct evidence of biofilm formation was absent in this case, considering the chronic nature of the patient’s condition, the potential role of biofilms in maintaining infection cannot be ignored. Biofilms can potentially shield bacteria from the host’s immune system and antibiotics, thus contributing to the persistence of infection [2].

LZD, the first oxazolidinone antibiotic, binds to the bacterial 23 S rRNA of the 50 S subunit and prevents the formation of a functional 70 S initiation complex, which is essential for bacterial RNA translation. Notably, bacterial rRNA consists of 5 S, 16 S, and 23 S types, and the 50 S ribosomal subunit is vital for this process [10]. LZD also has activity against several other toxins, including alpha-hemolysin and alpha-type phenol-soluble modulins, which are involved in S. aureus pathogenicity [11]. Compared with vancomycin, LZD has greater lung penetration and can inhibit toxin production. Two meta-analyses [5, 12] support the beneficial effects of LZD in treating ventilator-associated pneumonia or hospital-acquired pneumonia caused by MRSA. Although there was no statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality between the two groups, both datasets indicated that LZD was associated with a higher microbiological eradication rate than glycopeptides.

This patient had chronic renal insufficiency, with an eGFR of 18 ~ 40 ml/min/1.73 m², which restricted the use of vancomycin. Although we did not conduct further genetic analysis of the MRSA strain, LZD was used in combination with other antimicrobial agents owing to its superior ability to penetrate the lung [5]. Myelosuppression, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia, is a serious adverse effect of LZD use, particularly in relation to the development of sideroblastic anemia. Linezolid-induced anemia (LI-AN) has been the subject of recent clinical research. Elderly patients often have multimorbidity requiring complex treatment regimens, increasing their susceptibility to LI-AN. Ma et al. developed a risk prediction model for LI-AN in older patients, identifying the duration of linezolid treatment, kidney function, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score as potential risk factors [13]. 80% of elderly patients had high initial blood concentrations when LZD was administered at the recommended dose, and 63.33% of them had initial blood concentrations more than twice the upper limit of appropriate levels [14]. The occurrence of LI-AN in the present patient may also be related to drug overexposure and drug‒drug interactions [15]. Therefore, patients receiving LZD treatment, especially those with moderate or severe renal insufficiency, should be closely monitored through routine blood tests and plasma concentration measurements.

CZD has a distinct structure from LZD and belongs to a new class of oxazolidinone drugs. It has the potential to enhance binding to bacterial targets while reducing negative impacts on mitochondrial function [6]. Compared with LZD, CZD has a more favorable safety profile, which increases its clinical attractiveness. In the past two years, eight typical cases have been reported, where severe pneumonia caused by infections with methicillin-susceptible S. aureus [16, 17], MRSA [18], Enterococcus faecium [18–20], and Nocardia [18] improved to varying degrees after CZD use. The patients’ ages ranged from 28 to 101 years. These cases demonstrate the safety and efficacy of CZD in treating gram-positive bacterial pneumonia. Considering the patient’s advanced age, multimorbidity, renal insufficiency, and intolerance to LI-AN, CZD was selected, which achieved satisfactory clinical outcomes. This case offers valuable clinical evidence for the successful treatment of MRSA-induced necrotizing pneumonia in a long-term hospitalized patient with multiple organ dysfunctions.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the patient had a mixed infection with multiple pathogens and received LZD/CZD along with other antibacterial and antifungal treatments; this makes it challenging to evaluate the effectiveness of a single antibiotic precisely. Second, the patient’s advanced age and complex medical history may have influenced treatment outcomes, and the generalizability of the research findings to elderly patients with different levels of organ function and diverse multimorbidity remains to be determined. Finally, this study included only one patient, and further research involving a larger sample size is needed to assess the clinical utility of LZD/CZD more comprehensively.

Conclusion

Although CZD has not been formally incorporated into the treatment guidelines for severe pneumonia, it has demonstrated effectiveness, safety, and good tolerability in managing MRSA-induced necrotizing pneumonia in patients with multiple organ dysfunction. This case highlights the potential efficacy of a sequential linezolid‒contezolid approach for treating severe MRSA infections in the geriatric population. Future research should focus on accumulating similar cases and conducting well-designed, rigorously implemented clinical trials to validate our findings.
