A conserved E1 stem motif functions as a virulence switch in the emerging Getah virus
Authors: Zhaoying Xiang, Ganbo Li, Jiayi Wang, Chunpeng Ma, Xinna Ge, Peng Gao, Qiongqiong Zhou, Xin Guo, Lei Zhou, Jun Han, Yongning Zhang, Hanchun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGetah virus (GETV) is an emerging arthropod-borne zoonotic alphavirus that poses a growing threat to animal and public health, yet its virulence determinants remain poorly understood. Here, we report the first identification of a natural GETV variant isolated from the brain of diseased piglets. This variant harbors a 9-nucleotide deletion in the E1 glycoprotein stem region, resulting in the deletion of glutamine 397 (Q397) and valine 398 (V398), along with an asparagine‑to‑isoleucine substitution at position 396 (N396I) and the loss of phenylalanine 399 (F399). It also carries a threonine-to-methionine substitution at position 49 (T49M) in nonstructural protein 2 (NSP2). The NQVF motif (residues 396–399) in E1 protein is highly conserved among GETV strains and related alphaviruses, suggesting a shared functional role. Using reverse genetics, we demonstrated that the E1 deletion—but not the NSP2 mutation—drastically attenuates viral replication in vitro and completely abrogates lethality in neonatal mice by restricting systemic dissemination. Stepwise mutagenesis further revealed that the four‑residue motif functions synergistically as a virulence switch: single deletions partially reduce pathogenicity, while the quadruple deletion confers full attenuation, limiting tissue tropism and histopathology. Although both wild‑type and mutant viruses crossed the placental barrier in pregnant mice, the mutant exhibited reduced vertical transmission and maternal tissue replication. Our findings identify a highly conserved E1 stem motif as a critical regulator of GETV virulence, offering a strategic target for developing attenuated vaccines against GETV and other alphaviruses.

Introduction

The genus Alphavirus within the family Togaviridae encompasses a group of arthropod-borne viruses (arboviruses) notable for their considerable zoonotic and epidemic potential. Prominent members such as chikungunya virus (CHIKV), Semliki Forest virus (SFV), eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), and western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) can cause a spectrum of debilitating diseases, ranging from polyarthralgia to fatal encephalitis in both human and animal hosts [1–4]. Despite their significant public and veterinary health burden, licensed vaccines or specific antiviral therapeutics remain unavailable for most alphaviruses, highlighting an urgent need to elucidate their molecular pathogenesis [5,6]. Getah virus (GETV), a historically understudied member of this genus, has recently emerged as a pathogen of concern for livestock and public health [7–9]. Since 2018, frequent outbreaks in swine populations across Asia have been linked to reproductive failures in pregnant sows, neurological disorders in piglets, and mortality in severe cases, leading to substantial economic losses to the global swine industry [8–13]. Of particular concern, serological surveillance detected GETV-specific antibodies in febrile patients in an endemic region [14], suggesting a previously underappreciated risk of spillover to humans. These developments necessitate a deeper mechanistic understanding of GETV biology to inform the development of targeted countermeasures.

The molecular architecture of alphaviruses provides critical insights into their pathogenicity. The approximately 11.6 kilobase positive-sense RNA genome encodes four nonstructural proteins (NSP1–4), which orchestrate viral replication, and five structural proteins (Capsid, E3, E2, 6K/TF, E1) that constitute the virion [15,16]. During assembly, 80 trimeric spikes—each composed of E1-E2 glycoprotein heterodimers—are embedded within a host-derived lipid envelope [16–18]. The E1-E2 glycoprotein complex is pivotal for host cell entry: E2 primarily mediates receptor attachment, while E1 facilitates the subsequent membrane fusion event [16,19,20]. Extensive structural and functional studies on alphavirus glycoproteins have delineated key virulence determinants [21–25]. For instance, the A226V substitution in the E1 glycoprotein of CHIKV augments viral adaptability for mosquito transmission, while residue V80 in E1 is implicated in modulating viral infectivity and systemic spread in hosts [21,26]. In WEEV, mutations such as L149Q and K181E in the E2 glycoprotein reduce neurovirulence in mammals by altering receptor binding [27]. In contrast, the functional characterization of GETV glycoproteins remains limited. While bioinformatic analyses have identified several sites within the GETV E2 glycoprotein (e.g. residues 86, 323, and 253) under putative positive selection [10,28], yet only a few have been experimentally validated. Among these, the K253R mutation in E2 was shown to enhance viral attachment by increasing affinity for heparan sulfate, thereby contributing to virulence in a neonatal mouse model [29]. Notably, despite the central role of the E1 glycoprotein in the alphavirus life cycle, its specific contributions to GETV pathogenicity have yet to be investigated.

In this study, we applied functional virology methodologies to delineate specific molecular determinants within the E1 glycoprotein that underlie the attenuation of GETV. A clinical isolate, designated NM2022-Mutant, was initially characterized and demonstrated markedly attenuated pathogenicity, as evidenced by significantly reduced lethality in a neonatal mouse model when compared to contemporary epidemic strains. To pinpoint the genetic basis of this attenuation, we employed reverse genetics to construct a panel of isogenic virus variants. This included the generation and comparative assessment of (i) targeted single- and double-point mutations within the NSP2/E1 coding region and (ii) engineered deletion mutants of the E1 glycoprotein at residues 396–399. Systematic in vivo evaluation revealed that the four-residue motif (396–399) in the E1 glycoprotein operates collectively as a critical virulence regulator, essential for maintaining full pathogenic potential in the GETV neonatal mouse model.

Results

Genomic characterization of two co-circulating, genetically distinct GETV strains isolated from diseased piglets

Using Vero CCL81 cells, we successfully isolated two GETV strains from the brain tissue of piglets exhibiting neurological manifestations. Whole-genome sequencing of plaque-purified viruses directly from brain tissue homogenates at passage 0 (P0) revealed the co-existence of two genetically distinct GETV lineages: a wild-type (WT) strain and a naturally occurring mutant variant. The mutant genome harbored two major mutations: (i) a Threonine-to-Methionine substitution at position 49 (T49M) in NSP2, and (ii) a 9-nucleotide (nt) in-frame deletion within the E1 glycoprotein gene. This deletion specifically removes codons for glutamine 397 (Q397) and valine 398 (V398), while simultaneously introducing an asparagine‑to‑isoleucine substitution at position 396 (N396I) and eliminating phenylalanine 399 (F399) (Figure 1(a)). The two strains were designated as NM2022-WT and NM2022-Mutant, respectively. The E1 396–399 residues lie within the stem region linking the exodomain and transmembrane helix [30]. Structural prediction using Alphafold3 indicated that the mutant E1 protein adopts a shortened and irregularly coiled stem loop (Figure 1(b)). To evaluate the evolutionary conservation of the identified E1 396–399 motif, we performed a comparative sequence analysis. Alignment of 71 GETV strains—including NM2022-WT—with nine other representative alphaviruses, such as Ross River virus (RRV) and Semliki Forest virus (SFV), revealed that this region is highly conserved across these viruses (Figure 1(c,d)). The four-residue motif was identical between GETV and RRV, while SFV differed at only a single amino acid site. This strong conservation within the Alphavirus genus suggests that the E1 396–399 motif likely represents a key functional determinant shared among related alphaviruses. To investigate the origin of NM2022-Mutant, we performed additional rounds of plaque purification from the original clinical specimen. Subsequent genotyping demonstrated that 2.08% (1/48) of the purified viral clones contained a mixed WT/mutant genotype, while the remaining clones consisted exclusively of the WT genotype (Fig S1a). Furthermore, deep sequencing of the P0 homogenate via Illumina MiSeq platform enabled quantification of mutant allele frequencies. Analysis revealed that mutant alleles were present at frequencies of 47.19% (42/89) at the NSP2 T49M locus and 18.35% (29/158) across the E1 deletion region in the assembled sequence data. These findings confirm that NM2022-Mutant preexisted as a minor variant within a natural viral quasispecies present in the host-derived clinical sample, rather than arising as an artifact of the laboratory isolation process. In addition, to exclude the possibility that the observed mutations resulted from in vitro adaptation, the NM2022-WT strain was subjected to ten serial passages in Vero CCL81 cells. Sanger sequencing of viral RNA from passages 5 (P5) and 10 (P10) showed no evidence of de novo mutations at the loci defining NM2022-Mutant (Fig S1b). This demonstrates the genomic stability of GETV during propagation in this cell culture system and provides further evidence that the NM2022-Mutant originated from preexisting viral subpopulations within the natural infection.

The natural GETV mutant exhibits lower replication fitness and pathogenicity than the wild-type strain

To investigate the phenotypic consequences of the T49M mutation in NSP2 and the 9‑nt deletion in E1, we systematically compared the replication efficiency and pathogenicity of the WT and mutant GETV strains. In vitro replicative fitness was evaluated through plaque‑forming assays and multi‑step growth kinetics in permissive cell lines, while in vivo virulence was assessed using a neonatal mouse challenge model. Plaque assays in Vero cells revealed that the mutant formed significantly smaller plaques, with a mean area reduction of 10.3% relative to the WT (Figure 1(e,f)), despite only occasional moderate size variability was observed within both virus groups. Multi‑step replication kinetics in the same cell line showed that both strains reached similar peak titers at 24 h post‑infection (hpi). Thereafter, however, the mutant displayed attenuated growth, with viral titers reduced by 8.4‑fold at 36 hpi and 14.7‑fold at 60 hpi compared to the WT (Figure 1(g)). In contrast, replication kinetics in N2a cells were indistinguishable between the two strains (Figure 1(h)), and immunofluorescence staining confirmed equivalent infection rates (Figure 1(i,j)). Furthermore, the impact of these mutations on GETV pathogenicity was examined in a neonatal mouse model. One‑day‑old ICR mice were intracranially inoculated with either WT or mutant virus. By 10 days post‑infection, the WT‑infected group reached 50% mortality (7/14), whereas all mutant‑infected mice survived (Figure 1(k)). Collectively, these results indicate that while the mutant retains full replicative competence in N2a cells and shows only moderate attenuation in Vero cells, it is completely attenuated for lethality in neonatal mice. This pronounced discordance between in vitro replication and in vivo pathogenesis highlights the critical role of the T49M mutation and the E1 deletion in modulating GETV virulence.

The E1 9-nt deletion, not the NSP2 T49M mutation, is the key determinant of GETV replication attenuation in vitro

To delineate the individual and combined contributions of the T49M mutation in NSP2 and the 9-nt deletion in E1 to attenuated GETV replication, we employed an infectious cDNA clone platform to generate four isogenic viral variants: the rescued wild-type GETV (RvWT), a strain harboring only the NSP2 T49M point mutation (NSP2-T49M), a strain with only the E1 9‑nt deletion (E1-Mut), and a double mutant combining both alterations (NSP2-T49M/E1-Mut) (Figure 2(a)). These recombinants were first characterized in vitro for their replicative properties. Plaque assays revealed that the mean plaque area of both E1‑Mut and the double mutant was significantly reduced by 29% compared to RvWT, whereas the plaque size of NSP2‑T49M remained comparable to RvWT (Fig S2a and S2b). Multi-step growth kinetics in Vero cells demonstrated late-stage attenuation of the E1‑associated mutants (E1‑Mut and the double mutant) (Fig S2c). In N2a cells, the E1‑Mut strain replicated with fitness equivalent to RvWT throughout the time course. In contrast, both the NSP2‑T49M (at 24 and 48 hpi) and the double mutant (at 48 hpi) showed transient reductions in viral titer before recovering to near-wild-type levels by 72 hpi (Fig S2d). Immunofluorescence assays confirmed that all recombinants achieved comparable infection rates in N2a cells (Fig S2e and S2f). Collectively, these results indicate distinct cell-type-dependent replication phenotypes: E1‑associated mutants displayed late-stage attenuation specifically in Vero cells while maintaining near-wild-type fitness in N2a cells. Conversely, the NSP2‑T49M mutation alone did not affect replication in Vero cells but caused a transient replication delay in N2a cells. This cell-specific attenuation pattern suggests that the E1 deletion is the primary determinant of the observed in vitro replication deficit, whereas the NSP2 mutation may influence early replication dynamics in a cell-restricted manner. The E1 9‑nt deletion motif governs GETV virulence attenuation in vivo

To definitively delineate the individual and combined contributions of the T49M mutation in NSP2 and the 9‑nt deletion in E1 to GETV virulence, we conducted a comprehensive pathogenicity assessment of six viruses in neonatal mice: the original isolates (NM2022‑WT and NM2022‑Mutant) and the four recombinant variants (RvWT, NSP2‑T49M, E1‑Mut, and NSP2‑T49M/E1‑Mut). First, 50% lethal dose (LD50) values were determined following intracranial (i.c.) or subcutaneous (s.c.) inoculation. By the i.c. route, the LD50 of WT and RvWT were identical, while the NSP2‑T49M mutant exhibited a 2‑fold increase (Figure 2(b)). By the s.c. route, LD50 values for WT, RvWT, and NSP2‑T49M were approximately 10‑fold higher than those obtained via the i.c. route, indicating that the i.c. route is more virulent (Fig S3a). Strikingly, all viruses harboring the E1 deletion resulted in 100% survival across all tested doses by both inoculation routes (Figure 2(b) and S3a).

To further evaluate viral pathogenicity, mice were challenged with 6.25 × 102 TCID50 of each virus. Mice infected i.c. or s.c. with WT, RvWT, or NSP2‑T49M all succumbed within 10 days, whereas those receiving E1‑deletion mutants survived and exhibited significantly greater weight gain (Figure 2(c,d), S3b and S3c). Organ-specific viral loads, quantified by RT‑qPCR, revealed distinct tissue tropism patterns (Figure 2(e) and S3d). High levels of viral RNA were detected broadly—including in the heart, liver, brain, spleen, lung, kidney, and knee joints—in mice infected with WT, RvWT, or NSP2‑T49M. In contrast, E1 mutants were detected primarily in the brain and knee joints, with limited dissemination to other organs. These results indicate that the E1 deletion profoundly restricts viral replication and systemic spread in vivo. Histopathological analysis of seven tissues from the i.c. group corroborated these findings (Figure 2(f,g), S4a, S4b, and Table S1). In the brain, infection with WT, RvWT, or NSP2‑T49M induced severe lesions, including cytoplasmic vacuolization and nuclear pyknosis, whereas E1 mutants elicited only mild astrocytosis and microglial activation. Similarly, in peripheral tissues, E1‑mutant infections resulted in markedly attenuated pathology, with minimal cellular degeneration, hemorrhage, and inflammatory infiltration compared to the extensive damage observed with the other viruses. Altogether, these integrated virological, clinical, and histopathological data conclusively demonstrate that the 9‑nt deletion motif in E1 (spanning residues 396 − 399) is the key determinant of attenuated virulence, severely compromising GETV replication, dissemination, and pathogenicity in neonatal mice. In contrast, the NSP2‑T49M mutation alone has a negligible impact on overall disease outcome. Taken together, our results show that the E1 9‑nt deletion—not the NSP2 T49M mutation—is responsible for the severe attenuation of GETV virulence in neonatal mice.

The E1 residues 396–399 are collectively essential for efficient GETV replication in vitro

To precisely define the functional role of the E1 amino acid residues 396–399 in GETV replication, we generated a series of deletion mutants from the wild‑type infectious cDNA clone. These included four single‑residue deletions (Δ396N, Δ397Q, Δ398V, and Δ399F) and a quadruple mutant (Δ396–399NQVF). Additionally, a revertant virus was rescued from the Δ396–399NQVF cDNA clone to serve as a genetically matched control (Figure 3(a)). We first assessed the in vitro growth properties of these rescued viruses in Vero cells. Plaque assays revealed that the Δ396–399NQVF mutant produced significantly smaller plaques than the revertant, whereas all single‑residue deletion mutants exhibited plaque sizes indistinguishable from the revertant (Fig S5a and S5b). Multi-step growth kinetics in Vero cells showed that, although Δ396N and Δ398V yielded slightly elevated titers at 36 hpi, all mutants ultimately propagated with efficiency comparable to the revertant (Fig S5c). In contrast, infection of N2a cells revealed a cell-type-specific replication deficit. The Δ396–399NQVF mutant displayed significantly impaired kinetics compared to the revertant. Single‑residue mutants showed transient replication deficits at early time points (24 and 48 hpi) but reached peak titers equivalent to the revertant by 72 hpi (Fig S5d). Immunofluorescence assays corroborated these results, demonstrating a ~ 50% reduction in the infection rate for Δ396–399NQVF in N2a cells, while single‑residue mutants showed no significant difference from the revertant (Fig S5e and S5f). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the complete deletion of residues 396–399 substantially impairs GETV replication efficiency, particularly in neural cells, whereas single‑residue deletions exert only minor, transient effects on viral fitness in vitro. The E1 residues 396–399 synergistically contribute to GETV pathogenicity in neonatal mice

To evaluate the in vivo consequences of deleting E1 residues 396–399 individually and in combination, we assessed the pathogenicity of the engineered mutants in neonatal mice. Neonatal mice were intracranially inoculated with each rescued virus (revertant, Δ396N, Δ397Q, Δ398 V, Δ399F, and Δ396–399NQVF) and monitored for 10 days. For comparative context, parallel groups were infected with the parental WT isolate, the naturally attenuated Mutant, and the E1-Mut strain. Strikingly, all mice infected with any virus containing a deletion in the E1 396–399 region survived to the experimental endpoint and exhibited significantly greater weight gain than the WT and revertant groups (Figure 3(b,c)). Analysis of tissue viral loads by RT‑qPCR revealed distinct dissemination patterns (Figure 3(d)). The WT and revertant viruses exhibited broad systemic tropism, with viral RNA detected in multiple organs. Conversely, infection with the engineered mutants resulted in markedly restricted dissemination. Viral RNA for the quadruple mutant (Δ396–399NQVF) was detected predominantly in the brain and knee joint, mirroring the profile of the original Mutant (detected only in the brain). Single-residue deletion mutants displayed an intermediate phenotype, with viral RNA additionally detected in the heart (Δ396N, Δ397Q, Δ398 V) and kidney (Δ397Q, Δ398 V). Histopathological examination of brain tissues further corroborated these findings and defined a spectrum of neurovirulence (Figure 3(e,f) and Table S1). Severe neuropathology (as described above) was evident in mice infected with the WT, revertant, and–to a variable extent—the Δ397Q, Δ398 V, and Δ399F viruses. Inflammatory cell infiltration was observed across all infected groups. Notably, brain architecture was largely preserved in mice infected with the attenuated Mutant, E1-Mut, and Δ396–399NQVF strains, which exhibited minimal vacuolation or hemorrhage. Collectively, these integrated in vivo findings demonstrate a clear hierarchy of attenuation. Single-residue deletions within the E1 396–399 residues confer partial attenuation, sufficient to prevent mortality but not to fully abrogate neurovirulence or systemic spread. In contrast, the complete deletion of this region (Δ396–399NQVF) results in a profoundly attenuated phenotype, characterized by 100% survival, severely restricted tissue dissemination, and minimal histopathological damage, phenocopying the original attenuated isolates. This indicates that residues 396–399 function not independently, but in a synergistic manner as a critical collective determinant of GETV virulence in vivo.

Neither wild-type nor naturally attenuated GETV NM2022 causes reproductive disorders in pregnant mice

Given the established clinical association of GETV infection with reproductive disorders in swine [9,31,32], we sought to determine if the GETV NM2022-WT strain and its naturally attenuated variant (NM2022-Mutant) could similarly impair reproductive outcomes in a mammalian model. To this end, a pregnant mouse model was employed. Mice at early (E6), mid (E12), and late (E16) gestational stages were inoculated via the intramuscular (i.m.) or oronasal route with 105 TCID50 of virus and monitored until 3 days postpartum.

All infected mice, regardless of virus strain or inoculation route, delivered normally at term. No adverse reproductive events—such as miscarriage, stillbirth, fetal mummification, or weak litters—were observed (Figure 4(a) and S6a). Thus, under the conditions tested in this mouse model, neither the WT nor the attenuated mutant strain induced overt reproductive failure. To characterize viral dissemination, we quantified viral loads in maternal reproductive organs. In WT-infected mice, GETV RNA was readily detected in both the ovary and uterus following i.m. inoculation at E12 and E16, and following oronasal inoculation in the ovary at all stages and the uterus at E6 and E16. In contrast, the mutant strain exhibited a more restricted tissue tropism. After i.m. inoculation, viral RNA was detected only in the ovary at E16 and the uterus at E12 and E16. Following oronasal inoculation, viral RNA was confined to the ovary across all gestational stages, with no detection in the uterus (Figure 4(b) and S6b). Analysis of viral loads in 3-day-old neonates revealed distinct transmission patterns. WT infection led to widespread viral RNA detection in neonatal tissues via both inoculation routes. The mutant strain, however, showed a more limited capacity for vertical transmission: viral RNA was detected in neonates only after oronasal maternal inoculation and was undetectable following i.m. inoculation (Figure 4(c) and S6c). This reduced transplacental transmission provides strong evidence that the naturally occurring mutations attenuate the virus not only in neonatal mice but also in the context of pregnancy. Furthermore, assessment of the maternal humoral immune response showed that both strains induced GETV-specific IgG. However, IgG titers in sera from WT-infected dams were significantly higher than those from mutant-infected dams at the E6 and E12 time points, irrespective of inoculation route (Figure 4(d) and S6d). Critically, the mutant strain also displayed a consistently attenuated phenotype in the pregnant mice model, characterized by reduced replication efficiency in maternal tissues, a markedly restricted capacity for vertical transmission, and a dampened maternal antibody response compared to the wild-type virus. Discussion

The absence of approved vaccines (except in Japan) or effective therapies against GETV infection underscores the critical need to define its virulence determinants, which remain poorly understood. Using a combination of reverse genetics, in vitro characterization, and a neonatal mouse model, we identified—for the first time—a naturally occurring alteration within the E1 glycoprotein stem region (a 9‑nt deletion causing Q397 and V398 deletion, N396I substitution, and F399 elimination) functions as a key molecular switch for virulence. This deletion generated a naturally attenuated strain with reduced virulence that was able to circulate within pig populations. This finding represents a distinct contribution to the field of alphavirus pathogenesis. Previous studies have identified attenuating mutations in various viral proteins, including deletions in the 6K protein that impair virion release [33], modifications in the NSP3 hypervariable domain or capsid protein that reduce replication [34–37], and substitutions in the NSP2 C-terminus that dampen immune evasion [38–40]. In contrast, the T49M mutation in the N-terminal region of NSP2 did not significantly alter replicative capacity [41,42], suggesting functional redundancy at this site. While alphavirus glycoproteins are well-established determinants of emergence and pathogenesis [21–25], most characterized virulence sites localize to E2-receptor interfaces or the E1 fusion loop. Our work uniquely identifies a critical determinant within the E1 stem region—a structure essential for E1-E2 dimer stability and the regulation of membrane fusion [43–45]. Crucially, the attenuating mutation characterized here was isolated from a viral subpopulation in clinical specimens, indicating that reduced-virulence GETV strains may circulate naturally. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a naturally occurring virulence-attenuating mutation within the E1 stem region of any alphavirus, highlighting the necessity of genomic surveillance at this site to predict shifts in viral pathogenicity.

Previous studies have established that the hydrophobic pocket formed by the E1 stem region, the E2 domain D, and the E1/E2 transmembrane helices is a key structural motif for alphavirus assembly. Within this pocket, residues 397Q, 398V, and 399F of the E1 stem region mediate hydrogen-bonding interactions with pocket factor dioleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) [30]. Our structural prediction of the E1 stem region indicates that the mutant exhibits a shorter, irregularly coiled stem loop (Figure 1(b)). Given the deletion of amino acids at this critical interaction site, we speculate that the mutant E1 protein has a diminished binding affinity for DOPC, potentially compromising the structural stability of the E1-E2 lattice. However, accurately predicting the stem region’s interaction with DOPC is challenging due to the latter’s highly flexible and conformationally unconstrained nature. Therefore, future studies employing structural biology approaches—such as cryo-electron microscopy—will be necessary to fully elucidate how mutations in the E1 stem region affect viral structural integrity.

Our comparative sequence analysis further emphasizes the biological importance of this motif. The E1 396–399 sequence is highly conserved across 71 GETV strains and is nearly identical in RRV, with SFV differing by only a single residue (Figure 1(c,d)), suggesting a conserved functional role within the genus. Prior studies in SFV established that shortening the E1 stem impairs virion budding in BHK cells [43–45], implicating this region in virus assembly. Our findings extend this insight by demonstrating that a targeted deletion (Δ396–399) within the stem not only impairs growth in vitro but also completely abrogates lethal virulence in neonatal mice, positioning the E1 stem as a potent modulator of alphavirus pathogenesis. Interestingly, while the quadruple deletion caused significant replication deficits in N2a neuronal cells, triple-deletion variants retained near-wild-type fitness, yet both constructs were completely avirulent. This dissociation between neurotropic replication efficiency and lethal outcome suggests that virulence is governed by a mechanism independent of growth in neuronal cultures, raising the fundamental question of whether attenuation is driven by altered stem length or by the loss of specific residue-mediated interactions, a distinction requiring future structural and functional dissection.

Viral pathogenesis is a multifactorial process involving replication kinetics, tissue tropism, barrier penetration, and host immunity. Our data with the wild-type GETV strain corroborate prior reports of high neonatal mortality and broad systemic dissemination [29,33,37]. The attenuated mutant, however, exhibited a severely restricted tissue tropism, primarily infecting the brain and knee joints, indicating that its avirulent phenotype is likely due to impaired extra-neural dissemination—a hypothesis that warrants future spatiotemporal analysis of organ-specific replication kinetics. A limitation of our quantification method is the use of plasmid DNA for standard curve generation, which may not fully reflect reverse transcription efficiency or tissue matrix effects; thus, the absolute viral loads reported here should be considered approximate rather than definitive. Nonetheless, the relative differences between groups are reliable due to consistent sample processing. Notably, since the mutant virus caused 100% survival even upon direct intracranial inoculation, a defect in crossing the blood-brain barrier can be ruled out as the primary attenuation mechanism [46–50]. While histopathology revealed comparable inflammatory infiltration in both wild-type and mutant-infected brains, suggesting unaltered innate immune activation, a comprehensive assessment of adaptive immune responses—including neutralizing antibodies, T-cell activity, and cytokine profiles—is needed to fully delineate the host-pathogen interplay.

Our study revealed a notable contrast regarding gestational infection. Although GETV is clinically associated with reproductive disorders in swine [9,31,32] and has been reported to cause pathology in pregnant mice [30], the NM2022 strain used in this work did not induce overt reproductive issues via either intramuscular or oronasal challenge, despite evidence of transplacental transmission. Importantly, the mutant virus exhibited a significantly reduced vertical transmission capacity compared to WT, further supporting its attenuated phenotype.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the absence of overt reproductive pathology. First, the mouse model, while valuable for initial assessment of vertical transmission, may not fully recapitulate the reproductive pathogenesis of GETV in swine. Notable species differences exist, including placental structure, gestational length, and maternal–fetal immune interfaces, all of which could influence viral dissemination and tissue damage within the reproductive tract. Second, the potential for significant strain‑specific variation in GETV reproductive pathogenesis remains to be elucidated, along with the determinants governing this phenotype. Third, our study did not assess long-term reproductive outcomes, such as postnatal development, which may be more sensitive indicators of reproductive impairment. Therefore, the absence of overt pathology in this mouse model should be interpreted with caution and does not rule out clinically relevant reproductive disease in swine. Future studies in the natural host are necessary to determine whether the attenuated mutant also confers reduced reproductive pathogenicity in pigs. Beyond these considerations, several open questions merit further exploration. The impact of E1 stem deletions on vector competence and mosquito transmission efficiency remains unknown. Most importantly, the precise molecular mechanism of attenuation—whether through disruption of E1-E2 dimer stability, impairment of membrane fusion, or defects in virion maturation—warrants detailed future investigation.

In summary, our study identifies the E1 396–399 motif as a critical and naturally occurring virulence determinant in GETV. These findings provide fundamental insights into alphavirus pathogenesis and reveal a promising target for rational attenuation. The characterized mutation presents a compelling basis for the development of safer live-attenuated vaccine candidates not only against GETV but potentially against other pathogenic alphaviruses. Future work must focus on evaluating the genetic stability, safety, and broader protective efficacy of vaccines engineered based on this mechanism.

Methods

Ethics statement

The animal experiments were rigorously conducted in compliance with the animal welfare protocols (Ministry of Science and Technology, People’s Republic of China) and Laboratory Animal-Requirements of Environment and Housing Facilities (GB 14,925–2010, National Technical Committee). Experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical Committee of China Agricultural University (AW82903202-2–1). All procedures in this study were performed in compliance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.

Cells and viruses

Vero (ATCC CCL‑81) and mouse neuroblastoma N2a (ATCC CCL‑131) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cat: 12,491,015, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat: 16,140,071, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Cat: 15,140,122, Gibco, USA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The GETV NM2022-WT (GenBank accession No: PP537546) and NM2022-Mutant (GenBank accession No: PX955278) strains used in this study were isolated in-house from the brain tissue of deceased piglets presenting with neurological signs in Inner Mongolia, China, in August 2022 [51]. Viral stocks were propagated and titrated on Vero cells in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS.

Plaque assay

Viral plaque assays were performed to purify clonal isolates and quantify infectivity. For both procedures, viruses were first serially diluted 10‑fold in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. For plaque purification, confluent Vero cell monolayers in 6‑well plates were inoculated with 500 µL of each dilution. Following a 1‑hour adsorption period at 37°C under 5% CO2, the inoculum was replaced with an overlay of maintenance medium (DMEM with 2% FBS) containing 1% low‑melting‑point agarose (Cat: A8350, Solarbio, China). After 4 days of incubation under the same conditions, plaques were stained with 1% neutral red solution (Cat: G1310, Solarbio, China) for 2 hours, washed with PBS, and individual plaques were picked and resuspended in DMEM for further propagation. For standard plaque titration, the protocol was adapted: Vero monolayers in 12‑well plates were inoculated with 250 µL of viral dilution. Post‑adsorption, cells were overlaid with maintenance medium containing 1% methylcellulose (Cat: 9004‑67‑5, Sigma, USA). Following a 4‑day incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat: P1110, Solarbio, China), stained with 1% crystal violet, washed, and air‑dried. For plaque size quantification, images were imported into ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). A uniform threshold was applied to all images, and plaque areas were measured in pixels using the “Analyze Particles” function. Manual curation was performed to exclude artifacts or incorrectly identified objects. All measurement parameters were kept identical across samples.

RT-PCR and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 250 µL of viral suspension using Trizol® (Cat: R4801-01, Magen, China) and cDNA was then synthesized from the extracted RNA using the FastKing RT Kit (Cat: KR116, TIANGEN, China). Specific regions of the NSP2 and E1 genes were amplified by PCR with the KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (Cat: KMM-201, TOYOBO, Japan) using the following primers: NSP2-F, 5’‑CCCCTAGGCACCAGAGAACG‑3;’ NSP2‑R, 5’‑TGGGATGGCTGCTCCTGTTGG‑3;’ E1‑F, 5’‑ACGCACTCATCGGACTTC‑3;’ E1‑R, 5’‑ACTGGCCCTTTGAACTAATG‑3.’ The resulting PCR amplicons were subjected to either Sanger sequencing or Next‑Generation Sequencing (Illumina MiSeq platform) for subsequent sequence analysis.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

Infected cells were fixed with ice‑cold absolute ethanol for 10 min. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at 37°C with a laboratory‑generated GETV‑E2‑specific mAb 3G9. Following three washes with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at 37°C with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti‑mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cat: AB_2338590, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, USA) diluted 1:200. Cells were washed again three times with PBS and then stained with 4′,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI; Cat: 28,718‑90‑3, Solarbio, China) for 10 min at room temperature. Immunofluorescence images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti‑U fluorescence microscope.

Median tissue culture infectious dose 50 (TCID50) assay

Viral titers were determined by the TCID50 assay. Virus stocks were serially diluted 10‑fold in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. Confluent Vero cell monolayers in 96‑well plates were inoculated with 100 µL of each dilution in octuplicate and incubated for 48 h at 37°C under 5% CO2. Following incubation, cells were fixed with ice‑cold absolute ethanol. Viral antigen was then detected by IFA. The TCID50 was calculated from the infection pattern across replicates using the Reed–Muench method.

Viral growth curve determination

Vero or N2a cell monolayers in 24‑well plates were infected with virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. After a 1‑h adsorption period at 37°C under 5% CO2, the inoculum was removed, and cells were washed three times with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS). Cells were then maintained in 500 μL of DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS under standard culture conditions. At designated time points, cells and supernatants were harvested and subjected to two cycles of freeze‑thawing at ‑80°C to release cell‑associated virus. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The infectious virus titer in the supernatant was subsequently determined by TCID50 assay.

Infectious clone construction and virus recovery

The pCMV-HA vector was digested with ApaI (Cat: R0114V, NEB, USA) and NotI (Cat: R0189V, NEB, USA) to remove the HA tag, retaining essential elements such as the CMV promoter and SV40 poly(A) signal. A synthetic DNA fragment, encompassing nt 1–78 of the 5’UTR, nt 79–123 of NSP1, nt 10,970– 11,288 of E1, nt 11,289– 11,689 of the 3’ UTR, and a 39‑nt poly(A) tail, was inserted into the linearized vector via homologous recombination using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Cat: C115-01, Vazyme, China). This generated the intermediate plasmid pCMV‑GETV‑M (Fig S7a). Five overlapping fragments (A: nt 79–2178, B: nt 2139–4778, C: nt 4739–7502, D: nt 7463–9828, and E: nt 9789– 11,013), covering the complete genome of GETV strain NM2022, were amplified by RT‑PCR using the KOD One™ PCR Master Mix with viral cDNA as the template. Fragments A and B were first assembled into the intermediate plasmid, linearized with AflII (Cat: R0520V, NEB, USA) and BstBI (Cat: R0519V, NEB, USA), using the same ClonExpress kit, yielding pCMV‑GETV‑AB. Following an identical strategy, fragments C, D, and E were sequentially assembled into pCMV‑GETV‑AB to produce the full‑length infectious cDNA clone, pCMV‑NM2022‑WT (Fig S7b).

Mutant infectious clones were constructed as follows. First, the NSP2‑T49M amino acid substitution was introduced into pCMV‑NM2022‑WT using a fast multisite mutagenesis kit (Cat: FM201, TransGen, China). Subsequently, a series of E1 mutant fragments (E1‑Mut, Δ396N, Δ397Q, Δ398 V, Δ399F, Δ396‑399NQVF) were generated by overlap PCR using the KOD One™ Master Mix with pCMV‑NM2022‑WT as the template. These fragments were then assembled into vectors–either pCMV‑NM2022‑WT or pCMV‑NM2022‑NSP2‑T49M—that had been linearized with BstBI and PacI (Cat: R0547V, NEB, USA), yielding the respective mutant infectious clones. The revertant infectious clone was constructed analogously, except that pCMV‑NM2022‑Δ396‑399NQVF served as the template for overlap PCR. All primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

The resulting plasmids were transfected into Vero cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Cat: L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Upon the appearance of extensive cytopathic effect, the transfected cell cultures were harvested and subjected to two freeze‑thaw cycles at −80°C. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Successful rescue of recombinant viruses was confirmed by RT‑PCR and IFA performed on the clarified supernatants.

Animal infection models

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) ICR mice—including time-mated dams and pregnant mice at 18 days of gestation—were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Upon arrival, all mice were randomly housed in an individually ventilated cage system under SPF conditions at China Agricultural University. Throughout the study, each dam and her litter were co-housed in a separate cage. All animals were maintained under identical environmental conditions and provided with sterile water and appropriate food in accordance with the supplier’s recommendations.

Neonatal mouse model: For the LD50 determination, thirty-seven pregnant ICR mice at 18 days of gestation were randomly assigned to 36 challenge groups and one control group using a random number table generated in Excel. After delivery, each dam was allocated eight neonatal pups to foster. On postnatal day 1, pups (n = 8 per group) in the challenge groups were inoculated via i.c. injection with 25 µL of 10-fold serial dilutions of viruses (ranging from 6.25 × 102 to 6.25 × 10−3 TCId50), while pups in the negative control group received the same volume of DMEM. This experiment was repeated using the same procedure, but with the inoculation route changed to s.c. injection. The survival of suckling mice was monitored daily, and the LD50 of each virus strain was calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

For subsequent pathogenicity studies, two panels of viruses were evaluated in independent experiments. The first panel consisted of six GETV strains (WT, Mutant, RvWT, NSP2-T49M, E1-Mut, and NSP2-T49M/E1-Mut). The second panel comprised nine GETV strains (WT, Mutant, E1-Mut, Δ396N, Δ397Q, Δ398V, Δ399F, Δ396N–Δ399F, Revertant). For each virus in both panels, two independent experiments were performed to assess pathogenicity via different inoculation routes.

For the first virus panel, an initial experiment was conducted using the i.c. route. Seven pregnant ICR mice at 18 days of gestation were randomly assigned to six challenge groups and one negative control group. After delivery, each dam was allocated eight neonatal pups to foster. On postnatal day 1, pups in each challenge group (n = 8 per group) were inoculated intracerebrally with 25 µL of DMEM containing 6.25 × 102 TCId50 of the respective virus, while pups in the negative control group received the same volume of DMEM. A second experiment was then conducted using the same procedure, but with the inoculation route changed to s.c. injection, using another seven pregnant mice.

After completing the studies with the first virus panel, the second panel was evaluated using the same experimental design. For this panel, ten pregnant ICR mice were used for the i.c. route experiment, and another ten for the s.c. route experiment, following the identical procedure: nine challenge groups and one negative control group per route, with eight pups per group inoculated on postnatal day 1 with 25 µL of DMEM containing 6.25 × 102 TCID50 of the respective virus via the corresponding route, while control pups received DMEM alone.

Mortality and body weight of neonates were monitored daily for 10 days. Animals that either reached a humane endpoint (≥20% weight loss or signs of severe neurological impairment) or survived to the end of the observation period were euthanized. A full necropsy was performed immediately, and the brain, lung, heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and knee joints were collected for viral load quantification and histopathological assessment.

Pregnant mouse model: two independent experiments were conducted to assess pathogenicity via different inoculation routes: oronasal and i.m. In each experiment, 45 time-mated ICR dams were assigned to three gestational time points: early (embryonic day 6, E6), mid (E12), and late (E16), with 15 dams per time point. At each gestational time point, dams were randomly allocated to three treatment groups (n = 5 per group): GETV WT, Mutant, or DMEM (mock). Pregnant mice were inoculated with 50 µL containing 105 TCID50 of the indicated virus or an equivalent volume of DMEM via either the ON or i.m. route. Reproductive outcomes, including abortion rates and litter size, were recorded daily until 3 days postpartum. On postpartum day 3, all surviving dams and their pups were euthanized. Maternal blood was collected for serum separation and subsequent analysis of GETV‑specific antibodies by IFA. Uterine and ovarian tissues, as well as neonatal organs (brain, lung, heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and knee joints), were harvested for viral load quantification.

This study conducted animal experiments across the following periods: the LD50 studies were performed from September to October 2023; the pathogenicity studies using the neonatal mouse model were conducted from October to November 2023 and from September to October 2024; and the pathogenicity study using the pregnant mouse model was carried out from October to November 2024. Euthanasia of both dams and their pups was performed via cervical dislocation, strictly following the guidelines for the euthanasia of laboratory animals specified in the National Standard of the People’s Republic of China (GB/T 39,760–2021). To ensure objectivity, group allocation was performed by a researcher not involved in subsequent experiments. During inoculation, the investigator performing the injections was blinded to treatment allocation. Furthermore, all outcome assessments—including clinical signs, viral load, antibody detection, and pathology—were conducted by researchers blinded to treatment allocation, and data analysis was performed on coded samples.

Viral load quantification

Tissue samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in 1 mL of PBS and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. For neonatal tissues, four individual samples were combined to form a single pooled homogenate prior to centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted from 250 µL of the resulting supernatant using Trizol® reagent. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA with the FastKing RT Kit. Viral RNA was quantified by RT‑qPCR using FastFire qPCR PreMix (Cat: FP208, TIANGEN, China) with the following cycling protocol: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 15 s. The assay employed the following primer‑probe set targeting the partial sequence of NSP3 gene of GETV NM2022: forward, 5’‑CACCGTAACAGAAGCCGAAG‑3;’ reverse, 5’‑GGACTGCATCACTCTGTCCT‑3;’ TaqMan probe, 5’‑FAM‑AGCGGCCGCGTATCGAGCTG‑BHQ1‑3.’ A 10‑fold serial dilution of the pCMV‑NM2022‑WT plasmid (containing the whole sequence of NSP3 gene of GETV NM2022) was used to generate a standard curve, with the log10 of copy number plotted against Ct values. The curve demonstrated high linearity (R2 >0.98) and amplification efficiency >90%. The LOD was calculated by extrapolating the Ct value at the maximum cycle number (40 cycles) to the standard curve, yielding a copy number per reaction. This value was then converted to copies per gram of tissue using a constant conversion factor based on tissue weight, RNA elution volume, reverse‑transcription template volume, and qPCR input volume (all of which were uniform across samples). The final LOD was determined to be 4.9 × 103 copies/g. Samples with Ct values ≥40 were considered below the LOD and treated as “not detected.”

Sequence alignment

Amino acid sequences of the E1 glycoprotein from 71 GETV strains and ten representative alphaviruses were retrieved from GenBank; accession numbers are listed in Tables S3 and S4. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Clustal W algorithm implemented in DNASTAR Lasergene MegAlign software (version 11.1). Conservation across GETV strains was visualized as a sequence logo generated with WebLogo 3.7.4 (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com), where the height of each residue is proportional to its conservation frequency at that position. Comparative sequence analysis across alphaviruses was conducted in Jalview (version 2.11.2), and key functional residues were highlighted through manual annotation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were conducted as follows: two‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multi‑factorial designs; one‑way ANOVA followed by appropriate post‑hoc tests for comparisons among three or more groups; unpaired, two‑tailed Student’s t‑test for comparisons between two groups. Survival curves were compared using the log‑rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Statistical significance was defined as a p value < 0.05; p > 0.05 was considered not significant (ns).
