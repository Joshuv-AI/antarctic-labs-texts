Antibiotic Pharmacokinetics and Prostate Penetration in Bacterial Prostatitis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Rui Ge, Xinwen Nian, Benjuan Liu, Yunyun Yang, Zhuo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective treatment of bacterial prostatitis is limited by poor antibiotic penetration into prostatic tissue due to anatomical and physiological barriers, including the blood–prostate barrier, pH-dependent ion trapping, and protein binding.MethodsA systematic review was conducted to evaluate the intraprostatic pharmacokinetics of antibiotics. PubMed, Embase, and Medline were searched up to December 2024. In total, 44 studies reporting antibiotic concentrations in serum, prostate tissue, or prostatic fluids were included. Prostate tissue-to-serum ratios (PT/S) and pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed to assess prostate penetration.ResultsA total of 44 studies were included. Fluoroquinolones consistently demonstrated favorable intraprostatic penetration (PT/S ≥ 1), supporting their role as first-line agents. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX) showed moderate penetration driven primarily by trimethoprim, while macrolides and tetracyclines exhibited variable but measurable distribution. Most β-lactams penetrated poorly into non-inflamed prostate tissue, although selected cephalosporins achieved moderate concentrations in acute inflammatory settings. Fosfomycin showed variable intraprostatic exposure, indicating that tissue diffusion does not uniformly translate into clinical efficacy, particularly in chronic disease.ConclusionsAntibiotic efficacy in prostatitis is strongly influenced by intraprostatic pharmacokinetics rather than drug class alone. Integrating physicochemical properties, disease subtype, and clinical guideline recommendations is essential for rational antibiotic selection and optimized treatment strategies.Keywords: Prostatitis, Antibiotic, Pharmacokinetics, Drug biodistribution

Key Summary Points

Prostatitis treatment is often limited by poor intraprostatic antibiotic penetration and variable pharmacokinetic behavior.Fluoroquinolones achieve consistently high intraprostatic exposure and remain first-line agents across prostatitis subtypes.Antibiotics with favorable physicochemical properties—lipophilicity, weakly basic pKa, and low protein binding—are more likely to penetrate prostatic tissue effectively.β-lactams generally show poor penetration into non-inflamed prostate tissue, with limited roles in chronic disease but selected utility in acute or perioperative settings.This review systematically evaluates human data on antibiotic prostate tissue distribution and PT/S ratios to inform evidence-based therapeutic selection.

Introduction

Prostatitis is a common clinical syndrome classified into four categories: acute bacterial (ABP), chronic bacterial (CBP), chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome, and asymptomatic. Among these, acute and chronic bacterial prostatitis are definitively driven by bacterial infection and thus constitute the primary target for antimicrobial therapy [1–3]. Acute bacterial prostatitis often requires broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with agents achieving effective intraprostatic penetration and sufficient duration, as recommended by recent clinical guidelines, yet treatment success varies and antibiotic resistance continues to complicate management [4]. Currently, there is a lack of high-quality evidence to determine the optimal treatment duration and no established correlation between tissue pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes. This underscores the urgent need for a deeper understanding of antibiotic exposure levels within the prostate and their clinical significance [5].

The unique anatomical and physiological features of the prostate substantially limit antibiotic penetration. The blood–prostate barrier (BPB) and gradients in pH between blood and prostatic fluid influence drug distribution, and key physicochemical properties such as lipid solubility, protein binding, and dissociation constant (pKa) are major determinants of tissue penetration [6–8]. Recent pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) frameworks highlight that standard plasma measures may not predict tissue penetration accurately, especially in complex infection environments such as inflamed prostate tissue, where barriers and local inflammation can alter distribution [8–10].

Clinical practice guidelines for prostatitis management underscore the importance of achieving effective intraprostatic concentrations, yet recommendations regarding antibiotic selection and duration are often extrapolated from limited evidence [11]. For example, contemporary guidelines recommend agents such as fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole for CBP, but the evidence supporting optimal tissue penetration and clinical outcomes remains limited and heterogeneous [2]. Moreover, from a microbiological perspective, acute bacterial prostatitis is predominantly caused by Gram-negative uropathogens, particularly Enterobacterales, whereas chronic bacterial prostatitis may involve a broader spectrum of organisms, including atypical and intracellular pathogens. These microbiological characteristics further complicate treatment, as effective therapy requires not only sufficient antibiotic penetration into prostatic tissue but also activity against pathogens capable of intracellular persistence or biofilm formation [2, 12–15]. These microbiological features further underscore the importance of selecting agents with sufficient intraprostatic exposure and prolonged treatment duration.

Given these challenges, systematic integration of human studies reporting antibiotic concentrations in prostate tissue or prostatic fluid relative to serum levels is critical for elucidating the true penetration characteristics of antibiotics within the prostate. Understanding which antibiotic classes achieve reliable penetration under different pathological conditions, and clarifying how sampling timing, physicochemical properties, inflammatory status, and dosing strategies influence prostate exposure, can guide clinical decision-making and future research.

Methods

Review of Published Studies on Antibacterial Therapy in Prostate Disease

A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and MEDLINE databases was conducted from their inception up to 31 December 2024, focusing on English literature concerning antibacterial therapy for prostate diseases. Search terms combined keywords: ("Prostate" OR "Prostatitis" OR "Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP)" OR "prostatectomy") AND ("antibiotic" OR "antibacterial" OR "fluoroquinolone" OR "cephalosporin" OR "penicillin" OR "carbapenem" OR "macrolides" OR "minoglycoside" OR "tetracycline" OR "fosfomycin") AND ("pharmacokinetics" OR "penetration" OR "concentration" OR "tissue distribution"). References from related studies were also examined. Automated duplicate removal was performed using EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) prior to manual screening. The software was used solely to identify and remove duplicate records retrieved from different databases. No automated screening or study selection was conducted beyond duplicate identification. Following deduplication, all remaining records underwent independent manual screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts by two authors according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a senior investigator. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

All relevant articles retrieved and those from reference lists were screened. Inclusion criteria were: (1) the articles were based on human participants; (2) patients administered antibacterial agents for treatment or prophylaxis; (3) available administration and sampling time data; and (4) reported antibacterial concentrations in prostate tissue and/or prostatic fluid, with corresponding serum concentrations available for comparison where applicable.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) reviews, meta-analyses, or editorials; (2) animal or in vitro studies; (3) studies reporting only serum pharmacokinetics without measuring concentrations in prostate tissue or prostatic fluid fail to reflect drug penetration within the prostate; (4) studies lacking complete prostate-related pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., missing tissue/serum ratios); (5) duplicate publications or overlapping datasets; and (6) unavailable full text with abstracts containing insufficient quantifiable serum or tissue concentrations.

Data Extraction

Data on basic demographics, including dosage regimens (drug dosage, administration route, and frequency), age, weight, study design (number of patients, sample points, and sampling time), and prostate distribution parameters (serum and prostate tissue concentrations) were extracted from included studies. When available, time-matched sampling data were recorded to account for temporal variability in tissue penetration. The drug penetration ratio into prostate tissue was defined as the peak concentration (Cmax) or area under the drug–time curve (AUC) ratio, often used as an index of drug distribution into tissues. Owing to differences in tissue density caused by anatomical structure, fat content, inflammation, or disease (such as benign prostatic hyperplasia or malignant tumors), the specific gravity of prostate tissue was defined as 0.95–1.05 kg/L.

Results

Study Identification

In total, 579 studies were identified from PubMed, 1725 from Medline, and 1796 from Embase. After removing duplicates, 1954 studies were screened, and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Of the 61 studies assessed for eligibility, seven were review studies, three were unavailable, and three were not in English. Ultimately, 44 studies were included in the analysis. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses diagram for study identification is shown in Fig. 1. After the literature retrieval procedures, 44 studies were eventually retained for evaluation (Table 1). Cephalosporin, particularly cefazolin, shows promising prostate penetration with tissue concentrations reaching clinically effective levels for infection eradication. However, penicillin penetration is generally poor, limiting its utility for prostatitis treatment. Among fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin exhibit superior prostate tissue penetration, making them strong candidates for bacterial prostatitis treatment, as high tissue concentrations of these drugs are crucial for overcoming the BPB. Tetracyclines, such as minocycline, also demonstrate good penetration, although clinical efficacy may be influenced by protein binding and sampling time.

Prostate Tissue Concentration and Plasma Concentration

Antibiotic distribution between prostate tissue and systemic circulation critically determines treatment efficacy for prostatitis. This section summarizes pharmacokinetic data on antibiotic distribution between prostate tissue and plasma, with an overview of class-level penetration patterns presented in Table 2 and detailed tissue and serum concentrations with corresponding PT/S ratios presented in Table 3.

Cephalosporin Biodistribution in the Prostate

The biodistribution of cephalosporins, a widely used class of antibiotics [16–20], in the prostate has been the subject of several investigations. Cefazolin has shown promising results. In 1976, Litvak et al. reported higher cefazolin concentrations than cephalexin in both serum and prostatic tissues [21]. Sobels et al. evaluated the prostate tissue/serum (PT/S) ratio (mean ± standard deviation [SD]) following a single dose of cefazolin and found it to be 0.6 ± 0.3 at surgical initiation, indicating good prostate penetration [17]. These findings suggest cefazolin’s suitability for achieving clinically effective levels in prostate tissue.

Cefoperazone is another cephalosporin with demonstrated prostate penetration. Following intramuscular administration (1 g every 12 h), mean prostate tissue concentrations reached 22.8 ± 13.6 mg/kg and 23.2 ± 14.1 mg/kg at 60 and 90 min, respectively, which corresponded to approximately 60% and 65% of the simultaneous serum concentrations. Cefoperazone readily penetrates prostatic tissue, achieving concentrations above the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) for common pathogens, which supported its clinical efficacy in treating chronic bacterial prostatitis [22].

Penicillin Biodistribution in the Prostate

The penetration of penicillins, including mezlocillin, tazobactam, and piperacillin, into prostatic tissue remains limited. Mezlocillin diffusion into prostatic tissue, as reported by Naber, was highly dependent on prostate size and independent of drug infusion duration [23]. Smith et al. reported a median PT/S of 0.25 (0.18–0.41) for mezlocillin, significantly lower than azlocillin’s 0.35 (0.28–0.45) ratio [24]. A previous study administered piperacillin to patients undergoing transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP): the serum concentrations averaged 99.1 ± 54.8 mg/L, with prostate concentrations averaging 30.3 ± 29.2 mg/g. The mean serum:prostate ratio was 1:0.27. This indicates that piperacillin exhibits good penetration into prostate tissue [25].

Carbapenem Biodistribution in the Prostate

A prospective study evaluated prostatic penetration of ertapenem [26]. Mean prostatic tissue concentrations were 16.6 mg/L and 4.2 mg/L at 1 h and 4 h before surgery, respectively, with prostate-to-plasma ratios of 7.6 and 6.5 (P = 0.97). Ertapenem demonstrated excellent prostatic penetration, achieving tissue concentrations significantly above the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC₅₀) for common Gram-negative pathogens (including ESBL-producers) even 12 h after administration. These findings support ertapenem as a potential prophylactic option for prostatic procedures, particularly in patients colonized with multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [26].

Fluoroquinolone Biodistribution in the Prostate

Some studies comprehensively explored fluoroquinolone biodistribution in the prostate, including ciprofloxacin, enoxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin, and lomefloxacin.

Ciprofloxacin: Three studies analyzed ciprofloxacin distribution in the prostate tissue when used prophylactically for TURP. A study observed a preoperative concomitant administration of ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin, yielding a PT/S of 0.70 for ciprofloxacin and 0.37 for ofloxacin [27]. When ciprofloxacin was orally administered 1 h pre surgery, the mean tissue/serum ratio at the start of surgery was 2.2 ± 1.7 compared with 4.8 ± 2.5 2 h pre surgery [17]. An earlier study [28] in patients who underwent transrectal needle biopsy of the prostate found prostate-to-serum ratios of ciprofloxacin at 1 and 3 h to be 1.9:1.3 and 1.6:1, respectively. These studies demonstrated good tissue penetration of ciprofloxacin.

Several fluoroquinolones that are less commonly used today still demonstrate variable penetration into prostate tissue. Norfloxacin achieved prostate tissue concentrations comparable to serum levels, with PT/S ratios ranging from approximately 1.0 to 1.7 across studies [29–32]. Enoxacin consistently exhibited favorable distribution, with reported PT/S ratios between 1.4 and 2.54, indicating significantly higher tissue than plasma concentrations [33–36]. Ofloxacin showed a mean PT/S ratio of 2.1, indicating effective tissue accumulation [37]. Similarly, lomefloxacin is distributed effectively and homogeneously throughout the prostate zones, with a mean PT/S ratio of 2.0 [38, 39]. Although these agents demonstrate adequate prostate penetration, their current clinical use is limited owing to the availability of broader-spectrum or better-tolerated alternatives.

Fluoroquinolones generally exhibit good prostate tissue penetration. However, even within fluoroquinolones, intraprostatic exposure varies according to lipophilicity, pKa, and protein binding, highlighting that favorable penetration is agent specific rather than a uniform class effect. For example, ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin have the highest prostate tissue penetration (PT/S > 2) and are the best choices for bacterial prostatitis [17, 37]. Norfloxacin exhibits inconsistent penetration, which limits its clinical use [34], while lomefloxacin shows good ratios but requires further validation in larger cohorts [38, 39].

Macrolides Biodistribution in the Prostate

Azithromycin, as a macrolide antibiotic, exhibits excellent tissue penetration, achieving high concentrations, with concentrations significantly higher than plasma levels, and possesses a long half-life [40]. In a study by Foulds et al. [41], the mean concentration in prostatic tissue was 2.54 μg/g at 14 h after the last dose, while concurrent plasma concentrations were ≤ 0.1 μg/mL. Azithromycin was slowly eliminated from the prostate, with an estimated half-life of 60 h [40]. Notably, a mean concentration of 0.62 μg/g persisted even at 137 h post dosing, well above the MIC₉₀ for common urogenital pathogens such as Chlamydia trachomatis (0.25 μg/mL) [41]. These characteristics support its use in treating prostate infections. However, further research is needed to clarify the relationship between these properties and clinical efficacy in bacterial prostatitis.

Clarithromycin also effectively penetrates prostate tissue. Giannopoulos et al. [42] reported mean peak concentrations of 3.22 μg/g and 3.08 μg/g in the central and peripheral zones of the prostate adenoma, respectively, significantly exceeding plasma levels (1.51μg/g) and the MIC₉₀ values for common intracellular pathogens such as Ureaplasma urealyticum and Chlamydia trachomatis. These findings support its potential use in treating chronic abacterial prostatitis.

Aminoglycoside Biodistribution in the Prostate

Studies also evaluated the aminoglycoside tobramycin. Williams et al. administered a single intramuscular dose (80 mg) to patients undergoing transurethral resection. The mean serum and prostatic tissue concentrations were 2.8 mg/L and 2.7 mg/kg, respectively, yielding a prostate tissue-to-serum (PT/S) ratio of approximately 1.0. The study concluded that tobramycin could achieve inhibitory concentrations in the prostate against common pathogens [43], with no significant difference observed across different dosing frequencies [44].

Trimethoprim–Sulfamethoxazole Biodistribution in the Prostate

Trimethoprim (TMP) meets all of the theoretical requirements of diffusion into, and actual concentration in, human prostatic fluid. When TMP is combined with the sulfonamide sulfamethoxazole (SMX), commercially known as co-trimoxazole, potentiation of antibacterial activity is achieved and the development of resistant bacterial strains is less likely to occur [45].

TMP is a weakly basic, highly lipophilic drug with low protein-binding affinity. These properties may enable it to promote diffusion across the BPB and subsequently undergo ion trapping in the relatively acidic prostatic fluid, thereby achieving significant concentrations within prostate tissue [46]. Following a single intramuscular injection of co-trimoxazole (160 mg TMP/800 mg SMX), TMP prostate tissue concentrations were measured at 2.84 ± 0.36 μg/g, 2.28 ± 0.50 μg/g, and 2.26 ± 0.24 μg/g at 4, 8, and 12 h post injection, respectively. These levels were significantly higher than simultaneous plasma concentrations (0.70 ± 0.11, 0.98 ± 0.09, and 0.67 ± 0.07 μg/mL, P < 0.01), resulting in PT/S consistently > 2 across all time points [47]. Dabhoiwala et al. reported a mean PT/S ratio of 1.2 after a single oral dose and a significantly higher ratio of 2.05 (P < 0.01) following 1 week of therapy [48]. In the same year, a study demonstrated that following a single oral dose, the mean TMP concentration in prostate tissue was 0.36 ± 0.1 μg/g. Multiple oral dosing revealed significantly higher tissue accumulation (4.8 ± 1.2 μg/g, 5.2 ± 1.6 μg/g)[49]. These findings robustly confirm the effective penetration and dose-dependent accumulation of TMP in human prostatic tissue.

Conversely, owing to its weak acidity and higher protein binding rate, SMX exhibits significantly lower permeability. Wright et al. reported that mean prostatic tissue concentrations of SMX were 21.80 ± 2.58 μg/g, 10.65 ± 1.18 μg/g, and 16.60 ± 1.65 μg/g at 4, 8, and 12 h, respectively, which were substantially lower than simultaneous plasma concentrations (37.85 ± 2.56, 28.75 ± 2.54, and 24.10 ± 2.24 μg/mL), yielding PT/S ratios of approximately 0.58, 0.37, and 0.69 [47]. This aligns with findings from Dabhoiwala et al., where the mean PT/S ratios for SMX were 0.17 after a single dose and 0.35 after multiple doses [48]. Oosterlinck et al. further confirmed this trend, reporting PT/S ratios for SMX ranging from 0.27 to 0.53 depending on the dosing regimen [50]. Consistently, Madsen et al. found poor penetration of SMX across all groups, with PT/S ratios remaining below 0.5 (0.35 ± 0.10, 0.24 ± 0.06, 0.26), underscoring its limited distribution into prostatic tissue compared with plasma [49].

In conclusion, TMP effectively penetrates and concentrates in human prostatic tissue and fluid, often reaching levels several times higher than those in serum. In contrast, SMX demonstrates poor penetration, with tissue concentrations consistently below plasma levels, suggesting that the antibacterial activity of co-trimoxazole in the prostate may be primarily driven by TMP.

Tetracycline Biodistribution in the Prostate

Minocycline, a tetracycline derivative, exhibits variable but generally favorable penetration into prostatic tissues, as evidenced by the following studies: Patients receiving 500 mg minocycline preoperatively exhibited significantly higher drug concentrations in prostatic tissue, serum, and muscle than those receiving 300 mg minocycline. However, the PT/S ratio remained approximately 1:1 and was significantly higher than that in muscle and adipose tissue, suggesting proportional concentration increases across compartments with increasing dose [51]. Minocycline effectively diffused into BPH tissues with excellent tissue penetration.

Fosfomycin Biodistribution in the Prostate

The penetration of fosfomycin into prostatic tissue was evaluated in a prospective study by Gardiner et al. [52]. The mean plasma and urinary fosfomycin concentrations were 11.4 ± 7.6 μg/mL and 571 ± 418 μg/mL, respectively, at approximately 9.5 h post administration. The mean overall prostatic tissue concentration was 6.5 ± 4.9 μg/g (0.7–22.1 μg/g), with a mean prostate-to-plasma ratio of 0.67 ± 0.57. Notably, fosfomycin concentrations varied significantly between prostatic zones: mean levels in the transition zone (TZ) were 8.3 ± 6.6 μg/g, compared with 4.4 ± 4.1 μg/g in the peripheral zone (PZ). These data suggest that orally administered fosfomycin achieves meaningful intraprostatic concentrations in non-inflamed prostate tissue, supporting the potential of oral fosfomycin for prophylaxis against multi-drug resistant Gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB) in transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) biopsy and selected cases of prostatitis, though undetected cases appeared in a subsequent study [17].

Prostate Fluid Concentration and Plasma Concentration

Concentrations of some drugs were also measured in the prostate fluid, semen, and urine. This reflects drug concentration in the glandular ducts and secretory channels, potentially better approximating the actual drug concentration at the site of action (e.g., urethra and prostate ducts), allowing more direct assessment of drug effectiveness and safety at the target site. Furthermore, prostate fluid sampling is less expensive, requires less complex sample processing, and yields quicker results. For patients, prostate fluid collection via noninvasive methods, such as prostate massage, reduces risks such as infection and bleeding and has higher acceptance. Ten papers measured the prostate fluid concentration.

In a study by Goto et al. [53], the PT/S ratio of each drug was higher than the PF/S ratio. Among these, the PT/S of minocycline and ofloxacin (0.94 ± 0.39 and 1.49 ± 1.80, respectively) was significantly higher than that of piperacillin (0.21 ± 0.15). The PF/S of ofloxacin (0.89 ± 0.39) was significantly higher than that of the other groups, again demonstrating ofloxacin’s effectiveness’ lower permeability.

Ciprofloxacin, a more permeable fluoroquinolone, also achieves better therapeutic distribution in the prostate fluid. In healthy volunteers, Dalhoff et al. [54] found that after ciprofloxacin administration for 12–24 h, the concentration in expressed prostatic secretion was 15–0.9 mg/L, 30 times higher than that in the serum (compared with only twice the serum concentration at 1 h). This indicates significant diffusion into the prostate fluid. Regarding co-administration effects, compared with levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin had a lower PF/S ratio (0.248 versus 0.506) but a superior ejaculate-to-plasma ratio (4.85 versus 1.85) [55].

In patients undergoing TURP, ciprofloxacin’s PT/S ratios reached 2.27 ± 0.89 (single dose) and 2.14 ± 0.73 (repeated doses), with maximal tissue concentrations of 6.56 mg/kg (single dose). The prostate fluid to serum ratio ranged from 1.5% to 450%, exhibiting more variability than prostate tissue and potentially influenced by sampling time [56].

Multiple fluoroquinolones exhibit effective distribution into prostatic and seminal fluids. Ofloxacin reaches concentrations in ejaculate and seminal fluid approximately twice those in plasma, though levels in prostatic fluid are about one-third of plasma values [57, 58]. Fleroxacin shows a tissue–plasma ratio of 1.12, with plasma concentrations three times those in prostate secretions [59, 60]. Similarly, gatifloxacin demonstrates fluid-to-plasma ratios near or slightly above unity in ejaculate, seminal, and prostatic fluids [61]. Moxifloxacin also achieves prostatic fluid concentrations comparable to plasma levels, with a mean fluid/plasma ratio of 1.6 [62].

These studies indicate that fluoroquinolones have built-up permeability in prostate tissue, prostate fluid, and plasma, with significant accumulation in semen and prostate fluid. However, the effects of urine contamination on prostate fluid concentrations should be carefully considered.

Prostate and Plasma of C Maximum (Cmax) and Area Under the Curve (AUC)

It is worth noting that reported prostate-to-serum ratios varied substantially across studies, partly reflecting differences in sampling time after drug administration, as intraprostatic concentrations may persist or increase despite declining serum levels. Traditional methods relying on tissue biopsy and body fluid extraction are costly and ethically controversial, capturing the distribution state at a specific point in time only, which is subject to transient bias. As a result, direct comparison of PT/S ratios between studies should be interpreted with caution. Over the last decade, with the combined impetus of technological development, clinical requirements, and regulatory norms, AUC and Cmax have become primary pharmacokinetic analytical metrics. The AUC reflects the total exposure to a drug and correlates with the duration of efficacy, whereas the Cmax reflects the rate of absorption, an indicator of drug penetration into tissues. A PK/PD model was constructed, and both were utilized to reflect the ability of the drug to penetrate the prostate tissue, thus obtaining the drug distribution of broad-spectrum antimicrobials within the prostate. Cephalosporin Biodistribution in the Prostate

Nakamura et al. reported that the prostate-to-plasma concentration ratio of flomoxef was approximately 0.5 [63]. The study was divided into two dose groups, 500 mg and 1000 mg, in which the prostate/plasma Cmax ratios were 0.50 ± 0.19 and 0.48 ± 0.15, respectively, suggesting effective prostate tissue penetration. This review simultaneously calculated AUC0–1.5 (0.58 ± 0.11, 0.54 ± 0.12) and AUC0–∞ (0.48 ± 0.12, 0.42 ± 0.12) values of flomoxef, indicating rapid penetration into the prostate tissue and dose–linear pharmacokinetic properties.

Fluoroquinolone Biodistribution in the Prostate

In the same year, a pazufloxacin study determined prostate tissue concentrations for the first time [64]. The prostate tissue/plasma Cmax ratios of pazufloxacin were 0.82 ± 0.27 and 0.99 ± 0.34, respectively, and AUC0–1.5 ratios were 0.80 ± 0.12 and 0.98 ± 0.19, respectively. Ratios closer to 1 with increasing dose suggest better and more uniform distribution in the tissues.

Penicillin Biodistribution in the Prostate

For the piperacillin–tazobactam group (TZP), the prostate/plasma Cmax ratios were 0.360 ± 0.137 (low-dose group) and 0.401 ± 0.077 (high-dose group), with AUC0–∞ ratios of 0.354 ± 0.216 and 0.367 ± 0.149. Although prostate tissue penetration is 35–40%, its concentration can achieve antimicrobial effects from the perspective of tissue-associated PK/PD behavior, especially for common pathogens [65].

Carbapenem Biodistribution in the Prostate

PK/PD models have been constructed for the following carbapenems: meropenem [66] and donipenem [67], with similar dosing regimens. The prostate/plasma Cmax ratios were relatively stable across dose groups (approximately 0.18–0.19 for meropenem and 0.173 for doripenem). AUC0–∞ for meropenem was 0.15–0.16 and for doripenem was 0.187. It was concluded that, despite limited penetration, antibiotic concentrations are sufficient to achieve antimicrobial effects, and high-dose short-interval dosing regimens significantly improve this effect [66, 67].

The direct correlation between AUC and Cmax with drug efficacy or toxicity offers advantages in terms of ease of operation, data integration capability, and clinical relevance, particularly for individualized treatment and drug development in complex pathological states. With the popularization of modeling and simulation technologies (e.g., the PB/PK model), the applications of AUC and Cmax are expected to be further deepened in the future.

Discussion

Prostatic Distribution of Antibiotics across Classes and Implications for Clinical Efficacy across Prostatitis Subtypes

This review systematically summarizes human pharmacokinetic studies reporting antibiotic concentrations in prostatic tissue, prostatic fluid, or seminal fluid, highlighting marked differences in intraprostatic exposure among commonly used antimicrobial classes and their potential clinical implications across prostatitis subtypes. Fluoroquinolones consistently demonstrated favorable prostate penetration, with PT/S ratios frequently approaching or exceeding unity, supporting their long-standing role as first-line agents for bacterial prostatitis [17, 27, 37]. Macrolides and tetracyclines also demonstrate measurable penetration, although variability exists across specific agents and sampling protocols [40, 41, 51]. TMP–SMX exhibited moderate prostate penetration, with trimethoprim contributing predominantly to tissue exposure owing to its weakly basic properties and low protein binding [47, 50]. In contrast, most β-lactam antibiotics showed limited penetration into non-inflamed prostatic tissue, with PT/S ratios typically well below 0.5 [8], limiting their role in chronic disease but supporting their use in perioperative prophylaxis or acute inflammatory settings. Fosfomycin showed variable intraprostatic concentrations, indicating that measurable tissue diffusion does not necessarily translate into consistent clinical efficacy, particularly in chronic bacterial prostatitis [17, 52]. Consequently, current guidelines do not recommend fosfomycin as first-line therapy [68]. However, an ongoing multicenter randomized trial in Spain comparing oral fosfomycin with cefixime and ciprofloxacin as step-down therapy for acute bacterial prostatitis may help define its clinical utility. Collectively, these findings highlight the complex interplay between antibiotic physicochemical properties, prostatic compartment dynamics, and disease subtype in determining both intraprostatic exposure and clinical outcomes.

Class Heterogeneity and Major Exceptions

Despite the frequent use of antibiotic class-based generalizations, substantial heterogeneity exists within individual classes with respect to prostate penetration. Among β-lactams, while penicillins and many first-generation cephalosporins penetrate poorly, select cephalosporins and carbapenems have demonstrated measurable intraprostatic concentrations [8, 17, 21, 22]. Similarly, aminoglycosides are regarded as ineffective for prostatic infections owing to poor tissue diffusion; however, classical human studies summarized in comprehensive reviews have reported detectable prostatic fluid or tissue concentrations for agents such as tobramycin and netilmicin, representing notable intraclass exceptions [8, 43, 69]. Their clinical utility remains limited by parenteral administration and toxicity concerns [70]. Macrolides further illustrate class heterogeneity, as their clinical activity in chronic prostatitis may be partly explained by properties beyond extracellular tissue concentration, including high intracellular accumulation and anti-biofilm effects, which may compensate for only moderate PT/S ratios [14, 71, 72]. These observations emphasize that antibiotic selection for prostatitis should not rely solely on class designation but rather on individual drug characteristics and disease context.

Mechanistic Determinants of Prostatic Penetration

Antibiotic penetration into prostatic tissue is governed by multiple physicochemical and physiological factors. The pKa of a drug critically influences its degree of ionization across the pH gradient between blood and prostatic fluid, which is typically acidic; weakly basic antibiotics may undergo ion trapping, resulting in higher intraprostatic accumulation [8, 73, 74]. Lipophilicity is another major determinant, as lipid-soluble agents cross the BPB primarily by passive diffusion, whereas hydrophilic drugs exhibit restricted tissue entry [8]. Protein binding further modulates effective tissue exposure, since only the unbound fraction of an antibiotic is available for diffusion into prostatic tissue. High plasma protein binding reduces the free drug fraction available for passive diffusion into prostatic tissue and partly explains the divergent intraprostatic exposure observed among agents within the same antimicrobial class [8, 75]. Highly protein-bound agents, including many β-lactams, therefore show limited penetration despite adequate serum levels [8].

Integration with Clinical Guidelines and Therapeutic Implications

Current clinical guidelines consistently recommend fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim-based regimens as first-line therapy for both acute and chronic bacterial prostatitis, largely reflecting their reliable intraprostatic penetration and broad activity against Gram-negative uropathogens [68, 76]. β-Lactams, including cephalosporins and carbapenems, are primarily reserved for acute severe infections or initial intravenous therapy, where inflammation-associated barrier disruption may temporarily improve tissue exposure [9, 77]. Beyond drug selection, treatment duration is a critical determinant of clinical success. While acute bacterial prostatitis may respond to shorter treatment courses following rapid bacterial eradication, chronic bacterial prostatitis typically necessitates prolonged therapy to ensure sustained intraprostatic drug exposure. Particularly in chronic bacterial prostatitis, prolonged treatment durations are required to maintain intraprostatic concentrations above pathogen minimum inhibitory concentrations, particularly for intracellular or biofilm-associated organisms [9, 68, 78]. These considerations highlight that optimal antibiotic selection depends not only on antimicrobial spectrum but also on sustained prostate exposure, physicochemical drug properties, and disease chronicity.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that antibiotic penetration into the prostate varies widely across and within antimicrobial classes, reflecting complex interactions between drug physicochemical properties, protein binding, and prostate-specific anatomical and physiological barriers. Fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim-based regimens consistently achieve favorable intraprostatic exposure, supporting their continued role as cornerstone therapies for bacterial prostatitis.

Importantly, class-based generalizations may obscure clinically relevant exceptions, as select agents within traditionally unfavorable classes can achieve meaningful prostate concentrations under specific conditions, particularly during acute inflammation. An ideal antibiotic for prostatitis should therefore combine adequate lipid solubility, weakly basic characteristics, low protein binding, and sustained tissue exposure exceeding pathogen MICs. Integrating prostate pharmacokinetic data with microbiological profiles and guideline-based recommendations is essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving clinical outcomes in patients with prostatic infections.
