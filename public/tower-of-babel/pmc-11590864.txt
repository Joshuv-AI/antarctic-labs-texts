Trends of mortality from chronic respiratory diseases by sex and ethnicity in the USA: a secular analysis from 1979 to 2021 using data from death certificates
Authors: Yaxian Meng, Xiaojie Han, Qianqian Ji, Aijie Zhang, Yiqiang Zhan, Xiaoliang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver the past decades, the prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) has undergone significant changes. Analysing long-term mortality trends by sex and ethnicity can inform more targeted public health interventions.MethodsUsing data from death certificates, we calculated age-standardised mortality rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD), asthma and pneumoconiosis among the US population from 1979 to 2021, stratified by sex and ethnicity. Trend analyses of mortality rates were conducted using joinpoint regression models.ResultsMortality trends varied significantly by demographic groups and disease types. Specifically, COPD mortality exhibited an overall increasing trend among females and white individuals throughout the study period while remaining stable among males and black individuals. ILD mortality showed an overall increasing trends for both males and females as well as individuals of white ethnicity. Asthma mortality showed an increase before 1996 followed by a consistent decline across all populations from about 1996 to 2021. In contrast, pneumoconiosis mortality has risen among white individuals since 2001 with a notable increase among females after 2009.ConclusionOur analysis reveals fluctuating trends in CRD mortality rates from 1979 to 2021 with varying patterns across sex, ethnicity and disease types. These findings underscore the need for ongoing research and tailored interventions to address these disparities.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Asthma; Asthma Epidemiology; COPD epidemiology; Interstitial Fibrosis

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, have a significant impact on global health resulting in considerable morbidity and mortality.1 Recognising the severity of this issue, the WHO established the WHO Global Alliance against Chronic Respiratory Diseases.2 The prevalence of CRDs has been increasing due to factors such as the rapid growth of the ageing population, changes in population demographics and social development, economic shifts and changes in risk exposures.3 4

Although there has been a global decrease in age-standardised mortality rates for CRDs from 1990 to 20195 6 indicating successful implementation of measures to combat these diseases, the USA continues to experience higher mortality rates compared with 1980.4 However, previous studies have primarily focused on mortality trends in the total or white population with limited coverage before the year 1999, thus offering sparse insight into overall respiratory disease trends. Additionally, many studies have found substantial variations exist in mortality rates and their temporal shifts across sex, age groups and counties.4 The scarcity of studies examining potential ethnic disparity in CRDs highlights the necessity for comprehensive epidemiological studies to explore potential differences in mortality rates among different ethnic groups.

The primary aims of this study are to explore trends specific to different sex and ethnic groups by conducting a comprehensive analysis of mortality due to key CRDs including COPD, interstitial lung disease (ILD), asthma and pneumoconiosis in the USA. By focusing on the mortality rates for these diseases, the study aims to provide a current and thorough understanding of long-term trends across a period from 1979 to 2021.

Methods

Causes of death data

In this secular analysis, we used a publicly available data set containing causes of death, sourced from death certificates collected in the USA by the National Center for Health Statistics spanning the years 1979 to 2021.7 The National Center for Health Statistics codes the death certificates to produce comprehensive data on causes of death.7 In our analysis, we focused specifically on the underlying cause of death (UCOD) when identifying deaths related to respiratory diseases. From 1979 to 1998, fatalities were categorised based on the Ninth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9)8 with the adoption of the Tenth Revision (ICD-10) beginning in 1999.9 Due to the completeness of data from death certificates, this study did not take missing data into account. Additionally, we obtained annual population estimates from the US Census Bureau’s Population Estimates Programme10 which were stratified by sex, race and age groups.

Defining respiratory diseases-related deaths

In this study, we employed ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes derived from the underlying causes of death data to delineate pertinent outcomes. We specifically focused on CRDs with significant public health burdens including COPD, ILD, asthma and pneumoconiosis. For the period spanning from 1979 to 1998, the following codes were used to identify these diseases: COPD (490–492, 493.2, 496), ILD (515–516), asthma (493.0, 493.1, 493.9) and pneumoconiosis (495, 500–505). From 1999 to 2021, the codes for these diseases were as follows: COPD (J40–J44), ILD (J84), asthma (J45–J46) and pneumoconiosis (J60–J67). The detailed ICD codes for each disease across the specified periods are presented in online supplemental table S1.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we used robust statistical methods to analyse mortality using RStudio. The causes of death data were used to compile the number of deaths attributed to specific CRDs across different populations. These data were stratified by 5-year age groups, ranging from 0 to 4 years old up to 85 years and above. All rates were expressed as deaths per 100 000 individuals and were age-standardised to the US 2000 population. Characteristics of the US 2000 population were provided in online supplemental table S2. To assess the precision of the rates, we used a Poisson approximation to calculate SE.11 12

Employing joinpoint regression, we performed a trend analysis of the age-standardised rates. This was achieved through the application of the Joinpoint Regression Programme V.4.9.1 which uses segmented weighted least squares regression. The programme iteratively adds joinpoints to the regression model and determines the optimal number of joinpoints ranging from 0 to 5 based on the best fit to the data. Model fit evaluation was conducted using a permutation test. For each analysis, we reported the annual per cent change (APC) and 95% CI as well as the average annual per cent change (AAPC) for the entire study period.

Ethical considerations related to privacy and data protection were carefully observed throughout the study.

Results

Mortality trends by sex

We found trends in mortality due to COPD differ by sex. For females, the COPD mortality rate exhibited a consistent increasing trend rising from 11.07 in 1979 to 30.10 in 2021 with an average annual increase of 2.07% (95% CI: 1.57%, 2.58%). In contrast, the mortality rate for males demonstrated relative stability changing from 44.53 in 1979 to 36.28 in 2021 resulting in an average annual change of −0.63% (95% CI: −2.73%, 1.52%) (table 1). The detailed annual number of deaths as well as the crude and age-standardised mortality rates by sex throughout the study period are presented in online supplemental table S3. Specifically, both males and females experienced an increase in COPD mortality during 1979–1985. After 1985, the COPD mortality rate for males began to fall while that for females kept rising until 2017. Recent death rates due to COPD have decreased for both males and females (online supplemental table S4 and figure 1).

The mortality rate from ILD shows a significant increase for both males and females during the whole study period. For males, the ILD mortality rate increased from 2.54 in 1979 to 6.82 in 2021 by 1.96% (95% CI: 1.77%, 2.16%) per year. For females, the ILD mortality rate rose by 2.12% (95% CI: 1.88%, 2.35%) per year from 1.38 in 1979 to 3.94 in 2021. However, the growth rates slowed with the APC decreasing from 2.99% (95% CI: 2.72%, 3.25%) during 1979–2004 to 0.48% (95% CI: 0.16%, 0.80%) after 2004 for males and from 3.57% (95% CI: 3.26%, 3.89%) during 1979–2004 to 0.01% (95% CI: −0.38%, 0.40%) after 2004 for females.

The asthma mortality increased initially by 3.27% per year for males and 5.55% per year for female, remaining stable from 1989 for male and 1988 for female, before decreasing after 1996. For males, asthma mortality demonstrated a significant downward trend decreasing from 1.31 in 1979 to 0.77 in 2021 with AAPC of −0.82% (95% CI: −1.43%, −0.20%). Conversely, the decline trend for females was not statistically significant.

The mortality rate attributable to pneumoconiosis for males decreased by 3.45% per year (95% CI: −5.41%, −1.46%) from 2.02 in 1979 to 0.07 in 2010 after which it levelled off. The trend in rate for females is markedly different from that for males. The mortality rate from pneumoconiosis first increased substantially by 7.55% (95% CI: 0.13%, 15.52%) per year from 1979 to 1985 followed by stabilisation between 1985 and 2009. From 2009 to 2021, the rate for females exhibited accelerated growth of 9.48% (95% CI: 7.35%, 11.66%) per year. The absolute number of deaths among females increased from 21 in 1979 to 104 in 2021.

Mortality trends by ethnicity

The detailed annual number of deaths as well as the crude and age-standardised mortality rates by ethnicity throughout the study period are presented in online supplemental table S5. There were similar patterns in COPD mortality for whites and blacks. The rate had risen rapidly by 4.36% (95% CI: 2.67%, 6.07%) per year for white and 5.91% (95% CI: 4.26%, 7.58%) per year for blacks from 1979 and then decelerating for white with 1.88% (95% CI: 1.53%, 2.24%) per year and for blacks with 1.72% (95% CI: 1.18%, 2.27%) per year in about 1985 before levelled off in about 2000(online supplemental table S6, figure 2). The ILD age-standardised mortality for whites increased from 1.84 in 1979 to 5.52 in 2021 resulting in an average annual increase of 2.65% (95% CI: 1.99%, 3.31%). The rate for blacks increased substantially by 1.89% (95% CI: 1.10%, 2.69%) per year during 1979–1997 and remained stable after 1997.

The asthma mortality rate was higher among blacks than two times that among whites in 1979 which has been increasing from 1979 to 1997 and decreasing after 1997 for both populations. Compared with the rates in 1979, the mortality rates due to pneumoconiosis in 2021 were significantly lower for both white and black individuals with AAPC of −5.89% (95% CI: −8.63%, −3.07%) and −7.15% (95% CI: −10.71%, −3.46%), respectively. However, since 2001, the mortality rate of whites with pneumoconiosis has slightly increased by 2.47% (95% CI: 1.38%, 3.57%) per year. The corresponding number of deaths for this trend increased from 121 in 2001 to 236 in 2021.

Discussion

In this study, we analysed mortality trends for specific CRDs in the USA from 1979 to 2021 focusing on sex and ethnic differences. We observed significant variations in mortality rates across different demographic groups and disease types. Specifically, COPD mortality exhibited an overall increasing trend among females and white individuals throughout the study period while remaining stable among males and black individuals. ILD mortality showed overall increasing trend for both males and females as well as individuals of white ethnicity. Asthma mortality increased before 1996 but declined consistently across all populations from 1996 to 2021. In contrast, pneumoconiosis mortality has risen among white individuals since 2001 with a notable increase among females after 2009.

Previous studies have reported trends in mortality rates from COPD with an upward trend from 1969 to 1999 and a downward trend from 1999 to 2013.13 Our study confirms these findings, particularly for males. An increasing trend in COPD mortality has also been reported among females which may be due to the rising prevalence of COPD in this group.13,17 The trend difference between males and females is likely influenced by historical smoking patterns as smoking prevalence among females increased more slowly but peaked later. Females with COPD are also more susceptible to lung damage from smoking.14,16 Ethnic disparity in COPD mortality may be influenced by differences in diagnosis and treatment. Limited healthcare access for black individuals may lead to underdiagnosis and underutilisation of recommended treatments for COPD which may partly explain the consistently lower COPD mortality rates observed among black individuals compared with white individuals.18 19 Additionally, black individuals may die at younger ages from smoking-related cardiovascular or cancer-related causes before COPD, further contributing to the observed disparity.20 In contrast, the elevated COPD mortality rate among white individuals may be associated with their higher prevalence of the disease influenced by genetic factors, tobacco smoke exposure and other environmental factors as well as the ageing population.18 19

The mortality rate for ILD has shown a substantial increase over the entire study period regardless of sex and ethnicity. Higher mortality rates were observed among whites compared with blacks and among male compared with female. These findings align with trends identified in the UK and European Union where similar disparities have been noted.21 However, while these regions have recently experienced a decline in ILD mortality due to advancements in diagnosis and management, our study did not find a similar trend in the USA. This could be attributed to the rising prevalence and incidence of ILD in the country.22 Despite the introduction of antifibrotic agents such as nintedanib and pirfenidone in 2014 to slow lung fibrosis progression, their use remains limited.23 A US-based study showed that only 26% of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis had commenced antifibrotic therapy between 2014 and 2019.24 Nevertheless, growing evidence supports the benefits of these treatments which may improve future ILD outcomes.25

Asthma mortality rates significantly decreased after about 1996 consistent with previous findings.26 27 This decline is largely attributed to improved medical management, particularly the increased use of inhaled corticosteroids.28 However, notable disparities were observed with higher mortality rates among females and black individuals compared with males and white individuals.29 30 These differences may be due to varying susceptibility to respiratory infections, environmental exposures and disparities in access to quality healthcare.31

Pneumoconiosis, a respiratory disease caused by occupational exposure to certain dusts, has long been recognised as a risk factor for mortality. Occupational regulations and exposure limits for silica and coal dust were established and implemented in the 1960s and 1970s contributing to a reduction in exposure-related mortality.32 33 The decline in pneumoconiosis mortality during 1979–1998 may be attributed to a reduction in exposure to silica and coal dust as well as improved health protections and federal benefits for coal workers affected by pneumoconiosis.34 However, our study reveals a rising trend in pneumoconiosis mortality since 2001 contrasting with earlier findings that indicated declining death rates.35 36 This rise may be linked to advancements in diagnostic techniques that enhance disease detection as well as the increasing prevalence of coal worker’s pneumoconiosis in the 2000s.37 Furthermore, individuals diagnosed with pneumoconiosis often present with extremely severe and difficult-to-treat conditions.38 Additionally, we observed a growing number of pneumoconiosis deaths among females, although still lower than among males. This could be related to the rising participation of women in occupations with dust exposure and potential sex-specific vulnerabilities to respiratory diseases. Further research is needed to confirm this trend and targeted policies and interventions are necessary to improve the prevention and treatment of pneumoconiosis.

Additionally, this study found a significant decrease in COPD mortality in 2020 and 2021 across all populations, largely attributed to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic which caused substantial excess mortality globally. Although patients with COPD are at increased mortality risk when infected with COVID-19,39 a meta-analysis that encompassed 13 studies revealed that during the pandemic there was roughly a 50% reduction in hospital admissions for patients suffering from COPD exacerbations40 which further contributed to the decline in COPD mortality. A key factor contributing to the reduction may be decreased exposure to respiratory viruses, likely due to hand hygiene, the use of face masks, physical distancing among the general public and protective measures for individuals with COPD. In contrast, while ILD increases susceptibility, severity and mortality of COVID-19,41 42 we observed that ILD mortality continued its upward trend in 2020 and 2021, potentially due to the smaller population affected by COVID-19 which may have limited the overall impact on ILD mortality. Some studies indicate that asthma is not an independent risk factor for COVID-19 mortality.43 Furthermore, evidence about the association between pneumoconiosis and COVID-19 remains limited.

Our study offers valuable insights into the evolving trends of CRD mortality in the USA from 1979 to 2021 contributing to the field in several important ways. First, by extending the analysis over a more comprehensive time frame than previous research, we capture long-term patterns and historical factors that may have shaped current trends. This broader perspective provides a deeper understanding of the changing mortality landscape. Second, we address a critical gap in existing research by stratifying the analysis by ethnicity and sex, revealing important disparities in mortality rates across different population groups. This approach enhances our understanding of how CRDs disproportionately affect specific demographics. Finally, by focusing on disease-specific trends in COPD, ILD, asthma and pneumoconiosis, we uncover shifts in mortality dynamics for each condition offering more granular insights into these diseases. Through these contributions, our study provides a holistic view of the changing CRD landscape.

This analysis is subject to several important limitations that should be taken into consideration. First, the transition between two ICD versions during the study period could introduce spurious trends due to changes in cause-of-death coding. To mitigate this, we applied a consistent mapping of ICD codes to respiratory disease categories. Second, the reliance on UCOD data from death certificates may not fully capture the true mortality burden as these records are susceptible to inaccuracies. The lack of independent verification raises concerns about potential misclassification or the use of non-specific or implausible cause-of-death codes. Third, the potential for under-reporting of CRDs as well as the extent of under-reporting varies by geography and demographic group which could lead to inaccuracies in the reported mortality rates. Despite these challenges, we believe our findings are credible due to the reliability of our data sources and these findings provide an updated and extensive understanding of disease-specific mortality in the USA. Future research should address these limitations and explore additional data sources to provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of respiratory disease mortality.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the evolving trends of CRD mortality in the USA, revealing important findings across different diseases and demographic groups. Compared with the period before 2000, mortality rates for COPD, ILD and asthma have been somewhat controlled in the 2000s. Despite these improvements, CRDs continue to impose a substantial burden on public health. Our findings underscore the need for ongoing prevention and intervention efforts, particularly for COPD and for addressing disparities in disease outcomes across sex and ethnic groups. Future research should focus on uncovering the factors driving these trends and guiding the development of policies aimed at further reducing the burden of respiratory diseases.
