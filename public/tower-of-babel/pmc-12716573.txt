Real-world analysis and future trends of stroke-related mortality and burden in Pudong: a comprehensive study from 2005 to 2035
Authors: Zheng Luo, Chunyan Kuang, Yan Zhang, Yichen Chen, Wenchang Jia, Xiaoling Wu, Mengcen Qian, Huihui Lv, Xiaopan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesStroke is a major global health issue with rising incidence in China. Current estimates often focus on specific deaths, underestimating their impact. This study aimed to quantify and predict the all-cause and cause-specific burden of stroke using real-world data.DesignPopulation-based study.Participants and settingResidents in Shanghai Pudong, which covers an area of 1210.41 km² (467.34 mi²) and has a population of 3.17 million, spanning from 2005 to 2021.OutcomesStroke-related deaths in Pudong (2005–2021) were analysed by gender, age and subtype. Urgent action is needed to manage risk factors and prevent stroke.Keywords: Stroke, Aging, Mortality, Epidemiology

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally and the third leading cause of death and disability combined, as measured by disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) lost.1 2 The burden of stroke has increased substantially from 1990 to 2019, with a 70.0% increase in stroke incidence, a 43.0% increase in stroke-related deaths and a 143.0% increase in DALYs.3 Notably, 86.0% of stroke deaths and 89.0% of DALYs occur in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs).3 Furthermore, the economic cost of stroke is substantial, with an estimated global cost of over US$721 billion, representing 0.66% of the global gross domestic product.1 4 These data highlight the importance of implementing stroke prevention and management strategies to mitigate the disease’s impact.

In LMICs, stroke management is particularly challenging due to limited resources, awareness and technical capacity, especially in the context of rapid population ageing.5 6 Severe healthcare disparities exist in these countries, characterised by delayed stroke diagnosis and higher rates of poor clinical outcomes for patients who had a stroke. The in-hospital mortality rate for patients who had a stroke in these countries is 19.81%, with higher rates observed among those with haemorrhagic stroke.1 5 Even as the largest developing country in the world, China faces a significant stroke burden. In Shanghai Pudong, a developed region in China, the proportion of the population aged 65 and older increased from 14.21% in 2008 (meeting the WHO standard for an ‘aged society’, ≥14%) to 20.83% in 2018 (meeting the WHO standard for a ‘hyper-aged society’, ≥20%).7 This demographic shift is representative of China’s rapid reformation and urbanisation and likely contributes significantly to the stroke burden in the country.7

Moreover, similar to other developed cities in LMICs, Shanghai Pudong, the epitome of China’s reformation, is under great pressure to manage the many premature deaths that occur from strokes among residents, due to the dual pressures of rapid population ageing and the swift influx of people.8,10 Consistent, comparable and systematic analysis of long-term trends and patterns in stroke is essential to guide public policy and provide benchmarks for decision makers.11

Years of life lost (YLL) refers to the loss of life caused by premature death, and it can more accurately reflect the burden of a disease on society where it is difficult to carry out disease surveillance in the population.12 Studying the changes in YLL and the mortality differences in subgroups can be useful to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of intervention measures.13 Therefore, here we sought to analyse trends in stroke-related deaths, as well as specific trends that occurred in deaths from stroke in age, gender and subtypes from 2005 to 2021 in Shanghai Pudong. Using these data, we then sought to predict stroke-related deaths through 2035. With this work, we hope to contribute to helping the developed regions of LMICs that are in or entering an ageing society or hyper-aged society to better address the challenges posed by the increasing burden of stroke.

Methods

Data source

We analysed all-cause mortality data from 2005 to 2021 in Shanghai Pudong, which covers an area of 1210.41 km² (467.34 mi²) and has a population of 3.17 million.14 Data, including age, gender and cause of death, were collected from the Pudong Mortality Registration System. Periodic evaluations, data cleaning and compilation were conducted to ensure the completeness of the registration system, and no individual-level records were excluded. In Shanghai Pudong, death registration is legally required for social-security settlement and inheritance, guaranteeing 100% coverage. The rules for underlying-cause attribution and associated quality-control procedures have been reported previously,15 and protocols for assigning causes in unattended home deaths are also documented.16 The missing information was stroke-subtype details on some death certificates; these deaths were retained in the overall stroke analyses, but for subtype-specific calculations 6.41% (5 945) were classified as ‘unclassified stroke’.

Stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, and we distinguished two mortality definitions. ‘Stroke-specific death’ was assigned when stroke (ICD-10: I60–I64, I69.0–I69.4) was recorded as the underlying cause of death. ‘Stroke-related death’ was defined as any death certificate that mentioned stroke anywhere in the causal chain (underlying or contributing causes), thereby capturing fatal events where stroke was either the primary driver or a comorbid accelerator of mortality. Causes of death were determined by rigorously trained clinicians and verified by the local Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

Statistical analyses

Crude mortality rates (CMR) and age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRW) were calculated using Segi’s world standard population for neurological disorders and expressed per 100 000 population.17 Age-standardised rates per 100 000 person-years and their 95% CIs were calculated by the gamma method, assuming Poisson-distributed death counts. CMR and ASMRW were compared between genders using the Poisson approximation method and the Mantel-Haenszel test, respectively. YLLs were calculated using the WHO template and the average YLL (AYLL) was determined by dividing the total YLL by the number of death cases.18

Temporal trends in CMR, ASMRW and YLL rates were analysed using Joinpoint Regression Programme V.5.1.1.0 (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) and expressed as average annual per cent change (AAPC) with corresponding 95% CIs. Z-tests were conducted to assess whether the AAPC was statistically different from zero. Trends were described as ‘increasing’ or ‘decreasing’ if statistically significant (p<0.05); otherwise, they were described as ‘stable’.

Age was classified into six groups: 0–29, 30–44, 45–59, 60–69, 70–79 and ≥80 years. We compared mortality rates for each year from 2006 to 2021 with those from 2005 to determine whether changes in mortality rates were influenced by ageing or other factors. These comparisons were made using the decomposition method, which calculated and compared mortality rates for each 5-year age group from 0 to 4 to ≥85 years.

We used the R packages ‘forecast’ and ‘tseries” to project mortality and YLL rates from 2022 to 2035. These projections allowed us to better understand potential trends and plan targeted interventions. Line graphs were plotted with the X-axis representing years (2005–2035) and the Y-axis representing the rate values of the selected indicators.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA) and R (V.3.4.3). A p value of<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Baseline

From 2005 to 2021, there were 362 558 total deaths, including 92 779 stroke-related deaths. Of these, 63 917 were stroke-specific deaths, accounting for 68.89% (online supplemental figure S1).

Among the 92 779 stroke-related deaths, there were 148 underlying causes of death (online supplemental table S1). The top three diseases in this population were stroke, heart disease and diabetes mellitus (online supplemental figure S2A and online supplemental table S1).

Among the 63 917 stroke-specific deaths, individuals had a total of 198 090 causes of death. On average, each person had 3.10 different diseases. The top five comorbidities, regardless of gender, were hypertensive diseases, respiratory system diseases, heart diseases, diabetes mellitus and influenza/pneumonia. Notably, fewer males had heart disease compared with females (8.35% vs 10.72%, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure S2B and online supplemental table S2).

Mortality and years of life lost

Among stroke-related deaths, the median age at death was 82.77 year, the YLL and AYLL were 694, 634.31 years and 7.49 years per person. The CMR, ASMRW and YLL rate were 192.18 (95% CI 190.95 to 193.42) per 100 000 person-years, 60.77 (95% CI 60.35 to 61.20) per 100 000 person-years, 1438.86 (95% CI 1435.48 to 1442.250) per 100 000 respectively. CMR, ASMRW, YLL, YLL rate and AYLL were higher in males than in females, while females had a higher median age at death. For stroke subtypes, CMR, ASMRW, YLL and YLL rate were higher in ischaemic stroke than in haemorrhagic stroke, although haemorrhagic stroke had a higher AYLL. When assessing comorbidities, CMR, ASMRW, YLL and YLL rate were higher in heart disease-specific deaths, while diabetes mellitus-specific deaths had a higher AYLL (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in table 1). Among all deaths, 88 015 individuals were aged 60 or older, accounting for 94.87% of total deaths. The number of deaths, proportion, CMR, ASMRW, YLL and YLL rate increased with age (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in table 2).

Trends analysis and prediction

The proportion of stroke-related deaths among all deaths in Shanghai Pudong increased from 24.28% in 2005 to 26.96% in 2021, corresponding to an annual increase of 0.68% (p<0.001; online supplemental figure S3A, online supplemental table S3). Conversely, the proportion of stroke-specific deaths among all deaths decreased from 18.59% in 2005 to 17.63% in 2021, indicating an annual decrease of 0.37% (p=0.005; online supplemental figure S3B, online supplemental table S3). Additionally, the proportion of stroke-specific deaths among stroke-related deaths declined from 76.60% in 2005 to 65.41% in 2021, corresponding to an annual decrease of 1.03% (p<0.001; online supplemental figure S3C, online supplemental table S3).

In both stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths, the CMR showed significant increases from 2005 to 2021 among males, females and the total population, with the exception of stroke-specific deaths in females (p>0.05). Specifically, stroke-specific deaths decreased significantly among individuals aged 45–79 years and those over 80 years, while stroke-related deaths decreased significantly among individuals aged 0–29 years (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental table S4).

The ASMRW of both stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths showed significant decreases from 2005 to 2021 among males, females and the total population (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental table S5).

The YLL rates for stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths decreased significantly among females and individuals aged 44 years and older from 2005 to 2021. Similarly, YLL rates for stroke-specific deaths decreased significantly among males and the total population. In contrast, YLL rates for stroke-related deaths increased significantly among males and the total population (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental table S6).

The AYLL for both stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths decreased significantly among all individuals from 2005 to 2021 (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in online supplemental table S7).

The proportion of stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths decreased significantly among individuals aged 45–59 years and 70–79 years from 2005 to 2021. Conversely, these proportions increased significantly among individuals aged 60–69 years and those aged 80 years and older (all p<0.05; detailed data are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental table S8).

The CMR of stroke-related deaths is expected to rise at an AAPC of 0.69%, reaching 235.54 per 100 000 at 2035 (p<0.001). The ASMRW is expected to decrease at an AAPC of −1.89%, reaching 41.02 per 100 000 at 2035 (p<0.001). Furthermore, there is stability in the YLL rate of 1483.22 per 100 000 at 2035 (p=0.517, details shown in figure 2A, C and E and online supplemental table S9).

The CMR of stroke-specific deaths is expected to rise at an AAPC of 0.24%, reaching 141.96 per 100 000 at 2035 (p=0.08); however, the ASMRW is expected to decrease at an AAPC of −2.21%, reaching 25.54 per 100 000 at 2035 (p<0.001). There is also an increase in the YLL rate, currently measured at 950.69 per 100 000 at 2035 (p=0.001, details shown in figure 2B, D and F and online supplemental table S9).

Quantitative estimation of the impact of ageing on mortality rate

Among both stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths, ageing contributed significantly to mortality increases, with AAPC of 36.95% and 35.54%, respectively, from 2006 to 2021 (all p<0.05). Conversely, other factors contributed to notable downward trends during the same period, with AAPCs of −13.97% and −17.51% (all p<0.05).

We also observed that the CMR increased due to ageing in males, with a range of −0.33% in 2006 to 109.86% in 2021, although this increase was not uniform. Other factors contributed to a decrease in CMR in males, ranging from −62.33% in 2021 to −4.92% in 2007. Specifically, the AAPC for ageing-related increases and other factors was 37.53% and −13.46%, respectively, from 2006 to 2021. Additionally, the contribution of ageing to CMR increases was significantly lower in females than in males in 2021 (90.67% vs 109.86%; figure 3A and online supplemental table S10).

The increased rates of death caused by ageing and other factors during the period from 2006 to 2021 compared with the crude mortality rate of stroke during 2005 in Shanghai Pudong, China. AAPC, average annual per cent change.

When assessing stroke-specific deaths, the CMR increased due to ageing in males, with a range of −0.23% in 2006 to 76.06% in 2021. Other factors contributed to a decrease in CMR in males, ranging from −64.68% in 2021 to −8.20% in 2006. The AAPC for ageing-related increases and other factors was 35.99% and −20.63%, respectively, from 2006 to 2021. Additionally, the contribution of ageing to CMR increases was significantly lower in females than in males in 2021 (66.54% vs 76.06%; figure 3B and online supplemental table S10).

Discussion

As a populous country and a major nation burdened by stroke, China should actively address the public health issues caused by the increasing burden of stroke.19 The difficulty lies not only in how to control the relevant pathogenic factors that can cause stroke, but also in how to manage the health of elderly patients with the condition in the context of an ageing population and complex comorbidities. Further, it is important to consider how to prevent premature death or disability, thereby reducing the increasing disease burden.1 6 This study provides a comprehensive estimate of the mortality burden of stroke from the perspectives of all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality in Shanghai Pudong, China, which is a valuable supplement to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) estimates based on cause-specific mortality data.6 Accurate estimation of the disease burden in the population is essential for formulating targeted and more effective prevention and control measures.

Our findings indicate that stroke-specific mortality accounts for only 68.89% of stroke-related deaths. This suggests that current GBD studies, which are widely accepted and based on disease-specific mortality, may significantly underestimate the true health loss caused by stroke in the real world.3 6 Our previous research also showed that diabetes-specific mortality accounted for approximately 31.39% of related deaths. The varying proportions of disease-specific and related deaths make it extremely challenging to bridge this gap in disease burden estimation using a single mortality burden estimation model.7

Moreover, our research reveals a notable trend in stroke-related and stroke-specific deaths from 2005 to 2021 and projected through 2035: while the number of deaths and the CMR have increased, the ASMRW has declined over the 31-year period. The rising CMR may be attributed to the growing and ageing population in Shanghai Pudong, which naturally increases the number of deaths from age-related conditions such as stroke.19 Additionally, improvements in death reporting and documentation may contribute to the observed increase in recorded mortality rates.16 20 Conversely, the decline in ASMRW suggests that the risk of dying from stroke is decreasing when adjusted for age, reflecting significant public health achievements likely due to advancements in medical treatments, increased awareness and prevention efforts and better management of risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes and smoking.3 6 Furthermore, the GBD 2021 study highlights that while the absolute number of stroke cases and deaths has increased, there has been a stagnation in the reduction of stroke incidence since 2015, with some regions and age groups even experiencing increases. This underscores the need for continued efforts in prevention and treatment.6 And Cheng et al recently projected that global stroke deaths will rise by 50% between 2020 and 2050, driven almost entirely by population ageing and by LMICs where ≥60% of incident strokes are already attributable to modifiable comorbidities.21 Our data corroborate this forecast at the city level: we found that one-third of the recorded rise in stroke-related mortality since 2005 was explained by ageing alone, while hypertension, heart disease and diabetes dominated the multiple-cause death certificates. Integrating these comorbid conditions into future GBD models—rather than relying solely on underlying-cause coding—would therefore narrow the gap between modelled and observed stroke burdens and could better quantify the preventable fraction in rapidly ageing megacities across LMICs.

We also observed the following gender differences in mortality: (1) an increase in stroke-specific CMR among males, while remaining stable among females (which contrasts with the year-by-year decline in female stroke-related CMR); and (2) a greater decline in stroke-specific ASMRW compared with stroke-related deaths across genders. The lower rate of increase in stroke-specific CMR for males compared with stroke-related deaths may be due to a higher prevalence of risk factors among males, such as higher smoking rates, unhealthy lifestyles and hypertension, all of which are known to contribute to stroke.11 Additionally, differences in healthcare access and utilisation between genders may play a role. For example, men are often more likely to seek medical care in response to stroke symptoms.22 The stability in disease-specific CMR among females, despite a decline in female stroke-related CMR, may suggest that other factors are at play, such as lifestyle changes, better management of risk factors or improvements in healthcare that disproportionately benefit women.23

Our results indicate that, based on the rates from 2005, both stroke-related and stroke-specific CMR increase with age, contributing to an annual rise in CMR by more than 30%. In contrast, the cumulative effect of other factors effectively reduces CMR, with their contribution to mortality increases declining by more than 10% each year. Ageing significantly impacts the burden of stroke, with prevalence and mortality rates increasing sharply with age. In China, stroke is the leading cause of death and disability, and the ageing population is a key factor contributing to the growing stroke burden.24 In 2020, the prevalence, incidence and mortality rates of stroke in China were 2.6%, 505.2 per 100 000 person-years and 343.4 per 100 000 person-years, respectively.19 These data highlight the necessity for improved stroke prevention strategies targeting the general Chinese population. The risk of stroke rises markedly with advancing age due to multiple risk factors and is associated with poorer outcomes. For example, hypertension is the most prevalent risk factor for stroke, while other significant risk factors include diabetes, obesity, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and atrial fibrillation.6 25 Therefore, addressing the health needs and diseases of the elderly is a social necessity.

Unlike current datasets or literature that are limited to publishing or analysing data on the underlying cause of death, such as those from the GBD studies, our research has organised and analysed the full spectrum of causes of death in the mortality survey data from 3.17 million people in Shanghai Pudong. This approach has allowed us to uncover gaps and connections between stroke-related deaths and stroke-specific deaths. Our findings provide an effective basis for more accurately estimating the stroke disease burden in Shanghai, China, and potentially globally. Additionally, we have provided a quantitative estimation of the impact of ageing on the stroke disease burden, revealing that ageing is the primary driver of increasing stroke mortality in Shanghai Pudong. However, the cumulative effect of other factors has effectively mitigated the overall mortality rate. These results offer valuable quantitative data to support the continuation and targeted optimisation of local stroke prevention and control measures in response to an ageing population. They also enhance confidence for other LMICs to implement effective stroke prevention and control strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite the high coverage of the Pudong Mortality Registration System, the accuracy of cause-of-death coding depends on the certifying physician’s training and diagnostic practices; misclassification of stroke subtype or comorbid conditions cannot be ruled out. Second, the observed trends span 17 years of rapid healthcare reform, including the introduction of stroke units, thrombolysis services and expanded insurance coverage; these advances may have altered case fatality and thus the slope of mortality rates, but their individual effects could not be disaggregated. Third, the AutoRegressive Integrated Moving-Average (ARIMA) projections assume that past trends continue linearly and do not incorporate sudden policy shifts, healthcare disruptions (eg, COVID-19) or future interventions; consequently, long-term forecasts should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, migration in and out of Pudong was not captured, potentially biasing mortality rates if healthy-worker or retirement-related mobility differs by age or socioeconomic status.

The real-world burden of stroke and its disparities is far greater and more complex than current estimates suggest, particularly in the context of an ageing population and the presence of complex comorbidities. The decline in ASMRW for stroke-related deaths and disease-specific mortality in Shanghai Pudong—a developed region within a developing country—demonstrates the effectiveness of stroke prevention and control efforts in LMICs. However, the continuous increase in stroke burden driven by ageing, along with the prevalence of non-fatal stroke with complex comorbidities, urgently demands the expansion of effective intervention measures to address rapid population ageing and prevent stroke occurrence.
