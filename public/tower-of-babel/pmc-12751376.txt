Spontaneous uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus secondary to fulminant pelvic infection: a case report
Authors: Sisay Tesfaye Taye, Tesfaye Birhanu Abebe, Atsinagn Girma Borena, Getachew Shiferaw Adugna, Getnet Damena Tadesse, Balina Amsalu Mosisa, Wakuma Fulea Gutema, Haymanot Bekele Urga, Yakob Tadesse Fantu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSpontaneous uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus is an exceedingly rare and life-threatening gynecological emergency. This report details a unique case of such a rupture in a postmenopausal woman, secondary to a fulminant pelvic infection.Case presentationA 55-year-old grand multipara postmenopausal woman, with a history of recurrent lower abdominal pain, presented with severe abdominal pain, malodorous vaginal discharge, and fever of six days duration. Physical examination revealed a diffusely tender abdomen with guarding, and laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis and anemia. Abdominal ultrasound revealed intra-abdominal collection with an echo complex mass in the paracolic gutter, suggestive of abscess collection in the abdominoplevic cavity. Due to hemodynamic instability, the patient was resuscitated with crystalloids and underwent emergent exploratory laparotomy involving both surgical and gynecologic teams. Intraoperatively, about 2000 ml of purulent material was sucked out, and the uterine fundus was found ruptured, necrotic, and eaten up for which a total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was done. Microbiological analysis confirmed Escherichia coli infection, and histopathology ruled out malignancy, showing only inflammatory changes.ConclusionA spontaneous uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus resulted from fulminant pelvic infection, establishing chronic pelvic inflammatory disease as an etiology through direct tissue erosion. This case underscores the necessity of considering genital tract sepsis in postmenopausal women presenting with acute abdomen and highlights the critical importance of emergent surgical intervention, targeted antimicrobial therapy, and rigorous adherence to PID management guidelines for preventing this life-threatening complication.Keywords: Spontaneous uterine rupture, Non-scarred uterus, Non-gravid uterus, Pelvic infection, Woman health, Gynecology

Introduction

Uterine rupture is defined as the full-thickness separation of the uterine wall and the overlying serosa. It can be complete when all layers, including the peritoneum, are torn. It is incomplete when visceral peritoneum is intact. It may be primary, defined as occurring in a previously intact or unscarred uterus, or may be secondary and associated with a preexisting myometrial incision, injury, or anomaly [1, 2]. Spontaneous uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus represents an exceptionally rare and life-threatening event, typically arising from underlying pathological processes rather than mechanical trauma or prior surgical intervention [3]. While most uterine ruptures in non-pregnant women occur secondary to iatrogenic causes or trauma, rupture due to fulminant pelvic infection presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Though there are adequate documented cases related to spontaneous uterine rupture in pregnant woman, there is of few cases related to uterine rupture in non-gravid, unscarred uterus and even exceedingly rare due to fulminant pelvic infection among which this case, a rare occurrence of spontaneous atraumatic uterine rupture in unscarred and non-gravid woman caused by fulminant pelvic infection highlighting the critical role of infection-induced tissue necrosis in uterine wall failure, will add to the understanding of this gynecologic emergency.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old grand multiparous woman (gravida 6, para 6), seven years post-menopausal, presented to the gynecology emergency department with a six-day history of abdominal pain, fever, and foul-smelling vaginal discharge. The pain, initially localized to the lower abdomen, had become severe and diffuse over the preceding two days, accompanied by an increment in abdominal girth and repeated episodes of vomiting. Her history was significant for recurrent lower abdominal pain over the past year, managed on three separate occasions with oral antibiotics at a local health center. She reported that the current episode was distinct in its severity and the new development of foul-smelling discharge. Her obstetric history included six uncomplicated spontaneous vaginal deliveries, with no history of prolonged labor, manual placental removal, or puerperal sepsis. She had no prior uterine instrumentation, surgery, trauma, or postmenopausal bleeding. She denied tobacco use, alcohol consumption, or illicit drug use. Her medical history was negative for major comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, malignancy, or radiation therapy. On physical examination, the patient was acutely ill, pale, and in severe distress. Vital signs were notable for hypotension (87/58 mmHg), tachycardia (126 beats/min, feeble pulse), tachypnea (24 breaths/min), fever (38.2 °C), and hypoxia (oxygen saturation 88% on room air), requiring supplemental oxygen. Abdominal examination revealed a distended, tense abdomen with diffuse tenderness and guarding. Bimanual pelvic examination demonstrated bilateral adnexal tenderness and foul-smelling purulent vaginal discharge. Examination of other systems was unremarkable. Laboratory investigations revealed severe leukocytosis (29,800/µL) with neutrophilic predominance (92.1%) and anemia (hemoglobin 7.1 g/dL). Random blood glucose, renal and liver function tests, serum electrolytes, and arterial blood gas analysis were within normal limits. Retroviral screening was non-reactive.

Bedside ultrasonography showed a complex, multiseptated intraperitoneal fluid collection in the paracolic gutters and pelvis, hyperechoic mesenteric fat, and significant probe tenderness. Inflamed loops of ileum, cecum, and ascending colon were noted. The uterus contained a heterogeneous, air-filled collection with loss of the endometrial-myometrial junction, findings consistent with severe endomyometritis, uterine pneumatosis (suggesting emphysematous endometritis), and generalized peritonitis with abscess formation. Chest radiography and electrocardiography were unremarkable. Given the clinical presentation of an acute abdomen and sepsis, and considering the unavailability of advanced imaging (CT/MRI), the decision was made to proceed with emergent exploratory laparotomy. The differential diagnosis included perforated viscus, complicated pelvic inflammatory disease, diverticulitis, perforated peptic ulcer, tubo-ovarian abscess, and ovarian cyst rupture. Initial management included securing intravenous access, fluid resuscitation with normal saline, initiation of a blood transfusion, administration of analgesics, and supplemental oxygen. Following stabilization, the working diagnosis was generalized peritonitis secondary to intra-abdominal abscess from a fulminant pelvic infection. On the first day of her hospital admission, a combined gynecological and surgical team performed an exploratory laparotomy under general anesthesia following a single dose of ceftriaxone and metronidazole. A midline incision revealed 2000 mL of purulent fluid within the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 1). The uterine fundus was found to be spontaneously ruptured with areas of necrosis in an otherwise unscarred, nongravid uterus. Both fallopian tubes were edematous, with complete necrosis noted on the left side (Fig. 1). The bowel was inspected and found to be grossly normal. A total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was performed for source control. The surgical procedure involved sequential clamping and ligation of the round, broad, utero-ovarian, and cardinal ligaments and the uterine arteries with Vicryl sutures. The vaginal cuff was closed in a standard fashion, followed by thorough lavage of the abdominopelvic cavity and layered closure of the abdominal wall. The procedure was well tolerated, and the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit in stable condition. Postoperatively, she was monitored in the gynecologic ward with daily clinical assessments and laboratory follow-up.

Histopathological examination confirmed suppurative inflammation and transmural necrosis affecting the endometrium, myometrium, and bilateral fallopian tubes, with no malignant features identified. Given the clinical context of spontaneous postmenopausal rupture, a second pathological review was obtained and corroborated the absence of malignancy. Microbiological analysis of the abscess fluid identified a ceftriaxone-resistant Escherichia coli, with concordant bacteremia confirmed by blood cultures. Initial antimicrobial therapy was transitioned to ciprofloxacin and metronidazole due to a meropenem stockout. The patient completed a three-week antibiotic course and was counseled on post-oophorectomy health risks; after shared decision-making, hormone replacement therapy was deferred. During two years of follow-up, she has maintained an uncomplicated recovery without sequelae such as chronic pain, fistulae, or symptomatic pelvic adhesions.

Discussion

While the majority of uterine rupture cases arise in pregnant women with pre-existing uterine scars—particularly from prior cesarean deliveries—nontraumatic uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus remains an exceptionally rare event. Population-based studies, including data from the Netherlands, demonstrate a clear disparity in rupture risk between scarred (5.1 per 10,000 deliveries) and unscarred uteri (0.8 per 10,000 deliveries) [4]. In the absence of pregnancy spontaneous rupture typically results from pathological weakening of the uterine architecture, with documented etiologies including ischemia, distal cervical stenosis with hormonal therapy, congenital uterine anomalies, prior instrumentation, fulminant pelvic infection, previous surgeries, and grand multipara [5]. Connective tissue disorders, particularly vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, represent another rare but important risk factor due to inherent collagen defects. However, the most clinically significant mechanisms in non-gravid patients involve infectious or neoplastic processes: Spontaneous rupture of pyometra in postmenopausal woman is a rare occurrence, with an incidence of only 0.01–0.5% [6] (often secondary to cervical stenosis from postmenopausal atrophy, radiation fibrosis, or malignant obstruction) leads to increased intrauterine pressure and ischemic necrosis [7] The uterine fundus is the most common site of perforation, presumably due to decreased vascular flow at this location [8]; severe endomyometritis caused by virulent organisms (including E. coli, Klebsiella, anaerobic species, or fungal pathogens) progressively erodes the myometrium [9]; and malignancies such as uterine sarcomas or invasive cervical carcinomas may directly invade and weaken the uterine wall [10]. In one case report, cervical stenosis was attributed to causing a spontaneous uterine perforation in a non-gravid uterus, leading to an accumulation of secretions, fulminant pelvic infection prior to rupture, and subsequent necrosis of the uterine wall. Literature has shown that pelvic infections leading to uterine rupture can be caused by organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus haemolyticus, Proteus, Streptococcus agalactiae, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Sphingomonas paucimobilis, which were identified from infected fibroids [11, 12]. In rare instances, these infections can result in uterine rupture. Another report highlighted that Escherichia coli and anaerobes, including Bacteroides and Peptococcus species, were identified as etiologic agents from the culture of the peritoneal cavity in cases of spontaneous perforation of pyometra [13]. An exceptionally rare case identified Prevotella bivia as the causative agent of fulminant generalized peritonitis secondary to purulent salpingitis in a 39-year-old woman [14]. These reports demonstrate that, although rare, Escherichia coli can be implicated in fulminant pelvic infections, which, in turn, can lead to spontaneous rupture in unscarred and non-gravid uterus, as observed in our case. In this case, a postmenopausal woman with no uterine scarring or trauma suffered primary spontaneous uterine rupture due to an unexpected etiology: fulminant pelvic infection caused by E. coli. While the patient’s postmenopausal anatomy (including potential cervical stenosis) created a predisposition for pyometra formation, definitive cultures isolated E. coli, confirming acute endomyometritis as the principal disease process that led to rupture through non-traditional mechanisms. Her history of recurrent PID—marked by multiple prior treatments—suggests chronic, subclinical infection progressively eroded the myometrium through cycles of inflammation and microperforation, culminating in full-thickness necrosis. This sequence highlights two critical insights: first, that active infection alone can achieve sufficient myometrial destruction to cause rupture without mechanical risk factors; and second, that recurrent PID represents an underrecognized pathway to uterine compromise, particularly in patients with anatomic vulnerabilities like postmenopausal cervical stenosis. The case reframes our understanding of spontaneous rupture by demonstrating how untreated ascending infections can independently replicate the end-stage tissue damage typically seen in obstetric trauma or malignancy.

Diagnostic challenges abound given the nonspecific presentation, requiring clinicians to maintain high suspicion in non-gravid women presenting with acute abdominal pain (present in 60% of reported cases), vaginal bleeding (occurring in 11–67% of patients), and hemodynamic instability from hemorrhage or sepsis (seen in 29–46% of cases) [15, 16]. Imaging plays a crucial role, with bedside ultrasound often revealing suggestive findings like heterogeneous intrauterine fluid collections or gas shadows, while CT or MRI (when feasible) may demonstrate definitive evidence of uterine wall discontinuity and hemoperitoneum or purulent ascites [8]. However, as exemplified in this case, hemodynamic instability and/or resource limitations may preclude advanced imaging, necessitating emergent laparotomy based on clinical suspicion alone. Previous literature emphasizes that only about 33% of cases are correctly diagnosed preoperatively by imaging, underscoring the importance of clinical judgment [17, 18]. The definitive exclusion of a malignant etiology is a critical component in managing spontaneous uterine rupture in postmenopausal women [19]. This relies primarily on histopathologic examination of the surgical specimen. In our case, standard histology revealed only transmural necrosis and suppurative inflammation, with no evidence of sarcoma or carcinoma. While ancillary techniques like immunohistochemistry can be invaluable for confirming the diagnosis, particularly for ruling out subtle or poorly differentiated malignancies, such resources were not available in our setting. Management follows a damage-control paradigm, prioritizing hemorrhage control, source elimination, and physiologic stabilization [20]. Surgical intervention ranges from uterine repair in selected cases to total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy in scenarios with extensive necrosis or hemodynamic collapse, as necessitated in this patient. Uterine conservation may be considered in nulliparous women despite an estimated 4–19% recurrence risk in subsequent pregnancies [21]. Concurrent broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy must cover likely pathogens including anaerobes and Gram-negative organisms, with particular attention to organisms documented in similar cases—Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, Enterobacteriaceae (especially E. coli), and fastidious anaerobes like Bacteroides, Peptococcus, and Prevotella bivia [11, 13, 14]. This case offers critical clinical insights: it demonstrates that aggressive pelvic infection alone can cause uterine rupture without predisposing anatomical factors; highlights the lethal potential of diagnostic delay in resource limited settings given rapid progression to septic shock; and underscores the necessity of considering this diagnosis even in patients lacking classic risk factors. The microbiological profile further emphasizes the importance of broad-spectrum coverage and culture-directed therapy. Collectively, these observations establish uterine rupture as a multifaceted entity requiring prompt recognition and decisive intervention beyond the obstetric context. Importantly, this case reveals a vital preventive opportunity—the patient’s history of recurrent, inadequately treated pelvic inflammatory disease likely represented a chronic process culminating in catastrophic failure. This underscores the imperative for complete, guideline-adherent management of pelvic inflammatory disease [22]—ensuring adequate antibiotic courses, partner treatment, and follow-up—constitutes an essential public health strategy to prevent such catastrophic sequelae in regions with fragmented healthcare systems.

Conclusion

In summary, this report details a rare instance of spontaneous uterine rupture in a non-gravid, unscarred uterus resulting from fulminant pelvic infection, establishing chronic recurrent pelvic inflammatory disease as a primary etiological factor through direct tissue erosion. These findings highlight the critical importance of considering genital tract sepsis in the differential diagnosis for postmenopausal women presenting with an acute abdomen. This case reaffirms that timely surgical intervention and targeted antimicrobial therapy are essential in managing this life-threatening condition, while consistent, guideline-adherent management of pelvic inflammatory disease remains the cornerstone of prevention.

Patient perspective

For years, I endured recurring belly pains, eased temporarily by clinic medicines. But this time, the pain became unbearable—my stomach swelled, and a foul smell appeared. At the hospital, weak and trembling, I learned my womb had ruptured from infection, despite no surgery or bleeding. With six children and seven years past menopause, I never imagined this could happen. The terror of possibly having cancer faded when tests confirmed it was ‘just’ a severe infection. After treatment, I feel reborn—no more pain, no more hiding. My body is finally free from this hidden torment.
