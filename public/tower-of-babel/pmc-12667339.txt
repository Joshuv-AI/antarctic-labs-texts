Preschool children’s eating behaviors and caregivers’ feeding practices in relation to children’s weight in China: a network analysis
Authors: Haiyue Zhang, Zhe Yang, Hao Zhang, Jing Yuan, Xianjun Yang, Ying Liang, Zhijun Tan, Yue Wang, Wei Zhang, Yuhai Zhang, Xun Jiang, Lei Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the interactions between preschool children’s eating behaviors and their caregivers’ feeding practices, and to compare these patterns across underweight, normal weight, and overweight/obesity groups using a network model.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional survey (January–July 2021) included 3–6-year-old preschool children and their primary caregivers from seven cities in Shaanxi Province, China. Children’s eating behaviors and caregivers’ feeding practices were measured using the Chinese Preschoolers’ Eating Behavior Scale and the Chinese Preschoolers’ Caregivers’ Feeding Behavior Scale. Network centrality indices identified core and bridge behaviors, with stability assessed via case-dropping bootstrapping.ResultsThe network had 14 nodes, 82 non-zero edges (90.11% of all possible edges), and all networks showed good stability (correlation stability coefficient > 0.5). Among subgroups, the overweight/obese group had the highest mean edge weight (0.048), while the underweight group had the lowest non-zero edge proportion (45.05%). Across all samples, the strongest edge was between Feeding Responsibility and Content-Restricted Feeding (0.47), and the strongest cross-domain link was Emotional Eating-Weight Concerns (0.22), both stably confirmed by bootstrap. Feeding Responsibility had the highest strength (1.74) across groups, and Weight Concerns had the highest overall bridge strength (0.58), identifying it as a key connector. Edge weight distribution differed significantly between normal-weight and overweight/obese groups (p = 0.02).ConclusionsNetwork analysis identified Feeding Responsibility as core behavior and Weight Concerns as a critical cross-domain bridge, highlighting caregivers’ central role. These findings inform targeted interventions and nutritional strategies.Keywords: Eating behaviors, feeding practices, preschool children, network analysis

Introduction

Global and national data underscore the urgent need to address childhood nutritional challenges. According to the UNICEF–WHO–World Bank 2022 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, 148.1 million children under 5 were stunted, 45.0 million were wasted, and 37.0 million were overweight [1]. China, as the world’s most populous country, faces a dual burden of malnutrition: According to the National Health Commission, during the 14th Five-Year Plan period (2021–2025), overweight and obesity rates among children under the age of 6 in China have fallen from 10.4% to 9.7%; stunting and underweight rates have dropped to 4.5% and 1.4%, respectively [2]. Even so, the coexistence of undernutrition and overweight/obesity poses unique challenges. Undernutrition impairs children’s physical and cognitive development [3], whereas children who are overweight (or have obesity) are more likely to experience low self-esteem and depressive symptoms [4].

The preschool period (ages 3–6) is a critical developmental window for establishing dietary behaviors. Behaviors established in early childhood are strongly linked to adult eating habits [5,6], underscoring the importance of early interventions to promote lifelong nutritional health. A child’s nutritional status depends on two closely linked domains: the child’s dietary behaviors (e.g. emotional eating) and caregivers’ feeding practices (e.g. feeding responsibility). Changes in one domain often prompt adjustments in the other, and interactions between these domains shape nutritional outcomes. Importantly, these dynamics may differ by weight subgroup (underweight, normal weight, overweight/obesity).

Prior research shows that both family demographic factors (e.g. parental education, household income) and caregivers’ feeding practices (e.g. feeding responsibility) influence the development of children’s dietary behaviors [7,8]. When family demographics are controlled, caregivers’ feeding practices remain the primary driver of children’s eating behaviors [8,9]. However, the pathway from feeding practices to dietary behaviors to nutritional status is not unidirectional. For example, a child’s picky eating may lead caregivers to adopt more restrictive feeding strategies, which in turn further shape the child’s eating behaviors. This bidirectional cycle ultimately affects the child’s nutritional status [10–12]. Despite this, critical gaps persist: current research has not clearly identified which specific dietary and feeding behaviors constitute core associations within each weight subgroup, nor has it identified the behaviors that bridge the two domains. These gaps limit the precision of intervention strategies.

Children’s dietary behaviors are known to relate to weight in several ways. Yuan et al. [6] found a positive correlation between food responsiveness (eating desire) and BMI (β = 0.93, p < 0.001), but evidence on how satiety responsiveness (ability to recognize fullness) and emotional eating relate to overweight is inconsistent [13–15]. The impact of caregivers’ feeding practices is even more complex. For instance, caregivers who take primary feeding responsibility often use restrictive feeding to regulate children’s intake of high-sugar and high-fat foods, ensuring adequate nutrition [16,17]. However, long-term, unidimensional restrictive feeding may trigger children’s psychological reactance, potentially increasing cravings for restricted foods [5,18]. In addition, caregivers’ concerns about child weight may inadvertently increase children’s emotional eating, either through transmitting anxiety or through exerting excessive dietary control—an effect that may be more pronounced in children with overweight or obesity [19,20]. By contrast, responsive feeding (attentive to children’s hunger and satiety cues) is negatively associated with picky eating and promotes the development of positive food preferences [21,22].

Caregivers are key influencers of children’s nutrition. They shape the home food environment through modeling and feeding practices, guiding children’s dietary socialization and meal experiences [23]. By modeling healthy eating and offering nutrient-dense foods, caregivers can foster adaptive eating behaviors in children [24]. However, inappropriate feeding practices can have counterproductive outcomes. For example, excessive parental food restriction can reduce children’s exposure to diverse foods and impair satiety regulation, thereby increasing obesity risk [18]. This paradoxical effect warrants special attention in children with overweight or obesity [25].

Although previous studies have documented associations among children’s dietary behaviors, caregivers’ feeding practices, and children’s weight status, the complex interaction network among these factors remains unexplored. In particular, limited studies [26] have applied network analysis to characterize these relationships in preschoolers or to identify core or bridging behaviors. Identifying the core behavioral determinants of weight during early childhood is critical for developing targeted nutritional interventions, and network analysis provides a novel approach for this purpose. In a network, specific dietary behaviors and feeding practices are conceptualized as nodes, and their interactions as edges. Centrality analysis identifies the key behaviors that shape the network structure, and bridge centrality analysis pinpoints the critical cross-domain links. This approach also enables precise comparisons of network differences across weight subgroups.

To address these gaps, the present study used network analysis with two main goals: first, to identify the core feeding behaviors, core dietary behaviors, and the bridging behaviors connecting them across the total sample and within each weight subgroup (underweight, normal weight, overweight/obesity); and second, to compare the behavioral networks across these weight subgroups to examine how caregivers’ feeding practices relate to preschoolers’ dietary behaviors in each group. By achieving these goals, we aim to provide a scientific foundation for targeted nutritional interventions, defining precise evidence-based targets and addressing key gaps in current research.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January to July 2021 in seven cities in Shaanxi Province, China. We used stratified sampling to recruit a total of 10,236 preschool children (aged 3–6 years) and their primary caregivers. A primary caregiver was defined as an individual who was mainly responsible for the child’s daily care, including dietary management, sleep, and after-school/weekend physical activities.

Inclusion criteria for children were: (1) age 3–6 years, and (2) the caregiver provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were preschool children with chronic diseases affecting eating behavior (e.g. chronic gastritis, constipation, chronic diarrhea) in the past two months, or caregivers with communication barriers. Before data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the survey was conducted anonymously. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. 202011-04, Approval date: November 30, 2020) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Caregivers’ feeding practices (CPCFBS)

Caregivers’ feeding practices were assessed using the Chinese Preschoolers’ Caregivers’ Feeding Behavior Scale (CPCFBS) [27]. This 35-item scale comprises seven dimensions: Feeding Responsibility (e.g. ‘I prepare meals that meet my child’s nutritional needs’), Weight Concerns (e.g. ‘I worry about my child being overweight’), Content-Restricted Feeding (e.g. ‘I limit my child’s intake of high-fat foods’), Encourage Healthy Eating (e.g. ‘I praise my child for eating vegetables’), Behavior-Restricted Feeding (e.g. ‘I restrict my child from eating while watching TV’), Forced Feeding (e.g. ‘I force my child to eat when he/she says he/she is full’), and Supervised Eating (e.g. ‘I eat meals with my child to ensure proper eating habits’). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always), with higher scores indicating more frequent or prominent feeding behaviors. In the current sample, the CPCFBS showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.91 overall; subscale α range 0.65–0.88).

Children’s eating behaviors (CPEBS)

Children’s eating behaviors were assessed using the Chinese Preschoolers’ Eating Behavior Scale (CPEBS) [28]. This 38-item questionnaire evaluates seven dimensions of eating behavior: Food Fussiness (e.g. ‘My child only eats foods he/she chooses’), Food Responsiveness (e.g. ‘My child eats whenever I give him/her food’), Eating Habits (e.g. ‘My child can sit at the table and finish the meal quickly’), Satiety Responsiveness (e.g. ‘Usually my child becomes full after a few bites’), External Eating (e.g. ‘My child eats more when the food is changed’), Emotional Eating (e.g. ‘My child eats more when he/she is angry’), and Initiative Eating (e.g. ‘My child can feed him/herself’). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always) based on the frequency of the behaviors in the past month, with higher scores indicating more pronounced eating behaviors. The CPEBS demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.92 overall; subscale α range 0.74–0.87).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis

Children were categorized into weight status groups using age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles, following WHO growth standards. BMI z-scores were calculated based on WHO criteria: underweight (BMI z-score < −2 SD), normal weight (−2 SD ≤ z-score ≤ +1 SD), overweight (+1 SD < z-score ≤ +2 SD), and obesity (z-score > +2 SD) [29]. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Continuous variables (e.g. age, BMI) are presented as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Cases with missing data on more than 50% of items or with invalid anthropometric measurements were excluded. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Network estimation

We constructed Gaussian Graphical Models (GGMs) to estimate the partial correlation network of children’s eating and feeding behaviors, using the R package bootnet [30]. LASSO regularization was applied to obtain a sparse network. The Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC, γ = 0.5) was used to select the optimal tuning parameter, balancing model fit and parsimony. Network visualization was done with the R package qgraph, where nodes represent the 14 behavior dimensions (7 feeding, 7 eating) and edges represent significant partial correlations (p < 0.05) after controlling for all other nodes. Edge colors indicate the sign of the correlation (blue = positive, red = negative), and thickness reflects correlation strength.

Centrality and bridge centrality

We computed centrality indices to quantify the importance of each node. Strength centrality (the sum of absolute edge weights connected to a node) was used as the primary measure of node relevance in the network. All centrality measures were standardized (z-scores) for comparability [30]. To identify key bridging behaviors between the feeding and eating domains, we calculated bridge centrality statistics using the networktools package [31]. We defined bridge nodes as those with bridge strength values in the top 20% of the standardized distribution [31].

Network accuracy and stability

Network stability was assessed via bootstrapping procedures in bootnet [30]. We performed nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 samples) to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each edge weight; narrow CIs indicate precise edge estimates. We also conducted case-dropping subset bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) to calculate the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) for centrality measures. A CS-C above 0.5 is considered acceptable [30], although values above 0.25 are the minimum requirement.

Network comparison

We conducted network analysis with covariates and subgroup analysis. For the covariate-adjusted network analysis, the most significant factors identified from the multiple linear regression analyses were incorporated as confounders to control for their potential confounding effects on the network structure. Additionally, we performed subgroup analysis stratified by weight categories. Specifically, the Network Comparison Test (NCT) [32], implemented in R software with 1,000 permutations, was applied to compare network characteristics across three weight groups (underweight, normal weight, and overweight/obesity). Between-group comparisons included assessing global strength differences via permutation tests and individual edge weight differences via pairwise comparison of corresponding edges, with a two-tailed significance threshold of p < 0.05 applied to all tests.

Network structure

Figure 1 shows the dietary-feeding behavior network for the total sample. This network included 14 nodes and 91 possible edges. In the total network, 82 edges were non-zero (90.11%), and the mean edge weight was 0.045. The subgroup networks (Figure S1) had the following characteristics: the underweight group network had 41 non-zero edges (45.05%) with a mean edge weight of 0.042; the normal-weight group had 77 non-zero edges (84.62%) with mean weight 0.046; and the overweight/obesity group had 75 non-zero edges (82.42%) with mean weight 0.048.

Network of eating behaviors and caregivers’ feeding behaviors in preschoolers. Note. Edge thickness and color intensity correspond to the strength of nodal correlations, with darker, thicker edges indicating stronger associations. Yellow nodes represent the seven dimensions of eating behavior; and blue nodes denote the seven dimensions of feeding behavior. Solid blue edges indicate positive correlations; while red dashed edges indicate negative correlations.

Within the feeding-behavior community, the strongest connections were observed. The edge between Feeding Responsibility (FB1) and Content-Restricted Feeding (FB4) had the largest weight in the total sample (0.47) and in each subgroup (underweight 0.44; normal-weight 0.46; overweight/obesity 0.41). Other high-weight edges in feeding included FB2 (Weight Concerns)–FB3 (Encourage Healthy Eating) and FB3–FB7 (Supervised Eating), though these were smaller than FB1–FB4. The second-largest edge differed by group: in the underweight group it was between Behavior-Restricted Feeding (FB5) and Supervised Eating (FB7) (weight = 0.32), whereas in the normal-weight and overweight/obesity groups, the second-largest edge was between FB3 (Encourage Healthy Eating) and FB7 (Supervise Eating) (weights 0.28 and 0.26, respectively).

Within the eating-behavior community, the largest edge in the total and normal-weight networks was between Eating Habits (EB3) and Initiative Eating (EB7) (weights = 0.27 and 0.28). In the underweight group, the strongest edge was between EB3 (Eating Habits) and EB4 (Satiety Responsiveness) (0.27), while in the overweight/obesity group it was between Food Fussiness (EB1) and EB3 (Eating Habits) (0.25).

Among edges linking eating behaviors to feeding behaviors (cross-domain edges), the edge between Emotional Eating (EB6) and Weight Concerns (FB2) had the highest weight (0.22) and was consistent across all networks (total and subgroups) (Figure S1). Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for edge weights were relatively narrow (Figure S2), indicating precise estimates. Bootstrap difference tests confirmed that the strongest edges (e.g. FB1–FB4, EB6–FB2) had significantly larger weights than most other edges (Figure S3).

Centrality and bridge centrality

Figure 2 displays node strength and bridge strength for the total network. Feeding Responsibility (FB1) had the highest strength centrality (1.74), indicating it was the most connected node in the network. The next highest strengths were Initiative Eating (EB7, 1.52) and Eating Habits (EB3, 1.30), both of which were significantly lower than FB1’s strength.

Estimated network structure with corresponding centrality of each node and associated bridge behaviors.

For bridge centrality, Weight Concerns (FB2) had the highest bridge strength (0.58), followed by Emotional Eating (EB6, 0.49), Forced Feeding (FB6, 0.48), and Initiative Eating (EB7, 0.46). These nodes had much higher bridge strengths than other nodes, highlighting them as critical connectors between the dietary and feeding domains.

Subgroup analyses (Figure S4) showed a similar pattern. In the underweight group, FB1 had the highest strength (2.13), followed by EB6 (1.10) and EB3 (1.04). The highest bridge strength was FB2 (0.46), followed by EB6 (0.42) and FB1 (0.41). In the normal-weight group, FB1 again had the highest strength (1.76), followed by EB7 (1.55) and EB3 (1.36); the highest bridge strengths were FB2 (0.50), EB6 (0.48), and FB6 (0.45). In the overweight/obesity group, FB1 remained the most central (strength 1.72), followed by EB3 (1.29) and EB7 (1.27); the top bridge strengths were FB2 (0.59), FB6 (0.47), and EB7 (0.45). These results consistently identify FB1 (Feeding Responsibility) as the most influential node and FB2 (Weight Concerns) as the most influential bridge across all groups.

Network stability and accuracy

The network stability analysis showed favorable stability. The CS-C for strength and bridge centrality exceeded 0.5 in all groups, indicating robust estimates. Specifically, the total sample and the normal-weight and overweight/obesity groups each had CS-C values of 0.75 for both strength and bridge. The underweight group had slightly lower CS-C (0.67 for strength, 0.59 for bridge) (Figure 3, Figure S5), likely reflecting its smaller number of edges, but these values still indicate adequate stability [29].

Stability of the estimated eating behavior and caregivers’ feeding behavior network structure via case-dropping bootstrapping.

Network comparison

Based on the results of multiple linear regression analyses (Tables S2–3), child age and household income (factors exerting substantial influences on eating and feeding behaviors) were incorporated as covariates. A covariate-adjusted network was then constructed, with the corresponding results presented in the (supplementary materials Tables S4–5). No statistically significant differences were observed between the networks with and without covariates (p > 0.05) (Table S6). Additionally, network comparison tests were performed to assess differences across weight groups. Specifically, when comparing the normal-weight and overweight/obesity groups, a significant difference was detected in edge weight distributions (M = 0.09, p = 0.02), whereas no significant difference was found in global network strength (7.29 vs 7.05, p = 0.58). This indicates that while the overall connectivity was similar, specific edges differed. In contrast, the underweight group did not differ significantly from the normal-weight or overweight/obesity groups in global strength (5.82 vs 7.29 and 5.82 vs 7.05, both p > 0.5) or in overall edge weight distribution (p = 0.55 and p = 0.67, respectively).

However, specific edges showed significant differences. Between the underweight and normal-weight groups, the Behavior-Restricted Feeding–Supervised Eating edge was stronger in the underweight group (0.33 vs 0.22). Between the underweight and overweight/obesity groups, two edges differed: Satiety Responsiveness–Emotional Eating (0.01 vs 0.13) and Initiative Eating–Content-Restricted Feeding (0.09 vs 0.01). These subgroup-specific differences suggest particular behavioral linkages that may be more prominent in one weight group than another.

Discussion

Using network analysis, this study mapped the co-occurrence of feeding practices and eating behaviors in Chinese preschool children and examined how these patterns vary by weight status. We first identified core behaviors within each domain, then identified core and bridge behaviors in the combined network, and finally compared these networks across weight subgroups. Our findings highlight key behaviors influencing children’s nutritional outcomes and reveal how their interrelationships differ by weight group, providing insights for more targeted interventions.

First, we observed a consistent pattern of strong within-domain connectivity in feeding behaviors across all samples. The edge between Feeding Responsibility (FB1) and Content-Restricted Feeding (FB4) was the strongest in the total sample and in every subgroup, indicating that caregivers who take primary responsibility for their child’s feeding also tend to restrict unhealthy foods. This FB1–FB4 linkage appears to be a fundamental feature of feeding practices in our sample. It likely reflects the logical pairing of these behaviors: caregivers with high feeding responsibility prioritize controlling nutritional intake and thus restrict high-sugar and high-fat foods [17]. Culturally, this may also relate to societal concerns about childhood obesity in China [33]; parents aware of obesity risks may restrict unhealthy foods to ensure their child’s healthy growth [21,34]. This observation is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. who also employed network analysis to examine preschoolers’ feeding practices and confirmed the value of identifying key behavioral linkages for understanding the structure of feeding behaviors [26]. However, this core linkage also carries potential risks. Without positive guidance (e.g. behavioral modeling, repeated exposure to healthy foods), strict food restriction can trigger children’s ‘psychological reactance,’ increasing cravings for the restricted foods or reducing acceptance of healthy ones [35,36]. This paradox underscores the need for balanced feeding strategies when targeting the FB1–FB4 axis.

Consistent with its central role, Feeding Responsibility (FB1) had the highest strength centrality (1.74) in the total network, significantly greater than any other node, and this pattern remained consistent before and after adjustment for covariates. This suggests that FB1 serves as the ‘structural core’ of the feeding-eating behavior network, exerting the strongest influence on the overall pattern of behaviors. Practically, this means that interventions aimed at enhancing caregiver feeding responsibility could have broad impacts on children’s dietary outcomes across all weight groups [16,37]. In other words, promoting caregiver accountability for child nutrition may be a key entry point for supporting healthy eating in preschoolers. This emphasis resonates with FACILITY’s intergenerational approach, which recognizes caregiver practices and maternal factors (e.g. education, food knowledge) as integral to obesity prevention [38]. It also aligns with Calcaterra et al.’s work, which highlights maternal exercise and breastfeeding practices as foundational to reducing early childhood obesity risk [39]. Together, these insights and our findings support a staged, caregiver-centered intervention framework: target maternal health behaviors during pregnancy and breastfeeding, then reinforce caregiver feeding responsibility in the preschool years. This continuum addresses obesity risk across critical developmental windows more effectively than isolated interventions

Second, bridging behaviors between the feeding and eating domains were remarkably stable across groups. The strongest cross-domain link was between Emotional Eating (EB6) and Weight Concerns (FB2) in all samples. Correspondingly, FB2 had the highest bridge strength (0.58 overall) and EB6 also had a high bridge strength (0.49) in the total network, indicating that these two behaviors form the core inter-community connection. Mechanistically, high parental weight concern may exacerbate children’s emotional eating through two pathways: (1) parents’ anxiety about weight may be conveyed to the child, who then eats to alleviate negative emotions [20,23]; and (2) parents may respond to weight concerns by imposing excessive dietary control, prompting children to assert autonomy through emotional eating [24,40]. Notably, children’s frequent emotional eating can, in turn, increase caregiver weight concern, forming a self-reinforcing cycle. Since preschool children are still developing self-regulation and depend on caregivers for food variety [41,42], they may be especially susceptible to this ‘weight concern–emotional eating’ dynamic. Importantly, our results imply that interventions targeting childhood eating and weight issues should address both parental weight concerns and children’s emotional eating simultaneously. The consistently strong EB6–FB2 connection highlights these behaviors as primary family-level targets to break the cycle linking feeding practices, dietary behaviors, and weight.

Our stability analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. The CS-C values for strength and bridge centrality exceeded 0.5 in all groups, supporting the reliability of the identified core and bridge nodes. The underweight group had slightly lower CS-C values (0.67 for strength, 0.59 for bridge), likely due to its smaller network (fewer edges), but these values were still within acceptable limits [30].

Network comparison tests revealed that the overall network structure was largely similar across groups, with one notable exception. The edge weight distribution differed significantly between the normal-weight and overweight/obesity groups (p = 0.02), even though their global network strength did not. This suggests that while the overall connectivity is comparable, certain edges differ in strength. For example, the overweight/obesity group showed a much stronger association between Satiety Responsiveness (EB4) and Emotional Eating (EB6) than the underweight group (edge weight 0.13 vs 0.01). This likely reflects the characteristic pattern of emotional eating: children with overweight/obesity may be more prone to eat irrespective of satiety when experiencing negative emotions [13,14,43]. These subgroup-specific findings identify additional targets for precision interventions (e.g. addressing emotional eating that overrides satiety signals in children at risk for obesity).

Other edge differences (e.g. between initiative eating and feeding practices) further highlight distinct behavioral interactions in each weight group. Overall, these results emphasize that while the core network is common, nuances in behavioral linkages justify tailored strategies.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions; longitudinal studies with time-series network modeling are needed to unravel causal pathways. Second, as assessments of dietary behaviors and feeding practices relied on self-reports, biases in recollection (e.g. difficulties in recalling specific food items or feeding interactions over time) and/or social desirability (e.g. tendencies to report more ‘healthful’ behaviors than actually practiced) cannot be completely ruled out as potential influences on the outcomes. Future research should include objective measurements and larger, more diverse samples to improve generalizability.

Conclusions

Our network analysis of dietary and feeding behaviors among Chinese preschoolers identified two critical behavioral targets: caregivers’ feeding responsibility (FB1) as the structural core of the entire network, and caregivers’ weight concerns (FB2) as the key cross-domain bridge linking feeding practices to children’s eating behaviors. Beyond these core nodes, the strongest inter-behavioral edges (e.g. FB1 [caregivers’ feeding responsibility]–FB4 [content-restricted feeding], EB6 [children’s emotional eating]–FB2 [caregivers’ weight concerns]) and subgroup-specific interaction patterns (e.g. EB3 [healthy eating habits]–EB4 [satiety responsiveness] in underweight children vs. EB1 [food fussiness]–EB3 in overweight children) further underscore how child weight status modulates the interplay of feeding and eating behaviors. To address both undernutrition and overweight in preschoolers, intervention strategies should prioritize three actionable directions: (1) strengthening caregivers’ feeding responsibility (FB1)—for example, by equipping caregivers with skills to balance ‘restricting unhealthy foods (FB4)’ with ‘providing diverse healthy options’—to leverage its role as the structural core, thereby driving positive changes in the broader feeding-eating network; (2) mitigating excessive caregiver weight concerns (FB2) while reducing children’s emotional eating (EB6), such as through parent education on rational weight perception and child-focused activities teaching non-food-based emotion regulation; and (3) fostering children’s healthy eating habits (EB3) and initiative eating skills (EB7), with tailoring to weight subgroups (e.g. emphasizing EB3-EB4 alignment for underweight children, and EB1-EB3 integration for overweight children). Notably, these strategies address a key gap in current early childhood nutrition interventions, which often lack weight-specific tailoring despite documented differences in behavioral interplay across weight groups. The high stability of our network (CS-C > 0.5 in all groups) gives confidence to these recommendations. In sum, these findings provide guidance for designing precise, subgroup-tailored interventions to improve childhood nutritional outcomes.
