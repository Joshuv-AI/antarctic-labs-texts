Gut Microbiota and Hypertension: Mechanisms, Drug Interactions, and Translational Directions for Individualized Therapy
Authors: Guotong Sun, Hongshuai Shen, Zheng Xiao, Caiyun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension remains a major global health challenge, with suboptimal treatment and blood pressure (BP) control rates and apparent or confirmed resistant hypertension (RHT) affecting approximately 10–20% of treated patients. Increasing evidence suggests that the gut microbiota may contribute to BP regulation through interconnected metabolic, immune–inflammatory, intestinal barrier, gut–brain, and gut–kidney pathways. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) may support vasodilation, renal sodium handling, epithelial barrier integrity, and anti-inflammatory signaling through receptor- and tissue-specific mechanisms, whereas trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) has been associated with adverse vascular and cardio–renal phenotypes but may function either as a pathogenic mediator or as a biomarker of altered microbial-host metabolism or impaired renal clearance. Beyond the SCFA–TMAO axis, bile acid metabolism, tryptophan/indole derivatives, phenylacetylglutamine, uremic toxins, and the nitrate–nitrite–nitric oxide pathway provide additional mechanistic links between microbial ecology and BP phenotypes. This review synthesizes mechanistic, pharmacological, clinical, and translational evidence on microbiota–hypertension interactions, with particular emphasis on drug–microbiota bidirectionality. We propose a three-layer framework in which bacterial enzymatic transformation, host metabolic regulation, and epithelial transport/barrier functions jointly shape antihypertensive drug exposure and response. Human interventional evidence remains preliminary: colon-targeted acetylated and butyrylated high-amylose maize starch increased circulating SCFAs and reduced 24-hour systolic BP by approximately 5–6 mmHg in a small, short-duration trial of untreated essential hypertension, whereas probiotics and prebiotics generally show modest BP reductions of approximately 1–3 mmHg systolic and 1–2 mmHg diastolic in meta-analyses. These findings support microbiota-informed hypertension research and risk stratification, but clinical implementation, particularly in well-defined RHT populations, remains investigational.Keywords: hypertension, gut microbiota, short-chain fatty acids, SCFAs, trimethylamine N-oxide, TMAO, resistant hypertension, RHT, microbiota-informed therapy

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent and burdensome cardiovascular disorders worldwide and remains a leading risk factor for myocardial infarction, stroke, chronic kidney disease, and premature mortality.1–3 In 2019, an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years were living with hypertension, with more than two-thirds residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).4 Despite the widespread availability of antihypertensive therapies, global treatment and blood pressure (BP) control rates remain below 50% and 20%, respectively.1,5 Resistant hypertension (RHT) affects approximately 10%–20% of treated patients and continues to present substantial clinical challenges.6 These gaps highlight the need for new biological perspectives that can improve BP control and enable more precise therapeutic strategies across diverse patient populations.

In this review, resistant hypertension (RHT) is defined in accordance with contemporary guideline-based concepts as blood pressure that remains above the recommended treatment goal despite the concurrent use of at least three antihypertensive drug classes at maximal or maximally tolerated doses, typically including a renin–angiotensin system blocker, a long-acting calcium channel blocker, and a thiazide or thiazide-like diuretic. RHT also includes patients whose blood pressure is controlled but requires four or more antihypertensive agents. Apparent RHT refers to uncontrolled office blood pressure despite such treatment before exclusion of pseudo-resistance, including inaccurate blood pressure measurement, suboptimal drug dosing or combinations, poor medication adherence, and white-coat hypertension. True RHT requires confirmation of persistent uncontrolled blood pressure after these factors have been excluded, preferably using out-of-office blood pressure assessment and adherence evaluation. Controlled RHT refers to blood pressure controlled with four or more medications, whereas refractory hypertension represents a more severe phenotype characterized by uncontrolled blood pressure despite treatment with five or more antihypertensive agents, including a diuretic and preferably a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist when tolerated. These distinctions are important because microbiota-associated mechanisms and therapeutic responses may differ across apparent, true, controlled, and refractory RHT phenotypes.

Over the past decade, growing evidence has implicated the gut microbiota as a key contributor to BP regulation.7–9 Dysbiosis has been associated with perturbed metabolic signaling, altered immune and inflammatory activity, and endothelial dysfunction. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced through microbial fermentation activate G protein–coupled receptors and may contribute to renal and vascular homeostasis, whereas trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) has been associated with endothelial inflammation, arterial stiffness, and adverse cardio–renal outcomes. However, these associations are influenced by dietary precursor intake, microbial trimethylamine production, hepatic flavin monooxygenase activity, renal clearance, age, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, medication exposure, and analytical platform variability.7,8,10,11 Impaired intestinal barrier integrity permits translocation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), driving low-grade systemic inflammation that further exacerbates vascular dysfunction. In contrast, beneficial taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila have been associated with enhanced tight-junction expression and improved epithelial barrier function.12–14 A Phase II randomized crossover trial of colon-targeted acetylated and butyrylated high-amylose maize starch reported increased circulating SCFA concentrations and an approximately 5–6 mmHg reduction in 24-hour systolic BP in untreated patients with essential hypertension, although the small sample size and short follow-up duration limit conclusions regarding sustained efficacy, cardiovascular benefit, or applicability to resistant hypertension.15 Multi-omic and metabolomic studies further suggest inverse associations between SCFAs and BP phenotypes, and positive associations between TMAO and vascular risk, with observed effect sizes varying across populations and analytical platforms.16–18

Despite these advances, several gaps limit clinical translation. First, many existing reviews have focused primarily on broad microbiota–hypertension associations, selected microbial metabolites, or individual mechanistic axes, whereas fewer have integrated receptor-specific metabolite biology, gut barrier dysfunction, immune–neurohumoral signaling, and gut–kidney interactions within a graded evidence framework. Second, the clinical relevance of microbiota-related mechanisms remains difficult to interpret without explicit attention to resistant hypertension phenotypes, including apparent, true, controlled, and refractory RHT. Third, inter-individual variability in antihypertensive drug response has not been sufficiently connected to drug–microbiota bidirectionality. Oral antihypertensive agents can remodel the gut microbiota, whereas microbial enzymes may metabolize drugs directly or regulate host drug-metabolizing enzymes, such as cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A), and transporters, such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1), via pregnane X receptor (PXR) and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling, thereby altering drug exposure and therapeutic stability.19–22 Although preclinical and pharmacokinetic studies support these bidirectional interactions, their predictive value in clinical practice has yet to be established.

Accordingly, this review differs from prior summaries by explicitly integrating four dimensions that are often discussed separately: mechanistic pathways linking microbial ecology to BP regulation; bidirectional interactions between the gut microbiota and antihypertensive drug classes; phenotype-aware interpretation of RHT and other high-risk populations; and translational requirements for biomarker standardization, patient stratification, and trial design. We summarize metabolic, inflammatory, barrier-related, and neuro–renal–humoral mechanisms; describe drug–microbiota interactions across major antihypertensive classes; and propose a three-layer conceptual framework in which bacterial enzymatic transformation, host metabolic regulation, and epithelial transport/barrier functions jointly shape antihypertensive drug exposure and response. By combining evidence calibration, clinical phenotype definition, and translational strategy, this review aims to provide a clinically oriented framework for microbiota-informed hypertension research rather than a purely mechanistic overview.

Literature Search Strategy

To enhance methodological transparency and reduce potential citation bias, we conducted a structured literature search to identify relevant evidence on the relationship between gut microbiota and hypertension. PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase were searched for articles published from January 2010 to May 2026, with the final search conducted before manuscript resubmission. Search terms included combinations of the following keywords: “hypertension,” “blood pressure,” “resistant hypertension,” “gut microbiota,” “gut microbiome,” “dysbiosis,” “short-chain fatty acids,” “SCFAs,” “trimethylamine N-oxide,” “TMAO,” “intestinal barrier,” “lipopolysaccharide,” “inflammation,” “gut–brain axis,” “gut–kidney axis,” “antihypertensive drugs,” “drug–microbiota interaction,” “probiotics,” “prebiotics,” and “fecal microbiota transplantation.” Boolean operators were used to combine disease-related, microbiota-related, metabolite-related, and therapeutic terms. Reference lists of relevant reviews, clinical trials, and guideline documents were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Original experimental studies, observational human studies, randomized clinical trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus statements, and guideline documents were considered eligible. Studies were included if they addressed mechanistic, pharmacological, clinical, or translational aspects of gut microbiota in blood pressure regulation or hypertension management. Exclusion criteria included non-English publications, case reports, conference abstracts without full-text availability, studies lacking direct relevance to hypertension or blood pressure regulation, and articles with insufficient methodological or mechanistic information.

Evidence was prioritized according to study design and relevance. Original mechanistic studies and clinical trials were used to support direct biological and interventional claims, while systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline documents were used to contextualize broader clinical and translational implications. Particular attention was given to studies addressing resistant hypertension, microbial metabolites, intestinal barrier dysfunction, immune–inflammatory pathways, and bidirectional interactions between antihypertensive drugs and the gut microbiota. The final literature selection was based on relevance, methodological quality, recency, and contribution to the conceptual framework of this narrative review.

As this was a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, the search strategy was designed to support comprehensive and transparent evidence mapping rather than formal quantitative synthesis or risk-of-bias scoring. Because the evidence base spans in vitro studies, animal experiments, observational human cohorts, small interventional trials, systematic reviews, and guideline documents, claims were interpreted according to the underlying study design and level of causal support. Mechanistic conclusions derived primarily from experimental models were described as biologically plausible or preclinical evidence, whereas findings from observational studies were framed as associations rather than causal effects. Interventional findings from small or short-duration trials were presented as proof-of-concept evidence and were not extrapolated to sustained antihypertensive efficacy, cardiovascular outcome reduction, or resistant hypertension unless directly supported by the cited population. This calibrated terminology was used throughout the review to distinguish established evidence from emerging, hypothesis-generating, or translationally preliminary findings.

Mechanisms of Gut Microbiota in Hypertension

The gut microbiota contributes to BP regulation through interconnected metabolic, inflammatory, neurohumoral, and barrier-related pathways.7,19,23 Increasing experimental and observational evidence suggests that dysbiosis may participate in the development and maintenance of hypertension, although the directionality and magnitude of this contribution likely vary across host background, diet, renal function, medication exposure, and disease stage.9,19,24,25 The following subsections summarize four major mechanistic domains linking the gut microbiota to hypertensive phenotypes.

Microbial Metabolites and Blood Pressure Regulation

Fermentation of dietary fiber by gut microorganisms generates short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate. These metabolites are generally associated with favorable cardiovascular and metabolic phenotypes, but their effects on BP regulation are receptor-specific, tissue-dependent, and context-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial.8,26–28

SCFAs signal through several host receptors, including free fatty acid receptor 3 (FFAR3/GPR41), free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2/GPR43), GPR109A/HCAR2, and olfactory receptor 78 (Olfr78). FFAR3/GPR41 and FFAR2/GPR43 are expressed in vascular, immune, intestinal, and renal tissues and may influence vascular tone, sympathetic activity, inflammatory signaling, and renal sodium handling. GPR109A/HCAR2, which is activated mainly by butyrate and related ligands, has been implicated in epithelial barrier maintenance and anti-inflammatory immune regulation. In contrast, Olfr78 has been shown in murine models to respond to SCFAs and promote renin release, suggesting a counter-regulatory or pressor mechanism under certain conditions. Therefore, the net effect of SCFAs on BP likely depends on SCFA species, receptor subtype, receptor distribution, host disease state, and the balance between vasodilatory, natriuretic, immunomodulatory, sympathetic, and renin-mediated pathways.26,29,30

Receptor nomenclature and species differences also require clarification. In the current literature, GPR41 and GPR43 are also referred to as FFAR3 and FFAR2, respectively. Murine Olfr78 is commonly discussed in experimental BP studies, whereas the closest human ortholog is OR51E2, also known as prostate-specific G-protein-coupled receptor (PSGR). Because much of the evidence for Olfr78-mediated renin regulation derives from animal models, direct extrapolation to human hypertension should be made cautiously. This distinction is important when interpreting SCFA receptor biology as a translational target.

Clinical evidence for SCFA-enhancing interventions remains promising but preliminary. A phase II randomized, double-blind, crossover trial of colon-targeted acetylated and butyrylated high-amylose maize starch reported increased circulating SCFA levels and an approximately 5–6 mmHg reduction in 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP in untreated patients with essential hypertension. However, the small sample size and short intervention period support interpretation as proof-of-concept evidence rather than confirmation of sustained antihypertensive efficacy, cardiovascular outcome reduction, or efficacy in resistant hypertension.15

In contrast, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) has been associated with adverse BP, vascular, and cardio–renal phenotypes, but its biological interpretation remains complex. Dietary choline, phosphatidylcholine, and carnitine can be metabolized by gut microbiota into trimethylamine (TMA), which is subsequently oxidized to TMAO by hepatic flavin monooxygenases, particularly FMO3. Experimental studies suggest that TMAO may promote endothelial inflammation, oxidative stress, arterial stiffness, and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation through pathways involving NF-κB signaling and vascular inflammatory responses. However, human observational studies are subject to important confounding by dietary patterns, renal clearance, age, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, medication exposure, and differences in analytical platforms. Therefore, circulating TMAO should not be interpreted uniformly as a direct causal mediator of hypertension. In some settings, TMAO may contribute mechanistically to vascular and renal dysfunction, whereas in others it may function primarily as a biomarker of altered microbial metabolism, dietary exposure, impaired renal excretion, or broader cardiometabolic risk.31–33

Beyond the SCFA–TMAO axis, several additional microbial metabolic pathways may contribute to BP regulation and hypertensive target-organ injury. Gut microbial bile acid metabolism modifies the primary-to-secondary bile acid pool and may influence BP through bile acid receptors such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), which regulate metabolic inflammation, vascular function, energy homeostasis, and renal–fluid balance.34,35 Tryptophan-derived indole metabolites may modulate intestinal barrier integrity, immune activity, and vascular inflammation through aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)-dependent signaling, although their net cardiovascular effects may vary according to the specific metabolite and host inflammatory state.36,37 Phenylacetylglutamine, a gut microbiota-derived phenylalanine metabolite, has been linked to adrenergic receptor signaling and cardiovascular risk, suggesting a potential connection between microbial amino acid metabolism, sympathetic signaling, platelet activation, and vascular events.38 In patients with chronic kidney disease or impaired renal clearance, microbiota-derived uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate may accumulate and contribute to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, renal injury, and BP dysregulation.39 In addition, the nitrate–nitrite–nitric oxide pathway, which depends partly on nitrate-reducing oral and gut microbial communities, may influence vascular tone and BP through microbial conversion of dietary nitrate to nitrite and subsequent nitric oxide bioavailability.40,41

Taken together, microbial metabolites should be viewed as a multidimensional regulatory network rather than a simple SCFA-beneficial/TMAO-harmful binary axis. SCFAs, TMAO, bile acids, tryptophan/indole derivatives, phenylacetylglutamine, uremic toxins, and nitrate–nitrite–nitric oxide intermediates may interact with host vascular, renal, immune, epithelial, and neurohumoral pathways. The direction and magnitude of these effects depend on microbial ecology, diet, kidney function, medication exposure, host receptor expression, and disease stage. This broader metabolite-centered framework provides a more balanced basis for understanding microbiota-associated BP regulation and for developing microbiota-informed therapeutic strategies.

Immune–Inflammatory Responses and Blood Pressure Regulation

Microbial imbalance induces low-grade systemic inflammation, altering BP regulatory pathways.7 Hypertensive animal models often exhibit reduced microbial diversity, increased proportions of Gram-negative bacteria, and elevated circulating lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels.42,43 LPS activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)–NF-κB and MAPK signaling, amplifying cytokine production and oxidative stress, ultimately impairing endothelial function.44

Dietary salt provides an additional mechanistic link. High-salt intake disrupts gut microbial composition, reduces abundance of Lactobacillus murinus, and enhances Th17/IL-17A signaling, contributing to BP elevation. Restoration of L. murinus levels reduces IL-17A expression and partially reverses BP increases in experimental models.24 These findings underscore the role of immune–inflammatory pathways as central mediators between dysbiosis and hypertension.

Gut–Brain and Gut–Kidney Axes

The gut microbiota communicates bidirectionally with neurohumoral systems. SCFAs stimulate vagal afferents, reduce sympathetic tone, and improve autonomic balance. Conversely, dysbiosis-associated elevations in LPS, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) may increase blood–brain barrier permeability and activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, promoting sympathetic overactivation and BP elevation.7,45,46 Certain microbial metabolites, including γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) precursors, may additionally influence central BP regulation through neuroimmune pathways.7,47

In the gut–kidney axis, SCFAs act on renal tubular receptors, promoting natriuresis and exerting local anti-inflammatory effects, thereby supporting vascular homeostasis. In contrast, TMAO has been linked to glomerular endothelial injury, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and increased RAAS activation in experimental and observational settings, although elevated circulating TMAO in chronic kidney disease may also reflect impaired renal clearance rather than direct pathogenic activity alone.10,11,33,39 Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) frequently exhibit reduced microbial diversity and accumulation of uremic toxins, suggesting a bidirectional deterioration between microbial imbalance, renal dysfunction, and hypertension.39

Intestinal Barrier Function and Systemic Inflammation

The intestinal epithelial barrier prevents microbial components from entering systemic circulation. Downregulation of tight-junction proteins such as occludin, claudin, and ZO-1 increases gut permeability, allowing translocation of LPS and other bacterial fragments and promoting metabolic endotoxemia.48 Hypertensive animal models display increased intestinal permeability accompanied by elevated plasma LPS and proinflammatory cytokines, whereas butyrate or probiotic supplementation restores tight-junction integrity and lowers BP.49

Human studies similarly report higher serum lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) levels in individuals with hypertension, supporting the contribution of impaired gut barrier function to disease pathogenesis.23

Integrated Mechanisms and Research Perspectives

The interaction between the gut microbiota and hypertension can be conceptualized as a mechanistic cascade. Reduced SCFA production, altered microbial metabolite profiles, and elevated TMAO levels may contribute to impaired epithelial barrier integrity, LPS translocation, and activation of TLR4–NF-κB signaling, although the causal contribution of each metabolite likely varies across experimental models and human populations. The resulting systemic inflammation and sympathetic activation promote RAAS upregulation, vasoconstriction, and sustained BP elevation.19,23 Findings from germ-free animal models and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) studies provide experimental support for partial transferability of hypertensive phenotypes, supporting the hypothesis that the gut microbiota may influence BP regulation. However, the extent to which these findings translate to heterogeneous human hypertension populations remains uncertain. Nonetheless, population-level causal inference remains limited. Large prospective studies integrating metagenomic, metabolomic, and immunologic data are needed to quantify effect sizes and validate mechanistic pathways, and to evaluate the utility of these pathways for patient stratification.7,19,50

Interactions Between Antihypertensive Drugs and the Gut Microbiota

Accumulating experimental, pharmacokinetic, and early clinical evidence suggests that bidirectional interactions may exist between antihypertensive agents and the gut microbial ecosystem.19,20,51 Mechanistically, antihypertensive drugs can reshape microbial composition, metabolic pathways, and mucosal barrier integrity, thereby modulating inflammation, metabolism, and vascular homeostasis. Conversely, the microbiota can directly metabolize drugs or regulate host metabolic enzymes and transporters, thereby altering oral drug exposure and antihypertensive responses. The following sections summarize current evidence for both directions across pharmacological classes and propose a general three-layer interaction framework. Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ACEIs/ARBs)

Drug Effects on the Microbiota and Barrier Function

In addition to their canonical role in regulating vasoactive peptides, inhibitors of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) may contribute to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis.52,53 Reported patterns include increased microbial diversity; enrichment of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)–producing taxa (eg., Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus); upregulation of tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin, ZO-1); and reductions in inflammatory cytokines.54 Elevated levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are associated with vasodilation, enhanced renal sodium excretion, and anti-inflammatory signaling, may confer ancillary cardiovascular benefits mediated through the gut–inflammation–vascular axis.8 Whether ACEIs and ARBs reduce trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) concentrations remains uncertain, as findings have varied substantially across populations and analytical methodologies.55

Microbial Feedback on Drug Metabolism and Efficacy

Certain anaerobes (eg., Coprococcus comes) possess esterase activity that hydrolyzes esterified ACEIs such as quinapril in vitro; drug potency increases in germ-free or antibiotic-treated animals, supporting causal microbial biotransformation.20 Microbes may also influence absorption by altering luminal pH, mucus properties, and hydrolase activity, while metabolic and inflammatory milieus (for example, lower SCFAs with higher TMAO) can modify host responsiveness to RAAS blockade.51 Collectively, these findings suggest a bidirectional feedback relationship, whereby ACEIs and ARBs remodel the gut microecosystem while the microbiota concurrently modulates their pharmacodynamic properties.

Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

Drug Effects on Microbial and Metabolic Environments

By enhancing hemodynamic stability and endothelial function, calcium channel blockers (CCBs) may indirectly affect intestinal perfusion and alter the luminal metabolic microenvironment.56 Both animal experiments and preliminary human studies have reported modest alterations in gut microbial composition associated with CCB administration, although the direction and magnitude of these effects remain inconsistent and require further clinical validation.57

Microbial Regulation of CCB Pharmacokinetics

The gut microbiota can modulate CCB pharmacokinetics through both direct enzymatic interactions and indirect regulatory mechanisms.58 A direct mechanism involves Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, which expresses the deacetylase BT4096 capable of deacetylating diltiazem and thereby diminishing its pharmacological activity (a representative example of bacterial enzyme–mediated drug modification).21 Indirect evidence indicates that short-term microbial depletion leads to increased plasma concentrations of amlodipine. Conversely, in hypertensive animal models, reduced nifedipine exposure has been associated with intestinal CYP3A1 upregulation and concurrent alterations in gut microbial composition. These findings collectively support a mechanism by which microbial states modulate host metabolic enzymes and consequently influence drug clearance.59 Moreover, microbial metabolites such as indole derivatives, SCFAs, and secondary bile acids can activate the pregnane X receptor (PXR) and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Activation of these receptors regulates intestinal and hepatic CYP3A expression as well as P-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1) activity, ultimately modulating first-pass metabolism and drug efflux.60

Diuretics

Drug Effects on the Microbiota and Inflammatory Axes

In addition to their natriuretic effects, thiazide diuretics (such as hydrochlorothiazide [HCTZ]) may disrupt gut microbial homeostasis and activate the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling cascade.61 In animal studies, HCTZ has been shown to promote hepatic macrophage M1 polarization, increase pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and impair glucose metabolism. These findings suggest a potential mechanistic sequence linking drug exposure to gut dysbiosis, systemic inflammation, and metabolic disturbances that may underlie adverse clinical outcomes.61

Microbial Feedback on Drug Effects

These phenotypic changes can be partially reversed under antibiotic treatment or germ-free conditions. Moreover, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has been shown to transfer HCTZ-related metabolic alterations between hosts, further supporting a causal role of the gut microbiota in mediating these adverse effects.61 Although direct bacterial metabolism of diuretics has not yet been demonstrated, inflammation- and barrier-mediated alterations in drug absorption and distribution deserve further investigation.51

Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists and Aldosterone–Microbiome Interactions

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), including spironolactone, eplerenone, and the non-steroidal agent finerenone, are clinically important in hypertension phenotypes characterized by aldosterone excess, volume expansion, salt sensitivity, chronic kidney disease, and resistant hypertension (RHT).62,63 Spironolactone is a cornerstone add-on therapy for RHT, supported by the PATHWAY-2 trial, in which spironolactone provided greater blood pressure reduction than placebo, bisoprolol, or doxazosin in patients with drug-resistant hypertension.64 These clinical data establish MR blockade as a major component of RHT management, but they do not by themselves clarify whether microbiota-related mechanisms contribute to the antihypertensive response.62–64

Preclinical evidence suggests that aldosterone–mineralocorticoid receptor signaling may interact with gut microbial ecology, intestinal barrier function, sympathetic tone, and immune–inflammatory pathways.65,66 In spontaneously hypertensive rats, mineralocorticoid receptor blockade with spironolactone has been reported to reduce gut dysbiosis, improve gut integrity, attenuate inflammatory activation, improve endothelial function, and lower systolic blood pressure, potentially through reduced gut sympathetic drive.65 These findings support a biologically plausible link among MR activation, gut barrier dysfunction, microbial imbalance, vascular inflammation, and hypertension. However, this evidence remains primarily preclinical and should not be interpreted as proof that microbiota modulation mediates the clinical benefit of MRAs in human RHT.65

The aldosterone–microbiome relationship may be particularly relevant in primary aldosteronism, salt-sensitive hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and refractory hypertension, where mineralocorticoid excess, volume retention, renal dysfunction, and systemic inflammation frequently coexist.66,67 Steroidal MRAs such as spironolactone and eplerenone are established treatments for primary aldosteronism when surgery is not appropriate or bilateral adrenal disease is present.67 In contrast, finerenone has been studied mainly in cardio–renal metabolic disease and has more limited microbiome-specific evidence.68,69 Future studies should determine whether baseline microbial signatures, SCFA-producing taxa, intestinal permeability markers, or TMAO and uremic-toxin profiles predict MRA responsiveness, adverse effects such as hyperkalemia, or residual cardiovascular and renal risk.

Other Antihypertensive Classes: Emerging Signals

Current evidence regarding β- and α-adrenergic blockers, centrally acting sympatholytic agents, direct vasodilators, and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors in the context of gut microbiota modulation remains scarce and largely exploratory.19 Preliminary mechanistic evidence indicates that microbial metabolites, including phenylacetylglutamine and indole derivatives, may affect cardiovascular and antihypertensive response phenotypes by modulating sympathetic nervous system activity, adrenergic receptor signaling, endothelial function, or inflammatory tone. However, direct evidence linking these metabolites to altered responses to β-blockers, α-blockers, or centrally acting antihypertensive agents remains limited.38

Several studies have reported associations between SGLT2 inhibitor therapy and enrichment of butyrate-producing bacteria, improved intestinal barrier integrity, and reduced metabolic inflammation. Nevertheless, BP reduction with SGLT2 inhibitors is multifactorial and likely reflects natriuresis, osmotic diuresis, weight change, vascular effects, and cardio–renal metabolic improvements in addition to any microbiota-related mechanisms. Therefore, causal relationships between microbial alterations and BP reduction remain to be established.70

General Three-Layer Interaction Framework

Across different classes of antihypertensive drugs, the interplay between pharmacologic agents and the gut microbiota can be conceptualized as a unified three-layer molecular network. Within this framework, bacterial enzymes, host metabolic pathways, and epithelial transport and barrier functions collectively influence drug exposure. Together, these processes shape inter-individual differences in response phenotypes. At the microbial enzymatic layer, bacterial esterases and deacetylases directly modify the chemical structures of antihypertensive agents. Representative examples include BT4096-mediated deacetylation of diltiazem and Coprococcus-mediated hydrolysis of ester-type ACE inhibitors (ACEIs), both of which influence the relative abundance of pharmacologically active metabolites.71 At the host metabolic layer, microbial metabolites such as indole derivatives, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and secondary bile acids interact with host nuclear receptors, including the pregnane X receptor (PXR) and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Through these pathways, the microbiota regulates CYP3A, uridine 5′-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), and related enzymatic systems that govern first-pass metabolism and systemic clearance of antihypertensive agents.71 At the epithelial transport and barrier layer, cytokine activity, together with microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and bile acids, jointly modulates the expression of efflux transporters, including P-gp and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), and the integrity of epithelial tight junctions. These processes collectively determine intestinal absorption, tissue distribution, and systemic drug exposure.60,72 When integrated with the SCFA/TMAO–inflammation–barrier–gut–brain and gut–kidney regulatory axes discussed in Mechanisms of Gut Microbiota in Hypertension, this multilayered framework provides a mechanistic basis linking gut microbial ecology with antihypertensive pharmacodynamics. It also provides a conceptual basis for future stratified studies evaluating whether microbiota-informed markers can improve prediction of antihypertensive response or identify patients most likely to benefit from adjunctive microbiota-targeted interventions.

Clinical and Experimental Evidence

This section integrates findings from population-based studies, causal inference analyses, and interventional trials to delineate reproducible patterns and current uncertainties regarding gut microbiota, antihypertensive efficacy, biomarker potential, and resistant hypertension (RHT). Together, these data help define the translational basis for individualized, microbiota-informed therapeutic strategies.

Population-Level Associations Between Gut Microbiota and Antihypertensive Efficacy

Observational studies and systematic reviews have reported that untreated individuals with primary hypertension exhibit reduced microbial diversity, diminished SCFA biosynthesis pathways, and altered metabolic profiles.16,25,73,74 These alterations have been associated with 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) parameters and markers of arterial stiffness. In treated patients, microbial composition has been associated with variability in antihypertensive response, with two reproducible mechanistic themes emerging: (1) direct bacterial metabolism of orally administered agents; and (2) microbiota-mediated modulation of host metabolic enzymes (eg., CYP3A) and efflux transporters (eg., P-gp/ABCB1).75,76 Although effect sizes vary across geographic regions and sequencing platforms, the overall pattern suggests that microbial factors may contribute to treatment heterogeneity, but interpretation requires careful attention to confounding and methodological variability.

Important confounders in microbiota–hypertension studies include habitual diet, fiber intake, salt intake, alcohol consumption, smoking, age, sex, body mass index, adiposity, kidney function, diabetes, cardiovascular comorbidities, bowel habits, antibiotic exposure, probiotic or prebiotic use, and background antihypertensive therapy. Medication effects are particularly relevant because antihypertensive drugs, metformin, statins, proton pump inhibitors, glucose-lowering agents, and antibiotics may independently alter microbial composition or metabolite profiles. Technical sources of heterogeneity, including stool versus blood sampling, DNA extraction methods, sequencing platform, taxonomic annotation pipeline, metabolomic assay, storage conditions, and batch effects, further limit cross-study comparability. Future observational studies and trials should therefore collect standardized metadata, adjust for key demographic, dietary, renal, metabolic, and medication-related variables, and prespecify sensitivity analyses to distinguish microbiota-associated signals from confounding by host phenotype, treatment exposure, or analytical platform.7,19,51,60,74,77

Translational Evidence for Causal Relationships

Interventional and causal inference studies provide further support for microbiota–BP interactions. Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have reported short-term reductions in BP among selected participants, with effect magnitude appearing dependent on baseline microbial composition and metabolic state.9 However, durability of BP reduction remains limited, highlighting the need for longer-term evaluation.

Genetic instrumental-variable analyses (Mendelian randomization) complement these findings by demonstrating positive associations between genetically predicted TMAO levels and BP, arterial stiffness, and endothelial dysfunction, alongside inverse associations for SCFAs.9,78,79 Instrument strength, pleiotropy, and population-specific allele frequencies influence effect estimates, emphasizing the need for triangulation with randomized evidence.80 Overall, current data support a biologically plausible role for microbial metabolites in BP regulation, although causal relationships and clinical effect sizes require further validation.

However, the evidence base remains heterogeneous and should be interpreted cautiously. Not all observational studies have identified consistent microbial signatures of hypertension, and reported taxa-level associations often differ across cohorts, geographic regions, dietary backgrounds, sequencing platforms, and statistical pipelines. Some microbiota-targeted interventions have produced modest or short-lived BP changes, whereas others have shown limited or no clinically meaningful effect, particularly when baseline microbial composition, adherence, diet, medication exposure, and metabolic status are not accounted for. FMT studies remain exploratory, with uncertain durability, donor–recipient variability, and limited evidence in well-defined RHT populations. Similarly, Mendelian randomization studies can strengthen causal inference but remain sensitive to instrument strength, pleiotropy, population ancestry, and metabolite measurement assumptions. Therefore, current evidence should be viewed as supportive of biological plausibility and hypothesis generation rather than definitive proof of microbiota-directed antihypertensive efficacy.7,19,74,80,81

Microbiota-Targeted Interventions in Human Studies

Several microbiota-directed interventions have shown preliminary BP-related effects in human studies. Colon-targeted high-amylose maize starch modified with acetate and butyrate (HAMSAB) increases circulating SCFA concentrations. In a phase II randomized crossover trial, HAMSAB was associated with an approximately 5–6 mmHg reduction in 24-hour systolic BP among untreated patients with essential hypertension. These findings provide proof-of-concept evidence that enhancing colonic SCFA delivery may influence BP, but the short follow-up duration, limited sample size, and enrollment of untreated essential hypertension rather than resistant hypertension preclude conclusions regarding long-term BP control, cardiovascular outcomes, or efficacy in RHT.15,82 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of probiotic and prebiotic supplementation report modest BP reductions (approximately 1–3 mmHg systolic and 1–2 mmHg diastolic), with effect sizes influenced by the specific microbial strains used, dosage, and duration of intervention.83,84 In addition, high-fiber and polyphenol-rich dietary patterns (such as the DASH and Mediterranean diets) are associated with enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa and improvements in inflammatory and metabolic profiles, although definitive evidence for long-term cardiovascular outcome benefits remains limited.85,86

Best-practice recommendations for future clinical trials include preregistration (eg., ClinicalTrials.gov, PROSPERO), adherence to STORMS and CONSORT guidelines, and use of adaptive or enrichment designs where appropriate. Such measures may improve methodological transparency, enhance comparability across studies, and facilitate more robust evaluation of microbiota-targeted strategies for BP control. Future trials should also report null or neutral findings transparently, as these results are essential for defining the boundary conditions under which microbiota-targeted interventions may or may not influence BP.

Biomarkers and Stratification Clues

Reproducible biomarker patterns have emerged across studies, although their clinical interpretation requires careful attention to confounding and methodological variability. Higher circulating TMAO levels have been associated with elevated BP, increased arterial stiffness, impaired endothelial function, and adverse cardio–renal phenotypes. However, TMAO concentrations are influenced by dietary precursor intake, gut microbial TMA production, hepatic FMO3 activity, renal clearance, age, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, medication exposure, and analytical platform differences. Accordingly, TMAO may serve as a marker of microbial–host metabolic status or renal functional decline rather than a direct pathogenic mediator in all settings.10,11,18,31–33 In contrast, higher SCFA concentrations are generally associated with more favorable BP and metabolic phenotypes.16 Elevated LPS or lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) concentrations may reflect impaired intestinal barrier integrity and have been linked to adverse BP profiles.23

Variability in analytical platforms, specimen types, storage conditions, and reporting thresholds limits comparability across studies. Future biomarker studies should adopt a predefined pre-analytical standard operating procedure (SOP). For TMAO, plasma or serum should be selected a priori and used consistently within a study, preferably with fasting morning sampling, rapid processing, aliquoting, storage at −80°C, and minimization of repeated freeze–thaw cycles. Dietary intake, renal function, medication exposure, and sample collection timing should be recorded because these factors can substantially influence circulating TMAO concentrations. For SCFAs, fecal samples are most informative for estimating gut microbial production, whereas plasma or serum SCFAs reflect systemic exposure but are more susceptible to low concentrations and pre-analytical variability. Stool samples should be collected using standardized containers or stabilizing media, processed or frozen promptly, stored at −80°C when possible, and reported relative to wet weight, dry weight, or another predefined normalization method.

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is recommended for targeted quantification of TMAO and SCFAs. Assays should specify chromatographic conditions, derivatization strategy when applicable, multiple-reaction monitoring transitions, calibration range, lower limit of quantification, isotope-labeled internal standards, recovery, matrix effects, intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation, and quality-control procedures. For TMAO, stable-isotope dilution LC-MS/MS using an isotope-labeled internal standard is commonly used for sensitive quantification in biological matrices. For SCFAs, derivatization-based LC-MS/MS approaches, such as 3-nitrophenylhydrazine derivatization, may improve detection and comparability, particularly in fecal samples. Standardized reporting of these pre-analytical and analytical parameters will be necessary before TMAO, SCFAs, or related microbial metabolites can be reliably incorporated into clinical risk stratification or treatment prediction.77,87,88 When combined with host metabolic, renal, inflammatory, and transporter phenotypes, such biomarkers may eventually help refine patient stratification in microbiota-informed hypertension research.

Resistant Hypertension (RHT): Microbiota-Associated Features

Because RHT encompasses heterogeneous clinical phenotypes, microbiota-related findings should be interpreted according to whether the population represents apparent RHT, true uncontrolled RHT, controlled RHT, or refractory hypertension. Apparent RHT may include patients with pseudo-resistance caused by inaccurate blood pressure measurement, white-coat hypertension, suboptimal treatment regimens, or poor medication adherence, whereas true RHT requires confirmation of persistent uncontrolled blood pressure after these factors have been excluded. Patients with confirmed RHT or refractory hypertension may exhibit a proinflammatory microbial environment, reduced abundance of SCFA-producing taxa, impaired intestinal barrier function, and activation of TMAO-related or uremic-toxin pathways. These profiles may coexist with heightened sympathetic activity, RAAS activation, volume expansion, aldosterone excess, endothelial inflammation, and renal dysfunction. Baseline microbial and metabolic signatures have been linked to variable responses to RAAS inhibitors and calcium channel blockers, but future studies should stratify participants by RHT phenotype to improve clinical interpretability.

Microbiota-targeted interventions, including SCFA precursors, individualized FMT, and tailored dietary strategies, remain investigational in RHT. Importantly, the currently cited HAMSAB trial enrolled untreated patients with essential hypertension rather than patients with apparent, true, controlled, or refractory RHT. Therefore, the observed reduction in 24-hour systolic BP should not be extrapolated to RHT populations. At present, HAMSAB efficacy in RHT remains a hypothesis requiring dedicated clinical evaluation in well-defined RHT cohorts with standardized out-of-office BP assessment, adherence confirmation, background medication documentation, and adequate follow-up. Evidence for other microbiota-targeted strategies in RHT is also limited by small sample sizes, short study duration, and methodological heterogeneity, underscoring the need for microbiota-informed combination trials in high-risk populations.9,89

Individualized Therapy and Future Directions

Emerging evidence supports the biological plausibility and translational potential of incorporating microbiota-informed strategies into individualized hypertension research, although routine clinical implementation remains premature. Inter-individual differences in microbial diversity, metabolite production, and host drug-metabolizing pathways provide a biologically plausible basis for precision-oriented interventions in this setting.78,81 Within this context, a three-dimensional microbiota–metabolite–host model offers a structured framework for understanding therapeutic heterogeneity. Reduced microbial diversity and depletion of SCFA-producing taxa have been associated with unfavorable BP phenotypes; the SCFA–TMAO axis represents a candidate metabolic interface involving vascular, inflammatory, and neurohumoral processes; and variation in CYP3A activity, P-gp/ABCB1 expression, and related metabolic pathways may affect drug exposure and response.7,90 These dimensions may allow provisional classification of patients into subgroups, such as low-SCFA/high-TMAO, barrier-impaired/inflammation-active, or metabolism-limited, to support enhanced treatment individualization.

Sex as a biological variable should also be considered in microbiota-informed hypertension research. Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbiota composition, microbial metabolite profiles, immune–inflammatory responses, and BP-related phenotypes may differ between women and men.91,92 Sex hormones may contribute to these differences through bidirectional interactions with the gut microbiota, including estrogen–microbiome interactions that may influence vascular inflammation, endothelial function, intestinal barrier integrity, and microbial metabolic activity.91–93 In observational data using 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, sex-specific associations between gut microbial features, SCFA-related pathways, and essential hypertension have been reported, suggesting that microbiota–BP relationships may not be identical across sexes.94 Menopause, aging, diet, adiposity, and medication exposure may further modify these relationships.91,93 However, available evidence remains largely observational, and few interventional studies have been adequately powered to test sex-specific microbiota-mediated BP responses. Future microbiome studies and microbiota-targeted trials should therefore prespecify sex-stratified analyses, report menopausal status and hormone therapy when relevant, and evaluate whether microbial biomarkers or therapeutic responses differ by sex.

Several microbiota-targeted strategies have shown preliminary or modest BP-related effects within this stratified framework. SCFA-enhancing interventions such as HAMSAB have increased circulating acetate and butyrate concentrations and reduced 24-hour systolic BP by approximately 5–6 mmHg in a small, short-duration trial of untreated essential hypertension.15 However, their long-term efficacy, cardiovascular outcome effects, and applicability to RHT remain unproven.

Probiotics and prebiotics have shown modest BP-lowering effects in randomized trials and meta-analyses, with efficacy influenced by strain selection, dosage, baseline microbial composition, and treatment duration.83,84 Dietary patterns rich in fiber and polyphenols, including the DASH and Mediterranean diets, may promote enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa and improve inflammatory and metabolic profiles, supporting their role as general cardiometabolic strategies rather than microbiota-specific antihypertensive therapies.85,86 Precision fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) remains investigational; early studies suggest short-term BP or metabolic effects in selected populations, but donor–recipient compatibility, durability, safety monitoring, and efficacy in well-defined RHT populations require dedicated evaluation.9

Future research priorities include clarifying mechanistic pathways and strengthening causal inference. Multi-omic approaches integrating metagenomic, metabolomic, and immunologic datasets are needed to delineate interactions among the SCFA/TMAO axis, inflammatory signaling, neurohumoral activation, and drug metabolism. In parallel, biomedical signal-processing and machine-learning approaches developed for physiological signal analysis, activity-state recognition, and cardiovascular phenotype classification may provide complementary tools for integrating digital phenotyping with microbiome, metabolomic, and clinical datasets, although such approaches require hypertension- and microbiome-specific validation before clinical translation.95,96 Adequately powered randomized controlled trials should be designed to quantify clinically meaningful effects and to validate causal relationships. Advances in synthetic biology and controlled-release technologies may enable programmable modulation of microbial metabolic functions, such as targeted enhancement of SCFA production or regulation of TMA/TMAO conversion. Adaptive and enrichment-based clinical trial designs will be particularly valuable for evaluating microbiota-directed combination strategies in high-risk populations, including well-defined RHT cohorts. Such trials should specifically test whether SCFA-enhancing interventions such as HAMSAB provide incremental BP reduction beyond optimized guideline-based therapy, rather than extrapolating from untreated essential hypertension populations. Standardization of analytical platforms for SCFA and TMAO quantification, harmonized reporting metrics, and multicenter reproducibility will be essential for improving cross-study comparability and supporting clinical translation.

Collectively, these developments highlight an increasingly clear pathway toward microbiota-guided precision antihypertensive strategies. Continued advances in mechanistic integration, methodological rigor, and translational evaluation may help define whether microbiota-informed strategies can contribute to individualized BP management.

Conclusion

Current evidence supports an important association between the gut microbiota and BP regulation through metabolic, immune–inflammatory, neurohumoral, renal, vascular, and intestinal barrier-related pathways. Several findings are relatively well established at the mechanistic or associative level: SCFAs can signal through host receptors involved in vascular tone, renal sodium handling, epithelial barrier function, and immune regulation; gut barrier dysfunction and LPS-related inflammatory activation are linked to adverse BP phenotypes; and antihypertensive drugs may interact bidirectionally with the gut microbiota through microbial enzymatic transformation, host metabolic regulation, and epithelial transport or barrier pathways. In addition, clinical evidence supports the established role of spironolactone as an add-on therapy for RHT, although microbiota-mediated mechanisms of MRA response remain primarily preclinical.

Other areas should be considered emerging or hypothesis-generating. The causal role of specific microbial metabolites, particularly TMAO, remains incompletely defined because circulating concentrations are influenced by diet, renal clearance, cardiometabolic status, medication exposure, and analytical variability. Microbiota-targeted interventions, including SCFA-enhancing strategies, probiotics, prebiotics, dietary modification, and precision FMT, have shown preliminary, modest, or heterogeneous BP-related effects in selected study populations. Importantly, HAMSAB evidence is currently limited to untreated essential hypertension, and its efficacy as an adjunctive strategy in well-defined RHT populations has not been established.

Future research should therefore prioritize adequately powered randomized trials, longitudinal multi-omic studies, standardized pre-analytical and analytical methods, sex-stratified analyses, careful control of dietary and medication-related confounding, and phenotype-specific evaluation of apparent, true, controlled, and refractory RHT. With continued methodological refinement and clinical validation, microbiota-informed strategies may eventually help improve risk stratification and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from adjunctive approaches, but routine clinical implementation remains investigational.
