Concentration of information on discrete groups
Jonathan Hermon, Xiangying Huang, Francesco Pedrotti, Justin Salez

Abstract
Motivated by the Asymptotic Equipartition Property and its recently discovered role in the cutoff phenomenon, we initiate the systematic study of varentropy on discrete groups. Our main result is an approximate tensorization inequality which asserts that the varentropy of any conjugacy-invariant random walk is, up to a universal multiplicative constant, at most that of the free Abelian random walk with the same jump rates. In particular, it is always bounded by the number d d of generators, uniformly in time and in the size of the group. This universal estimate is sharp and can be seen as a discrete analogue of a celebrated result of Bobkov and Madiman concerning random d − d- dimensional vectors with a log-concave density (AOP 2011). A key ingredient in our proof is the fact that conjugacy-invariant random walks have non-negative Bakry-Émery curvature, a result which seems new and of independent interest.

Introduction

1.1
The Asymptotic Equipartition Property

Let
X
be a random variable taking values in a measured space
({\mathbb{X}},\mathcal{A},\pi)
and admitting a density
f
w.r.t.
\pi
. In Information Theory, the real-valued random variable

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\log f(X),

(1)

is known as the
information content
of
X
, because it quantifies the level of “surprise” or “unpredictability” inherent to the actual observation of
X
; see, e.g.,
[
12
]
. Its expectation
H(X):={\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{I}(X)]
is nothing but the
entropy
of
X
, which plays a fundamental role in many branches of mathematics, physics and computer science.

An essential property of the information content is
tensorization
: if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
are independent, each taking values in its own measured space, then the collection
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
(taking values in the resulting product space) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X_{1})+\cdots+\mathcal{I}(X_{d}).

(2)

In particular, when
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
are i.i.d. and
d
is large,
\mathcal{I}(X)
is highly concentrated around
dH(X_{1})
, by virtue of the law of large numbers: in words, the outcome of an experiment consisting of a huge number of independent and identically distributed observations is very likely contained in a deterministic set of “typical outcomes”, all of which are “roughly equally surprising”. This elementary fact happens to be the simplest instance of a very general phenomenon discovered by Shannon
[
31
]
, McMillan
[
24
]
and Breiman
[
7
]
, and known as the
Asymptotic Equipartition Property
. It applies to any ergodic process and can be considered as the very starting point of Information Theory. We refer the reader to the classical textbook
[
12
]
for details.

As any limit theorem, the Asymptotic Equipartition Property needs to be made quantitative in order to become truly effective, and this naturally amounts to controlling the variance of the information content:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\mathcal{I}(X)\right).

(3)

Because it quantifies the fluctuations around the entropy, this fundamental statistics has been beautifully called
varentropy
. It appeared a decade ago in the context of optimal data compression
[
21
]
, but has since then been shown to play an important role in completely different areas, such as importance sampling
[
9
]
or the cutoff phenomenon
[
28
,
29
]
. Just like the information content and entropy, the varentropy tensorizes: When
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
are independent, the collection
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{1})+\cdots+{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{d}).

(4)

In particular, while the entropy of a collection of i.i.d. random variables grows linearly with the dimension
d
, the fluctuations of the information content around the entropy are of order
\sqrt{d}
only: this constitutes a quantitative, non-asymptotic version of the Asymptotic Equipartition Property. Extending the latter to dependent data is a natural and important problem, on which very little progress has been made.

An emblematic result in this direction is the celebrated varentropy estimate of Bobkov and Madiman
[
5
]
for log-concave random vectors. Specifically, let
({\mathbb{X}},\mathcal{A},\pi)
be the
d-
dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with its Borel
\sigma-
field and the Lebesgue measure, and assume that the
{\mathbb{X}}-
valued random variable
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
admits a density of the form
f=e^{-V}
, where
V
is convex. Then, Bobkov and Madiman proved the existence of a universal constant
c<\infty
such that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle cd,

(5)

regardless of the distributional details of
X
. In other words, the information content of a log-concave random vector concentrates at least as well as if its coordinates were independent. This remarkable result has since then been reproved, refined and extended in several directions. In particular, the value of the constant has been sharpened to
c=1

[
25
, Corollary 6]
, which turned out to be optimal
[
16
]
. As noted by Chewi
[
10
]
, the estimate (
5
) can be seen as a special case of a certain dimensional improvement of the celebrated Brascamp-Lieb inequality
[
6
, Proposition 4.1]
. Moreover, the log-concavity assumption can be somewhat relaxed, at a well-understood price
[
15
]
.

For several reasons – one being the cutoff phenomenon, discussed below – it would be highly desirable to develop an appropriate analogue of the above result on discrete spaces, such as the
d-
dimensional Euclidean lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and its quotients. However, finding the right substitute for log-concavity seems far from obvious, and the various convex-analytical tools and changes of variables used in the proofs of (
5
) do not seem to admit a clear discrete counterpart. The main contribution of the present paper is a universal varentropy estimate of the form (
5
) for conjugacy-invariant random walks on arbitrary discrete groups, with the role of the dimension
d
being naturally played by the number of generators. This result actually follows from a more refined, time-dependent statement which compares the varentropy of the walk to that of its “free Abelian version”, in which the
d
coordinates evolve independently and without torsion.

1.2
Setup and main result

In the remainder of the paper,
{\mathbb{X}}
is a discrete group, equipped with the counting measure. We write
\circ
for the identity element,
x^{-1}
for the inverse of
x
, and
xy
for the product of
x
by
y
. By definition, the varentropy of a
{\mathbb{X}}-
valued random variable
X
is

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}(\log f(X)),

(6)

where
f\colon x\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}(X=x)
is the probability mass function of
X
. As motivated in the first section, our aim is to investigate the varentropy of certain
random walks
on
{\mathbb{X}}
.

Specifically, we fix a finitely-supported probability vector
\mu
on
{\mathbb{X}}
, and we consider the continuous-time Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
whose state space is
{\mathbb{X}}
, whose initial state is
X_{0}=\circ
, and whose generator acts on bounded functions
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall x\in{\mathbb{X}},\qquad(Lf)(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\left(f(xz)-f(x)\right).

(7)

In more concrete terms, the current position gets right-multiplied by a random sample from
\mu
at rate one. Beyond the finiteness of its support and the (harmless) normalization, the only structural assumptions that we shall impose on
\mu
are the following:

Assumption 1

(Reversibility and conjugacy invariance)
.

The measure
\mu
satisfies:

A1.

Conjugacy invariance:
\mu(xzx^{-1})=\mu(z)
, for all
x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}
.

A2.

Reversibility:
\mu(z)=\mu(z^{-1})
, for all
z\in{\mathbb{X}}
.

Note that the first condition is trivially satisfied when the group
{\mathbb{X}}
is Abelian. An emblematic non-Abelian example is of course the
transposition walk
, where
{\mathbb{X}}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is the symmetric group of order
n
and
\mu
the uniform measure on the set of transpositions.

In general, the value of
\mu(\circ)
is irrelevant in the definition (
7
), so we may safely assume that
\mu(\circ)=0
. We can then write

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}\left(\delta_{e_{i}}+\delta_{e_{i}^{-1}}\right),

(8)

for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, some rates
\mu_{1},\ldots\mu_{d}>0
and some group elements
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
, henceforth referred to as the
generators
of the random walk. To formulate our main result, we introduce the function
\mathcal{V}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle t\,\log^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right).

(9)

This function is easily seen to be continuously increasing from
\mathcal{V}(0)=0
to
\mathcal{V}(+\infty)=1
.

Theorem 1

(Main estimate)
.

There exists a universal constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{V}\left(\mu_{i}t\right).

Moreover, this estimate is sharp in the sense that the converse inequality holds (with a different value of
c
) when
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
which moves by
\pm 1
unit in the
i
-th direction at rate
\mu_{i}
, independently for each
1\leq i\leq d
.

Since the function
\mathcal{V}
is bounded, our result readily leads to a discrete analogue of the Bobkov-Madiman estimate (
5
), with the role of the dimension being naturally played by the number of generators of the walk:

Corollary 1

(Discrete analogue of (
5
))
.

There exists a universal constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle cd.

Remark 1

(Explicit values)
.

We will see in Section
2.3
that Theorem
1
holds with
c=43
while Corollary
1
holds with
c=16
. We have not tried to optimize those values.

Here again, the result is sharp except for the value of
c
, since the varentropy of the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is asymptotic to
d/2
in the large-time limit. Beyond this pleasant consequence, Theorem
1
has an interesting interpretation: it asserts that the varentropy of a reversible, conjugacy-invariant random walk is (up to universal multiplicative constants), at most the varentropy of the free Abelian random walk with the same rates. In view of the product structure of the latter, this principle can be seen as an
approximate tensorization
property for varentropy, reminiscent of the one that has been developed for the entropy of various spin systems with weak dependencies (see the recent work
[
8
]
and the references therein). Extending this approximate tensorization of varentropy beyond the present group-theoretical setup would lead to a substantial progress on our understanding of the cutoff phenomenon.

1.3
Relation to the cutoff phenomenon

Discovered four decades ago in the context of card shuffling
[
2
,
1
,
13
]
, the cutoff phenomenon is an abrupt transition from out of equilibrium to equilibrium undergone by certain Markov processes in the limit where the size of the state space tends to infinity: instead of decaying gradually over time, their distance to equilibrium remains close to the maximal value for a while and suddenly drops to zero as the time parameter reaches a critical threshold. Despite the accumulation of many examples, this phenomenon is still far from being understood, and identifying the general conditions that trigger it has become one of the biggest challenges in the quantitative analysis of finite Markov chains. Very recently, one of the authors provided a sharp criterion for the occurrence of a cutoff
[
28
,
29
]
, based on varentropy. In a nutshell, the latter reads

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}}}{{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{rel}}}}

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle 1+\sqrt{{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}}})},

(10)

where
{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}}
and
{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{rel}}}
respectively denote the mixing time and relaxation time of the chain, and where the notation
a\gg b
means that the ratio
a/b
diverges as the number of states tend to infinity. We refer the unfamiliar reader to
[
28
]
for details.
The potential use of this criterion to explain and predict cutoff clearly motivates the development of a varentropy theory for Markov processes on discrete state spaces, and this is exactly the program that we initiate in the present work.

A first, naive varentropy estimate was actually obtained in
[
28
]
for non-negatively curved chains, such as our conjugacy invariant random walk: it reads

\displaystyle\forall t\geq\frac{1}{4}{\mathrm{diam}}({\mathbb{X}}),\qquad{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,t\log^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}\right\},

(11)

where
c
is a universal constant,
\mu_{\min}
the minimum non-zero entry of
\mu
, and
{\mathrm{diam}}({\mathbb{X}})
the diameter of the Cayley graph generated by the support of
\mu
. As demonstrated in
[
28
]
, this is already sufficient to imply cutoff for a number of interesting models, including the simple random walk on ‘almost-all’ Abelian Cayley graphs of logarithmic degree. Our estimate is always better than (
11
): indeed, for
t\geq\frac{1}{4}{\mathrm{diam}}({\mathbb{X}})
, it reads

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle ct\,\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\log^{2}\left\{1+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu(z)t}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle ct\log^{2}\left\{1+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}t}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle ct\log^{2}\left\{1+\sqrt{\frac{4}{\mu_{\min}{\mathrm{diam}}({\mathbb{X}})}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle ct\log^{2}\left\{1+\sqrt{\frac{4}{\mu_{\min}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}\,t\log^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}\right\},

and each of those inequalities can be arbitrary loose for certain choices of the parameters
({\mathbb{X}},\mu,t)
.
In addition, our result has a number of substantial advantages over (
11
):

1.

It is uniformly bounded in time, instead of diverging as
t\to\infty
.

2.

It also applies to infinite groups, whereas (
11
) is void when
{\mathrm{diam}}({\mathbb{X}})=\infty
.

3.

It remains valid at short times, instead of being restricted to macroscopic scales.

4.

It is not affected by unlikely jumps, as opposed ot the factor
\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}})
in (
11
).

Thus, using our main estimate instead of (
11
) immediately leads to a much broader class of random walks exhibiting cutoff. To keep the exposition simple,
we only mention one emblematic application, which does not even use the full strength of Theorem
1
but only the simpler bound appearing in Corollary
1
.

Corollary 2

(A simple new condition for cutoff)
.

For reversible, conjugacy-invariant random walks on arbitrary finite groups, the cutoff phenomenon occurs as soon as

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{mix}}}}{{\mathrm{t}_{\textsc{rel}}}}

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle\sqrt{d},

where
d
denotes the number of generators of the walk.

2
Proof of Theorem
1

The starting point of our proof is the new observation that conjugacy-invariant random walks on groups are non-negatively curved in the sense of Bakry-Émery (see Section
2.1
). By virtue of the celebrated
local Poincaré inequality
, this reduces the task of controlling the varentropy to that of estimating the expected squared gradient of the logarithmic heat kernel. To achieve the latter, we introduce and analyze a particular measure-preserving transformation on the space of trajectories, which exploits conjugacy invariance in a crucial way (see Section
2.2
). The sharpness of our estimate is finally established in Section
2.3
, through the explicit analysis of the varentropy of the free Abelian random walk.

2.1
Bakry-Émery curvature

Introduced four decades ago in the context of diffusions on manifolds
[
3
]
, the Bakry-Émery theory of curvature has quickly become one of the most powerful tools in the quantitative study of geometric, probabilistic and functional-analytical properties of Markov semi-groups. We refer the reader to the textbook
[
4
]
for a comprehensive introduction. To keep the exposition simple, we shall here restrict our attention to discrete state spaces, as considered, e.g., in the seminal papers
[
14
,
30
,
22
]
. We let
\mathcal{A}:=L^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})
denote the algebra of bounded functions on
{\mathbb{X}}
. Given a Markov generator
L\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
, we define the associated
carré du champ operator

\Gamma\colon\mathcal{A}^{2}\to\mathcal{A}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma(f,g)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[L(fg)-fLg-gLf\right].

(12)

Similarly, the
iterated carré du champ operator

\Gamma_{2}\colon\mathcal{A}^{2}\to\mathcal{A}
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[L\Gamma(f,g)-\Gamma(f,Lg)-\Gamma(g,Lf)\right].

(13)

When
g=f
, we simply write
\Gamma(f)
and
\Gamma_{2}(f)
.
By definition, the
Bakry-Émery curvature
of the generator
L
is then simply the largest number
\kappa\in[-\infty,\infty)
such that the following functional inequality is satisfied:

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{A},\qquad\Gamma_{2}(f)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\kappa\,\Gamma(f).

(14)

Over the past years, a lower bound on the Bakry-Émery curvature of Markov generators on discrete state spaces has been shown to imply an array of powerful quantitative estimates on the underlying semi-group
[
11
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
23
]
. It turns out that conjugacy-invariant random walks on groups always have non-negative Bakry-Émery curvature. Interestingly, reversibility is actually not even needed. This new result generalizes a classical one due to Klartag, Kozma, Ralli and Tetali
[
20
]
, asserting that random walks on Cayley graphs of Abelian groups have non-negative Bakry-Émery curvature.

Theorem 2

(Conjugacy-invariant random walks are non-negatively curved)
.

The generator (
7
) has non-negative Bakry-Émery curvature.

Proof.

We make two simplifying observations. The first one is that our generator
L
commutes with the shift operator
T_{x}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
defined by
(T_{x}f)(z):=f(xz)
, for any
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
. It follows that for all
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
, we also have
\Gamma(T_{x}f,T_{x}g)=T_{x}\Gamma(f,g)
and
\Gamma_{2}(T_{x}f,T_{x}g)=T_{x}\Gamma_{2}(f,g)
. Consequently, the functional inequality (
14
) needs only be verified at a single point, say the identity element
\circ
. The second observation is that the functions
Lf,\Gamma(f,g)
and
\Gamma_{2}(f,g)
remain unchanged if we add a constant to
f
or
g
. Therefore, we may restrict the proof of (
14
) to test functions
f\in\mathcal{A}
that satisfy
f(o)=0
. With those simplifications at hand, we easily compute:

\displaystyle 2L\Gamma(f)(o)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\mu(w)\left[f^{2}(zw)-2f(z)f(zw)\right];

\displaystyle 2\Gamma(f,Lf)(o)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\mu(w)f(z)\left[f(zw)-f(z)-f(w)\right].

Consequently, we see that

\displaystyle 4\Gamma_{2}(f)(o)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\mu(w)F(z,w)+2\left(\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)f(z)\right)^{2},

(15)

where we have introduced the short-hand

\displaystyle F(z,w)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle f^{2}(zw)-4f(z)f(zw)+2f^{2}(z).

It is now time to use our assumption (A1): the function

(z,w)\mapsto(w,w^{-1}zw)
is a bijection on
{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
which preserves the measure
\mu\otimes\mu
. Thus, we may use it as a change of variables in the first sum at (
15
) to replace
F(z,w)
by
F(w,w^{-1}zw)
, or even by
\frac{1}{2}\left(F(z,w)+F(w,w^{-1}zw)\right)
. But by definition of
F
, we have

\displaystyle F(w,w^{-1}zw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{2}(zw)-4f(w)f(zw)+2f^{2}(w),

so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(F(z,w)+F(w,w^{-1}zw)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{2}(zw)-2\left(f(z)+f(w)\right)f(zw)+f^{2}(w)+f^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(f(zw)-f(z)-f(w)\right)^{2}-2f(z)f(w).

Inserting this back into the above computation finally gives

\displaystyle 4\Gamma_{2}(f)(o)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\mu(w)\left(f(zw)-f(z)-f(w)\right)^{2},

which is indeed non-negative, as desired.
∎

Remark 2

(Positive curvature)
.

When the rate function
\mu
is supported on elements of order
2
, the conclusion can be improved as follows: keeping only the diagonal terms in the last sum, and writing
\mu_{\min}
for the minimum non-zero value of
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle 4\Gamma_{2}(f)(o)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\mu(w)\left(f(zw)-f(z)-f(w)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu_{\min}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\left(f(z^{2})-2f(z)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\mu_{\min}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)f^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8\mu_{\min}\Gamma(f)(o).

because
f(z^{2})=f(\circ)=0
by assumption. Thus, a conjugacy-invariant random walk generated by elements of order
2
has Bakry-Émery curvature
\kappa\geq 2\mu_{\min}
. This estimate is sharp, as can be seen by considering the classical example of simple random walk on the hypercube, or the transposition walk on permutations.

Our interest in the Bakry-Émery curvature arises from the classical fact that it implies a
local Poincaré inequality
for the Markov chain under consideration. Here, the word ‘local’ refers to the fact that the underlying measure is the law of the chain at an arbitrary time
t
, rather than the usual equilibrium measure. This result is typically only stated for a more restrictive class of test functions than the one needed here, so we prefer to include a proof for completeness. As in the introduction, we let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote a random walk on
{\mathbb{X}}
with generator (
7
), starting from the identity.

Corollary 3

(Local Poincaré estimate)
.

Fix a function
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
and a time
t\geq 0
. If
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f(X_{t})\right]<\infty
, then
{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{2}(X_{t})\right]<\infty
and we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[f\left(X_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f(X_{t})\right].

Proof.

Let us first make the stronger assumption that
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded. As usual, we let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markov semi-group generated by
L
(i.e.
P_{t}=e^{tL}
). Our starting point is the following well-known integral formula, which is easily checked by differentiating both sides w.r.t.
t
(see, e.g.
[
32
, Problem 2.12.a]
): for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-\left(P_{t}f\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{t}P_{t-s}\Gamma\left(P_{s}f\right){\rm d}s.

Now, the functional inequality (
14
) is well known to imply (in fact, to be equivalent to) the following sub-commutation property: for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\forall s\geq 0,\qquad\Gamma(P_{s}f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-2\kappa s}\Gamma f.

Inserting this back into the previous identity, we arrive at

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-\left(P_{t}f\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}2e^{-2\kappa s}{\rm d}s\right)P_{t}\Gamma f.

(16)

When
\kappa\geq 0
, the integral is at most
2t
, and evaluating the resulting inequality at our initial state
\circ
yields exactly the desired result. Now, in the general case where
f
is not necessarily bounded, we may replace it with its
[-n,n]
-truncation
f_{n}:=(f\wedge n)\vee{-n}
and apply the first part of the proof to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[f_{n}\left(X_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f_{n}(X_{t})\right].

Introducing an independent copy
Y_{t}
of
X_{t}
, this can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(f_{n}\left(X_{t}\right)-f_{n}(Y_{t})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f_{n}(X_{t})\right].

(17)

Finally, observe that the
[-n,n]
-truncation enjoys the following monotonicity property:

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{X}},\qquad\left[f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\uparrow}

\displaystyle\left[f(x)-f(y)\right]^{2}.

Thus, on both sides of (
17
), the random variable inside the expectation is almost-surely non-decreasing in
n
, and we may safely invoke monotone convergence to conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(f\left(X_{t}\right)-f(Y_{t})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f(X_{t})\right].

But the right-hand side is finite by assumption, hence so is the left-hand side. By the independence of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
, this forces
{\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}(X_{t})]<\infty
, and the result is proved.
∎

Remark 3

(Ollivier curvature)
.

Conjugacy-invariant random walks are well known to be non-negatively curved in a different sense, namely according to the Wasserstein-based definition of Ollivier
[
26
,
27
]
. This implies the weaker local Poincaré inequality

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{Var}}\left[f\left(X_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t\,\mathrm{Lip}^{2}(f),

where
\mathrm{Lip}(f):=\sup\{|f(xz)-f(x)|\colon x,z\in{\mathbb{X}},\mu(z)>0\}
. The substantial gain obtained by replacing the “worst-case” gradient bound appearing on the right-hand side by its “expected” version
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f(X_{t})\right]
will turn out to be crucial for our purpose.

Remark 4

(Positive curvature)
.

When the Bakry-Émery curvature
\kappa
is strictly positive, we may alternatively bound the integral in (
16
) by
1/\kappa
instead of
2t
to obtain the uniform-in-time local Poincaré inequality

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{Var}}\left[f\left(X_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma f(X_{t})\right].

This applies, in particular, when
\mu
is supported on elements of order
2
as per Remark
2
. It leads to a substantial improvement of our varentropy estimate when
t
is large.

2.2
Gradient of the logarithmic heat kernel

Fix
t\geq 0
and let
f_{t}\colon x\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=x)
denote the probability mass generating function of
X_{t}
, also known as the
heat kernel
. In view of the above corollary, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma\log f_{t}(X_{t})\right].

(18)

Thus, our task now boils down to finding a sharp estimate on the expected squared gradient of the logarithmic heat kernel, and this is exactly the content of Proposition
1
below. We introduce the universal function
\mathcal{U}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log_{+}^{2}\left\{\frac{1+N_{t}}{t}\right\}\right],

(19)

where
\log_{+}(u):=\max\{\log u,0\}
is the positive part of the
\log
, and where
N_{t}
denotes a Poisson random variable with mean
t
. In the next section, this function will be bounded from above by a constant multiple of the explicit function
\mathcal{V}
defined at (
9
). Thus, our main estimate is a consequence of (
18
) and the following result.

Proposition 1

(Gradient estimate for the log-heat kernel)
.

For all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle 2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma\log f_{t}(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathcal{U}\left(\mu(z)t\right).

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of this proposition.
In order to estimate the term
\log\frac{f_{t}(x)}{f_{t}(xz)}
that appears when explicitating the definition of
\Gamma\log f_{t}(x)
, we establish a correspondence between random-walk trajectories that end up at
x
and random-walk trajectories that end up at
xz
, and use conjugacy-invariance to control the incurred change of measure. This is the content of the following technical lemma, which requires a bit of notation. Given
z\in{\mathbb{X}}
and a sequence
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
, we introduce the key quantity

\displaystyle\ell_{z}(w)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{w_{j}=(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{n})z(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}}.

(20)

Also, we let
{\mathrm{val}}(w):=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
denote the evaluation of the word
w
in the group.

Lemma 1

(Trajectorial correspondence)
.

Fix
z\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1]\to{\mathbb{X}}^{n+1}
such that for all
(w,i)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1]
,

(i)

{\mathrm{val}}(\phi(w,i))={\mathrm{val}}(w)z
;

(ii)

\mu^{\otimes{(n+1)}}(\phi(w,i))=\mu^{\otimes n}(w)\mu(z)
;

(iii)

|\phi^{-1}(\phi(w,i))|=1+\ell_{z}(w)
.

(iv)

A sequence
\eta\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n+1}
is in the image of
\phi
if and only if
\ell_{z}(\eta)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1]\to{\mathbb{X}}^{n+1}
be defined as follows: for any
(w,i)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1]
,

\displaystyle\phi(w,i)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},\xi,w_{i},\ldots,w_{n}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\textrm{ with }\xi

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(w_{i}w_{i+1}\cdots w_{n})z(w_{i}w_{i+1}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}.

(22)

In words, the sequence
\phi(w,i)
is obtained from the sequence
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
by inserting a certain element
\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}
at the
i-
th position,
\xi
being chosen precisely so that Property (i) is satisfied. The second property is clear, since we have

\displaystyle\mu^{\otimes(n+1)}(\phi(w,i))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu^{\otimes n}(w)\mu(\xi)\ =\ \mu^{\otimes n}(w)\mu(z),

thanks to Assumption A1. Finally, note that by construction, the pre-image
\phi^{-1}(\eta)
of a given sequence
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n+1})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n+1}
is exactly the set of pairs of the form

\left((\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{j-1},\eta_{j+1},\ldots,\eta_{n+1}),j\right),

where
j\in[n+1]
satisfies the constraint

\displaystyle\eta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta_{j+1}\cdots\eta_{n+1})z(\eta_{j+1}\cdots\eta_{n+1})^{-1}.

(23)

This readily implies (iv). Moreover, when we specialize this to the sequence
\eta:=\phi(w,i)
defined at (
21
), the constraint (
23
) rewrites as follows:

•

either
j=i
;

•

or
j>i
and
w_{j-1}=(w_{j}\cdots w_{n})z(w_{j}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}
;

•

or
j<i
and
w_{j}=(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{i-1}\xi w_{i}\cdots w_{n})z(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{i-1}\xi w_{i}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}
.

But thanks to the definition of
\xi
at (
22
), the condition in the case
j<i
simplifies to
w_{j}=(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{n})z(w_{j+1}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}
, and (iii) follows.
∎

Let us now exploit this trajectorial correspondence to relate the probabilities of the two events
\{X_{t}=x\}
and
\{X_{t}=xz\}
. We use the random walk representation

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{N_{t}},

(24)

where
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
denotes a sequence of i.i.d. samples from
\mu
, and
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a unit-rate Poisson point process, independent of
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. With this data at hand, we define

\displaystyle N_{t,z}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{N_{t}}),

(25)

and will later show that this is a Poisson random variable with mean
t\mu(z)
(Lemma
3
). The following identity relates the events
\{X_{t}=x\}
and
\{X_{t}=xz\}
, as promised.

Lemma 2

(Change-of-measure formula)
.

For any
t>0
and any
x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz,N_{t,z}\neq 0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\mu(z){\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+N_{t,z}}{\bf 1}_{(X_{t}=x)}\right].

Proof.

Since
N_{t,z}\leq N_{t}
, we can safely write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz,N_{t,z}\neq 0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz,N_{t,z}\neq 0,N_{t}=n+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(N_{t}=n+1){\mathbb{P}}(W_{1}\cdots W_{n+1}=xz,\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n+1})\neq 0).

Now, for each
n\geq 0
, we may invoke Lemma
1
to write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1}\cdots W_{n+1}=xz,\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n+1})\neq 0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\phi({\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1])}\mu^{\otimes{(n+1)}}(\eta){\bf 1}_{{\mathrm{val}}(\eta)=xz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(w,i)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times[n+1]}\frac{\mu^{\otimes{(n+1)}}(\phi(w,i))}{|\phi^{-1}(\phi(w,i))|}{\bf 1}_{{\mathrm{val}}(\phi(w,i))=xz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)\mu(z)\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}\frac{\mu^{\otimes{(n)}}(w)}{1+\ell_{z}(w)}{\bf 1}_{{\mathrm{val}}(w)=x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)\mu(z){\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})}{\bf 1}_{W_{1}\cdots W_{n}=x}\right].

Inserting this back into the previous computation and using the Poisson identity
(n+1){\mathbb{P}}(N_{t}=n+1)=t{\mathbb{P}}(N_{t}=n)
, we arrive at

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz,N_{t,z}\neq 0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\mu(z)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(N_{t}=n){\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})}{\bf 1}_{W_{1}\cdots W_{n}=x}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\mu(z){\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+N_{t,z}}{\bf 1}_{X_{t}=x}\right],

which concludes the proof of the identity.
∎

As announced earlier, the key quantity
N_{t,z}
featuring in the above identity happens to have a remarkable distribution, thanks to conjugacy invariance again.

Lemma 3

(Poisson law)
.

N_{t,z}
is a Poisson random variable with mean
t\mu(z)
.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and, for each
j\in[n]
, consider the event

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{W_{j}=(W_{j+1}\cdots W_{n})z(W_{j+1}\cdots W_{n})^{-1}\right\}.

Clearly,
A_{j}
belongs to the
\sigma-
field
\mathcal{F}_{j}:=\sigma(W_{j},\ldots W_{n})
, and moreover

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{j+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(W_{j+1}\cdots W_{n}zW_{n}^{-1}\cdots W_{j+1}^{-1}\right)\ =\ \mu(z),

(26)

by conjugacy invariance. This shows that the events
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
are independent, each having probability
\mu(z)
. Consequently, the random variable

\displaystyle\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{A_{j}},

has a Binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
\mu(z)
. Since
N_{t}
is independent of
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we deduce that the conditional law of
N_{t,z}:=\ell_{z}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{N_{t}})
given
N_{t}
is a Binomial distribution with parameters
N_{t}
and
\mu(z)
. But
N_{t}
itself is a Poisson random variable with mean
t
, so the result follows from the Poisson thinning theorem.
∎

We can finally prove our main gradient estimate.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma\log f_{t}(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)f_{t}(x)\log^{2}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(x)}{f_{t}(xz)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)\left(f_{t}(x)\vee f_{t}(xz)\right)\log^{2}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(x)}{f_{t}(xz)}\right\}.

But, by reversibility (Assumption A2), the bijective change of variables
(x,z)\mapsto(xz,z^{-1})

leaves the summand unchanged, while interchanging the roles of
x
and
xz
. We may exploit this symmetry to restrict the entire sum to those pairs
(x,z)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
satisfying
f_{t}(x)\geq f_{t}(xz)
, and multiply the result by
2
. This leads precisely to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma\log f_{t}(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mu(z)f_{t}(x)\log^{2}_{+}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(x)}{f_{t}(xz)}\right\}.

Now, fix
x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}
. Since
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz,N_{t,z}\neq 0)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}=xz)=f_{t}(xz)
, Lemma
2
provides the following estimate:

\displaystyle\frac{f_{t}(xz)}{f_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{t\mu(z)}{1+N_{t,z}}\right|{X_{t}=x}\right].

But the function
u\mapsto\log_{+}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{u}\right\}
is decreasing and convex, so Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\log^{2}_{+}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(x)}{f_{t}(xz)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\log^{2}_{+}\left\{\frac{1+N_{t,z}}{t\mu(z)}\right\}\right|X_{t}=x\right].

Inserting this back into the previous computation concludes the proof, since
N_{t,z}
has the same law as
N_{t\mu(z)}
by Lemma
3
.
∎

2.3
Explicit estimates

In view of (
18
) and Proposition
1
, only two tasks remain in order to complete the proof of our main theorem.

1.

Proving that the function
\mathcal{U}
defined at (
19
) is bounded by a constant multiple of the explicit function
\mathcal{V}
. This is the content of Lemma
4
below.

2.

Proving that conversely,
\mathcal{V}
is at most a constant multiple of the varentropy in the special case where
{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbb{Z}
and
\mu=\frac{1}{2}{\bf 1}_{\{-1,+1\}}
. This is Lemma
5
below.

Lemma 4

(Explicit estimate on
\mathcal{U}
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2+\frac{2}{t}&\textrm{ for all }t\geq 0;\\
2t\log^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)&\textrm{ for all }t\in[0,e^{-1}].\end{array}\right.

In particular, we have the uniform bounds
\mathcal{U}\leq 8
and
\mathcal{U}\leq 21.5\,\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Using the classical bound
\log u\leq u-1
in the definition of
\mathcal{U}
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(1+N_{t}-t\right)_{+}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(1+N_{t}-t\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2+\frac{2}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2+2e}{\mathcal{V}(\frac{1}{e})}\mathcal{V}(t)

where the last inequality is valid only when
t\geq\frac{1}{e}
, and uses the fact that
\mathcal{V}
is increasing. For
t\leq\frac{1}{e}
, we instead use the fact that
\log^{2}_{+}
is concave on
[e,\infty)
to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2t\log^{2}_{+}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1+N_{t}}{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2t\log^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8\mathcal{V}(t),

where the last line uses
1+\frac{1}{t}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{2}
. Combining those two estimates, we see that
\mathcal{U}\leq\max\left\{2+2e,8\right\}=8
, and that
\mathcal{U}\leq c\,\mathcal{V}
, where
c=\max\left\{\frac{2+2e}{\mathcal{V}(1/e)},8\right\}\approx 21.305
.
∎

Lemma 5

(Sharpness)
.

In the special case where
{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbb{Z}
and
\mu=\frac{1}{2}{\bf 1}_{\{-1,+1\}}
, we have

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\mathcal{V}(t),

for some universal constant
c>0
.

Proof.

The functions
t\mapsto{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})
and
t\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t)
are both continuous and positive on
(0,\infty)
, so their ratio is bounded away from
0
on every compact subset of
(0,\infty)
. Thus, we only need to investigate the asymptotics as
t\to 0
and as
t\to\infty
. To this end, we introduce an independent copy
(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and write

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{2}_{+}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(X_{t})}{f_{t}(\widetilde{X}_{t})}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{2}_{+}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(X_{t})}{f_{t}(\widetilde{X}_{t})}\right\}{\bf 1}_{X_{t}=0,\widetilde{X}_{t}\in\{-1,1\}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2f_{t}(1)f_{t}(0)\log^{2}_{+}\frac{f_{t}(0)}{f_{t}(1)}.

Now as
t\to 0
, we have
f_{t}(0)=1-o(1)
and
f_{t}(1)=t/2+o(t)
, so we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-o(1))t\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right),

which is of the same order of magnitude as
\mathcal{V}(t)
. On the other hand, in the limit
t\to\infty
, the Local Central Limit Theorem easily implies the convergence

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}f_{t}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{d}

\displaystyle g(B),

where
B
is a standard Gaussian random variable and
g
its density. Since
\widetilde{X}_{t}
is an independent copy of
X_{t}
, we actually have the joint convergence

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{t}f_{t}(X_{t}),\sqrt{t}f_{t}(\widetilde{X}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{d}

\displaystyle\left(g(B),g(\widetilde{B})\right),

with
\widetilde{B}
an independent copy of
B
.
But
(u,v)\mapsto\log_{+}^{2}\frac{u}{v}
is continuous on
(0,\infty)^{2}
, so

\displaystyle\log_{+}^{2}\left\{\frac{f_{t}(X_{t})}{f_{t}(\widetilde{X}_{t})}\right\}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{d}

\displaystyle\log_{+}^{2}\left\{\frac{g(B)}{g(\widetilde{B})}\right\},

Taking expectations and invoking Fatou’s Lemma, we conclude that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Varent}}(B).

The right-hand side is well known and easily seen to be equal to
1/2
. Since
\mathcal{V}(t)\to 1
as
t\to\infty
, the ratio
{\mathrm{Varent}}(X_{t})/\mathcal{V}(t)
does indeed remain bounded away from
0
as
t\to\infty
, and the proof is complete.
∎
