Lifshitz Quantum Mechanics and Anisotropic Josephson Junction
Chong-Sun Chu 1,2,3, Alfian Gunawan 1

Abstract
We consider quantum mechanics in spacetime with time anisotropy. Such Lifshitz quantum mechanics is characterized by a kinetic term with fractional derivatives. We show that, contrary to popular belief, local conservation of probability is respected when the probability current is properly identified. As an application we consider a Josephson Junction with an anisotropic insulating layer. We show that anisotropy affects the tunneling rate and can greatly enhance the working of Josephson Junction.



I
Introduction

Many interesting systems in physics are anisotropic.
For example, liquid crystal, magnetic materials and critical systems
in condensed matter physics are ubiquitously anisotropic.
The cosmic microwave background
also display traces of anisotropy that are thought to have originated from
quantum fluctuations occurred during inflation.
Often the fundamental physical law is isotropic but the
presence of a background leads to anisotropy.
An interesting instant of anisotropy is
when physics is invariant under the Lifshitz scaling transformation

t\rightarrow\lambda^{z}t,\quad x^{i}\rightarrow\lambda x^{i},\quad\lambda>0.

(I.1)

Here spatial isotropy is maintained while time is anisotropic for
an anisotropy exponent
z\neq 1
.
Such Lifshitz scaling symmetry occurs
naturally as symmetry of the critical point
of renormalization group (RG) flow for anisotropic systems.
Lifshitz scale invariant system can be recognized easily by the dispersion
relation of its massless modes:

\omega\sim k^{z},

(I.2)

where one can extract not only the anisotropy exponent
z

but also some mass scale
that is associated with the anisotropy. As the only possible
form that is compatible with the scaling symmetry (
I.1
),
(
I.2
) is independent of the
underlying origin of the anisotropy.
The symmetry for
z=2

was first proposed by Lifshitz
lif
to describe the
tricritical point of liquid helium
Hornreich:1975zz
.
The dispersion relation (
I.2
) is also featured
in modified gravity scenario with a ghost condensate

Arkani-Hamed:2003pdi
. In condensed matter physics,
a well known example
is the Rokhsar-Kivelson (RK) Quantum dimer
model
PhysRevLett.61.2376
;
Henley_2004

where a
continuum
z=2
Lifshitz scalar field theory emerges at the critical point.

The
z=2
anisotropic scaling symmetry (
I.1
)
has been intensively studied from the field theory point of view

Ardonne_2004
;
Fradkin:2006mb
;
Fradkin:2009dus
;
PhysRevB.80.184421
;
Hsu:2008af
;
Hsu:2010ag
;
Oshikawa:2010kv
;
PhysRevLett.107.020402
;
Zhou:2016ykv
;
MohammadiMozaffar:2017nri
;
Berthiere:2019lks
;
Angel-Ramelli:2019nji
;
Berthiere:2019lks
;
Angel-Ramelli:2020wfo
;
Berthiere:2023bwn
.
For example, it is known that
Lifshitz scalar field theory admits ground state called the
RK vacuum, with the properties that it
is local and is given by a superposition of
quantum states determined by some classical probability distribution

PhysRevLett.61.2376
.
The point
z=2
is
somewhat special since the Lifshitz scaling transformation (
I.1
)
combines with the Galilean boost to form
the Schrodinger group only for this value of
z
.
Study of field theory with general
z
was initiated in

Basak:2023otu
where with the
help of fractional calculus. It was shown that,
independent of whether there is an enlarged Schrodinger symmetry or not,
the ground state of the Lifshitz scalar field
always takes the form of RK.
The entropic properties of the RK vacuum
were also studied, both in field theory and by holography,
in
Basak:2023otu
.

Clearly, the physics of anisotropy is a subject of its own interest that
goes beyond quantum field theory. With general applicability in mind,
we explore in this paper
the physical effect of anisotropy in quantum mechanics. In particular
we consider a kind
of time anisotropy of the background space time specified by (
I.1
).
With the dispersion relation fixed to be of the form (
I.1
),
quantum mechanics of free particle
in the anisotropic spacetime reads

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi=H_{0}\psi,\quad H_{0}=-\frac{\hbar^{z}}{M^{z-2}}\frac{\Delta_{z}}{2m}

(I.3)

where
H_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian,
m
is the mass of the particle
and
M
is a mass scale associated with the anisotropy.
The fractional Laplacian
\Delta_{z}

of order
z
is
defined by its action on the basis of exponential functions by

\displaystyle\triangle_{z}\,e^{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}=({\bf k}^{2})^{z/2}e^{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(I.4)

The free (
I.3
) is Lifshitz scale invariant.
One may also add a potential and consider the

Lifshitz quantum mechanics
defined by

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi=H\psi,\quad H=-\frac{\hbar^{z}}{M^{z-2}}\frac{\Delta_{z}}{2m}+V.

(I.5)

Note that in general the scaling symmetry is broken except for
some special form of the potential,
e.g.
V\sim 1/|x|^{z}
,
in which case we obtain the
Lifshitz invariant quantum mechanics.
In analogous to conformal quantum mechanics
deAlfaro:1976vlx
,
it may be interesting to study further this kind of
Lifshitz invariant quantum mechanics.

Here is a summary of the paper.
In section 2, we consider the Lifshitz quantum mechanics in general.
There is a belief in the literature that local conservation
of probability is violated by a source term in Lifshitz quantum mechanics.
It turns out it is in fact the probability current
that is modified by anisotropy. And once the correct probability current is
employed, the local conservation of probability is respected.
In section 3, we consider an anisotropic
Josephson Junction that is made up of an anisotropic insulating layer
sandwiched by two normal superconducting materials. We
show that the critical current can be greatly enhanced
by anisotropy. We argue that anisotropic Josephson Junction has great
potential in quantum computing.

II
Lifshitz Quantum Mechanics

Consider Lifshitz quantum mechanics (
I.5
).
This kind of quantum mechanics is also refereed to as fractional
quantum mechanics, see for example
Laskin:2010ry

for some discussion.
It is easy to show that the Lifshitz
quantum mechanics (
I.5
) is unitary and
admits the local conservation law

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=0,

(II.1)

where the probability density takes the usual form

\rho=\psi^{*}\psi
, while the
probability current density is modified by anisotropy.
As the fractional derivative is defined using
the basis of exponential functions,
\boldsymbol{J}
can be most
easily obtained in the momentum space and we have

\boldsymbol{J}=-\frac{1}{2mM^{z-2}}\int\frac{d^{D}{p}\,d^{D}{q}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{D}}\,e^{i({\bf p}-{\bf q})\cdot{\bf x}/\hbar}\varphi^{*}({\bf q},t)\varphi({\bf p},t)\frac{(-{\bf q}^{2})^{z/2}-(-{\bf p}^{2})^{z/2}}{{\bf q}^{2}-{\bf p}^{2}}({\bf q}+{\bf p}),

(II.2)

where

\varphi({\bf p},t)
is the momentum space wave function
defined by the Fourier transform

\psi({\bf x},t)=\int\frac{d^{D}p}{(2\pi\hbar)^{D/2}}\,e^{i{\bf p}\cdot{\bf x}/\hbar}\varphi({\bf p},t).

(II.3)

This result can be obtained from the Noether theorem
associated with the phase symmetry

\psi\to\psi e^{i\theta}

of quantum mechanics
or by the direct manipulation of the wave equation (
I.5
).
Equivalently in coordinate space, it is

\mathbf{J}=\lambda_{M}^{z-2}\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\frac{\nabla_{x}-\nabla_{x^{\prime}}}{\nabla_{x}^{2}-\nabla_{x^{\prime}}^{2}}\left[(\nabla_{x}^{2})^{z/2}-(\nabla_{x^{\prime}}^{2})^{z/2}\right]\psi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t)\psi(\mathbf{x},t)\Big|_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}},

(II.4)

where

\lambda_{M}:=\hbar/M
is a length scale associated with the anisotropy.

We remark that in the form (
II.2
), it is easy to see
that instead of using
the kernel
\frac{{\bf q}+{\bf p}}{{\bf q}^{2}-{\bf p}^{2}}
in (
II.2
),
one may also use
any vector
\bm{\beta}
that satisfies

\bm{\beta}\cdot({\bf q}-{\bf p})=1

(II.5)

to produce a
\boldsymbol{J}
that observes the conservation law.
For example,
\bm{\beta}=\frac{{\bf q}-{\bf p}}{|{\bf q}-{\bf p}|^{2}}
is good too.
To see this, suppose

\bm{\beta}_{2},\bm{\beta}_{1}

both satisfy (
II.5
), then

(\bm{\beta}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{1})
is perpendicular to

({\bf q}-{\bf p})
, i.e.

\bm{\beta}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{1}=({\bf q}-{\bf p})\times{\bf s}
for some
{\bf s}
. This implies that
the
corresponding
\boldsymbol{J}
’s differ from each other by a curl term, and hence both
satisfy the same conservation law.
This kind of
ambiguity is always there even for the standard quantum
mechanics although it is not usually mentioned.
Like the Poynting vector, additional input from the physical setting such as
symmetry, boundary condition
or a good understanding of the underlying dynamics, is
usually needed to fix the choice of
\boldsymbol{J}
properly. This
is the case for the example that we will consider below.
We remark also that the local conservation of probability
in fractional quantum mechanics has been discussed
in
Tayurskii2012
;
Wei2016
;
Laskin2016

where a source term has been found, and
which has led to the confusion of non-conservation
of local probability. As we showed above,
the probability current is indeed modified by anisotropy
and local conservation of probability is observed in fractional
quantum mechanics. This conclusion is important
as we will use below the local conservation of probability
to derive a statement
about the macroscopic electric current in the Josephson Junction system.

III
Anisotropic Josephson Junction

As an example of how anisotropy may lead to interesting quantum physics,
we consider tunneling through an anisotropic region. A prominent
example where the tunneling effect has a macroscopic manifestation is the
Josephson effect where cooper pairs of electrons can flow between two
superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier.

Consider a SIS type
Josephson Junction that is made up of two superconductors separated by a thin
insulator. The two superconductors are assumed to be made of
ordinary isotropic materials while the insulator is made of
an anisotropic material.
The latter can be achieved by material engineering such that
the dispersion relation (
I.2
) arises and the insulator
behaves effectively as an anisotropic material.
In quantum mechanics, the superconductors have Landau-Ginzburg order parameters

\psi_{i}=\sqrt{n_{i}}e^{{\rm i}\theta_{i}},\quad i=1,2

(III.1)

that can be interpreted as the wave functions of the Cooper pairs in the two
superconducting regions. See figure 1.

Figure 1:
Two superconductors separated by a thin
insulator

Suppose the Cooper pairs have typical energy
E

in the regions 1, 2 and the insulator is represented as a potential
barrier
V_{0}>E
(see figure 2). Then the wave equation inside the insulator,

\lambda_{M}^{z-2}\frac{\hbar^{2}\Delta_{z}\psi}{2m}=(V_{0}-E)\psi

(III.2)

has solution

\psi=C_{1}\cosh\frac{x}{\zeta}+C_{2}\sinh\frac{x}{\zeta},

(III.3)

where

C_{1},C_{2}
are arbitrary constants and

\zeta:=\zeta_{0}\left(\frac{\zeta_{0}}{\lambda_{M}}\right)^{2/z-1},\quad\mbox{with}\;\;\zeta_{0}:=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m(V_{0}-E)}}.

(III.4)

Here
\zeta
is the tunneling penetration length
and
\zeta_{0}
is the tunneling penetration length in the
undeformed
z=2
(isotropic) case. Note that the penetration length is modified
by anisotropy through the presence of the anisotropy
length scale
\lambda_{M}
and anisotropy index
z
. Next let us determine
the tunneling current.

Figure 2:
Tunneling through a potential

Note that here we have assumed the Junction to be uniform in the transverse
directions and so we have effectively a one spatial dimension system.
In this case there is no room for any curl term and the
current is uniquely defined by (
II.4
). We obtain

J=\frac{z\zeta}{2\zeta_{0}}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{m\zeta_{0}}{\rm Im}(C_{1}^{*}C_{2}).

(III.5)

The coefficients
C_{1},C_{2}
can be determined by imposing the boundary
conditions at
x=\pm a
:

\psi(-a)=\sqrt{n_{1}}e^{{\rm i}\theta_{1}},\quad\psi(a)=\sqrt{n_{2}}e^{{\rm i}\theta_{2}}.

(III.6)

Taking into account the Cooper pair has charge
q=2e
,
we obtain the first Josephson equation for the tunneling current

J=J_{c}\sin\varphi,\quad\varphi=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}.

(III.7)

Here the critical current
J_{c}
is given by

J_{c}=\gamma J_{c0}

(III.8)

with

J_{c0}=\frac{q\hbar\sqrt{n_{1}n_{2}}}{m\zeta_{0}}\frac{1}{\sinh 2a/\zeta_{0}},\quad\gamma=\frac{z\zeta}{2\zeta_{0}}\frac{\sinh 2a/\zeta_{0}}{\sinh 2a/\zeta},

(III.9)

where
J_{c0}
is the critical current in the isotropic case and
\gamma
is an
modification factor due to anisotropy.
If we consider the application of
a voltage
V
across the Josephson Junction, then the phase becomes time
dependent and we have the second Josephson equation,

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=\frac{q}{\hbar}V.

(III.10)

Note that this result is unmodified by anisotropy.
The derivation is the same as usual. In fact, due to gauge invariance,
the wavefunction in
the superconductor is modified to have a phase factor

\psi\to\psi\exp\left(\frac{iq}{\hbar c}\int{\bf A}\cdot d{\bf x}\right)

(III.11)

when an electromagnetic field is introduced.
This results in a modification to the order parameter

\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}=(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})_{0}+\frac{q}{\hbar c}\int_{1}^{2}{\bf A}\cdot d{\bf x},

(III.12)

where the subscript “0” denotes the quantity before the electric field
is introduced.
This leads immediately to (
III.10
).

Note that in the usual isotropic case,
the critical current
J_{c0}
is exponentially suppressed by
a factor of

e^{-2a/\zeta}
since the Cooper pairs cannot tunnel
deep enough into the insulating region. However this may change in the
anisotropic situation. In fact, the Josephson current can be enhanced
(
\gamma>1
) if the penetration length is made bigger (
\zeta>\zeta_{0}
).
This happens if

z<2
and
\lambda_{M}<\zeta_{0}
or
z>2
and
\lambda_{M}>\zeta_{0}
. Thus
an anisotropic material has the potential of enhancing the working
of Josephson Junction. In particular, if the anisotropic
insulating material can be
engineered such that the penetration length
\zeta
get to become
of the same order as
a
,
then tunneling can become
much more effective and
J_{c}
may be greatly enhanced.
As illustration, let us consider an isotropic sample with

\zeta_{0}/2a=1/10
, in this
case the critical current is suppressed with a factor of

\sim e^{-10}\sim 10^{-5}
. Introducing anisotropy, for example with

z=0.8
and
\zeta_{0}/\lambda_{M}=10
, we can
have a huge amplification factor
\gamma\sim 10^{5}
; or with

z=5
and
\zeta_{0}/\lambda_{M}=1/10
, we have

\gamma\sim 10^{4}
. We remark that
the dispersion relation (
I.2
) is acausal for
z<1
due to the
existence of superluminal modes
Koroteev:2011zz
and the violation of
the null energy conditions of the holographic dual
Hoyos:2010at
.
Therefore a junction with
z\geq 1
may be more physical.
It is interesting to verify this prediction of enhanced tunneling
experimentally.

The amplification of critical current due to time anisotropy
may be useful for improving the performance of Josephson Junction
in quantum computing.
Josephson Junction also found useful application in
digital circuits as switching elements due to it’s high
switching speed and low power dissipation
Mukhanov2019
.
Some characteristic time constants in
Josephson Junction depends on the critical current, an
anisotropic Junction can have time constants that are
more useful for the switching purpose. Given the wide variety of
applications of Josephson Junction
Orlando:1991fas
,
anisotropic Josephson Junction
appears to be an interesting topic that deserves further study.
