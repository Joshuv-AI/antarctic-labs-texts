Engineering endoplasmic reticulum targeted metal–polyphenol curcumin nanomicelles for melanoma therapy
Authors: Yixun Zhang, Xuehua Wang, Hongli Zhang, Xin Zhou, Huichan He, Fen Zou, Yangjia Zhuo, Zhouda Cai, Qianfeng Xu, Wenjie Xie, Jundong Lin, Muqi Chen, Wenlian Zheng, Jianwei Chen, Jinsheng Liu, Huikang Yang, Weide Zhong, Ronghua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelanoma has a high incidence and mortality, and current therapies are limited. Here, we developed TCPC, an ER-targeted nanomedicine guided by omics analysis. It consists of curcumin-loaded PEG–PCL nanomicelles coated with a Tannic acid–Cu metal–polyphenol network. Guided by clinical transcriptome mining, we identified ER-stress vulnerabilities to inform material design. RNA-seq and imaging confirmed that TCPC preferentially accumulates in melanoma cells and localizes to the ER. TCPC markedly elevates CHOP expression, which triggers ER Ca²⁺ release, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, and apoptosis. Functionally, TCPC significantly suppresses cell viability, migration, and invasion in vitro. In melanoma xenografts, systemic TCPC treatment slows tumor growth, enhances intratumoral CHOP and Cleaved caspase-3, and induces apoptosis without systemic toxicity. Moreover, pharmacogenomic analyses revealed that CHOP upregulation correlates with increased sensitivity to several agents, and molecular docking highlighted Irinotecan and JQ1 as potential synergistic partners with TCPC. Collectively, this work demonstrates that nanomedicine guided by omics can couple organelle-specific delivery with ER-stress amplification to achieve potent antitumor efficacy. Overall, this work establishes an omics-guided materials strategy that integrates organelle-targeted delivery with ER-stress amplification for effective melanoma therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03941-7.Keywords: Endoplasmic reticulum stress, Melanoma therapy, Curcumin nanomicelle

Introduction

Melanoma is one of the most aggressive forms of skin cancer, with rapidly rising incidence [1–5].Treatment has advanced with surgery, targeted inhibitors, and immune-checkpoint blockade. However, patient outcomes are still poor. Frequent recurrence and drug resistance are the main reasons [6–10]. These challenges underscore the need for new therapeutic strategies that directly target melanoma vulnerabilities.

Growing evidence indicates that endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the unfolded‑protein response are central drivers of melanoma progression and therapeutic escape [11–16]. The ER orchestrates protein folding, Ca²⁺ storage, and redox homeostasis; persistent perturbation of these processes can activate pro‑apoptotic pathways. Accordingly, pharmacological reinforcement of ER stress has emerged as a promising anticancer approach [17–21]. Several proof-of-concept studies show that genetic or chemical restoration of ER dysfunction can suppress tumor growth and re-sensitize resistant lesions [22, 23], while the ER‑stress–SEC23A‑autophagy negative‑feedback loop has been identified as a key determinant of stress tolerance in gastric cancer [24].

Copper (Cu) biology offers a complementary lever to aggravate ER dysfunction. Beyond its canonical redox roles, Cu has recently been implicated in the regulated cell‑death program of cuproptosis [25, 26]. In renal cancer, cuprous‑oxide nanoparticles rewired intracellular Cu trafficking, provoked ER stress, and overcame sunitinib resistance [27]. Moreover, a hollow Ca/Cu bimetallic nanoplatform was shown to trigger cascade reinforcement of ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, producing cuproptosis, paraptosis, and apoptosis synergy in solid‑tumor models [28]. These studies collectively encourage the development of Cu‑enabled systems that target the ER for multi‑modal melanoma therapy.

Curcumin (Cur) is a pleiotropic phytochemical with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, including the inhibition of tumor proliferation and metastasis [29–33]. However, its clinical translation is hampered by poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, and lack of organelle specificity [34–36]. Metal–polyphenol networks (MPNs), formed by metal ions such as Cu²⁺ and polyphenolic ligands like tannic acid, offer a versatile platform for drug encapsulation and subcellular targeting due to their pH-responsive stability, intrinsic bioactivity, and tunable surface chemistry [37–39].

In this work, we rationally designed an ER-targeted nanotherapeutic, TA-Cu@PEG-PCL@Cur (TCPC). It consists of curcumin-loaded PEG-PCL micelles coated with a tannic acid–copper (TA-Cu) shell. Transcriptomic interrogation of The Cancer Genome Atlas melanoma cohort revealed that high ER‑stress gene‑signature scores correlate with poor overall survival, substantiating the clinical relevance of our strategy. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that TCPC selectively accumulates in melanoma cells, amplifies ER stress–C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) signaling, and elicits potent antitumor efficacy with minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. 1). Our findings establish a mechanistically informed, clinically motivated nanoplatform that harnesses Cu‑induced sustained ER stress to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

The part materials employed in this study were derived from those reported in our previous work [47, 48] CuSO4, and tannic acid were purchased from Aladdin (China). The A375 and SK-MEL-28 line were obtained from Procell (Procell, China). Cells were cultured in 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Gibco, USA). The Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was provided by Beyotime (China). For Western blot analysis rabbit anti- CHOP (1:1000, Servicebio, China) were used. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) employed, rabbit anti- Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:500, Servicebio, China), rabbit anti-Ki-67 (1:500, Servicebio, China), rabbit anti- CHOP (1:500, Servicebio, China), HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (1:50, Beyotime, China), and the diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit (MeilunBio, China). All additional chemical reagents employed in this study were of analytical reagent grade and used in their as-received state.

Bioinformatic analysis of ER Stress–Related signatures in melanoma

Transcriptomic data of melanoma patients were obtained from TCGA via the UCSC Xena platform, and ER stress–related gene sets were retrieved from the MSigDB. After normalization of the raw data, DEGs were identified using the “limma” package in R with thresholds set at |log₂ fold change| ≥ 0.585 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Unsupervised consensus clustering was then applied to these ER stress–related DEGs to classify patients into distinct subgroups, followed by Kaplan–Meier survival analyses for DSS and PFI with significance assessed by log-rank tests. To further examine transcriptional heterogeneity, differential expression between subgroups was analyzed and visualized by volcano plots and heatmaps. Functional interactions of these DEGs were explored through a PPI network constructed using the STRING database.

Nanomicelle fabrication

Curcumin-loaded PEG-PCL nanomicelles (PC) were prepared via hydrophobic encapsulation. TCPC nanoparticles were obtained by coating PC with a metal–polyphenol network formed from TA and Cu. In brief, PC nanoparticles were dispersed in aqueous solution, and TA solution was added under stirring. Subsequently, Cu was introduced to form a coordination network on the PC surface. In the absence of curcumin, the same procedure produced TA-Cu@PEG-PCL nanoparticles (TC).

Drug and metal loading

Curcumin loading efficiency was determined by measuring absorbance at 425 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Copper content was quantified via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

Particle size and surface charge

Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of PC, TC, and TCPC were measured using Dynamic light scattering (DLS). Samples were sonicated for 10 min prior to measurement to ensure uniform dispersion. To assess long-term storage stability, TCPC nanoparticles were stored at 4 °C for 7 and 14 days. Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured using DLS. In addition, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to monitor the Cu oxidation state during storage.

Morphology and elemental mapping

TEM was used to assess nanoparticle morphology. Samples were deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids and air-dried before imaging. Elemental composition and distribution of C, N, O, and Cu in TCPC were analyzed using scanning TEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy.

In vitro release study of Curcumin and Cu

TCPC nanomicelles were dispersed in 30 mL of four different media: PBS at pH 7.4, PBS at pH 6.5, 10% FBS at pH 7.4, and 10% FBS at pH 6.5. Samples were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking. At selected time points (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 h), 2 mL of the release medium was collected. The same volume of fresh medium was added to maintain sink conditions. The concentrations of Curcumin and Cu were measured separately, respectively. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Cumulative release percentages were calculated.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were treated with CTRL, PC, TC, or TCPC (10 µg/mL) for 24 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded, and viability was calculated relative to controls.

Wound healing assay

For migration analysis, A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to confluence. A scratch was made with a pipette tip, and cells were cultured in serum-free medium containing the indicated formulations. Wound closure was imaged after 24 h and quantified.

Transwell assays

Cell invasion was further examined using Transwell chambers with Matrigel coating. After 24 h of different treatments with CTRL, PC, TC, or TCPC (10 µg/mL) for 24 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained, and counted under a microscope.

Transcriptomic and enrichment analysis

RNA sequencing was performed to examine transcriptomic changes induced by TCPC in A375 melanoma cells. Cells were divided into untreated control and TCPC-treated groups, with five biological replicates in each group. Total RNA was extracted, and libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome, and gene expression levels were quantified. DEGs were identified using DESeq2, with thresholds of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1. PCA was applied to visualize global transcriptomic variation. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to illustrate gene expression patterns. Functional interpretation of DEGs was performed using GO analysis, with enrichment classified into biological process, molecular function, and cellular component categories. GSEA was further conducted to assess pathway-level changes, with normalized enrichment scores and FDR used to determine significance.

Real-Time quantitative

A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were treated with PBS or TCPC (10 µg/mL) for 24 h. Then, total RNA was isolated, reverse-transcribed to cDNA, and amplified with SYBR Green chemistry. β-actin served as the internal control. Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein was quantified by BCA assay, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBST, incubated with primary antibodies against CHOP (4 °C, overnight), washed, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were developed by ECL and analyzed by densitometry, normalized to β-actin.

Immunofluorescence

A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were seeded on coverslips and treated as indicated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 1% BSA. CHOP was detected by primary antibody and Alexa Fluor–labeled secondary antibody.

Confocal microscopy

A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were seeded on coverslips, treated as indicated, and labeled with ER-Tracker (20 min), Fura-2 AM (30 min), JC-1 (30 min), or DCFH-DA (20 min). Images were acquired on a laser scanning confocal microscope under identical settings. Co-localization between TCPC fluorescence and ER-Tracker signals was analyzed to confirm ER-targeting.

Flow cytometry

Cells were treated as described above, detached, washed, and resuspended in PBS or binding buffer for apoptosis assays. At least 10,000 single, live events were acquired per sample. ER-Tracker, Fura-2 AM, JC-1, and DCFH-DA signals were quantified as mean fluorescence intensity. Apoptosis was evaluated by Annexin V-APC/7-AAD staining (15 min, dark) and reported as early and late apoptotic fractions.

Animal experiments

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were used. All animal experiments were conducted with random allocation of mice to different experimental groups using a random number table to ensure unbiased assignment. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Forevergen Biosciences (Approval No. IACUC-AEWC-F250420001). A375 cells were suspended in matrix gel and injected subcutaneously.

For pharmacokinetic analysis, mice received free curcumin or TCPC via tail-vein injection at an equivalent curcumin dose of 5 mg/kg. Whole blood was collected at 1, 2, 4, 12, and 24 h post-injection. Serum was obtained by centrifugation and analyzed for curcumin concentration using a fluorescence microplate reader.

For biodistribution studies, IR780-loaded nanoparticles (TA-Cu@PEG-PCL@IR780) were prepared. Three groups were tested: PBS, Free IR780, and TA-Cu@PEG-PCL@IR780. Equal IR780 concentration (10 µg/mL) was used across groups. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. At 24 h, major organs and tumors were collected for ex vivo imaging and quantitative analysis.

For antitumor efficacy, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: CTRL, PC, TC, and TCPC. Treatments were administered via intravenous injection every other day. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded throughout the study. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors and major organs were collected for analysis. Tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for H&E staining and immunohistochemistry. Apoptosis and proliferation were assessed by cleaved caspase-3 and Ki-67 staining, respectively. ER stress in tumor tissues was evaluated via CHOP immunostaining.

In vivo biocompatibility and biosafety

Body weight and survival were monitored during treatment. Hemolysis was assessed in whole blood, and complete blood counts were measured to evaluate hematological parameters. Serum biochemical markers, including ALT, AST, and ALB, were analyzed to assess liver function. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected, fixed, and subjected to H&E staining for histopathological examination.

Bioinformatic analysis and molecular Docking

Drug sensitivity was predicted using public pharmacogenomic datasets. Correlations between CHOP expression and IC₅₀ values of anticancer agents were calculated to identify compounds with enhanced efficacy in high CHOP-expressing cells. For molecular docking, the CHOP crystal structure was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Ligands, Ibrutinib, Entospletinib, Irinotecan, Dasatinib, Sapitinib, UMI-77, JQ1, and AZD6482 were prepared and energy-minimized. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina within a defined CHOP binding pocket. Binding modes, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and docking scores were analyzed to assess affinity and stability.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using two-sample t-test or one-way ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results and discussion

ER Stress–Related gene signature is associated with melanoma prognosis

To explore the clinical relevance of ER stress in melanoma, we systematically performed transcriptomic analyses using publicly available datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) obtained from the UCSC Xena platform. ER stress–associated genes were obtained from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and intersected with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in melanoma, yielding a set of 275 ER stress–related DEGs (Fig. 2A). Unsupervised consensus clustering based on these 275 genes stratified melanoma patients into three subgroups—Cluster 1, Cluster 2, and Cluster 3—each representing unique transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2B). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses demonstrated that patients in Cluster 1 exhibited significantly better progression-free interval (PFI) (Fig. 2C) and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 2D) compared to those in Cluster 2, highlighting potential prognostic implications of the ER stress–related gene signature. To further understand the molecular underpinnings of these subgroups, we performed differential expression analysis between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. As illustrated in the volcano plot and heatmap (Fig. 2E and F), distinct transcriptomic alterations were observed. Specifically, applying a |log₂ fold change| ≥ 0.585 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 as thresholds, we identified 62 upregulated and 32 downregulated genes in Cluster 2 relative to Cluster 1, underscoring divergent biological processes between subgroups. To elucidate the potential functional interactions among these DEGs, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the data from STRING database. As shown in Fig. 2G, central nodes within the network were enriched in regulators associated with ER stress pathways, including protein folding, apoptosis, and the unfolded protein response (UPR).

(C, D) Kaplan–Meier curves for PFI (C) and DSS (D) across subgroups. (E) Volcano plot showing DEGs between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. (F) Heatmap of representative DEGs. ER stress has been shown to promote cancer progression and shape prognosis in many tumor types, highlighting its critical role in tumor biology and immune control [40–43]. Targeting ER stress is a promising strategy for melanoma, as multiple agents can induce lethal ER stress to limit tumor growth and boost therapeutic response [44–46]. Collectively, these findings indicate that ER stress-related transcriptional heterogeneity is closely associated with melanoma prognosis. This ER stress signature enables clear patient grouping and highlights key regulators in the PPI network, supporting ER stress pathway targeting as a promising option for melanoma.

Design and characterization of the TCPC nanomicelles

Based on our previous work [47, 48], PC have shown potential for melanoma treatment. However, further improvement in stability, tumor targeting, and subcellular delivery is needed. To improve the therapeutic performance of PC, we engineered a hybrid nanoplatform by coating PC with a metal–polyphenol network composed of tannic acid (TA) and copper ions (Cu), forming TCPC. This design aimed to enhance drug stability, facilitate ER-targeted delivery, and leverage copper-mediated ER stress to potentiate antitumor activity. PC nanoparticles were first prepared via hydrophobic encapsulation of curcumin. Subsequently, the phenolic groups of TA coordinated with Cu and deposited on the PC surface to yield TCPC (Fig. 3A). In the absence of Cur, the same coating produced TC nanoparticles (TC). The TCPC system achieved a Cur loading efficiency of 31.2% and a Cu loading efficiency of 66.4% as determined by ICP-MS. Dynamic light scattering measurements indicated that TCPC possessed similar hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 80 nm (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, TCPC nanoparticles maintained stable hydrodynamic diameters (~ 80 nm) and low PDI values (0.293 on Day 7 and 0.246 on Day 14) after storage at 4 °C (Figure S1 and S2). XPS analysis showed that the Cu 2p₃/₂ peak remained at 932.3 eV on both Day 7 and Day 14, indicating that the copper oxidation state remained + 1 during storage (Figure S3 and S4), which is consistent with previous reports of tannic acid–copper(I) complexes, where Cu⁺ species were stabilized by coordination with tannic acid [49]. These results demonstrate that TCPC nanoparticles exhibit both physical and chemical stability, supporting their reproducibility and potential translational application. TEM imaging confirmed a uniform spherical morphology of TCPC nanoparticles (Fig. 3C). Zeta potential measurements showed slight negative surface charges for PC, TC, and TCPC (− 1.55 ± 0.39, − 1.39 ± 0.60, and − 1.83 ± 0.18 mV, respectively), suggesting good colloidal stability (Fig. 3D). UV–Vis absorbance spectra displayed characteristic peaks at 250 and 425 nm for PC and TCPC, confirming successful incorporation of both Cur and Cu (Fig. 3E). Elemental mapping using scanning TEM revealed the distribution of C, N, O, and Cu within individual TCPC particles (Fig. 3F). Further, we measured the release of curcumin and Cu from TCPC micelles at pH 7.4 and 6.5 in either PBS or 10% FBS-containing medium. Both curcumin and Cu exhibited faster release at pH 6.5 than at pH 7.4 in the presence of serum. In contrast, their release remained low in PBS at both pH conditions (Figure S5 and S6). These findings indicate that TCPC nanomicelles display pH-responsive release behavior in serum-containing environments. Therefore, they are more likely to release their cargo under tumor-like acidic conditions. This tumor-specific release supports the potential of TCPC for targeted melanoma therapy.

These findings demonstrate that TCPC maintains favorable size, morphology, and surface properties while achieving high drug and Cu loading. The combination of metal–polyphenol coating and Cur encapsulation provides a versatile nanocarrier capable of stable, ER-targeted delivery for enhanced melanoma therapy.

TCPC inhibits melanoma cell proliferation and migration in vitro

To investigate the anti-tumor efficacy of TCPC, we first performed in vitro assays using two human melanoma cell lines, A375 and SK-MEL-28. Four treatment groups were established: control (CTRL), PC, TC, and TCPC.

Cell proliferation was measured by the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 4A, TCPC treatment markedly reduced cell viability compared with the other groups at 24 h, indicating enhanced antitumor effects. Specifically, at a concentration of 10 µg/mL, the viability of A375 cells were 78.55 ± 6.58% in the PC group and 65.72 ± 7.22% in the TC group, whereas it dropped to 45.57 ± 0.98% in the TCPC group. For SK-MEL-28 cells, the viability were 80.69 ± 2.58% in the PC group, 106.42 ± 11.36% in the TC group, and 81.02 ± 6.56% in the TCPC group, suggesting that the inhibitory effect of TCPC was more pronounced in A375 cells. Next, we examined the effect of TCPC on melanoma cell migration using wound healing and Transwell assays. As shown in Fig. 4B and C, the migration rate of A375 cells was 43.17 ± 1.19% in the CTRL group, 36.87 ± 3.30% in the PC group, 44.53 ± 1.48% in the TC group, and 16.49 ± 2.42% in the TCPC group. Compared with the CTRL group, both PC and TCPC reduced the migration of A375 cells, and the inhibitory effect was most significant in the TCPC group. In contrast, for SK-MEL-28 cells, the migration rates were 39.83 ± 7.73%, 33.25 ± 10.61%, 35.15 ± 9.83%, and 35.21 ± 2.91% in the Ctrl, PC, TC, and TCPC groups, respectively, and no significant difference was observed among these groups. Cell invasion was further assessed by Transwell invasion assays (Fig. 4D and E). For A375 cells, the number of invaded cells was 790 ± 35 in the Ctrl group, 816 ± 46 in the PC group, 771 ± 32 in the TC group, and 498 ± 58 in the TCPC group. For SK-MEL-28 cells, the values were 776 ± 59, 549 ± 121, 793 ± 69, and 543 ± 99, respectively. In both cell lines, TCPC treatment significantly reduced the number of invaded cells compared with the Ctrl, confirming a potent inhibitory effect on invasion.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that TCPC effectively suppresses melanoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro. The enhanced efficacy is likely attributed to improved delivery of curcumin and activation of Activation of relevant biological pathways. These findings provide a strong foundation for further exploration of the underlying molecular mechanisms.

TCPC induces transcriptomic changes associated with ER stress

To investigate the molecular basis of TCPC against melanoma, we performed transcriptome sequencing in A375 melanoma cells. Two groups were analyzed: untreated control and TCPC-treated cells, with five biological replicates in each group to ensure statistical reliability. As shown in Fig. 5A, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between the two groups. This result indicates that TCPC treatment caused marked changes in the global gene expression profile.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals ER stress induction by TCPC in melanoma cells. (A) PCA plot showing clear separation between CTRL and TCPC-treated A375 cells. (B) Volcano plot of DEGs. (C) Bar chart summarizing upregulated and downregulated DEGs. (D) Heatmap of representative DEGs. (E) GO enrichment analysis highlighting ER-related terms in the cellular component category. (F) GSEA enrichment plots of top pathways after TCPC treatment. (G) Enrichment plot for “response to ER stress” pathway. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 5)

Differential expression analysis further confirmed this shift. A total of 157 genes were upregulated and 671 genes were downregulated, as displayed in the volcano plot (Fig. 5B) and summarized in the bar chart (Fig. 5C). These changes were also clearly visualized in the clustering heatmap (Fig. 5D). Such broad transcriptional reprogramming suggests that TCPC triggers significant alterations in cellular signaling pathways. To clarify the functional implications, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. The DEGs were mapped to biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) categories (Figure S7 and S8). Since TCPC is designed to interact with intracellular structures, we focused on the CC category. Notably, the top 20 enriched pathways highlighted genes related to the endoplasmic reticulum lumen, ER membrane, and protein-folding machinery (Fig. 5E). This result suggests that ER function may be a primary target of TCPC.

To validate this hypothesis, we next carried out Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The top 30 enriched pathways included cytoplasmic translation, ribosome biogenesis, rRNA processing, and ER-related responses (Fig. 5F). Among these, the UPR and the response to ER stress were of particular interest, as they were directly linked to TCPC’s design principle. Indeed, the enrichment plot for “Response to endoplasmic reticulum stress” showed strong significance (P = 0; FDR = 0.0016; ES = 0.52) (Fig. 5G). Importantly, hallmark ER stress–related genes were markedly upregulated, including CHOP, Heat shock protein family A member 5 (HSPA5), and Tetratricopeptide repeat domain 23 like (TTC23L). These transcriptional changes provide compelling evidence that TCPC activates ER stress in melanoma cells.

Moreover, to better connect our bioinformatic analysis with the experimental findings, we further examined whether the transcriptomic changes were consistent with our initial prediction. As shown in the early bioinformatic screening, ER stress signatures were strongly associated with poor prognosis in melanoma. This finding guided the design of TCPC, which was intended to reach the ER and modulate ER stress–related pathways. Therefore, the strong upregulation of CHOP and HSPA5 in the RNA-seq results is in line with our original hypothesis. These genes are well-known markers of ER stress activation. Their increased expression after TCPC treatment confirms that the material acts on the ER as expected. Taken together, the bioinformatic prediction, the TCPC design strategy, and the transcriptomic validation form a coherent mechanistic framework that supports ER stress as a key target of TCPC.

Together, these transcriptomic data indicate that ER stress is a central cellular event induced by TCPC. This finding not only explains part of the anti-tumor mechanism but also raises the possibility that TCPC exploits ER dysfunction as a therapeutic vulnerability in melanoma. Therefore, we next examined whether ER stress activation by TCPC could translate into downstream apoptotic signaling and enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

TCPC triggers ER Stress–Mediated apoptosis through Ca²⁺ overload and mitochondrial dysfunction

Based on the transcriptomic data, we next validated ER stress activation at the molecular and cellular levels in A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells. Cells were divided into two groups: control and TCPC treatment.

We first measured the expression of ER stress–related genes using qPCR. As shown in Fig. 6A and Table S1, CHOP and HSPA5 were significantly upregulated in both A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells after TCPC treatment. For example, CHOP increased from 1.00 ± 0.06 to 2.07 ± 0.04 in A375 cells, and from 1.00 ± 0.07 to 2.10 ± 0.03 in SK-MEL-28 cells. HSPA5 also showed a marked increase (A375: 1.00 ± 0.02 vs. 2.84 ± 0.09; SK-MEL-28: 1.01 ± 0.09 vs. 1.61 ± 0.09). These changes were consistent with the transcriptome results. By contrast, TTC23L showed no significant change in A375 cells and even decreased in SK-MEL-28 cells. In addition, we measured the mRNA expression levels of key UPR sensors, including Protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), Activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) using qPCR, with tunicamycin (TM) serving as a positive control. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that TCPC selectively activated specific UPR branches in a cell-line–dependent manner. In A375 cells, TCPC increased PERK expression by 2.856-fold, whereas IRE1α and ATF6 showed no significant changes; by contrast, TM elevated PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6 by 2.373-, 2.675-, and 2.816-fold, respectively. In SK-MEL-28 cells, TCPC induced a marked 14.166-fold increase in PERK and a moderate 1.434-fold increase in IRE1α, while ATF6 remained unchanged; in comparison, TM increased PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6 by 2.539-, 4.385-, and 1.432-fold, respectively. For CHOP-related signaling, TCPC upregulated ATF4 (1.465-fold) and CHOP (1.287-fold) in A375 cells, whereas eIF2α remained unchanged; TM induced much stronger activation (ATF4: 1.872-fold, CHOP: 24.987-fold). In SK-MEL-28 cells, TCPC elevated eIF2α (1.394-fold) and CHOP (1.476-fold), while ATF4 showed no significant change; TM again produced markedly higher induction (ATF4: 6.418-fold, CHOP: 45.287-fold). These results collectively indicate that TCPC primarily activates the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/CHOP axis, with a magnitude of ER-stress intensity that is moderate but comparable in pattern to tunicamycin, confirming pathway specificity (Figures S9 and S10). CHOP is a key protein in ER stress and mediates stress-induced cell death, making it an attractive target for cancer therapy [50–54]. Therefore, we next examined CHOP expression to evaluate the effect of TCPC. Confocal microscopy revealed a clear increase in CHOP fluorescence in TCPC-treated cells (Fig. 6B). Western blotting further confirmed this observation (Figure S11-S13). We then explored the structural consequences of ER stress. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed irregular expansion and swelling of the ER in TCPC-treated cells compared with controls (Fig. 6C). We used confocal microscopy to check ER targeting. After 8 h, TCPC fluorescence partially overlapped with ER-Tracker Red in both A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells, indicating their co-localization. This confirms that TCPC accumulates in the ER (Figure S14 and S15). These findings are consistent with literature reports that TA-coated nanoparticles are internalized via clathrin-mediated or actin-dependent pathways [55], and that metal–polyphenol coatings can promote endosomal escape [56] and redistribute toward the ER [57]. The TA–Cu shell may enhance ER retention via local Cu release and interactions with ER proteins. We acknowledge that we did not experimentally determine the exact pathway, which will be addressed in future work using ER-specific markers and endocytosis inhibitors. In addition, ER-Tracker staining indicated an increase in ER content after TCPC treatment, which was further confirmed by flow cytometry (A375: 1.00 ± 0.01 vs. 1.10 ± 0.00; SK-MEL-28: 1.00 ± 0.00 vs. 1.11 ± 0.01) (Fig. 6D and E). These results collectively demonstrate that TCPC causes both molecular and morphological features of ER stress.

ER stress triggers apoptosis directly. It also disturbs calcium homeostasis. Excess Ca²⁺ overloads mitochondria, reduces membrane potential, and increases ROS. This highlights ER-mitochondria cross-talk. Guided by this mechanism, we examined Ca²⁺ changes after TCPC treatment. Confocal microscopy showed a significant increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ in both cell lines (A375: 1.00 ± 0.00 vs. 1.39 ± 0.02; SK-MEL-28: 1.00 ± 0.01 vs. 1.22 ± 0.04) (Fig. 6F and G). We then assessed mitochondrial function. JC-1 staining revealed a decrease in red fluorescence (J-aggregates) and an increase in green fluorescence (monomers), indicating a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 6H). Consistently, ROS levels were significantly elevated after TCPC treatment (A375: 1.00 ± 0.03 vs. 2.10 ± 0.05; SK-MEL-28: 1.00 ± 0.03 vs. 1.46 ± 0.00) (Fig. 6I and J). Finally, we evaluated apoptosis directly. Flow cytometry using 7-AAD/APC staining showed a marked increase in apoptotic cell populations after TCPC exposure (A375: 2.98 ± 0.21% vs. 8.97 ± 0.31%; SK-MEL-28: 2.96 ± 0.23% vs. 4.02 ± 0.14%) (Fig. 6K). However, our study was not able to directly determine whether the increase in cytosolic Ca²⁺ originates exclusively from ER Ca²⁺ release. This represents a limitation of our current study. Future experiments using ER-specific calcium indicators and pharmacological inhibition of ER Ca²⁺ channels will be required to clarify the contribution of ER-derived Ca²⁺ and its mechanistic link to mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings show that TCPC induces CHOP-dependent ER stress. This causes Ca²⁺ overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ROS accumulation. Both direct ER stress and ER–mitochondria crosstalk promote apoptosis in melanoma cells. This mechanism explains the strong anti-tumor effect of TCPC.

To further explore whether Cu-dependent pathways are also involved, we analyzed the transcriptional changes of key cuproptosis regulators. As shown in Figure S16 and S17, TCPC markedly decreased both Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) and Dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) expression in A375 cells, whereas it suppressed FDX1 but increased DLAT expression in SK-MEL-28 cells. These findings suggest that TCPC may influence copper-dependent mitochondrial pathways in a cell-line-specific manner, and that cuproptosis may occur alongside ER-stress–mediated apoptosis as a parallel mechanism contributing to melanoma cell death.

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution

TCPC demonstrated improved pharmacokinetic behavior compared with free curcumin. Serum curcumin concentrations remained consistently higher in the TCPC group across all time points, confirming prolonged circulation and enhanced stability of the nano-micelles (Figure S18). In vivo imaging using IR780-loaded nanoparticles further revealed strong and sustained tumor accumulation of TA-Cu@PEG-PCL@IR780 from 12 to 48 h, whereas the fluorescence of free IR780 declined rapidly (Figure S19). Because curcumin’s intrinsic fluorescence is too weak and unstable for reliable whole-animal imaging. Therefore, IR780, a near-infrared dye with similar hydrophobicity, was used as a surrogate to track the in vivo biodistribution of TCPC nanoparticles. Ex vivo imaging at 24 h showed that nanoparticles predominantly accumulated in the liver and tumor, consistent with typical biodistribution patterns of PEG-PCL–based nanocarriers. These findings demonstrate the effective tumor-targeting capability of TCPC in vivo.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of TCPC

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of TCPC in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using A375 melanoma cells in nude mice. Animals were randomly divided into four groups: CTRL, PC, TC, and TCPC. Intravenous injections were given every other day. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored during the treatment period (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B and C, TCPC treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth compared with the CTRL group. Relative tumor volume (V/V0) was used as the main parameter for comparison. On day 10, tumor volumes were 9.96 ± 2.08, 8.20 ± 1.96, 8.07 ± 1.09, and 6.70 ± 1.59 for CTRL, PC, TC, and TCPC groups, respectively. However, there was no significant difference in tumor weight among the groups (CTRL: 0.80 ± 0.37 g; PC: 1.14 ± 0.51 g; TC: 0.82 ± 0.16 g; TCPC: 0.78 ± 0.19 g). To provide a clearer quantitative assessment of therapeutic efficacy, we calculated the tumor growth inhibition (TGI%) for each treatment group. The TGI% values for the PC, TC, and TCPC groups were 17.67%, 19.01%, and 32.75%, respectively, compared with the CTRL group. These results show that TCPC still suppressed tumor progression, even though the tumor-weight difference did not reach statistical significance. To better understand this discrepancy, several factors may help explain the limited change in tumor weight. Drug distribution inside solid tumors can be uneven, which may reduce the overall response. The treatment period in this study was also short, which limits total drug exposure. Moreover, the fixed dosing interval may not be the most effective schedule for nano-drug delivery. TCPC also does not contain any active targeting ligand, which may reduce selective tumor accumulation. Together, these factors may contribute to the moderate tumor-weight changes observed in this study. These findings indicate that TCPC effectively reduced tumor volume but had limited impact on tumor mass. This discrepancy also highlights a limitation of our study. Future work may optimize TCPC efficacy by incorporating metal ions or combination therapy strategies.

To further characterize the biological effects, tumor tissues from each group were analyzed by H&E staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC). As shown in Fig. 7D and E, tumors from the TCPC group displayed typical apoptotic and necrotic features, including nuclear condensation and fragmentation. IHC staining revealed that cleaved caspase-3, an apoptosis marker, was significantly increased, while Ki-67, a proliferation marker, was markedly reduced in TCPC-treated tumors (Fig. 7F and G). These results suggest that TCPC inhibits tumor progression through both induction of apoptosis and suppression of proliferation. Given the transcriptomic findings in vitro, we next examined ER stress–related protein expression in tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 7H and I, CHOP levels were significantly elevated in the TCPC group compared with controls. This result closely matched the in vitro data, confirming that TCPC induces ER stress not only in cultured cells but also in vivo.

In summary, TCPC nanomicelles demonstrated clear antitumor activity in the melanoma xenograft model. The mechanism involves the activation of ER stress, which triggers apoptotic signaling and leads to suppression of tumor growth. These results validate our in vitro observations and highlight ER stress as a central pathway underlying the therapeutic effect of TCPC.

In vivo biocompatibility and biosafety evaluation

To evaluate the systemic safety of TCPC, we monitored body weight, survival, hematological parameters, and histopathology. During the entire treatment period, no significant weight loss or abnormal behavior was observed in any group (Fig. 8A and B). All mice survived, and body weight remained stable across CTRL, PC, TC, and TCPC groups, suggesting negligible systemic toxicity.

In vivo biocompatibility and biosafety of TCPC. (A, B) Body weight and percent survival monitoring during treatment. (C) Hemolysis assay showing negligible hemolytic activity. (D) Blood routine tests (RBC, WBC, PLT, Lymph, Mon, and Gran). (E) Serum biochemistry analysis of ALT, AST, and ALB. (F) HE staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney). Scale bar = 100 μm. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 5)

Moreover, blood hemolysis assays confirmed the absence of hemolytic activity (Fig. 8C), demonstrating excellent blood compatibility. Complete blood count analysis further supported these findings. The values of red blood cells (RBC: CTRL, 7.53 ± 0.50; PC, 7.16 ± 0.65; TC, 7.18 ± 1.09; TCPC, 7.51 ± 0.59 × 1012/L), white blood cells (WBC: CTRL, 2.66 ± 0.48; PC, 4.06 ± 2.97; TC, 3.34 ± 1.11; TCPC, 3.30 ± 0.65 × 109/L), and platelets (PLT: CTRL, 992.00 ± 235.98; PC, 721.20 ± 68.42; TC, 1087.20 ± 146.04; TCPC, 818.20 ± 464.67 × 109/L) remained within physiological ranges. Similarly, lymphocytes, monocytes, and granulocytes also showed no abnormal fluctuations, confirming stable hematological function.

To further assess hepatic safety, we analyzed serum biochemical markers, including ALT, AST, and ALB. The results demonstrated no significant elevation of liver enzymes (ALT: CTRL, 23.40 ± 1.82; PC, 27.26 ± 5.28; TC, 22.62 ± 0.32; TCPC, 30.08 ± 8.02 U/L; AST: CTRL, 100.21 ± 11.27; PC, 74.13 ± 18.37; TC, 102.14 ± 22.87; TCPC, 98.02 ± 12.43 U/L; ALB: CTRL, 32.40 ± 2.38; PC, 33.97 ± 1.30; TC, 33.55 ± 0.34; TCPC, 32.64 ± 2.22 g/L), all of which were comparable to those of the control group. These data indicated no detectable hepatotoxicity. Furthermore, histological examination of major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no obvious pathological changes across all groups (Fig. 7F). The tissue architecture remained intact, with no evidence of necrosis, inflammation, or other lesions. However, our study only tested short-term toxicity. We did not check long-term safety or copper accumulation in organs. Future studies will address these points to give a more complete evaluation of TCPC safety.

Taken together, these results clearly demonstrate that TCPC exhibits excellent biocompatibility and biosafety in vivo. The absence of systemic toxicity highlights its strong potential as a safe nanomicelle platform for melanoma therapy.

Bioinformatic analysis and molecular Docking reveal synergistic potential of CHOP activation

To further explore the translational relevance of CHOP upregulation in tumor therapy, we performed drug sensitivity prediction using public datasets. As shown in Fig. 9A, high CHOP expression was significantly correlated with enhanced sensitivity (lower IC₅₀ values) to several anticancer agents, including UMI-77, AZD6482, JQ1, Gemcitabine, and Irinotecan, indicating that tumors with elevated CHOP levels may be particularly responsive to these drugs. In contrast, for Dasatinib, Sapitinib, Ibrutinib, BPD-00008900, and Entospletinib, IC₅₀ values tended to increase with CHOP expression, suggesting decreased sensitivity to these agents in CHOP-high tumors. These results suggest that CHOP activation may serve as a key determinant of chemosensitivity, thereby providing a rationale for combining TCPC with existing therapeutic agents.

CHOP activation correlates with chemosensitivity and synergistic drug interactions. (A) Correlation of CHOP expression with drug sensitivity (IC₅₀) to UMI-77, Dasatinib, Sapitinib, AZD6482, JQ1, Gemcitabine, Ibrutinib, BPD-00008900, Entospletinib, and Irinotecan. (B) Molecular docking of Ibrutinib, Entospletinib, Irinotecan, Dasatinib, Sapitinib, UMI-77, JQ1, and AZD6482 with CHOP showing stable binding interactions

To substantiate these observations, molecular docking was employed to assess the binding affinity between selected drugs and CHOP-related targets. Molecular docking further supported these findings. Ibrutinib showed the strongest binding affinity (− 6.5 kcal/mol), comparable to Entospletinib (− 6.5 kcal/mol). Dasatinib and Sapitinib exhibited moderate affinities (− 5.8 kcal/mol each). Among the CHOP-sensitive agents, Irinotecan showed − 5.9 kcal/mol, UMI-77 − 5.7 kcal/mol, JQ1 − 5.3 kcal/mol, and AZD6482 − 5.0 kcal/mol. These results suggest that CHOP activation influences both drug sensitivity and drug–target interactions. These results suggest that CHOP activation enhances the efficacy of agents such as UMI-77, AZD6482, JQ1, Gemcitabine, and Irinotecan, despite their relatively weaker binding affinities, while reducing the effectiveness of Dasatinib, Sapitinib, and Ibrutinib, which display stronger interactions with CHOP-related targets. These docking data not only validated the bioinformatic predictions but also highlighted the complexity of CHOP-mediated drug response, which may involve both direct and indirect regulatory effects. Moreover, recent studies provide additional support for a pathway-based explanation of this synergy. For example, curcumin has been shown to activate ER stress through ATF6 and increase CHOP expression, thereby promoting apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells [58]. Another study demonstrated that curcumin enhances the anticancer effect of irinotecan by increasing ROS levels and activating ER stress, and both BIP and CHOP are required for this response [59]. These findings suggest that ER stress amplification, rather than direct CHOP binding, is a common mechanism that underlies the enhanced therapeutic effect. Therefore, the combination potential observed in our docking and drug-sensitivity analysis is most likely due to functional modulation of ER stress signaling. Specifically, TCPC increases CHOP expression, and agents such as JQ1 and irinotecan may further strengthen downstream ER stress responses. This provides a simple and rational explanation for the predicted synergy through functional modulation of ER stress signaling. However, it should be noted that these results are based solely on bioinformatic and molecular docking analyses. In vivo combination therapy experiments are required to validate the predicted synergistic effects of TCPC with agents such as JQ1 or irinotecan, representing a limitation of the current study.

Overall, integrating drug sensitivity prediction with molecular docking provides mechanistic insight into the role of CHOP in shaping the therapeutic landscape of melanoma. The combined results suggest that CHOP activation could potentiate the efficacy of certain chemotherapeutics, while potentially limiting the effectiveness of others, underscoring the importance of personalized drug selection in future translational applications.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered TCPC, an ER-targeted metal–polyphenol nanomicelle that links clinical transcriptome mining with rational material design for melanoma therapy. TCPC enables organelle-specific delivery of curcumin and copper, thereby amplifies ER stress, and triggers apoptosis through Ca²⁺ overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ROS accumulation. Transcriptome analysis confirmed ER pathway activation, and in vivo studies showed tumor suppression without systemic toxicity. Moreover, pharmacogenomic analysis and molecular docking suggest TCPC as a platform for rational drug combinations. Overall, this work establishes an omics-guided materials strategy that integrates engineering design with mechanistic validation. It advances ER-targeted nanomedicine for melanoma and offers a framework for clinically translatable combination therapies. Future studies will optimize dosing and expand testing in diverse melanoma models.
