A non-pregnant woman with elevated beta-HCG: A rare case of ovarian seminoma

Introduction

A rare type of germ cell tumor that occurs in the ovaries is ovarian seminoma, also known as dysgerminoma. Ovarian germ cell tumors are usually benign, but they can be malignant [1]. They make up only 2 % of all ovarian tumors but account for 33 % of malignant ovarian germ cell tumors [2]. Seminomas are most common in adolescent girls or young women and usually affect only one ovary [3]. Early symptoms of ovarian germ cell tumors can be difficult to detect [1], but early detection and treatment as soon as possible can significantly improve the prognosis [1]. Herein, we report the case of a 15-year-old female patient with a large malignant ovarian dysgerminoma to provide a comprehensive overview of the diagnosis and management of this pathology and to assist clinicians in making an early diagnosis. The work was reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria [4].

Case presentation

We present a 15-year-old patient from a nonconsanguineous marriage with no significant medical, surgical or family history of cancer. Prenatally, the patient had a karyotype with the presence of an isochromosome on the short arm of chromosome 12, noted as i12p. She had reached menarche at the age of 12 with regular cycles. She denied smoking, alcoholism and drug use. She presented to our emergency D department of obstetrics and gynecology at the Maternity and Neonatology Centre in Tunisia with subacute abdominal pain without associated nausea or vomiting. This was associated with fever and weight loss of 6 kg over 2 months. An abdominal examination revealed a firm abdominopelvic mass with irregular borders, more than 12 cm in length, located midway between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis, but there was no guarding or rigidity.

The 2D ultrasound showed a solid, heterogeneous ovarian mass with well-defined borders measuring 110 × 101 mm in the right ovary with fast arterial flow and low resistance in the internal vessels with a significant peritoneal effusion (Fig. 1). The serum cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) level was 293 U/mL. The BHCG level was 150 U/mL. There were no abnormalities in other laboratory tests such as alpha-fetoprotein and LDH. The patient therefore underwent an immediate abdominal and pelvic CT scan. This revealed a large right ovarian mass measuring approximately 100 × 90 × 90 mm with solid and fluid components and contrast enhancement. The right ovary was identified separately and appeared normal. An abdominal CT scan showed fluid in the peritoneal cavity with no evidence of regional lymphadenopathy or distant metastases (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Given these findings, the diagnosis of a malignant ovarian tumor was strongly suspected. Given her young age, and after a collegial decision within a multidisciplinary team, we decided to perform a right adnexectomy with an anatomopathological examination.

The patient underwent exploratory laparoscopy. Intra-operative findings included a large heterogeneous irregular solid cystic mass with a ruptured capsule, varicose veins and a large amount of fluid in the peritoneal cavity. There was no lymphadenopathy or invasion of surrounding structures. We performed a right salpingo-oophorectomy with peritoneal cytology.

Based on the histopathological analysis of the surgically resected specimen, the tumor is diagnosed as a dysgerminoma without other tumor components as a brown, lobulated, firm mass. The tumor is composed of large cells with clear cytoplasm and visible cell borders. The cells have large nuclei with prominent nucleoli and numerous mitoses are present. The proliferation is arranged in massive and wide spans supported by a fibrocollagenous stroma interspersed with lymphocytes. Peritoneal cytology shows the presence of tumor cells (Fig. 3). The patient was diagnosed according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification with FIGO IC and according to the TNM classification with T1C, N0, M0. The patient received a fertility preservation consultation prior to starting her polychemotherapy sessions, and she underwent 3 cycles of chemotherapy, including BEP: etoposide, cisplatin, and bleomycin, with satisfactory evolution.

Discussion

Ovarian seminomas, also known as dysgerminomas, account for only 2 % of all ovarian tumors and 33 % of malignant ovarian germ cell tumors [2]. These tumors develop from primordial germ cells similar to those found in testicular seminoma [5]. Ovarian seminoma is most common in women of reproductive age, with a peak incidence in women under 30, and accounts for two-thirds of all malignant ovarian neoplasms in women under 20. Maternal use of oral contraceptives and a high body mass index can increase the risk of ovarian dysgerminoma in girls, as can certain hereditary congenital anomalies, such as chromosomal abnormalities or genetic mutations, as in our case, or in people with gonadal dysgenesis or androgen insensitivity syndrome.

Seminomas have variable clinical manifestations, either asymptomatic, abdominal pain or distension, abdominopelvic mass as in our case, menstrual abnormalities, vaginal bleeding, nausea or fever [1]. Early detection is particularly difficult and there are often no symptoms in the early stages [3]. Acute abdominal pain is due to torsion, rupture of superficial tumor vessels or intra-abdominal bleeding.

The typical ultrasound finding is a pure solid tumor divided into different lobules, with irregular internal echogenicity, smooth lobulated contours, and well-defined borders, and richly vascularised on color/power Doppler, as in our case. However, less typical appearance may include large cystic areas [6]. Ovarian dysgerminoma presents as a large, well-encapsulated, wholly or predominantly solid mass on CT scan and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The average size of tumors is 17.0–7.8 cm, with a maximum diameter of more than 10 cm [7]. Calcifications may appear as a patchy pattern on CT imaging [6]. MRI has been shown to be superior to CT in characterizing adnexal masses [8].

According to the WHO 2020 tumor classification, dysgerminomas may be associated with elevated serum LDH (95 %), elevated HCG (5 %) or serum placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP) [9]. Other tumor markers that have been used in the evaluation of ovarian germ cell tumors, including dysgerminomas, are alpha-fetoprotein (a FP), inhibin A and B, CA125, and HE4 [10]. Hypercalcemia may develop as a paraneoplastic syndrome due to tumor production of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [11]. In our case, the BHCG level was 150 U/mL and the CA-125 level was 293 U/mL. LDH, PLAP, AFP, and calcium levels were normal. HE4 was not performed for financial reasons. A high level of a tumor marker is not sufficient to make the diagnosis and histological examination is crucial to confirm the presence of ovarian seminoma [9]. The typical histological pattern is easy to recognize. It consists of large nuclei, distorted from their basic spherical shape, with one or more nucleoli. The cells are arranged in an alveolar pattern separated by fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes with variable degrees of necrosis and iconic giant cells [10].

These neoplasms can be difficult to diagnose and immunohistochemical markers can be useful. OCT3/4, PLAP, D2–40, NANOG, and CD117 are often positive in dysgerminoma [4].

Depending on the stage and extent of the tumor and the desire for fertility, ovarian seminoma is usually treated with surgery and chemotherapy. Unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy may be sufficient for early-stage seminoma, although total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy may be required for advanced cases. Surgery may also include removal of as much cancer as possible from the pelvis and abdomen [3]. Chemotherapy is often used in conjunction with surgery for ovarian seminoma, especially in more advanced cases. Bleomycin, etoposide and cisplatin (BEP) is the most commonly used chemotherapy regimen and has been shown to be successful in treating ovarian seminoma [3]. Radiotherapy may be used in some cases of ovarian seminoma, particularly in the early stages of the disease. However, it is less commonly used than surgery and chemotherapy [3]. Early detection is crucial for a better prognosis. Circulating protein biomarkers, autoantibodies, ct DNA and miRNA, and proximal fluid sampling all require additional investigation to improve the sensitivity of initial screening [9]. It is likely that patients with risk factors, such as those in our case, will require close surveillance from an early age in order to detect seminomas at an early stage. This surveillance includes transvaginal ultrasound and probably positive tumor markers in these tumors. Further prospective studies could justify the interest and frequency of this surveillance. Seminoma with effective adjuvant therapy has a favorable overall survival rate of more than 20 % after 10 years of follow-up [10].

Conclusion

Seminomas are rare tumors. It has variable clinical manifestations, either asymptomatic, abdominal pain or distension, abdominopelvic mass as in our case, menstrual abnormalities, vaginal bleeding, nausea, or fever. The only way to guarantee a better prognosis is early detection.
