Impact of early clinical exposure on medical students’ competency development: a systematic review of evidence from Asian and African countries
Authors: Eman E Shaban, Hussam Elmelliti, Amira Shaban, Ahmed Shaban, Hany A Zaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly stages of medical training are vital to ensure the academic performance of medical students. In conventional medical programs, students tend to be exposed to clinical training in the final years of their studies. However, this strategy has been profoundly reconsidered in recent years, with growing evidence showing that early clinical exposure (ECE) might enhance cognitive and effective learning outcomes for medical students. Nonetheless, there is insufficient information on the particular influence of ECE on medical competencies. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to evaluate the impact of ECE on the competency development of medical students.MethodsAn online search for potential studies was conducted on five major electronic databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar) using keywords related to Early clinical exposure, competency, and medical students. This search was limited to English-written records. Furthermore, studies were included if they involved medical students subjected to ECE during pre-clinical years. Quality appraisal was conducted depending on the study designs, with the MINORS used for non-randomized studies and Cochrane risk of bias for randomized studies.ResultsFifteen studies enrolling 2579 medical students were reviewed. These studies included 1 randomized study, 7 cross-sectional studies, 2 quasi-experimental studies, 1 qualitative study, and 4 non-randomized interventional studies. Data from the included studies revealed that ECE significantly improves the clinical skills of medical students compared to the traditional teaching method. Studies also reported that the majority of medical students agreed (strongly agreed/agreed) that ECE improved their communication skills, problem-solving skills, empathy, professionalism, and confidence to deal with patients. Furthermore, evidence suggests that ECE improves the medical knowledge of students by motivating them to learn and bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-world clinical practice.ConclusionECE has the potential to enhance the competency of medical students. Therefore, this systematic review supports the integration of ECE in the undergraduate curriculum to ensure that students acquire the skills needed to provide high-quality patient care.Clinical trial registrationPROSPERO: CRD420251055152.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08103-9.Keywords: Early clinical exposure, Competency development, Patient care

Introduction

Medical education is an ever-growing field that aims to produce competent and compassionate healthcare professionals. Traditionally, medical education has been based on the “basic science first” approach, where medical students are exposed to clinical training in the later years of their education [1, 2]. However, this approach has been critically re-evaluated in recent years, with growing evidence suggesting that Early Clinical Exposure (ECE) can improve cognitive and effective learning outcomes for medical students [3].

ECE is an approach that integrates clinical experiences into the early years of medical training. The fundamental principle of this approach is that when medical students are exposed to clinical settings in the early stages of their training, they can thoroughly grasp medical theory and be prepared for the real world. Notably, ECE is conceptually different from traditional clinical training, since it entails early, often observational exposure aimed at contextualizing theoretical information rather than the organized clerkships of subsequent years. The organization and timing of clinical education vary substantially among nations, highlighting the need to explain this difference. In several medical schools in China, Early Clinical Exposure (ECE) often starts in the first or second year and frequently includes activities such as ward visits or patient contacts, combined with preclinical courses [4, 5]. In contrast, formal clinical practice training, such as the one-year clerkship, normally begins in the fourth year of the five-year undergraduate medical program. These contrasts emphasize that ECE is not only an earlier commencement of clerkship, but a pedagogical technique meant to integrate theoretical learning with actual exposure.

ECE has been reported to have several advantages in medical training. One of the major advantages is its potential to contextualize theoretical knowledge [6]. Medical students regularly deal with abstract topics in areas like anatomy, physiology, and biochemistry, which might feel detached from real-world applications. Therefore, ECE can help students observe firsthand how theoretical knowledge is applied in diagnosing, managing, and treating patients. This hands-on experience in clinical practice not only allows students to comprehend their studies better, but also gives them a sense of purpose and meaning that goes beyond what they learn in class [7]. Another advantage of ECE is that it fosters high levels of student engagement and motivation [8, 9]. Medical students get a strong sense of how important their education is when they see the real effects of their information on patients’ health. This increased knowledge often excites students about their studies, as they see their academic activities as having more value and being in line with their future careers as healthcare workers. With this sense of purpose, students may be more curious, diligent, and engaged in their learning, eventually improving their educational experience.

While ECE has gained significant attention in recent years, there is limited evidence on the specific impact of ECE on medical competencies. Therefore, the current systematic review synthesized the available research to understand the effects of ECE on competency domains, such as clinical skills, problem-solving skills, communication skills, empathy, knowledge, and professionalism.

Methods

Protocol

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted according to the guidelines of PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [10]. The protocol for this systematic review was pre-registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number [CRD420251055152]). The registration includes the review objectives, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data sources, and planned analysis methods. The full protocol can be accessed at:

https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251055152.

Search strategy and information sources

An online search for potential studies was run on five major electronic databases, namely PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. The search strategy used to retrieve studies from these databases involved combining keywords, such as Early clinical exposure, competency, medical students, communication skills, professionalism, clinical skills, knowledge, confidence, and empathy, using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” The database search was also supplemented by hand-searching bibliographies of previously published narrative reviews, grey literature, and included studies. However, grey literature such as theses and dissertations were eliminated during the search as they contain unpublished data that would have influenced the scientific research purpose of this systematic review. Complete details on the search strategy used in each database are shown in Appendix A.

Eligibility criteria

To be eligible for inclusion, studies had to adhere to the following PICOS criteria: Population (P): Medical students in the early phase (i.e., pre-clinical years) of their medical training. Intervention (I): Any type of ECE, e.g., observation and shadowing, direct interaction with patients and community, supervised clinical placements, simulated clinical scenarios, etc. Comparison (C): Traditional medical training or delayed clinical exposure. Studies without comparators were also included for a comprehensive synthesis. Outcomes (O): These will consist of clinical skills, communication skills, problem-solving skills, self-confidence, professionalism, knowledge, empathy, and attitudes. Study design (S): Randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-controlled studies, quasi-experimental studies, mixed method studies, and cross-sectional studies.

Studies that did not satisfy the above criteria or were published in languages other than English were ineligible. Additionally, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts, and narrative reviews were excluded.

Data extraction

Two review authors working individually used a pre-designed Excel spreadsheet to extract the data required for review and analysis. The data collected from each study included study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, setting, and the country where the study was conducted), patient characteristics (sample size and current year of medical training), type of ECE, duration of ECE, supervisors, and the reported outcomes. All discrepancies in the collected data were resolved via discussion between the reviewers or by seeking the input of a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

Included studies were critically assessed for methodological quality using the methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) and the Cochrane’s risk of bias (RoB) tool for randomized studies. Using the RoB tool embedded within the Review Manager software, studies were graded to have low, high, or unclear risk of bias across seven different domains. These domains included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias [11]. On the other hand, the MINORS was used to assess the quality of non-randomized studies based on 12 items, with the first 8 items specifically for non-comparative studies [12]. For each item, scores of 0, 1, and 2 were assigned if the items were not reported, inadequately reported, and adequately reported, respectively.

The results of the methodological assessment were further reviewed by an additional reviewer to ensure consistency and completeness. Any discrepancies in the ratings were resolved via discussions or by consulting a third reviewer to help settle the differences.

Data synthesis

We initially intended to conduct a quantitative meta-analysis, but due to the variations in study design, clinical settings, sample sizes, and types of ECE, we could not conduct a meta-analysis. Furthermore, several studies did not provide sufficient statistical data (e.g., means and standard deviations) needed for pooled analysis. Competency development was also measured with several tools, e.g., OSCE scores, multiple choice question (MCQ) tests, and other knowledge tests, limiting a pooled analysis. Therefore, we carried out a narrative synthesis as per the Cochrane guidelines [13]. Data regarding the perceptions of medical students on the impact of ECE was visually presented using a bar chart constructed with Microsoft Excel. On the other hand, continuous data such as the Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) and Objectively Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) were narratively synthesized to assess the impact of ECE on student empathy and clinical skills, respectively.

Results

Search results

The online search yielded a total of 1195 potential studies. 413 of these records were excluded for being duplicates. The remaining 782 records had their titles and abstracts thoroughly scrutinized, of which 735 articles irrelevant to the objective of the present study were excluded. Another 26 studies intended as editorials, commentaries, and narrative reviews were also excluded. Finally, the remaining 21 studies were subjected to the eligibility criteria, of which 15 met the inclusion criteria. The other 6 studies were excluded for the following reasons: 4 were non-English records and 2 were abstracts without full-texts (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Fifteen papers with 2579 medical students undergoing ECE were included and reviewed. Regarding the study design, 7 were cross-sectional, 2 were quasi-experimental, 1 was a qualitative study, 1 was a longitudinal randomized study, and the remaining 4 were non-randomized interventional studies. All these papers were published between 2012 and 2024 and conducted in countries like India, Iran, Ethiopia, China, and Taiwan. Most of the studies used real patient contact as a form of ECE. Other forms of ECE included case-based learning, shadowing, and pre-clerkship education (Table 1).

Clinical skills

Only three studies [14, 18, 22] in the current systematic review reported the impact of ECE on clinical skills. Mindaye and Tesfay (2022) reported that 52.7% of the medical students who underwent ECE through pre-clerkship education managed to score above the minimum passing score of 60% on the overall physical examination skill [18]. Furthermore, the authors found that about 87% of the students possessed the skills adequate for the respiratory system examination, and 85.7% demonstrated sufficient skills for abdominal examination. On the other hand, Rawekar and colleagues observed that the post-OSCE scores were significantly higher among medical students who received the ECE intervention compared to those subjected to the traditional teaching schedule (7.45 vs. 5.08; p < 0.0001) [22]. Similarly, Sekkizhar et al. [14] found that students in the ECE group had significantly higher mean scores than those in the conventional teaching group (18.01 vs. 14.32; p < 0.001), implying that students in the ECE group acquired and demonstrated improved blood pressure measuring skills.

Communication skills

Three included studies [14, 16, 24] reported the student’s perception on the impact of ECE on communication skills. The average data from these studies showed that the majority of the students agreed that ECE improved their communication skills (42.16% strongly agreed and 46.48% agreed) (Fig. 2). This finding suggests that ECE has the potential to improve the communication skills of medical students.

Bar Chart showing the students’ perceptions on the impact of ECE

Problem-solving skills

One cross-sectional study in the current systematic review reported that the majority of the students subjected to ECE perceived that this teaching method improved their problem-solving skills (Fig. 2), specifically considering alternatives in solving a problem and making reasonable conclusions [15]. This implies that ECE might have a positive impact on the development of problem-solving skills.

Professionalism and empathy

Only one study [17] reported the relationship between ECE and empathy among medical students. According to that study, medical students displayed significantly higher mean empathy scores (measured by the JSE-student version) after ECE compared to the whole sample population before ECE. This significant improvement in empathy scores was observed in both first and second-year students and both male and female students, implying that ECE has a positive impact on empathy regardless of gender and academic year.

In addition, a qualitative study by Liu and colleagues assessed the impacts of ECE on student professionalism based on five major dimensions of medical professionalism, specifically knowledge and clinical skills, teamwork, public health duties, interpersonal skills, and protection of patient rights. The authors of that study claimed that 59% of the students redefined their understanding of medical professionalism after ECE intervention.

Confidence and preparedness

According to Mindaye and Tesfay (2020) [18], the majority of medical students receiving the ECE intervention reported that this method increased their confidence to deal with patients (12.1% strongly agreed and 50.5% agreed) (Fig. 2). On the other hand, data from two studies suggested that about 75% of the students perceived that ECE increased their self-motivation to attend clinics (Fig. 2).

Knowledge

Several included studies reported that the majority of students believed that ECE not only improved their basic medical knowledge, but also motivated them to learn and helped them to correlate what they learn in the classroom with clinical settings (Fig. 2). Additionally, Warkar and colleagues reported that the mean test scores significantly increased from 48% to 66% after ECE intervention, suggesting that ECE has a significant effect on the learning gain of medical students [28].

Quality appraisal outcomes

Tables 2a and 2b in appendix B show the methodological quality of non-comparative and comparative studies, respectively. From these tables, we noticed that all studies scored a rating of zero under the unbiased assessment item. This was because blind evaluation of the study endpoints was not reported across the studies. Furthermore, only one study reported the prospective collection of data. On the other hand, Fig. 3 in appendix B shows the risk of bias summary. From this assessment, we observed that the randomized study had a high risk of performance bias because participants and researchers were not blinded to the intervention. Furthermore, the study had an unclear risk of selection and detection bias due to insufficient information regarding allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessors.

Discussion

The present systematic review synthesized evidence from diverse studies across the world to evaluate the effects of ECE on the competencies of undergraduate medical studies. The evidence from these studies broadly indicated that ECE interventions can positively influence various domains of competency, such as clinical skills, problem-solving skills, communication skills, empathy, professionalism, confidence, and medical knowledge. Therefore, integration of ECE during the early phases of medical training can significantly enhance competency-based medical education (CBME).

For aspiring medical professionals, education in empathy is essential since it improves the rapport between healthcare practitioners and patients, hence augmenting patient-physician satisfaction [29]. The data obtained from Ye and colleagues revealed that an empathy-focused ECE established and enhanced the empathy of medical students, which is a fundamental aspect of medical professionalism. However, the study did not show any significant differences between participants of different sexes and academic years, indicating that ECE may enhance the empathy of medical students irrespective of these factors. This finding contradicts prior research that has shown female students generally had significantly greater empathy ratings than male learners [30–32]. The disparity between this study finding and prior research might be attributed to differences in cultures and sources of students. Therefore, future research should employ several empathy measurements to effectively understand the mechanisms behind the changes in empathy among medical students.

In addition, the synthesized evidence revealed that ECE has the potential to enhance the communication skills of medical students. Therefore, by improving communication skills, ECE can aid medical students adopt a more patient-oriented approach to care, which is crucial in delivering high-quality medical care [33, 34]. Furthermore, studies provide proof that ECE may promote professionalism, which is vital in developing a doctor-patient interaction. As such, ECE has the ability to favorably enhance the acquisition of professional principles and ethical decision-making abilities [35, 36].

The present study also revealed that a great majority of the medical students believed that ECE improved their problem-solving skills, specifically when it comes to solving real-world problems of critically ill patients, providing reasonable conclusions, and encouraging them to employ alternative strategies when tackling patient problems. This finding is supported by previous research articles which reported that ECE can enhance the logical reasoning of medical students [37, 38]. This ability to think rationally can help medical students develop an analytical mind as they progress through their medical training.

While the current systematic review suggests that ECE can improve the competency of medical students, most outcomes relied on students’ self-reported perceptions of competency. Self-reported outcomes may provide useful insights into learners’ experiences, but they are inherently subjective and susceptible to social desirability bias, whereby students may potentially exaggerate their confidence or abilities to conform to anticipated standards. This dependence on self-reporting may restrict the capacity to draw definitive conclusions on actual improvement in competence. Furthermore, there was an overrepresentation of studies from India and Iran, raising questions regarding cultural transferability and curriculum heterogeneity. The medical education systems in these nations may vary from those in other areas regarding curricular frameworks, pedagogical traditions, and resource accessibility, which may influence both the design and the perceived efficacy of ECE. For instance, unlike in the United States, where there is a separate bachelor’s degree and postgraduate MD, in India, the undergraduate degree integrates internship within the MBBS program [39]. Furthermore, significant curricular diversity continues to exist within these settings, with ECE provided via various modalities, including community placements, clinician shadowing, or organized skills workshops.

Despite its benefits in competency development, ECE faces several challenges. For instance, Ewnte and Yigzaw reported that the three most common barriers of ECE implementation were heavy workload (78.6%), poor academic and health institutions linkages (59.5%), and lack of orientation on the process of implementation (35.7%) [15]. On the other hand, Warkar and Asia [28] reported that due to a large number of students per group, there was a significant waiting time to see the patients. This means that ECE can be time-consuming if not properly implemented. Contrary to these studies, Gupta et al. [20] reported that the faculty members disagreed that ECE was time-consuming or added any extra burden in routine schedule. This contradictory evidence might be attributed to the fact that this study employed ECE through laboratory reports, video clips, pictures, and case scenarios, while the other two studies employed ECE via real patient contact in hospitals.

Future research

Future studies should prioritize using objective outcome measures, such as blinded OSCE scores, standardized written or computerized knowledge tests, and structured empathy or professionalism scales. The adoption of these outcomes will not only reduce heterogeneity but also enable future reviews to conduct robust meta-analyses, thereby providing sufficient evidence on the efficacy of ECE in developing the competency of medical students. Most studies in the current systematic review were conducted in low and middle-income countries. Therefore, studies from high-income countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Europe are essential to ensure the global generalizability of our findings on ECE.

Limitations

The present systematic review has some fundamental limitations that should be taken into consideration. First, the majority of the studies were conducted in Asian countries (i.e., India, Iran, China, and Taiwan), with only two in Africa and none in Europe or America. Therefore, the findings of this systematic review might not be generalizable to the whole population across the world. Second, only English-written papers were reviewed, meaning that the data from non-English studies that could have been used to reinforce the scientific purpose of this research were intentionally omitted. This omission might have contributed to selection and publication bias. Finally, all the synthesized results regarding the impact of ECE on competency domains, such as clinical skills, problem-solving skills, communication skills, empathy, professionalism, confidence, and medical knowledge were based on the self-evaluation of medical students. Therefore, it is possible that the effects of ECE were over-estimated as students might not be sincere when answering the questionnaires.

Conclusion

In summary, the evidence in this systematic review has shown that ECE enhances various competency domains, including clinical skills, problem-solving skills, communication skills, empathy, and professionalism of medical students. Furthermore, evidence suggests that ECE can improve the knowledge of medical students by bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-world clinical experiences and motivating students to learn. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously since most studies had performance and selection bias. Moreover, this medical training approach faces challenges, such as time consumption, heavy workload, poor academic and health institutions linkages, and lack of orientation on the process of implementation. Nonetheless, the overall evidence in this study supports the incorporation of ECE in undergraduate curricula to ensure that medical students are fully prepared to offer high-quality care to patients.
