A pilot randomized controlled study of a brief mentalizing enhancement intervention for borderline personality disorder
Authors: Yogev Kivity, Nati Pasternak, Rotem Moshe-Cohen, Shir Ada Basson, Leeav Sheena-Peer, Noa Steinberg, Tom Livni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImpairments in mentalizing, the capacity for understanding mental states, are central to borderline personality disorder (BPD). This study examined a brief mentalizing intervention aiming to enhance the capacity and motivation for mentalizing in BPD.MethodsForty-eight adults with BPD (Sex: 81% female; Gender: 75% female) were randomized to a brief, single-session mentalizing or a control intervention. The mentalizing intervention involved prompts and practice in considering mental states underlying behaviors in interpersonal situations, while the control group involved an emotional sharing intervention. An observer-rated measure of mentalizing ability and a self-report measure of motivation for mentalizing were administered before and after the intervention. Self-report measures of subjective distress and the acceptability of the intervention were collected following the intervention.ResultsA significant improvement in mentalizing abilities was observed following mentalizing-enhancement (d = 0.69, p = .002), but not emotional sharing (d = -0.07, p = .64). Participants in the mentalizing-enhancement group reported significantly lower post-intervention distress than the emotional sharing group (d = 0.64, p = .03). Unexpectedly, the motivation for mentalizing decreased in both groups (mentalizing-enhancement/emotional sharing: d = -1.62/-1.16, respectively, ps < 0.001).DiscussionBrief and focused mentalizing interventions may enhance mentalizing and reduce distress in BPD and could be utilized for tailoring interventions for specific BPD deficits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40479-025-00331-1.

Introduction

Impairments in mentalizing – the ability to understand behaviors in terms of mental states – appear central to borderline personality disorder (BPD), suggesting that interventions enhancing mentalizing could aid BPD treatment [1]. Improving mentalizing may strengthen the capacity for emotion regulation and interpersonal functioning among individuals with BPD. In the current controlled study, we developed and tested a brief micro-intervention to promote mentalizing abilities, focusing on its effects on mentalizing capacity and subjective distress.

BPD is a severe psychological disorder characterized by turbulent relationships, identity instability, difficulty regulating emotions, and impulsive behavior [2]. Individuals with BPD tend to experience frequent fluctuations in mood and stress responses. They often struggle with emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and maintaining stable interpersonal relationships. These challenges increase the risk of self-harm and suicidal behaviors in this population. The prevalence of BPD is estimated at 1% of the general population, with higher rates among treatment-seeking populations in outpatient and inpatient settings (12–22%) [3].

According to some theorists, impaired mentalizing ability is a core factor underlying BPD [4]. Also known as reflective functioning (RF), mentalizing is defined as “the mental process by which an individual implicitly and explicitly interprets the actions of himself and others as meaningful on the basis of intentional mental states such as personal desires, needs, feelings, beliefs, and reasons” [1; p. xxi]. Through reflective capacities, mentalizing enables differentiating between inner and outer reality and understanding behaviors in terms of mental states [5, 6]. More specifically, mentalizing involves making sense of one’s own and others’ mental states, including inferring behaviors rooted in internal worlds [7]. Proper mentalizing allows for safely exploring environments, maintaining close relationships, benefiting from social connections, appropriately trusting others, and effectively regulating distressing emotions [7, 8]. Thus, mentalizing is associated with key capacities like emotion regulation, self-control, and interpersonal functioning, that are known to be impaired in BPD [9–11]. Given these multifaceted implications, mentalizing represents a fundamental ability underlying everyday social interactions [7].

Numerous studies provide evidence for compromised mentalizing capacities in individuals diagnosed with BPD [12]. For instance, research indicates that individuals with BPD demonstrate hypermentalizing tendencies, that is, forming extreme assumptions about others’ mental states that are not grounded in objective social cues [13]. Such deficits in mentalizing are theorized to underlie the interpersonal difficulties and affective dysregulation central to BPD [4]. Overall, empirical findings converge with prominent theoretical models emphasizing mentalizing impairments as core to BPD pathology.

On this theoretical basis, Fonagy and colleagues developed mentalization-based treatment (MBT) for BPD, which seeks to enhance mentalizing capacities specifically within interpersonally evocative contexts [1]. Additionally, recent findings from psychotherapy research demonstrate that interventions that explicitly prompt patients to consider mental states – that is, therapeutic interventions in which patients are directly encouraged to reflect on the thoughts, feelings, desires, and beliefs underlying their own and others’ behaviors in specific interpersonal situations – are associated with greater improvement in mentalizing in BPD patients, compared to interventions that do not include such prompting [8, 14, 15]. Despite these promising findings, traditional individual psychotherapy remains a costly intervention that cannot adequately meet the increasing burden of mental health [16]. This has spurred interest in developing briefer interventions that could enhance mentalizing capacity. Recent findings demonstrated that a 10-session group MBT program significantly improved mentalizing capacity in individuals with personality disorders, with increased mentalizing predicting improvements in emotional reactivity and social functioning [17]. Yet even such abbreviated formats require substantial time and resources. Therefore, further inquiry is needed into the potential benefits of brief, focused interventions aimed at enhancing mentalizing abilities, either as add-ons to existing treatment protocols or as standalone, low-intensity interventions. Such a focus is also consistent with recent writings emphasizing the relevance of mentalizing as a framework that guides a variety of clinical interventions rather than being a concept that is only relevant in the context of MBT [18].

In the present study, we developed a standardized single-session micro-intervention for enhancing mentalizing abilities, which was based on principles outlined in the MBT manual [19]. Focused interventions for psychopathology in general, and specifically for BPD, are in high demand. Brief interventions of this nature can yield rapid improvements, which may be particularly important within tumultuous circumstances. Furthermore, while not intended to replace long-term treatments, such interventions can help address the needs of individuals awaiting more comprehensive treatment, mitigating the challenges of prolonged waiting periods before obtaining comprehensive care.

Thus, the study aims to investigate the impact of a new, single session, mentalizing-enhancement intervention compared to a control emotional sharing intervention (without an instruction to consider mental states of self and others) among adults with BPD. We evaluated the acceptability of the intervention among the two groups, assessing whether participants found the intervention credible and whether they intended to utilize the acquired skills in the future. Moreover, we assessed the effect of the intervention on participants’ mentalizing abilities using segments taken from the Brief Reflective Functioning Interview (BRFI) [20] before and after the intervention. Additionally, we examined the intervention’s effect on participants’ motivation for mentalizing, measured before and after the intervention, because preliminary evidence suggests that motivation for mentalizing predicts actual mentalizing capacity [21]. Finally, to assess the immediate emotional impact of the interventions, we examined the level of distress after the intervention in both groups. Method

Participants

Fifty-one adult participants diagnosed with BPD were recruited through ads and randomly assigned to two groups: a mentalizing-enhancement intervention group and an emotional sharing intervention control group. Inclusion in the study required a primary diagnosis of BPD according to the Structured Interview for DSM-IV Personality (SIDP-IV). Exclusion criteria included acute and high risk of suicidality, severe substance use, severe eating disorders, and active psychosis (all of which would have rendered the BPD diagnosis secondary).

Three participants from the mentalizing-enhancement group dropped out prior to the intervention phase of the study, resulting in a final sample of 48 participants (23 in the mentalizing-enhancement group and 25 in the emotional sharing group). In terms of sex, the final sample comprised of 39 females (81.2%), and nine males (18.8%). In terms of gender, the final sample comprised of 36 females (75.0%), nine males (18.8%), and three individuals identifying as other (e.g., non-binary gender; 6.2%). Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 50 years (M = 25.98, SD = 7.64).

As this study is part of a larger project, the sample size was primarily determined by the broader research considerations. Sensitivity power analyses conducted in G*Power revealed that with the current sample size (n = 48) and α = 0.05, the study had 80% power to detect an effect size of d = 0.73 for between-group differences (independent samples t-test) and η² = 0.21 for an interaction effect (group × time).

Interventions

The interventions were delivered by four female research assistants that were either advanced undergraduate psychology students or have recently obtained their B.A. in psychology. The students were trained and supervised by the first and third authors – a licensed clinical psychologist with expertise in BPD and an advanced clinical psychology graduate student.

The mentalizing-enhancement intervention (see Sect. 2 of the Supplemental Materials for the full protocol, translated into English) was based on MBT principles [19]. It primarily focused on explicit, controlled mentalizing – deliberately pausing to reflect on mental states – as this is the type of mentalizing that psychotherapy aims to strengthen [22]. Moreover, the intervention mostly focused on internal aspects of mental states (thoughts and feelings) rather than external behavioral cues, which is consistent with the notion that individuals with BPD tend to rely excessively on external features and may benefit from practice in considering internal experiences [22]. The intervention included psychoeducation, in which participants learned on what mentalizing is, the importance of mentalizing, and the various aspects of mentalizing. Specifically, participants were taught that mentalizing involves both cognitive mentalizing (thinking about thoughts and beliefs) and affective mentalizing (being attuned to emotions and feelings), and that effective mentalizing requires integrating both dimensions – understanding through both “the head” and “the heart”. Participants also learned that mentalizing can be directed toward the self (reflecting on one’s own mental states) or toward others (considering others’ mental states). Additionally, the research assistants engaged the participants in open discussion on why mentalizing may be specifically important for them and in which situations they find it more challenging to engage in mentalizing. Moreover, participants were informed about possible pitfalls in mentalizing and, on the other hand, about skills that can assist in its implementation. For instance, participants were taught about obstacles such as overconfidence in interpreting others’ motives (e.g., immediately interpreting a partner’s fatigue as rejection) and avoidance of mental states through an excessive focus on cognitions rather than emotions or the other way around (e.g., a person finding out about their spouse’s serious illness might turn to searching for information on the internet without addressing the emotional impact). They were also introduced to the “Three D’s” framework to enhance their mentalizing abilities – a catchy and useful heuristic for Delay, Deliberate, and Develop which encourages participants to pause before reacting (Delay), be attentive to their internal feelings and consider their own and others’ mental states (Deliberate) and form an interpretation of the situation while remaining open to alternatives (Develop a hypothesis).

Finally, participants practiced applying mentalizing to three generic, hypothetical interpersonal situations. For example, one hypothetical scenario involved a workplace situation in which a participant was training a new employee who was progressing slowly. Participants were guided to consider both the new employee’s perspective (e.g., “Perhaps this is their first job in the field, and they do not know it well enough yet? Are they perhaps concerned about coming to a new place?“) and their own mental states (e.g., “Maybe I’m impatient because I’m in the midst of a family argument, and I also sometimes feel insecure at work”). This exercise illustrated how mentalizing involves attending to both self and other, and integrating both cognitive understanding (e.g., lack of experience) and affective understanding (e.g., worry, insecurity). Participants also practiced on three recent real-life interpersonal situations they chose from their own experiences. Research assistants provided feedback and guidance during these mentalizing exercises (such as suggesting that the participant consider additional options for understanding mental states). The structure of the emotional sharing intervention (see Sect. 2 of the Supplemental Materials for the full protocol, translated into English) was designed to be as similar as possible to that of the mentalizing intervention. It covered the same components (such as psychoeducation and application to examples) but focused solely on describing participants’ feelings and emotions about interpersonal situations without explicitly considering intentional mental states underlying behaviors (which is the central component of mentalizing).

Measures

Structured interview for DSM-IV personality (SIDP-IV) [23], translated to Hebrew [24]

The SIDP-IV is a valid and reliable semi-structured interview that assesses each of the ten DSM-IV personality disorders and was used to establish the diagnosis of BPD as the primary disorder. The interview includes 79 questions, each assessing a specific criterion of one of the personality disorders. The diagnosticians were advanced clinical psychology graduate students trained and supervised by the first author. All interviews were videotaped and to evaluate inter-rater reliability, 30 interviews (of the larger sample that also included the control groups) were double coded by a second independent diagnostician. For BPD diagnosis, the raw agreement was 96.67%, and kappa was 0.92. For all other personality disorders, the raw agreement was 70% and kappa = was 0.34.

Diagnosis interview for anxiety, mood, and OCD and related neuropsychiatric disorders (DIAMOND) [25]

The DIAMOND is a semi-structured interview designed to assess various psychiatric disorders according to DSM-IV criteria. It was used to evaluate exclusion criteria, which included severe substance addiction, eating disorders, and active psychosis. In addition, it was used to assess the comorbidity of other clinical disorders. The diagnosticians were advanced clinical psychology graduate students trained and supervised by the first author. To evaluate inter-rater reliability, two diagnosticians independently assigned diagnoses based on a subset of 30 interviews. Percent agreement and kappa values were as follows (grouped by DSM-5 categories): 100% agreement with kappa = 1.00 for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD); 80% agreement with kappa = 0.60 for anxiety disorders; 93.33% agreement with kappa = 0.76 for trauma- and stressor-related disorders; 96.67% agreement with kappa = 0.93 for depressive disorders; 93.33% agreement with kappa = 0.63 for eating disorders; and 96.67% agreement with kappa = 0.84 for substance use disorder. For the bipolar disorders, there was 100% agreement, but kappa could not be calculated because the prevalence was 0%.

Personality assessment inventory-borderline features scale (PAI-BOR) [26]

The PAI-BOR is a 24-item self-report measure designed to assess the severity of BPD symptoms across four domains: affective instability, identity problems, negative relationships, and self-harm. Items are rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (“false, not at all true”) to 3 (“very true”). Internal consistency in the current study was α = 0.86.

Depression, anxiety, stress scales-21 (DASS-21) [27]

The DASS-21 is a 21-item self-report measure of symptomatic distress comprising three 7-item subscales that assess depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms experienced over the past week. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applied to me very much or most of the time”). Internal consistency for the subscales in the current study ranged from α = 0.81 to 0.89.

The reflective functioning scale (RFS) [5] applied to an abbreviated BRFI [20]

Mentalizing ability was assessed using sections from the semi-structured Brief Reflective Functioning Interview (BRFI), which requires participants to reflect on their relationships with attachment figures. We used a shortened version of this interview which contains two parts: one focusing on the attachment figure and the other on the relationship with the attachment figure, with each part consisting of two questions: 1a) “Can you tell me about this person?“; 1b) “How do you think s/he came to be that way (that kind of person)?“; 2a) “Can you tell me about your relationship (with that person)?“; 2b) “Can you tell me how your relationship came to be that way?“.

To assess changes in mentalizing ability, the interview was conducted twice, before and after the intervention, each time focusing on one, non-parental attachment figure (because parental figures were included in a BRFI in an earlier stage of the larger study). To avoid possible order effects (e.g., participants identifying a closer attachment figure in the pre-intervention BRFI relative to the post-intervention BRFI), participants were asked to identify the two attachment figures in advance, and the research assistant randomly assigned these figures to the pre- or post-intervention administration. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Then, the RFS, in its original form, was applied to the sections of the BRFI. That is, although we used a shorter version of the BRFI, the RFS that was applied to it was used in its original form. Using the guidelines for the RFS, trained raters identify passages that contain qualitative RF markers, as Fonagy et al. [5] suggested, and how many of them were present. Four aspects of understanding mental states were considered: understanding the nature of mental states, linking mental states and behaviors, understanding the development of mental states, and applying mentalizing with regard to the interviewer. Transcribed passages were rated on a scale of − 1 (negative reflective functioning) to 9 (exceptional reflective functioning). Scores were calculated separately for each attachment figure as a measure of change in mentalizing. Due to a technical problem, the recording for one participant in the mentalizing-enhancement group was lost, resulting in a sample size of 47 for analyses involving mentalizing ability. The raters were four clinical psychology graduate students, trained to reliability by Howard Steele (one of the developers of the scale) prior to coding in the study and supervised weekly by the first author to discuss ratings to maintain reliability and avoid rater drift. Raters were blind to the type of intervention and to the order of the interview (i.e., which attachment figure was discussed first). Inter-rater reliability was evaluated on the full BRFIs that were administered at the beginning of the larger study. A subset of 24 interviews was independently double coded by a primary anchor coder and one of three additional trained coders. The anchor coder served as the reference point, as he was involved in coding throughout the entire period, whereas the other coders each coded only during specific periods of the study. The average Pearson correlation between coders’ pairs was r = .76 (raw correlations: 0.98, 0.90, 0.41), and the average Intra Class Correlation (2,1) was 0.69 (raw ICCs: 0.87, 0.85, 0.36). Due to the low reliability of one of the coders, a separate, reliable coder reviewed all the former coder’s ratings and made any necessary corrections. When removing the previous codes done by the former coder from reliability calculations, the average correlation was r = .94 and the average ICC was 0.86.

Motivation subscale of the mentalization scale (MentS-M)[28]

The MentS-M is a 10-item, 5-point Likert subscale of the MentS self-report questionnaire that specifically measures motivation for mentalizing. It includes statements such as “I find it important to understand what happens in my relationships with people close to me”. The Hebrew version, examined by Steinberg et al. [21] in a non-clinical sample, exhibited an internal consistency of α = 0.67. In the current study, internal consistency was α = 0.69 in the pre-intervention assessment and α = 0.49 in the post-intervention assessment.

Subjective units of distress scale (SUDS) [29]

The SUDS is a scale commonly used to assess subjective levels of distress. Participants were asked to rate their distress on a visual analogue scale ranging from no distress to extreme distress. Ratings were then converted into a 0–100 scale. Data for one participant in the mentalizing-enhancement group was missing, resulting in a sample size of 47 for analyses involving SUDS.

Acceptability of the intervention

We assessed the acceptability of the intervention by asking participants four questions: “To what extent were the skills provided to you clear?”, “To what extent were the skills provided to you convincing?”, “To what extent do the skills address a central difficulty in your daily life?”, “To what extent do you think you will use these skills in the future?”. Participants responded on a scale ranging from 1 (“Not at all”) to 7 (“To a very great extent”).

Procedure

Following an initial phone screening, participants were scheduled for an in-person diagnostic interview which included the SIDP-IV and the DIAMOND. Eligible participants returned for a baseline assessment, followed by a weeklong Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA; not included in the current research). Subsequently, participants returned for a post-EMA assessment and an intervention session. During the session, the pre- and post-intervention assessments were conducted immediately before and after the intervention, respectively. At the beginning of this session, participants completed the MentS-M questionnaire, along with additional measures that are not included in the current study. Following this, participants underwent the first part of the BRFI. They were then randomly assigned to and administered one of two intervention conditions. Following the intervention, participants completed the second part of the BRFI and as well as the MentS-M. Finally, participants rated their distress as well as the acceptability of the intervention.

Data-analysis

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study, and we follow the Journal Article Reporting Standards [30].

Data were analyzed using R version 4.4.1 [31] and the following packages: ggplot2 version 3.5.1 [32], afex version 1.3-1 [33], effectsize version 1.0.0 [34], and psych version 2.4.6 [35]. Group differences in changes in mentalizing ability and motivation from pre- to post-intervention were examined using planned contrasts comparing the levels of these variables at pre- and post-intervention within each group. This was done within the framework of two-way ANOVAs with group (mentalizing-enhancement vs. emotional sharing) as a between-subjects factor, time (Pre- vs. Post-Intervention) as a within-subject factor and mentalizing ability / motivation for mentalizing as dependent variables. Group differences in distress level as well as in the acceptability of the intervention were examined using independent samples t-tests. Partial η² was used as a measure of effect size in ANOVA while Cohen’s d was used in t-tests. We also report calculated Cohen’s d for pre-post controlled designs (ppc2) [36] as measure of group differences in pre-post changes in outcome measures.

For the MentS-M, which was the only multi-item self-report measure, data validity was evaluated by examining response variability. All participants showed variability across items at both before and after the intervention (minimum SD = 0.42), indicating no evidence of inattentive responding. Accordingly, no participants were excluded based on response validity criteria.

The duration of the interventions was also examined, revealing that the mentalizing-enhancement intervention (M = 49.21, SD = 19.90 min) was significantly longer than the emotional sharing intervention (M = 36.60, SD = 16.07 min; t(45) = 2.40, p = .02, d = 0.70, 95% CI [0.11, 1.29]). This difference in intervention duration, although somewhat small in absolute terms (13 min), is addressed as a potential limitation in the discussion.

Acceptability of the interventions

There were no significant differences between the two groups on any of the acceptability items, with ratings consistently high across all items for both interventions, including the degree to which the interventions were clear (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 6.22, SD = 1.17; emotional sharing: M = 6.68, SD = 0.56; t(46) = -1.78, p = .082, d = -0.51, 95% CI [-1.09, 0.07]), were convincing (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 5.35, SD = 1.58; emotional sharing: M = 5.84, SD = 1.21; t(46) = -1.21, p = .23, d = -0.35, 95% CI [-0.92, 0.22]), addressed a central difficulty in the participants’ daily lives (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 5.13, SD = 1.71; emotional sharing: M = 5.00, SD = 1.44; t(46) = 0.29, p = .78, d = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.48, 0.65]) and provided skill that the participants intended to use in the future (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 5.35, SD = 1.23; emotional sharing: M = 5.20, SD = 1.35; t(46) = 0.39, p = .69, d = 0.11, 95% CI [-0.45, 0.68]).

Change in mentalizing abilities

To examine our hypothesis regarding the interaction between intervention type and time on mentalizing abilities, a 2 (intervention: mentalizing-enhancement vs. emotional sharing) × 2 (time: pre- to post-intervention) ANOVA was conducted. The main effects of intervention, F(1, 45) = 0.17, p = .69, η²ₚ = 0.00, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00], and time, F(1, 45) = 1.62, p = .21, η²ₚ = 0.03, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00], were not significant. The planned contrasts examining the simple effects of time on mentalizing abilities for each group separately (Visualized in Fig. 1) showed that, as hypothesized, the mentalizing-enhancement group showed a significant improvement from pre- (M = 2.64, SD = 1.05) to post-intervention (M = 3.05, SD = 1.13), t(21) = 3.25, p = .002 with a moderate effect size (d = 0.69, 95% CI [0.22, 1.15]). In contrast, the emotional sharing group showed no significant change from pre- (M = 3.00, SD = 1.08) to post-intervention (M = 2.92, SD = 1.12), t(24) = -0.37, p = .64, with a negligible effect size (d = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.47, 0.32]). Despite the observed group differences in simple effects, the time × intervention type interaction effect was not significant, although the p value was close to significance, F(1, 45) = 3.58, p = .06, η²ₚ = 0.07, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00], ppc2 = 0.45.

Motivation for mentalizing

To examine our hypothesis regarding the interaction between intervention and time on motivation for mentalizing, a 2 (intervention: mentalizing-enhancement vs. emotional sharing) × 2 (time: pre- to post-intervention) ANOVA was conducted. In stark contrast to our hypothesis, the results revealed a significant main effect of time, F(1, 46) = 87.25, p < .001, η²ₚ = 0.65, 95% CI [0.52, 1.00], indicating a substantial overall decrease in motivation for mentalizing from pre- to post-intervention across both groups (visualized in Fig. 1). The main effect of intervention, F(1, 46) = 0.33, p = .57, η²ₚ = 0.01, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00] was not significant. Planned contrasts that examined the simple effects of time on motivation for mentalizing for each group separately showed that, contrary to our hypotheses, the motivation for mentalizing decreased significantly from pre- (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 4.11, SD = 0.55; emotional sharing: M = 4.15, SD = 0.52) to post-intervention (mentalizing-enhancement: M = 3.40, SD = 0.58; emotional sharing: M = 3.52, SD = 0.53) in both the mentalizing-enhancement group, t(22) = -7.77, p < .001, and the emotional sharing group, t(24) = -5.78, p < .001. Effect sizes were large for both the mentalizing-enhancement group (d = -1.62, 95% CI [-2.24, -0.98]) and the emotional sharing group (d = -1.16, 95% CI [-1.66, -0.64]), indicating substantial decreases in motivation for mentalizing in both groups, and the interaction between intervention and time was not significant, F(1, 46) = 0.32, p = .57, η²ₚ = 0.01, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00], ppc2 = -0.15.

To further explore this unexpected finding, we conducted a post-hoc analysis to examine whether the decrease was driven by specific MentS-M items. Given that the MentS-M includes both state-like and trait-like items, we considered the possibility that the observed decrease might have been primarily driven by the trait-like items, as participants may have realized – following the intervention – that they engage in mentalizing less than they had initially believed. However, the results revealed a consistent decrease across all items on the MentS-M scale, suggesting no additional substantive insights (see Sect. 1 of the Supplemental Materials for the full findings).

Distress levels

Hypothesis 4 posited that the post-intervention distress levels would be lower in the mentalizing-enhancement intervention group compared to the emotional sharing control group. An independent samples t-test comparing post-intervention distress levels (SUDS) between the two groups supported this hypothesis, revealing a significant difference, t(45) = 2.18, p = .03. Participants in the mentalizing group reported lower levels of distress (M = 17.30, SD = 19.10) compared to those in the emotional sharing group (M = 30.92, SD = 23.19) (Visualized in Fig. 2). The effect size, as measured by Cohen’s d, was medium (d = 0.64, 95% CI [0.05, 1.22]), indicating a moderate and meaningful difference in distress levels between the two groups.

The impact of outliers

Potential univariate outliers for the three key variables (RFS, MentS-M, and SUDS) were assessed visually using boxplots. No outliers were identified through this method. Subsequently, a quantitative analysis was performed by calculating standardized z-scores, with outliers defined as values exceeding ± 2.24. A total of four outliers were identified: one in pre-intervention MentS-M, one in post-intervention MentS-M, one in pre-intervention RFS, and one in SUDS. Each case was reviewed, and no indication of data entry error was found. Sensitivity analyses were then conducted by repeating the key analyses with the relevant outliers excluded. The main findings remained largely unchanged, with the exception that the time × intervention interaction for RFS, which had originally been close to significant, became statistically significant (as hypothesized). These results suggest that the findings are robust to the presence of extreme values (see Sect. 1 of the Supplemental Materials for the full findings).

Discussion

This pilot randomized controlled study aimed to investigate the effects of a brief, single-session mentalizing-enhancement intervention on mentalizing abilities, motivation for mentalizing, and distress levels in individuals with BPD. Our findings provide promising support for the efficacy of targeted, short-term mentalizing-enhancement intervention in this population, with improvements observed in mentalizing abilities and lower levels of distress relative to the control group, despite an unexpected decrease in motivation for mentalizing in both groups.

Our primary hypothesis regarding an improvement of mentalizing abilities was supported. The mentalizing-enhancement group demonstrated a significant improvement in mentalizing abilities from pre- to post-intervention, while the emotional sharing group did not. Importantly, this improvement was measured by independent observers while participants were reflecting on their relationship with significant others. Specifically, the mean reflective functioning score in the mentalizing group increased from 2.64 pre-intervention to 3.05 post-intervention, a moderate improvement of 0.41 points. This improvement could represent an important first step toward reducing impairments in mentalizing in BPD, but it should also be noted that the improvement corresponds to a moderate effect size and that both the pre- and post-intervention scores were relatively low. In general, effect sizes for changes in mentalizing seem modest in absolute terms. For example, Levy et al. [37] found that after a year of transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), a psychodynamic treatment that addresses mentalizing [8], participants with BPD improved by 1.25 points on the reflective functioning scale. In a more recent study, Fischer-Kern et al. [38] reported an improvement of 0.56 points after a year of TFP.

While the observed improvement in the mentalizing-enhancement group is significant, it is important to emphasize that this brief intervention is not intended to replace long-term treatment, nor should it be considered a standalone treatment. Rather, it could serve as a supplement to existing therapeutic approaches such as MBT and Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT). Within MBT, it could provide focused psychoeducation and structured practice in mentalizing skills. Within DBT, it could be incorporated as an additional module alongside existing skills training components. Additionally, this intervention could offer a low-intensity option for individuals awaiting long-term treatment (cf. Weinberg et al., 2006 [39]). This is consistent with findings by Huxley et al. [40], who found that a 4-session brief intervention, focusing on psychoeducation, self-care and developing a plan for future treatments, can reduce BPD symptoms and serve as a treatment step linking acute responses to longer-term treatment options for individuals with personality disorders. Notably, our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that even a single-session intervention may yield significant benefits, which has not, to our knowledge, been previously examined in individuals with BPD.

Importantly, Fischer-Kern et al. [38] found that improvements in mentalizing were significantly correlated with improvements in personality organization, suggesting that improved mentalizing may constitute a pathway toward reduced personality pathology. Moreover, a recent systematic review by Luyten et al. [41] reported that the majority of studies found mentalizing to be a mediator of change in psychotherapy, although findings were not entirely consistent across studies. In another systematic review, Vogt and Norman [42] found that mentalization-based therapy (MBT) led to significant reductions in both BPD symptoms and the severity of comorbid disorders, in addition to increasing quality of life. Thus, as mentalizing is considered a core impairment in BPD [4], enhancing this ability could reduce the severity of the disorder.

However, as we did not include a follow-up assessment, the sustainability of these effects over time remains uncertain, and we cannot determine whether they have impact on BPD symptoms. It is important to consider the possibility that the observed improvement may partly reflect a training effect. Given the findings of the study, this possibility seems unlikely, as the emotional sharing group also completed both BRFI assessments without showing comparable improvement. Nevertheless, given the structural similarity between the intervention exercises and the BRFI questions – both involving reflection on mental states underlying behaviors in interpersonal situations – it is possible that participants learned to respond in a more reflective manner on the assessment rather than achieving deeper or more enduring changes in their capacity to mentalize. Importantly, our results provide evidence that reflective functioning can be directly trained in a single session. Future studies could examine whether these changes are maintained over time and, if not, whether repeated practice over time, through multiple sessions or integration into ongoing therapy, could lead to more substantial internalization and sustained improvements.

Additionally, participants in the mentalizing-enhancement group reported significantly lower post-intervention distress compared to the emotional sharing group. These findings align with previous findings that suggest that mentalizing has a down-regulatory effect on distress. For instance, Kivity et al. [8] found that in-session mentalizing predicted lower emotional arousal in psychotherapies for BPD. Our results suggest that even a brief mentalizing intervention may have immediate effects on distress levels. However, a significant limitation should be considered when interpreting this finding. Unlike our assessment of mentalizing abilities and motivation, distress was only measured post-intervention. This methodological constraint introduces important considerations when interpreting our findings on distress levels. Specifically, we are unable to determine whether the observed between-group difference in distress is attributable to a pre-existing difference in distress (despite the random assignment to groups), a decrease in distress levels in the mentalizing-enhancement group, an increase in distress in the emotional sharing group, or a combination of these effects. It is possible that the emotional sharing intervention was more affectively activating and thus provoked greater distress, given its exclusive focus on emotional experience without the regulatory framework that mentalizing provides. The absence of pre-intervention distress data precludes a definitive interpretation of the intervention’s impact on this outcome measure.

Another methodological limitation concerns the unequal duration of the interventions. The mentalizing-enhancement session was significantly longer than the emotional sharing session, which raises the possibility that the observed group differences in change in mentalizing abilities and in distress levels may be partially attributable to differences in intervention duration rather than to the intervention content itself. However, although this difference was statistically significant, it was not substantial in absolute terms, making it less likely to account for the effects observed. A further limitation is the possibility that nonspecific factors, such as the therapeutic alliance, which have been found to be associated with effective mentalizing [43], may have differed between the intervention conditions. However, it should be noted that research assistants were instructed to adhere closely to the intervention protocols with no additional validation or empathic responses beyond what was scripted, making it less likely that there were substantial differences in these factors between conditions. Additionally, because the sample was predominantly female, the extent to which these conclusions generalize to male populations is limited and requires further research.

Contrary to our expectations, we observed a significant decrease in motivation for mentalizing in both groups. This unexpected finding may reflect the challenging nature of engaging with one’s own and others’ mental states, particularly for individuals with BPD. Both the mentalizing-enhancement and the emotional sharing control interventions involved introspection and sharing of one’s own emotions, processes that can be uncomfortable or distressing for this population. This discomfort may temporarily reduce the motivation to engage in mentalizing, even as actual mentalizing abilities improve and distress decreases. These results can also be partially attributed to the nature of the measure used to assess motivation to mentalize. In this measure, most items assess an individual’s general, trait-like motivation for mentalizing in their everyday life, rather than their state-specific motivation at the moment of assessment. It is possible that the decrease observed in this measure is related to an improved understanding of mentalizing following the intervention. As they gain a better grasp of what mentalizing entails, participants may become more aware of the difficulties associated with implementing it in their daily lives. This increased awareness could lead to a more realistic, and potentially lower, self-assessment of their motivation to mentalize. However, this explanation can only partly explain our findings, as it does not explain why the motivation for mentalizing decreased in both groups equally.

Despite the measurement challenges, our findings offer valuable insights into the potential of brief mentalizing interventions for individuals with BPD. Our results provide initial proof-of-concept that targeted mentalizing prompts can rapidly improve abilities highly relevant to BPD deficits. Given the high prevalence and severity of BPD, even transient gains from a single-session intervention are noteworthy. The potential for brief interventions to yield immediate benefits in mentalizing abilities and distress reduction is particularly significant, considering the centrality of mentalizing deficits in BPD and the challenges individuals with BPD face in regulating emotions.

Future research should build upon these findings by examining the long-term effects of brief mentalizing interventions, which components of the intervention (e.g., the psychoeducation part vs. the actual practice) were most influential in driving the increase in mentalizing, and which facets of mentalizing improved the most (e.g., cognitive vs. affective, self- vs. other-oriented). Future studies could also assess mentalizing and distress levels during the session of the intervention along with other in-session process variables, which would deepen our understanding of the intervention’s mentalizing learning processes, their impact on emotional arousal, and the in-session dynamics of the intervention, as well as differences in these processes between the two intervention conditions. In addition, future research should explore potential moderators of treatment efficacy (e.g., which patient profiles are most likely to benefit from the intervention), and utilize more sensitive measures of both mentalizing abilities and motivation. Replication with larger sample sizes is important to confirm and extend our findings. This preliminary study has paved the way for additional research aimed at further optimizing techniques to efficiently ameliorate specific mentalizing impairments in BPD. For instance, follow-up research could examine whether certain interventions effectively reduce hypermentalizing tendencies in BPD, whereas other techniques best address hypomentalizing. Future studies might also consider implementing multiple intervention sessions to assess whether repeated exposure to mentalizing techniques could lead to more substantial or sustained improvements.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides encouraging evidence for the potential of brief, focused mentalizing-enhancement interventions in improving outcomes for individuals with BPD. As we continue to refine our understanding of mentalizing processes and develop more precise measurement tools, we move closer to creating effective, accessible interventions that can significantly improve the lives of individuals struggling with BPD.
