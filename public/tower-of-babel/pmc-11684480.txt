Performance Analysis of Pipe-Jacking Waste Soils Solidified with Quick Lime and Fly Ash under Balanced Earth Pressure Conditions
Authors: Youjun Xu, Zhenyu Li, Zhigang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With its advantages of high cross-sectional utilization, shallow depth, and uninterrupted surface road traffic, the pipe-jacking method has been widely used in underground passages, metro stations, and other projects. However, this leads to a large volume of pipe-jacking waste soils that must be processed. Pipe-jacking waste soils are different from shield-tunneling waste soils. Therefore, it is not appropriate to simply use the same treatment method for shield-tunneling waste soils in the treatment of pipe-jacking waste soils. In this study, pipe-jacking waste soil samples were improved with 7% polyacrylamide (PAM) and 12% sodium-based bentonite solutions, with good performance being achieved. Based on this, quick lime and fly ash were used in the solidification of pipe-jacking waste soils, and experiments with different solution concentrations and solidification material additions were conducted, involving tests of compression, freeze–thaw cycling, wet–dry cycling, and microstructure. The results indicate that within the ranges of PAM and sodium-based bentonite addition ratios applied in this study, the solidification effects of quick lime and fly ash will not be significantly reduced during the improvement processes of pipe-jacking waste soils under balanced earth pressure conditions. Instead, it was found that there was an increase of up to 16% in the strength of pipe-jacking waste soils. Structural compactness can be primarily enhanced by gelatinous PAM, while sodium-based bentonite can promote the formation of hydrated colloids (such as C–S–H) and fill soil pores with hydrated gelatinous bentonite particles, thereby enhancing soil stability.

Introduction

With the rapid development of urban underground space, the earth pressure-balanced pipe-jacking method has been widely used in such engineering projects as pedestrian underpasses, subway station entrances and exits, tunnels underneath city roads, and urban underground commercial space development, representing a development direction of the construction technology for short-to-medium tunnels with rectangular cross sections in future cities.1 In order to ensure the smooth progression of the pipe-jacking projects, it is normally necessary to add high-molecule soil conditioners in waste soils on the excavation surfaces and in the soil pressure chambers to improve waste soils to an ideal state of fluid molding, thus achieving a dynamic balance between water and soil pressure on the excavation surfaces.2 Against the backdrop of extensive implementation of pipe-jacking projects, it is of critical importance to conduct research on the disposal and resource utilization of solid waste soils containing such conditioners as high-molecule materials. Current studies on resource utilization of waste soils mostly focus on waste soils in shield tunneling.24 Xiao et al.3 prepared Controlled Low Strength Material (CLSM) using shield-tunneling waste soils, water, cement, medium sand, and fly ash in certain proportions. CLSM can be used to replace some gelatinous backfill materials, achieving a strength exceeding the requirements of unconfined compressive strength (0.3–0.7 MPa) on high-quality soils. However, its 28 day unconfined compressive strength is less than 8.3 MPa. Horpibulsuk et al.4 added sodium silicate and sodium hydroxide to the soil mass as alkali activators to activate blast-furnace slag powder and fly ash powder. The experiment showed that blast-furnace slag powder and fly ash powder present good solidification effects on the soil mass. In the process of earth pressure-balanced shield-tunneling excavation, Grenni et al.5 used foam agents to improve the flowing properties of soils to be solidified. After a curing period of 28 days, they found that the solidified soil mass presented good mechanical properties. Therefore, they proposed that earth pressure-balanced shield-tunneling waste soils with the addition of foam agents exhibit the value of recycled use. Petitat et al.6 used a set of fully automatic processing systems to process gravel generated during excavation, with the purpose of immediately processing gravel during excavation for recycled use and reduction of environmental pollution. Cardoni et al.7 performed a biodegradation analysis on earth pressure-balanced shield-tunneling waste soils with the addition of two foam agents, with the finding that these two foam agents were almost completely degraded in waste soils after a period of 28 days. Hu et al.8 conducted reasonable recycling and reuse of gravel, sand, and water contained in waste soils generated in tunnel excavation to make filter cakes and unsintered bricks by replacing clay rather than disposing of them in landfills as wastes. Xie et al.25 used geopolymer to solidify construction waste and found that an 8% concentration of geopolymer had a relatively good solidification effect. Wang et al.26 developed a solidifying agent by combining various industrial wastes, such as steel slag, to create a mixture with effective solidification properties. This solidifying agent enhances the utilization of waste materials. Wu et al.27 used a blend of carbide slag oil and granulated blast-furnace slag to stabilize heavy metal-contaminated clay. This mixed solidifying agent demonstrated an effective stabilization performance.

Based on the above analysis, it can be seen that the most common method for treating waste soils in current engineering projects is to use solidification technology to solidify waste soils, with the influences of solidification agents on the soil mass components primarily considered. However, most studies do not focus on the impact of the impurities present in waste soil itself. In this study, we specifically explore the influence of the main impurities, which have not been adequately addressed in previous research. The mechanism of soil stabilization involving pipe-jacking muck, which contains PAM and sodium bentonite, remains unclear. The influence of soil conditioners on the solidification mechanisms of waste soils remains unclear. Pipe-jacking waste soils contain high-molecule conditioners. It is essential to explore the functioning mechanisms of conditioners in the solidifying processes of solidification agents on waste soils and carry out relevant research on solidification technology. Therefore, in this study, one industrial water supply pipeline project and one reclaimed water reuse project in a metal deep-processing industrial park in a certain city were used as the research background. For earth pressure-balanced pipe-jacking waste soils containing such conditioners as PAM and bentonite, quick lime and fly ash were tentatively used as solidification agents to solidify waste soils into road sub-base materials, thus exploring the solidification effects and mechanisms of waste soils from both macroscopic and microscopic perspectives.

Indoor Experimental Results and Discussion

Analysis of Unconfined Compressive Strength

In order to explore the influences of pipe-jacking waste soil conditioners on the unconfined compressive strength of solidified soils, tests of the unconfined compressive strength were carried out under different mixing ratios of conditioners and solidification agents. From Figure 4, it can be seen that under fixed mixing ratios of sodium-based bentonite solutions and PAM solutions, increases in the quick lime and fly ash contents can lead to certain increases in the unconfined compressive strength of solidified soils, with other conditions remaining unchanged. Specifically, under test conditions P9 and P10, unconfined compressive strength of higher than 0.5 MPa was measured, satisfying the requirements on road base materials for highways.

Unconfined compressive strength of pipe-jacking waste soils with different mixing ratios of quick lime/fly ash.

Figure 5 shows that under a certain mixing ratio of solidification agent, the unconfined compressive strength of solidified waste soil at 7 and 28 d slightly increases with the concentration of PAM solution. This indicates that PAM solutions present relatively insignificant influences on the solidification effectiveness of quick lime and fly ash, primarily due to the fact that PAM can form viscous and gelatinous substances in water. These viscous gels exhibit good adsorption and bonding capacities10 and increase the viscosity and adhesion properties of soil masses, facilitating the formation of dense structures of waste soils.

Unconfined compressive strength of waste soils under different concentrations of PAM solution.

Figure 6 shows that under a certain mixing ratio of solidification agent, the unconfined compressive strength of waste soil also slightly increases with the concentration of the sodium-based bentonite solution. This is primarily due to the fact that sodium-based bentonite can take the form of a colloid in water, presenting a certain capacity of adsorption.11 Bentonite colloids can be adsorbed in residual pores of waste soils solidified with quick lime and fly ash, achieving more stable structures among these soils.12 During the interactions between bentonite and water, exchangeable cations will be exuded from bentonite, leading to changes in the chemical properties of water solutions.13

Unconfined compressive strength of waste soils under different concentrations of sodium-based bentonite solutions.

Analysis of Wet-Dry Cycling

In order to explore the influences of wet–dry cycling on solidified soil masses, with the combination of unconfined compressive strength tests, mixing ratios of P2 and P7 were selected for the wet–dry cycling test in this study. Meanwhile, P5FM and P10FM represent wet–dry-cycling test blocks for mixing ratios P2 and P7, respectively (Figure 7).

Specimen appearances at different stages of wet–dry cycling.

From Figure 8, it can be seen that mass loss rates of waste soil samples increase significantly at stages 1–3 of the cycling and increase slowly thereafter. Throughout the cycling, height losses of the waste soil samples gradually increase. Through the observation of specimen appearances, it can be seen that after the soaking of specimens in water particles are peeled off and lost from specimen surfaces, resulting in decreases in their heights. Figure 9 shows that specimen strength at 28 days first increases and then decreases with the increase in the cycling number of the wet–dry test. The main reason is that during the process of wet–dry cycling, there are still hydration reactions between solidification agents and waste soil mixtures immediately after the solidification of specimen blocks. Furthermore, C–S–H gels generated by hydration products can fill the pores of waste soils. Therefore, waste soil pores generated during wet–dry cycling will be filled by products of hydration reactions, thus, in turn, leading to increased compactness and strength of specimen blocks.14 However, as the number of cycles increases, the internal moisture of specimen blocks is gradually exuded and released. This progressive drying influences the microstructural arrangement of particles and the integrity of the bonding matrix. When moisture leaves the pores, it reduces the internal cohesion provided by the hydration gels, potentially leading to microcracking as the soil matrix undergoes shrinkage and expansion stresses. Repeated drying and wetting also disrupt the stability of the gel structure, possibly leading to an increase in the porosity and the formation of microcracks. Over time, these cyclic stresses lead to a breakdown in the soil structure, as cohesive bonds weaken, and particle boundaries deteriorate. These periodic wetting and drying of specimen blocks can alter their internal microstructure, including the formation and expansion of cracks and the fragmentation of particles, thus affecting the overall strength of specimen blocks.15 Specimen block P10FM presents a superior performance of wet–dry cycling resistance to that of P5FM. This is primarily due to the fact that the presence of PAM gels can lead to lower porosity and higher initial strength of P10FM specimens.16 Meanwhile, with other conditions remaining unchanged, P10FM specimens with a higher initial strength present a better performance of wet–dry cycling. The results of the wet–dry cycling test show that improved waste soils present a slightly better performance of wet–dry cycling, and if original waste soils that are solidified can meet the requirements of highway road base soil materials, those improved pipe-jacking waste soils can also satisfy these requirements.

Analysis of Freeze–thaw Cycling

With a combination of unconfined compressive strength tests, mixing ratios P2 and P7 were selected for the freeze–thaw cycling test in this study. In the test, P5DW and P10DW represented freeze–thaw cycling specimen blocks for mixing ratios of P2 and P7, respectively. Figure 10 shows that during the cycling, the upper and lower parts of the P5DW specimen gradually peeled off, with cracks appearing on the side surface of this cylindric specimen. During the cycling, with the increase in the cycling number, cracks gradually penetrated throughout the entire P5DW specimen, with small fragments that start from the upper and lower sides turning into substantial chunk losses. Similarly, with the increase in the cycling number, cracks, and block peeling also appeared on the P10DW specimen. However, this specimen maintained a relatively intact shape after block peeling, with fewer fragments that were peeled off. From Figure 12, it can be seen that specimen blocks presented fewer mass losses at the cycling stages 1–3 and more substantial mass losses after 3–5 times of cycling. This is primarily due to the fact that freeze–thaw cycling will lead to surface and interior microcracks and particle breakage among specimen blocks.17 These microcracks and broken particles will further weaken the structural strength of specimen blocks, thus reducing the quality of these blocks. With the increase in the cycling number of freezing and thawing, internal damage and destruction of specimen blocks will gradually accumulate, resulting in continuous decreases in their quality.

Changes in specimen appearances during freeze–thaw cycling.

Figure 11 shows that the strength of P5DW and P10DW specimens at 28 days decreases with the increase in the freeze–thaw cycling number. Meanwhile, the P10DW specimen presents a superior performance of freeze–thaw cycling resistance to that of the P5DW specimen. The main reason is that freeze–thaw cycling will lead to volume changes in the interior moisture of soil masses during the freezing and thawing processes, thus damaging the microstructures of soil masses.18 Moisture will continuously permeate into soil masses during the process of freezing and thawing, which could result in loose particle structures of soil masses and weaken their cohesion and adhesion capacities, thus reducing their overall strength. At the same time, with the addition of PAM solutions, PAM gels in specimens will fill the gaps between soil particles,19 leading to higher and more stable strength of these specimens than that of specimens with no addition of PAM solutions. The results of the freeze–thaw cycling test show that those improved waste soils will present a slightly improved performance of wet–dry cycling, and if those original soils that are solidified can meet the requirements of highway road base soil materials, improved pipe-jacking waste soils can also satisfy these requirements (Figure 12).

Microstructural Experimental Results and Discussion

XRD Experiment

In order to further explore the influences of soil conditioners on the solidification effectiveness of quick lime and fly ash in earth pressure-balanced pipe-jacking waste soils, an XRD test was conducted to investigate the mineral components of pipe-jacking waste soils.

From Figure 13, it can be seen that waste soils with different mixing ratios of quick lime and fly ash present similar XRD graphs, indicating that there are no large amounts of new crystals generated during the solidification processes of quick lime and fly ash. This shows that the addition of these two substances will not significantly alter the crystal structures of materials.

XRD results of different samples.

Compared with waste soils under the P2 condition, waste soils under the P7 condition present slightly improved compressive strength. As shown in Table 7, with increases in the plagioclase and hornblende contents in waste soils under the P7 condition, PAM and sodium-based bentonite can improve the cohesion and compactness properties of materials to a certain extent.20 Waste soils under the PD5 condition present contents of primary minerals similar to those of waste soils under the P2 condition, with major differences in the inherent heterogeneity of soil masses. Their improved strength is primarily caused by the addition of PAM. With the addition of PAM, the adhesive forces between particles can be enhanced, the density of materials can be increased, and the control and workability of moisture can be improved.21 Therefore, soil aggregates will be formed,22 thus leading to slightly increased compressive strength of mixtures. Waste soils under the P2 condition exhibit slightly higher contents of high-hardness minerals such as quartz and plagioclase, which is possibly due to the fact that the addition of sodium-based bentonite can reduce the porosity of soils. Therefore, waste soils under the PD9 condition present a higher compressive strength than that of waste soils under the P2 condition.

SEM Experiment

In order to further explore the strength mechanism of solidified pipe-jacking waste soils, an SEM experiment was performed on waste soil samples under the PD9, PD5, P2, and P7 conditions.

Figure 14 shows that soil samples with no addition of sodium-based bentonite and PAM mostly present point-to-point and point-to-surface contact between soil particles. In addition, dense overall structures are formed among these soils after the generation of C–S–H gels through hydration. However, there are still certain volumes of pores in these soils. Sodium-based bentonite exhibits good expandability23 and fills gaps between soil particles, with a macroscopic manifestation of increased unconfined compressive strength of soils. PAM can take a viscous and gelatinous form in water and promote the bonding of particles, thus improving the consolidation of soils. Furthermore, the formation of −CH2–CHCONH2- (polyacrylamide gel)18 leads to increased amounts of gel particles in soil masses. The presence of gels in soil masses can optimize the microscopic contact forms of soil masses and fill the gaps between the soil particles. Under such circumstances, waste soil samples present a denser structure, which in turn improves the strength of these soil samples that are solidified to a certain extent.

IR Analysis

An infrared spectroscopy analysis was conducted in this study to investigate the chemical changes in pipe-jacking waste soils in depth. As shown in Figure 15, there is no presence of significant new peaks on the spectral curves of waste soils with the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite solutions within the concentration ranges in the experiment. This indicates that the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite solutions within the concentration ranges in this study will not lead to significant changes in the chemical components of earth pressure-balanced pipe-jacking waste soils that are solidified with quick lime and fly ash. Furthermore, it shows that earth pressure-balanced pipe-jacking waste soils that are solidified with quick lime and fly ash have a stable structure, which is not significantly affected by the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite solutions within the concentration ranges in this study.

Infrared spectroscopy analysis.

O–H peak represents the presence of hydroxyl (OH) groups or adsorbed water. It is observed in all of the samples. Despite the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite, there are no significant changes in the peak position or intensity, indicating that the water content and hydroxyl groups in the soil are not substantially altered by these additives. The similar intensity of the O–H peak across all samples suggests that the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite does not significantly affect the water retention characteristics of the solidified soils.

C–H peak is associated with the presence of organic compounds, specifically the PAM. The peak intensity increases slightly with the addition of PAM (in PD5 and P7), but the change is not significant, indicating that while PAM introduces organic material, it does not cause substantial chemical changes in the soil matrix. The C–H peak remains relatively weak compared to the silicate peaks, suggesting that the organic content does not dominate the chemical structure of the solidified soil.

Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al peaks correspond to the stretching vibrations of silicate bonds, which are the primary components of quick lime, fly ash, and clay minerals. There is no significant shift in the position or intensity of these peaks across all samples, confirming that the basic silicate structure of the solidified soils remains unchanged after the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite. This suggests that the solidified soil maintains its original silicate framework, indicating stability in the soil’s structural composition.

Al–O Bending peak is associated with the bending vibration of Al–O bonds in aluminosilicate minerals. Like the Si–O peaks, the position and intensity of the Al–O peak do not change significantly across the samples. This further supports the conclusion that the aluminosilicate structure of the solidified soils is not affected by the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite solutions within the experimental concentration ranges.

In conclusion, while no significant new peaks appear, the analysis of key characteristic peaks such as those of O–H, C–H, Si–O–Si, and Al–O shows that the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite does not lead to major changes in the chemical structure of the pipe-jacking waste soils. The stable positions and intensities of these peaks confirm that the structural integrity of the solidified soils remains unaffected by the additives, supporting the conclusion that the waste soils maintain their stable structure under the experimental conditions used in this study.

(1)The addition of polyacrylamide (PAM) and sodium-based bentonite within specific concentrations (0.2% PAM and 9% sodium-based bentonite) improves the strength of solidified pipe-jacking waste soils by filling gaps and stabilizing the structure. Under these specific conditions, the additives enhance the material’s properties, making it more suitable for use.(2)Wet–dry and freeze–thaw tests show that the solidified soils with 7% PAM (0.2% concentration) and 12% sodium-based bentonite (9% concentration) exhibit better durability, with strength reductions of 79 and 80.5%, respectively, after five cycles. These results indicate improved performance under environmental stress in this specific mix ratio.(3)XRD, SEM, and IR tests show no significant chemical changes or new crystal formations in the solidified soils with the specific ratio of quick lime/fly ash (21:79) and the additives. The additives maintain the stability of the solidified soils without altering the original structure.(4)Overall, the addition of PAM and sodium-based bentonite to quick lime/fly ash solidified pipe-jacking waste soils, under the given mix ratio, slightly improves compressive strength and durability, making the material suitable for use as road base material. These results support the use of treated waste soils in road sub-bases with this specific mix.
