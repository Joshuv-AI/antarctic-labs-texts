Abstract
Recent photometric surveys with JWST have revealed a significant population of mysterious objects with red colours, compact morphologies, frequent signs of active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity, and negligible X-ray emission. These ‘Little Red Dots’ (LRDs) have been explored through spectral and photometric studies, but their nature is still under debate. As part of the BlackTHUNDER survey, we have observed UNCOVER_20466, the second most distant LRD known ( z = 8.5 z=8.5 ), with the JWST/NIRSpec IFU. Previous JWST/NIRCam and JWST/NIRSpec MSA observations of this source revealed its LRD nature, as well as the presence of an AGN. Using our NIRSpec IFU data, we confirm that UNCOVER_20466 contains an overmassive black hole. However, our observed Balmer decrements imply negligible dust attenuation, resulting in a lower H ​ β \rm H\beta -based bolometric luminosity and λ Edd \lambda_{\rm Edd} ( ∼ 10 % \sim 10\% ) than previously found. Ly ​ α \rm Ly\alpha emission is strongly detected, implying f esc Ly ​ α ∼ 30 % f_{\rm esc}^{\rm Ly\alpha}\sim 30\% . The extremely high [ OIII ] ​ λ ​ 4363 \rm[OIII]\lambda 4363 / H ​ γ \rm H\gamma ratio is indicative of not only AGN photoionization and heating, but also extremely high densities ( n e ∼ 10 7 ​ cm − 3 n_{\rm e}\sim 10^{7}\,\rm cm^{-3} ), suggesting that this black hole at such high redshift may be forming in an ultra-dense protogalaxy.

Introduction

The advent of JWST has revolutionised the field of high-redshift galaxy evolution, including the spectroscopic confirmation of galaxies at
z>10
(within the first
\sim 500
Myr of the Universe; e.g.,
Curtis-Lake et al. 2023
;
Carniani et al. 2024
;
Naidu et al. 2025b
), the construction of UV luminosity functions out to high redshift (e.g.,
Donnan et al. 2024
;
Robertson et al. 2024
;
Whitler et al. 2025
), and new constraints on the role of galaxies in the epoch of reionisation (e.g.,
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Simmonds et al. 2024
). One of the more surprising findings was the discovery of numerous compact, red objects in JWST/NIRCam data, which were given the name ‘little red dots’ (LRDs;
Matthee et al. 2024
). While a few red, compact sources were found to be local brown dwarfs
(
Langeroodi & Hjorth 2023
;
Hainline et al. 2024
)
, LRDs are primarily high-redshift galaxies (i.e., peaking in number between
4.5<z<8.0
; e.g.,
Kocevski et al. 2025
).

The red nature of these galaxies is usually caused by a distinctive ‘v’-shaped continuum from rest-optical to rest-UV, which is caused by red UV slopes (
\beta_{\rm UV}\sim-1.5
) and positive optical slopes (
\alpha_{\rm opt}>0
where slopes are defined as
F_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta_{\rm UV}||\alpha_{\rm opt}}
; e.g,
Greene et al. 2024
). In addition, many show broad hydrogen Balmer emission lines (e.g.,
Furtak et al. 2023b
;
Greene et al. 2024
;
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Hviding et al. 2025
), and are undetected in X-ray (e.g.,
Ananna et al. 2024
;
Mazzolari et al. 2024
;
Maiolino et al. 2025b
;
Sacchi & Bogdán 2025
;
Yue et al. 2024
) or submm/radio observations (e.g.,
Casey et al. 2025
;
Mazzolari et al. 2025
;
Orozco et al. 2025
;
Setton et al. 2025
;
Xiao et al. 2025
).

The nature of these sources is still under debate, as their properties are not trivially explained by common galactic models. This ambiguity is partially due to the fact that their natures can be described either by active galactic nucleus (AGN)-dominated galaxies or compact, dusty, star-forming galaxies (e.g.,
Baggen et al. 2024
). Some investigations have looked into this by using different spectral energy distribution (SED) models: galaxy-only, AGN-only, and a combination of an AGN and host galaxy (hybrid; e.g.,
Kokorev et al. 2023
;
Leung et al. 2024
;
Tripodi et al. 2024b
;
Ronayne et al. 2025
). While these models can all fit the observed photometry of LRDs, the hybrid models return significantly lower stellar mass and dust attenuation (
A_{\rm V}
; e.g.,
Carranza-Escudero et al. 2025
).

Previous works have suggested that LRDs represent black holes (either single or binary systems) embedded in massive gaseous envelopes (e.g.,
Begelman & Dexter 2025
;
Inayoshi et al. 2025
;
Kido et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025b
;
Ji et al. 2025a
;
Naidu et al. 2025b
), possibly in the earliest phase of accretion (
Inayoshi 2025
;
Maiolino et al. 2025a
), while others explore whether their properties could be explained by self-interacting dark matter halos (e.g.,
Roberts et al. 2025
;
Feng et al. 2025
), super-Eddington accretion (e.g.,
Liu et al. 2025
), or population III supermassive stars (e.g.,
Jockel et al. 2025
;
Nandal & Loeb 2025
). Additional spectral observations are needed to constrain the nature of LRDs.

3
Spectral analysis

Due to the very compact nature of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
with respect to the JWST/NIRSpec IFU PSF, we do not spatially resolve the emission. Therefore, our primary analysis is focused on integrated spectra from the R100 and R2700 data cubes. While the field also contains a serendipitous source at lower redshift (‘BlackBolt-1’, see Appendix
B
), we defer detailed analysis of this other object to a future work.

3.1
Spectral extraction

The extraction aperture for
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
should be carefully considered, as it must be small enough to maximise the S/N of each line, but large enough to contain all significant emission. Because
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is very compact, it may be considered a point source for our observations, so the observed morphology will be dictated by the PSF.

We extract spectra from both the R100 and R2700 data cubes using a circular aperture of radius
0.125^{\prime\prime}
centred on the observed emission. Each spectrum is corrected for gravitational magnification (i.e.,
\mu=1.33
, as assumed by
Kokorev et al. 2023
) and aperture loss (see Appendix
C
for details of aperture loss correction).

As noted by other works (e.g.,
Übler et al. 2023
;
Übler et al. 2024
), the ERR extension of each data cube contains the wavelength-dependent noise variation, but underestimates the true uncertainty. To correct for this, we follow the standard approach of determining the RMS scatter in a spectral range with no significant emission lines and scaling the error spectrum from ERR to feature the same scatter.

3.2
Spectral fitting

Due to the availability of both R100 and R2700 data, we perform spectral fitting in two stages. First, we consider the data redwards of the Balmer limit (
\lambda_{\rm rest}<0.3645\,\mu
m; Section
3.2.1

Setton et al. 2024
;
Hviding et al. 2025
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
). This wavelength range is only covered by the R100 data. Next, we consider the data bluewards of the Balmer limit, and fit the R100 and R2700 data simultaneously (Section
3.2.2
).

3.2.1
Blue fit

First, we consider R100 data between
1050\,\textup{\AA}\lesssim\lambda_{\rm rest}\lesssim 3645\,\textup{\AA}

1.00\,\mu{\rm m}<\lambda_{\rm obs}<3.46\,\mu{\rm m}

, reduced
\chi^{2}
, and Bayesian Information Criterion [BIC]). The best fit was found to be
\rm[OII]\lambda 3727
/
\rm[OII]\lambda 3729

=0.7
, with increasingly poor fits for higher ratios. Thus, we fix
\rm[OII]\lambda 3727
/
\rm[OII]\lambda 3729

=0.7
in our model. This corresponds to an estimated density of
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/\rm cm^{-3})\sim 1.7
for
T_{\rm e}\sim 10^{4}
K, or
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/\rm cm^{-3})\sim 2.4
for more extreme temperatures (
T_{\rm e}\sim 10^{7}
K).

We note that while an initial fit was performed with broad and narrow components for each Balmer line (
\rm H\beta
,
\rm H\gamma
and
\rm H\delta
), this resulted in a poor fit. Because
\rm H\gamma
falls into the detector gap for the R2700 data, we cannot measure the narrow component of this line. The fit was improved by including broad components for all three lines, and a narrow component for
\rm H\beta
. The narrow component of
\rm H\beta
is found to have a flux
\sim 1/6^{\rm th}
that of the broad component, so our current data do not allow us to detect the narrow components of the weaker
\rm H\delta
or
\rm H\gamma
.

3.2.3
Fitting results

Following the fitting procedure of Sections
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
, we derive best-fit continuum and line parameters for
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
from both the R100 and R2700 data. The resulting R100 and R2700 fits are shown in Figures
1
and
2
, respectively. The best-fit blue and red models for the R100 data are concatenated to create a complete model. The best-fit values are listed in Table
2
.

Both spectra are well fit by the same intrinsic model, but we find a significant velocity offset (
182\pm 10
km s
-1
) and flux ratio (
0.85\pm 0.01
) between the R100 and R2700 data. As noted in Section
3.2.2
, these values are expected from calibration differences. The flux ratio is identical to the value assumed by
D’Eugenio et al. 2025b
, and falls within the scatter of
F_{\rm R100}/F_{\rm R2700}
ratios found by
Scholtz et al. 2025
. Other works find flux ratios of
>1
(e.g.,
Arribas et al. 2024
;
Ji et al. 2024
), which may be due to calibration differences (e.g., updates to calibration files) or an intrinsic scatter in this ratio. The velocity offset corresponds to
z_{\rm R100}-z_{\rm R2700}\simeq 0.006
, while the flux ratio implies that the grating flux is
\sim 17\%
higher than the prism flux. Each value is comparable to previous results (e.g.,
Bunker et al. 2024
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025c
;
Pérez-González et al. 2025
;
Jones et al. 2025b
).

The residuals in each fit are generally low (i.e.,
<2\sigma
). One exception is the fit of [OIII]
\lambda 5007
in the R2700 data (Figure
2
), which features a central flux excess with neighbouring negative troughs. This residual pattern has been interpreted to indicate the need for a second Gaussian component, possibly representing an outflow (e.g.,
Ginolfi et al. 2020
). This hypothesis is weakened by the fact that the addition of an outflow component to the model did not result in a better fit. Alternatively, it may be an example of the wavelength-dependent continuum oscillations (or ‘wiggles’) seen in NIRSpec IFU data around bright emission lines (e.g.,
Perna et al. 2023
). However, these wiggles are artifacts caused by single-spaxel extraction, and extend over wide swathes of each spectrum. Since we use a circular aperture, and the features are isolated to the proximity of [OIII]
\lambda 5007
, these are likely not wiggles. An additional possibility is that these artifacts are caused by a non-Gaussian LSF. In any case, the low amplitude of the residuals allows us to neglect them in our analysis.

In Table
2
, we include a comparison to the values of
Kokorev et al. 2023
, who observed
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
using the JWST/NIRSpec MSA in R100. While many of the same emission lines are detected, we note that our measured fluxes, broad linewidths, and redshifts are significantly different. Because
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
was positioned on the very edge of the MSA slit,
Kokorev et al. 2023
scaled their extracted spectrum to match measured NIRCam photometry. Our IFU data allow us to extract the full aperture-corrected spectrum from the source, resulting in more reliable fluxes. We find a similar placement of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
on the line ratio diagnostic plots of
Mazzolari et al. 2024
, adding credence to the classification of this source as an AGN (Section
4.5
).

If we fit the R100 data only, then we measure a
FWHM_{\rm B,Ba}
value within
1\sigma
of
Kokorev et al. 2023
and other works that analysed the R100 data (
Trefoloni et al. 2025
;
Greene et al. 2024
). But this linewidth is weighed down by the R2700 data during the simultaneous fit, resulting in a
\sim 2\sigma
smaller value. This difference may be caused by deviations from the fiducial LSF, which was computed assuming a fully illuminated IFU aperture. Since
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is a point source and the LSF is expected to vary over the field of view, such deviations are possible and may be explored in a future work.

Properties of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466

The spectral fits of the previous Section result in a number of constraints on line and continuum properties of

. Here, we utilise these best-fit values to characterise the black hole and host galaxy of this system.

4.1
Dust attenuation

Previously,
Kokorev et al. 2023
measured a best-fit ratio of Balmer line fluxes (
(F_{\rm H\gamma}/F_{\rm H\beta})_{\rm obs}=0.31\pm 0.06
) and derived
A_{V}
using:

A_{\rm V}=R_{\rm V}\frac{2.5}{k(\lambda_{\rm H\beta})-k(\lambda_{\rm H\gamma})}\log_{10}\left[\frac{(F_{\rm H\gamma}/F_{\rm H\beta})_{\rm obs}}{(F_{\rm H\gamma}/F_{\rm H\beta})_{\rm int}}\right]

(1)

assuming case B recombination and the reddening law found for the bar of the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC;
Gordon et al. 2003
). This reddening law is commonly adopted for high-redshift quasars (e.g.,
Greene et al. 2024
;
Mazzolari et al. 2025
;
Trefoloni et al. 2025
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025a
;
Ji et al. 2025a
). Using this line flux ratio and an intrinsic ratio of
(F_{\rm H\gamma}/F_{\rm H\beta})_{\rm int}=1/2.14
results in
A_{\rm V}=2.1^{+1.1}_{-1.0}
, or a colour excess of
E(B-V)\equiv A_{\rm V}/R_{\rm V}=0.8\pm 0.4
. However, the
\rm H\gamma
flux used in this calculation included a contribution from the broad component, while the
\rm H\beta
flux was from the narrow component only.

We derive further constraints on
E(B-V)
using all observed Balmer decrements. We only consider pairs of lines from the same emission origin (narrow or broad). The intrinsic ratios are derived using
pyneb
, assuming the ISM conditions found for a stack of
4<z<7
Type I AGN from JADES
(
Isobe et al. 2025
,
T_{\rm e}\sim 2\times 10^{4}
K,
n_{\rm e}\sim 10^{4}
cm
-3
)
. As seen in Table
3
, we are able to estimate the colour excess of the broad component using three ratios (
\rm H\delta
/
\rm H\beta
,
\rm H\gamma
/
\rm H\beta
, and
\rm H\delta
/
\rm H\gamma
). All three of these show negligible attenuation, with
E(B-V)
values within
2\sigma
of 0. When combined with the results of
Kokorev et al. 2023
, we find no significant evidence for reddening in the broad or narrow Balmer lines.

Of course, this result is dependent on a few assumptions. First, we adopt the SMC bar extinction curve of
Gordon et al. 2003
. Because each dust attenuation curve is normalised by the value at the approximate wavelength of the
\rm H\beta
-[OIII]
\lambda\lambda 4959{,}5007
complex (i.e., V-band), the choice of extinction law does not introduce strong changes in the observed values of these lines. As a test, we adopt the starburst reddening law of
Calzetti et al. 2000
, resulting in nearly identical values (i.e.,
<1\sigma
discrepant). However, we note that the choice of extinction curve will affect the observed rest-UV line fluxes and the continuum slope.

Next, we consider the fact that the intrinsic Balmer ratios are derived using
pyneb
and our fiducial ISM conditions. For most densities (
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/{\rm cm}^{-3})\lesssim 6
), these ratios feature a slight dependence on density (e.g.,
\rm H\gamma
/
\rm H\beta
changes by
\lesssim 5\%
between
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/{\rm cm}^{-3})=0-7
and
T_{\rm e}=(0.5-3.0)\times 10^{4}
K). At extreme densities (
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/{\rm cm}^{-3})\gtrsim 7
), the ratio transitions to be density dependent. Changing the temperature and density in these ranges does not result in a significant (i.e.,
>1\sigma
) change in the
E(B-V)
value.

An additional possibility is that we are observing a system where case B recombination is not applicable (e.g.,
Pirzkal et al. 2024
;
Scarlata et al. 2024
;
McClymont et al. 2025b
;
Sun & Yan 2025
). Our observed Balmer decrements suggest minimal dust attenuation (assuming case B), but the fact that our derived
E(B-V)
values are not significantly negative does not provide strong evidence against case B.

We note that
Kokorev et al. 2023
reported a large dust attenuation (
A_{\rm V}\sim 1.9
) from fitting an AGN-only model to the continuum of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
. At face value, this appears to disagree with the negligible attenuation found through analysis of Balmer ratios. However, as noted by other works, AGN-only models return higher dust attenuation than star+AGN models (e.g.,
Leung et al. 2024
). In addition, the Balmer and continuum emission may emerge from different regions, or feature a different geometry. Thus, this difference in
A_{\rm V}
could be used as evidence for a stellar component that contributes to the continuum emission, and/or a dusty AGN surrounded by less obscured line-emitting gas.

The non-detection of warm dust emission in a sample of 60 LRDs at
5\leq z\leq 8

(
Casey et al. 2025
)
suggests that this class of objects may not feature significant warm dust reservoirs (i.e.,
M_{\rm dust}<10^{6}\,M_{\odot}
).
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
only features a single ALMA non-detection, from the Deep UNCOVER-ALMA Legacy High-z (DUALZ) survey
(
Fujimoto et al. 2025
)
. They do not detect significant
\lambda_{\rm obs}=1.2
mm emission from
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, implying a
3\sigma
upper limit of
S_{\rm 1.2mm}<99\,\mu
Jy. From this single limit, it is not possible to solve the degeneracy inherent in FIR SED modelling (i.e., between dust temperature
T_{\rm dust}
, dust mass
M_{\rm dust}
, dust emissivity index
\beta_{\rm IR}
).

Assuming a modified blackbody model that accounts for the effect of the CMB at high redshift (e.g.,
Carniani et al. 2019
) and adopting a standard value of
\beta_{\rm IR}=1.8
suitable for most high-redshift galaxies (e.g.,
Witstok et al. 2023
), we find that this observed flux density upper limit does not constrain the dust mass (i.e., even models with
M_{\rm dust}=10^{10}
M
⊙
are in agreement) in the case of cold gas (
T_{\rm dust}=30
K, or the lower limit for LRDs found by
Li et al. 2025b
). By adopting the conservative limit of
M_{\rm dust}\leq M_{*}<10^{9.7}\,M_{\odot}
, we find that the upper limit is only met for
T_{\rm dust}\lesssim 40
K. Thus, while we may rule out a large reservoir of hot (
T_{\rm dust}>40
K) dust, additional ALMA observations (i.e., deeper and at different wavelengths) are required to say more.

4.2
Black hole mass estimation

The black hole mass may be estimated using the properties of the broad
\rm H\beta
line, which originates from the BLR nearby the black hole, and so-called single-epoch virial relations. The most common method is that of
Greene & Ho 2005
:

\log_{10}(M_{\rm\rm BH,GH05,H\beta}/M_{\odot})=(6.56\pm 0.02)+\\
(0.56\pm 0.02)\log_{10}\left(\frac{L_{\rm H\beta}}{10^{42}{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)+2\log_{10}\left(\frac{FWHM_{\rm H\beta}}{10^{3}{\rm km\,s^{-1}}}\right)

(2)

We also consider the relations of
Vestergaard & Peterson 2006
, who derived a similar method based on
\rm H\beta
:

\log_{10}(M_{\rm\rm BH,VP06,H\beta}/M_{\odot})=(6.67\pm 0.03)\\
+0.63\log_{10}\left(\frac{L_{\rm H\beta}}{10^{42}{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)+2\log_{10}\left(\frac{FWHM_{\rm H\beta}}{10^{3}{\rm km\,s^{-1}}}\right)

(3)

and a relation from the same work that replaces the
\rm H\beta
luminosity with the continuum emission underlying [OIII]
\lambda 5007
(
\lambda_{\rm rest}=5100\textup{\AA}
):

\log_{10}(M_{\rm\rm BH,VP06,5100}/M_{\odot})=(6.91\pm 0.02)\\
+0.50\log_{10}\left(\frac{\lambda L_{\lambda}(5100\AA)}{10^{44}{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)+2\log_{10}\left(\frac{FWHM_{\rm H\beta}}{10^{3}{\rm km\,s^{-1}}}\right)

(4)

where each relation has an intrinsic scatter of
\sim 0.4
dex.

We implement these relations with the best-fit properties of the R100 and R2700 data, resulting in values of
\log_{10}(M_{\rm BH}/M_{\odot})=7.4-7.6
, with uncertainties of
\sim 0.4
dex (i.e., with uncertainties dominated by the scatter of each relation; Table
3
). This value is lower than the value of
\log_{10}(M_{\rm BH}/M_{\odot})=8.0-8.2
(with uncertainties of
\sim 0.4
dex) derived by
Kokorev et al. 2023
, but not significantly (
<2\sigma
discrepant). This different value is partially due to the fact that we do not detect significant evidence for dust attenuation from the Balmer decrement, while
Kokorev et al. 2023
corrected
\rm H\beta
using
A_{\rm V}\sim 2
. In addition, the other work found a larger FWHM and line flux of the broad component of
\rm H\beta
.

It is worthwhile noting that the single-epoch methods have been calibrated on galaxies in the local Universe, and their application to high-redshift sources is not straightforward.
Indeed, recent works have suggested that, for LRDs, the broad emission is due to electron scattering rather than bulk motion around a black hole (
Rusakov et al. 2025
), resulting in a much lower (
\geq 2
dex)
M_{\rm BH}
estimate. While this would move many high-redshift AGN onto the local
M_{\rm BH}-M_{*}
relation (see Section
5
), this interpretation is the subject of debate (e.g.,
Brazzini et al. 2025
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025a
).
More recently,
Juodžbalis et al. 2025b
directly measured the black hole mass in a lensed LRD at
z=7
by resolving its sphere of influence, finding a mass fully consistent with the virial relations. We consider further these results for our discussion of the black hole
vs.
galaxy properties in Section
5
.

4.3
Luminosities & Eddington ratio

The bolometric luminosity can be found from the
\rm H\beta
broad luminosity using the relation from
Stern & Laor 2012
:

L_{\rm bol}=130\times L_{\rm H\beta,B}\times(F_{\rm H\alpha}/F_{\rm H\beta})

(5)

where
(F_{\rm H\alpha}/F_{\rm H\beta})=2.74
is the intrinsic ratio of these lines, assuming case B recombination and the fiducial ISM conditions of Section
4.1
6
6

6

Assuming a higher density (
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/{\rm cm}^{-3})=7
), as suggested by the analysis Section
4.5
, results in a fiducial
(F_{\rm H\alpha}/F_{\rm H\beta})=2.71
, or a decrease of
\sim 1\%
.
.

We find a value of

\log_{10}(L_{\rm bol})=44.72\pm 0.02
, which is considerably lower than the value of

\log_{10}(L_{\rm bol})=45.82^{+0.17}_{-0.28}
found by
Kokorev et al. 2023
. This difference is partially ascribable to our different treatments of dust attenuation (see Section
4.1
). If a high attenuation of
A_{\rm V}=2
is taken into account, then the intrinsic
\rm H\beta
flux increases by a factor of
\sim 20\times
, resulting in higher estimates of
L_{\rm bol}
. As a test, we applied this dust correction to our values, resulting in
L_{\rm bol}
values within
1\sigma
of
Kokorev et al. 2023
.

Similarly, the Eddington luminosity (e.g.,
Fabian 2012
) is expressed as:

L_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi GM_{\rm BH}m_{\rm p}c}{\sigma_{\rm T}}=1.26\times 10^{38}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{M_{\odot}}\right)[\rm erg\,s^{-1}]

(6)

This is commonly used to calculate the Eddington ratios (
\lambda_{\rm Edd}\equiv L_{\rm bol}/L_{\rm Edd}
). Regardless of the method used to derive the black hole mass, we find
\lambda_{\rm Edd}\sim 10\%
. This is lower than the value of
\sim 40\%
measured by
Kokorev et al. 2023
, but due to the large uncertainty on
L_{\rm Edd}
, they are
<2\sigma
discrepant. We note that both
L_{\rm bol}
and
L_{\rm Edd}
are functions of
L_{\rm H\beta}
(using the calibration of
Greene & Ho 2005
), and a higher
\rm H\beta
flux (or a lower
FWHM
of the broad component of
\rm H\beta
) will result in a higher
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
. Since we adopt the same equations, this lower ratio is due to a combination of different dust attenuation assumptions and different aperture loss corrections, as well as the different broad
\rm H\beta
flux and smaller
\rm H\beta
broad linewidth.

4.4
ISM conditions

While the broad emission opens a window into the nature of the black hole of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, the narrow lines instead allow us to characterise the host galaxy (or nearby gas; e.g.,
Inayoshi et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025b
).

To begin, we use our observed line ratios and the code
pyneb
to explore the electron density (
n_{\rm e}
) through the
\rm[OII]\lambda\lambda 3727,3729
line pair. As noted in Section
3.2.2
, we are not able to directly fit for the ratio of
\rm[OII]\lambda 3727
/
\rm[OII]\lambda 3729
using our data. An exploration of line ratios finds that a low ratio (
\sim 0.7
, implying
\log_{10}(n_{\rm e}/{\rm cm}^{-3})\sim 2
) yields a better fit. While this ratio has a weak dependence on
T_{\rm e}
, a low ratio may only be caused by a low-density environment (i.e., at any temperature).

Next, we consider the
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratio, which is a strong indicator of
T_{\rm e}
. This ratio is lower in our data (
0.13\pm 0.01
) than that of
Kokorev et al. 2023
. As noted in
Kokorev et al. 2023
, this ratio is much higher than expected for most galaxies. For an electron density of
n_{\rm e}\sim 10^{1-4}
cm
-3
,
pyneb
predicts a maximum ratio of
\lesssim 0.05
for
T_{\rm e}<2.5\times 10^{4}
K. While this line ratio is strongly dependent on
T_{\rm e}
for
n_{\rm e}<10^{5}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
, it transitions to a strong dependence on
n_{\rm e}
for
n_{\rm e}>10^{5}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
. While a ratio of
0.1-0.2
could be theoretically explained by a high density (
n_{\rm e}\sim 10^{6}
cm
-3
) and temperature (
\sim 2\times 10^{4}
K), this density is
\sim 2
dex higher than the average value found for
4<z<7
Type I AGN
(
Isobe et al. 2025
)
and
\sim 3
dex higher than the average value for SFGs at the redshift of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
(e.g.,
Isobe et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2025a
). While high
n_{\rm e}
is expected from some interpretations of LRD properties (e.g.,
Begelman & Dexter 2025
;
Rusakov et al. 2025
), these conditions are extreme.

Together, these tracers appear to present contrasting ISM conditions: the low
\rm[OII]\lambda 3727
/
\rm[OII]\lambda 3729
ratio requires low
n_{\rm e}
, while the high
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratio requires high
n_{\rm e}
. One possible explanation is a two-phase ISM, with both low- and high-density emission regions (e.g.,
Ji et al. 2024
;
Harikane et al. 2025
;
Usui et al. 2025
). Alternatively, the
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratio could suggest an extraordinarily high temperature (i.e.,
>2\times 10^{4}
K). Similarly high
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratios have been observed in other
z>7
galaxies (e.g.,
Schaerer et al. 2022
;
Katz et al. 2023
;
Cullen et al. 2025
), where they have been explained by a high degree of cosmic ray heating, the presence of high-mass X-ray binaries, or Wolf-Rayet stars. The extreme conditions implied by these ratios independently support the presence of an AGN in this galaxy (see
Mazzolari et al. 2024
;
Übler et al. 2024
, Section
4.5
).
These aspects will be discussed more thoroughly in the next Section, within the context of AGN and SF photoionization models.

Our observed line fluxes can also be used to estimate the gas-phase metallicity of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
using strong line ratio diagnostics (e.g.,
Curti et al. 2020
;
Cataldi et al. 2025
). We will adopt the calibrations of
Sanders et al. 2024
, as they were created using JWST observations of galaxies in a redshift range that contains
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
(i.e.,
2.1<z<8.7
). Using the narrow-line flux ratios (O3, O2, R23, O32, Ne3O2, see Table
4
for ratio definitions), we find a best-fit value of
12+\log_{10}(O/H)=7.78\pm 0.06
, or
0.12\pm 0.02
solar.

There are caveats with this metallicity derivation, including the possibility that
\rm[OII]\lambda\lambda 3727,3729
and [OIII]
\lambda\lambda 4959{,}5007
(which are in every line ratio) originate from different components of a multi-phase ISM, the possibility that the electron temperature in this object is exceptionally high, or the unproven applicability of a metallicity calibration created using star-forming galaxies to LRDs. But most works investigating LRDs assume a low metallicity (
\lesssim 20\%
solar; e.g.,
Kocevski et al. 2025
;
Donnan et al. 2025
), in agreement with the finding of
\lesssim 10\%
solar metallicity from individual studies of LRDs (e.g.,
De Graaff et al. 2025
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025a
;
Maiolino et al. 2025a
). Our metallicity estimate is therefore in line with previous findings for high-
z
LRDs.

AGN diagnostics and extreme densities

Since our NIRSpec data do not cover the wavelength range of
\rm H\alpha

\rm[NII]\lambda\lambda 6548,6584

As noted in previous works (e.g.,

Stark et al. 2010

From the R100 spectrum, we are able to estimate the

/

is estimated using our fiducial ISM conditions (see Section

) and

. Based on the best-fit fluxes of the narrow
\rm H\beta
and
\rm Ly\alpha

\rm UNCOVER\_20466

), as shown in Appendix

We also note that previous works found a link between O32 (tracing ionisation parameter) and

Roy et al. 2023

Some of the properties of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466

. But this other source is not an LRD, as it lacks broad Balmer emission or a v-shaped continuum. In addition, it is more metal poor. This highlights the diversity of strong LAEs in the early Universe.

4.8
Continuum properties

Based on JWST/NIRCam photometry, this source was classified as an LRD (
Labbe et al. 2025
). One of the defining characteristics of these objects is a v-shaped continuum, with a negative (positive)
F_{\lambda}
slope in the rest-UV (rest-optical). This was confirmed by the JWST/NIRSpec observations of
Greene et al. 2024
, who found
\alpha_{\rm opt}=0.5\pm 0.3
and
\beta_{\rm UV}=-0.7\pm 0.2
(i.e., the ‘red’ nature is not due solely to emission lines). Our fits of the R100 spectrum return a redder optical slope (
\alpha_{\rm opt}=1.8\pm 0.2
) and a similar UV slope (
\beta_{\rm UV}=-0.7\pm 0.1
). We note that these slopes meet the LRD criteria of
\alpha_{\rm opt}>0
and
-2.8<\beta_{\rm UV}<-0.37
used by classification studies (e.g.,
Kocevski et al. 2025
;
Hainline et al. 2025
), confirming the LRD nature of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
.

Because we fit the R100 spectrum in two parts (i.e., before and after the Balmer limit), there is a discontinuity at
\lambda_{\rm rest}=0.3645\,\mu
m with a red/blue flux ratio of
1.3\pm 0.2
(using the best-fit values and uncertainties of each continuum model). Balmer breaks are commonly observed in LRDs (e.g.,
De Graaff et al. 2025
;
Furtak et al. 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025a
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
), and this ratio falls within the lower end of the scatter for observed Balmer breaks at high redshift (e.g.,
Kuruvanthodi et al. 2024
;
Vikaeus et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2025
). However, the current data are not able to confirm a Balmer break in
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
.

5.1

;

;

;
Akins et al. 2025
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025a
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025a
;
Kiyota et al. 2025
;

;

;

;

First, we consider the distribution of black hole mass as a function of stellar mass (Figure
4
a). Most JWST-detected black holes lie above the local relation, reflecting overmassive black holes. Our new
M_{\rm BH}
value is lower than that of
Kokorev et al. 2023

\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is overmassive, similar to many JWST-detected AGN (
M_{\rm BH}\gtrsim 0.1\times M_{*}
). This is especially evident when comparing to the
Reines & Volonteri 2015

Even if our single-epoch black hole mass would be overestimated by
\sim 0.7
dex, as possibly indicated by the recent results for a highly super-Eddington black hole at
z\sim 2.3

(
Abuter et al. 2024
)
, UNCOVER_20466 would remain overmassive in the black hole mass – stellar mass plane. Furthermore, the low Eddington ratio we find in this work suggests that a possible correction towards lower black hole masses may not be as large
(
Abuter et al. 2024
, see discussion by)
. Additionally, the finding by
Juodžbalis et al. 2025b
that the direct BH mass measurement in a LRD at
z=7
being consistent with local virial relations, supports the idea that the BH mass inferred by us in UNCOVER_20466 is reasonably accurate.

The relation between black hole mass and galaxy velocity dispersion (also known as the ‘M-
\sigma
’ relation, Figure
4
b) has been found to be constant over a range of redshifts (
z<1
; e.g.,
Shen et al. 2015
). This connection represents a coeval evolution of the black hole and the material surrounding it. As already pointed out by
Maiolino et al. 2024
and
Juodžbalis et al. 2025a
, most of the JWST-detected AGN fall onto the local correlation, including
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
. One minor note is that the
\sigma_{\rm*}
measured from JWST data represents the narrow linewidth from the full source (corrected for instrumental broadening and converted to stellar dispersion), while the same value from local galaxies is extracted only from the bulge. However, most galaxies entering the local relation are ellipticals or bulge-dominated galaxies, i.e. where the bulge contains most of the stellar mass.

The last correlation is between black hole mass and dynamical mass (Figure
4
c). The JWST-detected AGN are scattered on both sides of the local relation, and
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
agrees with the
Bennert et al. 2021
relation.

We note that each of the low-redshift correlations originates from different samples. The
M_{\rm BH}
-
\sigma_{\rm*}
and
M_{\rm BH}
-
M_{\rm dyn}
correlations of
Kormendy & Ho 2013
are found using a sample of local elliptical and bulge galaxies, where
M_{\rm dyn}=M_{\rm bulge}
. Similarly,
Greene et al. 2020
fit
M_{\rm BH}
-
\sigma_{\rm*}
and
M_{\rm BH}
-
M_{\rm*}
using a sample of local spiral and elliptical galaxies. The sample used to derive the
M_{\rm BH}
-
M_{\rm*}
relation of
Reines & Volonteri 2015
consisted of local broad line AGN.
Bennert et al. 2021
combined local broad line AGN and quiescent galaxies to examine all three correlations. Since their data allow for the spatial decomposition of the bulge and disk of each source,
M_{\rm dyn}
is derived using the radius and velocity dispersion of the central bulge. On the other hand, the observed values for high-redshift galaxies are derived in similar ways.

Figure
4
a shows that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
has an overmassive black hole (with respect to the stellar mass), especially compared to AGN in the local Universe. Despite this, it already lies on correlations between
M_{\rm BH}
and bulge properties (velocity dispersion and dynamical mass, Figures

\rm UNCOVER\_20466
falls on the same correlations suggests that it may evolve along those relations into a present-day massive early-type galaxy.

5.2
Black hole growth through cosmic time

It is clear that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
features a significant black hole mass at an early cosmic epoch (
t_{\rm H}\sim 580
Myr). To explore this further, we compare the black hole mass to those of other
z>6
AGN (Figure
5
).

First, we consider a simple model for how a black hole will grow through cosmic time. Their growth rate may be derived using their Eddington luminosity and Eddington ratio (e.g.,
Li 2012
):

\dot{M_{\rm BH}}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm Edd}L_{\rm Edd}}{\epsilon_{o}c^{2}}

(8)

where
\epsilon_{o}
is the efficiency at which mass is converted to luminosity (here assumed to be 0.1). If each black hole has mass
M_{\rm seed}
at cosmic time
t_{\rm seed}
and we assume Eddington-limited accretion (
\lambda_{\rm Edd}=1
), then we may solve the above equation to determine the mass evolution:

M_{\rm BH}(t)=M_{\rm seed}e^{(t-t_{\rm seed})/{0.045\,\rm Gyr}}

(9)

We use this equation to calculate three growth curves for seeds at
z=25
: a direct collapse black hole with
M_{\rm seed}=10^{4}\,M_{\odot}
, a supermassive star with
M_{\rm seed}=10^{2}\,M_{\odot}
, and a seed tuned to match the observed properties of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
(
M_{\rm seed}=1.45\times 10^{3}\,M_{\odot}
).

When compared to the literature,
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is one of the highest-redshift massive black holes, lying close to other sources at
t_{\rm H}\sim 0.5-0.6
Gyr (i.e.,
Larson et al. 2023
;
Tripodi et al. 2024b
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025a
;
Taylor et al. 2025
). The black hole masses of these sources (which were also derived using Balmer line scaling relations) lie between the growth curves for the
10^{2}\,M_{\odot}
and
10^{4}\,M_{\odot}
seeds.

The application of
M_{\rm BH}
scaling relations based on broad Balmer emission is limited by the maximum
\lambda_{\rm obs}
of the data. Using the standard R100 wavelength limit of
\lambda_{\rm obs}=5.30\,\mu
m,
\rm H\alpha
may only be observed up to
z<7.07
, while
\rm H\beta
may be observed up to
z<9.90
. Recent works have successfully extended the wavelength range of JWST/NIRSpec data to
\lambda_{\rm obs}\lesssim 5.5\,\mu
m (e.g.,
D’Eugenio et al. 2025a
;
Donnan et al. 2025
; see also Dawn JWST Archive
8
8

8

https://dawn-cph.github.io/dja/
v4 NIRSpec data release), which allows for
\rm H\alpha
and
\rm H\beta
detection to higher redshifts. Balmer lines at even higher redshifts can be explored using the broad wavelength coverage of JWST/MIRI (e.g.,
Prieto-Jiménez et al. 2025
). While lower-order Balmer lines (e.g.,
\rm H\gamma
,
\rm H\delta
) are theoretically observable at higher redshift, their intrinsic faintness relative to
\rm H\alpha
and
\rm H\beta
make their detection difficult.

Instead, the black holes of higher-redshift galaxies may be measured in other ways. Three
z>10
AGN were have been characterised, where two of them feature similar black hole masses to
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
(
Goulding et al. 2023
;
Napolitano et al. 2024
). Due to the lack of observed Balmer lines, these masses were estimated using
L_{\rm bol}
relations. In addition, both are
X
-ray luminous, suggesting a different nature than LRDs. Finally, the currently highest-redshift black hole (GN-z11;
Maiolino et al. 2024
) was estimated using a MgII scaling relation, but falls on the same growth curve as
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
.

5.3
Evolution of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466

Here, we synthesise the previous subsections to consider the past and future evolution of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
. The correlation plots of Section
5.1
show that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
features a black hole that is overmassive compared to its stellar mass, while it lies on the
M_{\rm BH}-\sigma_{*}
and
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm dyn}
relations. Combined with the significant broad component of
\rm H\beta
(
FWHM_{\rm B,Ba}\sim 2500
km s
-1
) and low Eddington ratio (
\sim 10\%
), this can be interpreted as evidence for rapid black hole accretion in the past (accompanied by low SFR, or small change in M
∗
) which has declined.

Through a comparison of the
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
black hole mass to model and literature values, we find that while our
M_{\rm BH}
for
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is lower than previously found
(
Kokorev et al. 2023
)
, it still requires a heavy seed (i.e.,
M_{\rm seed}>10^{3}\,M_{\odot}
) at high redshift, assuming constant Eddington-limited accretion. Of course, a constant Eddington-limited accretion over nearly 600 Myr is not physical. The actual mass accretion rate will likely vary over time, with both sub- and super-Eddington periods (e.g.,
Li 2012
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Schneider et al. 2023
;
Trinca et al. 2024
). Regardless, the high black hole mass of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
and similar high-redshift AGN are more easily explained if they evolved from a heavy seed.

The future evolutionary track of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is non-trivial to determine, as we only have a constraint on the mass of the black hole and limits on the stellar and warm dust components. If there is a reservoir of molecular gas, then one possibility is that the black hole accretion remains low (as evidenced by the low
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
), while the gas is turned into stars. This would allow
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
to stay on the
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm dyn}
and
M_{\rm BH}-\sigma_{\rm*}
relations of Figure
4
c and
4
b, while moving closer to the
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm*}
relation of Figure
4
a. However, the gas reservoir of this galaxy is not yet explored, and future ALMA observations are required. We note that the non-detection of
\rm[CII]
or FIR emission in some LRDs
(
Xiao et al. 2025
)
suggests a small molecular gas reservoir. Altenatively, this system may have a substantial dark matter contribution, and later baryonic accretion may bring it closer to the local
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm*}
relation
(
McClymont et al. 2025a
)
.

However, the current black hole accretion rate is non-zero (i.e.,
\lambda_{\rm Edd}\sim 0.1
), so the black hole mass will increase with cosmic time. One extreme possibility is shown by the dotted line of Figure
5
, which shows how
M_{\rm BH}
would change with Eddington-limited accretion. This curve extends into the high-mass region of
z>7
QSOs
(
Fan et al. 2023
)
, but a more physical sub-Eddington (or variable) growth would result in a lower mass, more similar to other observed LRDs at
z\sim 6-8
.

Together, we find that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
likely evolved from a heavy seed at high redshift, featured strong AGN activity in the past, and at will either evolve by
z\sim 6
into a source similar to LRDs (assuming low or negligible black hole growth) or QSOs (assuming a high growth rate). Recent studies have found a sample of
z<4
galaxies with red inner regions and blue star-forming outskirts, either representing descendants of LRDs that have gained a blue envelope over time
(
Billand et al. 2025
)
or showing that LRDs may already have faint star-forming regions that are simply not detectable at high redshift
(
Rinaldi et al. 2025b
)
.
This represents a possible picture of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, but there are several questions that are not yet answered. First, we cannot yet constrain the formation mechanism for the ionised bubble (see discussion of
Fujimoto et al. 2024
) that results in a high
\rm Ly\alpha
escape fraction and equivalent width. This could be caused by significant UV emission from young stars in the past, or by AGN activity (e.g.,
Witstok et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
). In addition, the dust geometry in the host galaxy and around the central black hole is not constrained. We also note the extremely large
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratio suggests either abnormally high density or temperature.
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
represents one of the most extreme sources at high redshift, and requires additional observations to further constrain its properties.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we presented new JWST/NIRSpec IFU data of the
z=8.50
source
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, taken as part of the BlackTHUNDER survey. While this source is spatially compact (
R_{\rm e}\lesssim 200
pc), our data enable accurate spectral extraction using a PSF-defined aperture loss correction verified by comparison with JWST/NIRCam photometry. By extracting integrated spectra of both the PRISM/CLEAR and G395H/F290LP data cubes and fitting each, we are able to measure continuum and line properties, yielding constraints on the properties of this source.

•

We confirm the LRD status of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, with a v-shaped continuum (
\beta_{\rm UV}=-0.7\pm 0.1
,
\alpha_{\rm opt}=1.8\pm 0.2
) and compact morphology (
R_{\rm e}=0.23\pm 0.03
kpc, based on fits to NIRCam images). Similarly to many other LRDs, this source features broad Balmer emission (
FWHM=2503\pm 176
km s
-1
) and is FIR-faint (e.g.,
Casey et al. 2025
;
Fujimoto et al. 2025
).

•

The best-fit Balmer decrements are in agreement with the intrinsic values, suggesting negligible dust attenuation. The previous detection of higher attenuation (
A_{\rm V}\sim 2
;
Kokorev et al. 2023
) is discussed, including possible reasons for the disagreement with our fiducial values of
A_{\rm V}\sim 0
.

•

The broad
\rm H\beta
emission and underlying continuum was used to constrain the black hole properties (
M_{\rm BH}\sim 10^{7.5-7.6}\,M_{\odot}
,
\lambda_{\rm Edd}\sim 10\%
), which are slightly lower than previous estimates (
<2\sigma
discrepant). Using an upper limit on
M_{*}
from literature, we confirm that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
features an overmassive black hole.

•

Our observed line ratios are combined with empirical relations and
pyneb
to explore the properties of the host galaxy or gaseous envelope. We find a low
\rm[OII]\lambda 3727
/
\rm[OII]\lambda 3729
ratio and a high
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/[OIII]
\lambda 5007
ratio, which could be explained by a multi-phase ISM (i.e., diffuse gas and dense gas) or an exceptionally high temperature (
T_{\rm e}\gtrsim 10^{5}
K). Other line ratios reveal a low gas-phase metallicity of
0.12\pm 0.02
solar (from high-redshift line ratio calibrations) and a high ionisation parameter of
\log_{10}(U)=-1.3\pm 0.3
. While the applicability of these relations to high-redshift LRDs is not proven, this low-metallicity, high-ionisation, extreme environment aligns with the compact AGN nature of this source.

•

By leveraging an extensive grid of photoionization models, we have found that the extremely high
\rm[OIII]\lambda 4363
/
\rm H\gamma
ratio indicates that the gas in the host galaxy is not only ionized and over-heated by the AGN, but it also requires extremely high densities, of about
10^{7}
cm
-3
. Together with the very high redshift at which this object is found (being the second most distant LRD), this finding is suggestive of a black hole that is forming in an ultradense protogalaxy in the early Universe.

•

\rm Ly\alpha
emission is significantly detected, and is used to estimate an escape fraction of
f_{\rm esc}^{\rm Ly\alpha}\sim 30\%
. This places
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
on existing correlations of
EW_{\rm 0,Ly\alpha}
-
f_{\rm esc}^{\rm Ly\alpha}
and
EW_{\rm 0,Ly\alpha}
-
M_{\rm UV}
.

•

While
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
features an overmassive black hole, we find that it lies on local correlations of
M_{\rm BH}-\sigma_{*}
and
M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm dyn}
. Since these correlations were primarily determined using the properties of local bulges, the observable emission of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
may represent the progenitor of the central bulge of a lower-redshift galaxy.

•

We compare the black hole mass of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
to other
z>6
AGN. While our new
M_{\rm BH}
is lower than previous estimates, we find that it is still one of the highest-redshift and most massive black holes. Using a set of growth tracks assuming Eddington-limited accretion, we show that this source would likely require a heavy seed (
M_{\rm seed}\gtrsim 10^{3}
assuming
z_{\rm seed}\sim 25
).

•

The combination of properties is synthesised to predict the history and future evolution of this source. One possibility is that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
underwent a period of rapid black hole accretion in the recent past (which increased
M_{\rm BH}
and
\sigma_{\rm*}
of the host galaxy) which has now ended (as evidenced by the low
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
). Depending on future accretion, it may evolve into a black hole similar to those of QSOs (high accretion) or LRDs (negligible accretion) at
z\sim 6
.

Our data show that
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is an exceptional source in the early Universe, with typical LRD properties, an overmassive black hole, evidence for a large ionised bubble, and extreme ISM conditions. Additional observations at different wavelengths (e.g., JWST/MIRI, ALMA [CII]
158\mu
m) will help to reveal the complex nature of this source.

Appendix C
Details of aperture loss correction

When analysing the integrated spectra of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
, we choose an extraction aperture of radius
0.125^{\prime\prime}
in order to maximise the S/N of [OIII]
\lambda 5007
. This aperture captures most of the emission from this compact object, but some emission from the wings of the PSF is missed. This fraction of missed flux is not constant, as the NIRSpec IFU PSF size changes with wavelength. In order to recover the total flux, an aperture loss correction is required (see similar analyses for NIRSpec IFU data in
Arribas et al. 2024
;
Zamora et al. 2024
). In this Appendix, we detail how we calculate wavelength-dependent aperture loss corrections for our extracted spectra.

C.1
NIRSpec IFU PSF derivation

We begin by analysing the shape of the NIRSpec IFU PSF. An in-depth characterisation of the NIRSpec PSF was performed by
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
. This work found a strong evolution of the PSF FWHM with wavelength, as well as a significant difference in the PSF width across and along the NIRSpec slice directions (up to
\sim 40\%
). Although three approaches were utilised, no concrete PSF shape was found. The results of this work were adopted by many other NIRSpec/IFU studies (e.g.,
Marconcini et al. 2024
;
Venturi et al. 2024
). To verify these results, we analyse archival observations of calibration stars, and compare the results to empirical models.

Observations of stars were included in programs in order to calibrate the PSF of NIRSpec (PSFSTARUHSJ0439 in PID 1222, PI C. J. Willott; PSFSTARULASJ1342 in PID 1219, PI N. Luetzgendorf). Additionally, we consider the calibration data of PID 1537 (PI: K. D. Gordon), who observed the white dwarf G191-B2B with the JWST/NIRSpec IFU with all three R1000 gratings and all three R2700 gratings. For all of these observations, we downloaded the calibrated (stage 3) data cubes from the MAST archive and fit the stellar emission in each spectral channel of each cube with a 2D Gaussian model. The geometric mean of the best-fit semi-major axes were recorded, resulting in curves of PSF FWHM as a function of wavelength.

C.2
Comparison with observed curve of growth

While this fiducial PSF is in good agreement with the shapes of observed point sources, we note that Appendix
C.1
and other works (e.g.,
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
) used a two-dimensional Gaussian to model the PSF. In reality, the JWST/NIRSpec PSF is more complex, with a bright core and a fainter six-pointed ‘snowflake’ pattern of maxima and minima. While a 2D Gaussian model can describe the dominant central emission, the lower-level emission deviates from this axisymmetric model. We explore this by creating curves of growth (CoGs) for the observed [OIII]
\lambda 5007
emission in the R100 cube, our best-fit 2D Gaussian model to the PSF, and the fiducial PSF. The slope of these CoGs are directly related to the density of flux: a bright core will result in a rapid rise, while diffuse emission will create a more shallow increase.

To create the [OIII]
\lambda 5007
CoG, we extracted spectra using circular apertures centred on
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
with a range of radii (
0.025^{\prime\prime}-0.500^{\prime\prime}
), isolated the wavelength range around [OIII]
\lambda 5007
, fit the continuum and line emission in the resulting spectrum, and recorded the best-fit line flux for each aperture. The resulting CoG is shown as a red line in Figure
8
. In order to create a similar CoG for the fiducial PSF of Section
C.1
, we integrate the PSF within a range of apertures and normalise each flux to the value in the largest aperture (
r=0.5^{\prime\prime}
; see magenta line with circular markers in Figure
8
). Finally, we create CoGs for 2D Gaussian models with the same FWHM as the fiducial model, assuming a circular model (
q=1.0
) and an elongated model (
q=0.5
).

From this analysis, we see that all of the CoGs agree at small radii (
r\lesssim 0.125^{\prime\prime}
) and large radii (
r\gtrsim 0.4^{\prime\prime}
), but the 2D Gaussian model of the PSF deviates at intermediate radii (
0.125^{\prime\prime}\lesssim r\lesssim 0.400^{\prime\prime}
). This behaviour indicates diffuse emission in the PSF that is not present in the 2D Gaussian model. Because the full PSF follows the observed CoG more closely, we will use this fiducial PSF rather than a Gaussian approximation.

C.4
Comparison to NIRCam data

While this empirical model is a good match to observations of stars, it is not yet thoroughly tested on observational data of high-redshift galaxies, and thus may not be accurate. To validate this correction, we conduct a comparison of our R100 NIRSpec spectrum and archival JWST/NIRCam photometry of
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
.

First, we extract fluxes from each NIRCam image by fitting each with a 2D circular Gaussian model with a constant offset (to account for background emission), where the FWHM of the model is set to be the fiducial FWHM from JDOX (see Table
6
). In order to compare these photometric points to the NIRSpec data directly, we first convolve the R100 cube with the throughput curve of each NIRCam filter to create a set of pseudo-photometric images, and measure the flux using a circular aperture of
r=0.125^{\prime\prime}
. We apply the aperture loss correction derived in Appendix
C.3
to the NIRSpec fluxes, and compare the resulting values in Figure
10
. We limit the comparison to NIRCam filters at
\lambda_{\rm obs}>3\,\mu
m due to elevated noise on the red side of the NIRSpec data. The resulting fluxes are comparable.

Figure 10:
Comparison of the JWST/NIRSpec R100 spectrum and pseudo-photometry with JWST/NIRCam photometry (top panel). The pseudo-photometry was created using the throughput curves for each NIRCam filter, shown in the lower portion of the top panel. This results in ratio of NIRSpec to NIRCam photometric fluxes that are in broad agreement with the expected value from the empirical NIRSpec PSF (lower panel).

C.5
Note on variability

By comparing JWST/NIRSpec MSA and IFU data taken at different times,
Ji et al. 2025a
found that Abell2744-QSO1 featured significant variability. This analysis benefitted from the gravitationally lensed nature of the source: each of the three lensed images represented a different light travel time. Thus, they were able to show that
\rm H\beta
rest-frame equivalent width decreased by
\sim 50\%
over an observer-frame time of
\sim 20
yr.

While
\rm UNCOVER\_20466
is not multiply lensed, it is possible that we could use the different data to explore the variability of this source. This is because of the different observation times: the NIRCam data were taken in two epochs (2 November 2022 for PID 2561, 11 November 2023 for PID 4111), while the JWST/NIRSpec MSA data were taken on 31 July 2023 and the JWST/NIRSpec IFU were taken on 5 December 2024. But as demonstrated in Appendix
C.4
, we find good agreement between the NIRCam data taken on each date and the IFU data, and thus no significant evidence for variability on the observer-frame timescale of
\sim 2\,
yr.

\rm Ly\alpha

\rm Ly\alpha

Begley et al. 2024

Goovaerts et al. 2024
), and with
M_{\rm UV}
(e.g.,
Jones et al. 2024

Jones et al. 2025a

z<8

z>10

Witstok et al. 2025

\rm UNCOVER\_20466

\rm Ly\alpha
