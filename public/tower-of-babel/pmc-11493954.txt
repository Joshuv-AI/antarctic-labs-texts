Dynamic clustering based risk aware congestion control technique for vehicular network
Authors: Bhupendra Dhakad, Sadhana Mishra, Shailendra Singh Ojha, Jai Kumar Sharma, Sateesh Kumar, Mubarak Alrashoud, Jayant Giri, S M Mozammil Hasnain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to improve road safety and offer extra services to cars and their users, vehicular ad hoc networks, or VANETs, are essential parts of intelligent transportation systems (ITS). The primary aim of this research is to suggest and assess a new approach for mitigating network congestion in VANET technology through the implementation of dynamic grouping of vehicles for safety (DGVS). To do this, virtual regions are created around cars using the DBSCAN and K Mean method. This allows vehicles to communicate directly with others within the same DGVS, eliminating the need to broadcast messages across the entire network.The ultimate goal is to drastically decrease traffic while preserving vital data transfer for VANET traffic control and safety. The suggested technique determines the optimal transmission rate in accordance with the existing channel circumstances, yielding a balanced performance concerning both packet delivery and channel congestion. With this novel method, cars may only talk to other vehicles that are in the same DGVS, thus there’s no need to broadcast messages to the whole network. With the use of this technique, the efficacy of DGVS in reducing VANET congestion and enhancing network performance will be thoroughly assessed. The study’s conclusions demonstrate how well the suggested dynamic grouping of vehicles for safety (DGVS) technique works to reduce VANET congestion. It was shown through simulation-based studies that, in comparison to current approaches, DGVS dramatically decrease network congestion, resulting in notable gains in network performance and decreased communication delay. Additionally, the study found a number of possible uses for DGVS in the transportation industry, such as emergency response, traffic management, and accident prevention.Keywords: Dynamic grouping, DBSAN algorithm, Intelligent transport system, K mean algorithm, Vehicular ad-hoc networkSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

One of the emerging technologies that draw interest from academic and business researchers is VANET, which is developing at an unexpectedly rapid rate. In VANET, vehicles are considered either source or destination nodes, forming a mobile network that is characterized by fast topology changes and is extremely dynamic. For more than eight decades, researchers have been examining the enduring problem of road traffic safety. Since motor vehicles are the most common form of transportation, their safety is crucial. According to the most recent WHO global status report on road safety 2023, the number of road traffic deaths has decreased by 5% to 1.19 million yearly since 2010. However, traffic accidents continue to be a major worldwide health concern, posing a serious and growing danger of mortality to cyclists, pedestrians, and other road users who are more vulnerable. Inter-vehicular communication plays a critical role in enhancing vehicle safety in addition to driver behaviour and attitudes. Vehicle ad hoc networks, or VANETs, are becoming more widely acknowledged as essential parts of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) that improve road safety and efficiency by the government, car industry, and academic community. Disseminating safety-related information is made easier by VANETs, which allow direct connection between cars via onboard units (OBUs) or with infrastructure nodes like roadside units (RSUs). The position, speed, and acceleration of a vehicle are among the details that are periodically disseminated to nearby vehicles as basic safety messages (BSMs), also known as cooperative awareness messages (CAMs) in Europe. This allows for the execution of safety–critical applications, including lane change warnings, hard braking warnings, and collision avoidance. The efficient operation of several safety applications depends on the prompt and precise distribution of these notifications1,2.

75 MHz of the 5.9 GHz band will be available for vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication in the VANET via wireless access and specialized short-range communications for the vehicular environment (DSRC/WAVE). A further 30 MHz of airwaves are set aside for vehicle-to-everything (C-V2X) cellular connection. Dedicated to vehicle safety, channel 172 has a 10 MHz bandwidth and runs the 802.11p protocol, a contention-based random access MAC layer protocol. This kind of communication can result in several transmissions happening at once, which can lead to packet collisions and decrease communication dependability. The broadcasting of BSMs might easily cause congestion on this one channel as the number of cars rises, which will reduce transmission ranges and reception probabilities3,4.

It is commonly recognized that broadcasting a large number of BSM typically results in excessive latency, high bandwidth usage, and performance deterioration. Therefore, we need an efficient and effective approach that proactively solves the broadcast storm problem in terms of minimizing the massive amount of BSM for enhance the overall performance of the VANET. Because of the extremely mobile nodes and dynamic settings in vehicular ad hoc networks (VANETs), it is difficult to identify effective solutions for congestion and awareness. The traditional approaches address this issue by adjusting transmission parameters such as power and rate (often called beacon rate). However, this can lead to reduced situational awareness and increased inter-packet delay (IPD). Although achieving sufficient awareness is possible by finding an optimal combination of transmission power and rate that maintains channel load below a specific threshold, the optimization problem is non-convex, making it challenging to solve, as previously noted. Machine learning based congestion limiting mechanisms

Because of their dynamic nature, VANETs are highly challenging to analyse, yet this dynamic quality helps machine learning algorithms. Machine learning techniques may be used in a variety of VANET domains, such as multihop broadcasting, traffic congestion monitoring, and vehicle misbehaviour prediction. The numerous benefits of VANET in terms of efficiency, safety, and dependability are provided by ML Techniques. Moore Garret et al.5 suggested hybrid (active–passive) clustering for network congestion control, in which the vehicle position is embedded in the packet header and transmitted through the service channel to notify the cluster head. The passive technique is used to identify network congestion and reduce it by stopping the periodic beacon transmission on the control channel. This multi-channel technique makes VANETs more scalable; less congested, and has a faster propagation latency. Regin Rajan et al.6 suggested using density-based dynamic clustering, or DBDC, to lessen channel congestion. This method provides a proactive solution by determining the node density in a certain lane by comparing it to a threshold value determined by the training data set. The weight-based clustering and dynamic clustering for vehicles are used to compare the suggested method in terms of cluster head count, member life, and number of clusters. Mohanty Anita et al.7 focused on analysing clustering approaches to identify issues with congestion. This article presents an analysis, evaluation, and comparison of K Mean (based on centroid), FCM, and FKM (these clustering techniques are based on object). The results demonstrate that while all of these algorithms are comparable to one another in terms of congestion detection, fuzzy approaches are better suited for simulating extremely dynamic vehicular network scenarios. Rathore Nikhil et al.8 focuses on the channel collision detection and multi-agent stable clustering in VANETs. Static and mobile agents are used in this technique to provide stable communication between RSUs and vehicles. The RSU manager determines the size of the vehicle cluster, and the cluster leader (head) is selected based on the user’s improved connectivity and the vehicle’s speed. The pace of each cluster member determines the cluster longevity. In order to lessen channel congestion, they also concentrate on congestion detection and shortest path packet delivery. On NS2, the simulated results are computed in terms of cluster lifetime, transmission range, power consumption, throughput, etc. Tang Yujie et al.9 suggested a centralised routing system for predicting vehicle mobility that is based on artificial intelligence. The RSUs in the suggested route employ vehicle mobility prediction to estimate average delay and transmission probability. The suggested technique’s performance is computed with the assumption that the incoming vehicles adhere to a non-homogeneous Poisson process. Utilising the data from the RSUs, the SDN controller forecasts the best routing route. The findings of the simulation indicate that the suggested protocol reduced the transmission delay of VANET and is more resilient to changes in vehicle density. Liu et al.10 introduce a framework where vehicles utilize reinforcement learning (RL) to autonomously adjust their gearbox settings according to real-time changes in channel conditions. The simulations demonstrate that by tailoring suitable incentive functions, reinforcement learning methods offer a flexible approach to adaptive congestion control.

Transmission parameter based congestion limiting mechanisms

These types of congestion limiting techniques used optimal value of parameter like transmission power rate, channel parameters & transmission rate.

Congestion control by minimization of transmission power

By maintaining the vehicle’s transmission power, this technology dynamically modifies the beaconing transmission range, hence lowering the channel loads. Navdeti Chandrakant et al.11 suggested a congestion control system for vehicle communication that is based on an ideal node selection approach and adaptive, fairly distributed transmission power. In terms of packet delivery ratio, end-to-end latency, and packet reception probability, the suggested technique’s results are compared with those of the UV-CAST, DFAV, and DV-CAST techniques. Joseph et al.12 present an adaptive power control technique for VANETs that adjusts the transmit power of Basic Safety Message (BSM) packets based on vehicle speed, unlike fixed power methods. This approach aims to improve the reception of BSM packets by other vehicles. Simulation results show that it effectively reduces packet loss, Beacon Error Rates (BER), Channel Busy Time (CBT), and Inter-Packet Delay (IPD).

Congestion control by minimization of transmission rate

By adjusting the transmission rate, this technique lowers the channel load. Hajiaghajani Foad et al.13 suggested using an adaptive rate-based approach to congestion control in order to lower the channel load. The suggested method accounts for each vehicle’s contribution to channel congestion and determines the risk factor by applying the car-following model, which establishes the likelihood of a crash for each vehicle. In terms of throughput, PDR, and packet loss, the suggested solution performs better than the method described in IEE802.11p.

Congestion control by channel parameters

These types of techniques uses the channel parameters like size of contention windows, framing space etc. for reducing channel load. Abdelatif Sahraoui et al.14 suggested adding a cloud layer to the VANET’s layer design to help with channel congestion and traffic management. The suggested layer employs the fuzzy aggregation approach to propagate the anticipated data and delivers the cloud service and linked sensor data on demand. The outcomes of the simulation show that adding a cloud layer enhances network performance. Hsu et al.15 suggested the use of the Adaptable-Offset-Slot (AOS) congestion management method. Using this method, the contention window (CW) is resized based on the number of nearby cars. As the density of cars rises, so does the CW size. Hota et al.16 propose a method that optimizes throughput in vehicular networks by continuously adjusting the contention window (CW) parameter using a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework. This approach leverages local channel observations to improve performance, integrating learning-based MAC channel control with the IEEE 802.11p protocol. The actor-critic model used in this method significantly enhances throughput compared to the traditional IEEE 802.11p standard, as shown by simulation results.

Congestion control by message prioritization process

In this type of technique safety and non safety messages are prioritizing according to their impotence. Selvi et al.17 suggested broadcasting and adaptive scheduled partitioning methods to improve VANET transmission efficiency. In order to lower the retransmission rate, it modifies the beacon period and number of partitions. The number of partitions is determined by the density of vehicles in the network, and the lion algorithm, which is based on lion behaviours like mating, wandering, and capturing, optimises the data packet transmission rate of each partition. In addition, the suggested method chooses which message forwarding nodes to use and permits multidirectional, bidirectional, and directed communication. They assert that the suggested method can function in both urban and highway environments with varying network density. The findings suggest that the suggested methods offer superior efficiency, dependability, and reduced latency in contrast to the current VANET broadcasting strategy.

Congestion control by hybrid technique

This is the combination of transmission rate based, transmission power based, messages prioritization based and channel parameter based techniques. Liang Zijie et al.18 found the optimal parameter value for the stable and highly efficient VANET, which uses the DCC algorithm for congestion detection and avoidance. They also analysed the DCC algorithm for achieving the channel load and found the effect of transmission rate, transmission power, message generation rate, and number of vehicles on channel load for the platooning based ad-hoc network. Mansouri Adel et al.19 Using the 3GPP LTE-V2X technology for direct communication between vehicles without the need for infrastructure. They identify channel congestion using the listen before (LB) approach and employ the DCC algorithm’s idea and principles to minimise it. Karoui Mouna20 suggested a strategy for minimising channel congestion that combines the adaptive traffic control technology (DRC/ATCL) with data/message rate regulation. They used transmission probability to create the CBR in order to determine the channel load. The computation of performance outcomes is based on information loss and delay. Problem solving strategies

Many researchers focus on clustering messages to reduce congestion by eliminating duplicate message packets. However, our proposed technique applies clustering to vehicles to identify the nearest ones, thereby enhancing safety by preventing accidents. An adaptive transmission range is then used for vehicles not in immediate danger. The proposed algorithm shown in this section performs better than existing congestion control techniques.

Phase 1 Dynamic grouping of vehicles

In this phase dynamic grouping of vehicles is done with the help of density based and centroid based clustering. For the density based clustering DBSCAN algorithm is used and for centriod based clustering K Mean algorithm is used. In the first part of this phase, dynamic grouping of vehicles is done by using the density based grouping algorithm. According to this algorithm it detected the area where the concentration of data point is high and other data points are treated as noise.

Density based DBSCAN algorithm takes three inputs, position of vehicles at each time instant, epsilon value that denotes the radius of cluster which is depends on transmission or reception range of inbuilt OBU on vehicles, and minimum number of member should present in cluster, to provide the grouping of vehicles. The output of algorithm provides total number of groups, information about each group members and it also identifies the vehicles which do not belong to any group (Noise vehicles). Compared to group members, the vehicles which do not belong to any group have less information to share because they are in safe position therefore the noise vehicle or outlier vehicles can work on less transmission rate. By using this technique the load on the channel can be reduced. After identification of group members and outlier vehicles, group head is decided on the bases of distance between group members to the nearest RSU. The group member becomes group head which have minimum distance from any RSU of network. All the group members directly communicate to the group head and these group head communicate to the nearest RSU. This technique is far field communication centric technique because in this technique the choice of cluster head is based on distance between RSU and vehicles, means it promote the vehicle to RSU communication which enable long distance communication through RSU to RSU connection. In the second part of this phase the cetriod based k Mean algorithm is used at the place of DBSCAN. The nearest automobiles can avoid potentially dangerous circumstances since the K mean technique groups the cars depending on their intermediate distance from one another, accounting for the transmission range. In contrast to non-group member automobiles, these group member vehicles are designated for the transmission of a high volume of communications in order to mitigate risk. K Means receives three inputs for grouping: the number of iterations, the location of the automobiles (vehicles) at any given instant, and the number of automobiles themselves. The number of groups, together with each group’s leader and members, is the output of K mean. The number of active vehicles is always changing since they are moving, which causes their positions to vary as well. This leads to variations in the number of groups, the selection of group leaders, and the group heads’ locations. Consequently, the quantity of cars and their locations are arbitrary figures. The vehicle which is very near to the group centriod become group head and this group head farther communicate to the nearest RSU21. Because of the selection procedure of group head, the entire group member will be very near to the group head that promote the near field communication. All the group heads communicate to its nearest RSU and through RSU to RSU communication, the information will be propagate in the network. End to end delay

End to end delay comprises processing, queuing, propagation, and transmission delays. The overall delay will be affected by the velocity of the vehicles as well as the distance between the sender and receiving vehicles. The propagation delay is determined by the relative velocity of the transmitter and receiver, as well as their intermediate distance, and so the network’s overall latency fluctuates.

Conclusion and future scope

Data congestion in VANETs has been evaluated using throughput, Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR), and end-to-end latency, following the IEEE 802.11p standard. This paper introduces a dynamic clustering approach for vehicular communication that utilizes K-Means and DBSCAN algorithms. The comparative analysis, presented in a table, reveals that the K-Means-based dynamic clustering technique outperforms the DBSCAN-based method in scenarios where transmission power varies while packet size remains constant, and vice versa. Furthermore, the paper compares the throughput of the proposed Dynamic Vehicle Grouping Scheme (DVGS) using K-Means and DBSCAN with the existing Decentralized Congestion Control (DCC) technique. The results indicate that the DVGS technique, particularly with K-Means, performs better than the DCC in densely populated vehicular networks. The results section provides a quantitative description in tabular form. In the DVGS approach, the number of groups is determined by the transmission power and range, while group members adjust the adaptive transmission rate based on the Channel Busy Ratio (CBR). The K-Means-based method demonstrates higher throughput compared to the DBSCAN-based clustering technique. Performance metrics show that PDR increases with higher transmission power and more packets are successfully delivered to the destination. Packet size also influences the PDR. Factors such as transmission rate, contention window size, power, and range significantly impact channel congestion. The proposed DVGS technique has been analyzed using the K-Means and DBSCAN algorithms in conjunction with AODV routing protocols. Future research could explore the performance of the DVGS technique with alternative routing protocols to determine which protocol best complements the proposed approach.
