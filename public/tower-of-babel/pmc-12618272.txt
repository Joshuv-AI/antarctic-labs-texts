Comprehensive Benefits of No‐Lift Care in Older Adults With Dementia: Improvement in Contractures, Sleep Quality, and Psychosocial Function
Authors: Reiko Koyama, Junko Shida, Kenichi Kato, Junya Ito, Chisaki Uno, Yumi Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNo‐lift care may have comprehensive benefits for older adults with dementia or communication difficulties, not only by improving contractures and sleep quality, but also by positively influencing physical function, psychological well‐being, and social behavior.

Introduction

No‐lift care, a concept first proposed in Australia in 1988, is based on the principle of avoiding physically strenuous actions such as pushing, pulling, or lifting by human force [1]. Specifically, it integrates three core components—(1) mobility and transfer support, (2) appropriate positioning, and (3) the use of assistive devices—to ensure safe and comfortable care for both the care recipient and the caregiver. The effectiveness of this approach has been widely recognized from the perspective of occupational health and safety, including the prevention of caregiver back pain and the reduction of work‐related disorders [2], and its implementation has been steadily promoted. However, its adoption in Japan remains limited, and scientific evaluation of its physiological and psychological benefits for care recipients is still insufficient both domestically and internationally [3]. This lack of empirical evidence is considered one of the key factors delaying the wider dissemination of no‐lift care.

Most recipients of no‐lift care are bedridden older adults with dementia. In particular, when communication is impaired, it is difficult for the individual to verbalize their comfort or discomfort, and physical discomfort—such as pain or contractures—may manifest as so‐called “problem behaviors,” including resistance to care [4, 5]. Discomfort may also present as psychiatric symptoms such as delirium [5], leading to decreased sleep quality and frequent nighttime awakenings, ultimately resulting in a significant decline in quality of life (QOL) [6]. Although pharmacological interventions as well as non‐pharmacological approaches—such as aromatherapy and massage—have been attempted to address these challenges, such practices remain limited, and their effectiveness has not been clearly demonstrated [7]. Moreover, these issues not only cause suffering for the care recipients themselves but also impose substantial physical and psychological burdens on caregivers, representing a serious concern in care settings.

In our own special nursing home, we had been exploring effective support strategies in response to signs of QOL decline—such as poor sleep quality and frequent nighttime awakenings—observed among bedridden residents with impaired communication. It was in this context that we encountered the concept of no‐lift care. Focusing on its potential benefits for both residents and caregivers, we implemented no‐lift care throughout the facility. As a result, several residents demonstrated measurable improvements in sleep quality, along with positive changes across physiological and psychological domains. In particular, observed shifts in autonomic nervous system activity and psychological stabilization through sensory input suggest the involvement of multiple underlying mechanisms. This case thus serves as a valuable starting point for exploring the multifaceted benefits of no‐lift care for care recipients.

Objective

This case study focused on an older adult with dementia residing in a special nursing home. The aim was to investigate the effects of no‐lift care on the individual's overall QOL, including physiological and psychological domains such as sleep quality, through physical changes like postural improvement, as well as its influence on daytime activity levels and circadian rhythm.

Methods

No‐Lift Care Procedures

Transfers to and from the bed and wheelchair were performed without lifting or dragging the participant in a way that would impose undue physical burden [1]. Instead, assistive devices such as sliding sheets and boards were utilized. In addition, the following standard care interventions were consistently applied in response to daily activities such as toileting, transfers, and pre‐bedtime routines: Step‐In Motion Support

Sleep Monitoring

Sleep was monitored using MAMORUNO (ZIPCARE Inc.) [9], a noninvasive sleep assessment system. The device consists of an airbag sensor placed beneath the mattress and an environmental sensor attached to the bed frame, allowing for contact‐free monitoring of the participant's sleep status.

The system evaluates six sleep‐related indicators, including total sleep time and sleep efficiency. Based on these metrics, an overall sleep score ranging from 0 to 100 is calculated, with higher scores indicating better sleep quality. Case Presentation

This report presents two residents diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease living in a special nursing home. No‐lift care was initiated with Case A, which led to improvement in joint contractures and indications of more stable sleep. Based on these findings, sleep monitoring was additionally implemented for Case B.

Case A

Patient Information

An 86‐year‐old bedridden male requiring total assistance with nearly all activities of daily living, corresponding to a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 2–3.

Clinical Findings

He was diagnosed with Alzheimer's‐type dementia. Due to joint contractures, he presented with approximately 30° of head flexion, kyphotic posture, and approximately 90° of knee flexion. He also exhibited verbal and behavioral resistance to care.

Sleep status: No episodes of nighttime awakenings were observed.

Therapeutic Intervention

Positioning intervention involved the insertion of a boomerangshaped cushion from the upper back to the head and a wave‐shaped cushion from the thighs to the lower legs, supporting the body from beneath to facilitate pressure relief through “offloading.” Additionally, a cushion was placed beneath the soles of the feet to simulate a sensation of ground contact (Figure 2A,B).

(A) Before the initiation of positioning his posture shows marked flexion of the head, kyphosis, and approximately 90° of knee flexion due to contractures. (B) Positioning with cushions Boomerang‐shaped and wave‐shaped cushions are placed to support the head, back, thighs, and lower legs, enabling pressure offloading and promoting a more stable posture. (C) Six months after initiating positioning care contractures have improved; the head and knees are now in contact with the bed surface, increasing surface contact and postural stability, even without the use of positioning cushions.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Six months after the initiation of no‐lift care, including the described positioning techniques, a marked reduction in contractures was observed. Even when the positioning cushions were removed, the previously noted flexion of the head, kyphosis, and 90‐degree knee flexion had resolved. The head and both knees made direct contact with the bed surface, indicating a significant increase in contact area (Figure 2C). Verbal and behavioral resistance to care also decreased. Although nighttime awakenings had been rare initially, improvements were observed in facial expression and reduced muscular tension during sleep. The patient was later discharged from the facility due to family circumstances.

Case B

Patient Information

A 96‐year‐old female requiring partial daily assistance for most activities of daily living, with a CDR of 1–2.

Clinical Findings

The patient was diagnosed with Alzheimer's‐type dementia, with no significant core or peripheral symptoms related to dementia. She also had profound hearing loss and right‐sided osteoarthritis of the hip. In addition to pain in the lower limbs due to hip pathology, bilateral dorsal foot edema was observed. The right hip joint was in a fixed position of external rotation, while the left hip was in a fixed position of internal rotation (Figure 3A).

(A) At the start of positioning care the patient's posture shows asymmetry and poor alignment, including fixed hip rotation and limited lower limb contact with the bed surface. (B) Postural alignment of the left hip joint showing fixed internal rotation before intervention. (C) Postural alignment of the right hip joint showing fixed external rotation before intervention.

Sleep status: The patient had difficulty falling asleep and frequent nighttime awakenings, with an average sleep score of 78, indicating slightly poor sleep quality.

Therapeutic Intervention

Positioning care included placing a wave‐shaped cushion beneath the thighs and lower legs to provide support from below. To address the external rotation of the right hip, a long vertical cushion was inserted alongside the hip (Figure 3B,C).

Additionally, a cushion was placed under the soles of the feet to create a sense of ground contact.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Initially, the patient requested that the cushions be removed, saying, “Please take the pillow away.” Consequently, positioning was temporarily discontinued. However, she later reported, “It seems to hurt without the cushion,” prompting the resumption of positioning care.

One week after the introduction of no‐lift care, including positioning, the average sleep score improved to 86 (“good”). Improvements were seen in sleep duration, deep sleep time, reduced sleep onset latency, and fewer nighttime awakenings. After one month, the weekly average sleep score had further improved to 88, with continued improvements in sleep onset and reduced awakenings. Alongside pain relief and improved sleep quality, the patient became more active during the day, gaining independence in standing up and pulling up her pants during toileting.

One year after the initiation of care, the edema in both feet had decreased, and the left hip joint had improved to a mild internal rotation position (Figure 4). The average sleep score remained stable in the 80s, maintaining a “good” range.

Discussion

In the present case series, the introduction of no‐lift care was associated with the alleviation and suggested potential improvement in postural control. In one resident, these changes were accompanied by enhanced daytime activity and partial recovery of autonomy, indicating a possible chain of physiological, psychological, and social benefits. While preliminary, these findings suggest that no‐lift care may offer multifaceted benefits to care recipients and highlight a novel and internationally relevant approach to dementia care.

The effects of sustained comfortable positioning were evident as a reduction in head and knee flexion in Case A and an improvement in hip rotation in Case B. Both residents initially required full assistance for bed mobility; however, the use of assistive devices, such as sliding sheets, allowed for transfers without the need for lifting or dragging. Lifting and dragging typically provoke excessive muscular tension, and the accumulation of such stress may lead to contractures. These observations suggest that appropriate no‐lift care may have contributed to breaking this cycle of tension [10, 11, 12], potentially facilitating the alleviation of contractures and improvement in postural stability. Furthermore, stabilization of daily posture may have promoted thoracic expansion and improved respiratory function and oxygenation [13], which could positively affect autonomic nervous system balance [14].

In addition, the application of plantar stimulation and the creation of a ground‐contact sensation through foot support—administered as part of the standard care protocol—likely provided proprioceptive input through plantar reflexes and sensory feedback [15]. This sensory input may have contributed to the clarification and stabilization of body schema. The change in expression by Case B—initially requesting removal of the cushion but later stating, “It hurts without it”—may indicate a reacquisition of bodily awareness and a restoration of autonomous choice. Although Case A had difficulty verbalizing, a reduction in care refusal behaviors and improved acceptance of care were observed.

These findings support the understanding of no‐lift care not merely as a physical assistance technique but as an interactive, person‐centered approach that respects the comfort and autonomy of the care recipient. Expressions of refusal or complaints may be interpreted as manifestations of bodily sensation or self‐determination. For caregivers, sensitively detecting and responding to such nonverbal or indirect cues can directly contribute to psychological stability and improvement in QOL. This perspective aligns with the philosophy of Humanitude, which emphasizes the importance of communicating a sense of value and respect to the care recipient, as well as the principle of stimulating cognitive and emotional functions through sensory inputs such as standing and plantar stimulation [16, 17]. Even in individuals unable to stand, plantar stimulation may contribute to physiological and psychological improvement via sensory‐motor‐emotional networks [15], which may ultimately enhance wake–sleep rhythms, daytime activity, and functional independence.

Moreover, the improvement in sleep quality observed in one resident included decreased nighttime awakenings and prolonged deep sleep. This may be attributed to appropriate body support and pressure relief via no‐lift care, which likely improved local circulation and reduced muscle tension [10, 11, 12]. As a result, the body surface contact area increased, the center of gravity stabilized, and a more comfortable posture was maintained. Stable posture has been associated with autonomic regulation, including the promotion of parasympathetic dominance [18, 19], which may have supported the improvements observed in sleep indicators in this case.

Limitations and Future Directions

As a preliminary case series with only two participants who differed in baseline characteristics—including sex, cognitive status, and presenting problems—the findings should be interpreted with caution and are not intended to be generalizable. Nonetheless, the study offers novel global insight by suggesting that no‐lift care may have wide‐ranging effects—not only improving physiological aspects such as joint contractures and sleep quality, but also contributing to psychological stability and enhanced social engagement. Future studies should incorporate multifaceted assessments that include objective indicators of autonomic and brain activity, along with the accumulation of additional cases, to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these effects and to verify these preliminary and hypothesis‐generating findings.

Conclusion

This case report suggests that no‐lift care may offer comprehensive effects that extend beyond the alleviation of contractures, including improvements in sleep quality, physical function, psychological well‐being, and social behavior. Notably, the use of sleep monitoring demonstrated the potential to visualize the effects and comfort of no‐lift care even in older adults with dementia or severe communication difficulties. Future large‐scale and quantitative studies are warranted to examine the applicability and reproducibility of these preliminary findings and to validate the potential benefits observed in this small case series, thereby contributing to the development of evidence‐based guidelines for broader clinical implementation.
