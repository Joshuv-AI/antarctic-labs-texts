IRE1/p‑JNK/NRF2 axis-mediated GPX4 upregulation underlies ferroptosis resistance in rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes
Authors: Jiaxi Liu, Wenhui Xie, Yongping Cao, Liang Zhang, Yan Geng, Zhuoli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis fibroblast‑like synoviocytes (RA‑FLS) exhibit chronic endoplasmic reticulum stress with activation of the IRE1/phospho‑JNK arm of the unfolded protein response; yet, they remain resistant to apoptosis. Although ferroptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis, the mechanisms underlying ferroptosis resistance in RA‑FLS remain poorly understood.MethodsA total of 17 patients who underwent joint replacement were included (RA n = 9; OA n = 8). Primary fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS; ≥3 donors per assay) were analyzed by RT-qPCR and western blotting for IRE1, p-JNK, NRF2 and GPX4. Signaling was modulated with siRNA or inhibitors against ERN1 or NRF2. Ferroptosis was assessed by transmission electron microscopy, lipid peroxidation, Fe²⁺ and glutathione depletion; cell phenotypes were evaluated by invasion, wound-healing and CCK-8 assays. In vivo (≥ 4 per group), a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rat model received the IRE1 inhibitor 4µ8c; micro-CT, joint pathology and biochemical markers were evaluated.ResultsRA-FLS showed elevated IRE1/p-JNK, increased total and nuclear NRF2, and higher GPX4 versus OA-FLS, and were more resistant to ferroptosis. ERN1 knockdown reduced p-JNK, NRF2 and GPX4 and increased lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, glutathione loss and ferroptosis. NRF2 inhibition also sensitized RA-FLS to ferroptosis and attenuated aggressive phenotypes. In CIA rats, IRE1 inhibition partially attenuated bone destruction, lowered serum IL-1β/MMP9, and reduced synovial GPX4.ConclusionSustained IRE1/p-JNK/NRF2 signaling upregulates GPX4, linking ER stress to ferroptosis resistance in RA-FLS. Targeting IRE1 lowers GPX4 and restores ferroptotic sensitivity, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for RA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03827-5.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by chronic, destructive synovial inflammation that causes progressive joint damage and disability. Although conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and targeted agents can control disease activity, many patients still require long-term therapy and only 30–70% achieve an adequate response [1]. Fibroblast‑like synoviocytes (FLS) have been the key effector cells in RA. Compared with osteoarthritis (OA) FLS, RA‑FLS undergo a dramatic phenotypic transformation characterized by tumor-like activation, increased proliferation, resistance to apoptosis and heightened invasive capacity. These activated RA‑FLS produce pro‑inflammatory cytokines and secrete matrix metalloproteinases that degrade the cartilage extracellular matrix [2, 3]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that regulate this aggressive phenotype is a major goal of current RA research.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the unfolded protein response play dual roles in cell fate: moderate ER stress triggers adaptive responses to restore homeostasis, whereas excessive or persistent stress promotes programmed cell death [4, 5]. Our previous work demonstrated that the ER stress pathway was strongly activated in RA-FLS compared with OA-FLS. The IRE1/phosphorylated JNK (p‑JNK), branch of the ER, is markedly upregulated in RA‑FLS [6]. Despite this persistent stress signal, RA‑FLS do not undergo cell death, instead exhibit enhanced proliferation, suggesting presence of a “death‑resistant” state. Pharmacological inhibition of ER stress attenuates this survival advantage, however, the downstream pathways by which the IRE1/p‑JNK axis modulates this paradoxical phenotype remain undefined.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, nonapoptotic form of regulated cell death that has been extensively studied [7–10]. The contribution of ferroptosis to RA remains controversial: both increased and decreased ferroptotic activity have been reported in RA‑FLS [11, 12]. In mouse models, combining ferroptosis inducers with TNF-α antagonists reverts the ferroptosis resistance of RA‑FLS and ameliorates synovial inflammation [13]. These observations suggest that reduced sensitivity to ferroptosis is an important mechanism by which RA‑FLS maintain their pathological survival.

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a crucial antioxidant enzyme that detoxifies lipid hydroperoxides and suppresses ferroptosis [14–16]. NRF2 is a master transcription factor that coordinates antioxidant responses and directly upregulates genes such as GPX4 to maintain redox homeostasis [17]. In FLS, overactivation of NRF2/GPX4 signaling may contribute to ferroptosis resistance in RA.

NRF2 is well known for the antioxidant response; it can also be activated by unfolded protein response machinery [18]. Regarding JNK–NRF2 crosstalk, stress‑activated MAP kinases, including p38 and JNK, can facilitate NRF2 nuclear translocation by recruiting the Hsp90α-Pin1-Dynein transport machinery [19]. Some studies have shown that JNK-mediated phosphorylation of NRF2 can expose degradation motifs and accelerate its degradation [20], and knockdown of NRF2 exacerbates TNF-induced proliferation and invasion of RA-FLS by activating the JNK pathway [21].

Given that the IRE1/JNK axis may regulate NRF2 stability and NRF2 in turn controls GPX4 expression, we hypothesize that the IRE1/p‑JNK/NRF2 pathway determines the sensitivity of synovial fibroblasts to ferroptosis. By dissecting this signaling cascade, we aimed to clarify whether IRE1 promotes or impedes NRF2/GPX4 signaling in RA‑FLS and to determine how modulation of ER stress influences ferroptosis resistance. These insights may reveal new targets for RA intervention.

Materials and methods

OA and RA patients

The samples were obtained from RA and OA patients who attended the Peking University First Hospital. All of them fulfilled the criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology in 2010 for RA or 1991 for OA [22, 23]. A total of 17 patients undergoing joint replacement were included (RA, n = 9; OA, n = 8). Due to limited cell/protein yield and quality-control requirements, donor numbers varied by assay; n corresponds to the number of data points (dots) in each panel (each dot represents one donor). Biological replicates were independent donors; technical replicates were averaged per donor for statistical analysis. Because RA and OA samples were identified according to established clinical diagnoses during collection and primary FLS preparation, blinding was not applied in this part of the study. To minimize potential bias, assays were performed using predefined protocols and standardized experimental conditions.

The study was in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University First Hospital (2024 027 − 003). Informed consent was obtained from each patient. No identifiable human images or personal data are included in this article.

Isolation and culture of FLS

The synovial tissue was minced and placed in a dish containing type I collagenase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US) solution and digested for 2 h. The solid and liquid in the dish were collected and transferred to a new 15mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged. Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, US) was added to the tube, and the mixture was transferred to a new culture dish and digested in the incubator for 1 h. The solid-liquid mixture in the dish was then filtered. The filtrate was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded and PBS (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added for washing. The cell pellet was resuspended in complete medium and primary cell isolation and culture were initiated in the incubator. The successfully isolated primary cells were then passaged at a ratio of 1:2 [24]. Cells between passages P3 and P8 were used for experiments. This passage range was selected to obtain a relatively homogeneous fibroblast-like synoviocyte population after primary isolation, because very early passages may still contain residual non-fibroblast synovial cells and limited cell numbers, whereas later passages are more susceptible to culture-related phenotypic drift. Figures were taken under Olympus IX73 microscope with 4× objectives.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from FLS using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US), and reverse transcription was performed using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper; Vazyme Biotech Co., Nanjing, China). The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) served as the template for qPCR amplification, which was conducted using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US). The gene primers used are listed in Supplementary Material 1. Gene expression was normalized to that of β‑actin using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Western blotting was performed with primary antibodies against IRE1 (1:2000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), p-IRE1(1:1000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), p‑JNK (1:1000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), NRF2 (1:2000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and GPX4 (1:1000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China). β‑Actin or GAPDH (1:20000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China) served as loading controls. Secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‑conjugated goat anti‑mouse or anti‑rabbit IgG (1:20000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Bands were visualised using a Gene Gnome XRQ (Synoptics Ltd, Cambridge, United Kingdom).

Ferroptosis detection kit

Ferrous ion (Fe²⁺), glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using kits from Elabscience (Wuhan, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions; at least three biological replicates were analyzed for each readout.

Transmission electron microscopy

Adherent synovial fibroblasts with a cell density not exceeding 70% were washed. After digestion, the cells were centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were fixed at room temperature in the dark for 30 min and then transferred to 4 °C for storage. Transmission electron microscopy was performed using models HT7800/HT7700 (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

siRNA knockdown

Synovial fibroblasts were plated in antibiotic-free medium 24 h before transfection. For each well of a 6-well plate, siRNA-lipid complexes were prepared in Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US) by mixing siRNA (final 50nM) with Lipofectamine 3000 (5µL/well, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US) and incubated for 15 min. After 6 h, medium was replaced with complete DMEM (10%FBS). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for assays.

A non-targeting siRNA served as negative control. Knockdown efficiency was verified by qPCR (ΔΔCt, normalized to GAPDH) and western blot (normalized to GAPDH). All reagents were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The details of siRNA sequence are listed in Supplementary Material 1.

Matrigel invasion assay

Transwell inserts (24-well, 8 μm pores; Corning Inc, Corning, US) were coated with growth-factor-reduced Matrigel (1:3 in serum-free medium, 50µL/insert) and allowed to gel at 37 °C for 30–60 min. Synovial fibroblasts were serum-starved, detached, and seeded at about 1 × 105 cells/insert in 200µL serum-free medium. Lower chambers contained 600µL medium with 20% FBS. After 36 h at 37 °C, non-invaded cells on the upper side were removed with cotton swabs. Membranes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (20 min), rinsed, air-dried, and mounted. Invaded cells on the underside were imaged (200×) and counted in ≥ 3 random fields per insert; ≥3 technical replicates per condition were analyzed. Figures were taken under bright‑field on an Olympus IX73 microscope with 20× objectives. ImageJ was used for quantification.

Scratch (wound-healing) migration assay

Synovial fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to a uniform confluent monolayer. Cells were serum-starved. Linear scratches were made using a 200µL pipette tip held perpendicular to the plate. Fresh medium was added, and images were captured at 0 h and 24 h using identical microscope settings. Figures were taken under bright‑field on an Olympus IX73 microscope with 4× objectives. Wound closure was analyzed in ImageJ by thresholding and measuring the wound area at each time point. At least 3 random fields/well and ≥ 3 technical replicates were evaluated.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at about 5 × 10³ cells/well (100µL/well) and allowed to attach overnight. At each time point, 10µL CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) was added per well (final 10% v/v) and plates were incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was read at 450 nm. Medium-only wells served as blanks. Each condition was tested in ≥ 3 technical replicates and repeated in ≥ 3 independent experiments.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Synovial fibroblasts were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in the kit lysis buffer (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China, YJ201). The detection of endogenous proteins interaction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China, YJ201).

Collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rat model

Female wild‑type Wistar rats (7 weeks old, weighing about 120 g, SPF, Huafukang, Beijing, China) were selected to establish CIA model. Health: immunocompetent, experiment‑naïve on arrival. Rats were housed 4 per cage in individually ventilated cages with bedding and environmental enrichment. Cages were cleaned on a regular schedule and husbandry conditions were identical across groups. Bovine type II collagen (Chondrex, Redmond, WA, USA) was emulsified in an equal concentration of complete Freund’s adjuvant at a 1:1 ratio on ice, and total 500µL of the emulsion was injected into the tail and back to complete the first immunization. 7 days later, prepared type II collagen was emulsified with the same dose of incomplete Freund’s adjuvant and injected into the tails and back in the same manner to complete the second immunization.

The rats were randomly divided before experiment with n ≥ 4 in each group. We did not pre‑specify any inclusion or exclusion criteria beyond species, age and sex. The experimental unit was the individual rat. They were divided into the following groups: naïve control group; saline treatment group (intraperitoneal injection of saline at a dose of 100ul per day for 1 week); and 4µ8c treatment group (at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day in 100ul for 1 week). Because the saline and 4µ8c groups involved predefined treatment allocation during the experimental workflow, blinding was not applied in this part of the study. To minimize potential bias, animals were randomized before the experiment, outcome measurements were obtained using standardized protocols, and histologic staining was processed in the same batches under identical conditions.

To reduce pain and distress, procedures were performed by trained personnel and, when appropriate, under isoflurane anesthesia. Humane endpoints were predefined (severe ulceration/infection, sustained inability to ambulate) and would trigger euthanasia by CO₂ inhalation. No expected or unexpected adverse events were observed in any group. All the animal experiments and procedures were conducted following the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University First Hospital (No. J2025020).

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Group sizes were informed by prior CIA rat studies, pilot variability, and 3R considerations; we targeted ≥ 4 rats per group, and the exact n for each outcome is reported in the figure panels.

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) imaging

We used NMC-200 (Kunshan, Jiangsu, China) to reconstruct the hind paws of rats into three-dimensional bone structures. The details of scanning parameters and software are described in Supplementary Material 1.

ELISA

Serum was obtained from peripheral blood of the rats before and after treatment. The detection of IL‑1β, TNF‑α and MMP9 (Multi Sciences Biotech, Hangzhou, China) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Safranin O–Fast Green staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. Sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed in running tap water for 5 min. Fast Green was applied for 3 min, followed by 1% acetic acid differentiation for 10s. Sections were then stained in Safranin O for 5 min, dehydrated rapidly through 95% and 100% ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with resinous medium. Figures were taken under bright‑field on an Olympus IX73 microscope with 5× and 20× objectives.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed in citrate buffer and sections were cooled to room temperature. Sections were blocked with 5% normal serum for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies GPX4 (Abclonal,1:100, Wuhan, China). An HRP-polymer secondary system was applied for 30 min, signal was developed with DAB (fixed exposure time across batches), nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Slides from non-immunized control group, saline treatment group, and 4µ8c treatment group were processed in the same staining batches with identical retrieval and DAB times. Figures were taken under bright‑field on an Olympus IX73 microscope with 4× and 10× objectives.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (La Jolla, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using the Brown–Forsythe test. For two-group comparisons, unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t test was used as appropriate; when assumptions were not met, the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Holm–Šidák-adjusted multiple comparisons was used; when assumptions were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was applied. For repeated-measures data, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Šidák-adjusted multiple comparisons was used.

Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. n denotes biological replicates; technical replicates were averaged within each biological replicate before statistical analysis. No a priori power calculation was performed, and no data points, samples, or animals were excluded; no post hoc outlier removal was applied.

Results

Enhanced ER stress and reduced ferroptosis in RA‑FLS compared with OA‑FLS

Fibroblasts from synovial tissue were isolated and characterized. Compared with OA synovial tissue, RA synovial tissue exhibits marked synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, and pannus formation. Immunofluorescence of the cells confirmed a fibroblast phenotype: vimentin-positive and CD68-negative (Supplementary Material 2).

After successfully obtaining OA‑FLS and RA‑FLS, we first investigated gene expression of ER stress (ERN1 and ERN2), JNK(MAPK8, MAPK9, MAPK10) and ferroptosis‑related signaling pathways (NFE2L2 and GPX4) between OA‑FLS and RA‑FLS. RT-qPCR results showed that mRNA levels of ERN1, MAPK8, MAPK9, MAPK10, NFE2L2, and GPX4 were significantly elevated in RA-FLS relative to OA-FLS, whereas ERN2 transcripts were barely detectable in either cell type (Fig. 1A). In whole-cell lysates, WB showed elevated levels of IRE1(encoded by ERN1), p-JNK, NRF2(encoded by NFE2L2), and GPX4 in RA-FLS relative to OA-FLS (Fig. 1C), with higher p-IRE1 in RA-FLS than in OA-FLS (Fig. 1B). NRF2 is a transcription factor that translocates to the nucleus, we also extracted nuclear proteins and compared the nuclear expression of NRF2, and NRF2 was increased in RA-FLS (Fig. 1D).

We further selected different indicators to compare ferroptosis in the two cell groups. In RA-FLS, intracellular ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) was decreased, reduced glutathione (GSH) content was increased, and malondialdehyde (MDA) was decreased relative to OA-FLS (Fig. 1E). Then, we used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine mitochondrial ultrastructure, and OA-FLS exhibited more characteristic ferroptotic features, including mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae, and increased membrane electron density (Fig. 1F). These results indicate a marked attenuation of ferroptosis in RA-FLS. Finally, co‑immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated the protein interaction between p‑JNK and NRF2 in both OA-FLS and RA-FLS (Fig. 1G).

To further explore whether there is a relationship between ferroptosis and ER stress, we compared the expression of the key ER stress protein IRE1 in RA‑FLS before and after addition of the ferroptosis inducer RSL3. The WB results showed IRE1 expression decreased after RSL3 treatment (Supplementary Material 2).

ERN1 knockdown reduces NRF2 and GPX4 expression and increases ferroptosis in RA‑FLS

According to our previous finding, ER stress is enhanced and ferroptosis is reduced in RA‑FLS. ER stress is linked to ferroptosis via protein interaction between p‑JNK and NRF2. Therefore, we selected the most effective siRNA 2 (Fig. 2A and B) to knock down ERN1, the key ER stress gene encoding IRE1, and compared the expression of pathway‑related proteins and ferroptosis before and after knockdown. The results showed that ERN1 knockdown substantially reduced the expression of p‑JNK, total NRF2, nuclear NRF2 and the ferroptosis key protein GPX4 (Fig. 2C and D). Co‑immunoprecipitation tests demonstrated that the protein interaction between p‑JNK and NRF2 exists both before and after ERN1 knockdown (Fig. 2E).

NRF2 activation partially rescues ferroptosis and tumor-like phenotypes in ERN1-depleted RA-FLS

To further demonstrate the functional interaction between IRE1 and NRF2, we added an NRF2 activator (Nrf2 activator-4, MCE, Monmouth Junction, USA) after knocking down ERN1 to determine whether ferroptosis or cell phenotypes in RA‑FLS could be reversed.

Fe²⁺, GSH, MDA, and TEM readouts indicated a partial reversal of ferroptosis (Fig. 3A, B). Cell invasiveness (Fig. 3C), viability (Fig. 3D), and migratory capacity (Fig. 3E) were nearly restored to pre-ERN1-knockdown levels.

NRF2 inhibition promotes ferroptosis and attenuates aggressive phenotypes in RA-FLS

We used ML385 (a specific nuclear NRF2 inhibitor, MCE, Monmouth Junction, USA) to inhibit NRF2 expression and compared the expression of pathway‑related proteins and ferroptosis before and after NRF2 inhibition. We found that NRF2 inhibition markedly reduced the expression of p‑JNK, key ER stress proteins IRE1 and ferroptosis key protein GPX4 in RA‑FLS (Fig. 4A).

Moreover, Fe²⁺ increased, GSH decreased, and MDA increased (Fig. 4B). By TEM, characteristic ferroptotic ultrastructural features—mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae, and increased membrane electron density—were observed (Fig. 4C). IRE1 inhibition partially attenuates bone destruction in CIA rats, lowers serum IL-1β/MMP9, and Reduces Synovial GPX4

CIA rats were used to further verify the findings revealed by cell experiments. The experiment timeline and model-establishment results are shown in Fig. 5A and B. The IRE1 inhibitor 4µ8c (a small-molecule inhibitor of IRE1α, MCE, Monmouth Junction, USA) was administered intraperitoneally for 7 days. We found that injection of the IRE1 inhibitor partially ameliorated joint bone destruction in CIA rats (Fig. 5C and D) and reduced serum levels of IL‑1β and MMP9, although there was no statistically significant difference in the level of TNF‑α (Fig. 5E). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of GPX4 in joint synovial tissues from different groups showed that the IRE1 inhibitor decreased GPX4 expression in synovial tissue (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

RA‑FLS contribute to joint damage by proliferating, invading cartilage and secreting pro‑inflammatory mediators [2, 3]. Previous studies have reported that IRE1 inhibition alleviates inflammatory arthritis [25], and JNK inhibitors reduce serum IL‑6 and MMP‑3 levels in patients with RA and decrease MMP‑3 expression in synovial cells [26].

Our study demonstrates that persistent activation of the IRE1/p-JNK/NRF2 axis in RA-FLS regulates GPX4 upregulation and confers resistance to ferroptosis. Knockdown of ERN1 reduced NRF2 and GPX4 expression, leading to increased lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation and cell death. Co-IP results suggest a direct or indirect interaction between p-JNK and NRF2, an interaction that is associated with NRF2 nuclear accumulation and target-gene induction. Notably, although ERN1 knockdown reduced p-JNK and total NRF2 levels, it did not fully abolish their interaction, suggesting that the p-JNK and NRF2 association may be a basal interaction that is not strictly dependent on sustained IRE1 activation. Then, overactivation of NRF2 restored GPX4 levels and rescued ferroptosis resistance (Fig. 6). In vivo, IRE1 inhibition also sensitized RA-FLS to ferroptosis. Collectively, these findings provide a first mechanistic link between ER stress signaling and ferroptosis in RA and offer insight into how RA-FLS maintain their survival.

After 4µ8c treatment, there was no statistically significant change in serum TNF-α. This reflects that the IRE1/p-JNK/NRF2/GPX4 axis may more selectively regulate the production of certain inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-1β) or MMP9, while exerting a smaller effect on TNF-α, or that the major sources of TNF-α (e.g., macrophages) are less influenced by this pathway.

The limitations of this study include the following. Our experiments were performed mainly in vitro or in rats, and further validation in clinical trials is needed. 4µ8c treatment was short (7 days) with no dose–response or time-course. The complexity of ER stress and ferroptosis signaling warrants caution; prolonged ER stress may activate alternative death pathways such as apoptosis or autophagy [4, 5]. Our results indicate that, in RA-FLS and within this specific pathological context, the IRE1/p-JNK axis exerts a net effect of promoting NRF2 stabilization and nuclear translocation. Although some studies have reported that JNK can phosphorylate NRF2 and facilitate its degradation [20], the direction of this regulation appears to be cell type- and context-dependent. In RA-FLS, IRE1/p-JNK may cooperate with other factors, such as Pin1 and the dynein transport machinery [19], or attenuate degradative pathways, thereby ultimately enhancing NRF2 activity. In addition, FLS were studied at passages 3–8 to balance cell homogeneity and experimental feasibility; however, passage-dependent phenotypic alterations during in vitro culture cannot be completely excluded.

Future studies should investigate how other ER branches such as PERK and ATF6 influence ferroptosis in RA and whether modulation of NRF2/GPX4 affects immune cells beyond FLS. Despite these questions, our findings highlight a previously unappreciated link between ER stress and ferroptosis and offer a rationale for targeting the IRE1/p‑JNK/NRF2/GPX4 pathway in RA.

Conclusion

Sustained IRE1/p-JNK/NRF2 signaling upregulates GPX4 and promotes ferroptosis resistance in RA-FLS. Inhibiting IRE1 reduces GPX4 and restores ferroptotic sensitivity, supporting IRE1 as a potential therapeutic target for RA.
