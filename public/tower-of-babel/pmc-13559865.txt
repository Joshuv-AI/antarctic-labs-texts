Synthesis and Antifungal Activities of Perillyl Alcohol Based 1,3,4‐Thiadiazole‐Thioether Compounds
Authors: Yanlin Xiong, Yun Zhang, Yucheng Cui, Meijuan Hu, Meifang Zhuo, Wengui Duan, Qijin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA series of 33 perillyl alcohol based 1,3,4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds were designed and synthesized through active substructure splicing. Their structures were confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS, and their antifungal activities were evaluated in vitro. At a concentration of 50 mg/L, the target compounds exhibited varying inhibitory activities against Gibberella zeae (G. zeae), Botrytis cinerea, Rhizoctonia solani (R. solani), Rhizoctonia cerealis (R. cerealis), and Physalospora piricola (P. piricola). Among these, compound V15 (R = 3‐FC6H4) demonstrated the strongest effects against G. zeae, R. solani, R. cerealis, and P. piricola, with inhibition rates of 77.8%, 67.3%, 83.9%, and 86.1%, respectively. Notably, its activity against G. zeae (77.8%) surpassed that of the positive control chlorothalonil (73.1%), and its activity against P. piricola (86.1%) was comparable to that of chlorothalonil (89.3%). A preliminary three‐dimensional quantitative structure–activity relationship (3D‐QSAR) model for R. cerealis inhibition was established using comparative molecular field analysis (CoMFA), yielding a reliable model (r2 = 0.991, q2 = 0.547).Keywords: 1,3,4‐thiadiazole; antifungal activities; perillyl alcohol; thioether

Introduction

Fungal diseases and mycotoxin contamination represent significant threats to global food security and human health [1, 2]. Chemical fungicides are essential for disease control and ensuring crop yields; however, their prolonged overuse has led to the emergence of pathogen resistance. This has resulted in increased fungicide application, which in turn heightens health and environmental risks [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel fungicides that exhibit high efficacy and low toxicity. Natural bioactive substances, due to their structural diversity and specific biological activities, have emerged as a vital source of lead compounds for the development of new pesticides [11, 12, 13, 14]. The primary research and development strategies involving natural bioactive substances include the direct utilization of these natural active ingredients or enhancing their bioactivity through structural modification. This approach has successfully facilitated the development of fungicides such as ethylicin, hymexazol, and dimethomorph.

Perillyl alcohol (POH), chemically known as 4‐(1‐methylethenyl)‐1‐cyclohexene‐1‐methanol, is a natural monoterpenoid commonly found in the essential oils of various plants, including perilla, lavender, citrus, pomelo, caraway, cherry, cranberry, and peppermint [15]. Recognized for its high efficiency, broad‐spectrum efficacy, and low‐toxicity as a botanical pesticide, POH has garnered increasing attention from researchers globally [16]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that POH exhibits significant antimicrobial activity [17, 18, 19, 20, 21], while its derivatives have also exhibited potent and broad‐spectrum antimicrobial properties [17, 22, 23, 24, 25].

1,3,4‐Thiadiazole derivatives, a class of structurally distinct five‐membered heterocyclic compounds, exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antifungal [26, 27], herbicidal [28, 29], insecticidal [30, 31], and antiviral [32, 33] effects. Due to their versatile bioactivity, these compounds play a significant role in the molecular design of agrochemicals. To date, several commercial pesticides based on the 1,3,4‐thiadiazole scaffold have been successfully developed, including the herbicide flufenacet, the bactericide bismerthiazol, and the insecticide methidathion.

In recent years, thioether derivatives have garnered significant attention in crop protection due to their impressive and broad‐spectrum bioactivity profiles, which encompass antifungal, antibacterial, insecticidal, acaricidal, nematocidal, antiviral, herbicidal, and plant growth‐regulatory effects [34]. Initially recognized as a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry, this structural motif has evolved into a promising chemotype in agrochemical discovery, providing a versatile platform for the development of novel crop‐protective agents.

Most current structural modifications of perillyl alcohol focus on the derivatization of its hydroxyl group. However, our group's findings indicate that the hydroxyl moiety plays a crucial role in its antifungal activity [25]. Building on our previous research, which demonstrated that incorporating the 1,3,4‐thiadiazole scaffold into natural product frameworks can yield novel derivatives with potent antifungal properties [27, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40], and motivated by our sustained efforts in designing and synthesizing natural product‐based 1,3,4‐thiadiazole compounds from botanical precursors, we extended this strategy to develop a new series of perillyl alcohol‐derived 1,3,4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds. In this series, the 1,3,4‐thiadiazole pharmacophore was incorporated into the perillyl alcohol framework through a thioether linkage. Their structures were confirmed using 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS. Subsequently, we preliminarily evaluated their in vitro antifungal activities, employing three‐dimensional quantitative structure–activity relationship (3D‐QSAR) modeling to elucidate the structural basis underlying their antifungal efficacy.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

The synthesis of the target compounds is delineated in Scheme 1. Perillyl alcohol was used as the starting material. Initially, its hydroxyl group was protected via acetylation using acetic anhydride in pyridine at room temperature, affording perillyl acetate (II). Subsequently, compound II was treated with sodium hypochlorite to give the chlorinated intermediate 10‑chloroperillyl acetate (III). Finally, a one‑pot reaction was conducted between intermediate III and a series of substituted 1,3,4‑thiadiazole‑2‑thiols (IV) in the presence of sodium hydroxide. This reaction simultaneously removed the acetyl protecting group and introduced the 1,3,4‑thiadiazole moiety through thioether formation at the allylic position, yielding the target compounds V1–V33.

Antifungal Activity Test

As summarized in Table 1, the tested compounds exhibited varying inhibitory activities against five fungal pathogens: Gibberella zeae(G. zeae), Botrytis cinerea(B. cinerea), Rhizoctonia solani(R. solani), Rhizoctonia cerealis(R. cerealis), and Physalospora piricola(P. piricola). Among these, compound V15 demonstrated the highest activity against G. zeae, achieving an inhibition rate of 77.8%, which surpassed that of the positive control chlorothalonil (73.1%). For B. cinerea, compound V2 exhibited the highest inhibition rate of 63.9%. In the case of R. solani, both V15 and V17 again showed the highest activity with a 67.3% inhibition rate. Regarding R. cerealis, compounds V12 and V15 reached inhibition rates of 83.3% and 83.9%, respectively. Against P. piricola, compounds V7 and V15 displayed high inhibition rates of 83.3% and 86.1%, respectively. Notably, the activity of V15 against P. piricola (86.1%) was comparable to that of chlorothalonil (89.3%). Overall, compound V15 consistently exhibited the strongest inhibitory effects among the tested compounds against four of the five fungal strains (G. zeae, R. solani, R. cerealis, and P. piricola), underscoring its broad‐spectrum antifungal activity.

The results indicated that compound V15 exhibited the strongest activity against G. zeae among the tested compounds, with an EC50 value of 10.64 mg/L. Compounds V12 and V15 also demonstrated considerable activity against R. cerealis, with EC50 values of 32.12 and 26.12 mg/L, respectively. 3D‐QSAR Analysis

Based on the methodologies reported in references [41, 42, 43], a 3D‐QSAR analysis was conducted utilizing the Comparative Molecular Field Analysis (CoMFA) approach, as implemented in the SYBYL‐X 2.1.1 software. The dataset comprises 26 target compounds that feature a benzene ring, which were employed to investigate the effects of various substituents on the benzene ring concerning antifungal activity against R. cerealis. Of these, 22 compounds were designated as the training set for constructing the CoMFA model, while the remaining four compounds were reserved as the independent test set to evaluate the model's performance. The selection of the test set was based on comprehensive considerations. First, compounds V2 and V3 were selected as the two compounds with the best overall antifungal activity besides V15; compound V27 exhibited an upper‐intermediate level of activity, while V28 displayed a lower level. This selection resulted in a gradient distribution of biological activities that adequately represented the activity range. Second, we included target compounds with no substitution, ortho‐substitution, and para‐substitution on the benzene ring. For the ortho position, we incorporated both a strong electron‐withdrawing group (—NO2) and a strong electron‐donating group (—OC2H5) to serve as mutual controls. This approach ensured diversity in substitution positions and types, while also facilitating a direct comparison of the effects of different substituent groups at the same position on biological activity. The statistical parameters of the established 3D‐QSAR model for perillyl alcohol based 1, 3, 4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds are summarized in Table 3. The model exhibited a cross‐validated correlation coefficient (q2) of 0.547 (greater than 0.500), a non‐cross‐validated correlation coefficient (r2) of 0.991, a standard error of estimate (S) of 0.044, and a Fisher's F‐value of 132.107. These results confirm that the model possesses statistically robust predictive ability. As shown in Table 4 and illustrated in Figure 2, the experimental versus predicted inhibitory activities (expressed as ED values) of the tested compounds demonstrate good agreement. In the scatter plot (Figure 2), all data points are distributed closely along the X = Y line, further indicating a strong correlation between the predicted and observed activities, thereby supporting the reliability of the generated 3D‐QSAR model.

The CoMFA model indicated that steric and electrostatic field contributions accounted for 47.6% and 52.4% of the total influence, respectively, suggesting that the electrostatic field plays a more dominant role in the antifungal activity of perillyl alcohol based 1,3,4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds against R. cerealis. The three‐dimensional contour maps of the steric and electrostatic fields are presented in Figure 3. In the steric field contour map (Figure 3A), a yellow region near the meta‐position of the benzene ring indicates that the introduction of small‐volume substituents at this location is favorable for enhancing activity. Additionally, green contours around the ortho‐ and para‐positions suggest that the introduction of bulky groups in these regions is beneficial for antifungal activity. For instance, among meta‐substituted compounds, compound V13 (R = 3‐CH3C6H4, inhibition rate: 71.1%) exhibited higher activity than V14 (R = 3‐CF3C6H4, 68.4%); similarly, V15 (R = 3‐FC6H4, 83.9%) demonstrated greater activity than V16 (R = 3‐ClC6H4, 71.1%). These observations align with the model's indication that small‐volume substituents at the meta‐position of the benzene ring are favored for improving antifungal activity. Moreover, among ortho‐substituted analogues, the opposite trend is observed: V22 (R = 2‐CF3C6H4, 66.7%) exhibited greater activity than V20 (R = 2‐CH3C6H4, 65.8%), and V25 (R = 2‐BrC6H4, 65.8%) also outperformed V24 (R = 2‐ClC6H4, 44.7%), consistent with the model's prediction that bulky groups at the ortho‐position of the benzene ring enhance antifungal activity.

The steric (A) and electrostatic fields (B) of perillyl alcohol based 1,3,4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds.

In the electrostatic field contour map (Figure 3B), the blue and red contours indicate that the introduction of electron‐withdrawing groups at the meta‐position and electron‐donating groups at the ortho‐ and para‐positions of the benzene ring, respectively, enhances the antifungal activity of the tested compounds. For instance, when strong electron‐withdrawing substituents such as F, Br, and CF3 were introduced into the benzene ring, the antifungal activity of perillyl alcohol derivatives against R. cerealis demonstrated a consistent positional effect: meta > ortho > para. Specifically, among the fluoro‐substituted analogues, V15 (R = 3‐FC6H4, inhibition rate: 83.9%) exhibited significantly higher activity than the ortho‐substituted V23 (R = 2‐FC6H4, 63.2%) and the para‐substituted V6 (R = 4‐FC6H4, 48.4%). Similarly, in the bromo‐substituted series, V17 (R = 3‐BrC6H4, 74.2%) demonstrated greater activity than V25 (R = 2‐BrC6H4, 65.8%) and V8 (R = 4‐BrC6H4, 39.5%). The same trend was observed for trifluoromethyl‐substituted compounds: V14 (R = 3‐CF3C6H4, 68.4%) > V22 (R = 2‐CF3C6H4, 66.7%) > V7 (R = 4‐CF3C6H4, 12.5%).

Conclusions

In this study, we designed and synthesized 33 novel 1,3,4‐thiadiazole‐thioether compounds based on the perillyl alcohol scaffold using an active substructure splicing strategy. The structures of all target compounds were confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS analyses. Antifungal activity assays demonstrated that the incorporation of a 1,3,4‑thiadiazole moiety into the perillyl alcohol framework via a thioether linkage effectively enhanced the inhibitory activity. At a concentration of 50 mg/L, all target compounds exhibited discernible antifungal effects against G. zeae, B. cinerea, R. solani, R. cerealis, and P. piricola. Among these, compound V15 showed the strongest inhibition against G. zeae, R. solani, R. cerealis, and P. piricola, indicating promising broad‑spectrum antifungal potential. Furthermore, a statistically robust 3D‑QSAR model was developed using the CoMFA approach, which successfully elucidated the correlation between the structural features of the tested compounds and their inhibitory activities against R. cerealis. Collectively, this work provides valuable insights into the rational design and structural optimization of perillyl alcohol based heterocyclic thioether derivatives and serves as a useful reference for the development of novel natural‑product‑inspired agrochemical agents.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Instruments

Instrumentation

1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using an AVANCE NEO 400 MHz spectrometer and an AVANCE III HD 600 MHz spectrometer, both from Bruker, (Switzerland). High‑resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were obtained with a Xevo G2 Q‑TOF liquid chromatography‑mass spectrometer from Waters (USA). Melting points were determined using an X‑5 micro‑melting‑point apparatus from Gongyi Yuhua Instrument Co., Ltd. (China) and are reported as uncorrected.

Reagents and Chemicals

(S)‐(−)‐Perillyl alcohol (purity 90%) was obtained from Shanghai Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium hypochlorite aqueous solution, containing 11%–15% available chlorine was supplied by Alfa Aesar (China) Chemical Co., Ltd. All other reagents and solvents were of analytical grade and were used as received.

Chemistry

Synthesis of Perillyl Acetate (II)

Perillyl alcohol (10.0125 g, 65.82 mmol) was added to a 100 mL round‐bottom flask, followed by acetic anhydride (20 mL) and pyridine (20 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 5 h and monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC). Upon completion, the mixture was transferred to a 250 mL separatory funnel and sequentially washed with ethyl acetate (50 mL), saturated sodium bicarbonate solution (40 mL), and saturated sodium chloride solution (40 mL). The organic layer was collected, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to afford the crude product. Purification by silica gel column chromatography (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate, 30:1, v/v) yielded compound II as a colorless oil (12.0610 g, 94.5%).

Synthesis of 10‐Chloro‐Perillyl Acetate (III)

Compound II (3.0067 g, 15.49 mmol) was placed in a 100 mL round‐bottom flask, followed by dichloromethane (21 mL) and acetic acid (1.3 mL). The mixture was cooled in an ice‑water bath, and an aqueous sodium hypochlorite solution (15 mL, containing 11%–15% available chlorine) was slowly added via a constant‑pressure dropping funnel. The reaction was monitored by TLC analysis and allowed to proceed for 1 h. After completion, the mixture was transferred to a 250 mL separatory funnel and treated with saturated sodium sulfite solution (30 mL). The organic layer was separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with dichloromethane (2 × 20 mL). The combined organic extracts were sequentially washed with distilled water (40 mL) and saturated sodium chloride solution (40 mL), dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Purification by silica gel column chromatography (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate, 80:1, v/v) afforded compound III as a colorless oil (2.5546 g, 72.3%).

Synthesis of 1,3,4‐Thiadiazole Thiol Compounds (IV)

Acetyl hydrazine (1.5003 g, 20.25 mmol) and potassium hydroxide (0.6136 g) were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (31.5 mL) in a 100 mL round‐bottom flask. After complete dissolution, carbon disulfide (0.75 mL) was added dropwise under ice‑bath cooling, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 3 h. Upon completion, the mixture was subjected to suction filtration, and the collected solid was dried. The resulting white solid was then slowly added to a separate round‐bottom flask containing concentrated sulfuric acid (12 mL) maintained at 0°C–5°C. After stirring for 0.5 h, the reaction mixture was poured onto crushed ice, leading to precipitation. The resulting solid was collected by suction filtration. The filter cake was dissolved in a 10 wt% aqueous sodium hydroxide solution, followed by another suction filtration. The filtrate was acidified with 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid until white precipitate appeared. The precipitate was isolated by suction filtration, washed with water, and dried to afford 5‑methyl‑1,3,4‑thiadiazole‑2‑thiol (IV1) as a white solid. Derivatives IV2–IV33 were prepared following the same procedure.

Synthesis of Perillyl‐based 1,3,4‐Thiadiazole Thioether Compounds (V)

The synthesis of compound V1 is described as a representative example. 5‑Methyl‑1,3,4‑thiadiazole‑2‑thiol (0.2301 g, 1.74 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (15 mL) in a 50 mL round‑bottom flask. To this solution, compound III (0.2731 g, 1.20 mmol) was added, and the pH was adjusted to 9–10 by dropwise addition of a 10% aqueous sodium hydroxide solution. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 6 h and monitored by TLC. After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting oily residue was dissolved in ethyl acetate (60 mL) and transferred to a 250 mL separatory funnel. The organic layer was washed sequentially with water (2 × 41 mL) and saturated sodium chloride solution (41 mL), dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated. Purification by column chromatography (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 8:1 to 3:1, v/v) afforded the target compound V1. Compounds V2 to V33 were synthesized following the same method.

Antifungal Activity Test

The in vitro antifungal activity of the tested compounds against five plant pathogens (G. zeae, B. cinerea, R. solani, R. cerealis, and P. piricola) was preliminarily evaluated at a concentration of 50 mg/L using the agar dilution method. Chlorothalonil, a broad‐spectrum fungicide widely regarded as a reference standard in in vitro antifungal assays, served as the positive control under the same experimental conditions. Each compound was dissolved in acetone with sorpol‑144 as the emulsifier and diluted to prepare a 500 mg/L stock solution. A 1 mL aliquot of this stock solution was mixed with 9 mL of PSA medium in a petri dish to yield plates containing 50 mg/L of the test compound. Mycelial discs (4 mm in diameter) were subsequently placed on the compound‑containing plates, with parallel blank controls prepared under identical conditions. After a 48 h incubation period, the diameter of mycelial growth was measured and compared with the blank control.
