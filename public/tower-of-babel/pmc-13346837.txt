Optimizing Zirconium-89 labeling for chimeric antigen receptor T cell PET imaging by comparing desferrioxamine and oxine
Authors: Nare Ko, Seog-Young Kim, Sang Ju Lee, Hyunsu Soh, Hyori Kim, Ji-Min Ju, Sang-Jin Lee, Junho Chung, Seung Jun Oh, Jin-Sook Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to optimize Zirconium-89 (89Zr) labeling strategies for in vivo tracking of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell migration using positron emission tomography (PET). The performances of the [89Zr]Zr-p-isothiocyanatobenzyl-desferrioxamine (DFO) and [89Zr]Zr-oxine methods were compared to identify the most suitable chelator and activity concentration for cell labeling. CD19 CAR-Jurkat T cells were labeled with each [89Zr]Zr complex at different activity concentrations following optimization of the chelator amount and incubation time, and were then evaluated for cell viability, proliferation, and function over seven days. The refined synthesis procedures achieved radiochemical purities greater than 95%. Labeling efficiencies were 84.2 ± 5.4% for [89Zr]Zr-DFO and 43.7 ± 1.2% for [89Zr]Zr-oxine. [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells demonstrated high viability (94.2 ± 1.1%) and maintained 82% of baseline proliferation capacity over seven days, whereas [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells showed reduced viability (77.3 ± 7.8%) and a markedly impaired proliferation capacity, retaining only 7% of baseline. These results indicate that [89Zr]Zr-DFO provided superior labeling efficiency and cell preservation compared with [89Zr]Zr-oxine, supporting its potential use for PET-based tracking of CAR-T cells in cell therapy applications.Keywords: Zirconium-89 labeling, Direct cell labeling, CAR-T cell, Cell therapy, Cell tracking, Positron emission tomographySubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional cancer treatments1–3. CARs are modular fusion proteins composed of a tumor antigen-binding domain, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain4,5. Engineered CAR-T cells recognize target antigens via their binding domains and eliminate cancer cells through T cell activation6. Notably, CD19-specific CAR-T cells have received Food and Drug Administration approval and are commercially available for the treatment of B cell malignancies7–9. Encouraging results from clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy for refractory pre-B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and diffuse large B cell lymphoma10,11.

Effective in vivo tracking and monitoring are critical for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy. Various radiolabeling strategies have been extensively explored for this purpose, particularly using positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography imaging modalities. Gawne et al.12 and Kircher et al.13 highlighted the advantages of radionuclide-based imaging, including its high sensitivity and quantitative capability. Among these approaches, direct cell labeling with radioisotopes has garnered significant attention. Zirconium-89 (89Zr), a positron-emitting radioisotope with a relatively long decay half-life (78.4 h), is well suited for longitudinal in vivo cell tracking. Several reports have demonstrated the use of CAR-T cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr chelator complexes, such as [89Zr]Zr-p-isothiocyanatobenzyl-desferrioxamine (DFO) and [89Zr]Zr-oxine, for PET-based tracking14–16. The underlying mechanisms differ; [89Zr]Zr-oxine enters cells via passive diffusion, whereas [89Zr]Zr-DFO forms covalent bonds with cell surface proteins (Fig. 1a).

(a) [89Zr]Zr-DFO (p-isothiocyanatobenzyl-desferrioxamine) forms covalent bonds with cell membrane proteins, whereas [89Zr]Zr-oxine diffuses into cells for intracellular labeling. HBSS (Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution) and DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) were used for [89Zr]Zr-DFO preparation. (b) Preparation of [89Zr]ZrCl4 from [89Zr]Zr-oxalate through four steps: (1) trapping on the QMA cartridge, (2) anion exchange using 1 N HCl, (3) drying under a nitrogen stream, and (4) reformulation in HBSS buffer or 0.04 N HCl for the subsequent [89Zr]Zr-DFO or [89Zr]Zr-oxine synthesis. RT, room temperature.

Despite these advancements, radiolabeling processes can potentially affect cell functionality due to radiation exposure and chemical toxicity. Previous studies have reported varying cell responses to different labeling activities. Natural killer cells17and mesenchymal stem cells18 maintain functional integrity during specific activities, whereas white blood cells19 exhibit DNA damage at comparatively low exposures. A comparative analysis of [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine revealed that cell types such as human decidual stromal cells, rat macrophages, and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) retained high viability and proliferation capacity at moderate specific activities of 1.0–4.3 MBq/106 cells20. However, hPBMCs labeled with [89Zr]Zr-oxine showed reduced viability compared with those labeled with [89Zr]Zr-DFO. Moreover, residual DFO and oxine after complex formation may contribute to additional toxicity, highlighting the need to achieve high radiochemical yield (> 90%) to minimize unbound chemical contaminants because the purification of unreacted ions and chelating agents is challenging.

This study aimed to optimize the synthesis and cell-labeling conditions of [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes to enhance CAR-T cell labeling. We focused on evaluating the cell viability and functionality after labeling under different activity conditions to determine the most effective conditions for potential clinical applications.

Results

Optimization of [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes

The optimal amount of DFO was determined by evaluating the radiochemical yield of different amounts of DFO in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). As shown in Fig. 2a, the radiochemical yield of the [89Zr]Zr-DFO complex exceeded 95% within 60 min at DFO amounts of 1.4–7.0 nmol. In contrast, a lower DFO amount (0.7 nmol) resulted in a decreased radiochemical yield of 79.1 ± 36.2% after 2 h.

Time-dependent radiochemical yield at varying chelator amounts and comparison of labeling performance. (a) Radiochemical yield of [89Zr]Zr-DFO at different DFO amounts (0.7–7.0 nmol) over time. (b) Radiochemical yield of [89Zr]Zr-oxine at different oxine amounts (0.02–2.00 µmol) over time. (c) Labeling efficiency of CAR-Jurkat T cells with [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes at varying activities. (d) Cell-associated activity (kBq/106 cells) in CAR-Jurkat T cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-DFO versus [89Zr]Zr-oxine. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Similarly, the radiochemical yield of [89Zr]Zr-oxine reached 100% at oxine amounts of 1.0 or 2.0 μmol after 30 min of incubation (Fig. 2b). However, reducing the oxine amount to 0.02 μmol led to a marked decline in radiochemical yield to 31%.

Comparison of the labeling efficiency of CAR-Jurkat T cells with [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine

The labeling efficiencies of CAR-Jurkat T cells using the two [89Zr]Zr labeling methods were compared. Cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-DFO demonstrated high efficiencies, with 76.9–84.2% of total added radioactivity becoming cell-associated across the tested activity concentration range (111–333 kBq/100 μL; Fig. 2c). In contrast, [89Zr]Zr-oxine labeling yielded significantly lower efficiencies of 43.7% at 111 kBq/100 μL and decreasing to 24.8% at 333 kBq/100 μL (p < 0.01).

Consistently, the cell-associated activity per 106 cells was substantially greater with [89Zr]Zr-DFO (17.1–56.1 kBq/106 cells) compared to [89Zr]Zr-oxine (9.7–19.4 kBq/106 cells; Fig. 2d). Thus, [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeling resulted in 2- to threefold higher cell-associated activity per 106 cells under identical incubation conditions. This superior labeling efficiency may support improved detectability in PET-based cell tracking and is associated with better preservation of cellular integrity using the DFO method.

Evaluation of IL-2 secretory function of [89Zr]Zr-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells

The functional competence of the radiolabeled CAR-Jurkat T cells was assessed by measuring antigen-specific interleukin (IL)−2 secretion upon stimulation of CD19-expressing target cells (Fig. 3). [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells maintained robust IL-2 secretion levels (72.9 ng/mL at 12 h and 86.1 ng/mL at 36 h after 333 kBq labeling), comparable to unlabeled controls (87.1 ng/mL at 36 h; p > 0.84) with no statistically significant difference (Fig. 3a). The [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells showed preserved IL-2 secretion (78.7 ng/mL at 36 h; Fig. 3b). Importantly, IL-2 secretion was antigen-specific, as negligible cytokine release was observed following stimulation with CD19-negative cells. These results confirmed that neither surface labeling with DFO nor intracellular labeling with oxine impaired antigen recognition or early cytokine response within 36 h post-labeling.

IL-2 secretion assay following labeling of CAR-Jurkat T cells with [89Zr]Zr-DFO or [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes. (a) IL-2 secretion by CAR-Jurkat T cells after 12 and 36 h of co-culture with CD19-positive (Raji) and CD19-negative (K562) cells following [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeling. (b) IL-2 secretion after [89Zr]Zr-oxine labeling under similar conditions. Minimal IL-2 secretion observed in CD19-negative K562 cells reflects the antigen specificity of the CAR-Jurkat T cells, resulting in negligible or absent bars in the graph. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Long-term viability of [89Zr]Zr complex-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells

The long-term viability of the radiolabeled CAR-Jurkat T cells was monitored for seven days (Fig. 4). [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells retained high viability (> 80%) at 24 h across all tested activities, with greater reductions observed under higher activity conditions by Day 3. At the highest activities (56.1 kBq/106 cells), viability decreased to 63.1% but subsequently stabilized. In contrast, [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells exhibited a pronounced decline in viability, particularly at higher activities (≥ 16.5 kBq/10⁶ cells), with viability falling below 5% by Day 7. Comparative analysis revealed markedly higher viability of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells than that of [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells under comparable radioactivity levels. These findings highlight the cytotoxic differences between the surface and intracellular labeling strategies.

Effect of [89Zr]Zr labeling on CAR-Jurkat T cell viability over time. (a) [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells showed a gradual decline in viability, which became more pronounced under higher activity conditions after Day 3. (b) [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells exhibited a more rapid reduction in viability, particularly under higher activity conditions by Day 7. Viability trends were modeled using a one-phase exponential decay.

Long-term proliferative capacity of [89Zr]Zr complex-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells

The impact of radiolabeling on proliferative capacity was assessed over seven days (Fig. 5). [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeled cells retained substantial proliferation at lower labeling activities (81.8% of controls at 17.1 kBq/10⁶ cells on Day 7), whereas higher doses significantly impaired proliferation (~ 8% at 56.1 kBq/10⁶ cells by Day 7). Conversely, [89Zr]Zr-oxine labeling severely impaired cell proliferation, even at moderate radioactivity. Cells labeled at 9.7 kBq/106 cells declined to 23.0% of control proliferation by Day 3 and only 6.8% by Day 7. Nonlinear regression analysis using a one-phase exponential decay model showed a good fit (R2 > 0.95) for both labeling strategies. With increasing radioactivity, [89Zr]Zr-DFO–labeled cells exhibited progressively lower plateau levels, indicating reduced residual proliferative capacity at later time points. A similar pattern was observed in [89Zr]Zr-oxine–labeled cells; however, fitting was not feasible at higher activity levels due to the rapid loss of viable cells.

Impact of [89Zr]Zr labeling on CAR-Jurkat T cell proliferation. (a) CAR-Jurkat T cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-DFO at various activities (0, 17.1, 30.8, 42.6, and 56.1 kBq/106 cells) retained partial proliferative capacity, particularly under lower activity conditions. (b) CAR-Jurkat T cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-oxine (0, 9.7, 13.0, 16.5, and 19.4 kBq/106 cells) showed rapid loss of proliferative capacity, with near-complete loss under higher activity conditions by Day 7. Proliferation decline was modeled using a one-phase exponential decay curve.

Overall, [89Zr]Zr-DFO–labeled cells approached a stable proliferative plateau, whereas [89Zr]Zr-oxine–labeled cells showed a more pronounced and sustained decline without clear stabilization.

Morphological changes in [89Zr]Zr complex-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells

By Day 7, distinct morphological differences were observed between the labeling methods (Fig. 6). The [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells exhibited pronounced apoptotic features, including cell shrinkage, nuclear condensation, fragmentation, and membrane blebbing, particularly at higher radioactivity doses. In contrast, most [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells retained a blast-like morphology similar to that of the unlabeled controls, with less pronounced morphological abnormalities than the [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells, even under higher activity conditions. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that surface conjugation using DFO is structurally and functionally less damaging than intracellular oxine labeling.

Morphological assessment of CAR-Jurkat T cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr complexes. Microscopic images show CAR-Jurkat T cells labeled with (a) [89Zr]Zr-DFO and (b) [89Zr]Zr-oxine under different activity conditions. [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled cells exhibited morphological features of apoptosis, including nuclear condensation and membrane blebbing, while [89Zr]Zr-DFO-labeled cells largely retained normal morphology.

In summary, although both methods achieved efficient [89Zr]Zr labeling, surface labeling with DFO provided superior preservation of cell viability, proliferation, and morphology compared to oxine, offering critical guidance for PET-based CAR-T cell tracking applications.

Discussion

In this study, we directly compared Zirconium-89-based radiolabeling strategies for CAR-T cell tracking—[89Zr]Zr-DFO (surface labeling) and [89Zr]Zr-oxine (intracellular labeling) —with respect to labeling efficiency, viability, proliferation, and functional preservation. Although both methods showed high incorporation efficiencies (> 95%), [89Zr]Zr-DFO consistently demonstrated superior cellular labeling efficiency, viability, proliferation capacity, and functional preservation, emphasizing the critical role of labeling mechanism in determining radiobiological outcomes.

[89Zr]Zr is particularly well suited for long-term cell tracking owing to its long decay half-life (78.4 h). Consistent with previous studies by Bansal et al.16 and Sato et al.21, our results suggest that [89Zr]Zr-DFO may be a suitable strategy for PET-based tracking of CAR-Jurkat T cells while maintaining key cellular properties. In contrast, although [89Zr]Zr-oxine is easier to synthesize, its intracellular localization appears to induce greater cytotoxicity, as reflected by the reduced viability and impaired proliferation of cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-oxine. These results highlight the importance of minimizing intracellular radiotoxicity during therapeutic cell tracking.

Our findings should also be interpreted in the context of the study by Weist et al.15, which demonstrated the feasibility of PET tracking of adoptively transferred CAR-T cells using [89Zr]Zr-oxine. Although that study established the utility of [89Zr]Zr-oxine for in vivo imaging, our in vitro comparison suggests that [89Zr]Zr-DFO may offer advantages in terms of labeling efficiency and preservation of cell viability and proliferative capacity. Together, these findings indicate that the choice of radiolabeling strategy may need to be tailored according to the balance between imaging feasibility and cellular preservation.

In the past, [89Zr]ZrCl4 was prepared by purifying the [89Zr]Zr ion after the production or isolation from [89Zr]Zr-oxine, using methods such as Sep-Pak (QMA or C18) or Hydroxamate resin columns16,21,22. Recent studies have suggested that synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-complexes using [89Zr]Zr-oxalate may offer a more accessible method15,20. However, our data favor the use of the [89Zr]ZrCl4 method over the neutralized [89Zr]Zr-oxalate method for two reasons: 1) it achieves clinically relevant levels of initial radioactivity while minimizing the reaction volume; and 2) it uses a buffer more conducive to cellular conditions, avoiding specific buffer types and concentrations required to neutralize [89Zr]Zr-oxalate.

In our study, both [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine achieved chelation efficiencies greater than 95% using the refined [89Zr]ZrCl4 method. This high efficiency is critical for minimizing the chemical toxicity associated with unreacted chelators, a problem addressed by Holland et al.22 However, [89Zr]Zr-DFO enabled more efficient cell labeling and better preservation of cell viability and proliferative capacity, suggesting its potential suitability for future PET-based tracking of therapeutic cells.

The viability and functionality of cells after radiolabeling are crucial parameters for the clinical application of radiolabeled cells. Our results demonstrated that the viability and proliferative capacity of cells after radiolabeling with [89Zr]Zr-DFO exhibited a decline consistent with a one-phase exponential decay model. This indicates that the reduction in cell viability and proliferative capacity over time is proportional to the initial effect of radioactivity, and that the process stabilizes after an initial decrease. This plateau suggests that after the initial cellular stress caused by radioactivity, the remaining cell population reaches a new equilibrium, in which viability and proliferation are maintained at a lower but stable level. This stabilization is crucial for the long-term functionality of radiolabeled CAR-T cells, allowing the surviving cells to function and proliferate, albeit at a reduced level. In contrast, cells labeled with [89Zr]Zr-oxine showed a continuous sustained decline in viability even at lower levels of radioactivity (16.5 and 19.4 kBq/106 cells), with only 9.7 kBq/106 cells remaining viable until Day 3. Similar trends were observed in previous reports on other cell types, where radiolabeling with [89Zr]Zr-DFO was found to be less toxic than that with [89Zr]Zr-oxine23,24. This difference may be related to the distinct mechanisms of cell labeling: [89Zr]Zr-oxine undergoes intracellular diffusion, whereas [89Zr]Zr-DFO covalently binds to membrane proteins on the cell surface.

Nonlinear regression analysis of the one-phase exponential decay model revealed a trend toward lower decay constants (k) under higher activity conditions for [89Zr]Zr-DFO- and [89Zr]Zr-oxine-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells. The results revealed distinct trends in k values between the two labeling methods, correlating with the differences in cell proliferation capacity. These findings underscore the critical impact of labeling strategy on cell viability and proliferation. Although both labeling methods influenced cellular kinetics, the different cellular localization patterns of [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine may have contributed to the observed differences in cell viability and proliferation. However, the specific mechanisms underlying these differences were not directly examined in this study.

Notably, the optimized labeling conditions for Jurkat-based CAR-T cells may not be directly applicable to primary CAR-T cells or other immune cell types that may exhibit different responses to radiolabeling depending on their metabolic activity, membrane composition, or sensitivity to radiation. Furthermore, while we focused on optimizing labeling efficiency and cell viability, other factors such as the effects of radiolabeling on the immune response and long-term cell function in vivo have not been investigated in detail. These aspects require further investigation to fully understand the broader implications of [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeling in clinical cell therapy.

Another major challenge in the clinical application of [89Zr]Zr-labeled CAR-T cells is the integration of the radiolabeling process into a good manufacturing practice (GMP) workflow. The production of patient-specific CAR-T cells requires stringent GMP standards and incorporating radioisotopes such as Zirconium-89 (89Zr) into this process adds further complexity. Ensuring that the entire process, from the production of CAR-T cells to labeling with [89Zr]Zr, is performed under GMP conditions is essential for ensuring safety, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance. Standardized, automated, and closed-system labeling modules could facilitate [89Zr]Zr labeling.

Future studies should also evaluate alternative radiometals or labeling approaches with more favorable dosimetry and assess the long-term impact of radiolabeling on therapeutic efficacy, immune phenotype, and in vivo persistence. These investigations are critical for clinical translation of PET-guided cell therapy monitoring. In addition, the development of GMP-compliant radiolabeling procedures is necessary for the clinical application of PET-guided cell therapy monitoring.

In conclusion, the [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeling strategy outperformed the [89Zr]Zr-oxine strategy in terms of labeling efficiency and preservation of cell viability and function. Therefore, the optimized [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeling method may be a more suitable approach for PET-based tracking of CAR-T cells in cell therapy applications. This study contributes to the ongoing efforts to improve the clinical applicability of cell-based therapies using advanced imaging techniques.

Methods

Preparation of [89Zr]ZrCl4 from [89Zr]Zr-oxalate

[89Zr]Zr-chelator complexes were synthesized using [89Zr]ZrCl4, which was prepared from [89Zr]Zr-oxalate via anion exchange (Fig. 1b). Carrier-free [89Zr]Zr (specific activity > 150 MBq/nmol; BV Cyclotron, PerkinElmer, VU Amsterdam, NL) was retained as [89Zr]Zr-oxalate in 1.0 M oxalic acid within a QMA cartridge (Merck, Germany). The [89Zr]Zr4+ ions were then eluted using 1.0 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) to generate an aqueous solution.

The eluate was evaporated under a mild nitrogen stream at 120 ºC for 10 min. Approximately 96.2 ± 1.79% of the initial radioactivity (80.3 ± 37.0 MBq) was retained on the cartridge, and 89.5 ± 3.78% of the activity was eluted with 1 mL of 1.0 N HCl. After evaporation, the resulting [89Zr]ZrCl4 was reconstituted in either 150 μL Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) buffer (for [89Zr]Zr-DFO synthesis) or 60 μL of 0.04 N HCl (for [89Zr]Zr-oxine synthesis), achieving recovery efficiencies of 80.4 ± 13.7% and 80.8 ± 7.67%, respectively.

Preparation of [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes

[89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes were prepared following optimization of the coordination conditions with various amounts of DFO (0.7–7.0 nmol, Macrocyclics, TX, USA), oxine (0.02–2.0 µmol), and [89Zr]ZrCl4 (3.7–7.4 MBq).

For [89Zr]Zr-DFO, DFO (2.1 µg, 2.8 nmol) was dissolved in 135 µL of HBSS buffer containing < 0.04% v/v DMSO and mixed with 150 µL of [89Zr]ZrCl4 solution (52.9 ± 17.0 MBq). The pH was adjusted to 7.4–7.6 with 1.0 N NaOH (11.6 ± 2.2 µL), and the mixture was incubated overnight at 37 ºC in a thermomixer (Eppendorf, Germany). The chelation efficiency exceeded 95%, as determined by radio-thin layer chromatography using iTLC-SG plates (Agilent Technologies, USA) and 50 mM DTPA as the developing solvent.

For [89Zr]Zr-oxine, oxine (0.3 mg or 2.0 µmol) was mixed with [89Zr]ZrCl4 recovered in 162 µL of 0.04 N HCl (38.7 ± 23.5 MBq), containing Tween 80 (4 µL), 0.5 M Na2CO3 (80 µL), and 1.0 N HCl (40 µL). The solution was vortexed for 10 min at room temperature to complete complexation.

Preparation of CD19-specific CAR-Jurkat cells

CD19-specific CAR-Jurkat T cells were generated using a lentiviral vector encoding the 19BBZ CAR construct, as previously described25,26. Briefly, 293 T cells (ATCC, RRID:CVCL_0063) were transfected with lentiviral vectors using Lipofectamine 3000® (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Jurkat T cells (4 × 105 cells; ATCC, RRID: CVCL_0065) were seeded and infected with the lentiviral supernatant at a multiplicity of infection of 1000 in the presence of polybrene (8 μL/mL; Merck, Germany). After 72 h, the transduced Jurkat T cells expressing the CD19-specific CAR were enriched by fluorescence-activated cell sorting using a FACS Aria instrument (BD Biosciences, USA).

Labeling of CAR-Jurkat T cells with [89Zr]Zr-DFO and [89Zr]Zr-oxine complexes

Labeling of CAR-Jurkat T cells with either [89Zr]Zr-DFO or [89Zr]Zr-oxine was performed according to the optimized protocols. For [89Zr]Zr-DFO labeling16, 5 × 106 cells were washed once with HBSS buffer (pH 7.5) and resuspended in 500 µL HBSS. Different activity concentrations (111, 185, 259, or 333 kBq/100 μL) of [89Zr]Zr-DFO were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 ºC for 30 min with gentle shaking.

For [89Zr]Zr-oxine labeling21, the same number of cells were incubated with [89Zr]Zr-oxine under activity concentrations (111–333 kBq) in 20 μL of HBSS buffer at 4 ºC for 15 min with gentle shaking.

Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with 1 mL of HBSS buffer. The labeling efficiency was calculated by measuring the cell-bound radioactivity relative to the total added activity. Cell viability was assessed immediately after labeling using the trypan blue exclusion assay.

Evaluation of the function, viability, and proliferative capacity of [89Zr]Zr-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells

The functional integrity of [89Zr]Zr-labeled CAR-Jurkat T cells was evaluated by co-culturing them with CD19-positive Raji cells (ATCC, RRID:CVCL_0511) or CD19-negative K562 cells (ATCC, RRID:CVCL_0004) at a 4:1 effector-to-target ratio. After 12 and 36 h of co-culture, supernatants were collected and IL-2 secretion was measured using a commercial ELISA kit (RayBiotech, GA, USA). Unlabeled CAR-Jurkat T cells served as controls.

Cell viability and proliferation were assessed by trypan blue exclusion at 1 h and 1, 3, and 7 d post-labeling. Labeled and unlabeled cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in 6-well plates at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Relative viability and proliferation were normalized to the corresponding unlabeled controls. Morphological changes were assessed by optical microscopy (Revolve, RVL-100-G, Echo Laboratories, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated using linear regression and one-phase exponential decay models. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 11.0.0; GraphPad Software, CA, USA, https://www.graphpad.com/).
