Association of Lipoprotein(a) With Atrial Fibrillation in the Framingham Heart Study
Authors: Celestin Hategeka, Emelia J. Benjamin, Sarah R. Preis
Published: 2025

The prevalence and incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF) are rising in the United States and globally, with lifetime risk of approximately 33%.1 AF contributes significantly to risk of stroke, heart failure, myocardial infraction, dementia, and overall mortality.1 Identifying risk factors for AF is crucial for its prevention and management. Lipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)], a genetically determined risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), is elevated in 20% of the population and has pro-inflammatory, proatherogenic, and pro-thrombotic properties. Lp(a) levels are stable lifelong but can be influenced by thyroid disease, kidney disease, or hormone therapy. What is the clinical question being addressed?

Is Lp(a) associated with incident AF in the Framingham heart study?

What is the main finding?

Elevated Lp(a) levels are associated with a 1.2-fold increased risk of incident AF, which did not attain statistical significance (p = 0.07).

While associations between Lp(a) and ASCVD and calcific aortic valve stenosis are established, its link to AF remains mixed.3 Observational studies often show no association, while recent Mendelian randomization studies suggest higher Lp(a) levels increase AF risk.4 The mechanisms underpinning the relations between Lp(a) and AF have various plausible explanations. Lp(a) is a known causal factor for ASCVD and calcific aortic valve stenosis, both of which create the substrate for AF development. In addition, elevated Lp(a) concentrations may promote AF development by enhancing structural and electrical remodeling of the atria via chronic inflammation, increased oxidative stress, and calcification and stiffness of cardiac tissues.2 Using Framingham Heart Study (FHS), we examined the relationship between Lp(a) and AF, hypothesizing that elevated Lp(a) levels are associated with increased risk of incident AF.

Methods

We examined AF-free FHS Offspring and Omni 1 participants aged ≥45 years who attended an exam visit with Lp(a) measurements (1998-2001). Participants were followed from the date of their exam visit until they either received AF diagnosis, died, were lost to follow-up, or reached December 31, 2021, whichever occurred first. Study participants were diagnosed with AF, if an electrocardiogram (ECG) at FHS examination demonstrated AF or atrial flutter, or AF was documented in participants’ outside medical records, interim hospitalizations, outside ECGs, and ambulatory ECG monitoring results. Plasma Lp(a) levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay with monoclonal antibodies against the apolipoprotein (a) moiety and independent of the apolipoprotein (a) isoform size. Lp(a) was analyzed as elevated (>50 mg/dL) vs normal (≤50 mg/dL). All study protocols used were approved by the Boston University Medical Campus Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models, which account for the competing risk of mortality, were used to calculate subdistribution HRs (sHRs) and 95% CIs for the association between elevated Lp(a) and incident AF. We constructed 3 multivariable-adjusted models. We adjusted for age and sex. Our primary model additionally adjusted for known risk factors associated with AF established in the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology (CHARGE)-AF model (Figure 1).5 In secondary analyses, we adjusted for the CHARGE-AF model plus C-reactive protein, estrogen use, and glomerular filtration rate. All models were stratified by FHS cohort (Offspring, Omni 1). We assessed for effect modification by age group (≥65 vs <65 years old) and sex (female vs male). A 2-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results

We studied 3,378 participants (mean age 61 ± 9 years, 56% female, 4% Hispanic ethnicity, 90% White, 4% Black, 3% Asian, <1% other, and 3% unknown race). During an average of 17.0 ± 6.6 years of follow-up, 692 participants (12.1 cases per 1,000 person-years) were diagnosed with AF (Figure 1). The incidence rate of AF was 14.4 (95% CI: 12.0-17.1) per 1,000 person-years among participants with Lp(a) > 50 mg/dL compared with 11.7 (95% CI: 10.7-12.7) among those with Lp(a) ≤50 mg/dL. In the model adjusted for age and sex, Lp(a) > 50 mg/dL was associated with 24% increase in incident AF (sHR: 1.24 [95% CI: 1.02-1.51], P = 0.03) compared with Lp(a) ≤50 mg/dL. After additional adjustment for the CHARGE-AF risk factors, the magnitude of the sHR was roughly similar, however, it no longer was statistically significant (HR: 1.20 [95% CI: 0.98-1.47], P = 0.07). The magnitude of the sHR was unchanged in the model adjusted for sex, and the CHARGE-AF model plus C-reactive protein, estrogen use, and glomerular filtration rate (sHR: 1.20 [95% CI: 0.99-1.47], P = 0.07). There was no evidence of effect modification by either age group (P value for interaction = 0.41) or sex (P value for interaction = 0.93).

Discussion

Our community-based study of individuals free of AF at baseline revealed that increased Lp(a) levels were associated with a 1.2-fold increased risk of incident AF, which did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.07). Whereas the UK Biobank study used higher threshold for elevated Lp(a) (>70 mg/dL) than the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology recommended level used in our study, the magnitude of association was similar and modest.2

Although our study did not reveal a statistically significant association, and the relationship between Lp(a) and AF is inconsistent across observation studies partly due to differences in study design, population characteristics, and Lp(a) measurement methods and thresholds. The Mendelian randomization data raise the possibility that therapies modifying Lp(a) may be beneficial for AF. As such, we urge longitudinal studies measuring Lp(a), and particularly randomized trials of Lp(a)-lowering therapies to prespecify and study AF as an outcome. If the association between Lp(a) and AF is confirmed in future studies, it could have significant clinical implications. Measuring Lp(a) levels could be integrated into risk stratification models to identify individuals at higher risk of developing AF, potentially leading to closer monitoring or preventive interventions. Moreover, ongoing trials with Lp(a)-lowering drugs could offer new strategies for both preventing and treating AF and cardiovascular comorbidities associated with AF. The positive impact on AF could result from both their beneficial effects on ASCVD and through mechanisms that are specific to Lp(a).2

Our study employed a robust, longitudinal community-based cohort with rigorous protocols to ascertain AF risk factors. The FHS’ observational nature, predominantly European middle-aged to older participants, and limited AF type specificity limit causal inferences and generalizability.

In conclusion, although our study did not confirm a statistically significant association between Lp(a) and incident AF, we provide further data about the potential magnitude of association from null to a very modest 1.2-fold association. Additional research is needed to reconcile conflicting findings and determine the robustness of the association.
