Drivers of public participation in urban regeneration: an integrated choice and latent variable analysis
Authors: Mingxi Nie, Tao Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResident participation is essential to the success of urban regeneration, ensuring plans align with local needs and foster community consensus. However, limited studies have examined residents’ renewal preferences and the role of socio-psychological factors in shaping them. This study investigates how facility features, psychological factors, and individual characteristics influence the choice of renewable energy plans. Using data collected through a face-to-face survey, we estimated an integrated choice and latent-variable model. Results show that plans emphasizing green space, public activity areas, and electric vehicle charging stations are most favored. Among psychological factors, social influence, place attachment, and social trust emerge as key latent drivers. The relatively weaker sense of community among younger, short-term, and less educated residents highlights an opportunity to tailor communication and engagement efforts to increase their support for renewal initiatives. These findings highlight the importance of consensus-building in the renewal process and underscore the need for strategies that strengthen public acceptance, providing policymakers with a practical pathway to design effective urban regeneration programs.Keywords: Urban regeneration, Place attachment, Social influence, Sense of community, Latent variableSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

With continued urbanization, residential neighborhoods in many cities are under growing pressure. Common challenges include the loss of green space and declining environmental quality1,2. To address the mismatch between aging neighborhoods and evolving lifestyle needs, various urban regeneration strategies have been proposed, such as building repairs and green infrastructure improvements3,4. These initiatives together constitute urban regeneration that aims to preserve and enhance the social and physical environments of deteriorating communities5.

Despite the growing interest in neighborhood renewal, its effectiveness depends on its alignment with local development and residents’ daily needs. This is because residents are the primary stakeholders experiencing long-term impacts of renewal and are most directly engaged in neighborhood life. Residents’ preferences and acceptance are shaped not only by physical improvements, such as green areas or activity spaces, but also by intangible social dimensions, including relationships among neighbors and their participation. Renewal projects that focus solely on rebuilding physical structures risk weakening place attachment and fragmenting communities6. Prior studies have shown that social capital plays a key role in shaping neighborhood participation and neighborhood governance7.

In this context, participatory neighborhood renewal has gained increasing attention. Engaging residents in decision-making and co-creation helps ensure that renewal plans align with local needs8. Research shows that, beyond residents’ preferences for physical amenities, their psychological perceptions and socio-psychological factors also strongly influence their willingness to engage in renewal activities7,9. These factors include a sense of attachment10, trust in neighbors and organizations11, and social influence from peers and family. While these subjective perceptions are not directly observable, they significantly shape residents’ attitudes and behaviors. This network of trust, norms, and networks is often described as a neighborhood’s social capital12.

Social capital has been widely recognized as a key factor influencing residents’ participation in neighborhood affairs13. For example, Huang et al. 9 developed a multilevel structural equation model to examine the role of social capital in cooperative behavior. However, existing studies often focus on a single dimension of socio-psychological factors and rarely examine how perceptions of social capital shape preferences for physical facilities in neighborhood renewal. In other words, it is the combined influence of neighborhood social capital and the demand for physical facilities on neighborhood renewal choices. The potential link between social trust, attachment, or peer influence and the acceptance of environmental improvements remains underexplored.

In addition, emerging urban development and mobility patterns also shape neighborhood renewal. In recent years, many cities have shifted from expansion-driven growth to regeneration-oriented planning14. Enhancing fragmented urban spaces and increasing access to green areas and public facilities have become core strategies. Meanwhile, sustainability goals and technological advances have accelerated the adoption of electric vehicles and remote work15,16. Related infrastructure is increasingly integrated into daily life, such as electric vehicle (EV) charging stations and teleworking spaces17. Past research has focused on the siting and optimization of these facilities15. Little attention has been given to how neighborhood renewal could serve as a platform for embedding such facilities into the neighborhood environment. This remains an important but overlooked topic.

To address these gaps, this study contributes to the growing literature on neighborhood renewal by integrating latent attitudinal factors and emerging lifestyle infrastructure into the framework of renewal choice behavior. We designed a stated preference experiment that presented respondents with a series of neighborhood renewal scenarios and asked them to select their preferred options. These scenarios included both traditional neighborhood features, such as green space and public activity areas, and new amenities such as teleworking spaces and public EV charging stations that reflect emerging lifestyle needs. Participants were also asked to respond to a series of statements in an on-site questionnaire to assess their socio-psychological attitudes. Based on these responses, we estimated an Integrated Choice and Latent Variable (ICLV) model to capture how physical infrastructure, social contexts, and latent factors affect resident renewal participation.

Figure 1 shows a simplified structure of our model. Residents’ renewal choices are influenced by four main types of factors, including both direct and indirect: socio-demographic characteristics, socio-psychological variables, neighbors’ choices, and physical attributes of the renewal plan. The socio-psychological variables summarize latent attitudes such as place attachment, neighborhood trust, and social influence, which are measured through attitudinal questions in the survey.

Model structure concept

Given the institutional context of urban planning in China, residents are the primary participants in and beneficiaries of neighborhood‑level renewal initiatives aimed at improving the living environment. Accordingly, this study focuses on residents as the core research population. Although individuals may hold multiple social roles, such as workers, business owners, or tenants, their identity as residents remains the most direct and influential in shaping decisions about neighborhood renewal. The results of this research offer practical value to policymakers and practitioners in developing more inclusive, resident-oriented planning strategies.

The following section reviews related literature on public participation, neighborhood renewal, and socio-psychological influences on choice behavior. We then describe the data collection process, experimental design, and modeling framework. Finally, the model results and policy implications for neighborhood renewal were addressed.

Literature review

As urban regeneration shifts from large-scale city interventions to neighborhood-level initiatives more closely aligned with daily life, neighborhood renewal has become an increasingly important strategy for improving aging environments and enhancing residents’ quality of life18. In this process, a better understanding of residents’ preferences is essential to delivering high-quality, sustainable renewal outcomes.

Neighborhood renewal typically involves multiple stakeholders, including government agencies, developers, and residents, whose interests may not always align19–21. Currently, public participation takes various forms. Governments or institutions provide some mechanisms, while others are community-led initiatives to improve neighborhood living environments8,22. Across these efforts, residents can participate through evaluation, decision-making, or co-creation8,23. However, the mechanisms driving such engagement are predominantly top-down in many contexts24. Structured as part of broader regeneration policies rather than initiated by residents themselves, participation is often orchestrated by government-business entities through formal public hearings, feedback sessions, or surveys21. Existing studies point out several structural issues within these frameworks, such as the lack of supporting policies, inadequate public–private cooperation mechanisms, and information asymmetry among stakeholders25, which together create major barriers to effective renewal implementation. Residents often have limited influence over renewal decisions and express skepticism about the outcomes of public participation, which weakens the momentum for policy implementation25,26. As a result, a concerning gap has emerged: governments and institutions lack systematic evidence of residents’ specific needs, while residents lack trust in participation mechanisms and are reluctant to express opinions or become involved.

In response, researchers have examined residents’ motivations for participation and patterns of engagement in neighborhood renewal27,28. These studies typically draw on behavioral frameworks to identify internal and external factors that shape participation27,29. Social relational variables, such as sense of community, neighborhood trust, and social influence, have been shown to correlate closely with cooperative behavior and participation intentions30–32. Hays7 found that community attachment and neighborhood confidence significantly increase the likelihood of participating in organized collective activities. Similarly, van Casteren et al.33 emphasized the role of neighborhood leaders in generating peer effects through local social networks during renovation projects. They found that block leaders can significantly influence residents’ decisions to energy retrofit their buildings, especially among those living nearby. Among residents living within 200 m of the leader, the likelihood of retrofit adoption was 3 times the average, reflecting collective decision-making behavior.

The classical behavioral models often assumed individuals to be rational egoists guided by the “zero-contribution thesis,” predicting that they would avoid contributing to collective goods. However, this assumption contradicts empirical observations. Ostrom’s work demonstrates that communities can sustain cooperation even without direct government intervention or privatization. A key mechanism lies in collective-choice arrangements, which allow affected individuals to participate in the formulation of rules governing shared resources34. In collective action settings, some individuals act as conditional cooperators, initiating reciprocal behaviors and monitoring others’ compliance, thereby reinforcing shared norms and enabling cooperation to persist35. As a result, when people make collective decisions, they are influenced by social norms and may consciously or unconsciously adjust their choices in line with others’ behavior.

Coleman’s perspective similarly emphasizes that individual actions are embedded in a larger social system governed by norms, obligations, and interpersonal expectations36. The behavior of some individuals affects others through these social structures. Rather than viewing behavior solely through the lens of utility maximization, Coleman conceptualizes social capital as a resource derived from social relations that enables actors to coordinate and influence one another in collective decision-making37. From this perspective, neighborhood renewal can be understood as a collective decision context in which residents must consider not only their own interests but also how neighbors are likely to respond to renewal proposals. Social capital shapes renewal choices by reducing uncertainty, fostering cooperation, and helping residents align their decisions with perceived group norms. These theoretical insights provide an analytical foundation for examining how trust, attachment, and social influence affect residents’ evaluations of renewal proposals.

While current studies help explain why residents may choose to participate, they provide limited insight into preferences regarding the content of renewal plans. Participation is not only a matter of willingness but also depends on how people judge the proposed changes. However, policymakers or housing authorities often define renewal plans, and resident preferences are not systematically incorporated into planning processes38.

A broader perspective on the residential environment offers insights into this issue. As neighborhood renewal fundamentally aims to improve living conditions39, it is reasonable to assume that preferences for residential environments influence their willingness to support renewal. Previous studies have shown that housing choices are shaped by objective conditions such as location and access to public amenities40. For instance, environmental features such as green spaces and recreational areas play an important role in shaping residential satisfaction and decision-making41,42. These findings offer useful insights into residents’ preferences regarding renewal content. However, findings on existing built environments do not capture people’s attitudes toward emerging or planned facilities that have not yet been widely implemented.

In summary, existing studies on environmental factors and residential preferences do not fully capture the dynamics of neighborhood renewal. Meanwhile, research on socio-psychological factors tends to focus on participation motives rather than preferences for specific renewal contents. There is a lack of a comprehensive framework that integrates both dimensions to explain residents’ choices regarding concrete renewal proposals. Thus, this study introduces key socio-psychological latent variables into a stated-choice framework, including place attachment, neighborhood trust, and social influence. We applied an Integrated Choice and Latent Variable (ICLV) model to systematically examine how these socio-psychological factors and the physical attributes of renewal projects commonly shape resident choice behaviors—aiming to capture resident preferences under the combined influence of social capital and evolving lifestyles.

The ICLV model generally consists of two components: a multinomial discrete-choice model and a latent-variable model43. The discrete-choice component follows the random-utility maximization framework. The latent-variable component is highly flexible, allowing for relationships among latent constructs and for the influence of observable variables on these latent factors. This structure enables researchers to distinguish between the direct effects of observed variables and the indirect effects transmitted through latent attitudes44. Unlike traditional structural equation models (SEM) or mixed logit models that focus primarily on either latent constructs or observable factors, the ICLV framework integrates both within a unified system28,45. This combined specification allows for a comprehensive assessment of how socio-psychological factors and environmental attributes jointly influence individual choice behavior.

Methodology

This study was conducted in Nanchang, China. In 2021, the Chinese 14th Five-Year Plan emphasized the need to “coordinate urban planning, construction, and management; implement urban regeneration initiatives; and promote the optimization of spatial structure and quality enhancement in cities46.” In November of the same year, Nanchang was selected as one of the first pilot cities for urban regeneration by the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development47. Since then, the city has committed to investing in renewal projects. Government initiatives have specifically called for strengthening public participation, upgrading urban furniture, and expanding green spaces, plazas, and other public areas. According to official reports, 612 urban regeneration projects were planned and implemented in Nanchang by 2024, including 488 projects focused on improving older residential communities. These policies and actions have undoubtedly shaped public awareness of neighborhood renewal and have influenced residents’ daily lives in multiple ways.

To explore residents’ attitudes and preferences toward neighborhood renewal, we conducted a face-to-face survey that incorporated a stated-preference choice experiment—the first part of the questionnaire collected socio-demographic information. The second part presented respondents with hypothetical neighborhood renewal scenarios to examine how different features affect their willingness to support renewal. Stated-choice experiments have been widely adopted in housing choice, urban planning, and mobility research48–50. It allows respondents to evaluate multiple alternatives defined by a combination of attributes at different levels. The final section included a series of attitudinal statements to investigate the latent influence of socio-psychological factors on choice behavior.

Experimental design

Attributes and levels

In the stated choice experiment, respondents were presented with two neighborhood renewal plans and asked to select their preferred option. Additionally, a “keep present situation” alternative was provided, allowing respondents to opt out of both proposed plans if neither was acceptable.

Existing literature has shown that green space is a critical consideration in various types of housing decisions51. According to official regulations, a minimum green ratio of 30% is required for normal neighborhoods and 25% for older neighborhoods. Given these baselines, large-scale increases (such as doubling the green space) of a 60% or higher ratio in neighborhoods would be unrealistic. Therefore, we designed two feasible levels of improvement: a 25% and a 50% increase relative to the current level. These increments were selected to ensure that respondents could meaningfully perceive and evaluate the change, as smaller adjustments (e.g., 10%) may go unnoticed. A “no change” option was also included. Thus, the green space attribute in the experiment consists of three levels: no change, 25% increase, and 50% increase.

Public activity spaces are another important feature of many neighborhoods and take various forms depending on the local context, such as chess rooms, table tennis rooms, and reading rooms52–54. These facilities promote social interaction and encourage participation in neighborhood life55,56, while contributing to social capital and neighborhood sustainability5. Therefore, we included activity space in the experiment, with its size ranging from several dozen to several hundred square meters. Based on this context, we defined three levels for this attribute: no change, an increase of 50 m², and an increase of 100 m².

Driven by the vision of sustainable urban development, integrated green mobility strategies have been widely adopted across many countries for their economic and environmental benefits. Among these strategies, electric vehicles (EVs), plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs), and teleworking have emerged as key components16,57. Recent studies have explored the combined effects of EV adoption and telework on environmental outcomes and travel behavior58. To examine how these emerging mobility patterns influence neighborhood renewal preferences, this study incorporated two related attributes into the stated-choice experiment: public EV charging stations and telework spaces.

Research found that the availability of public charging infrastructure helps increase EV ownership17. However, space constraints in older neighborhoods and the prevalence of private chargers among existing EV users limited the feasibility of larger-scale installations59. Therefore, the number of public EV charging stations is set at three levels: no change, + 5 charging spots, and + 10 charging spots. In addition, teleworking space was defined as the number of available workstations in a neighborhood-based teleworking center, with three levels: no change, + 5 workstations, and + 10 workstations. The assumption is that providing only a limited number of seats in neighborhoods may be adequate, considering the modest demand for flexible workplaces.

As neighborhoods are shared living areas, people’s participation in neighborhood renewable initiatives is also influenced by peer behavior and collective preferences9. To account for this, we included a social influence attribute, measured as the proportion of neighbors choosing a given plan. This attribute has three levels: 20%, 50%, and 80%, representing minority, balanced, and majority neighborhood preferences, respectively. Previous studies have also used a similar approach to capture the impact of social influences on mobility choices, e.g., shared parking60, and shared cars61.

Overall, the stated choice experiment includes four environmental facility attributes and one social influence attribute. Table 1 summarizes the attributes and corresponding levels used in the experimental design. Given the number of attributes and levels, a full factorial design would generate 243 possible combinations (3⁵), which would be overly burdensome for respondents. To reduce cognitive load, an orthogonal fractional factorial design was employed, resulting in 27 distinct profiles. Each respondent was randomly assigned 9 choice tasks drawn from these 27 profiles.

The plans were randomly generated using an orthogonal design and are unlabeled, meaning that Plan A and Plan B held no fixed order or identity across tasks. Each plan consisted of five attributes, whose levels are detailed in Table 1: four physical facility variables (green space, activity space, teleworking space, and public EV charging stations), and one social variable (the proportion of neighbors assumed to support the plan). The facility attributes were each assigned three levels: “no change,” followed by two incremental improvement levels. The neighbors’ choice attribute had three levels as well. This design allows us to examine how changes in specific facility features and perceived social support influence residents’ renewal preferences. It also captures the degree to which different levels of change in each attribute affect individual decision-making.

Attitudinal statements

In addition to the stated-choice experiment, this survey collected attitudinal data to assess the influence of socio-psychological factors on the choice of neighborhood renewal. Existing studies show that factors such as residential satisfaction and environmental perception affect individual housing choice behaviors62,63. Moreover, factors such as place attachment and relationships with neighbors have also influenced people’s behaviors in the neighborhood32,64. Therefore, this study incorporates attitudinal indicators to capture the combined effects of environmental perceptions and socio-psychological orientations on renewal preferences.

Drawing on existing literature, we developed ten attitudinal statements to measure three key socio-psychological constructs relevant to neighborhood renewal: social influence, place attachment, and social trust13,65–67. Social influence refers to the extent to which others influence individuals’ decision-making. For instance, a resident may support or oppose renewal depending on the opinions of family members or close peers. Place attachment refers to emotional bonds with one’s neighborhood and the social connections formed within it. Some residents may resist renewal due to a reluctance to change a familiar setting, while others may support it in hopes of improving a valued place. Social trust captures the degree of trust placed in fellow residents, which may affect how individuals weigh others’ opinions and anticipate the collective outcomes of renewal initiatives.

Table 3 provides a detailed list of the attitudinal statements used in this study. Respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement with each statement. A seven-point Likert scale was used, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “neutral” to “strongly agree.” A score of 1 represents “strongly disagree,” while a score of 7 represents “strongly agree.”

Survey and data collection

The data was collected in person in Nanchang over two months starting in April 2024. A spatially stratified and quasi-random sampling approach was used across the core urban areas of Nanchang, covering six administrative districts and one county directly connected to the city. Efforts were made to ensure balanced representation across neighborhoods. Given the clear spatial distinction between newly developed and older urban areas in Nanchang, the sample included a diverse mix of respondents from both types of neighborhoods. It is worth noting that the survey was administered within clearly defined residential compounds, commonly referred to in China as Xiaoqu, which are housing complexes that serve as basic neighborhood units.

Before the formal survey, respondents were informed of the research objectives and data protection policy. Trained surveyors then administered the main questionnaire. A small team of student investigators, all with academic backgrounds in urban planning or architecture, received training to provide neutral, standardized clarification when necessary, ensuring that respondents understood the questions accurately. To minimize interviewer bias, all investigators received standardized training and followed a scripted protocol when interacting with respondents. Surveyors were instructed to limit their input strictly to neutral clarifications of technical terms and were prohibited from offering personal opinions, explanations, or suggestions that could influence responses. The entire survey process took approximately 15–25 min per respondent. Upon completion, each questionnaire was reviewed for completeness and internal consistency.

Respondents were recruited using two methods. First, investigators selected neighborhoods and conducted surveys in public areas, including residential compounds, plazas, and parks. To approximate random selection within space, investigators used a spatially stratified, random procedure: neighborhoods were first selected within each district, and respondents were then approached in a quasi-random manner within these neighborhoods. Second, in collaboration with neighborhood organizations, participants were recruited through public announcements and door-to-door visits. A total of 435 individuals were invited to participate in the survey, of whom 400 completed the questionnaire. After rigorous data screening (removing responses with missing data or logical inconsistencies), we retained 304 valid responses for analysis. Although the sample size is modest for this type of research, the use of panel data helps mitigate this limitation by expanding the number of observations. Each respondent completed 9 choice tasks with varying attribute combinations, yielding a total of 2,736 observations. Nevertheless, the study remains exploratory, and future research with larger, more diverse samples would be valuable for generalizability.

Sample characteristics of all respondents are presented in Table 2. The results show that 49.67% of participants were male and 50.33% were female. Compared to the general population of Nanchang, the proportion of female respondents is slightly higher. In terms of age distribution, 52.30% of respondents were aged 18–35, 36.84% were aged 36–55, and 10.86% were aged over 55. Overall, the sample’s distribution is generally consistent with Nanchang’s demographic structure. In addition, 65.13% of respondents held a university degree, which is higher than the general population’s educational attainment. This is understandable, given that the survey was conducted in urban residential areas where people are relatively well educated. This may also explain why highly educated people are more likely to participate in surveys and engage in neighborhood renewals.

Model specification

To further investigate residents’ participation in neighborhood renewal projects, this study applies an integrated choice and latent variable (ICLV) model that incorporates alternative-specific attributes, individual socio-demographic characteristics, and latent psychological factors. Due to its ability to incorporate unobserved preferences, the ICLV model framework has been widely applied in studies on housing choice, mobility behavior, and other related fields71,72. Essentially, the ICLV model extends traditional discrete choice models by embedding attitudinal variables into the utility function. It allows for the inclusion of both economic and psychological data to capture the unobserved heterogeneity that standard choice models often overlook73. Based on random utility theory, the utility of each neighborhood renewal option is composed of two components: alternative-specific attributes and latent attitudinal constructs. Most attributes show statistically significant effects and are consistent with theoretical expectations. Overall, respondents tend to favor renewal initiatives. Compared to the basic MNL model, the ICLV model shows a substantial improvement in model fit, with the R-squared increasing from 0.100 to 0.271. This improvement is attributed to the inclusion of latent variables and the consideration of the panel structure of the data. In the ICLV model, several attributes were specified as random parameters, and estimation was performed using 1,500 draws from the Modified Latin Hypercube Sampling (MLHS) to ensure numerical stability. Given its superior performance and more comprehensive behavioral representation, the discussion in the following sections will primarily focus on the ICLV model’s results.

Alternative-specific attributes

Results indicate that residents prefer green spaces and neighborhood activity spaces in neighborhood renewal. Both green space and activity space show significantly positive coefficients on increases levels, indicating that increases in their quantity lead to higher support from residents. Renewal options without green space expansion significantly reduce residents’ likelihood of participation, whereas increased green space leads to positive acceptance.

For public charging stations, the analysis shows that when renewal plans do not include additional charging facilities, residents’ willingness to choose those plans is significantly negative. In contrast, adding 5 or 10 charging stations increases plan acceptance. However, the estimated coefficients indicate that the influence of this variable is smaller compared to that of green space and activity space. Interestingly, and somewhat contrary to expectations, teleworking spaces did not exhibit a significant effect, either positive or negative on residents’ choices.

Moreover, model results confirm the significance of the neighbor’s variable. When 80% of neighbors support a given renewal option, respondents were significantly more likely to make the same choice. Conversely, when the stated level of support was 50% or lower, the effect became significantly negative. The negative coefficient at the 50% level suggests that a simple majority may not be sufficient to build broad agreement in neighborhood renewal decisions.

In addition, the significant standard deviation of the alternative-specific constant indicates heterogeneity in respondents’ choices. Individual preference heterogeneity was also found for green space, activity space, public EV charging stations, and neighbors’ choice.

Latent factors

The model results in Table 7 indicate that all three identified latent attitudinal factors significantly influence residents’ preferences for neighborhood renewal plans. First, social trust is positively associated with renewal choices. Secondly, Place attachment showed a highly positive correlation. Third, social influence also plays an important positive role in shaping renewal preferences. Individuals who are more likely to consider others’ opinions and be influenced by friends or family tend to show greater willingness to participate in neighborhood renewal. The performance of these three psychosocial factors reflects the impact of the sense of community and peer effects on individual decisions in neighborhood renewal.

In addition, the structure of the influence of individual characteristics on latent variables was identified. The structural model results of the latent variables are presented in Table 8. Overall, individual characteristics such as age, gender, education level, and years of residence significantly influence residents’ levels of trust, attachment, and susceptibility to social influence. Age has a significant effect across all three dimensions. Younger individuals (aged 18–35) are negatively associated with all three latent factors, indicating a stronger tendency toward independent decision-making and a weaker sense of trust or attachment to the neighborhood. In contrast, older residents tend to express higher levels of neighborhood attachment and are more likely to consider others’ opinions.

Discussions

The significant association between changes in green space and residents’ stated willingness to support neighborhood renewal aligns with previous findings suggesting that the presence and accessibility of green spaces enhance residential property values and play a crucial role in housing decisions55,77. Essentially, green space availability and accessibility are important factors associated with housing choice and price, and ideal housing conditions often include access to private gardens or proximity to public green areas78,79. However, in China’s predominantly collective housing layout, private gardens are typically unavailable. In this context, shared green spaces within neighborhoods serve as functional intermediaries between private and public green spaces. Since private and public green spaces are functionally substitutable80, neighborhood greenery may not only enhance the appeal of renewal proposals but also partially substitute for private green amenities, which serve as a vital complement to urban public green infrastructure. However, in countries with private land ownership, such as Japan or the United States, the outcomes may differ. In some neighborhoods, particularly suburban areas or high-end residential districts, households often have access to private green spaces within their own yards. As a result, the additional welfare gained from expanding public green spaces within the neighborhood may be lower than what is observed in Chinese residential compounds. Nonetheless, even in these contexts, densely populated apartment neighborhoods may still exhibit patterns similar to those identified in this study.

Activity spaces in neighborhood renewal operate in tandem with green spaces, providing physical platforms for social interaction. Creating or expanding such facilities can strengthen neighborhood cohesion and improve well-being. In older residential areas where elderly populations are concentrated, increasing neighborhood activity space contributes to age-friendly urban environments81. Notably, existing studies often use the broad terms “public space” or “open space,” which may include green spaces, squares, or undefined social venues82. In contrast, our study specifically defines activity spaces as designated indoors or multifunctional areas for daily social use, such as chess rooms, gyms, or spaces for dancing and performance. Our findings are consistent with strong resident demand for these facilities. The lower stated preference for renewal plans is associated with the absence of activity spaces, while expanded areas increase willingness to engage, highlighting a strong functional demand.

We also introduced social influence, represented by neighbors’ choices. Previous research has shown that individuals’ decisions are often influenced by their social networks, including peers and family members83–85. In the context of participatory neighborhood renewal, such conformity or peer effects are especially relevant, as renewal decisions are inherently collective and involve multiple stakeholders within the neighborhood. The effect observed for neighbors’ choice reflects this dynamic, demonstrating that residents are more inclined to support renewal plans that appear to have broader neighborhood endorsement. These findings underscore the importance of consensus-building in participatory planning. Policymakers should emphasize mechanisms that foster neighborhood collaboration to enhance collective engagement, such as neighborhood hearings, deliberation platforms, or participatory budgeting.

In the context of a growing shift toward green mobility and flexible work arrangements, this study further investigates how the inclusion of public EV charging stations and teleworking spaces in renewal plans affects residents’ preferences. Previous studies have identified notable disparities in the spatial distribution of EV charging infrastructure. For instance, research from California shows that charging stations are primarily concentrated in high-income, apartment-based communities86. Similarly, a recent study in Washington reports that most charging stations are located in commercial areas, with significant under-provision in residential neighborhoods15. While Esmaili et al. 86 argue that Washington’s charging infrastructure achieves a balance between utilitarian and capabilities-based equity, the current allocation remains largely utility-driven. It may fail to meet the everyday accessibility and convenience needs of residents in residential neighborhoods.

Our results indicate that the inclusion of public EV charging stations in renewal plans is significantly associated with resident support for the proposed renewal plan. Conversely, omitting these facilities yields a negative effect. In addition, prior research has shown that when governments subsidize or promote the construction of residential charging infrastructure, residents are more willing to purchase or lease electric vehicles87. This provides important implications for policymakers: incorporating public EV charging stations into neighborhood renewal not only enhances public support. Neighborhood renewal can also serve as a strategic policy tool that simultaneously promotes sustainable mobility and improves local environments.

Contrary to expectations, the inclusion of teleworking spaces in neighborhood renewal plans did not significantly affect residents’ preferences. It is widely acknowledged that remote work reduces commuting and contributes to energy conservation16. Other studies have raised concerns about its unintended consequences. For instance, remote workers may engage in more non-work-related trips, potentially offsetting the environmental benefits of reduced commuting88. Additionally, increased energy consumption at home or in third spaces may further undermine its sustainability potential89. These factors may challenge the long-term value or cost-effectiveness of neighborhood-based teleworking facilities.

We speculate that individuals engaged in full-time remote work are likely to have adequate home-based setups already. For potential or hybrid remote workers, neighborhood-level teleworking spaces may offer additional value to alter their preferences, although third places such as cafés may already fulfill their basic needs. Moreover, in the Chinese urban context, teleworking has not yet become a mainstream or institutionally supported work mode, and many employers still expect on-site presence. Although the pandemic temporarily increased remote work adoption, most organizations have since returned to traditional office routines. As a result, residents may not perceive neighborhood-based teleworking spaces as essential or sufficiently valuable to influence renewal choices. This socio-economic and cultural backdrop may help explain the non-significant influence observed in this study. Nonetheless, as remote and hybrid work become more institutionalized, such facilities may gain value as shared neighborhood resources and deserve further consideration in future neighborhood planning.

Regarding the influence of latent attitudes. Residents who express greater trust in their neighbors are more likely to support and engage in renewal projects. Second, place attachment also exerts a significant positive association consistent with prior research. Manzo & Perkins argue that emotional bonds to one’s community can motivate collective action aimed at improving local environments90. Moreover, the finding of social influence partially supports existing understandings of how neighborhood social dynamics can shape cooperative behavior. As noted by Huang et al.9, residents may make renewal choices not solely based on personal interests but also a desire to maintain harmonious neighborhood relationships or avoid conflict. In addition, theories of herding behavior further support this mechanism. When individuals face collective decisions under uncertainty, they tend to rely on the actions or judgments of others. Such expectations help individuals determine what is perceived as normal, appropriate, or fair within a given social context91. In our framework, the three latent variables of neighborhood trust, place attachment, and social influence summarize residents’ attitudes toward neighborhood social capital and indicate the situations to which they may rely on others when forming renewal preferences.

Social trust, place attachment, and social influence appear to be interrelated with residents’ conformity tendencies and their sense of community. These findings also validate the group influence mechanism captured by the neighbors’ choice variable in the choice experiment. For policymakers, fostering neighborhood trust and a stronger sense of community can promote collaborative behavior and enhance residents’ willingness to support renewal initiatives. Thus, strengthening neighborhood interaction may be an effective strategy to improve the efficiency and acceptance of neighborhood renewal, e.g., through local communities or participatory activities. However, the predominance of boundary residential compounds, relatively low residential mobility, and the strong role of local governments in directing renewal initiatives may amplify the effects of place attachment, social trust, and peer influence observed in this study. This also points to another implication. In countries or regions where neighborhood structures are more dispersed, stronger coordination led by local governments or community organizations may help unify residents’ opinions and strengthen collective decision-making. Reinforcing the notion of neighborhood blocks and fostering more structured community interactions may also help foster place attachment and build neighborhood trust.

Furthermore, this study also examines the relationship between individual characteristics and latent attitudes. The findings for age suggest that policy design must account for psychological heterogeneity across different age groups. Younger residents may exhibit a weaker sense of identification with the neighborhood environment and a stronger preference for independent judgment in social interactions, whereas older residents are more likely to maintain tighter social networks. These distinctions highlight the need for more diversified and age-sensitive approaches in the formulation and implementation of neighborhood renewal policies, particularly in communities composed of multiple age groups.

In addition, the previous findings that residential mobility can reduce generalized trust in others who are not specific groups, such as families and friends, but shows no significant association with particularistic trust toward specific individuals or groups92. Our findings extend this understanding by showing that at the neighborhood level, particularistic trust toward one’s neighbors is significantly higher among long-term residents. Long-term residents often develop more stable neighborhood networks and stronger emotional bonds, which may in turn influence their renewal preferences. Although the ICLV framework helps account for the relationship between observed characteristics and latent attitudes, potential endogeneity cannot be fully ruled out. Future studies could further explore these relationships through longitudinal data or by explicitly modeling the interdependence between residential histories and socio-psychological factors.

Overall, the findings suggest that residents’ renewal preferences are jointly associated with physical improvements, neighbors’ choice, and latent variables. Both facility attributes and latent attitudes play essential roles, and their correlations vary across different demographic groups. These insights highlight the need for renewal strategies that integrate physical design with community-building efforts. Building on these findings, the concluding section summarizes the main contributions of this study and outlines implications for future research.

Conclusions

As urbanized areas continue to age and new lifestyles and mobility patterns become more widespread, neighborhood renewal has become increasingly important in many developing countries. Neighborhood renewal is not only about physical improvements but is also dynamically linked to the development of neighborhood-level social capital. Against this backdrop, there is a growing need to better understand how residents make decisions under various neighborhood renewal strategies and how broader dimensions of social capital shape these decisions.

This study applies an integrated choice and latent variable model to provide empirical insights into residents’ choice behavior in the context of participatory neighborhood renewal. Using stated-preference data collected in China, the analysis provides evidence on how individuals in urban residential communities respond to different renewal proposals. This study examined how physical attributes, latent socio-psychological factors, and social influence shape residents’ preferences for neighborhood renewal. The findings show that green space, activity areas, and EV charging stations are the most related physical attributes. Future neighborhood renewal should prioritize optimizing these facilities. However, teleworking spaces did not show a significant association. Trust, place attachment, and social influence are significantly associated with residents’ support for renewal. In collective decision-making, residents’ individual opinions are influenced by neighborhood social capital. However, individuals may have different attitudes toward social capital. These results directly address the research questions by clarifying both the infrastructural and psychological determinants of renewal choice behavior.

A practical implication of these findings concerns how trust and a sense of community can be strengthened in real neighborhood renewal processes. These socio-psychological factors are not abstract constructs but can be operationalized through concrete planning interventions. Policymakers can prioritize coordination mechanisms that foster mutual understanding and collective support, such as neighborhood assemblies, participatory platforms, and co-creation workshops. These forums allow residents to shape renewal proposals together and may help cultivate mutual trust and shared norms. A sense of community can also be strengthened through regular neighborhood events, clear communication channels, and small-scale renewal projects that foster everyday interactions and reinforce residents’ sense of belonging. In addition, neighborhood agreements or charters that articulate collective expectations may enhance cooperation and reduce decision-making uncertainty. Embedding such mechanisms into renewal practice may improve residents’ acceptance of and engagement in renewal initiatives and help amplify the positive influence of social capital revealed in this study.

In summary, neighborhood renewal is not merely a physical intervention but also a social-psychological process involving neighborhood identity, trust, and cohesion. Future renewal policies should go beyond functional improvements and actively foster neighborhood belonging, mutual recognition, and resident engagement to strengthen the social foundations of sustainable neighborhoods.

While this study provides detailed insights into how residents respond to neighborhood renewal options, several avenues for future research remain. First, because the study is based on stated-preference data, responses may not fully reflect real-world behavior due to hypothetical bias or unobserved factors. Future studies could incorporate revealed-preference data from individuals who have experienced, are undergoing, or are resisting neighborhood renewal. Second, the design of renewal alternatives in this study prioritized generalizability. As such, it omitted some emerging facilities relevant to evolving urban mobility patterns. Such as vertical parking systems, low-altitude urban air infrastructure, and smart parcel delivery stations93. Third, the analysis focuses on a single city in China, where collective housing compounds, relatively low residential mobility, and strong state involvement in renewal create a specific institutional and spatial context. Some findings are therefore likely to be context-specific, such as the strong role of shared green spaces, the limited salience of teleworking spaces under the current work culture, and the importance of EV charging infrastructure given the rapid expansion of EV ownership in China. At the same time, several patterns are likely to extend to other dense urban settings, including the importance of neighborhood greenery and activity spaces, the positive association between local charging infrastructure and support for renewal, and the role of trust, place attachment, and social influence in shaping collective decisions.

Finally, this study centers on the resident perspective, without explicitly modeling the influence of local policy instruments or planning frameworks. However, neighborhood renewal varies greatly across different administrative systems, policy regimes, and developer-led models. Future research should examine how institutional and policy differences shape residents’ perceptions and project outcomes. Despite these limitations, this study offers a resident-centered view that may serve as a valuable reference for aligning neighborhood renewal initiatives with local needs, thereby improving both policy effectiveness and residents’ acceptance.
