Pavel Gvozdevsky

Abstract
. The celebrated Borel–Tits theorem provides a classification of abstract isomorphisms between (simple) isotropic groups over fields, showing that such isomorphisms arise from field isomorphisms and group-scheme isomorphisms. In this work, we extend the scope of this classification to certain class of group schemes over arbitrary commutative rings. Specifically, we prove that under suitable conditions abstract isomorphisms between the groups of points of isotropic, absolutely simple, adjoint group schemes over rings admit a description analogous to that in the classical setting: namely, they are induced by isomorphisms of ground rings and isomorphisms of the underlying group schemes. This result generalizes the classical theory to a far broader algebraic context and confirms that the rigidity phenomena observed over fields persist over rings.

1.
Introduction

The celebrated Borel–Tits theorem provides a fundamental description of abstract isomorphisms between isotropic simple algebraic groups over fields. This landmark result was established in their seminal 1973 paper
[
2
]
. Almost immediately, the theorem became an indispensable tool in the theory of algebraic groups, underpinning a wide range of applications. Its influence was recognized, for example, in a dedicated session of the Séminaire N. Bourbaki (Talk 435), and in the subsequent survey by R. Steinberg
[
55
]
, which distilled the core ideas and implications of the Borel–Tits theory.

Further developments culminated in a 2018 paper by A. Borel and J. Tits
[
3
]
, presenting a variation on the same theme. Despite these advances, one of the most significant and long-standing challenges in the area has been to extend the Borel–Tits framework beyond fields, particularly to arbitrary commutative rings and to anisotropic groups. This problem has remained open for decades and is widely regarded as both technically demanding and foundational for a deeper understanding of abstract homomorphisms in broader algebraic settings.

More specifically, Theorem by Borel and Tits
[
2
, Theorem 8.11]
says that if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are absolutely simple adjoint isotropic group schemes over infinite fields
K_{1}
and

and a

-group-scheme isomorphism

(with some exceptions). Actually, the Theorem says something a bit stronger, but for our purposes we only need this weaker version. The purpose of the present paper is to prove a similar statement for a broad class of group schemes over almost arbitrary commutative rings.

Such a result can be useful in many areas, in particular, it can play a decisive role in the study of the logic and model theory of isotropic reductive groups, cf.

The most common notion of an isotropic reductive group scheme over a ring is as follows: a reductive group scheme
G
is said to be isotropic with isotropic rank
\geqslant n
, where
n\geqslant 1
, if
G
contains a split torus of rank
n
as a closed subgroup-subscheme. This is the definition used, for example, in
[
42
]
and
[
53
]
. However, we were not able to prove the theorem on abstract isomorphisms in this generality; instead, we require
G_{1}
to be isotropic in the more restrictive sense of
[
59
]
. This means that the following requirements, which are not automatic in general, must be satisfied: the relative root system in the sense of
[
42
]
must actually be a root system; the corresponding root subgroups must make the groups
G_{1}(S)
into a root graded group in the sense of
[
65
]
and
[
58
]
for any
R
-algebra
S
(alternatively, one can say that
G_{1}
admits an isotropic pinning in the sense of
[
57
]
); and the resulting map from the absolute root system to the relative one must come from one of the Tits indexes. We believe it is natural to call this class of group schemes root graded isotropic group schemes and to refer to their groups of points as root graded isotropic groups.

The class of root graded isotropic group schemes includes, but is not limited to, special linear groups over Azumaya algebras; orthogonal groups corresponding to quadratic forms with positive Witt index; and unitary groups over Azumaya algebras with involution, associated with (anti)hermitian forms with positive Witt index. However, for example, if
P
is a projective module over the ring
R
, then a decomposition
P=P_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus P_{n+1}
, where each
P_{i}
has constant rank, leads to

being a root graded isotropic group only if all the
P_{i}
are isomorphic to each other, whereas
\Aut_{R}(P)
is always isotropic in the usual sense, even if the ranks of
P_{i}
are different from each other. Nevertheless, it follows from the results of
[
43
]
that over a semilocal ring all isotropic group schemes are root graded.

Additionally, for the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case where the group schemes are not just reductive, but also absolutely simple and adjoint. The case of group schemes that are not necessarily adjoint will be treated separately in the future paper.

.

Let

and

and

, that satisfy certain technical assumptions

Let

2.
Basic definitions, conventions and notation

Rings and algebras

The word ’’ring’’ always means a commutative associative ring with unity. If
R
is a ring, the term ’’
R
-algebra’’ means a commutative associative
R
-algebra with unity, unless specified otherwise (e.g., when Azumaya algebras are mentioned).

If
R
is a ring, we denote by
R^{*}
the group of its invertible elements.

Operations in groups

We use the standard notation for conjugation in groups:
h^{g}=g^{-1}hg
and
\vphantom{,}{}^{g}h=ghg^{-1}
. Commutators are left-normalized:
[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
.

The notation
[\cdot,\cdot]
may also refer to the Lie bracket in a Lie algebra, but the context will make it clear whether a calculation is performed in a group or an algebra.

Root systems

We usually use the notation
\widetilde{\Phi}
for a reduced irreducible crystallographic root system, and
\Phi
for an irreducible crystallographic root system that is not necessarily reduced (i.e., it may be of type
BC
).

For roots
\alpha
and
\beta
we write
\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle=\tfrac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}
, where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the inner product; and denote by
s_{\alpha}(\beta)
the reflection of
\beta
with respect to the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
.

The root
\alpha
is called long if
(\alpha,\alpha)\geqslant(\beta,\beta)
for any root
\beta
(in particular, in simply-laced systems every root counts as long). The root
\alpha
is called ultrashort if
2\alpha
is a root. The root
\alpha
is called short if it is neither long, nor ultrashort.

Schemes

We adopt the functorial point of view on the theory of schemes (see
[
16
]
). Thus, if
R
is a ring, instead of saying that certain functor
X
from the category of
R
-algebras to the category of sets is represented by a scheme over
R
, we will say that
X
is a scheme over
R
.

When we need to define a scheme over
R
, we specify what the set
X(S)
is for each
R
-algebra
S
, and we promise to do so in a way that makes the implied action of
X
on homomorphisms clear. The scheme in a more conventional sense (i.e., a locally ringed topological space) representing the functor
X
will be referred to as the underlying topological space of the scheme
X
.

A morphism of schemes, in this terminology, is a natural transformation of functors. If
\Theta\colon X\to Y
is a morphism of schemes over
R
, and
S
is an
R
-algebra we denote by
\Theta_{S}
the corresponding map
X(S)\to Y(S)
.

A group scheme over
R
, in this terminology, is a functor from the category of
R
-algebras to the category of groups, whose composition with the forgetful functor to the category of sets is a scheme.

If
G
is a group scheme over the ring
R
, and
S
is an
R
-algebra with an ideal
I\unlhd S
, we denote by
G(S,I)
the principal congruence subgroup of level
I
, i.e., the kernel of the reduction homomorphism
\rho_{I}\colon G(S)\to G(S/I)
.

The notation
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)
is reserved for the adjoint Chevalley–Demazure scheme associated with a reduced irreducible crystallographic root system
\widetilde{\Phi}
. The Chevalley–Demazure schemes are defined in
[
14
]
. For a ring
S
and an ideal
I\unlhd S
, we denote by
G(\widetilde{\Phi},S)
the corresponding adjoint Chevalley group, and by
G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
the corresponding principal congruence subgroup of level
I
.

Absolutely simple adjoint group schemes with a common root datum of the geometric fibers

For the purposes of the present paper, we do not need to define each term in the phrase “absolutely simple adjoint group scheme with a common root datum of the geometric fibers” separately; instead, we explain their combined meaning.

The group scheme
G
over the ring
R
is said to be an absolutely simple adjoint with a common root datum of the geometric fibers if there is an fppf-extension
S
of the ring
R
such that
G_{S}
is isomorphic as a group scheme over
S
to the adjoint Chevalley–Demazure scheme
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{S}
over
S
for some reduced irreducible crystallographic root system
\widetilde{\Phi}
.

Here
G_{S}
is a base change of
G
to
S
. The root system
\widetilde{\Phi}
here is called the absolute root system of
G
.

Isotropic rank

We say that the absolutely simple adjoint group scheme
G
with a common root datum of the geometric fibers has isotropic rank at least
l
if it contains a closed subgroup-subscheme isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{l}
, where
\mathbb{G}_{m}
is the multiplicative group scheme (i.e.
\mathbb{G}_{m}(S)=S^{*}
for any
R
-algebra
S
).

Pinnings and isotropic pre-pinnings.

Let
G
be an absolutely simple adjoint group scheme with a common root datum of the geometric fibers over the ring
R
. Recall
[
17

that a

of

consists of

\bullet
a maximal torus
T\leqslant G
with a chosen isomorphism

;

\bullet
a root datum

are the sets of roots and coroots of

;

\bullet
a basis
\Delta\leqslant\Phi
;

\bullet
trivializing sections

in the root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}=\Lie(G)
for
\alpha\in\Delta
, so that all root spaces are free

-modules of rank 1.

So if
G
has a pinning, then it is isomorphic to
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{R}
already over

and the root system
\Phi
in the corresponding root datum coincides with the absolute root system
\widetilde{\Phi}
.

Now following
[
57
]
, we say that an
isotropic pre-pinning
of
G
consists of

\bullet
a split torus
T\leqslant G
with a chosen isomorphism
T\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}^{l}
;

\bullet
a root system
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{l}
(with respect to some inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{l}
) together with a
chosen base
\Delta\leqslant\Phi
such that
\Phi
is the set of non-zero weights of
\mathfrak{g}
with
respect to
T
(or
R=0
);

Here unlike
[
57
]
we will not require the root subspaces to be free modules.

We say that an isotropic pre-pinning
(T,\Phi)
is contained in an isotropic pre-pinning
(T^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime})
if
T\leqslant T^{\prime}
, the inclusion is given by a constant surjective homomorphism
u\colon\mathbb{Z}^{l}\to\mathbb{Z}^{l}
of the corresponding abelian groups, and
u(\Phi^{\prime}\cup\{0\})=\Phi\cup\{0\}
(the last condition is vacuous if
R
is non-zero).

For every isotropic pre-pinning
(T,\Phi)
there is an fppf-extension
S
of the ring
R
such that after a base change to
S
the isotropic pre-pinning
(T,\Phi)
becomes contained in a pinning; therefore, inducing a map
u\colon\widetilde{\Phi}\cup\{0\}\to\Phi\cup\{0\}
. This map will be referred to as the corresponding map
u\colon\widetilde{\Phi}\cup\{0\}\to\Phi\cup\{0\}
of the isotropic pre-pinning
(T,\Phi)
.

We will usually make an assumption that the corresponding map
u\colon\widetilde{\Phi}\cup\{0\}\to\Phi\cup\{0\}
comes from one of the Tits indexes. The Tits index in question is always implied to be irreducible, because
\widetilde{\Phi}
is assumed to be irreducible. Recall that an irreducible Tits index
(\Phi,\Gamma,J)
consists of a reduced irreducible crystallographic root system
\widetilde{\Phi}
, a subgroup
\Gamma
of its group of outer automorphisms (i.e. the automorphism group of its Dynkin diagram), and a
\Gamma
-invariant subset
J
of vertices of the Dynkin diagram satisfying an additional condition (namely, that it may be constructed by a reductive group scheme over a field using its minimal parabolic subgroup). Then
\Phi
is the image of
\widetilde{\Phi}
in the quotient-space of the ambient vector space by
\Gamma
and the span of basic roots not in

.

Root subgroups and elementary subgroup

For every isotropic pre-pinning there exist root subgroup-subschemes
G_{\alpha}\leqslant G
uniquely defined by certain list of properties. If
\alpha\in\Phi
is not ultrashort, then
G_{\alpha}(S)
is an abelian group with a natural structure of
S
-module. In case where
\alpha
is ultrashort, we have
G_{2\alpha}(S)\leqslant G_{\alpha}(S)
and there is a natural action
(-)\cdot(=)
of the multiplicative monoid
S^{\bullet}
of the ring
S
on
G_{\alpha}(S)
by group endomorphisms from the right, which induces an
S
-module structure on the abelian quotient group
G_{\alpha}(S)/G_{2\alpha}(S)
. For the details see
[
56
]
or
[
57
]
.

The subgroup
E(S)\leqslant G(S)
generated by all the
G_{\alpha}(S)
is called the elementary subgroup. More generally, the elementary subgroup is defined in
[
42
]
for isotropic reductive groups as a subgroup generated by subgroups of points of two opposite unipotent radicals.

Weyl elements and isotropic pinnings

Let
G
be an absolutely simple adjoint group scheme with a common root datum of the geometric fibers over the ring
R
; and let
(T,\Phi)
be an isotropic pre-pinning on
G
. We adopt the following definitions from
[
57
]
.

For a root
\alpha\in\Phi
, an element
w_{\alpha}\in G_{\alpha}(R)G_{-\alpha}(R)G_{\alpha}(R)
is called a Weyl element if
\vphantom{,}{}^{w_{\alpha}}G_{\beta}(S)=G_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}(S)
for every root
\beta\in\Phi
and any
R
-algebra

.

An isotropic pre-pinning
(T,\Phi)
is called an isotropic pinning if Weyl elements

exist for every
\alpha\in\Phi
.

Automorphisms of an affine line as an algebraic ring

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then the forgetful functor from the category of
R
-algebras to the category of rings has no non-trivial automorphisms.

Proof.

Let
\eta
be an automorphism of the forgetful functor. Set

and

. So for any

we have
\eta_{A}(a)=f(a)

By construction we have

[formula]

f(g(x))=g(f(x))=x

Since all the

must be automorphisms of rings we have

[formula]

f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)

and

[formula]

f(xy)=f(x)f(y)\text{.}

Similarly, we have

[formula]

g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)\text{.}

Comparing free coefficients in

Clopen embeddings of group schemes

The following Lemma is a variation of
[
61
, Theorem 1.6.1]

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be set of prime numbers. Let
G
and H be affine group schemes of finite type over
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]
with
G
flat, and let
\varphi\colon G\to H
be a group-scheme homomorphism. Suppose that for all algebraically closed fields
K
with
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits K\notin\mathcal{S}
we have

(1)

\dim(G_{K})\geqslant\dim_{K}\Lie(H_{K})
;

(2)

the maps
G(K)\to H(K)
and
G(K[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2}))\to H(K[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2}))
given by
\varphi
are injective.

Then
\varphi
is simultaneously a closed embedding and an open embedding.

Proof.

Let us prove that for any algebraically closed field
K
with
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits L\notin\mathcal{S}
the homomorphism
\varphi_{K}\colon G_{K}\to H_{K}
is a clopen embedding.

First (2) implies that
\dim(G_{K})\leqslant\dim(H_{K})
. Together with (1), this implies that
G
, and
H
, are both smooth and have the same dimension. Now by
[
16
, Ch II, §5, Item 5.5 (a)]

\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\varphi_{K}
is etale. Together with the fact that by (2)
(\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\varphi_{K})(K)
is trivial this implies that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\varphi_{K}
is trivial; hence,
\varphi_{K}
is a monomorphism. Therefore,
\varphi_{K}
is a closed embedding by
[
16
, Ch II, §5, Item 5.1 (b)]
; and an open embedding by
[
16
, Ch II, §5, Item 5.5 (b)]
.

Further, if
k
is any field with
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k\notin\mathcal{S}
, then the descent from
K=\overline{k}
to
k
shows that
\varphi_{k}
is a clopen embedding.

Now consider the map
\varphi^{*}\colon\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]\to\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][G]
on the rings of regular functions on the schemes
H
and
G
induced by
\varphi
. Let
M\unlhd\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
be the kernel of
\varphi^{*}
. The fact that
\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a clopen embedding implies that
\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}\colon\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]\otimes\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][G]\otimes\mathbb{Q}
is a surjection with a kernel generated by an idempotent, which implies that
M/M^{2}
is a torsion group. As
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]
-algebra, the ideal
M
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
-module, and hence there is a bound on the orders of elements in
M/M^{2}
.

We now prove that
M=M^{2}
. Suppose that for some prime
p\notin\mathcal{S}
there is a nontrivial
p
-primary component of
M/M^{2}
. Then we can choose an element of largest
p
-power order, and it will give a nontrivial class in
M/(M^{2}+pM)
. The fact that
\varphi_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}^{*}
is a clopen embedding implies that
x=x^{\prime}+px^{\prime\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in M
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
. This implies that
px^{\prime\prime}\in M
. By assumption
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][G]
is a flat
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]
-algebra; hence it is torsion free. Therefore,
\varphi^{*}(px^{\prime\prime})=0
implies
\varphi^{*}(x^{\prime\prime})=0
; i.e. we have
x^{\prime\prime}\in M
; hence,
x\in M^{2}+pM
. This is a contradiction that shows that
M=M^{2}
.

Since
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]
-algebra, it follows by Nakayama’s lemma that
M
is generated by some idempotent
e\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
.

We now show that
\varphi^{*}
is surjective. Suppose we could find some
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][G]
not in the image. We know that
\varphi^{*}
becomes a surjection over
\mathbb{Q}
, so by appropriate multiplication we can assume that for some prime
p\notin\mathcal{S}
we have
px=\varphi^{*}(y)=\varphi^{*}((1-e)y)
. But
px
becomes zero when we tensor with
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Hence, we have
(1-e)y=ey^{\prime}+py^{\prime\prime}
for some
y^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][H]
. Multiplying by
(1-e)
we get
(1-e)y=p(1-e)y^{\prime\prime}
. Hence we have
p(x-\varphi^{*}((1-e)y^{\prime\prime}))=px-\varphi^{*}((1-e)y)=0
; and since
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}][G]
is torsion-free, it follows that
x=\varphi^{*}((1-e)y^{\prime\prime})
. This is a contradiction that shows that
\varphi^{*}
is surjective.

Since
\varphi^{*}
is surjective with a kernel generated by an idempotent, it follows that
\varphi
is a clopen embedding.
∎

Presentation of classical Lie algebras

Let
\widetilde{\Phi}
be a reduced irreducible crystallographic root system. Let
\mathfrak{g}({\mathbb{Z}})
be an integer span of the Chevalley basic in the simple Lie algebra of type
\widetilde{\Phi}
; and for any ring

\mathfrak{g}(R)=g(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R

can be viewed as a tangent Lie algebra to the simply connected Chevalley–Demazure scheme over

.

Let
u\colon\widetilde{\Phi}\cup\{0\}\to\Phi\cup\{0\}
be a map that comes from one of the Tits indexes. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R)
for

be the sum of root subspaces for all roots from

.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits\Phi\geqslant 2
. Let

be a field with

. If
\widetilde{\Phi}=G_{2}

can be defined as a Lie algebra over

Generators: elements from

for all
\alpha\in\Phi
.

Relations:

(R1) all the linear relations from spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
;

(R2) if

, then
[x,y]=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
,

;

(R3) if
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi
with

are either independent or equal, then
[x,y]=z
for all

,

is the actual Lie bracket of

and

in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{g}^{\dagger}
be the Lie algebra defined by generators and relations as above. We will write
[x,y]
for the Lie bracket in
\mathfrak{g}
and
[x,y]_{{\dagger}}
for the Lie bracket in
\mathfrak{g}^{\dagger}

\tau\colon\mathfrak{g}^{\dagger}\to\mathfrak{g}
be the homomorphism that is identity on each

Sublemma A
For any
\gamma\in\Phi
there are independent roots
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi
with
\alpha+\beta=\gamma
such that
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]=\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
(which by R3 implies that
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
).

Proof of Sublemma A.
In most of the cases we can find independent roots
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi
with
\alpha+\beta=\gamma
such that
\beta
is not ultrashort and
\gamma=s_{\beta}(\alpha)
; hence, it follows from Lemma

that
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]=\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
.

The only case, where we can not do as above, is the case where

is a long root and
\Phi
has type
C
or

. In this case we can take
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi
to be the short roots with
\alpha+\beta=\gamma
. It is easy to verify case by case, that we have
2\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subseteq[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]
; and since we have
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k\neq 2
, it follows that
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]=\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
.

Sublemma A is proved.

Sublemma B
If
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi
are such that
\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
, then
[x,y]_{{\dagger}}=[x,y]
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
,

.

Proof of Sublemma B.
If
\alpha
and
\beta
are either independent or equal, then this is just R3. Thus, we only have to consider the case, where
\Phi
has type
BC
and
\beta=-2\alpha
.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}
be an ultrashort root and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
be a short root such that
\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime\prime}=\alpha
. It follows from Lemma

that
x=[x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}]=[x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}]_{{\dagger}}
for some
x^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}
. Hence, we have

[x,y]_{{\dagger}}=[[x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}]_{{\dagger}},y]_{{\dagger}}=[[x^{\prime},y]_{{\dagger}},x^{\prime\prime}]_{{\dagger}}+[x^{\prime},[x^{\prime\prime},y]_{{\dagger}}]_{{\dagger}}=[x^{\prime},[x^{\prime\prime},y]_{{\dagger}}]_{{\dagger}}=[x^{\prime},[x^{\prime\prime},y]]_{{\dagger}}=\\
=[x^{\prime},[x^{\prime\prime},y]]=[[x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}],y]+[x^{\prime\prime},[x^{\prime},y]]=[x,y]\text{.}

Sublemma B is proved.

Now let
\mathfrak{h}^{{\dagger}}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}^{{\dagger}}
be the intersection of normalizers of
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
in
\mathfrak{g}
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
.

Sublemma C
For any
\alpha\in\Phi
we have
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}\leqslant\mathfrak{h}^{{\dagger}}
.

Proof of Sublemma C.
We must prove that
[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}

\alpha
and

[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}}=[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}+[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},[\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}}]_{{\dagger}}\text{.}

Let us prove that
[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}

\alpha+\beta\notin\Phi
, then we have
[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}=0

\alpha+\beta\notin\Phi

[[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}\leqslant[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\text{.}

Similarly, we have

Now, if

\beta^{\prime}

\beta^{\prime\prime}

\mathfrak{g}_{\beta^{\prime\prime}}

Sublemma C is proved.

Sublemma D
The algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{{\dagger}}
is the sum of subspaces
\mathfrak{h}^{{\dagger}}
and all the
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

Proof of Sublemma D.
By definition

and all the

Sublemma E

\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau

\mathfrak{g}^{\dagger}

Proof of Sublemma E.

\mathfrak{h}\leqslant\mathfrak{g}

. It is easy to see that
\tau(\mathfrak{h}^{{\dagger}})\leqslant\mathfrak{h}
; and by definition we have
\tau(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.

Now it follows from Sublemma D that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau\leqslant\mathfrak{h}^{{\dagger}}
. Hence, we have
[\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau,\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}]\leqslant\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
. Hence, we have

[\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau,\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}]_{{\dagger}}=\tau([\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau,\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}]_{{\dagger}})=[\tau(\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau),\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}]=0\text{.}

Therefore,
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau
is contained in the center of
\mathfrak{g}^{\dagger}
.

Sublemma E is proved.

Now we finish the proof of the lemma. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k\neq 2
, it follows that
\mathfrak{g}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. Hence there is a universal central extension
\pi\colon g^{*}\to g
. It is easy to see that
\tau
is surjective; hence, by Sublemma E we have
\pi=\tau\circ\pi^{\dagger}
for a unique homomorphism
\pi^{\dagger}\colon g^{*}\to g^{\dagger}
. It follows from Sublemma A that
\mathfrak{g}^{{\dagger}}=[\mathfrak{g}^{{\dagger}},\mathfrak{g}^{{\dagger}}]_{{\dagger}}
; hence,
\pi^{\dagger}
is surjective. Now it only remains to prove that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi^{\dagger}
.

Since we have
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k\neq 2
and, in case
\widetilde{\Phi}=G_{2}
, we also have
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k\neq 3
, it follows from
[
28
, Corollary 3.14]
that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi=0
unless
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits k=3
and
\widetilde{\Phi}=A_{2}
. So this is the only case we have to consider. Since we are assuming
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits\Phi\geqslant 2
, it follows that
u
must be a bijection.

In this last remaining case, in the notation of
[
28
]
we have
(\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi)_{0}=0
, which means that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi
is generated by elements
Z_{\gamma}^{*}
, where
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
is a degenerate sum for
\alpha
,
\beta\in\Phi=\widetilde{\Phi}
. By relations from
[
28
, Theorem 3.5]
, we have
Z_{\gamma}^{*}=\pm[X_{\alpha},X_{\beta}]
, where
\pi(X_{\alpha})\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\pi(X_{\beta})\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
.

Since
\alpha+\beta\notin\Phi
, applying R2 and Sublemma E we get

\pi^{\dagger}(Z_{\gamma}^{*})\in[\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau+\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\tau+\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]_{{\dagger}}=0\text{.}

Therefore, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits\pi^{\dagger}
.

’’Diagonal’’ automorphisms of classical Lie algebras

In the notation of the previous subsection consider the following functor from the category of rings to the category of groups.

\Diag(R)=\{(\sigma_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi}\colon\sigma_{\alpha}\in\Aut_{R}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R))\text{ and }\forall\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\;\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R)\;y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}(R)\\
(\alpha+\beta\in\Phi\;\wedge\;\alpha\notin\{-2\beta,-1/2\beta\})\Rightarrow\sigma_{\alpha+\beta}([x,y])=[\sigma_{\alpha}(x),\sigma_{\beta}(y)]\}\text{,}

where
\Aut_{R}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R))
is a group of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R)
as an
R
-module.

It is easy to see that
\Diag(-)
is an affine group scheme of finite type over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)
be the adjoint Chevalley–Demazure group scheme of type
\widetilde{\Phi}
; and let
L(-)\leqslant G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)
be the product of the subsystem subgroup for the subsystem
u^{-1}(0)
with the torus. It is known that
L(-)
is a smooth closed subgroup-subscheme of
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)
.

The adjoint action of
L(R)
preserves the subspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R)
; hence it defines a group-scheme homomorphism
\ad\colon L(-)\to\Diag(-)
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits\Phi\geqslant 2
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{2,3\}
if
\widetilde{\Phi}=G_{2}
; and
\mathcal{S}=\{2\}
otherwise. Then the homomorphism
\ad\colon L_{\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]}(-)\to\Diag_{\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]}(-)
is simultaneously a closed embedding and an open embedding.

Proof.

We will apply Lemma
4.7
; let us verify that all its assumptions hold. Firstly, the scheme
L(-)
is a semidirect product of Chevalley–Demazure scheme with a torus; hence, it is smooth; hence, it is flat. Secondly, for any
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}]
-algebra
R
the action of
G(\widetilde{\Phi},R)
on
\mathfrak{g}(R)
is faithful, and
\mathfrak{g}(R)
is generated as a Lie algebra by the root subspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(R)
for
\alpha\in\Phi
; hence, the map
\ad_{R}\colon L(R)\to\Diag(R)
is injective, which covers the assumption (2).

Now it remains to prove the assumption (1). Let
K
be an algebraically closed field with
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits K\notin\mathcal{S}
. It is easy to see that
\dim(L_{K})=\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits\widetilde{\Phi}+|u^{-1}(0)\cap\widetilde{\Phi}|
. Let us now calculate
\dim_{K}\Lie(\Diag_{K})
.

Let us denote
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(K)
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}(K)
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
. From the definition of the scheme
\Diag
we obtain

\Lie(\Diag_{K})=\{(\delta_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi}\colon\delta_{\alpha}\in\End_{K}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\text{ and }\forall\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\;\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\;y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\\
(\alpha+\beta\in\Phi\;\wedge\;\alpha\notin\{-2\beta,-1/2\beta\})\Rightarrow\delta_{\alpha+\beta}([x,y])=[\delta_{\alpha}(x),y]+[x,\delta_{\beta}(y)]\}\text{,}

where
\End_{K}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})
is the space of endomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
as a vector space.

It follows from Lemma
4.8
that any such collection
(\delta_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi}
uniquely define a derivation
\delta\in\Der_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
of the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Sublemma
Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}
be the tangent Lie algebra of the scheme
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{K}
. Then the action of
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{K}
on
\mathfrak{g}
induce an isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Der_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof of Sublemma.
The scheme
G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{K}
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
faithfully; hence the corresponding map
\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}\to\Der_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
is injective. Thus it remains to prove that
\dim_{K}\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}=\dim_{K}\Der_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Since the simply connected and the adjoint Chevalley–Demazure schemes have the same dimension and are both smooth, it follows that
\dim_{K}\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}=\dim_{K}\mathfrak{g}
. Further, clearly, we have
\dim_{K}\Der_{K}(\mathfrak{g})=\dim_{K}\mathfrak{g}-\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})+\dim_{K}H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathbb{Z}(\mathfrak{g})
is the center of
\mathfrak{g}
and
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
is the space of outer derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
. Thus it remains to prove that
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})=\dim_{K}H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
.

The algebra
\mathbb{Z}(\mathfrak{g})
is the tangent algebra to the scheme-center of the simply connected Chevalley–Demazure scheme. Therefore, when this center is the scheme
\mu_{n}
of
n
-th roots of unity, we have
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})=1
if
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits K\mid n
and
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
otherwise. In the only remaining case, where
\Phi=D_{2l}
and the scheme-center is
\mu_{2}\times\mu_{2}
we have
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})=2
if
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits K=2
(which is not the case by assumption) and
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
otherwise.

The space
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
is calculated in
[
26
, Proposition 4.2]
under the same assumptions on
\mathop{\mathrm{char}}\nolimits K
that we have; and its dimension does indeed coincide with
\dim_{K}Z(\mathfrak{g})
.

Sublemma is proved.

Sublemma together with what we show previously imply that
\Lie(\Diag_{K})
is isomorphic to the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\ad}
that consists of element whose adjoint action preserves the subspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. It is easy to see that this is exactly the subspace of elements whose components in root subalgebras with respect to grading by
\Phi
vanish. Therefore, we have
\dim_{K}\Lie(\Diag_{K})=\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits\widetilde{\Phi}+|u^{-1}(0)\cap\widetilde{\Phi}|
.

∎

\vphantom{,}{}^{\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}}(G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S))\subseteq G_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{(2)}(S)G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)

and

(G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S))^{\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}}\subseteq G_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{(2)}(S)G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)\text{.}

Proof of Sublemma E.

We write the proof for the first statement. The second statement is similar.

We must prove that
\rho_{I}(\vphantom{,}^{\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}}(G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)))\subseteq G_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{(2)}(S/I)
. We can do it by showing that elements from
\rho_{I}(\vphantom{,}^{\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}}(G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)))
satisfy the formulas form Lemma
4.5
taking
X_{\gamma}=\rho_{I}(\theta(G_{\gamma}^{(1)}))
for any
\gamma\in\Phi
.

Let us check the first formula suppose that
\gamma\in\Phi
is such that
s_{\alpha}(\beta)+\gamma\notin\Phi\cup\{0\}
, let
g\in G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)
, and
x_{\gamma}\in G_{\gamma}^{(1)}
. Then we have

[\rho_{I}(\vphantom{,}^{\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}}g),\rho_{I}(\theta(x_{\gamma}))]=\vphantom{,}^{\rho_{I}(\widetilde{w}_{\alpha})}[\rho_{I}(g),\rho_{I}(\theta(x_{\gamma}^{w_{\alpha}^{(1)}}))]\leqslant\vphantom{,}^{\rho_{I}(\widetilde{w}_{\alpha})}[G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S/I),G_{s_{\alpha}(\gamma)}^{(2)}(S/I)]=e\text{.}

The other two formulas can be verified similarly.

Sublemma E is proved.

Sublemma F.
Let

be a long root, and

\theta(x)=y_{x}\varepsilon_{x,\alpha}\varepsilon_{x,0}
(here for

for all non-ultrashort roots
\beta\in\Phi

Proof of Sublemma F.
It is easy to check case by case that if
\alpha
is a long root, then for any
\beta\in\Phi
that is not parity-equivalent to
\alpha
there exist a non-ultrashort root
\gamma\in\Phi
such that

is even and

is odd. Therefore, Sublemma A implies that

\theta(x)=y_{x}\varepsilon_{x,0}\prod_{\beta\sim\alpha}\varepsilon_{x,\beta}\text{,}

where

\varepsilon_{x,\beta}\in G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
(here for
\beta\in 2\Phi

\varepsilon_{x,\beta}=\varepsilon_{x,\beta/2}

\varepsilon_{x,\beta}\in G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)

Note that by Sublemma C all the roots in the parity-equivalence class of
\alpha
are either
\pm\alpha
or orthogonal to
\alpha
.

The next step is to show that
\varepsilon_{x,-\alpha}=e
. Choose
\beta\in\Phi
as follows: if
\alpha
lies in a subsystem of type
A_{2}
, then let
\beta
be a long root with
\angle(\alpha,\beta)=\pi/3

\beta
be a short root with
\angle(\alpha,\beta)=\pi/4

we have
[y_{x}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}]=[y_{x^{\prime}}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}]
. Clearly, the component in
G_{\beta-\alpha}^{(2)}(S)
at the left hand side vanishes; and at the right hand side such component comes from
[y_{x^{\prime}}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x,-\alpha}^{-1}]
. By umbrella assumptions
\rho_{I}(\theta(G^{(1)}_{\beta}))
generate
G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S/I)
as an
S/I
-module; hence, all possible
y_{x^{\prime}}
generate
G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S)
as an
S
-module; hence
[\varepsilon_{x,-\alpha},G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S)]
is either trivial or lies in
G^{(2)}_{2\beta-\alpha}(S)
if
\beta
is short. The commutator (taken modulo
G^{(2)}_{2\beta-\alpha}(S)
if
\beta
is short) is a non-degenerate pairing of

and
G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S)
(if the root
\beta

\varepsilon_{x,-\alpha}=e
.

The next step is to prove what is claimed about
\varepsilon_{x,0}
. First let us consider the case, where
\Phi
is not of type
C
or
BC
. In this case there is a non-ultrashort root
\gamma\in\Phi
such that

is odd. Therefore, we have

Now using what we prove before, we can compare the components in
L^{(2)}(S)
on the left hand side and on the right hand side of this equality and conclude that
\varepsilon_{x,0}=\varepsilon_{x,0}^{-1}
; hence, since

\varepsilon_{x,0}=e
.

Now suppose that
\Phi
is of type
C
or
BC
. We must prove that
\varepsilon_{x,0}
commutes with
G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)
for all non-ultrashort roots
\beta\in\Phi
such that
\beta\perp\alpha
. Since all possible
y_{x^{\prime}}
, where
x^{\prime}\in G^{(1)}_{\beta}
, generate
G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S)
as an
S
-module, it is enough to show that
\varepsilon_{x,0}
commutes with all such

.

First consider the case, where
\beta
is short. By Sublemma D we have
[y_{x}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}]=[y_{x^{\prime}}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}]

commutes with
\varepsilon_{x,0}

Now suppose that
\beta

. Let us compare the components in
G_{\beta}^{(2)}(S)
at the right and left hand sides of this equality. At the left hand side such component is trivial, since

. Therefore, we have

commutes with
\varepsilon_{x,0}^{-1}

commutes with
\varepsilon_{x,0}

The final step is to show that

. At the right hand side contribution to such component may come only from

, which is trivial as we prove previously, and from

. Now let us prove that
(\varepsilon_{x,\beta}^{-1})^{\widetilde{w}_{\beta}}\in G_{-\beta}^{(2)}(S)
. Indeed, since
\beta
is neither ultrashort nor a short root when
\Phi
is of type
C
or
BC
, by Lemma
4.5
it is enough to show that
(\varepsilon_{x,\beta}^{-1})^{\widetilde{w}_{\beta}}
commutes with
G_{\gamma}^{(2)}(S)
for all
\gamma
such that

. It is the same that to show that
\varepsilon_{x,\beta}^{-1}
commutes with
\vphantom{,}{}^{\widetilde{w}_{\beta}}(G_{\gamma}^{(2)}(S))

\vphantom{,}{}^{\widetilde{w}_{\beta}}(G_{\gamma}^{(2)}(S))\subseteq G_{s_{\beta}(\gamma)}^{(2)}(S)G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)

in

; hence,

.

Sublemma H.
Suppose that
\Phi
is of type
C
or
BC
, and let

Proof of Sublemma H.

3.1

Note that
\vphantom{,}{}^{\widetilde{w}_{-\beta}}(G^{(2)}_{2\beta-\alpha}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I))\subseteq G^{(2)}_{-\alpha}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)

\vphantom{,}{}^{\widetilde{w}_{-\beta}}(G^{(2)}_{2\beta-\alpha}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I))

for all

Subsublemma

. Suppose that either

commutes with
b_{-\beta}
, or

x

Hence, since
G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
is abelian the whole third factor is trivial. Now suppose that
\varepsilon_{b_{-\beta},\beta-\alpha}=e

\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}\varepsilon_{b_{-\beta}}^{-1}[y_{b_{-\beta}}^{-1},\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}]
in

. By assumption we have
\varepsilon_{b_{-\beta},\beta-\alpha}=e
; by Sublemma G we have
\varepsilon_{x,\beta-\alpha}=e

Proposition 7.2
.

Under the umbrella assumptions of this Section, there exist an element
g\in G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
such that for the homomorphism
\theta^{\prime}(x)=\theta(x)^{g}
we have
\theta^{\prime}(h^{(1)}_{\alpha})=h^{(2)}_{\alpha}
for all non-ultrashort
\alpha\in\Phi
; and
\theta^{\prime}(G^{(1)}_{\alpha})\subseteq G^{(2)}_{\alpha}(S)
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
.

Proof.

By assumption we have
\theta(h_{\alpha}^{(1)})=h_{\alpha}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha}
, where
\varepsilon_{\alpha}\in G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
. Further we can decompose the element
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
as
\varepsilon_{\alpha}=\prod_{\gamma\in\Phi\sqcup\{0\}\setminus 2\Phi}\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}
, where
\varepsilon_{\alpha,0}\in L^{(2)}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
and
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}\in G^{(2)}_{\gamma}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
for all
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi
. Let us prove that there exist an element
g^{\prime}\in G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
such that after we replace the homomorphism
\theta(-)
with the homomorphism
\theta(-)^{g^{\prime}}
we will have
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}=e
for all non-ultrashort
\alpha\in\Phi
and all non-ultrashort
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi
, and
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}\in G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
for all non-ultrashort
\alpha\in\Phi
and all ultrashort
\gamma\in\Phi
.

We will look for
g^{\prime}
in a form
g^{\prime}=\prod_{\beta\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi}g^{\prime}_{\beta}
, where
g^{\prime}_{\beta}\in G^{(2)}_{\beta}(S)\cap G(\widetilde{\Phi},S,I)
. The requirement on
g^{\prime}
is that for every
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi
and for every non-ultrashort
\gamma\in\Phi
the component of
h_{\alpha}^{(2)}(h_{\alpha}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha})^{g^{\prime}}
in
G^{(2)}_{\gamma}(S)
must be either trivial, or belong to
G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
. When we rewrite this in terms of
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}
and
g^{\prime}_{\beta}
we get two type of equation. The first type is that whenever
\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle
is even we must have
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}=e
. The second type is that whenever
\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle
is odd we must have
(g^{\prime}_{\gamma})^{2}=\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}^{-1}
or
(g^{\prime}_{\gamma})^{2}\in\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}^{-1}G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
if
\gamma
is ultrashort.

Let us show that the equations of the first type hold true automatically. Indeed, since
(h_{\alpha}^{(1)})^{2}=e
, it follows that
(h_{\alpha}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha})^{2}=e
. Looking at the component in
G^{(2)}_{\gamma}(S)
, when
\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle
is even, we get
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}^{2}=e
; and since
2\in S^{*}
, it follows that
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}=e
.

For the equations of the second type we must prove that there exists
g^{\prime}
that satisfies all of them simultaneously. Since
2\in S^{*}
we have no problem with extracting square roots of
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}^{-1}
(modulo
G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
if
\gamma
is ultrashort). Thus we only have to prove that for any non-ultrashort
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}\in\Phi
and any
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi
such that
\langle\gamma,\alpha_{1}\rangle
and
\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle
are odd we have
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}
(or
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}\in\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
), so that the equations do not contradict each other.

Since we have
h_{\alpha_{1}}^{(1)}h_{\alpha_{2}}^{(1)}=h_{\alpha_{2}}^{(1)}h_{\alpha_{1}}^{(1)}
, it follows that
h_{\alpha_{1}}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}h_{\alpha_{2}}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}=h_{\alpha_{2}}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}h_{\alpha_{1}}^{(2)}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}
, i.e
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{h_{\alpha_{2}}^{(2)}}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{h_{\alpha_{1}}^{(2)}}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}
. Comparing component in
G^{(2)}_{\gamma}(S)
, we obtain
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}^{2}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}^{2}
(or
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}^{2}\in\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}^{2}G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
); and since

, it follows that
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}
(or
\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1},\gamma}\in\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2},\gamma}G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
) as required.

Therefore, in the view of the above, we may assume that
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}=e
for all non-ultrashort
\alpha\in\Phi
and all non-ultrashort
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus 2\Phi
, and
\varepsilon_{\alpha,\gamma}\in G^{(2)}_{2\gamma}(S)
for all non-ultrashort
\alpha\in\Phi
and all ultrashort
\gamma\in\Phi
. Tis implies that
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
commutes with
h_{\alpha}^{(2)}
. Hence, the equation

implies that
\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{2}=e
; hence,
\varepsilon_{\alpha}=e
; i.e
\theta(h^{(1)}_{\alpha})=h^{(2)}_{\alpha}

7.1
.

8.
The scheme of adjustments

In this section we are assuming the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
; and additionally we are assuming that
\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}=\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}=\widetilde{\Phi}
.

Consider the following functor from the category of
R_{2}
-algebras into the category of sets:

\Pin(S)=\{f\colon(G_{2})_{S}\xrightarrow{\sim}G(\widetilde{\Phi},-)_{S}\mid f\text{ is an isomorphism of group schemes over }S\}\text{,}

where

to

Lemma 8.1
.

The functor
\Pin(-)
is smooth affine scheme of finite type over

.

Proof.

By
[
17
, Exp. XXIV, Section 7]
there are affine schemes that classify group-scheme homomorphisms from

to

and from

Now, clearly,

Now let

. Let

for

), and

be the elements from Lemma

.

For every

such that
X_{\alpha}
generate
G_{\alpha}^{(1)}(R_{1})
or
G^{(1)}_{\alpha}(R_{1})/G^{(1)}_{2\alpha}(R_{1})
