Thyroid hormone measurement items in patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis on levothyroxine monotherapy: management based on thyroid tissue volume
Authors: Mitsuru Ito, Yoshiki Furumura, Itaru Monno, Hanna Deguchi-Horiuchi, Chisako Imamura, Sawako Takahashi, Hiroyuki Yamaoka, Mako Hisakado, Kazuyoshi Kohsaka, Eijun Nishihara, Shuji Fukata, Mitsushige Nishikawa, Akira Miyauchi, Takashi Akamizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe have demonstrated that mild suppression of thyrotropin (TSH) using levothyroxine (LT4) is necessary to achieve normal free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels and a metabolic euthyroid state in athyreotic patients. Consequently, management strategies based on thyroid volume (TV) have been implemented in patients receiving LT4 at Kuma Hospital. While Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) can vary from atrophic to diffusely enlarged thyroid volumes.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we aimed to evaluate the composition of thyroid hormone measurement items (serum FT4, FT3, and FT4+FT3) in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy for HT, stratifying them by TV, as assessed using ultrasonography.ResultsWe identified 7,576 consecutive patients from their medical records. These patients were classified into five groups based on TV: atrophic thyroid (TV ≤ 10 mL); normal thyroid (TV 10–20 mL); mildly enlarged thyroid (TV 20–50 mL); moderately enlarged thyroid (TV 50–80 mL); and large thyroid (TV > 80 mL). In all patient groups, FT4 measurements constituted the majority or nearly half of all assessments. However, the proportion of FT3 measurements increased notably in the atrophic thyroid group (34%) and in the large goiter group (29%). Patients receiving LT4 were evaluated based on their TSH levels. In the reduced TSH group, FT3 accounted for nearly half of all measurements (47%). Conversely, in the normal TSH group, FT4 accounted for the majority of all measurements (76%).ConclusionThis study revealed that FT3, rather than FT4, was the predominant thyroid hormone measured in a significant number of patients with HT on LT4 monotherapy at our hospital. Furthermore, our study highlights a management approach whereby TSH levels were intentionally suppressed in some patients.

Background

The thyroid gland secretes two primary hormones: thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), the latter being the biologically active hormone. In healthy individuals, the thyroid gland secretes 100% T4 and approximately 20% T3. Consequently, approximately 80% of T3 is derived from the conversion of T4 to T3 in extrathyroidal peripheral tissues [1]. Athyreotic patients may present with a relative T3 deficiency while receiving levothyroxine (LT4) monotherapy. Previous studies [2–5] have demonstrated that patients with normal serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels tend to exhibit slightly lower serum-free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels among athyreotic patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy and are receiving LT4 therapy. Patients with mild and severe suppression of serum TSH levels had normal and elevated FT3 levels, respectively. Serum-free thyroxine (FT4) levels increased significantly in all groups; however, the extent of the increase varied according to the TSH level. Notably, relatively low serum FT3 levels have been observed in patients with atrophic thyroid who received LT4 treatment after radioiodine therapy for Graves’ disease [6].

Werneck et al. reported that a combination of high serum T4 and low serum T3 levels during T4 monotherapy affected the action of thyroid hormones in rat models, as reflected in the brain, liver, and skeletal muscle, resulting in signs of hypothyroidism despite normal serum TSH levels [7]. Previous studies in humans have shown that metabolic marker levels [8] and symptoms [9, 10] related to thyroid function exhibited no significant changes following mild suppression of TSH. However, lower serum T3 levels, which differed in both objective and subjective measures, were observed in participants with normal serum TSH levels receiving LT4 monotherapy [11]. Therefore, the presence of biochemical markers of thyroid function observed in both animal and human studies suggests that mild suppression of TSH is necessary to achieve normal FT3 levels and a metabolically euthyroid state in patients who develop hypothyroidism following total thyroidectomy or in those with atrophic thyroid conditions after radioiodine treatment. These patients are less likely to experience symptoms of hyperthyroidism. Serum FT3 levels in patients with sufficient thyroid tissue, such as those who have undergone hemithyroidectomy [12] and those with Hashimoto thyroiditis (HT) [5, 13], are comparable to those in preoperative or control groups with normal TSH levels.

LT4 monotherapy is recognized as the standard of care for treating hypothyroidism [14]. LT4 replacement therapy is initiated to achieve target TSH levels (which may be low following TSH suppression therapy or normalized following replacement therapy), to normalize thyroid hormone levels, and to alleviate symptoms and signs of hypothyroid (including biological and physiological markers) [14]. The simultaneous measurement of TSH, FT4, and FT3 is not covered by insurance in several regions of Japan; consequently, only TSH and FT4 are typically measured. Consequently, the following management strategies have been implemented at our hospital since 2021 for patients receiving LT4 monotherapy: (1) the serum TSH and FT3 levels of athyreotic patients, patients with atrophic thyroid glands, and those requiring TSH-suppressive therapy (such as patients with thyroid carcinoma) were measured. The treatment goal is to maintain TSH levels (mild to complete suppression; with target values individualized for each patient) and FT3 levels within the reference range. (2) The serum TSH and FT4 levels of patients with sufficient thyroid tissue (including those with autoimmune thyroiditis) who are receiving replacement doses of LT4 are also measured. The treatment goal for this group is to maintain the TSH and FT4 levels within the reference range [15].

Recently, we investigated the current protocol for managing patients receiving LT4 therapy at our hospital by examining the composition of thyroid hormone measurement items (FT4 only, FT3 only, and FT4 + FT3) according to the various etiologies of hypothyroidism [15]. As a result, FT3 emerged as the major thyroid hormone measured in patients receiving LT4 treatment, with serum TSH levels suppressed in nearly half of the patients. This observation may be attributed to the management guidelines at our hospital, which is a specialized facility for thyroid disease, where approximately half of the patients were asymptomatic athyreotic individuals or had atrophic thyroid glands following total thyroidectomy or 131I treatment for hyperthyroidism.

HT can vary from atrophic to diffusely enlarged thyroid volumes [16, 17]. In the present study, we examined the status of thyroid volume-based management in LT4-treated patients by analyzing the composition of thyroid hormone measurement items in patients with HT across a range of thyroid volume sizes.

This study aimed to evaluate and classify the composition of thyroid hormone measurement items (serum free thyroxine [FT4], FT3, and FT4+FT3) in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis.

Methods

Patients

The medical records of 7,576 consecutive patients with primary hypothyroidism caused by HT who received either replacement therapy with LT4 or TSH suppressive therapy with LT4 for thyroid volume reduction and underwent thyroid function tests and thyroid volume measurements using ultrasonography between January 2023 and December 2023 at Kuma Hospital were analyzed retrospectively. The diagnosis of HT is based on the presence of positivity for anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and/or anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), as well as a heterogeneous hypoechoic pattern observed on thyroid ultrasound examination. The exclusion criteria included: (1) follicular adenoma, adenomatous goiter, or thyroid malignancies; (2) thyroid dysfunction, such as Graves’ disease, subacute thyroiditis, thyroid dyshormonogenesis, or autonomously functioning thyroid nodules; (3) patients with HT who had undergone total thyroidectomy for giant goiter or acute exacerbation; (4) administration of medications known to affect thyroid function or thyroid hormone metabolism, such as a steroids, estrogen, amiodarone, lithium, β-blockers, sucralfate, and iron or iodine-containing drugs; (5) chronic or serious conditions, such as cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, renal, or pancreatic diseases, diabetes or hyperparathyroidism; and (6) pregnant or lactating women. In addition, patients who did not achieve the target TSH levels were excluded from the analysis. All patients had undergone thyroid function tests while in a stable condition for at least 6 months after initiating LT4 treatment. Data from the last visit were evaluated if patients had undergone repeated thyroid function tests during the study period. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient for the collection and use of blood test data and medical records for research purposes. Each patient was informed of their right to participate, and their refusal to participate would not result in any disadvantages. To ensure confidentiality, personal information, including identifiable data such as names, addresses, and ages, was anonymized, deleted, and kept password-protected when removed from the hospital, and encrypted during transmission or storage on external media. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kuma Hospital (no. 20,200,709-1) and has opted out of the requirement for individual consent (https://www.kuma-h.or.jp/).

Guidelines for management of LT4monotherapy at Kuma Hospital

The following management methods are recommended at Kuma Hospital for patients receiving LT4 monotherapy because only two thyroid function test items are covered by insurance [15].

(1) The serum TSH and FT3 levels are measured in athyreotic patients (such as those who have undergone total thyroidectomy), patients with atrophic thyroid glands (defined as thyroid volume ≤ 10 mL on ultrasonography; such as those who have received radioiodine treatment for Graves’ disease or have atrophic thyroiditis), and patients requiring TSH suppressive therapy for thyroid carcinoma or large goiter (thyroid volume > 80 mL on ultrasonography; such as HT with a large goiter). The treatment goal was to maintain TSH levels (mild to complete suppression; with target levels individualized for each patient) and FT3 levels within the reference range. Serum TSH levels within the normal range are considered acceptable in older adults and in patients with cardiac diseases or osteoporosis. (2) The serum TSH and FT4 levels of patients with sufficient thyroid tissue (defined as thyroid volumes of 10–80 mL on ultrasonography, such as those post-hemithyroidectomy or with autoimmune thyroiditis) who were receiving replacement doses of LT4 were measured. The treatment goal for these patients was to maintain the TSH and FT4 levels within the reference range. Mildly elevated serum TSH levels are considered acceptable in elderly patients.

However, many cases that did not necessarily conform to the guidelines were included, as the choice of measurement items was left to the discretion of individual physicians. There were instances where all three items (TSH, FT4, and FT3) were measured at the physician’s discretion, despite this practice being unapproved by insurance; these data were tabulated for this study.

Laboratory serum tests

The thyroid profile of each patient was determined after stabilizing thyroid function for at least 6 months post-treatment. Blood samples were collected in the morning or afternoon after the administration of LT4. Serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 levels were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLusys; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). The reference ranges followed at our hospital are as follows: TSH, 0.5–5.0 μIU/mL; FT4, 0.9–1.7 ng/dL; and FT3, 2.3–4.0 pg/mL. The intra-assay coefficients of variation were ≤ 10%, ≤8%, and ≤10% for the TSH, FT4, and FT3 assays, respectively. Serum levels of TgAb and TPOAb were measured using the same electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLusys; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), with normal ranges of < 40 IU/mL for TgAb and <28 IU/mL for TPOAb. Thyroid volume was measured using ultrasonography. The maximum width (W), thickness (T), and length (L) of the thyroid gland lobes were recorded. Thyroid volume was calculated using the following equation: thyroid volume = 0.70 × (WrTrLr + WlTlLl), where a constant of 0.70 was used to compare the thyroid volume measured using this method to the actual thyroid volume in patients with Graves’ disease [18].

Data analysis

Data on thyroid hormone measurements were summarized and represented using a pie chart, as appropriate. Statistical tests for the proportions of thyroid hormone measurement items in each patient group were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The statistical significance of clinical characteristics by patient group, controlled by thyroid hormone items according to each thyroid volume, was analyzed using an unpaired t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, with Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons. Significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the StatFlex software (version 6.0; Artech Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan).

Discussion

In our study, approximately half of the patients with atrophic thyroid (thyroid volume ≤ 10 mL) underwent the measurement of only FT4 (52%); however, the proportion of those who underwent the measurement of only FT3 has been increasing (34%). In contrast, most patients with normal to mild enlarged thyroids (thyroid volume, 10–80 mL) underwent measurement of only FT4 (66–79%). Similarly, half of the patients with a thyroid volume > 80 mL also underwent measurement of only FT4 (49%); however, the proportion of those who underwent measurement of only FT3 has also increased (29%). This trend may be based on the aforementioned management guidelines. The higher proportion of patients with large goiters undergoing measurement of FT3 only compared to those with normal to mildly enlarged goiters, could be attributed to the management guidelines recommending the FT3 levels measurements in patients receiving TSH suppressive therapy. These were further supported by the finding that serum TSH levels in the two groups (thyroid volume ≤ 10 mL and thyroid volume > 80 mL) of patients controlled with only FT3 were lower than the normal range.

In this study, serum FT4 levels decreased as goiter size increased in the group determined by FT4, whereas serum FT3 levels increased as goiter size increased in the group measured by FT3. The FT3/FT4 ratio increased as goiter size increased in patients measured for both FT4 and FT3. These results align with our previously reported findings in patients with HT, both with [13] and without [19] LT4 treatment. Prior studies have revealed that elevations in thyroidal deiodinase activity, predominantly D2 activity, are responsible for the elevated serum FT3 levels and FT3/FT4 ratios observed in various large goitrous thyroid diseases, such as those involving thyroglobulin gene mutations [20], McCune-Albright syndrome [21], and T3-predominant Graves’ disease [22]. In addition, we previously reported elevated thyroidal deiodinase activity (particularly D2 activity) at the post-translational level in patients with HT and large goiters. This increase in enzyme activity may account for the relatively high serum FT3/FT4 ratio observed in these patients [23]. We also demonstrated a positive correlation between deiodinase and thyroid volume [23]. These findings suggest that intrathyroidal T3 production by deiodinase is a substantial factor influencing the relationship between serum T3 levels and thyroid volume in patients with HT.

However, there were no significant differences in total cholesterol levels among the patient groups. Serum cholesterol level is considered a reliable indicator of metabolic status related to thyroid function. We and other investigators have reported that in athyreotic patients on LT4, mild suppression of TSH levels may be beneficial for normalizing metabolic indices, including lipoproteins [7, 8]. Based on these findings, the management strategy for patients with an atrophic thyroid is to maintain low TSH levels; indeed, serum TSH levels were low in the patient group with thyroid volumes < 10 mL in the present study. The absence of significant differences in serum total cholesterol levels among all the patient groups suggests that our management strategy is clinically effective.

Nearly half of patients with suppressed TSH levels underwent the measurement of only FT3. In contrast, patients with normal TSH levels predominantly underwent the measurement of only FT4. These guidelines follow the aforementioned guidelines. In some cases, serum FT3 levels were measured instead of FT4 levels, in some patients with normal TSH levels, adhering to the guidelines. TSH levels are mildly elevated during LT4 treatment in the elderly and in patients with cardiac disease. The presence of normal serum TSH levels reflect effective management in older adults and in patients with cardiac disease or osteoporosis receiving LT4, even among those with atrophic thyroids. In addition, serum FT4 levels are frequently elevated in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy with normal TSH levels. This reflects a transient increase in serum FT4 levels following oral administration of LT4 [24–26]. Therefore, serum FT3 levels may have been measured instead of FT4 levels in some instances.

This study had certain limitations. First, it was a retrospective study. Second, the decision to measure only FT4, FT3, or both FT4 + FT3 levels was at the discretion of the physician. Despite Kuma Hospital guidelines, serum FT4 levels were measured in many cases with low TSH levels (TSH < 0.5 mIU/L) or with large goiters (TV＞80 mL). These findings suggests that many physicians remain unconvinced about the clinical utility of FT3. Given that measuring TSH and FT4 is the standard method, and because thyroid volume was not measured in all cases, it is possible that TSH and FT4 levels were measured in cases that warranted TSH and FT3 measurements according to our guidelines owing to atrophic thyroid or giant goiter. If the guidelines were more comprehensive, the proportion of TSH and FT3 measurements might have increased, whereas that of TSH and FT4 measurements might have decreased. Third, it was challenging to determine whether the purpose of administering LT4 was solely for supplementation, TSH suppression, or both in several cases. Patients with suppressed TSH included those whose serum TSH levels were mildly suppressed to normalize serum FT3 levels, those receiving TSH suppressive therapy for large goiters, and those in whom LT4 was accidentally overdosed to normalize TSH levels. Fourth, our hospital has traditionally measured FT3, considering the influence of binding proteins. However, a recent review confirmed that the preference for total T3 measurement over FT3 due to greater reliability. Total T3 measurement should be considered for such patients in the future [27]. Finally, metabolic indices, heart rate, and thyroid-related physical symptoms, aside from total cholesterol levels, were not examined because this was a retrospective study. Further well-designed studies that include these factors should be conducted.

Conclusions

The serum FT4 levels, along with TSH, are generally measured in patients undergoing LT4 monotherapy for hypothyroidism. This study revealed that FT3, not necessarily FT4, was the thyroid hormone measured in a certain number of patients receiving LT4 monotherapy at our hospital. Furthermore, the management approach in some patients involved suppression of serum TSH levels. These findings may be attributed to the management guidelines based on the thyroid tissue volume. FT3 is a biologically active hormone that provides a more accurate reflection of the thyroid function under these circumstances [7–9]. Moreover, measuring only FT3 may yield better agreement with reference ranges compared to measuring only FT4 [28]. This study elucidated the current status of thyroid function tests, advocating for an integrative approach that measures FT3 as a more reliable indicator of thyroid hormone status in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy, particularly considering the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3. On the other hand, this study lacks clinical outcomes considering thyroid status beyond cholesterol levels, and insufficient verification exists regarding whether normal serum FT3 levels can guarantee adequate intracellular T3 concentrations and uniform thyroid hormone action across different tissues, thereby serving as a more reliable and physiologically appropriate indicator of thyroid hormone status. Moreover, TSH suppression reflects mild thyrotoxicosis, and it is well known that even a slight excess of thyroid hormone can affect cardiovascular and bone. In addition, regarding bone, there are reports that TSH independently and directly influences bone metabolism [29]. Further research is needed to verify whether our management approach (such as adjusting based on TSH and FT3 levels relative to thyroid volume, or achieving mild TSH suppression in patients with atrophic thyroid) is appropriate.
