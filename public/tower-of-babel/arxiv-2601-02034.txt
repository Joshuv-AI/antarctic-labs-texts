[

With the present paper, the author aims to provide some information about the
t
-expansions of the most basic modular forms for
\Gamma
, namely
the Eisenstein series
E_{q^{k}-1}
and the coefficient forms
g_{k}
, both of weight
q^{k}-1
and type
0
.

Each modular form for
\Gamma
has a
t
-expansion

f(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})t^{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

where
a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})
is a weak modular form of rank
r^{\prime}\vcentcolon=r-1
, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}=(\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{r})

is the tail of
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r})
, a point of the Drinfeld space
\Omega^{r}
. We are able to give closed
formulas for the
a_{n}
if

(a)

f=E_{q^{k}-1}
and
n<3(q-1)q^{k+r-2}
, see Theorem
2.8
;

(b)

f=g_{k}
and
n<3(q-1)q^{r-1}
(Theorem
3.1
);

and, in a less satisfactory manner, if

(c)

f=h
, the
(q-1)
-th root of the discriminant form
\Delta=g_{r}
, and
n\leq(q-1)(q^{2r-1}-q^{r})
(Theorem
4.5
).

For (a) and (b) we have to assume
q>2
, otherwise (as
\sum_{c\in\mathds{F}_{q}}c\neq 0
for
q=2
, in contrast with
q>2
) some annoying
extra terms appear that lead to weaker error estimates.

In case (a), there are precisely
k+1
non-vanishing coefficients
a_{n}
below the given bound with
n=q^{k}-q^{i}
(
0\leq i\leq k
);
in case (b), there are precisely
2k
(if
k<r
) or
2r-1
(if
k=r
) non-vanishing
a_{n}
below the bound, all with
n\leq q^{k}-1
;
in case (c), we have no similar description.

For (a), we start with the known
A
-expansion involving

\sum_{a\in A\text{ monic}}G_{q^{k}-1}(t_{a})

of Theorem
1.11
. Due to the structure of the quantities
t_{a}
and of the Goss polynomials

G_{q^{k}-1}
, there is heavy cancellation in the partial sums

\sum_{a\text{ monic of degree }d}G_{q^{k}-1}(t_{a}),

which leads to strong estimates and thereby to Theorem
2.8
. For (b) we could try to use (a) and the well-known
relationship (
1.4.6
) between the
E_{q^{i}-1}
and the
g_{j}
. This, however, turns out hopelessly
complicated. Instead, we use the presentation of the generic Drinfeld modulue
\phi=\phi^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
of rank
r
(where

\boldsymbol{\omega}
varies through the Drinfeld symmetric space
\Omega^{r}
) as a Tate object over
\phi^{\prime}=\phi^{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}}
,
its counterpart of rank
r^{\prime}=r-1
. This leads to the more efficient recursion formula (
3.3.6
) for the
g_{k}
, which
is better adapted to our problem. Together with Proposition
3.4
(a technical estimate for the Tate object), it allows
to show the formula (
3.1.1
) for the non-vanishing coefficients, that is, Theorem
3.1
.

The situation is different for (c), i.e., for the modular form
h
of weight
(q^{r}-1)/(q-1)
and type 1. In contrast with the Eisenstein
series
E_{q^{k}-1}
and the coefficient forms
g_{k}
(whose

-expansions are sparse), but in accordance with the case of rank
2

(see
[
11

),

has
“many”
non-vanishing coefficients. We restrict to estimating the contributions of monic

of degree

\geq 2
in the product formula

h=(h^{\prime})^{q}t\prod_{a\in A\text{ monic}}S_{a}(t)^{q^{r}-1}.

This leads to the error estimate in Theorem
4.5
, where we replace the above product by

(h^{\prime})^{q}t\prod_{a\text{ monic of degree }1}S_{a}(t)^{-1}.

So our results are twofold: determining the first few terms of an expansion, and estimating the error, that is the length of the following string
of vanishing coefficients. While for (a) and (c) the first task is easy, as we already dispose of the expansions
(
1.11.1
) and (
1.12.2
) and we are reduced to work out the

3.1

is far from obvious, and could be guessed only after calculating a number of examples. Therefore Theorem
3.1

should be regarded as the principal result of the paper.

We assume throughout that the rank
r
is larger or equal to
3

plentitude of literature with partially much sharper results than those that could be obtained from the methods of the present paper. Again

1.1.

Throughout,
\mathds{F}=\mathds{F}_{q}
is the finite field with
q
elements, of characteristic
p
, and
r\geq 3

a natural number, the
rank
. Both
q
and
r
are fixed and usually omitted from notation.

A=\mathds{F}[T]
(resp.
K=\mathds{F}(T)
) is the polynomial ring (resp. the field of rational functions) in an indeterminate
T
.
Further,
K_{\infty}=\mathds{F}((T^{-1}))
is the completion of
K
at its infinite place, with completed algebraic closure
C_{\infty}
.
The absolute value
\lvert\mathbin{.}\rvert
on
C_{\infty}
is normalized such that
\lvert T\rvert=q
.

\Omega=\Omega^{r}=\mathds{P}^{r-1}(C_{\infty})\smallsetminus\bigcup H(C_{\infty})

(where
H
runs through the hyperplanes defined over
K_{\infty}
) with its structure as a rigid-analytic space denotes the Drinfeld symmetric
space. Homogeneous coordinates
(\omega_{1}:\omega_{2}:\ldots:\omega_{r})
are normalized such that
\omega_{r}=1
, hence

\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in C_{\infty}^{r}\mid\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r}~K_{\infty}\text{-linearly independent and }\omega_{r}=1\}.

1.2.

The group
\Gamma\vcentcolon=\GL(r,A)
acts on
\Omega
through fractional linear transformations

(1.2.1)

\gamma\boldsymbol{\omega}=\aut(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\omega})^{-1}\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{r}\end{pmatrix},

where the right hand side is the matrix product and

(1.2.2)

\aut(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\omega})\vcentcolon=\sum_{1\leq i\leq r}\gamma_{r,i}\omega_{i}.

A holomorphic function
f
on
\Omega
is a
weak modular form
(or
weakly modular
) of
weight

k\in\mathds{Z}
and

type

m\in\mathds{Z}/(q-1)
if

(1.2.3)

f(\gamma\boldsymbol{\omega})=\aut(\gamma,\boldsymbol{\omega})^{k}(\det\gamma)^{{-}m}f(\boldsymbol{\omega})

holds for
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega
.

1.3.

An
A
-lattice in
C_{\infty}
is a finitely generated (thus free of some rank
\rho
)
A
-submodule
\Lambda
of

C_{\infty}
which is discrete in the sense that
\Lambda
intersects with each ball in at most finitely many points. Hence

\Lambda=\bigoplus_{1\leq i\leq\rho}A\omega_{i}
with
\rho

K_{\infty}
-linearly independent elements
\omega_{i}
. With each lattice

\Lambda
, we associate

•

its
exponential function

e^{\Lambda}(z)=z\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(1-z/\lambda)

(the primed product
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}
(resp. sum
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}
) is over the non-zero elements of the index set).
It is everywhere convergent and has a power series expansion

(1.3.1)

e^{\Lambda}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha_{i}(\Lambda)z^{q^{i}};

•

the
Eisenstein series

E_{k}(\Lambda)=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{{-}k}

(
k>0
and
k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
); otherwise it vanishes);

•

the
Drinfeld module

\phi^{\Lambda}=C_{\infty}/\Lambda
of rank
\rho
.

The latter is an exotic
A
-module structure given by the
a
-th operator polynomial
\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}
for each
0\neq a\in A
, which
makes the following diagram with exact rows commutative:

(1.3.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e^{\Lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e^{\Lambda}}

\phi^{\Lambda}
is uniquely determined already by
\phi_{T}^{\Lambda}
, which has shape

(1.3.3)

\phi_{T}^{\Lambda}(X)=TX+g_{1}(\Lambda)X^{q}+\cdots+g_{\rho}(\Lambda)X^{q^{\rho}}.

The only restriction on
\phi_{T}^{\Lambda}
is that the
discriminant

\Delta(\phi^{\Lambda})\vcentcolon=\Delta(\Lambda)\vcentcolon=g_{\rho}(\Lambda)

be non-zero.

1.4.

Let
C_{\infty}\{\{\tau\}\}
be the ring of formal power series in a non-commutative variable
\tau
with commutation rule

(1.4.1)

\displaystyle\tau c=c^{q}\tau

\displaystyle\text{for }c\in C_{\infty},

with sub-ring
C_{\infty}\{\tau\}
of polynomials. Then
C_{\infty}\{\{\tau\}\}
may be identified via
\tau^{n}\leadsto z^{q^{n}}
with the
ring of
\mathds{F}
-linear power series of shape
\sum c_{n}z^{q^{n}}
, where the product of the latter is given by insertion. The map

(1.4.2)

\phi^{\Lambda}\colon a\longmapsto\phi_{a}^{\Lambda}

is a homomorphism of
\mathds{F}
-algebras from
A
to
C_{\infty}\{\tau\}
, and we can write

(1.4.3)

e^{\Lambda}(z)=\sum\alpha_{i}z^{q^{i}}=\sum\alpha_{i}\tau^{i},\qquad\text{where }\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}(\Lambda).

Define the series
(\beta_{i})_{i\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
by
\beta_{i}={-}E_{q^{i}-1}(\Lambda)
(with
E_{0}(\Lambda)={-}1
, so

\beta_{0}=1
) and
\log^{\Lambda}\in C_{\infty}\{\{\tau\}\}
by

(1.4.4)

\log^{\Lambda}=\sum\beta_{i}\tau^{i}.

Then

(1.4.5)

e^{\Lambda}\cdot\log^{\Lambda}=\log^{\Lambda}\cdot e^{\Lambda}=1\quad\text{in}\quad C_{\infty}\{\{\tau\}\},

i.e., the two series are mutual inverses. The equation

\phi_{T}^{\Lambda}\cdot e^{\Lambda}=e^{\Lambda}\cdot T

from (
1.3.2
) leads to

(1.4.6)

\log^{\Lambda}\cdot\phi_{T}^{\Lambda}=T\cdot\log^{\Lambda}.

Comparing coefficients in (
1.4.5
) gives

(1.4.7)

\sum_{i+j=k}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}^{q^{i}}=\sum_{i+j=k}\beta_{i}\alpha_{j}^{q^{i}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }k=0,\\
0,&\text{if }k>0.\end{cases}

Similarly, from (
1.4.6
),

(1.4.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=k}\beta_{i}g_{j}^{q^{i}}=T\beta_{k}

\displaystyle(k\geq 0),

where
g_{0}=T
and all the
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{j}
,
g_{k}
depend on
\Lambda
. These formulas allow to recursively calculate any
two of the series
(\alpha_{i})
,
(\beta_{j})
,
(g_{k})
from the third. For example,

(1.4.9)

g_{k}={-}\sum_{1\leq j<k}\beta_{k-j}g_{j}^{q^{k-j}}-[k]\beta_{k}=\sum_{1\leq j<k}E_{q^{k-j}-1}q_{j}^{q^{k-j}}+[k]E_{q^{k}-1},

where
[k]
is short for
T^{q^{k}}-T\in A
and
E_{\ell}=E_{\ell}(\Lambda)
.

1.5.

For
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega
let
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
be the
lattice
\sum_{1\leq i\leq r}A\omega_{i}
. We further let
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}=(\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{r})\in\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega^{r-1}

and
\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}}=\sum_{2\leq i\leq r}A\omega_{i}
. Consider the function on
\Omega
:

(1.5.1)

t(\boldsymbol{\omega})=(e^{\Lambda^{\prime}}(\omega_{1}))^{-1}.

It is well-defined, holomorphic and nowhere vanishing on
\Omega
, and satisfies

(1.5.2)

t(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}})=t(\boldsymbol{\omega})\quad\text{if}\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(\omega_{1}+\lambda^{\prime},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{r})\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}.

If
f
is a weak modular form then (
1.2.3
) implies that
f
satisfies the same rule

f(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}})=f(\boldsymbol{\omega})
and, moreover, that
f
has a Laurent expansion

(1.5.3)

f(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})t^{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

for sufficiently small values of
t
, where the coefficients
a_{n}
depend only on
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}
.

1.5.4.

Now
f
is called
modular
if the coefficients
a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})
vanish identically for
n<0
, and is
a
cusp form
if even
a_{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})\equiv 0
for
n\geq 0
.

Remark 1.6
:

(i)

Suppose that
f
has weight

. Then the coefficient functions

are in fact weakly modular of

, see e.g.
[

]

(ii)

If

(iii)

The boundary condition

of

, see [14], but we will not use this fact.
