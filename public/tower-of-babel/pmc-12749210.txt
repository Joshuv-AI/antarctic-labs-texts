ViTrace detects viral signatures in tumor transcriptomes using a hybrid language model
Authors: Feng Zhou, Yushuang He, Fan Yang, Jin Gu, Fengzhu Sun, Shunzhi Zhu, Xiaobing Huang, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying viruses in tumor transcriptome helps to unravel the potential role of viruses in oncogenesis and tumor progression. Most of the current tools for virus identification in RNA-Seq data rely on sequence alignment, whose performance is constrained by fast mutations, large divergence, and the incompleteness of viral genomes. In this study, we develop ViTrace to detect viral sequences in human transcriptomic data by a hybrid language representation learning model, which integrates DNA contexts, position relationships and amino acid coding information. Although ViTrace is only trained on 13 species from 7 genera, it achieves 86.39% recall in 1179 absent-in-train virus strains of 935 species belonging to 167 genera across 10 phyla. Applied to single-cell RNA-seq data from esophageal and oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas, the model reveals tumor-, cell-, and patient-specific viral colonization patterns, uncovering both known and previously unreported viruses. Overall, ViTrace provides a scalable framework for guiding precision oncology and facilitating future discoveries of previously uncharacterized tumor-associated viruses.Subject terms: Sequencing, Virology

Introduction

Viruses play a significant role in some tumors by inducing cellular transformation or by promoting microenvironments for tumor growth1,2. Viruses are estimated to be responsible for approximately 15% of cancer cases worldwide3. The identification of viruses within intratumor tissue holds paramount significance for elucidating cancer pathogenesis and advancing therapeutic modalities4,5. Viral infections can disrupt normal cellular functions, leading to a cascade of immune responses and potentially triggering long-term health consequences. Previous studies demonstrated that approximately 7.3% of reads from tumor DNA and RNA sequencing data cannot be mapped to the human genome6, and about 1% of tumor RNA-Seq data cannot be mapped to human transcripts. The unmappable reads would potentially reflect the presence of viruses4.

A critical strategy in current viral identification methods is matching sequences against reference databases to identify viral sequences7. Kraken exemplifies this strategy by using a k-mer-based approach to rapidly classify sequences based on pre-built databases of known genomes8. Similarly, MetaPhlAn utilizes clade-specific markers to accurately identify microbial and viral sequences within metagenomic samples9. MEGAN10, on the other hand, performs taxonomic and functional analysis on sequence data by interpreting results from BLAST11 or DIAMOND12 alignments. Despite their effectiveness, the sequence-alignment strategy relies on comprehensive, accurate, complete or up-to-date prebuilt reference sequence database13. Compared to the incalculable variety of viruses on Earth, the known viruses cataloged in databases like NCBI are exceedingly limited, numbering in the range of 10414. Moreover, the extra high mutation rates, 10−8 to 10−6 substitutions per nucleotide per cell infection (s/n/c) for DNA viruses and from 10−6 to 10−4 s/n/c for RNA viruses, are up to a million times higher than that of their hosts15. This challenge is further amplified when analyzing short reads, where the short nucleoside sequences would reduce specificity in virus identification. Therefore, the fast mutations in virus genomes and the large number of unexplored viruses limit the performance of virus identification on sequence alignment.

Alignment-free strategy for viral sequence identification does not rely on sequence matching against reference databases, which captures the intrinsic features of viral sequences through statistical or machine learning approaches16. DeepVirFinder utilizes convolutional neural networks (CNN) to predict virus sequences infecting prokaryotic sequences17. ViraMiner integrates two distinct CNN branches with varying pooling techniques to achieve better accuracy18. Virtifier leverages an attention-based long short-term memory (LSTM) network to identify virus sequences from metagenomic data19. Nevertheless, these tools are designed to identify virus sequences from metagenomic sequencing data instead of from Human sequencing data. Recently, DeepViFi provides a semi-supervised framework to detect virus sequences from human genomic sequencing data by learning virus family representations20. viRNAtrap designs a CNN framework to identify viral sequences from transcriptomic sequencing over 6000 tumor tissues across 14 cancer types4, which significantly improves the identification of viruses. While CNNs have demonstrated strong performance in such tasks, traditional CNNs rely on fixed-size kernels, which may limit their ability to capture long-range dependencies in genomic data. Although advanced variants like dilated convolutions can mitigate this21, such designs are not always adopted, and modeling complex sequence context remains challenging.

Genomic sequences, like text, consist of linear strings of nucleotide sequences with underlying patterns and contextual relationships. Language models on genomic sequences capture complex biological contexts, intricate patterns, long-range dependencies, and be robust to nucleotide mutation, which help to comprehensively obtained the local and global signature of virus genomes. Therefore, we develop a hybrid language representation learning framework, ViTrace, to capture the viral signatures and detect virus sequences from human transcriptomic sequencing data. In order to capture the essential viral signature, ViTrace is a neural network with Transformer architectures in favor of a fully attention, which learns the viral signatures integrating DNA contexts, position relationship, amino acid coding information and dependencies across the entire sequence. ViTrace achieves superior advantages over five baseline methods in all evaluation criteria. To evaluate ViTrace’s performance to identify species from unseen taxonomies, we build an independent testing data from Human Virus Database (HVD)22, which is composed of 1179 absent-in-train virus strains of 935 (914 absent-in-train) species belonging to 167 genera (160 absent-in-train) across 10 phyla (6 absent-in-train). The “absent-in-train” taxonomy means a taxonomy that the species belongs to has never been trained. Moreover, trained by the meticulous designed hierarchical positive and negative set, ViTrace effectively distinguishes viruses from other microorganisms and exhibits steady high performance across various read lengths and virus proportions in sequencing data. When applied to multi-scale virome mapping of 208,659 esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma (ESCC) and 70,970 oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) cells, ViTrace enables single-cell resolution profiling of viral distributions across diverse cancer types, tissues, and cell types. It reveals tumor-specific and cell-type-specific viral tropisms, uncovers inter-patient heterogeneity in viral burden, and differentiates infection patterns between tumors and adjacent normal tissues. ViTrace is also applied to human RNA-Seq data from three representative human virus–host systems, where the mapping rate and coverage to the virus genome for each individual replicate under the given conditions help to understand the extent of viral infection and the behavior of these viruses under varying cellular environments. Furthermore, the framework of ViTrace is applicable to capture viruses in other host transcriptomic data, and we extend ViTrace to identify virus sequences from transcriptomic sequencing data of mouse, the model organism of tumor study.

Results

The framework of ViTrace

ViTrace uncovers viral signatures in tumor transcriptome sequencing data by a hybrid language representation model to extract the features from nucleotide and codon sequences, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The transcriptomic sequencing data are aligned to reference sequences of the human genome and transcriptome, then the unmapped reads are subsequently input into ViTrace. The nucleotide reads are tokenized and positioned, and converted into embedding vectors considering position and dependencies across the entire sequence. In addition, the nucleotide sequence is one-hot encoded and transferred to amino acid codon sequence by 64 codon filters. The features of codon sequences are extracted by CNN. This hybrid design enables ViTrace to integrate complementary sequence features at multiple levels for accurate viral sequence identification.

Overview of the ViTrace.

The transcriptomic sequencing reads are mapped to the human reference genome (GRC/h38) and transcriptome (ENST) using alignment tool Bowtie2. Unmapped reads are subsequently processed by ViTrace. The nucleotide-based channel employs tokenization and positions to convert reads into feature embedding, considering dependencies across the entire sequence. In the codon-based channel, the nucleotide sequence is represented with one-hot encoding and transferred to amino acid codon sequence by 64 codon filters. The features in the codon sequence are extracted by CNN. Each channel independently scores the inputting sequences through fully connected layer, and the probability of sequences from virus is integrated on the results from both channels. The identified viral reads are then subjected to experimental validation, including model performance evaluation and subsequent biological downstream analyses.

Performance evaluation: ViTrace outperforms five baseline tools on benchmark data

Five baseline methods, viRNAtrap4, DeepVirFinder17, ViraMiner18, DeepViFi20, and Seq2Seq4, are included for performance comparison. All of the models were trained and tested on the same set of Sim1 and evaluated by six metrics, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, ViTrace achieves the best performance on all of the six metrics. ViRNAtrap ranks as the second on five metrics, except precision. ViTrace achieves 0.91, 0.92, 0.82, 0.78, 0.90, and 0.83 in AUROC, AUPRC, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score, which exhibits 6–11% higher in the 6 metrics compared to the tool listed in rank 2.

In order to assess the essential reason for the significant improvement in ViTrace’s performance, ablation experiments were conducted. The full model (ViTrace) employs dual-channel processing: a nucleotide sequence channel using Transformer modules to extract DNA sequence signatures with positional relationships, and a codon sequence channel using CNN to capture the genetic information essential for the structure, function, and regulation of living organisms. Control models were systematically compared: ViTrace-T (Transformer-only on nucleotide sequences), ViTrace-C (CNN-only on codon sequences), and ViTrace-C-DNA (CNN-only on raw nucleotide sequences). As shown in Fig. 2b, nucleotide sequence-based channel (ViTrace-T) makes the major contribution to AUPRC, AUROC, and F1 score, and the incorporation of codon information only improves the 3 metrics by ~2%. It is notable that the incorporation of codon information significantly increases the recall but also greatly decreases the precision. However, in most application scenarios of virus identification, precision is less critical than recall4 because the following alignment or biological experiments would verify or filter the detected sequences. The nucleotide sequence-based channel combined the DNA sequence signature and positional relationship by tokenization and transformer modules. Furthermore, we implement an ablation experiment to evaluate the contribution of the both types of information by using CNN structure to extract the DNA sequence signature into model (ViTrace-C-DNA). As shown in Fig. 2b, the performance of ViTrace-C-DNA is lower than ViTrace by approximately 7–11% on the 6 metrics. Therefore, the ablation experiments demonstrate that the nucleotide sequences make a major contribution to identifying the virus sequences, and the codon sequences increase the virus recall rate. Among the sequence signature and positional relationship in nucleotide sequences, positional relationship significantly improves the performance. Meanwhile, ViTrace-C outperforms ViTrace-C-DNA across all evaluation criteria, demonstrating that the sequence signatures from codon sequences contain more information than those from nucleotide sequences.

To further assess the robustness of ViTrace across diverse sequencing scenarios, we evaluated its performance on Sim2 reads of varying lengths (75–300 bp) and compared it with baseline methods. As shown in Fig. 2c, ViTrace consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance across all six metrics as read lengths increase, demonstrating its adaptability to sequencing length variations. While ViraMiner achieves marginally higher Precision, this comes at the cost of severely compromised Recall, highlighting ViTrace’s superior ability to balance sensitivity and specificity. The performance gap between ViTrace and baseline tools widens progressively with increasing read lengths. For example, ViTrace’s Accuracy improves from 0.8308 (75 bp) to 0.933 (300 bp), a 10.22% increase, whereas the second-ranked method (DeepVirFinder) shows only a 9.91% improvement (0.7945 to 0.8936). This trend suggests ViTrace’s enhanced capacity to leverage longer reads for feature extraction, likely attributable to its hybrid architecture combining local convolutional patterns with global sequence dependencies. These results align with findings from the first benchmark dataset, reinforcing ViTrace’s generalizability across different data characteristics.

Performance evaluation: ViTrace can identify unseen virus sequences from the absent-in-train taxonomies

As described in the last section, ViTrace was trained in the dataset Sim1 built by viRNAtrap, whose virus reads are simulated from the genomes of 13 species of influenza virus, Dengue virus, West Nile virus, Ebolavirus, MERS coronavirus, Rotavirus, and Zika virus, from 7 genera across 4 distinct phyla. The testing set in Sim1 is also extracted from the same genomes of 13 species, which was implemented in the previous section. Therefore, in this section, we build an independent testing set Sim2 from the HVD to explore ViTrace’s performance in identifying never-seen viruses. Sim2 composed of testing reads simulated from 1179 absent-in-train virus strains of 935 (914 absent-in-train) species belonging to 167 genera (160 absent-in-train) across 10 phyla (6 absent-in-train). If ViTrace is able to effectively detect viruses from taxonomies never used for training, it would possibly identify unknown viruses that are absent in the current database or have never been discovered.

Figure 3a delineates the number of different-level taxonomy, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species, distributed in training and testing datasets. For instance, ViTrace was trained on the species from 5 classes, but it was tested on another 14 absent-in-train classes in the Sim2 testing data. Among the 14 absent-in-train classes, 5 are from the 4 trained phyla and 9 are from the 6 absent-in-train phyla. In total, Sim2 includes 935 absent-in-train species, 160 absent-in-train genera, 45 absent-in-train families, 20 absent-in-train orders, 14 absent-in-train classes, and 6 absent-in-train phyla. The higher of the taxonomy level indicates larger dissimilarity to the trained species. For the 352 strains from 341 species in the Sim2 testing data, any levels of the taxonomies lower than phylum they belonged to have not been seen during the training process. ViTrace achieves 86.39% recall rate in predicting the virus reads simulated on Sim2. As shown in Fig. 3b, for the 6 phyla that have never been trained, 3 of them achieve recall rates higher than 90%, 2 of them between 85 and 90%, and only 1 of them ~70%. It means that ViTrace really catches the virus signature and effectively identifies unseen virus sequences which are far away from the known viruses.

In more detail, Fig. 3c provides a visualization of ViTrace’s performance across 167 genera, and 160 of them have never been seen during the training (Supplementary Data 1. A total of 46, 65, 32, and 13 genera are identified with recall rates of [90%, 100%], [80%, 90%), [70%, 80%), and [60%, 70%), respectively. However, we observed that a few genera (e.g., Uukuvirus, Nyavirus, and Wenrivirus from the phylum Negarnaviricota) exhibited lower prediction scores (<0.6). We hypothesize this is primarily due to the absence of training data for these genera (n = 0 in both cases), which limits the model’s ability to learn their sequence features. Additionally, the high genetic divergence between these genera and the trained viruses within the same phylum may further hinder generalization. For instance, Uukuvirus (recall = 0.52) and Wenrivirus (recall = 0.58) belong to the Negarnaviricota phylum, but their genomic structures or conserved regions might differ significantly from the trained genera (e.g., Alphainfluenzavirus and Betacoronavirus). Moreover, the limited number of test sequences (m = 1 for both genera) could amplify stochastic effects in performance evaluation. This result highlights that while ViTrace demonstrates robust performance for most unseen genera, its effectiveness diminishes for taxa that are highly divergent phylogenetically. Future work will focus on expanding the diversity of training data and incorporating domain adaptation techniques to improve recognition of highly divergent viruses.

The experiment demonstrates the robust accuracies of ViTrace in detecting the sequences of unknown viruses from various levels of never-seen taxonomy, which guarantees reliability in discovering unseen, fast mutated, or rare viral instances in transcriptomic sequencing data.

Performance evaluation: ViTrace performs well under different data conditions

Because most RNA-Seq data in the the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database were sequenced to 48 bp read length, ViTrace was trained on the 48 bp simulated reads. Therefore, the performance of ViTrace on reads with various lengths is assessed. ViTrace model trained on 48 bp reads is applied directly to the reads set from 75 to 300 bp, which were simulated on Sim2. The strategy for dealing with longer reads is given in the Method section. As shown in Fig. 4a–c, ViTrace exhibits consistent and markedly increasing tendency on all of the six metrics with the read length. For the read length from 48 to 300 bp, the AUROC, AUPRC, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score increased from 87% to 98%, 88% to 98%, 78% to 93%, 75% to 95%, 85% to 91% and 79% to 93%, respectively. These results validated the effectiveness of ViTrace to deal with RNA-Seq data with various read lengths.

Both the AUROC and the AUPRC scores are provided, reflecting the accuracy and precision-recall balance of the model across different sequence lengths. d Illustrates the predictive capability of the model on mixed datasets with various ratios of viral to human reads, demonstrating the effect of data abundance on the performance of the model. e Radar chart visually represents the performance comparison of ViTrace and other advanced methods under different metrics on the Sim3 test set. f Display the performance of the model in predicting sequence lengths from 48 to 150 bp.

ViTrace is designed to capture the virus sequences from tumor RNA-Seq data. Because the simulated datasets may not fully reflect the complexity of human tumors and the ground truth of the read label is not available for real RNA-Seq data, we construct two hybrid data mixing the simulated reads from known viral genomes with a bulk and a single-cell RNA-Seq data of tumor tissue, respectively. In order to consider the situation of different virus abundance in tumor tissue, 0.01–0.1% of viral reads were mixed with real tumor RNA-Seq data. As shown in Fig. 4d, on the scRNA-seq hybrid dataset, ViTrace consistently achieves high recalls around 0.86, regardless of the viral proportion ranging from 0.01% to 0.1%. Similarly, ViTrace also demonstrates robust performance on the hybrid data mixed with bulk RNA-Seq, with the recall remaining around 0.84. It is observed that ViTrace exhibits significantly higher recall in the hybrid data with scRNA-seq data compared to bulk RNA-Seq data, which is attributed to the 98 bp read length in scRNA-seq, in contrast to the 48 bp read length in bulk RNA data of TCGA. It is consistent with the observation in Fig. 4a–c.

The experiments demonstrate that ViTrace is steadily accurate in capturing the virus sequences under various read lengths and under different virus abundances.

Performance evaluation: ViTrace effectively distinguishes viruses from other microorganisms

Human RNA-Seq data contains not only human and viral reads but also sequences from microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, and archaea. In response to this complexity, we developed a two-step hierarchical training strategy, aimed at enhancing ViTrace’s ability to differentiate viruses from these other microorganisms. We validated this capability through a series of experiments, comparing ViTrace’s performance across different read lengths and benchmarking it against other state-of-the-art methods.

ViTrace’s performance was compared to DeepViFinder and viRNATrap, two widely recognized tools for viral sequence identification. All methods were retrained using the Sim3 training data and evaluated on the Sim3 testing data. As shown in Fig. 4e, ViTrace outperforms both DeepViFinder and viRNATrap across all evaluation metrics. ViTrace achieves an AUC of 84.32%, which is 4.27% higher than DeepViFinder and 3.4% higher than viRNATrap. In addition, ViTrace demonstrates a superior AUPR value, further validating its strong ability to distinguish viral sequences from non-viral microorganisms. These results indicate that ViTrace provides more balanced performance across precision, recall, and F1 score, making it a reliable approach for identifying viral sequences in complex RNA-Seq data, even with shorter read lengths.

To evaluate the impact of read length on ViTrace’s performance, we tested the ViTrace on Sim3 testing data with read lengths of 48 bp, 100 bp, and 150 bp. As shown in Fig. 4f, the performance of ViTrace consistently improves as the read length increases across all six metrics. Specifically, from 48 to 150 bp, ViTrace’s performance improves by 4–5% across all metrics. The results confirm that ViTrace reliably captures viral sequences with high precision and recall, showing greater robustness at longer read lengths. This increased robustness is especially advantageous when handling variable RNA-Seq read lengths in real sequencing data.

Single-cell viral distribution profiling by ViTrace

We systematically characterized viral distribution patterns across 208,659 esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC)23 cells from 60 patients and 70,970 oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) cells from 16 patients using ViTrace24. Figure 5a, b demonstrates significant differences in viral distribution through three complementary visualizations. In both cancer types, cells were embedded into a unified transcriptomic space using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), with cell type annotations derived from published references (ESCC:Zhang et al.23; OPSCC: Sidharth et al.24) (Fig. 5a1, b1). Initial viral detection results, prior to decontamination, revealed widespread viral presence: 5577 infected cells in ESCC (2.68%) and 28,677 infected cells in OPSCC (40.41%) (Fig. 5a2, b2), with viral load quantified as the natural logarithm of total mapped viral reads per cell.

The 1st application scenario in single cell data: ViTrace reveals differential viral distribution before and after decontamination

To ensure these results reflected bona fide biological infections rather than technical contamination, we performed rigorous post-processing by aligning candidate viral sequences against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database using BLAST. Only viral candidates with significant hits in the nr database were retained. This stringent decontamination step dramatically refined the viral landscape: the number of virus-positive cells dropped to 195 in ESCC (0.09%, 195/208,659) and 11,786 in OPSCC (16.60%, 11,786/70,970) (Fig. 5a3, b3), demonstrating that the majority of initially detected viral reads in ESCC were likely environmental or technical contaminants, while a substantial portion of viral signals in OPSCC remained after validation, supporting their biological relevance. This difference suggests that the tumor microenvironment in OPSCC may be more conducive to viral persistence, likely due to the higher incidence of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infections in these tumors. These observations underscore the need for differential viral distribution analysis in different cancer types to better understand the unique viral dynamics within each tumor microenvironment.

The impact of decontamination on cell-type-specific viral detection was further quantified (Fig. 5a4, b4). In ESCC, all cell types showed near-complete loss of viral signal post-filtering: epithelial cells decreased from 3.6 to 0.1%, fibroblasts from 2.8 to 0.0%, and T cells from 1.3 to 0.1%, confirming that the majority of virus calls in this tumor type were false positives. In contrast, OPSCC retained substantial viral prevalence across multiple cell types even after validation. Epithelial cells exhibited a 36.1% infection rate, reflecting only a moderate decrease from 48.8% pre-decontamination. Other immune and stromal populations also retained validated viral signals, including macrophages (14.2%), plasma cells (13.9%), and conventional dendritic cells (cDCs, 16.7%). These patterns indicate authentic and biologically meaningful viral colonization across diverse cellular compartments in OPSCC.

The integration of ViTrace with a BLAST-based decontamination step has significantly enhanced the accuracy of viral detection in single-cell RNA-seq data. The refined viral distribution patterns highlighted important differences between ESCC and OPSCC, revealing that OPSCC tumors harbor a higher viral prevalence and more focused viral tropism in epithelial cells.

The 2nd application scenario in single cell data: ViTrace identifies viral infection differences across three cellular subtypes

To further demonstrate its utility, ViTrace was applied to profile viral infection across different cellular subtypes within OPSCC, with a focus on HPV-the dominant oncogenic virus in oropharyngeal cancers. Specifically, we examined three cellular-defined subtypes: atypical, basal, and classical25, across 11,740 single cells (9868 atypical; 432 basal; 1440 classical). As shown in Fig. 6a, cells were visualized by patient identity (left), subtype classification (middle), and HPV infection level [Ln(HPV_reads), right]. Atypical tumors showed a substantially higher burden of HPV-positive cells (6,727), compared to basal (11) and classical (3), highlighting a strong subtype-specific enrichment of HPV.

ViTrace further revealed notable inter-patient heterogeneity in HPV infection. Patients with the atypical subtype consistently exhibited high HPV infection rates, while those classified as basal or classical showed minimal or no detectable infection (Fig. 6b–c; Supplementary Fig. S1). Specifically, the atypical subtype was identified in 11 patients and accounted for the majority of HPV-positive cells, whereas the basal and classical subtypes, observed in 4 and 3 patients, respectively, showed sparse infection. These findings suggest that the atypical subtype is not only more prevalent but also more permissive to HPV colonization, potentially reflecting underlying molecular or immune differences that influence viral susceptibility.

The 3rd application scenario in single cell data: ViTrace reveals the virus distribution in OPSCC tumors and adjacent normal tissues

To investigate viral distribution differences between tumor and adjacent normal cells, we applied ViTrace to analyze paired cancer-adjacent tissue samples from 3 OPSCC patients. The dataset included 10,189 cells from tumor tissues and 4221 cells from matched adjacent normal tissues. ViTrace detected HPV-positive signals in 3695 cells (3681 in cancer tissues vs. 14 in adjacent normal tissues), demonstrating a significant predominance of HPV-positive cells in malignant lesions. Figure 6b, c illustrate cell-type annotations and HPV infection status in tumor and normal tissues. HPV-positive cells were almost exclusively found in the epithelial lineage, with a significant proportion of epithelial cells in tumors being infected. These findings highlight not only the differential expression of HPV between tumor and normal compartments, but also the virus’s strong tropism for epithelial cells. This cell-type-specific distribution provides critical insights into HPV’s interaction with the tumor microenvironment in OPSCC.

Figure 6d further illustrates HPV read counts stratified by patient and cell type. Epithelial cells consistently exhibited the highest HPV loads, represented by larger and more abundant bubbles, whereas immune cells such as B-cells, T-cells, and macrophages displayed minimal or sporadic HPV signals. Patient-specific variation in epithelial HPV load was evident (e.g., OP10 vs. OP13), suggesting inter-individual heterogeneity in viral burden. These results reinforce the notion that epithelial cells are the principal targets of HPV in OPSCC and offer a nuanced view of viral distribution across patients and cell populations. It is worth noting that Fig. 6d includes only patients with detectable HPV signals, patients without HPV detection were excluded from this visualization.

The 4th application scenario in single cell data: ViTrace discovers unknown tumor-associated viruses at single-cell resolution

Given the limited scope of existing databases on human cancer-associated viruses, our research aims to further explore potential viruses related to OPSCC and ESCC. Viral reads predicted by ViTrace were aligned to the comprehensive virus database (RVDBv26.0)26, which excludes non-viral elements and endogenous viral sequences to reduce false positives. For OPSCC, five candidate viruses (avian sarcoma virus (AvianV), chrysochromulina ericina virus (Chey), mustela putorius papillomavirus (MPPV), choristoneura fumiferana granulovirus (Cfgv), and pandoravirus (PandV)) were detected across multiple patients (Fig. 6e). A detailed summary of viral infection patterns stratified by patient and cell type is available in Supplementary Data 2. Although these viruses are not commonly annotated in OPSCC-related studies, they exhibited patient-specific presence with varying read counts, suggesting possible sporadic infections or contamination from yet uncharacterized viral reservoirs. For example, Cfgv and Chey were enriched in patient OP14, while MPPV reads were detected in OP10, indicating heterogeneity in viral presence even within the same tumor type.

For ESCC, five different candidate viruses (human herpesvirus 1 (HHV-1), human herpesvirus 6B (HHV-6B), aphis citricidus bunyavirus (ACBV), murine osteosarcoma virus (MOV), and human betaherpesvirus 6 A (HBHV)) were identified (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Data 3). While HHV-1 and HHV-6B have been occasionally reported in human cancers, the detection of ACBV, MOV, and HBHV in specific patients may represent either unknown host–virus associations or overlooked viral contaminants in the tumor microenvironment. Notably, patient P12 harbored a high number of HHV-6B reads, whereas P65 exhibited an abundance of HHV-1 reads, highlighting the capability of ViTrace to sensitively capture patient-specific viral patterns. These results demonstrate that ViTrace is not limited to detecting well-characterized oncogenic viruses but can also uncover candidate tumor-associated viruses at single-cell resolution.

Application of ViTrace for detecting diverse viruses in public bulk RNA-Seq datasets

To systematically evaluate ViTrace on unbiased, publicly available datasets, we collected a comprehensive set of bulk RNA-seq data from the NCBI SRA database, covering three major virus–host systems: (1) HPV16/18-positive cervical cancer cell lines (PRJNA43715027 and PRJNA66380128); (2) hepatitis B virus (HBV) chronic-infection sera (n=20) and one Huh7 supernatant control (PRJNA93308429); and (3) cross-species infection models involving SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, and MERS-CoV in seven human cell lines under nine multiplicities of infection (MOI) (PRJNA61503230, PRJNA62551831, PRJNA23394332). All datasets were processed with unified default parameters to detect viral reads and align them to reference genomes. In all virus-positive samples, ViTrace successfully identified the expected viral species, confirming its sensitivity in recognizing known viral signals across diverse sequencing contexts.

For each dataset, the total number of virus-predicted reads by ViTrace (i.e., reads classified as viral) and the number of reads successfully mapped to viral reference genomes were quantified. Corresponding mapping rates were then calculated (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). In the HPV-positive datasets, HeLa (HPV18-positive) and SiHa (HPV16-positive) cells consistently exhibited higher mapping rates compared to CaSki (HPV16-positive) cells. For example, SiHa replicates showed mapping rates exceeding 94%, and HeLa replicates from PRJNA663801 were above 92%, whereas CaSki ranged from 51.26 to 63.68%. In the HBV dataset, serum samples displayed wider variation (28.79–83.69%) and generally lower rates than HeLa and SiHa, suggesting more variable viral RNA abundance in vivo. These results suggest that viral RNA was more abundant and uniformly captured in HeLa and SiHa samples, likely reflecting higher viral genome copy numbers or more consistent viral transcriptional activity, whereas CaSki and HBV-positive serum samples exhibited greater variability in viral read recovery.

Genome coverage profiles revealed distinct enrichment patterns (Fig. 7b). In CaSki and SiHa cells, both HPV16 coverage profiles showed prominent peaks in the E7 region, consistent with its role in viral oncogene expression. However, distinct secondary enrichment patterns were observed: CaSki cells exhibited a higher peak in the E2 region, whereas SiHa cells displayed a notable peak in the E6 region, second only to E7. In HeLa cells, HPV18 coverage demonstrated a similar enrichment pattern in the E7 region; however, in the PRJNA663801 dataset, a minor peak was also detected in the L1 region, which was absent in the PRJNA437150 dataset, suggesting possible differences in viral integration sites or transcriptional activity between datasets. HBV coverage in Huh7/pTHBV1.3 cells was concentrated in the X gene, whereas serum samples showed high coverage across the Surface, Core, and X genes, consistent with multiple transcriptional units and replication stages.

For coronavirus infection models, ViTrace detected viral reads in all tested cell lines (A549-ACE2, Calu3, Caco2, NHBE, H1299, MRC5, Vero E6), with high mapping rates (>80%) for SARS-CoV-2 in A549-ACE2 (0.2 MOI) and Calu3 (0.3 MOI) cells, and for MERS-CoV in Vero E6 cells at both 3.0 MOI and 0.1 MOI. Lower mapping rates in NHBE and H1299 cells reflected reduced infection efficiency. Coverage profiles revealed virus-specific patterns: SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV showed a “U-shaped” distribution with high coverage at the 5′ and 3′ ends, while MERS-CoV exhibited more uniform coverage with slight terminal enrichment (Supplementary Fig. S5). Notably, genome coverage patterns differed substantially among HPV, HBV, and coronaviruses, reflecting their distinct genome organizations and transcription strategies. These distinct coverage signatures highlight ViTrace’s capacity to capture biologically meaningful viral genomic features across diverse infection contexts.

Discussion

Unraveling the presence and impact of previously uncharacterized viruses linked to the initiation and progression of cancer stands as a pivotal aim in cancer research. This pursuit holds the promise of catalyzing the development of more precise and efficacious cancer therapies. To this end, we introduce ViTrace, designed to capture the viral signatures and detect virus sequences from human tumor transcriptomic sequencing data. ViTrace employs a dual-channel strategy, in which the nucleotide sequence-based channel uses transformer modules to extract DNA sequence signatures and capture the relationships among different positions, while the codon-based channel utilizes multi-layer CNNs to extract genetic information. The integration of these two distinct yet complementary channels results in a combined prediction score, offering a more refined and comprehensive outcome.

ViTrace is trained and tested on simulated reads from hg19 genome and a limited selection of virus types, including 13 virus species from 7 genera across 4 phyla. The benchmarking experiments show that ViTrace significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art methods in all evaluation metrics. Moreover, we built an independent testing dataset from the HVD and the human reference genome GRC/h38 to assess the performance of ViTrace in identifying species from never-seen taxonomies. ViTrace achieves an 86.39% prediction recall on 1179 absent-in-train virus strains of 935 (914 absent-in-train) species belonging to 167 genera (160 absent-in-train) across 10 phyla (6 absent-in-train). This indicates ViTrace’s proficiency in capturing viral signatures and accurately identifying them, even when faced with previously unseen viral genera and phyla.

To accommodate different sequencing methods and the resultant disparities in read lengths, we simulated an independent testing data Sim2, which encompasses read lengths ranging from 48 to 300 bp. ViTrace trained on 48 bp reads is directly applied to the independent testing dataset Sim2 to evaluate its efficacy across a spectrum of read lengths. The results indicate that ViTrace achieves a consistent and notable improvement across all performance metrics as the read length increases, underscoring its robustness and adaptability in processing sequencing data of variable lengths. The outperformance on the complex hybrid dataset, which is mixed of simulated reads from known viral genomes and both bulk and single-cell RNA-Seq data from tumor tissues, further demonstrates the robustness of ViTrace in accurately detecting viral reads. Human RNA-Seq data contains not only the human transcriptome but also microorganisms such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, and archaea, each with distinct genomic features that prevent them from clustering together. The goal of this study is to accurately identify viral sequences. Thus, we designed a two-step hierarchical training strategy to improve the precision of viral detection. By comparing ViTrace with other state-of-the-art methods, we demonstrate its superior ability to distinguish viral sequences from other microorganisms, highlighting the ViTrace robustness and precision across varying read lengths.

Furthermore, we conducted a direct comparison between ViTrace and Kraken2 using exactly the same training set and testing set (Sim2). The results reveal a marked difference in performance profiles between the two methods (Supplementary Fig. S6). As a k-mer–based taxonomic classification tool, Kraken2 achieves perfect precision across all lengths, indicating that when it assigns a label, it is almost always correct. However, this comes with extremely low recall, reflecting its conservative tendency to only classify reads with exact or near-exact matches to the reference database. This limitation is especially pronounced for highly divergent viral sequences absent from the reference, which are left unclassified. In contrast, ViTrace attains high recall while maintaining strong precision, leading to a substantially higher F1-score. This balance demonstrates ViTrace’s ability to detect a far larger proportion of true viral reads, including those with no close reference in the database, while still minimizing false positives. The difference reflects fundamental methodological distinctions: Kraken2 is optimized for rapid and accurate classification given a comprehensive reference, whereas ViTrace leverages learned sequence representations to generalize to previously unseen viral genomes.

These findings suggest a complementary usage scenario: ViTrace can serve as a sensitive front-end detector to identify potential viral sequences—particularly unseen or divergent ones—followed by Kraken2 or similar tools for precise taxonomic assignment. This combined approach would integrate ViTrace’s broad detection capability with Kraken2’s high-confidence taxonomic classification, potentially yielding both sensitivity and specificity in metagenomic virus analysis workflows.

To thoroughly assess the performance of ViTrace on real data, we applied ViTrace to tumor transcriptomes of both scRNA-seq data and bulk RNA-Seq data. On scRNA-seq data encompassing 208,659 ESCC and 70,970 OPSCC cells, ViTrace revealed multi-scale disparities in viral distribution, beginning with tumor-type specificity. OPSCC exhibited significantly higher viral colonization than ESCC, underscoring inherent differences in tissue susceptibility to viral infiltration. Delving deeper into molecular subtypes, the Atypical cellular subtype emerged as a hotspot for viral recruitment, starkly contrasting with the Basal and Classical subtypes, which showed minimal viral engagement. At the cellular level, epithelial cells were identified as primary viral reservoirs, whereas stromal fibroblasts and immune subsets such as T-cells and plasma cells displayed context-dependently reduced viral interaction. Inter-patient heterogeneity further complicated this landscape, with viral abundance and cellular tropism varying markedly across individuals. Moreover, ViTrace successfully detected diverse viral species across multiple publicly available bulk RNA-Seq datasets, demonstrating high sensitivity and robustness across distinct virus–host systems. The observed viral genome coverage patterns reflected unique genome organizations and transcriptional activities of different viruses, highlighting ViTrace’s ability to capture biologically meaningful viral genomic features in varied infection contexts. Although we specifically conducted downstream analyses for ESCC and OPSCC, ViTrace can be extended to additional scRNA-seq datasets using the same experimental workflow, enabling further discoveries of oncogenic factors and mechanisms associated with various tumors.

The framework of ViTrace can also be extended to capture the virus sequences from other host transcriptomic data. Because the mouse is the model organism for cancer research, we extend ViTrace to a mouse version. The trained model achieves excellent predictive results on the test set, shown in Supplementary Fig. S7.

Despite the demonstrated robustness and accuracy of ViTrace, several aspects still require further refinement and optimization. First, the current training relies primarily on simulated reads, which may not fully capture the complexity and noise present in real-world sequencing data. Second, the diversity of viruses used in training is limited, potentially impacting ViTrace’s performance on rare or highly divergent viral strains. Third, while ViTrace shows high performance in controlled datasets, its sensitivity in detecting low-abundance viruses in samples with high host background remains to be further evaluated. Future work will focus on incorporating more comprehensive and diverse viral references, enhancing the model’s sensitivity to low viral loads. We also plan to explore the integration of large language models (LLMs) fine-tuned on viral and host genomic sequences, aiming to further improve the quality of feature extraction and recognition. These improvements will further strengthen ViTrace’s utility for detecting and characterizing viral infections in various biological and clinical settings. Overall, ViTrace aims to provide a comprehensive and adaptable framework for the detection and characterization of viral infections in diverse biological contexts, offering valuable insights into viral pathogenesis and its implications for human health.

Methods

Experimental design

In this study, we construct three simulated datasets and one hybrid dataset mixing real and simulated reads to evaluate the performance of ViTrace comprehensively. Then, four real datasets from single-cell RNA-Seq and bulk RNA-Seq, respectively, are applied to illustrate the effectiveness of ViTrace to help understand the potential role of viruses in human transcriptomes. The detail of the datasets is shown in Supplementary Data 4–6.

The first simulated dataset, Sim1, was constructed by viRNAtrap4. Sim1 serves as the primary benchmark for evaluating model performance under taxonomic overlap between training and testing data. ViTrace and other 5 baseline models, viRNAtrap4, DeepVirFinder17, ViraMiner18, DeepViFi20, and Seq2Seq4 are all trained and tested on Sim1. The second simulated dataset, Sim2, was constructed as an independent testing set to evaluate ViTrace’s performance to identify viruses whose taxonomy had never been seen during the training. Additionally, Sim2 evaluates robustness to variable read lengths (48–300 bp), simulating real-world sequencing platforms with diverse fragment sizes. The third simulated dataset, Sim3, was created to evaluate ViTrace’s ability to differentiate viruses from other microorganisms. By balancing viral and non-viral classes and enforcing strict genomic source separation, Sim3 quantifies cross-kingdom discrimination accuracy.

In order to evaluate ViTrace’s performance in real data, a hybrid dataset mixing simulated virus reads with real tumor RNA-Seq data is constructed to obtain ground truth of read labels. The different proportions of viral reads allow us to evaluate ViTrace’s capability under conditions of different tumor amounts. The real datasets, including bulk RNA-Seq and scRNA-seq from the SRA database33, are applied to evaluate the effectiveness of ViTrace under different application scenarios to explore the potential roles of viruses in human transcriptomes.

Benchmarking dataset construction

Sim1 was constructed using viRNAtrap to evaluate performance under taxonomic overlap. Human transcripts were sourced from the NCBI Human Genome Resources (hg19)14, and viral coding sequences were obtained from the Virus Variation Resource34, comprising 13 species across 7 genera (4 phyla). To balance class representation, human sequences were fragmented into 48 bp reads using a 48 bp sliding window (matching TCGA tumor RNA-Seq read lengths), while viral sequences were processed with a 2 bp sliding window to account for shorter genome lengths. All fragments derived from the same transcript were grouped to avoid data leakage. The training, validation, and testing data are composed of 8.0×106, 8.0×105, and 2.56×106 reads of 48 bp from human and virus sequences, respectively.

Sim2 was independently constructed to evaluate ViTrace’s ability to identify unseen viruses and process variable read lengths. Human transcripts were sourced from NCBI hg19, while viral sequences were derived from the HVD22. Sim2 includes 1179 absent-in-train virus strains from 935 (914 absent-in-train) species belonging to 167 genera (160 absent-in-train) across 10 phyla (6 absent-in-train), which are from HVD and generated by Mason (parameters: substitutionrate=0.001%, indel rate=0.002%)35, a read simulation tool. To ensure robustness, the dataset was partitioned into length-specific subsets (48 bp, 75 bp, 100 bp, 150 bp, 200 bp, 250 bp, 300 bp), and we strictly excluded species, genera, or phyla that overlapped taxonomically with Sim1.

Sim3 was used to evaluate ViTrace’s ability to distinguish viral reads from non-viral microbes. Positive samples were generated from 12,567 viral genomes (NCBI RefSeq), while negative samples included balanced representations of bacteria, fungi, and archaea (NCBI RefSeq). To mitigate class imbalance, bacterial/fungal/archaeal sequences were fragmented into 48 bp reads using a 200 bp sliding window, whereas viral sequences were processed with a 24 bp window. Fragments from the same source genome were grouped to prevent data leakage, and the dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets. The training, validation, and testing data are composed of 2.4×107, 2.4×106, and 2.86×106 reads of 48 bp from other microorganisms and virus sequences, respectively. Additionally, we simulated reads of different lengths to evaluate ViTrace’s ability to identify viruses from other microorganisms.

Feature representation

In the nucleotide sequence-based channel, the relatively short length of input reads, composed of a limited number of the four bases (A, T, C, G), necessitates treating each base as a separate token. To input sequences of reads composed of various tokens into the model, we convert them into numerical representations through two types of embeddings: token embedding and position embedding. Initially, a fully connected layer (FC layer) is employed for linear projection, generating a low-dimensional embedding vector for each token. As tokens in the sequence are represented as IDs, the FC layer acts as a learnable dictionary (lookup vocabulary) mapping from ID to vector. Similarly, position embedding encodes positional information via the FC layer, taking a vector of position indices as input. Both the position and token embedding layers produce outputs of the same dimensionality, which allows for their seamless integration by element-wise addition or concatenation. Thus, by utilizing token embedding and position embedding, we transform sequence reads into numerical representations for inputting into the transformer-based model. Although different sequencing methods may produce reads of varying lengths, in the TCGA database, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) reads for nearly all tumor types are standardized to a length of 48 bp. Therefore, to maintain consistency and facilitate analysis, we set the sentence length to 48 bp.

In the codon-based channel, for the input reads sequence with length Lr, we represent an input read sequence of length Lr using one-hot encoding. When encountering a non-ATGC base, it is replaced with a randomly selected base. Thus, an input reads sequence can be represented as Sr with a size of Lr×4. To capture more complex biological information at the codon level, we transform the one-hot encoded Sr into codon-based feature representations36. The codon transformer constructs a set of 64 distinct filters F, where each filter is a 3×4 matrix corresponding to a distinct codon. Given the existence of 64 unique codons, we employ a 64-dimensional one-hot matrix to encapsulate the feature representations of the sequence reads. Model components

In the nucleotide sequence-based channel, the feature matrices Mt and Mp generated by token embedding and position embedding are combined as Ms, which is the input of a three-layer transformer-based block, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The transformer block consists of two sub-layers: a multi-head self-attention layer and a fully connected feedforward layer. Around each sub-layer, skip/residual connections and layer normalization are employed, greatly facilitating the training process.

Each head can view the input data from different perspectives, facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the input and the capture of different aspects of the data from various angles. As shown in Fig. 1, the output pt of the 3-layer transformer block is then passed to the FC layer for predicting the sequence.

In the codon-based channel, the feature matrix Mc, transformed through codon conversion, is fed as input into a multi-layer CNN model. In this CNN model, a maximum pooling layer succeeds each convolution layer, retaining only the highest convolutional values, and utilizes the ReLU activation function. The output pc of the CNN model is then passed to the FC layer for scoring the sequence.

As shown in Fig. 1, we obtain sequence-based prediction pt and codon-based prediction pc through two separate channels. The final prediction is then integrated using function fpt,pc, ensuring that no potential virus reads are overlooked to maximize the prediction of virus reads. In this context, we choose the ‘max’ function for fpt,pc.

The outputs from the two channels are respectively used to calculate the binary cross-entropy loss against the ground truth. We optimize the model by minimizing the sum of these two losses, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate set at 0.001. The model is designed to stop training early if the loss does not decrease below the minimum loss value for 20 consecutive epochs. It’s worth noting that ViTrace is trained only once on the training data, and all subsequent experiments do not involve retraining the model.

Data pre-processing

In downstream tasks, we conducted separate analyses for bulk RNA-Seq data and scRNA-seq data. In the bulk RNA-Seq analysis, we obtained publicly available RNA sequencing datasets in FASTQ format directly from the NCBI SRA database, corresponding to well-characterized virus–host systems. To ensure data quality, we selected high-quality reads and performed alignment using Bowtie 237 against the GRC/h38 and ENST. Only the reads that did not map to these references were preserved, served as input for the ViTrace framework to predict viral reads.

In the scRNA-seq analysis, we downloaded SRA files of OPSCC tumors from the NCBI’s GEO website. These SRA files were then processed using SRA tools to convert them into raw Fastq files. Subsequently, Cell Ranger was employed to convert the Fastq files into BAM files. Alignment was performed using Bowtie 2 against the GRC/h38 and ENST. Only the unmapped reads were retained and converted into a Fasta file representing individual cells. These files were then inputted into ViTrace to predict viral reads. For a detailed workflow, please refer to the Supplemen8.

Filtering contaminants

To make the results more reliable, following the pipeline proposed by Marc et al.5, we filter out contaminated reads in our datasets. Specifically, we applied Bowtie237 for phiX174 phage alignment to discard the matching reads, for the phiX174 phage is usually used as a control in sequencing. Meanwhile, we dropped the reads of Caudovirales (taxonomy ID 28883) which are tailed bacteriophages primarily targeting bacteria, and Baculoviridae that infects insect cells. The remaining reads were considered as reliable input into ViTrace. For reads identified by the model as potential viruses, further validation was performed using BLASTX against the NCBI non-redundant (NR) protein database. Sequences exhibiting homology to known laboratory contaminants (e.g., cloning vectors, bacterial plasmids) or host-derived elements (e.g., endogenous retroviruses) were excluded based on stringent alignment thresholds (E−value<1e−5). Only high-confidence sequences that passed these filters were retained for downstream viral identification by ViTrace.

Strategies for handling long reads

ViTrace was initially trained on the reads with a length of 48 bp. To avoid extra training when handling the long reads that exceed 48 bp, we segmented them into the 48 bp-length reads by the stride of 48 bp. Specifically, if the final segment of a long read falls short of 48 bp, this final segment will be segmented from the terminal end to keep the uniform length of 48 bp. The predicted scores by ViTrace of all 48 bp-length segments were averaged to obtain the final score for the long read.
