Comparison of inguinal hernia repair outcomes between open and laparoscopic approaches in a pediatric population: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wahyudhy Adriansyah, Aditya Rifqi Fauzi, Verrell Christopher Amadeus, Tiara Putri Leksono, Qisthinadia Hazhiyah Setiadi, Andi Dwihantoro, Akhmad Makhmudi, Gunadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMany studies compared the outcomes of hernia repair in children between open and laparoscopic techniques. However, the results still need to be clarified. Moreover, the reports from developing countries are also limited.MethodsWe compared the outcomes of hernia repair between the open and laparoscopic techniques in children and associated them with risk factors.ResultsWe involved 92 children, consisting of laparoscopy (36 males and 10 females) and open (42 males and 4 females) methods. There was a significant difference between 2 groups regarding the gestational age and age at surgery (both P < 0.001). Laparoscopy had a shorter length of stay (LOS) than the open group (1.09 ± 0.28 days vs. 1.37 ± 0.57 days, P = 0.004), while the duration of surgery was similar in both groups (P = 0.801). Multivariate analysis revealed no significant association between the recurrence of hernia and risk factors, including the surgical procedure, sex, age at surgery, and prematurity (P > 0.05).ConclusionLaparoscopic surgery might have a beneficial effect on shorter LOS compared with open hernia repair. None of the risk factors is significantly associated with hernia recurrence after repair. A further prospective multicenter study with a larger sample size is necessary to clarify our findings.

INTRODUCTION

Inguinal hernia repair is the most common surgical procedure in the pediatric population. Its incidence is approximately 0.8% to 4.4% [1]. Surgical treatment is essential to reduce the risk of complications, including the incarceration of the bowel, testis, or ovary [2].

The recurrence rate after hernia repair in children varies among studies, ranging from 0.3% to 10.9% [3]. Some risk factors have been associated with hernia recurrence, including surgical approach, prematurity, and age at repair [3]. Many studies compared the outcomes of hernia repair in children between open and laparoscopic techniques [4]. The laparoscopic procedure makes the visualization of contralateral patent processus vaginalis feasible and simultaneously closed, while the open technique shows an advantage in the possibility of locoregional anesthesia to avoid repeated general anesthesia that might cause danger to the development of a child’s brain [5]. Moreover, the laparoscopic technique reveals a shorter length of stay (LOS) for bilateral hernia repair and a lower complication rate [6], whereas the open method shows a lower recurrence rate [7]. However, other studies showed conflicting findings [48]. Moreover, the reports from developing countries are also limited [46789]. A previous study from a developing country showed that the laparoscopic method might reduce the possibility of a second surgery for a metachronous contralateral inguinal hernia (MCIH) [9]. Our study aimed to compare the outcomes of hernia repair between the open and laparoscopic techniques in children.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Gadjah Mada/Dr. Sardjito General Hospital, Indonesia, approved the study beforehand (No. KE/FK/0908/EC/2021). Informed consent for study participation was obtained from the patient’s parents and/or legal guardians. The research has been performed following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient samples

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of pediatric patients with an inguinal hernia at Universitas Gadjah Mada Academic Health System Hospital, Indonesia, who underwent open and laparoscopic surgery from January 2015 to December 2020. The decision on the surgical approach was based on the attending surgeon’s discretion. We retrospectively included 92 children diagnosed with inguinal hernia (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code K40). The exclusion criteria were incomplete medical records, strangulated hernia, and chromosomal anomalies with different types of syndromes.

Risk factors and outcomes

The following risk factors were associated with the outcomes of inguinal hernia surgery: surgical approach, sex, age at surgery, and gestational age. Surgical approach, age at surgery, and gestational age were divided into open and laparoscopic technique, <6 and ≥6 months [10], and preterm and term, respectively.

The primary outcome was a recurrence of inguinal hernia, while the secondary outcome of LOS. The patients were discharged from the hospital if no complications were found.

Open hernia repair

Open hernia repair was conducted using a skin crease incision. An absorbable suture was used for high ligation of the sac. Subsequently, an absorbable suture was used to close the wound in layers.

Laparoscopic procedure

The subjects were supine positioned. After an infraumbilical incision, the abdomen was inflated and maintained with pneumoperitoneum of CO2 with a pressure of 10 mmHg. Three ports were used: a 3-mm scope at the umbilicus and 2 lateral ports of 3 mm at the pararectal region. A laparoscopic ring closure was conducted with a 4-0 absorbable purse-string suture with the cautious on spermatic vessels and vas deferens.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as numbers/percentages and means. The normality of the data distribution was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Mann-Whitney U-test and chi-square or Fisher exact tests were used to evaluate group differences. IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25 (IBM Corp.) was used for statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

We involved 92 children with inguinal hernia. Most of them were male (84.8%), born at term (70.7%), and aged ≥6 months at surgery (64.1%) [11]. There was a significant difference between the 2 groups regarding the gestational age and age at surgery (both P < 0.001). None of them suffered from surgical site infection (SSI) (Table 1).

The laparoscopic and open groups had 3 and 0 bilateral inguinal hernia repairs, respectively. In addition, no MCIH was found in the open group, while 3 of 46 cases (6.5%) were MCIH in the laparoscopic group.

Association between risk factors and recurrence of inguinal hernia after surgery

The recurrence rate of inguinal hernia was 7.6%. There was no significant association between risk factors and the recurrence of inguinal hernia, including surgical approach, sex, age at surgery, and gestational age (P = 0.677, P = 0.225, P > 0.999, and P > 0.999, respectively) (Table 2).

Association between surgical approach and both duration of surgery and length of stay after hernia repair

The LOS was significantly shorter in the laparoscopy than in the open procedure group (1.09 ± 0.28 days vs. 1.37 ± 0.57 days, P = 0.004). However, the duration of surgery was not significantly different between groups (P = 0.801) (Table 3).

Multivariate analysis of risk factors for recurrence of inguinal hernia after surgery

Multivariate analysis showed that none of the risk factors affected the recurrence of inguinal hernia (P > 0.05) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study shows that the laparoscopic group might have a shorter LOS than the open approach. These findings are consistent with previous studies [612]. It might be associated with minimal incisions and faster healing in the laparoscopic method. However, other studies show the same outcomes between open and laparoscopic techniques, including LOS and parents’ satisfaction [1314]. Our study provides new evidence that laparoscopic surgery has the advantage of shorter LOS than open hernia repair in a particular developing country, Indonesia.

We involved more male than female patients, with a ratio of 5.5:1, which is compatible with previous studies [1516]. This is due to the difference in the anatomical structure between males and females. A testis will descend from the intra-abdominal cavity to the scrotum through the processus vaginalis in males. If this process fails, it leads to a hernia [15].

We show an overall recurrence rate after hernia repair of 7.6%. This finding is still within the range of the reported recurrence rate after hernia repair in children in many studies, from 0.3% to 10.9% [3]. Our study also reveals that the recurrence rate did not significantly differ between the open technique (3.3%) and the laparoscopic procedure (4.3%). Our results are consistent with previous studies [1417]. In addition, the recurrence rate after open surgery is from 0 to 5%, whereas the recurrence rate after the laparoscopic repair is from 0 to 4.4% [14].

According to a previous study, inguinal hernia in patients with prematurity and young age is suggested to be repaired using an open rather than a laparoscopic technique. Our study was compatible with these findings [13]. Our study shows that 50% of the open group had a history of prematurity, while only 13% of the laparoscopic group had a history of prematurity. In addition, most open repair (67.4%) was performed in the younger age group (<6 months old), while most laparoscopic surgery (93.5%) was performed in the older age group (≥6 months old). Laparoscopic surgery in premature and young patients is challenging because of the relatively narrow operating field. It might lead to hernia recurrence, injury to adjacent tissues, prolonged surgical duration, and anesthetic problems [13].

In addition, prematurity as a risk factor for recurrence after hernia repair in children is controversial [3181920]. We failed to prove prematurity as a risk factor for the recurrence after repair. Our findings were compatible with multiple studies [318].

Sex is a controversial risk factor for recurrence after surgery in the pediatric population [2122]. Our study did not show any difference in the recurrence rate of inguinal hernia between male and female patients. These findings may be due to a patient selection bias in choosing patients receiving laparoscopic and open surgery in this study. Our study shows a predominance of females and more significant subjects in the laparoscopic group. Moreover, our findings are compatible with previous studies [1421].

Several studies showed that hernia repair at a young age is a risk factor for hernia recurrence [32122]. However, our study found no association between age at hernia repair and recurrence. Interestingly, we reveal no difference in surgical duration between the open and the laparoscopic techniques. The comparison of surgical duration between open and laparoscopic surgery showed conflicting findings [1723]. Some reports revealed that open repair had a shorter surgical duration than the laparoscopic approach [232425], while 1 study concluded otherwise [14].

SSI is not detected in our patients, either in the open or laparoscopic approach group. These findings might be due to none of the incarcerated and strangulated hernias being included in our study. The risk of SSI is higher in incarcerated and strangulated hernia because of bacterial contamination in the hernia sac of those hernia types [26]. A previous study reported that the SSI rate after hernia repair tends to be low [14]. In addition, no significant difference in SSI rate was noted between the open and laparoscopic approaches [4].

The incidence of MCIH in open surgery in children is approximately 6% [27]. Therefore, they suggested not performing routine contralateral inguinal exploration in children without MCIH risk factors, including males and those aged >1 year. Our study shows that no MCIH was found in the open group, while 6.5% of cases were MCIH in the laparoscopic group. Our findings further imply that conducting routine contralateral groin exploration in children without any MCIH risk factors is unnecessary.

Our research had limitations, including a small sample size, single institution, and retrospective design. Notably, our study might have a patient selection bias in choosing patients receiving laparoscopic versus open surgery in this study. There is a preponderance of female and larger patients in the laparoscopic group. These facts might be associated with the preference of the attending surgeon to choose the surgical approach. Moreover, the variation in the surgeon’s competence and experience, including the attending surgeon, trainee, and resident, who perform the surgery in this study, might affect the outcomes. Despite being a retrospective data analysis, proper patient selection is crucial in the comparison study.

Moreover, open hernia repair might be conducted on an outpatient basis and without hospitalization, as well as many of those performed laparoscopically, with hospitalization reserved for premature infants or infants under 1 month of age. Our study was not homogeneous in the number of children under 6 months and premature infants. Furthermore, our study did not compare some essential surgery outcomes, including patient satisfaction and postoperative pain.

In conclusion, laparoscopic surgery might have a beneficial effect on shorter LOS compared with open hernia repair. Moreover, none of the risk factors is significantly associated with hernia recurrence after repair. A further prospective multicenter study with a larger sample size is necessary to clarify and confirm our findings.
