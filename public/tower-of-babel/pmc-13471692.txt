Cervical spine chordoma: Imaging features and diagnostic challenges of a rare C6 localization
Authors: Salma EL Kadiri, Mehdi Dhamnia, Ouijdane Zamani, Aymane EL Farouki, Zakaria Toufga, Issam Ennafaa, Hassan Ennouali, Jamal EL Fenni, Rachida Saouab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChordomas are rare malignant tumors arising from remnants of the embryonic notochord. They are slow-growing but locally aggressive. They most frequently occur in the sacrococcygeal and skull base regions, while cervical spine involvement is rare. Their proximity to critical neurovascular structures makes diagnosis and management challenging.We report the case of a 62-year-old man presenting with a progressive left cervical mass, dysphonia, and a foreign body sensation in the lower neck. Clinical examination revealed a firm, immobile cervical swelling with mild displacement of adjacent laryngeal structures. MRI demonstrated a large, destructive osteolytic lesion centered at the C6 vertebra, suggestive of chordoma. Surgical treatment was performed. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed a conventional chordoma. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no neurological deficit.Chordoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of aggressive cervical spine lesions, particularly at atypical lower cervical levels. Multimodal imaging is essential for diagnosis, surgical planning, and assessment of tumor extent.Keywords: Chordoma, Cervical vertebrae, Magnetic resonance imaging, Dysphonia, Case report

Introduction

Chordomas are uncommon, slowly growing, but locally aggressive malignant tumors that develop from the notochord's embryonic remains. The cervical spine is still a rare primary site, accounting for around 6% of all cases, even though they mostly impact the sacrococcygeal and skull base regions [1].

These lesions pose serious diagnostic issues because of the cervical region's complicated anatomy and close proximity to important neurovascular structures. To assess the degree of soft tissue involvement and the distinctive osteolytic bone degradation, advanced imaging, especially Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), is crucial [2]. Planning the “gold standard” treatment, which entails radical surgical resection, requires an accurate radiological evaluation.

We present the case of a 62-year-old man who had a palpable neck mass and dysphonia due to a huge invasive chordoma localized on the C6 vertebra. The particular radiographic characteristics and surgical treatment of this uncommon condition are covered in this study.

Case report

A 62-year-old male with an unremarkable medical history presented with a six-month history of a slowly progressing, painless mass in the left lateral cervical region. The clinical onset was insidious, with the patient further reporting a gradual development of dysphonia (increasing hoarseness) and a persistent foreign body sensation in the lower neck.

The patient first consulted the Otolaryngology (ENT) department due to these symptoms. Upon initial inspection, a large, visible mass was noted on the left side of the neck. Physical examination confirmed that the swelling was hard, immobile, and nontender. Subsequently, flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy was performed, revealing significant displacement of the laryngeal structures and prominent bulging of the left hypopharyngeal wall, which was consistent with external compression. Given the tumor's impressive size and these clinical findings, the ENT surgeons suspected a deep-seated cervical malignancy and requested urgent radiological investigations.

MRI of the cervical spine, as an integral part of the diagnostic process, revealed a massive, invasive osteolytic lesion centered on the C6 vertebra with aggressive longitudinal expansion from C5 down to the T2 level. The mass shows a markedly heterogeneous signal, characterized by predominant T2-weighted hyperintensity and restricted diffusion, with heterogeneous enhancement following gadolinium administration (Fig.1).

(A) Sagittal T2-weighted MRI demonstrating a voluminous, heterogeneous hyperintense mass centered on C6 with anterior expansion into the prevertebral space, causing a significant mass effect on the larynx and involvement of the danger space (arrows). (B) Axial T2-weighted MRI demonstrates extensive destruction of the C6 vertebral body and involvement of the articular processes (arrows). (C) Axial Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) demonstrating restricted diffusion within the lesion, suggesting high cellularity. (D) Axial contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI showing heterogeneous enhancement of the mass, with complete encasement of the both transverse foramen while maintaining arterial patency (arrows).

Anteriorly, the tumor infiltrates the prevertebral space, exerting a severe mass effect on the pharyngoesophageal junction. Notably, while the rigid cartilaginous larynx is primarily displaced, the more compliant musculomembranous structures of the hypopharynx and proximal esophagus are significantly narrowed, correlating with the patient’s progressive dysphagia.

The lesion extends posteriorly to infiltrate the articular processes, with complete encasement of both transverse foramina; notably, both vertebral arteries remain patent without significant luminal narrowing.

Furthermore, the tumor involves the adjacent neural foramina, nearly encompassing the exiting nerve roots. Longitudinal spread is also observed within the “danger space.”

Following radiological diagnosis, the patient was referred to the neurosurgical team. Given the extent of the lesion and the need for both tumor control and spinal stability, a combined anterior and posterior approach was performed. The posterior stage consisted of decompression with laminectomy and instrumented stabilization, while the anterior stage allowed partial corpectomy of C6 and tumor resection. This combined strategy enabled adequate tumor debulking while ensuring spinal reconstruction and protection of adjacent neurovascular structures (Fig.2).

(A) Sagittal bone-window CT image showing partial C6 corpectomy and posterior instrumentation (arrow). (B) Axial soft-tissue-window CT image demonstrating the postoperative appearance following tumor resection.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical investigations confirmed the diagnosis of a conventional chordoma, with tumor cells showing positivity for Brachyury. The neurosurgical team managed the postoperative recovery uneventfully, with no new neurological impairments. The patient’s dysphonia improved significantly, and the surgical reconstruction was stable at the latest follow-up.

Discussion

Cervical chordomas present diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their rarity, locally aggressive behavior, and proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Although they grow slowly, they often reach a large size by the time of diagnosis due to their insidious clinical course and nonspecific symptoms [3,4]. Within the cervical spine, the upper cervical region, particularly C2, is most frequently affected, while lower cervical involvement is rare [3,5].

Clinically, cervical chordomas present with nonspecific, progressive symptoms related to bone destruction and mass effect on adjacent structures. The most common symptom is neck pain, while neurological deficits, dysphagia, and airway-related symptoms may be observed in advanced cases [3,4]. Dysphonia, as observed in our patient, is uncommon and likely reflects the significant local mass effect of the large C6 lesion that involves adjacent soft tissues.

In cases of destructive lesions of the lower cervical spine, especially at C6, the differential diagnosis is extensive and includes primary and secondary malignant bone tumors. Metastatic disease is the most common consideration in adults and can present as a lytic vertebral body lesion accompanied by a soft tissue mass. Plasmacytoma and multiple myeloma are important differential diagnoses, typically presenting as expansile osteolytic lesions with variable extraosseous extension.

Chondrosarcoma should also be considered, although it typically originates eccentrically from the posterior elements and exhibits similar chondroid matrix calcifications. Lymphoma can affect the vertebral body and paravertebral soft tissues, but it usually preserves the bone architecture to a greater extent than the soft tissue component. Although rare in the cervical spine, giant cell tumor may also be included in the differential diagnosis in appropriate clinical settings. In this context, imaging features such as a destructive lesion centered on the vertebral body with a large soft tissue component and marked T2 hyperintensity favor a diagnosis of chordoma, although histopathological confirmation is still necessary [4,6].

Imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis and preoperative planning. Computed tomography (CT) typically reveals aggressive osteolytic lesions with cortical destruction and occasional intratumoral calcifications [4]. Magnetic resonance imaging is the preferred method for evaluating tumor extent, especially soft tissue invasion, epidural extension, and its relationship with the spinal cord, nerve roots, and vertebral arteries. Chordomas classically exhibit low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences due to their myxoid matrix. They demonstrate heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration [4,6]. These imaging characteristics are essential for planning the surgical strategy.

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment. En bloc resection with negative margins has been shown to improve local control and long-term survival. However, this approach is often challenging in the cervical spine due to the proximity of critical anatomical structures [5]. Consequently, subtotal resection is often performed, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy. High-dose proton beam or carbon ion therapy has emerged as an effective option for improving local tumor control while minimizing damage to surrounding tissues [7].

Despite advances in multimodal treatment, chordomas are associated with a high risk of local recurrence due to their infiltrative growth pattern and difficulty achieving wide surgical margins. Therefore, long-term radiological follow-up is essential. Prognosis mainly depends on tumor size, anatomical location, and completeness of surgical resection [3,7]. This case underscores the importance of considering chordoma in the differential diagnosis of aggressive lower cervical spine lesions and highlights the crucial role of multimodal imaging in diagnosis, staging, and therapeutic planning.

Due to the anatomical constraints of the cervical spine and the proximity of critical neurovascular and aerodigestive structures, achieving wide surgical margins in cervical chordomas is often difficult, especially for extensive lesions. Consequently, a multidisciplinary approach combining maximal safe resection and adjuvant radiotherapy is often necessary for cases involving residual disease or high-risk features [3,5].

Although surgical resection is still the primary treatment, adjuvant radiotherapy is essential for improving local tumor control. In this context, proton beam therapy (PBT) has become the preferred treatment for skull base and spinal chordomas over conventional photon-based radiotherapy. PBT's physical properties, particularly the Bragg peak effect, enable high radiation doses to be delivered to the target volume while minimizing exposure to adjacent critical structures, such as the spinal cord and esophagus [6,7]. In the present case, the proximity of the tumor to the pharyngoesophageal junction further supports the potential advantage of PBT in reducing the risk of late, radiation-induced complications.

Conclusion

Cervical chordomas are rare tumors that typically originate in the sacrococcygeal region or the skull base. Spinal involvement is uncommon. This case highlights the exceptional nature of a voluminous chordoma located at C6, an unusual site within the mobile spine. The case underscores the importance of multimodal imaging for accurate diagnosis, local staging, and surgical planning. It also emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach that combines maximal safe resection and adjuvant radiotherapy. Recognizing this atypical presentation is crucial to avoiding diagnostic delays and optimizing patient management.
