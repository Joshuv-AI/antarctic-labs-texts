Cost-effectiveness analysis of home-based palliative care for end-stage cancer patients
Authors: Parnian Kubor, Ahmad Reza Pourghaderi, Maryam Moeeni, Reza Rezayatmand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal increases in cancer prevalence and the associated financial burden necessitate the development of highly cost-effective care models. Optimally managing scarce healthcare resources is crucial to balance the goals of maximizing survival for curable patients and quality of life for those with terminal illness. This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a novel home-based palliative care model for end-stage cancer patients that utilizes Artificial Intelligence (AI) for operational optimization, compared to usual care.MethodsThis case-control study compared costs and outcomes for two groups of end-stage cancer patients. The intervention group consisted of 94 patients receiving home-based palliative care from an Iranian NGO, Ala, which uses an AI-driven management system for resource planning. The control group included 113 patients receiving usual care, comprising outpatient services and hospitalization as needed. The groups were matched for cancer type, stage, age, and gender. We calculated the costs of inpatient and outpatient clinical and para-clinical services over a 6-month period. Outcome measures were hospital length of stay (LOS) and the proportion of deaths occurring at home.ResultsHome-based model demonstrated significant advantages. A higher proportion of deaths occurred at home in the intervention group compared to the control group (45.7% vs. 23.9%; p = 0.001). The mean LOS was lower for the intervention group (14.8 days vs. 20.5 days; p = 0.052). The mean total costs were also significantly lower for the intervention group (\$8,860 vs. \$13,172; p = 0.019). Incremental analysis confirmed home-based care as a dominant strategy, providing better outcomes at a lower cost.ConclusionHome-based palliative care model proved to be a dominant and cost-effective strategy for end-stage cancer patients, resulting in more home deaths, reduced hospital stays, and lower costs. We recommend the inclusion of such optimized home-based palliative care in health insurance benefits packages and suggest that the integration of AI for demand forecasting and resource planning is a key component for its scalability and efficiency.

Introduction

The global prevalence of chronic diseases, including cancer, is increasing. Recent analyses from the Global Burden of Disease study project a 47% rise in global cancer cases between 2020 and 2040, underscoring the growing burden on health systems worldwide [1, 2]. According to GLOBOCAN 2020 estimates, there were 19.3 million new cancer cases and almost 10 million deaths from cancer in that year alone. The disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in all world regions, regardless of their level of development [3].

The majority of cancer cases occur in the developing world, where access to curative treatment is often limited [4]. In these settings, palliative care is a cost-effective and essential component of comprehensive cancer care that responds to patient needs and improves quality of life [5]. The World Health Organization emphasizes palliative care as a core component of universal health coverage, an imperative reinforced by recent Lancet Commission reports [4].

A central tenet of palliative care is to respect patient preferences. Numerous studies indicate that most patients and their family members prefer the patient to die at home, which is considered an indicator of a higher quality of life [6–8]. A recent systematic review confirms this strong preference, highlighting the critical importance of care models that can honor this choice [9]. Research confirms that most cancer patients, particularly those with advanced chronic diseases, prefer to receive services at home [9, 10]. Evidence consistently demonstrates that individuals receiving home-based care are significantly more likely to die at home [11–13]. Furthermore, home-based palliative care is associated with less frequent hospital admissions, fewer emergency room visits, and a shorter length of stay in hospital [14–16].

In Iran, the need for efficient palliative care models is pressing. In 2020, there were 131,191 new cancer cases and 79,136 deaths [3], and a considerable portion of scarce critical healthcare resources, such as ICU beds, is occupied by end-stage cancer patients [17]. Moreover, home care is not covered by the main health insurance providers in the country. This study was therefore initiated to investigate the cost-effectiveness of a home-based cancer palliative care model developed by Ala Cancer Prevention and Control Center (MACSA), a leading not-for-profit organization in Iran. MACSA provides 24/7 remote consultation and home-based periodic and emergency visits, including medical, nursing, psychological, social work, spiritual, and rehabilitation services for end-stage cancer patients.

MACSA is a charity and non-governmental organization pioneering palliative care in Iran [18–20]. It offers a comprehensive package of services in a flexible setting of outpatient clinics, home hospitalization, hospitals, and hospices. Currently, MACSA is the largest home-based healthcare cluster in Iran and the key partner of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, which is responsible for leading home care service nationally in field of cancer support and palliative care under a strategic agreement. MACSA currently provides free services to cancer patients in Tehran and Isfahan and has machine learning According to MACSA’s service model patients with the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS) of 40 and below are eligible for home-based services. PPS is a valid instrument to measure physical functional performance in patients. It assesses five functional parameters i.e. degree of ambulation, ability to do activities and extent of disease, ability to do self-care, food and fluid intake, and state of consciousness. PPS has been found useful for purposes of identifying and tracking potential care needs of palliative care patients, particularly as these needs change with disease progression and survival prediction [21, 22]. MACSA developed and applied a novel Management Information System (MIS) empowered by advanced Artificial Intelligent (AI) tools where PPS (recorded in every visit) is a key input to effective predictive models to improve the quality of care and productivity by optimal resource and operations planning. The details explanation of the system can be found elsewhere [23].

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first cost-effectiveness analysis of home-based palliative care in Iran and the first to evaluate a model optimized with sophisticated AI tools. The findings are intended to inform insurance policies and support the inclusion of palliative home care services in benefits packages at the national level. We hypothesized that home-based palliative care for end-stage cancer patients would be a cost-effective strategy, leading to lower total healthcare costs and better outcomes compared to usual care.

Methods

Study design

This economic evaluation was conducted from the perspective of the Iranian health system, comparing the costs and outcomes of home-based palliative care with usual palliative care for end-stage cancer patients. The study employed a case-control design, where the intervention group (cases) received home-based care and the control group received usual care.

Study setting and participants

The study sample consisted of two groups of end-stage cancer patients: an intervention group that received home-based palliative care services from MACSA, and a control group that received usual care (primarily outpatient services and inpatient hospital care as needed).

Data for the control group were extracted from the records of Omid Hospital (a specialist cancer hospital in Isfahan) and the Vital Statistics Center of Isfahan Health Center. Patients were included in the control group if they: (1) had passed away at the time of data collection, (2) were certified by an oncologist as end-stage cancer patients, (3) were diagnosed with a common cancer (leukemia, lung, liver, stomach, breast, or colon cancer), and (4) were insured by the Social Security Organization (SSO) or Health Insurance Organization (HIO).

The intervention group comprised patients referred to MACSA from Omid Hospital. As mentioned above, MACSA is a non-governmental charity organization with the largest home care network for cancer patients in Iran. Its multidisciplinary team includes specialists, general practitioners, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, social workers, psychologists, and spiritual caregivers. MACSA uses the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS) to assess patients’ health status at admission and to track changes monthly. Patients with a PPS score of 40% or below are classified as end-stage and enrolled in the home care program. The intervention group inclusion criteria mirrored those of the control group, with the additional requirement that patients were categorized as end-stage based on their PPS score.

Sampling technique and group matching

A multi-stage matching process was used to select comparable groups and reduce confounding bias. First, patients who had received both usual care from Omid Hospital and home care from MACSA were excluded. Second, the two groups were manually matched based on key demographic and clinical characteristics: age, gender, type of insurance, and cancer type and stage. This process initially yielded 128 matched pairs. However, due to incomplete cost data for some patients, the final sample size was 94 in the intervention group and 113 in the control group.

Data collection and costing

From the health system perspective, only direct medical costs were calculated for the last six months of life. Cost data were gathered from insurance claims (SSO and HIO), the Iranian Ministry of Health’s database (SEPAS), and MACSA’s records.

Cost components included

Physician visits: Estimated from the frequency of dispensed drug prescriptions and para-clinical services (each prescription dispensation and every three para-clinical service records were considered equivalent to one physician visit). A weighted average cost was applied.

Para-clinical services and medication: Valued based on insurance claims data.

Hospitalization: Costs and length of stay were obtained from the SEPAS system.

Home care services: Costs were calculated based on MACSA’s service records and official tariffs, including medical specialist and GP visits (regular/on-call), nursing care, telephone consultations, psychological and spiritual care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, medication, and transportation.

Outcome measures and data analysis

The primary outcome measures were: hospital length of stay (LOS), and the proportion of deaths occurring at home (Home Death).

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, percentages) were used to summarize participant characteristics and costs. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables (e.g., costs, LOS) were made using independent t-tests, while categorical variables (e.g., home death rate, gender) were compared using Chi-square tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study found that home-based palliative care is a dominant strategy, providing better outcomes at a lower cost compared to usual care for end-stage cancer patients in Iran. Our hypothesis was supported, as the intervention group demonstrated significantly reduced total costs, shorter hospital stays, and a higher rate of home deaths.

The significant cost reduction was primarily due to substantial savings on inpatient care, which constituted 74% of the total cost in the control group compared to 53% in the intervention group. The sensitivity analysis confirmed that hospitalization cost is the most influential factor. Notably, the direct cost of providing home care was only \$281 per patient (less than 4% of the total cost), yet it was associated with a 33% reduction in overall costs. This demonstrates that a modest investment in home-based services can effectively curb high inpatient costs, which is crucial for containing national healthcare expenditures. The reduction in hospital length of stay also frees up critical resources, such as beds and specialist time, for other patients in need.

Our results are consistent with international studies. For instance, Bentur et al. and McCaffrey et al. also reported lower costs for cancer patients receiving home or hospice care [24, 25]. Other studies reported increasing patient satisfaction, and significant cost saving for palliative care in non-cancer patients [26–28]. As there is no prior cost analysis of palliative care for end-stage cancer patients in Iran, our results fill a critical evidence gap. However, a study on stroke patients in Iran similarly found home care to be less costly and more effective than hospital care [29].

The implications of these findings are relevant for several key stakeholders. For patients and their families, the significantly increased rate of home death directly addresses a well-documented preference, thereby contributing to an enhanced quality of life at the end of life and fostering a more dignified experience for all involved. For policymakers and health insurance providers, this study offers robust economic evidence to guide necessary policy changes. The inclusion of home-based palliative care in health insurance benefits packages emerges not merely as a clinical enhancement but as a financially prudent strategy, representing an efficient reallocation of scarce resources to achieve superior outcomes at a lower cost. For the broader health system, the widespread adoption of this model presents a viable solution to alleviate pressure on overburdened hospital services. By reducing the length of hospital stays, the model frees up critical resources, such as ICU beds and specialist time, for other patients. Furthermore, the integration of advanced AI tools for resource planning, as demonstrated in the MACSA model, underscores the potential for scalable, efficient, and intelligent management of palliative care services nationally.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of access to inpatient data from private hospitals means that the healthcare costs for patients who spent their last months in private clinics were not captured, potentially leading to an underestimation of costs in the control group. Second, the final sample size was reduced due to inconsistencies in data recording across different systems, which may affect the statistical power. Third, the study was conducted in a specific regional context in Iran, which may **limit the generalizability and external validity** of the findings to other provinces or countries with different healthcare infrastructures and financing mechanisms. Finally, the study did not capture out-of-pocket payments or indirect costs, such as caregiver time and travel expenses, which are important aspects of the economic burden on families.

Conclusion

This is the first cost-effectiveness study in Iran to demonstrate that home-based palliative care is a dominant strategy compared to usual care for end-stage cancer patients, resulting in decreased costs, increased home deaths, and reduced hospital stays. Based on these findings, we recommend that health insurance providers in Iran actively incorporate home-based palliative care services into their standard benefits packages. Furthermore, the successful MACSA model, particularly its use of AI for operational planning, should be studied and adapted for implementation in other regions of Iran to expand access to this effective form of care. Future research should aim to include a broader range of costs, such as out-of-pocket and indirect expenses, and should more comprehensively assess the impact on patient-reported outcomes like quality of life and family caregiver satisfaction.
