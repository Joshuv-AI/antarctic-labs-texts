The federal government’s 2017 commitment to $5 billion in targeted funding for mental health and substance use health (MHSUH) over 10 years1 signified an unprecedented federal effort to improve access to mental health care. Through bilateral agreements with provinces and territories, the government sought to close long-standing gaps that have persisted and broadened since psychiatric hospitals were first excluded from public hospital insurance in 1957.2 With the funding commitment approaching its scheduled conclusion in 2027, and as provincial governments negotiate the next phase of health-transfer agreements, now is the time to examine what this investment achieved, what lessons emerged from its implementation, and what role the federal government should play in financing MHSUH going forward.

The 2017 commitment emerged from nearly a decade of advocacy and policy development. In its 2012 strategy, the Mental Health Commission of Canada set a target to increase mental health’s share of health spending from 7% to 9%,3 anchored in a strong case for investment tied to an estimated $50 billion cost to the economy per year.4 The Better Access initiative in Australia and the Improving Access to Psychological Therapies program in the United Kingdom provided examples of how dedicated national investment could begin to address unmet need and strengthen workforce productivity.5 The federal government’s shift toward collaborative federalism also paved the way for shared federal–provincial–territorial health priorities in 2016,6 with federal leadership from Finance Minister Bill Morneau and Health Minister Jane Philpott.

Looking back at the 4 requirements for making the most of the $5 billion that I outlined in CMAJ in 2017,7 the transfer and associated investments in system infrastructure achieved mixed success in several domains in addressing long-standing MHSUH policy gaps. Transparency improved through public bilateral agreements and action plans,8 compared with the broad principles governing the Canada Health Transfer under the Canada Health Act. Accountability mechanisms advanced, particularly through the development of new indicators and reporting initiatives led by the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) with federal funding.9 Most existing indicators are based on hospital and physician billing data, which exclude MHSUH services provided by nonphysician providers and community agencies. Development of the community mental health services wait times indicator has begun to address this long-standing problem. However, limitations in expenditure tracking and outcome measurement make it difficult to determine whether the transfers increased overall MHSUH investment or simply displaced existing provincial and territorial expenditures, and whether MHSUH outcomes improved overall (particularly in light of the MHSUH effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and the opioid crisis). The least progress was made in fostering interjurisdictional learning and building an implementation research agenda dedicated to the targeted mental health transfers.10

Although attribution remains challenging, the transfer appears to have spurred investments that would otherwise have been unlikely to occur on the same scale. Bilateral agreements8 committed provinces and territories to specific initiatives that have expanded access to care in priority areas, which included youth mental health services, community-based care, and substance use programs. In many instances these commitments were honoured; initiatives included expanded integrated youth hubs such as Foundry in BC and Youth Wellness Hubs Ontario, community-based mental health programs such as Counselling Alberta, Rapid Access to Addictions Medicine clinics in Manitoba, and withdrawal management services in the Northwest Territories. Although MHSUH system infrastructure and program innovation still fall far short of what has been achieved in areas such as cancer care in Canada, these advances represent important progress in a sector in which decades of catch-up were needed after exclusion of important services from public funding.

In 2023, the federal government launched a Working Together transfer in response to health-system pressures emerging from the COVID-19 pandemic.8 Valued at $25 billion over 10 years, the new transfer allowed provinces and territories to decide how to allocate funds across 4 priorities — primary care, the health workforce, mental health, and health information. The 2017–2027 mental health transfers were integrated into the first tranche of Working Together bilateral agreements. Analyses undertaken by the Canadian Mental Health Association and the Canadian Alliance on Mental Illness and Mental Health11,12 suggest substantial variation in the share of new funding directed toward MHSUH. Some jurisdictions, including Alberta and Yukon, made extensive commitments to MHSUH, while British Columbia, Manitoba, and Prince Edward Island allocated none of their new federal funding to this priority.

While flexibility can support responsiveness to local needs, it also underscores a long-standing challenge in Canadian health financing: when MHSUH funding is not protected, investments may be crowded out by competing system pressures. Further, the federal government, in its 2026 spring economic update,13 announced that the annual Canada Health Transfer increase will fall from 5% to 3% in April 2028. Combined with newly dominant economic priorities such as infrastructure funding and defence, this reduced escalator will place additional pressure on provincial and territorial health budgets and, by extension, MHSUH spending.

The most important lesson of the 2017–2027 transfer is that clearly earmarked federal transfers can elevate MHSUH, a sector still catching up on system infrastructure, within provincial and territorial policy agendas, even when accountability mechanisms are imperfect.

As governments consider the future of targeted funding, 3 priorities emerge. First, the CIHI-led shared indicators framework should be sustained and expanded since it reflects a modest but important start in measuring MHSUH system quality. Second, a Canadian Institutes of Health Research call to evaluate targeted health transfers could strengthen the evidence needed to better maximize impact. Lastly, dedicated federal funding ensured that MHSUH was made a priority in 2017, whereas MHSUH’s position within the broader Working Together framework is much more contingent on provincial preference. A renewed dedicated MHSUH transfer, supported by expanded investments in system infrastructure, is warranted to build on the momentum of the past decade and bring MHSUH closer to parity with the other parts of the health sector.
