Repeated pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA1) in pregnancy associated with fetal growth restriction: case report and review of the literature
Authors: Hudi Youshaa, Yehia El Khawly, Saheed Shittu, Stephen Beer, Mohamed Abohafs, Sanaa Al Hassan, Kholode Al-Maslamani, Lolwa Alansari, Abdul Hadi Salem, Hakem Nazdaf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA) is a rare disorder characterized by aldosterone resistance, leading to hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis. It typically presents in infancy and often resolves spontaneously in childhood. Reactivation or recurrence during pregnancy is extremely rare and poses significant challenges for maternal and fetal management.CaseA 34-year-old woman pregnant G5P4 presented with threatened preterm labor and pruritus at 32 weeks of gestation. Laboratory findings revealed severe hyperkalemia (K + 6.2 mmol/L), hyponatremia (Na + 124 mmol/L). Her medical history was notable for childhood-onset pseudohypoaldosteronism, which resolved by the age of 5. Growth scan revealed fetal growth restriction, electrolyte disturbances were treated with insulin/dextrose infusion, sodium bicarbonate, and calcium gluconate, resulting in normalization of potassium levels. A 2190-gram (7%) female infant was delivered at 36 weeks of gestation for non-reassuring fetal CTG via uncomplicated cesarean section; the newborn had normal Apgar scores and umbilical cord pH.ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of reactivation of PHA 1 during pregnancy. The possibility that pregnancy can induce the recurrence of PHA underscores the importance of close monitoring and multidisciplinary management approach to improve maternal electrolyte complications and adverse fetal outcomes, such as growth restriction. Further studies are needed to better understand this rare phenomenon and improve care.

Introduction

Pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA) type 1, a rare inherited condition, was originally identified by Cheek and Perry back in 1958. It refers to a situation where the body’s tissues—particularly the kidneys—fail to respond adequately to aldosterone, even though the hormone’s levels in the blood may be normal or even elevated [1]. As a result of this resistance, affected individuals experience salt wasting, low sodium (hyponatremia), high potassium (hyperkalemia), and metabolic acidosis. This combination of imbalances can be life-threatening if not promptly recognized and managed [2, 3].

PHA type 1 typically manifests in infancy or early childhood and often shows spontaneous resolution with age. It’s quite rare for the condition to reappear later in life, particularly during adulthood [4]. There are two major subtypes of PHA type 1: the renal form, which follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and is usually linked to mutations in the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) gene, and the systemic form, inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, which involves mutations in epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) genes and tends to affect multiple organ systems [4, 5].

Pregnancy brings about major physiological changes, especially in the endocrine and renal systems. For example, aldosterone production can rise dramatically—by nearly tenfold—in order to support the expanded blood volume needed for fetal development [6]. Alongside this surge in hormones, increased kidney filtration and other dynamic shifts may unmask previously silent conditions like mineralocorticoid resistance [7]. While PHA is usually considered a pediatric disease, very few cases—fewer than ten worldwide—have been reported during pregnancy. Most of those involve PHA type 2 (Gordon syndrome), often linked to mutations in genes like KLHL3 [8–10].

On the other hand, PHA type 1 has been well described in newborns and infants, often surfacing through signs like prenatal fluid accumulation (hydrops fetalis) or severe salt-wasting episodes shortly after delivery [10, 11]. Still, no prior reports have documented a reactivation of previously resolved childhood-onset PHA type 1 during pregnancy.

This case highlights what we believe is, to the best of our knowledge, the first reported instance: reactivation of childhood PHA in a pregnant woman, complicated by significant electrolyte disturbances and fetal growth restriction (FGR). We describe the clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and collaborative care strategies that ultimately contributed to favorable outcomes for both the mother and the infant.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Gravida 5 para 4 came at 32 weeks gestation for threatened preterm labor in association with pruritus and decreased fetal movement.

Her past medical history was significant for childhood pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1, which had resolved spontaneously at the age of 5.

She was a known case of cholelithiasis and gestational diabetes on metformin and insulin. Her obstetric history included four cesarean sections, a previous neonatal death from congenital heart disease, and two previous episodes of obstetric cholestasis and gestational diabetes. No previous similar recurrence of PHA was reported in her earlier pregnancies.

Her second-trimester anomaly scan at 20 weeks was unremarkable, with fetal biometry consistent with gestational age. On admission, the patient had a good hemodynamic status with a blood pressure of 115/77 mmHg, a pulse rate of 104 beats/min, and a respiratory rate of 15 breaths/min.

Her symphysio-fundal height measured 29 cm. CTG showed regular contraction (one in 10 min) with a normal basal heart rate and variability. The cervical os was closed. Admission laboratory tests documented severe electrolyte disorder: hyperkalemia (K + 5.6 mmol/L), hyponatremia (Na + 128 mmol/L), and low bicarbonate (18.7 mmol/L).

Liver function tests showed elevated transaminases (AST 117 U/L, ALT 190 U/L) and bile acids (22.5 µmol/L). The patient was hospitalized due to the threatened preterm labor, and tocolysis with Atosiban, magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection, and betamethasone for fetal lung maturity were administered.

Ursodeoxycholic acid was initiated for obstetric cholestasis. During her admission, her condition worsened with dangerously peaked electrolyte levels: potassium to 6.2 mmol/L, sodium dropped to 124 mmol/L, and bicarbonate fell to 15 mmol/L, requiring transfer to the intensive care unit. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated compensated metabolic acidosis.

Diabetic ketoacidosis was ruled out based on normal glucose levels and absence of ketonuria. Other common causes of hyperkalemia, such as renal failure, adrenal insufficiency, and medication-induced causes (e.g., potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors), were also ruled out based on normal renal function tests and the absence of a relevant drug history. The electrolyte abnormalities persisted despite aggressive therapy, including the infusion of insulin/dextrose, calcium gluconate for cardio protection, and sodium bicarbonate and a potassium-free diet.

At that time, the patient’s father (a physician) provided us with additional information on the child’s medical history, for which she had a history of PHA and was treated with NaCl tablets and K binds until self-limited resolution at 5 years of age. A similar condition had affected her brothers/sisters and relatives that were resolved spontaneously by early childhood. This crucial history, in the context of the unexplained electrolyte imbalances, led to the specific hypothesis of PHA1 reactivation. A multidisciplinary team comprising intensivists, obstetricians, endocrinologists, and dietitians was assembled to manage this complex case. The patient was placed on an oral salt supplementation regimen combined with a low-potassium diet, which gradually led to normalization of her electrolyte levels. Throughout her hospitalization, she remained asymptomatic with normal electrocardiogram findings.

Obstetric ultrasound confirmed fetal growth restriction, with an estimated fetal weight of 1459 g (< 10th percentile). Initial Doppler studies were unremarkable. After 10 days of inpatient care, the patient was discharged with a serum potassium level of 4.5 mmol/L and continued regular antenatal follow-up at a joint diabetes and high-risk pregnancy clinic.

At 36 weeks’ gestation, she presented with reduced fetal movements. Cardiotocography (CTG) revealed reduced short-term variability and recurrent late decelerations, prompting an emergency cesarean section for a non-reassuring fetal status. This timely intervention likely prevented progression to significant fetal acidosis and resulted in the delivery of a vigorous 2190-gram female infant, whose weight represented the 7th percentile on the WHO growth chart. The newborn had excellent outcomes, with Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 10 min, respectively, and a normal cord blood pH. Both mother and baby had stable electrolytes postpartum and were discharged home on the second postnatal day without complications.

Discussion and literature review

Although pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA 1) is typically a pediatric condition, its occurrence in pregnancy remains quite rare and presents a set of challenges that can be easily overlooked. The body’s natural increase in aldosterone secretion during pregnancy—essential for expanding plasma volume and maintaining maternal-fetal circulation—can, paradoxically, unmask underlying mineralocorticoid resistance in susceptible individuals [7]. In someone with a remote history of PHA, this adaptation may actually trigger recurrence, as seen in our patient.

Interestingly, most of the reported cases involving PHA in pregnancy refer to PHA type 2, or Gordon syndrome. These patients are often present with hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and hypertension. A case in 2020, for example, described a woman with a KLHL3 mutation (classified as PHA2D) who developed preeclampsia but went on to deliver healthy twins after receiving thiazide diuretics and dietary sodium restriction [8]. Another case from Japan involved a woman with familial PHA2, who required similar treatment during pregnancy and delivered by cesarean after developing preeclampsia at 35 + 6 weeks [9]. These examples show how the hormonal shifts of pregnancy can unmask latent genetic syndromes, which may have remained silent otherwise.

PHA type 1, on the other hand, is more commonly seen in neonates or infants and often resolves spontaneously in early childhood. In many of those neonatal cases, the first sign may be polyhydramnios due to excessive fetal urinary salt loss. Reports from Italy, India, Turkey, and the UK have described such salt-wasting crises, usually presenting in the first few days of life, with many of the children showing resolution of symptoms by their first birthday [10–12]. However, the reappearance of PHA type 1 during pregnancy—decades after apparent resolution in early childhood—has not been previously documented to our knowledge.

In our case, the recurrence of electrolyte abnormalities during the third trimester, coincided with fetal growth restriction (FGR), raises important clinical questions. One possibility is that the aldosterone resistance led to chronic maternal sodium loss, which may have contributed to relative hypovolemia and poor placental perfusion. While speculative, this mechanism could explain the growth-restricted fetus, despite an otherwise stable maternal condition. More research is clearly needed to explore these physiological links, particularly in women with a known genetic or family history of PHA.

What this case really underscores is the need for heightened clinical awareness. A woman with a history of PHA—even one that resolved in childhood—may still be vulnerable to recurrence under the physiological stress of pregnancy. These patients might not present dramatically at first, and subtle electrolyte abnormalities could easily be missed unless clinicians are looking for them. Early detection and close follow-up may help prevent complications such as hyperkalemia or FGR. We believe this case should prompt clinicians to consider targeted monitoring protocols in pregnant women with a past diagnosis of PHA.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. Firstly, it is a single case report. Secondly, we were unable to perform genetic testing to confirm the specific mutation responsible for the patient’s PHA1, which would have provided definitive etiological confirmation. Finally, while the electrolyte disturbances and FGR were likely linked to PHA1 reactivation, potential contributions from other conditions present in this pregnancy, namely gestational diabetes and obstetric cholestasis, cannot be entirely ruled out as confounding factors.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but important phenomenon: the reactivation of childhood-onset pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1 during pregnancy. The recurrence of severe electrolyte disturbances, particularly in the third trimester, suggests that the physiological changes of pregnancy—especially the surge in aldosterone—can unmask or reactivate previously silent mineralocorticoid resistance.

In our patient, this reactivation was associated with fetal growth restriction, suggesting a possible effect on maternal volume status and placental perfusion. While the condition had resolved during early childhood, the stress of pregnancy appeared to bring it back to the surface. This underlines the need for careful follow-up of women with any prior history of PHA, even if symptoms have been absent for years.

Early recognition and monitoring of electrolyte status in such pregnancies could help avoid complications and improve outcomes for both mother and fetus. To this end, and based on this experience, we propose that pregnant women with a history of PHA, even if resolved, should be managed by a multidisciplinary team from early pregnancy. Proactive management should include regular electrolyte monitoring (e.g., every 2–4 weeks in the first and second trimesters, and weekly in the third trimester), a low-potassium diet, and ready access to oral salt supplementation. Serial growth scans should be performed to monitor fetal growth restriction. This vigilant approach may help prevent severe electrolyte imbalances and mitigate associated adverse fetal outcomes.

At present, data are limited, and further observational studies are needed to understand how often this reactivation happens, which patients are most at risk, and what management strategies work best in this unique setting.
