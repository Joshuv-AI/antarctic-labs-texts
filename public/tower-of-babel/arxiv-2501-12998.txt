Killing mean curvature solitons from Riemannian submersions
Diego Artacho, Marie-Amélie Lawn, Miguel Ortega

Abstract
. We present a new general construction of mean curvature flow solitons on manifolds admitting a nowhere-vanishing Killing vector field. Using Riemannian submersion techniques, we reduce the problem from a PDE to an ODE. As an application, we obtain new examples of rotators in hyperbolic space.



1.
Introduction

Let
M^{n}
and
N^{n+1}
be smooth oriented manifolds. Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
N
, and
\nabla
its Levi-Civita connection. A smooth one-parameter family of immersions
F\colon M\times[0,\varepsilon)\to N
is said to
evolve by mean curvature flow
if

(1)

\left(\frac{\partial F_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}=\vec{H}_{t}\,,

where
F_{t}(x):=F(x,t)
,
(\cdot)^{\perp}
indicates
g
-orthogonal projection to the normal bundle of
F_{t}
, and
\vec{H}_{t}
is the mean curvature vector of
F_{t}
, given by

\vec{H}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{i}\right)^{\perp}

if
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
is any local orthonormal frame along
F_{t}
.

A particular kind of solution to (
1
) has attracted much interest in recent decades. If
\mathcal{G}
is a smooth one-parameter family of isometries of
N
with
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}_{N}
, we say that a one-parameter family of immersions
F
evolving by mean curvature flow is a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton
if

F_{t}=\mathcal{G}_{t}\circ F_{0}\,.

In the class of solitons, also called self-similar solutions, solving (
1
) amounts to finding a manifold
M
and an immersion
F_{0}\colon M\to N
with mean curvature vector

(2)

\vec{H}=K^{\perp}\,,

where
K
is the Killing vector field determined by
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,

(3)

K(x)=\frac{d\mathcal{G}_{t}}{dt}\big\rvert_{t=0}(x)\,.

This was proved in the seminal work
[
HS00
]
. If
\nu
is a unit normal vector field to
F_{0}
, we can define the
mean curvature with respect to
\nu
to be
H:=g\left(\vec{H},\nu\right)
, and the vectorial equation (
2
) becomes

(4)

H=g\left(K,\nu\right)\,.

Those solutions which are invariant under a group of translations are the so-called
translators
, which have been extensively studied in the literature – see e.g.
[
CSS07
,
LTW11
,
MSHS15
]
. In
[
ALR19
]
, the concept of self-similar solutions is extended to a more general setting, allowing
\mathcal{G}
to be a one-parameter family of
conformal
maps. In particular, they study the example of a class of Riemannian warped products
P\times_{h}I
as the ambient space where a conformal vector field naturally arises from the distinguished one-dimensional direction
I
. For the study of mean curvature flow in warped products, we also refer to
[
CMR20
,
DS23
]
, with
[
ALO22
]
focusing on the Robertson-Walker case.

Graphical solutions to mean curvature flow were initially introduced by Ecker and Huisken
[
EH89
]
for hypersurfaces in Euclidean space, and later extensively studied by numerous authors in various ambient spaces – see e.g.
[
CSS07
,
HIMW19
,
LO22
,
LM19
]
. In all of them, they are graphs of solutions to a certain PDE. However, finding solutions to PDEs is generally a challenging task. A common strategy is to focus on solutions that are invariant under certain symmetries of the manifold, which can simplify the problem. We refer, for instance, to
[
CSS07
]
for the study of solutions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
invariant by rotational symmetries.

Our strategy is as follows. On a Riemannian manifold
(N,g)
, we first consider a Killing vector field
K
that is nowhere zero, and the goal will be to construct solutions to mean curvature flow that are solitons with respect to the flow of
K
. In order to obtain solitons which can be described as solutions to a PDE, we need the orthogonal distribution to
K
to be integrable, and all the leaves to be diffeomorphic to a fixed manifold
M
. In this way,
(N,g)
inherits locally a warped product structure, and the soliton will be the graph of a smooth function over a subset of
M
. Secondly, we shall restrict our attention to solutions that are invariant under certain symmetries of
N
, usually coming from Lie group actions. The main idea of this paper is the use of
special submersions
from
M
to a one-dimensional base space that preserve key geometric properties, which in some cases allow one to reduce the PDE to an ODE.

More precisely, we consider a manifold
(N,g)
endowed with a warped product structure

(N,g)\cong M\times_{\varphi}I:=\left(M\times I,g_{M}+\varphi dr^{2}\right)\,,

where
\left(M,g_{M}\right)
is a Riemannian manifold,
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval,
\varphi\colon M\to(0,+\infty)
is a smooth function, and
dr^{2}
is the Euclidean metric on
I
. This suggests a natural one-parameter group of isometries of
N
, namely
\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,r)=(x,r+t)
. Moreover, there is a natural class of hypersurfaces of
N
, namely graphs of smooth functions
u\colon M\supset\Omega\to I
, where
\Omega
is an open subset of
M
. In Section
2
, we show that such a hypersurface is a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton if and only if
u
satisfies a certain PDE - see Theorem
1
. This is a natural generalisation of the previously cited works. With this in mind, suppose
\pi\colon\left(M,g_{M}\right)\to\left(J,\alpha(s)ds^{2}\right)
is a Riemannian submersion, where
J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval,
\alpha\colon J\to(0,+\infty)
is smooth, and
ds^{2}
denotes the Euclidean metric on
J
. Furthermore, assume that both the function
u
and
\varphi
factor through
\pi
, i.e.
u=f\circ\pi
, and
\varphi=\widehat{\varphi}\circ\pi
for some smooth functions
f,\,\widehat{\varphi}:J\rightarrow I
. If, moreover,
\pi
has constant mean curvature fibres
h
, then the condition that the graph of
u
defines a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton on the total space is equivalent to
f
being a solution to an ODE on the base. This yields our main Theorem
4
, proved in Section
3
:

Theorem

(see Theorem
4
)
.

In our previous setting, the graph of
u=f\circ\pi
defines a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton on
N
if and only if
f
satisfies the ordinary differential equation:

f^{\prime\prime}=\left(\alpha+\widehat{\varphi}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}f^{\prime}}{2\widehat{\varphi}\alpha}-\frac{f^{\prime}h}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{\widehat{\varphi}}\right)\right)^{\prime}.

We can summarise our method schematically as follows:

(5)

\begin{aligned} \text{Killing}\\
\text{vector field}\end{aligned}\hskip 9.24994pt\rightsquigarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\begin{aligned} \text{Warped}\\
\text{product}\end{aligned}\hskip 9.24994pt\rightsquigarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\text{PDE}\hskip 9.24994pt\rightsquigarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\begin{aligned} \text{Riemannian}\\
\text{submersion}\end{aligned}\hskip 9.24994pt\rightsquigarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ODE}\hskip 9.24994pt\rightsquigarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Soliton. }

This procedure is very general and can be used to produce many solitons, including those in
[
BL25
]
. To illustrate our technique, we study solitons on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, where we find a new example of complete rotator – see Section
4
.

Finally, we outline the structure of the paper:

•

In Section
2
, we obtain the PDE that a function has to satisfy for its graph to be a soliton – see Theorem
1
.

•

In Section
3
, we obtain the ODE that results from imposing symmetry conditions on the soliton – see Theorem
4
.

•

In Section
4
, we use our new method to obtain a new example of complete rotator in hyperbolic space.

2.
The PDE

Let
\Omega\subseteq M
be open, let
u\colon\Omega\to I
be a smooth function, and let
F\colon\Omega\to M\times_{\varphi}I
be its graph
x\mapsto(x,u(x))
. In this section, we shall prove that
F
is a soliton, i.e., it satisfies equation (
4
), if and only if
u
satisfies a certain PDE (
6
). More specifically, we have the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(M,g_{M})
be an oriented Riemannian manifold,
\varphi\colon M\to(0,+\infty)
a smooth function,
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an interval and
(N,g)=(M\times I,g_{M}+\varphi dr^{2})
, where
r
is the usual coordinate in
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the one-parameter family of isometries of
N
given by
\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,r)=(x,r+t)
. Then, if
\Omega\subseteq M
is open, the graph of a smooth function
u\colon\Omega\to I
defines a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton on
N
if and only if
u
satisfies

(6)

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)=\frac{1}{W}-\frac{1}{2W\varphi}g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)\,,

where the function
W\colon M\to(0,+\infty)
is defined by

(7)

W=\sqrt{g_{M}(\nabla u,\nabla u)+\frac{1}{\varphi}}\,.

Proof.

The Killing vector field associated to the one-parameter family of isometries
\mathcal{G}
is just the coordinate vector field
\partial_{r}
corresponding to the interval
I
. It is easily verified that a unit normal vector field to
F
is given by

\nu=\frac{1}{W}\left(-\nabla u+\frac{1}{\varphi}\partial_{r}\right)\,.

Hence, the right-hand side of equation (
4
) is

(8)

g\left(\partial_{r},\nu\right)=\frac{1}{W}\,.

Next, we need to compute the mean curvature
H
of
F
. To this end, let
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
be a local
g_{M}
-orthonormal frame of
M
. The mean curvature
H
is given by

H=g\left(\vec{H},\nu\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma^{ij}g\left(\nabla_{F_{*}e_{i}}F_{*}e_{j},\nu\right)\,,

where
\gamma^{ij}
are the coefficients of the inverse of the matrix
\gamma_{ij}=g\left(F_{*}e_{i},F_{*}e_{j}\right)
. By definition of
F
as the graph of
u
,
F_{*}e_{i}=e_{i}+u_{i}\partial_{r}
, where
u_{i}=e_{i}(u)
. Note that
\nabla u=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}e_{i}
. Hence,

\gamma_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\varphi u_{i}u_{j}\quad\text{and thus}\quad\gamma^{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{W^{2}}u_{i}u_{j}\,.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla_{F_{*}e_{i}}F_{*}e_{j}=\nabla_{e_{i}+u_{i}\partial_{r}}\left(e_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{r}\right)=\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j}+u_{ij}\partial_{r}+u_{j}\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r}+u_{i}\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j}+u_{i}u_{j}\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r}\,,

where
u_{ij}=e_{i}(u_{j})
. Furthermore, using Koszul’s formula and the obvious facts that
[\partial_{r},e_{i}]=0
and
g(\partial_{r},[e_{i},e_{j}])=0
, we see that

2g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{i},e_{j}\right)=\partial_{r}\left(g\left(e_{i},e_{j}\right)\right)+e_{i}(g\left(\partial_{r},e_{j}\right))-e_{j}(g\left(\partial_{r},e_{i}\right))=\partial_{r}\left(g\left(e_{i},e_{j}\right)\right)=0\,.

and therefore
g(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}X,Y)=0
for any vector fields
X,Y
tangent to
M
and independent of
r
. Hence, using also that
0=\partial_{r}(\varphi)=\partial_{r}(g(\partial_{r},\partial_{r}))=2g(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r})
and that the leaves of the foliation are totally geodesic, we obtain

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{F_{*}e_{i}}F_{*}e_{j},\nu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\nu\right)+u_{ij}g\left(\partial_{r},\nu\right)+u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\nu\right)+u_{i}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\nu\right)+u_{i}u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\partial_{r}\right)-\frac{1}{W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{ij}}{W}

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{j}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r}\right)-\frac{u_{j}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{i}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\partial_{r}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{u_{i}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{\varphi W}g(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r})-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{W}g(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{ij}}{W}+\frac{u_{j}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{u_{j}}{W}g\left(\partial_{r},\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{i}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\partial_{r}\right)-\frac{u_{i}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{ij}}{W}+\frac{u_{j}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{u_{i}}{\varphi W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{j},\partial_{r}\right)-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right).

As
\partial_{r}
is a Killing vector field, we have that

(9)

\varphi_{i}:=e_{i}(g(\partial_{r},\partial_{r}))=2g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\partial_{r},\partial_{r}\right)=-2g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},e_{i}\right)=2g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}e_{i},\partial_{r}\right)\,.

Moreover,

g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},\nabla u\right)=e_{i}(g(e_{j},\nabla u))-g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j}\right)=u_{ij}-g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j})\,,

obtaining

(10)

g\left(\nabla_{F_{*}e_{i}}F_{*}e_{j},\nu\right)=\frac{1}{W}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j}\right)+\frac{u_{j}\varphi_{i}}{2\varphi W}+\frac{u_{i}\varphi_{j}}{2\varphi W}-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{W}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right).

Putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma^{ij}g\left(\nabla_{F_{*}e_{i}}F_{*}e_{j},\nu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{W}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\delta_{ij}\left[-u_{i}u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{j}\varphi_{i}}{2\varphi}+\frac{u_{i}\varphi_{j}}{2\varphi}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{W^{3}}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\left[-u_{i}^{2}u_{j}^{2}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{u_{i}u_{j}^{2}\varphi_{i}}{2\varphi}+\frac{u_{i}^{2}u_{j}\varphi_{j}}{2\varphi}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j}\right)+\frac{1}{W}\left[-\sum^{n}_{i=1}u_{i}^{2}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{u_{i}\varphi_{i}}{\varphi}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{W^{3}}\left[-|\nabla u|^{4}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{1}{\varphi}\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}u_{i}\varphi_{i}u_{j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{W^{3}}\left[-W^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+W^{2}\frac{g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)}{\varphi}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{W^{3}}\left[-|\nabla u|^{4}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)|\nabla u|^{2}}{\varphi}\right].

Bearing in mind the definition of
W
(
7
), we get

H=\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j})+\frac{1}{\varphi W^{3}}\left[-|\nabla u|^{2}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r},\nabla u\right)+\frac{1}{\varphi}g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)\right].

By (
9
), we see that
\nabla_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\varphi
. Hence,

(11)

H=\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j})+\frac{1}{\varphi W^{3}}g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varphi}\right).

Notice now that, if
W_{i}=e_{i}(W)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{div}(\nabla u)}{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)-g\left(\nabla\left(\frac{1}{W}\right),\nabla u\right)=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{W_{i}}{W^{2}}u_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2W^{3}}\left(-\frac{\varphi_{i}}{\varphi^{2}}+2g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,\nabla u)\right)u_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{W^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla u,e_{j})-\frac{1}{2\varphi^{2}W^{3}}g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u).

By using equation (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle H=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)+\frac{1}{W^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2\varphi}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varphi^{2}}\right)g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)\,,

and therefore

(12)

\displaystyle H=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)+\frac{1}{2W\varphi}g(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u)\,.

This, together with (
8
), yields the result.
∎

Remark 2
.

Some comments about other works are in order. First, our study is a generalisation of Lira and Martín’s work,
[

]
, where they studied translators in Riemannian products

\mathbb{R}

) can be obtained in a semi-Riemannian environment by adjusting the corresponding definition of

, where some constant functions

3.
The ODE

A geometric way to find solutions to the PDE (
6
) is to look for functions
u\colon M\supseteq\Omega\to I
that have some sort of symmetry. For example, when studying translating solitons in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
that are graphs of functions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, one tries to find rotationally invariant solutions to (
6
). This is an instance of a more general procedure by which one looks for solutions that are invariant under a cohomogeneity-one action of a Lie group on
M
by isometries. Generalising this approach, it is natural to look for solutions that factor through a certain
Riemannian submersion
.

Definition 3
.

A manifold
M\times_{\varphi}I
will be called
submersive
if there exist a Riemannian submersion
\pi:(M,g)\to(J,\alpha(s)ds^{2})
,
\alpha:J\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and smooth functions
\hat{\varphi}:J\to I
and
h:J\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi=\hat{\varphi}\circ\pi
and for each
s\in J
,
\pi^{-1}\{s\}
is a hypersurface in
M
with constant mean curvature
h(s)
with respect to
-\nabla\pi/\|\nabla\pi\|
.

Next, given an open subinterval
J^{\prime}\subset J
, consider a smooth function
f:J^{\prime}\to I
, so that we obtain the following commutative diagrams:

\textstyle{M\supset\pi^{-1}(J^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\scriptstyle{u}
\textstyle{(J\supseteq J^{\prime},\alpha(s)ds^{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{I}

\textstyle{M\supset\pi^{-1}(J^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{gr}(u)}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{M\times_{\varphi}I=N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi\times\mathrm{Id}}
\textstyle{J\supseteq J^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{gr}(f)}
\textstyle{J\times_{\widehat{\varphi}}I}

where
\mathrm{gr}(u)
and

are the graphs of

and

respectively, we obtain the following general result:

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the manifold
M\times_{\varphi}I
is submersive, and such that the previous commutative diagrams concerning
f
and
u=f\circ\pi
are satisfied. Then, the graph
\mathrm{gr}(u)
is a
\mathcal{G}
-soliton on
N
if and only if
f
satisfies the following ODE:

(13)

f^{\prime\prime}=\left(\alpha+\widehat{\varphi}(f^{\prime})^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}f^{\prime}}{2\widehat{\varphi}\alpha}-\frac{f^{\prime}h}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{\widehat{\varphi}}\right)\right)^{\prime}\,.

Remark 5
.

Note that the warping function
\varphi
essentially determines the projection
\pi
, since
\varphi
has to factor through it to allow our technique to work.

Remark 6
.

This result applies to general warped products
N=M\times_{\varphi}I
. In particular, it applies whenever
N
has a nowhere-vanishing Killing vector field with integrable orthogonal distribution, as discussed in Section
1
.

Proof.

This result is a consequence of Theorem
1
, and equation (
13
) is obtained by substituting
u=f\circ\pi
in (
6
). We now write the details of this computation. First, note that

\nabla u=\nabla(f\circ\pi)=(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)\nabla\pi\,,

and thus, using that
\pi\colon(M,g_{M})\to(J,\alpha(s)ds^{2})
is a Riemannian submersion, we get

g_{M}\left(\nabla u,\nabla u\right)=(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)^{2}g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)=\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)^{2}}{(\alpha\circ\pi)}\,.

Hence, substituting this into equation (
7
), we get

W=\sqrt{\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)^{2}}{(\alpha\circ\pi)}+\frac{1}{(\widehat{\varphi}\circ\pi)}}=\widehat{W}\circ\pi\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{W}=\sqrt{\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\varphi}}}\,.

A straightforward computation using the definition of
\widehat{W}
yields

\widehat{W}^{\prime}=\frac{f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}{\alpha\widehat{W}}-\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}\alpha^{\prime}}{2\alpha^{2}\widehat{W}}-\frac{\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}}{2\widehat{W}\widehat{\varphi}^{2}}\,.

Moreover, if
(e_{1},\dots e_{n})
is a local
g_{M}
-orthonormal frame of
M
with
e_{n}=-\sqrt{\alpha\circ\pi}\,\nabla\pi
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}{\nabla\pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{M}\left(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla\pi,e_{i}\right)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{i}\right)+g_{M}\left(\nabla_{-\sqrt{\alpha\circ\pi}\nabla\pi}\nabla\pi,-\sqrt{\alpha\circ\pi}\nabla\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}g_{M}\left(-\sqrt{\alpha\circ\pi}\nabla\pi,\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{i}\right)+(\alpha\circ\pi)g_{M}\left(\nabla_{\nabla\pi}\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(h\circ\pi)}{\sqrt{(\alpha\circ\pi)}}+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\circ\pi)\nabla\pi\left(g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)\right)=\frac{(h\circ\pi)}{\sqrt{(\alpha\circ\pi)}}+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\circ\pi)\nabla\pi\left(\frac{1}{(\alpha\circ\pi)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(h\circ\pi)}{\sqrt{(\alpha\circ\pi)}}+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\circ\pi)g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\left(\frac{1}{(\alpha\circ\pi)}\right)\right)=\frac{(h\circ\pi)}{\sqrt{(\alpha\circ\pi)}}-\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{2(\alpha\circ\pi)^{2}}\,.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W}\nabla\pi\right)=\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W}\mathrm{div}\left(\nabla\pi\right)+g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\left(\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W}\mathrm{div}\left(\nabla\pi\right)+\frac{(f^{\prime\prime}\circ\pi)W-(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)(\widehat{W}^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W^{2}}g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W}\mathrm{div}\left(\nabla\pi\right)+\frac{(f^{\prime\prime}\circ\pi)}{W(\alpha\circ\pi)}-\frac{(f^{\prime}\circ\pi)(\widehat{W}^{\prime}\circ\pi)}{W^{2}(\alpha\circ\pi)}\,,

and putting everything together we get that the left-hand side of the PDE (
6
) for
u=f\circ\pi
now reads

(14)

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\right)=\left(\frac{f^{\prime}h}{\widehat{W}\sqrt{\alpha}}-\frac{f^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}{2\widehat{W}\alpha^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{\widehat{W}\alpha}\left(1-\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{\widehat{W}^{2}\alpha}\right)+\frac{(f^{\prime})^{3}\alpha^{\prime}}{2\widehat{W}^{3}\alpha^{3}}+\frac{f^{\prime}\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}}{2\widehat{W}^{3}\alpha\widehat{\varphi}^{2}}\right)\circ\pi\,.

Similarly, the right-hand side of (
6
) is now

(15)

\frac{1}{W}-\frac{g_{M}\left(\nabla\varphi,\nabla u\right)}{2W\varphi}=\frac{1}{W}-\frac{f^{\prime}\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}g_{M}\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)}{2(\widehat{W}\circ\pi)(\widehat{\varphi}\circ\pi)}=\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{W}}-\frac{f^{\prime}\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}}{2\widehat{\varphi}\widehat{W}\alpha}\right)\circ\pi\,.

Finally, equating (
14
) and (
15
), we obtain the ODE (
13
).
∎

4.
An Application to the Hyperbolic Space

We now use the technique (
5
) to obtain new examples of solitons of the mean curvature flow. For
n\geq 2
, consider the half-space model of hyperbolic space

\mathbb{H}^{n+1}=\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{1}>0\right\},\qquad g=\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\,,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard flat metric of Euclidean space. Consider the one-parameter family of isometries
\mathcal{G}_{t}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, given by

\mathcal{G}_{t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},\mathrm{Rot}_{t}\left(x_{n},x_{n+1}\right))\,,\\

where
\mathrm{Rot}_{t}
denotes the rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by an oriented angle
t
. The Killing vector field corresponding to
\mathcal{G}
via (
3
) is

K=-x_{n+1}\partial_{n}+x_{n}\partial_{n+1}\,.

There is a warped product structure on
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{x_{n}=x_{n+1}=0\}
, namely

(16)

\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{x_{n}=x_{n+1}=0\}\cong\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\times_{\varphi}S^{1}\,,

where
\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\colon x_{n}>0\right\}
and
\varphi\colon\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\to(0,+\infty)
is given by
x\mapsto\left(x_{n}/x_{1}\right)^{2}
. The isometry (
16
) is given by

\left((x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}),e^{i\theta}\right)\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}\cos(\theta),x_{n}\sin(\theta))\,.

In the general theory, the second term in the warped product was a real interval, and now we have
S^{1}
. But smooth maps from an open interval to
S^{1}
correspond to maps from that interval to
\mathbb{R}
, by standard covering theory.

Now, take the submersion
\pi\colon\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\to(0,+\infty)
given by
\pi(x)=x_{n}/x_{1}
. Note that
\varphi
factors through
\pi
as
\varphi=\widehat{\varphi}\circ\pi
, where
\widehat{\varphi}(s)=s^{2}
. This submersion satisfies
g\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)=1/\left(\alpha\circ\pi\right)
, where
\alpha\colon(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is given by

(17)

\alpha(s)=\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,,

as shown in Appendix
B
. Observe that
\pi
has constant mean curvature fibres. Indeed, the mean curvature of the fibre
\pi^{-1}(\{s\})\subseteq\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
,
s>0
, with respect to the unit normal vector field
-\nabla\pi/\lVert\nabla\pi\rVert
is given by the function
h\colon(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

(18)

h(s)=(n-1)\frac{s}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}}\,,

which is derived in Appendix
B
. Finally, putting everything together, ODE (
13
) in this case reads

(19)

f^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{\big(\left(s^{4}+s^{2}\right)f^{\prime}(s)^{2}+1\big)\big(s-\left(ns^{2}+1\right)f^{\prime}(s)\big)-\left(2s^{2}+1\right)f^{\prime}(s)}{s(1+s^{2})}\,.\\

We will show that there exists a one-parameter family of smooth complete
\mathcal{G}
-solitons on
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
that can be constructed from solutions to (
19
). These examples are new. In particular, they are different from the ones exhibited in
[
dLRdS24
]
. In fact, our
\mathcal{G}
-solitons on
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
can be constructed from solutions
f\colon(s_{1},s_{2})\to\mathbb{R}
to (
19
), which can be parametrised by

\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\ni\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},y_{n}\right)\mapsto\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},y_{n}\cos\left(f(y_{n}/y_{1})\right),y_{n}\sin\left(f(y_{n}/y_{1})\right)\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\,.

Note that each subset
\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(c_{1},c_{2})=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\mid y_{1}=c_{1},\,y_{n}=c_{2}\}
,
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, is a
(n-2)
-dimensional horosphere. Indeed,
y_{1}=c_{1}
defines a
n
-dimensional horosphere, and
y_{n}=c_{2}
is a totally geodesic hyperplane. Thus, the intersection is a
(n-2)
-horosphere. And they are moved by this map to a
(n-2)
-horosphere in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Thus, all these
n
-dimensional examples are foliated by
(n-2)
-horospheres.
Defining
u=y_{1}
,
t_{i}=y_{i}/y_{1}
for
i=2,\dots,n-1
and
v=y_{n}/y_{1}
, we get a reparametrisation

\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\ni\left(u,t_{2},\dots,t_{n-1},v\right)\mapsto u\left(1,t_{2},\dots,t_{n-1},v\cos\left(f(v)\right),v\sin\left(f(v)\right)\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\,.

Hence, the soliton is determined by the curve
v\mapsto\left(v\cos\left(f(v)\right),v\sin\left(f(v)\right)\right)
.

To study (
19
), we define
w=f^{\prime}
,
p(s)=s^{3}+s
, and
q(s,x)=\big((s^{4}+s^{2})x^{2}+1\big)\big(s-(ns^{2}+1)x\big)-(2s^{2}+1)x
. In this way, (
19
) is equivalent to

(20)

f^{\prime}=w,\quad w^{\prime}(s)=\frac{q(s,w(s))}{p(s)}.

Note that
p(0)=0
and
q(0,0)=0
.

Firstly, we show that solutions to (
20
) can always be extended to infinity.

Lemma 8
.

Let
f\colon(s_{0},s_{1})\to\mathbb{R}
,
0<s_{0}<s_{1}
, be a solution to (
19
). Then, it can be extended (as a solution) to
f\colon(s_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

If we recall (
20
), it is enough to show that a solution
w
to the second equation can be extended to
(s_{0},+\infty)
. A simple computation shows that

q(s,x)=-s^{2}(s^{2}+1)(ns^{2}+1)x^{3}+s^{3}(s^{2}+1)x^{2}-((n+2)s^{2}+2)x+s.

It is easy to see that for fixed
s>0
,
q(s,x)\to-\infty
when
x\to+\infty
and
q(s,x)\to+\infty
when
x\to-\infty
. Then, there exist
x^{+}(s)>0
and
x^{-}(s)<0
such that
q(s,x)\leq-1
for any
x\geq x^{+}(s)
and
q(s,x)\geq 1
for any
x\leq x^{-}(s)
.

Now, given a solution
w:(s_{0},s_{1})\to\mathbb{R}
, assume by contradiction that
s_{1}
is a finite-time blow-up for
w
. Let

\displaystyle x^{+}

\displaystyle:=\sup\{x^{+}(s)\mid s\in[(s_{0}+s_{1})/2,s_{1}]\},

\displaystyle x^{-}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{x^{-}(s)\mid s\in[(s_{0}+s_{1})/2,s_{1}]\},

which are finite because
q
is a polynomial and
[(s_{0}+s_{1})/2,s_{1}]
is compact. There are two cases.

First,
w(s)\to+\infty
when
s\to s_{1}
,
s<s_{1}
. In particular,
w^{\prime}(s)\to+\infty
when
s\to s_{1}
. Then, there exists a sequence
\{r_{n}\}\subset[(s_{0}+s_{1})/2,s_{1})
such that
\{r_{n}\}\nearrow s_{1}
and
w(r_{n})\to+\infty
. Therefore, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n\geq m
,
w(r_{n})>x^{+}
, which implies
w^{\prime}(r_{n})=q(r_{n},w(r_{n}))/p(r_{n})<-1/p(r_{n})<0
. This is a contradiction.

Second,
w(s)\to-\infty
when
s\to s_{1}
,
s<s_{1}
. In particular,
w^{\prime}(s)\to-\infty
when
s\to s_{1}
.
Then, there exists a sequence
\{r_{n}\}\subset[(s_{0}+s_{1})/2,s_{1})
such that
\{r_{n}\}\nearrow s_{1}
and
w(r_{n})\to-\infty
. Therefore, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq m
, then
w(r_{n})<x^{-}
, which implies
w^{\prime}(r_{n})=q(r_{n},w(r_{n}))/p(r_{n})>1/p(r_{n})>0
for any
n\geq m
. This is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 9

Given

\mathbf{f}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}[0,+\infty)

Lemma 10
.

Given
s_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
,
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist
\mathbf{f}_{\pm}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}[s_{0},+\infty)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(s_{0},+\infty)
which are solutions to (

) such that
\mathbf{f}^{(2)}_{+}(s_{0})=\mathbf{f}^{(2)}_{-}(s_{0})=r_{0}
, and
\lim_{s\to s_{0}}\big(\mathbf{f}_{\pm}^{(2)}\big)^{\prime}(s)=\pm\infty
.

Proof.

We use the standard technique of the inverse map. Indeed, assume that
f
is a injective solution to (
19
), and let
\gamma
be a local inverse. Then, given
r
in some interval, it holds
r=f(\gamma(r))
, so that
1=f^{\prime}(\gamma)\gamma^{\prime}
, that is
0=f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)(\gamma^{\prime})^{2}+f^{\prime}(\gamma)\gamma^{\prime\prime}
. From here, using

(21)

\gamma^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{(2\gamma(r)^{2}+1)\gamma^{\prime}(r)^{2}-\big(\gamma(r)^{4}+\gamma(r)^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}(r)^{2}\big)\big(\gamma(r)\gamma^{\prime}(r)-n\gamma(r)^{2}-1\big)}{\gamma(r)^{3}+\gamma(r)}.

Now, given

\gamma(r_{0})=s_{0}

\gamma^{\prime}(r_{0})=0

\gamma^{\prime\prime}(r_{0})=s_{0}(ns_{0}+1)>0.

This means that
\gamma
is convex in a small neighbourhood of
r_{0}
, say
(r_{0}-\rho_{0},r_{0}+\rho_{0})
for some
\rho_{0}>0
. The restrictions of
\gamma
to the intervals
(r_{0}-\rho_{0},r_{0}]
and

are injective, and then they provide two inverse functions
\mathbf{f}_{\pm}^{(2)}:[0,\delta)\to\mathbb{R}
as desired, since
\lim_{s\to s_{0}}\big(\mathbf{f}_{\pm}^{(2)}\big)^{\prime}(s)=\lim_{r\to r_{0}}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}(r)}=\pm\infty
.
∎

Remark 11
.

It is relatively straightforward to show that, apart from the Killing vector field induced by rotations we used in this section, there are exactly two other Killing vector fields with integrable horizontal distribution in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, namely the ones associated via (
3
) to the following one-parameter families of isometries:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1}+t)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1})=e^{t}\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1})\,,

The Killing vector fields corresponding to these isometries (respectively translations and dilations) are given by

K^{(1)}=\partial_{n+1}\,\quad K^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\partial_{i}\,.

Each of these Killing vector fields induces a warped product structure on a certain open subset
N^{(i)}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
:

(22)

N^{(i)}\cong M^{(i)}\times_{c^{(i)}}I^{(i)}\,,

where
\left(M^{(i)},g_{M^{(i)}}\right)
is an oriented
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold,
I^{(i)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an open interval and
\varphi^{(i)}\colon M^{(i)}\to(0,+\infty)
is smooth. More specifically, we can take

\displaystyle M^{(1)}=\mathbb{H}^{n}\,,\quad I^{(1)}=\mathbb{R}\,,\quad c^{(1)}(x)=\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle M^{(2)}=\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\setminus\{(1,0,\dots,0)\}\,,\quad I^{(2)}=(0,+\infty)\,,\quad c^{(2)}(x)=\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}}\,,

where
\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\colon x_{n}>0\right\}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}=1\right\}
, both equipped with the induced metrics from hyperbolic space. In fact,
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
is a totally geodesic hyperplane. The isometries (
22
) are given by

\displaystyle M^{(1)}\times I^{(1)}\to N^{(1)}\,,

\displaystyle\left((x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})\,;

\displaystyle M^{(2)}\times I^{(2)}\to N^{(2)}\,,

\displaystyle\left((x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}),r\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto r(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})\,.

In each case, there exists a submersion
\pi^{(i)}\colon M^{(i)}\to J^{(i)}
, where
J^{(i)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval, through which
\varphi^{(i)}
factors as
\varphi^{(i)}=\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}\circ\pi^{(i)}
. This submersion is unique up to composition by diffeomorphisms
J^{(i)}\to\widetilde{J}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We can take

\displaystyle J^{(1)}=(0,+\infty)\,,\quad\pi^{(1)}(x)=x_{1}\,,\quad\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle J^{(2)}=(0,1)\,,\quad\pi^{(2)}(x)=x_{1}\,,\quad\widehat{\varphi}^{(2)}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,.

These submersions satisfy
g_{M^{(i)}}\left(\nabla\pi^{(i)},\nabla\pi^{(i)}\right)=1/\left(\alpha^{(i)}\circ\pi^{(i)}\right)
, where the
\alpha^{(i)}\colon J^{(i)}\to(0,+\infty)
are easily verified to be given by

\alpha^{(1)}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,,\quad\alpha^{(2)}(s)=\frac{1}{1-s^{2}}\,.

Each
\pi^{(i)}
has constant mean curvature fibres. Using similar calculations to the ones in Appendix
B
, it is possible to compute the mean curvature of the fibre
\left(\pi^{(i)}\right)^{-1}(\{s\})\subseteq M^{(i)}
,
s\in J^{(i)}
, with respect to the unit normal vector field
-\nabla\pi^{(i)}/\lVert\nabla\pi^{(i)}\rVert
, which is given by the function
h^{(i)}\colon J^{(i)}\to\mathbb{R}
,

h^{(1)}(s)=-(n-1)\,,\quad h^{(2)}(s)=-2(n-1)\frac{s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\,.

Finally, putting everything together, the ODE (
13
) in these cases reads, respectively,

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(f^{\prime}(s)^{2}+1\right)\left(nsf^{\prime}(s)+1\right)}{s^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{\big(s^{2}-\left(s^{2}-1\right)f^{\prime}(s)^{2}\big)\big(\left((2n-3)s^{2}+1\right)f^{\prime}(s)+s\big)+s^{2}f^{\prime}(s)}{s^{3}\left(1-s^{2}\right)}.

Straightforward computations show that the ODE for the
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
-solitons coincides with the one for the usual rotationally invariant translating solitons in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
after the change of variable
t=1/s
, and that we hence recover the rotationally invariant translating solitons studied in
[
MdOSHdS24
]
. Another recent study of
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
-solitons is
[
BL24
]
.

To the knowledge of the authors, the only paper about
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
-solitons is
[
dLRdS24
]
, which deals with solitons in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
using different methods. For the
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
-solitons described here, we can employ the same technique as before: similarly to Lemma
9
and Lemma
8

Appendix A
Some Technical Lemmas

In this appendix, we prove some technical results that allow us to obtain solitons in hyperbolic spaces in Section
4
. Let
I_{0}
,
I_{1}
be two open intervals, and let
p\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(I_{0})
,
q\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(I_{0}\times I_{1})
. We are interested in the solutions to the following ODE:

(23)

v^{\prime}(s)=\frac{q(s,v(s))}{p(s)}.

Define the vector field
X:I_{0}\times I_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
X(s,x)=(p(s),q(s,x))
.
The following result relates solutions to (
23
) and integral curves of
X
. In particular, we obtain a solution to (
23
) from almost any integral curve of
X
.

Lemma 12
.

(1)

Let
J_{0}
be an open interval and
\beta:J_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\beta(r)=(s(r),x(r))
, an integral curve of
X
such that
s
is a diffeomorphism onto its image. Then,
v:=x\circ s^{-1}
, is a solution to (
23
).

(2)

Given a solution
v
to (
23
) defined on an open interval, there exists a smooth function
s=s(r)
defined on some open interval such that
\beta(r)=(s(r),v(s(r)))
is an integral curve of
X
.

Proof.

Since
\beta
is an integral curve of
X
,

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(r)=p(s(r)),\quad x^{\prime}(r)=q(s(r),x(r)).

Since
s
is a diffeomorphism onto
J_{0}^{\prime}:=s(J_{0})
, we have that
s^{\prime}(s^{-1}(y))=p(y)
for any
y\in J_{0}^{\prime}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(s^{-1}(y))

\displaystyle=q\big(s((s^{-1}(y)),x(s^{-1}(y)\big)=q(y,v(y)).

Next,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(y)=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\prime}(s^{-1}(y))}{s^{\prime}(s^{-1}(y))}=\frac{q(y,v(y))}{p(y)}.

This proves (1). For (2), let
s=s(r)
be a solution to
s^{\prime}(r)=p(s(r))
in some open interval, and define the curve
\beta(r)=(s(r),v(s(r)))
. Since
v
is a solution to (
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(r)=(s^{\prime}(r),v^{\prime}(s(r))s^{\prime}(r))=\left(p(s(r)),\frac{q(s(r),v(s(r)))}{p(s(r))}s^{\prime}(r)\right)

\displaystyle=\big(p(s(r)),q(s(r),v(s(r)))\big)=X(\beta(r)).

This finishes the proof.∎

Now, we are going to solve some boundary problems related to (
23
).

Lemma 13
.

Let
(s_{0},x_{0})\in I_{0}\times I_{1}
such that
p(s_{0})=0
, and
q(s_{0},x_{0})=0
. Assume that the following condition holds:

(24)

p^{\prime}(s_{0})\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}(s_{0},x_{0})<0.

Then, the boundary problem

(25)

v^{\prime}(s)=\frac{q(s,v(s))}{p(s)},\quad v(s_{0})=x_{0},

has a unique solution
\mathbf{v}:J\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a suitable open interval
J\ni s_{0}
.

Proof.

We reuse the vector field
X:I_{0}\times I_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
X(s,x)=(p(s),q(s,x))
.
Note that
X(s_{0},x_{0})=(0,0)
. The differential of
X
at
(s_{0},x_{0})
is

DX(s_{0},x_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}p^{\prime}(s_{0})&\frac{\partial q}{\partial s}(s_{0},x_{0})\\
0&\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}(s_{0},x_{0})\end{pmatrix}.

Condition (
24
) implies that
DX(s_{0},x_{0})
is diagonalisable, and its eigenvalues have different sign. By Section 3.2 of
[
Wig03
]
, there are two
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
curves (the stable and the unstable submanifolds) passing through the point
(s_{0},x_{0})
and such that the tangent lines are spanned by the eigenvectors of
DX(s_{0},x_{0})^{T}
. One of them is parallel to
(0,1)
, so it cannot generate a solution as in Lemma
12
. However, the other curve can be parametrised by
\beta(r)=(s(r),x(r))
in some interval, with
\beta(0)=(s_{0},x_{0})
, providing a solution to (
25
) as in Lemma
12
, namely
\mathbf{v}(y)=x(s^{-1}(y))
, with boundary condition
\mathbf{v}(s_{0})=x_{0}
. The uniqueness of (local) integral curves of
X
implies that
\mathbf{v}
is unique.
∎

Appendix B
Calculation of the mean curvature (
18
)

Recall from Section
4
the Riemannian submersion

\pi\colon\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\to(0,+\infty)\,,\quad\pi(x)=\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}}\,,

where
\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\colon x_{n}>0\right\}
. In this appendix, we show that the mean curvature of the fibre
\pi^{-1}(\{s\})\subseteq\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
,
s>0
, with respect to the unit normal vector field
-\nabla\pi/\lVert\nabla\pi\rVert
is given by

h(s)=(n-1)\frac{s}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}}\,,

First, recall that the hyperbolic metric
g
on
\check{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
is

g=e^{2\gamma}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\,,

where
\gamma=-\log{x_{1}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Euclidean metric. Hence, the corresponding Levi-Civita connections
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\mathrm{Eucl}}
are related by the usual conformal change formula

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\mathrm{Eucl}}_{X}Y+X(\gamma)Y+Y(\gamma)X-\langle X,Y\rangle\mathrm{grad}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(\gamma)

(26)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\mathrm{Eucl}}_{X}Y+X(\gamma)Y+Y(\gamma)X+\frac{1}{x_{1}}\langle X,Y\rangle\partial_{1}\,.

Now, let
e_{i}=x_{1}\partial_{i}
, so that
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
is a global
g
-orthonormal frame of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. The gradient of
\pi
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(\nabla\pi,e_{i}\right)e_{i}=x_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(\nabla\pi,\partial_{i}\right)\partial_{i}=x_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial x_{i}}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}\left(-\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}^{2}}\partial_{1}+\frac{1}{x_{1}}\partial_{n}\right)=-x_{n}\partial_{1}+x_{1}\partial_{n}\,.

Thus, the square of its norm is

\lVert\nabla\pi\rVert^{2}=g\left(\nabla\pi,\nabla\pi\right)=1+\left(\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}}\right)^{2}=1+s^{2}\,,

which proves equation (
17
). A unit normal vector field to the fibres of
\pi
is then

\xi:=-\frac{\nabla\pi}{\lVert\nabla\pi\rVert}=\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}}\left(s\partial_{1}-\partial_{n}\right)\,.

An orthonormal frame of the fibre
\pi^{-1}(\{s\})
is given by

\left(x_{1}\partial_{2},\dots,x_{1}\partial_{n-1},T:=\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}}\left(\partial_{1}+s\partial_{n}\right)\right)\,.

Hence, the mean curvature of the fibre with respect to
\xi
is given by

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g\left(\nabla_{x_{1}\partial_{j}}x_{1}\partial_{j},\xi\right)+g\left(\nabla_{T}T,\xi\right)=x_{1}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g\left(\nabla_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{j},\xi\right)+g\left(\nabla_{T}T,\xi\right)

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g\left(\frac{1}{x_{1}}\partial_{1},\xi\right)+g\left(\nabla_{T}T,\xi\right)=(n-2)\frac{s}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}}+g\left(\nabla_{T}T,\xi\right)\,,

where the third equality uses (
26
). Finally, a straightforward calculation using (
26
) again shows that

\displaystyle\nabla_{T}T=\frac{x_{1}x_{n}}{x_{1}^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}\left(x_{n}\partial_{1}-x_{1}\partial_{n}\right)\,.

Putting everything together, we conclude the proof of equation (
18
).
